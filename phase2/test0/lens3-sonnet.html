Lens 3 · Bayesian inference

Link type. Inside the simplified model of Xie et al., the link is same-math: the model's prediction is Bayes' rule written out (Xie et al. 2022, p. 2). For a real LLM it is only a structural analogy: the paper proves a result inside a stylized model and tests it on synthetic data. It does not show that real LLMs compute posteriors (pp. 7, 10).

An instance first. Give a model this prompt (the text we feed it):

Albert Einstein was German
Mahatma Gandhi was Indian
Marie Curie was …

The most likely next word is “Polish” (Xie et al. 2022, p. 1). Nobody told the model the task, and no weight changed. This is in-context learning (ICL): learning a task from examples inside the prompt.

What happens, step by step (the numbers are ours, toy numbers, our world W4). Suppose the model holds two hidden ideas, called concepts, about what such a list could be. A: “the answer is the person's nationality”. B: “the answer is the person's occupation”. Before any example it has no favourite. This starting belief is the prior: 1/2 for A, 1/2 for B.

Each example is evidence. The likelihood of an example is how probable it is to see an example like this if the concept were true. Say 0.2 under A and 0.05 under B. After one example, we weigh each concept as prior × likelihood: A gets 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.10, B gets 0.5 × 0.05 = 0.025. Divide by the total, 0.125, to get the posterior, the belief after the evidence: A = 0.8, B = 0.2.

The pattern: every example multiplies A's weight by 0.2 and B's by 0.05, so the odds A : B grow by 4 each time (1 : 1, then 4 : 1, 16 : 1). After n examples, P(A) = 4n / (4n + 1).

The model does not simply pick the winner. It takes a weighted average, called marginalization. Say the right answer (a nationality) has chance 0.9 under A and 0.1 under B (ours). Then P(right) = 0.9 × P(A) + 0.1 × P(B):

The formula, as a short name for this pattern. Xie et al. write the prediction as

\[ p(\text{output}\mid\text{prompt})=\int p(\text{output}\mid\text{concept},\text{prompt})\;p(\text{concept}\mid\text{prompt})\,d(\text{concept}) \]

(Xie et al. 2022, p. 2)

Read aloud: “the chance of an output, given the prompt, is the sum over all concepts of two things multiplied: the chance of that output if that concept is the true one, and the chance that the concept is the true one, given the prompt.” The integral sign is a sum over continuous concepts; with two concepts it is our line above. The second factor comes from Bayes' rule (Gelman et al. 2013, p. 7):

\[ p(\text{concept}\mid\text{prompt})\;\propto\;p(\text{prompt}\mid\text{concept})\;p(\text{concept}) \]

“Posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior”; “proportional” means we divide by one total at the end. Xie et al. show that the weight of a wrong concept θ, compared with the true one θ*, is proportional to en·rn(θ) p(θ) (p. 6). Here rn is the average log of the likelihood ratio per example (“log” turns products into sums). In our toy, r = ln(0.05/0.2) = −1.386 per example, and e−1.386 n = 4−n.

Check. For n = 2: weights 0.5 × 0.04 = 0.02 and 0.5 × 0.0025 = 0.00125. P(A) = 0.02 / 0.02125 = 0.941. Same as the table and as 16/17.

Why a language model would do this. The authors assume every pretraining document is made by a hidden concept θ that sets the rules of a hidden Markov model (HMM): a text generator whose hidden state changes step by step and writes one word per step. They also assume the LM fits this pretraining distribution exactly (Xie et al. 2022, pp. 2–3). To continue a document well, such an LM must infer θ; a prompt of examples with one shared concept lets it do the same thing. If p(concept | prompt) concentrates on that concept, the LM “selects” it (p. 3).

Limits and hedges.

  • Theorem 1 says: under the assumptions and a distinguishability condition, the error rate approaches the best possible as n grows (Xie et al. 2022, p. 7). Distinguishability means the evidence per example must beat a fixed cost, because glued-together examples look odd to the model (pp. 4, 7).
  • The tests use a small synthetic dataset (GINC) with a 4-layer Transformer in the ablations. If the pretraining data has one concept only, or the prompt concept is unseen, ICL fails (p. 10). Larger models may gain beyond pretraining loss (p. 10), which a pure posterior story does not explain. The authors call it “a first step” (p. 12).
  • GPT-3's authors stay agnostic on whether ICL learns a task from scratch or only recognizes a pattern seen in training; they think word scrambling appears to be learned at test time but “cannot confirm” it (Brown et al. 2020, pp. 4, 24, 34).

What this lens says the model knows. Through this lens, the model knows a large menu of concepts learned in pretraining, with a prior weight on each, and a way to score how well a prompt fits each one. A prompt is not new teaching. It is evidence that shifts weight among concepts the model already has, and the answer is the weighted vote of all of them. So ICL can be strong when the right concept is in the menu and the examples tell it apart, and it cannot rise above what that concept itself can answer (the 0.9 ceiling in our toy). Whether real LLMs work this way is not shown.
