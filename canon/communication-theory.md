# Communication theory — Canon

## Scope
This catalog covers communication theory as an applied social-science field. It is organized into seven strands: (1) the map of the field (Craig's seven traditions); (2) transmission and cybernetic models; (3) media effects (limited effects, agenda setting, framing, spiral of silence, cultivation, uses and gratifications); (4) diffusion through networks; (5) social and critical theory of the public sphere and media (Habermas, Luhmann, Carey, McLuhan, Hall); (6) risk and crisis communication; and (7) misinformation and computational work. It leaves out interpersonal, organizational and rhetorical theory except where Craig's map introduces them, and it leaves information theory and network science to their own AI4Read fields. Granovetter (1973) appears only as a cross-reference. Two good wider-angle books were cut to keep the list at 28 items: McQuail's *Media and Mass Communication Theory* (McQuail & Deuze, 7th ed., SAGE 2020) and Peters' *Speaking into the Air* (Chicago, 1999). Read them if you want a textbook synthesis or a philosophical history of the idea of "communication". The selection was checked against three graduate and advanced syllabi: USC Annenberg COMM 525 (Gross, 2013), Colorado JOUR 7011 Proseminar in Communication Theory (Calabrese & Peck, 2003) and Wisconsin J565 Effects of Mass Communication (Rojas, 2011).

## Canon

### Strand: The map of the field

#### craig-1999-field
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Craig, R. T. (1999). Communication Theory as a Field. *Communication Theory*, 9(2), 119–161. DOI: 10.1111/j.1468-2885.1999.tb00355.x
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Craig argues that communication theory is a "practical discipline": a dialogue among seven traditions (rhetorical, semiotic, phenomenological, cybernetic, sociopsychological, sociocultural, critical), each of which defines communication differently and so sees different problems. It is the most widely used map of the field, and it shows which tradition a given theory or paper speaks from.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://academic.oup.com/ct/article/9/2/119/4133096

#### craig-2007-theorizing
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Craig, R. T., & Muller, H. L. (Eds.) (2007). *Theorizing Communication: Readings Across Traditions*. SAGE. ISBN 9781412952378.
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** grad
- **why:** An anthology built on Craig's 1999 map, with one unit per tradition. Each unit pairs primary sources (Saussure, Peirce, Buber, Gadamer, Watzlawick, Mead, Marx, Horkheimer and Adorno, Habermas) with an editors' introduction. It is the best single overview text for seeing the whole field through its original voices.
- **focus:** Unit II (ch. 5, "Communication Theory as a Field"); Unit I ch. 4 "A Cultural Approach to Communication"; Unit VI "The Cybernetic Tradition" (ch. 19–22); Unit IX "The Critical Tradition" (ch. 31–35)
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://collegepublishing.sagepub.com/products/227932

### Strand: Transmission and cybernetic models

#### shannon-1949-mathematical
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Shannon, C. E., & Weaver, W. (1949). *The Mathematical Theory of Communication*. University of Illinois Press. Contains Shannon's paper (from *Bell System Technical Journal*, 27, 379–423, 623–656, 1948) and Weaver's essay "Recent contributions to the mathematical theory of communication".
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** This book is the source of the "transmission model" (source, transmitter, channel and noise, receiver), the most influential and most criticized picture of communication. Shannon defines information as reduction of uncertainty and deliberately sets meaning aside. Weaver's essay then speculates about "semantic" and "effectiveness" levels, and that speculation is what social scientists took up. You need to read both to see what communication theory borrowed from engineering and what it misread.
- **focus:** Weaver's essay (whole); Shannon's Introduction and Part I "Discrete Noiseless Systems", especially §2 "The Discrete Source of Information" and §3 "The Series of Approximations to English"
- **access:** free: https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf (Shannon's paper only; Weaver's essay is need-copy)
- **translation:** 『通信の数学的理論』植松友彦訳, ちくま学芸文庫(筑摩書房), 2009
- **verified:** https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA00578004?l=en

#### wiener-1948-cybernetics
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Wiener, N. (1948/2019). *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*. MIT Press (2nd ed. 1961; MIT Press reissue 2019, ISBN 9780262537841).
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Wiener defines communication as control through feedback, the same in machines, organisms and societies. This is the root of Craig's "cybernetic tradition" and of systems thinking in Luhmann and in social simulation. Much of the book is technical mathematics, so read it for the framing ideas rather than cover to cover.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『サイバネティックス――動物と機械における制御と通信』池原止戈夫・弥永昌吉・室賀三郎訳, 岩波書店, 2011
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780262537841

#### lasswell-1948-structure
- **type:** chapter
- **citation:** Lasswell, H. D. (1948). The Structure and Function of Communication in Society. In L. Bryson (Ed.), *The Communication of Ideas* (pp. 37–51). Institute for Religious and Social Studies / Harper & Brothers.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Source of the formula "Who says what in which channel to whom with what effect?", which divided early mass communication research into control, content, media, audience and effects analysis. It also gives a functionalist account of what communication does for a society. It is short, and it explains why the field came to be organized around effects.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lasswell%27s_model_of_communication

### Strand: Media effects

#### lippmann-1922-publicopinion
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Lippmann, W. (1922). *Public Opinion*. Harcourt, Brace. (Project Gutenberg eBook #6456.)
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** People act on "pictures in their heads" (a pseudo-environment) rather than on the world itself, and the press and stereotypes build those pictures. The book anticipates agenda setting, framing and the problem of democratic competence a generation before these were tested empirically. It is the origin text of public-opinion and media-effects thinking.
- **focus:** Part I, ch. I "The World Outside and the Pictures in Our Heads"; Part III "Stereotypes" (ch. VI–X); Part VII "Newspapers", especially ch. XXIII "The Nature of News"
- **access:** free: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6456/pg6456-images.html
- **translation:** 『世論』(上・下) 掛川トミ子訳, 岩波文庫, 1987
- **verified:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6456

#### katz-1955-personalinfluence
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Katz, E., & Lazarsfeld, P. F. (1955). *Personal Influence: The Part Played by People in the Flow of Mass Communications*. Free Press. (Transaction re-issue 2005/2006 with a new preface by Katz.)
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The Decatur study puts the two-step flow and opinion leaders at the centre of the limited-effects paradigm: media messages are filtered through primary groups and interpersonal networks. It is the founding bridge between media effects and network science, and it is still the reference point for debates about influencers and one-step flow.
- **focus:** Ch. 1; Katz's 2006 preface "Lazarsfeld's Legacy: The Power of Limited Effects" (pp. xv–xxvii)
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781412805070

#### mccombs-1972-agendasetting
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** McCombs, M. E., & Shaw, D. L. (1972). The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 36(2), 176–187. DOI: 10.1086/267990
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** The Chapel Hill study shows that the issues the media emphasize become the issues voters consider important ("what to think about", not "what to think"). It reopened the case for strong cognitive media effects, and it set the template for a design you can compute directly: content salience against public salience.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/36/2/176/1853310

#### entman-1993-framing
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Entman, R. M. (1993). Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm. *Journal of Communication*, 43(4), 51–58. DOI: 10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Entman gives the standard working definition of framing: selecting and making salient some aspects of a perceived reality so as to promote a problem definition, a causal interpretation, a moral evaluation and a treatment recommendation. Most computational frame-detection codebooks still use these four functions.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1993.tb01304.x

#### goffman-1974-frameanalysis
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Goffman, E. (1974). *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience*. (Northeastern University Press edition, ISBN 0-930350-91-X.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The sociological source of "frame": the interpretive schemata (primary frameworks, natural and social) that answer "what is going on here?". Media framing research borrowed the word but mostly dropped Goffman's micro-sociology. Reading him shows what that borrowing lost and gives the theory more depth.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_analysis

#### noelleneumann-1974-spiral
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Noelle-Neumann, E. (1974). The Spiral of Silence: A Theory of Public Opinion. *Journal of Communication*, 24(2), 43–51. DOI: 10.1111/j.1460-2466.1974.tb00367.x
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** People fear isolation and use a "quasi-statistical sense" to read the climate of opinion. Those who think they are in the minority fall silent, which makes the majority look larger still, so the process feeds on itself. The result is a dynamic, feedback-driven theory of opinion that maps directly onto agent-based simulation and onto self-censorship online.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1974.tb00367.x

#### gerbner-1976-cultivation
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Gerbner, G., & Gross, L. (1976). Living with Television: The Violence Profile. *Journal of Communication*, 26(2), 172–199. DOI: 10.1111/j.1460-2466.1976.tb01397.x
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The founding statement of cultivation theory. Long-term, cumulative exposure to television's consistent symbolic world shapes viewers' beliefs about social reality: heavy viewers give the "television answer" and see a meaner, more dangerous world. It is the model for effects that are slow, diffuse and found at the level of the whole system.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.1976.tb01397.x

#### katz-1973-usesgratifications
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Katz, E., Blumler, J. G., & Gurevitch, M. (1973). Uses and Gratifications Research. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 37(4), 509–523. DOI: 10.1086/268109
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It turns the effects question around, from "what do media do to people?" to "what do people do with media?". It sets out the assumptions of the active-audience approach: social and psychological needs produce expectations, which produce media selection and gratifications. Selective-exposure and platform-choice research descends from this paper.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/37/4/509/1913518

#### scheufele-2007-framingpriming
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Scheufele, D. A., & Tewksbury, D. (2007). Framing, Agenda Setting, and Priming: The Evolution of Three Media Effects Models. *Journal of Communication*, 57(1), 9–20. DOI: 10.1111/j.0021-9916.2007.00326.x
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** It separates the three main cognitive-effects models by mechanism. Agenda setting and priming work through accessibility (what is salient in memory); framing works through applicability (which interpretive schema a message invokes). It is the conceptual hygiene needed before you operationalize any of the three.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0021-9916.2007.00326.x

#### bennett-2008-minimaleffects
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Bennett, W. L., & Iyengar, S. (2008). A New Era of Minimal Effects? The Changing Foundations of Political Communication. *Journal of Communication*, 58(4), 707–731. DOI: 10.1111/j.1460-2466.2008.00410.x
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Audience fragmentation and partisan selective exposure in a high-choice media environment may bring back "minimal effects", now for structural reasons (reinforcement, preaching to the converted) rather than because of interpersonal filtering. It started a major debate (see Holbert et al.'s reply in the same journal) on whether the mid-century theories still hold.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1460-2466.2008.00410.x

### Strand: Diffusion and networks

#### rogers-2003-diffusion
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Rogers, E. M. (2003). *Diffusion of Innovations* (5th ed.). Free Press. ISBN 0743222091.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It synthesizes thousands of diffusion studies into one framework: innovation attributes, communication channels, time and the social system. It explains the S-curve, adopter categories, opinion leaders, homophily and heterophily, and critical mass. It is the canonical bridge from communication to epidemiology-style and network-contagion models.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0743222091

#### centola-2007-complexcontagion
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Centola, D., & Macy, M. (2007). Complex Contagions and the Weakness of Long Ties. *American Journal of Sociology*, 113(3), 702–734. DOI: 10.1086/521848
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** For behaviours that need reinforcement from several contacts (complex contagions), the long "weak ties" that speed up simple information diffusion (Granovetter) can slow spread, and clustered, wide bridges matter instead. It updates two-step-flow and diffusion thinking with formal network models. The experimental follow-up is Centola (2010), *Science*, 329, 1194–1197.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1086/521848

### Strand: Social and critical theory

#### habermas-1989-publicsphere
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Habermas, J. (1962/1989). *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere: An Inquiry into a Category of Bourgeois Society* (T. Burger, Trans.). MIT Press.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** A historical-normative account of how a reasoning public of private persons arose in eighteenth-century coffeehouses, salons and the press. It then argues that this public sphere was "refeudalized" by commercial mass media, PR and the welfare state. It is the normative yardstick behind nearly every argument about deliberation, polarization and platform governance.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『公共性の構造転換』未來社, 1973 (第2版 1994)
- **verified:** https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262581080/the-structural-transformation-of-the-public-sphere/

#### carey-1989-cultural
- **type:** chapter
- **citation:** Carey, J. W. (1989). A Cultural Approach to Communication. In *Communication as Culture: Essays on Media and Society*. Unwin Hyman / Routledge (essay orig. 1975). Reprinted as ch. 4 in Craig & Muller (2007).
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It contrasts the "transmission view" (sending messages across space for control) with a "ritual view" (communication as the sustaining of shared beliefs and community over time). It is the best short antidote to treating communication only as information transfer, and it explains why sharing misinformation can work as identity ritual rather than as belief.
- **focus:** the essay "A Cultural Approach to Communication" (whole)
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://collegepublishing.sagepub.com/products/227932

#### hall-1973-encoding
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Hall, S. (1973). *Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse*. CCCS Stencilled Occasional Paper No. 7. Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies, University of Birmingham. (Revised as "Encoding/decoding" in Hall et al. (Eds.), *Culture, Media, Language*, Hutchinson, 1980, pp. 128–137.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Meaning is not transmitted intact. Producers encode it under institutional codes, and audiences decode it from social positions, which yields dominant-hegemonic, negotiated or oppositional readings of a "preferred reading". It is the cultural-studies answer to Shannon and Lasswell, and the basis of active-audience research on polarized interpretation.
- **focus:** whole (21 pp.)
- **access:** free: https://epapers.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/2962/1/Hall%2C_1973%2C_Encoding_and_Decoding_in_the_Television_Discourse.pdf
- **verified:** https://epapers.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/2962/

#### luhmann-2000-massmedia
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Luhmann, N. (2000). *The Reality of the Mass Media* (K. Cross, Trans.). Stanford University Press. (German orig. *Die Realität der Massenmedien*, 1996.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** A systems-theory account of mass media as a self-referential (autopoietic) social system that runs on the code information/non-information. "Whatever we know about our society, or indeed about the world in which we live, we know through the mass media." It is a short, demanding, non-normative counterweight to Habermas, and a natural fit for second-order cybernetic and simulation thinking.
- **focus:** whole (154 pp.)
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『マスメディアのリアリティ』木鐸社, 2005
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0804740771

#### mcluhan-1964-understandingmedia
- **type:** book
- **citation:** McLuhan, M. (1964/1994). *Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man*. McGraw-Hill (1964); MIT Press ed. 1994 with an introduction by L. H. Lapham.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** "The medium is the message": the structural properties of a medium reshape perception and social organization more than any content it carries. It is the founding text of medium theory (with Innis), aphoristic and uneven, but it is the source of questions about what platforms and LLM interfaces do independent of content.

### Strand: Risk and crisis communication

#### kasperson-1988-amplification
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Kasperson, R. E., Renn, O., Slovic, P., Brown, H. S., Emel, J., Goble, R., et al. (1988). The Social Amplification of Risk: A Conceptual Framework. *Risk Analysis*, 8(2), 177–187. DOI: 10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The social amplification of risk framework (SARF) borrows the signal-amplification metaphor from information theory. Risk signals pass through "amplification stations" (media, experts, groups), which magnify or dampen them and produce secondary "ripple" impacts far beyond the physical hazard. It is the integrating framework for crisis and risk communication and links directly to Kunhao's heatwave and climate work.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://doi.org/10.18419/opus-7221 (submitted version in the University of Stuttgart OPUS repository, as indexed by OpenAlex; the repository's bot check blocked direct inspection, so the file was not opened)
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x

#### slovic-1987-perception
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Slovic, P. (1987). Perception of Risk. *Science*, 236(4799), 280–285. DOI: 10.1126/science.3563507
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** The psychometric paradigm. Lay risk judgments are organized along "dread" and "unknown" factors rather than expected fatalities, which explains the systematic gaps between expert and public risk rankings. It is essential for understanding why risk messages fail and why some hazards act as "signal" events.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/bitstreams/0439bd1c-eca4-4e9e-95b7-3082de3bc55b/download (author manuscript, Univ. of Oregon repository)
- **verified:** https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/b46a3ce1-1379-4822-8193-b3ffb2663f37/full

#### coombs-2007-scct
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Coombs, W. T. (2007). Protecting Organization Reputations During a Crisis: The Development and Application of Situational Crisis Communication Theory. *Corporate Reputation Review*, 10(3), 163–176. DOI: 10.1057/palgrave.crr.1550049
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Situational Crisis Communication Theory (SCCT) applies attribution theory to crises. Stakeholders' attributions of crisis responsibility (shaped by crisis type, crisis history and prior reputation) determine reputational threat, and response strategies (deny, diminish, rebuild, bolster) should be matched to that threat. It is the dominant evidence-based theory of organizational crisis response.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1057/palgrave.crr.1550049.pdf
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.crr.1550049

### Strand: Misinformation and computational communication

#### vosoughi-2018-spread
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Vosoughi, S., Roy, D., & Aral, S. (2018). The spread of true and false news online. *Science*, 359(6380), 1146–1151. DOI: 10.1126/science.aap9559
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Using all fact-checked rumour cascades on Twitter from 2006 to 2017, the paper finds that falsehood diffused farther, faster, deeper and more broadly than truth, and that humans rather than bots drove the difference. The authors propose a novelty and emotion account. It is the landmark computational study tying diffusion-network metrics (cascade depth, breadth, structural virality) to misinformation.
- **focus:** whole (with supplementary materials for the cascade metrics)
- **access:** free: https://politics.media.mit.edu/papers/Vosoughi_Science.pdf
- **verified:** https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/the-spread-of-true-and-false-news-online/

#### lazer-2018-fakenews
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Lazer, D. M. J., Baum, M. A., Benkler, Y., Berinsky, A. J., Greenhill, K. M., Menczer, F., Metzger, M. J., Nyhan, B., et al. (2018). The science of fake news. *Science*, 359(6380), 1094–1096. DOI: 10.1126/science.aao2998
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** UG
- **why:** A short interdisciplinary agenda-setting statement. It defines fake news as fabricated information that mimics news-media content in form but not in organizational process or intent. It frames the problem as a joint failure of individuals and platforms, and it sets out the research and intervention agenda that much later computational work followed.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.07903
- **verified:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07903

#### lewandowsky-2012-misinformation
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Lewandowsky, S., Ecker, U. K. H., Seifert, C. M., Schwarz, N., & Cook, J. (2012). Misinformation and Its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13(3), 106–131. DOI: 10.1177/1529100612451018
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The canonical cognitive review of why retractions fail. Misinformation keeps influencing reasoning even after it is corrected (the continued influence effect) because corrections leave gaps in people's mental models, and familiarity, overkill and worldview pressures add to this. The review also shows what works: warnings, repetition of the correction, and alternative causal explanations. Later work has substantially qualified the "backfire" claims, so read it alongside newer replications.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100612451018

## Concept spine
- **Seven traditions** — Craig's map of the field: rhetorical, semiotic, phenomenological, cybernetic, sociopsychological, sociocultural, critical (grad)
- **Communication as practical discipline** — theory as a reflective resource for everyday metadiscourse about communication, not a unified science (grad)
- **Transmission view** — communication as sending signals across space to control or inform receivers (UG)
- **Ritual view** — communication as the maintenance of a shared community and its beliefs over time (UG)
- **Information (Shannon)** — reduction of uncertainty about which message, from a set of possible messages, was sent; independent of meaning (UG)
- **Entropy** — average uncertainty of a source, H = −Σ p log p; the limit for lossless compression (grad)
- **Noise and channel capacity** — the maximum rate at which information can be sent reliably over a noisy channel (grad)
- **Redundancy** — predictable structure in messages that guards against noise (UG)
- **Feedback** — output fed back as input to steer a system toward a goal; the core of cybernetics (UG)
- **Lasswell formula** — who says what in which channel to whom with what effect (HS)
- **Pseudo-environment** — the mediated "pictures in our heads" that people actually act on (UG)
- **Stereotype (Lippmann)** — a culturally supplied simplifying template that shapes perception before experience (UG)
- **Magic bullet / hypodermic model** — the (largely retrospective, partly mythical) idea that media inject effects directly into passive mass audiences (UG)
- **Limited effects paradigm** — the mid-century finding that media mostly reinforce existing attitudes, filtered by groups and predispositions (UG)
- **Two-step flow** — media influence passes through opinion leaders, who relay it to less attentive people (UG)
- **Opinion leader** — a person who disproportionately shapes others' views within their own social circle (UG)
- **Selective exposure** — the tendency to seek media that agree with existing views (UG)
- **Agenda setting** — media salience of issues transfers to public salience: "what to think about" (UG)
- **Attribute (second-level) agenda setting** — the salience of an issue's attributes also transfers to the public (grad)
- **Priming** — media emphasis changes the criteria people use to evaluate leaders or issues (grad)
- **Accessibility vs applicability** — salience in memory (agenda setting and priming) versus fit of an interpretive schema (framing) (grad)
- **Framing (Entman)** — selecting and emphasizing aspects of reality to promote a problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation and remedy (UG)
- **Frame (Goffman)** — a schema of interpretation that organizes experience: "what is going on here?" (grad)
- **Primary framework** — Goffman's basic natural or social frame that renders events meaningful (grad)
- **Equivalence vs emphasis framing** — logically identical descriptions (e.g., lives saved vs lost) versus selecting different considerations (grad)
- **Spiral of silence** — fear of isolation makes perceived minorities fall silent, so the dominant opinion appears larger and grows (UG)
- **Quasi-statistical sense** — people's intuitive monitoring of the distribution of opinion around them (grad)
- **Cultivation** — cumulative heavy exposure to a medium's consistent symbolic world shapes beliefs about social reality (UG)
- **Mainstreaming and resonance** — heavy viewing makes divergent groups' outlooks converge; effects are stronger when TV matches lived experience (grad)
- **Mean world syndrome** — heavy viewers overestimate danger and distrust others (UG)
- **Uses and gratifications** — audiences actively choose media to satisfy social and psychological needs (UG)
- **Knowledge gap hypothesis** — more media information widens the gaps between higher- and lower-SES groups (UG)
- **Third-person effect** — people believe media influence others more than themselves (UG)
- **New era of minimal effects** — fragmentation and partisan selection make persuasion rare and reinforcement common (grad)
- **Diffusion S-curve** — cumulative adoption over time follows a logistic-like curve (UG)
- **Adopter categories** — innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards (HS)
- **Homophily / heterophily** — similarity or difference between communicating individuals; homophily eases communication but limits novelty (UG)
- **Critical mass** — the adoption level beyond which diffusion becomes self-sustaining (UG)
- **Complex contagion** — adoption that needs reinforcement from several contacts, so clustered ties beat long weak ties (grad)
- **Public sphere** — the space where private persons reason together about common affairs, open in principle to all (grad)
- **Refeudalization** — the public sphere's collapse into staged publicity by organized interests and commercial media (grad)
- **Autopoietic media system** — Luhmann's mass media as a self-reproducing system operating on the code information/non-information (grad)
- **The medium is the message** — a medium's form restructures perception and social relations regardless of content (UG)
- **Encoding/decoding** — meaning is produced at encoding and re-produced at decoding; it can be dominant, negotiated or oppositional (grad)
- **Preferred reading** — the interpretation a text's encoding invites without guaranteeing it (grad)
- **Social amplification of risk** — social and individual "stations" magnify or attenuate risk signals, producing ripple effects (grad)
- **Psychometric paradigm (dread/unknown)** — risk perception organized by dread and unfamiliarity rather than expected harm (UG)
- **Crisis responsibility (SCCT)** — stakeholders' attribution of blame, which sets reputational threat and the appropriate response strategy (UG)
- **Continued influence effect** — retracted misinformation keeps shaping inference even after people accept the correction (UG)
- **Structural virality** — a cascade metric distinguishing broadcast-like spread from person-to-person chains (grad)

## Canonical instances
- **Erie County study (1940)** — panel study of the U.S. presidential campaign (*The People's Choice*) that found reinforcement rather than conversion and gave the first hint of a two-step flow — katz-1955-personalinfluence
- **Decatur study** — interviews with Decatur, Illinois women tracing influence in marketing, fashion, public affairs and movie-going to everyday opinion leaders — katz-1955-personalinfluence
- **Chapel Hill study (1968)** — undecided voters' issue priorities matched news emphasis almost perfectly in rank order — mccombs-1972-agendasetting
- **"Asian disease" problem** — Entman's illustration that logically equivalent "saved" and "die" wordings flip choices; cross-ref decision & game theory (prospect theory) — entman-1993-framing
- **"Television answer" survey items** — heavy viewers overestimate violence and their own chance of victimization, compared with light viewers — gerbner-1976-cultivation
- **Allensbach "train test"** — survey item asking whether people would discuss a contested issue with a stranger on a long train journey, used to measure willingness to speak out — noelleneumann-1974-spiral
- **Hybrid seed corn in Iowa (Ryan & Gross)** — classic S-curve diffusion study among farmers, retold by Rogers — rogers-2003-diffusion
- **Water boiling in a Peruvian village** — a failed health campaign showing that cultural compatibility and change-agent heterophily govern adoption — rogers-2003-diffusion
- **Online health-network experiment (Centola 2010)** — behaviour spread farther in clustered-lattice networks than in random networks, as complex contagion predicts — centola-2007-complexcontagion
- **Eighteenth-century coffeehouses and salons** — Habermas' paradigm case of a rational-critical bourgeois public — habermas-1989-publicsphere
- **Three Mile Island (1979)** — a small physical harm with enormous social ripple effects; the archetypal "signal event" in SARF — kasperson-1988-amplification
- **Expert vs lay risk rankings (nuclear power vs X-rays)** — the two groups rank hazards very differently; the gap is explained by the dread/unknown factor space — slovic-1987-perception
- **Warehouse-fire paradigm (Johnson & Seifert)** — a retracted "volatile materials" cause still drives participants' inferences — lewandowsky-2012-misinformation
- **Twitter rumour cascades 2006–2017** — false news reached more people faster and deeper than true news, with humans rather than bots responsible — vosoughi-2018-spread

## Bridges
- [same-math] Shannon information and entropy ↔ information theory: entropy and channel capacity — Shannon's half of the 1949 book *is* information theory; H = −Σ p log p is literally the same formula.
- [structural-analogy] Kasperson's amplification stations ↔ information theory: signal amplification and noise — SARF borrows the vocabulary of signal and amplifier, but there is no channel-capacity mathematics behind it.
- [same-math] complex contagion thresholds ↔ complex systems: threshold cascade models on networks — Centola & Macy's adoption rules are the same threshold dynamics studied in network cascade models.
- [structural-analogy] Rogers' diffusion S-curve ↔ complex systems: logistic and SI epidemic growth — the empirical adoption curve resembles epidemic models, but Rogers' framework is descriptive rather than a fitted dynamical system.
- [structural-analogy] spiral of silence ↔ decision & game theory: information cascades and pluralistic ignorance — individuals infer the majority from others' visible (non-)expression and act strategically, so public signals can diverge from private opinion.
- [structural-analogy] equivalence framing ↔ decision & game theory: prospect theory reference dependence — gain/loss wording shifts the reference point; Tversky & Kahneman's "Asian disease" problem is the shared canonical instance.
- [structural-analogy] continued influence effect ↔ Bayesian theory & causal inference: belief updating with causal models — corrections that remove a cause without supplying an alternative leave the causal model incomplete, so people fall back on the retracted cause.
- [structural-analogy] public sphere and refeudalization ↔ political science: deliberative democracy and the quality of democratic input — the same normative ideal, assessed against media-system structure.
- [structural-analogy] Lippmann's pseudo-environment and Goffman's frames ↔ theory of mind: mental models and schemas — action is guided by internal representations of situations built from limited cues.
- [structural-analogy] framing and priming effects ↔ LLMs: prompt sensitivity — equivalent or emphasis-shifted wordings change model outputs, so framing designs can be used to audit LLMs.
