# Economics & finance — Canon

## Scope
This canon treats economics and finance as the study of how decentralized agents form beliefs and expectations, exchange information through prices, contracts and stories, and occasionally coordinate on self-fulfilling crises. It runs through eight strands: on-ramps and core texts; markets as information processors (Hayek, lemons, signaling, efficient markets, Grossman–Stiglitz); expectations, higher-order beliefs and narratives (Keynes ch. 12, rational expectations, Lucas critique, Morris–Shin, Shiller); behavioral economics; finance theory (portfolio choice, option pricing, Kelly betting, adaptive and fat-tailed markets); crises and macro-finance (bank runs, Minsky, Kindleberger, Reinhart–Rogoff); Japan's bubble and lost decade; and complexity economics. It deliberately leaves out growth theory, trade, labor, public finance and econometrics as standalone strands. Kahneman & Tversky (prospect theory), Nudge, Sharpe's CAPM and Akerlof & Shiller's *Animal Spirits* are left to the behavioral/decision-theory canon, or covered here only through concepts, to keep the list at 30.

### Strand: Markets as information processors

#### hayek-1945-knowledge
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Hayek, F. A. (1945). The Use of Knowledge in Society. *American Economic Review*, 35(4), 519–530.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It recasts the economic problem as using knowledge that is dispersed, local and tacit, rather than as optimizing given known data. The price system is presented as a mechanism that compresses and transmits that knowledge, and Hayek himself compares it to "a system of telecommunications". It is the founding text for treating markets as information processors.
- **focus:** whole (short essay)
- **access:** free: https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html
- **verified:** https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/hykKnw.html

#### akerlof-1970-lemons
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Akerlof, G. A. (1970). The Market for "Lemons": Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 84(3), 488–500. https://doi.org/10.2307/1879431
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It shows that when sellers know quality and buyers do not, average-quality pricing drives good products out, and a market can unravel completely. This adverse-selection logic now explains insurance, credit, labor markets, and arguably markets for information and content too.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.2307/1879431

#### spence-1973-signaling
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Spence, M. (1973). Job Market Signaling. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 87(3), 355–374. https://doi.org/10.2307/1882010
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** It is the counterpart to Akerlof: the informed side takes a costly action, such as education, to reveal its type, and the outcome is a separating equilibrium in which beliefs and actions confirm each other. It is the prototype signaling game, and costly signaling reappears in biology, credentials and online reputation.
- **focus:** whole. A free companion is Spence's 2001 Nobel lecture "Signaling in Retrospect and the Informational Structure of Markets": https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/spence-lecture.pdf
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.2307/1882010

#### fama-1970-efficient
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Fama, E. F. (1970). Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work. *Journal of Finance*, 25(2), 383–417. https://doi.org/10.2307/2325486
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** It defines market efficiency as prices "fully reflecting" available information and introduces the weak, semi-strong and strong forms. It also makes the joint-hypothesis problem explicit: efficiency can only be tested together with a model of expected returns. Every later debate about bubbles, anomalies and behavioral finance argues with this paper.
- **focus:** whole. A free companion is Fama's 2013 Nobel lecture "Two Pillars of Asset Pricing": https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/fama-lecture.pdf
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.2307/2325486

#### grossman-1980-impossibility
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Grossman, S. J., & Stiglitz, J. E. (1980). On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets. *American Economic Review*, 70(3), 393–408.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** If prices fully revealed information, nobody would pay to gather it, so prices cannot be fully informative. Equilibrium therefore needs noise, and an "equilibrium degree of disequilibrium". The noisy rational-expectations model in this paper is the cleanest place to see price as a noisy Bayesian signal.
- **focus:** whole (the core model sections)
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v70y1980i3p393-408.html

#### stiglitz-2002-paradigm
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Stiglitz, J. E. (2002). Information and the Change in the Paradigm in Economics. *American Economic Review*, 92(3), 460–501. https://doi.org/10.1257/00028280260136363 (Nobel Prize lecture delivered 8 December 2001.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It is a long, accessible survey of the whole information-economics program: screening, signaling, efficiency wages, credit rationing, and the failure of the invisible-hand theorems under imperfect information. It works as a map that connects Akerlof, Spence, Grossman–Stiglitz and much of macro-finance.
- **focus:** whole (the Nobel PDF is the free version)
- **access:** free: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/stiglitz-lecture.pdf
- **verified:** https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2001/stiglitz/lecture/

### Strand: Expectations, higher-order beliefs & narratives

#### keynes-1936-general
- **type:** chapter
- **citation:** Keynes, J. M. (1936). *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*. Macmillan. Chapter 12, "The State of Long-Term Expectation".
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Chapter 12 is where Keynes argues that long-term expectations rest on fragile convention rather than calculable probability. It contains the "beauty contest" image of investors guessing what average opinion expects average opinion to be, and the "animal spirits" that drive investment. It is the origin of higher-order-belief thinking in economics.
- **focus:** Chapter 12 "The State of Long-Term Expectation"
- **access:** free: https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks03/0300071h/printall.html (public domain in Australia and in Japan, since Keynes died in 1946; still under copyright in the US until 2032, so check local law)
- **verified:** https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks03/0300071h/printall.html

#### muth-1961-rational
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Muth, J. F. (1961). Rational Expectations and the Theory of Price Movements. *Econometrica*, 29(3), 315–335. https://doi.org/10.2307/1909635
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** It proposes that agents' subjective expectations equal the model's own predictions, so expectations are endogenous and model-consistent rather than ad hoc. This became the default assumption of modern macro and finance, and it is the baseline that behavioral and narrative economics depart from.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.2307/1909635

#### lucas-1976-critique
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Lucas, R. E., Jr. (1976). Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique. *Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series on Public Policy*, 1, 19–46. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2231(76)80003-6
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Estimated behavioral relationships embed agents' expectations about policy, so they shift when policy changes. Models fitted to past data therefore mislead about interventions. This is the economist's version of the distinction between a structural (invariant) model and a reduced-form correlation.
- **focus:** whole. A free companion is Lucas's 1995 Nobel lecture "Monetary Neutrality": https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/lucas-lecture.pdf
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-2231(76)80003-6

#### morris-2002-public
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Morris, S., & Shin, H. S. (2002). Social Value of Public Information. *American Economic Review*, 92(5), 1521–1534. https://doi.org/10.1257/000282802762024610
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** It formalizes Keynes's beauty contest. When agents also want to match each other's actions, they over-weight public signals because those signals predict what others believe. As a result, more precise public information, such as central-bank communication, can lower welfare. It is the key model for how broadcast information shapes coordination, with direct relevance to media and misinformation research.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1257/000282802762024610

#### shiller-2019-narrative
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Shiller, R. J. (2019). *Narrative Economics: How Stories Go Viral and Drive Major Economic Events*. Princeton University Press. ISBN 9780691182292.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Shiller argues that contagious popular stories drive economic fluctuations and models their spread with epidemic (SIR-type) dynamics. Examples include the Laffer curve, Bitcoin, and stories of technological unemployment. It is the most direct bridge from economics to communication research and computational text analysis, and it matches Kunhao's narrative-economics data interests.
- **focus:** whole. A free precursor is Shiller's 2017 AEA presidential address, *AER* 107(4), 967–1004 (NBER WP 23075).
- **access:** need-copy (free precursor: https://www.nber.org/papers/w23075.pdf)
- **verified:** https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691182292/narrative-economics

#### shiller-2015-exuberance
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Shiller, R. J. (2015). *Irrational Exuberance* (revised and expanded 3rd ed.). Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400865536
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It is the empirical case that stock, bond and housing prices undergo speculative bubbles amplified by feedback loops, news media and "new era" stories. It is the data-driven companion to *Narrative Economics*. Shiller's 2013 Nobel lecture "Speculative Asset Prices" summarizes the research: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/shiller-lecture.pdf
- **focus:** whole (the chapters on cultural and psychological factors and on news media are most relevant; chapter numbers not verified here)
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400865536

### Strand: Behavioral economics

#### thaler-2015-misbehaving
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Thaler, R. H. (2015). *Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioral Economics*. W. W. Norton. ISBN 9780393080940.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It is a first-person history of how anomalies such as mental accounting, the endowment effect, self-control problems and limits to arbitrage pushed economics to admit "Humans" alongside "Econs". It is the most readable on-ramp to behavioral economics and behavioral finance. Thaler & Sunstein's *Nudge* and Kahneman & Tversky (1979) are cross-referenced to the decision-theory canon.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780393080940

### Strand: Finance theory

#### markowitz-1952-portfolio
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Markowitz, H. (1952). Portfolio Selection. *Journal of Finance*, 7(1), 77–91. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1952.tb01525.x
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It turns investing into a trade-off between mean and variance. Diversification falls out of the covariance matrix, and the efficient frontier is born. This is the linear-algebra core of all modern portfolio theory, CAPM and factor models.
- **focus:** whole. A free companion is Markowitz's 1990 Nobel lecture "Foundations of Portfolio Theory": https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/markowitz-lecture.pdf
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1952.tb01525.x

#### black-1973-options
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Black, F., & Scholes, M. (1973). The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities. *Journal of Political Economy*, 81(3), 637–654. https://doi.org/10.1086/260062
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** It prices an option by building a riskless hedge, so the price depends on volatility but not on anyone's beliefs about expected return. That is a striking case of beliefs dropping out of prices. The pricing equation is a diffusion (heat-type) PDE, and the paper founded derivatives markets.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1086/260062

#### kelly-1956-information
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Kelly, J. L., Jr. (1956). A New Interpretation of Information Rate. *Bell System Technical Journal*, 35(4), 917–926. https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1956.tb03809.x
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** A gambler with a private noisy channel who bets to maximize long-run wealth growth achieves a growth rate equal to Shannon's information rate of the channel. It is the literal bridge between information theory and investing, later popularized by Thorp, and it underlies "log-optimal" portfolio theory. This entry is cross-referenced to the information-theory canon.
- **focus:** whole (10 pages)
- **access:** free: https://archive.org/download/bstj35-4-917/bstj35-4-917.pdf
- **verified:** https://archive.org/details/bstj35-4-917

#### lo-2017-adaptive
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Lo, A. W. (2017). *Adaptive Markets: Financial Evolution at the Speed of Thought*. Princeton University Press. ISBN 9780691135144.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It reconciles efficient markets with behavioral finance through evolution. Market efficiency depends on the current population of strategies and the environment, so it waxes and wanes, and heuristics are adaptations rather than errors. It gives an ecological and complex-systems reading of finance.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780691135144

#### mandelbrot-2004-misbehavior
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Mandelbrot, B., & Hudson, R. L. (2004). *The (Mis)Behavior of Markets: A Fractal View of Risk, Ruin, and Reward*. Basic Books. ISBN 9780465043552.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It is a popular but serious critique of Gaussian finance. Price changes are fat-tailed, volatility clusters, and dependence is long-memory, so standard risk models badly underprice crashes. It is the conceptual entry point to power laws and scaling in markets.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780465043552

#### bodie-2023-investments
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Bodie, Z., Kane, A., & Marcus, A. J. (2023). *Investments*. McGraw-Hill Education. ISBN 9781264412662 (current edition; the 2020 edition is ISBN 9781260013832).
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It is the standard MBA and upper-UG investments text: portfolio theory, CAPM and factor models, efficient markets and behavioral finance, fixed income and derivatives. Use it to look up mechanics and formulas when reading the papers.
- **focus:** Use as a reference (chapter numbers not verified here).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781264412662

### Strand: Crises & macro-finance

#### diamond-1983-bankruns
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Diamond, D. W., & Dybvig, P. H. (1983). Bank Runs, Deposit Insurance, and Liquidity. *Journal of Political Economy*, 91(3), 401–419. https://doi.org/10.1086/261155 (Reprinted in Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis *Quarterly Review*, 24(1), Winter 2000.)
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Banks transform illiquid assets into liquid deposits, which creates two equilibria: a good one and a self-fulfilling run in which each depositor withdraws because they expect others to. It is the canonical model of a crisis driven purely by beliefs, and it explains why deposit insurance works. It contributed to the 2022 Nobel prize.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://www.minneapolisfed.org/research/qr/qr2412.pdf
- **verified:** https://minneapolisfed.org/research/quarterly-review/bank-runs-deposit-insurance-and-liquidity

#### minsky-1992-instability
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Minsky, H. P. (1992). The Financial Instability Hypothesis. Working Paper No. 74, Jerome Levy Economics Institute of Bard College. (Prepared for P. Arestis & M. Sawyer (Eds.), *Handbook of Radical Political Economy*, Edward Elgar, 1993.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** In about 10 pages, Minsky argues that stability breeds instability. Calm periods push financing from hedge to speculative to "Ponzi" positions, until a reversal forces debt deflation. It is the standard reference for the "Minsky moment" and complements the equilibrium-based Diamond–Dybvig model.
- **focus:** whole (short)
- **access:** free: https://www.levyinstitute.org/pubs/wp74.pdf
- **verified:** https://www.levyinstitute.org/pubs/wp74.pdf

#### aliber-2015-manias
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Aliber, R. Z., & Kindleberger, C. P. (2015). *Manias, Panics, and Crashes: A History of Financial Crises* (7th ed.). Palgrave Macmillan. ISBN 9781137525758.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Kindleberger's classic, updated by Aliber, uses a Minsky-style anatomy (displacement, boom, euphoria, distress, revulsion) to read four centuries of bubbles and panics, along with the role of the lender of last resort. It is the historian's complement to the formal models.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9781137525758

#### reinhart-2009-different
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Reinhart, C. M., & Rogoff, K. S. (2009). *This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of Financial Folly*. Princeton University Press. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400831722
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** UG
- **why:** It compiles a quantitative database of sovereign defaults, banking crises, inflations and currency crashes across eight centuries. Its main finding is that crises recur with striking regularity, while each generation's belief that "this time is different" recurs too. Use it as an empirical reference rather than reading it straight through.
- **focus:** whole as reference
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400831722

### Strand: Japan

#### koo-2009-holygrail
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Koo, R. C. (2009). *The Holy Grail of Macroeconomics: Lessons from Japan's Great Recession* (rev. ed.). John Wiley & Sons. ISBN 9780470824948 (1st ed. 2008, ISBN 9780470823873).
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** After the 1990 asset-price collapse, Japanese firms switched from maximizing profit to minimizing debt. This "balance-sheet recession" made monetary policy ineffective and required fiscal support. Koo is a Nomura economist, so the book is an influential practitioner view written from inside Japan, and it was widely re-read after 2008.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780470823873

#### hoshi-2004-japan
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Hoshi, T., & Kashyap, A. K. (2004). Japan's Financial Crisis and Economic Stagnation. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 18(1), 3–26. https://doi.org/10.1257/089533004773563412

- **level:** UG
- **why:** It is a concise, credible academic account of Japan's lost decade. Banks hid bad loans and kept lending to insolvent "zombie" firms, and the authors analyze the regulatory forbearance that let this happen. It counterweights Koo's demand-side story with a banking and incentives story.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/089533004773563412

### Strand: Complexity economics

#### arthur-1994-elfarol
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Arthur, W. B. (1994). Inductive Reasoning and Bounded Rationality. *American Economic Review*, 84(2), 406–411.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** In the El Farol bar problem, agents decide whether to attend a bar that is fun only if it is not crowded. Any commonly shared forecast defeats itself, so agents must use an evolving ecology of inductive hypotheses, and attendance self-organizes around capacity. It is the founding agent-based model of self-referential expectations and leads directly to the minority game and social simulation.
- **focus:** whole (6 pages)
- **access:** free: https://sites.santafe.edu/~wbarthur/Papers/El_Farol.pdf
- **verified:** https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v84y1994i2p406-11.html

#### farmer-2024-chaos
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Farmer, J. D. (2024). *Making Sense of Chaos: A Better Economics for a Better World*. Yale University Press. ISBN 9780300273779.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** A Santa Fe and Oxford physicist-economist makes the case for complexity economics: heterogeneous boundedly rational agents, agent-based simulation, out-of-equilibrium dynamics and data-driven models, applied to crashes, climate and policy. It is the up-to-date manifesto for the simulation-heavy approach closest to Kunhao's methods. It is cross-referenced to the complex-systems canon.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300273779/making-sense-of-chaos/

## Concept spine
- **marginal reasoning** — decisions are made by comparing the extra benefit and the extra cost of one more unit (HS)
- **equilibrium** — a state where no agent wants to change its action given what others do (UG)
- **Nash equilibrium** — each player's strategy is a best response to the others' strategies (UG)
- **coordination game / multiple equilibria** — games where agents gain by matching each other, so self-fulfilling good or bad outcomes are possible (UG)
- **social dilemma** — individually rational choices produce a collectively worse outcome (UG)
- **incomplete contracts** — not everything can be specified or enforced, so power and norms shape outcomes (UG)
- **market failure / externality** — markets fail to reach efficient outcomes because of spillovers onto third parties, public goods, market power or information problems (UG)
- **dispersed / tacit knowledge** — economically relevant knowledge is local and unwritten, so no planner can gather it (UG)
- **price as signal** — prices aggregate and transmit private information about scarcity and value (UG)
- **asymmetric information** — one side of a transaction knows more than the other (UG)
- **adverse selection** — hidden quality leads the worst types to dominate the market, which can unravel it (UG)
- **moral hazard** — hidden actions after a contract change behavior, as with insured parties taking more risk (UG)
- **signaling** — the informed party takes a costly action to credibly reveal its type (UG)
- **screening** — the uninformed party designs a menu of options so that types reveal themselves (UG)
- **separating vs pooling equilibrium** — whether different types are distinguished by their actions or not (grad)
- **efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)** — prices fully reflect past prices, public information, or all information (UG)
- **joint-hypothesis problem** — efficiency can only be tested together with a model of expected returns (grad)
- **Grossman–Stiglitz paradox** — fully informative prices would remove the incentive to acquire information (grad)
- **noise traders** — traders acting on non-information who make prices only partly revealing (grad)
- **adaptive expectations** — forecasts adjust mechanically toward past errors (UG)
- **rational expectations** — subjective forecasts equal the model's own conditional predictions (grad)
- **Lucas critique** — behavioral relations change when policy changes, because expectations change (grad)
- **Knightian uncertainty** — unknowns with no reliable probabilities, as distinct from measurable risk (UG)
- **conventional expectations** — Keynes's idea that people assume the current state persists unless there is reason to think otherwise (UG)
- **animal spirits** — spontaneous optimism or pessimism that drives investment beyond calculation (UG)
- **beauty contest / higher-order beliefs** — forecasting what others believe others believe (UG)
- **common knowledge vs public information** — what everyone knows that everyone knows, versus what is merely broadcast (grad)
- **social value of public information** — public signals can be over-weighted for coordination, so more precision may hurt welfare (grad)
- **economic narrative** — a contagious story that changes economic decisions (UG)
- **narrative contagion (SIR)** — stories spread and fade like epidemics (UG)
- **bounded rationality / heuristics** — limited cognition leads to rules of thumb instead of optimization (UG)
- **mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion** — systematic departures from the textbook "Econ" (UG)
- **limits to arbitrage** — mispricing can persist because correcting it is risky and costly (UG)
- **mean-variance optimization** — choosing portfolio weights to trade expected return against variance (UG)
- **diversification & covariance** — portfolio risk depends on co-movements, not just individual risks (UG)
- **efficient frontier** — the set of portfolios with maximum return for each level of risk (UG)
- **CAPM & beta** — in equilibrium, expected return is proportional to covariance with the market portfolio (UG)
- **no-arbitrage & risk-neutral pricing** — derivative prices follow from hedging, not from beliefs about expected returns (grad)
- **Kelly criterion / log-optimal growth** — bet fractions that maximize the long-run growth rate of wealth (UG)
- **fat tails & volatility clustering** — extreme moves are far more common than Gaussian models predict, and come in bursts (UG)
- **adaptive markets hypothesis** — market efficiency varies with the evolving ecology of strategies (UG)
- **maturity / liquidity transformation** — banks fund long illiquid assets with short liquid deposits (UG)
- **bank run equilibrium** — a self-fulfilling withdrawal panic in a coordination game (grad)
- **lender of last resort / deposit insurance** — public backstops that remove the run equilibrium (UG)
- **financial instability hypothesis (hedge/speculative/Ponzi)** — stability encourages ever-riskier financing (UG)
- **debt deflation** — falling prices raise real debt burdens, which forces asset sales and causes further price falls (UG)
- **balance-sheet recession** — firms and households pay down debt after a bubble, so demand collapses even at zero interest rates (UG)
- **zombie lending / regulatory forbearance** — banks keep insolvent borrowers alive to avoid recognizing losses (UG)

## Canonical instances
- **Market for lemons (used cars)** — hidden quality drives good cars out, and the market can collapse — akerlof-1970-lemons
- **Education as a job-market signal** — costly schooling separates high- from low-productivity workers even if it adds no skill — spence-1973-signaling
- **Hayek's tin example** — a price rise tells users to economize on tin without anyone knowing why tin is scarce — hayek-1945-knowledge
- **Keynes's newspaper beauty contest** — pick the faces that others will think others find prettiest — keynes-1936-general
- **Morris–Shin beauty-contest model** — a coordination motive makes agents over-weight public signals, so central-bank transparency can backfire — morris-2002-public
- **Grossman–Stiglitz informed vs uninformed traders** — equilibrium share of information gatherers when the price is a noisy signal — grossman-1980-impossibility
- **Diamond–Dybvig bank run** — two equilibria in one bank, where deposit insurance removes the bad one — diamond-1983-bankruns
- **El Farol bar problem** — 100 agents, a 60-seat bar, and self-defeating common forecasts — arthur-1994-elfarol
- **Kelly gambler with a private wire** — the growth rate of wealth equals the information rate of the tip channel — kelly-1956-information
- **Black–Scholes hedge** — a riskless portfolio of stock and option pins down the option price — black-1973-options
- **Laffer curve as a viral narrative** — a napkin sketch that spread and shaped tax policy — shiller-2019-narrative
- **Dot-com bubble (late 1990s)** — "new era" stories and feedback loops in stock prices — shiller-2015-exuberance
- **Tulip mania and the South Sea bubble** — historical manias read through the Minsky–Kindleberger anatomy — aliber-2015-manias
- **Japan's 1990 bubble burst and lost decade** — firms paying down debt and zombie banks — koo-2009-holygrail, hoshi-2004-japan
- **2008 global financial crisis** — a run on short-term wholesale funding and a debt overhang, the kind of crisis that "this time is different" thinking fails to anticipate — reinhart-2009-different, minsky-1992-instability

## Bridges
- [same-math] Kelly growth rate ↔ information theory: mutual information / channel capacity — the maximal exponential growth rate of the gambler's wealth equals the information rate of the side channel. It is the same log-expectation formula.
- [same-math] Markowitz portfolio variance ↔ linear algebra: quadratic forms and eigen-decomposition — portfolio risk is w'Σw, the efficient frontier comes from a constrained quadratic program, and principal components of Σ are statistical risk factors.
- [same-math] Grossman–Stiglitz / rational-expectations prices ↔ Bayesian theory: Gaussian signal extraction — traders update by precision-weighting a private signal and the price-as-signal (normal–normal conjugate updating).
- [same-math] Spence signaling and Akerlof lemons ↔ decision & game theory: Bayesian games and perfect Bayesian equilibrium — job-market signaling is a canonical signaling game, and lemons is an adverse-selection equilibrium under incomplete information.
- [same-math] Diamond–Dybvig bank runs ↔ decision & game theory: coordination games with multiple Nash equilibria — the run is a self-fulfilling equilibrium, structurally a stag hunt. Global-games refinements (Morris–Shin) select between equilibria.
- [same-math] Black–Scholes PDE ↔ physics / complex systems: heat (diffusion) equation — a change of variables turns the Black–Scholes equation into the heat equation.
- [structural-analogy] Keynes beauty contest / Morris–Shin higher-order expectations ↔ theory of mind: recursive belief modeling (level-k, "I think that you think") — the economic models formalize nested beliefs about others' beliefs. The cognitive-science models differ in their mechanics.
- [structural-analogy] Lucas critique ↔ Bayesian theory & causal inference: structural invariance under intervention (do-operator) — reduced-form correlations estimated under one policy regime do not survive an intervention that changes agents' expectations.
- [structural-analogy] Shiller's narrative contagion ↔ communication theory and network science: diffusion and SIR epidemics on networks — Shiller borrows epidemic models for stories, but the contact process and transmission parameters are not identified the way they are in epidemiology.
- [metaphor] Hayek's price system ↔ communication theory: a channel that compresses dispersed knowledge — Hayek's "system of telecommunications" image predates Shannon and has no formal entropy or capacity. Later work (Grossman–Stiglitz, rational inattention) supplies the formal version.
- [structural-analogy] El Farol / minority game ↔ social simulation and theory of mind: self-referential prediction — any forecast that is commonly believed defeats itself, so a heterogeneous, evolving population of predictors emerges. This is a template for LLM-agent social simulations.
