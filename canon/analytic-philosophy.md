# Analytic philosophy — Canon

## Scope
This canon covers four strands of twentieth-century analytic philosophy that matter most for a computational social scientist. (1) **Philosophy of language:** sense and reference, descriptions, meaning as use, meaning holism and indeterminacy, speech acts, implicature, direct reference and externalism, and convention. (2) **Epistemology:** the analysis of knowledge after Gettier, plus social epistemology and testimony, which are the philosophical core of misinformation research. (3) **Philosophy of science:** explanation and confirmation, falsification, paradigms, research programmes, realism vs. empiricism, and Bayesian confirmation. (4) **Logic and analytic method,** with on-ramps (textbooks, a two-volume history, and Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries). Philosophy of mind (intentionality, Dennett, folk psychology) is left to the theory-of-mind catalog and appears here only in Bridges, and ethics and metaphysics are excluded except where they are part of a listed work. Some canonical figures are covered by on-ramp chapters rather than standalone items: Feyerabend via Godfrey-Smith ch. 7 ("Lakatos, Laudan, Feyerabend, and Frameworks") and Davidson via Lycan ch. 9. Hacking's *Representing and Intervening* (CUP, 1983) was dropped for space; the realism debate is carried by Godfrey-Smith ch. 12 and van Fraassen. Grice's "Meaning" (1957), Lewis's "Languages and Language" (1975) and Hempel's 1945 confirmation paper appear as companions inside related items.

Syllabi triangulated: MIT OCW 24.251 *Introduction to Philosophy of Language* (Fall 2011; https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/24-251-introduction-to-philosophy-of-language-fall-2011/pages/readings), LSE PH201 *Philosophy of Science* (2025–26; https://www.lse.ac.uk/resources/calendar2025-2026/courseGuides/PH/2025_PH201.htm), U Victoria PHIL 351 *Epistemology* (Fall 2019; https://www.uvic.ca/humanities/philosophy/assets/docs/courseoutlines/2019_fall/351-rysiew.pdf), and U Washington Phil 550 *Epistemology of Testimony* (2015; https://faculty.washington.edu/conormw/Teaching/2015/Testimony_Spring_2015.htm).

## Canon

### Strand: Philosophy of language

#### frege-1892-sense
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Frege, G. (1892). Über Sinn und Bedeutung. *Zeitschrift für Philosophie und philosophische Kritik*, N.F. 100(1), 25–50. (English: "On Sense and Reference" / "On Sense and Nominatum"; anthologized e.g. in Martinich (ed.), *The Philosophy of Language*, OUP.)
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Frege asks why "the Morning Star is the Evening Star" is informative while "the Morning Star is the Morning Star" is not. His answer is that an expression has a *reference* (the object it picks out) and a *sense* (its mode of presentation). The distinction underlies all later semantics, and it is the original version of the question "same referent, different representation", which now comes up for embeddings and framing.
- **focus:** whole (short); the identity puzzle at the opening, the telescope analogy, and the treatment of sentences (reference = truth-value) and of indirect speech.
- **access:** free: https://www.deutschestextarchiv.de/book/show/frege_sinn_1892 (German original, Deutsches Textarchiv, CC BY-NC; plain text at https://www.deutschestextarchiv.de/book/download_txt/frege_sinn_1892)
- **translation:** 土屋俊訳「意義と意味について」, 黒田亘・野本和幸編『哲学論集』(フレーゲ著作集), 勁草書房, 1999.
- **verified:** https://clarin.bbaw.de/en/objects/dta:298/ ; https://www.deutschestextarchiv.de/book/show/frege_sinn_1892 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ゴットロープ・フレーゲ

#### russell-1905-denoting
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Russell, B. (1905). On Denoting. *Mind*, 14(4) [whole no. 56], 479–493. doi:10.1093/mind/XIV.4.479
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Russell shows how "The present King of France is bald" can be meaningful even though nothing answers to the description. He paraphrases it into quantified logical form: there is exactly one King of France, and he is bald. This became the model of "analysis": a sentence's grammatical form can mislead you about its logical form.
- **focus:** whole; the three puzzles (identity, excluded middle with "the King of France", negative existentials) and their solution.
- **access:** free: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Denoting
- **verified:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/mind/XIV.4.479 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Denoting

#### wittgenstein-1922-tractatus
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Wittgenstein, L. (1922). *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (trans. C. K. Ogden with F. P. Ramsey; intro. B. Russell). London: Kegan Paul. First published in German as "Logisch-philosophische Abhandlung", *Annalen der Naturphilosophie*, 1921.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The *Tractatus* holds that a meaningful proposition is a logical picture of a possible state of affairs, and that whatever cannot be pictured (ethics, logic's own form) can only be "shown". It inspired the Vienna Circle and is the foil for Wittgenstein's own later work. Read it to see the strongest version of the idea that language mirrors the world's structure.
- **focus:** the seven main numbered propositions and their first-level comments, then follow the numbering down only where needed. Read with Soames Vol. 1 Part Three.
- **access:** free: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5740 (Ogden translation, public domain in the US)
- **translation:** 野矢茂樹訳『論理哲学論考』岩波文庫, 2003.
- **verified:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/5740 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractatus_Logico-Philosophicus ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/論理哲学論考

#### wittgenstein-1953-investigations
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Wittgenstein, L. (1953/2009). *Philosophical Investigations* (trans. G. E. M. Anscombe, P. M. S. Hacker & J. Schulte; rev. 4th ed., ed. Hacker & Schulte). Chichester: Wiley-Blackwell. (German–English facing text.)
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** The later Wittgenstein rejects his own picture theory. For a large class of cases the meaning of a word is its use in a "language-game", a rule-governed social activity. Concepts like "game" hang together by family resemblance rather than shared essences, and following a rule is a public practice, not a private mental act. This is the philosophical ancestor of usage-based and distributional views of meaning.
- **focus:** §1 (Augustine's picture of language), §23 (multiplicity of language-games), §§66–71 (family resemblance), §§138–242 (rule-following, esp. §201), §243 ff. (private language).
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 鬼界彰夫訳『哲学探究』講談社, 2020. (Older: 藤本隆志訳, ウィトゲンシュタイン全集8, 大修館書店, 1976.)
- **verified:** https://www.biblio.com/9781405159296 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Investigations ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/哲学探究

#### quine-1951-dogmas
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Quine, W. V. O. (1951). Main Trends in Recent Philosophy: Two Dogmas of Empiricism. *The Philosophical Review*, 60(1), 20–43. doi:10.2307/2181906. Reprinted in Quine, *From a Logical Point of View* (Harvard UP, 1953).
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Quine attacks two dogmas: the line between truths true "by meaning" (analytic) and truths true "by fact" (synthetic), and the idea that each statement can be tested against experience on its own. His alternative is a "web of belief" in which experience confronts our theories only as a whole, and any statement can be kept if we adjust enough elsewhere. It is one of the most influential papers in twentieth-century analytic philosophy.
- **focus:** whole (about 24 pages); the closing section on empiricism without the dogmas states the "web of belief" picture.
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 飯田隆訳『論理的観点から――論理と哲学をめぐる九章』勁草書房, 1992 (contains 「経験主義の二つのドグマ」).
- **verified:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2307/2181906 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Dogmas_of_Empiricism ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ウィラード・ヴァン・オーマン・クワイン

#### quine-1960-word
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Quine, W. V. O. (1960). *Word and Object*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. New edition 2013, foreword by Patricia S. Churchland, preface by Dagfinn Føllesdal (ISBN 9780262518314).
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Quine imagines a linguist translating a completely unknown language from behavioural evidence alone. He argues that incompatible translation manuals can fit all the evidence equally well, so there is no fact of the matter about what "gavagai" refers to: rabbit, rabbit-stage or undetached rabbit-part. This "indeterminacy of translation" is the deepest philosophical treatment of the question of what fixes meaning given only input-output behaviour.
- **focus:** Ch. 2 (meaning and translation: radical translation, stimulus meaning, "gavagai"); Ch. 1 for the setting.
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 大出晁・宮館恵訳『ことばと対象』勁草書房, 1984.
- **verified:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_and_Object ; https://penguinrandomhouse.ca/books/656758/word-and-object-new-edition-by-willard-van-orman-quine-foreword-by-patricia-smith-churchland-preface-to-the-new-edition-by-dagfinn-fllesdal/9780262518314 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ウィラード・ヴァン・オーマン・クワイン

#### austin-1962-words
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Austin, J. L. (1962). *How to Do Things with Words* (The William James Lectures delivered at Harvard University in 1955; ed. J. O. Urmson). Oxford: Clarendon Press. 2nd ed. 1975, ed. J. O. Urmson & M. Sbisà (Clarendon / Harvard UP).
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Austin notices that many utterances do not describe anything; they *do* something, such as promising, naming a ship, or saying "I do". He separates the act of saying something (locution), what you do in saying it (illocution: warning, ordering), and its effects (perlocution: alarming, persuading). Speech-act theory is the philosophical basis for treating communication as action, which matters directly for crisis and risk communication.
- **focus:** whole (short lectures); performatives and felicity conditions in the early lectures; the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary distinction and the closing taxonomy of illocutionary forces.
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 飯野勝己訳『言語と行為――いかにして言葉でものごとを行うか』講談社学術文庫, 2019 (earlier: 坂本百大訳『言語と行為』大修館書店, 1978).
- **verified:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Do_Things_with_Words ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ジョン・L・オースティン

#### grice-1975-conversation
- **type:** chapter
- **citation:** Grice, H. P. (1975). Logic and Conversation. In P. Cole & J. L. Morgan (eds.), *Syntax and Semantics, Vol. 3: Speech Acts* (pp. 41–58). New York: Academic Press. Reprinted in Grice, *Studies in the Way of Words* (Harvard UP, 1989), pp. 22–40.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Grice explains how we routinely mean more than we say. Hearers assume speakers are cooperative, meaning informative, truthful, relevant and clear (the maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation and Manner). When a speaker seems to flout a maxim, the hearer infers a "conversational implicature". This is the foundation of pragmatics, of Rational Speech Act models, and of analyses of misleading-but-true communication.
- **focus:** whole. Read with its companion, Grice, H. P. (1957), "Meaning", *The Philosophical Review* 66(3), 377–388 (doi:10.2307/2182440), which defines speaker meaning as an intention to produce an effect in a hearer through the hearer's recognition of that intention.
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 清塚邦彦訳『論理と会話』勁草書房, 1998 (partial translation of *Studies in the Way of Words*).
- **verified:** https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/implicature/ (bibliography: "Cole & Morgan 1975: 41–58. Reprinted in Grice 1989: 22–40") ; companion: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2307/2182440 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ポール・グライス

#### kripke-1980-naming
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Kripke, S. A. (1980). *Naming and Necessity*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. (Lectures given at Princeton, January 1970; first printed in D. Davidson & G. Harman (eds.), *Semantics of Natural Language*, Reidel, 1972.)
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Kripke argues that proper names are not disguised descriptions. A name is a "rigid designator" that picks out the same individual in every possible world, and it gets its reference from a causal-historical chain of use, not from a description in the speaker's head. He also argues that some necessary truths, like "water is H2O", are discovered empirically. Together these overturned the Frege–Russell orthodoxy.
- **focus:** Lecture I (critique of descriptivism; rigidity), Lecture II (the Gödel/Schmidt case; the causal picture), Lecture III (natural kinds; necessary a posteriori). The 1980 preface is a useful summary.
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 八木沢敬・野家啓一訳『名指しと必然性――様相の形而上学と心身問題』産業図書, 1985.
- **verified:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naming_and_Necessity ; https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0674598466 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/名指しと必然性

#### putnam-1975-meaning
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Putnam, H. (1975). The Meaning of "Meaning". In K. Gunderson (ed.), *Language, Mind, and Knowledge*, Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science, Vol. 7 (pp. 131–193). Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment shows that two people in identical mental states can mean different things by "water" if their environments differ (H2O vs. XYZ). Hence "meanings just ain't in the head". He adds the "division of linguistic labour": ordinary speakers defer to experts to fix what "elm" or "gold" refers to. This is semantic externalism, and it bears on whether a text-only model can mean anything.
- **focus:** whole; the Twin Earth sections, the elm/beech example, the "division of linguistic labor" (and its universality hypothesis), and the later sections on stereotypes and semantic markers.
- **access:** free: https://conservancy.umn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/09012d92-398e-40b0-9f60-a3f37f05d2a5/content (direct PDF, University of Minnesota Digital Conservancy)
- **verified:** https://hdl.handle.net/11299/185225 ; https://conservancy.umn.edu/collections/c5c26c51-d6ad-435a-afb5-ce342d721170

#### lewis-1969-convention
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Lewis, D. (1969). *Convention: A Philosophical Study*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. Reissued Oxford: Blackwell, 2002 (foreword by W. V. Quine).
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Lewis uses Schelling-style game theory to define a convention: a regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem and that persists because everyone expects everyone else to conform, under common knowledge. He then uses this to explain the "platitude that language is ruled by convention", treating meaning as an equilibrium of signalling. This is the main hinge between analytic philosophy and game theory, and the ancestor of signalling-game models of the evolution of meaning.
- **focus:** Ch. I (coordination and convention; salience), and the last two chapters, "Signalling Systems" and "Conventions of Language". Free companion: Lewis, D. (1975), "Languages and Language", in Gunderson (ed.), Minnesota Studies Vol. 7, pp. 3–35, PDF at https://conservancy.umn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/361dde7c-8e3e-463c-a74b-eb5d79ce7f8a/content (handle https://hdl.handle.net/11299/185227).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewis_(philosopher) ; https://www.akademika.no/humaniora/filosofi/convention/9780631232568

### Strand: Epistemology

#### gettier-1963-jtb
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Gettier, E. L. (1963). Is Justified True Belief Knowledge? *Analysis*, 23(6), 121–123. doi:10.1093/analys/23.6.121
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** In under three pages, Gettier gives two cases in which someone has a justified, true belief that is true only by luck, so intuitively it is not knowledge. This broke the classical "justified true belief" analysis and launched sixty years of work on epistemic luck, reliability and safety. It is the field's standard example of a counterexample defeating a definition.
- **focus:** whole (Case I: Smith, Jones and the ten coins; Case II: the Ford/Barcelona disjunction).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://academic.oup.com/analysis/issue/23/6 ; https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/analys/23.6.121

#### goldman-1999-social
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Goldman, A. I. (1999). *Knowledge in a Social World*. Oxford: Clarendon Press / New York: Oxford University Press. 407 pp.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Goldman founded "veritistic" social epistemology: social practices such as testimony, argument, media, law and science are judged by how well they raise a community's level of true belief. The book treats the "marketplace of ideas" and communication technologies as empirical epistemic questions. That makes it the most direct philosophical foundation for misinformation research.
- **focus:** Part I chs. 1–3 (postmodern resistance; truth; the framework), Part II chs. 4–7 (testimony; argumentation; technology and economics of communication; speech regulation and the marketplace of ideas), ch. 8 (science).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/enhancements/fy0603/98043283-t.html ; https://discovery.inp.edu.eg/Record/2582/Details?print=1

#### coady-1992-testimony
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Coady, C. A. J. (1992). *Testimony: A Philosophical Study*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Most of what we know comes from other people's say-so, yet classical epistemology treated testimony as second-rate. Coady argues against "reductionism", the Humean idea that we must check a source's reliability from our own observation before trusting it. He holds that testimony is a basic source of evidence, like perception. This sets the terms of the reductionism vs. anti-reductionism debate on trust in sources.
- **focus:** Part 1 "The Problematic", Part 2 "The Tradition" (the reductive and analogical approaches; Reid's "Scottish fundamentalism"), Part 3 "The Solution"; Part 5 "The Applications" (experts, history, law) as relevant.
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://libcat.mcphs.edu/Record/in00000281791/TOC ; https://opac.lib.hit-u.ac.jp/opac/opac_link/bibid/8000068020

### Strand: Philosophy of science

#### hempel-1948-explanation
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Hempel, C. G., & Oppenheim, P. (1948). Studies in the Logic of Explanation. *Philosophy of Science*, 15(2), 135–175. doi:10.1086/286983
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** This paper states the deductive-nomological ("covering law") model: to explain an event is to deduce it from general laws plus initial conditions, so explanation and prediction have the same logical form. It is the benchmark that every later theory of explanation (causal, unificationist, mechanistic) defines itself against, and it frames the "explanation vs. prediction" debate in computational social science.
- **focus:** the opening sections that state the basic D-N pattern and its conditions of adequacy. Companion on confirmation and the raven paradox: Hempel, C. G. (1945), "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation (I.)", *Mind* 54(213), 1–26, doi:10.1093/mind/LIV.213.1. Hempel's essays are collected in *Aspects of Scientific Explanation and Other Essays in the Philosophy of Science* (Free Press, 1965, 504 pp.).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1086/286983 ; companion: https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1093/mind/LIV.213.1 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspects_of_Scientific_Explanation

#### popper-1959-logic
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Popper, K. R. (1959). *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*. London: Hutchinson. (Trans. of *Logik der Forschung*, Springer, 1934 [imprint 1935].) Routledge Classics ed., 2002, 544 pp.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Popper argues that no number of confirming observations can verify a universal theory, but a single reproducible counter-instance can refute it. So what makes a theory scientific is falsifiability, and good theories are bold ones that forbid a lot and survive severe tests ("corroboration"). It is the root of the demarcation problem and of the falsificationist logic built into significance testing.
- **focus:** Part I chs. 1 "A Survey of Some Fundamental Problems" and 2; Part II ch. 4 "Falsifiability", ch. 5 "The Problem of the Empirical Basis", ch. 6 "Degrees of Testability", ch. 10 "Corroboration, or How a Theory Stands up to Tests".
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 大内義一・森博訳『科学的発見の論理』(上・下) 恒星社厚生閣, 1971–72.
- **verified:** https://www.routledge.com/The-Lic/Popper/p/book/9780415278447 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Logic_of_Scientific_Discovery ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/科学的発見の論理

#### kuhn-1962-structure
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Kuhn, T. S. (1962/2012). *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (4th ed., 50th anniversary edition, with an introductory essay by Ian Hacking). Chicago: University of Chicago Press. 264 pp.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Kuhn's history shows that science mostly runs as "normal science", puzzle-solving within a shared paradigm. Anomalies pile up into crisis, and crises are resolved by revolutions after which old and new paradigms are partly "incommensurable". The book made the social organisation of science a philosophical topic, and it is the source of "paradigm shift" and much of science-of-science and community-dynamics thinking.
- **focus:** Hacking's introductory essay; chs. II–V (normal science, puzzle-solving, priority of paradigms); chs. VI–X (anomaly, crisis, revolutions as changes of world view); Postscript–1969 (the "disciplinary matrix" and community structure).
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 青木薫訳『科学革命の構造 新版』みすず書房, 2023 (from the 4th ed., with Hacking's essay); earlier 中山茂訳, みすず書房, 1971.
- **verified:** https://www.press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo13179781.html ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/科学革命の構造

#### lakatos-1970-falsification
- **type:** chapter
- **citation:** Lakatos, I. (1970). Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes. In I. Lakatos & A. Musgrave (eds.), *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge* (pp. 91–196). Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/CBO9781139171434.009
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Lakatos sits between Popper and Kuhn. The unit of appraisal is not a single theory but a research programme: a protected "hard core" surrounded by a "protective belt" of auxiliary hypotheses. A programme is progressive if its modifications predict novel facts, and degenerating if they only patch failures after the fact. This gives a practical vocabulary for judging whether a research line, including your own, is still productive.
- **focus:** the sections contrasting dogmatic, naive and sophisticated falsificationism, and the section on the methodology of research programmes (negative and positive heuristic).
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 森博監訳『批判と知識の成長』木鐸社, 1985 (translation of the whole volume).
- **verified:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/criticism-and-the-growth-of-knowledge/falsification-and-the-methodology-of-scientific-research-programmes/B1AAD974814D6E7BF35E6449691AA58F ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ラカトシュ・イムレ

#### vanfraassen-1980-image
- **type:** book
- **citation:** van Fraassen, B. C. (1980). *The Scientific Image*. Oxford: Clarendon Press. 235 pp.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Van Fraassen defends "constructive empiricism": science aims only at theories that are *empirically adequate*, meaning true about what is observable. Accepting a theory need not mean believing its claims about unobservables. It is the strongest modern anti-realist position and the standard opponent in the realism debate, and it applies directly to how we should read latent constructs in models.
- **focus:** whole; read alongside Godfrey-Smith ch. 12 (§12.6 "Van Fraassen's View").
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 丹治信春訳『科学的世界像』紀伊國屋書店, 1986.
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0198244274 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/バス・ファン・フラーセン

#### howson-2005-bayesian
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Howson, C., & Urbach, P. (2005). *Scientific Reasoning: The Bayesian Approach* (3rd ed.). Chicago/La Salle, IL: Open Court. 327 pp. ISBN 978-0-8126-9578-6. (1st ed. 1989; 2nd ed. 1993; the 3rd ed. is often cited as 2006.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** This is the standard philosophical defence of Bayesian confirmation theory. Evidence confirms a hypothesis when it raises its probability, and the same machinery is used to resolve the raven paradox, the Duhem problem and ad hoc hypotheses. It also mounts a sustained critique of Fisherian and Neyman–Pearson significance testing, so it doubles as a philosophy-of-statistics text for a working quantitative researcher.
- **focus:** Ch. 1 (problem of induction; Popper); Ch. 3 "The Laws of Probability" (Dutch book, updating rules); Ch. 4 "Bayesian Induction: Deterministic Theories" (4.d The Ravens Paradox, 4.e The Duhem Problem, 4.g Ad Hoc Hypotheses); Ch. 5 "Classical Inference: Significance Tests and Estimation".
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/toc/ecip0518/2005024868.html ; https://openlibrary.org/isbn/9780812695786 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Howson

### Strand: Logic & analytic method

## Concept spine
- **analysis** — explaining a puzzling concept or sentence by breaking it into clearer parts or restating it in clearer form (UG)
- **logical form vs. grammatical form** — a sentence's surface grammar can hide what it really asserts; analysis exposes the logical structure (UG)
- **validity / soundness** — an argument is valid if the conclusion cannot be false when the premises are true, and sound if it is also valid with true premises (HS)
- **use vs. mention** — talking *with* a word vs. talking *about* the word ("Tokyo is big" vs. "'Tokyo' has five letters") (HS)
- **explication** — Carnap's idea of replacing a vague everyday concept with a precise one that is useful for theory (UG)
- **sense (Sinn) vs. reference (Bedeutung)** — the way an expression presents its object vs. the object itself (UG)
- **Frege's puzzle** — why true identity statements like "a = b" can be informative when "a = a" is not (UG)
- **theory of descriptions** — "the F is G" means "exactly one thing is F, and it is G", so empty descriptions need no strange objects (UG)
- **picture theory of meaning** — the early Wittgenstein's view that a proposition is a logical picture of a possible fact (grad)
- **meaning as use** — for a large class of cases, a word's meaning is its use in a language (UG)
- **language-game** — a rule-governed activity in which words get their point, such as giving orders, reporting or joking (UG)
- **family resemblance** — members of a concept such as "game" share overlapping similarities, not one common essence (UG)
- **rule-following problem** — no finite set of past uses fixes what counts as "going on in the same way" (grad)
- **private language argument** — a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know is not possible (grad)
- **analytic / synthetic distinction** — true by meaning alone vs. true because of how the world is; Quine denied there is a principled line (UG)
- **confirmation holism (Quine–Duhem thesis)** — hypotheses face evidence only as a group, so a failed prediction does not tell you which one to give up (UG)
- **web of belief** — Quine's picture of knowledge as a connected network, with experience at the edges and logic at the centre, all of it revisable (UG)
- **indeterminacy of translation** — all behavioural evidence can be fitted by incompatible translation manuals (grad)
- **speech act** — an utterance considered as an action, such as promising, warning or naming (UG)
- **performative utterance** — an utterance that does what it says ("I apologize") instead of describing something (UG)
- **locutionary / illocutionary / perlocutionary act** — saying something / what you do in saying it / what you bring about by saying it (UG)
- **felicity conditions** — the conventional and contextual conditions under which a speech act succeeds (UG)
- **speaker (non-natural) meaning** — Grice: meaning something is intending an effect on a hearer through their recognition of that intention (UG)
- **cooperative principle & maxims** — the assumption that speakers try to be informative (Quantity), truthful (Quality), relevant (Relation) and clear (Manner) (UG)
- **conversational implicature** — what a speaker conveys beyond what they literally say, inferred via the maxims; it can be cancelled (UG)
- **rigid designator** — a term that picks out the same object in every possible world in which it exists (grad)
- **causal-historical theory of reference** — a name refers through a chain of uses going back to an initial "baptism", not through a description (grad)
- **necessary a posteriori** — truths that could not have been otherwise but are known only by investigation ("water is H2O") (grad)
- **semantic externalism** — what words mean depends partly on the speaker's physical and social environment, not only on what is in the head (grad)
- **division of linguistic labour** — ordinary speakers' terms refer via deference to experts who can tell elm from beech (UG)
- **convention** — a self-sustaining regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, maintained by mutual expectation (grad)
- **common knowledge** — everyone knows p, knows that everyone knows p, and so on without end (grad)
- **signalling system** — a sender–receiver convention that maps states to signals to acts so that both parties benefit (grad)
- **justified true belief (JTB)** — the classical analysis of knowledge that Gettier refuted (UG)
- **epistemic luck** — being right by accident in a way that undermines knowledge (UG)
- **reliabilism** — a belief is justified if it is produced by a process that tends to produce true beliefs (UG)
- **testimony: reductionism vs. anti-reductionism** — must trust in others be backed by independent evidence of their reliability, or is it a basic source of evidence? (grad)
- **veritism** — judging social practices by how much they increase true belief in a community (grad)
- **demarcation problem** — what separates science from non-science or pseudoscience (UG)
- **falsifiability** — a theory is scientific if some possible observation would refute it (UG)
- **corroboration** — Popper's term for a theory's record of surviving severe tests, which he distinguishes from probability (UG)
- **deductive-nomological explanation** — explaining an event by deducing it from laws plus initial conditions (UG)
- **paradigm / normal science** — a shared exemplar-plus-framework that defines legitimate puzzles, and the puzzle-solving it supports (UG)
- **anomaly → crisis → revolution** — Kuhn's cycle in which accumulated anomalies force a change of paradigm (UG)
- **incommensurability** — competing paradigms lack a fully shared standard of comparison or vocabulary (grad)
- **research programme (hard core, protective belt)** — Lakatos's unit of appraisal; progressive if it predicts novel facts, degenerating if it only accommodates them after the fact (grad)
- **underdetermination** — evidence alone does not settle which of several rival theories is true (UG)
- **scientific realism vs. constructive empiricism** — whether science aims at truth about unobservables or only at empirical adequacy (grad)
- **Bayesian confirmation** — E confirms H iff P(H|E) > P(H); belief revised by conditionalization (grad)
- **Dutch book argument** — if your degrees of belief violate probability theory, someone can offer you a set of bets that guarantees you a loss (grad)

## Canonical instances
- **Morning Star / Evening Star** — "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative because the two names share a reference (Venus) but differ in sense — frege-1892-sense
- **Frege's telescope** — the Moon (reference), the image in the telescope (sense, objective but perspectival) and each viewer's retinal image (private idea) — frege-1892-sense
- **The present King of France is bald** — false (not meaningless) on Russell's analysis because nothing satisfies "exactly one present King of France" — russell-1905-denoting
- **Augustine's picture of language** — the opening target of the *Investigations*: words name objects and sentences combine names — wittgenstein-1953-investigations
- **What is a game?** — board games, ball games and patience share no single common feature, only overlapping similarities (§66) — wittgenstein-1953-investigations
- **Gavagai** — a native says "gavagai" as a rabbit runs by; rabbit, rabbit-stage or undetached rabbit-part all fit the evidence — quine-1960-word
- **"I do" / naming a ship** — utterances that perform the act rather than report it, and that misfire if conditions fail — austin-1962-words
- **The recommendation letter** — "Mr. X's command of English is excellent, and his attendance at tutorials has been regular": flouting Quantity implicates "he is no good at philosophy" — grice-1975-conversation
- **Gödel / Schmidt** — if Schmidt actually proved incompleteness, "Gödel" still refers to Gödel, not to "the prover of incompleteness" — kripke-1980-naming
- **Twin Earth** — on Twin Earth "water" names XYZ, so molecule-for-molecule twins mean different things — putnam-1975-meaning
- **Elm and beech** — a speaker who cannot tell the trees apart still refers to elms with "elm", by deferring to experts — putnam-1975-meaning
- **The meeting-place problem / salience** — two people who must meet without communicating coordinate on a salient place; conventions generalize such solutions — lewis-1969-convention
- **Smith, Jones and the ten coins** — Smith's justified belief "the man who gets the job has ten coins in his pocket" is true only by luck — gettier-1963-jtb
- **The raven paradox** — "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens", so a white shoe seems to confirm it — hempel-1948-explanation (companion Hempel 1945); Bayesian treatment in howson-2005-bayesian §4.d
- **The Duhem problem** — when a prediction fails, logic alone cannot tell you whether to blame the hypothesis or an auxiliary assumption — howson-2005-bayesian

## Bridges
- [same-math] Bayesian confirmation / conditionalization ↔ Bayesian theory & causal inference: Bayes' rule and posterior updating — "E confirms H" is literally P(H|E) > P(H); Howson & Urbach and the SEP entry use the same probability calculus as Bayesian statistics.
- [same-math] Lewis's convention as coordination equilibrium ↔ decision & game theory: coordination games and Nash equilibrium — Lewis works in the same normal-form game formalism, and his "coordination equilibrium" is a stricter refinement of Nash equilibrium (each player also prefers that *no one* deviate), with common knowledge doing the epistemic work.
- [same-math] Lewis signalling systems ↔ decision & game theory: sender–receiver (Lewis–Skyrms) signalling games — a Lewis signalling system is an equilibrium of exactly such a game, and evolutionary game theory studies how meaning emerges in it.
- [structural-analogy] Gricean implicature and the cooperative principle ↔ communication theory: code model vs. inferential model of communication — Grice explains why decoding a signal under-determines what a speaker means; Rational Speech Act models formalize the same inference.
- [structural-analogy] Grice's speaker meaning (nested intentions to be recognized) ↔ theory of mind: recursive mental-state attribution / intentional stance — understanding an utterance requires modelling the speaker's intention about your recognition of their intention.
- [metaphor] Wittgenstein's meaning as use / language-games ↔ LLMs: distributional semantics and embeddings — "meaning is use" is often cited as an ancestor of "you shall know a word by the company it keeps", but Wittgenstein's "use" is social practice, not co-occurrence statistics.
- [structural-analogy] Quine's indeterminacy of translation ↔ linear algebra: rotational non-identifiability of embeddings / factor solutions — any orthogonal rotation of an embedding space preserves all inner products, so the data cannot single out one "correct" coordinate system, just as behaviour cannot single out one translation manual.
- [structural-analogy] Putnam's externalism & division of linguistic labour ↔ LLMs: grounding and retrieval — whether a text-only model's "water" refers to H2O depends on links to the world and to expert communities, not just on internal state.
- [structural-analogy] testimony and veritistic social epistemology ↔ complex systems: opinion dynamics and network epistemology — the SEP social-epistemology entry's network-epistemology models are agent-based models of belief spread, the same model family used to study misinformation diffusion.
- [metaphor] Kuhn's paradigm shifts / Lakatos's degenerating programmes ↔ complex systems: tipping points and punctuated change in scientific communities — a useful image for citation-network dynamics, not a shared mathematical model.
