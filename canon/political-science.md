# Political science — Canon

## Scope
This canon treats political science as the study of how individuals with limited information and conflicting preferences form beliefs, coordinate, and make binding collective choices. It covers seven strands. (1) On-ramps: two textbooks, one comparative and one rational-choice. (2) Collective choice and institutions: social choice, the economic theory of voting, collective action, the commons, exit/voice, jury theorems, bargaining under private information, and preference falsification. (3) Public opinion and political psychology: belief systems, the survey response, cues, framing, and motivated reasoning. (4) Polarization and media: affective polarization, media choice, and exposure to opposing views online. (5) Democracy, development, and political economy (kept short), (6) methods and computation (research design and text-as-data), and (7) Japan. International relations, public law, and normative political theory are out of scope, apart from Fearon on war as an information problem. Lippmann's *Public Opinion* (1922) belongs to the communication-theory canon and is cross-referenced rather than repeated. Graduate syllabi used to check the selection: MIT 17.200 (Graduate Seminar in American Politics I: Political Behavior), Rochester PSC 541 (U.S. Political Behavior, 2025), UCSD POLI 252 (American Politics: Behavior, 2009), and UGA POLS 8000 (Political Strategy: Rational Choice Theory, 2025).

## Canon

### Strand: On-ramps

#### clark-2017-principles
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Clark, W. R., Golder, M., & Golder, S. N. (2017). *Principles of Comparative Politics* (3rd ed.). Thousand Oaks, CA: CQ Press / SAGE.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** This is the standard introduction to comparative politics with an explicitly game-theoretic, cumulative-science approach. Simple strategic models (exit/voice/loyalty, the state as a protection racket, transition games) run through it alongside cross-national evidence. Its chapter on group decision making covers the Condorcet paradox, Arrow, and the median voter at undergraduate level, which makes it the gentlest way into strand 2.
- **focus:** Ch. 2 "What Is Science?"; Ch. 3 "What Is Politics?"; Ch. 4 "The Origins of the Modern State"; Ch. 6–7 (economic and cultural determinants of democracy); Ch. 11 "Problems with Group Decision Making"; Ch. 13 "Elections and Electoral Systems"; Ch. 15 "Institutional Veto Players".
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://edge.sagepub.com/principlescp3e

#### shepsle-2010-analyzing
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Shepsle, K. A. (2010). *Analyzing Politics: Rationality, Behavior, and Institutions* (2nd ed.). New York: W. W. Norton. 560 pp.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** This is the canonical rational-choice primer. It builds rationality, group choice, Arrow's theorem, collective action, and institutions (agenda power, committees, delegation) from first principles with little mathematics. Graduate formal-theory courses still assign it as the scaffold for Arrow and the institutions literature, and it is the best single bridge from game theory to political institutions.
- **focus:** Chs. 3–4 (group choice and Arrow's theorem; assigned for Arrow in UGA POLS 8000); Chs. 5, 6, 11–13, 15 (institutions, as assigned in the same syllabus).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://wwnorton.co.uk/books/9780393935073-analyzing-politics ; chapter use: https://spia.uga.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Ainsworth-Spring-25-8000.pdf

### Strand: Collective choice & institutions

#### arrow-1951-socialchoice
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Arrow, K. J. (1951). *Social Choice and Individual Values*. Cowles Commission Monograph No. 12. New York: John Wiley & Sons; London: Chapman & Hall. xi + 99 pp. (2nd ed. 1963; 3rd ed. Yale University Press 2012.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Arrow proves that no rule for aggregating individual rankings into a social ranking can satisfy a few mild-looking conditions at once (unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship). The result moved "what does the public want?" from rhetoric to an axiomatic, provable question. All of social choice theory, and Riker's skepticism about the "will of the people", starts here.
- **focus:** Ch. I "Introduction"; Ch. II "The Nature of Preference and Choice"; Ch. V "The General Possibility Theorem for Social Welfare Functions" (pp. 46–60). The 1951 first edition has Chs. I–VII.
- **access:** free: https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-09/m12-all.pdf
- **translation:** 『社会的選択と個人的評価』長名寛明訳、日本経済新聞社、1977（新版2013）
- **verified:** https://cowles.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2022-09/m12-all.pdf (title page, copyright and contents checked); translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ケネス・アロー

#### downs-1957-democracy
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Downs, A. (1957). *An Economic Theory of Democracy*. New York: Harper. Companion article: Downs, A. (1957). An economic theory of political action in a democracy. *Journal of Political Economy*, 65(2), 135–150. doi:10.1086/257897
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Downs models parties as vote-maximizers and voters as utility-maximizers, and from that derives spatial convergence (the median voter), the role of ideology as an information shortcut, the paradox of voting, and "rational ignorance": information costs more than a single vote is worth. This is the origin of treating citizens' information as an economic problem, which strands 3 and 4 then test.
- **focus:** whole (read the 1957 JPE article first as a 16-page summary)
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『民主主義の経済理論』古田精司監訳、成文堂、1980
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1086/257897 ; book listed in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/17-200-graduate-seminar-in-american-politics-i-political-behavior-fall-2016/pages/readings ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/アンソニー・ダウンズ

#### olson-1965-collectiveaction
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Olson, M. (1965). *The Logic of Collective Action: Public Goods and the Theory of Groups*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. (2nd printing 1971, with a new preface and appendix.) doi:10.4159/9780674041660
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Olson shows that shared interest does not produce collective action. Rational individuals free-ride on public goods, so large, diffuse groups (consumers, citizens) are under-organized relative to small, concentrated ones unless there are "selective incentives". This is the micro-foundation of interest-group politics, social movements, and the free-rider problem behind climate action.
- **focus:** Introduction; Ch. I "A Theory of Groups and Organizations"; Ch. II "Group Size and Group Behavior"; Ch. VI "The 'By-Product' and 'Special Interest' Theories".
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『集合行為論――公共財と集団理論』依田博・森脇俊雅訳、ミネルヴァ書房、1983（新装版1996）
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674041660 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/マンサー・オルソン

#### hirschman-1970-exitvoice
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Hirschman, A. O. (1970). *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty: Responses to Decline in Firms, Organizations, and States*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** A short, elegant book on the two feedback channels members have when an organization declines: leaving (exit, the market signal) or complaining (voice, the political signal). Loyalty governs the mix between them. It is a general theory of how information about quality gets back to the people who decide, and it applies directly to platform migration, partisanship, and emigration.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『離脱・発言・忠誠――企業・組織・国家における衰退への反応』矢野修一訳、ミネルヴァ書房、2005
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0674276507 ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/アルバート・O・ハーシュマン

#### riker-1982-liberalism
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Riker, W. H. (1982). *Liberalism Against Populism: A Confrontation Between the Theory of Democracy and the Theory of Social Choice*. San Francisco: W. H. Freeman. (Reissued Waveland Press, 1988.)
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Riker draws the political conclusion from Arrow and McKelvey. Because majority outcomes can cycle and be manipulated through agenda control and strategic voting, there is no coherent "popular will" to discover ("populism"). Elections can only let voters remove officials ("liberalism"). The book is canonical as a provocation and anticipates Achen & Bartels' "realist" view.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『民主的決定の政治学――リベラリズムとポピュリズム』森脇俊雅訳、芦書房、1991
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0716712458 ; syllabus use: https://spia.uga.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Ainsworth-Spring-25-8000.pdf ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ウィリアム・ライカー

#### grofman-1983-jurytheorems
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Grofman, B., Owen, G., & Feld, S. L. (1983). Thirteen theorems in search of the truth. *Theory and Decision*, 15(3), 261–278. doi:10.1007/BF00125672
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** This paper states and extends the Condorcet jury theorem: if voters are independently more likely than not to be right, majority accuracy goes to 1 as the group grows. It then works out what happens with unequal competence, weighted votes, and correlated judgments. It is the epistemic counterpart to Arrow: aggregation as information pooling rather than preference aggregation. It is also the formal ancestor of "wisdom of crowds" and ensemble or majority-vote labeling with LLMs.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00125672

#### ostrom-1990-commons
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Ostrom, E. (1990). *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/CBO9780511807763
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Ostrom answers Hardin and Olson empirically. Communities in Swiss and Japanese mountain commons, Spanish huertas, and Philippine irrigation systems sustain shared resources for centuries without privatization or a Leviathan. They do it with self-made rules that share recognizable "design principles" (monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, recognized rights to organize). The book won her the Nobel and is the key text for collective action that works.
- **focus:** "Reflections on the commons"; "An institutional approach to the study of self-organization and self-governance in CPR situations"; "Analyzing long-enduring, self-organized, and self-governed CPRs" (design principles); "Analyzing institutional change".
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『コモンズのガバナンス――人びとの協働と制度の進化』原田禎夫・齋藤暖生・嶋田大作訳、晃洋書房、2022
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511807763 (chapter titles via Crossref ISBN 9780511807763) ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/エリノア・オストロム

#### kuran-1991-surprise
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Kuran, T. (1991). Now out of never: The element of surprise in the East European Revolution of 1989. *World Politics*, 44(1), 7–48. doi:10.2307/2010422
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** People hide their true political preferences when expressing them is costly ("preference falsification"). Public opinion can therefore look stable while private discontent builds, until a small shift in thresholds sets off a cascade. This is why revolutions surprise even the people who make them. The paper is a canonical model of how private beliefs, public signals, and coordination interact, and it maps directly onto threshold models and pluralistic ignorance.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.2307/2010422

#### fearon-1995-war
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Fearon, J. D. (1995). Rationalist explanations for war. *International Organization*, 49(3), 379–414. doi:10.1017/S0020818300033324
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** War is costly, so rational states should always prefer some negotiated settlement. Fearon shows that fighting happens anyway because of private information combined with incentives to misrepresent it, commitment problems, or indivisible issues. This is the clearest demonstration in political science that conflict is often an information and credibility problem, and it carries over to any bargaining between groups.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020818300033324

### Strand: Public opinion & political psychology

#### converse-1964-beliefsystems
- **type:** chapter
- **citation:** Converse, P. E. (1964). The nature of belief systems in mass publics. In D. E. Apter (Ed.), *Ideology and Discontent* (pp. 206–261). New York: Free Press. Reprinted in *Critical Review*, 18(1–3), 1–74 (2006). doi:10.1080/08913810608443650
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Using panel surveys, Converse finds that most citizens' political attitudes are weakly "constrained" (they do not hang together ideologically) and unstable over time, as if many respondents answered at random ("non-attitudes"). Coherent belief systems are confined to a thin, informed elite. This is the founding challenge of public-opinion research, and every later text in this strand is partly a reply to it.
- **focus:** whole (pp. 206–261), especially the sections on constraint and on the stability of responses across panel waves.
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1080/08913810608443650 ; original pagination: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/17-200-graduate-seminar-in-american-politics-i-political-behavior-fall-2016/pages/readings

#### zaller-1992-massopinion
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Zaller, J. R. (1992). *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/CBO9780511818691
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Zaller's Receive–Accept–Sample (RAS) model treats a survey answer as a sample from whatever considerations are at the top of the head. Those considerations come from elite messages that a person has received (which depends on political awareness) and accepted (which depends on predispositions). It explains opinion instability, framing effects, and the non-monotonic "most persuadable are the moderately aware" pattern. It is the most important formal account of how elite communication becomes mass opinion.
- **focus:** Chs. 1–5 and 7 (as assigned in MIT 17.200); Ch. 1 "Introduction: The fragmented state of opinion research".
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511818691 ; chapter assignment: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/17-200-graduate-seminar-in-american-politics-i-political-behavior-fall-2016/pages/readings

#### lupia-1998-dilemma
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Lupia, A., & McCubbins, M. D. (1998). *The Democratic Dilemma: Can Citizens Learn What They Need to Know?* Cambridge: Cambridge University Press.
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Lupia and McCubbins argue that the real question is not how much citizens know but whether they can learn what they need from others. Using game theory and experiments, they show that a listener can be persuaded by a speaker only if the listener believes the speaker is knowledgeable and shares the listener's interests, or if external forces (penalties for lying, verification, costly effort) make the message credible. This is the theory of cues and trust, and it ties political competence to cheap talk and signaling.
- **focus:** Chs. 1–4, 6, 7, 9 (as assigned in UCSD POLI 252).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://openlibrary.org/isbn/0521585937 ; chapter assignment: https://polisci.ucsd.edu/_files/syllabi/FA09%20POLI%20252.pdf

#### bartels-2002-runningtally
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Bartels, L. M. (2002). Beyond the running tally: Partisan bias in political perceptions. *Political Behavior*, 24(2), 117–150. doi:10.1023/A:1021226224601
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Bartels contrasts "running tally" (Bayesian-style) accounts of partisanship with evidence that partisans perceive objective facts (inflation, unemployment) differently. Partisanship is not just updated by information; it filters the information itself. Opinions therefore need not converge as evidence accumulates, which is a central result for misinformation and polarization research.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1021226224601

#### taber-2006-skepticism
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Taber, C. S., & Lodge, M. (2006). Motivated skepticism in the evaluation of political beliefs. *American Journal of Political Science*, 50(3), 755–769. doi:10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00214.x
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** In experiments on affirmative action and gun control, participants rated attitude-congruent arguments as stronger, argued against incongruent ones, and sought out confirming sources. Attitudes became more extreme after balanced information, especially among the most sophisticated. This is the canonical demonstration of motivated reasoning in politics.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2006.00214.x

#### chong-2007-framing
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Chong, D., & Druckman, J. N. (2007). Framing theory. *Annual Review of Political Science*, 10, 103–126. doi:10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** This is the standard review of framing effects in political science. It defines frames as shifts in the weight people give to different considerations, separates framing from persuasion and priming, and sets out the conditions under which frames work: strength, competition, repetition, and predispositions. It is the most direct bridge between political science and Kunhao's framing and crisis-communication work.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/~jnd260/pub/Chong%20Druckman%20Annual%20Review%202007.pdf
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054

#### kahan-2013-motivatedreasoning
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Kahan, D. M. (2013). Ideology, motivated reasoning, and cognitive reflection. *Judgment and Decision Making*, 8(4), 407–424. doi:10.1017/S1930297500005271
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Kahan tests whether ideologically motivated reasoning comes from low cognitive reflection, a "bounded rationality" deficit. He finds the opposite: people higher in cognitive reflection show more identity-protective reasoning, not less. This is the core result behind the cultural-cognition account of polarization on climate change and other science-policy issues.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://jbaron.org/journal/13/13313/jdm13313.pdf
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1017/S1930297500005271

#### achen-2016-realists
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Achen, C. H., & Bartels, L. M. (2016). *Democracy for Realists: Why Elections Do Not Produce Responsive Government*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. doi:10.1515/9781400882731
- **tier:** core
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Achen and Bartels argue that both the "folk theory" of democracy (voters choose policies) and the retrospective-voting fallback (voters reward and punish performance) fail empirically. Voters punish incumbents for droughts and shark attacks, and their partisanship is a group identity rather than the product of policy reasoning. It is the most-assigned contemporary synthesis of public-opinion research and a direct heir of Converse.
- **focus:** Chs. 1–2 and 5–6 (as assigned in MIT 17.200); Ch. 5 "Blind Retrospection: Electoral Responses to Droughts, Floods, and Shark Attacks".
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400882731 ; chapter assignment: https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/17-200-graduate-seminar-in-american-politics-i-political-behavior-fall-2016/pages/readings

### Strand: Polarization & media

#### prior-2007-postbroadcast
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Prior, M. (2007). *Post-Broadcast Democracy: How Media Choice Increases Inequality in Political Involvement and Polarizes Elections*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/CBO9781139878425
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** Under broadcast TV, people with little interest in politics absorbed news by accident. Cable and the internet let them switch to entertainment, which widened the knowledge and turnout gaps and left a more partisan electorate. The key variable is relative preference for news versus entertainment. This is the canonical account of how the information environment changes who becomes informed.
- **focus:** Ch. 2 "Conditional Political Learning"; Ch. 4 "From Low Choice to High Choice…"; Ch. 7 "Partisan Polarization in the High-Choice Media Environment" (assigned in MIT 17.200 and Rochester PSC 541).
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139878425 (chapter titles via Crossref ISBN 9780521858724)

#### iyengar-2012-affect
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Iyengar, S., Sood, G., & Lelkes, Y. (2012). Affect, not ideology: A social identity perspective on polarization. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 76(3), 405–431. doi:10.1093/poq/nfs038
- **tier:** core
- **level:** grad
- **why:** The paper redefines mass polarization as social distance, meaning how much partisans dislike and distrust the other side, rather than how far apart their policy views are. It documents a sharp rise in that dislike, only loosely tied to policy disagreement and linked to negative campaigning. It launched the affective-polarization literature.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://pcl.sites.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj22066/files/media/file/iyengar-poq-affect-not-ideology.pdf
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfs038

#### iyengar-2019-affective
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Iyengar, S., Lelkes, Y., Levendusky, M., Malhotra, N., & Westwood, S. J. (2019). The origins and consequences of affective polarization in the United States. *Annual Review of Political Science*, 22, 129–146. doi:10.1146/annurev-polisci-051117-073034
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** This is the standard review of affective polarization: how it is measured (feeling thermometers, social distance, implicit and behavioral measures), what drives it (partisan sorting, identity, partisan media, campaigns), and what it does outside politics (dating, hiring, trust). Read it to get the current state of the field in one sitting.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** need-copy
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-051117-073034

#### bail-2018-opposingviews
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Bail, C. A., Argyle, L. P., Brown, T. W., Bumpus, J. P., Chen, H., Hunzaker, M. B. F., Lee, J., Mann, M., Merhout, F., & Volfovsky, A. (2018). Exposure to opposing views on social media can increase political polarization. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 115(37), 9216–9221. doi:10.1073/pnas.1804840115
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** In a field experiment, paid Twitter users followed a bot that retweeted the opposing side for one month. Republicans became more conservative, and Democrats showed a non-significant liberal shift. The result directly challenges the "burst the filter bubble" remedy and is a model of computational field experimentation.
- **focus:** whole
- **access:** free: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6140520/
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1804840115

### Strand: Democracy, development & political economy

#### putnam-1993-democracywork
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Putnam, R. D., with Leonardi, R., & Nanetti, R. Y. (1993). *Making Democracy Work: Civic Traditions in Modern Italy*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. doi:10.1515/9781400820740
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** UG
- **why:** Italy created identical regional governments in 1970, yet they performed very differently, north versus south. Putnam traces the difference to centuries-old "civic community": dense horizontal networks of association that build trust and norms of reciprocity ("social capital"). It is the book that made social capital, a network concept, central to explaining institutional performance.
- **focus:** Ch. 1 "Introduction: Studying Institutional Performance"; Ch. 6 "Social Capital and Institutional Success".
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『哲学する民主主義――伝統と改革の市民的構造』河田潤一訳、NTT出版、2001
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400820740 (chapter titles via Crossref) ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ロバート・パットナム

#### acemoglu-2012-nationsfail
- **type:** book
- **citation:** Acemoglu, D., & Robinson, J. A. (2012). *Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty*. New York: Crown Publishers.
- **tier:** reference
- **level:** UG
- **why:** This is a popular synthesis of the institutionalist political-economy program by two of the 2024 Nobel laureates. "Inclusive" political and economic institutions sustain growth, while "extractive" ones serve narrow elites, and critical junctures plus path dependence explain divergence between countries. ### Strand: Methods & computation

#### king-1994-designing
- **type:** book
- **citation:** King, G., Keohane, R. O., & Verba, S. (1994). *Designing Social Inquiry: Scientific Inference in Qualitative Research*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. doi:10.1515/9781400821211
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** "KKV" argues that qualitative and quantitative research share one logic of descriptive and causal inference. Its lessons include defining observable implications, avoiding selection on the dependent variable, and increasing the number of observations. It shaped political-science methods training for a generation, and the debates it provoked are part of the canon too.
- **focus:** Ch. 1 "The Science in Social Science"; Ch. 2 "Descriptive Inference"; Ch. 3 "Causality and Causal Inference"; Ch. 4 "Determining What to Observe".
- **access:** need-copy
- **translation:** 『社会科学のリサーチ・デザイン――定性的研究における科学的推論』真渕勝監訳、勁草書房、2004
- **verified:** https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400821211 (chapter titles via Crossref) ; translation: https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/ロバート・コヘイン

#### grimmer-2013-textasdata
- **type:** paper
- **citation:** Grimmer, J., & Stewart, B. M. (2013). Text as data: The promise and pitfalls of automatic content analysis methods for political texts. *Political Analysis*, 21(3), 267–297. doi:10.1093/pan/mps028
- **tier:** deep
- **level:** grad
- **why:** This is the field-defining survey of automated text analysis in political science, covering dictionaries, supervised classification, clustering and topic models, and ideological scaling. It is built around four principles, including "all quantitative models of language are wrong—but some are useful" and "validate, validate, validate". ## Concept spine
- **collective action problem** — when everyone benefits from a shared good but each does better by letting others pay, the good is under-provided (UG)
- **free rider** — someone who enjoys a public good without contributing to it (HS)
- **public good** — non-excludable and non-rival: you cannot keep non-payers out, and one person's use does not reduce another's (UG)
- **selective incentive** — a private reward or punishment given only to contributors, used to overcome free riding (UG)
- **common-pool resource (CPR)** — a resource from which exclusion is hard but use is rival (fisheries, pastures, irrigation water) (UG)
- **design principles (Ostrom)** — recurrent features of long-lived self-governed commons: clear boundaries, local rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, recognized rights to organize, nested enterprises (UG)
- **social choice function / social welfare function** — a rule that maps a profile of individual preference rankings to a collective choice or ranking (grad)
- **Condorcet winner** — an option that beats every other option in pairwise majority votes (UG)
- **cycling / intransitivity** — majority rule can prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, so no stable collective preference exists (UG)
- **Arrow's impossibility theorem** — no ranking-aggregation rule satisfies unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship at once (grad)
- **independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA)** — the social ranking of A versus B should depend only on individuals' rankings of A versus B (grad)
- **single-peaked preferences** — each voter has a favourite point on a line, and options get worse the further they are from it. This restriction removes majority cycles (UG)
- **median voter theorem** — with one dimension and single-peaked preferences, the median voter's ideal point beats all others under majority rule (UG)
- **spatial model of voting** — voters and parties located in a policy space, with voters choosing the nearest party (UG)
- **agenda control / agenda setter** — whoever decides the order or menu of votes can steer outcomes when majorities cycle (grad)
- **strategic (sophisticated) voting** — voting for a less-preferred option to influence the final outcome (UG)
- **paradox of voting** — the chance that one vote is decisive is tiny, so voting seems irrational when it has any cost (UG)
- **rational ignorance** — staying uninformed is individually rational when information costs more than its expected value to a single voter (UG)
- **Condorcet jury theorem** — if voters are independent and each more likely right than wrong, majority accuracy goes to 1 as the group grows (grad)
- **exit / voice / loyalty** — leaving versus complaining as feedback channels in declining organizations, with loyalty raising the use of voice (UG)
- **veto player** — an actor whose agreement is needed to change the status quo. More, and more distant, veto players mean more policy stability (grad)
- **institutions** — the "rules of the game": formal and informal constraints that structure interaction (UG)
- **path dependence** — early choices are self-reinforcing, so history constrains later options (UG)
- **principal–agent problem / delegation** — a principal (voters, legislature) cannot fully monitor an agent (politician, bureaucrat) whose interests differ (grad)
- **bargaining model of war** — war is inefficient, so it needs private information plus incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, or indivisibility (grad)
- **commitment problem** — a party cannot credibly promise not to exploit a future change in power (grad)
- **preference falsification** — publicly stating a preference different from one's private one under social pressure (UG)
- **revolutionary threshold / cascade** — each person joins once enough others have joined. Small changes in the distribution of thresholds can trigger sudden mass mobilization (grad)
- **belief-system constraint** — the degree to which a person's attitudes on different issues predict one another (grad)
- **non-attitudes** — survey answers that look like random responses because no real opinion exists (grad)
- **Receive–Accept–Sample (RAS) model** — opinion as a sample of considerations that the person has received and accepted, weighted by political awareness and predispositions (grad)
- **political awareness / sophistication** — attention to and knowledge of politics. It moderates both exposure to persuasion and resistance to it (grad)
- **heuristics / cues** — shortcuts (party label, endorsements) that let people with little information act as if they had more (UG)
- **conditions for persuasion (Lupia–McCubbins)** — a listener learns from a speaker only if the speaker is believed knowledgeable and to share the listener's interests, or if external forces make the speaker's message credible (grad)
- **framing effect** — changing the weight people give to considerations (not the facts themselves) by how an issue is presented (UG)
- **priming / agenda-setting** — media emphasis makes certain issues more salient, or makes them the standards used to evaluate leaders (UG)
- **motivated reasoning / motivated skepticism** — processing evidence to reach a desired conclusion: accepting congenial claims easily and scrutinizing uncongenial ones (UG)
- **identity-protective cognition** — reasoning that protects one's standing in a valued group, which can be stronger among the more cognitively skilled (grad)
- **partisan perceptual bias** — partisans perceive objective conditions (economy, war) differently, so beliefs need not converge with evidence (grad)
- **retrospective voting** — judging incumbents by past outcomes rather than by future policy promises (UG)
- **folk theory of democracy** — the idealized view that informed voters choose policies and governments respond (UG)
- **affective polarization** — growing dislike and distrust of the opposing party, as distinct from policy (ideological) divergence (UG)
- **partisan sorting** — party identity and ideology or other social identities line up more closely, even if positions do not become more extreme (grad)
- **selective exposure / media choice** — people choose media that match their tastes or politics, so the high-choice environment widens gaps between groups (UG)
- **social capital** — trust, norms of reciprocity, and networks of civic engagement that make collective action easier (UG)
- **inclusive vs extractive institutions** — institutions that spread rights and opportunity widely versus those that concentrate them in an elite (UG)
- **observable implications** — testable consequences of a theory, ideally many and varied (KKV) (grad)
- **text as data / validation** — treating documents as quantitative measurements whose validity has to be shown against human judgment and task-specific checks (grad)

## Canonical instances
- **Condorcet paradox** — three voters with rankings ABC, BCA, CAB produce a majority cycle A>B>C>A — arrow-1951-socialchoice
- **Arrow's general possibility (impossibility) theorem** — four reasonable axioms cannot all be satisfied by any ranking-aggregation rule — arrow-1951-socialchoice
- **Median voter / Hotelling–Downs convergence** — two office-seeking parties converge on the median voter's position on a single dimension — downs-1957-democracy
- **Paradox of voting (calculus of voting)** — the expected benefit of one vote is close to zero, so why does anyone turn out? — downs-1957-democracy
- **Free-rider problem and the "large group" result** — large latent groups stay unorganized unless they have selective incentives — olson-1965-collectiveaction
- **Tragedy of the commons vs Ostrom's counter-cases** — Törbel (Swiss alpine meadows), Hirano/Nagaike/Yamanoka (Japanese iriai forests), Valencia huertas, and Philippine zanjeras sustain commons through self-made rules — ostrom-1990-commons
- **Condorcet jury theorem** — majority accuracy rises toward 1 with group size when votes are independent and individually better than chance. Correlated errors break the result — grofman-1983-jurytheorems
- **Agenda manipulation / "heresthetic"** — controlling the voting order or adding a dimension changes the majority outcome — riker-1982-liberalism
- **Exit vs voice in a declining railway or school** — easy exit by the most quality-sensitive users drains the voice that could fix the decline — hirschman-1970-exitvoice
- **East European Revolution of 1989** — hidden private discontent plus thresholds produce a sudden, unforeseen cascade — kuran-1991-surprise
- **Converse's "non-attitudes"** — panel respondents' answers on policy items look close to random across waves — converse-1964-beliefsystems
- **Shark attacks of 1916 (New Jersey)** — voters punished the incumbent for events no government controls (blind retrospection) — achen-2016-realists
- **Motivated skepticism experiment** — balanced arguments on gun control or affirmative action polarize sophisticated participants — taber-2006-skepticism
- **Bail's Twitter bot experiment** — a month of exposure to out-party retweets made Republicans more conservative — bail-2018-opposingviews
- **Italian regional governments (1970 reform)** — identical institutions, divergent performance explained by civic traditions — putnam-1993-democracywork

## Bridges
- [same-math] Condorcet jury theorem ↔ information theory: majority decoding of a repetition code over a binary symmetric channel — both compute a binomial tail probability that majority voting over n independent noisy copies is correct, and both fail in the same way when errors are correlated.
- [same-math] median voter theorem ↔ statistics/optimization: the median as the L1 minimizer — the median ideal point is the point that minimizes the sum of absolute distances to voters' ideal points, the same object as least-absolute-deviation estimation.
- [same-math] collective action problem (Olson) ↔ decision & game theory: n-person Prisoner's Dilemma / linear public-goods game — Olson's argument is the dominant-strategy analysis of a public-goods game, and Ostrom's commons map onto repeated games with monitoring and graduated punishment.
- [same-math] bargaining model of war (Fearon) ↔ decision & game theory: bargaining under incomplete information and commitment — the bargaining range, screening, and inefficient breakdown are standard results of game theory applied to states.
- [structural-analogy] Lupia–McCubbins conditions for persuasion ↔ game theory & theory of mind: cheap talk (Crawford–Sobel) and trust attribution — a message informs only when the receiver models the sender's knowledge and interests. This is mentalizing put formally, and the same conditions apply when people decide whether to trust LLM outputs.
- [structural-analogy] partisan perceptual bias / motivated reasoning (Bartels, Taber & Lodge, Kahan) ↔ Bayesian theory: non-convergence of posteriors — a Bayesian with shared likelihoods should converge with others as evidence accumulates. Persistent divergence implies different likelihoods (source trust) or utility-laden belief, which are the formal hooks for modeling misinformation.
- [structural-analogy] preference falsification & cascades (Kuran) ↔ theory of mind / network science: common knowledge and threshold (Granovetter) models — private beliefs, public signals, and higher-order beliefs about others' beliefs set off coordination tipping points. This is the same structure as pluralistic ignorance and complex contagion.
- [structural-analogy] belief-system constraint (Converse) ↔ linear algebra: low-rank structure of the attitude matrix — "constraint" amounts to how much variance in a respondents × issues matrix the first principal component or ideal-point dimension captures (as in NOMINATE and IRT scaling).
- [structural-analogy] framing as reweighting of considerations (Chong & Druckman; Zaller's RAS) ↔ communication theory: framing & agenda-setting — the communication-science frame concept (Entman; Scheufele) is the message-side counterpart of political science's model of how audiences weight considerations.
- [metaphor] exit / voice ↔ communication theory: feedback channels in cybernetic control — exit is a quantity (market) signal and voice an articulated (message) signal returning to the decision-maker. Calling them "channels" helps thinking but is not a formal channel model.
