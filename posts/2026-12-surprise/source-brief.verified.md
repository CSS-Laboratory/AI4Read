## A. Before Shannon: the old measures

### 1. Nyquist: speed grows with the logarithm of the number of signal values
**S2: CORRECTED (one major, one minor).** S2 changes: (a) [major] removed "eight values should give four times the speed": by `W = K log m` eight values give log 8 / log 2 = **3** times the speed of two values; Pierce p. 38 prints "four times", an apparent slip in the source (see Traps). (b) [minor] `W = K log m` is from Nyquist's **1924** paper as Pierce reports it (pp. 35–36); Pierce's account of the 1928 paper (p. 39) is about redundant high frequencies, not this relation.
- **Claim.** Nyquist (in his 1924 paper "Certain Factors Affecting Telegraph Speed", as reported by Pierce) related telegraph speed `W` to `m`, the number of distinct current values: `W = K log m` [S2]. Going from two current values (on-off) to four doubles the speed; going to three (+1, 0, −1) raises it "by 60 per cent" (Pierce p. 38; log2 3 = 1.585, so +58.5%) [S2]. He also realized that fluctuations in attenuation, interference or noise, and power limits make many values hard to use.
- **Page.** Pierce pp. 35–38 (pdf 51–54) [S2]; Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1) cites Nyquist 1924 (p. 324) and 1928 (p. 617) as one basis of a general theory.
- **Quote.** Pierce p. 38 (pdf 54): "we should take the logarithm of the number of symbols in order to get the line speed".
- **Numbers and equations.** `W = K log m` (Pierce p. 36). Table of independent 0-or-1 choices: 2 choices give 4 combinations, 3 give 8, M give 2^M (Pierce p. 37). Quadruplex telegraph: four current values double the speed (p. 38). Eight values: 3 times the speed of two by the formula (ours, checked in Python) [S2].
- **Traps.** Pierce is explaining Nyquist in his own words ("Nyquist tells us ..."); Nyquist's 1924 and 1928 papers are UNSEEN (not in our library). `K` is a constant that depends on the number of values sent per second; do not drop it. "Number of signal levels" is correct for Nyquist, but his log is not yet a measure of *probability-weighted* information. [S2] Pierce p. 38 says eight current values "should enable us to send four times as fast as with two current values" (checked on the page image). That contradicts the `W = K log m` he has just stated (log2 8 = 3), and the same sentence lists only seven values in its first set ("0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7"). Never repeat "four times"; if the eight-value case is used, give 3 and label the computation ours.
- **Tensions.** Nyquist's relation counts what the line can carry; Hartley's counts what the sender can choose (our framing [S2]). Shannon p. 1 treats both papers as "a basis for such a theory".
- **Toy idea.** A café with a sign that can show 2, 4 or 8 colours. Each extra doubling of colours adds one "nod" of capacity.

### 2. Hartley: the logarithm of the number of possible sequences, and its stated condition
**S2: CORRECTED (minor).** S2 changes: (a) the link between Hartley's measure and MacKay's raw bit content is ours: MacKay never names Hartley (whole-text search). (b) The Cover tension now cites Cover's own bibliography, which dates Hartley 1928. (c) Cover pages are 1991-edition pages.
- **Claim.** Hartley (1928) pictured the sender as choosing symbols from a set and defined the information of a message as the logarithm of the number of possible sequences: `H = n log s`, `n` symbols chosen from `s` kinds. Pierce says this fits "our present knowledge" only when symbols are chosen independently and are equally likely. Hartley also noted that restrictions on selection (E more often than Z) should govern code lengths. Shannon p. 1: "As was pointed out by Hartley the most natural choice is the logarithmic function."
- **Page.** Pierce pp. 39–40 (pdf 55–56); Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1), note 2: Hartley, "Transmission of Information", Bell System Technical Journal, July 1928, p. 535; MacKay p. 74 (pdf 86) for the comparable modern quantity (the pairing with Hartley is ours [S2]); Cover p. 49 (pdf 71) for the date discrepancy; Cover p. 517 (pdf 541), ref. [133].
- **Quote.** Pierce p. 40 (pdf 56): "only if successive symbols are chosen independently and if any of the s symbols is equally likely to be selected".
- **Numbers and equations.** `H = n log s` (Pierce p. 40). MacKay's **raw bit content** `H0(X) = log2 |A_X|` is the same idea (our pairing; MacKay does not mention Hartley [S2]): "a lower bound for the number of binary questions that are always guaranteed to identify an outcome" (MacKay p. 74), and it "does not include any probabilistic element". For n symbols from s kinds: log2 (s^n) = n log2 s.
- **Traps.** (a) Pierce's sentence has two conditions, independence and equal probability; keep both. (b) Hartley's own paper is UNSEEN: everything about his wording comes from Pierce and Shannon. (c) Do not write that Hartley "ignored" frequency: Pierce says he noted that restrictions should govern code length, and that Morse "himself understood this" (p. 40); what Hartley lacked was a probability-weighted measure.
- **Tensions.** Cover p. 49 writes "In the 193Os [sic, OCR for 1930s], Hartley introduced a logarithmic measure of information for communication. His measure was essentially the logarithm of the alphabet size." The date conflicts with Shannon's footnote and Pierce (both 1928); Cover's own reference list dates the paper 1928 (p. 517, ref. [133]: "Bell Sys. Tech. Journal, 7: 535, 1928") [S2]. The page image of p. 49 reads "1930s", so the slip is in the 1991 book, not only in the OCR [S2]. The content ("logarithm of the alphabet size") agrees with the others.
- **Toy idea.** A 32-cup tray: Hartley says 5 bits for any cup, because it counts cups, not chances. The village in unit 7 gives rain 1 bit and snow 3 although there are four words.

### 3. Why a logarithm: the three reasons and the additivity check
**S2: CORRECTED (minor).** S2 changes: hedges restored. Shannon says the parameters "tend to vary linearly" and "One feels ... two punched cards should have twice the capacity". Pierce's four-lines step is "It also seems reasonable ... If this is so".
- **Claim.** Shannon gives three reasons for a logarithmic measure: it is practically more useful (time, bandwidth, number of relays "tend to vary linearly with the logarithm of the number of possibilities"), it is nearer to intuition ("One feels, for example, that two punched cards should have twice the capacity of one"), and it is mathematically more suitable [S2]. The base fixes the unit: base 2 gives "binary digits, or ... bits", a word he credits to J. W. Tukey. MacKay shows additivity: for independent `x` and `y`, `h(x, y) = h(x) + h(y)`. Pierce p. 80: four telegraph lines send four times as many binary digits as one; it "seems reasonable" that they send four times as much information, and "If this is so", information should be measured by the logarithm of the number of messages [S2].
- **Page.** Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1); MacKay p. 68 (pdf 80), eqs. 4.4–4.6; Pierce p. 80 (pdf 96).
- **Quotes.** Shannon p. 1: "A device with two stable positions, such as a relay or a flip-flop circuit, can store one bit of information." MacKay p. 68: "we might want any measure of the 'amount of information gained' to have the property of additivity". Pierce p. 80: "amount of information should be measured, not by the number of possible messages, but by the logarithm of this number".
- **Numbers and equations.** `N` devices store `N` bits since there are `2^N` states and `log2 2^N = N` (Shannon p. 1). `log2 M = 3.32 log10 M` (Shannon p. 1). `P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)` gives `log 1/P(x,y) = log 1/P(x) + log 1/P(y)` (MacKay p. 68). Tukey's word "bit": Shannon p. 1.
- **Traps.** "Bit" has two meanings in MacKay: a unit and a 0/1 variable (p. 32). Shannon's Theorem 2 (p. 11) derives `-K Σ p log p` from three requirements, but says the theorem and its assumptions "are in no way necessary for the present theory" (see unit 6); reasons 1–3 above are his motivation, not a proof of uniqueness.
- **Tensions.** None between sources; MacKay (additivity), Shannon (convenience) and Pierce (telegraph lines) give three routes to the same log.
- **Toy idea.** Rain today and snow tomorrow: 1 + 3 = 4 bits, the same as one of 16 equally likely pairs of weather (our check, W1 numbers).

## B. Yes/no questions, twenty questions, weighing

### 4. Equal chances: questions equal log2 of the number of options
**S2: VERIFIED.** Pages, quotes and numbers match (MacKay p. 70, p. 74; Cover pp. 5–6, 1991 edition). The rats-and-cups puzzle is correctly labelled ours.
- **Claim.** With 64 equally likely objects, six well-chosen yes/no questions identify one, and the answers read as the binary number. Cover's opening example: 32 equally likely outcomes need 5-bit labels, and the entropy is `log 32 = 5`. MacKay says the questioning strategy "defines a way of encoding the random variable x as a binary file".
- **Page.** MacKay p. 70 (pdf 82), Example 4.3 "sixty-three"; Cover pp. 5–6 (pdf 27–28), Examples 1.1.1–1.1.2; MacKay p. 74 for the lower bound.
- **Quotes.** MacKay p. 70: "Intuitively, the best questions successively divide the 64 possibilities into equal sized sets. Six questions suffice." MacKay p. 70: "Our questioning strategy defines a way of encoding the random variable x as a binary file."
- **Numbers and equations.** The six questions: x ≥ 32? x mod 32 ≥ 16? x mod 16 ≥ 8? x mod 8 ≥ 4? x mod 4 ≥ 2? x mod 2 = 1? Each answer has Shannon information content `log2 (1/0.5) = 1` bit, so the total is 6 bits. Example: 35 gives 100011 (MacKay p. 70). Cover Example 1.1.1: `H = -Σ (1/32) log (1/32) = log 32 = 5` bits (p. 5). Ours: one poisoned cup in 32 and five rats gives 5 answers at once; four rats cannot decide 32 cups because 2^4 = 16 < 32.
- **Traps.** The rats-and-cups puzzle is not in any source. It asks all questions at once (not adaptive); MacKay's game asks them in turn. For equal chances both need `log2 N`. The "twenty questions" game MacKay mentions is the general game; "sixty-three" is his dull version (p. 70).
- **Tensions.** MacKay and Cover agree on the number. MacKay's lower-bound wording (p. 74) says "guaranteed", meaning in the worst case, not on average.
- **Toy idea.** 32 cups and 5 rats (Kunhao's puzzle); the `stepper` of questions as a table of answers read as a binary number.

### 5. The weighing problem: the best question has outcomes as nearly equally likely as possible
**S2: CORRECTED (minor).** S2 changes: (a) MacKay's hedge restored: "Thus we might conclude" (p. 70). (b) UNSEEN item 4 resolved: Exercise 4.1 is marked "[2, p.69]", so its printed solution is Figure 4.2, "An optimal solution to the weighing problem" (p. 69, pdf 81). Exercise 4.9 is marked "[1 ]" with no page, so the book prints **no solution** to it (by MacKay's own convention, Preface p. xi). Our numbers were rerun in Python and match.
- **Claim.** 12 balls, one heavier or lighter, a balance with three outcomes. There are 24 hypotheses (12 balls × heavy or light) and 3^3 = 27 > 24 > 9 = 3^2, so "in principle" three weighings might do and two cannot. At each step of an optimal procedure the three outcomes are as close as possible to equiprobable. Weighing balls 1–6 against 7–12 cannot balance, so it rules out only half the hypotheses. MacKay: "Thus we might conclude" that an experiment's outcome is guaranteed to be most informative when its distribution is uniform [S2].
- **Page.** MacKay p. 66 (pdf 78, Exercise 4.1); p. 68 (pdf 80); p. 69 (pdf 81, Fig. 4.2, the printed solution to Exercise 4.1) [S2]; p. 70 (pdf 82); p. 84 (pdf 96), Exercise 4.9 (no printed solution) [S2]; Preface p. xi (pdf 11), the solution-page convention [S2].
- **Quote.** MacKay p. 68: "at each step of an optimal procedure, the three outcomes ('left heavier', 'right heavier', and 'balance') are as close as possible to equiprobable". MacKay p. 70: "the outcome of a random experiment is guaranteed to be most informative if the probability distribution over outcomes is uniform".
- **Numbers and equations.** First weighing must split 24 hypotheses 8:8:8; the second must give a 3:3:2 split (p. 70). Fig. 4.2 (p. 69): first weighing 1 2 3 4 against 5 6 7 8 [S2]. Our computation (rerun in Python at S2: 1.585, 1.000, 4.585 confirmed): 4 against 4 gives outcome probabilities 1/3, 1/3, 1/3 and 1.585 bits; 6 against 6 gives 1/2, 1/2, 0 and 1.000 bit; all 24 cases are `log2 24 = 4.585` bits. Exercise 4.9: some say 6 against 6 "conveys no information at all", others disagree; MacKay asks the reader to "Explain to the second group why they are both right and wrong" (p. 84). Ours: about *which ball*, 6 v 6 gives 0 bits (every ball is still possible); about *which ball and heavy or light* it gives 1 bit.
- **Traps.** "Uniform is most informative" is MacKay's own conclusion from this example, tied to `H(X) ≤ log |A_X|` (p. 70, p. 33); it is not a theorem about every question. The numbers 1.585 and 1.000 are ours, not printed in MacKay (Exercise 4.1(e) asks the reader for exactly these). MacKay prints no answer to Exercise 4.9 (marker "[1 ]", no solution page) [S2]; our answer above stays labelled ours. It was checked at S2: after 6 v 6 "left heavier", the 12 hypotheses 1+…6+, 7−…12− leave every ball equally possible (0 bits about which ball) and halve the 24 ball-and-direction hypotheses (1 bit).
- **Tensions.** None. Cover p. 15 makes the same point for a question tree ("An efficient first question ... splits the probability in half").

### 6. The village question tree: surprisal as questions, entropy as the average
**S2: VERIFIED.** Pages, quotes and numbers match. Eq. 5.61 read on the page image: `H(X) ≤ EQ < H(X) + 1`. Trap (c) confirmed, with a nuance (see below). Cover pages are 1991-edition pages.
- **Claim.** For a source with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8 (letters A–D in Shannon; four values in Cover), entropy is 7/4 bits per symbol, and the code A = 0, B = 10, C = 110, D = 111 reaches it. Asking "Is X = a?", then "= b?", then "= c?" gives an expected 1.75 questions, which is the minimum. In general the minimum expected number of questions lies between H(X) and H(X) + 1. Cover also states that a sequence of questions is equivalent to a code and the average number of questions to the average code length.
- **Page.** Shannon p. 18 (pdf 18); Cover pp. 14–15 (pdf 36–37), Example 2.1.2; Cover pp. 94–95 (pdf 116–117), §5.7 item 1, eq. 5.61; Cover p. 6 (pdf 28), horse race (entropy 2 bits, code 0, 10, 110, 1110, 111100–111111, average 2 bits against 3).
- **Quotes.** Cover p. 15: "The resulting expected number of binary questions required is 1.75." Cover p. 94: "We now digress to show the equivalence of coding and the game of 20 questions." Shannon p. 18: "successive symbols being chosen independently".
- **Numbers and equations.** `H = -(1/2 log 1/2 + 1/4 log 1/4 + 2/8 log 1/8) = 7/4` bits (Shannon p. 18). Average code length `N(1/2·1 + 1/4·2 + 2/8·3) = 7/4 N` (p. 18). Maximum entropy of the original set `log 4 = 2`, so relative entropy is 7/8 (p. 18; careful, "relative entropy" here means H/Hmax, not KL). Surprisals: 1, 2, 3, 3 bits (ours, `log2 (1/p)`). Cover's bound: `H(X) ≤ EQ < H(X) + 1` (p. 95, eq. 5.61; confirmed on the page image [S2]). Ours (to be run in code): shares 0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1 give H = 1.357 bits, and a Huffman tree gives lengths 1, 2, 3, 3 and an average of 1.5.
- **Traps.** (a) Powers of two: see trap 2 at the top. (b) The village is toyworld W1 (`toyworlds.md`: rain 1/2, sun 1/4, wind 1/8, snow 1/8); Shannon's letters are A–D and his entropy is "bits per symbol", not per day, unless the village sends one symbol per day. ## C. Surprisal and entropy

### 8. Entropy: the average surprisal, and its properties
**S2: VERIFIED.** All pages, quotes and numbers match: H(1/2,1/3,1/6) = 1.459 = H(1/2,1/2) + ½H(2/3,1/3); 1.5; .811; 1.75, 2, 0. One precision note, no change needed: Shannon's property 4 excepts a permutation of the p's, which leaves H unchanged (p. 12). The writer should keep that exception if "averaging raises H" is stated in full.
- **Claim.** Entropy is the average Shannon information content, `H(X) = Σ P(x) log2 1/P(x)`, with `0 log 1/0 = 0`. Another name is the uncertainty of X. It depends only on the probabilities, not on the values the outcomes take. `H ≥ 0`, with equality only when one outcome is certain; `H ≤ log |A_X|`, with equality only when all outcomes are equally likely. Entropy is additive for independent variables. Shannon asked for a measure with three properties (continuity; increasing in `n` for equal probabilities; breaking a choice into two successive choices gives a weighted sum), and Theorem 2 says the only such `H` is `-K Σ p log p`.
- **Page.** Shannon pp. 10–12 (pdf 10–12): requirements and Fig. 6 (p. 10), Theorem 2 and properties 1–2 (p. 11), property 4 (p. 12); MacKay p. 32 (pdf 44) and p. 33 (pdf 45); Cover pp. 13–14 (pdf 35–36); Pierce pp. 80–83 (pdf 96–99).
- **Quotes.** MacKay p. 32: "The entropy of an ensemble X is defined to be the average Shannon information content of an outcome". Cover p. 13: "It does not depend on the actual values taken by the random variable X, but only on the probabilities." Shannon p. 11: "Thus only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish." Shannon p. 10: "With equally likely events there is more choice, or uncertainty, when there are more possible events."
- **Numbers and equations.** `H(1/2, 1/3, 1/6) = H(1/2, 1/2) + 1/2 H(2/3, 1/3)` (Shannon p. 10, Fig. 6). MacKay's decomposition example: shares 1/2, 1/4, 1/4 give `H = 1.5` bits (p. 33, eq. 2.41). Entropy of an English letter drawn at random: "about 4.11 bits" for the probabilities in MacKay's Table 2.9 (p. 32). Fair coin 1 bit per toss; a coin turning up heads 3/4 of the time has `H = .811` bit per toss (Pierce pp. 82–83). Binary entropy is maximal (1 bit) at `p = 1/2` and 0 at `p = 0` or 1 (Shannon p. 11, Fig. 7; Pierce p. 83). Ours: village `H = 1.75`; uniform `H = 2`; only-rain `H = 0`; any averaging of probabilities toward equality raises H (Shannon p. 12, property 4).
- **Traps.** (a) Shannon's "relative entropy" is `H/Hmax` (p. 14); modern "relative entropy" is KL: avoid the term. (b) `H` is a property of the *distribution*, not of one message. ### 14. Who encodes and who decodes

**S2c: VERIFIED.** Written from Cover & Thomas 1991 pp. 86, 92–97, 101 and MacKay 2003 pp. 98–102, 105.
- **Claim.** Cover & Thomas: for a given distribution, an optimal prefix code, meaning one of shortest expected length, "can be constructed by a simple algorithm discovered by Huffman" (p. 92). The procedure (Example 5.6.1, p. 93): combine the two least likely symbols into one symbol whose probability is their sum, repeat "until we are finally left with only one symbol", then assign codewords; the two symbols combined at a step get codewords that differ only in the last bit. **Theorem 5.8.1** (p. 101): "Huffman coding is optimal, i.e., if C* is the Huffman code and C' is any other code, then L(C*) ≤ L(C')", where L is the expected length. Cover proves it by induction for a binary alphabet and says the proof "can be extended" to a D-ary alphabet (p. 101). Huffman coding is "greedy": it "coalesces the two least likely symbols at each stage", and the proof shows that "this local optimality ensures a global optimality of the final code" (p. 101). For questions (p. 94): "Since the Huffman code is the best source code for a random variable, the optimal series of questions is that determined by the Huffman code", and its expected number of questions satisfies `H(X) ≤ EQ < H(X) + 1` (eq. 5.61, p. 95; unit 6). **MacKay states it more plainly** (Algorithm 5.4, p. 99): "1. Take the two least probable symbols in the alphabet. These two symbols will be given the longest codewords, which will have equal length, and differ only in the last digit. 2. Combine these two symbols into a single symbol, and repeat." The code is built "backwards", from the leaves of the tree (p. 99); his chapter summary: "At each iteration, the two least probable symbols are combined" (p. 102). He sets the proof as Exercise 5.16, solved on p. 105.
- **Conditions (keep them with the claim).**
  1. *A known, fixed distribution.* Cover: "for a given distribution" (p. 92); for questions, "Assuming we know the probability distribution on the objects" (p. 94). MacKay: "If we wish to communicate a sequence of outcomes from one unchanging ensemble, then a Huffman code may be convenient", but Huffman codes "do not handle changing ensemble probabilities with any elegance" (pp. 100–101).
  2. *One outcome at a time, a whole number of digits per outcome.* MacKay calls the result "an optimal symbol code" (p. 100); "They are optimal symbol codes, but for practical purposes we don't want a symbol code" (p. 101). The extra cost is "between 0 and 1 bits per symbol" (p. 101); coding blocks of outcomes reduces it (p. 101).
  3. *The class it beats.* Cover's proof (§5.8) compares instantaneous (prefix) codes: Lemma 5.8.1 is about "an optimal instantaneous code" (p. 97). On p. 92, just before §5.6, Cover notes that uniquely decodable codes allow no other set of codeword lengths than prefix codes, so the bounds also hold for them.
  4. *The best script is not unique.* "There are many optimal codes: inverting all the bits or exchanging two codewords of the same length will give another optimal code" (p. 97). With ties, the lengths can differ: Cover's Example 5.7.3 (p. 96) gets lengths (2, 2, 2, 2) or (1, 2, 3, 3) for shares 1/3, 1/3, 1/4, 1/12, with the same expected length. MacKay: if there is more than one way to pick the two least probable symbols, "the choice may be made in any manner – the expected length of the code will not depend on the choice" (p. 99).
  5. *When the best script reaches H exactly.* Cover Theorem 5.3.1 (p. 86): the expected length of any instantaneous code is at least H, "with equality iff" every share is `D^-l` (for yes/no questions, a power of 1/2): the distribution is "D-adic".

- **Traps.** (a) *Bottom-up, not top-down.* Splitting the outcomes into two groups as near to equally likely as possible, again and again, is **not** always optimal. MacKay p. 100: "It is not the case, however, that optimal codes can always be constructed by a greedy top-down method" (Example 5.18: 2.53 vs 1.97); MacKay p. 98: bisecting "has the right spirit, as in the weighing problem, but it is not necessarily optimal; it achieves L(C, X) ≤ H(X) + 2"; Cover p. 97 on Fano's equal-split procedure: "This scheme, though not optimal in general, achieves L(C) ≤ H(X) + 2." So "choose the question whose answers are most nearly equally likely" (MacKay p. 70, unit 5) is about one experiment; it does not build the best whole script. (b) "Optimal" means the shortest *expected* length for the given distribution, among codes for one outcome at a time; individual outcomes can get longer codewords than `log2 1/p` (Cover p. 96; MacKay p. 99, "in some cases longer and in some cases shorter than the ideal codelengths"). (c) "Greedy" in Cover p. 101 means the merge rule is local; the global optimality is the theorem's result, not the meaning of the word. (d) MacKay: Huffman codes, "although widely trumpeted as 'optimal', have many defects for practical purposes" (p. 101); the defects (changing shares, the extra bit) are boundaries 2 and 6 of the post, not errors in the theorem. (e) Do not name David Huffman's paper or its date from outside knowledge: our sources give only Cover's reference "[138]" (p. 92) and MacKay's name for the algorithm.
- **Tensions.** Cover reaches the rule from codes and then maps it to questions (p. 94); MacKay builds it as a tree from the leaves (p. 99) and contrasts it with the top-down method that the weighing problem suggests (pp. 98–100). They agree on the procedure and on optimality.
