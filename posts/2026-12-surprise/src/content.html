How much is a surprise worth?

A surprise is worth the number of fair yes/no questions it saves you. The rarer the news, the more questions it saves, and the average over all the news a source can send is called its entropy.

1 · Motivation

Counting messages, and what the count misses

The pain (ours). Take a town where it rains most days. The weather office sends one report a day from a fixed list. “It rained today” and “It snowed in July” are each one pick from that list, so a measure that only counts the possible reports gives them the same number. Yet the first tells you almost nothing, and the second is news.

Counting is how engineers measured messages in the 1920s. Pierce, in his 1961 book, reports Nyquist’s result of 1924: the speed of a telegraph line depends on how many different current values it can send Pierce 1961, pp. 35–38; three values (plus, zero, minus) raise it “by 60 per cent” over two Pierce 1961, p. 38. In 1928 Hartley measured a message by the number of messages the sender could have picked (in fact by a function of that number, which step 1 explains) Pierce 1961, pp. 39–40. Pierce says this is acceptable only if successive symbols “are chosen independently” and each “is equally likely to be selected” Pierce 1961, p. 40. Hartley saw that some letters are chosen more often than others, E more than Z, and that this should govern the lengths of signals Pierce 1961, p. 40. What was missing (our reading) was a number that weighs each message by how likely it is. This post builds it in a coffee shop, checks it in a village, and asks what it leaves out. First, a guess.

Guess first (answered in step 4). A submarine hides in one of 64 squares, and every square is equally likely. You fire at one square and miss. Count news in yes/no questions: a piece of news is worth the questions it saves you. How many questions is that miss worth?

2 · Intuition

The café with a hatch

Picture a loud café. The grinder drowns every word, so customers and barista cannot talk, and the barista works in a back room where she never sees a customer. Orders pass through a hatch on paper tickets. The same card hangs on the wall by the till and on the wall in the back room: a list of yes/no questions in a fixed order. The customer answers the card’s questions on the ticket, one after another, and stops at the first yes. Every answer costs one second of the queue’s time. The owner knows from the cash register’s record that out of every eight regulars, four want a flat white, two a latte, one a mocha and one a matcha. What should the card say?

The old way: split the menu. The menu board is a grid with two drinks on top and two below. The card asks, “Is your drink on the left side of the board?” and then, “Is it the top one?” Every ticket has two answers, and the barista always knows the drink. But half of the regulars want the flat white and spend two answers each, and the one regular in eight who wants matcha spends the same two. The card split the drinks into equal halves and ignored how many customers stand behind each drink.

The rule that changes: split the customers. The card now says: “Flat white? Latte? Mocha?”, and the customer stops at her first yes. A yes to the first question settles four customers out of eight with one answer; a no leaves four. A yes to the second settles two more; a no leaves two. A no to the third leaves only the matcha, so the card needs no fourth question. The flat white costs one answer, the latte two, the mocha and the matcha three each. Over eight customers that is four plus four plus three plus three: fourteen answers, one and three quarters for each customer, against two for the old way. The rare drinks cost more, and that is fair: a matcha order is unusual news. The rarer the order, the bigger the surprise, and the more questions it is worth.

Three checks. If the card began with “Matcha?”, most tickets would start with a no, which rules out only one customer in eight: a question helps most when both answers are likely. If all four drinks sold equally, the clever card would save nothing, and a card built for the old shares would do worse than the plain board.

Who does what. The customer knows the drink and holds the card. Following it, she turns her drink into a row of yes and no on the ticket, for example no, no, yes. The ticket goes through the hatch. The barista never sees the customer: she sees only the ticket, and she holds the same card. She reads the ticket against the card and names the drink. The ticket alone says nothing: “no, no, yes” means mocha only under this card, so the same card must hang on both walls before the day starts. We call the card the script, and the row of answers on a ticket its string.

The parts of the café (later sections use these names):

  • The four drinks.
  • The share of regulars who want each drink (it can be out of date).
  • One question on the card, answered yes or no; one second.
  • The script: the card, the same on both walls.
  • The customer, who knows the drink and fills in the ticket.
  • The ticket: the string of answers for one customer.
  • The barista, who sees only the ticket and holds the same card.
  • A drink’s cost: the answers on its ticket.
  • The day’s average: answers per customer over the day.
  • The menu-board card: the old way.

Check yourself

(a) Which customers get the longest tickets, and why? (b) A day comes when all four drinks sell equally. Would the clever card still be best?

Answer (a)

The mocha and matcha customers, three answers each. Each is one regular in eight. The card spends its questions where the customers are.

Answer (b)

No. The average would be one plus two plus three plus three, divided by four: two and a quarter. The plain board costs exactly two. A card is built for the shares it was made for.

3 · Concrete · step 1

The weather village: equal chances first

Now leave the café for the field’s own setting. A village radio sends one word a day: rain, sun, wind or snow, with shares of days one half, one quarter, one eighth and one eighth: the owner’s numbers, with drinks swapped for weather words. Shannon’s example uses these shares Shannon 1948, p. 18, and so do Cover and Thomas Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 14–15; we call each symbol a day. Shannon has “successive symbols being chosen independently” Shannon 1948, p. 18, and so do we, though real weather is not like that (section 4). Each equation is followed by a line “In the café” naming the café parts it stands for.

Term · Three words in the question

Worth: the number of fair yes/no questions an outcome saves you, not usefulness. A question is fair when its two answers are equally likely. Surprise: how unlikely an outcome was under a stated model of the source (a list of how likely each message is), not a feeling. Information: Shannon’s engineering sense, one choice from a set of possible messages Shannon 1948, p. 1. Pierce says the confusion about how communication theory relates to physics “is sometimes aggravated when more than one meaning of information creeps into a discussion” Pierce 1961, p. 24.

Step 1 · Equal chances: count the halvings

Guess first. One of 32 cups of water is poisoned. A rat that drinks any poisoned cup dies within the hour, and you have one hour. All the rats drink at the same time, each from any cups you choose. What is the smallest number of rats that is always enough?

The instance. The cups-and-rats framing is ours. In MacKay’s version one of 64 integers is chosen and you ask yes/no questions: “Intuitively, the best questions successively divide the 64 possibilities into equal sized sets. Six questions suffice.” MacKay 2003, p. 70 What happens. With 32 cups, one good question halves them: 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. That is five halvings. The rats ask all five questions at once, because each rat’s fate, dead or alive, is one yes/no answer.

The pattern. 32 is two multiplied by itself five times, and 64 is six times. One answer of a fair yes/no question is one bit, a word Shannon credits to J. W. Tukey Shannon 1948, p. 1; here it is a unit of worth. From now on, “questions” and “bits” name the same unit.

The formula (E1). For \(N\) equally likely options, where \(N\) is a power of two, the number of questions \(k\) satisfies

\[N = 2^{k}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad k = \log_2 N .\]

Read aloud: “the number of questions is the number of times you must halve \(N\) to reach 1.” The symbol \(\log_2\) asks: two multiplied by itself how many times gives this number? For other \(N\), MacKay’s “raw bit content” \(\log_2 N\) is “a lower bound for the number of binary questions that are always guaranteed to identify an outcome” MacKay 2003, p. 74; “guaranteed” means in the worst case, not on average (ours).

The check. \(\log_2 32 = 5\) and \(\log_2 64 = 6\). Cover and Thomas open with the same case, \(\log 32 = 5\) bits Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 5. What it does not say: four rats cannot decide 32 cups, because \(2^4 = 16\) is less than 32 (ours).

In the café. \(N\) is the number of drinks on the menu when all sell equally, each with the same share. \(k\) is the number of questions on a ticket. The 2 in \(2^k\) is the two answers to one question. Halving the board is the menu-board card, the old way.

Guess first · answer

Five. Each rat gives one yes/no answer, so five rats give five answers at once, enough to tell \(2^5 = 32\) cups apart. Four rats give only \(2^4 = 16\) outcomes.

Look inside: Nyquist and Hartley in symbols

As Pierce reports it, Nyquist related the speed \(W\) of a line to the number \(m\) of current values by \(W = K\log m\), where \(K\) is a constant that depends on how many values are sent per second Pierce 1961, p. 36. Two values to four doubles the speed. Three values give \(\log_2 3 = 1.585\) times the speed of two, a rise of 58.5 per cent; Pierce says “60 per cent” Pierce 1961, p. 38. Eight values give \(\log_2 8 = 3\) times (ours). Pierce’s “four times” Pierce 1961, p. 38 is an apparent slip, and his first list of values shows only seven (the second has eight).

Hartley’s measure is \(H = n\log s\) for \(n\) symbols from \(s\) kinds: there are \(s^n\) messages, and \(\log_2(s^n) = n\log_2 s\) Pierce 1961, p. 40. Shannon gives three reasons for a logarithm. It is practically useful: time, bandwidth and the number of relays “tend to vary linearly” with the logarithm of the number of possibilities. It is nearer to intuition: “One feels, for example, that two punched cards should have twice the capacity of one.” And it is mathematically suitable Shannon 1948, p. 1. These are his motives, not a proof that no other measure could work. With base 2, \(N\) two-state devices store \(N\) bits, because \(\log_2 2^N = N\) Shannon 1948, p. 1.

3 · Concrete · step 2

Step 2 · Unequal chances: surprisal

The instance. The village again: rain 1/2, sun 1/4, wind 1/8, snow 1/8. The best questions go like the card: “Rain?”, then “Sun?”, then “Wind?”, and then only snow is left. Rain costs one question, sun two, wind three and snow three. Write yes as 0 and no as 1, and the strings are 0, 10, 110 and 111 Shannon 1948, p. 18 Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 15.

The question and the pattern. What rule gives the number of questions an outcome costs? A share of one half means halving the crowd once, one quarter twice, one eighth three times. The cost is how many times you must halve the whole crowd to be left with a group the size of that share.

The formula (E2). For an outcome \(x\) whose share is \(P(x)\), the cost is

\[h(x) = \log_2 \frac{1}{P(x)} .\]

Read aloud: “the surprisal of \(x\) is the log, base 2, of one over the share of \(x\).” MacKay calls it the “Shannon information content” MacKay 2003, p. 32; Gibson and colleagues say surprisal, and information content Gibson et al. 2019, p. 4. We use surprisal.

The check. For snow, \(\log_2 8 = 3\), and the four words give 1, 2, 3, 3, as the questions did. Move the slider to check MacKay’s table: share 0.001 is worth 10.0 questions, 0.01 gives 6.6, 0.1 gives 3.3, 0.2 gives 2.3, and 0.5 gives 1.0 MacKay 2003, p. 67. At share 1 the worth is 0: a sure thing tells you nothing.

A second concept: independent days add. Rain today, snow tomorrow. The days are independent, so the pair has share \(\tfrac12\times\tfrac18 = \tfrac1{16}\), and \(\log_2 16 = 4\). The separate costs are 1 and 3, and \(1 + 3 = 4\). In general, for independent \(x\) and \(y\), \(P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)\), and a log turns a product into a sum:

\[h(x,y) = \log_2\frac{1}{P(x)P(y)} = h(x) + h(y).\quad\text{(E3)}\]

MacKay’s reason: “we might want any measure of the ‘amount of information gained’ to have the property of additivity” MacKay 2003, p. 68. It needs independence, which the village assumes.

What it does not say. Only shares that are powers of one half (1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, …) cost a whole number of questions (ours). A share of 0.3 would cost 1.74, and a question cannot be asked 1.74 times. So Gibson and colleagues say the information content approximates the length of the shortest uniquely decipherable script (a script whose rows of answers can be cut back into words in only one way) Gibson et al. 2019, p. 4; step 6 returns to this.

In the café. \(x\) is a drink, here a weather word. \(P(x)\) is its share in the cash register’s record. \(h(x)\) is the drink’s cost: the answers on its ticket under the best script. “Log of one over the share” counts how often you must halve the crowd, one question at a time. Additivity: two customers in a row, their tickets joined.

3 · Concrete · step 3

Step 3 · The average: entropy

The instance. Take eight days that match the shares: four rain, two sun, one wind, one snow. Rain costs 1 question each, sun 2, wind 3, snow 3. The total is \(4 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 14\) questions in 8 days: 1.75 per day. The question. What one number describes the source? In words. Weigh each word’s cost by how often it comes, and add.

The formula (E4). The entropy \(H\) of a source is the average surprisal:

\[H = \sum_x P(x)\,\log_2\frac{1}{P(x)} .\]

Read aloud: “the sum, over all words, of the share of the word times its cost.” The sign \(\Sigma\) means add up. An outcome with share 0 contributes 0, by the convention that \(0\times\log 1/0\) is defined to be 0 MacKay 2003, p. 32. For the village,

\[\tfrac12\cdot 1 + \tfrac14\cdot 2 + \tfrac18\cdot 3 + \tfrac18\cdot 3 = 1.75 .\]

Cover and Thomas write of the same tree: “The resulting expected number of binary questions required is 1.75.” Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 15 Before and after the crucial change: two-question labels cost 2.000 questions a day; the script built for the shares costs 1.750. Try other shares below.

Three settings. “All equal”: \(H = 2.000\), the most possible for four words MacKay 2003, p. 33 Shannon 1948, p. 11. “Only rain”: \(H = 0.000\), and “no channel is required” Shannon 1948, p. 18. “Reversed”: \(H\) is unchanged, because it depends only on the shares Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 13.

A script has its own average (E5). A script that spends \(L(x)\) questions on word \(x\) averages

\[\mathrm{EQ} = \sum_x P(x)\,L(x),\]

where EQ is short for the expected number of questions. The village script on the village: \(\mathrm{EQ} = 1.750 = H\). On a village where all four words have share 1/4, the same script (lengths 1, 2, 3, 3) averages \(\tfrac14(1+2+3+3) = 2.25\), worse than the 2.000 of plain labels. On “only rain” it averages 1.000. The village script averages \(H\) only for its own shares, and it can cost more on others.

What it means. We measured the average number of fair questions per word, for a source whose shares we assume are known. It predicts the cost of the best script when the shares are powers of one half. It does not say what a word means or how good the script is on other shares.

In the café. \(\Sigma\): go through the day’s customers. \(P(x)\): how often that drink is ordered, its share. \(\log_2(1/P(x))\): the cost of that drink, its answers on the ticket. \(H\): the day’s average under the best script. \(\mathrm{EQ}\): the day’s average for whatever script hangs on the wall; \(L(x)\): the cost that script gives one drink.

Check yourself

Predict first, then use the widget: what does the village script cost on a day when all four words are equally likely? Why is it not 1.75?

Answer

2.25. The script spends 1, 2, 3, 3 questions, and with equal shares each count weighs one quarter. The script was built for the village shares.

Look inside: a second route to 1.75, Shannon’s grouping rule

Instance. The village. Steps. (i) The first question is “rain or not?”, with shares 1/2 and 1/2: one question. (ii) On the half of the days that are not rain, the shares inside that group are sun 1/2, wind 1/4, snow 1/4 (each old share divided by 1/2). The entropy of that group is \(\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac14\cdot2 = 1.5\) MacKay 2003, p. 33. (iii) We are in that group on half of the days, so

\[H = 1 + \tfrac12\times 1.5 = 1.75 \quad\text{(E6)}\]

Check. The same 1.75 as the tree. This is Shannon’s rule: if a choice is broken into two successive choices, \(H\) is the weighted sum of the two \(H\)s Shannon 1948, p. 10. In the café: the first question on the card, then the rest of the card for the customers who said no.

Look inside: a question is an experiment, the best weighing of twelve balls

Instance. MacKay’s weighing problem MacKay 2003, p. 66. Twelve balls look alike; one is heavier or lighter than the rest, and we do not know which. A balance scale has three outcomes: left pan down, right pan down, or balance. There are 24 hypotheses (12 balls, each heavy or light). Two weighings give at most \(3^2 = 9\) outcomes; three give \(3^3 = 27\), enough in principle MacKay 2003, p. 68.

Question and pattern. Which first weighing is best? A weighing is a question with three answers, and a good one has answers that are about equally likely: “at each step of an optimal procedure, the three outcomes (‘left heavier’, ‘right heavier’, and ‘balance’) are as close as possible to equiprobable” MacKay 2003, p. 68. Put four balls on each pan. The left pan goes down if the odd ball is heavy and on the left, or light and on the right: 8 of the 24 hypotheses. The right pan, likewise, 8. They balance if the odd ball is left out: 4 × 2 = 8. The split is 8 : 8 : 8. With six on each pan the pans can never balance MacKay 2003, p. 68, so the split is 12 : 12 : 0 (our count).

Formula (E7) and numbers (ours, rerun in code). The worth of a weighing is the entropy of its three outcomes:

\[H = \sum_{o} P_o \log_2 \frac{1}{P_o}, \qquad o \in \{\text{left},\ \text{right},\ \text{balance}\}.\]

Four against four: \(3\times\tfrac13\log_2 3 = \log_2 3 = 1.585\) bits. Six against six: \(\tfrac12\cdot 1+\tfrac12\cdot 1 + 0 = 1.000\) bit. All 24 cases need \(\log_2 24 = 4.585\) bits, so one weighing gives at most 1.585 of them. MacKay writes, with a hedge, “Thus we might conclude” that “the outcome of a random experiment is guaranteed to be most informative if the probability distribution over outcomes is uniform” MacKay 2003, p. 70. It is a conclusion from this example, tied to the bound on \(H\) MacKay 2003, p. 33, not a theorem about every question. In the café: a weighing is a question with three answers instead of two, and choosing the first weighing is choosing the card’s first question.

Look inside: names and a warning

“Uncertainty” is another name for \(H\) in Shannon, Pierce and MacKay; it names this number, not a feeling. \(H\) belongs to the source’s shares, not to one message. We avoid “relative entropy”, which Shannon used for \(H\) divided by its largest value Shannon 1948, p. 14, and which later books use for something else.

3 · Concrete · step 4

Step 4 · The submarine: one answer, six bits

The instance. A submarine hides in one of the 64 squares of an 8 × 8 grid, all equally likely. You fire at squares one by one MacKay 2003, pp. 70–72. The first shot hits with chance 1/64. The question. What are a hit and a miss worth, and what do many misses add up to? In words. A hit names the square among 64: six halvings. A miss crosses off one square, a sliver of the options; after 32 misses half of the squares are gone, exactly one question’s worth. A later hit names the square among the few left, so it is worth less. The total is the same whenever the hit comes.

A check by hand on four squares (ours). Misses are worth \(\log_2\frac43 = 0.415\), then \(\log_2\frac32 = 0.585\), then \(\log_2\frac21 = 1.000\). The sum is 2.000, which is \(\log_2 4\).

The formula (E8). With \(s\) squares left before a shot, a miss has chance \((s-1)/s\), so by E2 it is worth \(\log_2\frac{s}{s-1}\). A hit has chance \(1/s\), so it is worth \(\log_2 s\). Say the hit comes when \(n\) squares are left. Multiply the miss ratios from 64 squares down to \(n\): each numerator cancels the next denominator, and only \(64/n\) is left. Then add the hit:

\[\underbrace{\log_2\!\Big(\tfrac{64}{63}\times\tfrac{63}{62}\times\cdots\times\tfrac{n+1}{n}\Big)}_{\text{all the misses}}+\underbrace{\log_2 n}_{\text{the hit}} = \log_2 64 = 6 \quad\text{whatever } n \text{ is.}\]

This is MacKay’s product that collapses to a single fraction MacKay 2003, p. 72. Move the slider to choose which shot hits, and check the rows.

What it means. MacKay: “Now, it might seem a little strange that one binary outcome can convey six bits.” MacKay 2003, p. 71 His verdict, “I think the submarine example makes quite a convincing case”, is an argument, not a proof MacKay 2003, p. 72. One yes/no answer does not always carry one bit.

In the café. The squares are the drinks still possible, and they shrink as answers arrive. Suppose matcha is one order in 64 (ours) and the script begins with “Matcha?”. A no on the ticket costs 0.0227 of a question; a yes costs six. Whichever path the customer takes, the total worth of finding her drink is the same: the cost of that drink.

Check yourself

Five misses in a row from a fresh grid. Is the fifth miss worth more or less than the first? Predict, then set the slider to shot 6 and read the last miss, or compute \(\log_2\frac{60}{59}\).

Answer

Slightly more. The misses are worth 0.0227, 0.0231, 0.0235, 0.0238 and 0.0242, because fewer squares are left each time (MacKay cuts digits off and prints 0.0230 for the second; we round). Together they are worth \(\log_2\frac{64}{59} = 0.1174\) (ours).

Guess first · answer

Almost nothing. A first miss is worth \(\log_2\frac{64}{63} = 0.0227\) questions, MacKay’s figure too MacKay 2003, p. 71. A hit on the first shot would be worth six. One whole question’s worth takes 32 misses.

Look inside: Wenglish, where each letter carries a different amount

MacKay invents a language, Wenglish, with 5-letter words. The dictionary has \(2^{15} = 32{,}768\) words, all equally likely, so each word is worth 15 bits, an average of 3 per letter MacKay 2003, pp. 72–73. Read one letter at a time, a first letter “a” (chance about 0.0625) carries about 4 bits, and a first letter “z” (about 0.001) carries about 10. “A rare initial letter such as z indeed conveys more information about what the word is than a common initial letter” MacKay 2003, p. 73. The five letters of a word always add to 15.

Gibson and colleagues define uniform information density as “the hypothesis that the information content of an utterance per unit time should be roughly constant” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 3. Wenglish is a constructed example; it does not test that hypothesis.

3 · Concrete · step 5

Step 5 · Who encodes and who decodes

The instance (the café). The customer wants a mocha. On the card she answers “Flat white?” with no, “Latte?” with no, and “Mocha?” with yes. Write a yes as 0 and a no as 1: the string on her ticket is 1 1 0. The question. Which side encodes, and which decodes?

The roles, named. The encoder holds the original (here the drink) and the agreed script, and turns the original into a string. The decoder holds only the string and the same script, and turns the string back. In the café the customer encodes and the barista decodes. Nobody asks anything live: the card asks, the customer writes, and the ticket travels. Shannon’s diagram has the same roles: the receiver “ordinarily performs the inverse operation of that done by the transmitter, reconstructing the message from the signal” Shannon 1948, p. 2. The script is the codebook both sides hold before the day starts. MacKay writes that “Our questioning strategy defines a way of encoding the random variable x as a binary file” MacKay 2003, p. 70, which can sound as if the asker encodes. The strategy is the shared script; the one who knows the secret, applying it, makes the string.

In the café. The customer encodes: she holds the drink and the script. The ticket is the string. The barista decodes: she holds only the ticket and the same script.

Check yourself

A barista whose card lists the questions in a different order reads the ticket “no, no, yes”. What can go wrong, and what must both sides share?

Answer

She may name the wrong drink, because the same ticket means different things under different cards. Both sides must hold the same script before the day starts; the string alone carries nothing.

Look inside: Shannon’s twin game

The same two roles appear in Shannon’s guessing experiments. In 1951 he selected “a short passage unfamiliar to the person who is to do the predicting” Shannon 1951, p. 54. So the first person has not seen the text; he knows it only up to the current point, guesses the next letter, and is told whether he was right Shannon 1951, pp. 54–55. The record has a dash for each letter guessed correctly, and the correct letter copied in for each wrong guess Shannon 1951, p. 54; this is the reduced text. A twin, “mathematically, not just biologically identical”, with only that record will “respond in the same way when faced with the same problem” Shannon 1951, p. 55. Shannon then adds that “the reduced text can be considered to be an encoded form of the original” Shannon 1951, p. 55. Had the first person held the whole text in advance, every guess would be right and the record would carry nothing (our gloss).

A toy walk-through (ours). The predictor is a fixed ranked list of letters: E, T, A, and so on. The original is “TEA”. The encoding side finds T 2nd, E 1st, A 3rd, and writes 2 1 3. The decoding side, with only “2 1 3” and the same list, recovers “TEA”. (Shannon’s real predictor uses the preceding text.) The side with the original text and a predictor encodes; the twin, with only the record and an identical predictor, decodes. In both settings the shared script or predictor is what lets the string be read.

3 · Concrete · step 6

Step 6 · When the shares are not halves

Guess first. A new village has shares rain 0.7, sun 0.1, wind 0.1 and snow 0.1. With the best script of whole yes/no questions, how many questions does one day’s word cost on average?

The first village is too nice. Halves, quarters and eighths give whole numbers of questions, so the average equals \(H\) exactly. Do not take that as a rule. In general the best average of a script of whole questions lies between \(H\) and \(H+1\) Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 15:

\[H \le \mathrm{EQ}^{*} \lt H + 1 \quad\text{(E9)}\]

Here \(\mathrm{EQ}^{*}\) is the EQ of the best script. Cover and Thomas state it again in their chapter on codes (eq. 5.61), where they show “the equivalence of coding and the game of 20 questions” Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 94–95: a sequence of questions is a code. We quote the bound and do not prove it.

The 0.7 village (ours, run in code). Set the village widget to weights 7, 1, 1, 1. The entropy is \(H = 1.357\) bits. How do we build the best script? A rule, ours and not from our sources (notebook exercise 8 does it in code): repeatedly join the two rarest outcomes into one group, and every outcome inside the group gets one more question. Here two of the 0.1 words join into a group of 0.2, which then joins the third 0.1 word, and only then does the 0.3 group join rain. So rain gets 1 question and the others 2, 3, 3: “Rain?”, then “Sun?”, then “Wind?”. Then \(\mathrm{EQ}^{*} = 0.7\cdot 1 + 0.1\cdot 2 + 0.1\cdot 3 + 0.1\cdot 3 = 1.5\). Check against the bound: \(1.357 \le 1.5 \lt 2.357\). The gap, 0.143 question, is the price of whole questions.

Guess first · answer

4 · Abstract

Short answer, and what it really is

Short answer (our reading, not a finding)

How much? One outcome is worth \(\log_2(1/P)\) fair yes/no questions under a stated model of the source. The average over everything the source can send is the entropy \(H\): 1.75 questions a day for the village.

A surprise? Improbability under a model. The number belongs to the model, not to the event: two forecasters with different shares give the same snow different numbers (boundary 4).

Worth? Questions saved, not importance, truth or meaning. Shannon sets meaning aside for “the engineering problem” Shannon 1948, p. 1; that is a decision about scope, not a claim that meaning does not matter. Our verdict: the measure is a good price for improbability under a stated model, and a poor price for anything else. What would change it: evidence that the number predicts something beyond improbability (lens 2: one such claim, whose evidence we have not checked).

(a) What it really is

Strip away the cafés, villages and submarines. Surprisal re-writes a share in units that add for independent events; the logarithm turns a product of shares into a sum MacKay 2003, p. 68. Entropy is the average surprisal under the same shares, and depends only on the shares, not on what the outcomes are Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 13. It is 0 only when one outcome is certain, and at most \(\log_2\) of the number of outcomes, with equality only when all are equally likely MacKay 2003, p. 33 Shannon 1948, p. 11. Any change that moves the shares toward equality raises it Shannon 1948, p. 12: the village’s 1.75 becomes 2 when its shares are made equal. A mere swap of shares between outcomes leaves it unchanged. The surprisal approximates the length of the shortest uniquely decipherable script for the outcome Gibson et al. 2019, p. 4 (“approximates” is the authors’ word; “uniquely decipherable” means no string is the start of another, so a run of answers can be cut into words in only one way). This is E2 and E4 without the café.

Look inside: why this formula? Shannon’s three properties

Shannon asked for a measure with three properties: it is continuous in the shares; it grows with the number of options when all are equally likely; and if a choice is broken into two successive choices, the original \(H\) is the weighted sum of the two \(H\)s Shannon 1948, p. 10. His Theorem 2 says the only such \(H\) is \(-K\sum p\log p\). He also writes that the theorem and its assumptions “are in no way necessary for the present theory”, and: “The real justification of these definitions, however, will reside in their implications.” Shannon 1948, p. 11 We do not reproduce the proof.

4 · Abstract

(b) The same idea elsewhere

Lens 1 · Statistical mechanics

same-math (the form of the sum only) The sum \(-\sum p\log p\) appears in physics.

Shannon writes: “The form of H will be recognized as that of entropy as defined in certain formulations of statistical mechanics.” Shannon 1948, p. 11 Toy (ours): a box of four cells, with shares equal to the chance that a given particle sits in each. All in one cell gives 0; spread equally gives 2, the village’s numbers in another story. Pierce says the ideas behind the two entropies are “quite different” Pierce 1961, pp. 23–24.

Adds: the sum is about distributions wherever shares exist. Does not explain: that the two entropies are one thing.

Lens 2 · Psycholinguistics

structural analogy Surprisal is built from a share; what a share does inside a mind is an empirical claim, not a theorem.

Gibson and colleagues use the quantity directly: a listener’s difficulty in picking a colour chip, given a colour word, is “the surprisal of the color, given the word” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 7. For minds they write, as one premise in an argument about grammatical gender: “Since language processing effort is associated with predictability [104,105]” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 11. Read the hedge. They cite two studies we have not seen, and their word is predictability, not surprisal.

Adds: the one lens whose claim is about behaviour (ours), though the post cites only a reported association. Does not explain: cause. “Associated with” is not “caused by”.

Lens 3 · Communication theory

structural analogy Our café roles are Shannon’s transmitter, signal and receiver.

The customer is the transmitter, the ticket the signal and the barista the receiver Shannon 1948, pp. 1–2 (ours). The scope is narrow on purpose: “These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem” Shannon 1948, p. 1.

Adds: the roles, and an honest boundary for the engineering sense. Does not explain: meaning, truth or effect, where the study of communication in the social sciences begins (our summary; we have not read Weaver’s essay).

Lens 4 · Bayesian probability

structural analogy A share can be a degree of belief, so surprisal is relative to a believer.

The village’s shares come from a long record; a one-off event has none (ours). MacKay describes the Bayesian view, in which beliefs can be probabilities, even for a single proposition, and warns that the view that a Bayesian approach makes sense “is not yet a globally held view” MacKay 2003, p. 26. Our inference from E2: different beliefs give different surprisal.

Adds: a way to price one-off events. Does not explain: whether the beliefs are right.

Lens 5 · Search and decision

same-math A tree of yes/no questions is a code Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 94–95.

The designer’s view, in MacKay’s tentative conclusion (“we might conclude”): to find something fast, choose the experiment whose outcomes are most nearly equally likely MacKay 2003, p. 70.

Adds: from describing a source to choosing what to ask. Does not explain: questions whose costs differ, or answers that can be misheard.

Look inside: more hedges from Shannon, Pierce and from Gibson and colleagues

Shannon sets meaning aside, not the receiver: his introduction also lists a concern with “the nature of the final destination of the information” Shannon 1948, p. 1.

Averaged over words, the colour score is “the optimal number of yes–no guesses it takes a listener to guess which chip the speaker meant” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 7: our barista’s game, though the score also weighs how likely a speaker is to use the word. For word length, a word’s chance in context beat its frequency in a variety of languages, and the pattern “likely reflects lexicalization of processes that shorten predictable words” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 6.

Pierce: the idea that one entropy “can be fully useful without any reference at all to the other”; yet both “can be described in terms of uncertainty, in similar mathematical terms”, and efforts to marry the two have been “more interesting than fruitful” Pierce 1961, p. 24. He advises clearing our minds of the entropy of physics first Pierce 1961, p. 80.

Gibson and colleagues: there is currently “no method of generating baseline utterances and languages that is general and satisfactory across all cases” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 14. For taboo words and similar factors, “It is currently unclear whether such factors can be expressed as part of general communicative efficiency” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 15. Their abstract is bolder: these studies “show how a pervasive pressure for efficiency guides the forms of natural language” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 1. We do not write that surprisal predicts reading time or processing effort on the strength of this review.

For your research (3C), ours: Computation: a language model’s next-word share gives a surprisal under that model. Communication: a headline’s surprisal depends on whose model prices it. Collaboration: the same sum applies to shares of topics or ties. These are connections, not findings.

4 · Abstract

(c) Where it fails

Each boundary is an assumption you can find in section 3, with the place where the café breaks.

1 · Meaning. Assumption: E2 has only \(P(x)\) in it, so two words with equal share get equal surprisal, understood or not. Shannon sets meaning aside Shannon 1948, p. 1; the theory “as specified so far is entirely agnostic to the form and content of messages” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 4. The café breaks: the barista cares which drink it is; the script is blind to taste.

2 · A known, stable distribution. Assumption: section 3 gives the shares in advance: “These probabilities are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur” Shannon 1948, p. 10. For his model of a source, Pierce says the chances “must not change with time if the source is to be ergodic” Pierce 1961, p. 81. The café breaks: the cash register’s record gets old, and the village script costs 2.25 on a uniform day (E5).

3 · Independent days. Assumption: symbols “being chosen independently” Shannon 1948, p. 18, and E3 needs it. The café breaks: a rainy morning makes hot drinks likelier.

4 · One-off events, and whose model. Assumption: E2 needs \(P(x)\), and the village takes it from a record. The café breaks: a single event has no record. Two forecasters can write two shares for the same snow (ours):

5 · Estimation. Assumption: the village shares are given; in practice they are estimated, and “it is difficult to get reliable estimates of these information-theoretic quantities without very large datasets” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 14. The bias is our computation (notebook exercise 6): draw \(n\) village days, put the observed shares into the entropy formula, repeat 2,000 times (fixed seed); the true entropy is 1.75. The café breaks: the owner has seen only a few days.

5 · The CSS imagination

Whose model sets the price of a surprise?

C. Wright Mills argued that people need “a quality of mind” that shows how a life and its time fit together Mills 1959, pp. 4–6; the imagination “enables us to grasp history and biography and the relations between the two within society” Mills 1959, p. 6. His advice is not findings, and he hedges it: “There are definite ways, I believe, of stimulating the sociological imagination” Mills 1959, p. 212. The move names are ours (his list has seven items); pages are of the 2000 Oxford edition, and inside quotation marks we keep his 1959 wording. His “information” Mills 1959, p. 5 means facts and news, not Shannon’s sense Pierce 1961, p. 24.

Move 1 · Trouble and issue

Mills: “Perhaps the most fruitful distinction with which the sociological imagination works is between ‘the personal troubles of milieu’ and ‘the public issues of social structure.’” Mills 1959, p. 8 His example is hypothetical, not data: one man unemployed “in a city of 100,000” is “his personal trouble”, while “15 million men” unemployed “in a nation of 50 million employees” is “an issue” Mills 1959, p. 9. An issue, he says, “unlike even widespread trouble”, cannot be defined within everyday surroundings; there “The very structure of opportunities has collapsed” Mills 1959, pp. 8–9. So (our reading) an issue is not a trouble multiplied.

Our case (ours). One reader cannot tell which of her 200 messages matters, and blames herself for what she misses: a trouble. A ranking that orders messages by how surprising they are under its own model would price every message for everyone; that it does so is a hypothesis, not a finding. A test (ours, in the spirit of Mills’ distinction): if changing the ranking changes what many readers miss, and individual effort does not, the cause lies in an arrangement, not in the readers. Pierce, writing about art and its audiences, in the everyday sense of surprise: “We can be surprised repeatedly only by contrast with that which is familiar” Pierce 1961, p. 267. Whose idea of the familiar prices the message? (Our metaphor.)

Move 2 · Extremes and inverted scale

“Often you get the best insights by considering extremes—by thinking of the opposite of that with which you are directly concerned” Mills 1959, p. 213. A village where it always rains has \(H = 0\); with all four words equally likely, \(H = 2.000\), the most for four words (our reading). Now invert the scale: “What would pre-literate villages look like with populations of 30 millions?” Mills 1959, p. 215 Give the weather village 30 million listeners (ours). Each listener’s surprisal for snow is the same number. But one forecast now prices all of them: if its share for snow is wrong, it is wrong for 30 million people at once, and their surprise is correlated (ours). What changes is who writes the forecast, and who pays when it is wrong.

Move 3 · Another discipline’s eyes

Mills suggests asking how a political scientist, an experimental psychologist or a historian would see a problem Mills 1959, p. 214. Take one message, “snow tomorrow” (ours). A psycholinguist asks whether a listener’s processing effort tracks how predictable the word is, an association Gibson and colleagues state Gibson et al. 2019, p. 11. A journalist asks what counts as news: a rumour and a budget can carry the same surprisal, and meaning and consequence are what the number leaves out. Neither asks who owns the model.

Look inside: Mills on counting, both sides

Mills’ view of statistics is two-sided. He wrote that “an empiricism as cautious and rigid as abstracted empiricism eliminates the great social problems and human issues of our time from inquiry”, and on the same page: “If the problems upon which one is at work are readily amenable to statistical procedures, one should always try to use them” Mills 1959, p. 73. He also says that, “Nowadays at least”, he would never count or measure anything before he had played with its elements in an imagined world “in which I control the scale of everything” Mills 1959, p. 215: a point about the order of work, not a rejection of measuring. On extremes he adds that you should keep striving “to gain and to maintain a sense of proportion” Mills 1959, p. 214.

Two open research questions (ours; invitations, not findings)

1 · Computation and Communication. Do posts that are more surprising under a language model spread further, after controlling for topic and emotion? Data: posts with share counts and a topic label, plus token probabilities from one fixed language model. Method: turn the probabilities into one number per post (the sum of the words’ surprisals, or their mean; post length is a confound), then regress share counts on it with topic fixed effects (a separate baseline for each topic), and report how the result moves when the model changes. We have not checked the prior work on novelty and spreading.

2 · Collaboration and Communication. How does the entropy of a platform’s daily topic mix change around a crisis? Data: posts labelled by topic (for example by a large language model used as a labeller). Method: plug-in entropy (the formula applied to the observed shares) with bias correction and bootstrap intervals (resampling days to see the spread; boundary 5), before and after a dated event; this is Mills’ comparison move Mills 1959, p. 215, and it means something only if one model prices every case (boundary 4).

A market stall and a notebook

Transfer challenge

A market stall sells five items with shares 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/16. (a) Build the script of questions. (b) Compute the average number of questions and the entropy. (c) Change the shares to 1/4, 1/4, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8. What does the old script cost now, and what does the best script cost?

Hint

Halve the customers each time. The two rarest items get four questions each.

Solution (ours, run in code)

(a) Lengths 1, 2, 3, 4, 4. (b) \(\mathrm{EQ} = \tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac1{16}\cdot4+\tfrac1{16}\cdot4 = 1.875\), and \(H = 1.875\); they are equal because every share is a power of 1/2. (c) The old script costs \(\tfrac14(1+2+3)+\tfrac18(4+4) = 2.5\). The entropy is 2.25, and the new best lengths are 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, which cost 2.25. The old script stops being best because it was built for the old shares (E5 and boundary 2).

7 · Recall questions

Test yourself

Answer aloud, then open the card. ↻ marks questions that will return later.

↻ Why does the measure use a logarithm?

Chances of independent days multiply, and a log turns a product into a sum, so surprisals add MacKay 2003, p. 68.

↻ What is the surprisal of an outcome whose share is 1/16?

4 questions’ worth: \(\log_2 16 = 4\).

↻ What does “1.75 bits” mean for the village?

The best script of fair yes/no questions needs 1.75 questions per day on average, in this village.

↻ Why is 1.75 lower than 2, and when would the best script average exactly 2?

The shares are uneven, so common words get short strings. It averages exactly 2, for example, when four words are equally likely: then \(H = 2\), and no script of whole questions can do better.

What does entropy not measure?

Meaning, value, truth or importance. Shannon sets meaning aside for “the engineering problem”: scope, not denial Shannon 1948, p. 1.

Who encodes and who decodes?

The side with the original and the script encodes: it writes the string. The side with only the string and the same script decodes. In the café the customer writes the ticket and the barista reads it.

Name two assumptions the village model makes.

Any two of: the shares are known; the shares are stable; the days are independent. A fourth, for real data: enough days to estimate the shares.

Which statement of Gibson and colleagues is an empirical claim, and what does it say?

That language processing effort “is associated with predictability”, a premise they support with two studies we have not seen Gibson et al. 2019, p. 11. Not a theorem, and not about surprisal.
