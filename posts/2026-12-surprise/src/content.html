How much is a surprise worth?

A surprise is worth the number of fair yes/no questions it saves you. The rarer the news, the more questions it saves, and the average over all the news a source can send is called its entropy.

1 · Motivation

Counting messages, and what the count misses

In 1928 Hartley measured a message by how many different messages the sender could have picked (a logarithm of that number; step 1 explains) Pierce 1961, pp. 39–40. Pierce says this count is acceptable only if successive symbols are chosen independently and each “is equally likely to be selected” Pierce 1961, p. 40. Hartley himself saw that E is chosen more often than Z, and that this should govern the lengths of Morse signals, if messages are to be sent most swiftly Pierce 1961, p. 40. The count itself gives every possible message the same weight (our reading).

The pain (ours). In a town where it rains most days, the weather office sends one report a day from a fixed list. “It rained today” and “It snowed in July” are each one pick from that list, so a measure that only counts the possible reports gives them the same number. Yet the first tells you almost nothing, and the second is news. What was missing was a number that weighs each message by how likely it is. We build it in a coffee shop, check it in a village, and ask what it leaves out. First, a guess.

Guess first (answered in step 4). A submarine hides in one of 64 squares, and every square is equally likely. You fire at one square and miss. Count news in yes/no questions: a piece of news is worth the questions it saves you. How many questions is that miss worth?

2 · Intuition

The café with a hatch

Picture a loud café. The grinder drowns every word, so customers and barista cannot talk, and the barista works in a back room where she never sees a customer. Orders pass through a hatch on paper tickets. The same card hangs on the wall by the counter and on the wall in the back room: a list of yes/no questions in a fixed order. The customer answers them on the ticket, one after another, and every answer costs one second of the queue’s time. The owner knows from the cash register’s record that out of every eight regulars, four want a flat white, two a latte, one a mocha and one a matcha. What should the card say?

The old way: split the menu. The menu board is a grid with two drinks on top and two below. The card asks, “Is your drink on the left side of the board?” and then, “Is it the top one?” Every ticket has two answers, and the barista always knows the drink. But half of the regulars want the flat white and spend two answers each, and the one regular in eight who wants matcha spends the same two. The card split the drinks into equal halves and ignored how many customers stand behind each drink.

The rule that changes: split the customers. The card now says: “Flat white? Latte? Mocha?”, and the customer stops at her first yes. A yes to the first question settles four customers out of eight with one answer; a no leaves four. A yes to the second settles two more; a no leaves two. A no to the third leaves only the matcha, so the card needs no fourth question. The flat white costs one answer, the latte two, the mocha and the matcha three each. Over eight customers that is four plus four plus three plus three: fourteen answers, one and three quarters for each customer, against two for the old way. The rare drinks cost more, and that is fair: a matcha order is unusual news. The rarer the order, the bigger the surprise, and the more questions it is worth.

A check. If the card began with “Matcha?”, most tickets would start with a no, which rules out only one customer in eight: a question helps most when both answers are likely.

Who does what. The customer knows the drink and holds the card. Following it, she turns her drink into a row of yes and no on the ticket, for example no, no, yes. The barista never sees the customer: she holds the same card, reads the ticket against it and names the drink. The ticket alone says nothing, because “no, no, yes” means mocha only under this card. We call the card the script, and the row of answers on a ticket its string.

The parts of the café (later sections use these names):

  • The four drinks.
  • The share of regulars who want each drink (it can be out of date).
  • One question on the card, answered yes or no; one second.
  • The script: the card, the same on both walls.
  • The customer, who knows the drink and fills in the ticket.
  • The ticket: the string of answers for one customer.
  • The barista, who sees only the ticket and holds the same card.
  • A ticket’s length: how many answers are on it. Always a whole number.
  • An order’s worth: how much news the order carries, counted in questions: a rare order is worth more than a common one. For the four drinks above, worth and ticket length agree; section 3 shows when they do not.
  • The day’s average: answers per customer over the day.
  • The menu-board card: the old way.

Check yourself

(a) Which customers get the longest tickets, and why? (b) A day comes when all four drinks sell equally. Would the clever card still be best?

Answer (a)

The mocha and matcha customers, three answers each. The card spends its questions where the customers are.

Answer (b)

No. The average would be one plus two plus three plus three, divided by four: two and a quarter. The plain board costs exactly two.

3 · Concrete · step 1

The weather village: equal chances first

Now leave the café for the field’s own setting. A village radio sends one word a day: rain, sun, wind or snow, with shares of days one half, one quarter, one eighth and one eighth: the owner’s numbers, with drinks swapped for weather words. Shannon Shannon 1948, p. 18 and Cover and Thomas Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 14–15 use these shares. We call each day’s word a symbol. We take the days to be independent: one day’s word does not change the chances of the next day’s. Shannon has “successive symbols being chosen independently” Shannon 1948, p. 18 too, though real weather is not like that (section 4). Each equation is followed by a line “In the café” naming the café parts it stands for.

Term · Three words in the question

Worth: the number of fair yes/no questions an outcome saves you, not usefulness. A question is fair when its two answers are equally likely. Surprise: how unlikely an outcome was under a stated model of the source (a list of how likely each message is), not a feeling. Information: Shannon’s engineering sense, one choice from a set of possible messages Shannon 1948, p. 1.

Step 1 · Equal chances: count the halvings

Guess first. One of 32 cups of water is poisoned. A rat that drinks any poisoned cup dies within the hour, and you have one hour. All the rats drink at the same time, each from any cups you choose. What is the smallest number of rats that is always enough?

The instance. The cups-and-rats framing is ours; MacKay has one of 64 integers and says “Six questions suffice” MacKay 2003, p. 70. What happens. With 32 cups, one good question halves them: 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. That is five halvings. The rats ask all five questions at once, because each rat’s fate, dead or alive, is one yes/no answer.

The pattern. 32 is two multiplied by itself five times, and 64 is six times. One answer of a fair yes/no question is one bit, a word Shannon credits to J. W. Tukey Shannon 1948, p. 1. From now on, “questions” and “bits” name the same unit of worth.

The formula (E1). For \(N\) equally likely options, where \(N\) is a power of two, the number of questions \(k\) satisfies

\[N = 2^{k}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad k = \log_2 N .\]

Read aloud: “the number of questions is the number of times you must halve \(N\) to reach 1.” The symbol \(\log_2\) asks: two multiplied by itself how many times gives this number? For other \(N\), think of the worst case, the most questions you could ever need (ours). MacKay’s “raw bit content” \(\log_2 N\) is “a lower bound for the number of binary questions that are always guaranteed to identify an outcome” MacKay 2003, p. 74.

The check. \(\log_2 32 = 5\) and \(\log_2 64 = 6\). Cover and Thomas open with the same case, \(\log 32 = 5\) bits Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 5.

In the café. \(N\) is the number of drinks on the menu when all sell equally, each with the same share. \(k\) is the number of questions on a ticket. The 2 in \(2^k\) is the two answers to one question. Halving the board is the menu-board card, the old way. The rats are a ticket filled in all at once: five rats, five answers (ours).

Guess first · answer

Five. Each rat gives one yes/no answer, so five rats give five answers at once, enough to tell \(2^5 = 32\) cups apart. Four rats give only \(2^4 = 16\) outcomes.

Look inside: Nyquist and Hartley in symbols

As Pierce reports it, Nyquist’s result of 1924 relates the speed \(W\) of a telegraph line to the number \(m\) of current values by \(W = K\log m\), where \(K\) is a constant that depends on how many values are sent per second Pierce 1961, pp. 35–36. Two values to four doubles the speed. Three values give \(\log_2 3 = 1.585\) times the speed of two, a rise of 58.5 per cent; Pierce says “60 per cent” Pierce 1961, p. 38. Eight values give \(\log_2 8 = 3\) times (ours). Pierce’s “four times” Pierce 1961, p. 38 is an apparent slip, and his first list of values shows only seven (the second has eight).

Pierce says Hartley’s count is acceptable only if successive symbols “are chosen independently” and each “is equally likely to be selected” Pierce 1961, p. 40. A symbol here is one pick from the list, such as one current value or one letter.

Hartley’s measure is \(H = n\log s\) for \(n\) symbols from \(s\) kinds: there are \(s^n\) messages, and \(\log_2(s^n) = n\log_2 s\) Pierce 1961, p. 40. Shannon gives three reasons for a logarithm. It is practically useful: time, bandwidth and the number of relays “tend to vary linearly” with the logarithm of the number of possibilities. It is nearer to intuition: “One feels, for example, that two punched cards should have twice the capacity of one.” And it is mathematically suitable Shannon 1948, p. 1. These are his motives, not a proof that no other measure could work. With base 2, \(N\) two-state devices store \(N\) bits, because \(\log_2 2^N = N\) Shannon 1948, p. 1.

3 · Concrete · step 2

Step 2 · Unequal chances: surprisal

The instance. The village again: rain 1/2, sun 1/4, wind 1/8, snow 1/8. The best questions go like the card: “Rain?”, then “Sun?”, then “Wind?”, and then only snow is left. The ticket lengths are one, two, three and three answers. Write yes as 0 and no as 1, and the strings are 0, 10, 110 and 111 Shannon 1948, p. 18 Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 15.

The question and the pattern. How much news does one outcome carry, in questions? A share of one half means halving the crowd once, one quarter twice, one eighth three times. The worth of an outcome is how many times you must halve the whole crowd to be left with a group the size of its share. For these shares, worth and ticket length are the same number. For other shares the count of halvings is a fraction, and the formula below still gives it.

The formula (E2). For an outcome \(x\) whose share is \(P(x)\), the worth is

\[h(x) = \log_2 \frac{1}{P(x)} .\]

Read aloud: “the surprisal of \(x\) is the log, base 2, of one over the share of \(x\).” MacKay calls it the “Shannon information content” MacKay 2003, p. 32; Gibson and colleagues say surprisal, and information content Gibson et al. 2019, p. 4. We use surprisal for the worth.

The check. For snow, \(\log_2 8 = 3\), and the four words give 1, 2, 3, 3, as the tickets did. Move the slider to check MacKay’s table: share 0.001 is worth 10.0 questions, 0.1 gives 3.3, and 0.5 gives 1.0 MacKay 2003, p. 67. At share 1 the worth is 0: a sure thing tells you nothing. A share of 0.1 is worth 3.3 questions, but no ticket has 3.3 answers: a ticket’s length is whole, and a worth need not be.

A second concept: independent days add. Rain today, snow tomorrow. The days are independent, so the pair has share \(\tfrac12\times\tfrac18 = \tfrac1{16}\), and \(\log_2 16 = 4\). The separate worths are 1 and 3, and \(1 + 3 = 4\). In general, for independent \(x\) and \(y\), \(P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)\), and a log turns a product into a sum, because \(2^a\times 2^b = 2^{a+b}\): multiplying the crowds adds the halvings.

\[h(x,y) = \log_2\frac{1}{P(x)P(y)} = h(x) + h(y).\quad\text{(E3)}\]

This needs independence, which the village assumes.

What it does not say. Only shares that are powers of one half (1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, …) are worth a whole number of questions (ours); a share of 0.3 is worth 1.74. Step 6 returns to this.

In the café. \(x\) is a drink, here a weather word. \(P(x)\) is its share in the cash register’s record. \(h(x)\) is the order’s worth, in questions. A ticket’s length is whole, so it can equal the worth only for shares 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … (ours). “Log of one over the share” counts how often you must halve the crowd. Additivity: two customers in a row, their tickets joined.

3 · Concrete · step 3

Step 3 · The average: entropy

The instance. Take eight days that match the shares: four rain, two sun, one wind, one snow. Rain costs 1 question each, sun 2, wind 3, snow 3. The total is \(4 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 14\) questions in 8 days: 1.75 per day. The question. What one number describes the source? In words. Weigh each word’s worth by how often it comes, and add.

The formula (E4). The entropy \(H\) of a source is the average surprisal:

\[H = \sum_x P(x)\,\log_2\frac{1}{P(x)} .\]

Read aloud: “the sum, over all words, of the share of the word times its worth.” The sign \(\Sigma\) means add up. An outcome with share 0 contributes 0, by the convention that \(0\times\log 1/0\) is defined to be 0 MacKay 2003, p. 32. For the village,

\[\tfrac12\cdot 1 + \tfrac14\cdot 2 + \tfrac18\cdot 3 + \tfrac18\cdot 3 = 1.75 .\]

Cover and Thomas get the same 1.75 from the same tree. Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 15 Before and after the crucial change: two-question labels cost 2.000 questions a day; the script built for the shares costs 1.750. Try other shares below.

Three settings. “All equal”: \(H = 2.000\), the most possible for four words MacKay 2003, p. 33 Shannon 1948, p. 11. “Only rain”: \(H = 0.000\) Shannon 1948, p. 18. “Reversed”: \(H\) is unchanged, because it depends only on the shares Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 13.

A script has its own average (E5). Let \(\mathrm{EQ}\) stand for the expected number of questions. A script that spends \(L(x)\) questions on word \(x\) has

\[\mathrm{EQ} = \sum_x P(x)\,L(x).\]

The village script on the village: \(\mathrm{EQ} = 1.750 = H\). On “only rain” it averages 1.000, and on the equal-shares day of section 2’s check it cost 2.25. The village script averages \(H\) only for its own shares, and it can cost more on others.

What it means. We measured the average number of fair questions per word, for a source whose shares we assume are known. It does not say what a word means or how good the script is on other shares.

In the café. \(\Sigma\): go through the drinks, each weighted by its share. \(P(x)\): how often that drink is ordered. \(\log_2(1/P(x))\): the worth of that order. \(H\): the day’s average worth. Where the shares are halves it is also the day’s average ticket length under the best script; step 6 shows the general case. \(\mathrm{EQ}\): the day’s average ticket length for whatever script hangs on the wall; \(L(x)\): the ticket length that script gives one drink.

Check yourself

Predict first, then press “reversed” in the widget: rain and sun are now an eighth of the days each, wind a quarter, snow a half. What does the village script (1, 2, 3, 3 questions) cost a day now, and what is \(H\)? Why is the script not at \(H\)?

Answer

The script costs 2.625 a day, while \(H\) stays 1.750. The script puts its short tickets on rain and sun, which are now rare, and its long tickets on snow, which is now half the days. It was built for the village shares.

Look inside: a second route to 1.75, Shannon’s grouping rule

Instance. The village. Steps. (i) The first question is “rain or not?”, with shares 1/2 and 1/2: one question. (ii) On the half of the days that are not rain, the shares inside that group are sun 1/2, wind 1/4, snow 1/4 (each old share divided by 1/2). The entropy of that group is \(\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac14\cdot2 = 1.5\) MacKay 2003, p. 33. (iii) We are in that group on half of the days, so

\[H = 1 + \tfrac12\times 1.5 = 1.75 \quad\text{(E6)}\]

Check. The same 1.75 as the tree. This is Shannon’s rule: if a choice is broken into two successive choices, \(H\) is the weighted sum of the two \(H\)s Shannon 1948, p. 10. In the café: the first question on the card, then the rest of the card for the customers who said no.

Look inside: a question is an experiment, the best weighing of twelve balls

Instance. MacKay’s weighing problem MacKay 2003, p. 66. Twelve balls look alike; one is heavier or lighter than the rest, and we do not know which. A balance scale has three outcomes: left pan down, right pan down, or balance. There are 24 hypotheses (12 balls, each heavy or light). Two weighings give at most \(3^2 = 9\) outcomes; three give \(3^3 = 27\), enough in principle MacKay 2003, p. 68.

Question and pattern. Which first weighing is best? A weighing is a question with three answers, and a good one has answers that are about equally likely: “at each step of an optimal procedure, the three outcomes (‘left heavier’, ‘right heavier’, and ‘balance’) are as close as possible to equiprobable” MacKay 2003, p. 68. Put four balls on each pan. The left pan goes down if the odd ball is heavy and on the left, or light and on the right: 8 of the 24 hypotheses. The right pan, likewise, 8. They balance if the odd ball is left out: 4 × 2 = 8. The split is 8 : 8 : 8. With six on each pan the pans can never balance MacKay 2003, p. 68, so the split is 12 : 12 : 0 (our count).

Formula (E7) and numbers (ours, rerun in code). The worth of a weighing is the entropy of its three outcomes:

\[H = \sum_{o} P_o \log_2 \frac{1}{P_o}, \qquad o \in \{\text{left},\ \text{right},\ \text{balance}\}.\]

Four against four: \(3\times\tfrac13\log_2 3 = \log_2 3 = 1.585\) bits. Six against six: \(\tfrac12\cdot 1+\tfrac12\cdot 1 + 0 = 1.000\) bit. All 24 cases need \(\log_2 24 = 4.585\) bits, so one weighing gives at most 1.585 of them. MacKay writes, with a hedge, “Thus we might conclude” that “the outcome of a random experiment is guaranteed to be most informative if the probability distribution over outcomes is uniform” MacKay 2003, p. 70. It is a conclusion from this example, tied to the bound on \(H\) MacKay 2003, p. 33, not a theorem about every question. In the café: a weighing is a question with three answers instead of two, and choosing the first weighing is choosing the card’s first question.

Look inside: names and a warning

“Uncertainty” is another name for \(H\) in Shannon, Pierce and MacKay; it names this number, not a feeling. \(H\) belongs to the source’s shares, not to one message. We avoid “relative entropy”, which Shannon used for \(H\) divided by its largest value Shannon 1948, p. 14, and which later books use for something else.

3 · Concrete · step 4

Step 4 · The submarine: one answer, six bits

The instance. A submarine hides in one of the 64 squares of an 8 × 8 grid, all equally likely. You fire at squares one by one MacKay 2003, pp. 70–72. The first shot hits with chance 1/64. The question. What are a hit and a miss worth, and what do many misses add up to? In words. A hit names the square among 64: six halvings. A miss crosses off one square, a sliver of the options; after 32 misses half of the squares are gone, exactly one question’s worth. A later hit names the square among the few left, so it is worth less. The total is the same whenever the hit comes.

A check by hand on four squares (ours). Misses are worth \(\log_2\frac43 = 0.415\), then \(\log_2\frac32 = 0.585\), then \(\log_2\frac21 = 1.000\). The sum is 2.000, which is \(\log_2 4\).

The formula (E8). With \(s\) squares left before a shot, a miss has chance \((s-1)/s\), so by E2 it is worth \(\log_2\frac{s}{s-1}\). A hit has chance \(1/s\), so it is worth \(\log_2 s\). Say the hit comes when \(n\) squares are left. Multiply the miss ratios from 64 squares down to \(n\): each numerator cancels the next denominator, and only \(64/n\) is left. Then add the hit:

\[\underbrace{\log_2\!\Big(\tfrac{64}{63}\times\tfrac{63}{62}\times\cdots\times\tfrac{n+1}{n}\Big)}_{\text{all the misses}}+\underbrace{\log_2 n}_{\text{the hit}} = \log_2 64 = 6 \quad\text{whatever } n \text{ is.}\]

This is MacKay’s product that collapses to a single fraction MacKay 2003, p. 72. Move the slider to choose which shot hits, and read the readout.

What it means. MacKay: “it might seem a little strange that one binary outcome can convey six bits” MacKay 2003, p. 71. One yes/no answer does not always carry one bit.

In the café. The squares are the drinks still possible. Suppose matcha is one order in 64 (ours) and the script begins with “Matcha?”. A no on the ticket is worth 0.0227 of a question, a yes six. For any one customer, the worths of her answers add up to the worth of her drink, whatever order the card asks in (ours; the same chain of fractions as E8).

Check yourself

Five misses in a row from a fresh grid. Is the fifth miss worth more or less than the first? Predict, then set the slider to shot 6 and read the last miss, or compute \(\log_2\frac{60}{59}\).

Answer

Slightly more. The misses are worth 0.0227, 0.0231, 0.0235, 0.0238 and 0.0242, because fewer squares are left each time. Together they are worth \(\log_2\frac{64}{59} = 0.1174\) (ours).

Guess first · answer

Almost nothing. A first miss is worth \(\log_2\frac{64}{63} = 0.0227\) questions, MacKay’s figure too MacKay 2003, p. 71. A hit on the first shot would be worth six. One whole question’s worth takes 32 misses.

Look inside: Wenglish, where each letter carries a different amount

MacKay invents a language, Wenglish, with 5-letter words. The dictionary has \(2^{15} = 32{,}768\) words, all equally likely, so each word is worth 15 bits, an average of 3 per letter MacKay 2003, pp. 72–73. Read one letter at a time, a first letter “a” (chance about 0.0625) carries about 4 bits, and a first letter “z” (about 0.001) carries about 10. “A rare initial letter such as z indeed conveys more information about what the word is than a common initial letter” MacKay 2003, p. 73. The five letters of a word always add to 15.

Gibson and colleagues define uniform information density as “the hypothesis that the information content of an utterance per unit time should be roughly constant” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 3. Wenglish is a constructed example; it does not test that hypothesis.

3 · Concrete · step 5

Step 5 · Who encodes and who decodes

The instance (the café). The customer wants a mocha. On the card she answers “Flat white?” with no, “Latte?” with no, and “Mocha?” with yes. Write a yes as 0 and a no as 1: the string on her ticket is 1 1 0. The question. Which side encodes, and which decodes?

The roles, named. The encoder holds the original (here the drink) and the agreed script, and turns the original into a string. The decoder holds only the string and the same script, and turns the string back. In the café the customer encodes and the barista decodes. Nobody asks anything live: the card asks, the customer writes, and the ticket travels. Shannon’s diagram has the same roles (our mapping, a structural analogy): the customer is the transmitter, the ticket the signal, and the barista the receiver, which “ordinarily performs the inverse operation of that done by the transmitter, reconstructing the message from the signal” Shannon 1948, pp. 1–2. MacKay writes that “Our questioning strategy defines a way of encoding the random variable x as a binary file” MacKay 2003, p. 70, which can sound as if the asker encodes. The strategy is the shared script; the one who knows the secret, applying it, makes the string (our reading).

In the café. The customer encodes: she holds the drink and the script. The ticket is the string. The barista decodes: she holds only the ticket and the same script.

Check yourself

A barista whose card lists the questions in a different order reads the ticket “no, no, yes”. What can go wrong, and what must both sides share?

Answer

She may name the wrong drink, because the same ticket means different things under different cards. Both sides must hold the same script before the day starts; the string alone carries nothing.

Look inside: Shannon’s twin game

The same two roles appear in Shannon’s guessing experiments. In 1951 he selected “a short passage unfamiliar to the person who is to do the predicting” Shannon 1951, p. 54. So the first person has not seen the text; he knows it only up to the current point, guesses the next letter, and is told whether he was right Shannon 1951, pp. 54–55. The record has a dash for each letter guessed correctly, and the correct letter copied in for each wrong guess Shannon 1951, p. 54; this is the reduced text. A twin, “mathematically, not just biologically identical”, with only that record will “respond in the same way when faced with the same problem” Shannon 1951, p. 55. Shannon then adds that “the reduced text can be considered to be an encoded form of the original” Shannon 1951, p. 55. Had the first person held the whole text in advance, every guess would be right and the record would carry nothing (our gloss).

A toy walk-through (ours). The predictor is a fixed ranked list of letters: E, T, A, and so on. The original is “TEA”. The encoding side finds T 2nd, E 1st, A 3rd, and writes 2 1 3. The decoding side, with only “2 1 3” and the same list, recovers “TEA”. (Shannon’s real predictor uses the preceding text.) The encoding side is the predictor together with whoever holds the original text: the predictor guesses, the holder says right or wrong, and the record is written. The twin, with only the record and an identical predictor, decodes. In both settings the shared script or predictor is what lets the string be read.

3 · Concrete · step 6

Step 6 · When the shares are not halves

Guess first. A new village has shares rain 0.7, sun 0.1, wind 0.1 and snow 0.1. With the best script of whole yes/no questions, how many questions does one day’s word cost on average?

The instance. So far the best card came easily, because every share was a power of one half. Now build one. Tap two groups to put them under one question: every word in the two groups gets one more question on its ticket. When one group is left, the card is done. The table gives each word’s share, its ticket (whole questions) and its worth (E2, possibly a fraction). The button joins the two rarest groups.

What happens. The village day: press the button three times. Wind and snow join into 1/4; that joins sun; that joins rain. Start again and always join the first two groups instead: the tickets change, so the order of joins matters. The 0.7 day (ours, run in code): two 0.1 words join into 0.2, that joins the third 0.1 word, and then that group joins rain. Which 0.1 word gets 2 is a tie; any choice costs the same. The equal day: every word gets two questions.

The 0.7 average is \(0.7\cdot 1 + 0.1\cdot 2 + 0.1\cdot 3 + 0.1\cdot 3 = 1.500\) (ours).

Rain is worth 0.515 questions, but its ticket cannot be shorter than one, so its ticket is 0.485 longer than its worth. Over the day, the card’s average is 1.500 − 1.357 = 0.143 above \(H\): that gap is the price of whole questions.

The rule is Huffman’s. Join the two rarest outcomes (add their shares), give everything in the group one more question, and repeat until one group is left. Why the two rarest? They sit deepest on the card, with the longest tickets, so an extra question there falls on the fewest customers and costs the least in total (ours). Those two tickets have equal length and differ only in the last answer MacKay 2003, p. 99: the card’s last question separates exactly those two (our reading). Why does a join add one question to everyone under it? A join is the question that separates the two groups it merges, and every outcome in either group must answer it, so each gets one more (our reading). Cover and Thomas give this simple algorithm Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 92–93, prove that it gives the shortest average length among scripts in which no string is the start of another, as on our card: “Huffman coding is optimal” Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 101, and say the best series of questions is the one the Huffman code gives Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 94. MacKay states it as his Algorithm 5.4 MacKay 2003, p. 99. We do not reproduce the proof; notebook exercise 7 runs the rule in code.

Where it holds. It needs shares that are known Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 94 and do not change, and one word at a time MacKay 2003, pp. 100–101. “Best” means the shortest average: one word can get a ticket longer than its worth, as rain did, and the best card is not unique, since inverting every answer, or swapping two tickets of the same length, gives another Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 97. Splitting the words into two nearly equal groups, again and again, is not always best: in MacKay’s seven-outcome Example 5.18 it gives 2.53 against 1.97 MacKay 2003, p. 100.

The formula (E9). Call the average of the best card \(\mathrm{EQ}^{*}\). With the entropy \(H\), Cover and Thomas give Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 15:

\[H \le \mathrm{EQ}^{*} \lt H + 1 \quad\text{(E9)}\]

\(\mathrm{EQ}^{*}\) equals \(H\) exactly when every share is a power of one half Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 86. Cover and Thomas show “the equivalence of coding and the game of 20 questions” Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 94–95 (eq. 5.61): a sequence of questions is a code. We quote the bound without proof. The check, on the three days: \(1.750 = H\); \(1.357 \le 1.500 \lt 2.357\); \(2.000 = H\).

4 · Abstract

Short answer, and what it really is

Short answer (our reading, not a finding)

How much? One outcome is worth \(\log_2(1/P)\) fair yes/no questions under a stated model of the source. The average over everything the source can send is the entropy \(H\): 1.75 questions a day for the village.

A surprise? Improbability under a model. The number belongs to the model, not to the event: two forecasters with different shares give the same snow different numbers (boundary 4).

Worth? Questions saved, not importance, truth or meaning. Shannon sets meaning aside for “the engineering problem” Shannon 1948, p. 1; that is scope, not a claim that meaning does not matter. Our verdict: the measure is a good price for improbability under a stated model, and a poor price for anything else. What would change it: evidence that the number predicts something beyond improbability. Lens 2 has one such claim, hedged; we have not checked its evidence.

(a) What it really is

Strip away the cafés, villages and submarines. Surprisal re-writes a share in units that add for independent events; the logarithm turns a product of shares into a sum MacKay 2003, p. 68.

Entropy is the average surprisal under the same shares, and depends only on the shares, not on what the outcomes are Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 13. It is 0 only when one outcome is certain, and at most \(\log_2\) of the number of outcomes, with equality only when all are equally likely MacKay 2003, p. 33 Shannon 1948, p. 11. Any change that moves the shares toward equality raises it Shannon 1948, p. 12: the village’s 1.75 becomes 2 when its shares are made equal.

The surprisal approximates the length of the shortest uniquely decipherable script for the outcome Gibson et al. 2019, p. 4. “Approximates” is the authors’ word. “Uniquely decipherable” is theirs too (Cover and Thomas write “uniquely decodable”); it means a run of answers can be cut into words in only one way; scripts in which no string is the start of another, like our card, are one kind of these Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 80–81. This is E2 and E4 without the café.

Look inside: why this formula? Shannon’s three properties

Shannon asked for a measure with three properties: it is continuous in the shares; it grows with the number of options when all are equally likely; and if a choice is broken into two successive choices, the original \(H\) is the weighted sum of the two \(H\)s Shannon 1948, p. 10. His Theorem 2 says the only such \(H\) is \(-K\sum p\log p\). He also writes that the theorem and its assumptions “are in no way necessary for the present theory”, and: “The real justification of these definitions, however, will reside in their implications.” Shannon 1948, p. 11 We do not reproduce the proof.

4 · Abstract

(b) The same idea elsewhere

Lens 1 · Statistical mechanics

same-math (the form of the sum only) The sum \(-\sum p\log p\) appears in physics.

Shannon writes: “The form of H will be recognized as that of entropy as defined in certain formulations of statistical mechanics.” Shannon 1948, p. 11 Pierce says the ideas behind the two entropies are “quite different” Pierce 1961, pp. 23–24.

Adds: the sum is about distributions wherever shares exist. Does not explain: that the two entropies are one thing.

Lens 2 · Psycholinguistics

structural analogy Surprisal is built from a share; what a share does inside a mind is an empirical claim, not a theorem.

Gibson and colleagues use the quantity directly: a listener’s difficulty in picking a colour chip, given a colour word, is “the surprisal of the color, given the word” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 7. For minds they write, as one premise in an argument about grammatical gender: “Since language processing effort is associated with predictability [104,105]” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 11. Read the hedge. They cite two studies we have not seen, and their word is predictability, not surprisal.

Adds: the one lens whose claim is about behaviour (ours), though the post cites only a reported association. Does not explain: cause. “Associated with” is not “caused by”.

Lens 3 · Bayesian probability

structural analogy A share can be a degree of belief, so surprisal is relative to a believer.

The village’s shares come from a long record; a one-off event has none (ours). MacKay describes the Bayesian view, in which beliefs can be probabilities, even for a single proposition, and notes that it “is not yet a globally held view” MacKay 2003, p. 26.

Adds: a way to price one-off events. Does not explain: whether the beliefs are right.

Lens 4 · Search and decision

same-math A tree of yes/no questions is a code Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 94–95.

For one experiment, MacKay’s tentative conclusion (“we might conclude”) is that it is most informative when its outcomes are equally likely MacKay 2003, p. 70. A whole search differs: splitting every group as evenly as possible is not always the fastest script MacKay 2003, p. 100 Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 97, and step 6’s rule is Cover & Thomas 1991, p. 101.

Adds: from describing a source to choosing what to ask. Does not explain: a whole search built by even splits (step 6), questions whose costs differ, or answers that can be misheard.

Compared (ours). Lens 2 is a claim to test; lens 3 says a share belongs to a believer; lens 4 takes the shares as fixed and the average as the thing to minimise.

Look inside: more hedges from Shannon, Pierce and from Gibson and colleagues

Shannon sets meaning aside, not the receiver: his introduction also lists a concern with “the nature of the final destination of the information” Shannon 1948, p. 1.

Averaged over words, the colour score is “the optimal number of yes–no guesses it takes a listener to guess which chip the speaker meant” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 7: our barista’s game, though the score also weighs how likely a speaker is to use the word. For word length, a word’s chance in context beat its frequency in a variety of languages, and the pattern “likely reflects lexicalization of processes that shorten predictable words” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 6.

Pierce: the idea that one entropy “can be fully useful without any reference at all to the other”; yet both “can be described in terms of uncertainty, in similar mathematical terms”, and efforts to marry the two have been “more interesting than fruitful” Pierce 1961, p. 24. He advises clearing our minds of the entropy of physics first Pierce 1961, p. 80.

Gibson and colleagues: there is currently “no method of generating baseline utterances and languages that is general and satisfactory across all cases” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 14. For taboo words and similar factors, “It is currently unclear whether such factors can be expressed as part of general communicative efficiency” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 15. Their abstract is bolder: these studies “show how a pervasive pressure for efficiency guides the forms of natural language” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 1. We do not write that surprisal predicts reading time or processing effort on the strength of this review.

For your research (3C), ours: Computation: a language model’s next-word share gives a surprisal under that model. Communication: a headline’s surprisal depends on whose model prices it. Collaboration: the same sum applies to shares of topics or ties. Connections, not findings.

4 · Abstract

(c) Where it fails

Each boundary is an assumption you can find in section 3, with the place where the café breaks.

1 · Meaning. Assumption: E2 has only \(P(x)\) in it, so two words with equal share get equal surprisal, understood or not. Shannon sets meaning aside Shannon 1948, p. 1. The café breaks: the barista cares which drink it is; the script is blind to taste.

2 · A known, stable distribution. Assumption: section 3 gives the shares in advance, as Shannon does: “These probabilities are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur” Shannon 1948, p. 10. Pierce says the chances “must not change with time if the source is to be ergodic” Pierce 1961, p. 81 (ergodic, our gloss: its long-run averages do not depend on which run you watch). The café breaks: the cash register’s record gets old, and the village script costs 2.25 on a uniform day (E5).

3 · Independent days. Assumption: symbols “being chosen independently” Shannon 1948, p. 18, and E3 needs it. The café breaks: a rainy morning makes hot drinks likelier.

4 · One-off events, and whose model. Assumption: E2 needs \(P(x)\), and the village takes it from a record. The café breaks: a single event has no record. Two forecasters can write two shares for the same snow (ours):

5 · Estimation. Assumption: the village shares are given; in practice they are estimated, and “it is difficult to get reliable estimates of these information-theoretic quantities without very large datasets” Gibson et al. 2019, p. 14. The bias is our computation (notebook exercise 6): draw \(n\) village days, put the observed shares into the entropy formula, and repeat 2,000 times. 5 · The CSS imagination

Whose model sets the price of a surprise?

C. Wright Mills argued that people need “a quality of mind” that shows how a life and its time fit together Mills 1959, pp. 4–6. His advice is not findings: “There are definite ways, I believe, of stimulating the sociological imagination” Mills 1959, p. 212. The names of the moves are ours.

Move 1 · Extremes and inverted scale

“Often you get the best insights by considering extremes” Mills 1959, p. 213. In our reading, the village has two extremes: a village where it always rains (\(H = 0\)) and four equal words (\(H = 2.000\)). A third extreme is a share pushed toward 0: the worth of that word grows without bound (step 2’s plot), and a forecast that gives snow share 0 is refuted by one snowfall (ours). Now invert the scale: “What would pre-literate villages look like with populations of 30 millions?” Mills 1959, p. 215 Give the weather village 30 million listeners (ours). Each listener’s surprisal for snow is the same number, but one forecast now prices all of them: if its share for snow is wrong, it is wrong for 30 million people at once (ours). What changes is who writes the forecast, and who pays when it is wrong.

Move 2 · Trouble and issue

Mills: “Perhaps the most fruitful distinction with which the sociological imagination works is between ‘the personal troubles of milieu’ and ‘the public issues of social structure.’” Mills 1959, p. 8 His example is hypothetical, not data: one man unemployed “in a city of 100,000” is “his personal trouble”, while “15 million men” unemployed “in a nation of 50 million employees” is “an issue” Mills 1959, p. 9. An issue, he says, “unlike even widespread trouble”, “cannot very well be defined” within everyday surroundings; in his unemployment example, “The very structure of opportunities has collapsed” Mills 1959, pp. 8–9. So (our reading) an issue is not a trouble multiplied.

Our case (ours). One reader misses an important message among her 200 and blames herself: a trouble. A ranking that orders messages by how surprising they are under its own model would price every message for everyone, which is E2 with a single model; that it does so is a hypothesis, not a finding. A test (ours): if changing the ranking changes what many readers miss, and individual effort does not, the cause lies in an arrangement, not in the readers.

Move 3 · Another discipline’s eyes

Mills suggests asking how a political scientist, a psychologist or a historian would see a problem Mills 1959, p. 214. Take “snow tomorrow” (ours). A psycholinguist asks whether language processing effort is associated with how predictable an element is in context, a link Gibson and colleagues state Gibson et al. 2019, p. 11. A journalist asks what counts as news: a rumour and a budget can carry the same surprisal; meaning and consequence are what the number leaves out. The two questions predict different things (ours; neither is a finding): under one model, the same snow should cost every reader the same effort; the journalist expects attention to follow what the news changes for each reader, whatever its surprisal.

Look inside: Mills on counting, both sides

Mills’ word “information” Mills 1959, p. 5 means, in our reading, facts (“this Age of Fact”), not Shannon’s sense Pierce 1961, p. 24.

Mills’ view of statistics is two-sided. He wrote that “an empiricism as cautious and rigid as abstracted empiricism eliminates the great social problems and human issues of our time from inquiry”, and on the same page: “If the problems upon which one is at work are readily amenable to statistical procedures, one should always try to use them” Mills 1959, p. 73. He also says that, “Nowadays at least”, he would never count or measure anything before he had played with its elements in an imagined world “in which I control the scale of everything” Mills 1959, p. 215: a point about the order of work, not a rejection of measuring. On extremes he adds that you should keep striving “to gain and to maintain a sense of proportion” Mills 1959, p. 214.

Two open research questions (ours; invitations, not findings)

1 · Computation and Communication. Do posts that are more surprising under a language model spread further, after controlling for topic and emotion? Data: posts with share counts and a topic label, plus token probabilities from one fixed language model. Method: one number per post (the sum or the mean of the words’ surprisals; length is a confound), then a regression of share counts on it with topic fixed effects (a separate baseline for each topic), repeated with another model. We have not checked the prior work.

2 · Collaboration and Communication. How does the entropy of a platform’s daily topic mix change around a crisis? Data: posts labelled by topic (for example by a large language model used as a labeller). Method: plug-in entropy (the formula on the observed shares) with bias correction and bootstrap intervals (resampling days to see the spread; boundary 5), before and after a dated event; this is Mills’ comparison move Mills 1959, p. 215 (“comparable cases”), and it means something only if one model prices every case (boundary 4).

7 · Recall questions

Test yourself

Answer aloud, then open the card. ↻ marks questions that will return later.

↻ Why does the measure use a logarithm?

Chances of independent days multiply, and a log turns a product into a sum, so surprisals add MacKay 2003, p. 68.

↻ What is the surprisal of an outcome whose share is 1/16?

4 questions’ worth: \(\log_2 16 = 4\).

↻ What does “1.75 bits” mean for the village?

The best script of fair yes/no questions needs 1.75 questions per day on average, in this village.

↻ Why is 1.75 lower than 2, and when would the best script average exactly 2?

The shares are uneven, so common words get short strings. For example, with four equally likely words the best script averages 2, and \(H = 2\) too. Uneven shares such as 0.3, 0.3, 0.2, 0.2 also need 2 on the best card, though \(H\) is 1.971 (ours, run in code).

What does entropy not measure?

Meaning, value, truth or importance. Shannon sets meaning aside for “the engineering problem”: scope, not denial Shannon 1948, p. 1.

Who encodes and who decodes?

The side with the original and the script encodes: it writes the string. The side with only the string and the same script decodes. In the café: customer, then barista.

Two forecasters see the same snow. Why do they get different numbers, and which assumption of the village breaks?

A surprisal is built from a share, and a share belongs to a model. The village assumes known, stable shares; a one-off event has no record, so each forecaster writes her own (boundary 4).

How do you build the best card for any shares, and what does it cost?

Join the two rarest outcomes, give everything in the group one more question, and repeat. This is Huffman’s rule Cover & Thomas 1991, pp. 92–94. The best average is at least \(H\) and below \(H+1\); for shares 0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1 it is 1.500, with \(H = 1.357\).
