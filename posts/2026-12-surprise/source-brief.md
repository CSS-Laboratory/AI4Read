# Source brief: "How much is a surprise worth?"

**Citations.** "p. N" is the printed page; "(pdf N)" is the PDF page. Offsets: Shannon 1948 and Gibson 2019, printed = pdf. MacKay: printed = pdf − 12. Pierce: printed = pdf − 16. Cover & Thomas: printed = pdf − 22. Shannon 1951: printed = pdf + 49 (pdf 6 = p. 55). Quotes are at most 25 words. The Pierce text is OCR of a 1961 text (Dover 1980) with a few garbled lines; quotes from it were checked on the page and marked where the OCR layout is mixed. Logs are base 2.

**Five traps for the whole post**
1. *Three names, one quantity.* Shannon 1948 never writes "surprisal" or "information content" for a single outcome. He speaks of "choice", "uncertainty" and entropy (p. 10). MacKay calls `log2 (1/P(x))` the "Shannon information content" (p. 32). Gibson et al. call it "surprisal" (p. 3, p. 4). Say which source uses which name. (Not in our sources: who coined "surprisal". Do not say.)
2. *Powers of two make the village too nice.* Shares 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8 give whole numbers of questions, so the average equals H exactly. In general the best average lies between H and H + 1 (Cover p. 15). Say so, or the reader thinks 1.75 is the rule.
3. *The village assumes independent days* (Shannon p. 18, "successive symbols being chosen independently"). Real weather and real text are not independent.
4. *Hartley is not "the old wrong measure".* It is a logarithm already (Shannon p. 1; Pierce p. 40). It is right for equally likely, independent choices.
5. *Cover & Thomas dates Hartley "in the 1930s"* (p. 49). Shannon (p. 1, note 2) and Pierce (p. 39) say 1928.

## A. Before Shannon: the old measures

### 1. Nyquist: speed grows with the logarithm of the number of signal values
- **Claim.** Nyquist (1924 and 1928, as reported by Pierce) related telegraph speed `W` to `m`, the number of distinct current values: `W = K log m`. Two current values (on-off) to four doubles the speed; eight values should give four times the speed. He also warned that noise and power limits make many values hard to use.
- **Page.** Pierce pp. 36–38 (pdf 52–54); Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1) cites Nyquist 1924 (p. 324) and 1928 (p. 617) as one basis of a general theory.
- **Quote.** Pierce p. 38 (pdf 54): "we should take the logarithm of the number of symbols in order to get the line speed".
- **Numbers and equations.** `W = K log m` (Pierce p. 36). Table of independent 0-or-1 choices: 2 choices give 4 combinations, 3 give 8, M give 2^M (Pierce p. 37). Quadruplex telegraph: four current values double the speed (p. 38).
- **Traps.** Pierce is explaining Nyquist in his own words ("Nyquist tells us ..."); Nyquist's 1924 and 1928 papers are UNSEEN (not in our library). `K` is a constant that depends on the number of values sent per second; do not drop it. "Number of signal levels" is correct for Nyquist, but his log is not yet a measure of *probability-weighted* information.
- **Tensions.** Nyquist's relation counts what the line can carry; Hartley's counts what the sender can choose. Shannon p. 1 treats both as the starting point.
- **Toy idea.** A café with a sign that can show 2, 4 or 8 colours. Each extra doubling of colours adds one "nod" of capacity.

### 2. Hartley: the logarithm of the number of possible sequences, and its stated condition
- **Claim.** Hartley (1928) pictured the sender as choosing symbols from a set and defined the information of a message as the logarithm of the number of possible sequences: `H = n log s`, `n` symbols chosen from `s` kinds. Pierce says this fits "our present knowledge" only when symbols are chosen independently and are equally likely. Hartley also noted that restrictions on selection (E more often than Z) should govern code lengths. Shannon p. 1: "As was pointed out by Hartley the most natural choice is the logarithmic function."
- **Page.** Pierce pp. 39–40 (pdf 55–56); Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1), note 2: Hartley, "Transmission of Information", Bell System Technical Journal, July 1928, p. 535; MacKay p. 74 (pdf 86) for the modern name; Cover p. 49 (pdf 71) for the date discrepancy.
- **Quote.** Pierce p. 40 (pdf 56): "only if successive symbols are chosen independently and if any of the s symbols is equally likely to be selected".
- **Numbers and equations.** `H = n log s` (Pierce p. 40). MacKay's **raw bit content** `H0(X) = log2 |A_X|` is the same idea: "a lower bound for the number of binary questions that are always guaranteed to identify an outcome" (MacKay p. 74), and it "does not include any probabilistic element". For n symbols from s kinds: log2 (s^n) = n log2 s.
- **Traps.** (a) Pierce's sentence has two conditions, independence and equal probability; keep both. (b) Hartley's own paper is UNSEEN: everything about his wording comes from Pierce and Shannon. (c) Do not write that Hartley "ignored" frequency: Pierce says he noted that restrictions should govern code length, and that Morse "himself understood this" (p. 40); what Hartley lacked was a probability-weighted measure.
- **Tensions.** Cover p. 49 writes "In the 193Os [sic, OCR for 1930s], Hartley introduced a logarithmic measure of information for communication. His measure was essentially the logarithm of the alphabet size." The date conflicts with Shannon's footnote and Pierce (both 1928); Cover's own reference list cites the Bell System Technical Journal. The content ("logarithm of the alphabet size") agrees with the others.
- **Toy idea.** A 32-cup tray: Hartley says 5 bits for any cup, because it counts cups, not chances. The village in unit 7 gives rain 1 bit and snow 3 although there are four words.

### 3. Why a logarithm: the three reasons and the additivity check
- **Claim.** Shannon gives three reasons for a logarithmic measure: it is practically useful (relays, time, bandwidth vary linearly with the logarithm of the number of possibilities), it matches intuition (two punched cards hold twice as much as one), and it is mathematically convenient. The base fixes the unit: base 2 gives "binary digits, or ... bits", a word he credits to J. W. Tukey. MacKay shows additivity: for independent `x` and `y`, `h(x, y) = h(x) + h(y)`. Pierce p. 80: four telegraph lines carry four times as much as one, so information should be measured by the logarithm of the number of messages.
- **Page.** Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1); MacKay p. 68 (pdf 80), eqs. 4.4–4.6; Pierce p. 80 (pdf 96).
- **Quotes.** Shannon p. 1: "A device with two stable positions, such as a relay or a flip-flop circuit, can store one bit of information." MacKay p. 68: "we might want any measure of the 'amount of information gained' to have the property of additivity". Pierce p. 80: "amount of information should be measured, not by the number of possible messages, but by the logarithm of this number".
- **Numbers and equations.** `N` devices store `N` bits since there are `2^N` states and `log2 2^N = N` (Shannon p. 1). `log2 M = 3.32 log10 M` (Shannon p. 1). `P(x,y) = P(x)P(y)` gives `log 1/P(x,y) = log 1/P(x) + log 1/P(y)` (MacKay p. 68). Tukey's word "bit": Shannon p. 1.
- **Traps.** "Bit" has two meanings in MacKay: a unit and a 0/1 variable (p. 32). Shannon's Theorem 2 (p. 11) derives `-K Σ p log p` from three requirements, but says the theorem and its assumptions "are in no way necessary for the present theory" (see unit 6); reasons 1–3 above are his motivation, not a proof of uniqueness.
- **Tensions.** None between sources; MacKay (additivity), Shannon (convenience) and Pierce (telegraph lines) give three routes to the same log.
- **Toy idea.** Rain today and snow tomorrow: 1 + 3 = 4 bits, the same as one of 16 equally likely pairs of weather (our check, W1 numbers).

## B. Yes/no questions, twenty questions, weighing

### 4. Equal chances: questions equal log2 of the number of options
- **Claim.** With 64 equally likely objects, six well-chosen yes/no questions identify one, and the answers read as the binary number. Cover's opening example: 32 equally likely outcomes need 5-bit labels, and the entropy is `log 32 = 5`. MacKay says the questioning strategy "defines a way of encoding the random variable x as a binary file".
- **Page.** MacKay p. 70 (pdf 82), Example 4.3 "sixty-three"; Cover pp. 5–6 (pdf 27–28), Examples 1.1.1–1.1.2; MacKay p. 74 for the lower bound.
- **Quotes.** MacKay p. 70: "Intuitively, the best questions successively divide the 64 possibilities into equal sized sets. Six questions suffice." MacKay p. 70: "Our questioning strategy defines a way of encoding the random variable x as a binary file."
- **Numbers and equations.** The six questions: x ≥ 32? x mod 32 ≥ 16? x mod 16 ≥ 8? x mod 8 ≥ 4? x mod 4 ≥ 2? x mod 2 = 1? Each answer has Shannon information content `log2 (1/0.5) = 1` bit, so the total is 6 bits. Example: 35 gives 100011 (MacKay p. 70). Cover Example 1.1.1: `H = -Σ (1/32) log (1/32) = log 32 = 5` bits (p. 5). Ours: one poisoned cup in 32 and five rats gives 5 answers at once; four rats cannot decide 32 cups because 2^4 = 16 < 32.
- **Traps.** The rats-and-cups puzzle is not in any source. It asks all questions at once (not adaptive); MacKay's game asks them in turn. For equal chances both need `log2 N`. The "twenty questions" game MacKay mentions is the general game; "sixty-three" is his dull version (p. 70).
- **Tensions.** MacKay and Cover agree on the number. MacKay's lower-bound wording (p. 74) says "guaranteed", meaning in the worst case, not on average.
- **Toy idea.** 32 cups and 5 rats (Kunhao's puzzle); the `stepper` of questions as a table of answers read as a binary number.

### 5. The weighing problem: the best question has outcomes as nearly equally likely as possible
- **Claim.** 12 balls, one heavier or lighter, a balance with three outcomes. There are 24 hypotheses (12 balls × heavy or light) and 3^3 = 27 > 24 > 9 = 3^2, so three weighings might do and two cannot. At each step of an optimal procedure the three outcomes are as close as possible to equiprobable. Weighing balls 1–6 against 7–12 cannot balance, so it rules out only half the hypotheses. Conclusion: an experiment's outcome is most informative when its distribution is uniform.
- **Page.** MacKay p. 66 (pdf 78, Exercise 4.1); p. 68 (pdf 80); p. 70 (pdf 82); p. 84 (pdf 96), Exercise 4.9.
- **Quote.** MacKay p. 68: "at each step of an optimal procedure, the three outcomes ('left heavier', 'right heavier', and 'balance') are as close as possible to equiprobable". MacKay p. 70: "the outcome of a random experiment is guaranteed to be most informative if the probability distribution over outcomes is uniform".
- **Numbers and equations.** First weighing must split 24 hypotheses 8:8:8; the second must give a 3:3:2 split (p. 70). Our computation (to be run in code): 4 against 4 gives outcome probabilities 1/3, 1/3, 1/3 and 1.585 bits; 6 against 6 gives 1/2, 1/2, 0 and 1.000 bit; all 24 cases are `log2 24 = 4.585` bits. Exercise 4.9: some say 6 against 6 "conveys no information at all", others disagree; MacKay asks the reader to "Explain to the second group why they are both right and wrong" (p. 84). Ours: about *which ball*, 6 v 6 gives 0 bits (every ball is still possible); about *which ball and heavy or light* it gives 1 bit.
- **Traps.** "Uniform is most informative" is MacKay's own conclusion from this example, tied to `H(X) ≤ log |A_X|` (p. 70, p. 33); it is not a theorem about every question. The numbers 1.585 and 1.000 are ours, not printed in MacKay. The answer to Exercise 4.9 is in MacKay's solutions, which we did not read (UNSEEN, solution page not located).
- **Tensions.** None. Cover p. 15 makes the same point for a question tree ("An efficient first question ... splits the probability in half").
- **Toy idea.** The `lab` widget with one slider, k balls per pan.

### 6. The village question tree: surprisal as questions, entropy as the average
- **Claim.** For a source with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8 (letters A–D in Shannon; four values in Cover), entropy is 7/4 bits per symbol, and the code A = 0, B = 10, C = 110, D = 111 reaches it. Asking "Is X = a?", then "= b?", then "= c?" gives an expected 1.75 questions, which is the minimum. In general the minimum expected number of questions lies between H(X) and H(X) + 1. Cover also states that a sequence of questions is equivalent to a code and the average number of questions to the average code length.
- **Page.** Shannon p. 18 (pdf 18); Cover pp. 14–15 (pdf 36–37), Example 2.1.2; Cover pp. 94–95 (pdf 116–117), §5.7 item 1, eq. 5.61; Cover p. 6 (pdf 28), horse race (entropy 2 bits, code 0, 10, 110, 1110, 111100–111111, average 2 bits against 3).
- **Quotes.** Cover p. 15: "The resulting expected number of binary questions required is 1.75." Cover p. 94: "We now digress to show the equivalence of coding and the game of 20 questions." Shannon p. 18: "successive symbols being chosen independently".
- **Numbers and equations.** `H = -(1/2 log 1/2 + 1/4 log 1/4 + 2/8 log 1/8) = 7/4` bits (Shannon p. 18). Average code length `N(1/2·1 + 1/4·2 + 2/8·3) = 7/4 N` (p. 18). Maximum entropy of the original set `log 4 = 2`, so relative entropy is 7/8 (p. 18; careful, "relative entropy" here means H/Hmax, not KL). Surprisals: 1, 2, 3, 3 bits (ours, `log2 (1/p)`). Cover's bound: `H(X) ≤ EQ < H(X) + 1` (p. 95, eq. 5.61, "≤ EQ <": the OCR prints a garbled symbol, check the PDF image). Ours (to be run in code): shares 0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1 give H = 1.357 bits, and a Huffman tree gives lengths 1, 2, 3, 3 and an average of 1.5.
- **Traps.** (a) Powers of two: see trap 2 at the top. (b) The village is toyworld W1 (`toyworlds.md`: rain 1/2, sun 1/4, wind 1/8, snow 1/8); Shannon's letters are A–D and his entropy is "bits per symbol", not per day, unless the village sends one symbol per day. (c) `wiki/instances/twenty-questions.md` says Cover mentions twenty questions only in Problems 7 and 13; Cover §5.7 and the introduction (p. 6) say more. (d) Cover p. 15 says "Is X = a?" splits "the probability in half" only for this example.
- **Tensions.** Cover reaches the question tree from the entropy side (p. 15) and from the Huffman side (§5.7); Shannon reaches the code from the encoding side (p. 18). Cover's book calls the units "bits" in the text and the OCR shows nats as "nuts" (p. 13, OCR error).
- **Toy idea.** The barista's question tree, with nods as 1 and shakes as 0.

## C. Surprisal and entropy

### 7. Definition of surprisal (Shannon information content) and its numbers
- **Claim.** The Shannon information content of an outcome `x` is `h(x) = log2 (1/P(x))`, in bits. The less probable an outcome, the greater its information content. MacKay's table for English letters: e 3.5 bits, z 10.4 bits, space 2.4 bits. Gibson et al. call the same quantity *surprisal*, "the negative log probability of a discrete event", and say it approximates the length of the shortest uniquely decipherable code for the event.
- **Page.** MacKay p. 32 (pdf 44), eq. 2.34 and Table 2.9; p. 67 (pdf 79), Fig. 4.1; Gibson p. 3 (glossary) and p. 4 (Box 2, eq. I); Shannon p. 13 (pdf 13), the long-sequence statement.
- **Quotes.** Gibson p. 3: "Surprisal: the negative log probability of a discrete event." (Glossary text in the pdftotext copy is laid out in two columns; the line was read on the page image layout.) Gibson p. 4: "Information content approximates the length of the shortest uniquely decipherable code that can be written for the event x". Shannon p. 13: "the logarithm of the reciprocal probability of a typical long sequence divided by the number of symbols in the sequence".
- **Numbers and equations.** `h(x) = log2 1/P(x)` (MacKay 2.34; Gibson I). MacKay Fig. 4.1 table: p = 0.001 gives h = 10.0 bits (binary entropy `H2` 0.011); 0.01: 6.6 (0.081); 0.1: 3.3 (0.47); 0.2: 2.3 (0.72); 0.5: 1.0 (1.0) (p. 67). Table 2.9 values come from rounded probabilities (e = .0913 gives 3.45, shown 3.5). Shannon p. 13: `log p^-1 / N` is "almost certain" to be close to `H` for long sequences (Theorem 3; we only quote the plain statement, not the theorem).
- **Traps.** (a) Name trap 1. (b) `h(x)` belongs to an outcome *and a model*: same outcome, different `P`, different `h` (ours). (c) The code-length reading is an approximation (Gibson: "approximates"); whole numbers of questions only when `p` is a power of 1/2. (d) The "10.4 bits for z" depends on the document the table came from; MacKay's English corpus is not Shannon's.
- **Tensions.** Shannon's own per-letter figures for English (`F1` = 4.14 bits for 26 letters and 4.03 for 27, Shannon 1951, p. 54, table; not in the 1948 paper) are close to but not the same as MacKay's 4.11 (different texts). Do not mix them.
- **Toy idea.** The `plot` widget (surprisal curve and binary entropy; reproduce the p. 67 table).

### 8. Entropy: the average surprisal, and its properties
- **Claim.** Entropy is the average Shannon information content, `H(X) = Σ P(x) log2 1/P(x)`, with `0 log 1/0 = 0`. Another name is the uncertainty of X. It depends only on the probabilities, not on the values the outcomes take. `H ≥ 0`, with equality only when one outcome is certain; `H ≤ log |A_X|`, with equality only when all outcomes are equally likely. Entropy is additive for independent variables. Shannon asked for a measure with three properties (continuity; increasing in `n` for equal probabilities; breaking a choice into two successive choices gives a weighted sum), and Theorem 2 says the only such `H` is `-K Σ p log p`.
- **Page.** Shannon pp. 10–12 (pdf 10–12): requirements and Fig. 6 (p. 10), Theorem 2 and properties 1–2 (p. 11), property 4 (p. 12); MacKay p. 32 (pdf 44) and p. 33 (pdf 45); Cover pp. 13–14 (pdf 35–36); Pierce pp. 80–83 (pdf 96–99).
- **Quotes.** MacKay p. 32: "The entropy of an ensemble X is defined to be the average Shannon information content of an outcome". Cover p. 13: "It does not depend on the actual values taken by the random variable X, but only on the probabilities." Shannon p. 11: "Thus only when we are certain of the outcome does H vanish." Shannon p. 10: "With equally likely events there is more choice, or uncertainty, when there are more possible events."
- **Numbers and equations.** `H(1/2, 1/3, 1/6) = H(1/2, 1/2) + 1/2 H(2/3, 1/3)` (Shannon p. 10, Fig. 6). MacKay's decomposition example: shares 1/2, 1/4, 1/4 give `H = 1.5` bits (p. 33, eq. 2.41). Entropy of an English letter drawn at random: "about 4.11 bits" for the probabilities in MacKay's Table 2.9 (p. 32). Fair coin 1 bit per toss; a coin turning up heads 3/4 of the time has `H = .811` bit per toss (Pierce pp. 82–83). Binary entropy is maximal (1 bit) at `p = 1/2` and 0 at `p = 0` or 1 (Shannon p. 11, Fig. 7; Pierce p. 83). Ours: village `H = 1.75`; uniform `H = 2`; only-rain `H = 0`; any averaging of probabilities toward equality raises H (Shannon p. 12, property 4).
- **Traps.** (a) Shannon's "relative entropy" is `H/Hmax` (p. 14); modern "relative entropy" is KL: avoid the term. (b) `H` is a property of the *distribution*, not of one message. (c) "Uncertainty" in Shannon, Pierce and MacKay is a name for this number, not a psychological state. (d) Shannon calls the uniqueness theorem plausibility, not justification: "This theorem, and the assumptions required for its proof, are in no way necessary for the present theory." (p. 11) and "The real justification of these definitions, however, will reside in their implications." (p. 11).
- **Tensions.** Pierce p. 81 says restricting certain messages to be sent very often or very rarely "must decrease entropy"; Shannon's property 4 (p. 12) says equalising raises it; they agree.
- **Toy idea.** The `lab` widget with four sliders (village weights).

### 9. The submarine: one answer can be worth six bits, a miss almost nothing
- **Claim.** On an 8 × 8 grid with one hidden submarine, the first shot hits with probability 1/64. If it hits, the outcome carries `log2 64 = 6` bits. A miss at the first shot carries `log2 (64/63) = 0.0227` bits, the second `log2 (63/62) = 0.0230`. Thirty-two misses in a row add to exactly 1.0 bit, because the miss tells you the target is not among 32 squares: half the hypotheses. Forty-eight misses add to 2 bits. A hit on the 49th shot with 16 squares left is worth `log2 16 = 4` bits, and the total is 6 bits whenever the hit comes.
- **Page.** MacKay pp. 70–72 (pdf 82–84), Figure 4.3 and eqs. 4.8–4.14.
- **Quote.** MacKay p. 71 (pdf 83): "Now, it might seem a little strange that one binary outcome can convey six bits."
- **Numbers and equations.** `h(1)(y) = log2 64 = 6` bits (4.8); `h(1)(n) = log2 (64/63) = 0.0227` (4.9); `h(2)(n) = log2 (63/62) = 0.0230` (4.10); 32 misses: `0.0227 + 0.0230 + ... + 0.0430 = 1.0` (4.11). Telescoping product (4.14): `log2 (64/63 × 63/62 × ... × (n+1)/n × n/1) = log2 64 = 6` bits whatever `n`. Ours (checked by a one-line computation): the 32nd miss costs `log2 (33/32) = 0.0444`; Fig. 4.3 prints 0.0443 at move 32 and 0.0874 at move 48 (p. 71), which agree to rounding. The last term printed in eq. 4.11, 0.0430, equals the 31st term `log2 (34/33)`, so it looks like a small misprint in the book. UNSEEN at the PDF image: verify, and do not quote 0.0430.
- **Traps.** One binary answer does not carry "one bit" in general: its worth is `log2 1/p` for the outcome that happened. The 49th shot in Fig. 4.3 is a hit; "the submarine is hit on the 49th attempt" (p. 71). MacKay's own verdict, "quite a convincing case for the claim that the Shannon information content is a sensible measure" (p. 72, line broken across columns in the text copy, read on the page), is an argument, not a proof.
- **Tensions.** MacKay p. 72: the game of sixty-three "shows that the Shannon information content can be intimately connected to the size of a file that encodes the outcomes", pointing to compression (unit 6).
- **Toy idea.** The `stepper` over shots (rows computed by code first).

### 10. Wenglish: different letters carry different bits, and the word total is fixed
- **Claim.** In MacKay's made-up language Wenglish, words have 5 letters and the dictionary has 2^15 = 32,768 words used with equal probability, so each word is 15 bits and 3 bits per character on average. Read one character at a time, a first letter `a` (probability about 0.0625) carries about 4 bits and a first letter `z` (about 0.001) carries about 10 bits. Words that start with a rare letter are quickly identified.
- **Page.** MacKay pp. 72–73 (pdf 84–85).
- **Quote.** MacKay p. 73 (pdf 85): "A rare initial letter such as z indeed conveys more information about what the word is than a common initial letter."
- **Numbers and equations.** `log2 1/0.0625 ≈ 4` and `log2 1/0.001 ≈ 10` bits; the five characters of a word total exactly 15 bits; 2048 of the words begin with `a` and 32 with `z`; 26^5 ≈ 12,000,000 possible 5-letter strings (p. 72).
- **Traps.** Surprisal is per outcome *given what has been read so far*. Gibson's uniform-information-density hypothesis (information per unit time roughly constant, p. 3 glossary and p. 8) is an empirical claim about speakers, and Wenglish is a constructed example that does not test it.
- **Tensions.** Gibson p. 8: Maurits et al. "argue that SVO order produces the most uniform information profile"; this is an argument from co-occurrence statistics, not an experiment on readers.
- **Toy idea.** Notebook exercise 5: letter surprisal table from the reader's own text.

## D. What the number leaves out, and what it assumes

### 11. Meaning is set aside (Shannon), and "information" the word (Pierce)
- **Claim.** Shannon: messages often have meaning, but "these semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem"; what matters is that the message is one selected from a set of possible messages, and the system must work for each possible selection. Gibson et al.: information theory "as specified so far is entirely agnostic to the form and content of messages". Pierce: "information" in everyday speech is linked to knowledge, which confuses the discussion when the word is used in the communication-theory sense (uncertainty and its resolution). Pierce also notes the random pattern is "mathematically the most surprising" and "the dullest of all patterns", and that "We can be surprised repeatedly only by constrast with that which is familiar, not by chaos."
- **Page.** Shannon p. 1 (pdf 1); Gibson p. 4 (pdf 4); Pierce p. 24 (pdf 40), p. 264 (pdf 280), p. 267 (pdf 283).
- **Quotes.** Shannon p. 1: "These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem." Gibson p. 4: "information theory as specified so far is entirely agnostic to the form and content of messages". Pierce p. 24: "The confusion is sometimes aggravated when more than one meaning of information creeps into a discussion." Pierce p. 264 (OCR line breaks, one word garbled in the surrounding sentence): "mathematically the most surprising, the least predictable of all possible patterns". Pierce p. 267: "We can be surprised repeatedly only by constrast [sic] with that which is familiar, not by chaos."
- **Numbers and equations.** None. Shannon's reason is about design: "The system must be designed to operate for each possible selection, not just the one which will actually be chosen" (p. 1; the sentence goes on to say the choice is unknown at the time of design).
- **Traps.** (a) Shannon sets meaning aside for the *engineering* problem; he does not say meaning does not matter. Do not write "information theory says meaning is irrelevant". (b) Weaver's three levels (technical, semantic, effectiveness) are in Shannon & Weaver 1949, not in any source we hold. The canon entry says Weaver's essay is "where communication studies picked up" the model; we have not seen it. **UNSEEN: Weaver's essay.** (c) Pierce's art chapter uses "surprising" in the loose sense; the sentence is about patterns, and his "dullest" is a judgement.
- **Tensions.** Shannon's own introduction lists a concern with "the nature of the final destination of the information" (p. 1), so he is not claiming the destination is irrelevant, only the meaning of the message. Gibson (p. 15) notes that taboo words and other social utility "are currently unclear" whether they fit efficiency, which is the meaning-side limit in practice.
- **Toy idea.** Two weather words with the same probability, one that everyone in the village understands and one that nobody does: the same surprisal.

### 12. A known distribution, a stable source, one-off events
- **Claim.** Shannon sets up the measure for events whose probabilities "are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur". A source that can produce only one particular message has zero entropy and needs no channel (his example is a machine computing the digits of π). Pierce: an ergodic source's probabilities must not change with time. MacKay: probabilities can also be degrees of belief (the Bayesian or subjective view), which lets one-off propositions have probabilities, but then they depend on the assumptions. Gibson: information-theoretic quantities are estimated from corpora and "it is difficult to get reliable estimates ... without very large datasets".
- **Page.** Shannon p. 10 (pdf 10), p. 18 (pdf 18); Pierce p. 81 (pdf 97); MacKay p. 26 (pdf 38); Cover p. 13 (pdf 35); Gibson p. 14 (pdf 14).
- **Quotes.** Shannon p. 10: "These probabilities are known but that is all we know concerning which event will occur." Shannon p. 18: "If a source can produce only one particular message its entropy is zero, and no channel is required." Pierce p. 81 (OCR layout mixed): "must not change with time if the source is to be ergodic". MacKay p. 26: "you cannot do inference without making assumptions." MacKay p. 26: "some books on probability restrict probabilities to refer only to frequencies of outcomes in repeatable random experiments". Gibson p. 14: "it is difficult to get reliable estimates of these information-theoretic quantities without very large datasets".
- **Numbers and equations.** None needed. Ours (to be run in notebook exercise 6): the plug-in entropy of a small sample of village days is biased low; the size of the bias for n = 10, 30, 100, 1000 is a computation of ours, not in the sources.
- **Traps.** (a) The unseen part of Shannon's one-message example is its consequence: "no channel is required" is about *transmission*, not about the message being unimportant. (b) MacKay's Bayesian reading is *his* position in the book, offered as one way; the sentence about frequencies is a description of "some books". Do not attribute "one-off events have no frequency" to him. (c) Pierce's ergodic condition is for the model of a source; real sources can drift.
- **Tensions.** Shannon's definition needs a known distribution; MacKay's Cox-axiom route builds the distribution from a person's beliefs, so the same event gets different surprisal for different people. This is our inference from MacKay p. 26 plus eq. 2.34, labelled ours.
- **Toy idea.** Two forecasters with different beliefs about tomorrow's weather: the same snow is worth different bits.

### 13. Entropy in physics: the same form, a different story
- **Claim.** Shannon notes that `H = -Σ p_i log p_i` has "the form of entropy as defined in certain formulations of statistical mechanics", where `p_i` is the probability of a system being in cell `i` of its phase space, and that it is the `H` in Boltzmann's H theorem. Pierce says the entropies are "quite different" ideas, each fully useful without the other, and that efforts to marry them have been "more interesting than fruitful". He advises clearing one's mind of physics first. Cover's notes say entropy came from thermodynamics and Boltzmann's `S = k ln W`, and that comparisons with physics have been attempted.
- **Page.** Shannon p. 11 (pdf 11), note 8 (Tolman 1938); Pierce pp. 23–24 (pdf 39–40) and p. 80 (pdf 96); Cover p. 49 (pdf 71).
- **Quotes.** Shannon p. 11: "The form of H will be recognized as that of entropy as defined in certain formulations of statistical mechanics". Pierce p. 80: "it is best first to clear our minds of any ideas associated with the entropy of physics". Pierce p. 24 (OCR text mixed in the sentence): "the efforts to marry communication theory and physics have been more interesting than fruitful".
- **Numbers and equations.** Boltzmann's `S = k ln W` (Cover p. 49; also on his gravestone, Cover). Pierce p. 22: an increase in entropy means a decrease in available energy; in statistical mechanics, "a decrease in order", which Pierce says we must "in some way equate ... with knowledge" (p. 22; OCR paraphrase, UNSEEN exact wording).
- **Traps.** Link type is "same-math in form", not "same thing". The post must not write that information is physical entropy. Pierce p. 24 gives the reason for confusion: "information" in popular use is associated with knowledge.
- **Tensions.** Shannon says "will be recognized"; Pierce says the confusion is "still a confused and confusing matter" (p. 24). They are about different things: a similarity in the formula vs a claim of identity.
- **Toy idea.** A box of 4 cells with all molecules in one cell (entropy 0) vs spread equally (2 bits): the same function as the village.

## E. Roles and the psycholinguistic claim

### 14. Who encodes and who decodes
- **Claim.** In Shannon's block diagram the transmitter operates on the message to produce a signal (in telegraphy, an encoding operation) and the receiver "ordinarily performs the inverse operation ... reconstructing the message". MacKay: the yes/no strategy defines a way of encoding the variable as a binary file. Shannon 1951: the person who has the text guesses letters and writes the reduced text (the guess numbers); an identical twin who has only the reduced text recovers the passage by making the same guesses, so the reduced text is an encoded form of the original.
- **Page.** Shannon 1948 pp. 1–2 (pdf 1–2), Fig. 1; MacKay p. 70 (pdf 82); Shannon 1951 pp. 55–56 (pdf 6–7); Gibson p. 3 (pdf 3), Fig. 1.
- **Quotes.** Shannon 1948 p. 2: "The receiver ordinarily performs the inverse operation of that done by the transmitter, reconstructing the message from the signal." Shannon 1951 p. 55: "the reduced text can be considered to be an encoded form of the original". Shannon 1951 p. 55: "The twin (who must be mathematically, not just biologically identical) will respond in the same way when faced with the same problem."
- **Numbers and equations.** The 1951 example: 129 letters, 89 guessed right at the first guess (69%) in the single-guess version (p. 55); 102 letters, 79 right at the first guess in the guess-until-right version (p. 56). Do not mix the two versions.
- **Traps.** This is Kunhao's recorded confusion (learner state, 2026-10-15): he took the twin for the compressor. The one who holds the original (the text, or the secret drink) **encodes**; the one who holds only the code and the shared procedure (the twin, the barista with the script) **decodes**. The "shared procedure" (the twin's identical mind, or the question script) is the codebook that both must hold. In the 1951 game the first person sees the text up to the current point, guesses the next letter and is told if wrong; those guess counts are the reduced text. Do not reuse the numbers 69% and 77% as evidence for "1 bit per letter" in this post; the 1951 paper itself hedges that figure.
- **Tensions.** In twenty questions the *asker* picks questions and the *answerer* holds the secret, so the secret-holder encodes through the answers. In Shannon's twin game the *guesser* who holds the text encodes through the guess counts. Both fit "whoever holds the original encodes". In MacKay's wording the questioning *strategy* is the encoder (p. 70), which can make it sound as if the asker encodes: add the sentence that the strategy is shared in advance.
- **Toy idea.** Café roles table: customer (knows the drink, encodes), nods (message), script (codebook), barista (decodes).

### 15. The Gibson et al. claim about surprisal and processing, with its hedges
- **Claim.** Gibson et al. (2019) is a feature review of "How Efficiency Shapes Human Language". It defines surprisal (glossary p. 3; Box 2, p. 4), uses it in two roles, and mentions processing effort only through other work. (i) Colour words: the difficulty for a listener to pick the correct colour given the word "is computed as the surprisal of the color, given the word", and the average colour score is "the optimal number of yes-no guesses it takes a listener to guess which chip the speaker meant" (p. 7). (ii) Word length: in a variety of languages a word's probability in context (3-gram) was a stronger determinant of its length than its frequency [32], and the authors write that this pattern "likely reflects lexicalization of processes that shorten predictable words" (p. 6). (iii) Processing: "Since language processing effort is associated with predictability [104,105]" (p. 11), with 104 = Hale 2001 (a probabilistic Earley parser as a psycholinguistic model) and 105 = Levy 2008 (expectation-based syntactic comprehension); p. 9 speaks of "models of online comprehension difficulty [104–106]". Reference 107 is Smith & Levy 2013, titled "The effect of word predictability on reading time is logarithmic" (p. 18, title only). The abstract's strongest statement is "a pervasive pressure for efficiency guides the forms of natural language" (p. 1).
- **Page.** Gibson p. 1 (pdf 1), p. 3, p. 4, p. 6, p. 7, p. 9, p. 11, p. 14, p. 15, p. 18.
- **Quotes.** Gibson p. 11: "Since language processing effort is associated with predictability [104,105]". Gibson p. 6: "This pattern likely reflects lexicalization of processes that shorten predictable words". Gibson p. 7: "Formally, this difficulty is computed as the surprisal of the color, given the word." Gibson p. 14: "Currently, there is no method of generating baseline utterances and languages that is general and satisfactory across all cases." Gibson p. 1 (abstract; two-column layout in the text copy, checked on the page): "These studies show how a pervasive pressure for efficiency guides the forms of natural language".
- **Numbers and equations.** `h(x) = log_k (1/p(x))`, bits for `k = 2` (Box 2, eq. I); entropy `H[X] = Σ p(x) log 1/p(x)` (II). No reading-time numbers are reported in Gibson; the "logarithmic" link between predictability and reading time is the title of Smith & Levy 2013 (UNSEEN).
- **Traps.** (a) Gibson et al. is a *review*; the claim "language processing effort is associated with predictability" is a one-sentence pointer to other studies, not a result of the review. The hedge is "is associated with", not "is caused by" or "is proportional to". (b) "Surprisal" in the colour example (how hard it is to pick the chip from the word) is not reading time. (c) Efficiency claims need a baseline: Gibson p. 14 says there is currently no general method for generating baselines. (d) Gibson p. 15 lists open limits (taboo words, population differences) that efficiency may or may not explain. (e) The strong abstract sentence is the authors' summary of the field; keep it next to the p. 14–15 limits. Never write "surprisal predicts reading time" on Gibson's authority alone.
- **Tensions.** Shannon p. 1 and Gibson p. 4 both set meaning aside, but Gibson then applies the measure to human comprehension, which is a claim about minds, not a theorem. Gibson's `H[X|C]` bounds and information-locality ideas are for later weeks.
- **Toy idea.** Notebook exercise 5 (word surprisal in a paragraph under a unigram model), labelled as "a model, not a mind".
