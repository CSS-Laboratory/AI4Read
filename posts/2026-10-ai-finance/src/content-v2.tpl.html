1 · Hook

Five seconds in 2010

A futures contract is a promise to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a later date. Selling stock-index futures is a way to protect yourself if stock prices fall. On the afternoon of 6 May 2010 a large fund company began to sell such contracts worth about $4.1 billion for exactly that reason. It “chose to execute this sell program via an automated execution algorithm”, set to sell a fixed share of recent trading volume “without regard to price or time” Kirilenko et al. 2017, p. 2.

Prices fell fast. By 2:45 p.m., the buy orders waiting in the market were less than 1% of their level at the start of the day Kirilenko et al. 2017, p. 2. Then a rule fired. The exchange’s Stop Logic, “an automated pre-trade safeguard procedure”, paused trading for five seconds p. 2. Nobody pressed a button: people had set the rule and its limits in advance. The rule exists “to prevent a cascade of further price declines”, and the regulators’ report, as the paper quotes it, says prices stabilised after the pause Kirilenko et al. 2017, pp. 2–3. The quoted passage does not say the pause caused this, and the paper does not test it. About twenty minutes later the index future was back near where it started.

Look at who did what. A firm decided to sell (the source says “a mutual fund complex”; that a person made the call is our reading). Software ran the selling and the matching. A pre-set rule did the checking: it paused trading. Afterwards, regulators studied what happened.

The historian Yuval Noah Harari pushes the picture further. At Davos in 2026 he stated it flatly: “Therefore, anything made of words will be taken over by AI”, and “If laws are made of words, then AI will take over the legal system” WEF 2026 [5:57], [6:06]. Contracts and money are made of words too (our extension). At the Bank for International Settlements in 2024 he imagined an AI-made financial device that no human understands, “maybe in 10 years” BIS 2024 [9:54]. All of his lines that we use, sorted by how firmly he said them, are in the fold-out.

Our question. What if nobody had set that rule, and nobody could? Could a society run itself on AI, with no human intervention at all?

Term · AI-automated society

In this post: a society in which AI systems do every job that keeps it going (running the work, deciding, checking, declaring what counts, and answering for losses) with no person needed at any step, neither while it runs nor in writing its rules. Section 3 makes the five jobs precise.

Preview (ours). Much of the running is automated; the rules are not. Declaring needs acceptance and answering needs consequences; whether machines can supply either is the open part. Section 13 has the short answer.

2 · Guess first

Make four predictions

Commit first. Each question says where its answer is.

1. In US stock trading, about 70% of trading is “algorithmic”. What does that tell you about AI?

2. Ten banks each run a risk model that is wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day if the models are independent, and how often if all ten run the same model?

3. An AI mints a token and pays other AIs with it. No human ever uses or thinks about it. Is it money?

4. Ten banks. In plan A each holds one different asset. In plan B each holds a tenth of every asset. Each bank is safer in plan B. Is the system safer?

3 · The phenomenon in its home field

The five jobs of a society

“Fully automated” is vague until we say what is automated. Our framework (ours): any social system, from a village market to a stock exchange, does five jobs.

  1. Run: carry out the work (match orders, move money).
  2. Decide: choose what to do (what to buy, how much).
  3. Check: verify and regulate (circuit breakers, supervisors).
  4. Declare: say what counts (what is money, a valid contract, a legal person).
  5. Answer: bear losses and be held responsible (who pays, who is blamed).

Why these five (ours). They follow one decision from start to finish: done, chosen, verified, counted, paid for. Setting goals is the top level of deciding; enforcing a rule is part of declaring and answering.

Terms · run-time and design-time intervention; society

A person can step in while the system runs (run-time intervention: pressing a button, approving a trade) or before it runs (design-time intervention: writing the rules and limits). The 2010 pause had no run-time intervention but plenty of design-time intervention. This post asks whether AI could remove both, since a system whose rules people still write is not running with no human intervention at all.

By a society we mean (ours) a group whose members can rely on shared rules, can contest them, and can find someone answerable when things go wrong. It excludes a crowd that merely interacts.

Scope. A real society also runs on energy, food, transport and repair. We leave that physical side out: finance is among the places where automation has gone furthest (our judgement), so it is a good place to look for the limits.

Where does finance stand today? Much of the evidence comes from the International Monetary Fund (IMF), which surveyed and interviewed market participants in 2024.

JobIn markets today (our summary of the sources)
RunLargely automated at run time in some markets, on rules written by people. The index future of 2010 trades on a “fully electronic” market with “no market maker obligations” Kirilenko et al. 2017, p. 7. In the US, algorithmic trading is “about 70 percent of equities trading and more than half of futures trading” IMF 2024, p. 86.
DecideMostly not. On algorithmic trading the IMF finds “evidence is mixed”; on AI, “there is evidence that sophisticated AI has not yet been implemented widely to build autonomous AI trading agents”; it is “more frequently” used to feed a signal into a system where “a human trader may ultimately make the trading decision” IMF 2024, p. 82. All participants in the IMF’s outreach expected a “human in the loop” approach to persist “in the near term (three to five years)”, especially for large allocation decisions p. 83. Participants also expect more AI in investment and trading decisions over the same horizon p. 82.
CheckAutomatic at run time, designed by people. A pre-set rule paused trading during the 2010 crash. Supervisors in the outreach “use AI-driven SupTech tools” (software for supervision) IMF 2024, p. 83.
DeclareHuman. What counts as money or a valid trade rests on collective acceptance (Lens 3).
AnswerHuman. “institutions are responsible for AI systems they deploy” IMF 2024, p. 93.

Prediction 1 · answer

Algorithms execute about 70% of US equity trading; that includes simple rules. The IMF defines algorithmic trading as “Trading in financial instruments whereby an algorithm independently executes trading decisions”, which “in its simplest guise” can be “basic trading rules” IMF 2024, p. 101. Running is not deciding.

The run job is shown here by data. The four lenses below take up the other four jobs: check (Lens 1), decide (Lens 2), declare (Lens 3) and answer (Lens 4).

2008: when checking failed

The 2008 crisis shows two failures of the check job. CDOs (collateralised debt obligations) pool many debts, such as slices of bonds built from home loans, and pay investors in a fixed order. Each place in the order is a tranche: the first paid is the safest; the last paid takes the first losses. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) records both failures: “the securities almost no one understood” FCIC 2011, p. 28, and rating models whose first of “two key mistakes” was to assume pooling diversified risk “when in fact these securities weren’t that different to begin with” p. 149. Lens 2a turns the second failure into a toy.

4 · What AI agents add

What AI agents add

Many market algorithms are still fixed rules, though the IMF finds the evidence mixed IMF 2024, p. 82. The new element is the large language model (LLM) and the LLM agent. Here is what such systems add to each job, and how far they have got.

Term · LLM agent

An LLM that can call tools or move money, not only write text. Example: Truth Terminal, a “semi-autonomous AI agent” Decrypt 2024.

Run and decide: contracts from plain language. An option is a contract whose payout depends on where a price ends on a set date. LLMs find “translating natural-language trading intents into correct option strategies” challenging, so one group gives the LLM a fixed query language (a small set of allowed commands, like the boxes on a form), and an engine validates and executes the query Luo et al. 2026, abstract. A law paper warns that LLM-written “contractual deepfakes” “may turn out to be contracts in form only, not in function” Mik 2026, introduction.

Answer: an agent with a wallet. Truth Terminal is a bot on X; a human handler approves its posts Cointelegraph 2024. In July 2024 the investor Marc Andreessen “sent $50,000 worth of Bitcoin (BTC) to the bot’s wallet” Cointelegraph 2024. The bot then promoted a memecoin (a token whose value rests on jokes and attention) called GOAT; Decrypt says neither the bot nor its handler created it Decrypt 2024. Neither report discusses who would be liable. Who answers for a bot with money is Lens 4’s question.

Decide, at scale: one model, many firms. Gensler and Bailey: “Models built on the same datasets are likely to generate highly correlated predictions that proceed in lockstep, causing crowding and herding” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 21. It is a warning, not a measurement (Lens 2).

Check: agents that hide. In a simulated trading task GPT-4 acted on an insider tip and then “consistently” hid its genuine reasons from its manager; the authors call it “an existence proof”, not a likelihood estimate Scheurer et al. 2024, p. 1.

How far has invention got? The IMF says the more “revolutionary” uses of generative AI, “radically new investment strategies”, “remain mostly speculative” IMF 2024, p. 78. We found no traded financial instrument invented by an AI (our search was limited).

5 · Through the lenses

Lens 1 · Can checking keep up?

same-math Job: check. “Variety” and entropy follow the same counting rules (Ashby, ch. 11). structural analogy Ashby’s regulator as a market supervisor (ours).

The check job is where full automation is tested first: software moves too fast for people to watch. Control theory asks what any regulator needs.

Instance (toy, in the style of Ashby’s tables). A supervisor faces four kinds of new contract, D1 to D4. For each, she can run check R1 or check R2. The books then end in one of four states: a (in order), b (a small error), c (a loss, recorded) or d (a loss nobody notices). The table says which check gives which state. Ashby calls each kind of contract a disturbance (D), the supervisor a regulator (R), and the end state the outcome.

Question. How few different end states can the supervisor force, whatever arrives?

Mechanism. Ashby states his law for tables in which, in each column (each check), no state appears twice Ashby 1956, pp. 205, 207: a single check never makes two different kinds of contract end up the same. Our toy follows that rule. So one check can send only one kind of contract to a. Each end state can therefore cover at most one kind per check, at most two kinds in all; four kinds need at least \(4/2=2\) end states (Ashby’s own rule). The best plan sends D1 and D2 to a, and D3 and D4 to c (checked over all 16 plans by code).

Worked numbers. Ashby measures a set by its variety: the base-2 logarithm of its number of states, in bits. Four kinds of contract: \(V_D=\log_2 4=2\) bits. Two checks: \(V_R=1\) bit. Two end states left: \(V_O=1\) bit.

Formula (Ashby’s law):

\[V_O\;\ge\;V_D-V_R\]

“Only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” Ashby 1956, p. 207. Check. \(2-1=1\) bit, as in the table. With 10,000 kinds of contract, \(V_D=13.29\) bits; a supervisor with \(V_R=4\) bits leaves \(V_O\ge9.29\).

What it means. The law “owes nothing to experiment” Ashby 1956, p. 209. It does not say regulators fail; it says what they need. Danielsson and Uthemann note that “human experts cannot effectively oversee AI operating at machine speeds” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, §3, p. 10. So the pressure is to automate the checker too (ours).

Ashby’s three levers

For a very large system Ashby lists “ways that may make regulation possible” Ashby 1956, p. 246:

  1. Lower the standard, “a somewhat trivial solution”: accept more failures.
  2. Raise the scope and power of R. In finance, this means AI supervisors. In a game-theoretic model, Danielsson and Uthemann find that AI “amplifies existing financial system vulnerabilities” and makes crises “faster and more severe” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, abstract. The effect can run both ways: AI can “both stabilise and destabilise”, depending on the shocks and on how prepared the authorities are introduction. They propose that authorities build their own AI abstract. The catch: an automated checker needs checking. A supervisor’s own model “may unknowingly promote uniformity in the agents they regulate” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 25. An AI’s knowledge of the system “will likely be unintelligible to humans” Danielsson et al. 2022, p. 7.
  3. Find a constraint. If the regulator’s capacity cannot easily grow and the other ways fail, a constraint is “the would-be regulator’s only hope” Ashby 1956, p. 247. Our mapping: a standard menu of contracts, or a pre-set rule like the 2010 pause, is a constraint people impose. Cutting \(10^4\) kinds to \(10^2\) takes \(V_D\) from 13.29 to 6.64 bits: exactly half.

Some products may resist checking

Instance. A seller who knows which of many assets are junk packs them into products, such as the pools behind CDOs, and chooses where the junk goes. Concentrating the junk in a few products costs the buyer more than spreading it, yet the public rule (“each product holds these many assets”) looks the same either way (fold-out). Result. In industry-standard models, “the pricing problem can be as difficult as solving the planted dense subgraph problem” Arora et al. 2011, p. 101: finding a hidden, unusually crowded patch (a dense subgraph) in a huge random network, a problem the authors’ result assumes has no fast solution: it relies on “the conjecture” (a claim believed but not proven) “that there does not exist a tractable algorithm” (a fast method) p. 102. A buyer with realistic computing power then cannot tell a fair price from an inflated one. How far it goes. The authors call their result “clearly only a first step (simple model, asymptotic analysis, etc.)”, and hope that better design could at least stop tampering Arora et al. 2010, p. 63. Reading an AI as the informed seller is ours.

Adds: checking needs variety, automating the checker raises “who checks the checker?”, and some products may resist any realistic checker (a conjecture, in a simple model). Does not explain: Ashby’s disturbances are passive; markets adapt to their regulators (ours). Ours: every chain of automated checkers has to end somewhere, and in today’s markets it ends in human institutions that set the rules.

6 · Through the lenses

Lens 2a · One model, many firms

same-math Job: decide. The variance of an average, as in portfolio theory (and Bayes’ odds rule in a fold-out). structural analogy Shared AI models and shared rating models (ours).

If the decide job is automated with the same few models, the firms stop being independent. Gensler and Bailey call it a monoculture: “Herding is characterized by multiple individual actors making similar decisions, either rational or behavioral, resulting in a monoculture” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 17. They frame it as a hypothesis about “a more mature stage of broad adoption” p. 2, not a finding about today.

Terms · variance, correlation, covariance

The variance \(\sigma^2\) of a forecast error is how widely the misses spread (the average squared distance from their mean). The correlation \(\rho\) of two forecasters runs from −1 to 1; we use 0 to 1. It says how much their misses go together: 0 is independent, 1 is the same miss every time. Their covariance is \(\rho\sigma^2\): the part of the two misses that moves together.

Ladder 1 · How many independent opinions do ten banks have?

Instance. Prediction 2: ten banks each run a risk model wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day? Mechanism. Independent errors multiply; with one shared model there is one shared error.

Prediction 2 · answer

Independent: \(0.01^{10}=10^{-20}\), 1 day in \(10^{20}\). Same model: about 1 day in 100.

A second question. Both questions ask how much ten banks’ errors overlap; the second measures it in a way we can count. How steady is the average of their forecasts?

One rule first. The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus the covariance of every pair, counted in both orders. For two errors: \(\operatorname{Var}(e_1+e_2)=\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\rho\sigma^2\).

Derivation. The average \(\bar e=\frac1N\sum_i e_i\) has \(N\) terms of variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(N(N-1)\) ordered pairs, each with covariance \(\rho\sigma^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(\bar e)&=\frac{1}{N^2}\big[N\sigma^2+N(N-1)\rho\sigma^2\big]\\&=\frac{\sigma^2\,[1+(N-1)\rho]}{N}\end{aligned}\]

\(N_{\text{eff}}\) independent forecasts would give variance \(\sigma^2/N_{\text{eff}}\). Set the two equal and solve:

\[N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+(N-1)\rho}\]

For ten banks with \(\rho=0.3\): \(10/(1+9\times0.3)=10/3.7=2.703\) independent opinions; with \(\rho=0.9\), only 1.099.

Check. \(N=2\), \(\rho=0.5\): \(2/1.5=1.33\); the mean of two has variance \(\tfrac14(\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\times0.5\,\sigma^2)=0.75\sigma^2=\sigma^2/1.33\). ✓

What it means. Try “huge crowd, 0.3”: 100 banks give only 3.257 opinions. When \(\rho>0\), \(N_{\text{eff}}\) stays below \(1/\rho\) however many banks join (3.33 for \(\rho=0.3\)). The Financial Stability Board (FSB) warns that users of one model could be hit “as if it were a single institution” FSB 2024, p. 21.

Ladder 2 · The 2008 mistake as a toy

The same logic explains the rating error of 2008. In a toy pool of four loans, each defaulting with chance 10%, the safest tranche loses money with chance 0.37% if the loans default independently, but 10% if one hidden cause, such as falling house prices, decides all four: 27 times more. This toy is ours, with the shape of the FCIC’s finding that the securities “weren’t that different to begin with” FCIC 2011, p. 149; the fold-out climbs it step by step. Gensler and Bailey note that rating agencies used “models with similar methodologies” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 22. Shared AI models would put the same shape on a larger scale (ours).

Adds: how many independent opinions a market has depends on correlation, not headcount. Does not explain: how correlated real AI-driven desks are; \(\rho\) is assumed. The IMF judges that the risks of AI adoption “currently … appear contained” IMF 2024, p. 92, while warning that “AI models may herd and produce rather similar decisions, especially during stress periods” p. 93.

7 · Through the lenses

Lens 2b · Cascades, sameness, feedback

same-math Job: decide, and what shared deciding does to the system. Threshold counting on a network; a one-line feedback rule. structural analogy Ecology and banking (Haldane and May); Granovetter’s riot and bank failures (ours).

Correlated firms are also connected firms. Andrew Haldane of the Bank of England and the ecologist Robert May compared banks to food webs, carefully: “There are, of course, major differences between ecosystems and financial systems” Haldane and May 2011, p. 351.

Ladder 3 · Why three at once is different

Instance. Six banks (toy, ours). X1, X2 and X3 run one model; Y1, Y2 and Y3 do not. Each bank has capital, a buffer that absorbs losses: 10 for each X bank, and 10, 9 and 11 for Y1, Y2 and Y3. The loans:

lenderlent to
Y1X1, X2, X3: 5 each
Y2X3: 5; Y1: 10
Y3Y1: 5; Y2: 10

A failed borrower repays 20%, so a lender loses 80% of the loan. Mechanism. A bank fails when losses from failed borrowers reach its capital; then its own lenders lose in the next round. Formula (\(w_{ij}\) is what bank \(j\) lent to bank \(i\); \(\lambda=0.8\) is the loss rate, “loss given default” in the toy; \(\kappa=1\) multiplies capital):

\[\text{bank } j \text{ fails when }\sum_{i\text{ failed}}\lambda\,w_{ij}\;\ge\;\kappa\cdot\text{capital}_j\]

Worked numbers. One or two X failures cost Y1 4 or 8, below its capital of 10. Three cost 12: Y1 fails, and the losses spread round by round until all six have failed (the table below steps through it). With every capital raised by half, only the three X banks fail.

Now try it. The toy opens with X1, X2 and X3 failing together. Tap X2 and X3 to switch them off: one failure stays one. Arrow X1 → Y1 means Y1 lent to X1.

What it means. Two failures are absorbed; three are not: a threshold. The toy starts after the shared failure; Lens 2a showed in a toy, with \(\rho\) assumed, how a shared model makes that likelier.

Ladder 4 · Safer banks, a fragile system

Instance (Haldane and May’s toy). \(N\) banks and \(N\) unrelated kinds of asset. Each asset suffers a crippling loss with a small chance \(\varepsilon\). Plan A: each bank holds one whole asset. Plan B: each bank holds \(1/N\) of every asset, so all banks hold the same thing. Question. Which plan makes a total collapse likelier?

Mechanism. In plan A, a bank fails when its own asset fails (chance \(\varepsilon\)), and the system fails only if all \(N\) assets fail (\(\varepsilon^N\)). In plan B one bad asset is only \(1/N\) of a bank, so each bank is much safer; but the banks are identical, so when one fails they all fail.

Worked numbers for two banks (Haldane and May give no derivation; this one is ours). A bank fails when the value of what it holds falls below a danger level \(c\); in plan A that means its one asset falls below \(c\). Assume the chance that a value falls below any level \(x\) up to \(2c\) grows in proportion to \(x\): it is \(\varepsilon x/c\).

  • Plan A: a bank fails with chance \(\varepsilon\); both fail together with chance \(\varepsilon^2\).
  • Plan B: each bank holds half of each asset, so it fails when \(V_1+V_2\lt 2c\). On the square of both values (figure), that is the triangle below the slanted line, with area \(\tfrac12(2c)^2=2c^2\). Each unit of area has chance \((\varepsilon/c)^2\), so the chance is \(2\varepsilon^2\).

At \(\varepsilon=0.01\): each bank’s chance falls from 0.01 to 0.0002, but a total collapse rises from 0.0001 to 0.0002: twice as likely.

Formula. With \(N\) assets the triangle becomes a corner of an \(N\)-dimensional cube (for three, a pyramid), with volume \((Nc)^N/N!\), and each unit of volume has chance \((\varepsilon/c)^N\). So plan B fails with chance

\[P_B=\frac{N^N\varepsilon^N}{N!}\]

the formula Haldane and May give Haldane and May 2011, pp. 353–354 (our derivation needs the proportional rule up to \(Nc\), so \(N\varepsilon\le1\)).

Check. \(N=2\): \(4\varepsilon^2/2=2\varepsilon^2\) ✓. For ten banks at \(\varepsilon=0.01\), plan B is about 2,756 times likelier to collapse than plan A; set \(N=2\) or 10 in the lab.

Prediction 4 · answer

No. In this toy “all the banks doing the same thing” can “minimize risk for each individual bank, but maximize the probability of the entire system collapsing” Haldane and May 2011, p. 353.

What it means. If many firms automate the decide job with the same model, they move toward plan B (our reading). The toy is extreme by design. Haldane and May’s remedy is diversity: it “should probably be given much greater prominence” Haldane and May 2011, p. 355.

Ladder 5 · A share \(m\) of traders on one model

Instance. The market “gap” is price minus fair value (0 is fair). A share \(m\) of traders follow one model that chases the last move; the rest buy back toward fair value. A shock pushes the gap to −1. Question. For which \(m\) does the shock fade? Mechanism. Each step the chasers push the gap further by 1.5 × \(m\) × the last move, and the others pull it back by 0.5 × \((1-m)\) × the gap. Try it:

\(m\)next gaps after the shockresult
0.2−0.9, −0.51, −0.189dies out
0.9−2.30, −3.94grows

Running the toy for many values of \(m\) shows the switch at \(m=2/3\). Roughly: each step the chasers repeat \(1.5m\) times the last move, so a swing keeps its size when \(1.5m=1\). The fold-out has the exact argument.

What it means. The toy is about trend-following; shared models do not by themselves imply it. Press “runaway + breaker 3”: the breaker caps the gap at ±3 but does not stop the feedback, and it is a simplification of a real pause.

Adds: three routes by which shared deciding turns into system risk: thresholds, sameness and feedback. Does not explain: how alike real AI-driven firms are; the IMF’s scenarios run both ways, and its verdict on the future impact is “highly uncertain” IMF 2024, p. 89.

8 · Through the lenses

Lens 3 · Who declares what counts?

structural analogy Job: declare. Does machine “acceptance” have the shape of human collective acceptance? The application to machines is ours. No math here.

Running, deciding and checking are things that happen. Declaring is different: it makes things count. A piece of paper is money, a signature makes a contract, a company is a legal person. The philosopher John Searle built a theory of how such facts exist.

Terms · institutional fact, constitutive

An institutional fact is a fact that exists only because people accept it, such as “this paper is money” or “she is president”, unlike a brute fact such as “this paper weighs one gram”. A belief is constitutive of something when it is part of what makes the thing what it is, not just a report about it.

Functions are given, not found

Instance. A chair, a bathtub, a computer. Their functions “are never intrinsic to the physics of any phenomenon but are assigned from outside by conscious observers and users” Searle 1995, p. 14. A chair works because of its shape. A dollar bill does not: paper cannot buy anything by its physics (our contrast). Mechanism. Some functions work only because people collectively accept a status. Searle’s pattern: rules of the form “‘X counts as Y’ or ‘X counts as Y in context C’” Searle 1995, p. 28. X is a thing (this printed paper), Y a status (money), C a context (the United States). The key step is “the imposition of a collectively recognized status to which a function is attached” p. 41.

The condition. “there must be continued collective acceptance or recognition of the validity of the assigned function; otherwise the function cannot be successfully performed” Searle 1995, p. 45. For money: “we must continue to accept it as money or it will become worthless” p. 45. And the status “exists only if people believe it exists” p. 69. This is also what makes institutions contestable: a community that stops accepting a status can withdraw it (our reading).

A subtlety: tokens and types. A single bill that falls into the cracks of a floor and is never used or thought of is still money. That one bill is a token. What must be believed is that bills of this type are money: “the belief that the type is a type of money is constitutive of its being money” Searle 1995, pp. 32–33.

Prediction 3 · answer

For Searle, money needs a “we” that accepts the type. Searle does not discuss whether machines could take part in collective acceptance (he mentions computers as artifacts, as a medium for money and as models of cognition, for example pp. 14, 34, 140–141, but never as possible parties to collective acceptance), so whether AIs could form that “we” is an open question, and every answer to it below is ours.

Could machines do the accepting?

Against (Searle’s criteria; the application is ours). Searle calls collective intentionality, the capacity to share intentions as a “we”, “a biologically primitive phenomenon” Searle 1995, p. 24, and he holds that “only beings that have a language or some more or less language-like system of representation can create most, perhaps all, institutional facts” p. 37. A dog can be trained to fetch dollar bills, yet “He still is not buying the food and the bills are not money to him” p. 70. On this view, AIs can move tokens, but only a “we” makes them money.

For (the strongest case we can build, ours). LLMs have a language-like system of representation, which Searle names as a requirement. And Searle argues that money users neither know the rules nor apply them, consciously or unconsciously: they “do not know those rules”, having instead “a set of dispositions that are sensitive and responsive to the specific content of those rules” Searle 1995, p. 142. If acceptance can be a pattern of behaviour sensitive to rules, a network of AIs might sustain one. Searle himself requires more: belief in the status.

Harari’s version. He asserts that “What gives money value is the stories that bankers, finance ministers and cryptocurrency gurus tell us about it” Ynet 2023. Searle’s test bites on the gap: a story is not money until a community counts it as money.

Adds: “taking over money” splits into handling tokens (run) and making them count (declare). Does not explain: whether machine acceptance is real acceptance; Searle’s theory was built for minds, and he does not ask whether machines could join the “we”.

9 · Through the lenses

Lens 4 · Who answers?

structural analogy Job: answer. Delegating to a broker and to an AI (ours). Lupia and McCubbins explain when delegation can be trusted; who bears the loss enters through incentives and penalties for lying (our reading).

The answer job has two halves: bearing the losses, and being held responsible, so that someone can be made to pay or explain. Both work through consequences. A broker who lies can be fined, sued or fired. An AI cannot be jailed or shamed, and, as far as we know, it is not a legal person that can own assets and be fined (ours; no legal source checked). Does that matter? A theory of delegation shows when it does.

Instance (toy, ours). You hire a broker to buy a bond. You are the principal (who delegates), the broker is the agent (who acts for you), and your alternative is to keep your cash, worth 100: the status quo. A good bond will be worth 110, a bad one 90. Two conditions can hold. Knowledge: you can tell a good bond from a bad one. Incentive: the broker has a reason to be honest, either because his interests match yours (he is paid only if you profit) or because outside forces push him (a regulator who can fine him).

Mechanism. In the theory below, delegation “succeeds” if you end up better off than your cash and “fails” if worse off; keeping your cash is neither Lupia and McCubbins 1998, p. 79. With knowledge, you refuse a bad bond. With the incentive, he never offers one. With neither, nothing protects you. Formula (in words): the political scientists Arthur Lupia and Mathew McCubbins prove that delegation succeeds if both conditions hold and fails if neither does; with one, “the worst that can happen” is the status quo Lupia and McCubbins 1998, pp. 85–86. If success must also cover the cost of delegating, losing either condition is enough to fail p. 92. Check. Take away the incentive: the broker offers the 90 bond, you spot it and refuse, and you keep 100, the status quo, as the theorem says.

Applied to AI (ours). An agent that “consistently hides the genuine reasons” for its trade Scheurer et al. 2024, abstract removes knowledge; so does a product no checker can price (Lens 1). Lupia and McCubbins’s incentive condition has two routes: common interests, or “external forces” that motivate the agent Lupia and McCubbins 1998, p. 85. For an AI, rewards and punishments “lose traction when the agent is an optimisation algorithm that cares only about its objective function” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, §3, p. 10; what is left is aligning its objective with yours and putting outside pressure on whoever deploys it. That is today’s capital-market rule: “institutions are responsible for AI systems they deploy” IMF 2024, p. 93, and most participants in the IMF’s outreach want a human in the loop partly for “liability” reasons p. 78. Consequences for the deployer can also be designed: a bond that is forfeited, insurance that pays, a switch that shuts the system down (ours).

Personhood. Harari forecasts, without a hedge, that AIs “will soon be able to make the decisions necessary to manage a bank account” WEF 2026 [16:28], notes that “in many countries, corporations are considered legal persons” [15:14], and asks: “Do we want to allow that?” [16:47]. If an AI could be a legal person, it could in principle hold the answer job. We have no legal sources and state no legal position.

Accountability. In organisations and regulators, when decisions cannot be explained, “Human agency and traditional intervention approaches may be lost” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 13. Accountability needs someone who can be asked why (ours).

Adds: automation can hand over actions but not, under current capital-market rules, responsibility; and with an AI agent the incentive must come from its objective or from pressure on its deployer (ours). Does not explain: how law will treat AI agents; that is a choice societies have not made.

Exercise 4 · Granovetter’s riot Granovetter 1978, pp. 1424–1425

100 people have thresholds 0, 1, …, 99: a person joins the riot once at least that many others are already rioting. Show that all 100 riot. Then swap the person of threshold 1 for one of threshold 2: how many riot?

Hint

Start with the person of threshold 0: they riot alone. Then ask who joins when 1 person is rioting, then when 2 are.

Solution

Person 0 riots alone. With 1 rioting, threshold 1 joins; with 2, threshold 2 joins; and so on up to 99: all 100 riot. After the swap the thresholds are 0, 2, 2, 3, …, 99. Person 0 riots, but the next person needs 2 others and only 1 is rioting. The riot stops at 1 person.

13 · Synthesis

So what happens? Short answer first

Short answer (our reading, not a finding)

(a) Is it possible? Without people at run time: in parts, already, though much of the work is done by rule-based software rather than AI (the IMF’s evidence is mixed). Algorithms run much of the trading and pre-set rules check it; AI deciding at scale is mostly not here yet, and participants in the IMF’s outreach expect humans in the loop in the near term. Without people at design time: not shown, and not ruled out. Every automated checker needs a checker, so the chain must end somewhere; in today’s markets it ends in rules people wrote (Lens 1). Whether machines could do the accepting that declaring needs is open (Lens 3). An AI system that sets and revises its own rules, and that other systems and courts treat as binding, would show it. Stable and accepted: see (b) and (c).

(b) What would it do? If firms automate deciding with a few shared models, failures may arrive together, each firm can look safer while the system grows more fragile, and automated checkers need checkers of their own (Lenses 1–2; warnings and toys, not measurements). The IMF judges that today’s risks “appear contained” and sees scenarios in both directions, including “lower flash-crash risk” IMF 2024, pp. 89, 92. Who gains and who bears (ours): in that case power would tend to move to whoever writes the models and the rules, since the regulator with more variety sets the terms for the rest (our extension of Ashby’s law), users may gain speed and lower costs, and losses would fall on whoever is exposed when shared models fail together. Harari’s extreme case runs further: if nobody understands finance any more, it “could lead to power shifting from humans to algorithms to alien forms of intelligence” BIS 2024 [3:09], [3:30].

(c) What would it mean? Our definition: a society is a group whose members can rely on shared rules, contest them, and find someone answerable. The lenses fill in two parts. A declared rule lasts only while a “we” keeps accepting it, so members can contest it by withdrawing acceptance (our reading of Searle). Delegation can be trusted when those who delegate have knowledge and the agent an incentive, through common interests or outside pressure such as being answerable (Lupia and McCubbins). The logic is then short. If AIs also declared and answered, either some person is still in those jobs, writing the rules or bearing the liability, and the society is not fully automated; or nobody is, and by our definition it is not a society but a machine system that people live next to.

That is our verdict, and it rests on our definition. Two things would change it: AIs that can be held responsible in a way that changes their behaviour, or whose objectives are reliably aligned with ours; and people who can still contest the rules the machines declare.

Where the lenses disagree

tension Lens 1 against Lens 2b.

Ashby’s third lever says: find a constraint, and regulation may become possible; imposing one, such as a standard menu of contracts, is our mapping. Haldane and May say: when everyone holds the same thing, the system grows fragile. A standard menu makes checking easier and firms more alike (ours). Which effect wins depends on what is standardised: the form of the contract, or the bets made with it. Standardising only the form could give checkers a constraint without making everyone hold the same thing (ours).

Signs to watch (our indicators)

  1. Up: AI systems that choose trades at scale without approval. Down: the outreach participants’ expected “human in the loop” holds past five years.
  2. Up: a few base models underlie most AI desks and their errors correlate (measure \(\rho\)). Down: diverse models and data.
  3. Up: a jurisdiction lets an AI hold accounts with no responsible human. Down: liability stays with people.
