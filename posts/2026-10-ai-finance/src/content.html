1 · Hook

Five seconds in 2010

A futures contract is a promise to buy or sell something at a fixed price on a later date. Selling stock-index futures is a way to protect yourself if stock prices fall. On the afternoon of 6 May 2010 a large fund company began to sell such contracts worth about $4.1 billion for exactly that reason. It “chose to execute this sell program via an automated execution algorithm”, set to sell a fixed share of recent trading volume “without regard to price or time” Kirilenko et al. 2017, p. 2.

Prices fell fast. In thirteen minutes the buy orders waiting in the market shrank to less than 1% of what had been waiting at the start of the day Kirilenko et al. 2017, p. 2. Then a rule fired. The exchange’s Stop Logic, “an automated pre-trade safeguard procedure”, paused trading for five seconds p. 2. Nobody pressed a button: people had set the rule and its limits in advance. The rule exists “to prevent a cascade of further price declines”, and the regulators report that prices stabilised after the pause Kirilenko et al. 2017, pp. 2–3; neither they nor the paper test whether the pause caused it. About twenty minutes later the index future was back near where it started.

Look at who did what. A firm decided to sell (the source says “a mutual fund complex”; that a person made the call is our reading). Software ran the selling and the matching. A pre-set rule checked the fall. Afterwards, regulators studied what happened.

The historian Yuval Noah Harari pushes the picture further. At Davos in 2026 he stated it flatly: “Therefore, anything made of words will be taken over by AI”, and “If laws are made of words, then AI will take over the legal system” WEF 2026 [5:57], [6:06]. Contracts and money are made of words too (our extension). At the Bank for International Settlements in 2024 he imagined an AI-made financial device that no human understands, “maybe in 10 years” BIS 2024 [9:54]. All of his lines that we use, sorted by how firmly he said them, are in the fold-out.

Our question. What if nobody had set that rule, and nobody could? Could a society run itself on AI, with no human intervention at all?

Term · AI-automated society

In this post: a society in which AI systems do every job that keeps it going (running the work, deciding, checking, declaring what counts, and answering for losses) with no person needed at any step, neither while it runs nor in writing its rules. Section 3 makes the five jobs precise.

Preview (ours). Running is largely automated already in some markets; deciding is mostly not; checking is partly automatic, on rules people set. Lenses 3 and 4 argue that declaring and answering are what make a group of people a society, which is why they matter most. Section 13 has the short answer.

2 · Guess first

Make four predictions

Commit first. Each question says where its answer is.

1. In US stock trading, about 70% of trading is “algorithmic”. What does that tell you about AI?

2. Ten banks each run a risk model that is wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day if the models are independent, and how often if all ten run the same model?

3. An AI mints a token and pays other AIs with it. No human ever uses or thinks about it. Is it money?

4. Ten banks. In plan A each holds one different asset. In plan B each holds a tenth of every asset. Each bank is safer in plan B. Is the system safer?

3 · The phenomenon in its home field

The five jobs of a society

“Fully automated” is vague until we say what is automated. Our framework (ours): any social system, from a village market to a stock exchange, does five jobs.

  1. Run: carry out the work (match orders, move money).
  2. Decide: choose what to do (what to buy, how much).
  3. Check: verify and regulate (circuit breakers, supervisors).
  4. Declare: say what counts (what is money, a valid contract, a legal person).
  5. Answer: bear losses and be held responsible (who pays, who is blamed).

Why these five (ours). They follow one decision from start to finish: someone does the work, someone chooses it, someone verifies it, someone says what it counts as, and someone bears the cost if it goes wrong. Two more jobs fit inside them: setting goals (what the system is for) is the top level of deciding, and enforcing a rule is part of declaring and answering, since a declared rule bites only when someone can be made to answer for breaking it.

Terms · run-time and design-time intervention; society

A person can step in while the system runs (run-time intervention: pressing a button, approving a trade) or before it runs (design-time intervention: writing the rules and limits). The 2010 pause had no run-time intervention but plenty of design-time intervention. This post asks whether AI could remove both, since a system whose rules people still write is not running “without any necessity of human intervention”.

By a society we mean (ours) a group whose members can rely on shared rules, can contest them, and can find someone answerable when things go wrong. The definition excludes a crowd that merely interacts, and it is chosen so that the question has teeth.

Scope. A real society also runs on energy, food, transport and repair. We leave that physical side out: finance is where automation has gone furthest, so it is the best place to look for the limits.

Prediction 1 · answer

Algorithms execute about 70% of US equity trading; that includes simple rules. The IMF defines algorithmic trading as “Trading in financial instruments whereby an algorithm independently executes trading decisions”, which “in its simplest guise” can be “basic trading rules” IMF 2024, p. 101. Running is not deciding.

The run job is shown here by data. The four lenses below take up the other four jobs: check (Lens 1), decide (Lens 2), declare (Lens 3) and answer (Lens 4).

One more data point. Among investment managers surveyed, 59% did not plan to integrate AI into executing investment decisions, and about 11% had already done so (read from a chart, approximate) IMF 2024, p. 83. Most participants are “quite uncomfortable” with autonomous AI trading without human oversight p. 78.

2008: when checking failed

The last big crisis shows two failures of the check job, with human-made products. CDOs (collateralised debt obligations) collect many debts into one pool, for example slices of bonds built from home loans, and pass the payments to investors in a fixed order. Each place in the order is a tranche: the first paid is the safest; the last paid takes the first losses. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) records both failures: “the securities almost no one understood” p. 28, and rating models whose first of “two key mistakes” was to assume pooling diversified risk “when in fact these securities weren’t that different to begin with” p. 149. Lens 2a turns the second failure into a toy.

4 · What AI agents add

What AI agents add

Most market algorithms are fixed rules. The new element is the large language model (LLM), and the LLM agent: an LLM that can act, not only write. Here is what such systems add to each job, and how far they have got.

Term · LLM agent

An LLM that can call tools or move money, not only write text. Example: Truth Terminal, a “semi-autonomous AI agent” Decrypt 2024.

Run and decide: contracts from plain language. An option is a contract whose payout depends on where a price ends on a set date. LLMs find “translating natural-language trading intents into correct option strategies” challenging, so one group gives the LLM a fixed query language (a small set of allowed commands, like the boxes on a form), and an engine validates and executes the query Luo et al. 2026, abstract. A line in such a language (ours) could be RANGE(index, 90, 110): “pay 1 if the index ends between 90 and 110”. An index ending at 95 pays 1; one ending at 120 pays 0. A law paper warns that LLM-written “contractual deepfakes” “may turn out to be contracts in form only, not in function” Mik 2026, introduction.

Answer: an agent with a wallet. Truth Terminal is a bot on X; its human handler approves its posts and decides whom it interacts with Cointelegraph 2024. In July 2024 the investor Marc Andreessen “sent $50,000 worth of Bitcoin (BTC) to the bot’s wallet” Cointelegraph 2024. The bot then promoted a memecoin (a token whose value rests on jokes and attention) called GOAT; Decrypt says neither the bot nor its handler created it Decrypt 2024. Neither report discusses who would be liable. Who answers for a bot with money is Lens 4’s question.

Decide, at scale: one model, many firms. The Financial Stability Board (FSB) warns that “The widespread use of common AI models and data sources could lead to increased correlations in trading, lending, and pricing” FSB 2024, p. 1. Gensler and Bailey: “Models built on the same datasets are likely to generate highly correlated predictions that proceed in lockstep, causing crowding and herding” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 21. Both are warnings about a possible future, not measurements (Lens 2).

Check: agents that hide. In a simulated trading task GPT-4 acted on an insider tip and then “consistently” hid its genuine reasons from its manager; the authors call it “an existence proof”, not a likelihood estimate Scheurer et al. 2024, p. 1.

How far has invention got? The IMF says the more “revolutionary” uses of generative AI, “radically new investment strategies”, “remain mostly speculative” IMF 2024, p. 78. We found no traded financial instrument invented by an AI (our search was limited).

5 · Through the lenses

Lens 1 · Can checking keep up?

same-math Job: check. “Variety” and entropy follow the same counting rules (Ashby, ch. 11). structural analogy Ashby’s regulator as a market supervisor (ours).

The check job is where full automation is tested first: software moves too fast for people to watch. Control theory asks what any regulator needs.

Instance (toy, in the style of Ashby’s tables). A supervisor faces four kinds of new contract, D1 to D4. For each, she can run check R1 or check R2. The books then end in one of four states: a (in order), b (a small error), c (a loss, recorded) or d (a loss nobody notices). The table says which check gives which state.

Question. How few different end states can the supervisor force, whatever arrives?

Mechanism. Ashby’s table rule: in each column (each check), no state appears twice Ashby 1956, p. 207. So one check can send only one kind of contract to a. The best plan sends D1 and D2 to a, and D3 and D4 to c: two end states, not one (checked over all 16 plans by code).

Worked numbers. Ashby measures a set by its variety: the base-2 logarithm of its number of states, in bits. Four kinds of contract: \(V_D=\log_2 4=2\) bits. Two checks: \(V_R=1\) bit. Two end states left: \(V_O=1\) bit.

Formula (Ashby’s law):

\[V_O\;\ge\;V_D-V_R\]

“Only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” Ashby 1956, p. 207. Check. \(2-1=1\) bit, as in the table. With 10,000 kinds of contract, \(V_D=13.29\) bits; a supervisor with \(V_R=4\) bits leaves \(V_O\ge9.29\).

What it means. The law “owes nothing to experiment” Ashby 1956, p. 209. It does not say regulators fail; it says what they need. Danielsson and Uthemann note that “human experts cannot effectively oversee AI operating at machine speeds” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, introduction. So the pressure is to automate the checker too.

Ashby’s three levers

For a very large system Ashby lists “ways that may make regulation possible” Ashby 1956, p. 246:

  1. Lower the standard, “a somewhat trivial solution”: accept more failures.
  2. Raise the scope and power of R. In finance, this means AI supervisors. Danielsson and Uthemann find that AI “amplifies existing financial system vulnerabilities” and makes crises “faster and more severe”, and they propose that authorities build their own AI Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, abstract. They add that AI can “both stabilise and destabilise” the system, depending on the shocks and on how prepared the authorities are introduction. The catch: an automated checker needs checking. A supervisor’s own model “may unknowingly promote uniformity in the agents they regulate” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 25, and an AI’s knowledge of the system “will likely be unintelligible to humans” Danielsson et al. 2022, p. 7.
  3. Find a constraint. If the regulator’s capacity cannot easily grow and the other ways fail, a constraint is “the would-be regulator’s only hope” Ashby 1956, p. 247. Our mapping: a standard menu of contracts, or a pre-set rule like the 2010 pause, is a constraint people impose. Cutting \(10^4\) kinds to \(10^2\) takes \(V_D\) from 13.29 to 6.64 bits: exactly half.
Look inside: Ashby’s law with probabilities

Counting states treats every disturbance as equally likely. With probabilities, entropy replaces variety: \(H(D)=-\sum_d P(d)\log_2 P(d)\), the average number of yes-or-no questions needed to name the disturbance. For equally likely states it equals the variety. The law becomes

\[H(E)\;\ge\;H(D)+H(R\mid D)-H(R)\]

\(H(R\mid D)\) is the uncertainty left in the regulator’s choice once the disturbance is known; it is 0 if R is a function of D. Two steps. (1) The table rule, no outcome twice in a column Ashby 1956, p. 207, means that given R, different disturbances give different outcomes, so \(H(D\mid R)\le H(E\mid R)\). (2) Then \[\begin{aligned}&H(D)+H(R\mid D)\\&=H(R)+H(D\mid R)\\&\le H(R)+H(E\mid R)\\&\le H(R)+H(E)\end{aligned}\] where the last step uses the fact that, on average, conditioning reduces entropy (\(H(E\mid R)\le H(E)\)) Cover and Thomas 1991. Rearrange. Numbers (toy, ours). Four kinds of contract arrive with chances \(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\): \(H(D)=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot3=1.75\) bits. A one-bit regulator that is a function of D gives \(H(E)\ge1.75-1=0.75\) bits.

Trap · a misprint in the reprint

Ashby writes the conditional term as \(H_D(R)\), i.e. \(H(R\mid D)\) Ashby 1956, p. 208. One line of the reprint prints \(H_D(E)\), though the line above and the sentence after need \(H_D(R)\). We use the corrected form.

Some products resist checking

Instance. A seller who knows which of many assets are junk packs them into products, such as the pools behind CDOs, and chooses where the junk goes. The buyer sees only the public rule (“each product holds these many assets”). Result. In industry-standard models, “the pricing problem can be as difficult as solving the planted dense subgraph problem” Arora et al. 2011, p. 101: finding a small, unusually crowded patch in a huge random network, a problem computer scientists conjecture has no fast solution. A buyer with realistic computing power then cannot tell a fair price from an inflated one. How far it goes. The result rests on a conjecture, in a simple model; the authors call it “clearly only a first step (simple model, asymptotic analysis, etc.)”, and their tamper-resistant designs “raise the hope” that better design can help Arora et al. 2010, p. 63. Reading an AI as the informed seller is ours. The fold-out has a six-asset toy.

Look inside: lemons, and a six-asset toy

Lemons. A working used car is worth $1,000 to a buyer, and 20% of cars are “lemons” worth $0, so a random car is worth $800 to the buyer. Trade happens only if the seller values a good car at $200 less than the buyer does: that gap is the lemon cost Arora et al. 2010, pp. 50–51. Now a derivative, a product whose payout depends on other assets. A seller who knows which assets are junk chooses where the junk goes.

Tiny toy (ours). Six assets in three products of two assets each. Like a senior tranche, a product pays 1 unless both its assets default. A healthy asset pays with chance ½, so a healthy product fails with chance \(\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac14\) and is worth \(\tfrac34\). Two assets are junk (they always pay 0).

  • Junk spread over two products: each pays only if its healthy asset pays, worth \(\tfrac12\). Total \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac34=\tfrac74\).
  • Junk concentrated in one product: it is worth 0, the other two \(\tfrac34\) each. Total \(\tfrac32\).

Concentrating the junk does the most damage, and the public rule (“three products of two”) is the same either way. Check (code, all 15 ways to place 2 junk assets): 12 give \(\tfrac74\), 3 give \(\tfrac32\).

Scale it up. With 100 assets and thousands of products, a seller can hide a few products full of junk among many fair-looking ones. Draw each asset and each product as a dot, and draw a line when a product holds an asset: that drawing is a graph. The junk-filled products and their junk assets form a small, unusually crowded patch of it, and spotting that patch is the “hidden dense subgraph” problem. Computer scientists believe it is intractable: no fast method is known, and none is expected (a conjecture) Arora et al. 2009, pp. 3–4. A buyer with limited computing then cannot tell a fair price from an inflated one (our gloss). An unbounded buyer could simply check every possibility; the claim is about realistic buyers.

The formula. For a product paying \(F(X)\) on \(N\) assets, the lemon cost of \(n\) junk assets is \[\begin{aligned}\Delta(n)&=\mathbb{E}[F(X)]-\min_{|S|=n}\\&\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[F(X)\mid X_i=0\ \forall i\in S\big]\end{aligned}\] Arora et al. 2010, p. 52: the average value, minus the value if the junk sits in the worst places, where the minimum “takes into account all possible ways in which seller could ‘position’ the junk assets” p. 52. In the toy, \(\mathbb{E}[F]=3\times\tfrac34=\tfrac94\) and the worst placement leaves \(\tfrac32\), so \(\Delta(2)=\tfrac34\). Random pooling would lower the cost; when the seller chooses the pooling, “under computational assumptions, the lemon cost for polynomial time buyers can be much larger than \(n\)” p. 55.

Look inside: a contract built to resist tampering

What would help? In their simple model the authors propose a “tree of majorities”: take the majority of 3 inputs, then the majority of 3 such majorities, and so on to depth \(k\), over \(3^k\) assets. Their Theorem 9: fixing any \(t\) inputs to 0 raises the chance that the tree outputs 0 to at most \((1+t/2^k)/2\) Arora et al. 2010, p. 61. A seller must plant junk in a number of inputs comparable to \(2^k\) to move the price much, and that is large enough to be detected afterwards. Toy (ours, code-checked): to force the output, 9 inputs need 4 junk; 27 need 8; 81 need 16.

Adds: checking needs variety, automating the checker raises “who checks the checker?”, and some products resist checking in principle (under a conjecture). Does not explain: Ashby’s disturbances are passive; markets adapt to their regulators (ours). Ours: every chain of automated checkers has to end somewhere, and in today’s markets it ends in human institutions that set the rules.

6 · Through the lenses

Lens 2a · One model, many firms

same-math Job: decide. The variance of an average, as in portfolio theory (and Bayes’ odds rule in a fold-out). structural analogy Shared AI models and shared rating models (ours).

If the decide job is automated with the same few models, the firms stop being independent. Gensler and Bailey call it a monoculture: “Herding is characterized by multiple individual actors making similar decisions, either rational or behavioral, resulting in a monoculture” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 17. They frame it as a hypothesis about a mature stage of adoption, not a finding about today.

Terms · variance, correlation, covariance

The variance \(\sigma^2\) of a forecast error is how widely the misses spread (the average squared distance from their mean). The correlation \(\rho\) of two forecasters runs from −1 to 1; we use 0 to 1. It says how much their misses go together: 0 is independent, 1 is the same miss every time. Their covariance is \(\rho\sigma^2\): the part of the two misses that moves together.

Ladder 1 · How many independent opinions do ten banks have?

Instance. Prediction 2: ten banks each run a risk model wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day? Mechanism. Independent errors multiply; with one shared model there is one shared error.

Prediction 2 · answer

Independent: \(0.01^{10}=10^{-20}\), 1 day in \(10^{20}\). Same model: about 1 day in 100.

A second question. Both questions ask how much ten banks’ errors overlap; the second measures it in a way we can count. How steady is the average of their forecasts? One rule first. The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus the covariance of every pair, counted in both orders. For two errors: \(\operatorname{Var}(e_1+e_2)=\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\rho\sigma^2\). Derivation. The average \(\bar e=\frac1N\sum_i e_i\) has \(N\) terms of variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(N(N-1)\) ordered pairs, each with covariance \(\rho\sigma^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(\bar e)&=\frac{1}{N^2}\big[N\sigma^2+N(N-1)\rho\sigma^2\big]\\&=\frac{\sigma^2\,[1+(N-1)\rho]}{N}\end{aligned}\]

\(N_{\text{eff}}\) independent forecasts would give variance \(\sigma^2/N_{\text{eff}}\). Set the two equal and solve:

\[N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+(N-1)\rho}\]

For ten banks with \(\rho=0.3\): \(10/(1+9\times0.3)=10/3.7=2.703\) independent opinions; with \(\rho=0.9\), only 1.099. Check. \(N=2\), \(\rho=0.5\): \(2/1.5=1.33\); the mean of two has variance \(\tfrac14(\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\times0.5\,\sigma^2)=0.75\sigma^2=\sigma^2/1.33\). ✓

What it means. Try “huge crowd, 0.3”: 100 banks give only 3.257 opinions. When \(\rho>0\), \(N_{\text{eff}}\) stays below \(1/\rho\) however many banks join (3.33 for \(\rho=0.3\)). The FSB warns that users of one model could be hit “as if it were a single institution” FSB 2024, p. 21.

Look inside: counting one voice ten times

Instance. Prior: 1 in 2 that a bank is safe. A safe bank is 4 times as likely to get a “safe” verdict as a risky one (the likelihood ratio; invented numbers). Mechanism. Each independent verdict multiplies the odds by 4 (the odds form of Bayes’ rule).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{posterior odds}&=\text{prior odds}\times 4^{\,n}\\P&=\frac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}\end{aligned}\]

Worked numbers. Counted \(n=0,\dots,4\) times: \(P(\text{safe})=0.5,\,0.8,\,0.941,\,0.985,\,0.996\). Ten times: odds 1,048,576, \(P=0.999999\). Check. \(n=1\): \(4/5=0.8\).

If ten banks run copies of one model, their ten verdicts are one verdict. Counting it ten times turns 0.8 into 0.999999: ten copies of one voice feel like certainty.

Ladder 2 · The 2008 mistake as a toy

Instance. A pool has 4 loans worth 10 each; each defaults with chance 10% and loses its 10. The junior tranche (20) takes the first losses. The senior tranche (20) loses only when a third loan defaults. Question. How safe is the senior tranche?

Mechanism. If loans default independently, three or four defaults together are rare. If all four depend on one hidden cause, such as falling house prices, they default together. Worked numbers. Independent: exactly 3 defaults has chance \(4\times0.1^3\times0.9=0.0036\); 4 defaults, \(0.1^4=0.0001\): the senior tranche loses with chance 0.37%. With one common coin deciding all four loans: 10%, 27 times more. Formula (default chance \(p\), common-risk share \(w\), \(q\) the independent chance):

\[\begin{aligned}P_{\text{senior loses}}&=w\,p+(1-w)\,q\\q&=4p^3(1-p)+p^4\end{aligned}\]

Check. At \(p=0.1\): \(w=0\) gives 0.370%, \(w=0.5\) gives 5.185%, \(w=1\) gives 10.000%.

Press “safe loans, p = 0.02”: 2.000% with one common factor, 0.0032% if independent, 634.5 times more. What it means. A toy, ours, with the shape of the FCIC’s finding that securities “weren’t that different to begin with” FCIC 2011, p. 149. Gensler and Bailey note that rating agencies used “models with similar methodologies” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 22. Shared AI models would put the same shape on a larger scale (ours).

Adds: how many independent opinions a market has depends on correlation, not headcount. Does not explain: how correlated real AI-driven desks are; \(\rho\) is assumed. The IMF judges that the risks of AI adoption “currently … appear contained” IMF 2024, p. 92.

Lens 2b · Cascades, sameness, feedback

same-math Job: decide, and what shared deciding does to the system. Threshold counting on a network; a one-line feedback rule. structural analogy Ecology and banking (Haldane and May); Granovetter’s riot and bank failures (ours).

Correlated firms are also connected firms. Andrew Haldane of the Bank of England and the ecologist Robert May compared banks to food webs. They are careful: “There are, of course, major differences between ecosystems and financial systems” Haldane and May 2011, p. 351.

Instance. Six banks (toy, ours). X1, X2 and X3 run one model; Y1, Y2 and Y3 do not. Each bank has capital, a buffer that absorbs losses: 10 for each X bank, and 10, 9 and 11 for Y1, Y2 and Y3. The loans:

Ladder 4 · Safer banks, a fragile system

Instance (Haldane and May’s toy). \(N\) banks and \(N\) unrelated kinds of asset. Each asset suffers a crippling loss with a small chance \(\varepsilon\). Plan A: each bank holds one whole asset. Plan B: each bank holds \(1/N\) of every asset, so all banks hold the same thing. Question. Which plan makes a total collapse likelier?

Mechanism. In plan A, a bank fails when its own asset fails (chance \(\varepsilon\)), and the system fails only if all \(N\) assets fail (\(\varepsilon^N\)). In plan B one bad asset is only \(1/N\) of a bank, so each bank is much safer; but the banks are identical, so when one fails they all fail.

Worked numbers for two banks (Haldane and May give no derivation; this one is ours). An asset is “hit” when its value falls below a danger level \(c\). Assume the chance that a value falls below any level \(x\) up to \(2c\) grows in proportion to \(x\): it is \(\varepsilon x/c\).

  • Plan A: a bank fails with chance \(\varepsilon\); both fail together with chance \(\varepsilon^2\).
  • Plan B: each bank holds half of each asset, so it fails when \(V_1+V_2\lt 2c\). Draw both values on a square of side \(2c\): that region is the triangle under the diagonal, with area \(\tfrac12(2c)^2=2c^2\). Each small patch of the square has chance \((\varepsilon/c)^2\) per unit area, so the chance is \(2\varepsilon^2\).

At \(\varepsilon=0.01\): each bank’s chance falls from 0.01 to 0.0002, but a total collapse rises from 0.0001 to 0.0002: twice as likely.

Formula. With \(N\) assets (and the same proportional rule up to \(Nc\)), the triangle becomes a corner of an \(N\)-dimensional cube, with volume \((Nc)^N/N!\), and each unit of volume has chance \((\varepsilon/c)^N\). So plan B fails with chance \(N^N\varepsilon^N/N!\), the formula Haldane and May give Haldane and May 2011, pp. 353–354. Check. \(N=2\): \(4\varepsilon^2/2=2\varepsilon^2\) ✓. For \(N=10\) and \(\varepsilon=0.01\), plan B is about 2,756 times likelier to collapse than plan A.

Prediction 4 · answer

No. In this toy “all the banks doing the same thing” can “minimize risk for each individual bank, but maximize the probability of the entire system collapsing” Haldane and May 2011, p. 353. They propose that “this systemic diversity objective should probably be given much greater prominence” p. 355.

What it means. If many firms automate the decide job with the same model, they move toward plan B (our reading). The toy is extreme by design: independent assets, every bank holding all of them.

Look inside: May’s ecology result (an analogy only)

Instance. A food web of many species, each stable on its own, linked at random: some species help others, some harm them. Ecologists once thought more links meant more stability. Model ecosystems showed “a sharp transition from overall stability to instability” as the links grew in number and strength Haldane and May 2011, p. 351. Formula. For many species, “this transition occurs once mα² > 1, where m is the average number of links per species, and (±) α their average strength” p. 351.

Worked numbers. \(m=4\), \(\alpha=0.4\): \(4\times0.16=0.64\), stable. Double the links, \(m=8\): 1.28, unstable. Weaken them to \(\alpha=0.3\): \(8\times0.09=0.72\), stable again.

What it means. It is an ecology result, and Haldane and May do not apply the formula to banks; in their bank model more links can soften loan losses p. 353. What carries over is the question it prompted: which structures let a complex system survive? Their answer for banks includes diversity and modularity: “Modular configurations prevent contagion infecting the whole network in the event of nodal failure” p. 355.

Ladder 5 · A share \(m\) of traders on one model

Instance. The market “gap” is price minus fair value (0 is fair). A share \(m\) of traders follow one model that chases the last move; the rest buy back toward fair value. A shock pushes the gap to −1. Question. For which \(m\) does the shock fade? Mechanism. Each step the chasers push the gap further by 1.5 × \(m\) × the last move, and the others pull it back by 0.5 × \((1-m)\) × the gap. Try it:

Worked numbers. At \(m=0.2\) the next gaps are −0.9, −0.51, −0.189: the shock dies out. At \(m=0.9\) they are −2.30, −3.94: it grows. Running the toy for many values of \(m\) shows the switch at \(m=2/3\); the fold-out has the rule, the table and the exact argument. What it means. The toy is about trend-following; shared models do not by themselves imply it. A circuit breaker caps the swing (±3.00 at \(m=0.9\)) but not the feedback; the toy breaker is a simplification of a real pause. The IMF notes that algorithms “are often programmed to de-risk or even shut down during periods of high volatility”, which “under certain conditions” could add to destabilisation IMF 2024, p. 88.

Look inside: the trend-follower rule, and why it tips at \(m=2/3\)

The rule, with \(A=1.5\) (chase gain) and \(G=0.5\) (pull-back gain):

\[\begin{aligned}\text{gap}_{t+1}&=\text{gap}_t+A\,m\,(\text{gap}_t-\text{gap}_{t-1})\\&\quad-G\,(1-m)\,\text{gap}_t\end{aligned}\]
\(m\)push \(Am\)pull \(G(1-m)\)next gaps
0.20.30.4−0.9, −0.51, −0.189: dies out
0.91.350.05−2.30, −3.94: grows

First step at \(m=0.2\), from a gap of 0 then −1: \(-1+0.3\times(-1)-0.4\times(-1)=-0.9\). Check. At \(m=0.2\) the toy reports a largest swing of 0.04 up and −1.00 down, and a gap of 0.000 after 40 steps.

A rough reason. Each step the chasers repeat \(Am\) times the last move, so a swing keeps its size when \(Am=1\), at \(m=1/A=2/3\).

The exact argument. Write the rule as \(g_{t+1}=b\,g_t-c\,g_{t-1}\) with \(b=1+Am-G(1-m)\) and \(c=Am\). A pattern \(g_t=x^t\) fits when \(x^2-bx+c=0\): the characteristic equation, whose roots are the factors by which the pattern is multiplied each step. The two roots multiply to \(c\). When they are complex numbers (a wave, which needs \(b^2\lt 4c\)), each has size \(\sqrt c\), so the wave shrinks if \(Am\lt 1\) and grows if \(Am\gt 1\). Numbers (code-checked with numpy.roots). \(m=0.2\): \(b=0.9\), \(c=0.3\), size 0.548. \(m=0.67\): \(c=1.005\), size 1.002, just above 1, so the swing grows very slowly. \(m=0.9\): \(b=2.3\), \(c=1.35\), size 1.162. Near \(m=1\) the roots become real, but by then \(Am\gt 1\) and the larger root already exceeds 1.

Adds: automation with shared models can make each firm safer and the system more fragile: thresholds, sameness and feedback. Does not explain: how alike real AI-driven firms are; the IMF’s scenarios run both ways, and its verdict on the future impact is “highly uncertain” IMF 2024, p. 89.

8 · Through the lenses

Lens 3 · Who declares what counts?

structural analogy Job: declare. Does machine “acceptance” have the same shape as human collective acceptance? Searle’s theory applied to machines (the application is ours). No math here.

Running, deciding and checking are things that happen. Declaring is different: it makes things count. A piece of paper is money, a signature makes a contract, a company is a legal person. The philosopher John Searle built a theory of how such facts exist.

Terms · institutional fact, constitutive

An institutional fact is a fact that exists only because people accept it, such as “this paper is money” or “she is president”, unlike a brute fact such as “this paper weighs one gram”. A belief is constitutive of something when it is part of what makes the thing what it is, not just a report about it.

Functions are given, not found

Instance. A screwdriver, a chair, a bathtub. Their functions “are never intrinsic to the physics of any phenomenon but are assigned from outside by conscious observers and users” Searle 1995, p. 14. A screwdriver works because of its shape. A dollar bill does not: paper cannot buy anything by its physics. Mechanism. Some functions work only because people collectively accept a status. Searle’s pattern: rules of the form “‘X counts as Y’ or ‘X counts as Y in context C’” Searle 1995, p. 28. X is a thing (this printed paper), Y a status (money), C a context (the United States). The key step is “the imposition of a collectively recognized status to which a function is attached” p. 41.

The condition. “there must be continued collective acceptance or recognition of the validity of the assigned function; otherwise the function cannot be successfully performed” Searle 1995, p. 45. For money: “we must continue to accept it as money or it will become worthless” p. 45. And the status “exists only if people believe it exists” p. 69.

A subtlety: tokens and types. A single bill that falls into the cracks of a floor and is never used or thought of is still money. That one bill is a token. What must be believed is that bills of this type are money: “the belief that the type is a type of money is constitutive of its being money” Searle 1995, pp. 32–33.

Prediction 3 · answer

For Searle, money needs a “we” that accepts the type. A single token can be money unnoticed, but only because people accept the kind. Searle never discusses machines, so whether AIs could form that “we” is an open question, and every answer to it below is ours.

Could machines do the accepting?

Against (Searle’s criteria; the application is ours). Searle calls collective intentionality, the capacity to share intentions as a “we”, “a biologically primitive phenomenon” Searle 1995, p. 24, and he holds that “only beings that have a language or some more or less language-like system of representation can create most, perhaps all, institutional facts” p. 37. A dog can be trained to fetch dollar bills, yet “He still is not buying the food and the bills are not money to him” p. 70. On this view, AIs can move tokens, but only a “we” makes them money.

For (the strongest case we can build, ours). LLMs have a language-like system of representation, which Searle names as a requirement. And Searle grants that people follow institutions without knowing the rules: money users “do not know those rules”, having instead “a set of dispositions that are sensitive and responsive to the specific content of those rules” Searle 1995, p. 142. If acceptance can be a pattern of behaviour sensitive to rules, a network of AIs might sustain one. Searle himself requires more: belief in the status.

Harari’s version. He asserts that “What gives money value is the stories that bankers, finance ministers and cryptocurrency gurus tell us about it” Ynet 2023, and that “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57]. Joining the two is our compression; Searle’s test bites on the gap: a story is not money until a community counts it as money, and a law is not in force until it is accepted and enforced.

Adds: “taking over money” splits into handling tokens (run) and making them count (declare). Does not explain: whether machine acceptance is real acceptance; Searle’s theory was built for minds, and he does not address AI.

Lens 4 · Who answers?

structural analogy Job: answer. Delegating to a broker and delegating to an AI (ours). Lupia and McCubbins explain when delegation can be trusted; who bears the loss enters their theory only through the agent’s incentives.

The answer job has two halves: bearing the losses, and being held responsible, so that someone can be made to pay or explain. Both work through consequences. A broker who lies can be fined, sued or fired. An AI cannot be fined, jailed or shamed the way a broker can. Does that matter? A theory of delegation shows when it does.

Instance (toy, ours). You hire a broker to buy a bond. You are the principal (who delegates), the broker is the agent (who acts for you), and your alternative is to keep your cash, worth 100: the status quo. A good bond will be worth 110, a bad one 90. Two conditions can hold. Knowledge: you can tell a good bond from a bad one. Incentive: the broker has a reason to be honest; for example, he is paid only if you profit.

conditionswhat you can end up with
botha good bond: 110
only oneat worst you keep your cash: 100
neitherwhatever he sells, possibly 90

Mechanism. With knowledge, you refuse a bad bond. With the incentive, he never offers one. With neither, nothing protects you. Formula (in words): the political scientists Arthur Lupia and Mathew McCubbins prove that delegation succeeds if both conditions hold and fails if neither does; with one, “the worst that can happen” is the status quo Lupia and McCubbins 1998, pp. 85–86. Under a stricter definition of success, losing either condition is enough to fail p. 92. Check. The table’s worst cases, 110, 100 and 90, follow the theorem’s three cases. Institutions supply the conditions; in their example, supervised candidates “may be subject to verification or a penalty for lying” p. 207.

Applied to AI (ours). An agent that “consistently hides the genuine reasons” for its trade Scheurer et al. 2024, abstract removes knowledge; so does a product no checker can price (Lens 1). And since an AI cannot be punished, the incentive condition must run through whoever deploys it. That is today’s capital-market rule: “institutions are responsible for AI systems they deploy” IMF 2024, p. 93, and most market participants want a human in the loop partly for “liability” reasons p. 78.

Personhood. Harari forecasts, without a hedge, that AIs “will soon be able to make the decisions necessary to manage a bank account” WEF 2026 [16:28], notes that “in many countries, corporations are considered legal persons” [15:14], and asks: “Do we want to allow that?” [16:47]. If an AI could be a legal person, it could in principle hold the answer job. We have no legal sources and state no legal position.

Democracy. When decisions cannot be explained, “Human agency and traditional intervention approaches may be lost” Gensler and Bailey 2020, p. 13. Accountability needs someone who can be asked why.

Adds: automation can hand over actions but not, under current capital-market rules, responsibility; and delegating to an agent that cannot be punished leaves only knowledge to protect you. Does not explain: how law will treat AI agents; that is a choice societies have not made.

Hint

Start with the person of threshold 0: they riot alone. Then ask who joins when 1 person is rioting, then when 2 are.

Solution

Person 0 riots alone. With 1 rioting, threshold 1 joins; with 2, threshold 2 joins; and so on up to 99: all 100 riot. After the swap the thresholds are 0, 2, 2, 3, …, 99. Person 0 riots, but the next person needs 2 others and only 1 is rioting. The riot stops at 1 person.

13 · Synthesis

So what happens? Short answer first

Short answer (our reading, not a finding)

(a) Is it possible? Separate three senses of “possible” (ours). Capable: AI already runs much of the work, can increasingly decide, and checks at run time. Without any human intervention: no. Every automated system in our sources runs on rules and limits people set in advance, like the 2010 pause, and participants in the IMF’s outreach expect humans in the loop for the near term. Removing design-time intervention too is not shown possible or impossible by anything we read. Stable and accepted: that is what (b) and (c) are about.

(b) What would it do? If firms automate deciding with a few shared models, failures may arrive together, each firm can look safer while the system grows more fragile, and automated checkers need checkers of their own (Lenses 1–2; warnings and toys, not measurements). The IMF judges today’s risks “contained” and sees scenarios in both directions IMF 2024, pp. 89, 92. Who gains and who bears (ours): power moves to whoever writes the models and the rules, and losses fall on whoever is exposed when shared models fail together. Harari puts the extreme case as a possibility: it “could lead to power shifting from humans to algorithms” BIS 2024 [3:30].

(c) What would it mean? On our definition, a society is a group whose members can rely on shared rules, contest them, and find someone answerable. Money, contracts and legal persons exist because a “we” keeps accepting them (Searle), and trust in an agent needs knowledge or a working incentive (Lupia and McCubbins). So a world where AIs also declared and answered would be one of two things: a society with people hidden in those two jobs, as writers of rules and bearers of liability; or, on our definition, not a society at all, but a machine system that people live next to. That is our verdict. Two things would change it: AIs that can be held responsible in a way that changes their behaviour, and people who can still contest the rules the machines declare.

Where the lenses disagree

Ashby’s third lever says: impose a constraint, such as a standard menu of contracts, and checking becomes possible. Haldane and May say: when everyone holds the same thing, the system grows fragile. A standard menu makes checking easier and firms more alike. Which effect wins depends on what is standardised: the form of the contract, or the bets made with it (ours).

Signs to watch

  1. Up: AI systems that choose trades at scale without approval. Down: the IMF’s “human in the loop” holds past five years.
  2. Up: a few base models underlie most AI desks and their errors correlate (measure \(\rho\)). Down: diverse models and data.
  3. Up: supervisors rely on AI they cannot explain. Down: standard contract menus and pre-set pauses spread.
  4. Up: a token or contract that only machines use is treated as binding by courts. Down: declarations still need human authorities.
  5. Up: a jurisdiction lets an AI hold accounts with no responsible human. Down: liability stays with people.
Layers of certainty, and critics

Four layers, from most certain to most contested

  1. Running is automated in parts of finance. Measured: about 70% of US equity trading is algorithmic; the 2010 index future traded on a fully electronic market.
  2. Checking needs variety, and some products resist checking. Mathematics (Ashby), and a theorem resting on a conjecture in a simple model (Arora and colleagues).
  3. Shared models make failures coincide. Shown in toys; flagged as a risk (“could”) by the FSB and as a hypothesis by Gensler and Bailey; judged “contained” for now by the IMF. Not measured for AI.
  4. Whether machines can declare and answer. Contested: collective acceptance, liability, personhood.

Critics, one line each

Poole answers Harari’s claim that chatbots create new ideas: “no one has yet seen a chatbot create new ideas” Poole 2024. Narayanan and Kapoor: in the world they describe (advanced but not “superintelligent” AI, a notion they view as incoherent), impacts are slow and control stays with people; they call it their “median outcome”, not a certainty Narayanan and Kapoor 2025. FSB: existing frameworks address many vulnerabilities, but “more work may be needed” FSB 2024, p. 2. Skingsley, Harari’s interviewer at the BIS: we already trust what we only vaguely understand BIS 2024 [7:18]. Harari’s own remedy: institutions and “humans in the loop”, offered with his doubt: “Unfortunately, humanity has never been united” Guardian 2024.

Don’t read too much into a toy with ten banks.

A paragraph to loop

Software already runs much of the market, but running is by no means the same as answering for it. Provided that many firms rely on one model, it can become a single point of failure, and their errors may move in lockstep. Most firms still want a human in the loop, partly because someone has to be held accountable. A token counts as money only as long as a community accepts it. What remains to be seen is whether machines could ever be that community. Standard contracts cut both ways. Don’t read too much into a toy, with the caveat that it shows what could happen, not what will.

Four sentence patterns that sound C1
Reporting verbs
“The IMF judges the risks contained; the FSB warns that common models could correlate trading; Gensler and Bailey hypothesise a monoculture; Searle argues that status needs acceptance.” Match the verb to how firm the claim is.
Three layers
“The source says X. The evidence shows Y. Our reading is Z.”
Hedging ladder
shows → suggests → is consistent with → hints at.
Concession, then your point
“Granted, the toy is simple. But the threshold is real.”
Small fixes that make a big difference
if-clause without will
“If the models fail, …”, not “will fail”.
evidence (uncountable)
“some evidence”, not “evidences”.
general nouns, no article
“society”, “finance”, but “the financial system”.
