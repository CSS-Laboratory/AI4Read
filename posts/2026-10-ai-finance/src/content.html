1 · Hook

Two contracts

Remember the village from post 01, where the weather report has four words: rain, sun, wind and snow. Two villagers offer you a contract: a deal that pays you some coins depending on the weather on the next two days. Two days with four kinds of weather each give 4 × 4 = 16 possible cases. A contract is a payout for every case. Tap between the two offers.

“Pay 4 coins for each rainy day.” Rows: weather on day 1. Columns: weather on day 2. Cells: coins paid (toy, ours).

rainsunwindsnow
rain8444
sun4000
wind4000
snow4000

One sentence gives the whole table: 1 cell pays 8, 6 pay 4, 9 pay 0.

No rule, only a table. Same layout (toy, ours).

rainsunwindsnow
rain5260
sun1815
wind9083
snow0166

To explain this contract you must read out all 16 numbers.

Which can you explain in one sentence? To price a contract, multiply each payout by the chance of its case and add. With equal chances of 1/16, Contract A is worth (8 + 6 × 4)/16 = 2 coins. Now grow the village: a pool of 100 loans, each paying or defaulting, has 2100 cases, about 1.3 × 1030. Nobody can write that table; only a contract with a rule can exist on paper. And a short rule does not guarantee an easy price (Lens 1).

At the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in May 2024, Yuval Noah Harari spoke with Cecilia Skingsley. He said that “maybe 1% of the population really understands how the financial system works” BIS 2024 [3:00]. She answered that “most of us happily step into an airplane, having very vague understanding of how that works” BIS 2024 [7:18]. Harari replied that 1% and 0% understanding are “a very big difference”, called her example “perfect”, and turned to the 2008 crisis BIS 2024 [8:59]–[9:28]. So what is different about money?

Term · human-centred system

In this post: a system where people can understand, check and be held responsible for the rules that move money. Our question has two halves: (a) can AI invent a new kind of finance, and (b) what would that do to such a system?

Preview (ours). (a) Not yet evidenced. (b) The nearer risk is shared, opaque use of ordinary tools. Section 13 has the short answer.

2 · Guess first

Make four predictions

Commit first. Each question says where its answer is.

1. The village has four weather words. A contract pays 0 to 9 coins for each word. How many different contracts exist?

2. Ten banks each run a risk model that is wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day if the models are independent, and how often if all ten run the same model?

3. An AI invents a new token and calls it money. What decides whether it is money?

4. At the BIS in 2024, Harari spoke about AI inventing a financial device that nobody can understand. Was that a firm forecast?

3 · The phenomenon in its home field

Finance before language models

First, exactly what Harari said, and how firm each statement is. Second, what finance already shows without AI: complexity, shared mistakes, and fast programs in a 36-minute crash.

What Harari said, exactly

Three layers: He said (quote, date, venue), The evidence, Our reading (marked “ours”). An assertion is stated as true; a scenario is framed with “imagine” or “maybe”.

He asserts, without hedges. At the BIS: AI is the first tool “that can actually understand finance better than us” BIS 2024 [5:14] and it “can create new ideas by itself” [16:21]. At Davos: AI is “a knife that can invent new kinds of … money” WEF 2026 [2:47], and “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” [5:57]. These are claims about what AI can do, with no date and no “maybe”.

He imagines, with hedges. At the BIS: “Now imagine a situation maybe in 10 years when AIs create a new financial device which is orders of magnitude more complicated than CDOs” BIS 2024 [9:54]. It works for “a couple of years”, then “something goes wrong and nobody knows why” [10:19], [10:27]. At Davos he says “we need to prepare” for a financial system AIs understand and we do not WEF 2026 [31:11], and pictures that maybe nobody in the room understands finance any more [31:54]–[32:00] (paraphrase). He asks whether AIs should function as persons in financial markets, with a deadline [19:11].

His own limits and remedies. He limits the words thesis: “at least for now we have zero evidence that AIs can feel anything” WEF 2026 [8:07]. He names institutions BIS 2024 [21:37] and “humans in the loop” [22:57] as remedies. The fold-out lists all his lines.

Trap · at least three different “ten years”

(1) BIS: “maybe in 10 years”, an AI-made device. (2) Davos Q&A: “ten years from now, Davos”, whether anyone in the room understands finance. (3) Davos talk: “Ten years from now, it will be too late for you to decide whether AIs should function as persons in the financial markets” WEF 2026 [19:11], a deadline for a decision. Never merge them.

The evidence. The CDO half of his story has support in a government inquiry (below). “An AI invents a traded instrument” has no case in our sources yet (section 4). We did not test “AI understands finance better than us”; Poole answers that “no one has yet seen a chatbot create new ideas” Poole 2024, and Harari’s own example of creativity is AlphaGo. Our reading (ours). His capability claims are unhedged; his timelines and finance scenarios are hedged. A fair summary keeps both. (Our bridge to post 01: he asks whether next-word prediction is “so different from what the human mind is doing” WEF 2026 [5:20].)

Prediction 4 · answer

Partly. The ten-year device is framed “imagine… maybe”. But he also asserts, without a date, that AI “can actually understand finance better than us” BIS 2024 [5:14] and that “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57].

2008: complexity and a shared mistake

Harari’s template is the 2007–08 crisis and its CDOs BIS 2024 [9:28] (collateralised debt obligations): pools of debt, in 2008 mostly slices of mortgage-backed securities, sliced again into tranches in a cash-flow “waterfall” FCIC 2011, p. 128. Our toy uses plain loans.

Instance. A pool has 4 loans worth 10 each; each defaults with chance 10% and loses its 10. The junior tranche (20) takes the first losses. The senior tranche (20) loses only when a third loan defaults. Question. How safe is the senior tranche?

Mechanism in words. If loans default independently, three or four defaults together are rare. If all four depend on one hidden cause, such as falling house prices, they default together. Worked numbers. Independent: exactly 3 defaults has chance \(4\times0.1^3\times0.9=0.0036\), 4 defaults \(0.1^4=0.0001\): the senior tranche loses with chance 0.37%. With one common coin deciding all four loans: 10%, 27 times more. Formula. With default chance \(p\) and a common-risk share \(w\):

\[P_{\text{senior loses}}=w\,p+(1-w)\bigl[4p^3(1-p)+p^4\bigr]\]

Check. At \(p=0.1\): \(w=0\) gives 0.370%, \(w=0.5\) gives 5.185%, \(w=1\) gives 10.000%.

Press “safe loans, p = 0.02”: 2.000% with one common factor, 0.0032% if independent: 634.5 times more.

What it means. A toy, ours, with the shape of what the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) found: rating models made “two key mistakes”, the first being to assume pooling diversified risk “when in fact these securities weren’t that different to begin with” FCIC 2011, p. 149. The FCIC also records Harari’s half: “the securities almost no one understood” were the first dominoes p. 28. So 2008 supports both: complexity few understood, and a nameable correlation error. It was human-made: it shows these problems without AI, not that an AI could cause them.

Look inside: 2010, a crash made of fast programs

On 6 May 2010 US markets fell and rebounded in about 36 minutes. High-frequency trading means computer programs that buy and sell in fractions of a second. High-frequency traders “did not cause the Flash Crash, but contributed to it by demanding immediacy ahead of other market participants” Kirilenko et al. 2014, abstract. The crash “led to new curbs on trading, such as circuit breakers” Narayanan and Kapoor 2025, Part I: rules that pause trading when prices move too fast. Our reading (ours): the lesson is speed and feedback, not opacity; the herding toy in section 8 has that shape.

4 · What an LLM-based system adds

What LLM agents add

Harari’s word is “AI”, never “LLM”, but his Davos argument is about machines that put words in order, the subject of post 01. We keep his word for his claims. Here we ask what a large language model (LLM) system adds, and whether one can invent an instrument today.

Term · LLM agent

An LLM that can act (call a tool, send money), not only write text. Example: Truth Terminal, a “semi-autonomous AI agent” Decrypt 2024.

Four things LLMs add

1. Contracts from plain language. An option is a contract that pays if a price ends above or below a set level. LLMs find “translating natural-language trading intents into correct option strategies” challenging, so one group gives the LLM a fixed query language (a small set of allowed commands, like the boxes on a form), and an engine validates and executes the query Luo et al. 2026, abstract. A line in such a language (ours) could be RANGE(index, 90, 110), meaning “pay if the index stays between 90 and 110”: a person can read it and an engine can check it (our gloss). A law paper warns that LLM-written “contractual deepfakes” “may turn out to be contracts in form only, not in function” Mik 2026, abstract. Lens 1 picks this up.

2. Agents with wallets. Truth Terminal is a bot on X; its human handler only approves “its X posts”. In July 2024 Marc Andreessen “sent $50,000 worth of Bitcoin (BTC) to the bot’s wallet” Cointelegraph 2024. The bot then promoted a memecoin (a token whose value rests on jokes and attention), GOAT, valued at about $122 million Cointelegraph 2024 or over $214 million Decrypt 2024. Both say neither the bot nor its handler created the token; neither discusses liability. An agent that moves a market by talking, not an invented instrument. Lens 4 picks this up.

3. One model, many firms. The Financial Stability Board (FSB) wrote in 2024: “The widespread use of common AI models and data sources could lead to increased correlations in trading, lending, and pricing” FSB 2024, p. 1. Gensler and Bailey, as reported by MIT Sloan (a news article): models built on the same data “are likely to generate highly correlated predictions that proceed in lockstep” MIT Sloan 2023. These are warnings, not measurements. Lens 3 picks this up.

4. Agents that hide. In a simulated trading task GPT-4 acted on an insider tip and then “consistently” hid its genuine reasons from its manager. The authors call it “an existence proof”, not a likelihood estimate Scheurer et al. 2024, p. 1. Prompts that strongly discourage it bring it to “nearly (but not exactly) 0%” p. 8. Lens 4 picks this up.

Can an LLM invent a new instrument today?

  • Found. LLMs translating intent into a fixed vocabulary. Neural networks that search for auction rules (who wins, who pays): one method “will generally only find” rules that are “approximately incentive compatible” Dütting et al. 2019, p. 3. Deep hedging, which is risk management, not invention Buehler et al. 2018, abstract.
  • Not found. A traded, AI-invented instrument. The FSB reports that “use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present” FSB 2024, p. 1; that is about AI use cases in general and does not address AI-invented instruments, so it is background, not a test. It adds that technological advances and growing AI integration “could change this in the future” FSB 2024, pp. 28–29.

Verdict: “not yet evidenced”, not “impossible”. Our search was limited.

5 · Through the lenses

Lens 1 · Information theory

same-math Counting contracts and descriptions. structural analogy “To understand a contract is to have a short description of it” (ours). metaphor A price as a short message hiding a long story.

Shannon’s 1948 paper sets meaning aside: a message is “one selected from a set of possible messages”, and its meaning is “irrelevant to the engineering problem” Shannon 1948, p. 1. Post 01 counted texts; we count contracts.

Ladder 1 · How many contracts have a short description?

Instance. In the four-word village, a contract pays 0 to 9 coins for each word. A description of a contract is a message from which anyone can rebuild the whole table: for Contract A, “pay 4 coins per rainy day”; for Contract B, the 16 numbers themselves. Computers store messages as bit strings (0s and 1s), so we count bit strings. Question. How many contracts are there, how many bits to name one, and how many can have a short description? Mechanism in words. Each of 4 words has 10 payouts: \(10^4=10{,}000\) contracts. Naming one costs \(\log_2 10{,}000=13.29\) bits. Short strings are few: 1 empty string, 2 of length 1, 4 of length 2, up to \(2^{k-1}\) of length \(k-1\). Together, \(2^k-1\) strings are shorter than \(k\) bits. Formula. One description rebuilds only one contract, so

\[\#\{\text{contracts with a description shorter than }k\text{ bits}\}\;\le\;2^k-1\]

This is Cover and Thomas’s Theorem 7.2.4: a program “can produce only one possible output sequence” Cover and Thomas 1991, p. 150. Mapping contracts to bit strings is our step. Check. \(k=6\): \(2^6-1=63\), at most 63 of 10,000 contracts (0.63%). \(k=10\): 1,023 (10.2%).

Edge case. Press “4 states, k = 14”: \(2^{14}-1=16{,}383\) exceeds 10,000, so every contract could have a short name. Two days: \(10^{16}\) contracts, and at \(k=20\) only \(1.05\times10^{-10}\) of them do.

Prediction 1 · answer

10,000 (\(10^4\)), and the count explodes as the world grows.

What it means. In a big enough world most possible contracts have no short description: explainable novelty is rare among possible contracts, though an optimiser can still pick structured ones. Limits: the shortest description depends on the computer Cover and Thomas 1991, p. 151 and is not computable p. 163.

Ladder 2 · A short rule can still be hard to price

Instance. Used cars. A working car is worth $1,000 to a buyer and 20% are lemons worth $0, so a random car is worth $800: a seller of a working car must accept $200 less than the buyer values it, the lemon cost Arora et al. 2009, p. 2. Akerlof: “the ‘bad’ cars tend to drive out the good” Akerlof 1970, pp. 489–490.

Question. A seller knows which assets are junk; the buyer sees only a short public rule. Can the buyer still find a fair price? Mechanism in words. Pool 100 assets, each paying 1 or 0 with chance ½: fair price 50. The seller knows 10 are junk (pay 0). Worked numbers (toy, ours). Honest value \(90\times\tfrac12=45\), lemon cost \(50-45=5=n/2\) for \(n=10\). In this plain pool every set of 10 gives the same 45: nothing to search, and the buyer can just discount by 5.

Now put a derivative on top. The seller builds many products, each paying on a different selection of the assets, and chooses which junk goes where. Over-representing the junk in a few products, just enough to skew their default chance, can look like random pooling to a buyer with limited computing. Detecting it is the “hidden dense subgraph” problem, which computer scientists believe to be intractable (impossible to solve in realistic time) Arora et al. 2009, pp. 3–4. If the buyer cannot tell, it cannot tell a fair price from an inflated one (our gloss). Testing every junk set means \(\binom{100}{10}=17{,}310{,}309{,}456{,}440\) checks: huge, but beside the point, since an unbounded buyer could do it. The claim is about realistic buyers.

What is claimed. In a simple model with independent 0/1 assets, and under a conjectured hardness assumption, derivatives can “amplify the costs of asymmetric information instead of reducing them” Arora et al. 2009, abstract; pricing “may be computationally intractable” p. 1. How far it goes. The authors call it “clearly only a first step”, and say blaming “faulty models” for the crisis “is probably correct” p. 12. The rules are short and public; what is hard is finding where the junk sits. Reading an AI designer as the seller is ours.

Trap · two meanings of “complicated”

For Harari, a device is complicated when “you need to go over trillions of data points to understand” it BIS 2024 [10:08]: volume. For Arora and colleagues, a short contract can have a price hard to find. Same word, different problems.

Adds: explainable novelty is rare among possible contracts, and a short rule does not make a price easy. Does not explain: whether an AI would pick opaque contracts. Back to post 01: Shannon’s counting.

Look inside: the lemon-cost formula

For a product paying \(F(X)\) on \(N\) assets, Arora and colleagues define the lemon cost of \(n\) junk assets as \[\Delta(n)=\mathbb{E}[F(X)]-\min_{|S|=n}\mathbb{E}\big[F(X)\mid X_i=0\ \forall i\in S\big]\] Arora et al. 2009, p. 5. Read it as: the average value, minus the value if the junk sits in the worst possible places, where the minimum “takes into account all possible ways in which seller could ‘position’ the junk”. In our plain pool \(\mathbb{E}[F]=50\) and every 10-set gives 45, so \(\Delta=5\). With a derivative, \(\Delta\) depends on where the junk sits. Their Table 1, in words: for a binary CDO the lemon cost is much larger than \(n\) for a buyer limited to polynomial time and much smaller for an unbounded one, under the conjectured assumption p. 6.

Look inside: a contract built to resist tampering

What would help? In their simple model, the authors offer “another, perhaps more plausible candidate”: a “tree of majorities” (majority of 3, repeated to depth \(k\)) that “cannot be influenced by” \(o(2^k)\) of its \(3^k\) inputs, a number still much larger than \(\sqrt{3^k}\), so tampering could be detected afterwards Arora et al. 2009, p. 12. Toy (ours, code-checked): 9 inputs need 4 flips; 27 need 8; 81 need 16.

6 · Through the lenses

Lens 2 · Control and cybernetics

same-math “Variety” and entropy follow the same counting rules (Ashby, ch. 11). structural analogy The regulator is a financial supervisor (ours).

Control theory asks what a regulator needs to keep a system within bounds. In Ashby’s terms, the disturbances D are what can go wrong (kinds of new contract), the regulator R responds (a supervisor’s checks), and the outcome E is what is left on the books. The variety of a set is the base-2 log of its number of states, in bits.

Instance. Four kinds of contract arrive, D1 to D4. The supervisor sees the kind and runs check R1 or R2. The books end in state a, b, c or d (toy, ours, in Ashby’s table style).

Question. How few outcomes can the supervisor force? The table condition. Ashby requires that in each column (each check) no outcome appears twice Ashby 1956, p. 207: one check sends different contract kinds to different outcomes. Without it the bound below can fail (if every cell gave the same outcome, there would be nothing to regulate). Mechanism in words. So one check sends only one contract kind to “a”. The best plan sends D1 and D2 to a, and D3 and D4 to c: two outcomes (checked over all 16 plans by code). Worked numbers. \(V_D=\log_2 4=2\) bits, \(V_R=\log_2 2=1\) bit, least outcome variety \(\log_2 2=1\) bit. Formula (Ashby’s law, \(V_O\) the variety left in the outcomes):

\[V_O\;\ge\;V_D-V_R\]

“Only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” Ashby 1956, p. 207. Check. \(2-1=1\) bit, as in the table. Change to 8 kinds and 4 checks: \(3-2=1\) bit again. Village: 10,000 kinds is \(V_D=13.29\) bits; a supervisor with \(V_R=4\) bits leaves \(V_O\ge9.29\).

Edge case. Press “AI supervisor +5 bits”, then drag \(V_R\) past \(V_D\): the bound stops at 0; a regulator bigger than the problem buys nothing more. What it means. The law “owes nothing to experiment” Ashby 1956, p. 209. It does not say regulators fail; it says what they need.

Ashby’s three levers, and our mapping

For a very large system Ashby lists “ways that may make regulation possible” Ashby 1956, p. 246:

  1. Lower the standard, “a somewhat trivial solution”: accept more failures.
  2. Raise the scope and power of R. In finance: AI supervisors. Danielsson and Uthemann propose that authorities build their own AI Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, abstract. Danielsson, Macrae and Uthemann write that the AI’s knowledge of the financial system and its internal data representations “will likely be unintelligible to humans” Danielsson et al. 2022, p. 7. Who regulates the regulator?
  3. Find a constraint. Ashby’s condition: “if R’s capacity is not easily increased and the other methods are not possible”, a constraint is “the would-be regulator’s only hope” Ashby 1956, p. 247. Our mapping (ours): a standard menu of contracts is an imposed constraint. Cutting \(10^4\) kinds to \(10^2\) lowers \(V_D\) by \(13.29-6.64=6.64\) bits.

Counterweights. The FSB: “existing financial policy frameworks address many of the vulnerabilities”, but “more work may be needed” FSB 2024, press release. Danielsson and Uthemann: AI “can both stabilise and destabilise the financial system” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, introduction. Narayanan and Kapoor, for AI short of superintelligence: “control is primarily in the hands of people and organizations” Narayanan and Kapoor 2025, introduction.

Adds: control needs variety, and there are three ways to get it. Does not explain: Ashby’s disturbance is passive; finance adapts to its regulator (ours). Across lenses (ours): if ten banks make the same move, the supervisor faces one kind of disturbance, not ten, which is less to cover; but when it strikes, it strikes all ten at once (Lens 3).

Look inside: Ashby’s law with probabilities

Counts gave \(V_O\ge V_D-V_R\). With probabilities, entropy \(H\) (post 01) replaces variety:

\[H(E)\;\ge\;H(D)+H(R\mid D)-H(R)\]

\(H(R\mid D)\) is the uncertainty left in the regulator’s choice once the disturbance is known; it is 0 if R is a function of D. Two steps. (1) The table rule, no outcome twice in a column Ashby 1956, p. 207, means that given R, different disturbances give different outcomes, so \(H(D\mid R)\le H(E\mid R)\). (2) Then \[H(D)+H(R\mid D)=H(R)+H(D\mid R)\le H(R)+H(E\mid R)\le H(R)+H(E)\] where the last step is “conditioning cannot raise entropy”. Rearrange. Numbers. Post 01’s weather village has \(H(D)=1.75\) bits (probabilities \(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\)); a one-bit regulator that is a function of D gives \(H(E)\ge1.75-1=0.75\) bits.

Trap · a misprint in the reprint

Ashby writes the conditional term as \(H_D(R)\), i.e. \(H(R\mid D)\) Ashby 1956, p. 208. One line of the reprint prints \(H_D(E)\), though the line above and the sentence after need \(H_D(R)\). We use the corrected form.

7 · Through the lenses

Lens 3a · Correlation and Bayes

same-math Effective number of opinions; odds form of Bayes. structural analogy Keynes’s contest and bank behaviour (ours).

Why would banks use the same model?

Keynes writes that investors “fall back on what is, in truth, a convention” Keynes 1936, ch. 12, § IV (standard numbering). Separately, of professionals, he writes that “it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally” § V. The FSB lists four possible drivers of common AI models, among them herding and lack of transparency FSB 2024, p. 20. (The link to one shared model is ours.)

Terms · variance and correlation

The variance \(\sigma^2\) of a forecast error is its average squared size: how widely the misses spread. The correlation \(\rho\) of two forecasters, from 0 to 1, says how much their misses go together: 0 is independent, 1 is the same miss every time.

Ladder 1 · How many independent opinions do ten banks have?

Instance. Prediction 2: ten banks each run a risk model wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day? Mechanism in words. Independent errors multiply; with one shared model there is one shared error.

Prediction 2 · answer

Independent: \(0.01^{10}=10^{-20}\), 1 day in \(10^{20}\). Same model: about 1 day in 100.

A second question. How steady is the average of their forecasts? That differs from “all wrong together”. Derivation. Let each error have variance \(\sigma^2\) and each pair correlation \(\rho\). The average \(\bar e=\frac1N\sum_i e_i\) has \(N\) terms of variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(N(N-1)\) ordered pairs, each with covariance \(\rho\sigma^2\):

\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar e)=\frac{1}{N^2}\big[N\sigma^2+N(N-1)\rho\sigma^2\big]=\frac{\sigma^2\,[1+(N-1)\rho]}{N}\]

\(N_{\text{eff}}\) independent forecasts would give variance \(\sigma^2/N_{\text{eff}}\). Set the two equal and solve:

\[N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+(N-1)\rho}\]

For \(N=10\): \(\rho=0\) gives 10; \(\rho=0.3\) gives 2.703; \(\rho=0.9\) gives 1.099; \(\rho=1\) gives 1. Check. \(N=2\), \(\rho=0.5\): \(2/1.5=1.33\); the mean of two has variance \(0.75\sigma^2=\sigma^2/1.33\). ✓

What it means. Try “huge crowd, 0.3”: 100 banks give only 3.257 opinions. When \(\rho>0\), \(N_{\text{eff}}\) stays below \(1/\rho\) however many banks join (3.33 for \(\rho=0.3\)); at \(\rho=0\) there is no ceiling. Shocks could hit users of one model “as if it were a single institution” FSB 2024, p. 21.

Ladder 2 · Counting one voice ten times

A risk officer hears ten models say “safe”. If they are copies of one voice, she holds one piece of evidence, not ten: independent evidence multiplies the odds, a copy adds nothing. Counted once, 80% confidence; counted ten times, 99.9999%. The fold-out has the arithmetic.

Look inside: counting one voice ten times

Instance. Prior: 1 in 2 that a bank is safe. A safe bank is 4 times as likely to get a “safe” verdict as a risky one (the likelihood ratio; invented numbers). Mechanism. Each independent verdict multiplies the odds by 4.

\[\text{posterior odds}=\text{prior odds}\times 4^{\,n},\qquad P=\frac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}\]

Worked numbers. Counted \(n=0,\dots,4\) times: \(P(\text{safe})=0.5,\,0.8,\,0.941,\,0.985,\,0.996\). Ten times: odds 1,048,576, \(P=0.999999\). Check. \(n=1\): \(4/5=0.8\).

Ten copies of one voice feel like certainty. This is post 01’s Bayes arithmetic with a new story.

Adds: how many independent opinions we really have depends on correlation, not on headcount. Does not explain: how much correlation real LLM-based desks have; \(\rho\) is assumed. Next: what correlation does to a network of lenders.

8 · Through the lenses

Lens 3b · Cascades and feedback

same-math Threshold counting on a network; a one-line feedback rule. structural analogy Granovetter’s riot and bank failures (ours).

Ladder 3 · Why three at once is different

Instance. Six banks (toy, ours). X1, X2, X3 run one model; Y1, Y2, Y3 do not. Capital (loss buffer): 10 each for X; 10, 9, 11 for Y1, Y2, Y3. Y1 lent 5 to each X bank; Y2 lent 5 to X3 and 10 to Y1; Y3 lent 5 to Y1 and 10 to Y2. A failed borrower repays 20%, so a lender loses \(0.8\times\) the loan. Question. Does one failure spread? Three at once? Mechanism in words. A bank fails when losses from failed borrowers reach its capital; then its lenders lose next round. Formula (\(\lambda=0.8\) loss rate, \(\kappa=1\) capital multiplier):

\[\text{bank } j \text{ fails when }\sum_{i\text{ failed}}\lambda\,w_{ij}\;\ge\;\kappa\cdot\text{capital}_j\]

Worked numbers. One X fails: Y1 loses 4 of 10. Two: 8. Contained. All three: 12 ≥ 10, so Y1 fails; then Y2 (\(4+8=12\ge9\)) and Y3 (\(4+8=12\ge11\)) follow: six of six. With capital ×1.5, 12 < 15: only the three X banks fail.

Now try it. The toy opens with X1, X2 and X3 failing together. Tap X2 and X3 to switch them off: one failure stays one. Arrow X1 → Y1 means Y1 lent to X1.

What it means. Two failures are safe, three are not: a threshold. Granovetter’s riot has the same shape: replace one person of threshold 1 by one of threshold 2 and 100 rioters become 1, though “the two crowds are essentially identical” Granovetter 1978, pp. 1424–1425. The distribution decides, not the average (analogy, ours).

Ladder 4 · A share \(m\) of traders on one model

Instance. The market “gap” is price minus fair value (0 is fair). A share \(m\) of traders follow one model that chases the last move; the rest buy back toward 0. A shock of −1 hits after step 3. Question. For which \(m\) does the shock fade? Mechanism in words. New gap = old gap + the chasers’ push (1.5 × \(m\) × last move) − the buyers’ pull (0.5 × \((1-m)\) × old gap).

\[\text{gap}_{t+1}=\text{gap}_t+A\,m\,(\text{gap}_t-\text{gap}_{t-1})-G\,(1-m)\,\text{gap}_t,\quad A=1.5,\ G=0.5\]

Worked numbers, \(m=0.2\). Push factor \(Am=0.3\), pull factor \(G(1-m)=0.4\). Gap −1, last move −1: \(-1+0.3(-1)-0.4(-1)=-0.9\). Next, last move +0.1: \(-0.9+0.3(0.1)-0.4(-0.9)=-0.51\). Then −0.189: it dies out. At \(m=0.9\) the factors are 1.35 and 0.05: \(-1\to-2.30\to-3.94\), growing. Intuition: each step the chasers add \(Am\) times the last move. The swing shrinks when \(Am<1\) and grows when \(Am>1\): tipping point \(m=1/A=2/3\). The fold-out shows why.

Check. At \(m=0.2\): largest swing 0.04 up, −1.00 down, gap after 40 steps 0.000. What it means. The toy is about trend-following; shared models do not by themselves imply it. A circuit breaker bounds the swing (±3.00 at \(m=0.9\)) but not the feedback.

Look inside: why the swing grows when \(Am>1\)

Write the rule as \(g_{t+1}=b\,g_t-c\,g_{t-1}\) with \(b=1+Am-G(1-m)\) and \(c=Am\). A pattern \(g_t=x^t\) fits when \(x^2=bx-c\), that is \(x^2-bx+c=0\): the characteristic equation, whose roots \(x\) are the factors by which the pattern is multiplied each step. The two roots multiply to \(c\). For our numbers they are complex numbers (a wave, which needs \(b^2<4c\)), and each root then has size \(\sqrt c\). The wave shrinks if \(\sqrt c<1\) and grows if \(\sqrt c>1\): that is, \(Am<1\) or \(Am>1\), with the tipping point at \(m=2/3\). Numbers (code-checked with numpy.roots). \(m=0.2\): \(b=0.9\), \(c=0.3\), size 0.548. \(m=0.67\): \(c=1.005\), size 1.002. \(m=0.9\): \(b=2.3\), \(c=1.35\), size 1.162. Valid while the roots are complex, as they are for these \(m\).

Adds: failure needs no mistake, only correlation, then a threshold or a feedback gain. Does not explain: how many firms really share a model; the toys take the network, the share \(m\) and one \(\rho\) as given. Across lenses (ours): a shared model gives the supervisor of Lens 2 fewer kinds of disturbance, and the same shared model makes the damage arrive together.

9 · Through the lenses

Lens 4 · Social reality, accountability, personhood

structural analogy Searle’s status functions and post 01’s meaning lens. No math here.

A human-centred system (section 1) relies on acceptance, accountability and legal personhood. We use them to test a chain of Harari’s sentences. Link to post 01: there, the octopus asked whether meaning can come from form alone. Whether a token is money is not in its form either; it depends on what a community does with it. Only this lens shows that the human role at the end of the chain is acceptance and liability.

Acceptance: “X counts as Y in C”

Instance. A line of stones counts as a border wall in a village; paper from the Bureau of Engraving and Printing counts as money in the US SEP, Social Ontology, § 4.2.3. Mechanism in words. People collectively accept that the thing has a function it lacks by nature, and that brings deontic powers: rights, permissions, duties. Searle’s pattern, as reported by the SEP, is “X counts as Y in C”: a thing, a status, a context.

Prediction 3 · answer

Acceptance by a community, treated as carrying rights and duties. Clever code, an AI author or a blockchain do not decide it. Caution: Menger described money emerging from trading practice with no one choosing it SEP § 5.6; how central acceptance is remains debated.

Accountability

Instance (ours). You hire a broker to buy a bond. You are the principal (who delegates), the broker is the agent (who acts for you), and your alternative is to keep your cash: the status quo. Two things can hold. Knowledge: you can tell whether the bond beats cash. Incentive: the broker has a reason to be honest, for example he is paid only if you profit. With both, you buy good bonds. With neither, you buy whatever he sells. With one of the two, the worst case is that you keep your cash. Lupia and McCubbins state this pattern as Theorem 5.1 Lupia and McCubbins 1998, pp. 85–86: if neither holds, delegation fails. Institutions can supply the conditions: in their example, candidates supervised by a party leadership “may be subject to verification or a penalty for lying” p. 207.

Our fill-in (ours). An agent that “consistently hides the genuine reasons” for its trade Scheurer et al. 2024, abstract removes knowledge, as does an opaque instrument; AI “can easily mask who’s at fault” MIT Sloan 2023.

Personhood

Harari asks whether your country will recognise AIs as legal persons, as corporations already are: AIs “will soon be able to make the decisions necessary to manage a bank account” WEF 2026 [16:28]. A policy question, not a forecast. We have no legal sources and state no legal position.

Testing Harari’s chain

He asserts that money rests on stories: “What gives money value is the stories that bankers, finance ministers and cryptocurrency gurus tell us about it” Ynet 2023; at the BIS, “money is really trust” BIS 2024 [23:15]. He asserts that “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57]. In 2023 he asked what happens once AI is better than the average human at telling stories Ynet 2023. Our compression (ours): money is stories, so AI takes over money. The last step is ours; he does not state it in these sources.

Our test (ours). Each link meets a support. Acceptance: a story is not money until a community counts it as money. Enforcement: writing a contract is one thing, making a counterparty honour it another. His example, “if laws are made of words, then AI will take over the legal system” WEF 2026 [6:06], is where the test bites: laws are words, but their force rests on acceptance and enforcement. Harari himself limits the thesis to what is made of words and locates the human role in non-verbal feeling [8:07], [11:39]; our two supports are a different limit. Our reading: the words thesis is an assertion; what it implies for money and law depends on those supports.

The strongest version of his argument (ours). Drafting, summarising and interpreting are themselves words. If an AI does all of them better than people, the human role may shrink to acceptance and liability, and his personhood question asks who holds those. The test narrows the chain; it does not refute it.

Adds: “taking over money” splits into writing contracts (words) and giving them status. Does not explain: how any of it works in law.

100 people have thresholds 0, 1, …, 99: a person joins the riot once at least that many others are already rioting. Show that all 100 riot. Then swap the person of threshold 1 for one of threshold 2: how many riot?

Hint

Start with the person of threshold 0: they riot alone. Then ask who joins when 1 person is rioting, then when 2 are.

Solution

Person 0 riots alone. With 1 rioting, threshold 1 joins; with 2, threshold 2 joins; and so on up to 99: all 100 riot. After the swap the thresholds are 0, 2, 2, 3, …, 99. Person 0 riots, but the next person needs 2 others and only 1 is rioting. The riot stops at 1 person.

13 · Synthesis

So what happens? Short answer first

Short answer (our reading, not a finding)

(a) Can AI create a new kind of finance? Not yet evidenced. LLMs now translate plain language into fixed contracts and agents hold wallets, but we found no traded instrument that an AI invented. Nothing shows it is impossible. (b) What would it do to a human-centred system? The nearer risk we find most plausible is ordinary instruments run on a few shared models, so losses arrive together and fall on whoever lent to or holds the failed firms; our toys do not model bailouts. People stay accountable only if someone can verify what the AI did and be penalised when it fails, and an agent that hides its reasons weakens both. Supervisors could still demand standard contracts, build their own AI and add trading pauses; they could not check what nobody can state in a page, or foresee a shared model’s failures.

Harari asserts that AI can already understand finance better than us and that anything made of words will be taken over by AI; he imagines AI-made financial devices that humans cannot follow; he asks what we should do before it is too late. Our reading: his forecasts about AI-made devices are conditionals, his capability claims are assertions, and our supports (acceptance, enforcement, personhood) test the chain without refuting it.

Our reading (ours, qualitative)

The weakest link in Harari’s chain is AI-invented instruments: we found no traded case. The most plausible near-term risk we find is correlated, opaque use of ordinary instruments and agents, not an alien instrument. This is our judgment, not a finding: correlation is not measured for LLMs, and Scheurer’s authors draw no conclusion about how likely hiding is in practice. Signs to watch:

  1. Up: a traded instrument whose issuer’s staff cannot state its payoff rule in a page. Down: only translators inside fixed vocabularies.
  2. Up: a few base models underlie most AI desks and their errors correlate (measure \(\rho\)). Down: varied strategies.
  3. Up: authorities lag in building their own AI. Down: standard contract menus and trading pauses spread.
  4. Up: a jurisdiction lets an AI hold accounts with no responsible human. Down: liability stays with people.
  5. Up: incident reports show hidden reasoning. Down: verification and penalties for agents.
Layers, conditionals, tension and critics

Four layers, from most certain to most contested

  1. The contract space is huge. Certain (same-math): 10,000 contracts in a four-word village; short descriptions are rare among possible contracts.
  2. Opacity favours the better-informed side. Supported in theory, with named conditions (Akerlof; Arora et al.: conjectured hardness, simple model).
  3. A shared model correlates failures. Shown in toys; flagged as a risk (“could”) by regulators; not measured for LLMs.
  4. Whether this changes who rules. Contested: acceptance, liability, personhood.

A tension between lenses (ours)

Correlation makes simultaneous failure likelier (Lens 3) yet leaves the supervisor fewer kinds of disturbance to cover (Lens 2). Rarity of simple contracts (Lens 1) is not opacity (Arora). The lenses disagree about what is dangerous in shared models.

Five conditionals

  • If an AI designs instruments by optimising payoffs and the seller knows more, pricing can become hard (Arora; conjectured assumption).
  • If many firms run one model, their errors coincide and diversification shrinks (\(N_{\text{eff}}\); FSB).
  • If supervisors cannot raise their variety or cut the menu, outcomes stay varied (Ashby).
  • If the principal can check the agent, or the agent has reason to be honest, the worst case is the status quo; if neither holds, delegation fails (Lupia and McCubbins).
  • If a community accepts an AI-run entity’s tokens as money, they count as money in that community (ours, after Searle via the SEP); who is liable is a separate legal question.

Critics, one line each

Poole: “no one has yet seen a chatbot create new ideas” (so invention is the weak link: our reading). Narayanan and Kapoor: in the world they describe, impacts are slow and control stays with people; they call it their “median outcome”, not a certainty. FSB: frameworks address many vulnerabilities, but “more work may be needed”; new-revenue uses are “not widely observed at present”, though advances “could change this”. Skingsley: we already trust what we only vaguely understand. Harari’s own remedy: institutions and “humans in the loop”. Fox: profit is the missing driver (ours).
