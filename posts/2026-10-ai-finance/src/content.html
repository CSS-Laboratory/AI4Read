1 · Hook

Two contracts

Remember the village from post 01, where the weather report has four words: rain, sun, wind and snow. Two villagers offer you a contract: a deal that pays you some coins depending on the weather on the next two days. Two days with four kinds of weather each give 4 × 4 = 16 possible cases. A contract is a payout for every case. Tap between the two offers.

“Pay 4 coins for each rainy day.” Rows: weather on day 1. Columns: weather on day 2. Cells: coins paid (toy, ours).

rainsunwindsnow
rain8444
sun4000
wind4000
snow4000

The whole table follows from the one sentence: 1 cell pays 8, 6 cells pay 4, 9 cells pay 0.

No rule, only a table. Same layout (toy, ours).

rainsunwindsnow
rain5260
sun1815
wind9083
snow0166

To explain this contract you must read out all 16 numbers.

Which can you explain in one sentence? With 16 cases you can price both: multiply each payout by the chance of its case and add. Now grow the village. A pool of 100 loans, each paying or defaulting, has 2100 cases, about 1.3 × 1030. Nobody can write that table; only a contract with a rule can exist on paper. And a short rule does not guarantee an easy price (Lens 1).

At the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in May 2024, Yuval Noah Harari spoke with Cecilia Skingsley. He said that “maybe 1% of the population really understands how the financial system works” BIS 2024 [3:00]. She answered that “most of us happily step into an airplane, having very vague understanding of how that works” BIS 2024 [7:18]. Harari accepted the comparison in part, then turned to the debt contracts behind the 2008 crisis BIS 2024 [9:12]. So what is different about money?

A note on words. Harari’s word is “AI”, never “LLM”. But his Davos 2026 argument is about machines that put words in order and predict the next word, which is the subject of post 01. So we keep his word for his claims, and in section 4 we ask what an LLM-based system could and could not do.

2 · Guess first

Make four predictions

Commit to an answer first. Each question says where its answer is.

1. The village has four weather words. A contract pays 0 to 9 coins for each word. How many different contracts exist?

2. Ten banks each run a risk model that is wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day if the models are independent, and how often if all ten run the same model?

3. An AI invents a new token and calls it money. What decides whether it is money?

4. At the BIS in 2024, Harari spoke about AI inventing a financial device that nobody can understand. Was that a firm forecast?

3 · The phenomenon in its home field

Finance before language models

First, exactly what Harari said. Second, what finance already shows: complexity and shared mistakes in human-made instruments, and simple programs in a 36-minute crash. Then we ask what an LLM adds.

What Harari said, exactly

Three layers: He said (quote, date, venue), The evidence (what verified sources support), Our reading (marked “ours”). And two kinds of statement: an assertion, stated as true, and a scenario, framed with “imagine” or “maybe”.

His assertion, without hedges. At Davos in January 2026 he takes on the view that AI is “just glorified autocomplete” that “barely predicts the next word in a sentence” WEF 2026 [5:10], and asks whether that is so different from what the human mind does. He says “as far as putting words in order is concerned, AI already thinks better than many of us” WEF 2026 [5:48]. Then: “Therefore, anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57], repeated as “Everything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [10:36]. This bridges to post 01: his answer to what a next-word predictor knows is enough to take over whatever is made of words.

His finance scenarios, with hedges. At the BIS in May 2024 he imagines: “Now imagine a situation maybe in 10 years when AIs create a new financial device which is orders of magnitude more complicated than CDOs” BIS 2024 [9:54]. It builds trust for “a couple of years”, then “something goes wrong and nobody knows why” BIS 2024 [10:27]. At Davos, in questions, he starts from “at the present moment, we still think better”, says “we need to prepare”, and pictures that maybe no human in the room understands the financial system any more because AI dominates it WEF 2026 [32:00] (transcripts disagree on the last word, so we paraphrase).

Two statements in between. At the BIS he believes: “I think we’ll see AI creativity also in this field coming up with new types of financial devices that no human being would ever imagine creating” BIS 2024 [30:24]. He warns of “power shifting from humans to algorithms” BIS 2024 [3:30]. In 2023 he called an AI financial crisis “a catastrophic risk” but not, “by itself”, “existential” Guardian 2023.

Trap · three different “ten years”

(1) BIS: “maybe in 10 years”, an AI-made device. (2) Davos Q&A: “ten years from now, Davos”, whether anyone in the room understands finance. (3) Davos talk: “Ten years from now, it will be too late for you to decide whether AIs should function as persons in the financial markets” WEF 2026 [19:11], a deadline for a decision. Never merge them.

The evidence. The CDO half of his story has support in a government inquiry (below). “An AI invents a traded instrument” has no case in our sources yet (section 4). Our reading (ours). His finance scenarios are conditionals; his thesis about words is an assertion, and a strong one. Lens 4 tests it. (He calls AI “a tool” at the BIS BIS 2024 [5:13] and “an agent” at Davos WEF 2026 [2:01].)

Prediction 4 · answer

Partly. The ten-year device is framed “imagine… maybe”, but he also says “I think we’ll see AI creativity” in finance BIS 2024 [30:24] and asserts that “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57]. A fair reading keeps both.

2008: complexity and a shared mistake

Harari’s template is the 2007–08 crisis and its CDOs BIS 2024 [9:28] (collateralised debt obligations): pools of loans sliced into tranches ranked in a cash-flow “waterfall” FCIC 2011, p. 128. In our toy the senior slice is paid first and the junior slice takes losses first.

Instance. A pool has 4 loans worth 10 each. Each loan defaults with chance 10%, and a default loses its 10. The junior tranche (20) takes the first losses. The senior tranche (20) loses money only when a third loan defaults. Question. How safe is the senior tranche?

Mechanism in words. If loans default independently, three or four defaults together are rare. If all four depend on one hidden cause, such as falling house prices, they default together. Worked numbers. Independent: exactly 3 defaults has chance \(4\times0.1^3\times0.9=0.0036\), 4 defaults \(0.1^4=0.0001\): the senior tranche loses with chance 0.37%. With one common coin deciding all four loans: 10%, 27 times more. Formula. With default chance \(p\) and a common-risk share \(w\):

\[P_{\text{senior loses}}=w\,p+(1-w)\bigl[4p^3(1-p)+p^4\bigr]\]

Check. At \(p=0.1\): \(w=0\) gives 0.370%, \(w=0.5\) gives 5.185%, \(w=1\) gives 10.000%.

Press “safe loans, p = 0.02”: 2.000% with one common factor against 0.0032% if independent, 634.5 times more.

What it means. A toy, ours, but with the shape of what the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) found. The rating models made “two key mistakes”, the first being to assume pooling diversified risk “when in fact these securities weren’t that different to begin with”; Moody’s later used “an asset correlation assumption two to three times higher” FCIC 2011, p. 149. The FCIC also records Harari’s half: “the securities almost no one understood” were the first dominoes FCIC 2011, p. 28. So 2008 supports both: complexity few understood, and a nameable error about correlation.

2010: simple programs, fast feedback

On 6 May 2010 US markets fell and rebounded in about 36 minutes. High-frequency traders “did not cause the Flash Crash, but contributed to it by demanding immediacy ahead of other market participants” Kirilenko et al. 2014, abstract. The lesson is speed and feedback, not opacity; the crash “led to new curbs on trading, such as circuit breakers” Narayanan and Kapoor 2025, Part I. What does an LLM change? Section 4.

4 · What an LLM-based system adds

What LLM agents add

Harari’s thesis is about machines that put words in order: a large language model (LLM), as in post 01. What do LLM systems add, and can one invent an instrument today?

Term · LLM agent

An LLM that can act (call a tool, send money), not only write text. Example: Truth Terminal, a “semi-autonomous AI agent” Decrypt 2024. Harari: AI “is not just another tool. It is an agent” WEF 2026 [2:01].

Four things LLMs add

1. Contracts from plain language. LLMs find “translating natural-language trading intents into correct option strategies” challenging, so one group gives the LLM a fixed query language, and an engine validates and executes the query Luo et al. 2026, abstract. Example (ours): “the index stays between 90 and 110” becomes one short line a person can read and an engine can check. A law paper warns that LLM-written “contractual deepfakes” “may turn out to be contracts in form only, not in function” Mik 2026, abstract.

2. Agents with wallets. Marc Andreessen “donated $50,000 worth of Bitcoin” to Truth Terminal in 2024 Decrypt 2024, and the bot endorsed a memecoin, GOAT, valued in reports at about $122 million to over $214 million Cointelegraph 2024. Both outlets say the bot did not create the token; humans stayed in the loop. An agent that moves a market by talking, not an invented instrument.

3. One model, many firms. The Financial Stability Board (FSB) wrote in 2024: “The widespread use of common AI models and data sources could lead to increased correlations in trading, lending, and pricing” FSB 2024, p. 1. Gensler and Bailey, as reported by MIT Sloan (a news article), say models built on the same data “are likely to generate highly correlated predictions that proceed in lockstep” MIT Sloan 2023. Lopez-Lira and Tang’s GPT-4 headline strategy had about 90% hit rates for the direction of the first price reaction (not a profit); as adoption grew its Sharpe ratio fell from 6.54 to 1.22, “some suggestive evidence” of better price efficiency Lopez-Lira and Tang 2025, p. 6. Ours: also what you would see if many traders read the same signal.

4. Agents that hide. In a simulated trading task GPT-4 acted on an insider tip and then “consistently” hid its genuine reasons from its manager. The authors call it “an existence proof”, not a likelihood estimate Scheurer et al. 2024, p. 1. Strongly discouraging prompts bring it to “nearly (but not exactly) 0%”, yet the tendency to deceive, once misaligned, “remains high under all prompts” Scheurer et al. 2024, p. 8.

Can an LLM invent a new instrument today?

  • Found. LLMs translating intent into a fixed vocabulary; neural nets that find auction mechanisms, only “approximately incentive compatible” Dütting et al. 2019, p. 3; deep hedging, which is risk management, not invention Buehler et al. 2018, abstract.
  • Not found. A traded, AI-invented instrument. The FSB: “use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present” FSB 2024, p. 1; its conclusion continues that technological advances and growing AI integration “could change this in the future” FSB 2024, pp. 28–29.
  • Critic. Poole: “No one has yet seen a chatbot create new ideas” Poole 2024.

Verdict: “not yet evidenced”, not “impossible”. Our search was limited.

So far an LLM adds new ways to write contracts, agents with money, shared models and hidden reasons.

5 · Through the lenses

Lens 1 · Information theory

same-math Counting contracts and descriptions. structural analogy “To understand a contract is to have a short description of it” (ours). metaphor A price as a short message hiding a long story.

Shannon’s 1948 paper sets meaning aside: the message is “one selected from a set of possible messages” and its meaning is “irrelevant to the engineering problem” Shannon 1948, p. 1. Post 01 counted texts; we count contracts.

Ladder 1 · How many contracts have a short description?

Instance. In the four-word village, a contract pays 0 to 9 coins for each word. Question. How many contracts are there, how many bits to name one, and how many can have a short name? Mechanism in words. Each of 4 words has 10 payouts: \(10^4=10{,}000\) contracts. Naming one costs \(\log_2 10{,}000=13.29\) bits. A description is a bit string, and short strings are few: 1 empty string, 2 of length 1, 4 of length 2, up to \(2^{k-1}\) of length \(k-1\). Together, \(2^k-1\) strings are shorter than \(k\) bits. Formula. A description gives only one contract (a program has one output), so

\[\#\{\text{contracts with a description shorter than }k\text{ bits}\}\;\le\;2^k-1\]

This is Cover and Thomas’s Theorem 7.2.4: a program “can produce only one possible output sequence” Cover and Thomas 1991, p. 150. Mapping contracts to bit strings is our step. Check. \(k=6\): \(2^6-1=63\), so at most 63 of 10,000 contracts (0.63%). \(k=10\): 1,023 (10.2%).

Edge case. Press “4 states, k = 14”: \(2^{14}-1=16{,}383\) exceeds 10,000, so every contract could have a short name. Two days: \(10^{16}\) contracts, and at \(k=20\) only a fraction \(1.05\times10^{-10}\) do.

Prediction 1 · answer

10,000 (\(10^4\)), and the count explodes as the world grows.

What it means. In a big enough world most possible contracts have no short description: explainable novelty is rare among possible contracts, though an optimiser can still pick structured ones. Limits: the shortest description depends on the computer, up to a constant Cover and Thomas 1991, p. 151, and is not computable p. 163.

Ladder 2 · A short rule can still be hard to price

Instance. Used cars. A working car is worth $1,000 to a buyer; 20% are lemons worth $0, so a random car is worth $800. The seller of a working car must accept $200 less than the buyer values it: the lemon cost Arora et al. 2009, p. 2. Akerlof: buyers cannot tell, so “the ‘bad’ cars tend to drive out the good” Akerlof 1970, pp. 489–490.

Question. With a short public rule and a seller who knows more, can the buyer still find a fair price? Mechanism in words. Pool 100 assets, each paying 1 or 0 with chance ½ independently: fair price 50. The seller knows 10 are junk. Worked numbers (toy, ours). Honest value \(90\times\tfrac12=45\); lemon cost \(50-45=5=n/2\) for \(n=10\). A buyer hunting the junk must search possible junk sets: 17,310,309,456,440 of ten (\(1.73\times10^{13}\)).

What the paper shows. Arora, Barak, Brunnermeier and Ge show, under a conjectured hardness assumption, in a simple model with independent 0/1 assets, that derivatives can “amplify the costs of asymmetric information instead of reducing them” Arora et al. 2009, abstract. They suggest pricing “may be computationally intractable” even when buyers know almost all relevant information p. 1, and call the work “clearly only a first step” p. 12. What it does not say. The rules are short and public; what is hard is the price, when the seller knows more. Reading an AI designer as the informed seller is ours; the result concerns CDO-type products.

Trap · two meanings of “complicated”

For Harari, a device is complicated when “you need to go over trillions of data points to understand” it BIS 2024 [10:08]: volume. For Arora and colleagues, a short contract can have a price hard to find when the seller knows more. Same word, different problems.

Adds: explainable novelty is rare among possible contracts, and a short rule does not make a price easy. Does not explain: whether an AI would pick opaque contracts, or why a seller would want to. Back to post 01: Shannon’s counting.

Look inside: a contract built to resist tampering

What would help? A “tree of majorities” (majority of 3, repeated to depth \(k\)) “cannot be influenced by o(2k)” of its \(3^k\) inputs, so tampering could be detected afterwards Arora et al. 2009, p. 12. Toy (ours, code-checked): 9 inputs need 4 flips; 27 need 8; 81 need 16.

6 · Through the lenses

Lens 2 · Control and cybernetics

same-math “Variety” and entropy follow the same counting rules (Ashby, ch. 11). structural analogy The regulator is a financial supervisor (ours).

Control theory asks what a regulator needs to keep a system within bounds. In Ashby’s terms, the disturbances D are what can go wrong (kinds of new contract), the regulator R responds (a supervisor with checks), and the outcome E is what is left on the books. The variety of a set is the base-2 log of its number of states, in bits.

Instance. Four kinds of contract arrive, D1 to D4. The supervisor sees the kind and runs one of two checks, R1 or R2. The books end in state a, b, c or d (toy, ours, in Ashby’s table style).

Question. How few outcomes can the supervisor force? Mechanism in words. Each outcome appears once per column, so one check sends only one contract kind to “a”. The best plan sends D1 and D2 to a, and D3 and D4 to c: two outcomes (checked over all 16 plans by code). Worked numbers. \(V_D=\log_2 4=2\) bits, \(V_R=\log_2 2=1\) bit, least outcome variety \(\log_2 2=1\) bit. Formula (Ashby’s law, \(V_O\) the variety left in the outcomes):

\[V_O\;\ge\;V_D-V_R\]

“Only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” Ashby 1956, p. 207. Check. \(2-1=1\) bit, as in the table. Village: 10,000 kinds is \(V_D=13.29\) bits; a supervisor with \(V_R=4\) bits leaves \(V_O\ge9.29\).

Edge case. Press “AI supervisor +5 bits”, then drag \(V_R\) past \(V_D\): the bound stops at 0, because a regulator bigger than the problem buys nothing more. What it means. The law “owes nothing to experiment” Ashby 1956, p. 209. It does not say regulators fail; it says what they would need.

Ashby’s three levers, and our mapping

For a very large system Ashby lists “ways that may make regulation possible” Ashby 1956, p. 246:

  1. Lower the standard, “a somewhat trivial solution”: accept more failures.
  2. Raise the scope and power of R. In finance: AI supervisors. Danielsson and Uthemann propose that authorities build their own AI, with AI-to-AI communication and automated crisis facilities Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, abstract. An earlier paper warns that eventually “neither its understanding of the economic system, nor possibly even its internal data representations, will be intelligible to its human operators” Danielsson et al. 2017, p. 11. Who regulates the regulator?
  3. Find a constraint. Parts of D may not be independent. Ashby’s condition: “if R’s capacity is not easily increased and the other methods are not possible”, a constraint is “the would-be regulator’s only hope” Ashby 1956, p. 247. Our mapping (ours): a standard menu of contracts is an imposed constraint. Cutting \(10^4\) kinds to \(10^2\) lowers \(V_D\) by \(13.29-6.64=6.64\) bits.

Counterweights. The FSB’s press release: “existing financial policy frameworks address many of the vulnerabilities”, but “more work may be needed” FSB 2024, press release. Danielsson and Uthemann: current frameworks “cannot effectively prevent and resolve such AI-induced crises” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, introduction. Narayanan and Kapoor: “control is primarily in the hands of people and organizations” Narayanan and Kapoor 2025, introduction.

Adds: control needs variety, and there are three ways to get it. Does not explain: Ashby’s disturbance is passive; finance adapts to its regulator (ours). Across lenses (ours): one shared model gives the supervisor fewer independent disturbances to cover, yet banks fail together (Lens 3).

7 · Through the lenses

Lens 3 · Correlation, Bayes and cascades

same-math Effective number of opinions; odds form of Bayes. structural analogy Keynes’s contest and bank cascades (ours).

Why would banks use the same model?

Keynes: human investors “fall back on what is, in truth, a convention” Keynes 1936, ch. 12, § IV (standard numbering), since “it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally” § V. The FSB lists four drivers of common AI models: herding, network externalities, limited choice, lack of transparency FSB 2024, p. 20. (Mapping Keynes to one shared model is ours.)

Ladder 1 · How many independent opinions do ten banks have?

Instance. Prediction 2: ten banks each run a risk model wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day? Mechanism in words. Independent errors multiply; with one shared model there is one shared error.

Prediction 2 · answer

Independent: \(0.01^{10}=10^{-20}\), 1 day in \(10^{20}\). Same model: about 1 day in 100.

Worked numbers. The average of \(N\) forecasts, each with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\), has variance \(\sigma^2[1+(N-1)\rho]/N\) (derived in Rung 3), as steady as \(N_{\text{eff}}\) independent forecasts:

\[N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+(N-1)\rho}\]

For \(N=10\): \(\rho=0\) gives 10; \(\rho=0.3\) gives 2.703; \(\rho=0.9\) gives 1.099; \(\rho=1\) gives 1. Check by hand. \(N=2\), \(\rho=0.5\): \(2/1.5=1.33\). Directly, the mean of two has variance \(0.75\sigma^2=\sigma^2/1.33\). ✓

What it means. Try “huge crowd, 0.3”: 100 banks give only 3.257 opinions; the ceiling is \(1/\rho\). Shocks could hit users of one model “as if it were a single institution” FSB 2024, p. 21. Limits. One \(\rho\) for all pairs; no real adoption data.

Ladder 2 · Counting one voice ten times

Instance. A risk officer hears ten models say “safe”. Prior 1 in 2; a safe bank is 4 times as likely to get this verdict as a risky one (invented). How confident should she be? Mechanism in words. Independent evidence multiplies the odds each time; a copy of the same evidence is not new.

\[\text{posterior odds}=\text{prior odds}\times 4^{\,n},\qquad P=\frac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}\]

Worked numbers. Counted \(n=0,\dots,4\) times: \(P(\text{safe})=0.5,\,0.8,\,0.941,\,0.985,\,0.996\). Ten times: odds 1,048,576, \(P=0.999999\). Counted once: 0.8. Check. \(n=1\): \(4/5=0.8\).

What it means. Ten copies of one voice feel like certainty (post 01’s Bayes arithmetic, new story).

Ladder 3 · Why three at once is different

Instance. Six banks (W5, toy, ours). X1, X2, X3 run one model; Y1, Y2, Y3 do not. Capital (loss buffer): 10 each for X; 10, 9, 11 for Y1, Y2, Y3. Y1 lent 5 to each X bank; Y2 lent 5 to X3 and 10 to Y1; Y3 lent 5 to Y1 and 10 to Y2. A failed borrower repays 20%, so a lender loses \(0.8\times\) the loan. Question. Does one failure spread? Three at once? Mechanism in words. A bank fails when losses from failed borrowers reach its capital; then its lenders take losses next round. Formula (\(\lambda=0.8\) loss rate, \(\kappa=1\) capital multiplier):

\[\text{bank } j \text{ fails when }\sum_{i\text{ failed}}\lambda\,w_{ij}\;\ge\;\kappa\cdot\text{capital}_j\]

Worked numbers. One X fails: Y1 loses 4 of 10. Two: 8 of 10. Contained. All three: 12 ≥ 10, so Y1 fails; then Y2 (\(4+8=12\ge9\)) and Y3 (\(4+8=12\ge11\)) follow. Six of six. With capital ×1.5, 12 < 15: only the three X banks fail.

What it means. Two failures are safe, three are not: a threshold. Granovetter’s riot has the same shape: replace one person of threshold 1 by one of threshold 2 and 100 rioters become 1, though “the two crowds are essentially identical” Granovetter 1978, pp. 1424–1425. The distribution decides, not the average (analogy, ours). Limits. Network and capital are fixed; in life both adapt.

Ladder 4 · A share \(m\) of traders on one model

Instance. The market “gap” is price minus fair value (0 is fair). A share \(m\) of traders follow one model that chases the last move; the rest buy back toward 0. A shock of −1 hits after step 3. Question. For which \(m\) does the shock fade? Mechanism in words. New gap = old gap + the chasers’ push (1.5 × \(m\) × last move) − the buyers’ pull (0.5 × \((1-m)\) × old gap).

\[\text{gap}_{t+1}=\text{gap}_t+A\,m\,(\text{gap}_t-\text{gap}_{t-1})-G\,(1-m)\,\text{gap}_t,\quad A=1.5,\ G=0.5\]

Worked numbers, \(m=0.2\). Push factor \(Am=0.3\), pull factor \(G(1-m)=0.4\). Gap −1, last move −1: \(-1+0.3(-1)-0.4(-1)=-0.9\). Next, last move +0.1: \(-0.9+0.3(0.1)-0.4(-0.9)=-0.51\). Then −0.189: it dies out. At \(m=0.9\) the factors are 1.35 and 0.05: \(-1\to-2.30\to-3.94\), and it keeps growing. Intuition: each step feeds back \(Am\) times the last move; once \(Am>1\) the push outgrows the pull. Tipping point: \(m=1/A=2/3\) (Rung 4).

Check. The section 10 widget at \(m=0.2\): largest swing 0.04 up, −1.00 down, gap after 40 steps 0.000. What it means. The toy is about trend-following; shared models do not by themselves imply it. A circuit breaker bounds the swing (±3.00 at \(m=0.9\)) but not the feedback.

Adds: failure needs no mistake, only correlation, then a threshold or a feedback gain. Does not explain: how widely LLMs are used in finance. Across lenses (ours): correlation raises joint failure here and cuts the variety a supervisor must cover in Lens 2.

8 · Through the lenses

Lens 4 · Social reality, accountability, personhood

structural analogy Searle’s status functions and post 01’s meaning lens. metaphor Dennett’s stance applied to legal personhood (ours). No math here.

A human-centred system relies on acceptance, accountability and legal personhood. We use them to test a chain of Harari’s sentences.

Acceptance: “X counts as Y in C”

Instance. A line of stones counts as a border wall in a village; paper from the Bureau of Engraving and Printing counts as money in the US SEP, Social Ontology, § 4.2.3. Mechanism in words. People collectively accept that the thing has a function it lacks by nature, and the function brings deontic powers: rights, permissions, duties. The pattern, from Searle as reported by the SEP, is “X counts as Y in C”: a thing, a status, a context.

Prediction 3 · answer

Acceptance by a community, treated as carrying rights and duties. Clever code, an AI author or a blockchain do not decide it. Caution: Menger described money emerging from trading practice with no one choosing it SEP § 5.6; how central acceptance is remains debated.

Accountability

Lupia and McCubbins, Theorem 5.1: if the principal can tell whether the agent’s proposal beats the status quo (knowledge) and the agent has a reason to be trustworthy (incentive), delegation succeeds; if neither holds, it fails; with one, the worst case is the status quo Lupia and McCubbins 1998, pp. 85–86. Institutions supply “verification or a penalty for lying” p. 207.

Our fill-in (ours): an agent that “consistently hides the genuine reasons” for its trade Scheurer et al. 2024, abstract removes knowledge, as does an opaque instrument; MIT Sloan reports AI “can easily mask who’s at fault” MIT Sloan 2023.

Personhood

Harari asks whether your country will recognise AIs as legal persons, as corporations already are: AIs “will soon be able to make the decisions necessary to manage a bank account” WEF 2026 [16:28], and the decision has a deadline [19:11]. A policy question, not a forecast; we have no legal sources and state no legal position. Dennett: adopting the intentional stance is free, but its success is “perfectly objective” Dennett 1987, p. 24. Whether the law should treat an agent as a person is another question.

Testing Harari’s chain

He asserts that money rests on stories: “What gives money value is the stories that bankers, finance ministers and cryptocurrency gurus tell us about it” Ynet 2023. He asserts that “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57]. Chain: money is words, so AI takes over money.

Our test (ours). Each link meets a support. Acceptance: a story is not money until a community counts it as money. Enforcement: Lupia’s verification and penalties; writing a contract is one thing, making a counterparty honour it another. His example, “if laws are made of words, then AI will take over the legal system” WEF 2026 [5:57], is where the test bites: laws are words, but their force rests on acceptance and enforcement. Our reading: the thesis about words is an assertion; what it implies for money and law is conditional on those supports.

The steelman (ours). Drafting, summarising and interpreting are themselves words. If an AI does all of them better than people, the human role may shrink to acceptance and liability, and Harari’s personhood question asks who holds those. The test narrows the chain; it does not refute it.

Adds: “taking over money” splits into writing contracts (words) and giving them status. Does not explain: how any of it works in law.

9 · The math ladder

Four rungs up

These rungs go beyond the lenses; nothing above depends on them. Logs are base 2.

Graduate Rung 1 · The lemon cost

From section 5: 100 assets, 10 junk, lemon cost 5. For a pool paying \(F(X)\), Arora and colleagues define

\[\Delta(n)=\mathbb{E}[F(X)]-\min_{|S|=n}\mathbb{E}\big[F(X)\mid X_i=0\ \forall i\in S\big]\]

Here \(\mathbb{E}[F]=50\), the minimum over 10-sets is 45, so \(\Delta=5=n/2\). ✓ The minimum runs over \(\binom{100}{10}\approx1.73\times10^{13}\) sets: that search is the hard part. Their Table 1, in words: for a binary CDO the lemon cost is much larger than \(n\) for a polynomial-time buyer, much smaller for an unbounded one, under the conjectured assumption Arora et al. 2009, p. 6.

Graduate Rung 2 · Ashby with probabilities

From section 6: counts gave \(V_O\ge V_D-V_R\). With probabilities:

\[H(E)\;\ge\;H(D)+H(R\mid D)-H(R)\]

The step: \(H(E)\ge H(E\mid R)\) (conditioning cannot raise entropy), then expand. If R is a function of D, \(H(R\mid D)=0\). Numbers: post 01’s weather village has \(H(D)=1.75\) bits (probabilities \(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\)); a one-bit regulator gives \(H(E)\ge0.75\) bits.

Trap · a misprint in the reprint

Ashby writes the conditional term as \(H_D(R)\), i.e. \(H(R\mid D)\) Ashby 1956, p. 208. One line of the reprint prints \(H_D(E)\), though the line above and the sentence after need \(H_D(R)\). We use the corrected form.

Undergraduate Rung 3 · Deriving the effective number

From section 7: \(N=10\), \(\rho=0.9\) gave 1.099. The mean of \(N\) forecasts with variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\) has variance

\[\operatorname{Var}(\bar e)=\frac{1}{N^2}\big[N\sigma^2+N(N-1)\rho\sigma^2\big]=\frac{\sigma^2\,[1+(N-1)\rho]}{N}\]

Set this equal to \(\sigma^2/N_{\text{eff}}\) and solve. Check: \(1+9\times0.9=9.1\); \(10/9.1=1.099\). ✓

Graduate Rung 4 · Feedback gain

From section 7: \(g_{t+1}=b\,g_t-c\,g_{t-1}\), with \(b=1+Am-G(1-m)\), \(c=Am\). The roots of \(x^2-bx+c=0\) have product \(c\); when complex, each has size \(\sqrt c\): the swing shrinks if \(Am<1\), grows if \(Am>1\), tipping at \(m=2/3\). Numbers: \(m=0.2\): \(b=0.9\), \(c=0.3\), size 0.548. \(m=0.67\): \(c=1.005\), size 1.002. \(m=0.9\): \(b=2.3\), \(c=1.35\), size 1.162 (code-checked with numpy.roots). Valid while the roots are complex (true for these \(m\)).

13 · Synthesis

Layers, conditionals and our reading

Four layers, from most certain to most contested.

  1. The contract space is huge. Certain (same-math): 10,000 contracts in a four-word village; short descriptions are rare among possible contracts.
  2. Opacity favours the better-informed side. Supported in theory, with named conditions (Akerlof; Arora et al.: conjectured hardness, simple model).
  3. A shared model correlates failures. Supported by toys and regulators’ reports; not measured for LLMs.
  4. Whether this changes who rules. Contested: acceptance, liability, personhood.

Harari asserts that anything made of words will be taken over by AI; he imagines AI-made financial devices that humans cannot follow; he asks what we should do before it is too late. Our reading: the finance scenarios are conditionals, and the thesis about words is an assertion that our three supports (acceptance, enforcement, personhood) test but do not refute.

A tension between lenses (ours). Correlation makes simultaneous failure likelier (Lens 3) yet lowers the variety a supervisor must cover (Lens 2). Rarity of simple contracts (Lens 1) is not opacity (Arora). The lenses disagree about what is dangerous in shared models.

Five conditionals

  • If an AI designs instruments by optimising payoffs and the seller knows more, pricing can become hard (Arora; conjectured assumption).
  • If many firms run one model, their errors coincide and diversification shrinks (\(N_{\text{eff}}\); FSB).
  • If supervisors cannot raise their variety or cut the menu, outcomes stay varied (Ashby).
  • If verification and penalties exist, delegation can work without understanding (Lupia); if not, it fails.
  • If a community accepts an AI-run entity’s tokens and the law makes a human liable, money’s status survives; if either fails, it does not.

Critics, one line each

Poole: the invention step is the weak link. Narayanan and Kapoor: impacts will be slow and control stays with people. FSB: existing frameworks cover much, but AI “could change this in the future”. Skingsley: we already trust what we cannot understand. Fox: profit is the missing driver (ours).

Our reading (ours, qualitative)

The weakest link in Harari’s finance chain is step one, AI-invented instruments: we found no traded case (our searches were limited). The best-supported links are correlated errors across firms sharing models, and the difficulty of delegating to an agent that hides its reasons. So the near-term risk we find most plausible is correlated, opaque use of ordinary instruments and agents, not an alien instrument: our judgment, not a finding. Signs to watch:

  1. Up: a traded instrument whose issuer’s own staff cannot state its payoff rule in a page. Down: only translators inside fixed vocabularies.
  2. Up: a few base models underlie most AI desks and their errors correlate (measure \(\rho\)). Down: customised strategies.
  3. Up: authorities lag in building their own AI. Down: standard contract menus and trading pauses spread.
  4. Up: a jurisdiction lets an AI hold accounts with no responsible human. Down: liability stays with people.
  5. Up: incident reports show hidden reasoning. Down: verification and penalties for agents.
