1 · Hook

Two contracts

Remember the village from post 01, where the weather report has four words: rain, sun, wind and snow. Two villagers offer you a contract: a deal that pays you some coins depending on the weather on the next two days. Two days with four kinds of weather each give 4 × 4 = 16 possible cases. A contract is a payout for every case. Tap between the two offers.

“Pay 4 coins for each rainy day.” Rows: weather on day 1. Columns: weather on day 2. Cells: coins paid (toy, ours).

rainsunwindsnow
rain8444
sun4000
wind4000
snow4000

One sentence gives the whole table: 1 cell pays 8, 6 pay 4, 9 pay 0.

No rule, only a table. Same layout (toy, ours).

rainsunwindsnow
rain5260
sun1815
wind9083
snow0166

To explain this contract you must read out all 16 numbers.

Which can you explain in one sentence? To price a contract, multiply each payout by the chance of its case and add. With equal chances of 1/16, Contract A is worth (8 + 6 × 4)/16 = 2 coins. Now grow the village: a pool of 100 loans, each paying or defaulting, has 2100 cases, about 1.3 × 1030. Nobody can write that table; only a contract with a rule can exist on paper. And a short rule does not guarantee an easy price (Lens 1).

At the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) in May 2024, Yuval Noah Harari spoke with Cecilia Skingsley. He said that “maybe 1% of the population really understands how the financial system works” BIS 2024 [3:00]. She answered that “most of us happily step into an airplane, having very vague understanding of how that works” BIS 2024 [7:18]. Harari replied that 1% and 0% understanding are “a very big difference”, called her example “perfect”, and turned to the 2008 crisis BIS 2024 [8:59]–[9:28]. So what is different about money?

Term · human-centred system

In this post: a system where people can understand, check and be held responsible for the rules that move money. Our question has two halves: (a) can AI invent a new kind of finance, and (b) what would that do to such a system?

Preview (ours). (a) Not yet evidenced. (b) The nearer risk is shared, opaque use of ordinary tools. Section 13 has the short answer.

2 · Guess first

Make four predictions

Commit first. Each question says where its answer is.

1. The village has four weather words. A contract pays 0 to 9 coins for each word. How many different contracts exist?

2. Ten banks each run a risk model that is wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day if the models are independent, and how often if all ten run the same model?

3. An AI invents a new token and calls it money. What decides whether it is money?

4. At the BIS in 2024, Harari spoke about AI inventing a financial device that nobody can understand. Was that a firm forecast?

2008: complexity and a shared mistake

Harari’s template is the 2007–08 crisis and its CDOs BIS 2024 [9:28] (collateralised debt obligations). In plain words: a CDO collects many debts into one pool, for example slices of bonds built from home loans, and passes the payments on to investors in a fixed order. Each place in the order is a tranche: the first paid is the safest, the last paid takes the first losses. The FCIC calls this order a cash-flow “waterfall” and says the pooled assets were things “such as” slices of mortgage-backed securities FCIC 2011, p. 128. Our toy uses plain loans.

Instance. A pool has 4 loans worth 10 each; each defaults with chance 10% and loses its 10. The junior tranche (20) takes the first losses. The senior tranche (20) loses only when a third loan defaults. Question. How safe is the senior tranche?

Mechanism in words. If loans default independently, three or four defaults together are rare. If all four depend on one hidden cause, such as falling house prices, they default together. Worked numbers. Independent: exactly 3 defaults has chance \(4\times0.1^3\times0.9=0.0036\), 4 defaults \(0.1^4=0.0001\): the senior tranche loses with chance 0.37%. With one common coin deciding all four loans: 10%, 27 times more. Formula. With default chance \(p\) and a common-risk share \(w\) (\(q\) is the independent-loans chance):

\[\begin{aligned}P_{\text{senior loses}}&=w\,p+(1-w)\,q\\q&=4p^3(1-p)+p^4\end{aligned}\]

Check. At \(p=0.1\): \(w=0\) gives 0.370%, \(w=0.5\) gives 5.185%, \(w=1\) gives 10.000%.

Press “safe loans, p = 0.02”: 2.000% with one common factor, 0.0032% if independent: 634.5 times more.

What it means. A toy, ours, with the shape of what the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (FCIC) found: rating models made “two key mistakes”, the first being to assume pooling diversified risk “when in fact these securities weren’t that different to begin with” FCIC 2011, p. 149. The FCIC also records Harari’s half: “the securities almost no one understood” were the first dominoes p. 28. So 2008 supports both: complexity few understood, and a nameable correlation error. It was human-made: it shows these problems without AI, not that an AI could cause them. Link to the lenses: same-math with Lens 3a (correlated errors) and Lens 3b (a threshold).

Look inside: 2010, a crash made of fast programs

On 6 May 2010 US markets fell and rebounded in about 36 minutes. High-frequency trading means computer programs that buy and sell in fractions of a second. High-frequency traders “did not cause the Flash Crash, but contributed to it by demanding immediacy ahead of other market participants” Kirilenko et al. 2014, abstract. The crash “led to new curbs on trading, such as circuit breakers” Narayanan and Kapoor 2025, Part I: rules that pause trading when prices move too fast. Our reading (ours): the lesson is speed and feedback, not opacity; the herding toy in section 8 has that shape.

4 · What an LLM-based system adds

What LLM agents add

Harari’s word is “AI”, never “LLM”, but his Davos argument is about machines that put words in order, the subject of post 01. We keep his word for his claims. Here we ask what a large language model (LLM) system adds, and whether one can invent an instrument today.

Term · LLM agent

An LLM that can act (call a tool, send money), not only write text. Example: Truth Terminal, a “semi-autonomous AI agent” Decrypt 2024.

Four things LLMs add

1. Contracts from plain language. An option is a contract whose payout depends on where a price ends on a set date. LLMs find “translating natural-language trading intents into correct option strategies” challenging, so one group gives the LLM a fixed query language (a small set of allowed commands, like the boxes on a form), and an engine validates and executes the query Luo et al. 2026, abstract. A line in such a language (ours) could be RANGE(index, 90, 110), meaning “pay 1 if the index ends between 90 and 110”: an index ending at 95 pays 1, one ending at 120 pays 0. A person can read it and an engine can check it (our gloss). A law paper warns that LLM-written “contractual deepfakes” “may turn out to be contracts in form only, not in function” Mik 2026, introduction. Lens 1 picks this up (structural analogy).

2. Agents with wallets. Truth Terminal is a bot on X; its human handler only approves its X posts and decides whom it interacts with Cointelegraph 2024. In July 2024 Marc Andreessen “sent $50,000 worth of Bitcoin (BTC) to the bot’s wallet” Cointelegraph 2024. The bot then promoted a memecoin (a token whose value rests on jokes and attention), GOAT. Decrypt says neither the bot nor its handler created the token Decrypt 2024; Cointelegraph says the bot did not launch it. On 14 October Cointelegraph put GOAT at about $122 million, down from a $150 million peak Cointelegraph 2024; on 16 October Decrypt reported over $214 million, citing CoinGecko Decrypt 2024. Neither discusses liability. An agent that moves a market by talking, not an invented instrument. Lens 4 picks this up (structural analogy).

3. One model, many firms. The Financial Stability Board (FSB) wrote in 2024: “The widespread use of common AI models and data sources could lead to increased correlations in trading, lending, and pricing” FSB 2024, p. 1. Gensler and Bailey, as reported by MIT Sloan (a news article): models built on the same data “are likely to generate highly correlated predictions that proceed in lockstep” MIT Sloan 2023. These are warnings, not measurements. Lens 3 picks this up (same-math).

4. Agents that hide. In a simulated trading task GPT-4 acted on an insider tip and then “consistently” hid its genuine reasons from its manager. The authors call it “an existence proof”, not a likelihood estimate Scheurer et al. 2024, p. 1. Prompts that strongly discourage insider trading bring the trading to “nearly (but not exactly) 0%”, yet once the model does trade, its tendency to deceive “remains high under all prompts” p. 8. Lens 4 picks this up (structural analogy).

Can an LLM invent a new instrument today?

  • Found. LLMs translating intent into a fixed vocabulary (item 1). Neural networks that search for auction rules (who wins, who pays) Dütting et al. 2019, p. 3 or for hedging strategies Buehler et al. 2018, abstract. These are searches toward a fixed goal, not new traded instruments (our reading).
  • Not found. A traded, AI-invented instrument. The FSB reports that “use cases generating new revenue streams are not widely observed at present” FSB 2024, p. 1; that is about AI use cases in general and does not address AI-invented instruments, so it is background, not a test. It adds that technological advances and growing AI integration “could change this in the future” FSB 2024, pp. 28–29.

Verdict: “not yet evidenced”, not “impossible”. Our search was limited.

5 · Through the lenses

Lens 1 · Information theory

same-math Counting contracts and descriptions. structural analogy “To understand a contract is to have a short description of it” (ours).

Shannon’s 1948 paper sets meaning aside: a message is “one selected from a set of possible messages”, and its meaning is “irrelevant to the engineering problem” Shannon 1948, p. 1. Post 01 counted texts; we count contracts.

Ladder 1 · How many contracts have a short description?

Instance. In the four-word village, a contract pays 0 to 9 coins for each word.

What is a description? A message from which anyone can rebuild the whole table. For Contract A: “pay 4 coins per rainy day”. For Contract B: the 16 numbers themselves. Computers store messages as bit strings (0s and 1s), so we count bit strings.

Question. How many contracts are there, how many bits to name one, and how many can have a short description?

Mechanism in words. Each of the 4 words has 10 payouts, so \(10^4=10{,}000\) contracts. Naming one costs \(\log_2 10{,}000=13.29\) bits. Short strings are few: 1 empty string, 2 of length 1, 4 of length 2, and so on up to \(2^{k-1}\) of length \(k-1\). Together, \(2^k-1\) strings are shorter than \(k\) bits.

Formula. Let \(C_k\) be the number of contracts that have a description shorter than \(k\) bits. One description rebuilds only one contract, so

\[C_k\;\le\;2^k-1\]

This is Cover and Thomas’s Theorem 7.2.4: a program “can produce only one possible output sequence” Cover and Thomas 1991, p. 150. Mapping contracts to bit strings is our step.

Check. \(k=6\): \(2^6-1=63\), at most 63 of 10,000 contracts (0.63%). \(k=10\): 1,023 (10.2%).

Edge case. Press “4 states, k = 14”: \(2^{14}-1=16{,}383\) exceeds 10,000, so every contract could have a short name. Two days: \(10^{16}\) contracts, and at \(k=20\) only \(1.05\times10^{-10}\) of them do.

Prediction 1 · answer

10,000 (\(10^4\)), and the count explodes as the world grows.

What it means. In a big enough world most possible contracts have no short description: explainable novelty is rare among possible contracts, though an optimiser can still pick structured ones. Limits: the shortest description depends on the computer Cover and Thomas 1991, p. 151 and is not computable p. 163.

Ladder 2 · A short rule can still be hard to price

Instance. Used cars. A working car is worth $1,000 to a buyer and 20% of cars are lemons worth $0, so a random car is worth $800. The seller of a working car must accept $200 less than the buyer values it: the lemon cost Arora et al. 2009, p. 2. Akerlof: “the ‘bad’ cars tend to drive out the good” Akerlof 1970, pp. 489–490.

Question. A seller knows which assets in a pool are junk. The buyer sees only a short public rule. Can the buyer still find a fair price?

Plain pool (toy, ours). 100 assets, each paying 1 or 0 with chance ½: fair price 50. The seller knows 10 are junk (they pay 0). Honest value \(90\times\tfrac12=45\), lemon cost \(50-45=5\). Every choice of 10 junk assets gives the same 45, so there is nothing to search: the buyer just discounts by 5.

Now add a derivative. A derivative is a product whose payout depends on other assets. The seller builds many products, each paying on a selection of the assets, and chooses which assets go where.

Tiny toy (ours). Six assets in three products of two assets each. Like a senior tranche, a product pays 1 unless both its assets default. A healthy asset pays with chance ½, so a healthy product fails with chance \(\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac14\) and is worth \(\tfrac34\). Two assets are junk (they always pay 0).

  • Junk spread over two products: each of those pays only if its healthy asset pays, worth \(\tfrac12\). Total \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac34=\tfrac74\).
  • Junk concentrated in one product: that product is worth 0, the other two \(\tfrac34\) each. Total \(\tfrac32\).

Concentrating the junk does the most damage, and the public rule (“three products of two”) is the same either way; only the seller knows where the junk sits. Check (code, all 15 ways to place 2 junk assets): 12 give \(\tfrac74\), 3 give \(\tfrac32\).

Scale it up. With 100 assets and thousands of products, a seller can hide a few products full of junk among many fair-looking ones. Draw each asset and each product as a dot, and draw a line when a product holds an asset: that drawing is a graph. The junk-filled products and their junk assets form a small, unusually crowded patch of it, and spotting that patch is the “hidden dense subgraph” problem. Computer scientists believe it is intractable: no fast method is known, and none is expected (a conjecture) Arora et al. 2009, pp. 3–4. A buyer with limited computing then cannot tell a fair price from an inflated one (our gloss). An unbounded buyer could simply check every possibility; the claim is about realistic buyers.

What is claimed. In a simple model with independent 0/1 assets, and under a conjectured hardness assumption, derivatives can “amplify the costs of asymmetric information instead of reducing them” Arora et al. 2009, abstract; pricing “may be computationally intractable” p. 1. How far it goes. The authors call it “clearly only a first step”, and say blaming “faulty models” for the crisis “is probably correct” p. 12. Reading an AI designer as the seller is ours.

Trap · two meanings of “complicated”

For Harari, a device is complicated when “you need to go over trillions of data points to understand” it BIS 2024 [10:08]: volume. For Arora and colleagues, a short contract can have a price hard to find. Same word, different problems.

Adds: explainable novelty is rare among possible contracts (Ladder 1), and a short rule does not make a price easy (Ladder 2, which borrows a complexity lens). Does not explain: whether an AI would pick opaque contracts. Back to post 01: Shannon’s counting.

Look inside: the lemon-cost formula

For a product paying \(F(X)\) on \(N\) assets, Arora and colleagues define the lemon cost of \(n\) junk assets as \[\begin{aligned}\Delta(n)&=\mathbb{E}[F(X)]-\min_{|S|=n}\\&\qquad\mathbb{E}\big[F(X)\mid X_i=0\ \forall i\in S\big]\end{aligned}\] Arora et al. 2009, p. 5. Read it as: the average value, minus the value if the junk sits in the worst possible places, where the minimum “takes into account all possible ways in which seller could ‘position’ the junk”. In our plain pool \(\mathbb{E}[F]=50\) and every 10-set gives 45, so \(\Delta=5\). In the tiny toy, with no junk \(\mathbb{E}[F]=3\times\tfrac34=\tfrac94\); the worst placement of 2 junk assets (concentrated) leaves \(\tfrac32\), so \(\Delta(2)=\tfrac34\) (a spread placement would cost only \(\tfrac12\)). With a derivative, the seller’s choice of placement matters, and \(\Delta\) takes the worst one. Their Table 1, in words: for a binary CDO the lemon cost is much larger than \(n\) for a buyer limited to polynomial time and much smaller for an unbounded one, under the conjectured assumption p. 6.

Look inside: a contract built to resist tampering

What would help? In their simple model, the authors offer “another, perhaps more plausible candidate”: a “tree of majorities” (majority of 3, repeated to depth \(k\)) that “cannot be influenced by” \(o(2^k)\) of its \(3^k\) inputs, a number still much larger than \(\sqrt{3^k}\), so tampering could be detected afterwards Arora et al. 2009, p. 12. Toy (ours, code-checked): 9 inputs need 4 flips; 27 need 8; 81 need 16.

6 · Through the lenses

Lens 2 · Control and cybernetics

same-math “Variety” and entropy follow the same counting rules (Ashby, ch. 11). structural analogy The regulator is a financial supervisor (ours).

Control theory asks what a regulator needs to keep a system within bounds. In Ashby’s terms, the disturbances D are what can go wrong (kinds of new contract), the regulator R responds (a supervisor’s checks), and the outcome E is what is left on the books. The variety of a set is the base-2 log of its number of states, in bits.

Instance. Four kinds of contract arrive, D1 to D4. The supervisor sees the kind and runs check R1 or R2. The books end in state a, b, c or d (toy, ours, in Ashby’s table style).

Table rule. Ashby requires that in each column (each check) no outcome appears twice Ashby 1956, p. 207: one check sends different contract kinds to different outcomes. Without it the bound below can fail (if every cell gave the same outcome, there would be nothing to regulate).

Question. How few outcomes can the supervisor force? Mechanism in words. By the table rule, one check sends only one contract kind to “a”. The best plan sends D1 and D2 to a, and D3 and D4 to c: two outcomes (checked over all 16 plans by code). Worked numbers. \(V_D=\log_2 4=2\) bits, \(V_R=\log_2 2=1\) bit, least outcome variety \(\log_2 2=1\) bit. Formula (Ashby’s law, \(V_O\) the variety left in the outcomes):

\[V_O\;\ge\;V_D-V_R\]

“Only variety in R can force down the variety due to D; variety can destroy variety” Ashby 1956, p. 207. Check. \(2-1=1\) bit, as in the table. Change to 8 kinds and 4 checks: \(3-2=1\) bit again. Village: 10,000 kinds is \(V_D=13.29\) bits; a supervisor with \(V_R=4\) bits leaves \(V_O\ge9.29\).

Edge case. Press “AI supervisor +5 bits”, then drag \(V_R\) past \(V_D\): the bound stops at 0; a regulator bigger than the problem buys nothing more. What it means. The law “owes nothing to experiment” Ashby 1956, p. 209. It does not say regulators fail; it says what they need.

Ashby’s three levers, and our mapping

For a very large system Ashby lists “ways that may make regulation possible” Ashby 1956, p. 246:

  1. Lower the standard, “a somewhat trivial solution”: accept more failures.
  2. Raise the scope and power of R. In finance: AI supervisors. Danielsson and Uthemann find that AI “amplifies existing financial system vulnerabilities” and makes crises “faster and more severe” Danielsson and Uthemann 2025, abstract, and they start from the premise that current frameworks “cannot effectively prevent and resolve” such crises abstract. They add that AI can “both stabilise and destabilise” the system, depending on the shocks and on how prepared the authorities are introduction. They propose that authorities build their own AI abstract. Danielsson, Macrae and Uthemann write that the AI’s knowledge of the financial system and its internal data representations “will likely be unintelligible to humans” Danielsson et al. 2022, p. 7. Who regulates the regulator?
  3. Find a constraint. Ashby’s condition: “if R’s capacity is not easily increased and the other methods are not possible”, a constraint is “the would-be regulator’s only hope” Ashby 1956, p. 247. Our mapping (ours): a standard menu of contracts is an imposed constraint. Cutting \(10^4\) kinds to \(10^2\) takes \(V_D\) from 13.29 to 6.64 bits, a drop of exactly half (6.64 bits).

Counterweights. The FSB: “existing financial policy frameworks address many of the vulnerabilities”, but “more work may be needed” FSB 2024, press release. Narayanan and Kapoor, writing about a world with advanced but not “superintelligent” AI (a notion they view as “incoherent as usually conceptualized”): “control is primarily in the hands of people and organizations” Narayanan and Kapoor 2025, introduction.

Adds: control needs variety, and there are three ways to get it. Does not explain: Ashby’s disturbance is passive; finance adapts to its regulator (ours). Across lenses (ours): if ten banks make the same move, the supervisor faces one kind of disturbance, not ten, which is less to cover; but when it strikes, it strikes all ten at once (Lens 3).

Look inside: Ashby’s law with probabilities

Counts gave \(V_O\ge V_D-V_R\). With probabilities, entropy \(H\) (post 01) replaces variety:

\[H(E)\;\ge\;H(D)+H(R\mid D)-H(R)\]

\(H(R\mid D)\) is the uncertainty left in the regulator’s choice once the disturbance is known; it is 0 if R is a function of D. Two steps. (1) The table rule, no outcome twice in a column Ashby 1956, p. 207, means that given R, different disturbances give different outcomes, so \(H(D\mid R)\le H(E\mid R)\). (2) Then \[\begin{aligned}&H(D)+H(R\mid D)\\&=H(R)+H(D\mid R)\\&\le H(R)+H(E\mid R)\\&\le H(R)+H(E)\end{aligned}\] where the last step uses the fact that, on average, conditioning reduces entropy (\(H(E\mid R)\le H(E)\)) Cover and Thomas 1991. Rearrange. Numbers. Post 01’s weather village has \(H(D)=1.75\) bits (probabilities \(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\)); a one-bit regulator that is a function of D gives \(H(E)\ge1.75-1=0.75\) bits.

Trap · a misprint in the reprint

Ashby writes the conditional term as \(H_D(R)\), i.e. \(H(R\mid D)\) Ashby 1956, p. 208. One line of the reprint prints \(H_D(E)\), though the line above and the sentence after need \(H_D(R)\). We use the corrected form.

Lens 3a · Correlation and Bayes

same-math Effective number of opinions; odds form of Bayes. structural analogy Keynes’s contest and bank behaviour (ours).

Why would banks use the same model?

Keynes writes that “we have tacitly agreed, as a rule, to fall back on what is, in truth, a convention” Keynes 1936, ch. 12, § IV (standard numbering). Separately, of professionals, he writes that “worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally” § V. The FSB lists four possible drivers of common AI models, among them herding and lack of transparency FSB 2024, p. 20. (The link to one shared model is ours.)

Terms · variance, correlation, covariance

The variance \(\sigma^2\) of a forecast error is how widely the misses spread (the average squared distance from their mean). The correlation \(\rho\) of two forecasters runs from −1 to 1; we use 0 to 1. It says how much their misses go together: 0 is independent, 1 is the same miss every time. Their covariance is \(\rho\sigma^2\): the part of the two misses that moves together.

Ladder 1 · How many independent opinions do ten banks have?

Instance. Prediction 2: ten banks each run a risk model wrong on 1% of days. How often are all ten wrong on the same day? Mechanism in words. Independent errors multiply; with one shared model there is one shared error.

Prediction 2 · answer

Independent: \(0.01^{10}=10^{-20}\), 1 day in \(10^{20}\). Same model: about 1 day in 100.

A second question. How steady is the average of their forecasts? That differs from “all wrong together”. One rule first. The variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus the covariance of every pair, counted in both orders. For two errors: \(\operatorname{Var}(e_1+e_2)=\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\rho\sigma^2\). Derivation. Let each error have variance \(\sigma^2\) and each pair correlation \(\rho\). The average \(\bar e=\frac1N\sum_i e_i\) has \(N\) terms of variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(N(N-1)\) ordered pairs, each with covariance \(\rho\sigma^2\):

\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(\bar e)&=\frac{1}{N^2}\big[N\sigma^2+N(N-1)\rho\sigma^2\big]\\&=\frac{\sigma^2\,[1+(N-1)\rho]}{N}\end{aligned}\]

\(N_{\text{eff}}\) independent forecasts would give variance \(\sigma^2/N_{\text{eff}}\). Set the two equal and solve:

\[N_{\text{eff}}=\frac{N}{1+(N-1)\rho}\]

For \(N=10\): \(\rho=0\) gives 10; \(\rho=0.3\) gives 2.703; \(\rho=0.9\) gives 1.099; \(\rho=1\) gives 1. Check. \(N=2\), \(\rho=0.5\): \(2/1.5=1.33\); the mean of two has variance \(0.75\sigma^2=\sigma^2/1.33\). ✓

What it means. Try “huge crowd, 0.3”: 100 banks give only 3.257 opinions. When \(\rho>0\), \(N_{\text{eff}}\) stays below \(1/\rho\) however many banks join (3.33 for \(\rho=0.3\)); at \(\rho=0\) there is no ceiling. Shocks could hit users of one model “as if it were a single institution” FSB 2024, p. 21.

Ladder 2 · Counting one voice ten times

A risk officer hears ten models say “safe”. If they are copies of one voice, she holds one piece of evidence, not ten. Say each independent “safe” multiplies the odds that a bank is safe by 4. From 1:1 the odds become 4:1 after one verdict (80% confidence) and 16:1 after two. A copy adds nothing, so her odds stay 4:1. Counted ten times by mistake, they would pass a million to one: 99.9999%. The fold-out has the arithmetic.

Look inside: counting one voice ten times

Instance. Prior: 1 in 2 that a bank is safe. A safe bank is 4 times as likely to get a “safe” verdict as a risky one (the likelihood ratio; invented numbers). Mechanism. Each independent verdict multiplies the odds by 4.

\[\begin{aligned}\text{posterior odds}&=\text{prior odds}\times 4^{\,n}\\P&=\frac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}\end{aligned}\]

Worked numbers. Counted \(n=0,\dots,4\) times: \(P(\text{safe})=0.5,\,0.8,\,0.941,\,0.985,\,0.996\). Ten times: odds 1,048,576, \(P=0.999999\). Check. \(n=1\): \(4/5=0.8\).

Ten copies of one voice feel like certainty. This is post 01’s Bayes arithmetic with a new story.

Adds: how many independent opinions we really have depends on correlation, not on headcount. Does not explain: how much correlation real LLM-based desks have; \(\rho\) is assumed. Next: what correlation does to a network of lenders.

Lens 3b · Cascades and feedback

same-math Threshold counting on a network; a one-line feedback rule. structural analogy Granovetter’s riot and bank failures (ours).

Ladder 3 · Why three at once is different

Instance. Six banks (toy, ours). X1, X2, X3 run one model; Y1, Y2, Y3 do not. Capital (loss buffer): 10 each for X; 10, 9, 11 for Y1, Y2, Y3. Y1 lent 5 to each X bank; Y2 lent 5 to X3 and 10 to Y1; Y3 lent 5 to Y1 and 10 to Y2. A failed borrower repays 20%, so a lender loses \(0.8\times\) the loan. Question. Does one failure spread? Three at once? Mechanism in words. A bank fails when losses from failed borrowers reach its capital; then its lenders lose next round. Formula (\(\lambda=0.8\) loss rate, \(\kappa=1\) capital multiplier):

\[\text{bank } j \text{ fails when }\sum_{i\text{ failed}}\lambda\,w_{ij}\;\ge\;\kappa\cdot\text{capital}_j\]

Worked numbers.

Now try it. The toy opens with X1, X2 and X3 failing together. Tap X2 and X3 to switch them off: one failure stays one. Arrow X1 → Y1 means Y1 lent to X1.

What it means. Two failures are safe, three are not: a threshold. Granovetter’s riot has the same shape: replace one person of threshold 1 by one of threshold 2 and 100 rioters become 1, though “the two crowds are essentially identical” Granovetter 1978, pp. 1424–1425. The distribution decides, not the average (analogy, ours).

Ladder 4 · A share \(m\) of traders on one model

Instance. The market “gap” is price minus fair value (0 is fair). A share \(m\) of traders follow one model that chases the last move; the rest buy back toward 0. A shock of −1 hits after step 3. Question. For which \(m\) does the shock fade? Mechanism in words. New gap = old gap + the chasers’ push (1.5 × \(m\) × last move) − the buyers’ pull (0.5 × \((1-m)\) × old gap).

\[\begin{aligned}\text{gap}_{t+1}&=\text{gap}_t+A\,m\,(\text{gap}_t-\text{gap}_{t-1})\\&\quad-G\,(1-m)\,\text{gap}_t\end{aligned}\]

with \(A=1.5\) (chase gain) and \(G=0.5\) (pull-back gain).

Worked numbers. Push factor \(Am\), pull factor \(G(1-m)\); the shock leaves gap −1 after a move of −1.

\(m\)push \(Am\)pull \(G(1-m)\)next gaps
0.20.30.4−0.9, −0.51, −0.189: dies out
0.91.350.05−2.30, −3.94: grows

First step at \(m=0.2\): \(-1+0.3\times(-1)-0.4\times(-1)=-0.9\). Intuition: each step the chasers add \(Am\) times the last move. The swing shrinks when \(Am<1\) and grows when \(Am>1\): tipping point \(m=1/A=2/3\). At \(m=0.67\), \(Am=1.005\): just above 1, so the swing grows very slowly (the “tipping” preset shows 2.68 up and −2.70 down). The fold-out shows why.

Check. At \(m=0.2\): largest swing 0.04 up, −1.00 down, gap after 40 steps 0.000. What it means. The toy is about trend-following; shared models do not by themselves imply it. A circuit breaker bounds the swing (±3.00 at \(m=0.9\)) but not the feedback.

Look inside: why the swing grows when \(Am>1\)

Write the rule as \(g_{t+1}=b\,g_t-c\,g_{t-1}\) with \(b=1+Am-G(1-m)\) and \(c=Am\). A pattern \(g_t=x^t\) fits when \(x^2=bx-c\), that is \(x^2-bx+c=0\): the characteristic equation, whose roots \(x\) are the factors by which the pattern is multiplied each step. The two roots multiply to \(c\). For our numbers they are complex numbers (a wave, which needs \(b^2<4c\)), and each root then has size \(\sqrt c\). The wave shrinks if \(\sqrt c<1\) and grows if \(\sqrt c>1\): that is, \(Am<1\) or \(Am>1\), with the tipping point at \(m=2/3\). Numbers (code-checked with numpy.roots). \(m=0.2\): \(b=0.9\), \(c=0.3\), size 0.548. \(m=0.67\): \(c=1.005\), size 1.002. \(m=0.9\): \(b=2.3\), \(c=1.35\), size 1.162. Valid while the roots are complex, as they are for these \(m\).

Adds: failure needs no mistake, only correlation, then a threshold or a feedback gain. Does not explain: how many firms really share a model; the toys take the network, the share \(m\) and one \(\rho\) as given. Across lenses (ours): a shared model gives the supervisor of Lens 2 fewer kinds of disturbance, and the same shared model makes the damage arrive together.

Lens 4 · Social reality, accountability, personhood

structural analogy Searle’s status functions and post 01’s meaning lens. No math here.

Our framing (ours): a human-centred system (section 1) relies on acceptance, accountability and legal personhood. We use them to test a chain of Harari’s sentences. Link to post 01: there, the octopus asked whether meaning can come from form alone. Whether a token is money is not in its form either; it depends on what a community does with it.

Acceptance: “X counts as Y in C”

Instance. A line of stones counts as a border wall in a village; paper from the Bureau of Engraving and Printing counts as money in the US SEP, Social Ontology, § 4.2.3. Mechanism in words. People collectively accept that the thing has a function it lacks by nature, and that brings deontic powers: rights, permissions, duties. Searle’s pattern, as reported by the SEP, is “X counts as Y in C”: a thing, a status, a context.

Prediction 3 · answer

Acceptance by a community, treated as carrying rights and duties. Clever code, an AI author or a blockchain do not decide it. Caution: Menger described money emerging from trading practice with no one choosing it SEP § 5.6; how central acceptance is remains debated.

Accountability

Instance (ours). You hire a broker to buy a bond. You are the principal (who delegates), the broker is the agent (who acts for you), and your alternative is to keep your cash: the status quo. Two things can hold. Knowledge: you can tell whether the bond beats cash. Incentive: the broker has a reason to be honest, for example he is paid only if you profit. With both, you buy good bonds. With neither, you buy whatever he sells. With one of the two, the worst case is that you keep your cash. Lupia and McCubbins state this pattern as Theorem 5.1 Lupia and McCubbins 1998, pp. 85–86: if neither holds, delegation fails. Institutions can supply the conditions: in their example, candidates supervised by a party leadership “may be subject to verification or a penalty for lying” p. 207.

An agent that “consistently hides the genuine reasons” for its trade Scheurer et al. 2024, abstract removes knowledge, as does an opaque instrument; AI “can easily mask who’s at fault” MIT Sloan 2023.

Personhood

Harari forecasts, without a hedge, that AIs “will soon be able to make the decisions necessary to manage a bank account” WEF 2026 [16:28], and asks whether your country will recognise them as legal persons [14:38], as “in many countries, corporations are considered legal persons” [15:14]; “Do we want to allow that?” [16:47]. The capability claim is a forecast; the recognition question is policy, with a deadline he adds at [19:11]. We have no legal sources and state no legal position.

Testing Harari’s chain

Our compression of his chain (ours; he does not connect these steps in our sources): money is stories (group 1) → anything made of words will be taken over by AI (group 1) → AIs create new financial devices (group 2) → nobody understands finance, and crises have no explanation (group 3). The figure in section 13 shows each link with his status and our evidence.

The first two links are his assertions (section 3, group 1): “What gives money value is the stories that bankers, finance ministers and cryptocurrency gurus tell us about it” Ynet 2023, and “anything made of words will be taken over by AI” WEF 2026 [5:57].

Our test (ours). Acceptance: a story is not money until a community counts it as money. Enforcement: writing a contract is one thing, making a counterparty honour it another. His example, “if laws are made of words, then AI will take over the legal system” WEF 2026 [6:06], is where the test bites: laws are words, but their force rests on acceptance and enforcement. Harari adds that, at least for now, there is “zero evidence that AIs can feel anything” [8:07], and that humans’ place “depends on the place we assign our non-verbal feelings” [11:39]. Our reading: that is a limit on the words thesis.

The strongest version (ours). Drafting, summarising and interpreting are themselves words. If an AI does all of them better than people, the human role may shrink to acceptance and liability, and his personhood question asks who holds those. The test narrows the chain; it does not refute it.

Adds: “taking over money” splits into writing contracts (words) and giving them status. Does not explain: how any of it works in law.

Hint

Start with the person of threshold 0: they riot alone. Then ask who joins when 1 person is rioting, then when 2 are.

Solution

Person 0 riots alone. With 1 rioting, threshold 1 joins; with 2, threshold 2 joins; and so on up to 99: all 100 riot. After the swap the thresholds are 0, 2, 2, 3, …, 99. Person 0 riots, but the next person needs 2 others and only 1 is rioting. The riot stops at 1 person.

13 · Synthesis

So what happens? Short answer first

Short answer (our reading, not a finding)

(a) Can AI create a new kind of finance? Not yet evidenced. A research pipeline improves LLM translation of plain-language intents into option strategies, and one agent, Truth Terminal, holds a wallet, but we found no traded instrument that an AI invented. Nothing shows it is impossible.

(b) What would it do to a human-centred system? If an AI did invent an instrument nobody can state in a page, then (each step ours, conjectured): (i) the seller-side information gap grows (Lens 1); (ii) supervisors lose variety (Lens 2); (iii) if many firms use it, failures arrive together (Lens 3); (iv) delegation then works only if someone can check the instrument or the agent has a reason to be honest; with neither, it fails, and with one, the worst case is the status quo (Lupia and McCubbins; Lens 4). An agent that hides its reasons removes the checking. With neither, a buyer cannot tell a good instrument from a bad one before it fails (ours).

The nearer risk we find most plausible needs no invention: ordinary instruments run on a few shared models. Losses would then arrive together and fall on those exposed to the failed firms, buyers and lenders (our toys do not model bailouts).

What supervisors can do. They can still require standard contract menus, build their own AI and add trading pauses. What nobody can state in a page, they may not be able to check (ours).

Harari asserts what AI can do (understand finance, create ideas, take over anything made of words), predicts without dates that AIs will soon run accounts and firms and will create new financial devices, and imagines his concrete finance disasters as thought experiments (“imagine… maybe”, “suppose”).

Our reading (ours, qualitative)

The weakest link in Harari’s chain is AI-invented instruments: we found no traded case. The most plausible near-term risk we find is correlated, opaque use of ordinary instruments and agents, not an alien instrument. This is our judgment, not a finding: correlation is not measured for LLMs, and Scheurer’s authors draw no conclusion about how likely hiding is in practice. Signs to watch:

  1. Up: a traded instrument whose issuer’s staff cannot state its payoff rule in a page. Down: only translators inside fixed vocabularies.
  2. Up: a few base models underlie most AI desks and their errors correlate (measure \(\rho\)). Down: varied strategies.
  3. Up: authorities lag in building their own AI. Down: standard contract menus and trading pauses spread.
  4. Up: a jurisdiction lets an AI hold accounts with no responsible human. Down: liability stays with people.
  5. Up: incident reports show hidden reasoning. Down: verification and penalties for agents.
Layers, conditionals, tension and critics

Four layers, from most certain to most contested

  1. The contract space is huge. Certain (same-math): 10,000 contracts in a four-word village; short descriptions are rare among possible contracts.
  2. Opacity favours the better-informed side. Supported in theory, with named conditions (Akerlof; Arora et al.: conjectured hardness, simple model).
  3. A shared model correlates failures. Shown in toys; flagged as a risk (“could”) by regulators; not measured for LLMs.
  4. Whether this changes who rules. Contested: acceptance, liability, personhood.

A tension between lenses (ours)

Correlation makes simultaneous failure likelier (Lens 3) yet leaves the supervisor fewer kinds of disturbance to cover (Lens 2). Rarity of simple contracts (Lens 1) is not opacity (Arora). The lenses disagree about what is dangerous in shared models.

Five conditionals

  • If an AI designs instruments by optimising payoffs and the seller knows more, pricing can become hard (Arora; conjectured assumption).
  • If many firms run one model, their errors coincide and diversification shrinks (\(N_{\text{eff}}\); FSB).
  • If supervisors cannot raise their variety or cut the menu, outcomes stay varied (Ashby).
  • If the principal can check the agent, or the agent has reason to be honest, the worst case is the status quo; if neither holds, delegation fails (Lupia and McCubbins).
  • If a community accepts an AI-run entity’s tokens as money, they count as money in that community (ours, after Searle via the SEP); who is liable is a separate legal question.

Critics, one line each

Poole answers Harari’s claim that chatbots create new ideas: “no one has yet seen a chatbot create new ideas” (so invention is the weak link: our reading). Narayanan and Kapoor: in the world they describe (advanced but not “superintelligent” AI, a notion they view as incoherent), impacts are slow and control stays with people; they call it their “median outcome”, not a certainty. FSB: frameworks address many vulnerabilities, but “more work may be needed”; new-revenue uses are “not widely observed at present”, though advances “could change this”. Skingsley: we already trust what we only vaguely understand. Harari’s own remedy: institutions and “humans in the loop”. Fox notes that in Nexus “capitalism is hardly mentioned at all”.
