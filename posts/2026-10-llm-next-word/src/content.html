What does an LLM know when it predicts the next word?

A language model is trained on one job: guess the next piece of text. This post follows that one job through five fields and asks what kind of knowledge it needs, and what kind it can give you.

1 · Hook

The guessing game

Take a book you have never read. Cover the page and uncover it one letter at a time. Before each letter, guess what it will be. If you are wrong, guess again until you are right, and write down how many guesses you needed.

Claude Shannon ran this experiment and published it in 1951. Most of the time the answer was one: the person guessed right on the first try. In one run of this game, 79 of 102 letters were right on the first guess, about 77% Shannon 1951, p. 56. The list of guess numbers, which Shannon called the reduced text, is mostly 1s p. 56.

Then Shannon made a surprising point. Imagine an identical twin of the guesser, who never sees the book. There are two roles here. The first person is the encoder: they see the real text, play the game, and write down only the guess numbers. The twin is the decoder: they receive only those numbers and must rebuild the text. The twin never guesses a number. The twin guesses letters, exactly as the first person would, and the number tells the twin when to stop. Because the twins are identical, after the same text so far they try letters in exactly the same order. Shannon adds that the twin must be identical “mathematically, not just biologically” pp. 55–56.

A tiny example (ours). The text so far is “TH”, and after “TH” this predictor always tries E first, then A, then I. Suppose the next letter is really I. The encoder needs three tries, so it writes 3. The twin reads “3”, tries E (one), A (two), I (three), and stops: the letter is I. Nothing about the letter itself was sent, only the number 3.

Why does this make the message shorter? Because the guess numbers are very predictable: they are mostly 1s. Writing a plain letter needs about 4.7 bits (you will see why in Lens 1), but a stream that is almost all 1s can be stored with a very short code for “1” and longer codes for the rare 2s, 3s and 4s. The better the guesser, the more 1s, and the shorter the message. The lesson: a good predictor is also a good compressor.

Today we have machines that play exactly this game, at a huge scale. A large language model (LLM) reads text one token at a time. A token is a common word or a piece of a word; for example, a rare word may be split into two or three pieces (our example). For every possible next token, the model gives a probability. Here is an example from the GPT-3 paper Brown et al. 2020, p. 12:

Alice was friends with Bob. Alice went to visit her friend ____. → Bob

To fill that blank, you need more than spelling. You need to keep track of who is who. So here is the question of this post: to predict the next word this well, what does a machine have to know? Only which words follow which? Facts about the world? Something about the minds of the people who wrote the text?

3 · The phenomenon in its home field

How a language model is built

All the models in this post, from 2003 to 2020, are trained on the same task: guess the next token. Only the machinery changes. Here is the story in eight steps.

Step 1. A sentence is a chain of guesses

Start with an instance (our toy numbers). In some collection of text, a sentence starts with “the” 5% of the time. After “the”, the next word is “cat” 1% of the time. After “the cat”, the next word is “sat” 20% of the time. Then the chance that a sentence starts with “the cat sat” is

\[0.05\times0.01\times0.20=0.0001\]

Each factor is a conditional probability: the chance of the next word given the words before it. Multiplying them is the ordinary rule of probability (the chance of A and then B is the chance of A times the chance of B given A), used again and again. Bengio and colleagues write the general pattern like this Bengio et al. 2003, p. 1138:

\[\hat P(w_1,\dots,w_T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\hat P(w_t\mid w_1,\dots,w_{t-1})\]

Read it aloud: “the model’s probability of the words \(w_1\) to \(w_T\) is the product, for every position \(t\), of the probability of word \(w_t\) given all the words before it.” The hat on \(\hat P\) means “estimated by the model”. GPT-2 writes the same product Radford et al. 2019, pdf 2. So a machine that is good at the next-word game can also score whole texts.

Step 2. Counting runs out of data

The oldest way to get these probabilities is to count. To estimate the chance of “sat” after “the cat”, count how often “the cat sat” appears in a big collection of text, and divide by how often “the cat” appears. A model that uses the last \(n-1\) words this way is called an n-gram model (two words: bigram; three words: trigram).

The problem is size. Bengio and colleagues give a famous example p. 1137. Suppose the vocabulary has 100,000 words and you want the probability of every possible run of 10 words. Each of the 10 positions has 100,000 choices, so there are \(100{,}000^{10}=(10^5)^{10}=10^{50}\) possible runs. A full table would need up to \(10^{50}-1\) free numbers (minus one because all the probabilities must add up to 1, so the last one is fixed by the others). For comparison, a huge text collection has perhaps billions of words, around \(10^{9}\) to \(10^{11}\) (our rough figure). Almost every possible 10-word run never appears even once, so pure counting gives it probability zero. This is the curse of dimensionality: the number of possible cases grows much faster than the data.

Step 3. Give every word a vector (2003)

Bengio’s answer: give every word a short list of numbers, called a vector. Think of it as the word’s address on a map, but with 30 to 100 directions instead of two. The model learns these addresses (written \(C(i)\) for word \(i\)) at the same time as it learns to predict pp. 1139, 1141. Words that appear in similar places in sentences are expected to get similar addresses.

Why does this help? Because a lesson learned for one sentence now spreads to its neighbours on the map. Bengio’s example: if the model has seen “The cat is walking in the bedroom”, it can also give a sensible probability to “A dog was running in a room”, because cat/dog, walking/running and bedroom/room are expected to get nearby vectors pp. 1139–1140. On the Brown corpus, this model’s perplexity (a measure of the model’s surprise; rung 4 defines it) was about 24% better than the best counting model; on a news corpus, about 8% better p. 1148.

Step 4. Cheap vectors, big data (2013)

Bengio’s network has two slow parts. After looking up the vectors of the previous words, it passes them through a hidden layer: extra units (50 to 100 in Bengio’s experiments; 500 to 1,000 was typical by 2013, Mikolov notes), each mixing all the inputs and then bending the result with a curve (the function tanh). Then a final layer, called a softmax, gives a probability to every word in the vocabulary Bengio et al. 2003, pp. 1141–1142; Mikolov et al. 2013a, p. 3. That final layer is the slowest: Bengio’s own diagram marks it “most computation here” Bengio et al. 2003, p. 1142, because it must score every word. Mikolov and colleagues counted the work for one training example. With 10 previous words (\(N\)), vectors of size \(D\), a hidden layer of size \(H\) and a vocabulary of size \(V\), the cost is about \(N\times D+N\times D\times H+H\times V\) (Mikolov counts the numbers that must be touched for one example) Mikolov et al. 2013a, pp. 2–3. With a trick that makes the last term small (a word tree, so only about \(\log_2 V\) outputs are computed), “most of the complexity is caused by the term \(N\times D\times H\)”, the hidden layer p. 3. So once the output is made cheap, the hidden layer becomes the bottleneck.

So they removed the hidden layer p. 4. Their CBOW model (continuous bag of words) averages the vectors of the surrounding words, four before and four after, and predicts the word in the middle. Their skip-gram model does the reverse: from the middle word it predicts each nearby word p. 4. They admit these simpler models “might not be able to represent the data as precisely”, but can “possibly be trained on much more data efficiently” p. 4. Try the three designs:

The cheap vectors showed a surprise: some relations became directions on the map. The vector for biggest minus big plus small lands near smallest p. 5. Lens 2 tests how well this works.

Step 5. Attention (2017)

Look again at “Alice was friends with Bob. Alice went to visit her friend ____.” To guess the blank, the word “friend” must collect information from earlier words, and some earlier words matter much more than others: “Bob” matters a lot, “to” hardly at all. The Transformer does exactly this. Every position takes a weighted average of information from other positions, with large weights for the positions that matter. The weights come from how well a “query” vector at one position matches a “key” vector at another Vaswani et al. 2017, pp. 3–4. To predict text from left to right, a mask stops a position from looking at the future pp. 3, 5. (The 2017 model was built for translation and has two halves, an encoder that reads the source sentence and a decoder that writes the translation. GPT-style models keep only the decoder half Kaplan et al. 2020, p. 6.)

Tap a word. The bars show how much weight it gives to itself and each earlier word. The vectors behind this are toy numbers we chose by hand, not a trained model; rung 6 shows the arithmetic.

Step 6. Tasks are text too (2019)

GPT-2’s authors noticed that a task can be written inside the text. On the internet, “TL;DR:” (“too long; didn’t read”) is often followed by a short summary (our explanation of why the trick works). So put “TL;DR:” after a news article, and the model tends to continue with a summary Radford et al. 2019, pdf 6. The summaries were weak: they “just barely” beat random sentences from the article pdf 6. But the authors made a speculation that frames this whole post: a model with enough capacity will begin to infer and perform the tasks shown in text “in order to better predict them” pdf 3.

Step 7. Learning inside the prompt (2020)

First, what does “training” mean? The model makes guesses, checks the real next tokens, and then nudges each of its weights a tiny bit in the direction that would have made the right tokens more likely. The direction of that nudge is called the gradient, and one round of nudges is a gradient update. In practice the model guesses for a large batch of text at once (3.2 million tokens at a time for GPT-3 Brown et al. 2020, p. 8), then nudges every weight together. Training GPT-3 took on the order of a hundred thousand such rounds (our estimate: 300 billion ÷ 3.2 million).

GPT-3 has 175 billion weights and was trained on 300 billion tokens Brown et al. 2020, p. 8. After training, show it a few examples of a task in the prompt, for example three English words with their French translations and then a fourth English word, and it often continues the pattern. The authors call this in-context learning, and they stress that it happens with no gradient updates: the weights do not change pp. 3–4.

Prediction 2 · answer

None. In few-shot use, all “learning” happens in the forward pass, while the model reads the prompt; no nudges are made. The authors also say their terms are “agnostic” about whether the model learns the task from scratch or recognizes a pattern it saw in training p. 4, footnote 1.

Step 8. Smooth scaling laws (2020)

Term · held-out data

Text the model never saw during training, kept aside only for testing. Like exam questions that were not in the homework: a student who memorized the homework answers can still fail the exam. Measuring the loss on held-out text tells us how well the model generalizes, not how well it memorized. Kaplan calls it the test loss Kaplan et al. 2020, p. 4.

Kaplan and colleagues trained many Transformers of different sizes and measured their average surprise on held-out text, in nats per token (a nat is like a bit but uses natural logarithms; Lens 1). They found that “the test loss … can be predicted using a power-law” p. 4. For models trained long enough on enough data, the loss depends on the number of parameters \(N\) (weights, not counting the word vectors) like this p. 4:

\[L(N)=\left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{0.076},\qquad N_c\approx8.8\times10^{13}\]

Here \(N_c\) is a constant fitted to the data, and 0.076 sets how steep the line is p. 4.

What does this mean in plain words? Make the model twice as big, and the loss is multiplied by \(2^{-0.076}\approx0.95\): about 5% lower p. 5. Make it twice as big again, and you get the same 5% again. The improvement per doubling is the same at every size. That is what power law means, and it is why the curve is smooth: no sudden jumps, only a steady slope. On a graph where both axes are logarithmic, it is a straight line. The authors observed such trends over more than six orders of magnitude (a factor of more than a million) p. 3.

Try it · Grow the model

Move the slider to change the number of parameters. The dot moves along Kaplan’s fitted line. This is only the fitted formula. The authors measured models from about 10³ to 10⁹ parameters p. 4; beyond that the line is an extrapolation. They saw no sign of deviation at the top, but the loss must flatten out somewhere above zero p. 3.

Why does a smooth law matter? Because it lets you predict. Train small models cheaply, fit the line, and, if the trend continues, you can forecast the loss of a much bigger model before you build it (our reading); the authors conclude that performance “improves smoothly and predictably” as size, data and compute grow together p. 3. Two warnings. The constant \(N_c\) has “no fundamental meaning”; it depends on the vocabulary and tokenization p. 5. And the law is about loss, not about abilities: a smooth loss “may hide seemingly qualitative changes in capability” p. 19. In other words (our gloss), a model might gain a new skill suddenly while its average surprise drops only a little.

One training signal runs through all eight steps: make the next token less surprising. Every ability we see in an LLM either comes from this signal or rides along with it. The rest of the post asks what that signal can carry.

4 · Through the lenses

Lens 1 · Information theory

same-math The LLM training loss is Shannon’s average surprise. This is not an analogy; it is the same formula.

Shannon’s 1948 paper starts by setting meaning aside. The meaning of a message is “irrelevant to the engineering problem”. What matters is that a message is a selection from a set of possible messages Shannon 1948, p. 1. This choice made the theory possible. Keep it in mind, because it also limits what this lens can tell us.

A puzzle: 32 cups and 5 rats

You have 32 cups of water. Exactly one is poisoned, and each cup is equally likely to be the one. A rat that drinks even one drop of poison dies after 24 hours; a rat can drink from many cups at once. You may run only one round: all rats drink at the same time, and you look after 24 hours. What is the smallest number of rats that always finds the poisoned cup?

The answer is 5. Number the cups 0 to 31 and write each number in binary with 5 digits: cup 0 is 00000, cup 1 is 00001, …, cup 31 is 11111. Give each rat one digit position. Rat 1 drinks from every cup whose first digit is 1, rat 2 from every cup whose second digit is 1, and so on. After 24 hours, write 1 for a dead rat and 0 for a living one. The five results spell the poisoned cup’s number. If rats 1, 3 and 5 die, the cup is 10101 in binary, which is cup 21 (16 + 4 + 1). Fewer rats cannot work: 4 rats give only \(2^4=16\) possible patterns of dead and alive, not enough for 32 cups.

Try it · Find the poison

Tap a cup to poison it, or tap a rat to see which cups it drinks from. Each rat is one yes/no question asked at the same time: “Is the poisoned cup among mine?”

Each rat answers one yes/no question. One yes/no answer, when both answers are equally likely, carries one bit of information. Finding one cup among 32 equally likely cups needs \(\log_2 32=5\) bits, because \(2^5=32\).

Surprise

Now turn it around. Before the test, the poisoned cup is one of 32, so learning which one tells you 5 bits. A coin flip (2 outcomes) tells you 1 bit. Three coin flips have \(2^3=8\) outcomes, each with probability ⅛, and tell you 3 bits. The pattern: an outcome with probability \(p\) carries

\[\text{surprise}=-\log_2 p\ \text{bits}\]

Check: \(-\log_2\tfrac12=1\), \(-\log_2\tfrac18=3\), \(-\log_2\tfrac1{32}=5\). Rare events carry more surprise. Why a logarithm? Because when independent events happen together their probabilities multiply (½ × ½ × ½ = ⅛), and we want their surprises to add (1 + 1 + 1 = 3 bits). The logarithm turns multiplication into addition.

Entropy: the average surprise

The cups were all equally likely. Real sources are not. Take our village weather report (we use it again in the ladder): rain ½ of the days, sun ¼, wind ⅛, snow ⅛. Cover and Thomas give a nice reading of entropy as a game of yes/no questions Cover & Thomas, pp. 14–15. Ask the questions in the smart order, most likely first:

Half of the days you are done after 1 question, a quarter of the days after 2, and the rest after 3. On average:

\[\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot3=1.75\ \text{questions}\]

The same number comes from averaging the surprises (1, 2, 3 and 3 bits). That average is the entropy:

\[H(p)=-\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x)\]

Read it aloud: “for each outcome \(x\), take its probability times its surprise, and add them up.” For the weather, \(H=1.75\) bits. For the 32 cups, every cup has \(p=\tfrac1{32}\) and surprise 5, so \(H=5\) bits: the rat puzzle again. In general the smallest average number of yes/no questions is not always exactly \(H\); it lies between \(H\) and \(H+1\). For the weather it matches exactly Cover & Thomas, p. 15, and so do the 32 cups (5 questions, \(H=5\)). The answers to the questions can be written as a code: rain = 0, sun = 10, wind = 110, snow = 111. Shannon uses exactly this example Shannon 1948, p. 18. Notice that the skewed weather needs fewer questions (1.75) than four equally likely words would need (2): predictability saves bits, just as in Shannon’s game.

The cost of a wrong model

Now the key fact for LLMs. Imagine a forecaster who wrongly believes all four weather words are equally likely. She uses a code that fits her belief: every word gets 2 digits (00, 01, 10, 11). Her code always costs 2 bits per day, while the code that fits the true weather costs 1.75 bits per day on average. She pays 0.25 extra bits per day for her wrong belief.

The general rule: if the truth is \(p\) but your code (or your predictions) come from a model \(q\), your average cost is

\[H(p)+D(p\,\|\,q)\]

where \(D(p\,\|\,q)\) is the relative entropy, also called the KL divergence: the extra bits you pay for believing \(q\) when the truth is \(p\) Cover & Thomas, p. 18. \(D\) is never negative, and it is zero only when \(q=p\) p. 26. For our forecaster, \(D=0.25\).

In machine learning, the average cost \(-\sum_x p(x)\log_2 q(x)\) is called the cross-entropy and is written \(H(p,q)\):

\[H(p,q)=-\sum_x p(x)\log_2 q(x)=H(p)+D(p\,\|\,q)\]

Read the left part aloud: “the outcomes happen with the true probabilities \(p\), but each one is scored with the surprise the model \(q\) feels.” Why does it split into \(H(p)\) plus \(D\)? That is a two-line exercise in rung 3. An LLM is trained to make exactly this quantity as small as possible on real text, with \(p\) the true distribution of text and \(q\) the model (the training loss uses natural logs, so it comes out in nats, but the idea is the same). The entropy \(H(p)\) is fixed by the language. So the only part training can reduce is \(D\): the distance between the model and the truth about text.

Name trap

Cover and Thomas do not use “cross-entropy” in the machine-learning way: their historical notes list “cross entropy” as another name for relative entropy, that is \(D\) itself p. 49. And Shannon’s 1948 “relative entropy” means something else again: entropy divided by its maximum, \(H/H_{\max}\) Shannon 1948, p. 14. In this post, “cross-entropy” always means \(H(p,q)\) above.

Context lowers surprise

An instance first: after the letter Q, English almost always continues with U. If you know the previous letter is Q, the next letter carries almost no surprise. Knowing more of the past usually makes the next letter easier to guess.

Shannon calculated, from tables of letter, pair and triple frequencies, how uncertain the next letter of English is when you know the previous \(N-1\) letters. He called this \(F_N\). With 26 letters (no space): \(F_0=4.70\) bits (no knowledge at all: \(\log_2 26\)), \(F_1=4.14\) (you know how common each letter is), \(F_2=3.56\) (you also know the previous letter), \(F_3\approx3.3\) Shannon 1951, p. 54. In theory \(F_N\) never goes up as \(N\) grows, and it approaches the true entropy \(H\) Shannon 1948, p. 14. One careful point: knowing more lowers uncertainty on average. A particular context can make the next symbol more uncertain Cover & Thomas, p. 28.

Prediction 1 · answer

About 1 bit. Shannon wrote that long-range effects (up to 100 letters) seem to reduce the entropy to “something of the order of one bit per letter” Shannon 1951, pp. 50–51. His bounds at 100 letters were 0.6 to 1.3 bits p. 64. This is a hedged estimate from human guessers on one book, not a constant of nature pp. 56–57. For comparison, 4.7 bits is \(\log_2 26\): no knowledge at all.

How low can a model go?

Nobody knows the true entropy of a language. Kaplan and colleagues found that two of their trends contradict each other near a loss of about 1.7 nats per token. A nat is the same idea as a bit, but measured with natural logarithms (base \(e\approx2.718\)) instead of base 2; 1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits. The most obvious reading, they say, is that the scaling laws break down at or before that point. They add a conjecture: the number might be a rough estimate of the entropy per token of natural language. The estimate is uncertain by an order of magnitude Kaplan et al. 2020, pp. 17–18. A rough conversion, just for fun: 1.7 nats = 2.45 bits per token. Kaplan’s own footnote says their text has about 4.3 characters per token p. 18, which gives about 0.57 bits per character. Another route, with a GPT-3 token at about 0.7 words Brown et al. 2020, p. 24 and Shannon’s 4.5 letters per word without spaces Shannon 1951, p. 53, gives 0.78. So: roughly 0.6–0.8 bits per character, near Shannon’s “one bit”. Different texts, tokenizers and methods: treat it as a talking point, not a result.

What this lens says the model knows: exactly as many bits about text as it saves. It is a precise, measurable kind of knowledge. But Shannon built the theory by setting meaning aside, so this lens cannot tell you what those bits are about.

Lens 2 · Linear algebra

same-math Attention is matrix multiplication and a weighted average. structural analogy “Relations between words are directions in space” is an analogy, and it only partly holds.

Words as points, relations as arrows

Mikolov, Yih and Zweig took a model trained only to predict words. Nothing in it was built for grammar or meaning Mikolov et al. 2013b, p. 747. Yet relations showed up as roughly constant offsets: \(x_{apple}-x_{apples}\approx x_{car}-x_{cars}\) p. 746. To answer “a is to b as c is to ?”, compute

\[y=x_b-x_a+x_c\]

and pick the word whose vector points most nearly in the same direction as \(y\) p. 748. “Same direction” is measured by the cosine of the angle between two arrows: 1 if they point the same way, 0 if they are at a right angle, −1 if they point in opposite directions. It is computed from the dot product (multiply the matching entries and add: \((1,2)\cdot(3,4)=1\cdot3+2\cdot4=11\)) divided by the two lengths; rung 7 works an example by hand. Their best model got 39.6% of 8,000 grammar questions right; an older method got 16.5% pp. 748–749. The authors call these regularities a by-product of maximum-likelihood training p. 750. Later, with much more data, a skip-gram model reached 65.6% on a larger test Mikolov et al. 2013a, p. 9. So the arrows are real but rough: about 40% to two thirds of analogies work exactly.

Attention as a weighted average

Think of a library (our analogy). You walk in with a question (your query). Every book has a label on its spine (its key) and contents inside (its value). You compare your question with every label, and you read the books whose labels match best. In a Transformer, each position makes these three vectors from its own word: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what do I pass on?). The match between a query and a key is their dot product. The output at a position is an average of values, weighted by how well its query matches each key Vaswani et al. 2017, pp. 3–4. The softmax turns the match scores into weights that are positive and sum to 1 (rung 6 works it out by hand):

\[\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\]

Read the formula from the inside out: \(QK^{\top}\) holds every query–key dot product (a table of match scores), dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) shrinks them, the softmax turns each row of scores into weights, and multiplying by \(V\) takes the weighted average of the values. Here \(d_k\) is the length of each key vector (64 in the original model p. 5).

Why divide by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)? An instance (ours): a dot product of two 64-number vectors is a sum of 64 products. If each product is a random +1 or −1, the spread (standard deviation) of the sum is \(\sqrt{64}=8\), so scores of ±8 are common. Such scores make the softmax extreme: \(e^{8}\approx2981\) times more weight than \(e^{0}\), so almost all weight goes to one position; then small changes in the scores barely change the attention weights, so learning gets a tiny signal. Dividing by \(\sqrt{64}=8\) brings typical scores back to about ±1. The paper states the general fact: if the parts of \(q\) and \(k\) are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance \(d_k\). The authors suspect large dot products push the softmax into regions with tiny gradients (the learning signal from Step 7), so they scale them down p. 4, footnote 4. In one example sentence, some attention heads seem to link “its” to the noun it refers to; the authors say these heads are “apparently involved in anaphora resolution” p. 14, that is, in working out what a word like “its” or “she” refers to. A picture of attention weights is a hint, not proof of what the model computes.

What this lens says the model knows: relations between words, stored as positions and directions, and a way to mix information from earlier tokens. The geometry is real but approximate. Whether geometry is meaning is the question of Lens 4.

Lens 3 · Bayesian inference

same-math inside one simplified model of text, where it is a theorem. structural analogy for real LLMs, where it is not proved.

Here is a puzzle from Xie and colleagues Xie et al. 2022, p. 1:

Albert Einstein was German
Mahatma Gandhi was Indian
Marie Curie was → Polish

Nobody told the model the task is “nationality”. How does it know? Start with an instance (ours). Imagine a huge library where every document was written in one of a few styles: recipes, football reports, lists of famous people with their countries. You open a page at random and read “Albert Einstein was German. Mahatma Gandhi was Indian.” Which style is this? Before reading, you thought each style was about equally likely; this is your prior. The first line fits the “famous people and countries” style well and the recipe style badly; how well a style explains what you read is its likelihood. Multiply prior by likelihood, rescale so the numbers add up to 1, and you get your updated belief, the posterior. That is Bayes’ rule. After two lines, almost all your belief is on one style, so you predict “Polish” for Marie Curie.

Xie’s idea makes this precise: imagine that every document in the training data was written under some hidden concept \(\theta\) (Greek letter theta), such as a topic, a format, or a task. To predict the next token of a document well, a model has to guess which concept is active. If it predicts perfectly, it must do this guessing implicitly, by averaging over concepts p. 2. Read the formula below aloud: “the chance of an output given the prompt is the average, over all concepts, of the chance of that output under each concept, weighted by how believable each concept is given the prompt.” (The integral sign is just a sum over many concepts.)

\[p(\text{output}\mid\text{prompt})=\int p(\text{output}\mid\text{concept},\text{prompt})\;p(\text{concept}\mid\text{prompt})\;d(\text{concept})\]

A few-shot prompt is evidence about the concept. If the posterior \(p(\text{concept}\mid\text{prompt})\) concentrates on one concept, the model in effect “selects” it p. 3. In-context learning then looks like Bayesian inference, with no weight change at all.

Xie proves this inside a stylized world: documents come from a mixture of hidden Markov models. A hidden Markov model is a simple random writer with a hidden state (ours: a writer whose hidden mood decides which words are likely next; the mood can change from step to step, and we only see the words); the language model fits the training distribution exactly, and the concepts are distinguishable enough. Under these conditions, prediction reaches the best possible accuracy as the number of examples grows Theorem 1, p. 7. Their synthetic tests also show limits. With concepts not seen in training, in-context learning failed p. 10. Changing the order of examples moved accuracy by 10–40%, and zero-shot sometimes beat few-shot pp. 10–11.

What this lens says the model knows: a prior over the kinds of documents people write, and the ability to narrow it down from a prompt. This is a clean story, but it is a theorem about a model of text, tested on synthetic data. It does not show that real LLMs compute posteriors. Xie calls it “a first step” p. 12.

Lens 4 · Philosophy of meaning

structural analogy A thought experiment (an octopus on a cable) stands in for a language model. The argument works only if the analogy holds.

The octopus

Bender and Koller define a language model as any system trained only to predict strings. They define meaning as the relation between a linguistic form (anything observable: letters on a page, bytes, or the sounds of speech) and the speaker’s communicative intent (what the speaker wants to achieve: to inform, to ask for something, or just to socialize) pp. 5185–5187. From this, they argue that training on form alone “cannot in principle” lead to learning meaning Bender & Koller 2020, p. 5185.

Their story: two people, A and B, are stranded on two islands and talk through an underwater cable. A clever octopus, O, taps the cable. It learns the statistical patterns of their messages and starts to answer in B’s place. In small talk, O may pass. Then A writes that a bear is attacking and asks how to defend herself with some sticks. O has never seen a bear or a stick. It can only produce text that looks like an answer pp. 5188–5189. Bender and Koller do not say LMs learn nothing: they grant that BERT has “probably learned something about meaning”, and that language models learn “some reflection of meaning into the linguistic form” p. 5193.

Meaning without reference

Piantadosi and Hill reply that the octopus argument assumes that reference (pointing to things in the world) is what fixes meaning Piantadosi & Hill 2022, pdf 1–2. But many words have no clear referent, such as “justice” pdf 2. On the conceptual role view, meaning comes mainly from how a concept relates to other concepts, the way “force” is tied to “mass” and “acceleration” through \(f=m\cdot a\) pdf 2. Whether next-word prediction discovers such roles is, they say, an empirical question, with a “promising, partial affirmative” answer so far pdf 4. Their claim is conditional: if conceptual role theories are correct, LLMs likely already share the foundation of meaning pdf 5.

Two kinds of competence

Mahowald and colleagues suggest a cleaner split. Formal competence is knowing the rules and statistical patterns of a language. Functional competence is using language to do things in the world Mahowald et al. 2024, pp. 2–3. In the human brain, the language network is not used for arithmetic, logic or reasoning about other minds, and people with severe aphasia (loss of language after brain damage) can still do arithmetic and play chess p. 5. So in people, being good with language and being good at thinking can come apart. For English, LLMs’ formal competence is close to human level; their functional competence is “patchy” p. 2. A test of formal competence: “The keys to the old, wooden kitchen cabinet are…”, where the verb must agree with “keys”, not with the nearer word “cabinet” pp. 7–8.

What this lens says the model knows: it depends on your theory of meaning. If meaning needs a link to intentions and the world, form alone gives at best a reflection. If meaning is mostly relations between concepts, prediction may build part of it. That is why more data alone cannot settle this debate.

Lens 5 · Theory of mind

structural analogy Tests designed for children are given to machines. The open question is whether passing means the same thing in both cases.

Theory of mind (ToM) is the ability to think about what other people believe, even when their beliefs are false. You met one test in prediction 3: John did not see the cat being moved, so he falsely believes it is still in the basket. A false-belief test asks where John will look; a person who answers “in the box” is reporting the world, not John’s mind. A second classic test: a bag full of popcorn has a label that says “chocolate”. Sam has never seen inside. What does Sam think is in the bag? Kosinski 2024, § Study 1.1. To predict what Sam will say, you must track what Sam believes, not what is true. People who write stories describe false beliefs all the time, so a next-word predictor at least gets plenty of examples.

The claim

Kosinski gave 40 such tasks to a series of models. Scoring was strict: each task had 8 versions of the story (the false-belief story, three true-belief controls, and reversed versions of all four), each asked with 2 prompts, and a task counted only if all 16 prompts were answered correctly. A true-belief control is the same story changed slightly so that the character’s belief is true (for example, she looks inside, or someone tells her), so the right answer flips. The controls catch a model that always gives the “false-belief” answer without reading the story § Study 1.3, § Discussion § Abstract, § Study 3. ChatGPT-4 solved 75% of tasks (95% interval 66–84%), which he compares with 6-year-old children § Study 3. He suggests ToM may have emerged as a by-product of language training § Abstract, and he is careful: failures do not necessarily show inability, and successes do not prove ToM § Discussion.

The critics

Ullman made “trivial alterations” to such stories: small changes that, Ullman argues, obviously change the right answer. Make the popcorn bag transparent, so Sam can see the popcorn; now Sam should believe it holds popcorn, whatever the label says. Yet on one of two belief prompts, GPT-3.5 still gave “chocolate” a probability of 95% Ullman 2023, p. 3. His rule of evidence: one clear failure can outweigh many average successes, in the same way that a machine that answers 100 easy multiplications correctly but fails 213 × 261 has probably memorized answers rather than learned to multiply p. 2. Has ToM emerged? “Probably not” p. 8. Shapira and colleagues tested 15 models on 6 datasets and concluded that “no model robustly exhibits ToM on all datasets” Shapira et al. 2024, p. 2261.

Prediction 3 · answer

About 0%. In Shapira’s tests GPT-4 scored 97.5% on the original false-belief transfer items and 0% when the object was put on the container (Ullman’s change “in → on”), or when the container was transparent. In Ullman’s own run with GPT-3.5, the model said John thinks the cat is on the basket with probability 97% Ullman 2023, p. 6 Shapira et al. 2024, pp. 2262–2263. Shapira’s team suggests models might recognize the familiar Sally–Anne pattern rather than track beliefs p. 2262.

The twist

Strachan and colleagues compared models with 1,907 human participants. Humans also failed about half of the Ullman-style changes, which alter physical facts of the story Strachan et al. 2024, § False belief. GPT-4 matched or beat humans on irony, hinting and strange stories § Irony, § Hinting, § Strange stories, but was worse on faux pas (noticing that someone said something awkward without knowing it). Its faux pas errors were mostly “not enough information”; when the question asked what was more likely, GPT-4 answered perfectly. The authors read this as hyperconservatism, an unwillingness to commit, not a failure to infer § Understanding faux pas.

Version trap

Ullman p. 2 and Shapira pp. 2272–2273 criticize an earlier preprint of Kosinski’s paper, which reported higher scores. The published PNAS version, with stricter controls, reports lower ones (for example ChatGPT-4 down from 95% to 75%) Kosinski 2024, § Study 3. Always check which version a critique answers.

What this lens says the model knows: enough about how people write about beliefs to pass many standard tests, but not robustly under small changes. The two sides also disagree about evidence: does one failure decide (Ullman, Shapira), or can a failure have other causes, so that it does not necessarily show inability (Kosinski)? And should we judge behaviour (Kosinski’s Turing-style view) or the likely process behind it (Ullman, p. 9)?

5 · The math ladder

Ten rungs, from a coin flip to scaling laws

Each rung uses small numbers you can check by hand. Rungs 1–2 need only high-school math, rungs 3–7 are undergraduate, rungs 8–10 are graduate level. Logs are base 2 (bits) unless stated.

High school Rung 1 · The chain rule

Shannon’s 1948 paper has a tiny three-letter language, A, B and C, where each letter depends on the one before Shannon 1948, p. 6. The table gives the probability of the next letter in each row:

afterABC
A04/51/5
B1/21/20
C1/22/51/10

In the long run the letters appear with frequencies 9/27, 16/27 and 2/27 p. 6. What is the probability that a random stretch of text reads “BAB”?

\[P(\text{BAB})=P(\text{B})\cdot P(\text{A}\mid\text{B})\cdot P(\text{B}\mid\text{A})\] \[=\tfrac{16}{27}\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac45=\tfrac{64}{270}\approx0.237\]

That is the whole idea of Step 1: a text’s probability is a product of next-symbol guesses. Notice the zeros in the table: in this language “AA” and “BC” really never happen. In real text, many word sequences are possible but unseen. Counting gives them zero unless it backs off to shorter contexts, and that is the gap Bengio’s vectors try to close Bengio et al. 2003, pp. 1138–1139.

High school Rung 2 · Surprise and entropy

Meet the toy world we will reuse in later posts: a village whose weather report has four words.

Entropy is the average surprise, weighted by how often each word appears:

\[H=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot3=0.5+0.5+0.375+0.375=1.75\ \text{bits}\]

The code in the last row uses exactly as many bits as each word’s surprise, so its average length is also 1.75 bits. That is Shannon’s worked example Shannon 1948, p. 18; Cover and Thomas use the same numbers pp. 14–15. Compare: with four equally likely words you would need 2 bits, because \(\log_2 4=2\). Entropy is largest when all outcomes are equally likely Cover & Thomas, p. 27.

Undergraduate Rung 3 · Cross-entropy = entropy + KL divergence

A model \(q\) predicts the weather. On average, the surprise it feels is the cross-entropy \(H(p,q)=-\sum_x p(x)\log_2 q(x)\): the words come from the truth \(p\), but each surprise is computed with \(q\). The KL divergence is defined as \(D(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) Cover & Thomas, p. 18.

Exercise · Why is \(H(p,q)=H(p)+D(p\,\|\,q)\)?

Show it in two lines, using only the definitions above and one rule of logarithms.

Hint

Use \(\log\frac{a}{b}=\log a-\log b\) on the definition of \(D\). Or: inside \(-\log_2 q(x)\), add and subtract \(\log_2 p(x)\).

Solution \[D(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_x p\log_2 p-\sum_x p\log_2 q=-H(p)+H(p,q)\]

Move \(H(p)\) to the other side: \(H(p,q)=H(p)+D(p\,\|\,q)\). Check with numbers below: for the uniform model, \(2.00=1.75+0.25\).

The first part is the language’s own entropy. The second is the KL divergence. Example: the model that thinks all four words are equally likely, \(q=(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14)\), pays 2 bits per word: 1.75 for the language plus 0.25 for being wrong. A model with the order reversed, \(q=(\tfrac18,\tfrac18,\tfrac14,\tfrac12)\), pays

\[\tfrac12\cdot3+\tfrac14\cdot3+\tfrac18\cdot2+\tfrac18\cdot1=2.625\ \text{bits},\quad D=0.875\]

\(D\) is not symmetric. Cover and Thomas show a two-outcome example where \(D(p\|q)=0.2075\) but \(D(q\|p)=0.1887\) bits p. 19.

Undergraduate Rung 4 · Perplexity

Language-model papers often report perplexity instead of bits. It is 2 raised to the average surprise in bits (or \(e\) raised to it, if the log is natural; Bengio defines it as the exponential of the average negative log-likelihood p. 1141):

\[\text{perplexity}=2^{\,H(p)+D(p\|q)}\]

Read it as “the model is as unsure as if it were choosing among this many equally likely words.” For the weather: the true model has perplexity \(2^{1.75}\approx3.36\), the uniform model 4, the reversed model \(2^{2.625}\approx6.17\). In Bengio’s experiments on the Brown corpus, the neural model (averaged with a trigram model) reached a test perplexity of 252, while the best counting model, a class-based n-gram, reached 312 p. 1149. Units matter: Shannon works in bits per letter, Kaplan in nats per token p. 6. One nat is \(1/\ln 2\approx1.443\) bits.

Undergraduate Rung 5 · Context lowers entropy

Back to the A/B/C language. How uncertain is the next letter if you know nothing, if you know only the letter frequencies, and if you also know the previous letter?

  • Know nothing (3 equally likely letters): \(F_0=\log_2 3=1.585\) bits.
  • Know the frequencies \((\tfrac{9}{27},\tfrac{16}{27},\tfrac{2}{27})\): \(F_1=1.254\) bits.
  • Know the previous letter: each row of the table has its own entropy, 0.722 (after A), 1.000 (after B) and 1.361 (after C). Average them with the letter frequencies:
\[F_2=\tfrac{9}{27}(0.722)+\tfrac{16}{27}(1.000)+\tfrac{2}{27}(1.361)=0.934\ \text{bits}\]

Perplexity falls from 3 to 2.38 to 1.91. Shannon’s English table shows the same pattern with 26 letters: 4.70, 4.14, 3.56, about 3.3 bits Shannon 1951, p. 54. Shannon also showed that text generated from such tables looks more and more like English at each step Shannon 1948, pp. 6–7. Try it with the A/B/C language:

Undergraduate Rung 6 · Softmax and attention by hand

The softmax turns any list of scores into probabilities: \(\mathrm{softmax}(s)_i=e^{s_i}/\sum_j e^{s_j}\). Bengio’s model ends with one p. 1142; attention uses one inside. Take three tokens with 2-dimensional vectors, and let queries equal keys for simplicity:

\[Q=K=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\2&2\end{pmatrix}\]

Token 3 looks at tokens 1–3 (the mask hides nothing earlier). Its scores are dot products divided by \(\sqrt{d_k}=\sqrt2\):

\[\tfrac{(1,1)\cdot(1,0)}{\sqrt2}=0.707,\quad \tfrac{(1,1)\cdot(0,1)}{\sqrt2}=0.707,\quad \tfrac{(1,1)\cdot(1,1)}{\sqrt2}=1.414\]

Exponentiate: \(e^{0.707}=2.028\), \(e^{0.707}=2.028\), \(e^{1.414}=4.113\). The sum is 8.169, so the weights are 0.248, 0.248 and 0.503. The output is the weighted average of the values:

\[0.248\,(1,0)+0.248\,(0,1)+0.503\,(2,2)\approx(1.255,\,1.255)\]

Token 1 can only see itself, so its weight is 1 and its output is \((1,0)\). Token 2 sees tokens 1–2 with weights 0.330 and 0.670.

Undergraduate Rung 7 · Analogies with cosines

A 3-dimensional toy: man \((1,0,0)\), woman \((0,1,0)\), king \((0.6,0,0.8)\), queen \((0,0.6,0.8)\). For “man is to king as woman is to ?”, compute

\[y=x_{king}-x_{man}+x_{woman}=(-0.4,\,1,\,0.8),\qquad \|y\|=\sqrt{0.16+1+0.64}=1.342\]

The cosine of two vectors is their dot product divided by both lengths. For queen:

\[\frac{0\cdot(-0.4)+0.6\cdot1+0.8\cdot0.8}{1\cdot1.342}=\frac{1.24}{1.342}=0.924\]

For woman 0.745, king 0.298, man −0.298. Queen wins. Real papers differ on a detail: Mikolov 2013a removes the three input words from the search p. 5; whether Mikolov 2013b does is not stated. If woman had been closer than queen, that choice would decide the answer.

Graduate Rung 8 · In-context learning as a posterior

A toy version of Xie’s formula with two concepts, “nationality” (\(\theta^*\), the true one) and “profession” (\(\theta'\)). Prior ½ each. Each example in the prompt has probability 0.2 under \(\theta^*\) and 0.05 under \(\theta'\). Given the concept, the right answer to the query has probability 0.9 under \(\theta^*\) and 0.1 under \(\theta'\). These numbers are invented for the toy.

After \(n\) examples, Bayes’ rule gives

\[P(\theta^*\mid n)=\frac{\tfrac12(0.2)^n}{\tfrac12(0.2)^n+\tfrac12(0.05)^n}=\frac{4^n}{4^n+1}\]

and the probability of the right answer is \(0.9\,P(\theta^*\mid n)+0.1\,(1-P(\theta^*\mid n))\):

Accuracy climbs toward 0.9, the best possible here. Xie writes the general case in a form that shows why: the weight on concept \(\theta\) is proportional to \(e^{n\,r_n(\theta)}p(\theta)\), where \(r_n\) is the average log-likelihood ratio of the prompt under \(\theta\) versus \(\theta^*\) p. 6. Under their conditions, \(r_n\) for a wrong concept tends to a negative number, so its weight shrinks exponentially pp. 6–7. The catch is the prompt format: examples glued together are unlike any training document, and that mismatch adds noise. Their Condition 1 says the concepts must differ by more than the noise added at the start of the prompt and at the delimiters between examples p. 7.

Graduate Rung 9 · Power laws

Kaplan’s fit for loss as a function of non-embedding parameters \(N\), for models trained to convergence on enough data, is p. 4:

\[L(N)=\left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{0.076}\]

Double \(N\): the ratio is \((N/2N)^{0.076}=2^{-0.076}=0.949\). So each doubling cuts the loss by about 5%, the same factor at every size. On log-log axes a power law is a straight line: \(\log L=0.076\log N_c-0.076\log N\). The constant \(N_c\) has “no fundamental meaning” p. 5. Data must grow too: to avoid overfitting, \(D\gtrsim(5\times10^3)\,N^{0.74}\), so an 8× bigger model needs about \(8^{0.74}\approx4.7\) times more data, which the authors round to 5× pp. 3, 12. A power law cannot fall forever: the loss must flatten out somewhere above zero p. 3. Kaplan locates a likely breakdown at or before \(L^*\sim1.7\) nats per token, where two of his trends contradict each other, and conjectures that \(L^*\) might roughly estimate the entropy of language pp. 17–18.

Link back to Rung 3: the loss Kaplan fits is \(H+D\). Scaling only shrinks \(D\). The floor is \(H\).

Graduate Rung 10 · Bits from guess numbers

How did Shannon turn guess numbers into bits? Let \(q_i\) be the fraction of letters guessed right on the \(i\)-th try. He proved two bounds on \(F_N\) Shannon 1951, p. 61:

\[\sum_i i\,(q_i-q_{i+1})\log_2 i\;\le\;F_N\;\le\;-\sum_i q_i\log_2 q_i\]

The upper bound is intuitive: the reduced text is a code for the text (the twin can decode it), so the text cannot carry more information than the entropy of the guess numbers. The upper bound holds for any predictor; the lower bound is proved for an ideal predictor only, and human data have sampling error pp. 61, 64.

6 · Synthesis

So what does it know?

The five lenses give four layers of answer, from the most certain to the most contested.

  1. Bits about text. Certain and measurable. A lower loss means a smaller \(D(p\|q)\): the model is closer to the true distribution of text. This is knowledge in Shannon’s sense, with meaning set aside.
  2. Structure in text. Well supported. Grammar (formal competence near human level in English), relations between words (directions that work for about 40–66% of analogies), and task formats that a prompt can select (proved in a stylized model).
  3. Meaning. Depends on your theory. Bender and Koller: at most a reflection, if meaning is form plus intent. Piantadosi and Hill: possibly part of the foundation, if meaning is conceptual role.
  4. Minds. Contested. Many tests are passed, but small changes often break the result. Humans fail some of those changes too.

The loss tells you how much a model knows about text. It cannot tell you what kind of knowledge that is. To learn that, you have to change the test in small ways and see what breaks (Ullman’s method), or look inside at the relations the model has built (Piantadosi and Hill’s suggestion, pdf 3).

Notice how the lenses connect. Shannon’s twin decodes text from guess numbers. Xie’s model decodes the concept from a prompt. Ullman’s trivial alterations ask whether the model decoded the belief or only the story pattern. All three are questions about what information the predictor has extracted, and the information-theory lens alone cannot answer the last two.

8 · Where this shows up in your work

Collaboration, Communication, Computation

Computation · LLM surrogate labeling

If few-shot prompting works by selecting a concept (Lens 3), your examples are evidence, not instructions. In Xie’s synthetic tests (which mirror earlier GPT-3 results), example order alone moved accuracy by 10–40% p. 10. Randomize the order of examples across runs and report the spread, not one number.

Computation · Cross-lingual LLM safety

Tokens cover different amounts of text in different languages, so per-token loss or perplexity cannot be compared directly. Use bits per character or per byte (Exercise 3). A model can look “confident” in one language only because its tokens are shorter there.

Computation · Explainable representations

Directions in embedding space are real but rough: exact analogies work about 40–66% of the time in Mikolov’s tests. Before you name a direction (“the misinformation axis”), check how often it gives the exact answer, and against which candidate set.

Communication · Framing and misinformation

Surprise is measurable. The average surprise of a post under a model trained on one community’s text is a number in bits. That number measures distance from the community’s usual language. It does not measure whether the post is true; Shannon set meaning aside, and so does the number.

Collaboration · Social simulation with LLM agents

Agents that “reason about other agents” may pass standard belief stories and still fail under small changes (Lens 5). Add perturbation tests to your simulation checks: change one physical fact the agent should notice, and see whether its beliefs about others update.

9 · Recall questions

Test yourself

Answer aloud or in your head, then open the card. Questions marked ↻ will come back in later posts.

↻ Why is a good predictor also a good compressor?

Because the sequence of guess numbers is a code for the text: an identical predictor can decode it (Shannon’s twin). Better prediction means more 1s, so a shorter code.

↻ The training loss of an LLM splits into two parts. Name them, and say which one training can reduce.

\(H(p)\), the entropy of the language, and \(D(p\|q)\), the KL divergence between truth and model. Training can only reduce \(D\).

What is perplexity, and what is it for the uniform weather model?

2 raised to the average surprise in bits: the number of equally likely choices with the same uncertainty. Uniform model: \(2^2=4\).

In attention, why divide the scores by \(\sqrt{d_k}\)?

If the parts of \(q\) and \(k\) are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, \(q\cdot k\) has variance \(d_k\). Large scores push the softmax into regions with tiny gradients, the authors suspect; scaling keeps them moderate.

↻ In Xie’s account, what does a few-shot prompt do?

It is evidence about a hidden concept. The posterior over concepts concentrates, and the model in effect selects the concept, with no weight change. This is proved only inside a stylized model of text.

What does the octopus argument assume about meaning, according to Piantadosi and Hill?

That reference (links to things in the world) determines meaning. On a conceptual role view, relations between concepts carry most of the meaning, so form could carry some of it.

Formal vs functional competence: give one example of each.

Formal: making the verb agree in “The keys to the old, wooden kitchen cabinet are…”. Functional: using language to reason or act in the world, for example to plan a defense against a bear.

↻ Why does it matter which version of Kosinski’s paper a critic answers?

Ullman and Shapira answered the preprint, which reported higher scores. The PNAS version used stricter controls and reports lower scores (ChatGPT-4: 75%).

Shapira argues that earlier work over-generalized from a handful of examples.
an upper / lower bound on
The entropy of the guess numbers is an upper bound on the entropy of the text.
to be at ceiling
On the basic false-belief test, both humans and GPT-4 were at ceiling.
a talking point, not a result
Our bits-per-letter conversion is a talking point, not a result.
the burden of proof lies with
Ullman thinks the burden of proof lies with those who claim the ability has emerged.

From B2 to C1: how native speakers talk about evidence

B2 English says what is true. C1 English says how sure you are, how much it matters, and where the argument turns. These expressions are common in seminars and reviews, and each example uses an idea from this post.

Expressions for discussing evidence
to read too much into something
Don’t read too much into one attention map; it is a hint, not proof.
on the face of it
On the face of it, GPT-4 passes the false-belief test. Change one word and it fails.
that’s a big if
Xie’s theorem works if the model fits the training data exactly, and that’s a big if.
to boil down to / to hinge on
The octopus debate boils down to what you think meaning is. Everything hinges on that one choice.
to cut both ways
The perturbation argument cuts both ways: humans fail some of those changes too.
to tease apart
Mahowald and colleagues try to tease apart formal and functional competence.
to stop short of
Piantadosi and Hill stop short of saying that LLMs understand language as we do.
a far cry from
Summaries that just barely beat random sentences are a far cry from real summarization.
a bit of a stretch
Calling it “learning” is a bit of a stretch: not a single weight changes.
to take something with a grain of salt
Take our 0.6–0.8 bits per character with a grain of salt; the corpora are different.
the jury is still out (on)
The jury is still out on whether machines have theory of mind.
to lend weight to
Strachan’s human data lend weight to the idea that some perturbations are simply hard.
to give someone pause
A drop from 97.5% to 0% should give us pause.
as a rule of thumb
As a rule of thumb, each doubling of model size cuts the loss by about 5%.
to sidestep a question
Shannon deliberately sidesteps the question of meaning.
Four sentence patterns that sound C1
Hedging ladder
shows → indicates → suggests → is consistent with → hints at. “Xie’s results are consistent with concept selection; they do not show that GPT-3 computes a posterior.”
Concession, then your point
“Granted, the loss falls smoothly. But that tells us little about when a skill appears.” / “While it is true that … , …”
Cleft sentence for focus
“What Shannon showed was that prediction and compression are the same problem.” / “It is the output layer, not the hidden layer, that dominates Bengio’s cost.”
Inversion for emphasis (writing, formal talks)
“Not only does the model fail, but it fails with 97% confidence.” / “Only when the output is made cheap does the hidden layer become the bottleneck.”
Small fixes that make a big difference
research (uncountable)
“do research” or “conduct a study”; not “a research” or “researches”.
discuss / explain
“discuss the result” (no “about”); “explain it to me” (not “explain me”).
in my view
Use “in my view” or “as I see it”, not “according to my opinion”.
a / the
“the entropy of English” (one specific quantity), “a model of text” (one of many), “Ø language models” (in general).
How to practise (15 minutes a day)
Shadowing
Loop one audio chapter. Speak along with it, half a second behind, first at 1×, then at 1.25×. Copy the stress and the pauses, not only the words.
Upgrade drill
Select a sentence in this post, write your own version in a note, then tap “Ask Claude” with: “Upgrade this to C1 and explain each change.” Save the answer as a note.
90-second retell
Close the page and explain one lens aloud in 90 seconds, as if to a colleague. Use one hedge, one concession and one expression from this list. Record it once a week to hear your progress.
Collocation notebook
Collect verb + noun pairs, not single words: draw a distinction, raise a question, make a case, address a concern, run an experiment.
