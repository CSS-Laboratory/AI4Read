## MAJOR

**M1 · Step 4 · "Bengio's network has a slow part" (the hidden layer is presented as the slow part of Bengio's network)**
- Post: "Bengio’s network has a slow part. After looking up the vectors of the previous words, it passes them through a hidden layer … Then a final layer gives a probability to every word in the vocabulary <cite>pp. 1141–1142</cite>." A reader takes the hidden layer to be the slow part.
- Source: in Bengio's network as built, the bottleneck is the **output** layer. Bengio's Figure 1 (p. 1142) writes "most computation here" on the arrow from the tanh layer to the softmax. Mikolov 2013a, p. 3: "Q = N × D + N × D × H + H × V, where the dominating term is H × V." Only with a binary-tree output does "most of the complexity [come from] the term N × D × H". The post's next sentences give that condition, but the opening sentence contradicts both sources. Our own lab readout agrees with the sources: 500,000,000 of the 502,505,000 operations are H·V.
- Fix: "Bengio’s network has two slow parts. After looking up the vectors of the previous words, it passes them through a hidden layer … Then a final layer gives a probability to every word in the vocabulary <cite>Bengio et al. 2003, pp. 1141–1142</cite>. That final layer is the slowest: Bengio’s own diagram marks it ‘most computation here’ <cite>p. 1142</cite>." Then keep the Mikolov sentences. After "…the hidden layer <cite>p. 3</cite>", add: "So once the output is made cheap, the hidden layer becomes the bottleneck."

11. **Step 7 · "Training is billions of such nudges."** The post defines one round of nudges as a gradient update, so this reads as billions of updates. GPT-3 used batches of 3.2M tokens (Brown Table 2.1, p. 8), so 300B tokens is roughly 300×10⁹ / 3.2×10⁶ ≈ 94,000 rounds (our arithmetic; the batch was ramped up early, so slightly more). Each round nudges all 175 billion weights at once. The post's sentence "The model makes a guess, checks the real next token, and then nudges…" also suggests one update per token. Fix: "In practice the model makes guesses for a large batch of text (3.2 million tokens at a time for GPT-3 <cite>Brown et al. 2020, p. 8</cite>), then nudges every weight at once. Training GPT-3 took on the order of a hundred thousand such rounds (our estimate: 300 billion ÷ 3.2 million)."

12. **Step 8 lab note · "real models deviate from it outside the range the authors measured".** No source supports this as a fact. Kaplan p. 3: "We observe no signs of deviation from these trends on the upper end, though performance must flatten out eventually before reaching zero loss." The measured range is about 10³ to 10⁹ non-embedding parameters (Figure 2 caption, p. 4). The slider goes to 10¹⁰, beyond that range. Fix: "This is only the fitted formula. The authors measured models from about 10³ to 10⁹ parameters <cite>p. 4</cite>. Beyond that the line is an extrapolation: they saw no sign of deviation at the top, but the loss must flatten out somewhere above zero <cite>p. 3</cite>."

    (b) "A model can gain a new skill suddenly while its average surprise only drops a little." This glosses p. 19 "may hide", and turns "may" into "can". Fix: "In other words (our gloss), a model might gain a new skill suddenly while its average surprise drops only a little."

15. **Lens 1 · "For these two examples it matches exactly <cite>Cover & Thomas, p. 15</cite>."** Cover & Thomas p. 15 covers only the ½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛ example ("The resulting expected number of binary questions required is 1.75. 16. **Lens 1 · forecaster sentence: the 1.75 is an average.** "Her code always costs 2 bits per day, while the code that fits the true weather costs 1.75." The fitted code costs 1, 2 or 3 bits on a given day, and 1.75 only on average. Fix: "…while the code that fits the true weather costs 1.75 bits per day on average."

17. **Lens 2 · √d_k example wording.** "small changes in the weights barely change the output" is ambiguous. In this post "weights" means the model's weights (Step 7), but the mechanism is about attention scores. Also, for 64 random ±1 terms the standard deviation is 8, while the typical absolute size is about 6.4 (simulated). Fix: "the spread (standard deviation) of such a sum is \(\sqrt{64}=8\), so scores of ±8 are common … almost all weight goes to one position; then small changes in the scores barely change the attention weights, so learning gets a tiny signal."

18. **Lens 4 · definition of form.** The post says form is "the visible shape of an utterance: letters, words, sentences", cited to p. 5185. Bender & Koller define it more broadly: "any observable realization of language: marks on a page, pixels or bytes in a digital representation of text, or movements of the articulators" (p. 5186). Their list of communicative intents ("to convey some information … or to ask them to do something; or simply to socialize") is on p. 5187. Fix: "a linguistic <dfn>form</dfn> (anything observable: letters on a page, bytes, or the sounds of speech) and the speaker’s <dfn>communicative intent</dfn> (what the speaker wants to achieve: to inform, to ask for something, or just to socialize) <cite>pp. 5185–5187</cite>".

19. **Lens 5 · Kosinski's true-belief controls.** The post says controls are stories "where the character does see what happened, so the right answer flips". Kosinski § Study 1.3 describes them as "false-belief stories minimally modified to remove the factors leading the protagonist to develop a false-belief". The three types are: the protagonist looks inside / is present; the label matches the contents (or there is no transfer); the protagonist is told. Only some involve seeing. The purpose ("If a respondent maintains that a protagonist should hold false-beliefs in a true-belief scenario, it suggests that they are guessing…") is in § Study 1.3 and § Discussion, which the post does not cite. Fix: "three <dfn>true-belief controls</dfn>, the same story changed slightly so the character’s belief is true (for example, she looks inside, or someone tells her), so the right answer flips … The controls catch a model that always gives the ‘false-belief’ answer without reading the story <cite>§ Study 1.3, § Discussion</cite>." (Optional: "each asked with 2 prompts, one about the facts and one about the character’s belief".)

20. **Lens 5 · "small changes that a person would see at once change the right answer".** This conflicts with the post's own next section, where Strachan's humans "also failed about half of the Ullman-style changes". Ullman argues the changes should be trivial; he did not test people. Fix: "small changes that, Ullman argues, obviously change the right answer."
