# True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

*Page references:* each source PDF page holds two book pages. The metadata says printed pages are unknown, but the page images show them. So I cite both, as (p. printed, pdf N).

## L0 — One line
Having beliefs and desires means being a system whose behaviour is reliably predictable by treating it as a rational agent; the patterns this reveals are objective, but they are visible only from that stance.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks what it is to believe something. Realists say belief is an inner state, like a virus. Interpretationists say it is a matter of point of view. He argues both are partly right. The *intentional strategy* treats a system as a rational agent: attribute the beliefs and desires it ought to have, then predict that it acts to satisfy them. It works well on people, and less well on animals, thermostats and plants. A system that is predictable this way is a *true believer*. The patterns found are objective, but imperfect, so rival interpretations can tie. A 1987 "Reflections" section uses Conway's Game of Life to defend this view. (pdf 13, p. 15; pdf 25, p. 39)

## L2 — Key ideas

- **Two views of belief, and a middle path.** Realism treats belief as an inner fact, like a virus. Interpretationism treats it as relative, like "has style". Dennett says belief is objective, but it can be discerned only from a predictive strategy, and it is confirmed only by that strategy's success. (pdf 13, pp. 14–15)
- **Three predictive stances.** The physical stance uses physical laws. The design stance uses what a thing was designed to do. The intentional stance treats the thing as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. Each stance is used when the one below it becomes impractical. (pdf 14, pp. 16–17)
- **Attribution rule: "what it ought to have".** Beliefs are what the system ought to believe, given its situation and purposes. Desires are what it ought to want. Rationality is assumed to be high and revised downward when needed. (pdf 15–16, pp. 18–21)
- **True believers mostly believe truths.** False beliefs need a special story, and that story is built mostly from true beliefs. (pdf 15, pp. 18–19)
- **The strategy is very powerful, even if it is not exact.** It cannot predict a chess move exactly, but it cuts the legal moves down to a few. It lets us chain predictions over long causal arcs that no physics could predict. (pdf 18, pp. 24–25)
- **The Martian thought experiment.** Laplacean Martians who predict everything physically would still miss a real pattern. Many physical ways of doing something are equivalent at the level of beliefs and desires. (pdf 18–19, pp. 25–27)
- **Belief is a matter of degree.** A thermostat's "beliefs" are weakly fixed to the world. As a system becomes richer, its interpretation becomes practically unique. No magic moment separates a thermostat from us. (pdf 21–22, pp. 30–33)
- **Why the strategy works.** One answer is "design", which is evolution for people. The harder answer is about internal mechanism. A language of thought is a likely but not necessary hypothesis. (pdf 22–23, pp. 33–35)
- **Real patterns and empty questions.** The Game of Life shows an objective pattern at a higher level than physics. When the patterns are imperfect, rival interpretations may tie, and then there is no "deeper fact". (pdf 25–26, pp. 38–41)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the problem of belief attribution (pp. 13–15)
- *Death Speaks.* The chapter opens with Maugham's story of the merchant's servant who flees Death to Samarra. Death says her gesture was surprise, because she had an appointment with him in Samarra that night. (pdf 12, p. 13)
- Talk about belief is everywhere in the social sciences. It is risky in exotic cases: religious beliefs, animals, infants, computers, and contradictory or wildly false beliefs. Example: a man in a bar asks a biologist "Are rabbits birds?" and says "Damn!" at the answer. Could he *really* have believed they were? (pdf 12–13, pp. 13–14)
- Familiar cases look easy and objective. So some think the beliefs could be confirmed by "finding something inside the believer's head". On that view, physiological psychology could in principle override any "black box" method based on external criteria. (pdf 13, p. 14)
- Two opposing views:
  - *Realism* compares having a belief to being infected with a virus: an objective inner fact that observers can guess at. (pdf 13, pp. 14–15)
  - *Interpretationism* compares it to being immoral, having style, or being a good wife. We say "it depends on what you're interested in." (pdf 13, p. 15)
- **Thesis.** Belief is a perfectly objective phenomenon, yet it can be discerned only by someone who adopts a certain predictive strategy. Its existence can be confirmed only by assessing that strategy's success. (pdf 13, p. 15)
- The intentional strategy, to a first approximation, treats the object as a rational agent with beliefs, desires and other states exhibiting *intentionality* (Brentano's term). (pdf 13, p. 15)
- **Definition.** To be a true believer is to be an *intentional system*: a system whose behaviour is "reliably and voluminously predictable" via the intentional strategy. Dennett notes his earlier arguments (1971, 1976b, 1978a) won few converts. (pdf 13, p. 15)

### The Intentional Strategy and How It Works (pp. 15–22)
- *Strategy versus explanation.* A strategy can be good without our knowing why it works. Astrology is the example. Its predictions work only through luck or vagueness. Suppose it did work on some "astrological systems". We could then ask *how* the strategy works (its rules) and *why* it works. Those are different questions. The class of astrological systems is empty. (pdf 14, p. 16)
- **Physical stance.** Determine the physical constitution of the system and the impinging inputs, then apply the laws of physics. Laplace's version covers the whole universe. Modest versions are the chemist's, and the cook's who predicts what a pot left on the burner will do. It always works in principle, which Dennett calls a dogma of the physical sciences. (pdf 14, p. 16)
- **Design stance.** Ignore the physical details and predict that the object behaves *as designed*. Users predict a computer without knowing its physics. Anyone predicts an alarm clock without knowing whether it is spring or silicon. More detail means descending to a less abstract level of design. Only the designed behaviour is predictable this way. An alarm clock filled with liquid helium needs the physical stance. Biological things (hearts, stamens, pistils) are also designed systems. (pdf 14, pp. 16–17)
- **Intentional stance.** Four steps: (pdf 14, p. 17)
  1. Decide to treat the object as a rational agent.
  2. Work out what beliefs it ought to have, given its place in the world and its purpose.
  3. Work out what desires it ought to have, on the same grounds.
  4. Predict that it will act to further its goals in the light of its beliefs. Practical reasoning from these beliefs and desires will often, not always, yield a decision. That decision is the prediction.

**Intuition note.** The stances trade accuracy against effort. The intentional stance gives up knowing the mechanism, and in return gives predictions where the other stances are unusable.

**Populating heads with beliefs.** (pdf 15, pp. 18–19)
- Exposure to *x* (sensory confrontation over a suitable time) is *normally sufficient* for knowing about *x*. Abnormal ignorance faces a high threshold. "I didn't know the gun was loaded" from someone who was present, sighted and awake meets utter scepticism. (p. 18)
- We learn only the *relevant* truths. Example: the ratio of spectacle-wearers to trousered people in a room is learnable but of no interest. (p. 18)
- **First rule:** attribute as beliefs all truths relevant to the system's interests (desires) that its experience so far has made available. This attributes somewhat too much, because people forget. It also misses false beliefs. (p. 18)
- False beliefs need "a special genealogy" made mostly of true beliefs. Two paradigm cases:
  - S falsely believes *p* because S truly believes that Jones, who is clever and not deceiving, told him *p*.
  - S falsely believes there is a snake on the barstool because S truly believes he seems to see one, and so on. (p. 18)
- Sophisticated beliefs, true or false, arise from reasoning that traces back to mundane beliefs. So **true believers mainly believe truths**. If beliefs could be counted, all but the smallest part (say under ten percent) would be attributable by the first rule. (p. 19)
- Footnote 1 (p. 19): this is near-trivial once we separate *belief* from *opinion*. Opinions are linguistically infected states, roughly bets on a formulated sentence. The Democritus example: even if all his scientific claims were false, they would be a small part of his beliefs. He also had countless humdrum standing beliefs (which house he lived in) and perceptual beliefs. Theory-ladenness does not make these mainly false. Dennett cites Churchland 1979 for how tight the theory–experience link can be.
- The first rule is a derived rule. The fundamental rule is: **attribute those beliefs the system ought to have**. (p. 20)

**Desires.** (pdf 16, pp. 20–21)
- Attribute the desires the system ought to have: survival, no pain, food, comfort, procreation, entertainment. Citing one of these ends the "Why?" game. (p. 20)
- Derived rules: attribute desires for what the system believes good for it. Better, attribute desires for what it believes to be the best means to other ends it desires. Bizarre or harmful desires need special stories, like false beliefs. (p. 20)
- Language complicates things. It lets us express very specific desires, such as the two-egg mushroom omelette. It also forces us to commit to desires more stringent than we would otherwise have. Once you have said what you want, you have an interest in satisfying exactly that. Example: "I'd like some baked beans, please." "Yes sir. How many?" (p. 20)
- The same pressure exists for belief. Our linguistic environment forces us to give precise verbal form to convictions that lack hard edges. Focusing on the results of this pressure misleads us into thinking beliefs and desires are *sentences stored in the head*. Those are special cases and may not model the whole domain. (p. 21)

**Rationality.** Start with the ideal and revise downward. Assume people believe all implications of their beliefs and hold no contradictory pairs. Only the implications relevant to the moment matter. Real irrationality and finite inference power are set aside for chapter 4 and Cherniak 1986. (pdf 16, p. 21)

**Use of the strategy.** (pdf 16–17, pp. 21–22)
- People use it "all the time", and it works with people almost all the time. Example: why Oxford colleges should not each grant degrees whenever they like. The reasons are easy to generate. Our use is so habitual that we overlook it. (pp. 21–22)
- It works on most mammals, and on birds, fish, reptiles, insects and spiders. It works on clams (once a clam believes danger is about, it keeps its shell shut until it is convinced the danger has passed). It works on a chess computer that "knows" a line of play would lose its rook. It works on a thermostat that turns off the boiler when it "comes to believe" the room is warm. (p. 22)
- It works on plants: in a place with late spring storms, plant apple varieties cautious about concluding it is spring. It even works on lightning, which "always wants" the best path to ground, but is sometimes tricked into the second-best path. (p. 22)

### True Believers as Intentional Systems (pp. 22–33)
- The cases above are a motley mix of serious attributions, metaphors and frauds. Sorting those that *really* have beliefs from those that merely *as if* have them would be a Sisyphean labour or end by fiat. Better: even in the worst cases the strategy works at least a little. That marks the class of intentional systems, as against objects for which it never works. (pdf 17, pp. 22–23)
- **The lectern objection.** The lectern could be "a rational agent believing it is at the centre of the civilized world and wanting to stay there". It stays put, so the strategy predicts it. Dennett's reply: the strategy gives no predictive power we did not already have. We tailored the beliefs to fit. With people, animals and computers it is often the only practical strategy. (pdf 17, p. 23)
- A further objection: this is only a limit of our capacities. A Laplacean physicist needs neither design nor intentional shortcuts. Engineers understand a thermostat without anthropomorphizing it. It would be intolerable if something were a believer for one observer and not for a cleverer one. That would be "a particularly radical version of interpretationism". Dennett says his earlier presentation sometimes invited that reading and he now discourages it. **The decision to adopt the stance is free, but the facts about its success or failure are perfectly objective.** (pdf 17–18, pp. 23–24)
- **Power of the strategy.** (pdf 18, pp. 24–25)
  - *Chess.* Many legal moves exist, but only perhaps half a dozen have anything to be said for them. So the strategy yields few high-probability moves even when it cannot name one. (p. 24)
  - *Real world.* It cannot predict a stock trader's exact trades or a politician's exact words. It can be very confident that the trader will not buy utilities today, or that the politician will side with the unions against his party. This neutrality about implementation allows *chaining predictions*. (p. 24)
  - *Example.* The US Secretary of State announces he is a KGB agent. We can predict dozens of consequences. The President confers with the Cabinet, the Secretary is relieved of duty pending investigation, a news conference follows, then stories, editorials, letters to the editor. This is an arc of causation in space-time that no practical extension of physics or biology could predict under any description. (pp. 24–25)
- **Nozick's Martians.** Vastly superior beings treat us as engineers treat thermostats. They predict by Laplacean physics, for example the ink marks on the "Closing Dow Jones Industrial Average" tape, and never use the intentional stance. Are we then not believers? Then belief would be "in the eye of the beholder", given the beholder's limits. (pdf 18, p. 25)
  - Dennett's reply: the Martians would miss a perfectly objective *pattern*, describable only from the intentional stance, that supports generalizations and predictions. (pp. 25–26)
  - *Stockbroker.* The Martians predict the exact finger movements and vocal-cord vibrations of a broker ordering 500 shares of General Motors. If they do not see that indefinitely many other movement patterns, even by other people, would have left the market unchanged, they miss a real pattern. (p. 26)
  - There are indefinitely many ways of *being a spark plug*, and of *ordering 500 shares*. There are "societal sockets" where any of them has the same effect. There are "pivot points" where what people do depends on whether they believe *p* or desire *A*, and not on how else they differ. (pdf 19, p. 26)
- **The prediction contest.** (pdf 19, pp. 26–27)
  - A Martian and an Earthling watch Mrs. Gardner's phone call: "You're coming home early? Within the hour? And bringing the boss to dinner? Pick up a bottle of wine… and drive carefully."
  - The Earthling predicts that a large metallic vehicle will stop in the drive within an hour and disgorge two humans, one holding a paper bag with a bottle of alcohol.
  - The Martian makes the same prediction, but only by computing a vast number of physical interactions. Example: the deceleration at intersection *A*, five miles away, which avoids a collision. The Earthling's success would look like magic.
- Fatalists such as astrologers think there are patterns in human affairs that impose themselves *come what may*. They are wrong, but almost right. There are patterns that impose themselves "not quite inexorably but with great vigor", absorbing physical perturbations that might as well be random. These are the patterns of beliefs, desires and intentions. (p. 27)
- *Flaw in the thought experiment.* The Martian treats the Earthling as an intelligent being with beliefs and desires. Even the fix of the Earthling disguising himself as a Martian "might obscure the moral". The moral is **the unavoidability of the intentional stance towards oneself and one's fellow intelligent beings**. This unavoidability is itself interest-relative. One can take a physical stance towards a person, oneself included, but one keeps the intentional stance towards oneself, and towards fellows if one wants to learn what they know (Hampshire). (p. 27)
- Footnote 2 (p. 28): if the Martian included the Earthling in his physical stance, he would predict the X-ray modulations. But his written prediction would still read like a Ouija board. The Martian would be baffled how this isolated mechanism produced a *true* sentence. Footnote 3 (p. 28): beings with no use for communicating, predicting or observing would not be called intelligent.
- Intelligent beings, even Martians, view themselves as intentional systems. "Where there are intelligent beings, the patterns must be there to be described." (pdf 20, p. 28)
- **Imperfections.** The strategy works "as well as it does, which is not perfectly". No one is perfectly rational, unforgetful or immune to malfunction. Where beliefs seem contradictory or self-deceived, the canons of interpretation fail to give stable verdicts. Compare: a partly melted amplifier cannot be drawn as a wiring diagram. (p. 28)
- *Strong realism* says the person really has particular beliefs the strategy cannot divine. Dennett's *milder realism* says that in degenerate cases there is no fact of the matter about which beliefs a person has. But "when and why there is no fact of the matter is itself a matter of objective fact". This is not relativism. (pdf 20, pp. 28–29)
- Beliefs are interest-relative, and different cultures might attribute different beliefs. Even so, there are objective facts about how well each rival scheme predicts. Two schemes may tie, and that too can be a fact. The presence of one pattern does not exclude another. Radically different equally good interpretations are metaphysically important but practically negligible for humans. (p. 29)
- Footnote 4 (p. 29): McCarthy's cryptography analogy. The larger the cipher text, the less chance of two systematically unrelated decipherings.

**Intuition note.** A believer is not a thing with a secret inner label. It is something whose behaviour fits a rational-agent description, and that fit is a measurable and imperfect fact.

**The thermostat and the growth of semantics.** (pdf 20–22, pp. 29–33)
- The perverse claim remains: *all there is* to really believing *p* is being an intentional system for which *p* occurs as a belief in the best (most predictive) interpretation. But for interesting systems this "shallow" criterion puts a severe constraint on internal constitution. (p. 29)
- The thermostat is granted about half a dozen beliefs ("the room is too cold", "the boiler is on") and fewer desires. This is too much, since it has no concept of heat. So *de-interpret*: it believes the *A* is too *F* or *G*; if it wants the *A* more *F*, it should do *K*. Attach it to other devices and it could regulate a water tank or train speed. (p. 30)
- Now enrich it. Give it several ways of learning the temperature (a shivering-occupant detector, an "ear"). Give it geography ("Winnipeg in December"). Give it behavioural versatility (choosing fuel, buying from the cheapest dealer, checking weather stripping). This gives belief-like states *more to do*. (p. 30)
- In formal terms, the class of indistinguishably satisfactory models of the formal system in its internal states gets smaller. Eventually a unique semantic interpretation is practically, but never in principle, dictated (cf. Hayes 1979). Then we say it has beliefs *about heat* and *about this very room*. We say so because we cannot imagine another niche where it would work. (pp. 30–31)
- The belief is about *that* boiler because the state is *fastened* to it (footnote 5, p. 31: the ancestor of de re belief; see chapter 5). With rich perception and behaviour, substituting a different link changes the system's organization. The system notices and changes state. This is a two-way constraint of growing specificity between device and environment. It is the familiar idea that the organism *mirrors* the environment, or contains a *representation* of it. (p. 31)
- **Key statement:** we do not attribute beliefs only to things in which we *find* internal representations. When the strategy works, we try to interpret some internal states as representations. What makes a feature a representation is its role in regulating the behaviour of an intentional system. (pdf 22, p. 32)
- **No magic moment** between thermostat and human, only demanding degrees. **Twin Earth** (Putnam): a planet identical to Earth except for a property beneath our discrimination (their "water" has a different chemical analysis). You would be swapped without noticing. A thermostat's Twin Earths are easy to devise, but ours must be virtual replicas. Your boiler beliefs are about the boiler in your cellar, since no other boiler fits. The principles and problems of interpretation are the *same* for people and thermostats, differing hugely in degree. Understanding a simple intentional system's internals tells little about a human's. (pp. 32–33)

### Why Does the Intentional Strategy Work? (pp. 33–35)
- The question is ambiguous. For a thermostat, one answer is "it was well designed to be easily comprehended and manipulated this way", which is true but uninformative. The other answer, how the machinery works, is readily available. (pdf 22, p. 33)
- For people, the first answer is that evolution designed us to be rational, which is true, brief and uninformative. The harder question is how the machinery works, and we cannot yet answer it. Knowing how the *strategy* works does not help much. (p. 33)
- *Skinnerian behaviourist answer.* Belief and desire talk is shorthand for unimaginably complex histories of response and reinforcement. Even if true, it says little about internal regulation. (pp. 33–34)
- *Language-of-thought answer.* For each predictively attributable belief there is a functionally salient internal state, decomposable like the believed sentence. The inferences we attribute are mirrored by causal processes, and logical form is copied by structural form. Brains would be symbol-manipulating systems, roughly like computers. Dennett thinks some version, much attenuated, will prove correct. (p. 34)
- But it is not obvious. People confuse two empirical claims. First: intentional description yields an objective real pattern (confirmed beyond scepticism). Second: that pattern is *produced by* another roughly isomorphic real pattern inside brains. Doubting the second is not doubting the first. (p. 34)
- *Best simple argument for the second.* Designing systems of rising complexity runs into **combinatorial explosion**. Increasing a parameter by ten percent can increase internal complexity by orders of magnitude. The brain, though finite, compact, reliable and swift, solves this. "Elegant, generative, indefinitely extendable principles of representation" must be responsible. The only model we have is human language. So the argument is "what else could it be?" That justifies pursuing the hypothesis, as scientific tactics. Footnote 6 (p. 35): all proposed models fall victim to combinatorial explosion in some way, so one should temper enthusiasm for what Fodor calls "the only game in town". (pdf 23, pp. 34–35)
- Last line of the chapter proper: one does not understand even a true empirical hypothesis while believing it necessarily true. (p. 35)

### Reflections: Real Patterns, Deeper Facts, and Empty Questions (pp. 37–41)
- Dennett restates the main worry about his view: is belief attribution in the eye of the beholder, or are there objective truths? His position is on the knife-edge between simple realism and simple relativism. It is "a *sort* of realism", because the patterns the Martians miss are really there. (pdf 24, p. 37)
- **Game of Life** (Conway; Gardner 1970). The world is a grid of cells, each ON or OFF, with eight neighbours, and time advances in discrete ticks. The single law: (pdf 24–25, pp. 37–38)

> Each cell, in order to determine what to do in the next instant, counts how many of its eight neighbors is ON at the present instant. If the answer is exactly two, the cell stays in its present state (ON or OFF) in the next instant. If the answer is exactly three, the cell is ON in the next instant whatever its current state. Under all other conditions, the cell is OFF. (p. 38)

- In formula form (added step): let $n$ be the number of ON neighbours. Then
$$
\text{next}(c)=\begin{cases}\text{current}(c) & n=2\\ \text{ON} & n=3\\ \text{OFF} & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$
- This is a deterministic "Laplacean playpen": the physical stance predicts with perfect accuracy. (p. 38)
- Some configurations are interesting: blinkers, gliders and glider guns, puffer trains, eaters, and so on. Knowing their behaviour lets us take the **design stance** to build larger assemblages. A triumph is a universal Turing machine. (p. 38)
- A **Turing-machine stance** predicts by calculating the function the machine computes and translating the output back into ONs and OFFs. This ignores both Life physics and design details. It works provided no stray gliders or debris destroy it. (pdf 25, p. 39)
- Is the pattern "real"? So long as it lasts, yes, and it may last long if "armor" insulates it from noise. Its reality is independent of its history of origin. Seeing it needs a leap of insight, and nothing is hidden in the Life world. (p. 39)
- **Claim.** The intentional stance gives a vantage point for similarly useful patterns. They are objective ("there to be detected") but not *out there* independently of us, because they are made partly from our own reactions. They are patterns "made to order for our narcissistic concerns" (Akins 1986). They are easy for us to see, and visible only from that stance. Footnote 1 (p. 39): Anscombe's "order which is there whenever actions are done with intentions" has no easily stated location; it is as hard to say where it is as where the patterns are in Life. (p. 39)
- Martians might find it hard but can aspire to know our regularities, as we can aspire to know the world of the spider or fish. Dennett declines Rorty's radical perspectivism. (pdf 26, p. 40)
- Because the patterns are imperfect, there will be *uninterpretable gaps*. Rival interpretations can tie for first place, so no further fact could settle what the system *really* believed. This extends Quine's indeterminacy of radical translation to internal "behaviour" too. "Radical translation begins at home" (Quine 1969, p. 46, quoted p. 40). A quoted Quine passage says the question whether the foreigner really believes *A* or *B* is one whose significance he doubts. (p. 40)
- "Brain Writing and Mind Reading" (1975) applies the same point to any language of thought one might find. Davidson (1974a) is quoted: indeterminacy marks that some apparent distinctions are not significant. Footnote 2 mentions Wheeler's reading of Derrida. (pp. 40–41)
- **Parfit.** Questions of personal identity may have indeterminate cases. Parfit's club example: a club lapses, then members form another with the same name and rules. If no rule or legal fact settles it, "this is the same club" is neither true nor false. Such a question is **empty**. There is no "deeper fact". The belief that "our identity must be determinate", and likewise that the contents of beliefs must be determinate, "dies hard". Nagel (1986) is introduced as someone who cannot abandon it (the quotation is cut off). (pdf 26, p. 41)

**Intuition note.** The Life world shows how a higher-level pattern can be fully real and fully visible without being a new physical ingredient. It also shows that you can miss it while knowing every physical fact.

## Toy example (ours)

*Part 1: the Life blinker, by hand.* Use coordinates (x, y). Put three ON cells in a horizontal row: (0,0), (1,0), (2,0). All others are OFF.

Apply the law (p. 38) to each relevant cell:
- (1,0), the centre. Its ON neighbours are (0,0) and (2,0), so n = 2. It stays ON.
- (0,0). Its only ON neighbour is (1,0), so n = 1. It goes OFF. By symmetry (2,0) has n = 1 and goes OFF.
- (1,1). Its neighbours (0,0), (1,0) and (2,0) are all ON, and the other five are OFF. So n = 3. It turns ON. By symmetry (1,−1) also has n = 3 and turns ON.
- (0,1). Its ON neighbours are (0,0) and (1,0), so n = 2. It is OFF, so it stays OFF. By symmetry (2,1), (0,−1) and (2,−1) are also OFF.
- Every other cell has at most 1 ON neighbour, so it stays OFF.

Result: the ON cells are (1,−1), (1,0), (1,1), a vertical row. Applying the same law again returns the horizontal row, by symmetry (added step).

*Part 2: pattern-level prediction.* After checking this once, we can say "the blinker flips every tick" and predict tick 101 without computing any cell. Tick 101 is an odd number of flips, so it is vertical. Skipping the cell arithmetic is the same kind of move as skipping physics for the design stance. It is the miniature of what Dennett means by a higher-level real pattern (p. 38–39).

## Concepts
- [[intentional-stance]] — predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires suited to its situation (pdf 14, p. 17)
- [[physical-design-stance]] — the other two strategies: predict from physical laws, or from what the thing was designed to do (pdf 14, pp. 16–17)
- [[folk-psychology]] — the everyday practice of attributing beliefs and desires and predicting by them; used by everyone "all the time" (pdf 16, p. 21)
- [[intentionality]] — Brentano's "aboutness" of mental states; the mark of the states the strategy attributes (pdf 13, p. 15)
- [[propositional-attitude]] — belief that *p* and desire that *p*, attributed through the strategy (pdf 13, p. 15; pdf 20, p. 29)
- [[rational-agent-assumption]] — start from perfect rationality and revise downward; attribute what the system *ought* to believe and want (pdf 16, pp. 20–21)
- [[machine-theory-of-mind]] — Dennett applies the stance to thermostats, chess computers and robots (pdf 17, p. 22; pdf 21, pp. 30–31)
- [[real-patterns]] (new) — objective regularities visible only at a certain level or from a certain stance (pdf 18–19, p. 25; pdf 25, p. 39)
- [[interpretationism-vs-realism]] (new) — the dispute over whether belief is an inner fact or relative to interpretation, and Dennett's "sort of realism" (pdf 13, pp. 14–15; pdf 24, p. 37)
- [[indeterminacy-of-interpretation]] (new) — rival interpretations may tie, leaving no further fact (pdf 26, p. 40)
- [[language-of-thought]] (new) — the hypothesis that internal states mirror the sentence structure of beliefs (pdf 23, p. 34)
- [[combinatorial-explosion]] (new) — the argument that brains must use generative representation to avoid exploding complexity (pdf 23, p. 35)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] intentional stance as inversion of a rational-planning model ↔ theory-of-mind: inverse planning and Bayesian ToM — both infer beliefs and desires from behaviour by assuming rational action.
- [structural-analogy] "attribute what the system ought to believe and want" ↔ theory-of-mind: rational-agent assumption / principle of efficiency — same constraint used to infer goals.
- [structural-analogy] true believers mainly believe truths, with false beliefs having a genealogy in true ones ↔ theory-of-mind: first-order false-belief tasks — false beliefs are tracked against a background of true ones.
- [metaphor] design-stance versus physical-stance prediction ↔ social science: choice of level of description (agent-based models versus micro-level mechanisms).
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's thermostat with "de-interpreted" predicates ↔ AI evaluation: construct validity of mental-state attributions to machines — whether passing a test shows the same capacity.
- [structural-analogy] interest-relative real patterns ↔ computational social science: emergent macro-level regularities that survive changes of micro-level implementation (spark plugs, "societal sockets").

## Questions this raises
- If success of prediction defines belief, how does one measure "success" for systems like chatbots, which are built to imitate our prediction targets?
- Where exactly is the line between a real pattern and an artefact of our "narcissistic concerns"? Could this be tested?
- If the language-of-thought hypothesis is only "the only game in town", what alternatives (distributed representations) could also produce the same intentional pattern?
- How often do rival intentional interpretations tie in practice? Does it matter for human groups with different cultures?
