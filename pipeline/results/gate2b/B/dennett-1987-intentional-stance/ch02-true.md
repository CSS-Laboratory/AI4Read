# True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

## L0 — One line
Dennett argues that belief is objective, yet is discerned only by someone who predicts a system by treating it as a rational agent, and that success of that strategy is what makes a system a believer.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Is believing a matter of objective fact, or only of an observer's interpretation? Dennett answers: both. Belief is objective, but it can be discerned only by adopting the intentional stance: predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires. A true believer is a system this strategy predicts reliably and voluminously. The strategy captures objective patterns, describable only from that stance. Dennett adds that, because the patterns are imperfect, rival interpretations could tie, leaving no further fact. For rich systems like us, this loose criterion still constrains internal organisation.

**Prerequisites:** no mathematics. You need only the everyday idea of belief and desire, the idea of predicting behaviour from physical laws, and the idea of a "level of description".

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Thesis.** Dennett rejects the assumption that two views are exclusive and exhaustive. *Realism* says belief is like an internal "virus". *Interpretationism* says belief is like being "immoral" or "stylish", a matter of interpretation. His view: belief is objective, but discerned only from the standpoint of a predictive strategy, and confirmed only by assessing that strategy's success (pp. 14–15).
- **Three strategies.** Physical stance: apply physics to a system's constitution; that it will always work in principle is, Dennett says, a dogma of the physical sciences. Design stance: assume the system behaves as designed. Intentional stance: treat it as a rational agent, attribute the beliefs and desires it ought to have, and predict it acts to further its goals. Sometimes it is more effective to switch to the design stance, and sometimes even the design stance is practically inaccessible; then one can adopt the intentional stance (pp. 16–17).
- **Attribution rules.** Attribute the truths relevant to the system's interests that its experience made available. More basically, attribute the beliefs and desires it *ought* to have, starting from ideal rationality and revising downward. One consequence is that true believers mainly believe truths (pp. 18–21).
- **Wide use, coarse predictions.** The strategy works on people almost all the time, on most other mammals most of the time, and also on other animals, some artifacts, plants and even lightning (pp. 21–22). It predicts coarse-grained outcomes, not exact moves, and this helps in chaining predictions (pp. 21–25).
- **Dennett's reply to Nozick's Martians.** Nozick imagines Martians who are Laplacean super-physicists. Dennett replies that they might predict us by physics alone, but if they did not also see us as intentional systems they would miss objective patterns that are describable only from that stance. Dennett adds that the unavoidability of the stance towards oneself and fellow intelligent beings is itself interest relative, that is, it depends on what the observer wants to find out (pp. 25–28).
- **Imperfection.** No one is perfectly rational, so there are degenerate cases where there is no fact of the matter about what is believed. When and why this happens is itself an objective matter. Rival interpretations may be about equally good, though this is practically negligible for human beings (pp. 28–29).
- **Thermostat to human.** Enriching a device's links to the world shrinks the class of satisfactory interpretations of its states, until eventually systems arise for which a unique interpretation is practically (never in principle) dictated. So the "shallow" criterion yields a robust notion of belief (pp. 29–33).
- **Why it works.** One answer is design (by engineers, by evolution), which is true but uninformative. The harder question concerns the internal machinery. Dennett thinks a language-of-thought hypothesis (that brain states are structured like sentences), if one allows great latitude for attenuating its bold claim, will prove correct. But he insists it is not obviously true (pp. 33–35).
- **Reflections.** Using Conway's Game of Life (a two-dimensional grid world with one simple law) as a model, Dennett argues that patterns can be objectively there to be detected, yet discerned only from a certain point of view (in Life nothing is hidden, but seeing the Turing-machine pattern takes a leap of insight). He ties his indeterminacy claim to Quine's, Davidson's and Parfit's views (pp. 37–41).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Epigraph: "Death Speaks" (p. 13)
Maugham's story: a merchant's servant sees Death in the Baghdad market and flees to Samarra on the merchant's horse. The merchant asks Death why she threatened the servant. Death answers that it was only a start of surprise, because she had an appointment with him that night in Samarra (p. 13). Dennett returns to the story's "fatalistic inevitability" on p. 27.

### Opening: two pictures of belief attribution (pp. 13–15)
- Talk about belief is ubiquitous in the social sciences, and there is much controversy (p. 13). Belief attribution seems risky for exotic, religious or superstitious beliefs. It also seems risky for non-human animals, infants, computers and robots, and for apparently healthy adults whose beliefs are contradictory or wildly false (pp. 13–14).
- Example: a man in a bar phones a biologist and asks "Are rabbits birds?". On hearing "No" he says "Damn!". Could he really have believed rabbits were birds? "Perhaps, but it would take a bit of a story" (p. 14).
- In such cases attribution looks subjective and relativist. In ordinary cases it looks easy and objective. For the easy cases it is tempting to think that one could confirm attributions by *finding something inside the believer's head*. On this picture, physiological psychology could in principle trump "black box" methods that use external criteria (p. 14).
- The two views (Dennett's own caricatures, which he says do not fairly represent any serious theorist; p. 15):
  - **Realism**: whether you believe p is like whether you are infected with a virus, an objective internal matter of fact (pp. 14–15).
  - **Interpretationism**: it is like whether a person is immoral, has style or talent, or would make a good wife. Answers begin "well, it all depends on what you're interested in" (p. 15).
- These are typically seen as mutually exclusive and exhaustive. **Dennett:** "I think this is a mistake." His thesis: belief is a perfectly objective phenomenon, but it can be discerned only from the point of view of someone adopting a certain predictive strategy. Its existence can be confirmed only by assessing that strategy's success (p. 15).
- Plan. (i) Describe the *intentional strategy*, "to a first approximation" treating the object as a rational agent with beliefs, desires and other mental states exhibiting *intentionality* (a term Dennett attributes to Brentano and others). (ii) Argue that any *system* whose behaviour is well predicted by it is "in the fullest sense" a believer. To be a true believer is to be an *intentional system*: one whose behaviour is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy. Dennett notes he has argued this before with few converts (p. 15).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett wants a middle position. Whether someone believes something is not a free choice of the observer (so it is objective), but you can see it only if you take up a certain way of predicting. The test of belief is how well that way of predicting works.

### The Intentional Strategy and How It Works (pp. 15–22)

**Strategies in general (pp. 15–17).**
- There are good and bad strategies. Example of a bad one: astrology, which feeds date and hour of birth into an algorithm. We have good reasons to believe it does not work (p. 16). Suppose it did work on some people. We could call them "astrological systems". We could learn *how* the strategy works from astrologers, but *why* it works is another question: "Having a good strategy is one thing; knowing why it works is another." As far as we know, the class of astrological systems is empty (p. 16).
- **Physical stance (strategy).** Determine the system's physical constitution (perhaps down to the microphysical level) and the physical nature of the impingements on it, and apply the laws of physics. Laplace's version is grand and impractical; there are modest local versions (the cook and the pot). That it will always work *in principle* is "a dogma of the physical sciences". Dennett ignores the minor complications of subatomic indeterminacies (p. 16).
- **Design stance.** Sometimes it is more effective to ignore the physical details and assume the object has a certain design, so that it behaves *as it is designed to behave*. Examples: computer users predict a computer without knowing the physics. Anyone can predict an alarm clock from its exterior (p. 17). More accurate predictions need a less abstract level of description of the design (p. 17).
  - Only *designed* behaviour is predictable this way. For an alarm clock full of liquid helium, one reverts to the physical stance. Many biological objects (plants, animals, kidneys, hearts, stamens, pistils) are also designed systems (p. 17).
- **Intentional stance.** "Sometimes even the design stance is practically inaccessible" (p. 17). Then (p. 17):
  1. decide to treat the object as a rational agent;
  2. figure out what beliefs that agent ought to have, given its place in the world and its purpose;
  3. figure out what desires it ought to have, on the same considerations;
  4. predict that it will act to further its goals in light of its beliefs.
  - A little practical reasoning from those beliefs and desires will in "many—but not all" instances yield a decision about what the agent ought to do. That is what you predict it *will* do (p. 17).

> **Intuition note.** Each stance is used when the one before it is impractical: the physical stance needs the physical details, the design stance relies on an assumed design, and the intentional stance works from what a rational agent in the system's position ought to believe and want (pp. 16–17).

**Attributing beliefs (pp. 17–19).**
- Truisms: sheltered people tend to be ignorant, and exposed people come to know all about what they are exposed to. *Exposure* to x (sensory confrontation over a suitable period) is the **normally sufficient** condition for knowing about x. It is only normally sufficient, but this is not a big escape hatch. Our threshold for abnormal ignorance is high: "I didn't know the gun was loaded", said by someone present, sighted and awake during loading, meets utter scepticism (p. 18).
- We do not learn *all* truths our sensory history offers, only the *relevant* ones. Example: I do not typically learn the ratio of spectacle-wearers to trousered people in a room (p. 18).
- **First rule:** attribute as beliefs all the truths relevant to the system's interests (or desires) that the system's experience to date has made available. It attributes somewhat too much (we are forgetful), and it fails to capture false beliefs (p. 18).
- False beliefs require "a special genealogy", consisting in the main of true beliefs (p. 18).
  - Case 1: S believes falsely that p because S believes truly that Jones told him p, that Jones is pretty clever, that Jones did not intend to deceive him, and so on.
  - Case 2: S believes falsely that there is a snake on the barstool because he truly believes that he seems to see one and is sitting in a bar not a yard away.
  - The falsehood "has to start somewhere" (hallucination, illusion, misperception, memory deterioration, fraud), but the false beliefs that grow do so "in a culture medium of true beliefs" (p. 18).
- Arcane and sophisticated beliefs, true or false, require tracing a lineage of mainly good reasoning from beliefs already attributed (p. 19).
- **Consequence:** true believers mainly believe truths. If anyone could devise an agreed method of individuating and counting beliefs (Dennett doubts it), all but the smallest portion (say, less than ten percent) of a person's beliefs would be attributable under the first rule (p. 19).
- **Footnote 1 (p. 19).** Some find "most beliefs must be true" obvious, citing Quine, Putnam, Shoemaker, Davidson and Dennett. Others find it incredible. Dennett suggests that probably each side is calling a different phenomenon "belief".
  - He distinguishes *belief* from *opinion* in his technical sense. Opinions are linguistically infected, relatively sophisticated states, "roughly" states of betting on the truth of a formulated sentence. Once the distinction is made, the claim that most beliefs are true is "near" trivial.
  - Example: Democritus, with (for argument's sake) entirely false physics. Even if every claim attributable to him is false, those claims are a small fraction of his beliefs, which include vast numbers of humdrum beliefs and millions of perceptual beliefs.
  - **Objection:** all his beliefs are theory-laden, and his theory is false. **Dennett replies:** the least theoretical compatriot also had theory-laden observation beliefs. Had Democritus forgotten his theory, his observational beliefs would be largely untouched. To the extent his theory played a discernible role, one may couch his humdrum beliefs in its terms, but this will not yield a "mainly false" catalogue. The effect of theory on observation is nevertheless often underrated (p. 19; Churchland 1979 cited).

**The fundamental rule and desires (pp. 19–21).**
- The first rule is "a derived rule, an elaboration and further specification of the fundamental rule: attribute those beliefs the system *ought to have*" (pp. 19–20).
- Desires are attributed in the same way: we attribute the desires the system *ought to have*. On a first pass these are the familiar basic desires: survival, absence of pain, food, comfort, procreation, entertainment. Citing one of these typically ends the "Why?" game (p. 20).
- Derived desire rules: attribute desires for what a system believes good for it, and for what it believes the best means to other ends it desires. Bizarre or detrimental desires, like false beliefs, require special stories (p. 20).
- Verbal behaviour complicates this. Language lets us express and form very specific desires ("a two-egg mushroom omelette…"). It also forces us to commit to desires more stringent in their satisfaction conditions than we would otherwise have. We often say more specific things than we antecedently meant, and then acquire an interest in satisfying exactly the declared desire (p. 20). Dennett illustrates with a customer asking for baked beans and being asked "How many?" (pp. 20–21).
- We are socialised to accept such demands without noticing (p. 21). There is a parallel for belief: our linguistic environment forces us to give precise verbal expression to convictions that lack "hard edges". If one ignores this distorting effect, one may be misled into thinking it is obvious that beliefs and desires are like sentences stored in the head. Believing that a formulated sentence is true is a special case and "may not be reliable models for the whole domain" (p. 21).

**Rationality (p. 21).**
- Start with the ideal of perfect rationality and revise downward as circumstances dictate. So one begins by assuming people believe all the implications of their beliefs and hold no contradictory pairs. There is no clutter problem, because one only needs the system to be rational enough to reach the implications relevant to its present predicament (p. 21).
- Irrationality and finitely powerful inference raise knotty interpretive problems. Dennett sets them aside here (see his chapter 4 and Cherniak 1986) (p. 21).

**Use of the strategy (pp. 21–22).**
- People use it "all the time" and it works with people almost all the time. It is the only strategy for attributing belief and desire that "we all know now" (p. 21).
- Illustration: why would it not be a good idea to let individual Oxford colleges grant degrees whenever they saw fit? The answer is a long story but easy to generate, with widespread agreement on the main points, because we can think of the reasons people would then have for acting in ways we would not want. Our use of the strategy is so habitual and effortless that its role is easily overlooked (pp. 21–22).
- It also works on most mammals most of the time (for example, in designing traps), and on birds, fish, reptiles, insects, spiders and clams. It works on some artifacts (the chess-playing computer, the thermostat) (p. 22).
- It works for plants: in a locale with late spring storms, plant apple varieties that are cautious about concluding that it is spring. An electrician told Dennett that lightning "always wants to find the best way to ground", but sometimes gets tricked into second-best paths. One protects a pump by making a better path more obvious (p. 22).

> **Intuition note.** The same four-step recipe is used on people, mammals, clams, chess programs and even storms. How well it works differs a lot between these. Dennett uses the range to ask what separates real believers from things we merely treat "as if".

### True Believers as Intentional Systems (pp. 22–33)

**The class of intentional systems (pp. 22–24).**
- The assortment is "motley": serious attributions, dubious ones, useful metaphors, perhaps frauds. Sorting out which systems *really* have beliefs would be "a Sisyphean labor" or would end by fiat (p. 22).
- Dennett's better start: even where we are surest the strategy works for the wrong reasons, it works at least a little. This distinguishes intentional systems from objects for which the strategy never works (pp. 22–23).
- Does the definition exclude anything? The lectern can be construed as a rational agent believing it is at the centre of the civilised world and wanting to stay there. It "stays put", so the prediction is right. Dennett: "If it is, anything at all is" (p. 23).
- What disqualifies it? The strategy "does not recommend itself": it gives no predictive power we did not antecedently have, since we tailored the beliefs and desires to fit. For people, animals or computers, the intentional strategy is often the only practical one (p. 23).
- **Objection (as Dennett voices it):** this is no difference in nature, only a reflection of our limited capacities.
  - A Laplacean omniscient physicist could predict a computer or human body without the design or intentional strategies.
  - Engineers grasp a thermostat without anthropomorphising.
  - Even the cleverest engineers may find it practically impossible to think clearly about a time-sharing computer system or a remote-controlled space probe without lapsing into the intentional stance, but that is "human epistemic frailty".
  - The objector then asks (rhetorical questions, p. 23): would we classify such artifacts with true believers on "such variable and parochial grounds"? Would it not be intolerable to hold that something is a believer from one observer's viewpoint but not from that of a cleverer observer? The implied answer is yes, intolerable (pp. 23–24).
- **Dennett's reply:** that would be "a particularly radical version of interpretationism". Some have thought he espoused it, and he admits his presentation has sometimes invited that reading, but he now wants to discourage it. "The decision to adopt the intentional stance is free, but the facts about the success or failure of the stance, were one to adopt it, are perfectly objective" (p. 24).

**Predictive power (pp. 24–25).**
- Once adopted, the strategy is an extraordinarily powerful tool, a fact concealed by our focus on dubious cases (p. 24).
- Chess: the game is interesting because the opponent's moves are unpredictable, except in forced cases. But in a typical position there are many legal moves and only a few, "perhaps half a dozen", with anything to be said for them. Even when the strategy cannot single out one move, it dramatically reduces the live options (p. 24).
- Real world: it cannot predict a stock trader's exact trades or a politician's exact words. It can give high confidence for less specific predictions (the trader will not buy utilities today; the politician will side with the unions against his party) (p. 24).
- This inability to predict fine-grained descriptions is "a source of strength": the neutrality about details of implementation permits *chaining predictions* in complex cases (p. 24).
- Example: the US Secretary of State announces he is a paid KGB agent. We can predict dozens of unremarkable but salient consequences: the President confers with the Cabinet, the Secretary is relieved pending investigations, a news conference, stories, editorials, letters to editors. Dennett says this describes an arc of causation in space-time "that could not be predicted under *any* description by any imaginable practical extension of physics or biology" (pp. 24–25).

**Nozick's Martians and Dennett's reply (pp. 25–28).**
- **Objection (Nozick, as reported by Dennett):** suppose superior Martians are Laplacean super-physicists. For them we are like thermostats to clever engineers. They see Wall Street as subatomic particles, and can predict days ahead the ink marks on the "Closing Dow Jones Industrial Average" tape. They never treat any of us as intentional systems. Would we be right to say that from *their* point of view we are not believers at all? If so, our status as believers is "in the eye of the beholder", provided the beholder shares our intellectual limitations (p. 25).
- **Dennett replies:** the Martians would be missing "something perfectly objective": the *patterns* in human behaviour describable from the intentional stance, and only from it, which support generalisations and predictions (p. 25).
  - Stockbroker: the Martians predict the exact finger motions and vocal-cord vibrations as he orders 500 GM shares. But indefinitely many different motions, even of different individuals, could be substituted without perturbing the market. If they miss this, they miss a real pattern (p. 26).
  - Analogy: there are indefinitely many ways of *being a spark plug*. Likewise there are indefinitely many ways of ordering 500 GM shares. There are "societal sockets" where any of them has about the same effect, and "pivot points" where what people do depends on whether they believe that p or desire A, and on nothing else in which they differ (p. 26).
- A predicting contest (Mrs. Gardner's kitchen): she answers the phone and says to the caller ("dear") that he or she is coming home early, within the hour, bringing the boss to dinner, and tells them to pick up wine and drive carefully. The Earthling predicts that a vehicle will stop in the drive within an hour and two humans will emerge, one holding a bag with a bottle of alcoholic fluid. The Martian can make the same prediction, but only with far more information about many interactions (for example, the car decelerating at intersection A to avoid a collision). To someone lacking the intentional strategy, the Earthling's success would "look like magic", as marvellous as the Samarra appointment (pp. 26–27).
- Fatalists (for example astrologers) believe a pattern in human affairs imposes itself "come what may". They are wrong but "almost right": there are patterns that impose themselves "not quite inexorably but with great vigor", absorbing perturbations that might as well be random. We characterise them in terms of beliefs, desires and intentions of rational agents (p. 27).
- Dennett admits a flaw in the thought experiment: the Martian treats the Earthling opponent as an intelligent being with beliefs and desires. A disguise story (the Earthling learns Martian) would fix that but "might obscure the moral": **the unavoidability of the intentional stance with regard to oneself and one's fellow intelligent beings.** This unavoidability is itself interest relative. One can adopt a physical stance towards an intelligent being, oneself included, but not to the exclusion of keeping an intentional stance towards oneself at a minimum, and towards one's fellows *if* one intends, for instance, to learn what they know (Hampshire is credited with this point) (p. 27).
- We can perhaps suppose the Martians fail to recognise us as intentional systems, but we cannot suppose them to lack the concepts. If they observe, theorise, predict and communicate, they view *themselves* as intentional systems. "Where there are intelligent beings, the patterns must be there to be described" (p. 28).
- Footnote 2 (p. 28): an Oxford audience member said that a Martian who included the Earthling in his physical-stance purview would not be surprised by the prediction. Dennett agrees, but says that when the Martian writes the prediction down word by word, as on a Ouija board, he would still be baffled. A mechanism dressed as a Martian yields a *true* Martian sentence about an event from which it was informationally isolated.
- Footnote 3 (p. 28): there might be "marvelous" invulnerable entities that lack observing, predicting and communicating, but Dennett cannot see what would lead us to call them *intelligent*.

> **Intuition note.** Dennett answers Nozick by saying that the Martians might be able to predict us by physics alone, but if they did not also see us as intentional systems they would be missing a pattern that is really there. In his example the pattern is shown by the many different physical ways of ordering shares that lead to the same social result.

**Incompleteness and indeterminacy (pp. 28–29).**
- The objective fact is that the strategy *works as well as it does*, "which is not perfectly". No one is perfectly rational, unforgetful, all-observant, or immune to fatigue, malfunction or design imperfection. So there are circumstances beyond its power to describe, as a melted amplifier cannot be drawn on a normal wiring diagram. In mild cognitive pathology (contradictory beliefs, self-deception) the canons of interpretation do not give clear, stable verdicts (p. 28).
- **Strong realism** would say the person really has particular beliefs and desires that the strategy cannot divine. **Dennett's milder realism:** in such degenerate cases there is no fact of the matter about exactly which beliefs and desires the person has. But this does not collapse into relativism or subjectivism, since when and why there is no fact of the matter is itself objective (pp. 28–29).
- One can acknowledge the *interest relativity* of attributions (different cultures may attribute different beliefs to the same person). Then there are still objective facts about how well each rival scheme predicts. Two rival schemes may be about equally good and better than any others, and that is something about which there can be a fact of the matter. The objective presence of one pattern does not rule out the objective presence of another (p. 29).
- Radically different interpretations of equal warrant are theoretically (or "metaphysically") important but practically negligible once one restricts attention to human beings (p. 29). Footnote 4: McCarthy's cryptography analogy: the larger the body of cipher text, the less chance of dual, systematically unrelated decipherings (p. 29).

**The "perverse claim" and the thermostat (pp. 29–33).**
- Dennett states his claim: *all there is* to being a true believer is being a system whose behaviour is reliably predictable via the intentional strategy. So all there is to really believing p is being an intentional system for which p occurs as a belief in the best (most predictive) interpretation. But for truly interesting and versatile systems this "apparently shallow and instrumentalistic criterion" puts a severe constraint on internal constitution, "and thus yields a robust version of belief after all" (p. 29).
- Thermostat. One may grant it about half a dozen beliefs and fewer desires (the room is too cold or too hot; the boiler is on or off; if it wants the room warmer it should turn on the boiler). This is too rich: it has no concept of heat or boilers. So *de-interpret*: it believes "the A is too F or G", and if it wants the A more F it should do K. Attached to other input and output devices it could regulate a water tank or a train's speed. Its link to the world is too impoverished to grant a rich semantics to its belief-like states (p. 30).
- Now enrich it: several ways of learning the temperature (an "eye" that distinguishes huddled shivering occupants, an "ear"), some geography (Winnipeg in December), and behavioural versatility (choosing fuel, buying from the cheapest reliable dealer, checking weather stripping). This adds internal complexity and gives belief-like states "more to do". The more is added, the less amenable the device is to controlling anything but a room-temperature system. In formal terms, the class of indistinguishably satisfactory models of its internal formal system shrinks (pp. 30–31).
- Eventually a unique semantic interpretation is "practically (but never in principle) dictated" (cf. Hayes 1979). Then we say the device (or animal or person) has beliefs *about heat* and *about this very room*, "not only because of the system's actual location in, and operations on, the world, but because we cannot imagine another niche in which it could be placed *where it would work*" (p. 31).
- The simple thermostat's state is "about that boiler" because it is *fastened* to it. Footnote 5 calls this the ancestor of the ideas lumped under *de re* belief (belief about a particular thing) (p. 31). With richer perception and behaviour, substitutions of links cannot be made without changing the system's organisation. There is a two-way constraint: fix the device in one state and it demands a specific environment; put it in a changed environment and its sensory attachments drive it into a new, appropriate state. This is what we allude to when we say the system *mirrors* the environment, or contains a *representation* of it (p. 31).
- Dennett (italicised, p. 32): it is not that we attribute beliefs and desires only to things in which we find internal representations. Rather, when we discover an object for which the intentional strategy works, we endeavour to interpret some of its internal states or processes as internal representations. What makes an internal feature a representation could only be its role in regulating the behaviour of an intentional system (p. 32).
- There is "no magic moment" in the transition from a simple thermostat to a system that really has an internal representation. Simple thermostats, fancier thermostats, robots and finally ourselves have progressively more demanding representations (p. 32).
- Twin Earth (Putnam's thought experiment, as Dennett uses it): a planet just like Earth but differing in some property beneath our discriminative threshold (what is called water has a different chemical analysis). If you were swapped with your Twin Earth replica you would never be the wiser, just like a thermostat that cannot tell whether it regulates temperature, speed or water volume. Radically different Twin Earths are easy to devise for a thermostat, but your internal organisation puts a much more stringent demand on substitution (p. 32).
- Which boiler are *your* beliefs about? The boiler in your cellar. Fixing referents requires facts about your actual embedding in the world, as in the thermostat's case. The principles and problems of attributing beliefs to people are the *same* as for thermostats. The differences are of degree, "but nevertheless of such great degree" that understanding a simple system's internal organisation gives very little basis for understanding a human's (pp. 32–33).

> **Intuition note.** A thermostat's states mean little because many different jobs could use the same wiring. The more varied the ways a system is tied to its world, the fewer other worlds could fit it, so the meaning of its states gets sharper. For Dennett this is a matter of degree with no sharp line.

### Why Does the Intentional Strategy Work? (pp. 33–35)
- The question is ambiguous, with two sorts of answer (p. 33).
  - Thermostat: it works because the thermostat is well designed. This is true but uninformative if we want the features of its design. For a thermostat these features are easy to discover, so the second answer (how the machinery works) is readily available (p. 33).
  - Person: evolution has designed human beings to be rational, to believe what they ought to and want what they ought to want. This guarantees that using the strategy on us is a "safe bet" and is true and brief, but "strikingly uninformative". The harder version asks how the machinery Nature has provided works. "We just do not know" (p. 33).
- **Skinnerian behaviourist (as Dennett reports):** the strategy works because belief and desire imputations are shorthand for complex histories of response and reinforcement ("wants ice cream" means that ingesting it was reinforced in the past, creating a propensity). Dennett replies that even if this were all true, it would tell us very little about how internal machinery regulates such propensities (pp. 33–34).
- **Currently more popular account (language of thought):** the account of how the strategy works and of how the mechanism works will roughly *coincide*. For each predictively attributable belief there is a functionally salient internal state, decomposable into parts as the believing sentence is decomposable into words. The *logical* form of what is believed is copied in the *structural* form of the states, and inferences are mirrored by physical causal processes. Brains would then be symbol-manipulating systems in rough analogy with computers (p. 34).
  - **Dennett's view:** "provided one allows great latitude for attenuation of the basic, bold claim, I think some version of it will prove correct" (p. 34).
- But he does not think it obvious. Those who do confuse two empirical claims: (1) intentional-stance description yields an objective, real pattern in the world (the pattern the Martians missed), confirmed "beyond scepticism"; (2) that pattern is *produced by* another real pattern roughly isomorphic to it within the brains of intelligent creatures. Doubting (2) is not doubting (1). There are reasons for (2) but "not overwhelming" ones (p. 34).
- His simplest account of the reasons: as we ascend from thermostat to robot to human, efforts to design systems with the requisite behaviour run into **combinatorial explosion**. Raising some parameter (inputs, degrees of freedom, vocabulary) by about ten percent tends to raise internal complexity by orders of magnitude, enough to swamp the largest machines. The brain solved this: a finite, compact, reliable, swift network that can learn almost without limit. So "some elegant, *generative*, indefinitely extendable principles of representation must be responsible". Our only model of such a system is human language, so the argument for a language of thought comes down to "what else could it be?" (p. 35).
- That is good enough reason to pursue the hypothesis as a matter of scientific tactics, but its "inevitable rightness is far from assured". He closes: "One does not well understand even a true empirical hypothesis so long as one is under the misapprehension that it is necessarily true" (p. 35). Footnote 6: every language-of-thought model so far proposed falls victim to combinatorial explosion in one way or another. This should temper enthusiasm for what Fodor calls "the only game in town" (p. 35).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett separates three things. The pattern in behaviour is certainly real. A matching pattern in the brain is a plausible but open hypothesis. How the brain does it is, he says, not yet known. Dennett says that people who find the language-of-thought theory obvious are confusing the first claim with the second.

### Reflections: Real Patterns, Deeper Facts, and Empty Questions (pp. 37–41)
(This section is printed after the chapter. The pages given end in mid-argument on p. 41.)

**The balancing act (p. 37).** Dennett says that perhaps the major source of disquiet about his view is its position between simple realism and simple relativism on the observer-relativity of belief attributions. His view is "a *sort* of realism": the patterns the Martians miss are really, objectively there to be noticed or overlooked. He asks how the Martians, who "know everything" about the physical events, could miss them, and what it means for a pattern to be objectively there yet visible only from one point of view (p. 37).

**The Game of Life (pp. 37–39).** (Conway's two-dimensional microworld, cited via Gardner 1970.)
- A grid of cells (the intersections of a graph paper grid). At each instant each cell is ON or OFF. Each cell has eight neighbours (four adjacent, four diagonal). Time advances in discrete ticks (pp. 37–38).
- The single law, as Dennett states it (p. 38): each cell counts how many of its eight neighbours are ON. If exactly two, it stays in its present state. If exactly three, it is ON next instant whatever its current state. Under all other conditions it is OFF.
- (added step) With $n$ = number of ON neighbours: $n=2$ → keep the current state; $n=3$ → ON; $n\in\{0,1,4,5,6,7,8\}$ → OFF. This follows directly from the stated law.
- The whole deterministic "physics" is that one unexceptioned law. One can predict "with perfect accuracy" the next instant of any configuration. So the Life world is a "fine Laplacean playpen", where finite creatures can adopt the physical stance and predict with supreme confidence (p. 38).
- Some configurations are more interesting: things that blink back and forth between two configurations, things that grow and disintegrate, "glider guns" that emit "gliders" (configurations that reproduce themselves a few cells over and glide across), puffer trains, strafing machines, eaters, antibodies, space rakes. Understanding their behaviour allows the *design stance*: designing larger assemblages. One triumph is a universal Turing machine, a configuration that can be interpreted as the state-switching, symbol-reading and symbol-writing of a simple computer, which can be "programmed" to compute any computable function (p. 38).
- **Turing machine stance:** ignoring both the Life physics and the design details, calculate the function being computed and translate the output back into ONs and OFFs. One can predict its future state "with precision, efficiency, and a modicum of risk", provided no stray gliders or noisy debris collide with it (p. 39).
- Is the pattern real? So long as it lasts it is, and with "armor" against noise it may survive quite some time. It *may* owe its existence to the intentions of its designer, but its reality "in any interesting sense" (longevity, robustness) is strictly independent of historical facts about its origin (p. 39).
- Whether one can *see* the pattern is another matter. In one sense it is visible to anyone watching the unfolding, since nothing is hidden and there is "no backstage". But it takes a big leap of insight to see this unfolding *as* the computation of a Turing machine. Ascending to that point of view is optional and might be difficult for many (p. 39).

**Application to the intentional stance (pp. 39–40).**
- Dennett claims the intentional stance provides a vantage point for discerning similarly useful patterns. They are objective ("there to be detected"), but not "out there" entirely independent of us, because they are patterns composed partly of our own "subjective" reactions to what is out there: "patterns made to order for our narcissistic concerns" (Akins 1986). It is easy for us to perceive patterns visible from the intentional stance "and only from that stance" (p. 39).
- Footnote 1 (p. 39): Anscombe spoke of an "order which is there whenever actions are done with intentions", without saying where in the world. Dennett now thinks he sees why: it is as hard to say where the intentional order is as to say where the patterns are in the Life world. If you "look at" the world in the right way the patterns are obvious. If you look at or describe it in any other way, they are in general invisible.
- Martians might find it extremely difficult, but they can aspire to know our regularities as we can aspire to know the world of the spider or fish (p. 40).

**Indeterminacy (pp. 40–41).**
- Dennett: "So I am a sort of realist." He declines Rorty's radical perspectivism. But when objective patterns fall short of perfection, "as they always must", there will be uninterpretable gaps. It is always possible in principle for rival intentional interpretations to tie for first place, so that no further fact could settle what the system really believed (p. 40).
- This is a direct extension of Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of radical translation, now applied also to patterns in dispositions to "behave" internally. Quine says "radical translation begins at home" (p. 40).
- **Quine (quoted, p. 40):** the black-box metaphor can mislead. The problem is not hidden facts that more brain physiology would uncover. To expect a distinctive mechanism behind every distinct mental state is one thing. To expect one for every purported distinction phrased in mentalistic language is another.
- Dennett's 1975 argument ("Brain Writing and Mind Reading") applies the same considerations to translating any "language of thought" one might find once behaviourism is abandoned for cognitive science (p. 40). Footnote 2 (p. 40): Wheeler (1986) finds support for this in Derrida; there is a covert non-Quinean belief that inner speech directly expresses thought, which Dennett says is attacked in his chapter 8.
- **Davidson (quoted, p. 41):** indeterminacy of meaning does not represent a failure to capture significant distinctions. It marks that certain apparent distinctions are not significant. If there is indeterminacy, it is because, when all the evidence is in, alternative ways of stating the facts remain open.
- **Parfit:** he argues that principles for settling personal identity will leave open puzzling indeterminate cases. Parfit calls the belief that every identity question has a simple yes-or-no answer "the view that our identity must be determinate" (p. 41). Dennett says we are equally attracted to the view that the contents of our beliefs must be determinate, and resist the suggestion that "Do I believe there is milk in the fridge?" could lack a yes-or-no answer (p. 41).
- Parfit's club example: a club stops meeting, and years later some members form a club with the same name and rules. If there is no rule or legal fact either way, and the people would not give an answer, "This is the same club" is *neither true nor false*. Parfit calls such a question **empty**: there may be nothing we do not know (p. 41).
- Dennett: in cases like the club there is no "deeper fact" to settle the matter. For personal identity the supposition that there is (or must be) one "dies hard" and is one of the brute convictions Nagel (1986) cannot abandon (the quote is cut off on p. 41).

> **Intuition note.** In Life, the physical level already gives exact predictions. The higher "computer" level gives shortcuts that carry a modicum of risk. Dennett's point is that the higher-level pattern is still real, though one may need the right viewpoint to see it. He claims the same for intentional patterns, with the extra feature that rival readings of an imperfect pattern may tie.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two thermostats are set to 19 °C and switch the boiler ON when they sense a temperature below 19, otherwise OFF. T1 uses a bending metal strip. T2 uses a digital sensor.

*Intentional strategy (steps 2–4, p. 17):* attribute the desire "room at least 19 °C" and the belief that follows from the sensor reading.
- Reading 17: 17 < 19, so it "believes the room is too cold", so predict ON.
- Reading 21: 21 < 19 is false, so it "believes the room is not too cold", so predict OFF.
- Both thermostats get the same predictions (ON at 17, OFF at 21). A physical-stance prediction would need different calculations: the strip's bending at each temperature for T1, the sensor circuit for T2.

*A false belief with a "genealogy" (p. 18):* if T2's sensor is stuck at 25 while the room is 17, then 25 < 19 is false, so it "believes" the room is warm and we predict OFF. The room stays cold, so the belief is false. The falsehood starts in a faulty percept. This is a very degenerate intentional system (p. 29–30). With only two or three states, many other jobs could be given the same wiring.

## Concepts
- [[intentional-stance]] — predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have (pp. 15, 17)
- [[physical-design-stance]] — physical: predict from constitution and laws of physics; design: predict from what the system is designed to do (pp. 16–17)
- [[intentionality]] — the term Brentano and others use for the feature of mental states that Dennett refers to here; he names it but does not analyse it (p. 15)
- [[intentional-system]] (new) — a system whose behaviour is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy; Dennett says to be one is to be a true believer (p. 15)
- [[rational-agent-assumption]] — start with perfect rationality (believes implications, no contradictory pairs) and revise downward as circumstances dictate (p. 21)
- [[interpretationism]] (new) — the view that belief ascription is like judging immorality or style: relative to interests; Dennett rejects it as the whole story (p. 15)
- [[realism-about-belief]] (new) — belief is an objective internal state, like a virus infection; "strong realism" says the person has particular beliefs even when the strategy cannot divine them (pp. 14–15, 28)
- [[real-pattern]] (new) — an objectively present regularity (e.g. in behaviour) that is visible only from a certain stance or level (pp. 25, 34, 39)
- [[interest-relativity]] (new) — which attributions fit can depend on the interests of the interpreter, and the stance is unavoidable only relative to interests (pp. 27, 29)
- [[language-of-thought]] (new) — hypothesis that brain states have structure copying the logical form of what is believed (p. 34)
- [[combinatorial-explosion]] (new) — internal complexity of a designed system rises by orders of magnitude when a parameter rises modestly (p. 35)
- [[indeterminacy-of-radical-translation]] (new) — Quine's thesis that rival translations may fit all evidence; Dennett extends it to internal states (p. 40)
- [[empty-question]] (new) — Parfit's term for a question with no answer when there is nothing we fail to know (p. 41)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's steps 2–4 (attribute beliefs and desires the agent ought to have, then predict action) ↔ theory-of-mind: Bayesian inverse planning, which starts from a forward model of rational action — both treat the agent as rational (p. 17).
- [structural-analogy] Life: a physical level, a design level and a computational level on one substrate ↔ computational social science: macro-regularities in agent-based models that can be described without tracking micro-level detail (pp. 38–39).
- [metaphor] Dennett's criterion that belief is whatever the best (most predictive) interpretation uses ↔ LLM evaluation: judging a mental capacity by behavioural test success and asking whether the test measures what it claims to (p. 29).

## Questions this raises
- If the best (most predictive) interpretation decides what is believed, how is "best" to be measured when two rival interpretations are about equally good (p. 29)?
- Is there any point on the thermostat-to-human ladder where "real" representation begins, or is it only a matter of degree (p. 32)?
- What kind of evidence could separate the claim that behaviour has an objective intentional pattern from the claim that the brain has an isomorphic internal pattern (p. 34)?
- Dennett says false beliefs need a "special genealogy" of mainly true beliefs (p. 18). How could this be tested on real agents?
