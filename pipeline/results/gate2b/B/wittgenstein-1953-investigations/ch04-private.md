# Private language and sensation (§§243–312)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein tests the idea of a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, his own immediate sensations; he offers as one possibility that sensation words are tied to natural expressions of sensation, and he argues that if sensation grammar is modelled on "object and name", the supposed private object "cancels out" (§293, p. 106e).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can there be a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations (§243)? Wittgenstein tests this with a diary sign "S" and finds no criterion of correctness: whatever seems right would be right (§258). He offers one possibility: sensation words are tied to natural expressions of sensation and replace them (§244). The beetle-in-the-box case shows that if we model the grammar of sensation words on "object and name", the object drops out as irrelevant (§293). Asked if he is a behaviourist, he rejects a picture of inner processes, not the processes (§§305–308). Philosophy aims to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle (§309).

**Prerequisites:** everyday ideas of meaning and justification. "Grammar" here means how words are used; a "language-game" is an activity of using words; an "ostensive definition" fixes a word's meaning by pointing; a "criterion of correctness" is a standard that could show a use to be right or wrong; a "behaviourist" is someone who says everything except behaviour is fiction (§307).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **The target.** A "private language" has words that refer to what only the speaker can know, so no one else can understand it (§243, p. 95e). Wittgenstein asks whether such a language is conceivable.
- **Sensation words replace natural expressions.** One possibility: a hurt child cries, and adults teach him new pain-behaviour in words. The verbal expression of pain replaces crying; it does not describe it (§244, p. 95e).
- **"Only I can know" is questioned.** Wittgenstein says it is "in one way false, and in another nonsense". With normal use of "know", other people very often know that I am in pain. "I know I'm in pain" can hardly be said of me (except perhaps as a joke): what could it mean, except perhaps that I am in pain? (§246, pp. 95e–96e). "Sensations are private" is compared to "One plays patience by oneself" (§248, p. 96e). Something that looks empirical may be really grammatical (§251, p. 96e).
- **The diary of "S" fails.** Private ostensive definition (pointing inwardly at a sensation) leaves no criterion of correctness. "One would like to say: whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'correct'" (§258, pp. 98e–99e). Looking up a table in the imagination is not looking up a table; justification appeals to an independent authority (§§265–268, pp. 100e–101e).
- **Idle-wheel image.** If "S" is made useful through a blood-pressure gauge, it then seems indifferent whether I identify the sensation correctly. A wheel that can be turned though nothing else moves with it is not part of the mechanism (§§270–271, p. 101e).
- **Beetle in the box.** If the word "beetle" has a use, it is not as the name of a thing; the thing in the box "cancels out". So if sensation grammar is modelled on "object and name", the object drops out as irrelevant (§293, pp. 106e–107e).
- **Who can have sensations.** Only a living human being, or what behaves like one, can be said to have sensations. Other uses (dolls) are called secondary, and the fairy-tale pot case is left open (§§281–284, pp. 103e–104e).
- **Doubt and justification.** An expression of doubt whether one's own sensation is pain "has no place in the language-game". If expressions of sensation are excluded, doubt looks legitimate (§288, p. 105e). Wittgenstein asks what "I am justified before myself" means and says that using a word without justification is not using it wrongfully (§289, p. 106e).
- **Inner processes.** Asked whether he is a behaviourist in disguise, Wittgenstein says: "If I speak of a fiction, then it is of a grammatical fiction." He does not deny the mental process; what he denies is that the picture of an inner process gives the correct idea of the use of the word (§§305–308, pp. 109e–110e).
- **Method.** What we are tempted to say is not philosophy but its raw material; "The philosopher treats a question; like an illness" (§§254–255, pp. 97e–98e).

### A. §§243–245 — The question, and one account of how sensation words are learned
- **Monologue speakers.** A human being can encourage himself, give himself orders, blame himself, ask and answer questions. So one could imagine people who speak only in monologue. An explorer might translate their language, since he also hears their resolutions and decisions (§243, p. 95e).
- **The new case.** Is it conceivable that there be a language in which a person writes down or voices his inner experiences (feelings, moods) for his own use? A question (voice unclear): can't we do so in ordinary language? Reply: that is not what is meant. The words are to refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations, so another person cannot understand the language (§243, p. 95e).
- **How do words refer to sensations?** This is the same question as how a human being learns the meaning of names of sensations. One possibility: words are connected with primitive, natural expressions of sensation and used in their place. A hurt child cries; adults teach him exclamations and later sentences, new pain-behaviour (§244, p. 95e).
- **Objection:** "So you are saying that the word 'pain' really means crying?" **Wittgenstein:** on the contrary, the verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it (§244, p. 95e).
- **Rhetorical question:** "How can I even attempt to interpose language between the expression of pain and the pain?" (§245, p. 95e). The text gives no answer here.

*Intuition note.* The first account does not start from a private inner object that a word then labels. It starts from what the child already does, crying, and says words are connected with it and used in its place. Wittgenstein presents this as one possibility, not as a full theory.

### B. §§246–255 — Is privacy a fact or grammar? "This pain", sameness, method
- **§246.** **Interlocutor:** only I can know whether I am really in pain; another person can only surmise. **Wittgenstein:** in one way this is false, in another nonsense. If "know" is used as normally, other people very often know that I am in pain. **Interlocutor:** but not with the certainty with which I know it. **Wittgenstein:** it cannot be said of me (except perhaps as a joke) that I know I'm in pain; what is it supposed to mean, except perhaps that I am in pain? Others cannot be said to learn of my sensations only from behaviour, because I cannot be said to learn of them; I have them. It makes sense to say others doubt whether I am in pain, not to say it of myself (pp. 95e–96e).
- **§247.** "Only you can know if you had that intention" may be said when explaining the word "intention"; then it means "that is how we use it". Here "know" means that the expression of uncertainty is senseless (p. 96e).
- **§248.** "Sensations are private" is comparable to "One plays patience by oneself" (p. 96e).
- **§§249–250.** Are we perhaps over-hasty in assuming that a baby's smile is not pretence, and on what experience is that assumption based? Lying is a language-game that has to be learned. "Why can't a dog simulate pain? Is it too honest?" One might teach it to howl as if in pain, but the right surroundings for real simulation would still be missing (p. 96e).
- **§251.** "I can't imagine the opposite" does not mean that my imagination is too weak. We use these words to fend off something that looks like an empirical proposition but is really a *grammatical* one. Wittgenstein also asks why one says "I can't imagine the opposite" and not "I can't imagine what you say" (p. 96e). Example: "Every rod has a length" means something like: we call something "the length of a rod" but nothing "the length of a sphere". To imagine it I just imagine a rod. The picture plays a different role here than with "This table has the same length as the one over there", where I understand what a picture of the opposite would be. The picture that goes with the grammatical proposition could only show what is called the length of a rod; "what should the opposite picture be?" (p. 97e). A parenthesis notes a remark about the negation of an a priori proposition (p. 97e).
- **§252.** To "This body has extension" we could reply "Nonsense!" but are inclined to say "Of course!" The text asks "Why?" and leaves it open (p. 97e).
- **§253.** "Another person can't have my pains": which pains are mine, and what is the criterion of identity? Compare physical objects, where we can say "not the chair you saw yesterday, but exactly the same as it". In so far as it makes sense to say my pain is the same as his, it is possible for us both to have the same pain. Siamese twins might feel pain in the same place. A man who strikes his breast and says "this pain!" has not defined a criterion of identity by emphasizing "this"; the emphasis only creates the illusion of a case where we know such a criterion and need reminding (p. 97e).
- **§254.** Replacing "the same" by "identical" is a typical philosophical expedient, as if the issue were shades of meaning. That is in question in philosophy only where we must give a psychologically accurate account of the temptation to use a mode of expression. What we are tempted to say is not philosophy but its raw material. Example: what a mathematician says about the objectivity and reality of mathematical facts is not philosophy of mathematics, but something for philosophical treatment (pp. 97e–98e).
- **§255.** "The philosopher treats a question; like an illness" (p. 98e).

*Intuition note.* The claim "my sensations are private" looks like a discovery about minds. Wittgenstein asks what it would mean: he calls it "in one way false, and in another nonsense" (§246, p. 95e–96e). He says that something which looks like an empirical proposition may really be a grammatical one (§251, p. 96e), and he compares "Sensations are private" to "One plays patience by oneself" (§248).

### C. §§256–264 — The diary of "S"
- **§256.** If my sensation words are tied to my natural expressions, my language is not "private"; someone else might understand it as well as I. So suppose I had no natural expression of sensation, only sensations, and simply associate names with sensations and use them in descriptions (p. 98e).
- **§257.** **Objection (voice unclear):** if people did not manifest pain, it would be impossible to teach a child "toothache". **Wittgenstein:** suppose the child is a genius and invents a name himself. Then he could not make himself understood. Does he understand the name without being able to explain it? What does it mean that he "named his pain", how did he manage it, and for what purpose? Saying "he gave a name to his sensation" forgets that much must be prepared in the language for naming to make sense. The grammar of "pain" is what has been prepared; it "indicates the post where the new word is stationed" (p. 98e).
- **§258 (the diary).** I want to record the recurrence of a sensation by writing the sign "S" in a calendar. A definition of the sign cannot be formulated, but I can give myself a kind of ostensive definition: I write or say "S" and concentrate my attention on the sensation, "pointing inwardly". Wittgenstein asks what this ceremony is for. **Interlocutor (voice unclear):** a definition fixes meaning, and concentrating attention does it, since I commit the connection to memory. **Wittgenstein:** "I commit it to memory" can only mean that this process makes me remember the connection correctly in the future, and here I have no criterion of correctness. "One would like to say: whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'correct'" (pp. 98e–99e).
- **§259.** Are the rules of the private language impressions of rules? "The balance on which impressions are weighed is not the impression of a balance" (p. 99e).
- **§260.** **Interlocutor:** "I believe that this is the sensation S again." **Wittgenstein:** perhaps you believe that you believe it. Did the diarist then make a note of nothing whatever? Do not take it for granted that a mark in a calendar is a note: a note has a function, and "S" so far has none. A parenthesis asks whether everyone who speaks when alone is talking to himself (p. 99e).
- **§261.** What reason do we have to call "S" the sign for a *sensation*? "Sensation" belongs to our common language, so its use needs a justification everybody understands. It would not help to say "he has *Something*", for "has" and "something" are also common language. In the end one would like to emit an inarticulate sound, but such a sound is an expression only in a particular language-game, which then has to be described (p. 99e).
- **§262.** Someone who has privately explained a word must inwardly resolve to use it in a certain way. How does he resolve that? Does he invent the technique of applying the word, or find it ready-made? (p. 99e).
- **§263.** **Interlocutor:** "Surely I can (inwardly) resolve to call this 'pain' in the future." A second voice (voice unclear) asks: is it certain you resolved this, and was concentrating attention enough? The text comments "An odd question" (p. 100e).
- **§264.** A quoted view (not commented on there): "Once you know what the word signifies, you understand it, you know its whole application" (p. 100e).

*Intuition note.* In the diary case I have no criterion of correctness: whatever will seem correct to me would be correct, and Wittgenstein says this means we cannot talk of "correct" here (§258). The sign "S" also has, so far, no function (§260).

### D. §§265–268 — Imagined justification
- **§265.** Take an imagined dictionary-like table. A real dictionary justifies translating X as Y. Is looking one up *in the imagination* also justification? **Interlocutor:** yes, a "subjective justification". **Wittgenstein:** justification consists in appealing to an independent authority. **Interlocutor:** but I can appeal from one memory to another, e.g. check a train time by recalling the timetable page. **Wittgenstein:** no; that procedure must actually call forth the *correct* memory. If the mental image of the timetable could not itself be tested for correctness, how could it confirm the first memory? It is like buying several copies of the morning paper to check that what it says is true. Looking up a table in the imagination is no more looking up a table than the image of an imagined experiment's result is the result of an experiment (p. 100e).
- **§266.** The look of a clock can serve to find the time in more than one way, for example to guess it, or by moving the hands until they "look right". A parenthesis mentions looking at a clock in one's imagination (p. 100e).
- **§267.** Imagining loading tests to justify the dimensions of an imagined bridge is imagining what is called justifying them. But would we call it justifying an *imagined choice* of dimensions? (p. 100e).
- **§268.** My right hand cannot give my left hand money. It can put money in the left hand and write a deed of gift while the left writes a receipt, but the practical consequences would not be those of a gift: one asks "Well, and now what?" The same could be asked of a private explanation, when one says the word and directs attention to a sensation (p. 101e).

*Intuition note.* In the timetable case, a check can confirm the first memory only if the check can itself be tested for correctness (§265). In the hand case, the practical consequences would not be those of a gift (§268).

### E. §§269–271 — Criteria, the manometer, the idle wheel
- **§269.** There are behavioural criteria for a man's not understanding a word (it means nothing to him), for his "thinking he understands" (attaching a wrong meaning), and for understanding it correctly. The second case might be called "subjective understanding". Sounds no one else understands but which I "appear to understand" might be called a "private language" (p. 101e).
- **§270.** Suppose I find from experience that whenever I have a certain sensation a manometer shows my blood pressure rising. Then I can report rising pressure without apparatus. Now it seems quite indifferent whether I recognized the sensation correctly: even a regular mistake would make no difference. This alone shows that the supposition of mistake was "sham": a knob that looks adjustable but is not connected to the mechanism. Why call "S" the name of a sensation? Perhaps because of the way the sign is used in this language-game. Why a "particular sensation", the same each time? Because, by supposition, we write "S" every time (p. 101e).
- **§271.** **A quoted case (voice unclear):** a person who cannot remember what "pain" meant and so calls different things by it, yet uses it according to the usual symptoms and presuppositions, "in short, he uses it as we all do". **Wittgenstein:** a wheel that can be turned though nothing else moves with it is not part of the mechanism (p. 101e).

*Intuition note.* If a supposed inner item can be turned freely while nothing else moves with it, Wittgenstein says it is not part of the mechanism (§271). In §270 the same holds for a mistake in identifying the sensation.

### F. §§272–280 — Private colour impressions
- **§272.** The essential thing about private experience is not that each person has his own specimen, but that nobody knows whether others have this or something else. It would be possible, though unverifiable, that one section of mankind had one visual impression of red and another section a different one (p. 102e).
- **§273.** Questions: does "red" signify something "confronting us all", so that everyone should have another word for his own impression? Or does "red" signify something known to all and also, for each person, something known only to him (or "refers to" it)? (p. 102e).
- **§274.** Saying "refers to" rather than "signifies" something private does not help us grasp the word's function. But it is the psychologically apter expression for an experience in doing philosophy: a sidelong glance at my colour impression, as if to say "I know all right what I mean" (p. 102e).
- **§275.** Look at the blue sky and exclaim "How blue the sky is!" spontaneously. It does not occur to you that the impression belongs only to you; you exclaim it to others without qualms; if you point, it is at the sky. You lack the "pointing-into-yourself feeling" that often accompanies naming sensations when thinking about a private language. "Consider what 'to point at something with one's attention' means" (p. 102e).
- **§276.** **Interlocutor:** "don't we at least mean something quite definite when we look at a colour and name our colour impression?" **Wittgenstein:** it is as if we detached the impression from the object like a membrane; this ought to arouse suspicion (p. 102e).
- **§277.** How can one even be tempted to think a word means at one time the colour known to all and at another the "visual impression" I am now getting? I do not turn the same kind of attention on the colour in the two cases. Meaning the impression that belongs to me alone, I immerse myself in the colour, as when I cannot get my fill of it. That is why this experience is easier to produce with a bright colour or a memorable colour scheme (pp. 102e–103e).
- **§278.** "I know how the colour green looks to me" surely makes sense? **Wittgenstein:** certainly; which use of the sentence do you have in mind? (p. 103e).
- **§279.** Imagine someone saying "But I know how tall I am!" while laying his hand on his head (p. 103e).
- **§280.** A man paints a picture to show how he imagines a stage set. **A first-person voice ("And now I say"):** the picture has a double function: it informs others, and for the painter it is also a picture of his image, which it cannot be for anyone else; his private impression of the picture tells him what he imagined. **Wittgenstein asks:** what right do I have to speak of a representation or information in this second case, if those words were correctly used in the first? (p. 103e).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein points to a difference in the kind of attention: when one "means" the private impression, one immerses oneself in the colour (§277). In spontaneous exclamation one simply points at the sky (§275). He calls "refers to" a psychologically apt expression for an experience in doing philosophy (§274).

### G. §§281–287 — Who or what can be said to have sensations
- **§281.** **Interlocutor:** "But doesn't what you say amount to this: that there is no pain, for example, without pain-behaviour?" **Wittgenstein:** it amounts to this: only of a living human being and what resembles (behaves like) a living human being can one say it has sensations, sees, is blind, hears, is deaf, is conscious or unconscious (p. 103e).
- **§282.** **Interlocutor:** in a fairy tale a pot can see and hear. **Wittgenstein:** it can also talk. **Interlocutor:** a fairy tale only invents what is not the case, it does not talk nonsense. **Wittgenstein:** it is not so simple. Is it untrue or nonsensical that a pot talks? Do we have a clear idea of the circumstances in which we would say so? Even a nonsense poem is not nonsense in the way a baby's babble is. We do say of dolls that they are in pain, but this use of "pain" is secondary. Imagine people who said it only of inanimate things and pitied only dolls. A parenthesis says that children of a tribe that does not know trains could play "trains" without knowing it imitates anything; the game would not make the same kind of sense to them (pp. 103e–104e).
- **§283.** What gives us the idea that beings or things can feel? Is it that education drew my attention to feelings in myself and I transfer the idea to objects? **Wittgenstein:** "I do not transfer my idea to stones, plants." Imagine having frightful pains and turning to stone. How do I know, with eyes shut, that I have not? If so, in what sense does the stone have pains, and why should the pain have a bearer at all? "Only of what behaves like a human being can one say that it has pains." One must say it of a body, or of a mind which some body has; and "how can a body have a mind?" (p. 104e).
- **§284.** Looking at a stone and imagining sensations, the idea seems as odd as ascribing them to a number. Looking at a wriggling fly, the difficulties vanish and pain gets a foothold. A corpse seems inaccessible to pain: our attitude and all our reactions to the living and the dead differ. To the objection that this cannot come simply from the movements of living things, Wittgenstein suggests this is a case of the transition "from quantity to quality" (p. 104e).
- **§285.** Think of recognizing facial expressions and describing them without giving measurements; and of imitating a face without seeing one's own in a mirror (p. 105e).
- **§286.** Is it absurd to say a body has pain? In what sense does my hand not feel pain, but I in my hand? How is the issue decided? If someone has a pain in his hand, the hand does not say so (unless it writes), and one does not comfort the hand but the sufferer: one looks into his eyes (p. 105e).
- **§287.** How am I filled with pity for this human being, and how does it show what the object of my pity is? "Pity, one may say, is one form of being convinced that someone else is in pain" (p. 105e).

*Intuition note.* In these remarks Wittgenstein points to creatures that behave in life-like ways and to our reactions to them (looking into the eyes, pity). Where the text says "only of what behaves like…", it is worded as a point about what one can *say*; it is not worded as a claim about what exists behind behaviour.

### H. §§288–292 — Doubt, justification, description
- **§288.** "I turn to stone, and my pain goes on." What if I were mistaken and it was no longer pain? **A voice (unclear):** surely I cannot be mistaken here; it means nothing to doubt whether I am in pain. **Wittgenstein:** if someone said "I don't know if what I have is a pain or something else", we would think he did not know what "pain" means, and explain it, e.g. by gestures or by pricking him and saying "See, that's pain!" He may understand this explanation rightly, wrongly or not at all, and will show which by his use of the word. If he said "I know what 'pain' means; what I don't know is whether *this* is pain", we would shake our heads. That expression of doubt has no place in the language-game. But if expressions of sensation (human behaviour) are excluded, it looks as if one might legitimately begin to doubt: with the normal language-game abolished, one needs a criterion of identity for the sensation, and then error is possible (p. 105e).
- **§289.** **Interlocutor:** "When I say 'I am in pain', I am at any rate justified before myself." **Wittgenstein** asks what this means, perhaps "if someone else could know what I'm calling 'pain', he would admit I was using the word correctly". Using a word without justification does not mean using it wrongfully (p. 106e).
- **§290.** It is not that I identify my sensation by criteria; I use the same expression. "It is not as if the language-game ends with this; it begins with it." **Objection:** does it not begin with the sensation I describe? **Wittgenstein:** "describe" may trick us: "I describe my state of mind" and "I describe my room" are different language-games (p. 106e).
- **§291.** What we call descriptions are instruments for particular uses, like an engineer's drawings. Thinking of a description as a word-picture of facts is misleading; we think only of pictures on walls, which merely show how a thing looks (such pictures are "idle") (p. 106e).
- **§292.** Do not always think you read off what you say from the facts and depict them in words by rules: you would still have to apply the rule in the particular case without guidance (p. 106e).

*Intuition note.* In §288 doubt about "is this pain?" has no place in the normal language-game. It looks as if doubt becomes legitimate once expressions of sensation are excluded; then a criterion of identity is needed and error is possible. In §§290–291 descriptions are instruments for particular uses, and describing a state of mind and describing a room are different language-games.

### I. §§293–295 — The beetle in the box; "from my own case"
- **§293.** If I say I know what "pain" means only from my own case, must I not say that of others too? How can I generalize "irresponsibly" from one case? Everyone tells me he knows what pain is only from his own case. **The beetle:** suppose everyone has a box containing something called a "beetle"; nobody can look into another's box; everyone says he knows what a beetle is only by looking at *his* beetle. Everyone might have something different, even something constantly changing. If the word "beetle" still had a use, it would not be as the name of a thing. The thing in the box does not belong to the language-game, not even as a "Something": the box might be empty. One can "divide through" by it; it cancels out, whatever it is. So if we construe the grammar of the expression of sensation on the model of "object and name", the object drops out as irrelevant (pp. 106e–107e).
- **§294.** If you say he sees a private picture which he describes, you have assumed something about what is before him, and you can describe it more closely. If you admit you have no idea what kind of thing it is, what seduces you to say he has something before him? It is like saying "He has something, but I don't know whether it is money, or debts, or an empty till" (p. 107e).
- **§295.** "I know … only from my own case": is this empirical? No. Grammatical? Wittgenstein imagines that everyone said it (not that people do): it might be a kind of exclamation. Even if it gives no information, it is a picture, like an allegorical painting. When we look into ourselves in philosophy, we often see such a picture: a "pictorial representation of our grammar. Not facts; but, as it were, illustrated turns of speech" (p. 107e).

*Intuition note.* Beetle: if the word "beetle" has a use at all, it is not as the name of a thing in the box, and whatever is in the box "divides out". The same holds for sensation words if their grammar is modelled on "object and name". Wittgenstein does not conclude that the sensation is a Nothing (§304).

### J. §§296–303 — The "Something", pictures of pain, belief and knowledge
- **§296.** **Interlocutor:** "There is a Something there which accompanies my cry of pain, and it is on account of this that I utter it; and this Something is important and frightful." **Wittgenstein asks:** "Only to whom are we telling this? And on what occasion?" (p. 107e).
- **§297.** If water boils in a pot, steam comes from the pot, and a picture of steam from a picture of the pot. What if one insisted that something must also be boiling in the picture of the pot? (p. 107e).
- **§298.** Our wish to say "This is the important thing" while pointing inwardly at the sensation shows how inclined we are to say something not informative (p. 107e).
- **§299.** Being unable to help saying something when doing philosophy, being irresistibly inclined to say it, does not mean being forced into an assumption, or having immediate insight into, or knowledge of, a state of affairs (p. 108e).
- **§300.** **Interlocutor:** not only the picture of behaviour belongs to the language-game with "he is in pain", but also the picture of the pain. **Wittgenstein:** this is a misunderstanding. Pain in the imagination is not a picture and cannot be replaced in the language-game by anything we would call a picture. Imagined pain enters the language-game in a sense, but not as a picture (p. 108e).
- **§301.** What is in the imagination is not a picture, but a picture can correspond to it (p. 108e).
- **§302.** Imagining another's pain on the model of my own is "none too easy": I must imagine pain I do not feel on the model of pain I do feel. It is not a transition from pain in one place to pain in another (hand to arm), nor imagining that I feel pain in some part of his body (which would also be possible). Pain-behaviour can indicate a painful place, but the sufferer is the person who manifests pain (p. 108e).
- **§303.** **Interlocutor:** "I can only believe that someone else is in pain, but I know it if I am." **Wittgenstein:** one can resolve to say "I believe he is in pain" instead of "He is in pain", but that is all. What looks like an explanation or a statement about a mental process only exchanges one way of talking for another that seems apter while we do philosophy. "Just try — in a real case — to doubt someone else's fear or pain!" (p. 108e).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein does not forbid saying "I believe he is in pain"; he denies that doing so gives a deeper account of what is going on.

### K. §§304–312 — Nothing and Something, inner processes, behaviourism, fly-bottle, "private exhibition"
- **§304.** **Interlocutor:** surely there is a difference between pain-behaviour with and without pain. **Wittgenstein:** "What greater difference could there be?" **Interlocutor:** yet you again and again reach the conclusion that the sensation itself is a Nothing. **Wittgenstein:** "Not at all. It's not a Something, but not a Nothing either!" The only conclusion was that a Nothing would render the same service as a Something about which nothing could be said. What is rejected is the grammar that tends to force itself on us here. The paradox disappears only if we make a radical break with the idea that language always functions in one way, always serves the same purpose: to convey thoughts, which may be about houses, pains, good and evil, or whatever (pp. 108e–109e).
- **§305.** **Interlocutor:** surely in remembering an inner process takes place. **Wittgenstein:** what gives the impression that we want to deny anything? When one says "an inner process does take place", one wants to add "After all, you see it", and means that inner process by "remembering". The impression of denial arises from our setting our face against the *picture* of an inner process. What we deny is that this picture gives the correct idea of the use of "remember"; the picture, with its ramifications, stands in the way of seeing the use as it is (p. 109e).
- **§306.** Why deny a mental process? "There has just taken place in me the mental process of remembering…" means nothing more than "I have just remembered…". To deny the process would be to deny remembering, that anyone ever remembers anything (p. 109e).
- **§307.** **Interlocutor:** "Aren't you nevertheless a behaviourist in disguise? Aren't you nevertheless basically saying that everything except human behaviour is a fiction?" **Wittgenstein:** "If I speak of a fiction, then it is of a grammatical fiction" (p. 109e).
- **§308.** How does the philosophical problem about mental processes, states and behaviourism arise? The first step escapes notice: we talk of processes and states and leave their nature undecided, thinking perhaps we shall know more about them later. That commits us to a particular conception of what it is to learn to know a process better ("the decisive movement in the conjuring trick"). The analogy then falls to pieces, and we have to deny the "yet uncomprehended process in the yet unexplored medium". Then it looks as if we denied mental processes; "naturally we don't want to deny them" (pp. 109e–110e).
- **§309.** "What is your aim in philosophy? — To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (p. 110e).
- **§310.** I tell someone I am in pain; his attitude is belief, disbelief, suspicion and so on. If he says "It's not so bad", does that prove he believes in something behind my utterance? **Wittgenstein:** "His attitude is proof of his attitude." Imagine the words "I'm in pain" and the reply "It's not so bad" replaced by instinctive noises and gestures (p. 110e).
- **§311.** "What greater difference could there be?" (the question recurs from §304). **A first-person voice (voice unclear; it seems to be the interlocutor's view, since the remark then calls the exhibition an illusion):** in the case of pain I believe I can *privately* exhibit the difference, whereas I can show anyone the difference between a broken and an unbroken tooth. For the private exhibition one need not have actual pain; imagining it is enough, e.g. screwing up the face a little. **Wittgenstein asks:** do you know that what you exhibit is pain and not, say, a facial expression? And how do you know what to exhibit before you do it? "This private exhibition is an illusion" (p. 110e).
- **§312 (begins).** **Interlocutor (voice unclear):** aren't the tooth and pain cases similar? The visual impression in one corresponds to the pain sensation in the other, and I can exhibit the visual impression to myself as little or as well as the sensation of pain. **Wittgenstein** then asks us to imagine that surfaces of things (stones, plants) have regions that cause pain in our skin when we touch them, perhaps through chemical composition, which we need not know. We would speak of pain-patches on a leaf just as we now speak of red patches. Suppose it is useful to notice these patches and their shapes, since we can infer important properties of objects from them (p. 110e). The supplied pages end here, mid-remark.

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein separates two things: the mental process (not denied; §§306, 308) and the picture of an inner process as giving the correct idea of the use of a word (denied; §305). The pages of this unit stop before the end of §312, so I cannot report how the pain-patch case is used to conclude.

## Toy example (our own, not from the text)
**Idea:** a private "inner something" can vary without changing how a word is used, so it does not fix the word's use (compare §270 and §293).

A six-day diary. I write "S" on days 1, 2, 4 and 6 (4 entries). A blood-pressure gauge shows a rise on exactly those days.

| Day | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "S" written | yes | yes | no | yes | no | yes |
| Gauge: rise | yes | yes | no | yes | no | yes |

Rule (ours): pressure is rising if and only if "S" is written. On the 4 S-days it predicts a rise: 4 correct. On the 2 other days it predicts no rise: 2 correct. Total 4 + 2 = 6 of 6.

Now take two hidden stories about the inner quality on the S-days:
- Story X: "tingle", "tingle", "tingle", "tingle".
- Story Y: "tingle", "warmth", "tingle", "itch".

Both stories give the same entries and the same prediction score, 6 of 6. The difference in use is 6 − 6 = 0. So whatever differs between X and Y "cancels out" of how "S" is used. Note: this only illustrates Wittgenstein's claim; it does not prove it.

## Concepts
- [[private language argument]] — the discussion of whether there can be a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations (§243, p. 95e; §258, p. 99e)
- [[language-game]] — an activity of using words in which an expression has or lacks a place; used here for doubt, lying, "S" and "pain" (§249, p. 96e; §288, p. 105e)
- [[meaning as use]] — "perhaps" a sign is a name of a sensation because of the kind of way it is employed in a language-game, not because of a private object (§270, p. 101e)
- [[rule-following problem]] — with no criterion of correctness, "seems right" collapses into "is right"; also, a rule must still be applied without guidance (§258, p. 99e; §292, p. 106e)
- [[grammatical-remark]] (new) — a statement that looks empirical but may really record how an expression is used (§251, p. 96e; §295, p. 107e)
- [[criterion-of-identity]] (new) — what counts as "the same" pain; emphasis on "this" does not supply one (§253, p. 97e)
- [[natural-expression-of-sensation]] (new) — primitive pain-behaviour (e.g. crying) that taught verbal expressions replace (§244, p. 95e)
- [[behavioural-criteria-of-understanding]] (new) — behaviour that shows non-understanding, mistaken understanding or correct understanding (§269, p. 101e)
- [[idle-wheel]] (new) — a part that turns while nothing else moves; used for an inner item that makes no difference to use (§§270–271, p. 101e)
- [[grammatical-fiction]] (new) — Wittgenstein's reply to the charge that he calls the inner a fiction: "If I speak of a fiction, then it is of a grammatical fiction" (§307, p. 109e)

## Canonical instances
- [[diary-of-S]] — private ostensive definition with no criterion of correctness (§258, pp. 98e–99e)
- [[beetle-in-the-box]] — the thing in the box cancels out of the use of the word (§293, p. 106e)
- [[manometer-S]] — a sensation sign made useful by an outside gauge; correct identification then seems indifferent (§270, p. 101e)
- [[imagined-timetable]] — checking one memory with an imagined image gives no independent test (§265, p. 100e)
- [[right-hand-gives-left-hand]] — a transaction whose consequences are not those of a gift, compared to private explanation (§268, p. 101e)
- [[inverted-red]] — unverifiable possibility that people's red impressions differ (§272, p. 102e)
- [[stone-and-fly]] — sensation ascription fits the wriggling fly, not the stone (§284, p. 104e)
- [[fly-bottle]] — the aim of philosophy (§309, p. 110e)
- [[pain-patches]] — imagined pain-causing patches on surfaces (§312, p. 110e)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] beetle-in-the-box ↔ statistics: a parameter that does not enter the likelihood or any observable prediction is not identified — an unobservable that "divides through".
- [structural-analogy] "no independent check" (§265) ↔ measurement validity: a gauge calibrated only against itself cannot show error; compare the "balance" remark in §259.
- [structural-analogy] idle wheel (§§270–271) ↔ model comparison: a component whose removal changes no prediction does no explanatory work.
- [metaphor] fly-bottle (§309) ↔ any field where a framing of the problem traps the inquirer — a picture of the way out, not a result.

## Questions this raises
- If sensation words are anchored in natural expression and shared reactions, what does Wittgenstein say about cases with no such expression? (He only supposes it, §§256–257.)
- What exactly is the difference between "a Nothing" and "a Something about which nothing could be said" (§304), and what use does that difference have in the argument?
- Does the idle-wheel image (§§270–271) show too much, e.g. that inner states never matter to use? What does §304 add?
- How does §308's remark on the "yet unexplored medium" bear on the thought that science might later tell us more about mental processes?
