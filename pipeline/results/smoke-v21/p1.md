# Private language and sensation (§§243–312)

## L0 — One line
A "language" whose words name only the speaker's own sensations has no criterion of correct use, and the "inner object" picture of sensation adds nothing to how sensation-words work in our shared language-games.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Wittgenstein asks whether there could be a language whose words name only what the speaker can know: his own sensations. The inner "naming ceremony" (the diary sign "S") fails because nothing separates remembering the sign's meaning correctly from merely seeming to. The remarks also deny that sensation words work like names of inner objects. In the beetle-in-the-box image, the thing in the box "cancels out" of the use of the word. Sensation words replace natural expressions of pain and are used in public language-games. He denies that this makes him a behaviourist, since he does not deny inner processes. The aim is to show "the fly the way out of the fly-bottle".

**Prerequisites:** Wittgenstein's notions of language-game and "grammar" as the rules governing how a word is used; ordinary talk of sensations (pain, colour impressions); the idea of a criterion (a public test for applying a word).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Setting the target.** The "private language" is one whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations, so no one else can understand it (§243, p. 95).
- **Sensation words grow out of natural expression.** "Pain" replaces crying; it does not describe it (§244, p. 95). This makes the language public from the start.
- **"Only I can know" is grammar, not discovery.** It is false or nonsense in ordinary use of "know" (§246, p. 95). "Sensations are private" is compared to "One plays patience by oneself" (§248, p. 96).
- **No criterion of correctness.** In the diary case the private "definition" cannot fix correct later use: whatever seems correct would be correct, so "correct" has no application (§258, p. 99).
- **Justification needs something independent.** Imagined look-ups are not look-ups (§265, p. 100). A sign with no function is not even a note (§260, p. 99).
- **The beetle in the box.** If sensation grammar is modelled on object and name, the object "drops out of consideration as irrelevant" (§293, pp. 106–107).
- **Sensation-ascription is tied to living, behaving beings.** One says "it has sensations" only of a living human being or what resembles one (§281, p. 97). Doubt about one's own pain has no place in the language-game (§288, p. 98).
- **No denial of inner processes.** The target is the picture of an "inner process" as the model for how words like "remember" are used (§§305–308, pp. 109–110). The philosopher "treats a question; like an illness" (§255, p. 98), and the aim is to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle (§309, p. 110).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §§243–245: The idea of a private language
- Speaking only in monologue (encouraging, ordering, blaming oneself) is imaginable. An explorer could translate it, because he hears the resolutions and decisions (§243, p. 95).
- The new case is a language for one's inner experiences "for his own use". Its words refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations, so another person cannot understand it (§243, p. 95). Wittgenstein sets this apart from describing sensations in ordinary language.
- How do words refer to sensations? That is the question how a person learns the meaning of such names (§244, p. 95). One possibility: words are connected with primitive, natural expressions of sensation and used in their place. A child hurts itself and cries. Adults teach it exclamations and then sentences, that is, new pain-behaviour (§244, p. 95).
- **Interlocutor:** "So 'pain' really means crying?" **Reply:** No. The verbal expression replaces crying and does not describe it (§244, p. 95).
- Rhetorical question: "How can I even attempt to interpose language between the expression of pain and the pain?" (§245, p. 95). The text gives no explicit answer; §244 suggests that the expression itself is what language replaces.

*Intuition note.* Sensation words start as a trained replacement for natural reactions. So the first teaching of "pain" already happens in public, between people.

### §§246–255: Privacy, knowing, and grammar
- **Interlocutor:** only I can know whether I am in pain; others only surmise. **Wittgenstein:** in one way this is false, in another nonsense (§246, p. 95). With "know" used normally, other people often know that I am in pain. It cannot be said of me (except perhaps as a joke) that I know I am in pain. Others do not learn of my sensations from behaviour only; I do not "learn" of them, I have them. It makes sense that others doubt whether I am in pain, but not that I do (§246, pp. 95–96).
- "Only you can know if you had that intention" can be said when explaining the word "intention": it states how we use the word. Here "know" means that expressing uncertainty is senseless (§247, p. 96).
- "Sensations are private" is comparable to "One plays patience by oneself" (§248, p. 96).
- Lying is a language-game that must be learned (§249, p. 96). A dog cannot simulate pain, because the right surroundings for real simulation are missing (§250, p. 96).
- "I can't imagine the opposite" does not report weak imagination. It fends off something that looks empirical but is grammatical (§251, p. 96). Example: "Every rod has a length" means roughly that we call something "the length of a rod" but nothing "the length of a sphere". The picture of a rod has a different role here than in "This table has the same length as the one over there". For the grammatical proposition there is no "opposite picture" (§251, p. 97).
- "Another person can't have my pains": what is the criterion of identity for pains? Compare "two exactly the same" chairs (§253, p. 97). If it makes sense that my pain is the same as his, then we can both have the same pain. Siamese twins are a conceivable case of the same place (§253, p. 97). Emphatically saying "this" does not define a criterion of identity. It only creates the illusion that we have one (§253, p. 97).
- What we are "tempted to say" is not philosophy but its raw material (§254, pp. 97–98). The philosopher treats a question like an illness (§255, p. 98).

*Intuition note.* "Only I can know" sounds like a deep fact about minds. Wittgenstein reads it as a statement of how the words "know" and "doubt" are used, and this changes what the statement can claim.

### §§256–271: The diary of "S" and the lack of justification
- If sensation words are tied to natural expressions, the language is not private (§256, p. 98). So suppose a person had no natural expression of sensation and simply associated names with sensations (§256, p. 98).
- A "genius child" who invents a name for his pain could not make himself understood. Much must be prepared in a language for naming to make sense. The grammar of "pain" is what is prepared; it "indicates the post where the new word is stationed" (§257, p. 98).
- **The diary case (§258, pp. 98–99).** I record the recurrence of a sensation by writing "S" in a calendar. A definition cannot be formulated, but I try an "inward pointing": I concentrate my attention on the sensation while writing the sign. This is meant to commit the connection to memory. But that can only mean the process makes me remember the connection correctly later, and here there is no criterion of correctness. "Whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct." That means we cannot talk about "correct" here.
- Rules of the private language would be mere impressions of rules. "The balance on which impressions are weighed is not the impression of a balance" (§259, p. 99).
- **Interlocutor:** "I believe this is S again." **Reply:** perhaps you believe that you believe it. A note has a function, and "S" so far has none (§260, p. 99).
- "Sensation" is a word of the common language. Saying "he has Something" does not help, since "has" and "something" are also common language (§261, p. 99). An inarticulate sound is an expression only in a particular language-game (§261, p. 99).
- Inward resolutions to use a word a certain way raise the question: did he invent the technique of applying it, or find it ready-made? (§262, p. 99). The question "are you sure it was enough?" is called odd (§263, p. 100).
- **Justification (§265, pp. 100–101).** An imagined dictionary-like table cannot justify a translation, since justification appeals to an independent authority. **Interlocutor:** I can check one memory against another, for example by recalling a timetable page. **Reply:** that works only if the recalled image can itself be tested for correctness. Otherwise it is like buying several copies of the same morning paper to confirm what it says. Looking up a table in the imagination is no more looking up a table than the image of an experiment's result is a result.
- Further parallels: the look of a clock may serve in several ways (§266, p. 101). Imagined load-tests justify an imagined bridge, but not an imagined choice of dimensions (§267, p. 101). My right hand cannot give my left hand money; a private explanation of a word faces the question "Well, and now what?" (§268, p. 102).
- There are public criteria for not understanding a word, for merely thinking one understands it, and for understanding it correctly. Sounds that no one else understands but that I "appear to understand" might be called a "private language" (§269, p. 102).
- **Manometer (§270, p. 102).** Suppose "S" is useful: whenever I have a certain sensation, a manometer shows my blood pressure rising. Then it becomes "quite indifferent" whether I recognised the sensation correctly. A regular mistake would make no difference. So the supposition of the mistake was "sham", like a knob not connected to the mechanism. Why call "S" the name of a sensation? Perhaps because of how the sign is used in this language-game (§270, p. 102).
- A wheel that turns while nothing else moves is not part of the mechanism (§271, p. 102).

*Intuition note.* A rule needs a check that can say "wrong". If the speaker's own sense of "right" is the only check, the rule does no work. The manometer case shows that when a sensation word does work, the work is done by public facts.

### §§272–280: "Red" and the private colour impression
- **Statement:** the essential point of private experience is not that each has their own specimen but that nobody knows whether others have the same thing or something else. It is then possible, though unverifiable, that part of mankind sees one red and part another (§272, p. 102).
- Does "red" signify something common, plus something known only to each person? Perhaps "refers to" something only he knows (§273, p. 102). Saying "refers to" does not help us grasp the function, but it is psychologically apt, as if one cast a "sidelong glance" at one's own impression (§274, p. 102).
- Spontaneously saying "How blue the sky is!" brings no idea that the impression belongs only to you. You point at the sky, not "into yourself" (§275, p. 102).
- Detaching the colour impression from the object "like a membrane" should arouse suspicion (§276, p. 102). The "private" case differs in the kind of attention: I immerse myself in the colour (§277, pp. 102–103).
- "I know how green looks to me": ask what use of the sentence is meant (§278, p. 103). Compare "I know how tall I am!" with a hand on one's head (§279, p. 103).
- A stage-set picture allegedly has a second function for its maker: it is the picture of his image. Wittgenstein asks what right one has to speak of a "representation" in this second case if the words were correctly used in the first (§280, pp. 103–104).

### §§281–288: Who has sensations?
- **Interlocutor:** "So there is no pain without pain-behaviour?" **Wittgenstein:** it amounts to this: only of a living human being and what resembles (behaves like) one can one say it has sensations, sees, is conscious (§281, p. 104).
- Fairy-tale pots can see and hear, but they also talk. Saying a pot talks is neither simply untrue nor simply nonsense, and we lack a clear idea of when we would say it. Applying "pain" to dolls is a secondary use (§282, pp. 104–105).
- A stone cannot properly be thought to have pains. Why should the pain have a bearer at all? Only what behaves like a human being has pains (§283, p. 105). A wriggling fly makes the difficulties vanish; a corpse seems inaccessible to pain; our reactions to living and dead differ (§284, p. 105).
- A body having pain: one comforts the sufferer and looks into his eyes, not the hand (§286, p. 106). Pity is one form of being convinced that someone else is in pain (§287, p. 106).
- **§288 (pp. 106–107).** Doubting whether I am in pain "means nothing". Someone who says "I don't know if this is pain" is taken not to know the word. We would explain it, for example by pricking him and saying "See, that's pain!" He might understand it rightly, wrongly or not at all, and his use would show which. The temptation to doubt arises when we remove the expressions of sensation from the language-game. Then we need a criterion of identity for the sensation, and the possibility of error returns (§288, p. 107).

### §§289–300: Description, the beetle, and the "Something"
- "I am justified before myself": this might mean that someone who could know what I call "pain" would admit my use is correct. But using a word without justification does not mean using it wrongfully (§289, p. 107).
- I do not identify my sensation by criteria; I use the same expression, and the language-game begins with that, not ends (§290, p. 107). "Describe" misleads; descriptions are instruments for particular uses, like an engineer's drawings (§§290–291, p. 107). One does not simply read off what one says from facts, because one must still apply the rule without guidance (§292, p. 107).
- **The beetle in the box (§293, pp. 106–107).** Everyone has a box with a "beetle" and says he knows what a beetle is only from his own. Each box might hold something different, changing, or nothing. If the word "beetle" has a use nevertheless, it is not as the name of a thing. One can "divide through" by the thing in the box. If the grammar of the expression of sensation is construed on the model of "object and name", the object drops out as irrelevant (§293, p. 107).
- **Wittgenstein's challenge:** if you say he sees a private picture that he describes, you must be able to describe it more closely. Otherwise, why say he has something at all? Compare "He has something. But I don't know whether it is money, or debts, or an empty till" (§294, p. 107).
- "I know it only from my own case" is not empirical. It may be grammatical, like an exclamation or an allegorical picture: "illustrated turns of speech", not facts (§295, pp. 107–108).
- **Interlocutor:** a "Something" accompanies my cry and is important and frightful. **Reply:** to whom are we telling this, and on what occasion? (§296, p. 108). The urge to say "This is the important thing" shows we are inclined to say something that is not informative (§298, p. 108). Being irresistibly inclined to say something is not being forced into an assumption or having insight (§299, p. 108).
- The picture of pain does not enter the language-game of "pain"; imagined pain enters in a sense, "only not as a picture" (§§300–301, p. 108).

*Intuition note.* The beetle image shows that a private object cannot be what a sensation word is about. A word can only have the use that other speakers can see it having.

### §§302–312: Belief, doubt, inner processes
- Imagining another's pain on the model of my own is not simply transferring pain from one place to another. The sufferer is the person who manifests pain (§302, pp. 108–109).
- "I can only believe he is in pain, but I know it if I am": one can resolve to say "I believe" instead of "He is". But this only exchanges one way of talking for another. Try, in a real case, to doubt someone's fear or pain (§303, p. 109).
- **Interlocutor:** there is a difference between pain-behaviour with and without pain, so you reduce the sensation to Nothing. **Wittgenstein:** "Not at all." It is not a Something, but not a Nothing either. A Nothing would serve as well as a Something about which nothing can be said. We rejected the grammar that forces itself on us. The paradox disappears only by breaking with the idea that language always serves one purpose, conveying thoughts (§304, pp. 109–110).
- **Interlocutor:** in remembering, an inner process takes place. **Wittgenstein:** we deny not the process but that the picture of an "inner process" gives the correct idea of the use of "remember" (§305, p. 110). "There has just taken place in me the mental process of remembering" means no more than "I have just remembered". Denying the process would mean denying that anyone remembers (§306, p. 110).
- **Interlocutor:** you are a behaviourist in disguise. **Reply:** any "fiction" is a grammatical fiction (§307, p. 110).
- The problem arises by an unnoticed first step. We speak of processes and states and leave their nature undecided, as if we might know more later. That already commits us to a conception of finding out about a process. When the analogy falls apart, we are led to deny the "yet uncomprehended process in the yet unexplored medium", and it looks as if we denied mental processes (§308, pp. 110–111). Aim of philosophy: "To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (§309, p. 111).
- If I say "I'm in pain" and someone replies "It's not so bad", this does not prove that he believes in something behind my utterance. His attitude is proof of his attitude. One can imagine instinctive noises and gestures in place of the words (§310, p. 111).
- The "private exhibition" of the difference between pain and no pain is an illusion. Imagining pain (for example, screwing up the face) gives no assurance that what I exhibit is pain and not a facial expression (§311, p. 111).
- §312 (the text given breaks off): the pain-patches thought experiment. Surfaces cause pain on touch, and we speak of pain-patches as we speak of red patches. The remark continues beyond the pages given (§312, p. 111).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Beetle-box game with three people.** Rules: when a bell rings, each person says "beetle" and puts one coin in the pot if his box is non-empty by his own judgement.
- Contents set 1: A has a stone, B has nothing, C has a leaf.
- Contents set 2: A has a coin, B has a bug, C has a feather.
- Round 1: bell, A says "beetle", B says "beetle", C says "beetle". Pot gets 3 coins under either set, since each person judges by his own box.
- Round 2: same.
- Public record after two rounds under either set: A, B, C each said "beetle" twice and put in 2 coins, so the pot holds 6 coins (3 + 3).
- The records for sets 1 and 2 are identical, so any rule of the game can be stated without reference to the box contents. The contents "cancel out" (§293). This illustrates the main idea: a word's use can be fully specified publicly, and what each person privately has adds nothing to it.

## Concepts
- [[private language argument]] — a language whose words name only the speaker's own sensations cannot be a language, since it lacks criteria of correct use (§§243, 258, p. 95, 99).
- [[language-game]] — a rule-governed activity in which a word has its point; sensation words belong to public games (§§244, 261, 270, p. 95, 99, 102).
- [[meaning as use]] — the sign "S" counts as a name of a sensation only through how it is employed (§270, p. 102).
- [[rule-following problem]] — if whatever seems right is right, there is no "correct" (§258, p. 99).
- [[logical form vs. grammatical form]] — claims that look empirical ("sensations are private") are read as grammatical (§§248, 251, 295, p. 96, 96, 108).
- grammatical proposition (new) — a statement that looks like an empirical one but states how we use words, e.g. "Every rod has a length" (§251, pp. 96–97).
- criterion of correctness / criterion of identity (new) — a public standard that makes it possible to be right or wrong in applying a word (§§253, 258, p. 97, 99).
- natural expression of sensation (new) — primitive pain-behaviour (crying) that words for sensation replace (§244, p. 95).
- inner process picture (new) — the model of states and processes as inner objects, rejected as a guide to the use of words like "remember" (§§305, 308, pp. 110–111).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] "No criterion of correctness" for private S ↔ analytic-philosophy: the rule-following problem — both ask what could make a use of a word right or wrong.
- [metaphor] Pain-behaviour replacing crying (§244) ↔ speech-act theory: verbal expressions treated as acts rather than descriptions (canon link; not stated in this unit).
- [structural-analogy] "Divide through by the thing in the box" ↔ underdetermination of a theory by evidence — in both, an item that makes no difference to the observable use is idle.

## Questions this raises
- If only the first-person "I know I am in pain" is senseless, how does Wittgenstein explain that "I am in pain" is informative to others (§§246, 310)?
- Is the no-criterion argument of §258 a claim about memory, meaning, or both?
- Why does the author think the behaviourist reading fails, given §281's link between sensation and behaviour (§§281, 307)?
- Do the animal and inanimate examples (§§250, 282–284) show that ascribing sensation depends on a shared form of life?
