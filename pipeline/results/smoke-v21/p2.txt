# Private language and sensation (§§243–312)

## L0 — One line
In Wittgenstein's diary case, a "language" whose words refer only to the speaker's own sensations has no criterion of correct use, and if sensation-words are modelled on "object and name", the inner object "drops out of consideration as irrelevant".

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Wittgenstein asks whether there could be a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know: his own sensations. In the diary case, the inner "naming ceremony" with the sign "S" fails: nothing separates remembering the sign's meaning correctly from merely seeming to. Treating sensation words as names of inner objects does not help: in the beetle-in-the-box image the thing in the box "cancels out". Asked whether he is a behaviourist in disguise, he answers that any "fiction" he speaks of is grammatical; he does not deny inner processes. The aim is to show "the fly the way out of the fly-bottle".

**Prerequisites:** Wittgenstein's notions of language-game and "grammar" as the rules governing how a word is used; ordinary talk of sensations (pain, colour impressions); the idea of a criterion (a standard that makes it possible to speak of using a word "correctly"); "behaviourist" as the interlocutor uses it in §307: someone who says everything except human behaviour is a fiction.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Setting the target.** The "private language" is one whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations, so no one else can understand it (§243, p. 95).
- **Sensation words and natural expression.** One possibility Wittgenstein describes: words are connected with natural expressions of sensation and used in their place, so "pain" replaces crying and does not describe it (§244, p. 95). If my words for sensations are tied to such expressions, my language is not "private" (§256, p. 98).
- **"Only I can know" is treated as grammatical.** In ordinary use of "know" the claim is false in one way and nonsense in another (§246, p. 95). "Sensations are private" is compared to "One plays patience by oneself" (§248, p. 96). "I can't imagine the opposite" fends off something that looks empirical but is really grammatical (§251, p. 96).
- **No criterion of correctness.** In the diary case the private "definition" cannot fix correct later use: whatever seems correct would be correct, so one cannot talk about "correct" (§258, p. 99).
- **Justification needs something independent.** Imagined look-ups are not look-ups (§265, p. 100). A sign with no function is not even a note (§260, p. 99).
- **The beetle in the box.** If the grammar of sensation-expressions is modelled on object and name, the object "drops out of consideration as irrelevant" (§293, pp. 106–107).
- **Sensation-ascription is tied to living, behaving beings.** One says "it has sensations" only of a living human being or what resembles one (§281, p. 103). Doubt about whether one's own state is pain has no place in the language-game (§288, p. 105).
- **No denial of inner processes.** The target is the picture of an "inner process" as the model for how words like "remember" are used (§§305–308, pp. 109–110). The philosopher "treats a question; like an illness" (§255, p. 98), and the aim is to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle (§309, p. 110).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §§243–245: The idea of a private language
- Speaking only in monologue (encouraging, ordering, blaming oneself) is imaginable. An explorer might succeed in translating such a language; this would let him predict their actions, since he also hears them making resolutions and decisions (§243, p. 95).
- Wittgenstein then asks whether it is conceivable that there be a language in which a person could write down or give voice to his inner experiences "for his own use". The words of this language are to refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations, so another person cannot understand it (§243, p. 95). He sets this apart from describing sensations in ordinary language ("that is not what I mean", §243, p. 95).
- How is the connection between a name and the thing named set up for sensations? That is the same question as how a person learns the meaning of names of sensations (§244, p. 95). One possibility: words are connected with primitive, natural expressions of sensation and used in their place. A child hurts itself and cries. Adults teach it exclamations and then sentences, that is, new pain-behaviour (§244, p. 95).
- **Interlocutor:** "So you are saying that the word 'pain' really means crying?" **Reply:** On the contrary. The verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it (§244, p. 95).
- §245 asks one question: "How can I even attempt to interpose language between the expression of pain and the pain?" (§245, p. 95). No answer is given in the remark itself.

*Intuition note.* On the possibility §244 describes, sensation words start as a trained replacement for natural reactions. On that picture the first teaching of "pain" would happen between people, not inside one person.

### §§246–255: Privacy, knowing, and grammar
- **Interlocutor:** only I can know whether I am really in pain; another person can only surmise it. **Wittgenstein:** in one way this is false, and in another nonsense (§246, p. 95). If "know" is used as normally, other people very often know that I am in pain. **Interlocutor:** but not with the certainty with which I know it myself. **Wittgenstein:** it cannot be said of me at all (except perhaps as a joke) that I know I am in pain. Other people cannot be said to learn of my sensations only from my behaviour, for I cannot be said to learn of them: I have them. It makes sense to say that other people doubt whether I am in pain, but not to say it about myself (§246, pp. 95–96).
- "Only you can know if you had that intention" might be said to someone when explaining the word "intention": then it means "that is how we use it". Here "know" means that the expression of uncertainty is senseless (§247, p. 96).
- "Sensations are private" is comparable to "One plays patience by oneself" (§248, p. 96).
- Wittgenstein asks whether we are over-hasty in assuming that a baby's smile is not pretence. Lying is a language-game that must be learned like any other (§249, p. 96). Why can't a dog simulate pain? Even if it could be taught to howl as if in pain, the right surroundings for real simulation would still be missing (§250, p. 96).
- "I can't imagine the opposite" does not mean that one's powers of imagination are unequal to the task. We use these words to fend off something whose form produces the illusion of an empirical proposition but which is really grammatical (§251, p. 96). Example: "Every rod has a length" means something like: we call something "the length of a rod" but nothing "the length of a sphere". The picture of a rod has a different role here than in "This table has the same length as the one over there", where one understands what a picture of the opposite would be. For the grammatical proposition Wittgenstein asks: "And what should the opposite picture be?" (§251, p. 97). On "This body has extension": we could answer "Nonsense!" yet feel inclined to say "Of course!", and he asks why (§252, p. 97).
- "Another person can't have my pains": what is the criterion of identity for pains? Compare "two exactly the same" chairs (§253, p. 97). In so far as it makes sense to say that my pain is the same as his, it is also possible for us both to have the same pain; Siamese twins are a conceivable case of pain in the same place (§253, p. 97). Striking one's breast and saying "this pain" does not define a criterion of identity; the emphasis merely creates the illusion of a case in which we already have such a criterion and only need reminding of it (§253, p. 97).
- Substituting "identical" for "the same" is called a typical expedient in philosophy (§254, p. 97). What we are "tempted to say" is not philosophy but its raw material (§254, pp. 97–98). The philosopher treats a question like an illness (§255, p. 98).

*Intuition note.* "Only I can know" sounds like a deep fact about minds. In §246 Wittgenstein calls it false in one way and nonsense in another. In §247 he notes that, said while explaining "intention", it can mean "that is how we use it".

### §§256–271: The diary of "S" and the lack of justification
- If my words for sensations are tied up with my natural expressions of sensation, my language is not "private"; someone else might understand it as well as I. So suppose I had no natural expression of sensation, but only sensations, and simply associated names with them (§256, p. 98).
- **Interlocutor:** without manifestations of pain it would be impossible to teach a child "toothache". **Wittgenstein:** suppose the child is a genius and invents a name by himself. Then he could not make himself understood when he used the word. Much must be prepared in the language for mere naming to make sense. The grammar of "pain" is what is prepared; it "indicates the post where the new word is stationed" (§257, p. 98).
- **The diary case (§258, pp. 98–99).** I keep a diary of the recurrence of a sensation by writing the sign "S" in a calendar. A definition of the sign cannot be formulated. An objector says I can give myself a kind of ostensive definition; I speak or write the sign while concentrating my attention on the sensation, and so, as it were, point to it inwardly. This is meant to commit the connection between sign and sensation to memory. But that can only mean that the process makes me remember the connection correctly in the future, and in this case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: "Whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct." That only means that here we cannot talk about "correct".
- Wittgenstein asks whether the rules of the private language are impressions of rules, and answers with an image: "The balance on which impressions are weighed is not the impression of a balance" (§259, p. 99).
- **Interlocutor:** "Well, I believe that this is the sensation S again." **Reply:** perhaps you believe that you believe it. A note has a function, and this "S" so far has none (§260, p. 99).
- "Sensation" is a word of our common language, so its use needs a justification everybody understands. Saying "he has Something" does not help, since "has" and "something" also belong to the common language (§261, p. 99). An inarticulate sound is an expression only in a particular language-game (§261, p. 99).
- Someone who has given himself a private explanation of a word must inwardly resolve to use it in a certain way. Wittgenstein asks whether he invents the technique of applying the word or finds it ready-made (§262, p. 99). To the claim that one can inwardly resolve to call this "pain", the reply asks whether one is sure it was enough to concentrate attention on the feeling; this is called "an odd question" (§263, p. 100).
- **Justification (§265, p. 100).** Wittgenstein considers a dictionary-like table that exists only in the imagination. **Interlocutor:** then it is a subjective justification. **Wittgenstein:** justification consists in appealing to an independent authority. **Interlocutor:** I can check one memory against another, for example by recalling a timetable page. **Reply:** this works only if the procedure actually calls forth the correct memory; if the mental image of the timetable could not itself be tested for correctness, how could it confirm the first memory? It is like buying several copies of today's morning paper to confirm that what it said is true. Looking up a table in the imagination is no more looking up a table than the image of the result of an imagined experiment is the result of an experiment.
- The look of a clock may serve to determine the time in more than one way (§266, p. 100). Imagining load-tests on a bridge's material is imagining what is called justifying the choice of dimensions; Wittgenstein asks whether we would also call it justifying an imagined choice of dimensions (§267, p. 100). Why can't my right hand give my left hand money? Afterwards one asks, "Well, and now what?", and the same could be asked of a private explanation of a word given by directing attention to a sensation (§268, p. 101).
- There are criteria in a man's behaviour for his not understanding a word, for his "thinking he understands" it, and for his understanding it correctly. Sounds that no one else understands but that I "appear to understand" might be called a "private language" (§269, p. 101).
- **Manometer (§270, p. 101).** Suppose "S" has a use: whenever I have a certain sensation, a manometer shows that my blood pressure is rising. Then it seems quite indifferent whether I have recognised the sensation correctly. A regular mistake would make no difference, and this alone shows that the supposition of the mistake was "merely sham", like a knob not connected to the mechanism. Why call "S" the name of a sensation? Perhaps the kind of way the sign is employed in this language-game (§270, p. 101).
- Imagine a person who cannot remember what "pain" meant but uses it as we all do. A wheel that can be turned though nothing else moves with it is not part of the mechanism (§271, p. 101).

*Intuition note.* In the diary case the only check on "right" is the speaker's impression that it is right, and Wittgenstein concludes that one cannot talk about "correct" there (§258). In the manometer case, the use of "S" is tied to a publicly checkable reading, and whether the sensation was recognised correctly seems idle.

### §§272–280: "Red" and the private colour impression
- The essential thing about private experience is not that each person has his own specimen, but that nobody knows whether other people also have this or something else. It would thus be possible, though unverifiable, that one section of mankind had one visual impression of red and another section another (§272, p. 102).
- Does "red" signify something that confronts us all, so that everyone should have another word for his own impression? Or does "red" signify something known to all and, in addition, for each person, something known only to him? Or perhaps it refers to something known only to him (§273, p. 102). Saying "refers to" rather than "signifies" does not help us grasp the function, but it is the more psychologically apt expression for a particular experience in doing philosophy, as if one cast a "sidelong glance" at one's own colour impression (§274, p. 102).
- Saying spontaneously "How blue the sky is!" brings no idea that the impression belongs only to you. You point at the sky, not "into yourself" (§275, p. 102).
- The objection "don't we at least mean something quite definite?" leads to the picture of detaching the colour impression from the object "like a membrane". This ought to arouse suspicion (§276, p. 102). Wittgenstein says the "private" case differs in the kind of attention: I immerse myself in the colour (§277, pp. 102–103).
- **Interlocutor:** "I know how the colour green looks to me" surely makes sense. **Wittgenstein:** certainly; what use of the sentence are you thinking of? (§278, p. 103). Compare "But I know how tall I am!", said with a hand on top of one's head (§279, p. 103).
- A stage-set picture is said to have a double function: it informs others, and for its maker it is also the picture of his image. Wittgenstein asks what right one has to speak of a representation in this second case if these words were correctly used in the first (§280, p. 103).

### §§281–288: Who has sensations?
- **Interlocutor:** "But doesn't what you say amount to this: that there is no pain, for example, without pain-behaviour?" **Wittgenstein:** it amounts to this: only of a living human being and what resembles (behaves like) one can one say that it has sensations, sees, is blind, hears, is deaf, is conscious or unconscious (§281, p. 103).
- **Interlocutor:** in a fairy tale a pot too can see and hear. **Wittgenstein:** certainly, but it can also talk. He asks whether it is untrue or nonsensical to say that a pot talks, and whether we have a clear idea of the circumstances in which we would say so. We do say of an inanimate thing that it is in pain, for example when playing with dolls, but this use of the concept of pain is a secondary one (§282, pp. 103–104).
- What gives us so much as the idea that things can feel? Wittgenstein asks in what sense a stone that had pains would have them, and why the pain should have a bearer at all. "Only of what behaves like a human being can one say that it has pains" (§283, p. 104). A wriggling fly makes the difficulties vanish, and a corpse seems inaccessible to pain; our attitude to what is alive and to what is dead is not the same (§284, p. 104).
- Think of the recognition and description of facial expressions, which does not consist in giving measurements of the face (§285, p. 105). To the question whether it is absurd to say that a body has pain: if someone has a pain in his hand, one does not comfort the hand but the sufferer, looking into his eyes (§286, p. 105). Pity, one may say, is one form of being convinced that someone else is in pain (§287, p. 105).
- **§288 (p. 105).** **Interlocutor:** I surely can't be mistaken here; it means nothing to doubt whether I am in pain. **Wittgenstein:** if someone said "I don't know if what I have is a pain or something else", we would think, perhaps, that he does not know what the word "pain" means, and we would explain it, perhaps by gestures or by pricking him and saying "See, that's pain!" He might understand this rightly, wrongly or not at all, and his use of the word would show which. If he said he knows what "pain" means but not whether this is pain, we would merely shake our heads. The expression of doubt has no place in the language-game. But if expressions of sensation are excluded, it looks as if I might legitimately begin to doubt. The temptation arises because, if the normal language-game is set aside, I need a criterion of identity for the sensation, and then the possibility of error also exists.

### §§289–301: Description, the beetle, and the "Something"
- **Interlocutor:** "When I say 'I am in pain', I am at any rate justified before myself." Wittgenstein asks whether this means: "If someone else could know what I am calling 'pain', he would admit that I was using the word correctly." To use a word without a justification does not mean to use it wrongfully (§289, p. 106).
- I do not identify my sensation by criteria; I use the same expression. The language-game does not end with this; it begins with it. Perhaps the word "describe" tricks us here (§290, p. 106). Descriptions are instruments for particular uses, like an engineer's drawings; thinking of a description as a word-picture of the facts is misleading (§291, p. 106). Don't always think that you read off what you say from the facts, for you would still have to apply the rule in the particular case without guidance (§292, p. 106).
- **The beetle in the box (§293, pp. 106–107).** If I say that I know what "pain" means only from my own case, must I not say that of other people too? Suppose everyone had a box with something in it that we call a "beetle". No one can look into anyone else's box, and everyone says he knows what a beetle is only by looking at his own. Each box might hold something different, something constantly changing, or nothing. If the word "beetle" has a use nonetheless, it is not as the name of a thing. The thing in the box does not belong to the language-game at all; one can "divide through" by it. If we construe the grammar of the expression of sensation on the model of "object and name", the object drops out of consideration as irrelevant (§293, pp. 106–107).
- If you say that he sees a private picture before him, which he is describing, you have made an assumption about what he has before him, and so you can describe it or do describe it more closely. If you admit you have no idea what kind of thing it is, Wittgenstein asks what seduces you into saying that he has something before him. Compare "He has something. But I don't know whether it is money, or debts, or an empty till" (§294, p. 107).
- "I know… only from my own case": Wittgenstein asks what kind of proposition this is. Not an empirical one; perhaps a grammatical one. If everybody said it, it might be a kind of exclamation. Even if it gives no information, it is a picture, "illustrated turns of speech" rather than facts, a pictorial representation of our grammar (§295, p. 107).
- **Interlocutor:** a Something accompanies my cry of pain, and it is important and frightful. **Wittgenstein:** to whom are we telling this, and on what occasion? (§296, p. 107). If water boils in a pot, a picture of steam comes out of a picture of the pot; Wittgenstein asks about insisting that something must also be boiling in the picture of the pot (§297, p. 107). The urge to say "This is the important thing" while pointing to the sensation shows how much we are inclined to say something that is not informative (§298, p. 107). Being irresistibly inclined to say something, in philosophical thought, does not mean being forced into an assumption or having insight into a state of affairs (§299, p. 108).
- It is a misunderstanding to say that the picture of pain enters into the language-game with the word "pain". Pain in the imagination is not a picture; imagined pain enters the language-game in a sense, "only not as a picture" (§300, p. 108). What is in the imagination is not a picture, but a picture can correspond to it (§301, p. 108).

*Intuition note.* In the beetle image, if the word "beetle" has a use at all, it is not as the name of a thing in the box, and on the object-and-name model the object drops out (§293). Wittgenstein does not say here that a word has only the use others can see.

### §§302–312: Belief, doubt, inner processes
- Imagining another's pain on the model of my own is not easy and is not simply a transfer of pain from one place to another. The person who is suffering is the person who manifests pain (§302, p. 108).
- **Interlocutor:** "I can only believe that someone else is in pain, but I know it if I am." **Wittgenstein:** one can resolve to say "I believe he is in pain" instead of "He is in pain", but that is all; this only exchanges one way of talking for another that seems more apt while doing philosophy. Try, in a real case, to doubt someone else's fear or pain (§303, p. 108).
- **Interlocutor:** surely you admit there is a difference between pain-behaviour with pain and without it. **Wittgenstein:** "Admit it? What greater difference could there be?" **Interlocutor:** yet you reach the conclusion that the sensation itself is a Nothing. **Wittgenstein:** not at all. It is not a Something, but not a Nothing either. The conclusion was only that a Nothing would render the same service as a Something about which nothing could be said; what we reject is the grammar that tends to force itself on us. The paradox disappears only if we make a radical break with the idea that language always functions in one way, always serves the same purpose: to convey thoughts (§304, pp. 108–109).
- **Interlocutor:** in remembering, an inner process takes place. **Wittgenstein:** what we deny is that the picture of an inner process gives the correct idea of the use of the word "remember"; this picture stands in the way of seeing the use as it is (§305, p. 109). "There has just taken place in me the mental process of remembering…" means nothing more than "I have just remembered…". Denying the mental process would mean denying that anyone ever remembers anything (§306, p. 109).
- **Interlocutor:** aren't you a behaviourist in disguise, saying that everything except human behaviour is a fiction? **Wittgenstein:** if I speak of a fiction, it is of a grammatical fiction (§307, p. 109).
- The problem arises through a first step that escapes notice: we talk of processes and states and leave their nature undecided, thinking we may know more later. That commits us to a certain conception of what it means to learn to know a process better. When the analogy falls to pieces, we are led to deny the "yet uncomprehended process in the yet unexplored medium", and it looks as if we had denied mental processes. Naturally we do not want to deny them (§308, pp. 109–110). Aim of philosophy: "To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (§309, p. 110).
- If I tell someone I am in pain, his attitude will be that of belief, disbelief, suspicion, and so on. If he says "It's not so bad", Wittgenstein asks, "Doesn't that prove that he believes in something behind my utterance of pain?" His answer: "His attitude is proof of his attitude". One can imagine the words replaced by instinctive noises and gestures (§310, p. 110).
- The difference between a broken and an unbroken tooth can be exhibited to anyone, but in the case of pain I believe I can privately exhibit the difference to myself. This can be done by imagining pain, for instance by screwing up one's face. Wittgenstein asks whether you know that what you are exhibiting to yourself is pain and not a facial expression, and how you know what to exhibit before you do it. He concludes that this private exhibition is an illusion (§311, p. 110).
- §312 (the text given breaks off): the interlocutor asks whether the cases of the tooth and the pain are not similar, since the visual impression in the one corresponds to the sensation of pain in the other. Wittgenstein then asks us to imagine that surfaces have patches that cause pain in our skin when touched, so that we would speak of pain-patches on a leaf just as we speak of red patches, and that it is useful to notice them and infer properties of objects from them. The remark continues beyond the pages given (§312, p. 110).
