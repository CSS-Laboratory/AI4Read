# English pack · Could a society run itself on AI?

C1/C2 only · 29 items · source post: https://khyang-lab.org/ai4read/2026-10-ai-finance/

## Expressions

- **to conflate X with Y** (C1): to treat two different things as if they were the same. *Don't conflate algorithmic trading with AI trading: many algorithms follow simple rules.*
- **in lockstep** (C1): moving or changing together, at exactly the same time and in the same way. *Models trained on the same data may move in lockstep.*
- **a single point of failure** (C1): one part of a system whose failure stops or harms the whole system. *One shared model can become a single point of failure.*
- **to be held accountable (for)** (C1): to be made responsible for something and required to explain it or bear its consequences. *Institutions are held accountable for the AI systems they deploy.*
- **a human in the loop** (C1): a person who must check or approve what an automated system does before it takes effect. *Most participants in the IMF's outreach want a human in the loop in any AI-based strategy.*
- **to confer a status on** (C2): to give someone or something an official status, right or quality. *For Searle, collective acceptance confers on a printed piece of paper the status of money.*
- **to be underpinned by** (C1): to be supported or made possible by something that lies beneath it. *On Searle's account, money is underpinned by continued collective acceptance.*
- **to presuppose** (C1): to need something to be true or to exist already in order to work or make sense. *Accountability presupposes someone who can be asked why.*
- **to devolve onto** (C2): (of a duty or responsibility) to pass to someone else. *Responsibility cannot simply devolve onto a system that cannot be punished, so it falls on whoever deploys it.*
- **to compound** (C1): to make a problem or difficulty worse by adding to it. *If many firms chase the same signal, their trades may compound a shock rather than absorb it.*
- **to be at odds with** (C1): to disagree with or not match something. *Imposing one standard menu of contracts can be at odds with Haldane and May's call for diversity.*
- **to hinge on** (C1): to depend completely on one particular thing. *Whether a machine-only token is money hinges on whether machines can do the accepting.*
- **to cut both ways** (C1): to have both good and bad effects, or to support both sides of an argument. *Standard contracts cut both ways: they make checking easier, but they make firms more alike.*
- **that’s a big if** (C1): used to say that a condition is very uncertain. *Every firm on one model? That’s a big if.*
- **to read too much into something** (C1): to think something has more meaning or importance than it really has. *Don’t read too much into a toy with ten banks.*
- **with the caveat that** (C1): with the warning or limitation that. *The toy helps, with the caveat that its numbers are invented.*
- **what remains to be seen is whether** (C1): used to introduce a question that has not been answered yet. *What remains to be seen is whether machines can do the accepting.*
- **by no means** (C1): not at all; certainly not. *Running a market is by no means the same as answering for it.*

## Sentence patterns

- **Reporting verbs calibrated to strength** (C1): Choose the reporting verb to match how firm the source is: judge, warn, hypothesise, argue, concede.. *The IMF judges that the risks currently appear contained; the FSB warns that common models could correlate trading; Gensler and Bailey hypothesise a monoculture.*
- **Cleft sentence for focus** (C1): Put the new or important information between 'It is … that' to control where the listener's attention falls.. *It is declaring and answering, not running, that make a group of people a society, on our reading.*
- **Inversion with 'were … to'** (C2): A formal way to state an unlikely condition: 'Were X to do Y, …' instead of 'If X did Y, …'.. *Were every firm to rely on one model, their errors would be likely to coincide.*
- **Inversion after a negative adverbial** (C2): After 'rarely', 'seldom', 'not until' or 'only when' at the start of a clause, put the auxiliary before the subject. Formal writing and talks.. *Rarely does a single toy settle a question about real markets.*

## Fixes

- **comprise (no 'of')** (C1): ~~The five jobs comprise of running, deciding, checking, declaring and answering.~~ → The five jobs comprise running, deciding, checking, declaring and answering. 'Comprise' takes a direct object; use 'consist of' if you want 'of'.
- **stacked hedges** (C1): ~~Shared models could possibly perhaps make failures coincide.~~ → Shared models could make failures coincide. One well-chosen hedge is enough; piling up 'could', 'possibly' and 'perhaps' sounds unsure rather than careful.
- **imply vs infer** (C1): ~~From the survey we imply that deciding is still mostly human.~~ → From the survey we infer that deciding is still mostly human. Evidence or a speaker implies (suggests); a reader or listener infers (concludes).
- **dangling modifier** (C2): ~~Having automated the checker, a new question arises: who checks it?~~ → Having automated the checker, we face a new question: who checks it? An opening participle phrase must describe the subject that follows; a question cannot automate a checker.

## Practice

- **Production drill**: Cover the expression, read the definition and the sentence with a gap, and say the expression aloud before you check. Recognising an expression is not the same as being able to use it.

## Paragraph to loop

Software already runs much of the market, but running is by no means the same as answering for it. Were many firms to rely on one model, their errors would be likely to move in lockstep, and that model could become a single point of failure. Most participants in the IMF's outreach want a human in the loop, partly because accountability presupposes someone who can be asked why. A token counts as money only while a community keeps accepting it, so whether a machine-only token is money hinges on whether machines can do the accepting, and what remains to be seen is whether they ever could. Standard contracts cut both ways: they make checking easier, yet they make firms more alike. We should not read too much into a toy with ten banks, with the caveat that it shows what could happen, not what will.
