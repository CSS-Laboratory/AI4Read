# English pack · What does an LLM know when it predicts the next word?

Schema /2 (passage-centred) · C1/C2 only · source post: https://khyang-lab.org/ai4read/2026-10-llm-next-word/

## Passage

0. A language model is trained to do one job: guess the next piece of text.
1. It is scored by its loss, which measures how closely those guesses match real text.
2. What it knows comes in four layers, from the most certain to the most contested.
3. First come bits about text, which are measurable.
4. That is knowledge in Claude Shannon's sense, and his theory sidesteps the question of meaning.
5. Next is structure, such as grammar, which is well supported.
6. Whether the model grasps meaning stands or falls on what you take meaning to be.
7. **As for minds, that is, tracking what people believe, the jury is still out.**
8. **Models pass many tests, but small changes often break the result, which should give us pause.**
9. **Yet people get some of those altered tests wrong too, and researchers disagree on whether one failure counts against the model.**
10. **A low loss is not tantamount to understanding, and in and of itself it cannot tell you what kind of knowledge the model has.**
11. You can tease apart the kinds by changing the test in small ways and seeing what breaks.

### Gist

- What four layers of knowledge does the speaker describe, and which is the most contested? → Bits about text, structure such as grammar, meaning, and minds. The question of minds is the most contested.
- What does a low loss not tell you, and how can you find out more? → It does not tell you what kind of knowledge the model has. One way to find out is to change the test in small ways and see what breaks.

## Patterns

- **Inversion after a fronted ordinal (First come X, next comes Y)** (C1): Put an ordinal or sequencing word first and the verb before its subject, so the list reads as a sequence: 'First come bits about text'. The verb agrees with the subject that follows, not with the fronted word. In the passage: “First come bits about text”. - **Nominal clause 'what you take X to be'** (C1): 'What you take X to be' asks for the speaker's view of X's nature and fits after 'depends on' or 'stands or falls on': 'Whether A is true stands or falls on what you take B to be'. ## Fixes

- **verb agrees with the subject after inversion** (C1), sentence 3: ~~First comes bits about text, which are measurable.~~ → First come bits about text, which are measurable. After a fronted 'First' or 'Next', the verb still agrees with the subject that follows: 'First come bits' (plural), but 'First comes grammar' (singular).
- **no article with uncountable abstract noun** (C1), sentence 4: ~~That is a knowledge in Claude Shannon's sense, and his theory sidesteps the question of meaning.~~ → That is knowledge in Claude Shannon's sense, and his theory sidesteps the question of meaning. 'Knowledge' is uncountable and takes no 'a' or plural in general use. 'A knowledge of X' is possible only with a modifier ('a working knowledge of X'). Say 'knowledge' or 'a piece of knowledge'.
- **whether, not if, in a subject clause** (C1), sentence 6: ~~If the model grasps meaning stands or falls on what you take meaning to be.~~ → Whether the model grasps meaning stands or falls on what you take meaning to be. A clause that is the subject of a sentence and expresses a yes/no question starts with 'whether', not 'if'.
- **no article in 'give someone pause'** (C1), sentence 8: ~~Models pass many tests, but small changes often break the result, which should give us a pause.~~ → Models pass many tests, but small changes often break the result, which should give us pause. In the idiom 'give someone pause', 'pause' takes no article; 'a pause' means a short break in time or speech.
