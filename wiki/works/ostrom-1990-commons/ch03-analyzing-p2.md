# Analyzing Long-Enduring, Self-Organized, and Self-Governed CPRs (part 2/2)

## L0 — One line
Long-enduring self-governed commons differ in their specific rules but share seven design principles (plus an eighth for large systems) that Ostrom offers, speculatively, as essential conditions helping to account for how appropriators (the resource users) monitor each other and comply conditionally without external enforcement.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Ostrom ends the case studies with the Philippine *zanjera* irrigation communities (farmer-run irrigation associations). She then asks what the enduring cases have in common. Their operational rules (day-to-day rules of use) differ, so rules cannot explain endurance across cases. What recurs is a set of design principles: clear boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions (penalties that rise with the offence), cheap conflict resolution, recognised rights to organise, and nesting (layered organisation). She tries to show that these principles can change incentives. In many cases appropriators (resource users) monitor each other cheaply and with private benefit, and they commit to rules conditionally. She treats the list as speculative, not as proven necessary conditions.

**Prerequisites:** the commons dilemma and free riding; repeated games and discount rates (how much future payoffs count); the idea of a public good.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The *zanjera* federation mobilises about 39 labour days per working member per year with about 94% attendance, yet allocates water less than technically optimally. Members still see its allocation formulas as legitimate (pp. 86–88).
- All the enduring cases face uncertain, complex environments. Their populations are stable, with low discount rates and strong norms, and the participants are fairly homogeneous (pp. 88–89).
- Specific operational rules differ across cases, so they cannot explain robustness across settings. The differences themselves help, because rules must fit local conditions (p. 89).
- Eight design principles (Table 3.1) are offered as speculative. Ostrom does not yet claim they are necessary (pp. 90–91).
- Boundaries alone are not enough, because insiders can still overharvest. Appropriation and provision rules must also be tailored and modifiable by those affected (pp. 91–93).
- Reputation and shared norms are not sufficient by themselves, and no external authority was present enough to enforce rules day to day. Compliance rests on internal monitoring and graduated sanctions (pp. 93–94).
- In many of the cases monitoring is cheap, because the rules make it a by-product of use, and it brings private rewards and information. Ostrom offers this to explain why people monitor despite the presumed second-order dilemma (the problem that nobody will pay to monitor) (pp. 95–97).
- Contingent self-commitment and mutual monitoring reinforce each other. The required compliance rate varies by setting and need not be 100% (pp. 98–100).
- Conflict resolution, minimal recognition of rights to organise, and nested enterprises complete the set (pp. 100–102).

### Zanjera irrigation communities in the Philippines (pp. 82–88)
**Origins and structure.**
- The earliest record of the Ilocos Norte irrigation societies is from Spanish priests in 1630. Ostrom thinks the *zanjeras* may come from mixed traditions, including Spanish ones (p. 82).
- Like the huertas, they put small irrigator communities at the centre. These communities set their own rules, choose officials, guard their systems and maintain their canals (p. 82).
- There were 686 communal systems in Ilocos Norte in 1979 (p. 82).
- Under a *biang ti daga* ("sharing of the land") contract, tenants build an irrigation system on unirrigated land in return for rights to a defined part of the produce. The landowner keeps ownership. Use rights last only while the system is maintained (p. 82).
- Each original participant gets one *atar* (share). The total number of atars is fixed at formation (p. 82).
- One atar gives one vote, the right to farm a proportionate share of the land, and the duty to give one day of work per declared work season plus a share of construction materials (p. 82).
- This lets people gain land and water use rights without prior money assets (pp. 82–83).
- The area is split into three or more sections, and each farmer holds a plot in each. Members are therefore in symmetrical positions, with land at both head and tail. In dry years the burden is shared quickly by not irrigating the bottom section (p. 83).
- Some tail-end parcels pay the officials. This rewards service and gives leaders an incentive to get water to the tail (p. 83).
- Members elect a *maestro* (executive officer), a secretary, a treasurer and a cook. Larger associations also choose foremen and team leaders. The maestro cannot rely on persuasion alone, because rules contain inducements and sanctions (p. 83).

**The Bacarra-Vintar federation (Siy 1982).**
- Nine *zanjeras* share a 100-metre brush dam of bamboo, banana leaves, sand and rock. The river destroys it every rainy season, in some years three or four times (p. 83).
- Most of the zanjeras began independently. Two were already associated in the nineteenth century, a third joined under a 1906 written agreement, others joined through the 1950s, and the last two joined at their formation (pp. 84–85).
- In 1978 the federation incorporated as a private corporation. The 1976 Philippine Water Code defined only individuals or "juridical persons" as eligible to obtain water rights, and members wanted secure rights in the federation's name (p. 85).
- The heads of the zanjeras form the board of directors. In 1980, 431 individuals held shares or parts of shares in at least one zanjera. Zanjeras had 20 to 73 members each, and each handles its own finances (p. 85).
- The board decides when to rebuild the dam. Rebuilding takes about a week and involves several hundred people (p. 85).
- The five zanjeras with the most atars each provide one work team. The four smaller ones form two teams. The dam is divided into seven sections, roughly in proportion to team size and terrain difficulty, so teams can watch each other and compete (p. 85).
- Distributory canals are handled inside each zanjera by *gunglos*, teams of 5–10 members (p. 85).

**Obligations and compliance.**
- Siy computed obligations (work, meetings, celebrations) for 1980. A full atar owner owed between 32 days (Nibinib) and 86 days (Santo Rosario). The average across the federation was 53 days per atar (pp. 85–86).
- Some atars are held jointly, so the average is about 39 days per working member. At five days a week this is about two months of unpaid work (p. 86).
- About 16,000 man-days were supplied in the year (p. 86).
- Siy notes that few rural organisations in developing countries mobilise voluntary labour on this scale (p. 86).
- Members were absent a little over 2 of 39 days, an attendance rate of about 94%. Fines were fully paid in five zanjeras, and only one had a substantial problem with payment (p. 86).
- Over time atars fragment. For example, a founder's three sons each take one-third of the duties and one-third of the land (p. 86).
- Zanjeras respond by naming one person responsible for each atar's duties, which spares the association from monitoring work inside an atar. Some also require prior approval before a share is sold or tenants work the land (p. 86).
- Prospective members are screened. Erring members may be suspended or expelled and their land confiscated (Siy 1982, p. 101, quoted p. 86). Competition for land is still fierce (p. 86).

**Water allocation.**
- Water is usually more than enough for current crops and soils. When it is abundant, anyone can irrigate at will (p. 86).
- When it is scarce, rotation is set among zanjeras and among canals. In extreme dryness, downstream zanjeras get the full flow for several nights, and other members stand guard over the temporary checks (p. 87).
- Parcels with the greatest need get precedence (p. 87).
- Water shares are roughly proportional to labour and material contributions and to atar shares. Three zanjeras give 48% of the labour and materials and get 55% of the water. Three give 30% and get 25%. Three give 22% and get 20% (p. 87).
- Several downstream zanjeras harvest only one crop a year, but two crops are possible in the higher zanjeras (p. 87). Siy shows that water could be reallocated to raise lower-zanjera productivity without loss at the head end (p. 87).
- **Siy's caution:** shifting water would require shifting obligations. A zanjera might be unable to meet new demands, which could undermine the stability or solidarity of the whole organisation (Siy 1982, p. 146, quoted p. 87).
- The federation falls short on technical efficiency but does well at mobilising people. Members see the allocation as following legitimate formulas they devised themselves (pp. 87–88).
- Ostrom says that Chapter 5 will show expert-designed systems built without irrigator participation often failed on both efficiency and organised action (p. 88).
- Many lower-zanjera members also belong to other zanjeras and own well-watered land. In a survey, lower-zanjera members more often reported water shortage. None, however, had anything to say about fairness of allocation, and 65% named the annual dam damage as their main problem (p. 88).

*Intuition note:* The zanjeras fall short of technical efficiency by an engineer's test, yet their rules are accepted and they mobilise labour year after year. Siy's caution about stability and solidarity (p. 87) suggests that members' acceptance of the rules may matter for keeping the system going.

### Similarities among enduring, self-governing CPR institutions (pp. 88–90)
- **Shared features.**
  - All face uncertain, complex environments (erratic rainfall; engineering plus farming skills needed) (p. 88).
  - Populations are stable. People share a past and expect a shared future, so reputation matters, and discount rates are low (p. 88).
  - Extensive norms define "proper" behaviour. Long-term self-interest reinforces them (p. 89).
  - None of the cases has participants who vary greatly in assets, skills, knowledge, ethnicity or race (p. 89).
  - The resource systems meet the test of sustainability. The institutions meet Shepsle's criterion of institutional robustness, in that the rules were devised and modified under collective-choice and constitutional-choice rules (p. 89).
  - These cases were chosen because they endured while others failed (p. 89).
- **Rules differ.** Examples: rights and duties go to family units in Japan, to inherited male property owners in the Swiss mountains, to the parcel of land in eastern Spain, and to a contract among usufructuary right-holders in the Philippines. Operational rules therefore cannot explain robustness across settings (p. 89). Part of Ostrom's explanation is nonetheless that rules differ, because they fit local physical, cultural and economic conditions (p. 89).
- **Design principles.** She means by this an essential element or condition that helps explain success in sustaining the CPR and in winning generations of compliance (p. 90).
  - The list is "still quite speculative". She is not yet willing to call the principles necessary conditions (p. 90).
  - She is willing to speculate that, after further scholarly work, it will be possible to identify a set of necessary design principles containing the core of this list (p. 91).
  - For the principles to be a credible explanation, Ostrom says she needs to show that they can affect incentives so that appropriators will commit to rules, monitor each other and replicate the institutions across generations (p. 91).

### Design principle 1: Clearly defined boundaries (pp. 91–92)
- Defining the users and the resource boundary is a first step. Without it, no one knows what is managed or for whom, and outsiders may reap the benefits of insiders' efforts or destroy the resource (p. 91).
- With many potential appropriators and high demand, free withdrawal could push the discount rate toward 100%. The higher the rate, the closer the situation is to a one-shot dilemma where overuse is the dominant strategy (p. 91).
- Ciriacy-Wantrup and Bishop used boundaries as the single defining feature of common property versus open access. Ostrom says making boundaries one of seven principles puts it in better perspective (pp. 91–92).
- Closing the boundaries is not enough. A limited group can still dissipate rents or destroy the resource (Clark 1980), so rules limiting appropriation or requiring provision are needed (p. 92).

### Design principle 2: Congruence with local conditions (p. 92)
- Appropriation rules (time, place, technology, quantity) are related to local conditions and to provision rules (labour, materials, money) (p. 92).
- The four close Spanish huertas have different rules. Only Alicante has substantial storage (Tibi Dam, 1594), and only there is there a Sunday water auction, with dam-level information shown (p. 92).
- In the others, water is tied to land. Valencia uses rotation, with farmers taking what they can use, in a set order. Murcia and Orihuela, with scarcer water, use a tighter rotation of gate-open time (p. 92).
- In all, those who get the highest share of water pay the highest share of fees. No single regional rule set would fit all of them (p. 92).

### Design principle 3: Collective-choice arrangements (pp. 93–94)
- When those affected can modify the rules, the rules can be better fitted to local settings. With the first three principles, appropriators should be able to devise a good rule set if rule-change costs stay low (p. 93).
- Good rules do not ensure compliance. Having designed and agreed to the rules does not explain compliance over centuries, or even the later commitment of the original agreers. Ostrom puts it this way: agreeing ex ante is easy, and following the rules ex post, when temptations arise, is the achievement (p. 93).
- External enforcement cannot explain compliance, because no external authority had enough presence in the cases to play any role in day-to-day enforcement (p. 93).
- **Repeated-game models** (Axelrod; Kreps et al.; T. Lewis and Cowens) predict cooperation without external enforcement.
  - They need very specific information conditions that are rarely met in the field.
  - Players cooperate while all others do, and all defect forever after any deviation.
  - The models assume that everyone's past moves are freely known, so no monitoring appears in them (p. 93).
- In the cases, reputation and shared norms are not sufficient on their own for stable long-run cooperation. If they were, appropriators could skip investing in monitoring and sanctions. All enduring cases show such investments (pp. 93–94).

### Design principles 4 and 5: Monitoring; graduated sanctions (pp. 94–99)
- In these institutions the participants themselves monitor and sanction, and the initial sanctions are surprisingly low. Participants seem to have overcome the presumed second-order dilemma (the text layer is garbled here; the image was not provided) (p. 94).
- **Quasi-voluntary compliance** (Levi 1988a) means compliance that is voluntary in many situations but backed by coercion of the noncompliant if caught (p. 94).
  - **Levi:** taxpayers comply when they are confident that rulers keep their bargains and that other constituents keep theirs. No one wants to be a "sucker" (p. 94).
  - Earlier work on contingent behaviour (Axelrod; Lewis and Cowens) saw it as an alternative to coercion. Levi sees coercion as an essential condition: enforcement raises confidence that one is not a sucker (p. 95).
  - In Levi's theory the enforcer is normally an external ruler, but the theory does not exclude other enforcers (p. 95).
- CPR appropriators supply their own enforcement, which deters rule-breakers and reassures quasi-voluntary compliers (p. 95).
- **Objection (Elster):** punishment is costly to the punisher while its benefits are diffuse (p. 95). Ostrom's reply is that the costs and benefits of monitoring must have a different configuration than assumed. Either costs are lower, or benefits to the individual are higher, or both (p. 95).
- **Low costs.**
  - Rotation systems put the two actors most concerned about cheating in direct contact. The irrigator ending a turn wants to overrun, and the next one waits nearby and would like to start early. Each deters the other at no extra monitoring cost (p. 95).
  - The Alanya fishing-site rotation works the same way (pp. 95–96). So do many work teams in the Swiss and Japanese mountain commons (p. 96).
  - Rules themselves therefore affect the cost of monitoring (p. 96).
- **Private rewards.**
  - Finding a violator brings status and prestige, and the violator loses status (p. 96).
  - Spanish huerta guards keep part of the fines, and Japanese detectives keep the sake they collect (p. 96).
  - Guards are accountable to the appropriators and can be fired easily. Appropriators also watch the guards, so the system has redundancy and one failure does not cascade (p. 96).
  - Appropriators who design some of their own rules (principle 3) can learn to craft enforceable ones (p. 96).
- **Information benefit.**
  - In the cases, information on behaviour and outcomes is costly. A monitor gains the information needed for a contingent strategy, including the compliance rate, as well as providing a public good (pp. 96–97).
  - If no violation is found, the monitor learns that no one is being suckered and can safely keep complying (p. 97).
  - If a violation is found, the monitor can learn the circumstances, help set the sanction and decide whether to keep complying (p. 97).
  - If the offender usually follows the rules but faced a severe problem, a modest sanction may suffice. The monitor may need similar understanding later (p. 97).
  - Repeated violators raise a real threat to quasi-voluntary compliance. The monitor can then be expected to escalate the sanctions (p. 98).
- **Offender's view.** Being caught stops the infraction and may return contraband harvest. It signals that others are likely to be caught too. It also imposes a fine plus loss of reputation (p. 98).
  - A large fine on a person with an unusual problem may produce resentment. Graduated punishments, applied by sanctioners who know the circumstances, may be far more effective than a major fine on a first offender (p. 98).
- **Required compliance rate.**
  - Earlier trigger-strategy models assume 100% compliance is needed, and any infraction triggers relentless punishment. M. Taylor (1987) posits less than 100%. Ostrom says trigger models do not describe the observed cases (p. 98).
  - Acceptable rates vary by setting and circumstances. Tolerance may be high in a depression if the higher infraction rate seems temporary and not a threat to survival (p. 99).
  - **McKean (1986) on Yamanaka:** during the 1930s depression nearly everyone broke the rules. Yet violators stole only out of desperation, and witnesses stayed silent out of sympathy and confidence that the problem was temporary (p. 99).
  - In other situations 100% compliance may be essential, because one infraction can do great harm and the private gain is great. In Shiwa during a drought, all adult males patrolled the dikes nightly in mutual surveillance (p. 99).
- **Summary.** When appropriators design their rules (3), enforced by local appropriators or those accountable to them (4), using graduated sanctions (5), with defined users (1) and rules fitted to local conditions (2), commitment and monitoring problems are solved together (p. 99).
- **Contingent self-commitment.** Ostrom words the commitment this way: follow the rules the group has devised in all instances except "dire emergencies", if the rest of those affected make a similar commitment and act accordingly (pp. 99–100). Such commitments motivate occasional monitoring, and monitoring supports the commitments, especially where rules lower monitoring costs (p. 100).

*Intuition note:* People follow rules while they believe most others do. Watching others, which is cheap here, is how they check that belief. Light first penalties may help keep an honest offender in the system.

### Design principle 6: Conflict-resolution mechanisms (pp. 100–101)
- In field settings, applying rules is never unambiguous, even when appropriators monitor. Ostrom's example rule is "each irrigator must send one individual for one day to help clean the irrigation canals". It raises questions: who counts as an individual (child, elderly person), how long is a "day", and does cleaning next to your own farm count (p. 100)?
- Some people will exploit such ambiguity, and honest errors also happen (p. 100).
- If some may send weak workers while others send strong ones, the strong senders will feel like suckers. Eventually only children and old people would be sent and the system would break down (pp. 100–101).
- People who make honest mistakes need an acceptable way to make up for them. Otherwise rules may seem unfair and compliance may fall (p. 101).
- Such mechanisms do not guarantee enduring institutions. But Ostrom finds it hard to imagine a complex rule system lasting without them. They are sometimes informal, with leaders as resolvers, and in some cases, such as the Spanish huertas, the potential for conflict over a very scarce resource is so high that well-developed court mechanisms have been in place for centuries (p. 101).

### Design principle 7: Minimal recognition of rights to organise (p. 101)
- Appropriators often make rules without forming formal jurisdictions, as in many inshore fisheries (p. 101).
- If outside officials give at least minimal recognition to these rules, the fishers may be able to enforce them. If outside officials presume that only they can set rules, it will be very difficult to sustain a rule-governed CPR over the long run (p. 101).
- Someone who wants to evade local rules can appeal to the external government. Chapter 5 will examine cases where this principle is not met (p. 101).

### Design principle 8: Nested enterprises (pp. 101–102)
- All the more complex enduring CPRs meet it. The huertas have three or four nested irrigator levels inside local, regional and national government (pp. 101–102).
- The Philippine federation has two levels (p. 102). Problems at a tertiary canal differ from those at a secondary canal and from those at the main diversion works (p. 102).
- Rules at one level without rules at other levels give an incomplete system that may not endure (p. 102).
- The chapter closes by stating that the principles are meant to explain why participants will replicate the institutions and sustain the CPR. The next chapter turns to how individuals supply themselves with new institutions (p. 102).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Six irrigators take turns at one gate, 60 minutes each. Irrigator A would gain 10 extra minutes by overrunning. Suppose the sanction for a first offence is losing 20 minutes of A's next turn (graduated: a larger penalty is held back for repeat offenders).
- **Neighbour waits at the gate** (rotation layout). Chance of being seen is 1, so the expected sanction is 1 × 20 = 20 minutes. Net for A is 10 − 20 = −10, so A does not overrun. The next irrigator was waiting anyway, so monitoring costs the neighbour 0 extra minutes.
- **No one nearby.** Suppose the chance of being seen is 0.1. The expected sanction is 0.1 × 20 = 2 minutes, so A's net is 10 − 2 = +8, and A overruns. Monitoring would now cost others extra time.
- **Compliance rate.** If one of six overruns on a given day, 5/6 ≈ 83% of turns comply. The text says the compliance rate others will tolerate varies by setting (p. 99). This is the sense of "contingent" commitment.

## Concepts
- [[common-pool-resource-cpr]] — shared resource system governed by its users. Here, irrigation systems and mountain commons (p. 88).
- [[design-principles-ostrom]] — essential conditions that help explain endurance, offered as speculative, not proven necessary (p. 90).
- [[institutions]] — rules devised, modified and enforced by appropriators, judged for robustness (p. 89).
- [[free-rider]] — someone who sends weak workers to a labour day, leaving others as "suckers" (p. 100).
- [[selective-incentive]] — monitors receive private rewards such as status, part of fines, or sake (p. 96).
- [[quasi-voluntary-compliance]] (new) — compliance that is voluntary but conditional on others' compliance and on coercion of the noncompliant (Levi, p. 94).
- [[contingent-self-commitment]] (new) — commitment to follow the rules except in dire emergencies, provided others do (pp. 99–100).
- [[institutional-robustness]] (new) — institutions whose rules have been devised and modified over time under collective-choice and constitutional-choice rules (Shepsle's criterion, p. 89).
- [[second-order-dilemma]] (new) — the presumed problem that no one will pay to monitor and sanction. The text layer is garbled at p. 94 (see Reading notes).
- [[appropriator]] (new) — user who withdraws units from a CPR (p. 91).
- [[discount-rate]] (new) — how little future benefits count. Low where people expect a long shared future (p. 88).

## Canonical instances
- [[zanjera-bacarra-vintar-federation]] — nine zanjeras, a dam destroyed each rainy season and rebuilt, high labour mobilisation and attendance (pp. 83–88).
- [[spanish-huertas]] — differing water rules fitted to local conditions, and courts for conflicts (pp. 92, 101).
- [[japanese-villages-yamanaka-shiwa]] — tolerance of infractions in a depression versus 100% mutual surveillance in a drought (p. 99).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Quasi-voluntary compliance ↔ political-science work on tax compliance (Levi): conditional cooperation backed by enforcement.
- [structural-analogy] Trigger-strategy models (defect forever after one deviation) ↔ repeated-game theory: Ostrom says the cases do not fit them.
- [structural-analogy] Monitoring as a costly public good ↔ the collective action problem: here the cost is offset by private rewards.
- [metaphor] Nested enterprises ↔ layered governance in federal systems.

## Questions this raises
- If the design principles are not proven necessary, how could they be tested against failed cases?
- How far does the low-cost, by-product monitoring argument carry over to resources where cheating is hard to see?
- How much does homogeneity of participants (p. 89) limit where the principles apply?
- When outsiders do not recognise local rights (principle 7), what happens to the other principles?
