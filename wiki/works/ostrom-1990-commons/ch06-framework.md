# A framework for analysis of self-organizing and self-governing CPRs

## L0 — One line
Static game models cannot explain when small-scale commons users build their own rules, so Ostrom offers a framework of situational variables (benefits, costs, norms, discount rates, outside regime) that shape how individuals judge a rule change.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks why some appropriators (users of a common-pool resource, CPR) supply themselves with rules and others do not. Ostrom says the Chapter 1 models are not wrong. They are special models that hold key variables fixed, and they are out of range for small, communicating groups. Standard collective-action theory is also too sparse. It ignores incremental change, the external political regime, and information and transaction costs. She builds a framework, not a model. It shows how situational variables (features of the setting) shape judgments about benefits, costs, norms and discount rates. Institutional change is predicted only with conditions, never guaranteed. Her policy lesson is that outside governments can help or hinder.

**Prerequisites:** the Chapter 1 models (tragedy of the commons, prisoner's dilemma, Olson's collective action); the Chapter 3 design principles; the idea of expected benefit minus expected cost; discount rate.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The Chapter 1 models predict well when conditions approximate their assumptions (no communication, independent action, fixed structure), but they cannot predict outside that range. Applying them to small CPRs is applying them "out of range" and can destroy local institutional capital (pp. 183–184).
- Contingent commitment (a promise conditional on others' compliance), mutual monitoring and graduated sanctions reinforce each other. Together they make commitment safe and credible (pp. 186–187).
- Standard collective-action variables (group size, minimum group, discount rate, similar interests, leaders) leave anomalies unexplained. Theories also miss incremental change, external regimes, and information and transaction costs (pp. 188–191).
- Success in larger systems came through nested enterprises. Small units were organized first, and institutional capital accumulated (pp. 189–190).
- A framework asks which questions to ask. A model gives precise predictions from fixed assumptions. Both are needed (pp. 191–192, 214–215).
- Individuals judge benefits, costs, norms and discount rates from information. That information depends on situational variables such as number of appropriators, past rules, autonomy to change rules, and exclusion technology, and it shapes judged transformation and monitoring costs (pp. 194–207).
- Rule choice is human judgment under uncertainty, with known biases. Losses weigh more than gains, recent events weigh more than distant ones, and familiar rules are favoured (pp. 207–210).
- Incremental change is more likely when six internal conditions hold in a "zero condition" setting (remote CPR, indifferent regime), and the regime changes this. Facilitative regimes can help, while paternalist or corrupt ones make supply harder. Success is never assured (pp. 210–214).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction: why a framework is needed (pp. 182–185)
- The three Chapter 1 models all predict that users will not cooperate so as to achieve collective benefits. Users are seen as trapped in a static situation and unable to change their own rules (p. 182).
- The cases are small-scale CPRs, the largest with about 15,000 appropriators, each within one country. Some, not all, appropriators solved "second-order dilemmas" (the problem of supplying the institutions themselves) (p. 182).
- Marketable rights to the flow of resource units were developed in Alicante and in three California groundwater cases. The resource systems did not become private property (p. 182).
- No success case involved direct regulation by a central authority. Most arrangements mixed public and private instruments (p. 182).
- Ostrom does not claim appropriator-made institutions are optimal. The Mojave case shows this. Long survival of the resources and institutions shows at least a minimal level of "solution" (p. 183).
- The models "are not wrong". They are special models with extreme assumptions, so they predict well in situations that approximate those assumptions (p. 183).
- Examples are the collapse of the Pacific sardine and Antarctic blue whale fisheries (p. 183).
- They are far less useful for small CPRs. There people communicate repeatedly in a local setting, can learn whom to trust, and can build social capital from shared norms and reciprocity (pp. 183–184).
- Applying such models to small CPRs applies them out of range and can do more harm than good. Policies that assume helplessness can destroy accumulated institutional capital, as in the Nova Scotian fisheries (p. 184).
- Simplifying assumptions often equal setting a parameter to a constant. Setting a variable equal to a constant usually narrows, not broadens, a model's range (p. 184).
- Static models show what people do in a situation they cannot change. They do not show what people do when they can craft institutions. Hence the policy recommendation that someone external must change the structure (pp. 184–185).
- Several cases moved from unorganized independent decisions to organized, sequential, contingent or frequency-dependent decisions. These were Alanya, Törbel, the Japanese villages, Valencia, Ilocos Norte, the California basins, and even Mawelle (p. 185).
- The Sri Lankan settlement farmers changed only after external agents started small-scale changes. Bodrum and Izmir fishers appear unable to change. The Mojave users may mine their basin dry (p. 185).

### The problems of supply, credible commitment and mutual monitoring (pp. 185–192)
**Commitment and monitoring.**
- Chapter 3's design principles 1–5 (boundaries; rules fitted to local conditions; local design; accountable monitors; graduated sanctions) let fallible, norm-adopting individuals make a safe, advantageous, credible commitment (pp. 185–186).
- The commitment is to follow the rules so long as (1) most similarly situated individuals do and (2) the long-term expected net benefits exceed those of short-term dominant strategies (p. 186).
- It is safe because anyone can lower their own compliance if others break rules too much. "I will if you will" is credible when monitoring exists, since unprovoked deviations are likely to be found (p. 186).
- Graduated sanctions forgive occasional lapses or emergencies but raise severity for continued infractions (p. 186).
- If infractions go undetected, rule-breakers may revise their estimate of how well monitoring deters others. Compliance will then "cascade downward" unless monitoring efforts are increased. Ostrom concludes that monitoring and graduated sanctions are necessary to keep rule-following high (pp. 186–187).
- Contingent compliance needs information on others' compliance. Serving as a monitor supplies it. Contingent strategies make monitoring more likely, and monitoring makes contingent strategies more likely (p. 187).
- Graduated sanctions work first for information and later for deterrence. The explanation does not rest on a single variable. Reinforcing arrangements give consistent, but not perfect, compliance (p. 187).

> *Intuition note.* Rules, monitoring and conditional promises hold each other up. Each person keeps the promise because others are watching and complying, and watching is worthwhile because others also conditionally comply. If monitoring is not kept up, compliance can cascade downward.

**Institutional supply: where standard theory falls short.**
- Recent collective-action theory looks at variables internal to the situation. These are the number of decision makers, the minimum number needed for the benefit, the discount rate, similar interests, and leaders or other assets (p. 188).
- Alanya fits these: about 100 fishers, low discount rate, same technology, and they organized without anyone holding substantial assets (p. 188).
- Bodrum (400) and Izmir (1,700) fit too: mixed discount rates, many technologies, four to six subgroups, and they did not organize (p. 188).
- There are anomalies. Two successful groundwater basins had 700 and 750 pumpers, substantially different interests and relatively high discount rates. The Spanish huertas had 2,400–13,500 irrigators. Mawelle, with just over 200 fishers, similar interests and low discount rates, failed. Neither leadership nor production function explains the differences (p. 188).
- The theories are "too sparse" (key variables missing) and "difficult to interpret" (unclear policy implications). For example, knowing that group size makes organizing harder does not itself tell policy makers what to do. Ostrom asks whether one should presume small groups look after themselves and larger groups need external governors, and says the anomalous cases show this is an inappropriate implication (pp. 188–189).

**Three missing elements.**
1. *Incremental, self-transforming change.* Successful larger systems used nested enterprises (design principle 8) (p. 189).
   - In the huertas the basic unit is the tertiary canal, and several are three or four layers deep (p. 189).
   - In the Philippines, zanjera work teams of 5–10 and zanjeras of 20–75 members came first, then federation (p. 189).
   - In Gal Oya (Sri Lanka), small face-to-face groups came first, ending in four layers (p. 189).
   - In the Raymond, West and Central basins, a small voluntary association for information came first. Mojave tried at once to assign rights for 15 basins and an underground river (pp. 189–190).
   - Starting small builds social capital. Current theories do not stress "accretion of institutional capital" (p. 190).
2. *The external political regime.* In Mawelle a small group could not enforce its rules, and officials would not enforce the national exclusion rule. In Newfoundland, local institutions were weakened when authorities refused to recognize them (p. 190). Ostrom argues the regime helped in southern California and hindered in Mawelle and Newfoundland (p. 190).
3. *Information and transaction costs.* Assuming complete free information and ignoring transaction costs does not fit settings where information is scarce, biased and costly. It also makes mutual monitoring hard to explain (pp. 190–191).

**Models versus frameworks.**
- Models give precise predictions by holding variables constant. Models do not say how to find the structure of a situation (p. 191).
- Each assumption fails to direct attention to something. Complete information hides how people obtain information. Independent action hides whether people consider others' responses. Zero-cost monitoring hides monitoring rules. Fixed structure hides rule change (pp. 191–192).
- A framework points to the variables and relations to study. It yields questions, not precise predictions. Ostrom builds one to summarize lessons from successful and failed efforts. Theorists can refine it into models, and empirical work can use it to test relative importance (p. 192).

### A framework for analyzing institutional choice (pp. 192–195)
- "Institutional-choice situation" covers constitutional-choice and collective-choice situations. Collective choices affect operational rules directly. Constitutional choices affect them indirectly, by creating and limiting powers and by deciding who is represented (p. 192).
- The same individuals face operational and institutional choices, so the same conception of the individual applies. It has four internal variables: expected benefits, expected costs, internalized norms, and discount rates (p. 193).
- Individuals select strategies whose expected benefits exceed expected costs. Without knowledge of situational variables, this prediction is vacuous, so most of the explanatory weight is on situational variables (p. 193).
- Figure 6.1: information about benefits of proposed rules and costs of transforming, monitoring and enforcing them feeds expected benefits and costs. Information about shared norms and other opportunities feeds norms and discount rate. These produce support or non-support for changing the status quo (SQ) rules (p. 193).
- The choice is to support the status quo or support a change. An aggregation rule turns supports into an outcome (p. 194).
- Evaluating expected benefits depends on information about benefits of alternative versus status quo rules. Evaluating costs depends on (1) up-front transformation costs and (2) net monitoring and enforcement costs (p. 194).
- The analyst need only find the summary variables to predict strategies if three conditions hold (p. 194):
  1. accurate summary measures exist for each summary variable;
  2. individuals translate net benefits and costs into expectations completely and accurately;
  3. individuals behave in a straightforward, not strategic, way.
- Condition 1 amounts to a valid and reliable benefit–cost analysis having been done. In the field, individuals must invest resources to get this information (p. 194).
- Many field costs are not monetized. Philippine farmers know the cost of 50 days on the zanjera and their yield response. Labour is recorded only in an attendance book. Close participants can judge accurately, while administrators in a distant center find it far harder because many costs and benefits are not recorded in the information available to outsiders (pp. 194–195).
- Condition 2 means unbiased weighting of all information. Condition 3 means no opportunism: honest revelation, contribution whenever fair cost formulas exist (p. 195).
- Few field settings meet even one or two conditions. One must go beyond summary variables to the situational variables behind them (p. 195).

> *Intuition note.* "Benefits exceed costs" tells you little until you know what people actually know about benefits and costs. The framework's job is to list what shapes that knowledge.

### Evaluating benefits (pp. 195–198)
- Five questions: expected flow and value under proposed versus status quo rules; variability; quality; how long the resource lasts; and whether conflict falls, stays or rises (p. 196).
- Nine situational variables (Figure 6.2): number of appropriators, size of the CPR, temporal and spatial variability, current condition, market conditions, amount and type of conflict, availability of data on these, status quo rules, and proposed rules. The first, number of appropriators, is included in most collective-action theories; the rest are rarely considered (p. 196; Figure 6.2, p. 197).
- Larger systems, more appropriators, and less predictable flows and prices make accurate information costlier. Regular records can offset this. Records may be kept where fishers land all their catch at one port. A purchaser who shares data (as in a marketing cooperative) helps. A monopolist buyer may withhold data and know more than the fishers (p. 196).
- Official monitors (the watermaster, Swiss and Japanese local officials) give appropriators information they would not otherwise get, and appropriator organizations usually increase the information obtained and shared (p. 197).
- Perceived benefit therefore depends on objective conditions, the information current arrangements produce, and the proposed rules. Information about benefits "must be searched for, organized, and analyzed" (pp. 197–198).

### Evaluating costs (pp. 198–205)
**Transformation costs (p. 198).** These are resources spent considering a rule change (Buchanan and Tullock). If expected up-front costs exceed the net benefits, no further cost calculation is made and the status quo is kept. Otherwise, ex post monitoring and enforcement costs are also weighed (p. 198).
- Costs rise with the number of decision makers, the heterogeneity of interests, and the proportion needed to change rules. They fall with skillful leaders (p. 198).
- Because they are up-front, they are less affected by discount rates. The total is unaffected by individuals with large assets at stake. The likelihood that someone pays is higher where some will gain substantially (p. 198).
- Type of proposed rule matters. A private discussion association costs far less than a local public jurisdiction that can tax everyone (pp. 198–199).
- Rules usually change sequentially, with cheap, beneficial changes first. This builds experience. Sometimes one or two people gain enough to pay the whole cost, so not every step is a second-order dilemma. Small gains change the calculus for larger changes (pp. 199–200).
- Shared norms matter. Confrontational strategies raise costs sharply. Fear that others will form minimal winning coalitions to impose costs on losers affects willingness to adopt changes that would reduce the inclusiveness of future rules (p. 200).
- Rules adopted earlier affect later transformation costs: changes in operational rules alter the level and distribution of benefits, and major changes can raise or lower conflict (p. 200).
- Autonomy to change rules matters. Highly centralized regimes attempt to use the same operational rules everywhere; if so, locals must convince the authority to change rules or grant an exception. This costs time in an honest regime. Bribes may secure change, or block it, in a corrupt one (p. 200).
- With local autonomy, required procedures range from informal consultation to petitions, special elections, legislation and court proceedings. The more inclusive the aggregation rule, the higher the decision costs and the lower the losses to those the status quo protects (pp. 200–201).
- Where regular procedures exist, appropriators may estimate costs precisely (a lawyer for a charter, experienced organizers for 1,000 signatures). Where officials have wide discretion, estimating costs may be difficult, and past payments may deter attempts. Ostrom expects less rule innovation under discretionary regimes (p. 201).
- Remote CPRs usually have more autonomy, as affected by the number and effectiveness of officials and by transport and communications (p. 201).
- Past decisions shape later options (p. 202).
  - In Raymond Basin, assigning a defined quantity to each pumper led to a market in water rights, which also appeared in West and Central basins (p. 202).
  - A watermaster lowered the cost of a pump tax (p. 202).
  - Proportional rights ruled out later rules that protected municipal companies above others (p. 202).
  - Status quo rules protect some and expose others. They usually hold a privileged procedural position. Choices open some paths and close others (p. 202).

**Monitoring and enforcement costs (pp. 202–205).**
- Appropriators may monitor in the course of their work (fishers watching for outsiders) or by rotation (irrigators inspecting canals). Courts and police also use resources (pp. 202–203).
- Six variables (Figure 6.4, p. 203): size and structure of the CPR, exclusion technology, appropriation technology, marketing arrangement, proposed rules, and legitimacy of rules in use (p. 203).
- Larger resources cost more to fence or patrol. For many natural resources such as fisheries, fencing is physically impossible, and inshore fisheries (lagoons, bays) cost less to protect than offshore ones. For groundwater, boundary studies are expensive, but once boundaries are established a renegade well may be hard to hide, and the main exclusion cost may then be legal action (pp. 203–204).
- Costs tend to be lower where users can see or hear each other, where all return to one place so catches can be inspected, where shared norms support self-made rules, and where recording facilities are cheap (p. 204).
- Rules differ in cost. Frequent monitoring costs more. Unambiguous prohibitions cost less than rules that need information on who acts and why (p. 204).
- Season dates (Swiss, Japanese commons) cost less than individual quotas. Technology limits (Nova Scotia) cost less than quantity limits (p. 204).
- Rules that bring would-be cheaters together with those harmed are easier to monitor. Examples are irrigation time slots in Murcia and Orihuela, and the Alanya rules (pp. 204–205).
- Seasonal or annual quotas are costlier. Whether quotas are feasible depends on regularity of flow, storage, records and the value of the units (p. 205).
- Legitimacy granted by external authorities matters. The Mawelle case shows that when external authorities refuse to enforce a local exclusion rule, locals may be unable to keep entrants out. Where national governments respect local property systems, exclusion is cheaper. Where they do not (Nova Scotia, Newfoundland), exclusion costs can become very high. In some cases government-supported modern fleets have invaded inshore fisheries once "owned" by local fishers (p. 205).

### Evaluating shared norms and other opportunities (pp. 205–207)
- Weights on benefits and costs depend on internalized norms and discount rates (pp. 205–206).
- Coleman: internalized norms carry an internal cost for breach (guilt). Shared norms carry social displeasure from the group. A shared norm is often internalized, giving both costs (p. 206).
- Appropriators who live near the CPR and interact in many other settings are apt to develop strong norms (Figure 6.5, p. 206). Small-boat fishers' hostility to trawlers is not just about technology. Trawler operators often live elsewhere, belong to different groups, and are outside the local network of reputation (p. 206).
- Those drawn into outside economic opportunities are more likely to use a high discount rate than those who expect themselves and their children to depend on the CPR. Shared norms can affect discount rates as much as information about other opportunities does (pp. 206–207).

### The process of institutional change (pp. 207–210)
- Competitive markets let theorists predict that surviving firms maximize profit (Alchian). Price summarizes much information. Profit maximization does not predict which firms survive or what innovations occur (p. 207).
- CPR settings rarely push participants toward efficiency as strongly. No single variable like price exists. Short-term profit maximization may destroy the CPR. Uncertainty over new rules is greater, since outcomes depend on many later choices about interpretation, compliance, monitoring and sanctioning (pp. 207–208).
- Ostrom proposes viewing institutional choice as "informed judgments about uncertain benefits and costs" subject to known biases from social psychology (p. 208).
  - Losses weigh more than gains. This makes talk of "crises" understandable. It also suggests that resources that can be destroyed fast (clustering fish) are harder to govern than more resilient ones (p. 208).
  - Ostrom expects individuals to accept rules restricting appropriation when clear indicators of degradation are generally seen as accurate predictors of future harm, or when leaders convince others a "crisis" is impending (p. 208).
  - Gilles and Jamtgaard: information arrives at different speeds. Milk yield is seen daily, wool quality quickly, and meat quality later and maybe never by herders. Groundwater pumpers face harder information problems still (pp. 208–209).
  - Up-front transformation costs are easier to calculate than uncertain future benefits and sometimes are substantial, so they weigh heavily. Appropriators are unlikely to pay them unless the discounted net benefit is expected to be large (p. 209).
  - Recent events are overweighted. Rule changes should follow a run of bad yields, not good ones. Water-institution proponents "pray for dry weather" before decision points. When yields vary widely, accurate average estimates are costly (p. 209).
  - Proposed rules are likely to come from a repertoire already familiar to proposers. Individuals are less likely to adopt unfamiliar rules than rules used elsewhere that are known to work fairly well. West and Central basins learned from Raymond (p. 209).
  - Wrong lessons can be learned. Mojave users applied litigation and special districts at the interbasin level first, instead of incremental basin-level steps (p. 210).
  - Benefits differ by individual. Upstream Hawthorne in West Basin may gain little from restricting appropriation. Proposed rules have strong distributional effects (Libecap) (p. 210).

> *Intuition note.* People do not compute the right answer. They judge it with limited data and well-known biases. This helps make sense of why Ostrom expects rule change after a run of bad yields, and why familiar rules are more likely to be adopted than unfamiliar ones.

### Predicting institutional change (pp. 210–214)
- Ostrom rejects the idea that appropriators cannot supply institutions. She also rejects the opposite presumption that they will adopt new rules whenever net benefits exceed net costs. Net benefits and costs must be discovered and weighed by judgment, in complex settings made harder by others' strategic behavior (p. 210).
- Designing institutions is hard however homogeneous or well informed the group. Temptations to shirk and free-ride mean success "can never be assured". Strong pressures toward unique outcomes are more likely to produce the deficient equilibria of the Chapter 1 models. Capacities for reflection, communication and self-commitment make solutions possible, but not necessary. Users with rules that work may not search for better ones (pp. 210–211).
- **Zero condition** (a remote CPR under an indifferent regime): incremental rule change is more likely when these hold. Ostrom says they are weakly ordered, from those she thinks most important to those she thinks somewhat less important (p. 211):
  1. most appropriators share the judgment that they are harmed if they do not change;
  2. most are affected similarly by the proposed changes;
  3. most highly value continued activity from the CPR (low discount rates);
  4. information, transformation and enforcement costs are relatively low;
  5. most share generalized norms of reciprocity and trust (initial social capital);
  6. the group is relatively small and stable.
- Ostrom considers the first five more important than group size (p. 212).
- Most modern CPRs are not isolated. Nearby economic activity changes population, resource value and neighbouring CPRs, so the regime's orientation matters (p. 212).
- **Facilitative regime.** One that allows local autonomy, invests in enforcement agencies, and provides general choice and conflict-resolution arenas can help (p. 212). Ostrom contrasts this with centralization (Games 2–4 versus Game 5 of Chapter 1), and California's Department of Water Resources with Canada's Department of Fisheries and Oceans in Newfoundland (p. 212).
- Raymond, West and Central pumpers used geologic information from the U.S. Geological Survey and the California Department of Natural Resources. They also used an equity court proceeding for a legitimate settlement, and they drafted state legislation that the legislature enacted, which gave the foundation for special districts. Costs were shared with the state (p. 212).
- Officials reviewed district boundaries to exclude non-beneficiaries. The court would not approve a settlement that took water rights from some and gave them to others. Ostrom says it is "highly doubtful" these pumpers would have succeeded under an indifferent regime. Mojave shows success is not guaranteed even with such a regime (p. 213).
- **Paternalist regime with honest officials.** Honest, hard-working officials may well supply well-adapted institutions in some CPRs. But once officials say it is their job, appropriators without local institutions are likely to wait for the government, and the temptation to free-ride is hard to overcome. Some try to present "facts" to officials so as to favour themselves, and the best-resourced succeed most. Uniform rules are hard to make seem effective and fair, and monitoring costs are higher (pp. 213–214).
- **Corrupt centralized regime.** Local institutions outside the law would need a very homogeneous, well-informed group with very low discount rates. A more probable result is Kirindi Oya in Sri Lanka, where no one cooperated and all lived in "a hydrologic nightmare" (p. 214).

### A challenge to scholarship in the social sciences (pp. 214–216)
- The framework is not a single model because one cannot encompass this many variables within a single model (at least with current methods), and a model must set some variables to zero or a fixed value. Standard assumptions produce particularized models, not universal theories (p. 214).
- Social science should join mapping the whole terrain with tractable models for niches. Each CPR is a niche in that terrain (pp. 214–215).
- The "intellectual trap" in relying entirely on models is that scholars presume they are omniscient observers, and then address proposals to governments they model as omnicompetent (p. 215).
- Sugden (as quoted by Ostrom) says economic theory sees the world through the government's eyes, with the government rushing in "like the US Cavalry in a good Western" (p. 215).
- Ostrom reads Rolph's study of the southern California basins as an example. Rolph sees only "the government" as the actor and is puzzled that users got private rights. She does not see users using courts, legislatures and local authorities (pp. 215–216).
- Ostrom says the models social scientists tend to use support centralization in four ways (p. 216):
  1. users are seen as short-term maximizers only;
  2. users are seen as trapped until an authority acts;
  3. their institutions are ignored or called inefficient without checking what they do;
  4. the solutions offered rest on idealized markets or states.
- Social scientists need model-building, plus earlier thinkers (Hobbes, Montesquieu, Hume, Smith, Madison, Hamilton, Tocqueville) and fields such as public and social choice, transaction-cost economics, new institutional economics, law and economics, and game theory, carried into lab and field inquiry (p. 216).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three farmers each judge a rule change that would cost 20 labour-days up front (transformation cost). It would bring 10 labour-days of extra yield per year for 4 years, and cost 1 labour-day per year of monitoring. The only difference between them is the discount factor per year (a stand-in for the discount rate). Each supports the change if discounted net benefit is above 0.

Sum of discount factors over 4 years:
- Farmer A, factor 0.95: 0.95 + 0.9025 + 0.857375 + 0.81450625 = 3.52438125. Benefit = 35.2438; monitoring = 3.5244. Net = 35.2438 − 20 − 3.5244 = **+11.72**. Supports.
- Farmer B, factor 0.8: 0.8 + 0.64 + 0.512 + 0.4096 = 2.3616. Benefit = 23.616; monitoring = 2.3616. Net = 23.616 − 20 − 2.3616 = **+1.25**. Barely supports.
- Farmer C, factor 0.5: 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + 0.0625 = 0.9375. Benefit = 9.375; monitoring = 0.9375. Net = 9.375 − 20 − 0.9375 = **−11.56**. Does not support.

This shows three points from the chapter. The same rule can look good or bad depending on the discount rate (pp. 206–207). The up-front cost does not shrink with discounting, so it dominates for impatient users (pp. 198, 209). Whether the change passes also depends on the aggregation rule: with two of three needed, it passes, and with all three needed, it fails (p. 201). The numbers 10, 20 and 1 are themselves information that must be searched for (p. 198).

## Concepts
- [[common-pool-resource-cpr]] — a resource whose units are scarce and shared. The chapter studies small-scale CPRs with up to about 15,000 users (p. 182).
- [[design-principles-ostrom]] — features of rules (boundaries, local design, local monitors, graduated sanctions, nested enterprises) that make commitment and monitoring credible (pp. 185–186, 189).
- [[social-capital]] — shared norms and patterns of reciprocity built over time. Appropriators use them to build institutions (p. 184).
- [[institutions]] — rules that appropriators can supply and change. Prior rule choices shape later options (pp. 184, 202).
- [[path-dependence]] — current decisions build on past ones, which open some paths and close others (p. 202).
- [[free-rider]] — an appropriator who lets others pay for new institutions. The temptation is hard to overcome when someone else agrees to pay the costs, as when officials take responsibility (p. 213).
- [[collective-action-problem]] — supplying institutions is itself one. Chapter 6 lists standard variables and calls the theories built on them too sparse (pp. 188–189).
- [[nested-enterprises]] (new) — larger organizations built on already organized smaller units (design principle 8) (p. 189).
- [[contingent-commitment]] (new) — rule-following promised on condition that most others comply and that long-term expected net benefits are greater than from short-term strategies. Monitoring makes the announced commitment credible (pp. 186–187).
- [[transformation-costs]] (new) — up-front resources spent considering and changing rules (p. 198).
- [[institutional-choice-situation]] (new) — constitutional-choice and collective-choice situations together, which affect the rules used in operational situations (p. 192).
- [[situational-variables]] (new) — factors behind the summary variables of benefits, costs, norms and discount rates (pp. 195, 197–199, 203, 206).
- [[framework-vs-model]] (new) — a framework lists variables and questions. A model gives precise predictions by fixing variables (pp. 191–192).
- [[loss-weighting-judgment-bias]] (new) — individuals weight losses more than gains and recent events more than distant ones (pp. 208–209).

- [[mawelle-fishery]] — a small group could not exclude entrants, and officials did not enforce the national exclusion rule (pp. 190, 205).
