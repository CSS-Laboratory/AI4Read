# Ostrom, E. (1990). *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/CBO9780511807763

## In one paragraph
Policy advice on shared resources usually says to use either central control or privatization. Ostrom argues that this advice rests on three models (the tragedy of the commons, the prisoner's dilemma and the logic of collective action) that are used as metaphors and treat users as helpless. She shows that users of small-scale common-pool resources can write, monitor and enforce their own rules. Her evidence is long-lived mountain commons, Spanish huertas, Philippine irrigation and Los Angeles groundwater basins. She compares these with failed and fragile cases. From this she draws a speculative set of design principles and a framework for explaining when self-governance emerges. Change is usually incremental, and outside governments can help or hinder it. The book matters because it shows that collective action can work without a Leviathan or privatization.

## The path through the work
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch01-reflections]] — The standard tragedy-of-the-commons models are often used as metaphors for policy, in which users are helpless and only the state or only the market can save a shared resource; Ostrom calls both claims too sweeping and wants a theory of how users sometimes govern themselves.
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch02-institutional]] — Theories of the firm and the state explain how outsiders organize collective action, but no accepted theory explains how users of a common-pool resource organize themselves, so Ostrom builds a framework (varied problem structures, nested rule levels, working rules) to study successful and failed cases.
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch03-analyzing-p1]] — Swiss and Japanese mountain commons and Spanish *huerta* (irrigated farmland) systems have lasted for centuries while the users themselves made, monitored and revised their own rules, with high compliance and fines that Ostrom reports as low.
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch03-analyzing-p2]] — Long-enduring self-governed commons differ in their specific rules but share seven design principles (plus an eighth for large systems) that Ostrom offers, speculatively, as essential conditions helping to account for how appropriators (the resource users) monitor each other and comply conditionally without external enforcement.
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch04-analyzing]] — Pumpers in Los Angeles-area groundwater basins escaped a race to pump the most water by building new rules step by step, and Ostrom suggests that supplying institutions can be analysed as incremental changes to the rules already in force, rather than as a separate one-step leap.
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch05-analyzing]] — In Ostrom's failed and fragile cases, appropriators were large and divided, lacked arenas for collective choice, or faced outside governments that overrode or ignored their rules, and these cases meet fewer of her design principles than the robust ones.
- [[ostrom-1990-commons/ch06-framework]] — Static game models cannot explain when small-scale commons users build their own rules, so Ostrom offers a framework of situational variables (benefits, costs, norms, discount rates, outside regime) that shape how individuals judge a rule change.

## Big ideas across units
- **The standard models are special cases, not universal laws.** Used as metaphors, they fix constraints that users could change, and they cast the state and the market as the only fixes. Ch. 6 says they are "out of range" for small, communicating groups. Units: ch01-reflections, ch02-institutional, ch06-framework.
- **Self-organization faces nested dilemmas.** Supplying rules, making credible commitments and monitoring each other are each a second-order problem, and creating institutions is itself a collective dilemma. Units: ch02-institutional, ch03-analyzing-p2, ch04-analyzing.
- **Monitoring and sanctions work when they are cheap, mutual and graduated.** Appropriators watch each other, often as a by-product of using the resource. Fines start low and rise with the offence. Conditional commitment and monitoring reinforce each other. Units: ch03-analyzing-p1, ch03-analyzing-p2, ch04-analyzing (the watermaster), ch06-framework.
- **Design principles.** Ch. 1 points ahead to them. Ch. 3 states them speculatively, ch. 5 checks them against failures and fragile cases, and ch. 6 builds them into the framework. Units: ch01-reflections, ch03-analyzing-p1, ch03-analyzing-p2, ch05-analyzing, ch06-framework.
- **Rules must fit local conditions.** Alanya's design drew on fishers' local knowledge. Allocation rules are tied to each resource, and the specific operational rules differ across enduring cases. Units: ch01-reflections, ch03-analyzing-p1, ch03-analyzing-p2.
- **Institutional change is incremental and nested.** Rules change step by step from the status quo. Los Angeles pumpers built on earlier agreements. Large systems grew from small units, and Gal Oya organized small units first. Units: ch02-institutional, ch04-analyzing, ch05-analyzing, ch06-framework.
- **The outside regime can enable or undermine self-governance.** Examples are the Tibi Dam intrusion, California's facilitative regime, Mawelle and Port Lameron, and the policy lesson of ch. 6. Units: ch03-analyzing-p1, ch04-analyzing, ch05-analyzing, ch06-framework.
- **A framework is different from a model.** Ostrom builds a framework of situational variables and judgment under uncertainty. Her predictions are conditional, and success is never assured. Units: ch02-institutional, ch06-framework.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch01-reflections (the problem and the three models), ch02-institutional (the questions and key terms) and ch03-analyzing-p2 (the design principles). The catalog's focus list also names ch04-analyzing (institutional change).
- **Evidence for the principles:** ch03-analyzing-p1 supplies the Swiss, Japanese and Spanish cases that ch03-analyzing-p2 draws on. Read it for the details, but its main points are summarized in p2.
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch05-analyzing (failures and fragile cases) and ch06-framework. Neither is in the catalog's focus list. Ch. 5 is mostly case detail, and its table result is stated in its L1. Ch. 6 is the most abstract chapter and recasts the earlier material.
- **Dependencies:**
  - ch02-institutional needs the ch01 models.
  - ch03-analyzing-p1 and p2 need ch02's puzzles of commitment and monitoring.
  - ch04-analyzing needs ch02 and ch03 (second-order dilemma, design principles).
  - ch05-analyzing needs the ch03 design principles.
  - ch06-framework needs the ch01 models and the ch03 design principles.
