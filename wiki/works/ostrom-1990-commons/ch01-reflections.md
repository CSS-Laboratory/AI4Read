# Reflections on the Commons

## L0 — One line
The standard tragedy-of-the-commons models are often used as metaphors for policy, in which users are helpless and only the state or only the market can save a shared resource; Ostrom calls both claims too sweeping and wants a theory of how users sometimes govern themselves.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Ostrom asks how shared natural resources can best be governed, since policy advice so often reduces to two prescriptions: central control or privatization. She argues that such advice has relied largely on three models (Hardin's tragedy, the prisoner's dilemma, Olson's logic of collective action). These models are often used as metaphors that assume users cannot change their own rules. She uses simple games and the Alanya fishery to illustrate that users can design, monitor and enforce their own rules. Both external prescriptions also depend on strong assumptions about information, monitoring and cost. She calls for an empirically grounded theory of self-organized collective action and sets the scope of the book: small-scale, renewable common-pool resources.

**Prerequisites:** payoff matrices; dominant strategy and Nash equilibrium (a profile where no player gains by switching alone); expected value; the idea of public goods.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Three models (tragedy of the commons, prisoner's dilemma, logic of collective action) share one core: free-riding (enjoying a benefit without contributing) when someone cannot be excluded from the benefits others provide (pp. 2–6).
- The danger is metaphorical use. Constraints fixed for analysis get taken as fixed in the field unless outside authorities change them, which casts users as helpless prisoners (pp. 6–8).
- "Leviathan" (central control) reaches the optimal outcome on assumptions: accurate information, monitoring, sanctions without fail and zero running cost. In Ostrom's example with 0.7 sanction accuracy, the herders end up worse off than in the unregulated game (pp. 9–12).
- Privatization is also underspecified. Splitting the herders' meadow assumes fodder is spread evenly over time, and it is unclear what private rights mean for fish or water (pp. 12–13, 22).
- Game 5 (a herder game with a self-financed contract) models users who write and fund their own binding contract. They may rely on local information and monitor each other, and such arrangements may be mistaken for having no agreement (pp. 15–18).
- Alanya's inshore fishery (lots, rotating sites, self-monitoring) is neither private property nor central regulation, shows no sign of overcapitalization, and its design relied on the fishers' local knowledge (pp. 18–20).
- Institutional details matter. A rule set used in one physical environment may have very different consequences in another (pp. 22–23).
- Method and scope: study small-scale renewable common-pool resources (CPRs; 50 to 15,000 users) as a biologist studies a simple model organism, to build a theory of self-organization (pp. 24–28).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 1–2)
- A 1989 *New York Times* story: catches of cod, flounder and haddock on the Georges Bank are about a quarter of 1960s levels. Everyone agrees the cause is overfishing, but not on the remedy. Congress members want new national law, and fishers' representatives blame past federal regulation (p. 1).
- Advocates of central regulation, privatization, and regulation by those involved press their prescriptions in many arenas. Scholars are no more agreed (p. 1).
- Ostrom's observation: neither state nor market is uniformly successful. Some communities have used institutions resembling neither to govern resources fairly successfully over long periods (p. 1).
- Aims of the book: (1) critique the foundations of policy analysis for natural resources; (2) present empirical cases of successful and unsuccessful governance; (3) begin building better tools for understanding self-governing institutions (p. 2).

### Three influential models (pp. 2–7)
**The tragedy of the commons (pp. 2–3).**
- Hardin (1968) imagines a pasture "open to all." Each herder gets the direct benefit of his own animals but bears only a share of the delayed costs of overgrazing. So each adds animals. Hardin: each man "is locked into a system that compels him to increase his herd without limit" (p. 2).
- Ostrom lists predecessors: Aristotle, Hobbes's state of nature, Lloyd, and Gordon (1954) on fisheries (no assurance that fish left behind today will be there tomorrow) (pp. 2–3). Dales (1968) is noted too.
- Standard resource economics concludes that with open access to a common-pool resource (CPR; a shared resource, here one from which at least some potential beneficiaries cannot be excluded, p. 22), total withdrawal exceeds the economically optimal level (p. 3).
- The label has been applied to many problems (Sahel famine, firewood crises, acid rain, congressional overspending, urban crime, international cooperation, Cyprus), and Hardin himself used it for overpopulation (p. 3).

**The prisoner's dilemma (PD) (pp. 3–5).**
- Hardin's model is often formalized as a PD (p. 3). Let $L$ be the maximum number of animals the meadow can feed well in a season. "Cooperate" = graze $L/2$. "Defect" = graze as many as is profitable, assumed more than $L/2$ (p. 4).
- **Game 1 (Hardin herder game)** payoffs, (row, column), as read from the text and Fig. 1.1 (p. 4):

| | Cooperate | Defect |
|---|---|---|
| **Cooperate** | (10, 10) | (−1, 11) |
| **Defect** | (11, −1) | (0, 0) |

- It is a noncooperative game: communication is forbidden, impossible or irrelevant unless modelled, and agreements are non-binding unless binding agreements are built into the game. "Complete information" means all players know the structure and payoffs (p. 4).
- Defect is a dominant strategy (best whatever the other does). Mutual defection is the equilibrium and is the third-best result for both. It is not Pareto-optimal. A Pareto-optimal outcome has no alternative strictly preferred by at least one player and at least as good for the others. Both players prefer (C, C) to (D, D), so the equilibrium is Pareto-inferior (p. 5).
- The paradox that individually rational strategies lead to collectively irrational outcomes explains the attention: Campbell says it casts doubt on our understanding of rationality. More than 2,000 papers had been written on it at one count (p. 5).

**The logic of collective action (pp. 5–6).**
- Olson (1965) challenged group theory, which assumed that people with common interests act voluntarily to further them. His most-quoted claim: unless the group is small, or there is coercion or some other special device, rational self-interested individuals will not act to achieve common interests (p. 6).
- Olson's argument rests largely on the premise that someone who cannot be excluded from a collective good once produced has little incentive to contribute. Ostrom says the book is less pessimistic than many who cite the passage assert. Olson treats intermediate-size groups as an open question, defined by how noticeable each person's actions are, not by head-count (p. 6).
- Common heart of all three models: the free-rider problem. If everyone free-rides, no collective benefit is produced. If some provide and others free-ride, provision is below optimal (p. 6).
- The models are powerful because they capture real aspects of many problems. They are dangerous as foundations for policy because constraints assumed fixed for analysis get taken as fixed in the field "unless external authorities change them." The prisoners cannot change the district attorney's constraints. Not all resource users are so incapable. Ostrom wants to ask how users' capabilities to change the rules of the game can be enhanced (pp. 6–7).

> *Intuition note.* The three models say one thing: if you cannot keep non-payers out, each person gains by letting others pay. They are good at showing how this can happen. The author's complaint is about treating the trap as unchangeable by the people in it.

### The metaphorical use of models (pp. 7–8)
- These models are variants of a still-evolving theory of collective action. Early sweeping conclusions have given way to qualified knowledge. Recent work on dynamic aspects (Axelrod; Kreps and Wilson) has begun to give more optimistic predictions. The open puzzles are taken up in Chapter 2 (p. 7).
- Much CPR writing, however, accepts the earlier models and a "remorseless tragedy" uncritically. A metaphor points to similarity in one or two variables. These models have been used to suggest further similarities are present, evoking helpless individuals destroying their own resource (pp. 7–8).
- Examples of this usage:
  - **The Economist (1988):** says fisheries can be managed successfully only if it is recognized that managers must have "effective hegemony" over fishers.
  - **Canadian fisheries minister LeBlanc (1980):** unrestrained self-interest in fisheries leads people to destroy their neighbours and themselves. Ostrom says the implication, that Canadian fisheries universally fit this description, is empirically incorrect (p. 8).

### Current policy prescriptions (pp. 8–14)
**Leviathan as the "only" way (pp. 8–12).**
- **Ophuls (1973):** environmental problems cannot be solved through cooperation, and even if the tragedy is avoided, it will "only" be through Leviathan (pp. 8–9).
- **Hardin (1978):** sees only "private enterprise" or "socialism" as alternatives and says change needs "whatever force may be required." Heilbroner suggests "iron governments"; Ehrenfeld and Carruthers and Stoner call for external or public control. Such advice has been widely followed, especially in the Third World (p. 9).
- **Game 2.** Suppose a central authority imposes a penalty of 2 on anyone it considers to defect. It is assumed to know $L$ and to detect and penalize every defector. Payoffs (p. 10, Fig. 1.2):

| | C | D |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | (10, 10) | (−1, 9) |
| **D** | (9, −1) | (−2, −2) |

  The solution is (C, C), with 10 each rather than 0 as in Game 1. Ostrom's conditional: if the authority accurately determines capacity, unambiguously assigns it, monitors actions and unfailingly sanctions, then it can generate an optimally efficient equilibrium. The cost of creating and running the agency is treated as exogenous and left out of the game (p. 10).
- **Game 3.** Now the agency knows $L$ but only incompletely observes herders' actions. It punishes defection with probability $y$ (correct) and fails to with $1-y$. It punishes cooperation with probability $x$ (erroneous) and correctly does not with $1-x$. Payoffs (p. 11, Fig. 1.3):

| | C | D |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | $(10-2x,\,10-2x)$ | $(-1-2x,\,11-2y)$ |
| **D** | $(11-2y,\,-1-2x)$ | $(-2y,\,-2y)$ |

  Game 2 is the special case $x=0,\ y=1$ (p. 11).
- **Game 4.** Take $x=0.3,\ y=0.7$ (both kinds of sanction correct with probability 0.7). Payoffs (p. 12, Fig. 1.4):

| | C | D |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | (9.4, 9.4) | (−1.6, 9.6) |
| **D** | (9.6, −1.6) | (−1.4, −1.4) |

  This is again a PD. Herders defect. Ostrom reports the equilibrium as lower than the unregulated Game 1 outcome (0, 0). (The p. 11 text prints it as (−1.6, −1.6), but the figure and the Game 3 formula give −1.4; see Reading notes.) Given Game 1's capacity and profits, the agency must impose sanctions correctly with probability greater than 0.75 to avoid pushing herders to (D, D) (pp. 11–12).

> *Intuition note.* A central regulator's neat solution rests on strong assumptions: accurate information, reliable sanctions, free administration. In Ostrom's example, where it gets sanctions right only 70% of the time, defecting again pays more than cooperating, and the herders end up worse off than with no regulator.

**Privatization as the "only" way (pp. 12–13).**
- **Demsetz, Johnson, Smith, Sinn, Welch.** Smith: "the only way" to avoid the tragedy is to end common property by creating private rights. Welch: full property rights are "necessary" to avoid overgrazing inefficiency. Welch asserted that privatization was the optimal solution for all common-pool problems; his major concern was how to impose ownership on unwilling users (p. 12).
- In the herder case, the meadow is split in half; each herder then plays a *game against nature* (no other player on his plot), but must pay for fences, monitoring and sanctioning to keep the division. Each is presumed to graze $X/2$ animals; the page prints $X/2$ without defining $X$ (probably the $L$ of the earlier games; see Reading notes) (pp. 12–13).
- This assumes the meadow's fodder is homogeneous over time. If rain falls erratically, one herder may earn nothing while the other prospers. Dividing the commons may impoverish both and cause overgrazing where forage is temporarily short. Herders could trade fodder or insure each other, but setup costs would be substantial, and are not needed while they share a larger area (p. 13).
- It is unclear what private rights mean for nonstationary or fugitive resources (water, fisheries). Clark: the tragedy is hard to counteract in marine fisheries "where the establishment of individual property rights is virtually out of the question." Even with unitized, quantified, salable rights, the resource system is likely still owned in common (p. 13).

**The "only" way? (pp. 13–15).**
- Both camps claim some $X$ is necessary and sufficient. Both accept that institutional change must come from outside and be imposed. Yet their $X$'s contradict, so both cannot be right. Ostrom does not argue for either position; she argues that both are too sweeping (p. 14).
- Her position: many solutions exist for many problems. "Getting the institutions right" is difficult, time-consuming and conflict-involving. It needs reliable time-and-place information and a broad repertoire of culturally acceptable rules. The capacity of users to escape dilemmas varies by situation (p. 14).
- Institutions are rarely purely private or public. Many successful CPR institutions mix "private-like" and "public-like" features. "Successful" means enabling productive outcomes where temptations to free-ride and shirk are ever present (pp. 14–15).
- A competitive market, the epitome of private institutions, is itself a public good: individuals can enter whether or not they paid for it. No market lasts long without underlying public institutions (p. 15).

**An alternative solution: Game 5 (pp. 15–18).**
- Herders can themselves make a binding contract. In noncooperative theory, "binding" means unfailingly enforced by an external actor, just as the penalty in Game 2 was assumed to be. Ostrom adds a parameter $e$ (cost of enforcing the agreement) and a negotiation stage. A contract is enforceable only if unanimous, so any proposal not splitting capacity and enforcement cost equally would be vetoed (pp. 15–16, Fig. 1.5).
- Equilibrium: both share the sustainable yield and enforcement costs equally, as long as each herder's share of the cost is less than 10. The payoff in the figure is $10-\tfrac{e}{2}$ each (pp. 15–16).
- Players can always guarantee no worse than the (D, D) outcome of Game 1. They do not depend on a distant official's information. If the enforcer charges too much, neither herder agrees (p. 16).
- Ostrom presents this as merely one way, not the only way, but one almost totally ignored by both policy-analysis and formal-theory literature. She asks whether herders might hire a private agent as enforcer and says this is not as farfetched as it seems: many long-term business exchanges have PD structure, and a frequent practice is private arbitration rather than a civil court; athletic leagues typically employ private monitors (pp. 16–17).
- An arbitrator helps resolve disputes within rules the parties chose. With several arbitrators, the payoff-dominant equilibrium is to pick the one with the lowest $e$ (p. 17).
- Key difference from Games 2–3: participants design their own contract using the information they have. Herders who use the same meadow year after year have detailed, relatively accurate information. They have an incentive to report infractions. Arbitrators may not need to hire monitors, whereas a regulatory agency always needs to hire its own, which creates a principal–agent problem. Ostrom *believes* Game-3-like situations, where incomplete information produces sanctioning errors, occur more often than policy literature presumes (p. 17).
- Analysts may mistake a "Game 5" for a "Game 1". If enforcement is not by an external government agency, some presume there is none, and label such arrangements "informal" (p. 18).
- Enforcement in real CPRs varies: government agencies, hired community monitors, or users as their own monitors (p. 18).
- Game 5 is "no panacea." Herders may misjudge capacity, monitoring may break down, and the external enforcer may fail ex post. The simple games are meant to challenge a mind-set, not to describe real institutions (p. 18).

> *Intuition note.* In Game 5 the same people who face the dilemma write the rule and pay for enforcement. Because they know the meadow and watch each other, they may need less outside information than a regulator would. This is one possible way out, not the only one.

**An empirical alternative: Alanya, Turkey (pp. 18–21).**
- Source: Berkes (1986b). Of about 100 local inshore fishers, many work in two- or three-person boats. Half belong to a producers' cooperative. In the early 1970s, unrestrained use led to hostility and sometimes violence. Competition for better spots raised costs and uncertainty (p. 19).
- Rules developed by trial and error over more than a decade (p. 19):
  - Each September, a list of eligible fishers (all licensed fishers, co-op members or not) is prepared.
  - All usable sites are named and listed. They are spaced so a net at one site does not block fish for adjacent sites.
  - Sites and assignments run September–May.
  - In September, fishers draw lots for the named sites.
  - September–January, each day each fisher moves east to the next location. After January, they move west. This gives equal chances at stocks migrating east to west and back.
- Effects: spacing optimizes production at each site, all boats get equal chances at the best spots, no resources are wasted searching or fighting over a site, and no overcapitalization is apparent (p. 19).
- Monitoring is a by-product of the rotation. A fisher assigned a good spot will certainly use it. Anyone cheating by going to a good spot on a poor day will be seen by the fishers entitled to it, who will defend their rights. Others support them because their own turn will come. Few infractions occurred and were handled at the local coffeehouse (pp. 19–20).
- Not private property, but rights and duties are well defined. Not centralized, but national legislation giving cooperatives jurisdiction over "local arrangements" helped officials legitimize their role. Local officials accept the signed agreement each year. Monitoring and enforcement are left to fishers (p. 20).
- Central officials could not have crafted the rules without a full-time staff fishing the area for an extended period. Mapping sites needed fishers' time-and-place information and a decade of experiment (p. 20).
- Alanya is one case of many documented CPR arrangements (irrigation systems, communal forests, inshore fisheries, grazing and hunting territories). Chapters 3–5 give more (pp. 20–21).
- Game 5 and the empirical cases offer theoretical and empirical alternatives to the assertion that users cannot extricate themselves. Ostrom: the key to her argument is that some individuals have broken out of the commons-dilemma trap while others remain trapped (p. 21).
- Key question: why have some groups escaped the trap and others not? Possible internal factors: no capacity to communicate, no way to build trust, no sense of shared future, or powerful members who gain from the status quo and block change. Possible external factors: authorities who deny autonomy or are indifferent to or gain from the dilemma; rapid external change leaving no time to adjust; perverse incentives created by central policy. As long as analysts presume users cannot change things themselves, they do not ask which variables help or hinder them (p. 21).

**Policy prescriptions as metaphors (pp. 21–23).**
- **Centrists** presume unified authorities act as in textbooks: valid science, adequate information, error-free implementation, routine monitoring and sanctioning (p. 22).
- **Privatizers** presume dividing rights yields efficient use. The empirical evidence (De Alessi 1980) that private firms in electricity, transport and medical services tend to be more efficient than public ones concerns different goods. Whether the same holds where some beneficiaries cannot be excluded is, Ostrom says, a different question. Privatizing a CPR need not give the results of privatizing an airline. Privatizing may even mean granting the exclusive harvest right to one person or firm (p. 22).
- Both camps often advocate oversimplified, nearly "institution-free" institutions. An assertion that central regulation is necessary says nothing about how the agency is constituted, limited, informed, staffed or monitored. An assertion that private rights are necessary says nothing about how rights are defined, measured, paid for or adjudicated (p. 22).
- Ostrom says systematic studies by scholars of the "new institutionalism" teach that institutional details matter. Whether equilibria exist and whether they are improvements depends on the institutions' structure. Any institutional arrangement can be viewed as a game in extensive form (options, sequencing, information, rewards and punishments), and the physical environment also shapes the game. So a rule set may have very different consequences in a different environment (pp. 22–23).

**Policies based on metaphors can be harmful (p. 23).**
- Nationalizing forests in Third World countries was advocated on the ground that villagers cannot manage forests sustainably. Where villages had owned and regulated forests for generations, nationalization meant expropriation. Agencies issued regulations but could not hire enough foresters, and low-paid foresters took bribes. The result: nationalization created *open-access* resources where limited-access common property had existed (p. 23).
- Documented for Thailand, Niger, Nepal and India. Similar problems arose with inshore fisheries where national agencies assumed exclusive jurisdiction over coastal waters (p. 23).

### A challenge (pp. 23–28)
- **Theory vs. model.** Theory searches for regularities by abstraction. Models add further simplification, fixing many parameters. Analysts may get trapped in their own intellectual web and apply a powerful theory everywhere. Ostrom says a theory's power is "exactly proportional" to the diversity of situations it can explain, but every theory has limits. Confusing a model (e.g. a perfectly competitive market) with the theory it represents can limit applicability further (p. 24).
- Scientific knowledge includes knowing a theory's limits. Illustration: the conviction that all physical structures could be described by perfect forms (circles, squares, triangles) limited astronomy until Kepler found Mars's orbit was elliptical (p. 24). Godwin and Shepard (1979) warned that policy scientists were "forcing squares, triangles and ellipses into a circular paradigm" with the commons-dilemma model. Many analysts still presume common-pool problems are all dilemmas that participants cannot escape (p. 24).
- **The missing tool.** Policy analysis lacks an adequate theory of voluntary self-organization by principals who keep the residuals of their own efforts. Examples: law firms, cooperatives, and the CPRs of Chapter 3 (p. 25).
- Until such a theory is developed and accepted, major policy decisions will continue to presume that individuals cannot organize themselves and need external authorities. Without an adequate theory, one cannot predict or explain when self-organization alone will fail, nor begin to ascertain which interventions might help. For example, a theory that omits self-organization will not see the value of a court system for monitoring and enforcing self-negotiated contracts (p. 25).
- **Method.** Combine the "new institutionalism" with a biologist's strategy. Assumption: individuals try to solve problems as well as they can, with similar limited capabilities, and not "incompetent, evil, or irrational" versus omniscient. Where the problems she observes involve unpredictability, poor information, mistrust, complexity and transaction difficulties, her explanations must take these into account (pp. 25–26).
- Biologists often study the simplest organism in which a process shows in clarified or exaggerated form, chosen because the process can be studied more effectively in it, not because it is representative. Ostrom's "organism" is the **CPR situation** (defined in Chapter 2) (p. 26).
- **Scope.** Small-scale CPRs inside one country; 50 to 15,000 users heavily dependent on the CPR for economic return. These are mainly inshore fisheries, small grazing areas, groundwater basins, irrigation systems and communal forests. Small scale makes it more likely that study can identify underlying similarities and processes. Strong dependence means users are strongly motivated to try to solve problems. If self-organization fails, it is not because the benefits were unimportant (p. 26).
- **Limits.** (1) renewable not nonrenewable resources; (2) substantial scarcity, not abundance; (3) users can harm one another substantially but cannot produce major external harm for others. This excludes asymmetrical pollution and cartels able to affect market price (p. 26).
- **Cases.** Not a random sample. They were chosen as clear on (1) governing long-enduring CPRs, (2) transforming institutions, and (3) failing to overcome problems (pp. 26–27).
- From them Ostrom develops "reasoned conjectures", seeks the underlying design principles of institutions of successful long-term managers, and compares successes with failures to find internal and external factors (p. 27).
- Relevance: she cites an estimate that 90% of the world's fishermen and over half the fish consumed come from small-scale inshore fisheries (Panayotou). She expects lessons to extend somewhat to local public goods. Any effort at collective action must handle free-riding, commitment, supplying new institutions and monitoring compliance (pp. 27–28).
- **Plan of the book.** Ch. 2 defines a CPR situation and individual choice and asks questions any theory of collective action must answer. Chs. 3, 4, 5 are empirical (long-enduring institutions; origin and development; failures and fragilities). Ch. 6 draws the conjectures together for institutional design (p. 28).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Use Game 1 payoffs ($C,C=10,10$; $C,D=-1,11$; $D,D=0,0$) and the penalty of 2 from Games 3–4.

**Imperfect regulator.** Expected payoffs: (C,C) $10-2x$; cooperator against defector $-1-2x$; defector against cooperator $11-2y$; (D,D) $-2y$.
- *Accurate agency:* $x=0.2,\ y=0.9$. (C,C) $=10-0.4=9.6$. Cooperator vs. defector $=-1-0.4=-1.4$. Defector vs. cooperator $=11-1.8=9.2$. (D,D) $=-1.8$. Against a cooperator, C ($9.6$) beats D ($9.2$). Against a defector, C ($-1.4$) beats D ($-1.8$). So (C, C) is the equilibrium.
- *Sloppy agency:* $x=0.4,\ y=0.6$. (C,C) $=9.2$. Defector vs. cooperator $=11-1.2=9.8$. Cooperator vs. defector $=-1-0.8=-1.8$. (D,D) $=-1.2$. Against a cooperator, D ($9.8$) beats C ($9.2$). Against a defector, D ($-1.2$) beats C ($-1.8$). Defection is dominant, and each gets $-1.2<0$, worse than Game 1.
- *(added step)* Defect beats cooperate against a cooperator iff $11-2y>10-2x$, i.e. $y-x<\tfrac12$. The same inequality arises against a defector. With $x=1-y$ this is $y<0.75$, which matches Ostrom's 0.75 threshold (p. 11). Here $y-x=0.7$ in the first case (above the cutoff) and $0.2$ in the second (below).

**Self-financed contract (Game 5 logic).** Per the figure, each herder gets $10-e/2$ if they agree. Fallback is (D, D) $=0$.
- If $e=8$: share $=4$, payoff $=6>0$. Both agree.
- If $e=24$: share $=12$, payoff $=-2<0$. Neither agrees. The break-even is a share of 10, as in the text (p. 16).

## Concepts
- [[tragedy-of-the-commons]] (new) — Hardin's model: users of a shared resource each gain fully from adding use but bear only part of the cost, so overuse follows (p. 2)
- [[prisoners-dilemma]] (new) — game in which each player's dominant strategy (defect) yields a Pareto-inferior equilibrium; used to formalize the tragedy (pp. 3–5)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — Olson's "logic": a group benefit may not be produced voluntarily if non-contributors cannot be excluded (p. 6)
- [[free-rider]] — one who enjoys benefits others provide without contributing. The shared core of the three models (p. 6)
- [[common-pool-resource]] — a resource used by many; Ostrom's concern is CPRs where at least some potential beneficiaries cannot be excluded (p. 22), and her focus is small-scale renewable CPRs (pp. 3, 22, 26)
- [[public-good]] — Ostrom notes a competitive market is itself a public good, since one can enter without paying for it (p. 15)
- [[pareto-optimality]] (new) — no outcome strictly preferred by someone and at least as good for all others; the dilemma's equilibrium lacks it (p. 5)
- [[noncooperative-game]] (new) — game where agreements are non-binding unless binding contracts are modelled into the game (p. 4)
- [[metaphorical-use-of-models]] (new) — invoking a model's image and assuming further similarity with a field setting without checking variables (pp. 7–8)
- [[leviathan-prescription]] (new) — the claim that central external coercive control is necessary for CPRs (pp. 8–9)
- [[privatization-prescription]] (new) — the claim that private property rights are necessary (p. 12)
- [[self-financed-contract-enforcement]] (new) — Game 5: users agree on rules, pay an enforcer, and veto unequal terms (pp. 15–16)
- [[principal-agent-problem]] — a regulatory agency must ensure its own hired monitors do their job (p. 17)
- [[institutions]] — institutional arrangements viewed as games in extensive form; details of options, sequence, information and payoffs change outcomes (p. 23)
- [[design-principles-ostrom]] — flagged as a goal: underlying features of institutions of long-lasting successful CPRs. Not stated in this chapter (p. 27)

## Questions this raises
- If the 0.75 threshold for sanction accuracy depends on payoffs, how would it change when the penalty or the gain from defecting differs?
- Game 5 assumes an enforcer who unfailingly enforces what the herders agree. What would make a private or community enforcer credible?
- What do the internal and external factors listed on p. 21 predict about which groups succeed?
- Do results from small-scale CPRs (50–15,000 users) carry over to large or international commons, or to local public goods?
