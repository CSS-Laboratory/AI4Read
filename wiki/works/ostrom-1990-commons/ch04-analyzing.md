# Analyzing Institutional Change

## L0 — One line
Pumpers in Los Angeles-area groundwater basins escaped a race to pump the most water by building new rules step by step, and Ostrom suggests that supplying institutions can be analysed as incremental changes to the rules already in force, rather than as a separate one-step leap.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter 3's long-lived commons have lost their origins. This chapter asks how users create new rules when creating rules is itself a collective dilemma. Ostrom traces three Los Angeles groundwater basins (Raymond, West, Central). Pumpers formed associations, shared information, and used litigation to negotiate proportional cutbacks ("mutual prescription": each pumper's open use counted as a claim against all others). They then created a replenishment district and a polycentric (many-centre) set of public enterprises. Costs stayed substantially below replacing the basins. Ostrom concludes that institutional change in these basins was incremental and sequential, that a facilitative (state-supported) political regime lowers its costs, and that origins and reform can be treated by one theory: change in at least one status quo rule.

**Prerequisites:** dominant strategy and prisoner's-dilemma logic; externalities; the idea of rules versus outcomes; Chapters 2–3 (second-order dilemma, design principles).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Chapter 3's cases show that robust self-governance is feasible (though not that it is likely), but their origins are lost in time. Creating institutions is a second-order collective dilemma (Bates; the problem of supplying the rules that would solve a first-order dilemma), so a study of origins must face this supply problem (p. 103).
- Before reform, California's overlapping water doctrines, uncertainty about supply and use, and the "rule of capture" (water belongs to whoever pumps it) produced a pumping race, with a pumping-cost externality (one pumper's withdrawals raise others' costs) and a strategic externality (water left unpumped may be taken by rivals) (pp. 106–110).
- In the Raymond Basin, litigation changed the "default condition" (the outcome if the parties fail to agree). Parties agreed to "mutual prescription" (each pumper's open use counted as a claim against all others), sharing the cutback proportionately instead of fighting over seniority (pp. 112–114).
- West and Central Basin negotiators built on this model. Central Basin was faster and cheaper than West (about $450,000 versus about $3 million) (pp. 122–124).
- Compliance has been high with little formal sanctioning. A neutral, public watermaster (a court-appointed monitor) makes extractions close to common knowledge, so each pumper can see what the others do (pp. 125–126).
- Pumpers then built a replenishment district (a public body that finances water to refill the basins) and a polycentric (many-centre) system of public enterprises. Ostrom says it is neither centrally owned nor centrally regulated and that it incorporates her design principles (pp. 132–136).
- The explanation: change came in many small steps with low initial costs, one after another, in a facilitative regime (California, a home-rule state whose facilities reduce local costs) (pp. 137–139).
- Theory: define rules by three deontic operators (forbid, require, permit), so any recurring situation can be described by status quo rules (rules in force until changed). Origins and changes can then be analysed with one theory (pp. 139–142).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 103–104)
- Chapter 3's institutions survived long under uncertainty. They show that robust self-governance is feasible, though not that it is likely. Their origins are lost, so we do not know who proposed what or how change happened (p. 103).
- Studying origins must face the supply problem of Chapter 2. **Bates's point:** a new institution's collective benefits make its creation a second-order collective dilemma, "subject to the very incentive problems it is supposed to resolve" (p. 103).
- Questions to ask: group size, structure, who initiated, who paid, what information, what risks, which broader institutions were used. Case studies of ongoing institutions rarely answer these. Once rules are in place, the incentives facing appropriators are entirely different from those facing the earlier ones (pp. 103–104).
- Case choice: Ostrom and Weschler did fieldwork in the late 1950s and early 1960s (meetings, memoranda, interviews). Blomquist later extended and updated the information. Enough time has passed to judge stability and efficiency. Three basins that used negotiated settlements are examined: Raymond, West, Central (p. 104).

### The competitive pumping race

**The setting (pp. 104–106).** Basins are former streambeds, now underground reservoirs refilled by rain (p. 104).
- *Cheap supply.* In 1985 imported water cost $240 per acre-foot wholesale. Pumping averaged about $134. For the 282,458 acre-feet pumped from the three basins in 1985, replacement would have cost at least $28 million more per year (pp. 104–106).
- *Storage, valued even more.* A water tower costs about $57,500 per acre-foot of capacity. Engineering standards recommend short-term storage of 16% of use. West Basin's annual demand is 327,435 acre-feet, so 52,400 acre-feet of storage would be needed. Replacement would cost about $3.01 billion (p. 106).
- *Destruction.* Pumping more than the **safe yield** (average replenishment) compacts the strata. Drawing coastal basins below sea level lets in saltwater. Overextraction threatened all basins until the affected users changed institutions (p. 106).

**The logic of the water-rights game (pp. 106–110).**
- *Rights.* Overlying landowners held riparian-type rights. *Katz v. Walkinshaw* (1903) made them "correlative": in shortage, each takes a proportionate share. *San Bernardino v. Riverside* (1921) limited them to beneficial use (p. 107).
- *Appropriators* pump water for use on land they do not own (most water companies). They may take "surplus" water. Among them, seniority ("first in time") decides priority. The most senior are still potentially subordinate to overlying owners (pp. 107–108).
- *Prescription.* An appropriator taking nonsurplus water openly and continuously for more than five years could perfect prescriptive rights, which are superior to the rights of overlying owners and appropriators. The same acts could instead leave the appropriator junior to overlying owners. The outcome depended on whether the court found a surplus in the prior five years. Nobody knew safe yield or others' pumping (p. 108).
- *Open-access CPR with two pressures.* (1) A pumping-cost externality: each withdrawal lowers the water level and raises others' lifting costs. (2) A strategic externality (Negri): under the "rule of capture", water not pumped today may be pumped by rivals, so the incentive to forgo pumping now is undermined. The two reinforce each other. Without institutional change, independent pumpers will severely overexploit (pp. 108–109).
- *Timing risk for overlying owners (Blomquist):* sue too early and the court finds surplus, so the owner pays costs for no remedy. Sue too late and the appropriator may have prescribed (p. 109).
- *Information.* A watermaster could be appointed in litigation to survey supply and use, with costs shared. In past cases, signs such as falling water tables had not been accepted by the court as enough evidence of a shortage to declare that no surplus existed (pp. 109–110).
- *Result.* Attorneys advised producers to pump as much as needed and defend later (Krieger 1955). A pumping race occurred in every basin (p. 110).
- *Outcome.* By the 1960s the race was halted in all coastal basins. Water rights were established everywhere except Orange County, which uses a pump tax. Special districts, pump taxes, replenishment and injection wells followed. Public arenas were used, but solutions were not imposed by outside authorities: participants used public arenas to impose constraints on themselves (p. 110).

*Intuition note:* Each pumper gains by pumping now and shares the harm with everyone. Legal uncertainty led attorneys to advise producers to pump as much as needed and defend later. So, in Ostrom's account, the race was the logical outcome of how the rights were defined (pp. 108–110).

### The litigation game

**Raymond Basin (pp. 111–114).** Small basin (40 square miles), inland. Its safe yield had been exceeded steadily since 1913. Pasadena produced as much as the other 30 producers combined (p. 111).
- *Olson's model:* if the producers had been a "privileged group", Pasadena would have borne all the costs of stopping the race. Pasadena approached, but did not reach, the position of a dominant actor in such a group. Ostrom says the prediction is consistent with some of Pasadena's actions, not all. From 1914 to 1923 Pasadena replenished the basin with floodwater. In the late 1920s it helped found the Metropolitan Water District (p. 111).
- In the 1930s Pasadena stopped making independent contributions that benefited non-contributors. It failed to negotiate a voluntary cutback and sued Alhambra and 30 others in 1937 (p. 111).
- *Referee.* The state referee's draft report (March 1943, about $53,000) found withdrawals of 29,400 acre-feet, a safe yield of 21,900, and an annual overdraft of 8,500 acre-feet per year as printed (pp. 111–112; see Reading notes). He recommended cutting to the safe yield. The parties now shared one authoritative "image" of the problem (p. 112).
- *Default condition.* Without agreement, the judge would decide who bears the cutback, which was unpredictable. The judge might, for example, give overlying owners priority and treat the rest as "surplus" for appropriators by seniority, or might find no surplus, in which case senior appropriators might get prescriptive rights and overlying owners would bear most of the cutback (p. 112).
- *Figure 4.2 (simplified).* If overlying owners pump 12,000 acre-feet and appropriators 18,000 (total 30,000), everyone accepts that a cutback to 22,000 will occur. Worst case for overlying owners (point B): appropriators keep 18,000, overlying owners get 4,000. Worst case for appropriators (point A): overlying owners get 12,000, appropriators get 10,000. Line AB spans the range, and a full trial would be long and costly (p. 112).
- *Settlement.* After six months of negotiation, 30 of 32 parties signed a stipulation. One attorney, Kenneth Wright, represented 16 parties. A holdout later joined. The California-Michigan Land and Water Company never signed and challenged the judgment (p. 113).
- *Mutual prescription.* Signatories said the safe yield had long been exceeded and that each producer's open, continuous, notorious withdrawal was adverse to all others. So each had prescribed against all the others. They agreed to **share the cutback proportionately**, shown as point D, instead of litigating who ranked first. Figure 4.2 labels D at 8,760 (overlying) and 13,140 (appropriators). *(added step: these equal 0.73 × 12,000 and 0.73 × 18,000, and 8,760 + 13,140 = 21,900, the referee's safe yield.)* They guaranteed proportional shares if the safe yield changed, and created an exchange pool for annual leasing of rights (p. 113).
- *Judgment (23 Dec 1944).* The judge adopted the agreement: all decreed rights have equal standing, pumping beyond them is enjoined, and the state's Division of Water Resources acts as watermaster. Rights can be leased or sold if recorded. The parties pay two-thirds of watermaster costs and the state pays the rest. The California Supreme Court upheld the case and the U.S. Supreme Court declined review (p. 114).
- *Results.* The race ended faster and more cheaply than a trial would have achieved. Rights are firm and marketable, and most small right-holders sold to water companies. There are now 17 active producers, almost all municipal or private water companies; only three overlying landowners still produce water from the basin (p. 114).

*Intuition note:* The court's threatened ruling made "no agreement" a risky gamble: the outcome was unclear and a full trial would be long and costly. Treating all current pumping as equally prescriptive removed the fight over seniority and let each party share the loss in proportion (pp. 112–113).

**West Basin (pp. 114–122).** 170 square miles, on the coast. Compared with Raymond it had (1) about 500 named parties, (2) no dominant producer, (3) unequal saltwater risk (coastal wells fail first). Offsets: 19 producers accounted for about 85% of withdrawals, and Raymond had already supplied a formula seen as fair (pp. 114–115).
- *Information first.* Overdraft came a decade later than in Raymond. Wartime industry made it worse. In the early 1940s coastal wells grew saltier, and many producers believed this was only a "local" problem. In 1943 nine coastal municipalities agreed they needed a common image of the basin and signed a cooperative agreement with the U.S. Geological Survey and the Flood Control District. Their 1944 report was grim: no natural barrier to the sea, and the whole basin was threatened (p. 115).
- *Association.* In December 1944 an ad hoc committee recommended (1) a permanent association, (2) a survey of alternative water sources, (3) legal action like Raymond's. All three were followed. The West Basin Water Association gave an open forum, kept extensive open minutes, and sent a weekly newsletter (1946–1954) whose motto was "let there be no surprises, either pleasant or unpleasant" (p. 116). Engineer Conkling advised a municipal district to import water from MWD. Who would get the basin's storage rights remained open (p. 116).
- *Litigation (October 1945).* Three appropriators started it. Wright, who had been Pasadena's attorney, was the attorney for one of them and presented the mutual-prescription idea (p. 116).
- *Opponents.* Dominguez, the largest producer, opposed. Inglewood first opposed (its lawyers cited its senior status) but became active after the Raymond decision was upheld. Hawthorne, inland, believed it was protected and held out for years. Starting litigation again changed the default: the court could impose its own judgment (p. 117).
- *Referee (four years).* Overdraft since 1920, safe yield 30,000 acre-feet per year. Withdrawals reached 90,000 by 1952. Even supporters of mutual curtailment opposed a two-thirds reduction. Injection wells looked promising, but the default was a court-ordered two-thirds cutback (pp. 117–118).
- *Legal Settlement Committee* (six attorneys, five engineers, 11 members). Members still represented their firms but were accountable to the association and reported quarterly. The negotiated cutback had to lie between 90,000 and the referee's 30,000. If it was not above 30,000, some litigants would prefer to contest in court. Engineers found that 25–30% could be cut without serious harm, if an exchange pool existed (p. 118).
- *Base year.* Estimated "prescriptive rights" totalled 44,387 acre-feet using 1944 and 63,728 using 1949. A member argued that 1949 (63,000) was halfway to the Division's target, left water for peaks, and gave up no acquired rights. The committee proposed 1949 for an **interim** agreement (p. 119).
- *Contingent contract.* No signatory had to cut until producers holding at least 80% of "Prescriptive Rights, 1949" had signed and the court approved. This meant no one would be a "sucker" and the joint cuts would matter. By November 1954, 82.5% had signed. The referee became watermaster. Water levels rose, except under Hawthorne (p. 119).
- *Non-signers.* California Water Service (an initiator that bore much of the litigation cost) held its pumping to its allocation. Its refusal to sign shifted watermaster costs back onto those who gained more rights under the agreement, but it caused no physical harm to others. Hawthorne increased pumping, over 2,250 acre-feet above allocation by 1960, saving at least $100,000 (see Reading notes). Its trough sat 30–40 feet below surrounding levels, and costs fell on signatories (p. 120).
- *Hawthorne's view (Hawthorne officials, as reported):* municipal needs outrank industrial ones. It saw the agreement as favouring industry and said beach cities were giving up their rights (p. 120).
- *Final settlement.* The committee met weekly in 1957–58. Obstacles: tracing transactions, some demanding cuts to the safe yield, others waiting on barrier experiments, and fear of an appeal (pp. 120–121). Hawthorne's mayor was put on the Executive Committee, but this failed (p. 121). In February 1960 Dominguez, El Segundo and an oil company showed support by bringing signed copies. By summer 1961, 82% of adjudicated rights had signed (p. 121). Judgment came in August 1961 after a short trial, 16 years after the litigation started. Ninety-nine parties had adjudicated rights of 64,065 acre-feet. Hawthorne appealed and lost, and the case closed 18 years after it began (pp. 121–122).
- *Costs.* About $3 million (Blomquist), 10 times Raymond's but one-tenth of storage replacement. Annualised over 50 years: $0.50 per acre-foot in Raymond and $2.50 in West. Annual monitoring in 1985: about $3.00 (Raymond) and $2.40 (West) per acre-foot (p. 122).

**Central Basin (pp. 123–124).** 277 square miles, about 750 well owners. Overdraft began in 1942. West Basin shields it from the ocean on the west, with a small southern exposure (saltwater from 1950) (p. 123).
- Pushed by downstream neighbours, producers formed the Central Basin Water Association in 1950, shared a part-time director with West Basin, and wanted to avoid the court reference procedure and involving tiny producers (p. 123).
- They hired a private engineering firm for the initial survey and reached general agreement on the type of settlement before going to court in 1962. An interim agreement (79% of rights) was approved about 10 months after filing. It set a 20% proportional cutback and working rules modelled on West Basin. Watermaster service began October 1962 and a voluntary cutback of about 45,000 acre-feet followed (pp. 123–124).
- The final settlement (over 75% of rights) was approved in October 1965, effective October 1966, four years after initiation, at about $450,000 (p. 124).

**Comparison (pp. 124–125).** Pasadena withdrew about half of Raymond's water, had a large stake, and bore more than its share of litigation costs. It acted before pumpers got used to heavy overdraft, and the main asymmetry (overlying versus appropriator) was resolved by mutual prescription (p. 124). West Basin's three disadvantages made negotiation longer and costlier. By the end it had become difficult to cut back to the safe yield and still serve the growing urban population, so other ways to raise supply were needed (p. 124). Central Basin pumpers could wait and watch. They acted before a cut much larger than 20% was needed and used a private firm for information, saving time and money (p. 125).

**Conformance (pp. 125–126).** After 45, 35 and 27 years, and 107 combined years of water use, infractions have been "insignificant" despite the temptation (p. 125).
- Each watermaster has extensive monitoring and sanctioning authority, and its monitoring is public. Each party reports extractions yearly and receives a list of everyone's. Records are cross-checked and meters can be calibrated. Information thus approximates "common knowledge" (p. 125).
- The watermaster tries to be neutral. It reports infringements and leaves enforcement to right-holders (an official, 1960: avoiding being "an active party"). Moneta Water Company over-pumped for a couple of years. The watermaster devoted several pages of its annual report to the company, which then complied. Hawthorne now stays within its allocation. Apart from a few isolated incidents, the original litigants complied without formal sanctions. Legal action has been necessary, however, against new pumpers who pumped without first buying rights (pp. 125–126).
- Ostrom's account of why compliance is high: each pumper wants total extraction limited to keep storage and flow values. Noncompliance would be known by the end of the next water year, and continued noncompliance is likely to bring legal sanctions, loss of reputation and informal sanctions. The system is seen as fair. Right-holders pay two-thirds of costs and can petition for a new watermaster (p. 126).

*Intuition note:* Compliance is not mainly due to formal sanctions. Everyone sees everyone's pumping, rights-holders can sue, and users control the monitor. This is why a cheater is expected to be found (pp. 125–126).

### The entrepreneurship game (pp. 127–133)
After the West interim agreement, producers saw litigation was not enough for long-term regulation. Five years later they created a new public enterprise (p. 127). Unresolved: (1) cutbacks fell short of the safe yield, so replenishment must speed up or cuts must deepen; (2) saltwater intrusion, where injection wells (an experiment partly state-funded) proved technically and economically feasible, but who pays was open; (3) boundaries, since once West cut pumping while Central did not, water flowed east. The basins are interconnected and Central has a good recharge area (pp. 127–128).
- No existing agency had the right authority or boundaries. Producers feared losing control to large agencies. After a 1954 meeting of 45 agencies, a "Committee of Twelve" drafted legislation (p. 128).
- *Legislation (1955).* (1) Anyone producing at least 25 acre-feet a year files annual extraction notices with the state (helps later adjudications). (2) The Water Replenishment District Act. A district could be created after: signatures from 10% of voters, stated limits on taxing power, Department of Water Resources agreement that the area benefits, and a majority vote. It may use pump taxes and, to a limited extent, property taxes. Its organic law tells it to investigate contracting with existing agencies before building its own staff. Taking other areas' needs into account helped rapid passage (pp. 128–129).
- Ostrom treats the Act as a general "constitution" from which each district drafts a specific one (p. 129). Issues: water source for the barrier, boundaries, internal electoral divisions, taxing powers. Reclaimed Hyperion water proved infeasible short-term, so MWD water was needed (pp. 129–130).
- *One district or two?* West Basin's list: reasons for (same purpose, more resources, greater political strength, a Long Beach seawater-intrusion route (probably included in a larger district), probably more cross-fault flow, lower administration costs). Reasons against: injection would be unique and necessary to West, and Central would control that program if a large district were formed; pumping was cut only in West; ultimate cuts might differ; West would give up control of local tax rate and assessment; a local district could initiate proceedings to ensure financial replenishment from Central; and extensive Central recharge might contribute free water to West (pp. 130–131). West producers sat at the end of the groundwater "pipeline". The chairman of the West Basin committee wrote that, if a joint district were chosen, a morally binding policy statement should assure an effective barrier program, curtailment of pumping in Central, and board divisions that include territory of both basins (p. 131).
- Both associations judged the benefits larger and gave assurances (p. 131). The seven-page August 1958 proposal named three purposes (repel intrusion, recharge, reduce pumping to safe limits). The district could not buy water with ad valorem tax funds. A $6.00 per acre-foot levy was estimated to buy 165,000 acre-feet from MWD (the average annual overdraft), run by a five-member board with minimal staff (p. 132). Ostrom calls it a "constitution": it sets decision rules for future collective choices, and it was attached to the formal petitions (p. 132).
- The district was approved in November 1959 by a vote of 4 to 1 (p. 133).

### The polycentric public-enterprise game (pp. 133–136)
- The district has powers to tax, to sue and to provide collective goods. The private associations still advise on policy. About a half-dozen agencies take part, so a polycentric (many-centre) public-enterprise system emerged rather than one central authority. It restored water levels, completed the freshwater barrier, and is working on pumping troughs (p. 133).

Ostrom says total costs are substantially lower than destruction. They would be lower still had earlier settlement been possible, and Central did better by learning from the others (p. 133).
- *Supply links.* The district first depended on monopoly supplier MWD. When MWD unilaterally raised its price in 1966, the district and associations bargained hard but failed. The district then negotiated with the Los Angeles County Sanitation Districts for reclaimed water. In 1987 it sought to raise purchases from 30,000 to 50,000 acre-feet per year at about $8.00 per acre-foot, versus $153 from MWD (p. 134).
- *Operations.* An exchange agreement with the Flood Control District lets the district keep a skeletal staff (director and secretary). The county has no full monopoly, because the district can use other suppliers or build its own staff. It once insisted that part of a barrier design be contracted to a private firm (pp. 134–135). The watermaster serves both producers and the district, and the two share extraction records. The system runs by negotiation among many actors and arenas, and strict majority rule is rarely used (p. 135).
- Private associations stay active: officials report to them, engineers ask tough questions, and many elected officials were long active in them (p. 135).
- *Privatization?* Rights are separable from land and traded, but no one owns the basins themselves. A polycentric set of limited-purpose enterprises with private participation manages them, and Ostrom says the system is neither centrally owned nor centrally regulated (p. 136).
- *Design principles.* Ostrom says the arrangement incorporates the full set from Chapter 3: boundaries through litigation, congruence of appropriation and provision rules with local conditions, collective choice through associations and districts, court-appointed monitoring, mostly modest informal sanctions (formal available), conflict resolution by the court and association meetings, recognized rights to organize, and nested units. She says she believes, given their stability so far and their conformance with these principles, that these CPR institutions are robust and will survive for a long time (p. 136).

### The analysis of institutional supply (pp. 136–142)
**First-order versus second-order dilemma (pp. 136–137).** A pumping race is the first-order dilemma. Each pumper has a dominant strategy to pump what is privately profitable. The long overdraft supports this prediction. Theorists would then predict that pumpers would not invest in supplying institutions, since that is a second-order dilemma no easier than the first. The case studies do not support that prediction: pumpers formed associations, paid for litigation, drafted and passed legislation, created taxing districts, and spent endless hours informing themselves (p. 137).

**Incremental, sequential, self-transforming change in a facilitative regime (pp. 137–139).**
- Investment came in many small steps with low initial costs in home-rule California, where state facilities reduce the costs of local institutional supply. Participants rarely had to move simultaneously without knowing what others did. Early successes gave intermediate benefits before larger investments. Each change altered the incentives for the next (p. 137).
- Neighbouring basins learned from one another through overlapping participants and interbasin arenas (p. 138).
- Voluntary associations gave face-to-face forums. Discussion alone did not change pumping, but it led to litigation and enforceable limits. They also provided information to all pumpers at once. Sharing costs of this public good taught members that voluntary cooperation can work. Dues were modest and roughly proportional to withdrawals (p. 138).
- The state helps: courts where individuals may sue, a subsidy of one-third of litigation costs, technical help from the Department of Water Resources and the U.S. Geological Survey, and a home-rule tradition. Legislators seldom back organic laws that face strong opposition, but when affected parties have discussed proposals, such laws often pass with close to unanimous support (pp. 138–139).
- Ostrom suggests that a similar group in a different regime "might not be able" to supply itself with new microinstitutions. The line between central regulation and providing arenas and rules for microinstitutional change is often blurred (p. 139).

*Intuition note:* Small first steps (a forum, shared data) paid off quickly and cheaply. So participants could gather intermediate benefits and learn from one another before paying for larger steps, and state facilities helped reduce the costs (pp. 137–139).

**Reformulating the analysis (pp. 139–142).**
- *Rules.* Institutional rules are prescriptive statements that forbid, require or permit an action or outcome (the three **deontic operators**). Ostrom's starting assumption is that all recurring situations are shaped by a set of institutional rules. One of the three operators must be contained in a statement for it to be considered a rule (p. 139). Some analysts count only required or forbidden actions, which leaves some recurring situations not rule-governed. Including "permit" means a set of rules can always be identified (pp. 139–140). Ask two questions about any action or outcome: is it required, is it forbidden? Anything neither required nor forbidden is permitted. So the absence of a rule forbidding or requiring an action is logically equivalent to the presence of a rule permitting it (p. 140).
- *Hobbes.* The state of nature, with no required or forbidden actions, is logically equivalent to a situation where everything is permitted whatever the effect on others. A CPR with no restrictions is the same, and its governing rules are all **default rules**. Status quo rules continue until changed (p. 140).
- *Origin versus change.* The common view treats origin as a one-step move from no rules to rules (non-incremental, costly), and change as incremental and cheaper. Ostrom treats both as alterations of at least one status quo rule, so one theory covers both. A change in any rule affecting the set of participants, the strategies available, control over outcomes, information, or payoffs is an institutional change (p. 140).
- *Costs.* Costs vary across rules, regimes, levels and time. They do not depend only on whether the arrangement is new (p. 141). Creating voluntary associations cost little. Creating the district cost much. Changing water rights took years of litigation. Cheap early changes let participants collect benefits before costly ones. Each change became the foundation for the next (p. 141).
- *Dilemma or not.* A second-order dilemma viewed as one large step "may or may not" be a dilemma when viewed as sequential and incremental. The net payoffs of solving a small part of a large second- or third-order problem may be sufficiently high and spread such that some participants will voluntarily provide initial second-order benefits, though they are unwilling to provide first-order solutions on their own (p. 141).
- *Two levels.* Constitutional-choice processes decide whether to change status quo rules on who is eligible and how collective choices are made. Collective-choice processes do the same for operational choices. Both are structured by rules. The outcome of collective choice is frequently conceptualised as a "policy space", leaving unspecified what it contains. Ostrom says it can be thought of as a set of rules on who is required, forbidden or allowed to do what (pp. 141–142).
- *Explaining change* requires asking how participants weigh the net expected benefits and costs of the status quo rules against an altered set. Chapter 6 develops this. Chapter 5's failure cases are examined first (p. 142).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three pumpers share a basin with a safe yield of 70 acre-feet. Current pumping: A = 50, B = 30, C = 20, total 100.
1. *Proportional cut (mutual prescription logic).* Factor = 70/100 = 0.7. A: 50 × 0.7 = 35. B: 30 × 0.7 = 21. C: 20 × 0.7 = 14. Total 35 + 21 + 14 = 70.
2. *Court risk.* Suppose a judge might rule that A and C hold senior rights and take their full amounts. B would then get 70 − 50 − 20 = 0. If B fears this, 21 beats 0. A's best court outcome (50) beats 35, but A also risks worse outcomes, so A may accept the sure share. This is hypothetical.
3. *Contingent contract.* Rule: nobody must cut until signers hold at least 80% of base pumping (80 of 100). If only A signs, 50 < 80, so no one is bound and no one is a "sucker". If A and B sign, 50 + 30 = 80, which meets the threshold, so the cut binds those two. This mirrors West Basin's 80% rule (p. 119).

## Concepts
- [[collective action problem]] — in this chapter, supplying new institutions is itself a second-order dilemma, as is the pumping race at the first order (pp. 103, 136).
- [[common-pool resource (CPR)]] — a groundwater basin without limits on who may withdraw is an open-access CPR (p. 108).
- [[design principles (Ostrom)]] — the district system is said to incorporate the full set from Chapter 3 (p. 136).
- [[institutions]] — in this unit, sets of rules that forbid, require or permit actions (p. 139).
- [[public good]] — information about the basin, funded by voluntary association dues (p. 138).
- [[free rider]] — (our label; the text does not use the term) benefiting without contributing: Pasadena stopped independent actions that benefited non-contributors (p. 111); Hawthorne held out and pumped above its allocation (p. 120); the text calls a cooperator who cuts while others do not a "sucker" (p. 119).
- [[second-order collective dilemma]] (new) — the problem of supplying a new institution that would solve a first-order dilemma (p. 103).
- [[rule of capture]] (new) — unowned reserve belongs to whoever pumps it, so not pumping today means rivals take it (p. 109).
- [[mutual prescription]] (new) — each producer's open and adverse withdrawals prescribed against all the others, so the cutback is shared proportionately (p. 113).
- [[default condition]] (new) — the outcome if parties fail to agree. Litigation changed it from a return to the pumping race to a decision by the judge (p. 112).
- [[contingent agreement]] (new) — a signer's commitment starts only once enough other large producers commit (p. 119).
- [[watermaster]] (new) — a neutral, court-appointed monitor who reports extractions publicly and does not itself initiate punitive action (pp. 114, 125–126).
- [[polycentric system]] (new) — limited-purpose governmental enterprises with active private participation, with no single owner or central regulator (pp. 133, 136).
- [[status quo rules / default rules]] (new) — the rules in force until changed. In an unregulated CPR they are all default rules (p. 140).
- [[deontic operators]] (new) — forbid, require, permit. One of the three must be contained in a statement for it to count as a rule (p. 139).
- [[institutional supply]] (new) — creating new institutions and changing existing ones, which Ostrom proposes to analyse with one theory (p. 140).

## Canonical instances
- [[pumping-race]] — rule of capture and overlapping doctrines make heavy pumping the "logical outcome" (pp. 106–110).
- [[raymond-basin-settlement]] — Pasadena's suit and the proportional "mutual prescription" settlement (pp. 111–114).
- [[west-basin-settlement]] — large-group negotiation, contingent interim agreement, a holdout (Hawthorne) (pp. 114–122).
- [[central-basin-settlement]] — learning from earlier basins; about $450,000 and four years (pp. 123–124).
- [[replenishment-district-creation]] — constitution-like proposal, 1959 vote of 4 to 1 (pp. 127–133).
- [[los-angeles-polycentric-groundwater-system]] — district, county, watermaster, MWD and associations (pp. 133–136).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Default condition (outcome if no agreement) ↔ bargaining model of war: the disagreement outcome shapes what each side accepts in a settlement.
- [structural-analogy] Status quo rules that persist until changed ↔ veto player: whether the status quo changes depends on who must agree to move it.
- [structural-analogy] Contingent contract with an 80% threshold ↔ revolutionary threshold / cascade: commitment depends on enough others committing.
- [metaphor] Polycentric overlapping agencies ↔ institutions / nested enterprises: no one centre, yet coordinated (design principle of nesting).

## Questions this raises
- Why did some basin parties (Dominguez, Inglewood) shift from opposition to participation, and what generalises from that?
- How far do the conclusions depend on a facilitative political regime? What would happen in a regime without home rule or state subsidies?
- Would the same incremental path work for a CPR where information is harder to share or where no court can enforce rights? (Chapter 5's failure cases are the next test.)
- What variables make a rule change costly or cheap, as promised for Chapter 6?
