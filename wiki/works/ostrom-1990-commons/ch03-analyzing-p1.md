# Analyzing Long-Enduring, Self-Organized, and Self-Governed CPRs (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
Swiss and Japanese mountain commons and Spanish *huerta* (irrigated farmland) systems have lasted for centuries while the users themselves made, monitored and revised their own rules, with high compliance and fines that Ostrom reports as low.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Ostrom asks how appropriators (people who withdraw resource units) keep commitments and monitor one another without outside enforcers. She studies commons that are over 100 years old, some over 1,000. Part 1 covers Törbel (Switzerland), three Japanese villages, and four Spanish huertas (irrigated farming areas). In these cases, appropriators play a major role in monitoring one another, fines are reported as surprisingly low, and the Japanese villages used escalating penalties. She does not call these institutions optimal, but successful. She also questions the presumption that private property is the obvious solution for land, since villagers kept communal property beside private property by choice. The cases are the evidence base for the design principles.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a common-pool resource (CPR) and free riding; the Chapter 2 puzzles of commitment and mutual monitoring; simple percentages and rates.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Cases were chosen for long survival (over 100 to over 1,000 years) with rules made and monitored by the appropriators. "Durable" does not mean "unchanged": rules were revised by the users through their own decision rules (pp. 58–59).
- The cases target two puzzles: commitment and mutual monitoring. Compliance is high despite big temptations and fines that are usually small relative to the gain from cheating. Appropriators themselves play a major role in monitoring each other, and the guards are rarely outside agents (p. 59).
- Ostrom does not call these institutions "optimal", but she is willing to call them successful. She seeks the design principles that successful CPR institutions share (pp. 59–60).
- Swiss and Japanese villagers kept private and communal property side by side by choice. This challenges the presumption that private property is the obvious solution for land (pp. 60–61, 63).
- Rules are tailored to the resource and cheap to monitor: cow rights tied to wintering capacity, timber assigned by lot, and fixed rotation orders for water (pp. 62, 65, 67, 73).
- Monitoring is layered and reciprocal. Guards, a syndic (elected canal official) and a water court watch the farmers, and the farmers watch them. Sanctions are graduated and fines kept low (pp. 68, 73–76).
- Castellón fine books imply a recorded infraction rate of about 0.008 of the opportunities to steal water. This remains a high conformance rate even if doubled, tripled or quadrupled (p. 75).
- Rule systems differ across places (*turno*, a fixed order of turns, versus *tanda*, a fixed time slot; water tied to land versus water as a tradable right). Outside governments sometimes intruded, as with the Tibi Dam in Alicante (pp. 73, 76, 78, 81).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 58–61)

**Selection criteria.** Ostrom looks at field settings where (1) appropriators devised, applied and monitored their own rules and (2) both the resource systems and the institutions survived for long periods. The youngest institutions are over 100 years old, the oldest over 1,000. They survived droughts, floods, wars, pestilence and economic and political change. Cases: mountain grazing and forest CPRs in Switzerland and Japan; irrigation in Spain and the Philippines (p. 58).

**"Long-enduring" is not "unchanged".** The settings are complex and varied over time, so getting operational rules right on the first try would be almost impossible (p. 58). She uses Shepsle's idea: an institution is "essentially" in equilibrium if changes follow an *ex ante* plan that is part of the original institution (p. 58). Here the appropriators designed basic operational rules, created organizations to manage operations, and modified rules in light of experience using their own collective-choice and constitutional-choice rules (p. 58).

**Puzzles addressed.** The cases show how groups of self-organized principals solve the problem of commitment and the problem of mutual monitoring. The supply of institutions comes in Chapter 4 (p. 59).
- Rules constrain appropriation and mandate provisioning. Many chances to gain from breaking rules arose (stealing water in a dry season, skipping maintenance work, illegal timber) while expected sanctions were comparatively low. Conformance was nonetheless remarkable (p. 59).
- Much is spent on monitoring, but the "guards" are rarely external agents. Appropriators monitor each other, so mutual monitoring, which has aspects of a second-order dilemma (a problem of providing the monitoring itself), is somehow solved. Fines are surprisingly low, rarely more than a small fraction of the gain from breaking rules (p. 59).
- Her conclusion (later in the chapter) will be that commitment and monitoring are strategically linked, and that monitoring yields private benefits to the monitor as well as joint benefits (p. 59).

**Lessons sought.** Rules differ across settings. But with long trial-and-error learning, harsh environments and low costs of changing their own rules, one can expect the appropriators to have "discovered" some underlying principles of good institutional design (p. 59). She does not claim optimality, because uncertainty and the difficulty of measuring benefits and costs make that nearly unmeasurable (pp. 59–60). She is willing to call the institutions successful. Users had autonomy and the incentives and means to improve rules. Swiss and Japanese commons have been sustained, if not enhanced, over centuries of intensive use. This record has not been matched by most irrigation systems built in the past 25 years (p. 60). She therefore seeks design principles shared by successful institutions and how they shape incentives. Chapter 5 will ask whether "failure" cases share them (p. 60).

**Private property presumption.** Two further questions are raised.
1. Many resource economists accept that private rights are hard for fugitive resources (groundwater, oil, fish), but presume that private rights to arable or grazing land are an obvious solution. **Dasgupta and Heal's view:** with private rights, "the resource ceases to be common property and the problem is solved at one stroke" (p. 60). Many property-rights theorists presume one of two bad outcomes is likely under communal ownership: the commons is destroyed because no one can be excluded, or negotiating allocation rules costs too much even if exclusion is achieved (pp. 60–61).
2. What the cases show instead is private and communal property side by side, where villagers controlled institutions and property rights. Villagers in both countries chose to retain communal property as the basis of land use. It cannot be seen as a relic from a land of plenty. Had its transaction costs been excessive compared with private property, villagers had many chances to change tenure (p. 61).

Third World relevance: Ostrom says the Philippine *zanjera* case, the last in the chapter, gives a strong affirmative answer, and that all the design principles present in the Swiss, Japanese and Spanish cases are also present there (p. 61). She says the analysis rests on a limited number of cases but may have wider application (p. 61).

> **Intuition note.** The chapter picks cases by their outcome (long survival with self-made rules). It then asks what those cases share. Many resource economists presume private property is the obvious solution for land; her cases show villagers deliberately keeping communal property beside it.

### Communal tenure in high mountain meadows and forests

**Törbel, Switzerland (pp. 61–65).** A village of about 600 people in the Vispertal trench of the upper Valais canton, described by Netting. Netting's key environmental features are steep slopes with many altitude microclimates, little precipitation, and exposure to sunlight. Peasants plant private plots with grain, vegetables, fruit trees and hay; herdsmen tend village cattle on communal alpine meadows in summer and make cheese (pp. 61–62).
- *Boundaries and membership.* Legal documents go back to 1224. Five types of property are communal: alpine meadows, forests, "waste" lands, irrigation systems, and paths and roads (p. 62). In 1483 residents signed articles forming an association to regulate the alp, forests and waste lands (p. 62). **Netting (1976):** foreigners who bought land could not acquire communal rights, and landownership did not automatically confer them. Current holders of land and water rights decided on admitting outsiders (p. 62). A 1507 inventory shows firmly established boundaries. Access was strictly limited to citizens (p. 62).
- *Allocation rule.* A 1517 regulation says no citizen could send more cows to the alp than he could feed over the winter. Netting reports it is still enforced, and it imposed substantial fines (p. 62). A local official (*Gewalthaber*) administers it and keeps half of the fines. The "wintering" rule is used in many Swiss villages for "cow rights". It is relatively easy to monitor, because all cows go to the mountain and must be counted immediately, since the count fixes each family's cheese share (p. 62).
- *Organization.* All citizens vote on village statutes. The alp association (all local citizens owning cattle) meets annually on general rules and elects officials. The officials hire alp staff, fine misuse, arrange manure distribution, and organize annual maintenance (roads, paths, avalanche-damaged corrals) (pp. 62–63). Labor contributions or fees are usually proportional to cattle sent. Village officials mark timber trees, which are assigned by lot to groups of households that then harvest them (p. 63).
- *Private side.* Meadows, gardens, grainfields and vineyards are private, with condominium-type arrangements for relatives' shares. Equal inheritance among legitimate offspring gives equal access to the commons, but family property is not divided until surviving siblings are relatively mature. Before nineteenth-century population growth, use was held in check by late marriage, high celibacy, long birth spacing and emigration (p. 63).
- *Why communal for some land.* **Netting (1976)** rejects the view that communal tenure is a holdover, since for at least five centuries villagers have known both systems and matched tenure to land use. He argues communal tenure suits conditions where (1) value of production per unit of land is low, (2) frequency or dependability of use or yield is low, (3) the possibility of improvement or intensification is low, (4) a large territory is needed, and (5) relatively large groups are needed for capital investment (p. 63). Communal tenure also imposes conservation on the whole community (p. 63). Yields are low, but productivity has been maintained for centuries. Tight controls prevented overgrazing, and the CPR was enhanced by weeding, manuring and road building (p. 64).

**Wider Swiss pattern (Picht 1987).** Netting says Törbel is not a prototype. Legal forms vary, but his main findings are consistent with many Swiss places (p. 64). Four-fifths of alpine territory is owned in some form of common property (villages, corporations, cooperatives); the rest belongs to cantons or to private owners or co-owners. Some villages reallocate grazing rights among several meadows every decade or so (p. 64). All local regulations define access and set authority rules limiting appropriation. Most use proportional allocation, based on one of five bases: wintering capacity, meadowland owned, hay produced, value of land owned in the valley, or cooperative shares owned. A few allow equal numbers of animals for every citizen (p. 64). Overuse is rarely reported. Where it occurred, entry plus authority rules were not limiting enough, or several villages used one alp without overarching rules (p. 64). **Picht:** users are the main decision unit and have considerable autonomy to set and revise statutes and limits. Their organizations are nested in larger organizations (village, canton, federation) in which they are seen as legitimate (p. 64).

**Low transaction costs.** The timber procedure: the forester marks ripe trees; eligible households form work teams, divide cutting, hauling and stacking into roughly equal stacks, and a lottery assigns stacks. No harvesting is allowed at other times. This combines careful assessment of the forest with easy-to-monitor, fair allocation (p. 65). Work days and days of reckoning (cheese distribution and cost assessment) are combined with festivities to reduce costs (p. 65). The rising value of labor is an exogenous change. Villages that need unanimity to change their institutions adjust more slowly than those with less inclusive rules (p. 65).

> **Intuition note.** The Törbel rules make cheating easy to see (cows are counted, trees are marked) and make shares feel fair (lots, proportional work). Ostrom says such rules keep monitoring costs low, reduce conflict and are considered fair by participants.

**Hirano, Nagaike and Yamanoka, Japan (pp. 65–69).** McKean estimates about 12 million hectares of forest and uncultivated mountain meadow were held in common by thousands of villages in the Tokugawa period (1600–1867), and about 3 million hectares are today. She has "not yet turned up an example of a commons that suffered ecological destruction while it was still a commons" (p. 65).
- *Setting.* The three villages resemble Törbel (steep mountains, microclimates, private rice, vegetable and horse farming). Commons yield timber, thatch, fodder, fertilizer plants, firewood and charcoal. They meet Netting's five conditions (p. 66).
- *Governance.* An assembly, usually of heads of households with decision-making authority, governed each village. Political rights rested on cultivation rights, taxpaying duties or landownership, and varied across villages (p. 66). Villagers shared a clear image of which lands were private and which common, and held that common lands needed management for the peasants' long-term interests (p. 66). The household was the smallest unit of account. The *kumi* (several households) was often an accounting and distribution unit. A household could not split without village permission, and access went to households, not individuals, so large households had no advantage (p. 66). Population growth was extremely low (0.025% for 1721–1846) and ownership patterns stable (p. 66).
- *Authority rules.* The assembly specified how much of each valued product a household could harvest and when. Rules were tailored to the environment, to the economic role of each product, and to the need to minimize monitoring costs of labor inputs, outputs and compliance (p. 67). The headman usually set the start date. For abundant plants the date only ensured maturity and propagation, with no amount limit. For scarce products, other harvesting rules applied (p. 67). *Example (McKean):* winter fodder from a closed reserve. Each *kumi* gets a zone by annual rotation; each household sends exactly one adult. At the temple bell, each *kumi* cuts in step as a "drill team" with sickles, grass dries, two representatives per household bundle it, the haul is divided into one cluster per household, and clusters go to households by lottery (p. 67).
- *Labor duties.* Households owed a share of collective work (annual burning with nine-foot firebreaks, mountain-opening days, specific timber or thatch cutting). Accounts recorded who contributed what. Only illness, family tragedy or lack of spare able-bodied adults excused absence. Otherwise punishment followed (p. 67).
- *Monitoring and sanctions.* Because the mountain was closed except at set times, anyone found there at other times was visibly breaking the rules. Most villages hired "detectives" who patrolled daily on horseback in pairs. In some villages it was a prestigious post for a young man; in others eligible males rotated into it; one village used citizen's arrest (p. 68). Written codes specified escalating penalties depending on the offender's past behavior. An occasional infraction was handled quietly (detective could demand cash and sake). Violators were deprived of the contraband harvest (kept by the village), equipment and horses; they paid the village a fine to retrieve equipment and horses. Fines ran from very low to extremely high according to seriousness and the culprit's willingness to make amends. The most serious sanctions, occasionally used, were ostracism or banishment (p. 68).
- *Violations.* Compliance was very high, but violations occurred. One cause was impatience before opening day. Another was genuine disagreement with the headman. In a Hirano example, about thirty people, including heads of leading households, entered the closed commons en masse to cut trellis poles, believing the headman had set opening day too late. Fines were imposed, but as a donation to the village school instead of the usual sake (pp. 68–69). McKean stresses that long-term success of the locally designed rule systems indicates "it is not necessary for regulation of the commons to be imposed coercively from the outside" (p. 69).

> **Intuition note.** In the Japanese cases sanctions started small and rose with repeat or serious offences, and occasional rule-breaking was met with fines that fitted the situation. Compliance was high without outside enforcement.

### Huerta irrigation institutions (pp. 69–81)

**Overview (pp. 69–71).** On May 29, 1435, 84 irrigators on the Benacher and Faitanar canals in Valencia met at the monastery of St. Francis to approve regulations on water rights, sharing in good and bad years, maintenance duties, elected officials and fines. The canals and many rules were older. Valencia was recaptured from the Muslims in 1238, and some rules predate that. So for at least 550 years, and probably close to 1,000, farmers have met to specify and revise rules, select officials and set fines and assessments (p. 69). The region is semiarid with highly variable rainfall; water was never abundant, so conflict has always lurked just under the surface of everyday life and has erupted between irrigators, with officials, and between downstream and upstream groups (p. 69). Even so, the institutions have proved adequate for resolving conflicts, allocating water predictably and ensuring stability (pp. 69–70). *Huertas* are well-demarked irrigation areas around towns (Valencia, Murcia, Orihuela, Alicante; Figure 3.1, p. 70). Sources: Maass and Anderson (1986) and Glick (1970) (p. 71).

**Valencia (pp. 71–76).**
- *Physical setting.* The Turia River is divided into eight major canals serving a 16,000-hectare huerta. Over 80% of farms are under 1 hectare and few exceed 5. Two or three crops are harvested per year, and each farmer chooses his cropping pattern. River flow is highly variable. No dams had been built on the Turia until the turn of this century; substantial upstream storage came only in 1951 with the Generalisimo Dam (228 million cubic meters). Groundwater was never a major source (p. 71).
- *Water rights.* The right to water inheres in the land. Pre-reconquest irrigated land is *regadiu*, the rest is *seca* (dry), and some land (*extremales*) gets water only in abundance. Each piece of *regadiu* gets canal water proportional to its size (p. 71).
- *Organization.* Irrigators of seven major canals form autonomous irrigation communities. Each community's *syndic* (chief executive) attends two weekly tribunals (p. 71). The *Tribunal de las Aguas* meets Thursdays outside the Cathedral's Apostles' Door. There are no lawyers but many onlookers. A presiding officer questions parties and witnesses, and the court members (excluding the syndic of the canal involved) decide on the facts at once, applying that canal's rules. Final decisions are recorded, not the proceedings (pp. 71–72). Syndics may then hold a second tribunal as a coordinating committee for seasonal low water and intercanal problems (p. 72). The *hereters* (landowners eligible for canal water) meet every second or third year to elect the syndic and other officials. The syndic enforces rules, makes authoritative physical allocations in disputes, levies fines, and sets order and timing in severe shortage (subject to weekly Tribunal review). He must own and farm land served by the canal, and usually has a small staff of ditch-riders and guards whom he appoints (p. 72). Medieval hereters also elected inspectors (*veedors*); today they elect an executive committee (*junta de gobierno*) of delegates from major service areas, which decides on annual maintenance shutdown and work organization (p. 72).
- *Three conditions.* Rules depend on officials' declaration of abundance, seasonal low water, or extraordinary drought (p. 72). In declared abundance (relatively infrequent), farmers take as much water as they need whenever it is present (p. 72).
- *Turno under seasonal low water (most frequent).* Distributory canals are placed in a rotation, and farms on each distributory receive water in fixed order from head to tail. **Maass and Anderson:** a farmer who opens his headgate takes all the water he needs without time limit, defining need by his crops. He may not waste water; if he misses his turn he waits for the next rotation (p. 73). So (1) the order is fixed and (2) each farmer decides how much to take if he does not waste. Nobody knows exactly when his turn will come, but each knows he can take what he needs when it does (p. 73).
- *Extraordinary drought.* Rules change so that farms with crops most in need get priority. At first farmers are expected to shorten their own turns. As drought continues, the syndic and representatives decide more and more how long each may have water. A procedure exists for switching rule regimes. It is needed less now because of the Generalisimo Dam (p. 73).
- *Monitoring (Figure 3.2).* Monitoring is very high. A farmer waiting for his turn tends fields near the canal and can watch those ahead and the ditch-riders he pays. Ditch-riders patrol and are watched by the syndic, who can lose respect and his job if allocation is unfair. Challenges to a syndic, ditch-rider or other irrigator can be aired weekly before the Tribunal (p. 74). Medieval honor norms probably raised the potential for conflict, but actual violence never came close to that potential (p. 74).
- *Castellón fine books, 1443 and 1486 (Glick).* Castellón's huerta is "nearly similarly structured". There were 441 fines in 1443 and 499 in 1486, with similar offense distributions, which indicates stability. Guards assessed more than one fine per day. About two-thirds of actions were initiated by guards, a third by farmers (p. 74). 42% concerned clear temptation to obtain unlawful water (forbidden or stolen water, illegal canal checks, force, irrigating without right). The rest concerned harm to others (flooding, wasting water) (pp. 74–75). Two-thirds of those fined in a year were one-time offenders. Of the repeaters, 41% were involved in two actions, 25% three, 15% four, 8% five and 12% more than five (p. 75; the printed shares add to 101%).
- *Conformance estimate.* About 1,000 hearths in fifteenth-century Castellón. With a rotation of about two weeks, each irrigator had about 25 opportunities a year to take water illegally, so about 25,000 opportunities versus 200 recorded instances of illegal taking. That gives a recorded infraction rate of $200/25{,}000 = 0.008$ (p. 75). Not all infractions were detected, but one could double, triple or quadruple the rate and still have a remarkable conformance rate (p. 75). About one-third of hereters would meet a guard at some point in a full year, so news of monitoring reached irrigators regularly (p. 75). For modern times Ostrom only suspects, from the number of ditch-riders and the weekly court, that high enforcement is needed (p. 75).
- *Fines.* The syndic received two-thirds of each fine and the accuser one-third. Authorized fines were high, but actual fines were "very low (a few pennies at the most)" and varied with gravity, economic conditions and probably ability to pay (**Glick**, p. 75). Glick says this gave flexibility to rigid rotation. Sometimes the cost of waiting for a legal turn would be extraordinarily high. Low fines kept guards from antagonizing farmers who generally followed the rules, while detection still brought some humiliation. Harsh punishment of a usually compliant person who errs under desperate conditions can breed resentment (**Oliver 1980**, as cited) (pp. 75–76).
- *Harassment was rare.* One "particularly fractious individual" made 5 theft accusations and was accused 13 times in 1486. Ten of the 18 incidents were conflicts between two families. Such cases are extremely rare; Glick sees the absence of chronic conflict as a tribute to both the distribution system and the guards' vigilance (p. 76).

> **Intuition note.** Valencia combines a fixed order (no one can jump the queue) with farmer-chosen amounts. Every actor is watched by others, so it is hard to cheat unseen. Fines stay small, which (as the text says of Castellón) kept guards from deeply antagonizing farmers who generally followed the rules.

**Murcia and Orihuela (pp. 76–78).** The Segura River runs through the Murcia huerta and then the Orihuela huerta. Murcia has 13,300 farms (83% under 1 hectare). Orihuela has 4,888 farms (64% under 1 hectare, 86% under 5). Water rights are tied to land, with *regadiu* and *seca* stable for centuries. Rainfall is lower and more variable than in Valencia, and terrain is more varied, so highland versus lowland watering is stressed (p. 76).
- *Tanda.* Each farmer has a fixed time period (*tanda*) to withdraw water. He knows when and for how long, but not how much water will be available. Advantages over *turno*: more certainty for planning, and more incentive to economize since he allocates a limited time slice. Disadvantage: it is rigid, especially as farms are bought, sold, divided or combined (p. 76).
- *Low water.* The officials, in consultation with city officials, declare when there is too little water to continue the regular *tanda*. Under extraordinary low-water conditions, each community posts a new schedule for each rotation (about every two weeks), saying which crops get precedence (pp. 76–77).
- *Structure.* About 30 irrigation communities in Murcia, 10 in Orihuela, and a few more just below Orihuela. Guards are mostly nominated by farmers of the section they patrol, report violations, witness charges, and help distribute water (p. 77). Both huertas have huerta-wide organizations. Murcia's syndics meet yearly in a general assembly, elect an executive commission, and approve a budget and taxes. Orihuela's syndics meet every three years to elect a water magistrate, lieutenant and solicitor. Orihuela's city is hardly involved in irrigation (p. 77).
- *Courts.* Murcia's *Consejo de Hombres Buenos* has five canal syndics and two inspectors, chosen monthly by lottery from names in two bowls so each canal is fairly represented. It meets Thursdays in City Hall, presided over by the mayor (or deputy), who votes only to break ties. Procedures are "oral, public, summary, and cheap" (p. 77). Orihuela's court has one judge. Charges go to a court officer, the accused is summoned within days, a confession brings immediate sentence, and otherwise the magistrate tries to broker an agreement (pp. 77–78).

**Alicante (pp. 78–81).**
- *Setting.* The Monnegre River rises near the sea and drains only a small area, so water is scarcer than in Valencia or Murcia-Orihuela. The basic right is closer to Murcia's: fixed time periods. Shortly after the recovery from the Muslims, time rights were separated from land and traded in their own market (p. 78). The huerta has 3,700 hectares in 2,400 farms (63% under 1 hectare, 93% under 5). Farmers grow cereals and vegetables between fruit and nut trees (p. 78).
- *Tibi Dam (1594)*, built on farmers' initiative, doubled usable water. Rights to "new water" went to owners of huerta land whose assessments paid for the dam. "Old water" rights stayed with prior holders but may be sold or rented only to owners of land eligible for new water, so they cannot leave the huerta (p. 78). Local irrigators have also sought other sources and contracts with private water companies (p. 78).
- *Scrip market.* Before each full rotation, the syndicate posts dates and times scrip is issued. Rights-holders receive scrip in denominations from one hour to one-third of a minute, and Tibi scrip is fully exchangeable. New-water farmers rarely have enough and buy more in an informal Sunday market, at a formal auction, or on market days (p. 79). At the auction, the community sells about 90 hours it owns (rights assigned to it in 1926 for regular income) plus unclaimed surplus scrip. Minimum offer is one hour, but scrip is divisible. The community posts public information: reservoir storage, all water delivered last rotation (names and amounts), and all water sold at auction (names, hours, prices) (**Maass and Anderson**, p. 79). Farmers tell the ditch-rider how much time they want. They may pay up to three days after rotation, so scrip also covers use already taken, and the price is higher near the end of a rotation (pp. 79–80). Price falls in wet years and can become very high in seasonal low water, which can cause conflict between old-right and new-right holders (p. 80).
- *Community organization.* There is one irrigation community for the whole huerta. Voting in the general assembly requires owning 1.8 hectares, voting for the executive commission 1.2 hectares, and serving on it 3.6 hectares. Votes are not weighted by land owned (p. 80). The commission has 12 *sindicos* serving four years (half rotating every second year), and one is chosen director. The assembly meets annually for the budget and taxes, and special meetings are possible. Both bodies have been very active in seeking new water and better contracts with private firms (p. 80). Regular expenses are assessed on holders of all water rights, collected in three designated rotations when scrip is applied for. Extraordinary expenses are assessed similarly, due at other rotations (p. 80). Ditch-riders open and close all farm headgates in Alicante (farmers do this themselves in Valencia and Murcia-Orihuela) but have less discretion over who gets water and when. One employee accounts for the water from the regulating basins until it reaches a ditch-rider, who is in turn accountable for it (pp. 80–81).
- *National authority.* Alicante saw more national control than the other huertas. Large structures can be seized by a rent-seeking ruler. The Tibi Dam passed to royal ownership for a century in 1739. After it returned to Alicante in 1840, farmers did not win back the right to select syndicate officers for another 25 years. The Civil War again interrupted farmers' control, and they chose their own officials again only from 1950 (p. 81).
- *Regional contrast.* Eastern Spain's autonomy was not typical of Castile, whose more centralized institutions shaped Spanish national institutions. By the end of the thirteenth century the Aragonese *cortes* had secured the power to legislate and, under some conditions, limit the king's legislation; Castile's *cortes* was seldom summoned (Veliz, quoted p. 81). Ostrom only wonders whether Latin American history might have differed had the monarchy been modeled on Aragon (p. 81).
- *Efficiency comparison (Maass and Anderson).* Leaving out water costs and administrative costs, Alicante lets farmers be most efficient in using other inputs, Valencia is least efficient of the Spanish systems, and Murcia-Orihuela is in between. All systems give positive benefits and have shown a remarkable capacity to survive. A Murcian historian's 1887 conclusion about the stability of Murcia's democratic, representative institutions begins on p. 81 and continues beyond this unit (p. 81).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A village with 10 farmers uses a fixed-rotation water system. One rotation takes 2 weeks, so each farmer gets $52/2 = 26$ turns a year. Opportunities to steal water: $10 \times 26 = 260$ per year. Suppose guards record 2 thefts in a year. Recorded infraction rate: $2/260 \approx 0.0077$. Suppose guards miss most thefts, so the true count is 4 times larger: $8/260 \approx 0.031$. Conformance is still $1 - 0.031 = 0.969$, about 97%. This mirrors Ostrom's Castellón logic (p. 75). The key point is that the *recorded* rate sets a floor, and even generous multipliers leave compliance high.

## Concepts
- [[common-pool-resource]] — a resource system such as a mountain meadow, forest or irrigation system whose users (appropriators) here devised and monitored their own rules (p. 58); a formal definition is not given in these pages
- [[design-principles-ostrom]] — features shared by successful CPR institutions. Here only announced as the goal; Ostrom does not list them in this part (pp. 60–61)
- [[institutional-equilibrium]] (new) — Shepsle's idea: an institution is "essentially" in equilibrium when changes follow an *ex ante* plan built into it (p. 58)
- [[mutual-monitoring]] (new) — appropriators watch one another, and also officials and guards; guards are rarely external agents (pp. 59, 74)
- [[graduated-sanctions]] (new) — penalties that escalate with seriousness and repeat offending; fines are described as surprisingly low (pp. 59, 68, 75)
- [[proportional-allocation-rule]] (new) — appropriation rights scaled to a quantity such as wintering capacity or land size (pp. 62, 64, 71)
- [[rotation-allocation-turno-tanda]] (new) — turno fixes the order and lets the farmer take as much as he needs without waste; tanda fixes the time slice (pp. 73, 76)
- [[communal-vs-private-tenure-matching]] (new) — villagers matching tenure to land use; Netting says communal tenure is better suited when his five conditions hold (p. 63)
- [[institutions]] — rules and organizations that the appropriators themselves designed and revised here (pp. 58, 64)

## Canonical instances
- [[torbel-alpine-commons]] — Swiss village with wintering rule, lotteries for timber, and private/communal mix (pp. 61–65)
- [[japanese-village-commons]] — Hirano, Nagaike and Yamanoka: household rights, detectives, graduated penalties (pp. 65–69)
- [[valencia-huerta]] — turno rotation, Tribunal de las Aguas, syndic, mutual monitoring (pp. 71–76)
- [[castellon-fine-books]] — 441 fines in 1443 and 499 in 1486, recorded infraction rate of 0.008 (pp. 74–75)
- [[murcia-orihuela-huertas]] — tanda time slices, lottery-chosen water court, huerta-wide organizations (pp. 76–78)
- [[alicante-huerta]] — tradable water rights, scrip auction, periods of national control over the Tibi Dam and syndicate (pp. 78–81)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Alicante's tradable scrip and auction ↔ economics: tradable permit markets — rights to a scarce resource are traded under a community cap.
- [structural-analogy] Lottery assignment of timber stacks, fodder clusters, and court membership ↔ social choice: random assignment to treat participants equally.
- [structural-analogy] Many varied cases sharing common features (p. 59) ↔ methodology: observable implications — a theory should predict features across varied settings.
