# Analyzing Institutional Failures and Fragilities

## L0 — One line
In Ostrom's failed and fragile cases, appropriators were large and divided, lacked arenas for collective choice, or faced outside governments that overrode or ignored their rules, and these cases meet fewer of her design principles than the robust ones.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Earlier chapters showed communities solving commons problems. This chapter asks why others did not. Ostrom reviews failures (Bodrum and Izmir fisheries, the Mojave groundwater basins, Mawelle fishery, Kirindi Oya irrigation) and fragile cases (Alanya, Gal Oya, Port Lameron Harbour). Causes include size, heterogeneity, no arena for collective choice, and central governments that deny local rule-making or shield favourites from enforcement. Gal Oya shows that outside "catalysts" can help farmers organize. In a 14-case table, the failures show at most three design principles; fragile cases fall in between. She says this is enough support to warrant further analysis, but more work is needed before high confidence.

**Prerequisites:** the design principles from Ch. 3; what a common-pool resource and free riding are; "rent dissipation" (entry continues until a resource's surplus profit is competed away).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Turkish fisheries at Bodrum and Izmir failed in a setting of "benign neglect": heterogeneous fisher groups, no overarching arena for rules, weak outside enforcement (pp. 145–146).
- Mojave groundwater users did change institutions, yet failed. They did not agree on key questions about the problem. Ostrom says people who lack a shared image of their problems, do not break complex problems into parts, and do not recognize diverse interests as legitimate are unlikely to succeed even when institutional means exist (pp. 148–149).
- Mawelle fishers had good allocation rules but could not keep an entry limit. Outside politicians could be persuaded not to enforce it. Rule-making autonomy was missing (pp. 155–157).
- Kirindi Oya shows interlocking perverse incentives for farmers, landowners, politicians and engineers. Internal factors (size, diversity, poverty, no control structures) and an unwilling central regime appear to explain the lack of capacity for self-governance (pp. 164–166).
- Gal Oya shows a turnaround: bottom-up organizing by "catalysts", small units first, consensus decisions, official recognition (pp. 168–173).
- Port Lameron's local rules are effective but fragile because federal Canada does not recognize them and applies one policy to the whole coast (pp. 175–177).
- In Table 5.2 no failure case has more than three of the design principles. Fragile cases are intermediate. Ostrom treats this as warranting further work (pp. 179–181).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 143–144)
Ostrom says the success stories were needed against the "presumption of failure" in policy writing. Now come failures and fragile cases (p. 143). She previews:
- Bodrum and the Bay of Izmir (Turkey): no effective rules, overcrowding, rent dissipation.
- San Bernardino County, California: still in overdraft after litigation and a special district.
- A Sri Lankan fishery: rotation rules work, but an entry rule could not be enforced.
- Sri Lankan irrigation systems: officials changed administrative structures several times without gaining farmer cooperation, but a bottom-up experiment reversed this in one large system.
- Nova Scotia and Newfoundland: local inshore rules exist but are not recognized by the national government. Ostrom says this case is not yet a failure (pp. 143–144).

### Two Turkish inshore fisheries (pp. 144–146)
**Bodrum.** In 1983: 100 small inboard boats, 11 trawlers, 2 purse seiners, 9 bottom seiners, about 400 fishers (p. 144). Until the 1970s it had a successful inshore fishery. In that decade the government encouraged some fishers to build trawlers and rarely enforced the three-mile limit (p. 144). Early trawler profits drew entrants until fleet revenue was below costs. Berkes calls it a textbook case of rent dissipation (p. 145). Total yield stayed about the same, but catch per unit of effort fell sharply. The local cooperative failed to mediate and had disappeared by 1983. Six groups of fishers with distinct interests now compete (p. 145).

**Bay of Izmir.** In 1983: 1,800 fishers; 700 small inboard boats, 30 bottom seiners, and purse seiners (text layer reads 2). Izmir is itself a large urban center (metropolitan population over 1 million) with high demand for fresh fish. The result is an overcapitalized fishery. Trawlers were not the problem. The causes were quick gains, many fishers, subgroups with conflicting interests, and no overarching arena for rules and conflict resolution. Cooperatives each represented one subgroup, so "no operational rules" allocated fish or limited crowding (p. 145).

**Turkey's national setting** was "benign neglect". Licenses were required but not limited. Rules on spawning seasons, equipment and a three-mile trawler exclusion existed. The Ministry of Agriculture, which made the rules, employed no enforcement agents (p. 146).

**Conclusion.** The failure "cannot be attributed to a single cause". The groups were large, with heterogeneous interests and time horizons. Given the different technologies, any rule limiting use would tend to favour one subgroup over another. Where the regime offers no arenas for low-cost, enforceable agreements, the costs of self-organization are very hard to meet (p. 146).

*Intuition note:* Size and diversity make agreeing on rules costly, and a state that neither enforces nor offers an arena leaves fishers alone with that cost. Ostrom names no single cause.

### California groundwater basins with continuing CPR problems (pp. 146–149)
Many southern California basins solved their problems (Ch. 4). San Bernardino County basins did not. The county is the largest in the US, 83% desert, with 15 groundwater basins, some interconnected and some independent (pp. 146–147). Overdraft was reported in some basins in the 1950s. The Mojave Water Agency was created by state law and a 1960 election to levy a land tax towards the Feather River aqueduct, giving a claim to 50,800 acre-feet of imported water per year at marginal cost (p. 147).

Two views of the agency existed (p. 147):
- A wholesaler/insurance device. Smaller, polycentric enterprises (as in Los Angeles County) would then settle rights within subareas.
- The primary water-management institution for the whole area. Most initial council officials held this view. They hired attorney James Krieger, and litigation began in 1966.

Five issues had no consensus (p. 148): (1) one river, interconnected basins, or independent basins; (2) overdraft everywhere or locally; (3) coequal pumpers or prior rights; (4) a settlement with large upper-area pumpers or with many small lower-area pumpers; (5) separating water rights from land in an undeveloped region.

Krieger and the staff treated each question as having one legitimate answer: a single basin with documented overdraft, coequal rights, and a settlement with pumpers using 500 acre-feet or more per year. Others disagreed. In 1964 the state Department of Water Resources denied overdraft in two large subbasins, while the agency called overdraft an "unquestionable fact" (p. 148). No voluntary associations arose to discuss the issues. Conflicts ran along many lines (large/small pumpers, development/no-growth, industry/agriculture, locals/experts, appointed/elected staff), with recall elections. Litigation was suspended in 1974 and no action has since limited pumping (pp. 148–149).

Now all agencies document overdraft, and the entitled Feather River water "flows by" to others. No acceptable plan for a distribution system and its financing exists (p. 149).

**Conclusion (p. 149).** Unlike the Turkish fishers, Mojave residents could initiate major institutional change. Their one regional instrument did not fit diverse problems. Even with considerable capability there is "no guarantee" of success. People who lack similar images of their problem, do not break complex problems into subparts, and do not recognize diverse interests as legitimate are "unlikely" to succeed even when institutional means exist.

*Intuition note:* Having legal tools is not enough. People must first share a view of the problem, split it into manageable parts, and accept that others have legitimate interests.

### A Sri Lankan fishery: Mawelle (pp. 149–157)
**Setting.** About 300 Sinhalese fishers, three technologies. The focus is beach seines (madelia, "big net"), half-mile-long nets that can take about a ton at once; the peak season (September–October) can bring up to a third of the annual catch. A net lasts about five years and costs about three times average annual household income. The beach has room for only two nets at once. 20–30 nets would use most of them well, but there are 100 nets and the average net was used only seven times in 1971: severe overcapitalization (pp. 149–150). Ostrom calls Mawelle another classic case of rent dissipation, with a twist. Its fishers had elaborate access rules but could not sustain an entry rule limiting the number of nets (p. 150).

**Appropriation rules (pp. 150–152; Fig. 5.1, Table 5.1).** Nets are named and put in a sequence. Each owner knows the net before and after his. There are two launching sites (harbor side, rock side). A net may launch on the harbor side once the net preceding it has been used. After a harbor dawn run it is eligible for a rock-side dawn run, and later may launch on the rock side at any time once the net following it has been used (p. 150). Table 5.1 shows the stylized sequence, with each net's place shifting one step per day.

Fishers explain why they use this system rather than simple rotation (pp. 150–152):
1. The harbor side gives the biggest catches. The rock side is more consistent when fish are few.
2. The first catch of the morning is largest, and morning prices are highest.
3. Weather changes the number of hauls, so fixed hours would be inefficient.
4. Preparing and restacking nets takes much labor, so one use per rotation would cost more.

Disputes about the sequence are rare, except when the rules themselves are challenged. Alexander saw none in two years, although the village has high violence (three murders and 17 serious assaults in his stay) (p. 152).

**Ownership and the entry problem (pp. 152–155).** The system evolved when there were about 20 nets and gave equal, profitable incomes. A net has eight shares, because at least eight men are needed to haul it. Shares are freely sold, though other shareholders hold a "strong practical veto" (p. 152). Population grew 70% between 1901 and 1931, disproportionately among beach-seining families, whose sons wanted access. Alexander's logic: with 20 nets (160 shares) a share gets 1/160 of the catch. If two sons jointly inherit it each gets 1/320, whereas if one joins in building a new net they each get 1/168. (Fractions inferred from the text layer; see Reading notes.) With 20 nets and eight shares, 1/160 follows by arithmetic (p. 153).

- **1933:** national law required registration and limited nets to those in use (32 registered). It also let outsiders buy shares in existing nets, opening access beyond the original kin groups. The net limit was not enforced (p. 153).
- **Early 1940s:** a road, an ice factory and a Fish Sales Union raised demand. Fish prices rose fourfold between 1938 and 1941. By 1945, 71 nets were in use. At first this was profitable even though each added share had negative marginal product. Share prices kept rising for two decades (p. 153).
- **Ownership shifts:** entrepreneurs bought shares in several nets and hired wage laborers. A single-share owner got the flush season only about every third year by 1971, and then earned below subsistence in the other two years. Alexander computed the optimal holding for an owner working his own shares as 6.5 shares. In 1971, "9.5" of the fishers (58%) owned 5 shares or less (printed so; see Reading notes). Faction heads also bought shares to employ followers (pp. 153–154).
- **Enforcement efforts:** petitions in 1940, 1942, 1945, then 1946, which the government agent accepted, limiting nets to the 77 then registered. Even the three largest shareholders backed it. Seven nets were added in the next two decades (versus 39 in the prior decade) by those who offered inducements (p. 154).
- **1964:** entrepreneur David Mahattea went through the local MP and the district revenue officer. The officer refused at first, then ordered nets registered after conflict. A second faction leader supported an opposition candidate and won more registrations. 24 new nets joined 84 existing ones (pp. 154–155).
- **Counter-maneuver:** after net 83 launched, one family owning shares in nets 1 and 84 launched net 1 instead, starting a new sequence that skipped net 84 and the new nets 85–108. It was repeated once, then challenged in 1966, when nets 1 and 85 launched together and fishers brawled at sea. Police, whom Mahattea had warned in advance, stayed for weeks to ensure the 24 nets joined. The national government then froze nets at 108. Burning in conflicts left 99 in use (p. 155).

**Ostrom's reading (pp. 155–157).** This was not ignorance. The fishers knew the consequences, and the sequencing rules had worked for years. The case shows what happens when appropriators lack autonomy to make and enforce new rules (p. 155). British-era village headmen were abolished in 1965 and replaced by weak civil-service posts held by outsiders; four men served in one year, none longer than a month (pp. 155–156). Villagers must seek permission from distant officials for daily matters, and politics runs on patronage given to faction leaders for votes. No arena was provided for constitutional-choice or collective-choice decisions. The rotation system was codified in 1933, before the economic change, and villagers lost the right to change their rules (p. 156). Officials could be persuaded, through promises of votes and perhaps even bribes, not to enforce a rule that most local fishers wanted. In any effort to close entry, some participants are strongly motivated to free ride on others' restrictions. If they can make an "end-run" around local authorities, enforcement can fail even when most appropriators prefer it (pp. 156–157).

In other southern-coast villages near markets, one entrepreneur often bought up the shares and ran the seine as a single firm with wage labor. Ostrom says private ownership "may have been" the only viable arrangement there, not because it was "the only way" but because the external regime would not allow local rule determination and enforcement. External intervention that prevents enforcement against political favourites undermines the viability of common-property arrangements (p. 157).

*Intuition note:* Mawelle's rules for sharing the catch worked for many years. The problem was who could close entry. When outsiders can overrule local enforcement, those who gain from entry can win officials over, with inducements or promises of votes, and get the rule left unenforced.

### Irrigation development projects in Sri Lanka (pp. 157–173)
**The problem (pp. 157–159).** Heavy investment (restoration began under the British in the nineteenth century) has raised rice output, but output falls short of planners' expectations. In the Gal Oya project's evaluation, discounted costs exceeded discounted benefits by 277 million rupees. At Uda Walawe the area actually irrigated was one-third of the plan (p. 158).

One source is water use. Paddy yield is highly sensitive to water scarcity and relatively insensitive to excess. Flooded fields also save weeding labor. Farmers rarely pay full water costs. Water taken from the upper reaches in excess of crop needs and passed downstream would not lower upstream yields but would greatly raise downstream yields. Hence, if farmers follow unconstrained preferences, they apply far more water than is economically justified (p. 158).

| Water use per hectare | Feet |
|---|---|
| Realistic actual use in major projects (total) | 12–15 |
| — major (maha) season | 5–6 |
| — minor (yala) season | 7–9 |
| Best recorded (tightly controlled pilot) | 8.4–10.2 |
| Mahaweli 1969 plan (two crops) | 8.3 |
| Mahaweli 1977 re-estimate | about 7.5 |

(pp. 158–159). The plans assumed water would be treated as scarce and strictly allocated, and Ostrom says neither presumption was appropriate (p. 159). Reaching planned levels would need high farmer organization, and central efforts have not changed incentives. The dominant behaviour is to take as much water as the fields will hold, legally or illegally, and to stay out of efforts that would impose limits. Upstream irrigators harm downstream ones, and the lack of reliable rules raises production and transaction costs for all (p. 159).

**Kirindi Oya (pp. 160–166).** Built by the British (1876 on the left bank, a similar scheme thirty years later on the right, completed 1920), it serves about 2,500 acres. Most irrigators are poor tenants; many large landowners live elsewhere (p. 160). Rain (50–75 inches) is concentrated in October–December, and water is released from shallow bunds in brief bursts (p. 160).

- **1920–1958, dual executive.** The Irrigation Department handled the tank, main canal and release (fixed dates, fixed salaries). A separate line ran through the *vel vidane*, the lowest official, who was beholden to the larger landowners, was paid a share of the yield, and could enforce water rules. He reported to an assistant government agent (AGA) of the Revenue Department (pp. 160–161). The two departments clashed. AGAs delayed releases to save the first crop, which often meant the second crop failed and maintenance deteriorated, and large landowners manipulated the conflict (pp. 161–162). A strong AGA in the early 1920s made the system work: coordination plus predictable releases gave *vel vidanes* strong motives to enforce discipline (p. 162).
- **1958 change** (to be more democratic): the Revenue Department left cultivation; *vel vidanes* were replaced by administrative secretaries of Cultivation Committees. Each committee set its own cultivation dates. This raised flexibility but lowered coordination across canals, and releases spread over a long season mean more seepage from unlined canals (p. 162). The secretary had fewer enforcement powers and was paid a fixed per-acre assessment whether or not land was cultivated. He needed farmers' votes but no longer answered to outside agencies (p. 163).
- **Decline.** Large landowners often captured committee posts. One technical assistant, one maintenance overseer and two water-issue laborers covered 11 major outlets and over 2,500 acres. Farmers blocked channels easily, and neighbours who were harmed did not censure those who benefited. From 1973 to 1977 the minister of agriculture appointed committee members, so the local MP effectively controlled the appointments. By the mid-1970s control over water theft was "virtually nonexistent": 200 poaching reports, none pursued; one unofficial channel watered 80 acres for about fifteen years (p. 163). Several secretaries said they filed cases for unpaid acreage taxes (their pay) but not for poaching. The chief engineer concluded that effectively no law was left (p. 164).
- **1977 and 1980:** committees were abolished and replaced by appointed cultivation officers. An elected track leader post in 1980 is anomalous (chosen and paid by farmers but under officers' orders), so no one but farmers can allocate water or coordinate in some villages (p. 164).

**Dominant strategies (p. 164).** Farmers flood their fields whatever the means, since others steal water with impunity. Large landowners keep political contacts for protection. Politicians interfere in order to provide "spoils". Irrigation staff have few incentives to work on operation and maintenance: recruitment is by exams, promotion by seniority, status comes from designing and constructing works, and pay is higher on construction. Moore's analysis (pp. 164–165): recruitment impedes interaction with cultivators; exam performance is not linked to work performance; there are few incentives; and the bureaucracy's organization "consistently if unwittingly" degrades performance. Staff are also overworked and underpaid (p. 165).

**Other projects (pp. 165–166).** It appears that similar problems afflict some other projects in Sri Lanka and elsewhere in Asia and the Third World (p. 165). Long canals with few control structures make measurement and tail-end delivery hard. Control structures are easily tampered with, and two young technical assistants said they feared assault. Police have usually treated water offences as trivial, fines are very light, and connected irrigators may never be prosecuted (p. 165). Many settlements are heterogeneous, and settlers were selected for being landless with large families; allotments are supposed by law to pass intact to the next generation. This fuels sibling rivalry, and the share of young men staying on family farms has fallen to 10–15% in some projects. Using water to cut weeding labor seems to make economic sense for one family, but subsidized water costs more than the marginal cost of underused labor (pp. 165–166).

**Why self-governance fails (p. 166).** Ostrom says the lack of capacity for self-governance appears to stem from internal factors and from the regime structure. Internal factors: (1) very many farmers; (2) mostly poor, recently recruited settlers with little attachment to land or each other; (3) extreme ethnic and cultural diversity; (4) wealthier farmers can control water by illegal or questionable means, so potential leaders can look after themselves without leading; (5) no physical control structures. These are "exacerbated" by spoils politics at the centre, which will not enforce rules impartially. Those who want to avoid enforcement can obtain central help, undermining new local institutions (p. 166).

Is the situation "inexorable tragedy"? Ostrom answers that unless local institutions change a great deal, a firm yes is the only answer. Whether local institutions can change is the key question. Gal Oya leads her to a "qualified affirmative answer": "grim, but not hopeless" (pp. 166–167).

**Gal Oya left bank (pp. 167–173).** The system was completed in 1950. The left bank (GOLB) was designed for about 65,000 acres and about 19,000 farmers. By the late 1970s Uphoff called it a "hydrological nightmare": broken, silted channels, destroyed controls, a smaller irrigated area, and endemic mistrust. Farmers saw local Irrigation Department (ID) staff as corrupt; ID engineers saw farmers as part of the problem. Tail-end farmers were mostly Tamil and upstream farmers Sinhalese (p. 167). The original design called for regimentation and law enforcement. The final plan stressed organizing farmers to supply free labor. The Agrarian Research and Training Institute (ARTI) with Cornell's Rural Development Committee rejected the plan's goal of devising and testing a single model of "farmer organization" for all farmers within four years. They chose "catalysts", institutional organizers (IOs): college graduates (many then unemployed) with farm backgrounds, from large settlements if possible, who had to be willing to live in the area (pp. 167–168).

IOs got about six weeks of training, worked in teams of four or five per distributary canal, and met weekly (p. 168). Each IO first met each farmer on a field canal, surveyed the area, then met small groups informally to plan self-help. The IO formed working committees for specific problems (a gate, a silted channel), identified problems for ID officials, and only after farmers had benefited from group action helped them select a farmer-representative by consensus (pp. 168–169). Organization was started before physical rehabilitation, so farmers could discuss designs with engineers. ARTI/Cornell argued to ID officials that labor contributions were likelier if farmers were consulted at design (p. 169).

The structure (p. 169):
- **Field channel organization (FCO):** about 12–15 farmers, a problem-solving unit, often with no regular meetings, agendas or records.
- **Distributory channel organization (DCO):** 200–800 acres, about 100–300 farmers; a general assembly and committees of FCO representatives; officials chosen by consensus, nonpartisan.
- **Area council:** four areas at branch-canal level.
- **Project committee:** farmers can take part directly in policy discussion.

**Results (pp. 169–171).**

| Indicator | Reported result |
|---|---|
| Field representatives saying rotation gives equity | 98% |
| Farmers feeling assured of adequate water under rotation | 79% |
| Farmers calling channel-clearing record poor | 80% before FCOs; 6% in 1986 |
| Farmers reporting no water conflict in their field channel (1985) | 77% |
| GOLB officials saying official–farmer relations improved | over 70% |

(pp. 170–171). Rotations often involved deliberate efforts by upstream (head-end) farmers to make water available to tail-enders. On a channel straddling Sinhalese and Tamil areas (unmaintained for years, with past murders over water), 15 Tamil and 12 Sinhalese farmers cleared it in three days. This brought 1,000 more acres under cultivation, benefiting 300 families who harvested two rice crops (p. 170). Opposition from powerful farmers had disappeared by 1983, and it was not unusual to find farmers from all parties holding offices. In the 1981 communal violence, Sinhalese farmer-representatives went to the homes of Tamil ID officials to protect them (p. 171).

**Weaknesses (p. 171).** Farmers were to build field channels unpaid, while private contractors profited from similar work. Only 30–60% of field channels were completed. IO turnover was 95%, some IOs had inadequate training, supervision was thin, and ID itself could not keep to its planned schedule. The program's modest cost was "more than offset" by yield increases from water rotation (p. 171).

**Lessons (pp. 172–173).** Farmers and officials alone probably would not have overcome the situation without external intervention, but this was not central regimentation. Trust and reciprocity were built face to face before larger organizations. Formal organizations followed no single externally authorized model. Consensus was the dominant decision rule at all tiers. Taking day-to-day problem solving out of politicized channels was important. External agents "may" help appropriators overcome perverse incentives even after generations of distrust. The intervention need not be large or expensive, and large numbers of catalysts need not stay in the field for long; but for such a program to succeed, farmers and officials must come to see the organizations as legitimate and permanent (pp. 172–173).

*Intuition note:* In Kirindi Oya every actor does the sensible thing for himself, and the sum is bad. At Gal Oya outsiders did not impose rules. They helped small groups win small, visible gains, which helped build trust, and bigger organizations came after that trust was nourished.

### The fragility of Nova Scotian inshore fisheries (pp. 173–178)
**Fragile institutions** are still in use and effective, but sit in a broader setting that makes their continued use doubtful (p. 173).

**Port Lameron Harbour (A. Davis).** Almost all 99 fishers descend from settlers of the late eighteenth century. 10 of the 52 boats fish offshore (image of p. 173 reads this way). Inshore boats cost under $30,000, offshore around $50,000. Average crews are 1.8 and 2.5. The inshore fishery runs from late March to December (p. 173). The inshore zone extends about 25 km out and 20 km along the coast. It is divided into subzones, each for a technology, on pragmatic grounds (for example, gill nets for herring and mackerel go beyond the harbor but near shore, because nearer in they would restrict travel in and out of the harbor and farther out the strong currents could destroy them). Four offshore captains bought ground-fish gill nets in 1975 when federal subsidies were offered. Their use interfered with others, and all four had disposed of the gear by 1980 (p. 174). Zoning reduces externalities and is a low-cost apportioning system. A rule based on an observable factor (technology in use) is much cheaper to monitor than one based on quantity of fish harvested (p. 174).

Claims rest on tenure. Fishers see lobster territory (up to 40% of yearly income) as exclusively theirs. They claim first access (and the right to refuse access in scarce years) to the remaining zones. All fishers police boundaries, and local fishers must bear the cost because they cannot call on external authorities. Davis's example: a Port Lameron fisher whose long-line gear was crossed by an outsider radioed to complain. Others listened in and added approving remarks, and the implied threat of cutting the gear brought an apology (p. 175).

**Why fragile (pp. 175–177).** The rules are not recognized by federal authorities (the Department of Fisheries and Oceans). Earlier, the provinces played a much more important role in regulating inshore fisheries, particularly Newfoundland, whose regulations basically codified local fishing rules into law (p. 175). Federal policy takes the opposite stance: it gives "little credence" to customary regulation and presumes the entire coast is open access. Ostrom ties this to the two-option view of Ch. 1 (private property or government regulation); with private property ruled out, the federal government keeps the regulation role (pp. 175–176). Canada claimed 200-mile jurisdiction in 1976, which helped with open-access offshore fisheries and encouraged planners to think they could "rationalize" all aspects including inshore (p. 176).

Licensing steps led fishers to fear a coming reduction in fisher numbers, so inactive people and Port Lameron fishers took out "defensive" licenses. Copes reports Newfoundland registrations rose from 15,351 (1974) to 35,080 (1980) with an estimated 21,297 persons actually fishing (p. 176). When federal officials froze herring gill-net licenses without notice, conflict "exploded" and protests ran along the coast. Officials backed off long enough for licenses to be issued. Fishers' view was that officials act arbitrarily and without local knowledge (pp. 176–177).

**Ostrom's assessment (p. 177).** Policy has been to develop one standard set of regulations rather than strengthen local systems. Further counterproductive reactions may mean failing to control the open-access deep-sea fishery and losing control of some inshore fisheries. If officials developed nested rules that help enforce local regulations and focused new effort on the far-offshore fisheries, the local rule system could survive indefinitely. If one policy continues, "eventual deterioration" seems probable. She doubts any national agency can have the time-and-place information to tailor rules (p. 177).

**Other examples (pp. 177–178).** Bahia, Brazil: poor black raft fishers' sea tenure is unrecognized, and national codes make waters open to any Brazilian boat registered in a Brazilian port (Cordell and McKean). Nepal: the "Private Forests Nationalization Act" was proclaimed to protect forests but disrupted communal control. Messerschmidt reports villagers began free riding by over-exploiting forests. In 1978 Nepal reversed policy and encouraged return of forest land to village control, with quite encouraging forestation results (p. 178). Ostrom says similar stories can be told for many other parts of the world.

### Lessons to be learned from comparing the cases (pp. 178–181)
Two analyses are planned. First, compare the cases with Ch. 3's design principles. If failed and fragile cases show principles similar to robust ones, the principles might be rejected as unhelpful for telling robust, fragile and failed institutions apart. Second, analyse situational and regime factors affecting the capacity to change institutions (that is Ch. 6) (p. 178).

Ostrom says the long-enduring Ch. 3 cases fit by construction, because the principles summarize them. She presumes the Raymond, West and Central basin institutions robust, since they have survived 30–40 years. Failures show none, one, two, or three principles. No more than three characterize any case where appropriators were clearly unable to solve their problems (p. 179). She treats Alanya as fragile. Its rules solve an assignment problem but not entry. If more fishers wanted access, Mawelle-type rent dissipation could arise. In the past, collective choices were made partly through a local co-op and partly through the coffeehouse; without a regular arena for collective choice it would be difficult to adjust rules if conditions changed (p. 179). Gal Oya has boundaries, congruent monitored rules and collective-choice arenas, but she is unwilling to call it robust until farmers' rights are recognized and guaranteed and conflict-resolution mechanisms exist. A new ID leadership that presumed farmers had little to offer could endanger it (p. 181).

Fragile cases are intermediate: enough principles for immediate problems, but one would be hesitant to predict endurance without further institutional development toward the full set (p. 181). The set of cases is limited. More empirical and theoretical work is needed before high confidence. She and colleagues are collecting a larger set of cases to see if the pattern in Table 5.2 holds. Table 5.2 gives enough support that further analysis "appear[s] warranted" (p. 181).

*Intuition note:* The table is a check, not a proof: the principles came from the robust cases, so the real test is whether failed and fragile cases lack them. The failures mostly lack them, and the fragile cases have some of them. Ostrom keeps her claim modest.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A fishery has a fixed yearly catch worth 480 units (simplifying assumption: extra nets add no catch; Mawelle's marginal product was negative, p. 153). Each net costs 40 units a year. Net profit ("rent") = 480 − 40 × (number of nets).
- 5 nets: each gross 480 ÷ 5 = 96, net 96 − 40 = 56; total rent 480 − 200 = 280.
- 6 nets: each gross 480 ÷ 6 = 80, net 40; total rent 480 − 240 = 240.
- 10 nets: each gross 48, net 8; total rent 480 − 400 = 80.
- 12 nets: each gross 40, net 0; total rent 480 − 480 = 0 (rent fully dissipated).

At 5 nets, a new entrant earns 80 − 40 = 40 > 0, so entry looks attractive to him, even though total rent falls from 280 to 240. Entry continues while a new net earns above zero, until profit per net is about zero at 12 nets. An entry limit at 5 nets keeps everyone at 56, but only if no one can get an official to look away. The text's Mawelle story is that outsiders did exactly that (pp. 154–157).

## Concepts
- [[rent-dissipation]] (new) — entry continues until fishery revenue no longer exceeds cost, so the surplus is lost (pp. 144–145, 150)
- [[common-pool-resource-cpr]] — shared resource (fishery, groundwater, irrigation water, forest) where users' rules matter (p. 143)
- [[design-principles-ostrom]] — the Ch. 3 features of robust institutions, here used as a check on failed and fragile cases (p. 178)
- [[fragile-institution]] (new) — locally devised rules still working, but set in a regime that makes their survival doubtful (p. 173)
- [[free-rider]] — someone who benefits from restrictions on others without accepting them; here seen in Mawelle, Nepal (pp. 157, 178)
- [[collective-choice-arena]] (new) — a place where appropriators can discuss and change their own rules; its absence recurs in the failures (pp. 146, 156, 179)
- [[institutions]] — rules of the game; here also the administrative arrangements governing irrigation (pp. 160–164)
- [[institutional-organizer-catalyst]] (new) — outsider who helps farmers solve small problems and form organizations bottom-up (pp. 168–169, 172)
- [[external-regime-autonomy]] (new) — whether outside governments recognize and enforce local rule-making; in this chapter's cases it often does not (pp. 156–157, 175)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Mawelle's end-run via the MP and revenue officer ↔ political science: patronage politics weakening agents' enforcement of rules a local majority wants
- [structural-analogy] Kirindi Oya's dominant strategies at every level (farmers, landowners, politicians, engineers) ↔ collective action problem: a stable but inefficient outcome that no actor can change alone
- [structural-analogy] Table 5.2 (14 cases, yes/weak/no coding) ↔ research design: small-N comparison testing a theory's implications against failure cases

## Questions this raises
- Table 5.2 codes cases with "yes/weak/no". How reliable is that coding, and could a different coder get a different pattern?
- Which situational and regime factors let appropriators change their own rules (Ch. 6 analyses this)?
- Gal Oya depended on outside catalysts. How long do such organizations last once the catalysts leave?
- If private ownership emerged at Mawelle-type villages because the external regime blocked local enforcement, what other outcomes would a recognizing regime have allowed?
