# Hayek (1945), "The Use of Knowledge in Society"

## L0 — One line
The economic problem of society is not merely allocating "given" resources but using knowledge that is dispersed among many people, and the price system does this by passing on only the information each person needs.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Hayek asks what problem a rational economic order must solve. Standard economic calculus assumes one mind has all the data. Hayek argues that real knowledge is never concentrated: it exists as scattered, incomplete bits, especially knowledge of local circumstances of time and place. Economic problems arise from change, and central statistics cannot capture such knowledge. Decisions must therefore be decentralised, but each decision-maker still needs to fit into the whole. Prices do this: they condense information about relative scarcity and guide people without anyone surveying the whole. Hayek concludes that equilibrium analysis starts from assumed knowledge and so skips the real problem, which is how a market process communicates and acquires knowledge.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand; the idea of substitutes; marginal rate of substitution (the rate at which a person trades one good for another); a rough idea of central planning versus markets.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The textbook optimum problem is a matter of pure logic if all data are given to one mind. Society's real problem is different: the data are never so given (§ I).
- Planning is unavoidable. The dispute is central versus decentralised planning, and efficiency depends mainly on which uses existing knowledge better (§ II).
- Scientific knowledge is not all knowledge. Unorganised knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place is socially valuable. It can be used only if the decisions that depend on it are left to the people who hold it or made with their active cooperation (§ III).
- Economic problems arise from change. Statistics lump items together, so they cannot carry the knowledge needed for fast adaptation (§ IV).
- Decentralisation is needed, but local deciders also need to know relative scarcities. Prices condense this, like a few dials on a machine, so participants need very little knowledge to act well (§§ V–VI).
- Hayek calls the price system's working a "marvel" that evolved without design. Adaptation need not be "perfect" in the equilibrium sense (§ VI).
- Schumpeter's claim that consumers' valuations "ipso facto" fix factor values wrongly assumes all facts are in one mind. Equilibrium analysis is only a preliminary to the main problem (§ VII).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. The problem restated
- **Familiar assumptions:** we have all relevant information, a given system of preferences, and complete knowledge of available means. Then the problem is purely logic. The answer is implicit in the assumptions (§ I).
- The conditions of the optimum are known: marginal rates of substitution between any two commodities or factors must be equal in all their different uses (§ I).
- Hayek says this is "emphatically not" society's problem. The data are never "given" to a single mind and can never be (§ I).
- Knowledge exists only as dispersed bits that are incomplete and often contradictory (§ I).
- So the problem is how to secure the best use of resources known to any member of society, for ends whose relative importance only these individuals know. It is a problem of using knowledge not given to anyone in its totality (§ I).
- Hayek thinks recent theory, especially some uses of mathematics, has obscured this. He suggests that many current disputes have a common origin in a misconception about the economic problem of society, due to an erroneous transfer to social phenomena of habits of thought from natural science (§ I).

*Intuition note:* A calculator can solve a puzzle only when the numbers are typed in. Hayek says that in society the numbers are scattered across many separate heads, so the hard part is getting them used.

### II. Planning: who does it?
- All economic activity is "planning", meaning interrelated decisions on allocating resources. With many collaborators, plans must partly rest on knowledge held by someone else (§ II).
- The dispute is not whether to plan, but whether centrally, by one authority, or among many individuals. Competition means decentralised planning. Delegation to organised industries, which Hayek equates with monopoly, is a "halfway house" few like (§ II).
- Which system is more efficient depends mainly on which makes fuller use of existing knowledge. Either all knowledge reaches one authority, or individuals receive the extra knowledge they need to fit their plans together (§ II).

### III. Kinds of knowledge
- The answer depends on the relative importance of different kinds of knowledge (§ III).
- Hayek admits that for scientific knowledge a body of chosen experts may be best placed, though this shifts the difficulty to selecting the experts. He says this is only a small part of the wider problem (§ III).
- The other kind is unorganised knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place. Practically every individual has some advantage here, but it can be used only if decisions are left to him or made with his active cooperation (§ III).
- Examples: knowing of an underused machine, a skill, or a surplus stock. The shipper who fills half-empty tramp-steamer journeys, the estate agent who knows temporary opportunities, and the arbitrageur who profits from local price differences all perform useful functions (§ III).
- Hayek finds it curious that such knowledge is regarded with contempt. He thinks this prejudice affects attitudes to commerce compared with production. He says even economists err when they treat such knowledge as "given" (§ III).
- The reproach of irrationality against the existing order often rests on this knowledge not being available to everyone. This overlooks that the method for spreading it is the very problem to be solved (§ III).

*Intuition note:* A professor's theory and a dock worker's knowledge of which ship is half empty are both useful. The first is general rules; the second cannot enter statistics sent to a central authority.

### IV. Change and statistics
- Planners and their opponents differ on how significant and frequent changes are. If plans could be fixed for long periods, comprehensive planning would be much easier (§ IV).
- Hayek stresses that economic problems arise "always and only" in consequence of change. If things go on as expected, no new decision is needed. Believing change has become less important therefore implies that economic problems have too (§ IV).
- He says that, as far as he can ascertain, business experience does not support the belief that decisions are needed only rarely. In a competitive industry, keeping costs from rising takes constant struggle, and the same technology can produce at a wide range of costs (§ IV).
- Aggregates look stable, which may make economists forget the small changes. But this stability cannot be explained by the law of large numbers: the number of elements is too small (§ IV).
- Goods keep flowing through "constant deliberate adjustments", such as B stepping in when A fails to deliver. Even a large plant depends on a market environment for tiles, stationery and other unexpected needs (§ IV).
- The knowledge Hayek describes cannot enter statistics. Statistics must lump together items that differ in location and quality, which may matter for a specific decision. So central planning based on statistics cannot take direct account of time and place and must leave such decisions to the man on the spot (§ IV).

### V. Decentralisation and prices
- If we agree that the problem is mainly rapid adaptation to local change, it would seem to follow that ultimate decisions must be left to those familiar with the circumstances. Hayek says it cannot be solved by sending all knowledge to a central board (§ V).
- This answers only part of the problem. The man on the spot also needs information to fit his decisions into the wider system (§ V).
- He needs to know only how much harder or easier things have become to get, or how much more urgently the alternatives are wanted, not why. The causes matter to him only through their effect on his own concrete things (§ V).
- **Analogy from the calculus of choice:** even a single mind running a small, self-contained system could proceed only by using rates of equivalence ("values", marginal rates of substitution). These are numerical indices attached to each scarce resource. They cannot be derived from the thing's own properties, but condense its significance in the whole means-end structure. Using them, he can adjust quantities one by one without solving the whole puzzle from scratch (§ V).
- So in a system with dispersed knowledge, prices can coordinate separate people's actions, as subjective values coordinate one person's plan (§ V).
- **Tin example:** a new use for tin appears, or a source of supply is lost. It does not matter which, and Hayek says this is significant. Users need only know that tin is more profitably used elsewhere, so they must economise. If only some know of the new demand and switch resources, others fill the gaps from other sources. The effect spreads to substitutes and substitutes of substitutes, with most participants knowing nothing of the cause (§ V).
- The whole acts as one market because individual fields of vision overlap enough that information passes through many intermediaries. One price, or local prices linked by transport costs, brings about a solution that, Hayek says, it is "just conceptually possible" a single all-knowing mind might have reached (§ V).

*Intuition note:* A price is a short message: "this is scarcer or more wanted than before, so use less of it." People can act on it without knowing the story behind it.

### VI. The price system as communication
- Hayek says we must see the price system as a mechanism for communicating information. It does this less perfectly as prices grow more rigid. Even then, adjustment may partly work through other terms of the contract (§ VI).
- Its most significant feature is the economy of knowledge: only essential information is passed on, and only to those concerned. Calling it a system of telecommunications, where producers watch a few "dials", is, he says, more than a metaphor (§ VI).
- Adjustments are probably never "perfect" in the equilibrium sense. Hayek fears that assuming near-perfect knowledge has made us somewhat blind to the price mechanism's true function and led to misleading standards of efficiency (§ VI).
- The "marvel" is that, without orders and with perhaps only a handful knowing the cause, tens of thousands of people use a scarce material more sparingly (§ VI).
- Hayek is convinced that if the mechanism were the result of human design, and its users understood that their decisions matter far beyond their immediate aim, it would be acclaimed as a great triumph. Its misfortune is that it is not designed and its users usually do not know why they do what they do (§ VI).
- **Reply to advocates of "conscious direction":** the problem is how to extend the use of resources beyond the span of any one mind's control. That means providing inducements so that individuals do the desirable things without being told (§ VI).
- This is a problem for nearly all social phenomena, including language and cultural inheritance. He calls it the central theoretical problem of all social science (§ VI).
- Whitehead (quoted by Hayek): civilization advances by extending the number of operations we can perform without thinking about them. Hayek applies this to the use of rules and symbols whose meaning we do not understand (§ VI).
- The price system is one such formation, which people stumbled on without understanding. Hayek denies claiming that the best system grew up by miracle. Man developed the division of labour because he happened on a method that allowed it. Otherwise some other kind of civilisation, like the termites' "state", might have arisen (§ VI).
- Nobody has yet designed an alternative that keeps features dear even to its critics, such as free choice of pursuits and free use of one's own knowledge and skill (§ VI).

### VII. The methodological dispute
- The dispute over whether rational calculation needs the price system is no longer only political. Hayek cites Mises (derided 25 years earlier), Trotsky ("economic accounting is unthinkable without market relations"), Lange (who promises Mises a statue), and Lerner (the price system induces individuals, seeking their own interest, to serve the general interest). Hayek reads the remaining dissent as intellectual and methodological (§ VII).
- **Schumpeter's claim (as quoted):** consumers who evaluate consumers' goods "ipso facto" also evaluate the means of production. Hayek links this to a positivist approach that treats phenomena as objectively given quantities, almost without human minds (§ VII).
- **Hayek's reply:** taken literally the claim is untrue. Hayek reads "ipso facto" as presumably meaning that factor valuation follows necessarily from consumer valuation, and says this too is not correct: implication can be meaningfully asserted only of propositions present to one mind. Factor values depend also on conditions of supply. Only a mind knowing all the facts could derive the answer (§ VII).
- Showing that all facts, if known to one mind, would determine the solution does not solve the problem. We must show how interactions among people with partial knowledge produce a solution. Assuming all knowledge given to one mind "assumes the problem away" (§ VII).
- Hayek says this is not a simple slip but points to a wrong approach that disregards imperfect knowledge. Any approach, such as that of much of mathematical economics with its simultaneous equations, that starts from the assumption that people's knowledge matches the objective facts systematically leaves out what is our main task to explain (§ VII).
- He does not deny a useful role for equilibrium analysis. But it does not deal with the social process and is "no more than a useful preliminary" to the main problem (§ VII).
- **Footnote:** Hayek says Pareto and Barone only stated conditions that a rational allocation must satisfy, which match competitive equilibrium conditions. That differs from knowing how to find such an allocation in practice. Hayek says Pareto himself explicitly denied it could be solved without the market (§ VII, note 1).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three firms use tin. Profit per unit of output is revenue minus other costs minus tin cost (tin per unit × price $p$ per kg).
- Firm C: 5 kg per unit; revenue 100; other costs 40. Profit $=60-5p$. Break-even $p=12$.
- Firm A: 2 kg per unit; revenue 50; other costs 20. Profit $=30-2p$. Break-even $p=15$.
- Firm B: 1 kg per unit; revenue 30; other costs 12. Profit $=18-p$. Break-even $p=18$.

At $p=10$: C earns $60-50=10$, A earns $30-20=10$, B earns $18-10=8$. All use tin.
Somewhere, a new buyer bids tin up to $p=13$. Nobody tells the firms why.
- C: $60-5(13)=60-65=-5$, so it cuts tin use.
- A: $30-2(13)=4$, so it keeps going.
- B: $18-13=5$, so it keeps going.

Tin moves toward the uses where a kilogram is worth most (B, A, then C). No firm learned the cause or looked at the others. The price alone carried the needed information.

## Concepts
- [[dispersed-tacit-knowledge]] — Hayek's emphasis: knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place, unorganised, held by individuals, never given in total to anyone. Hayek calls it "unorganized", not "tacit" (§ III).
- [[price-as-signal]] — prices condense information on relative scarcity and pass it, in abbreviated form, only to those concerned (§§ V–VI).
- [[equilibrium]] — Hayek treats equilibrium analysis as a useful preliminary that assumes knowledge matches the facts and so omits the social process (§§ VI–VII).
- [[marginal-reasoning]] — marginal rates of substitution and "values" serve as indices that let even one mind adjust step by step (§§ I, V).
- [[central-vs-decentralized-planning]] (new) — the real dispute: one authority planning everything, versus many individuals planning separately (competition) (§ II).
- [[man-on-the-spot]] (new) — the person with direct knowledge of local circumstances; decisions that depend on them are left to him (§§ IV–V).
- [[economy-of-knowledge]] (new) — how little each participant needs to know to act rightly, because prices pass on only essential information (§ VI).

## Canonical instances
- [[tin-price-example]] — a price change spreads through users, substitutes and substitutes' substitutes without anyone knowing the cause (§ V).
- [[arbitrageur-shipper-estate-agent]] — people whose gains rest on local, fleeting knowledge perform useful functions (§ III).
- [[schumpeter-ipso-facto]] — a case of assuming all facts are given to one mind (§ VII).
- [[pareto-barone-socialist-calculation]] — stating equilibrium conditions is not the same as finding the allocation in practice (§ VII, note 1).
- [[termite-state-counterexample]] — another type of civilisation that might have arisen if man had not stumbled on the price system; it appears in Hayek's reply to those who say he claims the system arose by miracle (§ VI).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Price as condensed message ↔ finance (price as signal): prices carry private information to others who do not know its source.
- [structural-analogy] "Interactions of people each with partial knowledge produce a solution" ↔ agent-based modeling: simulating heterogeneous agents who each hold only local information.
- [metaphor] Prices as "dials" of a telecommunications system ↔ information theory: compression of information into a few signals.

## Questions this raises
- If prices pass on information, who has an incentive to collect the information that prices then reveal?
- How well do prices work when they are rigid? Hayek notes that adjustment may shift to other contract terms (§ VI). When does that suffice?
- Can knowledge that is not "statistical" be reported to a planner in some other form, or only used on the spot?
- If equilibrium models assume knowledge matches the facts, what would a model of the process of learning look like?
