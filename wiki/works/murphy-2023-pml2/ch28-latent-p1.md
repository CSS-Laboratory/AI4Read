# Latent factor models, part 1: mixtures and factor analysis

## L0 — One line
A latent factor model explains observed data x through a few hidden factors z via a simple decoder, and by changing the prior on z and the likelihood of x you obtain mixtures, factor analysis, PPCA, CCA and many other classical models.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter 27 motivates discovery: infer meaningful hidden causes z of data x, using a prior to resolve ill-posed inversion. Chapter 28 builds a family of "shallow" latent factor models, $z\sim p(z)$, $x|z\sim$ ExpFam$(f(z))$ with a linear decoder. First come mixture models (GMMs, Bernoulli mixtures, Gaussian scale mixtures) with applications to image restoration and classification. Then factor analysis (FA): a Gaussian with low-rank-plus-diagonal covariance, with EM fitting, rotation unidentifiability, probabilistic PCA, mixtures of FA, paired-data models (supervised PCA, PLS, CCA), exponential-family and nonlinear extensions. The unit stops at the start of GP-LVM.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian conditioning and marginals; Bayes' rule; EM; eigendecomposition; matrix inversion/determinant lemmas; exponential family; log-likelihood and MAP.

**Terms used above:** decoder = the map from $z$ to the mean (parameters) of $x$; ExpFam = exponential family; GMM = Gaussian mixture model; FA = factor analysis; PPCA = probabilistic PCA; PLS = partial least squares; CCA = canonical correlation analysis; MAP = maximum a posteriori estimate; ill-posed = many latent values give the same observation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Discovery uses latent variable models whose latents are meaningful to the user. The inverse map x→z is often ill-posed, so a prior is needed (p. 927).
- A mixture model is a discrete latent $z$ chosen with probability $\pi_k$, then $x\sim p_k$. Marginalising $z$ gives a convex combination of components. The posterior over $z$ ("responsibility") gives soft or hard clustering (pp. 929–931).
- Gaussian scale mixtures write Student-t, Laplace, spike-and-slab and horseshoe as Gaussians with random scale, which is often computationally convenient (pp. 932–934).
- A GMM prior on image patches plus a linear-Gaussian likelihood can be used for denoising, deblurring and inpainting via (approximate) MAP estimation, which works well empirically (pp. 934–936).
- Mixture classifiers can be trained with a hybrid generative/discriminative loss (p. 938). Numerical care (log-sum-exp, low-rank lemmas) is needed (pp. 938–939).
- Mixture parameters are unidentifiable because of label switching, so computing posterior means of cluster parameters such as $\mu_k$ from Bayesian inference is meaningless (p. 939).
- FA gives $p(x)=\mathcal N(\mu, WW^T+\Psi)$ with $O(LD)$ parameters, with closed-form posterior and EM. Rotating the factors ($W\to WR$, $R$ orthogonal) leaves the likelihood unchanged, so $W$ and the latent factors are not uniquely identifiable (pp. 940–944).
- PPCA ($\Psi=\sigma^2 I$) has a closed-form MLE and recovers PCA as $\sigma^2\to0$. Mixtures of FA are locally linear, and with SGD they give competitive face generators with exact likelihood (pp. 944–953).
- Supervised PCA, PLS and CCA are FA with shared or private latent subspaces. Exponential-family and nonlinear versions need approximate inference (pp. 954–958).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 27 Discovery methods: an overview

**27.1 Introduction.** Prediction and generation are useful for discovery but not sufficient. Neural nets often learn features that are hard to interpret and unstable to training details (p. 927). The approach here is a latent variable model: observed $x$ is generated by (often low-dimensional) latent factors $z$, assumed meaningful to the user, so evaluation needs domain expertise (p. 927).

Example: inferring a 3D scene $z$ from an image $x$. The map $z\to x$ can be many-to-one ("perceptual aliasing"), so $x\to z$ is ill-posed and a prior $p(z)$ is needed. A simple choice is the MAP estimate (p. 927):
$$\hat z(x)=\arg\max_z p(z|x)=\arg\max_z \log p(z)+\log p(x|z)\tag{27.1}$$
In vision this is "vision as inverse graphics" or "analysis by synthesis" (Fig. 27.1, p. 928). Often parameters $\theta$ must also be inferred from $D=\{x_n\}$, and sometimes the model structure too (p. 927).

**27.2 Overview of Part V (p. 928).** Ch. 28: simple LVMs for fixed-dimensional vectors. Ch. 29: sequences. Ch. 30: graphs. Ch. 31: non-parametric Bayesian models, where complexity grows with data. Ch. 32: representation learning with neural networks. Ch. 33: interpreting predictive models.

*Intuition note.* When many hidden states give the same observation, data alone cannot pick one. A prior $p(z)$ is then needed to make the estimate well-defined (p. 927).

### 28 Latent factor models

**28.1 Introduction (p. 929).** An LVM is any model with some variables always hidden. Here: $z\sim p(z)$ (28.1), $x|z\sim\mathrm{Expfam}(x|f(z))$ (28.2), where $f$ is the *decoder* (linear in this chapter). It resembles a GLM whose input is hidden. Changing $p(z)$ and $p(x|z)$ gives the classical models of Table 28.1 (p. 930). The focus is posterior inference of meaningful latents, not realistic sampling (p. 929).

| Model | $p(z)$ | $p(x\mid z)$ |
|---|---|---|
| FA/PCA | $\mathcal N(z\mid0,I)$ | $\mathcal N(x\mid Wz,\Psi)$ |
| GMM | $\sum_c \mathrm{Cat}(c\mid\pi)\mathcal N(z\mid\mu_c,\Sigma_c)$ | $\mathcal N(x\mid Wz,\Psi)$ |
| MixFA | $\mathrm{Cat}(c\mid\pi)\mathcal N(z\mid0,I)$ | $\mathcal N(x\mid W_cz+\mu_c,\Psi_c)$ |
| NMF | $\prod_k \mathrm{Ga}(z_k\mid\alpha_k,\beta_k)$ | $\prod_d \mathrm{Poi}(x_d\mid\exp(w_d^Tz))$ |
| Simplex FA (mPCA) | $\mathrm{Dir}(z\mid\alpha)$ | $\prod_d \mathrm{Cat}(x_d\mid W_dz)$ |
| LDA | $\mathrm{Dir}(z\mid\alpha)$ | $\prod_d \mathrm{Cat}(x_d\mid Wz)$ |
| ICA | $\prod_d \mathrm{Laplace}(z_d\mid\lambda)$ | $\prod_d\delta(x_d-w_d^Tz)$ |
| Sparse coding | $\prod_k\mathrm{Laplace}(z_k\mid\lambda)$ | $\prod_d\mathcal N(x_d\mid w_d^Tz,\sigma^2)$ |

(Table 28.1, p. 930. $k=1{:}L$ indexes latents, $d=1{:}D$ indexes observed dimensions. ICA needs $L=D$. Only the FA/PCA, GMM and MixFA rows are treated in this unit; the other rows have later sections.)

### 28.2 Mixture models

**Definition (p. 929–930).** A convex combination of simple distributions:
$$p(x|\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_kp_k(x),\quad 0\le\pi_k\le1,\ \sum_k\pi_k=1\tag{28.3}$$
Equivalent hierarchical form with latent $z\in\{1..K\}$:
$$p(z|\theta)=\mathrm{Cat}(z|\pi),\qquad p(x|z=k,\theta)=p(x|\theta_k)\tag{28.4–28.5}$$
Generative story: sample component $z$, then sample $x$ from it. Marginalising $z$ recovers (28.3):
$$p(x|\theta)=\sum_k p(z=k|\theta)p(x|z=k,\theta)=\sum_k\pi_kp(x|\theta_k)\tag{28.6}$$

**28.2.1 GMMs (pp. 930–932).** $p(x)=\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(x|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$ (28.7). With enough components a GMM can approximate any smooth density on $\mathbb R^D$ (p. 930). For clustering one first fits $\hat\theta$ (usually the MLE, via EM or SGD), then computes the posterior over each point's cluster by Bayes' rule:
$$r_{nk}\triangleq p(z_n=k|x_n,\theta)=\frac{p(z_n=k|\theta)p(x_n|z_n=k,\theta)}{\sum_{k'}p(z_n=k'|\theta)p(x_n|z_n=k',\theta)}\tag{28.8}$$
$r_{nk}$ is the *responsibility* of cluster $k$ for point $n$. Hard clustering takes
$$\hat z_n=\arg\max_k r_{nk}=\arg\max_k[\log p(x_n|z_n=k,\theta)+\log p(z_n=k|\theta)]\tag{28.9}$$
Using responsibilities fractionally is soft clustering (p. 931). With a uniform prior over $z_n$ and spherical Gaussians $\Sigma_k=I$, hard clustering reduces to
$$z_n=\arg\min_k\|x_n-\hat\mu_k\|_2^2\tag{28.10}$$
That is, assign each point to its nearest centroid. This is the basis of K-means (p. 932).

**28.2.2 Bernoulli mixtures (p. 932).** For binary data, $p(x|z=k,\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D\mathrm{Ber}(y_d|\mu_{dk})=\prod_d\mu_{dk}^{y_d}(1-\mu_{dk})^{1-y_d}$ (28.11), where $\mu_{dk}$ is the probability that bit $d$ is on in cluster $k$. Fit with $K=20$ to MNIST without labels, the cluster means look like digits. Some digits appear several times, because the model does not know the true number of classes (Fig. 28.3, p. 932).

**28.2.3 Gaussian scale mixtures (GSMs) (pp. 932–934).** Let $x=\epsilon z$, $z\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2)$, $\epsilon\sim p(\epsilon)$ (multiplicative noise). Then $x|\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\epsilon^2\sigma_0^2)$ and
$$p(x)=\int\mathcal N(x|0,\sigma_0^2\epsilon^2)p(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon\tag{28.12}$$
Different $p(\epsilon)$ give different distributions. The expanded parameterisation (keeping $\epsilon$ explicit) is often computationally convenient, since given $\epsilon$ the model is Gaussian (p. 933). Examples:
- **Student-t:**
$$\mathcal T(x|0,\sigma^2,\nu)=\int_0^\infty\mathcal N(x|0,z\sigma^2)\,\mathrm{IG}(z|\tfrac\nu2,\tfrac\nu2)\,dz=\int_0^\infty\mathcal N(x|0,z\sigma^2)\chi^{-2}(z|\nu,1)\,dz\tag{28.13}$$
It is an infinite superposition of Gaussians of different widths, with wider tails than a Gaussian of the same variance. It tends to a Gaussian as $\nu\to\infty$, because the inverse Gamma becomes a delta function (p. 933).
- **Laplace:** an infinite weighted sum of Gaussians with gamma-distributed precision:
$$\mathrm{Laplace}(x|0,\lambda)=\int\mathcal N(x|0,\tau^2)\,\mathrm{Ga}(\tau^2|1,\tfrac{\lambda^2}{2})\,d\tau^2\tag{28.14}$$
- **Spike and slab:** $\epsilon\sim\mathrm{Ber}(\pi)$, so $\epsilon^2=\epsilon$ and
$$p(x)=\sum_{\epsilon\in\{0,1\}}\mathcal N(x|0,\sigma_0^2\epsilon)p(\epsilon)=\pi\mathcal N(x|0,\sigma_0^2)+(1-\pi)\delta_0(x)\tag{28.15}$$
The $\delta_0$ is the spike. The Gaussian acts like a "uniform slab" for large enough $\sigma_0$. Useful in sparse modelling (p. 933).
- **Horseshoe:** $\epsilon\sim\mathcal C_+(1)$ (half-Cauchy). It has a spike at 0 like Student and Laplace, but heavy tails that do not asymptote to zero. So it kills small parameters without over-regularising large ones (pp. 933–934).

*Intuition note.* A "random-width Gaussian" is a mixture with a continuum of components. Choosing how the width is distributed decides whether you get heavy tails or exact zeros.

**28.2.4 GMMs as prior for inverse imaging (pp. 934–937).** Infer a clean image $x$ from corrupted $y$ with a linear-Gaussian forward model
$$p(y|x)=\mathcal N(y|Wx,\sigma^2I)\tag{28.16}$$
with known $W$ (p. 935): $W=I$ for additive noise (denoising), a convolution kernel for blur (deblurring), a diagonal matrix with zeros at occluded places (inpainting), a convolution kernel for downsampling (super-resolution). The inversion is ill-posed (many $x$ give the same $y$), so a prior is needed (p. 935).

[ZW11] partition the image into patches with a GMM prior $p(x_i)=\sum_kp(c_i=k)\mathcal N(x_i|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)$, $K=200$ components, fitted on 2M clean patches (p. 935). The MAP component comes from the marginal likelihood (Eq. 2.129):
$$c_i^*=\arg\max_c p(c)p(y_i|c)=\arg\max_cp(c)\,\mathcal N(y_i|W\mu_c,\sigma^2I+W\Sigma_cW^T)\tag{28.17}$$
and the patch posterior is approximated by
$$p(x_i|y_i)\approx p(x_i|y_i,c_i^*)\propto\mathcal N(x_i|\mu_{c_i^*},\Sigma_{c_i^*})\mathcal N(y_i|Wx_i,\sigma^2I)\tag{28.18}$$
computed by Bayes' rule for Gaussians (Eq. 2.121c). For whole images [ZW11] minimise
$$E(x|y)=\frac1{2\sigma^2}\|Wx-y\|^2-\mathrm{EPLL}(x),\qquad \mathrm{EPLL}(x)=\sum_i\log p(P_ix)\tag{28.19–28.20}$$
($P_i$ projects out patch $i$.) Overlapping patches overcount pixels, so this is not a valid likelihood, but optimising it (by "half quadratic splitting") works well empirically (p. 935; Fig. 28.4, p. 934). Principled alternatives: a multiscale model [PE16], or Gibbs sampling over overlapping patches [FW21]. The latter yields posterior samples from $p(x|y)$, which can look better than the mean or mode. For example, if $y$ is a grey-scale version of $x$, the MAP estimate is grey but posterior samples are colour images (p. 936).

*Why it works (28.2.4.1, p. 936).* The log prior for a patch in component $k$ (as printed, with zero mean) is
$$\log p(x_i|c_i=k)=\log\mathcal N(x_i|0,\Sigma_k)=-x_i^T\Sigma_k^{-1}x_i+a_k\tag{28.21}$$
With $\Sigma_k=V_k\Lambda_kV_k^T$ (eigenvalues $\lambda_{k,d}$, eigenvectors $v_{k,d}$):
$$\log p(x_i|c_i=k)=-\sum_{d=1}^D\frac1{\lambda_{k,d}}(v_{k,d}^Tx_i)^2+a_k\tag{28.22}$$
So the eigenvectors act as templates, each with a weight (the eigenvalue). Each component has its own template set (Fig. 28.5), and mixing components gives a powerful model of natural patches (p. 936).

*Speed-ups (28.2.4.2, p. 937).* Store solved pairs $(y,f(y))$ and train an amortised inference network on them [RW15]. Alternatively, drop the generative model and train a discriminative $\hat f(y)$ on artificially corrupted pairs $(y=C(x),x)$. This works very well but is limited to the corruptions $C$ it was trained on, so one network per linear operator $W$ and sometimes per noise level (p. 937).

*Blind inverse problems (28.2.4.3, p. 937).* If $W$ is unknown, inference is much harder. One approach is EM-like on a set of images from the same likelihood: E step $\hat x=\arg\max_xp(x|y,\hat W)$, M step $\hat W=\arg\max_Wp(y|\hat x,W)$ [Ani+18] (they use a GAN prior). With two independent noisy samples $y_1,y_2$ of the same $x$, one can learn the posterior-mode estimator directly, using a form of cycle consistency [XC19] (p. 937).

**28.2.5 Mixtures for classification (pp. 937–939).** A mixture defines the class-conditional density in a generative classifier:
$$p(y=c|x)=\frac{p(y=c)p(x|y=c)}{\sum_{c'}p(y=c')p(x|y=c')}=\frac{p(y=c)p(x|y=c)}{Z}\tag{28.23}$$
with $p(y=c)=\pi_c$ and, for real features, $p(x|y=c)=\sum_{k=1}^{K_c}\alpha_{c,k}\mathcal N(x|\mu_{c,k},\Sigma_{c,k})$ (28.24). Benefits: missing data (marginalise $x^m$ to get $p(x^v|y=c)$) and semi-supervised learning (fit $\sum\log p(x^l_n,y^l_n)$ on labelled and $\sum\log p(x^u_n)$ on unlabelled data) (p. 937).

*Hybrid training (28.2.5.1, p. 938).* Generative classifiers $p(x,y)$ can be much less accurate than discriminative $p(y|x)$, which are optimised for the labels and do not "waste" capacity on irrelevant input detail. The hybrid objective [BT04; Rot+18] is
$$\mathcal L(\theta)=-\lambda\underbrace{\sum_n\log p(x_n,y_n|\theta)}_{\mathcal L_{gen}}-(1-\lambda)\underbrace{\sum_n\log p(y_n|x_n,\theta)}_{\mathcal L_{dis}},\quad0\le\lambda\le1\tag{28.25}$$
With unlabelled data (second trade-off $0\le\kappa\le1$, so that unlabelled data do not overwhelm labelled data when $N_u\gg N_l$):
$$\mathcal L_{gen}(\theta)=\kappa\sum_{n=1}^{N^l}\log p(x^l_n,y^l_n|\theta)+(1-\kappa)\sum_{n=1}^{N^u}\log p(x^u_n|\theta)\tag{28.26}$$
Alternatively, change the model: $p(x,y)=p(y|x,\theta)p(x|\tilde\theta)$ with joint priors $p(\theta,\tilde\theta)$ (p. 938).

*Optimisation (28.2.5.2, p. 938).* Use unconstrained parameters: $\pi_{1:C}=\mathrm{softmax}(\tilde\pi_{1:C})$, likewise for $\alpha_{c,1:K}$. The means are already unconstrained. Covariances use a diagonal-plus-low-rank form
$$\Sigma_{c,k}=\mathrm{diag}(d_{c,k})+S_{c,k}S_{c,k}^T\tag{28.27}$$
with $S_{c,k}$ an unconstrained $D\times R$ matrix, $R\ll D$. Add $\epsilon I$ for positive definiteness, and use softplus $d_{c,k}=\log(1+\exp(\tilde d_{c,k}))$ for positivity.

*Numerics (28.2.5.3, p. 938–939).* With $\tilde\alpha_{ck}=\log\alpha_{ck}$, $\ell_{ck}=\log\mathcal N(x|\mu_{ck},\Sigma_{ck})$, $\beta_{ck}=\tilde\alpha_{ck}+\ell_{ck}$, $M=\max_k\beta_{ck}$, $\beta'_{ck}=\beta_{ck}-M$:
$$\ell_c=\log\sum_k\alpha_{ck}\mathcal N(x|\mu_{ck},\Sigma_{ck})=\log\sum_ke^{\beta_{ck}}=M+\log\sum_ke^{\beta'_{ck}}\triangleq\mathrm{logsumexp}(\{\beta_{ck}\}_k)\tag{28.28–28.29}$$
(each $e^{\beta'_{ck}}$ can be computed without underflow). The class posterior is stabilised the same way, with $L=\max_c\ell_c$, $\tilde\ell_c=\ell_c-L$, $\tilde Z=\sum_c\pi_ce^{\tilde\ell_c}$:
$$p(y=c|x)=\frac{\pi_ce^{\ell_c}}{Z}=\frac{\pi_ce^{\ell_c-L}}{e^{-L}Z}=\frac{\pi_ce^{\tilde\ell_c}}{\tilde Z}\tag{28.30}$$
and $\log p(x)=\log\tilde Z+L$. For the Gaussian log density with $\Sigma=A+SS^T$, $A=\mathrm{diag}(d)+\epsilon I$, use
$$\det(A+SS^T)=\det(I+S^TA^{-1}S)\det(A)\tag{28.31}$$
$$(A+SS^T)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}S(I+S^TA^{-1}S)^{-1}S^TA^{-1}\tag{28.32}$$

**28.2.6 Unidentifiability (p. 939).** Mixture parameters are unidentifiable because of label switching. Take a 2-component GMM fitted to data from a true 2-component GMM $\theta^*$. The MLE converges to $\hat\theta$ minimising $D_{KL}(p(x|\theta^*)\|p(x|\hat\theta))$, but there are two equally good modes, $(\hat\mu_1=\mu_1^*,\hat\mu_2=\mu_2^*)$ and the swapped one, since cluster identity is irrelevant. Hence the posterior mean of the $\mu_k$ from Bayesian inference is meaningless. [Ste00] proposes a decision-theoretic approach in which questions are asked after a suitable permutation of labels (p. 939).

*Intuition note.* The model fits the data equally well however the cluster names are shuffled, so the posterior mean of a label-specific parameter such as $\mu_k$ is meaningless. The remedy the author cites [Ste00] is to ask questions about the clustering after a suitable permutation of the labels.

### 28.3 Factor analysis

**28.3.1 Basics (pp. 940–944).** FA is the linear-Gaussian LVM
$$p(z)=\mathcal N(z|\mu_0,\Sigma_0),\qquad p(x|z,\theta)=\mathcal N(x|Wz+\mu,\Psi)\tag{28.33–28.34}$$
$W$ is the $D\times L$ *factor loading matrix*, $\Psi$ a diagonal $D\times D$ covariance (p. 940).

*28.3.1.1 Low-rank plus diagonal covariance.* The marginal is Gaussian (Eq. 2.129):
$$p(x|\theta)=\int\mathcal N(x|Wz+\mu,\Psi)\mathcal N(z|\mu_0,\Sigma_0)dz=\mathcal N(x|W\mu_0+\mu,\Psi+W\Sigma_0W^T)\tag{28.35–28.36}$$
$$\mathbb E[x]=W\mu_0+\mu,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}[x]=W\mathrm{Cov}[z]W^T+\Psi=W\Sigma_0W^T+\Psi\tag{28.37}$$
So WLOG $\mu_0=0$ (absorb $W\mu_0$ into $\mu$) and $\Sigma_0=I$ (use $\tilde W=W\Sigma_0^{-1/2}$ as printed — $\Sigma_0^{1/2}$ is what makes $\tilde W\tilde W^T=W\Sigma_0W^T$ — so $\mathrm{Cov}[x]=\tilde W\tilde W^T+\Psi$, Eq. 28.38). $\Psi$ should be restricted to be diagonal; otherwise $\tilde W=0$ could model any covariance and ignore the latents. Final model:
$$p(z)=\mathcal N(z|0,I),\quad p(x|z)=\mathcal N(x|Wz+\mu,\Psi),\quad p(x)=\mathcal N(x|\mu,WW^T+\Psi)\tag{28.39–28.41}$$
Example ($L=1,D=2,\Psi=\sigma^2I$): an isotropic Gaussian "spray can" $p(x|z)$ slides along the line $wz+\mu$, giving an elongated correlated Gaussian $\mathcal N(x|\mu,ww^T+\sigma^2I)$ (Fig. 28.6, p. 941). In general
$$C=\mathrm{Cov}[x]=WW^T+\Psi\tag{28.42}$$
with $O(LD)$ parameters, between full covariance $O(D^2)$ and diagonal $O(D)$ (p. 941).

*28.3.1.2 Posterior (p. 941–942).* By Bayes' rule for Gaussians (Eq. 2.121c):
$$p(z|x)=\mathcal N(z|\mu_{z|x},\Sigma_{z|x})\tag{28.43}$$
$$\Sigma_{z|x}=(I+W^T\Psi^{-1}W)^{-1}=I-W^T(WW^T+\Psi)^{-1}W\tag{28.44}$$
$$\mu_{z|x}=\Sigma_{z|x}[W^T\Psi^{-1}(x-\mu)]=W^T(WW^T+\Psi)^{-1}(x-\mu)\tag{28.45}$$
To avoid inverting the $D\times D$ matrix $C$, use the matrix inversion lemma with the $L\times L$ matrix $\mathbf L=I+W^T\Psi^{-1}W$:
$$C^{-1}=\Psi^{-1}-\Psi^{-1}W(I+W^T\Psi^{-1}W)^{-1}W^T\Psi^{-1}=\Psi^{-1}-\Psi^{-1}W\mathbf L^{-1}W^T\Psi^{-1}\tag{28.46–28.47}$$

*28.3.1.3 Likelihood (p. 942).* With $\tilde x=x-\mu$:
$$\log p(x|\mu,C)=-\tfrac12[D\log(2\pi)+\log\det C+\tilde x^TC^{-1}\tilde x]\tag{28.48}$$
$$\tilde x^TC^{-1}\tilde x=\tilde x^T[\Psi^{-1}\tilde x-\Psi^{-1}W\mathbf L^{-1}(W^T\Psi^{-1}\tilde x)]\tag{28.49}$$
costing $O(L^3+LD)$, and by the determinant lemma
$$\log\det C=\log\det\mathbf L+\log\det\Psi\tag{28.50}$$
costing $O(L^3+D)$.

*28.3.1.4 EM (pp. 942–943).* The MLE can be found by gradient ascent or by EM [RT82; GH96b]. The author says EM can converge faster and automatically satisfies positivity constraints on $\Psi$. Assume standardised data ($\mu=0$). E step, expected sufficient statistics:
$$E_{x,z}=\sum_nx_n\mathbb E[z|x_n]^T,\quad E_{z,z}=\sum_n\mathbb E[zz^T|x_n],\quad E_{x,x}=\sum_nx_nx_n^T\tag{28.51–28.53}$$
$$\mathbb E[z|x]=Bx\tag{28.54}$$
$$\mathbb E[zz^T|x]=\mathrm{Cov}[z|x]+\mathbb E[z|x]\mathbb E[z|x]^T=I-BW+Bxx^TB^T\tag{28.55}$$
$$B\triangleq W^T(\Psi+WW^T)^{-1}=W^TC^{-1}\tag{28.56}$$
M step:
$$W^{new}=E_{x,z}E_{z,z}^{-1}\tag{28.57}$$
$$\Psi^{new}=\tfrac1N\mathrm{diag}(E_{x,x}-W^{new}E_{x,z}^T)\tag{28.58}$$

*28.3.1.5 Missing data (p. 943).* Under missing-at-random, partition $x=(x_1,x_2)$, $W=[W_1,W_2]$, $\mu=[\mu_1,\mu_2]$ with $x_2$ missing:
$$p(z|x_1)=\mathcal N(z|\mu_{z|1},\Sigma_{z|1}),\ \ \Sigma_{z|1}^{-1}=I+W_1^T\Sigma_{11}^{-1}W_1,\ \ \mu_{z|1}=\Sigma_{z|1}[W_1^T\Sigma_{11}^{-1}(x_1-\mu_1)]\tag{28.59–28.61}$$
$\Sigma_{11}$ is the top-left block of $\Psi$. EM can be modified "in the obvious way".

*28.3.1.6 Unidentifiability (pp. 943–944).* $\mathrm{Cov}[x]=W\mathbb E[zz^T]W^T+\mathbb E[\epsilon\epsilon^T]=WW^T+\Psi$ (28.62), with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\Psi)$. Let $\tilde W=WR$ with $R$ orthogonal ($RR^T=I$). Then
$$\mathrm{Cov}[x]=WRR^TW^T+\Psi=WW^T+\Psi\tag{28.63}$$
So the likelihood is unchanged. Multiplying by $R$ is like rotating $z$, which makes no difference for an isotropic Gaussian. Hence $W$ and the latent factors are not uniquely identifiable ("factor rotations problem"). Ways to break the symmetry (p. 943–944):
- *Orthogonal columns, ordered by eigenvalue* (as in PCA/PPCA). This still does not ensure identifiability, since one can multiply $W$ by another orthogonal matrix without changing the likelihood.
- *Lower-triangular $W$* (popular among Bayesians): visible feature 1 depends only on factor 1, feature 2 on factors 1–2, etc. E.g. $L=3,D=4$:
$$W=\begin{pmatrix}w_{11}&0&0\\w_{21}&w_{22}&0\\w_{31}&w_{32}&w_{33}\\w_{41}&w_{42}&w_{43}\end{pmatrix}\tag{28.64}$$
with $w_{kk}>0$. The number of parameters is $D+DL-L(L-1)/2$, equal to the number of uniquely identifiable FA parameters (excluding the mean). (Footnote: $D$ for $\Psi$, $DL$ for $W$, minus the $L(L-1)/2$ degrees of freedom of $R$: $L-1$ in the first column, $L-2$ in the second, and so on.) Disadvantage: the first $L$ "founder variables" shape the factor interpretation and must be chosen carefully.
- *Sparsity-promoting priors on $W$* ($\ell_1$, ARD, spike-and-slab), called sparse FA. They do not necessarily give a unique MAP estimate but encourage interpretable solutions.
- *Informative rotation* chosen heuristically for sparsity, e.g. varimax.
- *Non-Gaussian priors on $z$*, which can sometimes uniquely identify $W$ and the factors.

*Intuition note.* FA is a Gaussian whose covariance is $L$ shared directions plus independent noise per coordinate. Because only $WW^T$ enters the likelihood, any rotation of the factors fits equally well, and interpretation needs extra constraints or assumptions (several are listed in 28.3.1.6).

**28.3.2 Probabilistic PCA (pp. 944–946).** PPCA [TB99; Row97] is FA with orthogonal columns of $W$ and $\Psi=\sigma^2I$, so $p(x)=\mathcal N(x|\mu,C)$, $C=WW^T+\sigma^2I$. Advantages: a closed-form MLE (versus FA), and a proper likelihood (versus plain PCA), so it extends easily, e.g. to mixtures (p. 944).

*MLE (28.3.2.1, pp. 944–945).*
$$\log p(X|\mu,W,\sigma^2)=-\tfrac{ND}2\log(2\pi)-\tfrac N2\log|C|-\tfrac12\sum_n(x_n-\mu)^TC^{-1}(x_n-\mu)\tag{28.65}$$
The MLE of $\mu$ is $\bar x$. Then
$$\log p=-\tfrac N2[D\log(2\pi)+\log|C|+\mathrm{tr}(C^{-1}S)],\ \ S=\tfrac1N\sum_n(x_n-\bar x)(x_n-\bar x)^T\tag{28.66}$$
[TB99; Row97] show that the maximum must satisfy
$$\hat W=U_L(\Lambda_L-\sigma^2I)^{1/2}R\tag{28.67}$$
$U_L$ ($D\times L$) holds the top-$L$ eigenvectors of $S$, $\Lambda_L$ the eigenvalues, and $R$ is an arbitrary orthogonal matrix (WLOG $R=I$). The covariance is
$$C=WW^T+\sigma^2I=U_L(\Lambda_L-\sigma^2I)U_L^T+\sigma^2I\tag{28.68}$$
and the noise MLE
$$\sigma^2=\frac1{D-L}\sum_{i=L+1}^D\lambda_i\tag{28.69}$$
the average distortion of the discarded dimensions. If $L=D$ the author says the estimated noise is 0 and the model collapses to $z=x$ (p. 945).

*Noise-free limit (28.3.2.2).* At $\sigma^2=0$, $R=I$:
$$\hat W=U_L\Lambda_L^{1/2},\qquad\hat C=\hat W\hat W^T=U_L\Lambda_L^{1/2}\Lambda_L^{1/2}U_L^T=S_L\tag{28.70–28.71}$$
$S_L$ is the rank-$L$ approximation of $S$, the same as standard PCA (p. 945).

*Posterior (28.3.2.3, pp. 945–946).* From the FA results:
$$p(z|x)=\mathcal N(z|\sigma^{-2}\Sigma W^T(x-\mu),\Sigma),\quad\Sigma^{-1}=I+\sigma^{-2}W^TW=\tfrac1{\sigma^2}\underbrace{(\sigma^2I+W^TW)}_{M}\tag{28.72–28.73}$$
$$p(z|x)=\mathcal N(z|M^{-1}W^T(x-\mu),\sigma^2M^{-1})\tag{28.74}$$
At $\sigma^2=0$, $M=W^TW$ and $\mathbb E[z|x]=(W^TW)^{-1}W^T(x-\bar x)$ (28.75), the orthogonal projection onto the latent space, as in PCA (p. 946).

*EM (28.3.2.4, p. 946).* [Row97] shows EM works at $\sigma^2=0$. With $\tilde Z=Z^T$ ($L\times N$ posterior means) and $\tilde X$ the centred data stored as columns:
$$\tilde Z=(W^TW)^{-1}W^T\tilde X\ \text{(E step)},\qquad W=\tilde X\tilde Z^T(\tilde Z\tilde Z^T)^{-1}\ \text{(M step)}\tag{28.76–28.79}$$
Here $\Sigma=\mathrm{Cov}[z|\tilde x]=0$ was used in (28.77). The M step resembles multi-output linear regression $W=(\sum y_nx_n^T)(\sum x_nx_n^T)^{-1}$, with the observed inputs replaced by the expected latents. [TB99] showed the only stable fixed point of EM is the global optimum: $W$ spans the same subspace as the top $L$ eigenvectors of $S$. To get orthogonal, ordered columns one must orthogonalise afterwards (cheap), or modify EM [AO03] (p. 946).

*Intuition note.* PPCA is PCA with a noise model attached. As noise goes to zero it reproduces PCA exactly, and with noise it gives a full posterior over the latent code instead of just a projection.

**28.3.3 Mixture of factor analysers (MFA) (pp. 946–953).** Make the model only locally linear: a weighted combination of FA models [GH96b], i.e. a mixture of linear manifolds approximating a curved one, or a GMM whose components have diagonal-plus-low-rank covariance (p. 947).

*Model (28.3.3.1).* Sample $m_n\sim\mathrm{Cat}(\pi)$; if $m_n=k$, sample $z_n$ and map through $W_k$ ($L\to D$); add noise:
$$p(x_n|z_n,m_n=k,\theta)=\mathcal N(x_n|\mu_k+W_kz_n,\Psi),\ p(z_n)=\mathcal N(z_n|0,I),\ p(m_n)=\mathrm{Cat}(m_n|\pi)\tag{28.80–28.82}$$
$$p(x|\theta)=\sum_k\pi_k\int\mathcal N(z|0,I)\mathcal N(x|W_kz+\mu_k,\Psi)dz=\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(x|\mu_k,\Psi+W_kW_k^T)\tag{28.83–28.85}$$
With $\Psi=\sigma^2I$ this is a mixture of PPCA models (Fig. 28.8). It needs $O(KLD)$ parameters instead of $O(KD^2)$, which can reduce overfitting (p. 948).

*EM (28.3.3.2, pp. 948–949).* Responsibilities $r_{ij}=p(m_i=j|x_i,\theta)\propto\pi_j\mathcal N(x_i|\mu_j,W_jW_j^T+\Psi)$ (28.86). With $w_j=\mathbb I(m=j)$, $B_j=W_j^T(\Psi+W_jW_j^T)^{-1}$:
$$\mathbb E[w_jz|x_i]=r_{ij}B_j(x_i-\mu_j)\tag{28.87}$$
$$\mathbb E[w_jzz^T|x_i]=r_{ij}(I-B_jW_j+B_j(x-\mu_j)(x-\mu_j)^TB_j^T)\tag{28.88}$$
M step for the augmented loading matrix $[W_j^{new}\ \mu_j^{new}]\triangleq\tilde W_j^{new}$, with $\tilde z=[z;1]$:
$$\tilde W_j^{new}=\Big(\sum_ir_{ij}x_i\mathbb E[\tilde z|x_i,w_j]^T\Big)\Big(\sum_ir_{ij}\mathbb E[\tilde z\tilde z^T|x_i,w_j]\Big)^{-1}\tag{28.89}$$
$\mathbb E[\tilde z|x_i,w_j]=\binom{\mathbb E[z|x_i,w_j]}{1}$ (28.90), and $\mathbb E[\tilde z\tilde z^T|x_i,w_j]$ is the block matrix with $\mathbb E[zz^T|x_i,w_j]$ and $\mathbb E[z|x_i,w_j]$ on top (printed with tildes there, apparently a misprint), $\mathbb E[z|x_i,w_j]^T$ and $1$ below (28.91). Then
$$\Psi^{new}=\tfrac1N\mathrm{diag}\Big(\sum_{ij}r_{ij}(x_i-\tilde W_j^{new}\mathbb E[\tilde z|x_i,w_j])x_i^T\Big),\qquad\pi_j^{new}=\tfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nr_{ij}\tag{28.92–28.93}$$

*SGD (28.3.3.3, p. 949).* Mixtures can also be fitted by SGD [RW18], possibly with an inference network. [Zon+18] jointly learn a GMM on a deep autoencoder (a nonlinear MFA extension), with good anomaly-detection results.

*Model selection (28.3.3.4, p. 949).* $K$ and $L$ can be chosen by discrete search using marginal or validation likelihood. To estimate $L$ faster, start at maximal size and prune with ARD (automatic relevance determination), implemented by variational Bayes EM [Bis99; GB00]. Figure 28.9 shows Hinton diagrams (square size proportional to the entry) of the weight matrices for each cluster; many of them are sparse. Data were generated from 6 clusters with intrinsic dimensionalities 7, 4, 3, 2, 2, 1, which the method estimated (p. 950). Figure 28.10: with small samples per cluster the method prefers simpler models, with more clusters and larger effective dimensions as the sample size grows. At 64 and 128 points per cluster it recovers 1, 7, 4, 3, 2, 2, and at $N=8$ ARD found two different solutions (p. 950). This is "in accord with the Bayesian Occam's razor" (p. 949). ARD estimates $L$ but a discrete search over the number of components is still needed, using "birth" and "death" moves [GB00], reversible-jump MCMC [LW04] (slow, hard to implement), or non-parametric priors with Gibbs sampling [PC09] (p. 949).

*MixFA for image generation (28.3.3.5, pp. 949–953).* Following [RW18]: CelebA faces, $K=300$ components, $L=10$, $D=64\times64\times3=12{,}288$. Fitted by SGD using the efficient likelihood of Sec. 28.3.1.3. $\mu_k$ is initialised by K-means and $W_k$ by per-component FA, then fine-tuned end-to-end (p. 951). The author says the results are competitive with deep generative models of Part IV although no neural network is used. $W_k$ learns $L$ basis functions for the faces assigned to cluster $k$, similar to Sec. 28.2.4.1 (pp. 951–952). Advantages over VAEs and GANs: (1) [RW18] showed it captures more modes of the data distribution than VAEs and GANs; (2) the exact likelihood is available, so outliers can be found (Fig. 28.13); (3) imputation from arbitrary missingness patterns (p. 952).

Imputation: with $x=(x_1,x_2)$, $x_1$ observed,
$$k^*=\arg\max_kp(c=k)p(x_1|c=k)\tag{28.94}$$
$$\log p(x_1|\mu_k,C_k)=-\tfrac12[D_1\log(2\pi)+\log\det C_{k,11}+\tilde x_1^TC_{k,11}^{-1}\tilde x_1]\tag{28.95}$$
with $C_{k,11}$ the top-left $D_1\times D_1$ block of $W_kW_k^T+\Psi_k$. Then $p(z|x_1,k^*)$ comes from (28.59), $\hat z=\mathbb E[z|x_1,k^*]$, and
$$\hat x=W_{k^*}\hat z+\mu_{k^*}\tag{28.96}$$
Use $x'=[x_1,\hat x_2]$ (observed pixels unchanged; Fig. 28.14). This conditions on an arbitrary subset of pixels, whereas some other models (e.g. autoregressive models) can only predict the bottom right given the top left, since they work in raster-scan order (p. 953).

**28.3.4 FA for paired data (pp. 953–956).** Two paired views $x\in\mathbb R^{D_x}$, $y\in\mathbb R^{D_y}$, following [Vir10].

*Supervised PCA (28.3.4.1, p. 954).* Shared latent:
$$p(z_n)=\mathcal N(0,I_L),\ p(x_n|z_n)=\mathcal N(W_xz_n,\sigma_x^2I),\ p(y_n|z_n)=\mathcal N(W_yz_n,\sigma_y^2I)\tag{28.97–28.99}$$
$z_n$ captures features common to $x_n$ and $y_n$, and $\sigma_x,\sigma_y$ control the emphasis on each signal (Fig. 28.15a). With a prior on $\theta=(W_x,W_y,\sigma_x,\sigma_y)$ it is called Bayesian factor regression. For scalar $y_n$, marginalising $z_n$:
$$p(y_n|x_n,\theta)=\mathcal N(y_n|x_n^Tv,\ w_y^TCw_y+\sigma_y^2),\ C=(I+\sigma_x^{-2}W_x^TW_x)^{-1},\ v=\sigma_x^{-2}CW_xw_y\tag{28.100–28.102}$$
For classification replace $p(y|z)$ by $\mathrm{Ber}(y_n|\sigma(w_y^Tz_n))$ (28.103). The marginal predictive is then not closed-form, and techniques like exponential-family PCA are used [Guo09]. The model is symmetric in $x,y$. To predict $y$ via the bottleneck, one may upweight $y$ [Ris+08]:
$$p(X,Y,Z|\theta)=p(Y|Z,W_y)p(X|Z,W_x)^\alpha p(Z)\tag{28.104}$$
with $\alpha\le1$ chosen by cross-validation.

*Partial least squares (28.3.4.2, pp. 954–955).* An asymmetric form: part of the input variance has a private subspace $z^x_i$, the rest ($z^s_i$) is shared with the output:
$$p(z_i)=\mathcal N(z^s_i|0,I_{L_s})\mathcal N(z^x_i|0,I_{L_x}),\ p(y_i|z_i)=\mathcal N(W_yz^s_i+\mu_y,\sigma^2I),\ p(x_i|z_i)=\mathcal N(W_xz^s_i+B_xz^x_i+\mu_x,\sigma^2I)\tag{28.105–28.107}$$
With $v_i=(y_i;x_i)$:
$$p(v_i|\theta)=\mathcal N(v_i|\mu,WW^T+\sigma^2I),\ W=\begin{pmatrix}W_y&0\\W_x&B_x\end{pmatrix},\ WW^T=\begin{pmatrix}W_yW_y^T&W_yW_x^T\\W_xW_y^T&W_xW_x^T+B_xB_x^T\end{pmatrix}\tag{28.108–28.110}$$
$L$ should be large enough that the shared subspace does not capture covariate-specific variation. MLE in this model is equivalent to PLS (p. 955).

*Canonical correlation analysis (28.3.4.3, pp. 955–956).* A symmetric unsupervised version of PLS, with shared $z^s$ and private $z^x,z^y$ [BJ05]:
$$p(z_i)=\mathcal N(z^s_i|0,I_{L_s})\mathcal N(z^x_i|0,I_{L_x})\mathcal N(z^y_i|0,I_{L_y})\tag{28.111}$$
$$p(x_i|z_i)=\mathcal N(B_xz^x_i+W_xz^s_i+\mu_x,\sigma^2I),\ p(y_i|z_i)=\mathcal N(B_yz^y_i+W_yz^s_i+\mu_y,\sigma^2I)\tag{28.112–28.113}$$
$$p(v_i|\theta)=\mathcal N(v_i|\mu,WW^T+\sigma^2I_D),\ W=\begin{pmatrix}W_x&B_x&0\\W_y&0&B_y\end{pmatrix},\ WW^T=\begin{pmatrix}W_xW_x^T+B_xB_x^T&W_xW_y^T\\W_yW_x^T&W_yW_y^T+B_yB_y^T\end{pmatrix}\tag{28.114–28.116}$$
[BJ05] showed the MLE is equivalent to classical CCA. The PGM view makes generalisations easy: more than two views (generalised CCA), nonlinear (deep CCA), exponential-family CCA (p. 956).

**28.3.5 FA with exponential-family likelihoods (pp. 956–957).** Replace the Gaussian output by an exponential-family member, $p(x_n|z_n)=\exp(\mathcal T(x)^T\theta+h(x)-g(\theta))$ (28.117), with natural-parameter matrix $\Theta=ZW$ ($Z$: $N\times L$, $W$: $L\times D$). This is exponential family FA. Without conjugacy with the Gaussian prior, the exact posterior and marginal likelihood are not computable, so exact MLE is out (p. 956). [CDS02] give coordinate ascent for a deterministic variant, exponential family PCA, alternating point estimates of $z_n$ and $W$. This can be seen as degenerate variational EM with a delta-function posterior. [GS08] improve it to find the global optimum, and [Ude+16] extend it to generalised low rank models (p. 957). It is often preferable to use a non-degenerate (e.g. Gaussian) posterior via the variational lower bound, since the number of latent variables grows with the number of data cases and point estimates risk overfitting (p. 957).

*Binary PCA (28.3.5.1, p. 957).* $p(x|z)=\prod_d\mathrm{Ber}(x_d|\sigma(w_d^Tz))$ (28.118). Data: $N=150$ bit vectors of length $D=16$, each made by choosing one of three binary prototypes and flipping bits at random. Fitted by variational EM [Tip98] with $L=2$. The posterior means $\mathbb E[z_n|x_n,\hat W]$ form three clusters (Fig. 28.18b). The reconstruction is $p(\hat x_{nd}=1|x_n)=\int dz_n\,p(z_n|x_n)p(\hat x_{nd}|z_n)$ (28.119). Thresholding at 0.5 gives the denoised data (Fig. 28.18d).

*Categorical PCA (28.3.5.2).* $p(x|z)=\prod_d\mathrm{Cat}(x_d|\mathrm{softmax}(W_dz))$ (28.120), fitted by variational EM [Kha+10].

**28.3.6 FA with DNN likelihoods (VAEs) (pp. 957–958).** Let the $z\to x$ map be a neural net: $p(x|z)=\mathcal N(x|f(\cdot;\theta),\sigma^2I)$ (28.121), "nonlinear factor analysis" (also with other output distributions). The posterior and MLE are no longer exact. The VAE (Ch. 21) fits it by amortised variational inference, but other methods such as MCMC also work (p. 958).

**28.3.7 FA with GP likelihoods (GP-LVM) (p. 958, begins).** Replace the linear decoder $f(z)=Wz$ in $p(y|z)=\mathcal N(y|f(z),\sigma^2I)$ by a Gaussian process, one per output dimension [Law05]. Notation switches to $y$ for the output, with latents $z$ as GP inputs. The unit ends as the author starts from PPCA.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
FA with $D=2$, $L=1$, $\mu=0$, $W=\binom11$, $\Psi=I$ (so $\sigma^2=1$). Observe $x=(2,2)^T$.

1. Covariance (28.42): $C=WW^T+\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Only 3 distinct entries, which are produced by $W$ (2 numbers) and $\Psi$.
2. Posterior covariance (28.44, first form): $W^T\Psi^{-1}W=1+1=2$, so $\Sigma_{z|x}=(1+2)^{-1}=1/3$.
3. Posterior mean (28.45): $\Sigma_{z|x}W^T\Psi^{-1}x=\tfrac13(2+2)=4/3$.
4. Check with the second forms. $C^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$, because $\det C=4-1=3$. Then $C^{-1}x=\tfrac13(4-2,\,-2+4)=(2/3,2/3)$, and $W^TC^{-1}x=4/3$ ✓. Also $1-W^TC^{-1}W=1-\tfrac13(2-1-1+2)=1-\tfrac23=\tfrac13$ ✓.
5. Lemma check (28.47, 28.50): $\mathbf L=1+W^TW=3$, so $C^{-1}=I-W\tfrac13W^T=I-\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2/3&-1/3\\-1/3&2/3\end{pmatrix}$ ✓. And $\log\det C=\log\det\mathbf L+\log\det\Psi=\log3+0=\log 3$ ✓ ($\det C=3$).
6. Rotation problem (28.63): with $R=-1$ (a $1\times1$ orthogonal matrix), $\tilde W=-W$ gives $\tilde W\tilde W^T=WW^T$, so the same $C$ and likelihood. But the posterior mean becomes $-4/3$. The sign of the factor is not identifiable, only $WW^T$ is.

## Concepts
- [[latent-variable-model]] (new) — probabilistic model with variables that are always hidden; here a single layer $z$ with decoder $f(z)$ (p. 929)
- [[mixture-model]] (new) — convex combination $\sum_k\pi_kp_k(x)$, equivalently a discrete latent picking the component (p. 929)
- [[gaussian-mixture-model]] (new) — mixture whose components are Gaussians; used for clustering and as a density model/prior (p. 930)
- [[responsibility]] (new) — posterior $p(z_n=k|x_n,\theta)$ of cluster $k$ for point $n$, via Bayes' rule (p. 931)
- [[gaussian-scale-mixture]] (new) — $x=\epsilon z$ with random scale $\epsilon$; yields Student-t, Laplace, spike-and-slab, horseshoe (p. 932)
- [[spike-and-slab-prior]] (new) — $\pi\mathcal N(0,\sigma_0^2)+(1-\pi)\delta_0$; a sparsity prior (p. 933)
- [[map-inverse-problem]] (new) — recover latent $x$ from corrupted $y$ with a prior to regularise an ill-posed inversion (p. 927, p. 935)
- [[label-switching]] (new) — permuting cluster labels leaves the likelihood unchanged, so cluster parameters are unidentifiable (p. 939)
- [[factor-analysis]] (new) — linear-Gaussian LVM with $p(x)=\mathcal N(\mu,WW^T+\Psi)$, $\Psi$ diagonal (p. 940)
- [[factor-rotation-problem]] (new) — $W\to WR$ ($R$ orthogonal) leaves the likelihood unchanged (p. 943)
- [[probabilistic-pca]] (new) — FA with orthogonal $W$ columns and $\Psi=\sigma^2I$; closed-form MLE; PCA as $\sigma^2\to0$ (p. 944)
- [[mixture-of-factor-analyzers]] (new) — mixture of FA models; locally linear; GMM with diagonal-plus-low-rank covariances (p. 947)
- [[automatic-relevance-determination]] (new) — prior that prunes irrelevant weights, used to estimate effective latent dimension (p. 949)
- [[canonical-correlation-analysis]] (new) — FA of two views with a shared and two private subspaces; MLE equals CCA (p. 955)
- [[exponential-family-factor-analysis]] (new) — natural parameters $\Theta=ZW$ with a non-Gaussian likelihood; needs approximate inference (p. 956)
- [[log-sum-exp-trick]] (new) — subtract the max before exponentiating to avoid underflow (p. 938)
- [[bayesian-occams-razor]] — ARD/MFA prefers simpler models when data are scarce (p. 949)
- [[posterior-distribution]] — used for responsibilities, $p(z|x)$ in FA, and posterior samples in imaging (p. 931, p. 941)
- [[variational-inference-elbo]] — used for ARD in MFA and for non-degenerate posteriors in expfam FA (p. 949, p. 957)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Responsibilities $r_{nk}$ (28.8) ↔ Bayesian inference: posterior over a discrete hypothesis from prior × likelihood, normalised
- [same-math] M step of PPCA-EM ↔ multi-output linear regression (author notes this, p. 946), with latent expectations in place of inputs
- [same-math] Hard clustering with $\Sigma_k=I$, uniform prior ↔ K-means nearest-centroid rule (28.10)
- [structural-analogy] Scale mixture over $\epsilon$ (28.12) ↔ hierarchical model: a latent group-level variance integrated out gives heavy-tailed marginals
- [structural-analogy] ARD pruning of weights ↔ Bayesian Occam's razor: marginal-likelihood-based models shrink as data shrink (p. 949)
- [structural-analogy] Label switching ↔ rotation unidentifiability in FA: both are symmetries of the likelihood that make some parameters meaningless
- [metaphor] FA "spray can" slid along a line (Fig. 28.6) ↔ convolution of a noise kernel along a latent direction

## Questions this raises
- How should one decide among the several ways of breaking factor-rotation symmetry when the goal is interpretation, given each has costs (founder variables, non-unique MAP)?
- How reliable is the overlapping-patch EPLL objective, which overcounts pixels, compared with the principled multiscale or Gibbs approaches?
- When MFA's exact likelihood is available, how does it compare with VAE/GAN likelihoods for outlier detection at high dimension?
- What happens to the shared/private split in PLS and CCA if $L_s$ is chosen too large or too small?
