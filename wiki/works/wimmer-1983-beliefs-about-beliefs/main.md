# Beliefs about beliefs: representation and constraining function of wrong beliefs in young children's understanding of deception

## L0 — One line
Children of about 4–6 years become able to represent another person's wrong belief as distinct from what they know to be true, and then use it to predict that person's actions and utterances.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The authors ask when children can explicitly represent another person's wrong belief. In a story, an object is moved while the protagonist is away, and the child must say where he will look. None of the 3–4-year-olds, but most 6–9-year-olds, pointed to the old location. Failures were mostly not memory failures. Children who passed also built truthful or deceitful utterances relative to the protagonist's belief, whatever their age. In a story task, 4½-year-olds mostly failed to see a deceptive plan, while 5½-year-olds mostly succeeded. The authors suggest that the ability to represent relations between persons' epistemic states (what each person knows or believes) emerges at about 4–6 years. They reject a pure memory or processing-capacity explanation.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of belief versus fact; reading small contingency tables and "p < 0.05" as "reliable difference"; no further math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Wrong-belief task: the child watches an object moved while the protagonist is absent, and must say where the protagonist will look. Only a child who represents the protagonist's belief apart from his own knowledge can answer "x" (p. 106).
- Pass rates rise with age. Pointing at the old location x in both stories: 4 of 12 four-to-five-year-olds, 11 of 12 six-to-seven-year-olds, 11 of 12 eight-to-nine-year-olds (p. 110). Experiment 2 gave the 3–4-year-olds no reliable evidence of success (p. 114).
- Errors were not memory failures: about 80% of 4–6-year-olds who erred remembered the initial location, and nearly all correct answerers knew the real location (all in Experiment 1; all but one 3–4-year-old in Experiment 2) (pp. 110, 115).
- Children who passed the belief question mostly also built truthful or deceitful utterances relative to the belief, with no clear age difference. The belief representation "constrains" interpretation of intentions (pp. 110–111, 119).
- Making the object vanish (Disappear condition) helped 4–5-year-olds. A "Stop and Think" reminder did not help them (pp. 114, 124). The authors read this as partial support for the idea that similar descriptions of belief and fact interfere with each other (pp. 112, 115).
- Constructing a deceitful utterance stayed hard for about half the 4–6-year-olds even when the belief was correct (x–x condition: the object is put back in the same place) (p. 118).
- In Experiment 4, 4½-year-olds mostly judged a deceiver's false utterance as true, even in the extremely deceptive context where it contradicted the given facts. 5½-year-olds mostly judged it false in the deceptive context (pp. 122–123).
- The authors argue against a pure memory or processing-capacity explanation (the idea that children simply grow in how much they can hold in mind at once). They suggest that a new skill seems to emerge at 4–6 years: representing relations between persons' epistemic states (pp. 125–126).

### Abstract and Introduction (pp. 103–107)
**Abstract (p. 103–104).** Understanding another's wrong belief requires explicit representation of the wrongness of that belief relative to one's own knowledge. Children aged 3–9 saw a protagonist put an object in x, then saw it moved to y in his absence. Correct pointing to x in both sketches: none of the 3–4-year-olds, 57% of 4–6-year-olds, 86% of 6–9-year-olds. Among 4–6-year-olds' errors, only about 20% involved forgetting the initial location (p. 104). The abstract also reports that, independent of age, children who answered the belief question correctly also gave correct utterance answers 85% of the time, and that in a story-like task 4–5-year-olds judged a critical utterance to be a lie only 28% of the time against 94% for 5–6-year-olds (p. 104).

**Background (p. 104).**
- Premack and Woodruff asked whether chimpanzees have a "theory of mind", the ability to impute mental states to self and others.
- Pylyshyn's reading: such a creature represents not only a state of affairs but also its own relation to it (wanting, believing) explicitly. He calls this *meta-representation*.
- Deceiving a competitor is a good test. It requires conceptualising the deceived person's wrong belief as a subgoal.
- Woodruff and Premack taught deceptive pointing to 2 of 4 chimpanzees, only after 5 months of training.

**Why use children (p. 105).** Natural deception is a very conservative measure, because it needs many extra planning steps. Language gives another route. Cited findings:
- Bretherton and Beeghly: at 2½ years most children spontaneously used words about perception, volition, emotion and knowledge.
- Hood and Bloom: 3-year-olds referred to intentions in answer to "why" questions.
- Shultz et al.: 3–5-year-olds separate intended acts from mistakes and reflexes.
- 4-year-olds understand some aspects of "know", "guess", "remember" and "forget".

**Absence of knowledge (pp. 105–106).** Harder meta-representation is needed when one must represent the difference between one's own and another's relation to the same content.
- In one of the studies by Marvin et al. and Mossler et al., children saw a TV sketch of a boy entering a house and asking his sister for a biscuit. They then watched it again in the presence of their mother, with the sound off.
- 60% of 4-year-olds and 85% of 5-year-olds said the mother knew he entered but not that he asked for a biscuit (pp. 105–106).
- So from 4 years children distinguish their own knowledge from another's absence of knowledge (p. 106).

**The present problem (p. 106).** Representing another's *definite belief* that differs from what the subject knows is not studied developmentally. The paradigm comes from Bennett, Dennett and Harman. The subject and another person see state x. In the other's absence the subject sees x change to y. The subject then knows y and knows the other still believes x.
- Maxi puts chocolate in cupboard x. His mother moves it to y while he is away. The child points to where Maxi will look.
- Only a child who represents the belief that the chocolate is in x apart from the fact that it is in y can point to x.
- Pointing avoids both mental-verb wording and verbal answers (p. 106).

**Constraining function (pp. 106–107).** The practical importance of a belief representation is its use as a frame of reference for interpreting or anticipating the other's actions; that is, these interpretations and anticipations have to be constrained to the realm of the other's beliefs. For this to happen the belief representation has to be firmly established. As a test of firmness, Maxi is described as wanting to say something wrong (competitive) or true (cooperative).
- Competitive: Maxi, who believes x, decides to tell his brother something wrong. The correct answer is "y", which is actually true. The egocentric reading takes "wrong" relative to the child's own knowledge.
- Cooperative: Maxi intends to tell Grandpa the truth. The correct answer is "x", which is actually false.
- Hence there is a temptation to make Maxi say something *actually* wrong or true. It meets little opposition if the child is unsure about the difference between own knowledge and Maxi's beliefs (p. 107).

*Intuition note.* A child who has not firmly separated what is actually the case from what Maxi thinks is tempted to read "wrong" and "true" against reality. The authors expect the odd-looking correct answers (saying the true place to deceive) to occur only once that separation is firm.

### Experiment 1 (pp. 107–111)
**Method (pp. 107–109).**
- 36 children in Salzburg: twelve each aged 4–5 (4;0–4;7), 6–7 (6;0–6;11), 8–9 (8;1–9;3).
- Three hiding locations (boxes). Paper cut-out figures show the protagonist's absence and presence.
- Each child heard two stories (Story 1: Maxi and the chocolate; Story 2: a girl's favourite book in a kindergarten, reshelved by a caretaker). Each existed in a cooperative and a competitive version. Story 1 always came first; version assignment was counterbalanced.
- Table 1, Story 1: Maxi puts the chocolate in the blue cupboard and leaves. His mother moves it to the green cupboard and leaves. Maxi returns hungry.
- Questions: **Belief** (where will Maxi look for the chocolate?). In the cooperative version Maxi asks Grandpa for help; in the competitive version Maxi thinks he will tell his big brother something completely wrong. The **Utterance** question is where Maxi will say the chocolate is.
- Then the box was opened. The **Reality** question (where the chocolate really is; asked if the box was empty) and the **Memory** question (does the child remember where Maxi first put it) followed (p. 109).

**Results: belief (p. 110).** All errors were pointing to y. None pointed to the third location z. Table 2 (number of correct answers out of 2 stories):

| Age | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4–5 | 4 | 2 | 6 |
| 6–7 | 11 | 0 | 1 |
| 8–9 | 11 | 1 | 0 |

Collapsing to a 2×2 table (4–5-year-olds vs older; correct in both vs wrong at least once): χ² = 10.82, df = 1, p < 0.01 (p. 110).
- Memory control: in 80% of cases where children pointed to y, they answered the Memory question correctly.
- All children who passed the Belief question also passed the Reality question (p. 110).

**Results: utterances (pp. 110–111).** There was no discernible story effect, so the data were pooled. Table 3 (each child contributed two stories, so counts total twice the number of subjects):

| Belief answer | Version | Utterance answer | 4–5 | 6–7 | 8–9 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correct (x) | Competitive | same: x | 1 | 3 | 1 | 5 |
| | | different: y or z | 4 | 8 | 11 | 23 |
| | Cooperative | same: x | 4 | 10 | 9 | 23 |
| | | different: y or z | 1 | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| Incorrect (y) | Competitive | same: y | 7 | 1 | 0 | 8 |
| | | different: x or z | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| | Cooperative | same: y | 5 | 1 | 0 | 6 |
| | | different: x or z | 2 | 0 | 1 | 3 |

- Competitive version, correct-belief children: 23 gave a deceitful utterance (y: 20, z: 3), 5 did not. χ² = 11.57, df = 1, p < 0.001.
- Cooperative version, correct-belief children: 23 directed the friend to x, 4 to y. χ² = 13.37, df = 1, p < 0.001 (p. 110).
- Among children with a correct belief answer, the proportion of correct utterance answers stayed roughly constant across ages (p. 111).
- Correct-belief children had to name the actually false place to be truthful, and the actually true place to deceive. Many reacted with an ironical smile to the counterproductive deceit (p. 111).
- Most children who failed the belief question answered y to the utterance question whether the intention was truthful or deceitful (p. 111).

*Intuition note.* Once the belief is represented correctly, using it to build a truthful or deceitful message showed no clear age dependence in these data (roughly constant proportions). In this experiment the age differences appeared mainly in the belief question.

### Experiment 2 (pp. 112–116)
Two explanations of the youngest children's failure are tested (p. 112).
1. *Impulsivity.* Young children react fast and less accurately (Kagan and Kogan). Many 4–5-year-olds seemed to point to y automatically. A "Stop and Think" reminder was added.
2. *Interference.* The description of the belief (the chocolate is in the blue cupboard) resembles the description of the fact (it is in the green cupboard). The true description may overwrite the belief. In a Disappear condition the object is used up or taken away, so the descriptions are dissimilar.

**Method (pp. 112–113).**
- 92 children: 20 aged 3–4, 42 aged 4–5, 30 aged 5–6.
- Standard Displacement: as in Experiment 1.
- Stop-and-Think Displacement: before the question, the child was told to think carefully and was reminded what Maxi did before going to the playground (p. 113).
- Disappear: the mother grates all the chocolate into a cake and takes it to Granny. In story 2 the caretaker takes the book home for repair. The belief question asked what Maxi will do. A prompt asking where he will look for the chocolate followed if there was no clear answer (p. 113).

**Results: belief (pp. 113–115).** Table 4 (number correct out of 2 stories; 3–4-year-olds have no Standard Displacement row in the table):

| Age | Condition | n | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3–4 | Stop-and-Think | 10 | 0 | 0 | 10 |
| | Disappear | 10 | 0 | 3 | 7 |
| 4–5 | Standard | 14 | 6 | 1 | 7 |
| | Stop-and-Think | 14 | 4 | 2 | 8 |
| | Disappear | 14 | 11 | 0 | 3 |
| 5–6 | Standard | 10 | 5 | 0 | 5 |
| | Stop-and-Think | 10 | 10 | 0 | 0 |
| | Disappear | 10 | 10 | 0 | 0 |

- Totals: 46, 6, 40. The distribution is bimodal (few children correct once). Rejecting a single-peaked distribution: χ² = 25.13, df = 1, p < 0.001 (p. 113).
- 5–6-year-olds were perfect in both new conditions but only half were correct in Standard Displacement. Standard differs reliably from each other condition (Fisher, p < 0.025).
- 4–5-year-olds differ across conditions (χ² = 7.43, df = 2, p < 0.05), due to the Disappear condition only. There is no evidence that Stop-and-Think helped them (p. 114).
- 3–4-year-olds: no reliable evidence of correct ascription in either condition (p. 114).
- Answers to the Disappear question:
  - 5–6-year-olds: 50% complete answers with x; the other 50% gave non-specific answers first, then x after the prompt (p. 114).
  - 4–5-year-olds: 39.3% correct and specific; 50.0% non-specific then x after the prompt; 10.7% said Maxi would search behind the scenes (p. 115).
  - 3–4-year-olds: no first answers. After the prompt only 15% said x, and 85% said behind the scenes (p. 115).

**Memory controls (pp. 115–116).** Table 5: wrong belief answers (−Belief) and correct memory answers among them (+Memory):

| Condition | 3–4: −Bel / +Mem | 4–5 | 5–6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | – | 53.6 / 100.0 | 50.0 / 100.0 |
| Stop-and-Think | 100.0 / 50.0 | 64.3 / 66.7 | 0.0 / – |
| Disappear | 85.0 / 23.5 | 21.4 / 66.7 | 0.0 / – |

- For 4–6-year-olds, 83.7% of wrong belief answers came with correct memory, replicating Experiment 1 (80%). For 3–4-year-olds only 37.8%, which suggests the task was too complex for most of them (p. 115).
- All correct belief answers were followed by correct Reality answers, except one 3–4-year-old (p. 115).
- Counterintuitive trend: if interference caused errors, memory for the earlier location should also be better in Disappear. Yet 4–5-year-olds with a wrong belief answer in Disappear remembered correctly only 66.7%, against 81.8% in the two Displacement conditions together (p. 116).

*Intuition note.* Making the true state dissimilar from the belief helped 4–5-year-olds (Disappear), which fits interference. Telling children to think carefully did not help them, so impulsivity is not supported for that group. The memory data do not fit the interference account, so the support is only partial.

### Experiment 3 (pp. 116–119)
**Question (pp. 116–117).** Failing the belief question may have kept 4–5-year-olds from following the rest of the story. Experiment 1 gave only about 1/3 correct deceitful utterances in this age group. The **x–x Replacement** condition removes the belief problem: the mother takes the chocolate from x and puts it back in x. If belief difficulties explained the low rate, performance should be higher in x–x than in x–y.

**Method (p. 117).** 29 children aged 4–5 (12 in x–x, 17 in x–y) and 24 aged 5–6 (12 in each). The authors report an allocation error that gave unequal group sizes. Each child heard one cooperative and one competitive story.

**Results (pp. 117–119).**
- Table 6 (x–y condition, number correct out of 2): 4–5-year-olds 6, 2, 9; 5–6-year-olds 8, 1, 3. The age contrast is not reliable (χ² = 1.66, df = 1, p > 0.10) (p. 118).
- 73.1% of wrong belief answers came with correct Memory answers. All correct belief answers had correct Reality answers (p. 118).
- Competitive version, deceitful utterance: 18 of 29 (62.1%) in x–y. In x–x all subjects answered x to the belief question, but only 11 of 24 (45.8%) gave a correct utterance (y or z). This was not better than x–y, contrary to expectation (p. 118).
- A 5% confidence interval is reported as allowing, in the extreme case, at least more than one third of x–x subjects (6 of 24) to be wrong. The sentence is unclear (see Reading notes) (p. 118).
- Conclusion: building a deceitful utterance stays difficult for 4–6-year-olds even without complicated belief assumptions. This agrees with Shultz and Cloghesy, who found signs of deceptive behaviour in a Hide-and-Seek variant not before the age of 5½ years (p. 118).
- Among x–y children with a correct belief answer: competitive, 11 of 15 correct utterances; cooperative, all 16 correct. The answer pattern depended on story version (χ² = 13.7, df = 1, p < 0.001) (pp. 118–119).
- Experiments 1 and 3 pooled, 4–6 vs 6–9 years: no reliable age difference for competitive (χ² = 0.06, p > 0.90) or cooperative versions (χ² = 0.22, p > 0.70). This suggests no age difference in using a correctly built belief representation (p. 119).

### Experiment 4 (pp. 119–123)
**Rationale (pp. 119–120).** Half of the children had trouble building a deceitful utterance in x–x, although the intention was stated almost explicitly. Studies of lying (Piaget, Stern, Wimmer et al.) show children of this age easily judge an utterance false if it does not match reality. The authors infer that the difficulty lies in understanding deceptive plans, here the plan to induce a false belief in an opponent (p. 119).
- Fairy tales (many built on deceptive ploys) are liked at this age. The younger children might like them for archetypal figures and conflicts instead (Bettelheim).
- The task structure differs. In Experiments 1 and 3 the child is told the goal, the anticipated conflicting goal, and the intention to deceive, and must infer the utterance. In fairy tales the listener is told the two goals and the utterance and must infer the plan (pp. 119–120).

**Design (pp. 120, 122).**
- Two stories (Table 7): Nancy tells Thomas on the swing "you've got to go home, your mom needs you"; Markus says "I can't go, my foot hurts. I hurt my foot playing football". The critical utterance was identical across contexts.
- **Deceptive context:** conflicting goals plus the utterance suggest a deceptive plan. Inferring the plan is needed to judge the utterance false.
- **Extremely deceptive context** (4½-year-olds only): facts are added that contradict the utterance. Pilot work showed 4-year-olds tended to judge the utterance true in the deceptive context.
- **Informative context** (control): facts make the utterance true.
- Subjects: 24 children aged 4½ (4;3–4;8) and 16 aged 5½ (5;4–5;8). Eight per age group got each context. A further eight 4½-year-olds got the extremely deceptive context (p. 122).
- Test questions: (a) "Is it really true…?" and (b) "Why did X say…?" (p. 121).

**Results (pp. 122–123).** Table 8 (judgments of the critical utterance; table heads "4–5" and "5–6" years):

| Story | Answer | 4½: Extreme | 4½: Deceptive | 4½: Informative | 5½: Deceptive | 5½: Informative |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (Nancy) | True | 6 | 5 | 8 | 0 | 6 |
| | False | 2 | 3 | 0 | 8 | 2 |
| 2 (Markus) | True | 6 | 6 | 7 | 1 | 8 |
| | False | 2 | 2 | 1 | 7 | 0 |

- No story difference. In the informative context both ages tended to judge appropriately (true). The younger group tended to judge the utterance true even in the deceptive and extremely deceptive contexts. Almost all 5½-year-olds were context sensitive.
- Deceptive vs informative context for 5½-year-olds: Fisher p < 0.005 for both stories. Deceptive context, 4½ vs 5½: p < 0.025 (p. 122).
- Question (b), appropriate answers (a reference to the deceiver's real goal, or to the source of the information): 88% in the informative context at all ages. In the deceptive context 4½-year-olds gave 50% appropriate answers and 5½-year-olds 94%. The difference between contexts for 5½-year-olds is reliable, p < 0.025 for both stories (pp. 122–123).
- The 4½-year-olds' misjudging even in the extreme context came as a surprise. Markman and Wimmer found that even older children had trouble detecting contradictions between story parts (p. 123).
- 5½-year-olds may find it easier to infer a deceptive plan from goals and an utterance than to construct a deceitful utterance. The gap cannot be very large, because the 6–7-year-olds of Experiment 1 could construct deceitful utterances (p. 123).

### General discussion (pp. 123–126)
**Two models (pp. 123–124).** Representing wrong beliefs requires two models of the world (cf. Johnson-Laird) and an explicit representation of the falseness relation between them. Children aged 6 and over coped with this in the explored situation.
- Adults find syllogisms needing two or more models hard (Johnson-Laird and Steedman). So that difficulty may not be due only to the number of models.
- Part of it could be the factual form of syllogisms. Facts usually let one specify a single model, whereas talk about beliefs directs attention to people holding different models (p. 124).

**The age step (p. 124).**
- In the Disappear condition, 78% of the 4–5-year-olds represented the wrong belief correctly. Only 15% of the 3–4-year-olds' answers were correct.
- Marvin et al. found 80% of 4–5-year-olds, but only 5% of 3–4-year-olds, represented another's absence of knowledge.
- The close match is notable. Representing absence of knowledge needs no particular proposition, so interference should be even less likely. Yet 3–4-year-olds did no better there than with wrong belief. It could be argued that the real-life situations of Marvin et al. let children show their competence better than stories do; but their 3–4-year-olds did no better there than in the story format (p. 124).

**Constraint (pp. 124–125).** Nearly all children (any age) who construed the wrong belief could also build a deceitful and a truthful utterance. This needs a firmly bounded representation of Maxi's mental contents, strong enough to resist the temptation to make the utterance simply "true" or "false" (pp. 124–125).

**Alternative explanations (pp. 125–126).**
- *Failure to infer beliefs because of poor memory.* Bryant and Trabasso showed that when memory for facts is ensured, 4-year-olds draw transitive inferences. Here 80% of 4–6-year-olds who failed to infer the belief remembered the facts. So memory lapses do not explain the failure (p. 125).
- *Processing-capacity growth (Pascual-Leone's view, Case's view).* The authors say one could argue that a child of 4 or younger has at best enough capacity to remember the real events, whereas at 6 the capacity has grown enough to hold the wrong belief as well. The authors find this implausible because 4–6-year-olds represent not only a wrong belief but also the protagonist's intentions and actions in relation to it. If mental development depended mainly on an increase in processing capacity, they would expect this added complication not to be understood for at least another two years (p. 125).
- *Automaticity (Case's emphasis).* Practice on inferring beliefs would take years, and the data show no such delay (p. 125).

**Conclusion (p. 126).** The ability to understand another's beliefs, how they will act on them, and deception is not a mere side effect of memory or capacity growth. A novel skill seems to emerge between 4 and 6 years. Related abilities emerge in the same period:
- understanding another's absence of knowledge;
- constructing a deceitful utterance (x–x);
- inferring a deceptive plan (Experiment 4);
- understanding the competitive nature of Hide and Seek.

All depend on representing two different or conflicting epistemic states. The authors suggest that around 4–6 years the ability to represent the relationship between two or more persons' epistemic states emerges and becomes firmly established (p. 126).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Locations: drawer A, drawer B, drawer C. Ana puts a key in A and leaves. Ben moves the key to B. Ana returns.
- **Belief question.** Ana believes "A". A child who keeps her belief apart from reality says A. A child who answers by reality says B.
- **Competitive utterance** (Ana wants to mislead Ben, and believes the key is in A). A belief-based child makes her say "not A", for example B or C. B is actually where the key is.
- **Cooperative utterance** (Ana wants to help Ben). A belief-based child makes her say A, which is actually false.
- **Reality-based child**, belief question B, competitive utterance B, cooperative utterance B. This matches the "same: y" rows of Table 3.

Count check using Table 3's correct-belief rows: competitive 23 of 28 correct (5 + 23 = 28), cooperative 23 of 27 correct (23 + 4 = 27). Total 46 of 55 ≈ 83.6% correct.

## Concepts
- [[theory-of-mind]] — ability to impute mental states to self and others (Premack and Woodruff, as cited) (p. 104)
- [[metarepresentation]] (new) — explicitly representing one's own or another's relation (believing, wanting) to a represented state of affairs; Pylyshyn's term (p. 104)
- [[first-order-false-belief]] — understanding that another holds a definite belief differing from what one knows is true (p. 106)
- [[unexpected-transfer-task]] — object moved from x to y in the protagonist's absence; where will he look? (p. 106)
- [[reality-bias]] — the egocentric reading of "wrong" or "true" against one's own knowledge, instead of the protagonist's belief (p. 107)
- [[knowledge-vs-ignorance-attribution]] — representing another's lack of knowledge, which children manage from about 4 years (pp. 105–106)
- [[age-4-transition]] — abrupt improvement from age 3–4 to 4–5 on belief and knowledge tasks (p. 124)
- [[constraining-function-of-belief-representation]] (new) — a firm belief representation limits interpretation of the other's intentions, actions and utterances to the realm of his beliefs (p. 106)
- [[deception-understanding]] (new) — inferring or constructing a plan to induce a false belief in another (pp. 119–120)
- [[processing-capacity-account]] (new) — view (Pascual-Leone, Case) that development reflects growth in central capacity; here argued to be implausible as the main explanation (p. 125)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Child's two models (own knowledge vs protagonist's belief) ↔ POMDP / BToM: agent's belief state kept distinct from the world state — both separate what is true from what the agent represents.
- [structural-analogy] Constructing an utterance relative to the other's belief ↔ Rational Speech Act: a speaker chooses messages by modelling the listener's state of knowledge — both make the message depend on the other's mental state.
- [structural-analogy] Inferring a deceptive plan from goals plus an utterance ↔ inverse planning: infer goals and plans from observed behaviour — both reason backward from what an agent did.

## Questions this raises
- If the Disappear condition lowers interference, how far does the pass rate depend on how a task is worded, and which pass rate counts as the child's "true" competence?
- Why does building a deceitful utterance stay hard in the x–x condition when children readily call false statements false? What exactly is missing?
- The authors argue against capacity and automaticity accounts. What evidence besides the timing of belief, intention and action understanding could separate a "new skill" from graded growth?
- Do the 3–4-year-olds fail because they lack belief representation, or because the task is too complex for them? (Only 37.8% remembered the facts, p. 115.)
