# Wimmer, H., & Perner, J. (1983). Beliefs about beliefs: Representation and constraining function of wrong beliefs in young children's understanding of deception. *Cognition*, 13(1), 103–128. doi:10.1016/0010-0277(83)90004-5

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[wimmer-1983-beliefs-about-beliefs/main]].

## In one line
Children of about 4–6 years become able to represent another person's wrong belief as distinct from what they know to be true, and then use it to predict that person's actions and utterances.

## The gist
The authors ask when children can explicitly represent another person's wrong belief. In a story, an object is moved while the protagonist is away, and the child must say where he will look. None of the 3–4-year-olds, but most 6–9-year-olds, pointed to the old location. Failures were mostly not memory failures. Children who passed also built truthful or deceitful utterances relative to the protagonist's belief, whatever their age. In a story task, 4½-year-olds mostly failed to see a deceptive plan, while 5½-year-olds mostly succeeded. The authors suggest that the ability to represent relations between persons' epistemic states (what each person knows or believes) emerges at about 4–6 years. They reject a pure memory or processing-capacity explanation.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of belief versus fact; reading small contingency tables and "p < 0.05" as "reliable difference"; no further math.
