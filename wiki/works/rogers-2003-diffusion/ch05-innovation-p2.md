# The Innovation-Decision Process, part 2: re-invention, confirmation, discontinuance, and evidence for stages

## L0 — One line
Adopters actively reshape innovations (re-invention) and sometimes drop them later (discontinuance), and tentative evidence supports the idea that adoption is a staged process over time.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part asks what happens after the "adopt or reject" decision, and whether the stage model is real. Rogers argues that innovations, or at least many of them, are not fixed: users re-invent them. Adoption is often not final: people seek reinforcement at the confirmation stage, and many discontinue, especially later adopters. Rogers says a definitive answer on stages is impossible, partly because mental processes are hard to probe and partly because most diffusion research is variance research, not process research. Still, several studies tentatively support stages (Generalization 5-12). Two related models, the hierarchy-of-effects and the stages-of-change, are described; the stages-of-change is used to segment audiences and to measure intervention effects (family planning in Tanzania; drunk-driving offenders in New Mexico).

**Prerequisites:** the five-stage innovation-decision model (knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation); adopter categories; the idea of a change agent.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Re-invention is more likely for complex or general-purpose innovations and for heterogeneous user problems, and may be more frequent later in diffusion; loosely bundled innovations can be fitted flexibly by adopters. It can also be cosmetic localization (pdf 247–248).
- Re-invention changes the picture of adopters: on many occasions they are active participants who help co-construct the innovation, not only acceptors or rejecters (pdf 249).
- At the confirmation stage people seek reinforcement and try to avoid or reduce dissonance; they may reverse the decision if they meet conflicting messages (pdf 251–252).
- Discontinuance can be replacement or disenchantment. Later adopters discontinue more often (Generalization 5-11); discontinuance rose from 14% (innovators/early adopters) to 40% (laggards) in an Ohio study (pdf 252–253).
- Discontinuance cases (smoking, organic farming) illustrate discontinuance linked to changing norms, media depiction and regulation (smoking), and a farmer once classed as a laggard who was later called a superinnovator (organic farming) (pdf 255–258).
- Whether stages exist is hard to test: mental processes are hard to probe, and most diffusion research is variance research, with process research scarce (pdf 258–260).
- Tentative evidence supports stages (Generalization 5-12): clearest for knowledge and decision, somewhat less so for persuasion, with only limited data for implementation and confirmation (pdf 260–262).
- The hierarchy-of-effects and stages-of-change models parallel the stages; the latter segments audiences by readiness and measures intervention effects (pdf 262–265).

### Why does re-invention occur? (pdf 247–248)
Rogers lists reasons, some in the innovation and some in the adopter:
- **Complexity.** More complex, harder-to-understand innovations are more likely to be re-invented. This may be a simplification or even a misunderstanding (pdf 247).
- **Ignorance or inadequate learning.** This is more likely when adopters have little contact with change agents or earlier adopters. Example: a geographic computer system was re-invented more often when change agents created only awareness-knowledge than when they consulted at the implementation stage (pdf 247).
- **Bundling.** General concepts or tools with many uses (computer, Internet) are more likely re-invented. A *tightly bundled* innovation has interdependent components, so adopting one element means adopting the others. A *loosely bundled* innovation can be flexibly fitted. So designers can influence re-invention (pdf 247).
- **Heterogeneous problems.** Re-invention is greater when individuals and organizational problems are more heterogeneous (pdf 248).
- **Local pride.** "Pseudo-re-invention" is cosmetic change or just a new name. Havelock found that locals say the innovation is local (353 U.S. school superintendents) (pdf 248).
- **Change-agency influence.** Most agencies oppose re-invention, but decentralized diffusion systems may encourage it. DARE was not managed by a federal agency, so each school did its own thing (pdf 248).
- **Organizational structure.** Innovations are adapted to the adopting organization's structure (pdf 248).
- **Later in diffusion.** Later adopters profit from earlier adopters' experience. Example: a state law may become more comprehensive as loopholes are closed (pdf 248).

**Conclusion.** Adopters are often active participants who struggle to give meaning to the idea. An innovation is "not a fixed entity"; users shape it as they learn by using it (pdf 249). Examples:
- The telephone was conceived as a business tool, but early callers, especially women, embedded sociability in it.
- The French Minitel was designed as one-to-many but became many-to-many.
- SMS was created by cell-phone users, probably Japanese teenagers (pdf 249).

*Intuition note:* An innovation is shaped partly by its users after release. Studying only "adopt or reject" misses what adopters did with the idea.

### Case: horse culture of the Plains Indians (pdf 250)
The Plains Indians copied the whole horse culture from the Spanish, including saddles, stirrups, the crupper and the lariat. One aspect was re-invented: the travois, a drag frame once pulled by dogs. They called the horse a "dog", enlarged the travois, and harnessed the horse to it. Only later did they ride (pdf 250).

### The confirmation stage (pdf 250–252)
- Adopt/reject is often not the end. Mason found that Oregon farmers sought information after deciding as well as before (pdf 250–251).
- At confirmation the decision-maker seeks reinforcement and may reverse the decision if exposed to conflicting messages (pdf 251).
- **Dissonance** is an uncomfortable internal disequilibrium that people try to reduce by changing knowledge, attitudes or actions (Festinger). It operates in three ways (pdf 251):
  1. Awareness of a need leads to information-seeking (knowledge stage).
  2. A favorable attitude without adoption (the KAP-gap) pushes toward adoption (decision and implementation stages).
  3. After deciding, new information may lead to discontinuance, or pro-innovation messages after a rejection may lead to later adoption (confirmation).
- Reversing a decision is often hard (for example, a cash outlay). So individuals frequently seek only information expected to support the decision, which is selective exposure. Some conflicting information still gets through (pdf 252).

### Discontinuance (pdf 252–254)
- **Definition:** a decision to reject an innovation after having adopted it (pdf 252).
- **Extent:** Leuthold found in Wisconsin that the discontinuance rate was just as important as the adoption rate in determining the level of adoption at a given time. In a given year there were about as many discontinuers as first-time adopters (pdf 252).
- **Two types:**
  - *Replacement:* rejecting an idea to adopt a better one that supersedes it (tetracycline, calculators, CDs, e-mail) (pdf 252).
  - *Disenchantment:* dissatisfaction with performance. Possible causes include no perceived relative advantage, a safety decree, or misuse (pdf 253).
- **Generalization 5-11:** later adopters are more likely to discontinue than earlier adopters (pdf 253). Rogers suggests that disenchantment from misuse seems more common among later adopters than earlier ones, who have more formal education and perhaps more understanding of the scientific method. He adds that later adopters have fewer resources, which may prevent adoption or cause discontinuance.
- **Data:**

| Study | Innov./early adopters | Early majority | Late majority | Laggards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ohio farmers (Bishop and Coughenor) | 14% | 27% | 34% | 40% |
| Canadian farmers (Leuthold) | 18% | 24% | 26% | 37% |

  (pdf 253)
- High discontinuers have less formal education, lower socioeconomic status and less change-agent contact, like laggards (pdf 253). So many "laggards" adopted but then discontinued, which challenges the earlier assumption that they are simply slow (pdf 253).
- Discontinuance may signal that the innovation was not routinized. Rogers says "perhaps" attributes such as relative advantage and compatibility are negatively related to discontinuance. He expects low relative advantage to mean slow adoption and fast discontinuance (pdf 253–254).
- **Organizations also discontinue.** Greve's study: by 1993, 90% of radio stations with "easy listening" had dropped it, influenced by corporate contacts with other stations. "Jumping ship" was itself a diffusion process (pdf 254).
- Burt, whom Rogers calls an eminent diffusion scholar, wrote: "As much as change is about adapting to the new, it is about detaching from the old." Rogers adds that adoption of a new idea "almost always" means discontinuing a previous one (pdf 254).

*Intuition note:* Leuthold found that discontinuance matters as much as adoption for the level of adoption at a given time. Ignoring discontinuers can hide this, and can misjudge who is slow (many laggards adopt, then drop).

### Case: discontinuance of smoking (pdf 255–256)
- Half of all ever-regular smokers (still alive) have quit in the U.S. (pdf 255).
- Economic factors were not the main cause. A 1989 California 25-cent tax and the 2000 industry settlement raised prices, yet 25% still smoked (pdf 255).
- Other factors: the 1964 Surgeon General's report, pack warnings, the 1971 end of broadcast ads, restrictions on where smoking is allowed, airline bans, and second-hand smoke evidence. The image of smoking shifted from "cool" to negative (pdf 255).
- City ordinances diffused from Las Cruces (1995) to other towns, 1995–2002. The issue was increasingly framed as health, not economics. Where enough people framed it as individual rights, as in Alamagordo, the ordinance was rejected in 2002 (pdf 255–256).
- Cessation was due in part to price, and more so to changes in media depiction and city restrictions (pdf 256).

### Case: forced discontinuance and organic farming (pdf 257–258)
- In his 1954 Iowa study (148 farmers), Rogers classed a farmer who rejected agricultural chemicals as a laggard. Rogers then thought his attitude irrational (pdf 257).
- Later came the 1972 DDT ban and bans on DES, antibiotic swine feed supplements and 2,4-D. In 1980 the USDA reversed its opposition to organic farming (pdf 257).
- Organic farmers are mostly commercial, progressive and better educated, with larger farms, which are characteristics of innovators. The USDA's integrated pest management (IPM) diffused partly because insects developed pesticide resistance (pdf 257–258).
- Conclusion: by present standards the farmer was a "superinnovator" (pdf 258).

### Are there stages? (pdf 258–262)
- A definitive answer is impossible, since mental processes are hard to probe. Stages may be a useful simplification, perhaps a social construction. Sharp distinctions are not expected (pdf 258–259).
- **Intellectual roots:** Dewey and Mead criticized stimulus–response as an oversimplification. They proposed stimulus–interpretation–response. This influenced the rural sociologists who first posited stages (pdf 259).
- **Process vs variance research** (pdf 260):
  - *Process research* determines the sequence of events over time, usually with qualitative methods and in-depth interviews. Statistics are seldom used.
  - *Variance research* determines covariances among variables, not time order, usually with quantitative methods.
  - Most diffusion research is variance research on cross-sectional data. Scholars have often missed this distinction (pdf 260).
  - The scarcity of process research is a basic reason for the lack of definitive understanding (pdf 260).
- **Evidence for stages** (pdf 261):
  - In Beal and Rogers's Iowa study, most respondents recognized a series of stages and reported different channels or sources at different stages.
  - None of the 148 adopted immediately after awareness of a new weed spray. For a livestock feed, 63% of adopters reported a different year for knowledge than for decision.
  - Skipped stages: none skipped knowledge or decision; a few skipped trial.
  - Coleman: physicians reported different channels at knowledge and persuasion.
  - LaMar: 262 California teachers resembled the farmers.
  - Kohl: all 58 Oregon superintendents reported all stages.
- **Generalization 5-12:** stages exist in the innovation-decision process. The evidence is clearest for knowledge and decision, somewhat less so for persuasion, and limited for implementation and confirmation (pdf 262).

*Intuition note:* Much of the evidence comes from respondents reporting the sources and timing of their own steps. Rogers calls the evidence tentative and says process research, which is scarce, is what is needed to test stages properly.

### Hierarchy-of-effects (pdf 262)
- McGuire's model: individuals usually move cumulatively from knowledge change to overt behavior change, in stages parallel to the innovation-decision stages (Table 5-1).
- Channels play different roles. Mass media are generally more effective for knowledge, interpersonal channels more likely for persuasion.
- A campaign will have greater effects near the top of the hierarchy, since knowledge is easier to change than behavior, at least in the relatively short term. Example: a six-month intervention might raise knowledge for 35% of the audience but move only 2–3% to adopt (pdf 262).

### Stages-of-change (pdf 262–265)
- Prochaska's five-stage model is widely used in public health (pdf 262–263). The stages are:
  1. Precontemplation (as printed: aware of a problem and begins to think about overcoming it).
  2. Contemplation (seriously thinking, no commitment yet).
  3. Preparation (intends to act soon).
  4. Action (changes behavior).
  5. Maintenance (consolidates the change) (pdf 263–264).
- **Table 5-1 (from the image, pdf 263).** Each row below pairs an innovation-decision stage with its hierarchy-of-effects items and its Prochaska stage:

| Innovation-decision stage | Hierarchy-of-effects items | Stage-of-change |
|---|---|---|
| I. Knowledge | 1 recall of information; 2 comprehension of messages; 3 knowledge or skill for effective adoption | Precontemplation |
| II. Persuasion | 4 liking; 5 discussion with others; 6 acceptance of message; 7 positive image of message and innovation; 8 support from the system | Contemplation |
| III. Decision | 9 intention to seek more information; 10 intention to try | Preparation |
| IV. Implementation | 11 acquiring more information; 12 regular use; 13 continued use | Action |
| V. Confirmation | 14 recognizing benefits; 15 integration into routine; 16 promoting to others | Maintenance |

- The model implies a cascading progression. A changing decisional balance moves people: "pros" rise and "cons" fall. Self-efficacy increases at each successive stage (pdf 264).
- **Smokers' readiness (Prochaska):** 10–15% prepared for action, 30–40% in contemplation, 50–60% in precontemplation. A smoking cessation program will fail if it assumes all three are equally ready. This is one reason for dropouts and relapse (pdf 264).
- Stages classify individuals into readiness segments, each with a tailored program. Communities can be classified too (pdf 264).
- The model can also measure a diffusion intervention by how far it moves people through the stages (pdf 265). Two studies use this approach:
  - **Tanzania (Vaughan and Rogers):** the radio soap *Twende na Wakati*, 1993–97, about family planning. Annual surveys covered about 3,000 households. Dodoma was a control for 1993–95. Exposure had some direct effect on listeners, but the main impacts were in stimulating peer communication. About 23% of listeners adopted a method (pdf 265).
  - **MADD Victim Impact Panels (Polacsek et al.):** 813 DWI offenders in New Mexico were randomly assigned to a panel or not. All attended a mandatory DWI school. The unit ends at "No difference was found between drunk drivers…" (pdf 265).

## Toy example (ours)
Aim: show why discontinuance matters for the *level* of adoption. A town has 100 farmers, 20 of whom use a practice. In one year, 8 non-users adopt (first-time adopters) and 7 users discontinue.
- Users at year end: 20 + 8 − 7 = 21.
- Counting only adoptions would give 20 + 8 = 28, an overstatement of 7.
- Here discontinuers (7) nearly equal first-time adopters (8), so the level barely moves. This matches Leuthold's finding that the two rates were about equal in a given year (pdf 252).
