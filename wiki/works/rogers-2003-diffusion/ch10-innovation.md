# Innovation in Organizations

## L0 — One line
Organizations adopt innovations through a five-stage process (agenda-setting, matching, redefining/restructuring, clarifying, routinizing) in which implementation is complex and does not always follow the adoption decision, and in which the innovation and the organization both usually change.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Organizations, not only individuals, adopt many innovations. Early research scored organizations on "innovativeness" (earliness of adoption) and found low correlations with structure variables; size was consistently related to it. Rogers describes a shift to tracing the innovation process over time. Some structures may help initiation (planning up to the adopt decision) but hinder implementation (putting into use). Champions (people who push an idea through) often contribute to success. Implementation typically is mutual adaptation: the innovation is re-invented (modified by adopters) and the organization restructured. Perceptions and framing can matter more than objective results (Diamond Lane). Sustainability is more likely with participation, re-invention, fit and champions. The chapter matters because many new communication technologies have failed at implementation.

**Prerequisites:** the individual innovation-decision process, adopter categories and critical mass (earlier chapters); correlation as "how strongly two variables move together"; independent and dependent variables.

## L2 — Key ideas
- There are three decision types (optional, collective, authority), plus contingent decisions; collective and authority decisions are those in which an organization is usually the deciding system (pdf 494–495).
- Early studies treated each organization as one unit, using one top executive's answers. They found low correlations, and the validity of the executive-only data was in doubt (pdf 500–501).
- Larger organizations are more innovative (Gen. 10-1). Rogers says size is probably a stand-in for slack resources, expertise and other unmeasured variables (pdf 502–504).
- Structural variables may work in opposite directions at initiation and implementation (Gen. 10-2), which may explain the low correlations (pdf 505–506).
- Champions help innovations succeed (Gen. 10-3). Champions are not necessarily more powerful, and in many cases people skills may matter more than power; powerful champions were typical for costly, visible or radical innovations (pdf 508).
- The process has five stages: two of initiation, three of implementation, divided by the adopt decision (pdf 515).
- Implementation is mutual adaptation of innovation and organization (Gen. 10-5). Re-invention and internal origin help acceptance (pdf 518–520).
- Sustainability is helped by participation, re-invention, fit and a champion. The Diamond Lane case shows perceptions and haste can doom an objectively successful innovation (pdf 523–528).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pdf 494)
The chapter deals mainly with collective and authority decisions, where an organization is usually the system deciding. It traces a shift from studies comparing more and less innovative organizations to studies of the innovation process, with a stage model. These stress implementation, which most earlier diffusion research did not study. Organizational innovation is more complex than individual decision-making: a number of people typically take part, perhaps both champions and opponents. Implementation is "mutual adaptation" of innovation and organization (pdf 494).

### Types of Innovation-Decisions (pdf 495)
- **Optional:** made by an individual independent of others. Examples: Iowa farmers and hybrid seed corn; doctors and a new drug.
- **Collective:** made by consensus among system members, such as a city no-smoking ordinance. Each individual must then comply.
- **Authority:** made by a few people with power, status or expertise. Example: Nokia's chief executive banned e-mail attachments for security.
- **Contingent:** possible only after a prior innovation-decision. Example: a doctor's decision to adopt a new procedure can be made only after the hospital has decided to buy a necessary piece of equipment. Sequences of the other types can form a contingent decision.

### Organizations (pdf 496–499)
An organization is a stable system of people working together toward common goals through a hierarchy of ranks and a division of labor (pdf 496). Predictable structure comes from five features:
1. Predetermined goals.
2. Prescribed roles: sets of activities attached to positions, which persist as individuals come and go.
3. An authority structure.
4. Written rules and regulations.
5. Informal patterns, which emerge over time and serve important functions. Bureaucracies often try to depersonalize relationships (pdf 496–497).

Despite this stability, innovation goes on all the time in most organizations, even though barriers exist (pdf 497). Weber described bureaucracies as an "iron cage" of authoritarian control. It first works rationally, but effectiveness is often lost as rules are over-enforced and leaders turn impersonal, yet members keep supporting it (pdf 497).

**Virtual organizations** (pdf 497–499). A virtual organization is a network of geographically distant employees linked by electronic communication, doing most work at a distance. Rogers' example is Complexica: about a dozen employees, several million dollars in annual sales, a Santa Fe headquarters with the president and one computer, and staff in places such as London and Vienna (pdf 497–498). Reported advantages:
- lower office, parking and commuting costs;
- staff located near customers or suppliers;
- more flexibility, less hierarchy, permeable boundaries (pdf 498–499).

Some US software firms hire teams in India so work continues around the clock; the Indian engineers are paid about 10 percent of US salaries (pdf 498–499). Some investigations found virtual employees show less organizational identification, lower satisfaction and higher turnover. Virtual communication may cause more conflict, and training may help (pdf 499). Virtuality can itself be seen as an innovation diffusing among firms (pdf 499).

*Intuition note:* Organizations are built to be stable, yet innovation goes on in most of them all the time. Rogers notes that virtuality can be considered an innovation, so the very form of an organization can be new.

### Organizational Innovativeness (pdf 499–501)
Diffusion research began with individual deciders. When extended to doctors and teachers, it ignored that they work inside organizations. Later studies made the organization the unit of adoption, but each organization was reduced to one individual's answers (usually the top executive). Methods were copied from individual studies. Hundreds were done by the 1970s, then attention turned to tracing the process over time (pdf 499–500).

Findings: many characteristics parallel individual ones (larger organizations, like richer individuals, are more innovative). Some have no individual counterpart: **system openness** (degree members link to outsiders) is positively related, and **formalization** (emphasis on following rules) negatively related (pdf 500).

Why the studies became "passé" (pdf 501):
1. Correlations between organizational qualities and innovativeness were low. Innovativeness was a composite score over ten to twenty innovations, so process (over-time) aspects were lost in a cross-sectional "variance research" design.
2. A top executive's answers may not represent the organization. In Bingham and Frendreis (1978), eight officials in each of 276 cities answered about three budgeting innovations. The chief executive and the financial officer agreed on whether the city had adopted only about two thirds of the time.

### Size and Organizational Innovativeness (pdf 502–504)
**Generalization 10-1: Larger organizations are more innovative** (pdf 502).
- Mytinger (1968), 40 California health departments: size of department and of community was "perhaps the most compelling concomitant to innovativeness" (pdf 502).
- Mahler and Rogers (1999), 324 German banks, 12 communication technologies. Scores used the number adopted and how early. Bank assets correlated .75 with innovativeness and number of employees .70 (pdf 502–503). The bank adopter-category distribution was approximately similar to that of individuals (pdf 503).

This may seem surprising given the business wisdom that small firms are more flexible, but the relationship holds across many studies (pdf 503). Two reasons are offered:
- Size is easy to measure, so almost every study includes it.
- Size is probably a surrogate for several dimensions that lead to innovation: total resources, slack resources (resources beyond ongoing needs), technical expertise and structure. These "lurking" variables were not well measured, and size may be a convenient stand-in for them (pdf 503–504).

*Intuition note:* Size predicts innovativeness well, but Rogers suggests it may be only a proxy. What really drives innovation could be the extra resources and expertise that big organizations tend to have.

### Structural Characteristics (pdf 504–506)
Independent variables fall into leader characteristics (attitude toward change, +), internal structure, and external characteristics (system openness, +) (Figure 10-2, pdf 504). Internal structure variables, with their usual sign:

| Variable | Definition | Sign with innovativeness |
|---|---|---|
| Centralization | power concentrated in a few people | − |
| Complexity | members have high knowledge and expertise (range of specialties, professionalism) | + |
| Formalization | emphasis on following rules | − |
| Interconnectedness | units linked by interpersonal networks | + |
| Organizational slack | uncommitted resources | +, especially for costly innovations |
| Size | — | + |

Centralization restricts the range of ideas considered, but it can encourage implementation once a decision is made (pdf 504–505). Complexity helps members grasp an innovation's value, but may make consensus on implementation hard. Formalization inhibits considering innovations but encourages implementing them (pdf 505).

**Generalization 10-2:** each structural variable may relate to innovation in one direction during initiation and the opposite direction during implementation. Low centralization, high complexity and low formalization facilitate initiation but make implementation difficult (Zaltman et al., 1973) (pdf 505–506). Separating the subprocesses better explains the low correlations (pdf 506).

*Intuition note:* A structure that generates ideas (loose, expert-rich) may be poor at executing them, and a tight structure the reverse. Ignoring the split between the two phases may help explain the low correlations Rogers reports.

Newer-style studies (pdf 506–507):
- **Fennell (1984):** 173 Illinois companies with at least 250 workers, two related innovations (alcoholism counseling; insurance coverage for treatment). Adopting the insurance facilitated adopting the counseling. Larger size and complexity related to adopting the insurance. Rogers calls for research that does not assume innovations spread independently.
- **Meyer and Goes (1988):** 12 medical innovations in 25 hospitals, so 300 innovation-decisions were the units. Each was scored on a nine-point scale from awareness (1) through regular use (8) to expansion (9). Perceived attributes (observability, low risk, low complexity) explained 40 percent of variance. Environmental, organizational and leadership variables explained 11 percent. Large, complex, aggressively marketing, urban hospitals were more innovative. The hospital's chief executive often mattered, so champions were crucial.

### The Role of Champions (pdf 507–510)
A champion is a charismatic individual who throws his or her weight behind an innovation, overcoming indifference or resistance. Opponents can block routinization (pdf 507).

**Generalization 10-3: the presence of an innovation champion contributes to success.** Schön: "The new idea either finds a champion or dies." (pdf 507).
- Day (1984): champions tended to be powerful for costly, visible or radical innovations. For less radical ones they are often middle managers (pdf 508).
- Goodman and Steckler (1989), 10 programs in Virginia health agencies: the best champions were assistant or division directors. They held a key linking position, understood people's aspirations, and had interpersonal and negotiating skills. They acted as brokers, orchestrating a coalition that redefined the innovation and the organization. Rogers compares them to opinion leaders in a community (pdf 508).
- Howell and Higgins (1990), 25 champions matched with 25 nonchampions: champions were higher risk takers, more innovative and more influential, but not necessarily more powerful (pdf 508).

**El Paso, 2001** (pdf 508–510): A public-health master's intern, assigned to research for a coalition, was asked more and more for facts (ordinances elsewhere, business effects, restaurant-owner attitudes). He testified about his own health problems as a restaurant worker and became a day-to-day lobbyist who sensed each council member's leaning. The council voted 7 to 1 for the ordinance in June 2001. A respected council member, elected mayor just before the vote, also acted as champion. Few citizens knew of the intern (pdf 510).

Elsewhere, a fifteen-year-old girl chaired the anti-smoking coalition in Silver City, New Mexico. A ten-year-old boy with asthma was a main champion in Lubbock. Rogers says these examples suggest champions come in all ages, with varied power and abilities. Their exact characteristics perhaps depend on the innovation and the organization (pdf 510).

### The Innovation Process in Organizations (pdf 510–512)
Zaltman and colleagues (1973) marked a turning point. The main dependent variable often became implementation (putting into use) rather than adoption (the decision). After the early 1970s, studies centered on one innovation over time, often a new communication technology (pdf 510–511). Data come from key actors' recalled perceptions, organizational records and other sources (pdf 511).

Studies exploded after the 1980s for two reasons (pdf 511):
- business-school scholars became interested (the University of Minnesota program led by Van de Ven, begun 1983: 30 scholars, 14 in-depth case studies);
- new communication technologies were widely introduced, and many implementations failed.

Interactive innovations like e-mail give little advantage to the first adopters, so adoption is slow until critical mass (pdf 511–512). Usage can be measured from computer records (pdf 512). The research assumes organizational variables act over and above the sum of individuals ("supercharger") (pdf 512). Van de Ven and Rogers (1988) note that difficulties may show a poor fit between innovation and problem, so structure is not necessarily a negative influence (pdf 512).

### Adoption of New Communication Technologies (pdf 513–514)
These technologies need much learning and time.
- **Heikkilä (1995):** in a Finnish company, learning to use a PC took months and often 20 to 25 percent of employees' time, with colleagues' help. Past writing seriously underestimated the effort (pdf 513).
- **Igbaria et al. (1994):** 471 managers in 54 companies. Computer anxiety was a barrier. Anxiety was negatively related to fun, which helped employees start learning. Perceived usefulness was six times more influential than fun in explaining use. Training can relieve anxiety, and equipment alone is unlikely to increase use without training (pdf 513–514).
- **Volvo, Gothenburg:** in 1985 employees got computers and were ordered to adopt e-mail. E-mail accounts rose from 18 to 40 percent but the number of actual users stayed about the same, since internal mail and telephones were convenient. Top-leader support, department managers, peers and local support centers helped adoption pass critical mass. After five years most of 7,400 employees used e-mail regularly (pdf 514). So diffusion takes time even when leaders strongly favor an innovation (pdf 514).

### Stages in the Innovation Process (pdf 515–525)
Five stages (Figure 10-3). Later stages cannot be undertaken until earlier ones are completed, explicitly or implicitly. **Initiation** (stages 1–2) is all information gathering, conceptualizing and planning up to the adopt decision. **Implementation** (stages 3–5) is all events, actions and decisions in putting the innovation into use (pdf 515, 518).

**1. Agenda-setting** (pdf 515–517). A general organizational problem is defined, creating a perceived need. It is how needs bubble up and are prioritized, and it includes searching the environment for innovations. It often takes an extended period, several years. The Minnesota program concluded innovations are not initiated "on the spur of the moment" by a single incident or entrepreneur (pdf 516).
- A **performance gap** (expectation versus actual performance) can impel the search. **Generalization 10-4: a performance gap can trigger the innovation process.** In most Minnesota cases a shock reached a threshold of attention (pdf 516).
- Organizations scan the environment opportunistically. March: "Answers often precede questions." Most organizations face many problems but know few innovations, so starting from a wanted solution gives a good chance of finding a matching problem (pdf 516).
- Wildemuth (1992), 43 computer adoptions in three corporations: problems were identified rationally, but no search for innovations followed; purchases were opportunistic. Sometimes need triggers the process, sometimes knowledge of an innovation creates a need (pdf 517).
- Walker (1977): whoever shapes the legislative agenda magnifies influence. Rogers adds that setting the agenda in an organization is "tremendously powerful" (pdf 517).

**2. Matching** (pdf 517–518). A problem from the agenda is fit with an innovation, and the match is planned and designed. Members test feasibility ("reality testing") and anticipate benefits and problems. Judging a mismatch leads to rejection before implementation. Good matching is key to whether the idea is sustained, and fit is a type of compatibility. Goodman and Steckler (1989) found "finding a home" was crucial to later sustainability. The match decision is the watershed between initiation and implementation (pdf 517–518).

**3. Redefining/restructuring** (pdf 518–521). The innovation is re-invented to fit the organization, and the organization is modified to fit it. Tyre and Orlikowski (1994) found only a brief window in which an innovation could be modified before routinization (pdf 518). Structure change examples: a new unit for an online training system; an e-mail system giving every employee direct access to the chief executive (pdf 518).

**Generalization 10-5: both the innovation and the organization usually change.** The innovation almost never fits perfectly, and organizations often underestimate the changes needed (pdf 519).
- **Kodak's Solagen** (Leonard-Barton, 1988a): a chemical process cut gelatin production from six weeks to 48 hours. Operators had to read dials and decide in minutes, not days with a pole in the pit, and could not cope. Implementation failed despite obvious advantages (pdf 519).
- Internally generated innovations are generally more likely to be implemented, since they fit and participants see them as "theirs." Minnesota: units not involved in development or re-invention tend to see an innovation as "an external mandate" (pdf 519–520).
- Rogers calls this stage social constructionism, in which perceptions of problem and innovation are modified together (pdf 520).

*Radical innovations* (pdf 520–521). A radical innovation is a major change amounting to a new paradigm. Radicalness is indexed by the knowledge members must acquire. The more radical, the more uncertainty and the harder the implementation.
- Dewar and Dutton (1986): 40 footwear firms, six innovations. Large firms had more technical specialists and adopted more radical innovations. Incremental ones were adopted more equally by large and small firms.
- Radical innovations may need an unstructured decision process, one with no predetermined routines.
- Gibson and Rogers (1994), the MCC R&D consortium (20 electronics firms, 1982): firms had to learn to collaborate while competing. By 1994 several hundred consortia existed and the idea had become less radical and more routine.

*Intuition note:* Implementation is a two-way adjustment, not just fitting a tool into a fixed organization. Innovations made inside, or flexible enough to reshape, feel like the organization's own and are accepted more easily.

**4. Clarifying** (pdf 521–523). The innovation spreads and its meaning becomes clearer. Too-rapid implementation can be disastrous (Diamond Lane, below).
- **Alamogordo, NM, 2002:** a council member champion pushed for an immediate vote. Many residents framed the issue as individual rights, not public health. Help from the neighboring Las Cruces coalition was resented. The council voted it down, and a later referendum failed narrowly (pdf 521–522).
- A policy can be framed in several ways. A no-smoking ordinance can be framed as health, economic or individual-rights issue, which affects approval (pdf 522).
- Practical meaning must be settled (fine amount, who enforces, who posts signs) (pdf 522).
- Clarifying is social construction: members ask what it does and whom it affects, and build a common understanding through talk. Champions usually play an important role (pdf 523).

**5. Routinizing** (pdf 523–525). The innovation is incorporated into regular activities and loses its separate identity, ending the process. **Sustainability** is the degree an innovation continues after initial adoption efforts end (also called "institutionalization") (pdf 523).
- More participation makes sustainability more likely. Authority decisions are at risk if the authorities leave, and collective decisions usually sustain better (pdf 523–524).
- More re-invention is positively related to sustainability, since members regard the innovation as their own (pdf 524).
- **O'Loughlin et al. (1998):** 189 heart-disease prevention interventions in Canada. About 44 percent were sustained and another 35 percent continued on shaky grounds. Sustainability related to re-invention, fit and a local champion (pdf 524).
- Sustainability is not universally positive, since an ineffective innovation may rightly be discontinued (pdf 524).
- **Bach (1989):** 67 clinic physicians and assistants were surveyed before, one day after and 14 weeks after a log-booklet launch. One third adopted within two weeks. This may have happened because the innovation met a specific need and originated inside. Clarifying was almost unnecessary (pdf 524–525).

### The Santa Monica Freeway Diamond Lane Experiment (pdf 526–528)
In the mid-1970s one lane in each direction of Interstate 10 was restricted to buses and cars with at least three people, to improve flow, cut gasoline use and reduce pollution (pdf 526).
- **Objective results:** the lane carried 90 percent as many people in 30 percent as many vehicles. Travel time fell from 20 to 15 minutes, and non-preferential lanes were one minute faster. Occupancy rose from 1.20 to 1.35 persons per vehicle, bus ridership rose 250 percent and car pools tripled (pdf 526).
- **Outcome:** public reaction was very negative, and the experiment ended after five months. The lane looked nearly empty to stalled drivers. Reactions included nails thrown on the lane, cardboard passengers and an opposition group. About 90 percent of several thousand letters opposed it. CALTRANS saw the reaction as temporary. The city council voted against it, and a lawsuit ended it (pdf 526–527).
- **Causes:** the project was developed in haste after crises (the 1974 oil shortage, the end of freeway building). A similar lane on the Shirley Highway was added to existing lanes, but in Los Angeles a lane was taken away, throwing other lanes into a crawl. Launch day was a Monday ("Mad Monday"). Accidents rose from 11 to 59 per week in the first week, later 25. Highway Patrol cooperation was poor, and ticketing eventually stopped (pdf 527–528).
- **Lessons:** perceptions shape public acceptance, not objective indicators. Media coverage can shape acceptance. Adding a lane differs from taking one away. The case shows the crucial importance of implementation (pdf 528).

### New Communication Technologies in Organizations (pdf 528–529)
Internet-related innovations are a favorite research topic, and the technologies can also record diffusion data. Feeney (2002) used Blackboard at Temple University, which logged date and course for 2,800 course adoptions over 30 months, unobtrusively, without recall data (pdf 528–529).

### Summary (pdf 529–531)
Rogers restates the key points. Organizational innovativeness research found larger organizations more innovative (10-1) and low correlations, perhaps because of opposite effects across subprocesses (10-2). Champions contribute to success (10-3) and may be powerful or lower-level coordinators. The process has initiation (agenda-setting, matching) and implementation (redefining/restructuring, clarifying, routinizing). A performance gap can trigger it (10-4), and both innovation and organization usually change (10-5). Sustainability is more likely with widespread participation, re-invention and a champion. Routinization ends the process (pdf 529–531).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show how opposite effects at two phases can produce zero correlation in a combined score (Gen. 10-2). Take four hypothetical organizations. Formalization $F$ is 0 (low) or 1 (high). Scores run 0–10, and total $T = I + M$ is the sum of the initiation score $I$ and the implementation score $M$.

| Org | F | I | M | T |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 8 | 3 | 11 |
| B | 0 | 7 | 2 | 9 |
| C | 1 | 3 | 8 | 11 |
| D | 1 | 2 | 7 | 9 |

Means: $\bar F = 0.5$, $\bar I = 20/4 = 5$, $\bar M = 20/4 = 5$, $\bar T = 40/4 = 10$.

- Deviations of $F$: $-0.5, -0.5, 0.5, 0.5$, with $\sum (F-\bar F)^2 = 4 \times 0.25 = 1$.
- Deviations of $I$: $3, 2, -2, -3$. The cross-products sum to $-1.5 - 1 - 1 - 1.5 = -5$, and $\sum (I-\bar I)^2 = 9+4+4+9 = 26$. So $r_{F,I} = -5/\sqrt{1 \times 26} = -5/5.099 \approx -0.98$.
- Deviations of $M$: $-2, -3, 3, 2$. The cross-products sum to $1 + 1.5 + 1.5 + 1 = 5$, and $\sum (M-\bar M)^2 = 26$. So $r_{F,M} \approx +0.98$.
- Deviations of $T$: $1, -1, 1, -1$. The cross-products sum to $-0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 - 0.5 = 0$, so $r_{F,T} = 0$.

A study using only the combined score $T$ would find formalization unrelated to innovativeness, though it strongly affects each phase in opposite directions. This is a simplified illustration, not a claim that real effects cancel exactly.

## Questions this raises
- If size is only a proxy, which of slack, expertise or structure matters most, and how could a study separate them?
- Why does re-invention raise sustainability, and could too much re-invention destroy the innovation's benefits?
- Meyer and Goes find that innovation attributes explain far more variance than organizational variables. Does this weaken the case for studying organizations at all?
- How can managers learn what perceived outcomes will be before launching, as the Diamond Lane failure suggests they should?
