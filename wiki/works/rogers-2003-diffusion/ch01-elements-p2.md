# Elements of Diffusion, part 2: Time and the Social System

## L0 — One line
Diffusion unfolds over time (decision stages, adopter categories, S-shaped adoption) inside a social system whose structure, norms, leaders and decision types shape who adopts, and when.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers continues his four elements of diffusion with the last two: time and the social system. Time enters in three ways: the five-stage innovation-decision process, individual innovativeness (adopter categories), and a system's rate of adoption (an S-curve). The social system matters because its structure, norms, opinion leaders, change agents and type of decision-making (optional, collective, authority, contingent) affect adoption and its consequences. The Ryan and Gross hybrid corn study illustrates all four elements and helped found the classical research tradition. The point: many aspects of diffusion cannot be explained by individual traits alone, and Rogers concludes that diffusion is fundamentally a social process.

**Prerequisites:** chapter-1 definitions from part 1 (innovation, communication channel, homophily/heterophily, relative advantage); cumulative frequency and percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Time enters diffusion in three ways: the innovation-decision process, innovativeness (how early a member adopts compared with others in the system), and rate of adoption (how fast a system adopts) (pdf 59).
- Five stages (knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation) are an uncertainty-reducing information process. Mass media can effectively convey the early, knowledge-stage information; near peers are particularly likely to convey the evaluative information sought at persuasion and especially decision (pdf 60).
- Cumulative adoption over time is usually S-shaped; its steepness varies by innovation and by system (pdf 62–63).
- A system has a direct effect on diffusion through structure and norms, beyond individual traits. The two Korean villages show this (pdf 63–66).
- Opinion leaders are credible insiders who conform to norms. Change agents are outside professionals who are often heterophilous to clients (different from them in education, status and the like), and aides (less than fully professional change agents) help bridge the gap (pdf 66–68).
- Four decision types (optional, collective, authority, contingent) exist; optional, collective and authority decisions differ in speed, and the type for a given idea can change over time (pdf 68–70).
- Consequences can be desirable or not, direct or indirect, anticipated or not. Change agents rarely predict an innovation's meaning (pdf 70–71).
- The hybrid corn study established the customary method and, without network data, suggested a social snowball. Its missing network data is a noted weakness (pdf 72–76).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 3. Time (pdf 59)
Time is often ignored in behavioral research. Including it is a strength of diffusion research, but measuring it by respondent recall can be criticized (see Chapter 3). Time enters in three ways: (1) the innovation-decision process, (2) innovativeness, (3) rate of adoption (pdf 59).

**The innovation-decision process.** This is the passage of an individual (or other decision-making unit) from first knowledge, to forming an attitude, to a decision to adopt or reject, to implementation, to confirmation (pdf 59). The five steps are:
1. Knowledge: learning that the innovation exists and gaining some understanding of how it works.
2. Persuasion: forming a favorable or unfavorable attitude.
3. Decision: activities leading to a choice to adopt or reject.
4. Implementation: putting it into use. Re-invention (users changing the innovation) is especially likely here.
5. Confirmation: seeking reinforcement for a decision already made. The person may reverse it if exposed to conflicting messages (pdf 59).

The process is information-seeking, aimed at gradually decreasing uncertainty (pdf 59–60).
- At knowledge, people mainly want *software information*: the cause-effect understanding embodied in the technology, i.e. what it is and how and why it works. Mass media can transmit this effectively (pdf 60).
- At persuasion, and especially decision, people increasingly seek *innovation-evaluation information*: advantages and disadvantages in their own situation. Interpersonal networks with near peers are particularly likely to convey it. Mass media are "not very important" here because their messages are general (pdf 60).
- Subjective evaluations by others are especially likely to influence the decision stage, and perhaps confirmation (pdf 60).

*Adoption* is a decision to make full use of an innovation as the best course available. *Rejection* is a decision not to adopt. Decisions can be reversed. *Discontinuance* is rejecting after earlier adoption, because of dissatisfaction or replacement by a better idea. Later adoption after earlier rejection is also possible. Both occur during confirmation (pdf 60).

The stages usually occur in time order. Exceptions are possible, e.g. decision before persuasion when an authority orders adoption (pdf 60). The *innovation-decision period* is the time needed to pass through the process; it varies from many years to rapid (pdf 61). The discussion mainly concerns individual-optional decisions. When organizations or communities decide, the process is more complicated because several individuals are involved (see Chapter 10) (pdf 61).

> *Intuition note:* Adopting is not one moment but a chain in which a person slowly gets less unsure. Early on they ask "what is this?"; later they ask "is it good for me?", and neighbors answer that better than media do.

**Innovativeness and adopter categories.** Innovativeness is the degree to which an individual or unit adopts new ideas relatively earlier than other members of its system. Adopter categories classify members by innovativeness: (1) innovators, (2) early adopters, (3) early majority, (4) late majority, (5) laggards (pdf 61). Category labels are shorthand, and members of each category have much in common. A typical late-majority member has lower socioeconomic status, uses mass media little, and learns of new ideas from peers (pdf 61). Innovators actively seek information, have high mass media exposure, and have networks reaching outside the local system. They are able to cope with higher levels of uncertainty than other categories; as the first to adopt, they cannot depend on other members' subjective evaluations (pdf 61–62). Chapter 7 gives fuller portraits (pdf 62). The measure rests on the relative time of adoption (pdf 62).

**Rate of adoption.** This is the relative speed with which members of a system adopt an innovation (pdf 62). Plotted cumulatively, adopters form an S-shaped curve:
- Few adopt per period at first (innovators).
- The curve then climbs as more adopt in each period.
- It levels off as fewer non-adopters remain, and reaches an asymptote (pdf 62).

Most innovations follow an S-curve, but its slope varies. Why some spread faster is addressed in Chapter 6 (pdf 62). The rate is usually measured as the time for a certain percentage of system members to adopt. Its unit of analysis is the innovation in a system, whereas innovativeness has the individual as unit (pdf 62). Innovations perceived to have greater relative advantage, compatibility and the like have a faster rate (pdf 62). The same innovation can also have different rates in different systems. The system acts directly through norms and system-level qualities, and indirectly through its members' behavior (pdf 63).

> *Intuition note:* "Rate" describes the whole system's curve, "innovativeness" describes one person's earliness. The curve is slow at the start, fast in the middle, slow at the end, because at the start only a few innovators adopt, and at the end few non-adopters remain (pdf 62).

### 4. A Social System (pdf 63)
A social system is a set of interrelated units engaged in joint problem solving to reach a common goal. Units may be individuals, informal groups, organizations or subsystems (examples: peasant families in a Peruvian village, doctors in a hospital, US consumers). A shared objective binds it. Diffusion occurs within the system, which is a boundary for diffusion (pdf 63).

**Social structure and diffusion.** Structure is the patterned arrangement of units. It gives regularity and stability, allows prediction of behavior, and so "decreases uncertainty" (pdf 63). A formal example is a bureaucracy's hierarchy, where higher ranks can issue orders (pdf 63–64). Informal structure lies in interpersonal networks. *Communication structure* is the differentiated elements recognizable in patterned communication flows. Homophily tends to form cliques. A complete lack of structure would mean everyone talks with everyone with equal probability, as with strangers meeting at first. Regular patterns soon appear, and they partly predict when members adopt (pdf 64). Structure can facilitate or impede diffusion. Katz (1961) compares studying diffusion without structure to studying blood circulation without veins and arteries (pdf 64). Relatively few studies address structure; untangling its effects from the characteristics of the individuals is complicated (pdf 64).

**Example of system effects (Rogers and Kincaid, 1981; Korea).** Two women are alike in being illiterate, married, aged 29, with two children, husbands who are high-school graduates, and five-acre farms. They live in different villages. Family planning was adopted by 57% of eligible couples in Village A and 26% in Village B. Contraceptives were promoted equally by the national program. Rogers predicts Mrs. A is more likely to adopt: her friends and neighbors have adopted and encourage her, and A's leaders are committed to family planning while B's are not (pdf 65). So structure matters over and above individual characteristics. Individual innovativeness depends on both individual traits and the system (pdf 65).

> *Intuition note:* Two nearly identical people can behave differently because their surroundings differ. Rogers reads the 57% vs 26% gap as a system effect, over and above the individual characteristics of the members (pdf 65).

**System norms.** In the same study of 24 villages, adoption levels and chosen methods varied greatly. One village had 51% IUD adoption and one vasectomy adopter. Another had 23% vasectomy adoption. A "pill village" had all adopters choosing pills. The national program offered the same "cafeteria" of methods for ten years, so the main explanation was village norms (pdf 65–66). Norms are established behavior patterns. They define a range of tolerable behavior and a guide for members (pdf 66). Norms can be barriers, e.g. food-habit norms (the Peruvian water-boiling case; sacred cows in India; no pork for Muslims and Jews; polished white rice), at the level of nation, religion, organization or village (pdf 66).

**Opinion leaders and change agents.** The most innovative member is often seen as a deviant with low credibility, so his or her role in persuading others is very limited (pdf 66). *Opinion leadership* is the degree to which a person informally influences others' attitudes or overt behavior in a desired way with relative frequency. It is not a function of formal position. It is earned and kept by technical competence, social accessibility and conformity to norms (pdf 66). In change-oriented systems leaders are more innovative; where norms oppose change, leaders reflect that (pdf 66). Leaders can head the spread of ideas or the opposition (pdf 67). Compared with followers they are:
- more externally exposed, so somewhat more cosmopolite;
- somewhat higher in socioeconomic status;
- more innovative, depending in part on system norms.

Their most striking feature is a central place in interpersonal networks. They can lose respect by deviating too far from norms, or be "worn out" by change agents who overuse them (pdf 67).

A *change agent* is an outside professional who influences clients' innovation-decisions in a direction a change agency considers desirable. The aim is usually adoption, but sometimes slowing or preventing adoption of undesirable innovations. Change agents often use opinion leaders as lieutenants (pdf 67). They are usually university-trained, so they tend to be heterophilous from clients, which hampers communication. *Aides* are less than fully professional change agents who contact clients intensively. They are usually homophilous with clients and so bridge the heterophily gap (pdf 67–68).

**Types of innovation-decisions.** Adoption or rejection may be by an individual or by the whole system (pdf 68).
1. *Optional*: made by an individual independent of others' decisions, though norms and networks may still influence it. The Los Molinas housewife's water-boiling decision is the example. The classical diffusion model grew out of optional decisions (hybrid corn, a new antibiotic) (pdf 68).
2. *Collective*: made by consensus among system members; all usually must conform (pdf 68).
3. *Authority*: made by a few who hold power, status or expertise; members simply implement. Example: a computer company's president ordered a dress code (pdf 68–69).

These lie on a continuum of individual say in the decision, from almost complete (optional) to none (authority) (pdf 69). Collective and authority decisions are more common in organizations. Optional decisions predominate in agriculture and consumer behavior (pdf 69). Generally, authority decisions yield the fastest rate (depending on how innovative the authorities are). Optional decisions are usually faster than collective ones. Authority decisions may be circumvented during implementation (pdf 69).

Decision type can change over time. For seat belts: optional installation, then a 1966 federal law (collective); fastening stayed optional. A 1974 seat belt–ignition interlock made fastening an authority decision for a year, until Congress reversed it after negative public reaction. In the late 1980s state laws made use somewhat more authoritarian (pdf 69–70).

For smoking, the evidence on second-hand smoke led to airline bans and an EPA carcinogen classification. By 1990 many communities had no-smoking ordinances, and by 2002 about 2,400 cities had adopted. Each city's choice was optional, but then everyone in the city was bound (authority). Only 25% of US adults smoked in 2002 (pdf 70).

A fourth type, *contingent*, can be made only after a prior innovation-decision (pdf 70). Two or more tandem decisions are required, each optional, collective or authority. Examples: seat belts before the 1966 law, and no-smoking ordinances (pdf 70).

> *Intuition note:* Decision types differ in how many people choose. Authority is generally the fastest (depending on how innovative the authorities are) but may be circumvented in implementation, and the same behavior can move between types as laws and policies change.

**Consequences of innovations.** Consequences are the changes that occur to an individual or system because of adopting or rejecting an innovation (pdf 71). Three classifications:
- desirable vs undesirable (functional vs dysfunctional);
- direct vs indirect (immediate vs second-order result);
- anticipated vs unanticipated (recognized and intended, or not).

Change agents expect desirable, direct, anticipated effects, but often get unanticipated, indirect, undesirable ones. Example (Sharp, 1952): missionaries gave steel axes to an Australian aborigine tribe. The result included breakdown of family structure, prostitution, and "misuse" of the axe. Change agents can often predict *form* (visible appearance) and perhaps *function* (contribution to way of life), but seldom *meaning* (clients' subjective perception) (pdf 71). See Chapter 11 (pdf 71).

### Case: Diffusion of hybrid corn in Iowa (pdf 72–76)
Ryan and Gross (1943) is called the most influential diffusion study, despite 5,200-plus later ones. It contains all four elements (pdf 72).

**Background.**
- Hybrid corn was released to Iowa farmers in 1928.
- It yielded about 20% more per acre than open-pollinated varieties, resisted drought better, and suited mechanical pickers.
- Seed lost hybrid vigor after one generation, so farmers had to buy seed yearly instead of saving their own.
- It increased dependence on agribusiness (pdf 72).

**Study design.**
- Bryce Ryan arrived at Iowa State in 1939. Neal Gross joined in summer 1941 and did most interviewing.
- The method was survey interviews with all farmers in two small communities, using a structured questionnaire. It asked the year of adoption (the main dependent variable), the channels used at each stage, and the share of acreage in hybrid each year. It also asked education, age, farm size, income, travel to Des Moines, magazine readership and other variables (pdf 72–73). The text calls the data "qualitative", but the contrast it draws suggests quantitative (see Reading notes).
- Gross interviewed 345 farmers. Twelve with under 20 acres and 74 who began farming after hybrid corn began to diffuse were dropped, leaving 259 (pdf 73). Analysis was by hand tabulation and desk calculator (pdf 74).
- Anecdotes: Gross averaged 14 interviews a day, where 4 is considered hardworking today (pdf 73).

**Impact.** The 1943 paper in *Rural Sociology* became the "founding document" of the specialty. Earlier agricultural diffusion studies created no tradition because they set no paradigm. Ryan and Gross established the customary method, retrospective survey interviews. They popularized "diffusion" but did not use "innovation" (pdf 74). Crane (1972) found they launched fifteen of the eighteen most widely used intellectual innovations in the rural sociology diffusion tradition (pdf 76).

**Findings.**
- All but 2 of 259 adopted between 1928 and 1941. Cumulative adoption was S-shaped: 10% by 1933, 40% by 1936, then leveling off (pdf 74).
- Innovators had larger farms, higher incomes, more education, and more trips to Des Moines, so they were more cosmopolite (pdf 74).
- The decision period from first knowledge to adoption averaged about nine years, despite high relative advantage. Farmers typically tried hybrid on about one acre, then took three or four years before planting 100% (pdf 74–75).
- Channels: salesmen were typically first heard of. Neighbors were most cited for persuasion. Salesmen mattered more for earlier adopters, neighbors for later ones (pdf 75).
- Farmer-to-farmer exchange of experience was at the heart of diffusion. The rate took off once enough positive experience accumulated. The threshold was 1935, after which halting diffusion "would have been impossible" (pdf 75).

**Weakness.** Ryan and Gross should have asked sociometric questions ("From which other farmers did you obtain information?"), which a complete enumeration would have allowed. Instead data were collected "as if they were unrelated respondents in a random sample" (Katz et al., 1963) (pdf 75). Still, they sensed a social snowball: the behavior of one affects others, and success on a few farms stimulates the rest (pdf 75). Rogers concludes that diffusion is fundamentally a social process (pdf 75–76).

### Summary (pdf 76–79)
The summary restates the chapter's definitions: diffusion; communication; information as a difference that affects uncertainty among alternatives; the four elements; innovation, technology (hardware and software), five attributes, re-invention; channels (mass media better for knowledge, interpersonal for attitudes); heterophily and homophily; time; social system, structure, norms, opinion leadership, change agent, aide; four decision types; consequences (pdf 76–79). It adds no new argument; some definitions it restates (e.g. information, technology, the attributes of innovations) are not developed in these pages.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A village has 20 farm families and a new seed. Adopters per year: 1, 1, 2, 4, 5, 4, 2, 1 (total 20).
- Cumulative: 1, 2, 4, 8, 13, 17, 19, 20.
- Percent of 20: 5, 10, 20, 40, 65, 85, 95, 100.
- Few adopt at first, then 4–5 per year in the middle years, then few again: an S-shape.
- Rate of adoption, measured as time to reach at least 50%: 5 years (13/20 = 65%; year 4 was only 40%).

A second village that reaches 50% only in year 8 has the same innovation but a slower rate. Rogers would look to system effects (norms, structure) to explain the difference. An individual's innovativeness is just their adoption year (e.g. year 1 versus year 7) relative to the others.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] S-shaped cumulative adoption ↔ population growth or logistic growth: slow start, rapid middle, saturation at a ceiling.
- [structural-analogy] Opinion leaders as central, norm-conforming nodes ↔ network analysis: high-centrality nodes relaying influence.
- [metaphor] Structure as "veins and arteries" (Katz) ↔ epidemiology: spread depends on contact pathways.
- [structural-analogy] Hybrid corn's 1935 takeoff threshold ↔ critical mass (the text does not name the term).

## Questions this raises
- How can network (sociometric) data show *who* influenced whom, which Ryan and Gross lacked?
- If the system explains much of adoption, how can one separate individual from system effects statistically?
- Rogers says authority decisions are fastest but may be circumvented. When does the speed gain beat poor implementation?
- How can change agents anticipate an innovation's *meaning* if they seldom predict it?
