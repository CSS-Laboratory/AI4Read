# A History of Diffusion Research (part 1/3): European roots, paradigms, anthropology, early sociology, rural sociology, start of education

## L0 — One line
Diffusion research began in separate disciplinary "invisible colleges", took its basic paradigm from the Ryan and Gross hybrid corn study (rural sociology), and later merged into one tradition, with gains and costs.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Where did diffusion research come from, and what did its split-then-merged history do to the field? Rogers traces European forerunners (Tarde, Simmel, the diffusionist anthropologists). He then describes the first traditions: anthropology, early sociology, rural sociology and the start of education. Rural sociology supplied the paradigm, grew with agricultural funding, and declined once its main questions were answered. By the mid-1960s the traditions began citing one another and merged, yielding a "sameness" of approach. The chapter also asks how accepting the classical diffusion model limited the originality and appropriateness of diffusion research.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of adoption of an innovation (Chapter 1 hybrid corn study); survey versus fieldwork methods; no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Diffusion research began in separate "invisible colleges" that found similar results. Cross-tradition citations doubled between 1965 and 1968, and the traditions merged (pdf 80–81).
- Tarde already saw adoption as the key outcome, the S-curve, opinion leaders starting the takeoff, and compatibility, but he had no empirical tools (pdf 82–83).
- Simmel's "stranger" and the diffusionist anthropologists gave later work the ideas of social distance, networks, and the importance of diffusion (pdf 83–84).
- Rogers uses Kuhn's paradigm cycle to explain diffusion research. The Ryan and Gross study set the basic paradigm; the cycle is rise, consensus, then declining interest, but the final stage does not seem to have begun, except perhaps in rural sociology (pdf 86–87).
- Anthropology (participant observation, small systems) gives depth, a respondent's viewpoint and attention to culture. Its results are less certain to generalize (pdf 88–90). The Bali case shows how an indigenous knowledge system influenced the fate of an innovation (pdf 91–94).
- Early sociology (Bowers' ham radio study) added quantitative analysis but no paradigm (pdf 94–95).
- Rural sociology had funding, a dense network and a common framework. Rogers says interest declined because of the paradigm's very success: it became stale, not inadequate (food surpluses also shifted attention). Hightower claimed colleges of agriculture had almost totally ignored consequences (pdf 95–102).
- Education studies stressed school wealth and organizational decisions; adoption speeds varied from 5 to about 50 years (pdf 102–103).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: invisible colleges and merger (pdf 80–81)
- In the 1940s–50s diffusion research began in independent disciplinary "enclaves". Each studied one kind of innovation (rural sociologists: farm innovations; educators: teaching ideas). Yet each found similar things, e.g. the S-shaped curve over time and higher socioeconomic status of innovators (pdf 80).
- **Research tradition:** a series of studies on a similar topic, each influenced by earlier ones. Each is an **invisible college**: scholars linked by exchanging findings though spatially dispersed (pdf 80).
- **Cross-tradition citation index** (Rogers with Shoemaker 1971): for each empirical publication, the number of research traditions other than the author's cited in its footnotes and bibliography. The average stayed under 1.0 in the 1940s, 1950s and early 1960s. Between 1965 and 1968 it suddenly doubled (pdf 80–81).
- Rogers says all traditions have now merged into one large invisible college. This is "not an unmixed blessing": studies show "sameness", pursue few issues with stereotyped approaches, and show an "unnecessary standardization" of perspectives. The chapter asks how accepting the classical model limited originality and appropriateness (pdf 81).

### European beginnings (pdf 81–84)
**Tarde and imitation (pdf 82–83).**
- Tarde was a French lawyer and judge around 1900. His "laws of imitation" asked why, of 100 innovations conceived at once, ten spread and ninety are forgotten (pdf 82).
- Rogers credits him with several later themes. "Imitation" corresponds to today's "adoption". Adoption or rejection is the key outcome. Adoption rate usually follows an S-curve. The takeoff begins when opinion leaders use the idea, so network thinking is implicit. Diffusion is a social process of interpersonal communication (pdf 82).
- One law: the more similar an innovation is to already accepted ideas, the more likely adoption. Today: perceived compatibility is positively related to rapid adoption (pdf 82).
- Tarde saw invention and imitation as "the elementary social acts" (pdf 82). His insights were not followed empirically for about forty years, until Ryan and Gross, because tools for quantitative studies were lacking (pdf 82–83).

*Intuition note:* Tarde already asked one of the field's main questions: why do some ideas spread and most do not? He proposed an answer (social copying, with takeoff starting when opinion leaders adopt) before anyone could test it empirically.

**Simmel's stranger (pdf 83–84).**
- Simmel defined the stranger as a member of a system who is not strongly attached to it. Such a person has a unique view of the system (pdf 83).
- Later scholars derived social distance, heterophily, cosmopoliteness and the ideal of objectivity from this concept. Simmel also pointed toward communication networks: group affiliations act as "a system of coordinates" that fix a person's position, so networks influence and often constrain action (pdf 83–84).
- The innovator, as a type of stranger, can more easily deviate from norms by adopting first (pdf 84).

**British and German-Austrian diffusionists (pdf 84).**
- These anthropologists were not influenced by Tarde. Diffusionism held that social change results from innovations spread from one original source, and denied parallel invention. Rogers says parallel invention has frequently occurred (pdf 84).
- It has no following today because of its extreme claim that diffusion alone explains social change. The current view: change comes from invention and diffusion, which usually occur sequentially. Their main contribution was drawing attention to diffusion. American anthropologists studied diffusion from the 1920s and indirectly influenced Ryan and Gross (pdf 84).

### The rise of the research traditions (pdf 85)
- Nine main traditions, each a discipline or subdiscipline, usually studying one main type of innovation. Table 2-1 (data shown here only for four of the nine): anthropology 4% of diffusion publications (tribes or peasant villages; participant and non-participant observation and case studies); early sociology (no percentage; secondary data and statistics); rural sociology 20% (individual farmers; surveys); education 8% (school systems, teachers, or administrators; mailed questionnaires, survey interviews and statistical analysis) (pdf 85).

### Paradigms and invisible colleges (pdf 86–88)
- Kuhn: a field starts with a "revolutionary paradigm" giving model problems and solutions. Young scientists rush in, consensus forms, and after some years the invisible college loses interest as exciting findings dry up. Rogers cites Kuhn, Price, Crane. The paradigm reduces the researcher's uncertainty by structuring one general approach (pdf 86).
- Diffusion research followed these stages "rather closely" (Crane 1972). The final stage of demise does not seem to have begun, except perhaps in certain traditions like rural sociology (pdf 87).
- The Ryan and Gross (1943) hybrid corn study set out the basic paradigm, followed by many scholars in many disciplines. Publications rose sharply, as Kuhn's theory would predict (pdf 87).
- Figure 2-1 (rural sociology publications by year): 434 diffusion publications 1941–1981. Of 331 US and European publications, 36 (13 percent, as printed) were European and one Australian. 103 (24 percent) were from Latin America, Africa and Asia. Location is where data were gathered (pdf 88). The image shows the US/Europe line peaking near 35 around 1958 and the developing-nations line peaking near 21 around 1964 (approximate readings) (pdf 87).
- Diffusion research is a kind of communication research that began outside the field of communication. The hybrid corn study preceded the first communication departments (about 1960) (pdf 88).

### Anthropology tradition (pdf 88–94)
- Oldest and most distinctive in method. Most anthropologists avoid personal interviews, random sample surveys and computer analysis. They use **participant observation**: adopting respondents' perspective by sharing daily life, often living years in a village (pdf 88–89).
- Limits: small systems, one-person operations, so generalizability is uncertain. Example: can Peru's health service apply Wellin's (1955) study of the failed water-boiling campaign in Los Molinas to other villages? Rogers says we do not know the answer on the basis of anthropological research (pdf 89).
- Advantages: if the anthropologist succeeds in empathizing, the account tells the story from the respondents' viewpoint, which helps overcome the pro-innovation bias displayed in much other diffusion research. Gives a holistic view of culture and relationships, and so suits studying consequences (e.g. Pelto's 1973 study of the snowmobile among Skolt Lapps in Finland) (pdf 89).
- Anthropologists often show that planners failed to account for expected adopters' cultural values, so programs failed or had unanticipated consequences (pdf 89–90).
- Compared with other traditions, anthropology focuses more on transfer of technology between societies (Wissler 1914, 1923: horses from Spanish explorers to American Indian tribes; corn growing from Indians to settlers). Anthropologists note the importance of indigenous knowledge systems. Other traditions seldom used participant observation but carried forward some of anthropology's theoretical leads, such as perceived compatibility (pdf 90).

**Boxed case: Miracle rice in Bali (pdf 91–94).**
- Bali's terraced rice is irrigated through a hierarchy of Hindu priests and water temples. At the top is the high priest, the Jero Gde. At the local level are 1,300 subaks, each a water users' cooperative of about a hundred farmers (pdf 91–92).
- The system does more than deliver water. Pests migrate between fields, so a single farmer cannot control them without coordinating with neighbors; the solution is for hundreds of farmers in several neighboring subaks to plant, irrigate and harvest simultaneously and then fallow. The Jero Gde balances pest control (synchronized planting) against water supply (staggered planting), depending on rainfall and pest levels. Rice experts, if they knew of the system, dismissed it as unimportant; Lansing said modern irrigation experts thought it "mere religious nonsense". Until Lansing investigated, no one understood how the farmers' decisions were coordinated (pdf 92).
- In the 1970s officials introduced Green Revolution rice (three crops a year, pesticides, fertilizer). Many farmers abandoned the water-and-fallow system. Disease, insect and rat populations rose, and yields "dropped precipitously". Farmers returned to the temple system and dropped the miracle varieties (pdf 93).
- In the late 1980s Lansing and an ecological biologist built a computer simulation (rainfall, planting schedules, pests per subak), "The Goddess and the Computer". The Jero Gde concluded that the highest yields resembled the farmers' eight-hundred-year-old strategies. Asked what he thought of the analysis, he smiled mysteriously and replied with a question: surely Lansing did not think he came to work on this by coincidence? (pdf 93–94).

*Intuition note:* An innovation that is better elsewhere can fail when it breaks a local system that quietly does a needed job. Lansing's case shows that studying a local system closely over time can reveal such hidden functions.

### Early sociology (pdf 94–95)
- Traces to Tarde; most of the ten studies appeared from the late 1920s to the early 1940s. Important for influence, not volume or method sophistication. Typically traced one innovation over a region to understand social change (pdf 94).
- Bowers (1937, 1938), on ham radio, was probably the first in this tradition to use primary respondent data (mailed questionnaires to 312 operators). He was first to find that interpersonal channels are more important than mass media for later adopters than for earlier adopters. Operators rose from about 3,000 (1914) to 64,000 (1935), following an S-curve. He related city size and region to adoption rate (pdf 94–95).
- Early sociology used quantitative analysis, as most later traditions did, but lacked the paradigm that would set off widespread research (pdf 95).

### Rural sociology (pdf 95–102)
- Formed the basic paradigm and produced the most studies. Up to 1964, 423 of 950 diffusion publications (45 percent); now 20 percent per Table 2-1. Korsching: diffusion research "has declined in rural sociology, but it has not vanished" (pdf 95).
- Its home was land-grant colleges of agriculture (teaching, research, extension) that valued agricultural production, so diffusion research seemed useful. Extension workers used the diffusion model as their main theory. Interest faded when surpluses and the farm crisis arrived, and when agribusiness companies used the model in ways rural sociologists disliked ("misused", pdf 96).
- **Hybrid corn study (pdf 96–97).** Ryan's study was funded in 1941 by the Iowa Agricultural Experiment Station. Officials were puzzled that such an advantageous innovation spread slowly. Rogers adds that uncertainty and risk were one reason Iowa farmers were deliberate in adopting. The study set three lasting questions: what variables relate to innovativeness; what is the adoption rate and what explains its speed (e.g. perceived attributes); what role channels play at each stage of the innovation-decision process. Almost all other traditions copied the template "whether they knew it or not" (pdf 97).
- **Invisible college (pdf 97–98).** In the 1950s, Ph.D.s trained at Madison, Columbia and Ames spread diffusion programs (Rogers included). Crane's sociometric questionnaire covered 221 scholars. Two large cliques (27 and 32 scholars; a **clique** = subsystem interacting more within itself), each around a leading scholar. The four largest cliques included all eight "high producers" (10+ publications), who communicated closely. But 101 of 221 were isolates or in unconnected cliques, few of them productive (pdf 98).
- The network gave consensus and cumulative progress but implicitly discouraged radical deviation. Re-invention was ignored since it was not yet a concept (pdf 98).
- Funding and context: experiment stations and USDA produced many farm innovations. People fed by the average US farmer rose from 14 (1950) to 26 (1960) to 47 (1970). Rogers says these scholars were in the right place (colleges of agriculture) at the right time (pdf 98–99).
- **Developing nations (pdf 99–100).** In the 1960s US land-grant models were exported with USAID and foundation funding. Early studies (Rahim 1961, Bangladesh; Deutschmann and Fals-Borda 1962, Colombia) suggested that new ideas spread among peasants in a pattern generally similar to media-saturated settings; Rogers states that the diffusion process and its concepts and models were cross-culturally valid. Its applicability was later questioned (Chapter 3). Growth reflected the assumption that technology was the heart of development. About 30 percent of all diffusion studies are in Third World nations (pdf 99–100).
- **Decline (pdf 100–101).** US publications fell after 1958, developing-nation ones after 1967. Rogers' explanation: the paradigm succeeded in answering its main questions. Crane: much innovative work was done before the field gained many new members (pdf 100). From 1941 to 1966 "research innovations" averaged about 40 per five years while publications rose from 6 to 187. So the ratio of innovations to publications fell almost to zero in the last two intervals: the literature consisted increasingly of replications. Unlike Kuhn's general case, the paradigm was not shown inadequate nor replaced (pdf 101).
- Food surpluses led policy makers to doubt the value of raising production. After 1975 some turned to conservation and ecology innovations, gender (hedgerow farming in Africa), and failures of agricultural innovations (pdf 101).
- **Hightower (1972)** (his view, as reported): colleges of agriculture drove the agricultural revolution but almost totally ignored consequences. Rogers illustrates with the tomato harvester (his narration; details in Chapter 4): mechanized harvesting needed hard tomatoes. Consumers got cheaper tomatoes, but many disliked them, they had fewer vitamins, and thousands of farm laborers and small farmers lost work (pdf 101–102). Some rural sociologists now ask whether colleges serve farmers if production gains drive families out (pdf 102).

*Intuition note:* A research field can decline because it succeeded, not because it was disproved. When the main questions are answered, new studies mostly repeat old ones, and talent leaves.

### Education (pdf 102–103; the text breaks off)
- Many studies but less theoretical contribution. Teachers work in organizations; most teachers and administrators are involved in collective or authority decisions, whereas US farmers and consumers mainly make optional decisions. Share of all diffusion publications: 23 studies in 1961 (5%), 71 in 1968 (6%), 336 in 1981 (11%), 359 in 1994 (9%); new studies have since "slowed to a trickle" (pdf 102–103).
- **Teachers College studies:** the early educational diffusion studies were almost all done at Columbia's Teachers College under Paul Mort. They aimed to show local control related to innovativeness, which Mort valued. Unit of analysis: school system; mailed questionnaires to superintendents or principals. The best single predictor of innovativeness was spending per student; wealth "appeared to be a necessary prerequisite". Mort: the average school lags 25 years behind best practice (pdf 103).
- Adoption times vary: kindergartens about fifty years (1900–1950); driver training eighteen years (1935–1953); modern math five years (1958–1963). The last two were heavily promoted by change agencies (insurance companies and auto manufacturers for driver training); the text breaks off at "National Science" (pdf 103).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Rogers says rural sociology's research front "grew stale". Imagine a field where new study variables (research innovations) appear at a fixed rate of 40 per five years.
- Period A: 10 publications. Ratio = 40 ÷ 10 = 4.0 innovations per publication.
- Period B: 200 publications, still 40 innovations. Ratio = 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2.
Publications grew 20-fold, while novelty per paper fell from 4.0 to 0.2, a factor of 20. Most papers in B are replications.

Citation index: paper 1 cites two education and one marketing study, so index = 2 traditions. Paper 2 cites none from outside, index = 0. Paper 3 cites one anthropology study, index = 1. Average = (2 + 0 + 1) ÷ 3 = 1.0.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Invisible college, cliques and isolates (Crane's network) ↔ network science: clustered subgroups linked by hubs.
- [structural-analogy] Rise and decline of publications on diffusion ↔ the diffusion S-curve: the field's own growth follows a spread-then-saturation shape.
- [metaphor] Simmel's stranger as innovator ↔ weak ties in social networks: a loosely attached person brings or tries new things.
