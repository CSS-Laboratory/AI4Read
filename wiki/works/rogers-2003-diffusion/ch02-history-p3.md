# A History of Diffusion Research, part 3: news diffusion, communication, marketing, geography, general sociology, and a typology

## L0 — One line
Diffusion research now spans many disciplines, Rogers identifies eight main types of diffusion study, and he ends by challenging future scholars to widen the area of digging.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers finishes his history of diffusion research. He asks what the communication, marketing, geography and general-sociology traditions each contributed, and what kinds of questions diffusion studies ask. A news-diffusion case (September 11), a social-marketing discussion, an electric-car campaign and a Freedom Summer study show the traditions at work. Marketing can run experiments but is biased toward sponsors. Geography stresses space. Sociology stresses person-to-person ties. Rogers then lists eight main types of diffusion research, with one or two studies for each. He warns that a paradigm also builds in assumptions that are hard to see, and challenges future scholars to look elsewhere.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an innovation and its adoption; correlation versus causation; opinion leaders; mass media versus interpersonal channels.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- News of September 11 spread fast. Awareness reached 99% by noon, and person-to-person telling played a major role, especially for those who heard later (pdf 127–128).
- News diffusion research flourished after 1960 and then levelled off. Rogers says a research front's decline is not inevitable (pdf 129).
- The communication tradition can study any type of innovation, so it can concentrate on the process of diffusion (pdf 130).
- Marketing predicts adoption rates (the Bass model) and can run experiments. Its sponsor bias risks trivial theory (pdf 131, 138–139).
- Social marketing applies commercial techniques to nonprofit goals through five essentials (pdf 133–135).
- Geography shows space matters, using Hägerstrand's simulation with a "neighborhood effect" (pdf 139–140).
- Joining Freedom Summer was best predicted by strong ties to participants. The idea spread as an ideology through social ties (pdf 142–143).
- Rogers identifies eight main types of diffusion research. Innovativeness dominates, with almost two thirds of generalizations (pdf 144).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The part starts inside the chapter, at the September 11 case, and ends with the chapter summary.

### Case: diffusion of news of the September 11 attacks (pdf 127–129)
- A survey of 127 respondents in Albuquerque found the news affected people emotionally more than past news events. Rogers says the terrorists seemed "quite successful" at creating terror, and patriotism also rose (pdf 127).
- The event was highly salient and dominated media coverage. Later events kept the story going (pdf 127).
- First attacks: 6:46 A.M. Albuquerque time. The share of respondents who knew rose from 7% (6–7) to 34% (7–8), 70% (8–9) and 92% (9–10). By noon 99% knew, which is 120 of 121 answering (pdf 127–128).
- Rogers attributes the speed to high salience and the complete dominance of broadcasting (pdf 128).
- After a slow start the curve took off around 7:00. People felt they had to share the news and their feelings (pdf 128).
- 88 of 127 (69%) told someone, reaching 418 people, an average of 4.8 each. 80% of the tellers told one to four people. Two told more than fifty (pdf 128).
- Mean first hearing by mass media: 8:38 A.M. By interpersonal channels: 10:03, about 1½ hours later. Media created initial awareness, and those people told others (pdf 128).
- 59% said they were personally affected. 86% did something: two thirds prayed, 21% attended memorials, 16% gave money, 8% gave blood. More than one third displayed a flag (pdf 128–129).

**Contributions of news diffusion research (pdf 129).**
- Publications rose after Deutschmann and Danielson (1960), then levelled off to about one study a year from the 1970s.
- DeFleur (1987) wrote that the tradition "has all but run out." Rogers says interest declined as most intriguing questions were answered and the field became stuck in a rut, "perhaps" by following the 1960 paradigm too closely.
- He says the rise-and-fall pattern is not inevitable. A field can reorient.
- Possible contribution: the studies drew communication scholars to the diffusion paradigm.
- They also illuminated how mass media messages stimulate interpersonal talk. Rogers offers possible reasons for passing news on: unresolved puzzles (Princess Diana's death; who was responsible for September 11), and boasting or status-seeking. Future studies need to explore these.

*Intuition note:* In the September 11 survey, media gave first awareness on average earlier than friends did. People who heard from the media then told others, and interpersonal channels played a major role, especially for those who heard later.

### Later communication research (pdf 130)
- From the early 1960s, communication scholars studied technological ideas (agricultural, health, education, family planning) in developing nations. Deutschmann's Colombian village study was a landmark.
- In the 1970s they turned to U.S. innovations, sometimes with communities or organizations as adopters.
- Since about 1990, studies have "exploded," many on the Internet and new communication technologies.
- Advantage: there is no content limit, unlike education, rural sociology or public health. This frees researchers to focus on the diffusion process. The tradition contributed concepts and methods: credibility, parasocial interaction, network analysis and the semantic differential.

### Marketing (pdf 130–139)
- About 16% of all diffusion publications (Table 2-1, not visible here) (pdf 131).
- The Bass model (Bass, 1969) estimates the rate of adoption of a new product. It began with consumer durables and has since been applied widely. Dekimpe is quoted as calling it the most popular model in marketing. Tests often add variables such as price (pdf 131).
- Many new products fail. Rogers says only one in hundreds survives. Much marketing diffusion research sits in confidential company files, restricting scholarly access (pdf 131).
- The tradition emphasizes the rate of adoption, perceived attributes, and the marketing mix of advertising and personal selling. It also studies amount of use, such as minutes of phone use (pdf 131–132).
- It leads in global diffusion research. Example questions: mobile service began in Japan in 1979, ten years before many nations. Why was adoption much faster in Finland and Scandinavia than in Japan? (pdf 132).
- Rogers defends marketing against its negative image. He argues that long-term success requires matching consumer needs with products (pdf 132).

**Social marketing (pdf 132–135).**
- Coercion (helmet laws, smoking bans) is one way to change behavior. Social marketing, defined as "the application of commercial marketing strategies to the diffusion of nonprofit products and services," is another (pdf 132–133).
- It began with Wiebe's 1952 question about selling brotherhood like soap. It has been applied to energy conservation, smoking, safer driving, HIV/AIDS, family planning and more. It mainly aims to change behavior that is held back by inertia (pdf 133).
- Fox and Kotler (1980): most social marketing problems will be more formidable than commercial ones (pdf 133).
- Nirodh condom campaign, India, 1970s. The product was renamed from "French letters," a taboo innovation (a new idea seen as extremely private). It had mass advertising, sales through tea shops and cigarette stands, and a subsidy so each cost two cents. Market research gave feedback at every step (pdf 133).
- Five essentials (pdf 134–135):
  1. Audience segmentation: special messages to subaudiences (e.g., smokers over 45 who are overweight; young married couples; young male motorcyclists for organ donation).
  2. Formative research: pretesting messages early. The Healthy Highways Project for truck drivers failed until such research showed how to think like a lower-class truck driver.
  3. Positioning the innovation through name and logo (Nirodh, Panther, Preethi, Catapult; Sri Lanka's thumb-and-finger logo).
  4. Very low price and convenient distribution (free condoms from Bangkok police, "Cops and Rubbers").
  5. Use channels the planners control, such as paid advertising, rather than public service announcements.
- ORT in Egypt appears in Chapter 9 as a very successful case (pdf 135).

**Case: opinion leaders and mavens, electric cars (pdf 136–138).**
- California and Arizona mandated that at least 10% of sales be nonpolluting. GM committed $2 billion (pdf 136).
- The IMPACT prototype had a 100-mile range and needed three to four hours of charging at 220 volts. 150 prototypes were rolled out in eighteen cities. In Sacramento 7,000 people applied. A questionnaire measured opinion leadership and innovativeness, and several hundred people per city got test drives (pdf 136).
- These were "mavens," people with high interest and expertise in a product, often sought as opinion leaders. After the drive each got a 30-minute Q&A with an engineer, a photograph, and fifty cards with an ID number and toll-free number to hand to friends. Word of mouth created "buzz" (pdf 136–137).
- Lessons: "IMPACT" was a bad name because mavens worried about crashes. It was renamed EV-1. The range limited use until 220-volt outlets spread (pdf 137).
- EV-1 went on sale in late fall 1997. Sales were modest, and within a year the two states postponed their mandates. Interest faded until hybrids appeared in 2000 (pdf 138).
- GM paid a high cost but learned how to stimulate interpersonal communication. It applied some lessons to GPS (pdf 138).

**Advantages and disadvantages (pdf 138–139).**
- Advantage: sponsors let marketing scholars run experimental interventions. Other diffusion scholars seldom control how an innovation is introduced (examples: the Taichung experiment, the Tanzania Project) (pdf 138).
- Disadvantage: source bias. Marketers' needs come before consumers' needs, and the research may be methodologically sophisticated but theoretically trivial. We may know more about deodorant scents and beer taste than about diffusion theory (pdf 138–139).

*Intuition note:* Marketing scholars can test ideas in the field because firms fund them. The same close tie to sellers may steer the questions toward what sellers want to know.

### Geography (pdf 139–140)
- About 4% of publications. In 1961 there were only three, all by Hägerstrand. In 1995 there were 160 studies. Rogers says little further research has been done since (pdf 139).
- Space is the crucial variable. Hägerstrand built a mathematical model with a "neighborhood effect": adoption is more likely to pass to a nearby adopter in the next unit of time. This was built in as adoption probabilities that decrease with distance from the previous adopter (pdf 139).
- He entered a map of the Swedish countryside, started from the first adopter of a farm innovation, simulated the spread, and compared the result with actual rate and geographic spread (pdf 139).
- If the simulation does not match reality, the model is adjusted. The outcome is a model of abstracted rules. Space matters for adoption. Sociology, marketing and political science also study spatial factors (pdf 139–140).

### General sociology (pdf 140–143)
- A residual category: sociologists' studies not in early, rural or public-health sociology. About 9% in 2002. Topics: campus shantytowns, hate-crime laws, airplane hijackings, ordination of women, Freedom Summer recruitment. The emphasis is on social relationships in person-to-person spread (pdf 140).

**Case: networks in recruitment to Freedom Summer (pdf 141–143).**
- Joining a protest amounts to adopting an innovation, here an ideology (pdf 141).
- McAdam (1986) obtained applications of 720 volunteers and 239 selected students who withdrew, and interviewed 80 of them. Each applicant listed ten people to keep informed. This allowed measurement of direct and indirect (weak) ties to participants and withdrawals (pdf 142).
- Weak ties, prior activism, race, distance, major and other personal traits did not explain going or withdrawing. Being male, older and active in campus organizations were somewhat related. The best predictor by far was strong ties to participants or an activist. A close friend who withdrew pushed others to withdraw. Some withdrawals resulted from opposition by parents or other adults (pdf 142).
- Rogers concludes that interpersonal links explained participation more than any other single factor. Perhaps because an ideology has no hardware, its social construction through interpersonal communication is especially crucial. Veterans founded later movements: anti–Vietnam War, feminist, environmental (pdf 142–143).

*Intuition note:* Who joined the risky project was best predicted by who the person was close to. Indirect (weak) ties did not explain it.

### Trends in research traditions (pdf 143)
- Publications rose after Ryan and Gross (1943) in each decade, with slower growth since 1980. More disciplines and broader innovations are involved. Rural sociology tailed off, geography waned after the mid-1990s, and communication, marketing and public health stay strong. World events partly determine which fields study diffusion (pdf 143).

### A typology of diffusion research (pdf 144–147)
Eight main types (Table 2-2 is not shown in these pages, only its title). Innovativeness is the most popular, with almost two thirds of all empirical generalizations (pdf 144).
1. **Earliness of knowing.** Mayer et al. (1990), Challenger, Phoenix phone interviews. Half heard within thirty minutes. Early knowers mostly heard by radio or television, late knowers by interpersonal channels. Mass-medium hearers then told others, and interpersonal hearers turned to media to confirm (pdf 144).
2. **Rate of adoption of different innovations.** Fliegel and Kivlin (1966b): 229 dairy farmers, 15 attributes of 33 innovations. Relative advantage (profitable, low risk) and compatibility with values predicted faster adoption. Complexity, observability and trialability were less highly related (pdf 144–145).
3. **Innovativeness.** Deutschmann and Fals Borda (1962b) found Colombian innovators resembled Ohio innovators: more cosmopolite, better educated, larger farms. Mohr (1969): 120 health departments. The most innovative had more money, a committed director and larger size (pdf 145).
4. **Opinion leadership.** Kelly et al. enlisted bartenders to identify opinion leaders in gay bars and trained them in safer-sex messages. HIV infection fell in intervention cities versus controls. Further tests are underway in several nations (pdf 145–146).
5. **Diffusion networks.** Coleman et al. (1966): similarity (age, religion, hometown, medical school) shaped who talked to whom. Professional affiliation was most important, and doctors working together discussed the new drug more (pdf 146).
6. **Rate of adoption in different social systems.** Rogers and Kincaid (1981): why the same innovation spreads faster in some systems. Fastest of 24 Korean villages had higher mass media exposure, better-connected leaders and more change-agent contact. Economic resources mattered less (pdf 146).
7. **Communication channel usage.** Ryan and Gross (1943): farmers first heard of hybrid seed from salesmen, but neighbors persuaded. They first suggested stages (knowledge, persuasion). Salesmen mattered more for earlier adopters, neighbors for later ones (pdf 146–147).
8. **Consequences.** Sharp (1952): steel axes given to the Yir Yoront by missionaries disrupted age and sex roles. Consequences were unanticipated, far-reaching and disruptive (pdf 147).

### Summary (pdf 149)
- Diffusion research began as enclaves but has become one integrated body of concepts, as Rogers says. A research tradition is a series of studies in which later ones are influenced by earlier ones. Nine traditions were described.
- A paradigm enables coherent research but standardizes assumptions and biases that are hard to recognize. Rogers quotes his 1962 call to expand the area of digging and dig deeper where theory suggests.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A neighborhood effect on a line of five farms, positions 1–5. Farm 3 has adopted. Say each non-adopter adopts in the next period with probability $0.4/d$, where $d$ is the distance to farm 3.
- Farms 2 and 4: $d=1$, so $p=0.4$ each.
- Farms 1 and 5: $d=2$, so $p=0.2$ each.
- Expected new adopters: $0.4+0.4+0.2+0.2=1.2$. Of these, $0.8$ come from the neighbors and $0.4$ from the far farms.
Closer farms are twice as likely to adopt. Hägerstrand's probabilities differ. This only shows the idea of probabilities falling with distance.
