# Contributions and Criticisms of Diffusion Research

## L0 — One line
Diffusion research is valuable and well established, but four inherited weaknesses (pro-innovation bias, individual-blame bias, the recall problem, neglect of equality) limit what it can explain, and each has proposed remedies.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This chapter asks what assumptions and biases limit diffusion research, the study of how new ideas spread through social systems. Rogers first lists its strengths: a shared paradigm for many disciplines, and practical use. He then names four weaknesses: pro-innovation bias, individual-blame bias, reliance on respondents' recall of adoption time, and neglect of equality (innovation often widens socioeconomic gaps). He links these to the field's origins in sponsored, survey-based studies of a very profitable innovation. He proposes remedies: in-process data, field experiments, network analysis, and studying rejection and system-level causes. Recognizing that a bias may exist is, the author says, among the first and most important steps in guarding against it.

**Prerequisites:** Basic diffusion vocabulary (innovation, adopter, change agent, S-shaped adoption curve, opinion leader); survey vs. experiment; correlation vs. causation.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Diffusion research is influential across many fields. Its paradigm gives scattered studies a common frame and a clear, easy method. That same ease may have limited theory (pdf 152–154).
- Every paradigm has blind spots ("trained incapacity"). Criticism helps a field become aware of them; almost none appeared before the 1970s (pdf 155).
- Pro-innovation bias is the implicit view that an innovation should spread to everyone, fast, unchanged and unrejected. It comes from funding, retrospective methods and choice of successful innovations. Rejection can be rational from the adopter's view (pdf 156, 160–163, 167).
- Individual-blame bias puts responsibility on individual adopters rather than on the system, source or channel. It leads to misdefined problems and self-fulfilling stereotypes of late adopters (pdf 170–173).
- Cause and blame are different things. Researchers should not treat a sponsor's blame definition as a scientific cause (pdf 174).
- One-shot surveys depend on recall and cannot settle time order or causality. Field experiments, multiple data points, point-of-adoption data and archives help (pdf 178–182).
- Diffusion often widens socioeconomic gaps, especially in developing nations. Two field experiments suggest this is not inevitable (pdf 182–187).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening and "The Status of Diffusion Research Today" (pdf 151–154)
- **Aim.** The chapter reviews the main critiques and points to possible improvements. The critiques began in the 1970s and should be taken seriously. Diffusion research is still highly regarded for explaining behaviour change and for improving social-change programs (pdf 151).
- **Reach.** Diffusion findings appear in textbooks of many fields (social psychology, marketing, public health, rural sociology and others). Government agencies, foundations and companies use and fund diffusion work. U.S. federal R&D labs are required by law to transfer technologies to private firms (pdf 151–152).
- **History of status.** Fliegel and Kivlin (1966b), two members of the field, called diffusion a "bastard child" of the parent interests in social change. Status has since risen. Downs and Mohr (1976) called innovation "possibly the most fashionable of social science areas" (pdf 152).
- **Reasons for the appeal (the author's list):**
  1. *Multidisciplinary paradigm.* It gives common ground to economists (growth), organization scholars, social psychologists, sociologists and anthropologists, political scientists and communication scholars (pdf 152–153).
     - Innovations are a type of message whose effects are relatively easy to isolate. The author compares this to radioactive tracers in plant growth (pdf 153).
     - Including time as an essential element gives conceptual and analytical strength (pdf 153).
  2. *Pragmatic appeal.* It promises ways to get research results used (pdf 154).
  3. *Generalization.* The paradigm lets scholars turn findings into theory. Without it the body of studies might be "a mile wide and an inch deep". Studies before Ryan and Gross (1943) did not add up for lack of a paradigm (pdf 154).
  4. *Easy method.* Data are not hard to gather and analysis is well laid out (mainly cross-sectional surveys on innovativeness). This may also have restricted theoretical advance (pdf 154).

*Intuition note.* Diffusion research is popular because it offers one simple frame for many fields, and the frame is easy to apply. The author warns that the ease may also have kept the theory narrow.

### Criticisms of Diffusion Research: why criticism matters (pdf 155)
- The 1940s formed the paradigm, the 1950s saw proliferation in the U.S., the 1960s expansion to developing nations, and the 1970s the start of introspective criticism. Until then almost nothing critical was written. The author says this absence may have been the field's greatest weakness (pdf 155).
- Every field simplifies reality, and these assumptions often go unrecognized. **Kaplan (1964):** training creates a "trained incapacity": the more we know about doing something, the harder it is to do it differently. The author adds that this is to some extent necessary and that every field has blind spots. Self-assessment through criticism helps a field progress (pdf 155).

### The Pro-Innovation Bias (pdf 155–167)
- **Definition.** Pro-innovation bias is the implication that an innovation should be diffused and adopted by all members of a system, diffused more rapidly, and neither re-invented nor rejected. It is seldom stated openly; it is assumed and implied (pdf 156).
- **Consequences.** Researchers ignore the study of ignorance about innovations. They underemphasize rejection and discontinuance, overlook re-invention (though much has recently been done), and fail to study antidiffusion programs against "bad" innovations such as crack cocaine or cigarettes. So what we know about diffusion is unnecessarily limited (pdf 156).

**Case: pure drinking water in Egyptian villages (Belasco, 1989) (pdf 157–159).**
- Egyptian delta villagers use stagnant canals for washing, sanitation and drinking. The canals host schistosomiasis snails and diarrhea-causing bacteria. The health ministry, funded by USAID, built a piped chlorinated water system. More than half of served villagers still preferred canal water. Almost no one drank only piped water, though most knew from a media campaign that canal water was contaminated (pdf 157–158).
- Belasco used both surveys of female water gatherers in three villages and ethnographic observation. The author calls this unique among diffusion studies. He found the piped water was less appropriate than experts claimed (pdf 158).
- **Reasons found:**
  - Politicians' promises overextended the system. Broken shut-off valves wasted water and lowered pressure. The innovation lacked compatibility with users' needs (pdf 158).
  - Chlorinated water tasted "chemical". Some believed it weakened sex drive. A rumor said the government added chemicals for family-planning purposes (pdf 158).
  - Villagers stored canal water in a *zir* (earthen vase) where sediment settles. They perceived this as purification, although bacteria and parasites remained (pdf 158–159).
  - Social reasons: women met at the canal to exchange news. Spigot queues were unpleasant, with pushing and fights. Supply was shut off for hours or days in summer, forcing even those who preferred piped water onto the canal. Some women poured piped water into the zir (pdf 159).
  - Villagers washed for prayer with tap water but drank canal water. Religious leaders (opinion leaders) could have promoted pure water, but change agents did not use them (pdf 159).
- **Lesson.** Villagers are not as irrational as they appear. Perceptions of an innovation count, and using the users' perceptions rather than the technologists' is essential to overcoming the bias (pdf 159).

*Intuition note.* From the engineers' side the piped water was obviously better. From the women's side it was bad-tasting, unreliable and socially worse. To understand adoption, you must ask how users see the innovation.

**Reasons for the bias (pdf 160–162).**
- *Historical.* Hybrid corn was very profitable for the Iowa farmers of Ryan and Gross (1943). Most other innovations have much less relative advantage, and many should not be adopted by many individuals. A field that began with a less profitable innovation might have avoided the bias (pdf 160).
- **Downs and Mohr (1976):** the act of innovating still carries strongly positive value and is still associated with improvement. The author adds that innovation is a good word like "motherhood" (pdf 160).
- *Funding.* Change agencies fund much research, and their purpose is to promote innovations. Their view is often accepted by the researchers they sponsor, consult and hire from (pdf 160).
- *Retrospective method.* Successful diffusion leaves a visible adoption rate that can be investigated afterwards. Failed efforts leave few traces, and re-invention is hard to classify as "adoption" (pdf 160–161).
- *Choice of innovation.* This is implicit and largely unintentional (pdf 161). Sponsors bring innovations they want spread (examples: cell phones, "five-a-day" nutrition, the Internet, no-smoking ordinances). Researchers also choose innovations with rapid adoption because they look dynamic and policy-relevant (pdf 161).
- **Result.** We know more about rapid than slow diffusion, adoption than rejection, and continued use than discontinuance. We know too much about successes and too little about failures, which might be intellectually more valuable. The author calls for some studies with an "anti-innovation bias" to balance this (pdf 161–162).

**Overcoming it (pdf 162–167).**
1. *In-process research.* Do not only study diffusion after it is complete (Figure 3-1: one data point, late on the S-curve). Gather data at two or more points during diffusion (Figure 3-2) (pdf 162, 165–166). Designs include field experiments before and after an intervention (Taiwan family planning; Tanzania Project) and opinion-leader experiments (pdf 162).
2. *Choose innovations carefully.* A scholar might also study an unsuccessful innovation that failed in the same system and period as a successful one. Study a wider range of innovations (pdf 163).
3. *Accept that rejection, discontinuance and re-invention may be rational from the individual's viewpoint.* Adopters may know local conditions that a change agent does not. Example: Mexican change agents told campesinos to stop interplanting corn and beans. Later research showed interplanting gave higher yields, so the villagers knew more than the Ph.D. agronomists (pdf 163).
   - Re-invention is how an innovation is changed to fit the adopter. Early studies treated an innovation as invariant. Scholars no longer assume it is "perfect" for all (pdf 163).
4. *Study the broader context* (Figure 3-3): the policy decision to diffuse, the relation to other innovations and replaced practices, and the decision to do the R&D (pdf 163–164, 166).
5. *Study motivations for adoption and for rejection.* Such "why" questions are hard to answer by survey, but we should not give up (pdf 164).
   - Economic motivation is often assumed. Prestige may also matter. Becker (1970a, 1970b) found prestige motives important for health departments. **Mohr (1969):** much innovation in health organizations is "slack" innovation, driven more by prestige than by effectiveness or profit.
   - The author guesses that profit may matter more in private organizations, and that prestige matters for fashions and similar goods. The author says we do not really know, because so few researchers have assessed motives (pdf 164–165).
6. *View the innovation through respondents' eyes.* **Eveland (1979):** there is nothing inherently wrong with a pro-innovation value system, and spreading good ideas can be a public duty. The author adds that even then adopters may see things differently. Adoption or rejection is always "right" in the eyes of the decider, at least at the time. Past researchers over-relied on too-rationalistic models, and the first step is to recognize that the bias may exist (pdf 167).

**Case: preference for sons in India and China (pdf 168–169).**
- The author says pro-innovation bias is at least partly justified, since most diffusion benefits most adopters. Yet a generally beneficial innovation can be disastrous for some adopters and, in a few cases, for society (pdf 168).
- Ultrasound sex determination spread to rural India and China, where sons are strongly preferred. Reasons given: sons carry the family name and care for parents, there is no state pension, and daughters require dowries (pdf 168).
  - In India the technology appeared in the 1980s and was outlawed in 1984, but the law was not enforced. In Punjab the boy-to-girl birth ratio rose from 114:100 (1991) to 126:100 (2001). The ratio in the U.S. and most of the world stays at 105–107:100 (pdf 168).
  - China (one-child policy since 1979): ratio 108 (1982), 113 (1990), 117 (2000), and 130 in Hubei. It is estimated that by 2020, 30–40 million young men will be unable to marry (pdf 169).
- **Luthra (1984)** concludes that harmful technologies should be studied as well as beneficial ones (pdf 169).

### The Individual-Blame Bias (pdf 169–178)
- **Source bias** is a tendency for diffusion research to side with the change agencies rather than potential adopters. The author speculates the field might have been called "problem solving" or "evaluation of innovations" if audiences had shaped it. He asks what would differ if the hybrid corn study had been sponsored by a farmers' organization rather than the Iowa Agricultural Experiment Station (pdf 169).
- **Individual-blame** is the tendency to hold an individual responsible for his or her problems rather than the system. **System-blame** is the reverse. The author's image: "If the shoe doesn't fit, there's something wrong with your foot" versus blaming the shoe maker (pdf 170).
- Some causes are individual, but many lie in the system. Social policies limited to individual interventions cannot be very effective in solving system-level problems. A frequent error is to overstress individual-blame (pdf 170).
- **Cases of initially individual-blame definitions (pdf 170–171):**
  - Lead paint posters blamed low-income parents, not manufacturers or landlords. Mid-1990s federal law required disclosure of lead-free status for rentals and sales.
  - Highway safety campaigns (urging drivers not to drink and drive, to buckle up and to slow down) targeted drivers while accident rates rose. Nader's (1965) book helped redefine the problem as unsafe cars and highways. Legislation for safer cars and highways followed, and the traffic fatality rate decreased. Individual factors still mattered (MADD helped secure tougher penalties).
  - A Chicago job-training program stressed punctuality, but only a quarter of trainees had a clock or watch. It also ignored unreliable transport, and refused to buy alarm clocks (Caplan and Nelson, 1974).
  - In each case the program did not succeed until system-blame factors were also recognized (pdf 171).
- **In diffusion models.** **Havens (1975):** variables used to predict innovativeness indicate the individual's success or failure within the system, not the system's. Examples: education, size of operation, income, cosmopoliteness, mass media exposure. Some system-blame variables were included (change agent contact, credit), but the source or channel is seldom implied to be at fault (pdf 171–172).
- **Late adopters.** They are labeled "traditional" or "irrational". A closer look might show the innovation suits them less (smaller operations, fewer resources), so non-adoption could be rational. The stereotype can become a *self-fulfilling prophecy*: change agents skip contact, so adoption becomes even less likely (pdf 172–173). Individual-blame is often in everybody's interest except those blamed (pdf 173).
- **Recycling study (Derksen and Gartell, 1993; Edmonton vs. Calgary).** Individual environmental attitudes related to recycling only for people with curbside collection. Edmonton had a program, Calgary did not, and Edmonton had much more recycling. The unit of analysis should have shifted to the city, with individual adoption contingent on the community decision (pdf 173).
- **Why diffusion research shows the bias (pdf 173–178):**
  1. *Sponsors' problem definitions.* Researchers may accept an individual-blame definition from a change agency (**Caplan and Nelson, 1973:** such research often helps blame people for their predicament) (pdf 173–174).
  2. *Cause sometimes confused with blame.* Cause should be established empirically. Blame depends on values and beliefs. Researchers should attribute cause only on evidence (pdf 174).
     - Social scientists are not value-free in choosing problems, though research conduct should be objective (pdf 174).
     - Three examples show that defining the problem correctly matters:
       - Gun-safety video: children said "Fingerprints". The researchers rethought their problem definition (pdf 174).
       - Ivory Coast teen pregnancy: students named "sugar daddies" as the real cause. A play on this won the national contest. Redefining the problem as system-blame led to an effective intervention (pdf 174–175).
       - Swedish tractor: farmers wanted a sound system rather than more power. Sales shot up after the cab was soundproofed and a CD player added (pdf 175).
  3. *System change seems harder.* The author replies that the first step toward system change is a more accurate problem definition (pdf 175).
  4. *Accessibility and tools.* Individuals are more accessible, and surveys channel researchers to individual units (Ryan and Gross studied Iowa farmers but not seed salespeople or the experiment station). Officials may be equally "to blame". The California tomato harvester, designed large and expensive, helped drive small farmers out (pdf 175–176).
     - Anthropological, qualitative traditions have been least accepting of individual-blame (pdf 176).
     - **Barton (1968)** calls the random-sample survey a "sociological meat-grinder" that tears individuals from their social context (pdf 176).
- **Network analysis as a partial remedy.** Communication network analysis identifies communication structure by analyzing relational data, using a relationship as the unit of analysis (pdf 177). The Ryan and Gross study had no network data. The refocusing had to wait for later studies, especially Coleman and colleagues (1966). Sociometric questions asking respondents who gave them information about the innovation make the link the unit of analysis (pdf 177).
- **Overcoming it (pdf 177–178):**
  1. Use units other than individuals alone.
  2. Keep an open mind about causes, and guard against agency definitions.
  3. Involve all participants (including adopters and rejectors) in defining the problem.
  4. Include social and communication-structure variables as well as individual ones.
  5. Study source and system questions: who owns and controls R&D, which agency diffuses, and for whose benefit.
- An individual-blame approach is not always inappropriate, but in almost all cases it is not a complete explanation (pdf 178).

*Intuition note.* The author suggests that a system-blame view would also ask whether the R&D source or the change agency is at fault, not only the individual adopter. A person may be "late" because the innovation did not fit their situation.

### The Recall Problem in Diffusion Research (pdf 178–182)
- **Time.** Time is one of the four essential elements of diffusion (Chapter 1). It gives tracer-like benefits but also methodological problems (pdf 178).
- **Recall.** Studies depend on respondents' self-reported date of adoption. Accuracy varies with the innovation's salience, length of recall period, and individual differences (pdf 178–179).
  - Concern about forgetting leads to "firehouse" speed in news-event research. Mayer and colleagues (1990) found accurate recall of Challenger-disaster details for at least several weeks, which the author calls reassuring. The general problem remains (pdf 179).
- **Design.** Most studies are correlational analyses of one-shot cross-sectional surveys, following Ryan and Gross. The author wants "moving pictures", not "snapshots". One-shot surveys make the process nearly "timeless". With data from only one point in time, time can be measured only by recall, "a possibly weak reed" (pdf 179).
- Alternatives (not widely used in past research, though experiments and case studies have been used more recently): (1) field experiments, (2) longitudinal panel studies, (3) archival records, (4) case studies with several respondents who check each other (pdf 179–180).
- **Causality.** **Mohr (1966):** factors such as wealth or size may be causes of innovativeness, effects of it, linked in reciprocal causality over time, or both may be caused by an outside factor. One-shot surveys cannot tell us much about time order or causality (pdf 180).
  - In about 60% of diffusion research the dependent variable is innovativeness (Table 2-2). It is often unclear whether "leads to" means "causes" (pdf 180).
  - For X to cause Y: (1) X precedes Y in time, (2) X and Y co-vary, and (3) X has a "forcing quality" on Y, that is, a theoretical basis for affecting it (pdf 180).
- A **field experiment** is conducted under realistic conditions, usually with pre- and post-intervention surveys. Examples: an entertainment-education soap opera broadcast in one area but not another (Rogers et al., 1999); opinion leaders used in some systems and not others. The author recommends much greater use, to avoid recall problems and compare strategies (pdf 180–181).
- **Other remedies for recall (pdf 181–182):**
  - *Multiple data points* divide the recall period into shorter segments (Figure 3-2).
  - *Point-of-adoption studies* collect data at the clinic, dealer or store at adoption. In Tanzania, data from seventy-nine clinics checked survey recall (pdf 181–182).
  - *Strategy list for surveys:* choose recently, rapidly diffused salient innovations (this may raise pro-innovation bias). Cross-check against other sources: in the Coleman drug study, doctors' recall generally agreed with prescription records, but doctors reported adopting tetracycline slightly earlier. Pretest questions and train interviewers. Use Internet computer records when available (pdf 182).

*Intuition note.* Asking people years later when they adopted something leans on memory. Gathering data during or at adoption, or running an experiment, removes that weakness and also helps with time order.

### The Issue of Equality in the Diffusion of Innovations (pdf 182–187)
- Researchers have paid little attention to who gets the benefits (Chapter 11). Where studied, diffusion often widens the gap between higher and lower socioeconomic segments. This can occur in any system but is especially noted in developing nations (pdf 182–183).
- **Development context.**
  - Diffusion research in developing nations in the 1960s seemed cross-culturally valid: S-shaped curves and innovators of higher status, cosmopoliteness and uncertainty tolerance (Colombia, Bangladesh) (pdf 183).
  - In the 1970s critics, some local, questioned the cultural importation of the paradigm. About 16% of all diffusion studies are in Latin America, Africa and Asia (pdf 183–184).
  - Four elements of the dominant development paradigm (Rogers, 1976): industrialization-led growth; capital-intensive imported technology; centralized planning; causes of underdevelopment seen as lying mainly within the developing nation. The author calls the last "individual-nation blame". The classical diffusion model fit this paradigm (pdf 184).
  - Development is now defined as a widely participatory process of social change for social and material advancement, including greater equality, for the majority through greater control over their environment (Singhal and Rogers, 2001). Women's empowerment gained attention because technology could subordinate women further (pdf 184–185).
- **Bordenave (1976)** argued that typical diffusion questions (how innovations spread; adopter categories; opinion leaders) miss the main development issues (pdf 185). His alternative questions (a just social structure as goal):
  - What criteria choose the innovations (public welfare, exports, low urban prices, or elite profit)?
  - How does social structure influence individual decisions?
  - Are the innovations appropriate, well proven and suited to the stage of development?
  - What are the consequences for employment, migration to cities and income distribution?
  - Will the innovation widen or narrow socioeconomic gaps? (pdf 185–186)
- **Structure.** In one Colombian village, one landowner held half the land and was also the most innovative farmer (Rogers with Svenning, 1969). Power, wealth and information are concentrated in a few hands in most developing nations. The classical model, **Bordenave argued**, when used uncritically did not touch social-structure change (pdf 186).
- **Mechanism of the gap.** Agencies assist more innovative, wealthier, better-educated clients. Such farmers have the means and credit, and their large farms matter for total production. Change agents concentrate on the clients most homophilous (similar) to themselves, which widens the benefits gap (pdf 186–187).
- **Not inevitable.** Field experiments by Shingi and Mody (1976, India) and Röling and colleagues (1976, Kenya) designed diffusion approaches that narrowed gaps. They introduced appropriate innovations to lower-status clients through a special program. This suggests that with effective communication strategies the socioeconomic structure need not be a major barrier, and greater equality may be possible (pdf 187).

### Summary (pdf 187–188)
- Early studies left an "indelible stamp" on approaches, concepts, methods and assumptions. The author calls it ironic that innovation study has itself been traditional (pdf 187).
- The four criticisms: pro-innovation bias, individual-blame bias, the recall problem, and the equality issue. Alternatives were proposed for each (pdf 187–188).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
*Main idea: studying only successful diffusions misleads (pro-innovation bias via selection).*
Suppose ten innovations were launched, with final adoption percentages: 90, 80, 70, 60, 20, 15, 10, 5, 5, 5.
- True average of all ten: (90+80+70+60+20+15+10+5+5+5) = 360, and 360 / 10 = 36%.
- A retrospective study picks "innovations that diffused widely" (above 50%): the first four. Their sum is 90+80+70+60 = 300, and 300 / 4 = 75%.
- The study would conclude that innovations typically reach 75%, while the real figure is 36%. The six failures left few traces and were never studied.
- Pairing each success with a failure from the same period (the author's remedy, pdf 163) brings the failures into the data.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Pro-innovation bias via retrospective success selection ↔ statistics: survivorship/selection bias — failed cases leave no trace, as in the toy example.
- [structural-analogy] Cause vs. blame; cross-sectional correlation vs. time order ↔ causal inference: the three conditions for causation (pdf 180).
- [structural-analogy] Individual-blame vs. system-blame ↔ framing: how a problem is defined shapes the solution (canon link; not stated in this unit).
- [metaphor] "Sociological meat-grinder" (Barton) ↔ unit-of-analysis problem in network science: random sampling destroys relational structure.

## Questions this raises
- How can one study failed or rejected innovations when they leave few traces, and how many "anti-innovation" studies are needed to rebalance the field?
- When is an individual-level explanation sufficient, and how would a researcher test the system-blame alternative?
- Are the two field experiments (India, Kenya) enough to show that diffusion need not widen gaps? What conditions made them work?
- If adoption or rejection is always "right" in the eyes of the adopter at the time (pdf 167), how should one evaluate harmful innovations such as ultrasound sex selection?
