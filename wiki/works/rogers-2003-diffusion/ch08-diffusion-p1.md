# Diffusion Networks (part 1): opinion leaders, homophily, measurement

## L0 — One line
Interpersonal networks are important in convincing people to adopt innovations, and opinion leaders, who are somewhat ahead of their followers but stay close to their system's norms, are important in determining how fast adoption takes off.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do interpersonal networks carry information about a new idea, and who leads this process? Rogers argues that opinion leaders (people who informally influence others' views or behaviour with relative frequency) are important in determining the speed of adoption. They have more media exposure, outside contact, social participation, status and innovativeness than followers, but they stay close to system norms. Network links are mostly homophilous (between similar people), which makes communication easy but slows spread. Heterophilous (between different people) "bridge" links are especially important for carrying new information across groups. Opinion leaders can be found with four measurement methods. They also exist among organizations. Experiments reported in this part are presented as supporting their use in interventions.

**Prerequisites:** the five-stage innovation-decision process (knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation); the idea of an S-shaped adoption curve; adopter categories (innovators, laggards); change agent (a professional who promotes an innovation); basic survey vs. experiment.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The S-curve takes off once opinion leaders adopt and tell others (pdf 377). In the Pittsburgh modern-math case, adoption appeared to jump as a direct result of the clique's leaders adopting, not after the isolated innovator (pdf 379).
- The hypodermic needle model (powerful direct media effects) was replaced by the two-step flow idea (media → opinion leaders → followers). Rogers calls two steps an oversimplification; mass media are primarily knowledge creators, while interpersonal networks matter more for persuasion (pdf 381–382).
- Homophily (similarity of communicating pairs) makes communication effective but can slow diffusion across a system. Rarer heterophilous "bridges" (links between dissimilar people) are especially important for carrying innovation information across cliques (informal friendship groups) (pdf 383–384).
- When networks are heterophilous, followers usually seek leaders somewhat more competent than themselves, but not too much more. Leaders who become too innovative risk losing their followers' trust (pdf 385–386, 398).
- Four methods measure opinion leadership: sociometric (ask whom people consult), key informants (ask well-informed members to name leaders), self-designating (people rate their own influence), observation (an investigator records communication behaviour). They are about equally valid, and leadership structures are stable in the short term (pdf 386–390).
- Generalizations 8-1 and 8-2 describe network links; 8-3 to 8-9 describe leaders. System norms decide whether leaders are innovators (pdf 384–397).
- Organizations (U.S. states, cities) also have opinion leaders (pdf 399–400).
- Rogers reports that all completed experiments in Table 8-2 found opinion-leader interventions effective, though some replications of one HIV study did not (pdf 388, 400–401).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Chapter opening (pdf 377).** The chapter studies how interpersonal networks convey evaluations of an innovation and so reduce an individual's uncertainty. *Opinion leadership* is the degree to which an individual can informally influence others' attitudes or overt behaviour in a desired way with relative frequency. The diffusion curve is S-shaped because, once opinion leaders adopt and tell others, adopters per period grow exponentially. Central idea: interpersonal communication drives diffusion by creating a critical mass of adopters (pdf 377).

**Case: modern math in Allegheny County (pdf 378–380).**
- Modern math was a new curriculum package (textbooks, audiovisual aids, summer institutes) with federal sponsorship (pdf 378). Thirty-eight superintendents were studied.
- The first adopter ("I", 1958) was a *sociometric isolate* (no network links to the other 37). Innovators like him interact mainly with cosmopolite friends outside the system (pdf 378). A *cosmopolite* has contacts outside the local system.
- Figure 8-1 is a *sociogram* (map of communication links). The shaded *clique* (informal friendship group) has six members, with three main opinion leaders. They adopted in 1959 and 1960 (pdf 378–379).
- Cumulative adopters: 1 (1958), 5 (1959), 15 (1960), 27 (1961), 35 (1962), 38 (end of 1963). The spurt in 1959–61 appeared to follow directly from the leaders' adoption (pdf 379). The figure's per-year counts are 1, 4, 10, 12, 8, 3 (pdf 379).
- The innovator was *too* innovative to be a role model. The others waited for the three leaders. A change agent should concentrate on such leaders because they leverage promotion (pdf 379).
- Many friendship arrows link people who adopted in the same or adjacent years. A small difference suggests a source competent but not too different (pdf 380). Only 32 of the 38 are drawn (pdf 380).

> *Intuition note:* Adoption appeared to take off not with the first adopter but after the well-connected, conforming leaders adopted. A leader is typically a little ahead of followers, not far ahead.

**Models of communication flows (pdf 380–382).**
- *Hypodermic needle model:* media have direct, immediate, powerful effects on atomized masses. It drew on Hearst newspapers and the Spanish-American War, Goebbels's propaganda, and Madison Avenue (pdf 380).
- Rogers judges it based on intuitive theorizing from unique events, too simple, mechanistic and gross; it ignored opinion leaders (pdf 381).
- *Two-step flow:* it arose serendipitously from the 1940 Erie County panel study (600 voters, six months). Few voting decisions were directly influenced by media (pdf 381). **Lazarsfeld et al.:** ideas often flow from radio and print to opinion leaders and then to less active people (pdf 381). Step 1 is mainly information; step 2 adds interpersonal influence (pdf 381).
- Rogers's criticisms: communication may take one step or many. The model ignored the innovation-decision stages. Mass channels are primarily knowledge creators, interpersonal networks matter more for persuasion. Followers also use media (pdf 382). The hypothesis still focused study on opinion leadership (pdf 382). Rogers also says the model was widely tested and found generally useful, though the oversimplified two-step idea is scarcely mentioned by scholars today (pdf 381–382).

> *Intuition note:* Media tell people about things; people they know help them decide. Rogers keeps the insight but not the neat "two steps".

**Homophily and heterophily (pdf 383–386).**
- *Homophily:* degree to which communicating pairs are similar (beliefs, education, status). *Heterophily:* the opposite (pdf 383). Ideas are exchanged most often between homophilous people.
- Reasons: communication is more effective with shared meanings. Heterophilous contact needs more effort and may cause cognitive dissonance (discomfort from beliefs that conflict). Homophily and effective communication breed each other (pdf 383).
- Heterophilous links connecting two cliques are "bridges". They are especially important for innovation information (Granovetter's "strength of weak ties") (pdf 384). Homophily accelerates diffusion but limits it to a close-knit network. Diffusion can ultimately occur only through links at least somewhat heterophilous (pdf 384).
- Homophily as barrier: innovations enter via high-status members; with high homophily they spread horizontally, not vertically. Change agents should then work with several sets of opinion leaders (pdf 384).
- **Generalization 8-1:** Interpersonal diffusion networks are mostly homophilous (pdf 384).
- Van den Ban (Netherlands): only 3% of opinion leaders had farms under 50 acres, but 38% of farms were that small. Small farmers copied the large farmers' expensive machinery, although labour-intensive horticulture suited them better (pdf 385).
- Two Indian villages: the traditional village had more homophilous networks (by caste), with elderly, less educated leaders. In the progressive village the rice variety spread downward across castes through heterophilous links, aiding rapid diffusion (pdf 385).
- **Generalization 8-2:** When networks are heterophilous, followers seek leaders of higher status, education, media exposure, cosmopoliteness, change-agent contact and innovativeness (pdf 385). Heterophily is usually toward somewhat greater competence, and the general pattern remains homophily (pdf 386).

> *Intuition note:* Similar people understand each other, so news moves quickly inside a group. To spread beyond a close-knit group, diffusion ultimately needs links between somewhat different people, which may be rare.

**Measuring opinion leadership (pdf 386–390; Table 8-1 on pdf 389).**
1. *Sociometric:* ask whom people seek for advice; leaders get the most choices. Highly valid (followers' perceptions) but needs many respondents and works best when most members respond (pdf 386). Limited-choice questions capture only strongest ties. Rogers suggests that sociometric questions perhaps should allow unlimited choices; a *roster study* (list of all members) measures weak as well as strong links (pdf 387).
2. *Key informants:* knowledgeable informants nominate leaders; nearly as accurate, especially in small systems with well-informed informants (pdf 387). In Taos County, leaders were those nominated by two or more informants (pdf 387).
3. *Self-designating:* "Do people come to you for advice more often than to others?" It relies on accurate self-image; suits random samples (pdf 387).
4. *Observation:* high validity, but obtrusive, works in very small systems and may need patience (pdf 388).

- Observation example: Kelly et al. (1997) in gay bars in four U.S. cities. Bartenders watched customers for ten days and listed who was greeted often, greeted others and seemed liked. Men named by several bartenders (about 8% of customers) became trained opinion leaders with traffic-light badges. Safe-sex behaviour rose 45% (condom-protected anal intercourse) versus four control cities (pdf 388). Similar results elsewhere in the U.S., but not in London gyms or Scottish bars; Rogers notes these later studies did not train the minimum of 15% of the audience that Kelly feels is necessary (pdf 388). The approach did not assume every member needs direct contact with a leader, because the message spreads through the leader's networks; Rogers likens it to STOP AIDS in San Francisco (pdf 389).
- Measures correlate positively but not fully. Rogers says the choice among the four can be based on convenience, as all are about equally valid (pdf 389).
- Leaders are stable: five Missouri communities stayed stable over 1989–95 despite floods and hog-farm arrival (pdf 389–390). Over decades leaders must change (pdf 390).
- Typical distribution: a few individuals receive much opinion leadership, most have none or little (pdf 390).

**Case: alpha pups and Pox (pdf 391–392).** A firm asked thousands of Chicago boys (8–13) "Who's the coolest kid you know?" and climbed the chain until someone said "Me" (sociometric plus self-designating). This yielded 1,600 "alpha pups". At a focus group, each of eight alpha pups was paid $30 and given ten Pox units to hand out to friends. Hasbro called this "viral marketing". Alpha pups seeded 900 of 1,400 Chicago-area schools, then Pox went on sale at about $25. Girls were not targeted (pdf 391–392).

**Monomorphic vs. polymorphic leadership (pdf 392).** *Polymorphism:* the degree to which an individual acts as opinion leader on many topics; *monomorphism:* the degree to which one does so on only a single topic. It seems to vary with topic diversity, system norms and other factors. In Decatur (four topics), one third of leaders led in more than one area. Village leaders in developing countries often lead on many topics (pdf 392).

**Case: Paul Revere's ride (pdf 393–395).** Revere's alarm reached Lincoln by 1:00 A.M., Sudbury by 3:00, Andover by 5:00 and Ashby (about 45 miles) by 9:00, rousing a 750-square-mile area (pdf 393). William Dawes left at the same time on an equally fast horse but knocked on random doors, and Waltham never got an effective alarm (pdf 394–395). Revere knew which doors to pound, and the people he woke rallied neighbours and sent riders. Rogers says this set an S-shaped curve in motion (pdf 394–395).

**Characteristics of opinion leaders (pdf 395–398).** Rogers treats leader/follower as a dichotomy for clarity (pdf 395).
- **8-3:** more mass-media exposure than followers (pdf 395).
- **8-4:** more cosmopolite. **Burt:** leaders are "at the edge of things", brokers between groups (pdf 396).
- **8-5:** more contact with change agents (pdf 396).
- **8-6:** greater social participation. Revere belonged to many clubs and taverns, so he was socially accessible (pdf 396).
- **8-7:** higher socioeconomic status (pdf 397).
- **8-8:** more innovative than followers, but not necessarily innovators (pdf 397).
- **8-9:** When norms favor change, leaders are more innovative; when not, they are not especially innovative (pdf 397). In traditional Colombian villages, leaders were only slightly more innovative than their followers, and were older and less cosmopolite (pdf 397).
- **Herzog et al. (Brazil):** in traditional communities neither leaders nor followers are innovative; in modern ones both are; in middle-range communities leaders try new ideas first (pdf 398).
- Common change-agent error: choosing leaders who are too innovative. This moves the heterophily gap between leaders and followers. Over-contact can turn leaders into innovators who lose followers. A leader must show prudent judgment and "look over his or her shoulder" (pdf 398).

> *Intuition note:* Leaders are trusted because they are ahead, but only a little. If they run too far ahead, followers may doubt their judgment and see them as an unrealistic model.

**Opinion leader organizations (pdf 399–400).**
- Walker scored 50 U.S. states on 88 programs. The five most innovative ("national league") were New York, Massachusetts, California, New Jersey, Michigan: large, urban, industrial and rich (pdf 399).
- In each region certain states acted as opinion leaders. If an innovation was first adopted by a state other than these opinion-leader states, it spread to other states slowly or not at all (pdf 399).
- Interviews with officials in ten states: officials look to similar neighbours. Iowa (ranked 29th) followed Wisconsin (10th) more than Michigan or California (pdf 399). Follower states often copied exact wording, including a typographical error (pdf 400).
- Summary: a law starts in one or more national-league states, then regional leaders adopt it, then it spreads rapidly in the region. Regional leaders connected the five innovators to the other 45 states (pdf 400).
- Smoking ordinances: Las Cruces, NM, was an early adopter and a regional opinion-leader city. Within a few years a dozen southwestern cities followed, partly via a community-health coalition (pdf 400).

**Do opinion leaders matter? (pdf 400–401, part ends mid-sentence).** Working through opinion leaders is the most common way to use a network (pdf 400).
- Lomas et al. (1991): 76 doctors in 16 hospitals. After two years, chart audit and feedback had no measurable result, while the opinion-leader approach produced an 85% increase in adoption of vaginal birth guidelines (pdf 401).
- Rogers reports that all completed experiments in Table 8-2 found opinion-leader interventions effective (HIV prevention, mammography, heart disease) (pdf 401). Most were randomized controlled trials (RCTs): an intervention is randomly assigned to a treatment condition but not to a control condition. Control effects are subtracted from treatment effects to remove contemporaneous change (pdf 401). Rogers sees a possible trend toward experiments in diffusion research (pdf 401).
- The section turns to why leaders agreed to take part (pdf 401). The text breaks off here.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ten people. I is an isolate. L has contacts A, B, C, D. A–E, B–F, C–G and D–H are further ties. Rule: I adopts in period 1; L adopts in period 2; anyone adopts one period after the person they follow adopts.
- Period 1: I → cumulative 1.
- Period 2: L → 1 + 1 = 2.
- Period 3: A, B, C, D → 2 + 4 = 6.
- Period 4: E, F, G, H → 6 + 4 = 10.

Cumulative: 1, 2, 6, 10. The jump (+4) follows the leader L's adoption, as in the Pittsburgh clique. If only I had adopted and L never had, adoption would stay at 1, because I has no ties. L's adoption in period 2 is what sets off the other eight.

- [structural-analogy] bridges / weak ties ↔ network science: ties connecting clusters carry non-redundant information.
- [structural-analogy] Burt's leader as broker "at the edge" ↔ structural holes in network theory: the leader spans group boundaries.

## Questions this raises
- If leaders must be near their followers, how many leaders are needed? Kelly's stated 15% minimum (pdf 388) suggests a threshold.
- Do bridges help or conflict with leaders' conformity, since leaders are brokers (pdf 396) yet norm-bound (pdf 398)?
- Can measured leadership (four methods, only partly agreeing, pdf 389) be a stable trait, given topic-specific leadership (pdf 392)?
- Why did some gay-bar and gym replications fail (pdf 388)? Training share, or something else?
