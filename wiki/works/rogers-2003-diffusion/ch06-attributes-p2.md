# Attributes of Innovations and Their Rate of Adoption, part 2: compatibility (rest), complexity, trialability, observability, mobile-phone case

## L0 — One line
People judge a new idea by how it fits their own experience, needs and perceptions, so perceived compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability help predict how fast it is adopted.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part finishes the discussion of compatibility, then covers complexity, trialability and observability. The question is which perceived features of an innovation predict its rate of adoption. Adopters compare a new idea with past experience, values and needs. Compatibility, trialability and observability are positively related to rate of adoption, and complexity negatively (Generalizations 6-2 to 6-5, the last three put as suggestions). So change agents should learn clients' perceptions: name and position innovations carefully, respect indigenous knowledge, and measure the five attributes in each study. A long Finnish mobile-phone case applies all five attributes. It matters because adoption depends on perception, not only on expert-judged performance.

**Prerequisites:** the "rate of adoption" idea and the roles of change agent and adopter; relative advantage (earlier in Chapter 6); adopter categories (Chapter 5).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Compatibility (fit with values, past experience, needs) can speed adoption, but it can also cause misuse, such as the Punjab tractor blankets (pdf 313). It can also bring innovation negativism after a failure (pdf 314–315).
- Generalization 6-2: perceived compatibility is positively related to rate of adoption. It may matter somewhat less than relative advantage, and in some studies the two were not empirically distinct (pdf 319).
- Innovations are often perceived as bundles (technology clusters). Names and market positions shape perceived compatibility, and positioning research rests on measuring compatibility, while acceptability research studies the perceived attributes of an ideal innovation (pdf 320–324).
- The "empty vessels fallacy" treats adopters as blank slates. Ignoring indigenous knowledge systems and their practitioners has led to failed or reversed adoption (pdf 325–328).
- Generalizations 6-3 to 6-5 (put as suggestions): complexity is negatively related, and trialability and observability positively related, to rate of adoption (pdf 328–330).
- Earlier adopters rate trialability as more important than later adopters do, because peers act as a "vicarious trial" for later adopters (pdf 329).
- The Finnish cell-phone case shows all five attributes working together, with the innovation re-invented over time (pdf 331–336).
- Summary: measure the five perceived attributes anew in each study. Other variables also affect rate of adoption, but past research mostly studied the attributes (pdf 337–338).

### Compatibility (continued) (pdf 313–315)
- **Kansas sorghum.** Brandner and Straus (1959) found hybrid sorghum on 28% of sorghum acreage in northeastern Kansas in the first year it was available. Experts did not yet recommend it. Hybrid corn, adopted earlier, made it compatible. In dry western Kansas, where corn was not grown, adoption was slow (pdf 313).
- **Misadoption.** Compatibility with a previous idea can cause overadoption or misadoption (pdf 313).
- **Punjab tractors (Carter, 1994).** Tractors gave prestige, like the bullocks they replaced. Farmers did not do basic maintenance, so new tractors broke down after a year or two. A foreign consultant's five-colour maintenance chart did not help. A blanket salesman then came. Within ten days virtually every tractor had a blanket on its hood, despite the expert's warning of overheating. Keeping a power source warm fitted bullock care. Cleaning filters did not, so past experience had negative consequences (pdf 313).
- **Pueblos (Hawley, 1946).** Eastern Pueblos, with a father-oriented family structure, accepted a religion with a male deity. Western Pueblos, with mother-centered beliefs, rejected it violently. Rogers suggests that stressing the female aspects (the Virgin Mary) might have helped (pdf 314).
- **Tension.** A completely congruent idea would not be an innovation to adopters. The more compatible an innovation, the less behaviour change it represents. A highly compatible innovation can still be useful as a first step that paves the way for less compatible ones (pdf 314).
- **Art.** Lievrouw and Pope (1994): for artworks the reverse of the usual pattern may hold. Works too derivative of older ones are unlikely to succeed, so artworks "must be somewhat radical" to diffuse rapidly (pdf 314).
- **Innovation negativism** (Arensberg and Niehoff, 1964): how far one innovation's failure conditions a potential adopter to reject future ones (pdf 314). So change agents should start with an innovation of high relative advantage, then build compatible ones on it. India's family-planning programme began with the IUD, widely discontinued in the 1960s. Family planning in north India has never been able to recover (pdf 315).

*Intuition note:* Fit can help adoption, but it can also lead to misuse, and a failed first innovation can leave people wary of later ones.

### Compatibility with needs (pdf 315)
- A felt need that an innovation meets is one indicator of compatibility. Assessing needs takes empathy and rapport; informal probing, advisory committees and surveys are sometimes used. People may not feel a need until they learn of the idea. Generated needs may reflect only the change agent's needs. When felt needs are met, a faster rate of adoption usually occurs (pdf 315).

### Case: Photovoltaics on a Million Roofs (pdf 316–317)
- Kaplan (1999): only 2.5% of US power companies had adopted photovoltaics (PVs), though most managers had high technical knowledge (a "KAP-gap"). PVs are decentralized, modular and easily disconnected from the grid, a misfit with utility practice. The more radical and disruptive an innovation and the less compatible, the slower the adoption (pdf 316).
- Most individuals do not evaluate an innovation solely, or perhaps at all, by scientific performance; rather they rely on the subjective evaluations of peers. Pioneers exist (Gainesville, Sacramento, the Hopi). A trailer with PV equipment parked at a Hopi home for a week raises trialability (pdf 316).

### Case: "The Daughter-in-Law Who Doesn't Speak" (pdf 318–319)
- A UNDP "multifunctional platform" in Mali: a 10-horsepower diesel motor driving a husker, saw, pump, grinder/blender and generator. Cost about $4,000, half raised by the Sanankoroni Women's association. Daily earnings were $12, one third going to operators and maintenance. The first nine months yielded $380, where per-capita income is $300 per year (pdf 318).
- About 300 villages have one. A 45-kg sack of corn took three days of pounding and now takes 15 minutes. Girls can attend school and mothers can join literacy classes or start businesses (pdf 318–319).
- The machine was too costly for one person, but women's collective associations fit village values and experience. Rogers calls it highly compatible, with considerable relative advantage (pdf 319).

### Compatibility and rate of adoption (pdf 319)
- **Generalization 6-2:** the compatibility of an innovation, as perceived by members of a social system, is positively related to its rate of adoption. Past research suggests it may be somewhat less important than relative advantage. In certain studies the two were not empirically distinct, although they are conceptually distinct (pdf 319).

### Technology clusters (pdf 320–321)
- A **technology cluster** is one or more distinguishable technology elements perceived as interrelated. Adopting one idea may trigger others, and a change agency may promote a package (pdf 320).
- Asian package: IRRI rice, fertilizer and chemicals were recommended together. Villagers adopted faster than for separate diffusion. Farmers got total plus synergistic yield effects, often tripling rice yields (pdf 320). Package effects have seldom been studied, and packaging should follow users' perceived compatibility (pdf 320).
- Silverman and Bailey (1961): 107 Mississippi corn farmers, three interrelated ideas (fertilizer, hybrid seed, thicker planting). Adopting only the last gave lower yields than adopting none. Most farmers adopted all three or none, and 8% used unsuccessful combinations (pdf 320).
- Merchandisers use tie-ins, for example a washer with a dryer. Some "hook on" an unwanted product to a compatible, high-advantage one (pdf 320–321).
- LaRose and Atkin (1992): among 18 telecommunications innovations in a sample of 1,400 US adults, they found narrow clusters, not one general high-tech cluster. Examples: ATMs, toll-free numbers and phone credit cards formed one cluster, e-mail and PC another. VCR ownership stood apart (pdf 321).

### Naming an innovation (pdf 321–322)
- The name affects perceived compatibility, and so rate of adoption. Examples: "Cue" (obscene in French) and "Nova" (no va, "no-go", in Spanish). Technical names such as "2,4-D weed spray" and "intrauterine device" confuse adopters. Mali's "Daughter-in-Law" name works better (pdf 321–322).
- The Copper-T in South Korea was a poor name: Korean has no letter "T", and copper is a base metal with unfavourable connotation (pdf 322). Rogers recommends a receiver-oriented, empirical approach to naming (pdf 322).

### Positioning an innovation (pdf 322–323)
- Basic assumption: people treat a new idea as they treat ideas perceived as similar. If X is seen as similar to B but unlike A and C, and price and access are equal, X should attain about half of B's former consumers and not affect A or C. If we can learn why B and X are seen as similar to each other but different from A and C, X can be positioned (name, colour, packaging and the like) to maximize its distance from all three. This rests on measuring compatibility accurately (pdf 322–323).
- Tetracycline was similar to two earlier antibiotics but superior in lacking unpleasant side effects (Coleman et al., 1996) (pdf 323).
- Harding et al. (1973), Copper-T in Korea. Step 1: a small sample named 29 attributes of 18 contraceptive methods. Step 2: another sample rated all 18 methods on the 29 attributes. Recommendations: stress long lifetime, reliability, no interference with intercourse, and newness. Also consider altering the string, which was linked to bacteria and inflammation (pdf 323).

### Acceptability research (pdf 324–325)
- **Acceptability research** investigates the perceived attributes of an ideal innovation so R&D can create it (pdf 324).
- WHO in the 1970s: men and women in developing nations strongly disliked methods needing genital manipulation (IUD, condom, diaphragm). This incompatibility was one reason for poor adoption. WHO research aimed at methods without genital handling, such as Depo-Provera and Norplant (pdf 324).
- HIV/AIDS: HIV spread to 40 million people worldwide, and another 20 million have died. Condoms are often seen as incompatible with pleasure, messy and needing genital handling. Even in nations where the epidemic is widespread, officials convinced only about 10% or so of sexually active people to adopt safer sex. Rogers asks whether condoms could be positioned as more acceptable and suggests acceptability research "could help" (pdf 324–325).

### Indigenous knowledge systems (pdf 325–328)
- The **empty vessels fallacy**: assuming adopters are blank slates, which denies that compatibility matters. Analysis of indigenous knowledge systems has helped overcome it in agriculture, health and family planning (pdf 325).
- Flavier (Philippines) explained that pills work like the iping-iping seeds that stop hens laying (pdf 325).
- Bali (Lansing, 1991): water temples and priests schedule irrigation, including fallow periods that kill pests. Extension workers ignored this. IRRI rice had a shorter growing season and clashed with the schedule. Yields fell and farmers dropped it (pdf 326).
- Why ignored: strong belief in relative advantage leads technocrats to dismiss existing practice. The innovation is "new wine" poured into "old bottles". The remedy is to understand clients' prior experience (pdf 326).
- Useful knowledge exists: Squanto's fish in the corn hill; morphine, quinine and codeine from herbs; the pill's ingredient from a Mexican yam; many HIV-positive people turn to traditional herbs that may have anti-retroviral qualities. Anti-retrovirals (since 1996) cost more than most people in developing countries can afford; 95% of the 40 million HIV-positive people live there (pdf 326–327).
- Traditional practitioners (midwives, curanderos) are seen as "quacks" by modern counterparts but are credible to poor rural and urban women. Some programmes train midwives and pay small incentives per adopter recruited (pdf 327).
- When midwives were ignored or attacked, they spread rumours (India: dais said the husband of a wife who had adopted an IUD could not become disengaged). Adoption plateaued, then fell (pdf 327–328).
- Indonesia trained 54,000 bidans to replace dukuns. The dukuns were older, respected, well connected and cheaper. Most bidans could not earn a living and quit (pdf 328).

### Complexity (pdf 328–329)
- **Complexity:** how far an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use. **Generalization 6-3:** perceived complexity is negatively related to rate of adoption. The evidence is "not entirely conclusive" (pdf 328).
- It may matter less than relative advantage or compatibility for many innovations, but for some it is a major barrier (pdf 328).
- Home computers: the first adopters (hobbyists, engineers) did not find them complex. Later adopters faced frustration with connecting parts, running software, manuals and jargon-talking sellers. They eventually coped through user clubs and friends. Machines became more user-friendly, and adoption reached about 50% of US households by 2002 (pdf 328–329).

### Trialability (pdf 329)
- **Trialability:** how far an innovation can be tried on a limited basis. Ideas that can be tried "on the installment plan" generally are adopted faster. A trial gives meaning and dispels uncertainty. **Generalization 6-4:** perceived trialability is positively related to rate of adoption. A trial may involve re-invention (pdf 329).
- Earlier adopters rate trialability as more important (Gross, 1942; Ryan, 1948). Later adopters have peers as vicarious trial. Laggards go from trial to full use faster than innovators and early adopters (pdf 329).

### Observability (pdf 330)
- **Observability:** how far results are visible to others. **Generalization 6-5:** perceived observability is positively related to rate of adoption (pdf 330).
- Technology has hardware (tool) and software (information base) aspects. Software is less observable, so software-dominant innovations usually adopt more slowly. Example: "safer sex" is ambiguous (abstinence, monogamy, condoms) and spread slowly to a small share of those at risk (pdf 330).

### Case: cellular telephones and the lifestyle revolution (pdf 331–337)
- **History.** First offered in the US in 1983; 130 million sold in the next decade and 1.1 billion worldwide in the following one. Finland: 104% adoption in the 18–34 group (some people own two or more), and 25% of households have only a mobile phone (pdf 331).
- **First adopters** were male executives with company phones. They cost about $3,000, were brick-sized and mostly car-mounted, and were used by contractors. Later price fell to $200 or less, phones shrank, and non-business use predominated (pdf 331).
- **Fashion.** Nokia made phones a fashion accessory. Engineers at the Rikala bar in Salo painted their identical phones in 1993, which began the colour phones. Nokia then designed phones for each market segment (pdf 332). Designer Frank Nuovo's design (oval holes, a feeling of coherence) is described. Teenagers became the fastest-growing segment after businessmen, and the customer became an individual, not a household (pdf 332–333).
- **Segments (Figure 6-3).** The figure shows Finland's adoption rising slowly from 1981 and steeply from the mid-1990s to about 75% by 2002, with labels Businessmen, Households and Individuals. Businessmen came first (time-saving), then households after the 1994 critical mass (sociability), then individuals (lifestyle). New services (SMS after 1994, ringtones after 1998) kept re-inventing the phone (pdf 333–334).
- **Five attributes (pdf 334–336):**
  - *Relative advantage:* saved about two hours a week for businessmen, hands-free driving, emergency use, status. more than 40,000 "Cellular Phoneys" ($16 non-working replicas) were sold (pdf 334–335).
  - *Compatibility:* connects to the existing phone system, so no early critical mass was needed. Names were nontechnical ("handy" in Germany), and styles were personalizable (pdf 335).
  - *Complexity:* works like a regular phone. SMS (about 1 billion a day) was a user re-invention and suits Finns, who "hate to talk" (pdf 335).
  - *Trialability:* borrowing a friend's phone, rental cars, a free first month, free phones (pdf 335–336).
  - *Observability:* use in public places showed status, visually and by sound (pdf 336).
- **Espoo visit (pdf 336–337).** Nokia nearly failed in 1988. CEO Ollila sold other units and focused on mobile phones. The first GSM call was made on a Nokia phone in 1991. Nokia spends 9.6% of sales on R&D (usual 2–3%), has 54 R&D centers and launched 20 models in 2002 (pdf 336–337).

*Intuition note:* The cell-phone case fits the chapter's claim: a phone that was easy to try, easy to see, easy to use and compatible with the old phone system had an "almost ideal set" of attributes, which the author gives as one reason for its very rapid adoption.

### Summary (pdf 337–338)
- Five perceived attributes predict rate of adoption, and measures should be built anew in each study rather than borrowed scales (pdf 337).
- Other variables also affect rate of adoption: type of innovation-decision, communication channels, the social system, and change agents' efforts. Most past research stressed the five attributes (pdf 337–338).
- Restated: overadoption is adoption when experts feel the idea should be rejected. Preventive innovations diffuse more slowly than incremental ones (pdf 338).
- Naming and positioning are means of making an innovation more compatible. Change agents often ignore indigenous knowledge systems (pdf 338).
- Basic theme: change agents and scholars must understand how potential adopters perceive new ideas (pdf 338).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Positioning arithmetic. A market has three ideas: A has 100 buyers, B has 100, C has 100 (300 total). Customers perceive new idea X as similar to B but unlike A and C. Price and access are equal. Following the positioning assumption (pdf 322), X wins about half of B's former buyers: 0.5 × 100 = 50. B keeps 100 − 50 = 50. A and C stay at 100 each. Total: 50 + 50 + 100 + 100 = 300. If X were instead positioned far from all three, the text says it gains "a unique niche" (pdf 323).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Positioning (new idea takes share from the perceptually closest one) ↔ market competition: substitutability by perceived similarity — only as the text states it.
- [structural-analogy] Vicarious trial through peers ↔ social proof in social-influence models — peers' adoption lowers need for own trial.
- [metaphor] "New wine in old bottles" ↔ schema theory: new information is interpreted through existing frames.

## Questions this raises
- Can compatibility and relative advantage be measured separately, given that some studies found them empirically indistinct (pdf 319)?
- When compatibility causes misadoption (tractors), how should change agents use it without harm?
- Does acceptability research on condoms exist, and did repositioning them raise adoption (pdf 325)?
- How do segments (businessmen, households, individuals) change which attributes matter (pdf 334)?
