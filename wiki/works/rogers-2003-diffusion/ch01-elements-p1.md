# Elements of Diffusion (part 1/2): cases, definition, innovation, channels

## L0 — One line
Diffusion is the communication of a new idea through channels, over time, within a social system, and a better idea does not spread on its merits alone.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do useful innovations spread slowly or not at all? Rogers opens with three cases: failed water boiling in a Peruvian village, the very slow adoption of citrus against scurvy (Lancaster's trial in 1601, Navy adoption in 1795), and the non-adoption of the Dvorak keyboard. He then defines diffusion as a special kind of communication about new ideas, which involves uncertainty. He names four elements: innovation, communication channels, time and social system. This part covers the first two. Perceived attributes of an innovation (relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability) help explain how fast it is adopted. Re-invention, mass-media versus interpersonal channels, and homophily versus heterophily shape how ideas spread.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an S-shaped growth curve; basic notions of social groups and norms. No mathematics is needed.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Even clearly advantageous innovations often diffuse slowly. The Peru, scurvy and Dvorak cases show this (pdf 37, 44, 48).
- In Los Molinas, the innovation clashed with local belief and the change agent worked with the "wrong housewives" (a "sickly one" and a social outsider). Rogers concludes that diffusion is a social process, more than a technical matter (pdf 41).
- Diffusion is communication about new ideas. Rogers defines communication as convergence through information exchange, not as one-way transfer. Newness brings uncertainty, and information reduces it (pdf 42–43).
- Four elements appear in every diffusion study or campaign: innovation, channels, time, social system (pdf 49).
- An innovation is whatever is perceived as new by the adopter. Five perceived attributes help explain adoption rate, and relative advantage and compatibility are especially important (pdf 50, 53–55).
- Innovations are not necessarily fixed: many users re-invent them, and some innovations form interrelated technology clusters (pdf 53, 55–56).
- Mass media are usually fastest for awareness. Interpersonal channels are more effective for persuasion, especially between similar people (pdf 57).
- Diffusion needs some heterophily (difference), but similarity makes communication effective. This is a built-in tension (pdf 58).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pdf 37).** A Machiavelli epigraph says that creating a new order of things is hard and dangerous. Rogers says that adopting a new idea is difficult even when its advantages are obvious. Many innovations need many years to spread, so speeding up diffusion is a common practical problem (pdf 37).

### Case 1: Water boiling in a Peruvian village (pdf 38–42)
- **Setting.** The public health service promotes latrines, garbage burning, fly control, disease reporting and water boiling. Villagers do not link sanitation to illness (pdf 38).
- **Campaign.** In Los Molinas (200 families), a two-year campaign by health worker Nelida persuaded only eleven housewives. She visited every home and gave intensive effort (15–25 visits each) to 21 families. Eleven of these now boil water (pdf 38–39).
- **Mrs. A (custom-oriented adopter).** She boils water because of the local hot–cold belief system, not germ theory. By local custom, only the ill drink "hot" boiled water, and raw water is seen as very cold. She boils water because she sees herself as ill. She "adopted the innovation, but for the wrong reason" (pdf 39).
- **Mrs. B (persuaded adopter).** She is an outsider from the Andes with marginal status. The community is not her reference group, so she can deviate from its norms. She values Nelida's approval (pdf 40).
- **Mrs. C (rejector).** She represents the majority. She does not understand germ theory and is a firm believer in the hot–cold rule that only the sick drink boiled water. Her objections are tagged as hers: **Mrs. C argues** that microbes could not survive in water that would drown people, and that real threats like poverty are enough to worry about (pdf 40).
- **Rogers's reasons for failure** (pdf 41–42):
  1. Boiling was culturally incompatible with the hot–cold norms. Only people not integrated into local networks risk defying the norm.
  2. Interpersonal networks mattered in adoption or rejection. Mrs. B boiled water to win Nelida's approval, not because she understood the health reasons.
  3. Nelida worked with a "sickly one" and an outsider, who were not social models. She ignored the village opinion leaders, so adoption did not reach a critical mass after which diffusion would be self-sustaining.
  4. Poor housewives saw Nelida as a "snooper" and a social stranger. Communication is more effective with people similar to the change agent, so those who most need help are least likely to accept it.
  5. Nelida was "innovation-oriented" rather than "client-oriented." She talked about germ theory, which villagers could not and did not need to understand.

> *Intuition note.* Rogers explains the failure by the social setting (local beliefs, networks, who was approached first), not by the technical merit of boiling. He says diffusion is a social process even more than a technical matter.

### What is diffusion? (pdf 42–43)
- **Diffusion:** the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system. It is a special type of communication because its messages concern new ideas (pdf 42).
- **Communication:** a process in which participants create and share information to reach mutual understanding. It is a process of convergence (or divergence), a two-way exchange, not a one-way linear act (Rogers and Kincaid, 1981). A linear view may fit single acts, such as a change agent persuading a client. But such acts are one part of a larger exchange that may run through several cycles (pdf 42–43).
- **Uncertainty:** the degree to which a number of alternatives are perceived for an event, along with their relative probabilities. It implies a lack of predictability, structure and information (pdf 43).
- **Information:** a means of reducing uncertainty. Rogers defines it as a difference in matter-energy that affects uncertainty where a choice exists among alternatives. A technological innovation embodies information and reduces uncertainty about cause–effect relationships (pdf 43).
- **Social change:** the process by which alteration occurs in the structure and function of a social system. Diffusion is one kind. Others are revolution, drought or earthquake, and government policy (pdf 43).
- **Terminology:** some authors reserve "diffusion" for spontaneous spread and use "dissemination" for directed spread. Rogers uses "diffusion" for both planned and spontaneous spread (pdf 43).

### Case 2: Scurvy in the British Navy (pdf 44–45)
- Many technologists believe advantageous innovations "sell themselves." Rogers says seldom is this the case (pdf 44).
- Of da Gama's 160 men in 1497, 100 died of scurvy (pdf 44).
- **1601:** Lancaster gave lemon juice to the sailors on one of four ships. By the halfway point, 110 of 278 sailors on the other three ships had died. Those on the lemon-juice ship stayed healthy (pdf 44).
- **1747:** Lind tested citrus against five other supplements and found that citrus cured patients (pdf 44).
- **1795:** The Navy adopted citrus, 48 years after Lind's trial, and scurvy was wiped out. **1865:** the Board of Trade did the same for the merchant marine (pdf 45).
- **Possible reasons:** competing remedies had champions, Cook's reports did not support citrus, and Lind was not prominent. The Admiralty readily accepted other innovations (new ships, new guns), so it did not resist all innovations. Rogers concludes that more than relative advantage is necessary for adoption (pdf 45).

### Case 3: Nondiffusion of the Dvorak keyboard (pdf 46–48)
- **QWERTY** dates from 1873. Sholes designed it to minimize jamming by making common letter sequences awkward and slowing typists. Jamming later ceased to be a problem as typewriters improved (pdf 46).
- **Dvorak** (1932) built his layout from time-and-motion studies. Its features (pdf 47):
  - Home row: A, O, E, U, I, D, H, T, N, S.
  - Hand alternation, with vowels on the left and major consonants on the right.
  - Typing share on the home row: 70% on Dvorak versus 32% on QWERTY.
  - Left-hand load: 44% on Dvorak versus 57% on QWERTY.
- **Rogers's claims** include that QWERTY takes twice as long to learn and causes more errors (pdf 46–47).
- Almost no one uses Dvorak after 70+ years. Rogers points to vested interests (manufacturers, sales outlets, teachers, typists). He also cites the effort of about a week's training. He concludes that "superior technological innovations do not necessarily diffuse themselves" (pdf 48).

### Four main elements (pdf 49)
Innovation, communication channels, time and social system appear in every diffusion study and campaign (pdf 49). Figure 1-2 shows three S-shaped curves of percent adopting over time, for Innovations I, II and III. I rises fastest and III slowest. A hatched "take-off" zone marks II's early rise, between roughly 10% and 35% on the axis (pdf 49).

### 1. The Innovation (pdf 50–56)
- **Innovation:** an idea, practice or object perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption. Perceived newness, not objective newness, determines reaction. Newness may concern knowledge, persuasion or the decision to adopt (pdf 50).
- **Research questions:** how earlier adopters differ from later ones; how perceived attributes affect rate of adoption; and why the S-curve "takes off" at about 10–20% adoption, when networks activate and a critical mass begins using the innovation (pdf 50).
- **Not always desirable:** mechanical tomato pickers were adopted rapidly by large Californian growers but were too expensive for small ones, and thousands left tomato production. About half of U.S. households own a PC; the other half see little use for one or use a computer at work or in a cybercafé (pdf 50).
- **Technology.** A technology is a design for instrumental action that reduces uncertainty in cause–effect relations. It has a hardware aspect (the tool) and a software aspect (the information base). Some technologies are almost all information (Marxism, Christianity, a no-smoking ordinance). Such idea-only innovations have relatively lower observability and a slower adoption rate (pdf 51).
- **Hardware-first products.** Examples are VCRs and video game players. The "shaver-and-blades" strategy sells hardware cheaply and software dearly to speed diffusion (pdf 51).
- **The innovation-decision** is essentially an information-seeking and information-processing activity. Learning of an innovation creates uncertainty about its consequences. Seeking information reduces it to a tolerable level, then a decision is made, and use gives further evaluative information (pdf 52).
- **Typical questions:** What is it? How does it work? Why? What are the consequences? What are the advantages and disadvantages in my situation? (pdf 52).

> *Intuition note.* Adopting is framed as managing doubt: people look for facts until they feel safe enough to decide. This links to the uncertainty and information definitions above.

- **Technology cluster:** distinguishable elements perceived as closely interrelated. Potential adopters' perceptions draw the boundaries. Examples are paper recycling alongside bottle and can recycling (Rogers says "presumably" these form a cluster), and the Green Revolution package (miracle seeds, fertilizer, pesticides, thicker planting), which approximately tripled yields if adopted entirely. Studying each innovation as independent is a distortion of reality (pdf 52–53).
- **Five perceived attributes** help explain differing adoption rates. Cellular phones and VCRs spread in a few years; the metric system and seat belts took decades (pdf 53).
  1. **Relative advantage:** perceived betterness over the idea it supersedes (economic, prestige, convenience, satisfaction). The greater it is, the more rapid the adoption (pdf 53–54).
  2. **Compatibility:** perceived consistency with values, past experiences and needs. Contraception where religious beliefs discourage it, and water boiling in Los Molinas, are incompatible examples. Incompatible ideas often require a new value system first, which is slow (pdf 54).
  3. **Complexity:** perceived difficulty to understand and use. Examples are germ theory and the Dvorak keyboard (pdf 54).
  4. **Trialability:** how far it can be tried on a limited basis. Ryan and Gross (1943) found that every one of their Iowa farmer respondents tried hybrid seed corn on a partial basis first (pdf 54).
  5. **Observability:** how visible results are to others. Solar water heaters cluster by block in California, while home computers are low in observability (pdf 55).
- **Conclusion:** more relative advantage, compatibility, trialability and observability, and less complexity, mean faster adoption. Past research indicates these five are the most important characteristics in explaining rate of adoption, and the first two are particularly important (pdf 55).

> *Intuition note.* All five attributes are in the eye of the adopter. An objectively better idea can still spread slowly if people do not see its advantage or cannot fit it into their lives.

- **Re-invention:** the degree to which a user changes or modifies an innovation during adoption and implementation (studied from the 1970s). Rogers recalls that in 1954 he simply ticked "adopter" for an Iowa farmer who used weed spray in unusual ways (pdf 55–56).
- Re-invention is measured as departure from the change agency's core version. Hybrid seed corn and GM corn with a "killer gene" allow little re-invention. Other innovations are flexible and are re-invented in many ways. Rogers says later chapters show an innovation diffuses more rapidly when re-inventable, and adoption is more likely to be sustained (pdf 56).

### 2. Communication channels (pdf 56–58)
- At its most elementary, diffusion needs an innovation, a unit with knowledge or experience of it, a unit without it, and a channel connecting them. The channel is the means by which messages get from one individual to another (pdf 56–57).
- **Mass media channels** are usually the most rapid and efficient way to create awareness-knowledge. **Interpersonal channels** (face-to-face) are more effective in persuasion, especially between similar people. Internet channels have become more important for certain innovations (pdf 57).
- Most people do not evaluate innovations on scientific studies. They depend mainly on subjective evaluations from near peers who have already adopted. This suggests that the heart of diffusion is modeling and imitation of previously adopting network partners (pdf 57).
- **Homophily:** how similar interacting individuals are in attributes such as beliefs, education and socioeconomic status. **Heterophily** is the opposite. People in free-choice situations tend to choose similar others. Homophily makes communication more frequent, more effective and more rewarding (pdf 57–58).
- **The tension.** Participants in diffusion are usually quite heterophilous, as when a change agent is more technically competent than clients, and this often leads to ineffective communication. But if two people are identical in technical grasp, there is no new information to exchange. Some heterophily is therefore necessary. Ideally people would be homophilous on everything except the innovation, but knowledge of an innovation is highly related to socioeconomic status and education, so they usually differ on all of these (pdf 58).

> *Intuition note.* Diffusion needs a gap in knowledge, but communication is more effective between similar people. A change agent, who is more technically competent than clients, illustrates this tension.

## Toy example (ours, using the text's figures)
Compare Los Molinas with the 10–20% take-off zone Rogers describes (pdf 50).
- Village size: 200 families (pdf 38).
- 10% of 200 = 0.10 × 200 = 20 families. 20% of 200 = 0.20 × 200 = 40 families.
- Adopters after two years: 11 families, so 11 / 200 = 0.055, or 5.5% (the text says "about 5 percent," pdf 41).
- 11 < 20, so adoption sat below the 10–20% zone. This fits Rogers's point that the rate did not reach a critical mass (pdf 41). Our arithmetic only illustrates it; Rogers gives no threshold for Los Molinas.
- Gap: at least 20 − 11 = 9 more adopter families would be needed just to reach 10%.

## Questions this raises
- How do the five attributes combine in practice, and are they equally important in every setting?
- How can a change agent find the opinion leaders and tell them apart from marginal early adopters like Mrs. B?
- If interpersonal channels persuade best but near peers are homophilous, how does a new idea cross social boundaries?
- Why do the scurvy and Dvorak cases fail to spread, and are those reasons the same as in Los Molinas?
