# The Change Agent (part 2/2): aides, credibility, opinion leaders, demonstrations, extension, centralized vs decentralized systems

## L0 — One line
Change agents succeed more when they are credible, work through opinion leaders and strengthen clients' own judgment. Diffusion systems range from expert-led (centralized) to user-led (decentralized), and each type suits different conditions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part asks how change agents can be more effective, and how diffusion systems should be organized. Rogers says that cheap, near-peer aides have more safety credibility (they are seen as trustworthy) but less competence credibility (they are seen as less expert) than professionals. Agents gain leverage by working through opinion leaders (not innovators). Demonstrations help, but experimental and exemplary demonstrations serve different goals. A long-range approach should build clients' ability to evaluate innovations. The U.S. agricultural extension service is a relatively centralized model. Decentralized systems are likely to fit local needs more closely and encourage self-reliance. They fit poorly when expertise is needed or when people feel no need. Real systems are usually hybrids, and a hybrid can be built to fit a situation.

**Prerequisites:** the adoption process (knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation); homophily/heterophily; opinion leaders, innovators and critical mass (earlier chapters).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Aides cost far less than professionals and are more homophilous (more similar) with low-status clients. They have less competence credibility (seen as less expert) but more safety credibility (seen as more trustworthy) (pdf 473–474).
- Credibility is positively related to agent success (Gen. 9-10). Commercial agents are credible mainly for how-to information, and usually not for persuasion (pdf 474–475).
- Aides who imitate professionals' dress and manner may destroy the function they were hired for: bridging the gap (heterophily) between professionals and clients (pdf 475–476).
- Working through opinion leaders saves effort and gives local sanction (Gen. 9-11). Agents who target innovators (the first adopters) may raise awareness but persuade few, and overusing opinion leaders can wear out their credibility (pdf 478–479).
- Demonstrations may raise observability (how easily others can see an innovation in use). Experimental demonstrations test an innovation; exemplary ones spread it, and they need opposite attitudes and visibility (pdf 479–480).
- Raising clients' ability to evaluate innovations is positively related to success (Gen. 9-12), but most agencies seldom reach this goal (pdf 481).
- The U.S. extension service's relative success is linked to flexibility, client participation and a research–extension link, but it is criticized for ignoring rural social problems (pdf 482–485).
- Decentralized systems are likely to fit local needs better, but ineffective innovations may spread without quality control, and an innovation people do not want will not diffuse. Hybrids are possible (pdf 487–491).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(Page references are pdf numbers; no printed numbers are visible.)

**Para-professional aides (pdf 473).** An aide is a less than fully professional change agent who contacts clients intensively. Aides cost much less per client; in Asian health programs thirty aides cost about the same as one doctor. Their main advantage is greater homophily with lower-status clients. Rogers says that, for many clients, personal acceptability is as important as technical expertise, or more important. Example: family-planning aides are usually women, who discuss contraception with female clients more easily than mostly male doctors can. Selecting aides by gender, education and acquaintance with clients reduces social distance. Rogers says aides "halve" it.

**Change agent credibility (pdf 473–474).** *Competence credibility* = perceived knowledge and expertise. *Safety credibility* = perceived trustworthiness. Aides are seen as near peers, so clients are unlikely to suspect selfish motives. Heterophilous sources are perceived as competent; homophilous sources are perceived as safe. An ideal agent balances both, for example by being homophilous in social traits but heterophilous in technical competence. An aide who has adopted the innovation may have an ideal combination. Example: peer educators in HIV prevention among commercial sex workers (CSWs). Most are former CSWs, and some still work. Program experience has shown that this experience is essential to their trustworthiness and credibility. Both groups are homophilous in socioeconomic status, since poverty drives most into sex work. They get brief training, may distribute or sell condoms, and the income from sales may be their pay. They train CSWs to negotiate condom use.
**Generalization 9-10:** agents' success is positively related to credibility in clients' eyes (pdf 474).

*Commercial agents (pdf 474–475).* Clients often see them as low in credibility, suspecting a push for overadoption to earn commissions. For agricultural innovations they matter relatively more at the knowledge and implementation stages. At implementation a client buys a small trial amount and needs how-to information. Persuasive credibility goes to near peers and noncommercial sources. Commercial sources can create awareness: about 80% of doctors named detail men and company publications as their source of knowledge about tetracycline (Coleman et al., 1966). Detail men were not credible at the persuasion and decision stages.

*Intuition note:* Trust and expertise are different things, and distance from clients helps one while closeness helps the other. Aides are less expert than professionals but are seen as more trustworthy.

**Inauthentic professionalization (pdf 475–476).** Aides may take on the dress, speech or marks of professionals. For example, HIV peer educators demand uniforms and badges. They usually identify with their supervisors but cannot get the degree. This may destroy the heterophily-bridging function. If aides are made aware of the problem, they will usually correct it.

**Baltimore Needle-Exchange Project (pdf 477).** From 1994 Valente used bar codes on free clean needles. About 200,000 needles were distributed each month, and almost as many were returned. 5,369 individuals took part in 1995–1997. Valente built a network matrix of the 5,369 individuals who received needles by the 5,369 who returned them, who were sometimes the same person. 64% of needles were distributed by 9% of users ("satellite exchangers"), who sold them for about $1 each. About 20 syringes were given per contact. The average user handled 149 syringes in two years, the typical satellite exchanger 1,046 (about seven times as many). The network showed entry points for outreach through opinion leaders.

**The use of opinion leaders (pdf 478–479).** **Generalization 9-11:** agents' success is positively related to working through opinion leaders. The reasons given:
- Scarce agent time is leveraged. In Latin America each extension worker was responsible for ten thousand farmers.
- Leaders give local sponsorship and sanction.
- After leaders adopt, it may be impossible to stop the spread.

Agents sometimes mistake innovators for leaders. Leaders have followers; innovators adopt first and are often seen as deviants. Focusing on innovators may raise awareness but persuades few. Agents can also "wear out" leaders by making them too innovative or too identified with the agent.

**Demonstrations (pdf 479–480).** Demonstrations raise observability. Examples are U.S. extension fertilizer plots, with and without fertilizer, harvested and compared with neighbors invited. Evaluations show they can be effective, especially for easily observable innovations before critical mass. They may account for as much as 10% of the U.S. federal R&D budget (Baer et al., 1977). Two functions (Myers, 1978):
- *Experimental* demonstration evaluates effectiveness under field conditions. It is successful if the evaluation is adequate, positive or negative. It has low public visibility, and managers should be healthily skeptical. Example: AIDS vaccine field trials.
- *Exemplary* demonstration facilitates diffusion. It should have high visibility, and managers should be optimistically assured.

These are often confused, or one demonstration is asked to do both. Demonstrations combine the agent's competence credibility with the demonstrator's safety credibility, and are better with a respected opinion leader. Example: OPTIONS in Ontario. Thirty-four agencies visited a similar demonstration agency. None adopted, but the demonstration moved them through the trial stage.

**Client's evaluative ability (pdf 481).** **Generalization 9-12:** agents' success is positively related to increasing clients' ability to evaluate innovations. Agents usually pursue short-range goals instead. Rogers says self-reliance should be the goal, but it is seldom reached. Evidence: in the Indonesian Norplant program, many adopters did not know it could be discontinued.

**The agricultural extension service (pdf 482–485).** It is widely admired and copied. Rogers's main evidence of success is the rise of U.S. farm productivity after World War II.
- *History:* first effective approach in Broome County, NY, 1911, funded by the Chamber of Commerce Farm Bureau, with part of the pay from a railroad (hence "county agent"). It spread with the Smith-Lever Act (1914) and World War I production needs. The American Farm Bureau Federation (1919) split from extension, but they remain friendly (pdf 482–483).
- *Scale and funding:* 3,000 employees in 1920, 17,000 now. Funding is 40% federal, 40% state, 20% county (pdf 483).
- *State specialists:* about 4,000 (given as 17% of employees). They link university research to county agents, and usually were county agents first (pdf 483–484).
- *Broader mission:* home economics agents, then 4-H agents, then marketing and consumer programs by the 1940s. Farmers fell from 35% of the U.S. population (1910) to under 2% (pdf 484).
- *Eight conclusions (pdf 485):* (1) flexible change in response to the environment; (2) client participation in needs, planning and evaluation; (3) research oriented to utilization; (4) specialists close to researchers; (5) more effective for production technology than for other content; (6) a total innovation-development system; (7) success measured by growth in funds and personnel, due to adjustment and AFBF/elite support; (8) criticized for overemphasizing production and neglecting rural social problems, some of which stem from its own success.

**Centralized vs decentralized systems (pdf 486–491).**
- *Classical model* (pdf 486): an expert source (often R&D) diffuses a uniform package, and the adopter is passive. It owes its popularity to extension success and the Ryan and Gross hybrid corn study. Schön (1971) criticized it for missing decentralized systems. There innovations come from many local sources and evolve through horizontal networks. Rogers describes lead users inventing, with high re-invention and adopters acting as their own change agents. Example: the solar-energy association in the Dominican Republic.
- *Table 9-1 (image, pdf 487),* centralized vs decentralized:

| Characteristic | Centralized | Decentralized |
|---|---|---|
| Decision power | national administrators and technical experts | widely shared; local control |
| Direction of diffusion | top-down | peer, horizontal |
| Source of innovations | formal R&D | experimentation by nonexperts, often users |
| Who decides what diffuses | top administrators and experts | local units, by informal evaluation |
| Role of clients' needs | innovation-centered (technology push) | problem-centered (technology pull) |
| Re-invention | low | high |

- *Continuum (pdf 487–489):* the dichotomy is oversimplified, and real systems are usually hybrids. In Figure 9-2 the classical model sits near the centralized end, with the extension service slightly less centralized (image, pdf 488).
- *Communication model (pdf 489):* centralized systems are linear and one-way. Decentralized ones follow a convergence model, where participants create and share information to reach mutual understanding. Decentralized systems assume users can decide soundly. That capacity is greatest when (1) all users are highly educated experts (e.g. cardiovascular surgeons) or (2) the technology is unsophisticated (e.g. home energy conservation).
- *Advantages (pdf 489):* better fit to users' needs, sense of control, minimal agent–client heterophily, motivation that may be more cost-efficient, self-reliance, public popularity.
- *Disadvantages (pdf 490):* (1) hard to apply technical expertise, and ineffective innovations may spread without quality control; (2) nonexperts lack diffusion strategies, so site visits dominate and may overload hosts, and users may lack knowledge of problems and available innovations; (3) an innovation the government wants but people do not feel a need for will not diffuse (family planning, recycling, carpooling).
- *Conclusions (pdf 491):* decentralized systems are most appropriate under certain conditions, such as innovations that need little technical expertise among users with relatively homogeneous needs. Hybrids can combine, for example, central coordination with local decisions and expert technical evaluation.

*Intuition note:* Who controls the decisions matters. When users are capable and feel the need, user control gives a good fit. When expertise or a government goal is central, a more centralized or hybrid system fits better.

**Summary (pdf 491–493).** It restates the chapter. Interventions are actions aimed at behavior change, and targeting customizes messages to audience segments. Change agents face social marginality and information overload. They have seven roles (the last is a terminal relationship). Generalizations 9-1 to 9-4 and 9-9 to 9-12 link success to eight factors. Generalizations 9-5 to 9-8 link agent contact to clients' higher SES, social participation, education and cosmopoliteness (pdf 492).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An agent can make 10 contacts. The system has 100 clients, 10 of them opinion leaders. Assume (for illustration only) that each leader who adopts persuades 4 different non-leader followers, and non-leaders persuade nobody.
- Assume every client contacted adopts. Contacting 10 leaders: 10 + 10×4 = 50 adopters.
- Contacting 10 clients who are not leaders (assuming each persuades nobody): 10 adopters.
- Leverage is 50/10 = 5 times. This is the "economy of effort" of Gen. 9-11, in made-up numbers.
