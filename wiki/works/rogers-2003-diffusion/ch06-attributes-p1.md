# Attributes of Innovations and Their Rate of Adoption (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
How fast an innovation spreads is substantially explained by how potential adopters *perceive* five of its attributes (relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability). This part covers the framework, its measurement, relative advantage in detail, and the start of compatibility.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do some innovations spread within years while others stall? Rogers argues that adopters' perceptions of an innovation's attributes, not experts' objective ratings, are one important explanation of its rate of adoption (other variables also matter). He lists five attributes and reviews how researchers measure them. This part then treats relative advantage (economic, status, overadoption, preventive innovations, incentives, mandates) and begins compatibility (values, past ideas). Cases include rap music, the failed N-Trak soil test, seat belts, miracle rice and clitoridectomy in Ghana. It matters because change agents can influence perceptions, for example by positioning, incentives or design.

**Prerequisites:** adopter categories, the innovation-decision process and opinion leaders (earlier chapters); the idea of variance explained; Likert scales and factor analysis (only named, not explained).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Rate of adoption is the relative speed of adoption in a social system, usually measured as the number of people adopting per period. Five perceived attributes explain a large share of its variance (the part of the differences in rates that they account for), but other variables also matter. Relative advantage means seen as better than what it replaces, and compatibility means seen as consistent with values, experience and needs. Complexity, trialability and observability are only named and illustrated by survey items in these pages (pdf 287–289).
- Perceptions count: an innovation's rate is affected by how adopters perceive it, not by how experts classify it (pdf 290).
- Attributes can be measured with scale items. Rogers generally advises building new items for each study. Respondent-elicited attributes (Kearns) were covered mostly by the five standard ones (pdf 290–293).
- Relative advantage is often among the strongest predictors of rate. Its subdimensions include money, status, effort and immediacy of reward (pdf 301).
- Preventive innovations (adopted now to lower the chance of a future unwanted event) generally have a slow rate of adoption, partly because their reward is delayed and uncertain and the avoided event is a "nonevent." Campaigns such as Stanford's show they can be diffused, but special efforts are needed (pdf 302–305).
- Incentives (payments given to encourage adoption) raise relative advantage but may change who adopts and lower the quality of adoption. Mandates (adoption required by a government or similar body) are another way a system pressures individuals, used where public resistance to voluntary incentives is strong (pdf 305–309).
- Compatibility with values, past ideas and needs reduces uncertainty. Incompatibility with cultural values can block adoption; with miracle rice, taste incompatibility led farmers to keep growing traditional rice for home use (pdf 309–312).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening and the rap example (pdf 285–287)
- The chapter epigraph (Thomas and Znaniecki): situations perceived as real are real in their consequences (pdf 285).
- Innovations differ in speed. About 71 percent of adult Americans adopted the Internet between 1989 and 2002; another consumer innovation may level out below 20 percent (pdf 285).
- Diffusion research has studied "people" differences much more than "innovation" differences. Treating all innovations as equivalent is called "dangerously incorrect." How an innovation is named and positioned can modify reactions (pdf 285).
- **Rap case.** Rap began around 1980 as a radical innovation. Most radio stations refused to play it, mainly because its fans were not an important audience for advertisers. Airplay drives charts, so this embargo pushed rap to the fringes (pdf 286).
- **Greenberg's reasoning:** because of its underclass origins, rap appealed to middle- and upper-class youths who wished to rebel against their parents' establishment (pdf 286).
- Rap spread via homemade cassettes and local independent labels. Later, rap radio arose in every U.S. city (pdf 287).
- Rogers' interpretation: suburban white teenagers perceived rap as compatible with the values they want to express, which are opposition to adult values and parental control (pdf 287).

### Rate of adoption and its determinants (pdf 287–289)
- **Rate of adoption:** the relative speed with which members of a social system adopt an innovation. It is usually the number adopting per specified period (e.g. a year), a numerical indicator of the steepness of the adoption curve (pdf 287).
- Five perceived attributes explain "from 49 to 87 percent" of the variance in rate (Rogers, 1995) (pdf 287). The Figure 6-1 caption says they explain "about half" (pdf 288).
- Other variables (Figure 6-1): (1) type of innovation-decision, (2) communication channels, (3) nature of the social system (norms, network interconnectedness), (4) extent of change agents' promotion efforts (pdf 287–288).
- Individual-optional decisions are generally adopted faster than organizational ones. The more persons involved in a decision, the slower the rate. Rate can be sped up by changing the decision unit so that fewer people are involved (pdf 288).
- If interpersonal channels (not mass media) create awareness-knowledge, as often for later adopters, the rate is slowed (pdf 288–289).
- Change agent effort and rate may not be direct and linear. The greatest response comes when opinion leaders adopt, usually at about 3–16 percent adoption. After a critical mass is reached, spread continues with little promotion (pdf 289).
- Little research compares the relative contribution of the five variable types. This chapter concentrates on the perceived attributes (pdf 289). Rogers states that subjective evaluations of an innovation, formed from personal experience and conveyed by interpersonal networks, drive the diffusion process and thus determine its rate of adoption (pdf 289).

*Intuition note:* Speed of spread is not a property of the product alone. It also depends on who decides, how people hear about it, and how the system is structured. Perceived attributes are only one block of causes, though a well-studied one.

### Research on the attributes (pdf 289–294)
- A standard classification scheme of attributes is an eventual goal, not yet reached. The five attributes are empirically interrelated but conceptually distinct, chosen from past work and for generality and succinctness (pdf 289).
- Individuals' perceptions, not attributes as classified by experts or change agents, affect the rate (pdf 290).
- **Measurement.**
  - Holloway (1977) factor-analyzed Likert-type items from a hundred high school principals. He found general support for the framework. However, the distinction between relative advantage and compatibility was not very clear-cut, and status conferral emerged as a sixth dimension (usually seen as a sub-dimension of relative advantage) (pdf 290).
  - Moore and Benbasat (1991) pooled 75 items, ran four rounds of expert sorting, and surveyed 540 employees in seven companies. The innovation was personal workstations. The result was 28 items for the five attributes plus a 15-item short form. Three further attributes were measured: voluntariness, image, result demonstrability (pdf 290–291).
  - Sample items: "Using a personal workstation improves the quality of work I do" (relative advantage); "Learning to use… is easy for me" (actually measures simplicity, the opposite of complexity) (pdf 291).
  - Validity check: adopters scored higher than nonadopters on relative advantage, compatibility, trialability and observability, and lower on complexity (pdf 291).
  - Rogers generally discourages reusing existing scales. Items should be created afresh for each study, because the attributes are expressed differently in each setting (pdf 291).
- **Organizations as units.** Goldman (1992) surveyed 116 March of Dimes local-chapter directors five months after a campaign launched. Compatibility with needs, simplicity, relative advantage and observability were related to adoption and implementation (pdf 292).
- **Eliciting attributes (Kearns, 1992).** Eight computer innovations were studied among 127 Pittsburgh-area municipalities. Respondents were given triplets of innovation cards and asked how two were alike and different from the third. This produced 25 respondent-generated attributes (pdf 292–293).
  - The 25 attributes explained 27 percent of the variance in rate. The five main attributes alone explained 26 percent (pdf 293).
  - Rogers concludes that the five cover the main attributes for most respondents, but scholars should stay open to others (pdf 293).
- **Postdiction versus prediction.** Generalizations from past research can be used to predict future rates ("acceptability" research). The ideal design measures attributes at $t_1$ to predict rate at $t_2$ (pdf 293–294). Three approaches: extrapolate from similar past innovations; describe a hypothetical innovation to potential adopters; study acceptability in pre-diffusion stages such as test marketing. None is ideal, and data gathered before or during adoption decisions would be more valuable (pdf 294).

### Case: an agricultural innovation that failed (pdf 295–297)
- Kremer et al. (2001) studied the N-Trak soil nitrogen test in Iowa in the 1990s. Farmers had mailed samples to distant labs and waited weeks. N-Trak ($125, Hach Company) gave immediate results, allowing late-spring side-dressing of nitrogen. It was meant to cut over-application, saving money and reducing runoff (pdf 295).
- Sales: 1,700 kits in 1990, 17 in 1999 (pdf 296).
- **Reasons, by attribute:**
  - Low relative advantage: labor-intensive at a busy season, and farm sizes grew. A farmer with 500 acres of corn needed about a thousand samples. Labs also cut costs and turnaround, reducing N-Trak's comparative advantage (pdf 296).
  - Incompatible with anhydrous ammonia, which most farmers preferred and which must be applied before planting (pdf 296).
  - Low observability: a whole crop season was needed to see results, and neighbors had little chance to observe them (pdf 297).
  - Design: farmers were not involved, so local knowledge was not considered (pdf 297).

*Intuition note:* The five attributes can explain failure as well as success. The N-Trak case shows an innovation failing on several attributes at once, partly because its designers ignored farmers' own situation.

### Relative advantage (pdf 297–309)
- **Definition:** the degree to which an innovation is perceived as better than the idea it supersedes. It is often expressed as economic profitability or social prestige (pdf 297).
- **Economic factors.**
  - Initial cost matters. PalmPilots at a few hundred dollars were seen as a bargain; 65 percent of purchasers had heard about them from another person (pdf 297–298).
  - The VCR sold for over $1,200 in 1980 and under $50 several years later (24 times cheaper by 2002). Rogers says its absolute relative advantage increased greatly. A characteristic of the innovation changed during diffusion, so a one-time cross-sectional measure may give only a partial picture (pdf 298).
- **Status.** Tarde (1903) observed that status seeking was a main reason for imitation. For certain innovations such as new clothing fashions, prestige is almost the sole benefit, and it may fade as more people adopt, which pressures for newer fashions. Fads diffuse fast and are rapidly discontinued (pdf 298).
  - Harvestore silos (from $50,000 up) are status-conferring. Experts recommend much cheaper silos (pdf 299).
  - Status motives seem more important for innovators, early adopters and early majority than for late majority and laggards. They have been understudied. Direct questions probably underestimate them (pdf 299).
- **Overadoption:** adoption by an individual when experts feel he or she should reject. Possible causes: insufficient knowledge, inability to predict consequences, and/or status conferral (pdf 299–300).
  - Rogers' main concern is objective, not subjective, rationality (pdf 300).
  - Implication: a change agent's role may include preventing "too much" adoption (pdf 300). Examples: Harvestores, CAT scanners bought without justification, over-powered home computers, and Rogers' own hand calculator purchase in the 1970s (pdf 300).
  - It can happen when one attribute is so attractive that it overrules all others (pdf 300).
- **Relative advantage and rate.**
  - Innovation diffusion is an uncertainty-reduction process, and people seek information about relative advantage (pdf 301).
  - Relative advantage is "a ratio of the expected benefits and the costs of adoption." Subdimensions include economic profitability, low initial cost, less discomfort, prestige, time and effort saved, and immediacy of reward (pdf 301).
  - **Generalization 6-1:** the relative advantage of an innovation, as perceived by members of a social system, is positively related to its rate of adoption (pdf 301).
  - Early studies used U.S. commercial farmers, for whom economic aspects were central. In Kivlin and Fliegel's study of small-scale farmers, a decrease in discomfort, but not economic profitability, was positively related to rate (pdf 301–302). Fliegel et al. (1968) found Indian (Punjabi) farmers more like small-scale Pennsylvania farmers than like large-scale U.S. farmers. They apparently attached greater importance to social approval and less to financial return (pdf 302).
- **Preventive innovations.**
  - **Definition:** a new idea adopted now to lower the probability of an unwanted future event. The reward is delayed and uncertain, and the avoided event is a "nonevent," invisible and hard to comprehend (pdf 302–303). An *incremental* innovation, by contrast, gives a near-term outcome (e.g. hybrid seed corn with a 20 percent yield gain) (pdf 302; Figure 6-2, pdf 303).
  - "Births averted" is not very meaningful to a peasant family (pdf 303).
  - Seat belts: if completely adopted, they could save 9,238 lives per year. In 2002, 73 percent of Americans buckled up, the highest rate yet attained in the U.S. but the lowest among industrialized countries. Of about thirty thousand annual crash deaths, 60 percent are unbelted (pdf 303).
  - **Nonadopters' views (as Rogers reports them):** some are risk takers, some insist on an individual right, some say belts wrinkle clothes or take time. Rogers summarizes their position as cost and effort exceeding benefits, with accident probability felt to be negligible (pdf 303).
  - Preventive innovations are more difficult to diffuse than incremental ones (pdf 304).
- **Stanford Heart Disease Prevention Program** ("1970s and 1980" as printed). It was a set of communication sub-campaigns using mass media to recruit at-risk people to small-group classes. It used formative research (pretesting messages) and positive role models. Results showed an important reduction in heart-disease risk (pdf 304–305). Rogers' conclusion: preventive innovations can be facilitated effectively, but special efforts are needed, particularly to emphasize relative advantage (pdf 305).
- **Incentives.**
  - **Definition:** direct or indirect payments in cash or kind to encourage behavior change. The main function of an adopter incentive is to increase relative advantage (pdf 305).
  - Five contrasts: adopter vs diffuser (e.g. finder's fee); individual vs system (e.g. Indonesian village incentive); positive vs negative (Singapore denied maternity leave and free hospital costs for a third child); monetary vs nonmonetary; immediate vs delayed (pdf 305–306).
  - Some incentives only encourage trial (free samples). Others are pump-priming for early adopters. U.S. solar-heating tax rebates in the 1970s were halted at 5–10 percent adoption, expecting self-generating diffusion (pdf 306–307).
  - **Rogers' (1973) conclusions from family-planning research:**
    1. Incentives increase rate. Adopter incentives raise relative advantage, diffuser incentives raise observability, and an adopter incentive can act as a cue-to-action (pdf 307).
    2. Adopter incentives lead to adoption by people different from those who would otherwise adopt. A large incentive brought in the lowest-socioeconomic-status adopters (pdf 307).
    3. Incentives increase the quantity of adopters, but the quality of adoption may be lower, and sustainability may lessen (pdf 307).
  - Incentives raise serious ethical issues needing study (pdf 307).
- **Mandates.** Where there is strong public resistance to voluntary incentives, a government may mandate adoption (pdf 308).
  - China's one-child policy led to a sharp fall in population growth, but also to other unwanted behavior (pdf 308).
  - Los Angeles' Article XV required organizations of 100+ employees to raise average occupancy to 1.5 employees per vehicle, using positive and negative incentives and fines (pdf 309).
  - Rogers: mandates are a mechanism through which the system pressures individuals to recognize relative advantage, particularly of a preventive innovation (pdf 309).

*Intuition note:* Relative advantage is a ratio of expected benefits to costs, and immediacy of reward is one of its subdimensions; the more advantage is perceived, the faster the rate. Incentives raise perceived advantage from outside, and mandates press individuals to recognize it, but adopter incentives may bring different adopters and a lower quality of adoption.

### Compatibility (pdf 309–312, part ends mid-section)
- **Definition:** the degree to which an innovation is perceived as consistent with existing values, past experiences and needs. A more compatible idea is less uncertain and more familiar. Compatibility can concern (1) sociocultural values and beliefs, (2) previously introduced ideas, (3) client needs (pdf 309).
- **Values and beliefs.**
  - Los Molinos residents saw water boiling as incompatible with their hot-cold classification. Soil conservation conflicts with U.S. farmers' production value (pdf 309–310).
  - Urban India's left-hand-unclean norm persists although washing is now easy (pdf 310).
  - **Miracle rice (IRRI):** it often tripled yields, but taste had been ignored. South Indian adopters sold it and planted traditional rice for home use. **IRRI breeders' response (1960s):** people will learn to like the taste. Forty years later, farmers still kept traditional rice, and miracle rice sold about 20 percent cheaper. IRRI has since begun breeding for taste (pdf 310–311).
  - Designs made for one culture may clash in another. IBM's bar-code reader summed to $9,999.99, which was inadequate in Italy under high inflation. Lotus 1-2-3 faced Indian number conventions (lakhs, crores; reversed comma and period) (pdf 311).
  - **Clitoridectomy.** The World Health Organization estimate is 130 million women and girls. Medical experts object because of infection, feminists because it expresses patriarchal control, and **defenders claim** it ensures chastity and raises marriage potential (pdf 311). In northern Ghana (Mensch et al., 1999), a girl found not a virgin was shamed, and the ritual marked the passage to womanhood (pdf 311–312). After the 1994 ban and a diffusion campaign, only one fourth of females aged 15–19 in the area studied had been cut by 1998. Rogers says this change, accompanied by rising female education, growing freedom from parental control and a developing money economy, led to more adolescent births to unmarried mothers, more abortion and more maternal mortality in first births. "The end of a tradition led to a new set of social problems" (pdf 312).
- **Previously introduced ideas.** Compatibility with a preceding idea can speed up or retard adoption. Old ideas are the main mental tools for judging new ones (pdf 312). In a Colombian peasant community (Fals Borda, 1960), farmers put fertilizer on top of potato seed as they had used manure, damaging the seed, and over-sprayed insecticide as if watering (pdf 312).

*Intuition note:* People understand the new through the old. If the new idea fits existing beliefs, it is easier to give meaning to. If it does not fit, adoption can be slowed, and people may read the new idea in old terms and misuse it, as the Colombian farmers did.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A village of 100 people. Innovation X is a cheap seed that gives a visible gain next season. Innovation Y is a vaccine whose benefit is avoiding a disease that may never occur anyway.

Suppose villagers perceive X as benefit 6 and cost 2, so the ratio is 6 ÷ 2 = 3. Suppose they perceive Y as benefit 6 but uncertain and distant, so they discount it to 1 against cost 2, giving 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5.

Observed adopters per year: X has 10, 20, 30, so the rate is about 20 per year (60 ÷ 3). Y has 1, 2, 3, so the rate is about 2 per year (6 ÷ 3).

These numbers are illustrative only. They show the pattern Rogers describes: a higher perceived benefit-to-cost ratio goes with a faster rate (Generalization 6-1), and delay and uncertainty lower the perceived ratio for preventive innovations.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Perceived-attribute model of adoption ↔ framing (communication theory): both claim that the audience's interpretation, not the objective content, drives the outcome.
- [metaphor] Pump-priming incentives, with adoption "self-generating" after a threshold ↔ critical mass: both describe a tipping level beyond which promotion is unneeded.
- [structural-analogy] Compatibility (new ideas judged through old ideas) ↔ Lippmann's stereotypes: familiar templates shape how new information is understood.

## Questions this raises
- If perceived attributes explain 49–87 percent of variance (or about half), what explains the rest, and how do the other four variable types compare?
- Can perceptions be changed by communication, such as positioning and naming, without changing the innovation itself?
- Why were Kearns's five attributes nearly as predictive (26 vs 27 percent) as 25 respondent-generated attributes? Does that depend on the context?
- When do incentives and mandates produce lasting adoption rather than low-quality adoption?
