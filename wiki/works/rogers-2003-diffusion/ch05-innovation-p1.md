# The Innovation-Decision Process (part 1: model, knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, re-invention)

## L0 — One line
Adopting an innovation is a process over time, in five stages, in which a person reduces uncertainty, and at the implementation stage users often change the innovation itself (re-invention).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How does an individual move from first hearing of a new idea to using it? Rogers proposes five stages: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation. This part covers the first four and re-invention. The process as a whole is essentially a way of coping with uncertainty about the new idea. Needs and awareness can influence each other. Attitudes and action can diverge (the "KAP-gap": knowledge and attitudes without matching practice). The order of the first three stages may depend on the culture ("culture-bound"). Rejection can happen at any stage. Adopters often modify innovations, and this can speed adoption and help sustainability. Confirmation, the fifth stage, is not covered in detail in this part.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an "innovation" (a new idea, practice or object) and of adopters, change agents and social systems; attitude vs. behaviour.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The innovation-decision process is a series of choices over time, not an instant act; it is mainly a way of dealing with uncertainty about newness (pdf 228).
- Five stages: knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation (pdf 229).
- Needs and awareness: research gives no clear answer on which comes first (pdf 232). *Selective exposure* (attending to messages that fit one's existing attitudes) and *selective perception* (interpreting messages through those attitudes) lend support to the view that a need usually comes first, but an innovation can also create a need (pdf 231–232).
- Three kinds of knowledge are distinguished: awareness (that the innovation exists), how-to (what is needed to use it properly) and principles (why it works) (pdf 232–233). Earlier knowers generally resemble innovators and early adopters but are not necessarily earlier adopters (pdf 234).
- Favourable attitudes do not always lead to adoption (the "KAP-gap" between knowledge/attitudes and practice, found for preventive innovations such as contraceptives); a cue-to-action, an event that turns a favourable attitude into behaviour, can sometimes close the gap (pdf 236–237).
- Small-scale trial is one way to cope with uncertainty, and a peer's trial can substitute at least in part for one's own. Rejection can occur at every stage. The knowledge–persuasion–decision order may be culture-bound (pdf 237–239).
- Implementation is the first overt behaviour change, and it still involves uncertainty (pdf 239–240).
- Re-invention is common. Generalizations 5-8 to 5-10 link it to faster adoption and sustainability, and it is not necessarily bad (pdf 241–246).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction and model (pdf 228–229)
- Definition: the process through which a decision-making unit passes from first knowledge, to attitude, to decision to adopt or reject, to implementation, to confirmation (pdf 228).
- It is essentially coping with uncertainty about a new alternative. Perceived newness is a distinctive aspect of innovation decisions (pdf 228).
- The chapter focuses on optional decisions by individuals; this is a basis for the later treatment of organizations (pdf 228).
- The notion of stages was conceptualized by Ryan and Gross (Iowa seed corn study), though not identical to today's. The typical farmer learned of the seed, sought more information, tried it on a few acres, and adopted fully several years later (pdf 228–229).
- The five stages (pdf 229):
  1. Knowledge: exposure to the innovation's existence and some understanding of how it functions.
  2. Persuasion: forming a favourable or unfavourable attitude.
  3. Decision: activities leading to a choice to adopt or reject.
  4. Implementation: putting the idea into use.
  5. Confirmation: seeking reinforcement of the decision made; the person may reverse it if exposed to conflicting messages.

*Intuition note:* Think of a funnel with feedback. People first learn, then feel, then choose, then act, then check whether they were right. Taken together, the stages are a way of coping with uncertainty about the new idea.

### The knowledge stage (pdf 230–234)
**Needs or awareness first?**
- Some observers say people are relatively passive when first exposed. Coleman and colleagues (1966) concluded that physicians learned of new drugs mainly through channels they did not seek (salespeople, journal ads). Later they actively sought information, usually from peers (pdf 230–231).
- *Selective exposure*: tending to attend to messages consistent with existing attitudes. *Selective perception*: tending to interpret messages in terms of existing attitudes (pdf 231).
- Hassinger (1959) argued that people seldom expose themselves to innovation messages unless they first feel a need, and that exposure has little effect unless the innovation is seen as relevant to their needs and consistent with their attitudes and beliefs. Rogers says there is "much" in these ideas to support Hassinger's view that need usually precedes awareness. Example: a farmer could drive past a hundred miles of hybrid corn in Iowa and never "see" it (pdf 231).
- A *need* is dissatisfaction when desires outweigh actualities. Learning that an innovation exists may create a need, so innovations can lead to needs as well as vice versa. Change agents may create needs (pdf 231–232).
- Perceived needs are not a complete explanation: people may not recognize problems, and perceived needs may differ from experts' views. Dale: we may want food and not need it, or need vitamins and fail to want them (pdf 232).
- Research gives no clear answer on which comes first. Need probably comes first for a pesticide against a new garden bug. An innovation may create the need for many others, perhaps especially consumer products such as CDs, DVDs, cell phones (pdf 232).

**Three types of knowledge** (the process is "essentially" information seeking to reduce uncertainty) (pdf 232–233):
- *Awareness-knowledge*: that an innovation exists. It may motivate seeking the other two. Such seeking is concentrated at the knowledge stage but may also occur at the persuasion and decision stages.
- *How-to knowledge*: what is needed to use it properly (how much, how). More is needed for complex innovations. Too little before trial and adoption makes rejection and discontinuance likely. Few studies deal with it, "although it must be" a fundamental variable (pdf 233).
- *Principles-knowledge*: the functioning principles (e.g., germ theory for boiling water, vaccination, latrines). Adopting without it is usually possible, but misuse is more likely and discontinuance may result (pdf 233).
- Change agents mostly work on awareness (mass media could often do this more efficiently). Rogers suggests they could perhaps play their most distinctive and important role by focusing on how-to knowledge. They usually leave principles to formal education (pdf 233).

**Early versus late knowers** (pdf 234). Earlier knowers have, compared with later knowers:
- more education (5-1) and higher social status (5-2);
- more exposure to mass media (5-3) and to interpersonal channels (5-4);
- more contact with change agents (5-5) and more social participation (5-6);
- more cosmopolite outlook (5-7).

Earlier knowers are generally similar to innovators and early adopters, but are not necessarily earlier adopters. Knowing is different from using. Attitudes often intervene between knowledge and decision. Consideration stops at knowledge if the information is not seen as relevant or knowledge is insufficient (pdf 234).

*Intuition note:* Knowing about an idea is not the same as using it. People tend to notice what fits their existing interests and attitudes, and attitudes and judgments of relevance often come between knowing and deciding.

### The persuasion stage (pdf 235–237)
- *Attitude*: a relatively enduring organization of beliefs about an object that predisposes action. Thinking at this stage is mainly affective (feeling); at the knowledge stage it was cognitive (pdf 235).
- Rogers's "persuasion" means attitude formation or change, not necessarily in the direction a source such as a change agent intends (pdf 235).
- The person becomes more involved: seeks information, judges credibility, interprets messages. Selective perception matters. Perceived relative advantage, compatibility and complexity are especially important here (pdf 235).
- *Vicarious trial*: mentally applying the idea to one's present or future situation ("What if I adopt?") (pdf 235).
- Because of uncertainty, people seek social reinforcement from peers. Mass media messages are too general to give it (pdf 235).
- *Innovation evaluation information* is sought at the persuasion and decision stages and answers the question of an innovation's advantages and disadvantages in one's own situation. Most people seek it from near peers, whose subjective opinions are more accessible and convincing, rather than from scientific evaluations (pdf 236).
- It is assumed that persuasion leads to consistent behaviour, but often attitudes and actions differ. *KAP-gap* (knowledge, attitudes, practice): a discrepancy frequently found for preventive health innovations such as contraceptives in developing nations: surveys show almost all people of childbearing age know about family planning and are favourable, but perhaps only 15–20 percent of fertile-aged parents use contraceptives (Rogers et al., 1999) (pdf 236).
- *Preventive innovation*: adopted to avoid a possible unwanted future event. The benefit is uncertain, motivation is relatively weak, and adoption is relatively slower than for nonpreventive innovations. Example: in South Africa, where more than 22 percent of adults are HIV-positive, one chance in 1,000 of infection per unprotected heterosexual contact (Singhal and Rogers, 2003); the text asks whether condom use is then likely (pdf 236).
- A *cue-to-action* can sometimes close the gap: an event that crystallizes a favourable attitude into behaviour. Examples: a pregnancy scare, incentives from a change agency, a peer's positive experience, a sibling's death from lung cancer before quitting smoking (pdf 237).

*Intuition note:* Liking an idea is not the same as doing it, especially when the benefit is only avoiding a risk that may never happen. A trigger event can tip the balance.

### The decision stage (pdf 237–239)
- *Adoption*: decision to make full use of an innovation as the best course available. *Rejection*: decision not to adopt (pdf 237).
- One way to cope with uncertainty is trial on a partial basis. Most people try before adopting, and innovations that can be divided for trial are generally adopted more rapidly. Free samples usually speed adoption. Example: 1930s Iowa seed corn salesmen gave a bag enough for about an acre, and farmers adopted on all acreage in the next few years (pdf 237–238).
- "Trial by others" (a peer's trial) can substitute at least in part, for some people and innovations. Demonstrations can speed diffusion, especially if the demonstrator is an opinion leader (pdf 238).
- Each stage is a potential rejection point (e.g., forgetting at the knowledge stage). *Discontinuance*: rejecting after having adopted. *Active rejection*: considering (including trying) and then not adopting. *Passive rejection* (nonadoption): never really considering. Past research seldom distinguished them, and rejection behaviour has received little scholarly attention, perhaps owing to the pro-innovation bias (pdf 238).
- The linear order knowledge → persuasion → decision is often assumed, but sometimes it is knowledge → decision → persuasion. Korean village: after a change agent's IUD presentation, 18 women raised hands and all went to the clinic, so an "optional" decision became almost collective through group pressure (Rogers and Kincaid, 1981). Also China's "group planning of births", and a study of Indonesian villages that suggests adoption rate rises but quality of decisions is dubious and needs high change-agent contact (pdf 238–239).
- Individualistic cultures put individual goals first; collectivistic cultures put collective goals first. Group pressure might be more acceptable in the latter. So the author says the model's sequence may be somewhat culture-bound (pdf 239).

### The implementation stage (pdf 239–240)
- Until now the process was mental. Implementation is overt behaviour change. Problems in how to use the idea arise here (pdf 239).
- It usually follows the decision directly unless held up (e.g., 2001 hybrid cars in the US: waits of up to six months) (pdf 239–240).
- Uncertainty persists: the person wants to know where to obtain the innovation, how to use it, and what operational problems to expect and how to solve them. Active information seeking usually takes place; change agents mainly give technical assistance (pdf 240).
- Problems are usually more serious for organizations: implementers often differ from decision makers, and structure may resist the innovation (pdf 240).
- The stage ends when the idea is institutionalized and loses its separate identity. For many individuals this may end the process; for others a confirmation stage follows (pdf 240).

### Re-invention (pdf 240–246)
- Early diffusion study treated adoption as exact copying. Sometimes it is (California's 1931 Fair Trade Law was copied by ten states with its three typographical errors). Often the idea changes as it diffuses (pdf 240–241).
- *Re-invention*: the degree to which a user changes or modifies an innovation during adoption and implementation. Until about the 1970s it was thought not to occur or to be rare; reports of it were treated as "noise" and adopters seen as passive. Most re-invention occurs at implementation, so it could not be studied until implementation data were gathered (pdf 241).
- Adoption measured as stated intention at the decision stage may have measured innovation as the individual expected it to happen, rather than as it actually occurred. This is an argument for measuring adoption at implementation (pdf 241).
- Invention is discovery or creation. Adoption is using an existing idea. The line blurs if an innovation is not fixed, hence "re-invention"; its opposite is "fidelity" (pdf 241–242).
- Charters and Pellegrin (1972) were the first to recognize it: "differentiated staffing" in four schools could mean widely differing things to staff, and nothing to some (pdf 242).
- Re-invention can be encouraged if inventions are designed with it in mind. Earlier organizational research assumed that adoption by one adopter looks much like adoption by another; the author says recent investigations show this assumption should be seriously questioned (pdf 242).
- Evidence (pdf 242–243):
  1. 56 percent of school adopters (National Diffusion Network) implemented only selected aspects. In 20 percent, important changes were made (Emrick et al., 1977).
  2. In about 80 percent of 111 scientific-instrument innovations, the lead user dominated the process, building a prototype (von Hippel, 1976).
  3. Of 104 adoptions by California mental health agencies, 55 involved re-invention and 49 were unchanged (Larsen and Agarwala-Rogers, 1977).
  4. About half of 53 agencies' adoptions of GBF/DIME involved at least some re-invention (Eveland et al., 1977).
  5. DARE: begun in Los Angeles in 1983; after ten years, more than 5 million fifth- and sixth-graders were taught 19 lessons by uniformed police officers. Rapid adoption is attributed to the drug problem's place on the public agenda and to re-invention. Many schools skipped the gang lesson. All adopters kept the basic idea of a uniformed officer teaching the material. Some schools re-invented DARE much more completely, keeping only certain anti-drug lessons and discarding the rest. Evaluations showed little lasting effect, and many schools discontinued it (pdf 243–244).
- **Generalization 5-8:** re-invention occurs at implementation for many innovations and adopters (pdf 244).
- **Generalization 5-9:** a higher degree of re-invention leads to a faster adoption rate. The logic: flexible innovations fit a wider range of conditions (pdf 244).
- **Generalization 5-10:** higher re-invention leads to higher sustainability, the degree to which an innovation continues to be used after a diffusion program ends. Example: adopting condom use has little impact in preventing HIV infection unless condoms continue to be used (pdf 244).
- Support: Goodman and Steckler (ten Virginia health programs; a key factor was fit with local objectives, i.e., compatibility); a New Mexico study of eight health innovations; other studies (pdf 244).
- French cancer centre (80 doctors, Couquand et al., 1997): over two years, guideline-conforming breast cancer treatment rose from 19 to 54 percent, and colon cancer adherence from 50 to 70 percent. One explanation: re-invention, and the doctors helped write the guidelines (pdf 244–245).
- **Not necessarily bad** (pdf 245–246):
  - R&D agencies may see re-invention as distortion, and some design "re-invention proofing". Change agents find performance hard to measure, because adoption rate becomes ambiguous.
  - Measurement: count elements differing from the "core" version. Core elements are the features responsible for effectiveness (Eveland et al., 1977; Kelly et al., 2000).
  - In the clean indoor air ordinances, all prevented smoking in public places and all included the core element of preventing second-hand smoke, but they differed in important ways in the sites covered. Less comprehensive ones were easier to get adopted. Later-adopting southwestern cities generally adopted more comprehensive ones.
  - Adopters generally value re-invention and may overemphasize how much they did (Rice and Rogers, 1980). Options include modification or selective rejection of components.
  - Flexibility may reduce mistakes and allow customization. Berman and Pauly (1975): re-invented educational innovations were more likely to continue and less likely to be discontinued. The page breaks off mid-sentence while describing how the innovations and the schools engaged with each other (pdf 246).

*Intuition note:* Adopters are not photocopiers. They often adjust the idea to their situation, and the text links a higher degree of re-invention to faster adoption and greater sustainability (Generalizations 5-9, 5-10).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Using the core-element method (pdf 245): suppose a school health program has 5 core elements: A, B, C, D, E. Three schools adopt it.
- School 1 keeps A, B, C, D, E: 0 of 5 differ → 0% re-invention (fidelity 100%).
- School 2 drops E and changes D: 2 of 5 differ → 2/5 = 40%.
- School 3 keeps only A and C: 3 of 5 missing → 3/5 = 60%.

All three are counted as "adopters". An adoption count of 3 would hide these differences. Measuring at implementation shows them.

## Questions this raises
- If need and awareness can each come first, how could a study tell which sequence occurred for a given person?
- How much re-invention can an innovation take before its core elements are lost and it is no longer the same innovation?
- Does the knowledge–decision–persuasion order in collectivistic settings change the quality or sustainability of adoption?
- Why do near peers outweigh scientific evaluations, and what does this mean for change agents?
