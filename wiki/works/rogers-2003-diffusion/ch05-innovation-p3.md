# Communication channels, the Bass model and the innovation-decision period

## L0 — One line
Which communication channel is relatively more important in adoption differs by stage of the innovation-decision process and by adopter category, and later adopters have a longer decision period.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers asks which communication channels move people through the innovation-decision process, and how long the process takes. Using the tetracycline study and farm studies, he finds that mass media and cosmopolite channels (links to sources outside the social system) are relatively more important for knowledge, and interpersonal and localite channels for persuasion. Earlier adopters rely relatively more on mass media and cosmopolite channels. Marketing's Bass model builds on the mass-media/interpersonal contrast to forecast adoption. Earlier adopters also pass through the process faster, and knowledge spreads faster than adoption. The Internet can mix mass and interpersonal features, as the Hotmail case shows. This bears on how change agents can speed adoption.

**Prerequisites:** the five stages (knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation, confirmation); adopter categories (innovators to laggards); the S-shaped cumulative adoption curve.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Doctors learned of tetracycline mostly from drug companies, but relied on near peers (colleagues at their own level) for evaluation; scientific evaluations alone did not convince them (pdf 267–268).
- Innovators try a new idea more tentatively than laggards. The reason given is uncertainty: innovators cannot lean on peers' experience (pdf 268–269).
- Generalization 5-13: mass media are relatively more important at knowledge, interpersonal channels at persuasion (pdf 271).
- Generalization 5-14: cosmopolite channels are relatively more important at knowledge, localite channels at persuasion. In developing nations, cosmopolite interpersonal channels perhaps partly play the role mass media play in developed nations (pdf 273).
- The Bass model forecasts aggregate adoption from media (p) and interpersonal (q) influence and market potential (m). It weights interpersonal influence much more heavily (pdf 275–276).
- Generalizations 5-15 and 5-16: earlier adopters rely relatively more on mass media and cosmopolite channels than later adopters do (pdf 277–279).
- Generalizations 5-17 and 5-18: awareness grows faster than adoption, and earlier adopters have shorter decision periods (pdf 280).
- The Internet mixes one-to-many reach with personalization. Hotmail spread through personalized messages carried by users' own e-mails (pdf 282).

"VIP" is not defined on these pages. This "variance type" research helps clarify the process by intervening in it. ### Channels in the innovation-decision process for tetracycline (pdf 267–269)
- **The case.** "Gammanym" (tetracycline) was a new antibiotic with fewer side effects, studied among Illinois doctors by Coleman et al. (1966). Within two months 15% of doctors had tried it, 50% four months later, and after seventeen months it dominated antibiotic prescriptions. Its relative advantage was so large that most doctors' peer networks gave very positive messages. The study showed the importance of interpersonal networks (pdf 267).
- **Knowledge stage.** Awareness information seldom comes from sources people actively seek. Active search is possible only after people know the idea exists and know which sources can inform them. Channel importance also depends in part on what is available to the audience (pdf 267). 80% of doctors first heard of gammanym from drug companies: 57% from detail men, 18% from mailings, 4% from house magazines, 1% from journal ads (pdf 267–268).
- **Persuasion and decision stages.** Near peers became more important and the commercial role somewhat less. Doctors judged the firms less credible than peers at this point. Scientific evaluations were communicated but did not convince them to adopt. Coleman et al. concluded that colleagues' everyday experience can substitute, at least in part, for the doctor's own testing; expert-level teaching cannot (pdf 268).
- **Evidence that peers' experience can substitute in part for one's own.** Adopters in months 1–2 (19 doctors) wrote prescriptions for an average of 1.5 patients each. Months 3–4 (22 doctors) averaged 2.0. Months 5–8 (23 doctors) averaged 2.7 (pdf 268). Iowa farmers adopting hybrid corn before 1939 planted 15% of acreage in hybrids in their first year, those adopting in 1939–1940 planted 60%, and those adopting in 1941–1942 (laggards) planted 90% (pdf 268–269).
- **Explanation.** Most innovative adopters were fully informed of the scientific evaluations, but this did not reduce their uncertainty about how the idea would work for them. They had to experiment themselves, because no one else had adopted yet. Later adopters profit from accumulated peer experience, which makes a personal trial less necessary (pdf 269).

*Intuition note:* Scientific information did not remove the doctors' and farmers' uncertainty (pdf 269). Peers' experience reduced it for later adopters. Innovators had no peers to learn from, so they tested the idea themselves, on a small scale.

### Communication channels by stages (pdf 269–273)
**Categories.** A *source* originates a message; a *channel* is the means by which it gets from source to receiver. Channels are classed as (1) interpersonal versus mass media and (2) localite versus cosmopolite (pdf 269–270).
- Mass media can reach a large audience rapidly, create knowledge and spread information, and change weakly held attitudes (pdf 270).
- Strongly held attitudes are formed and changed mainly by interpersonal channels, which are more effective against resistance or apathy. This is one reason why peers matter for later adopters and laggards (pdf 270).
- Interpersonal channels give a two-way exchange, which often lets them overcome selective exposure, selective perception and selective retention. They also persuade people to form or change strongly held attitudes (pdf 270).

**Generalization 5-13:** *Mass media channels are relatively more important at the knowledge stage, and interpersonal channels at the persuasion stage* (pdf 271).
- Sill (1958): to maximize the probability of adoption, channels must follow a time sequence from mass media to interpersonal. Copp et al. (1958) describe a sequence from mass media to groups to individuals (pdf 271).
- Using a channel inappropriate to the stage was associated with later adoption, because it delayed progress (pdf 271).
- 2,4-D weed spray (Beal and Rogers, 1960; 148 Iowa farmers): mass media were more important at knowledge. The share mentioning an interpersonal channel rose from 37% at knowledge to 63% at persuasion (pdf 271).
- Where mass media are scarce, the pattern differs. Colombian villagers used interpersonal channels heavily even at knowledge. In Bangladeshi villages mass media were seldom mentioned, and cosmopolite interpersonal channels were very important and seemed in some ways to play a similar role (pdf 271–272). Examples are a farmer at a machinery show in Des Moines or a doctor at a meeting in Chicago (pdf 272).
- Rogers with Shoemaker (1971) compared 23 innovations (mostly agricultural) in the US, Canada, India, Bangladesh and Colombia. Mass media were relatively more important at knowledge in both developed and developing nations. Media use was higher in developed nations: 52% at persuasion and 18% at decision, against 29% and 8% in developing nations. Cosmopolite interpersonal channels were especially important at knowledge in developing nations (pdf 272).
- Bolivia: Valente and Saba (1998) found that for people whose personal networks included few contraceptive adopters, a media campaign could move them to adoption. Rogers says this suggests that, under certain conditions, mass media may substitute for interpersonal communication (pdf 272).
- EndNote, with over 200,000 copies sold, diffused widely via peer networks, in part because the company bought no advertising. A diffusion study would show spread almost entirely via interpersonal channels (pdf 272–273).

**Generalization 5-14:** *Cosmopolite channels are relatively more important at the knowledge stage, and localite channels at the persuasion stage* (pdf 273). Cosmopolite channels link an individual with sources outside the social system. Interpersonal channels may be local or cosmopolite; mass media are almost entirely cosmopolite (pdf 273).
- Combining cosmopolite interpersonal and mass media channels, the meta-research gives these shares of cosmopolite channels:

| Nations | Knowledge | Persuasion |
|---|---|---|
| Developing | 81% | 58% |
| Developed | 74% | 34% |

- These data suggest that the role of mass media in developed countries is perhaps partly replaced by cosmopolite interpersonal channels (change agents, visits outside, outside visitors) in developing countries (pdf 273).

*Intuition note (our reading):* Rogers says innovations enter a system from external sources (pdf 279), which fits outside-reaching channels at the knowledge stage. These pages give no separate reason for localite channels at persuasion.

### The Bass forecasting model (pdf 273–277)
- Frank Bass proposed it in 1969 for forecasting the diffusion of new consumer products. Rogers calls it the most important single impetus to the marketing literature on diffusion. Large firms have used it, and Dekimpe states that it is the most widely used theoretical model in marketing (pdf 273–274).
- **Assumptions.** Potential adopters are influenced by mass media and interpersonal channels. Media-driven adoptions occur throughout but are concentrated early. Interpersonal-driven adoptions grow during the first half of the process, then decline, giving a bell-shaped curve (S-shaped when cumulative). Adoption in the first half is assumed symmetrical with the second half (pdf 274).
- **Contribution.** It is a predictive model, forecasting the total number of adopters per period (aggregate, not individual) from pilot launches, managerial judgment or analogous products. It also supplies a mathematical formula for the rate of adoption (pdf 274–275).
- **Parameters** (Figure 5-2): p, adopters due to media messages; q, adopters due to interpersonal channels; m, an index of market potential. The parameters give much greater weight to interpersonal communication (pdf 275–276). The key quantity to predict is the number of adopters from the time of prediction to the mean time of adoption, where the curve has an inflection point. Cumulative adopters can then be estimated from the symmetry (pdf 276). The source gives no formula in these pages.
- **Growth and limits.** Publications on the model: 13 in the 1970s, 82 in the 1980s, at least 35 since 1990. Most studies cover consumer durables, North America and Europe, and single nations. Media coefficients might differ in developing nations (pdf 275).

### Communication channels by adopter categories (pdf 277–279)
**Generalization 5-15:** *Mass media channels are relatively more important than interpersonal channels for earlier adopters than for later adopters* (pdf 277).
- When innovators adopt, almost no one in their system has experience. Later adopters have an ample store of local interpersonal experience (pdf 277).
- The author says "perhaps" earlier adopters need less interpersonal influence, because a media message is enough to push the venturesome over the threshold. Less change-oriented later adopters require stronger, more immediate influence, especially from peers (pdf 277–278).
- Figure 5-3 (2,4-D): interpersonal channels were more important at persuasion than at knowledge for all adopter categories except innovators (pdf 278).

**Generalization 5-16:** *Cosmopolite channels are relatively more important than localite channels for earlier adopters than for later adopters.* Innovations enter a system from external sources, and first adopters are more likely to depend on cosmopolite channels. Earlier adopters then act as interpersonal and localite channels for later ones. A footnote says this concerns channel use, not cosmopoliteness (Gen. 7-25) (pdf 278–279, 284).

### The innovation-decision period (pdf 279–281)
- **Definition.** It is the time for an individual or organization to pass through the process. It is usually measured from first knowledge to the decision, though strictly it should run to confirmation. That is often impractical because confirmation may last indefinitely (pdf 279).
- **Speeding diffusion.** Change agents can create knowledge earlier or shorten the time from awareness to decision. Almost all Iowa hybrid corn farmers had heard of it before more than a handful planted it, and Rogers says shortening the decision period is one of the main methods of speeding diffusion for most individuals (pdf 279).
- **Generalization 5-17:** *The rate of awareness-knowledge is more rapid than the rate of adoption.* In the weed spray study, 1.7 years separated 10% awareness from 10% adoption, and 3.1 years separated 92% awareness from 92% adoption (pdf 280).
- The average decision period was 9.0 years for hybrid corn and 2.1 years for the weed spray. Innovations with characteristics like high relative advantage and compatibility are adopted faster and have shorter periods (pdf 280).
- **Generalization 5-18:** *Earlier adopters have a shorter innovation-decision period.* Figure 5-4 gives average years for the 2,4-D weed spray:

| Category | Years |
|---|---|
| Innovators | 0.40 |
| Early adopters | 0.55 |
| Early majority | 1.14 |
| Late majority | 2.34 |
| Laggards | 4.65 |

  Innovators perhaps gain part of their position by learning earlier, but the data suggest the main reason is a shorter period (pdf 280–281).
- **Why shorter?** Innovators have more favorable attitudes, so messages face less resistance. They may also use more technically accurate sources (e.g., scientists) and trust them more, and may cope better with uncertainty and abstractions. Later adopters can observe earlier adopters' results (pdf 281).

### How the Internet is changing the process (pdf 282)
The Internet allows one-to-many reach like mass media, but e-mail can be personalized like interpersonal communication. It can largely remove the cost of communicating across space. Hotmail (launched July 4, 1996) had 12 million users eighteen months later, when it was sold to Microsoft for $400 million. A promotional line was included in every e-mail sent through Hotmail, creating an S-shaped curve of cumulatively increasing promotional messages. The message came from the person who sent the e-mail, so Rogers calls the ad highly personalized. Hotmail bought almost no mass-media advertising. Internet viruses can travel worldwide in a day or two (pdf 282).

### Summary (pdf 283–284)
- The five stages: knowledge (exposed to the innovation's existence and gains an understanding of how it functions); persuasion (forms a favorable or unfavorable attitude); decision (activities leading to a choice to adopt or reject); implementation (puts the innovation into use); confirmation (seeks reinforcement, but may reverse the decision if exposed to conflicting messages) (pdf 283).
- The summary also restates earlier-chapter topics that are not discussed on these pages: earlier versus later knowers (Gen. 5-1 to 5-7), re-invention and sustainability (5-8 to 5-10), discontinuance (5-11), and that stages exist, "although further study of this issue is needed" (5-12) (pdf 283).
- It restates Generalizations 5-13 to 5-18 as above, and the footnote on 5-16 (pdf 284).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Twenty farmers each name their main channel at two stages.
- Knowledge: 14 name mass media, 6 interpersonal. Interpersonal share = 6/20 = 30%.
- Persuasion: 8 name mass media, 12 interpersonal. Share = 12/20 = 60%.
- The share rises from 30% to 60%, the pattern of Generalization 5-13 (Beal and Rogers found 37% to 63%).

Decision periods: laggards 4.65 years against innovators 0.40 years gives 4.65 ÷ 0.40 = 11.625. So laggards took about 11.6 times as long (Figure 5-4, pdf 281).

- [structural-analogy] Interpersonal influence on adoption ↔ complex contagion: both stress reinforcement from peers for later or reluctant adopters.
