# Consequences of Innovations, part 2: Equality, Gaps and the Digital Divide

## L0 — One line
Innovations usually widen socioeconomic gaps between early and late adopters and between the high and low in status, but special efforts by diffusion agencies can narrow or at least avoid widening them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers asks who benefits from an innovation, not only how much good it brings. Typically innovators and early adopters have more resources, receive much of the change-agent contact, and profit first, so average gains can hide a growing gap. Case studies (Colombian coffee, irrigation wells in Pakistan and Bangladesh) suggest that social structure partly shapes this. A field experiment in India suggests that gaps can be narrowed, with a "ceiling effect" strategy (content the higher-status group already knows, so the lower-status group catches up). The digital divide shows a similar pattern for the Internet. This part also opens with a DDT story showing that even a dramatically effective innovation can have perverse consequences.

**Prerequisites:** adopter categories (innovators … laggards); opinion leaders; homophily; the direct/indirect and desirable/undesirable classes of consequences from part 1.

## L2 — Key ideas
- DDT saved millions of lives, but mosquito resistance and environmental criticism followed, and Soper, an absolutist, rejected using it sparingly as one tool among several; Rogers' lesson is that even dramatically effective innovations can have perverse consequences (pdf 555–556).
- Consequences have two dimensions: the average level of "Good" and how equally it is distributed (pdf 557–558).
- Gap-widening is the usual pattern: earlier adopters and the already high-status gain more (Generalizations 11-4, 11-5) (pdf 560).
- Social structure partly determines equality of consequences (Generalization 11-6); the same technology gave different results in Pakistan and Bangladesh (pdf 563).
- Gaps widen mainly because "ups" have better information access, better peer-evaluation access and more slack resources; each reason has matching strategies (pdf 564–567).
- Special efforts can narrow, or at least not widen, gaps (Generalization 11-7); the Indian TV field experiment is one piece of evidence (pdf 568).
- The digital divide shows a similar pattern (Internet users resemble earlier adopters), and may shift from an access divide to a learning or content divide (pdf 569–570).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Case: The Mosquito Killer (DDT) (pdf 553–556)
- Müller discovered DDT in the late 1930s while seeking a moth protectant; he won a Nobel Prize in 1948. For many years no practical uses were found; they came in World War II, when malaria disabled troops: 10,000 of 17,000 men of the First Marine Division were incapacitated (pdf 553).
- A 1943 duck-pond test showed DDT's residual power. It was rushed into the Pacific war; an air strike on Saipan stopped dengue fever (pdf 553).
- Fred Soper (Rockefeller Foundation) led spraying campaigns with strict discipline: mapped areas, numbered houses, sectors, inspectors, and supervisors paid a bonus for finding missed mosquitoes. Walls and ceilings were sprayed because mosquitoes alight there; the coating killed on contact for six months or more (pdf 554).
- Results: in Brazil 4,000 sprayer-inspectors eradicated *gambiae* in one year. In Sardinia, 25,000 inspectors and 256 tons of DDT over five years cut cases from 10,000 a year to four in 1950 (pdf 554). Soper then persuaded WHO to start a Global Malaria Eradication Programme. In India malaria fatalities fell to zero by the early 1960s (pdf 555).
- Problems: a few mosquitoes in any large population are resistant through random mutation, and they breed on while others die. In the Solomon Islands some mosquitoes entered, bit and left without alighting on walls (pdf 555). Countries cancelled campaigns and WHO dropped the programme in 1969. **Soper's view:** the failures came from lack of discipline; he urged heavier doses, but resistant mosquitoes flourished most where spraying was heaviest (pdf 555).
- **Carson's claim** (*Silent Spring*, 1962): DDT was used without concern for environmental consequences. The U.S. EPA banned general use in 1972 (pdf 555–556).
- Rogers' comment: Soper was an absolutist who scoffed at experts urging drainage of breeding areas and sparing use of DDT as one tool among several. Even dramatically effective innovations can have perverse consequences (pdf 556).

*Intuition note:* A success can run into problems: a few mosquitoes were resistant, and resistant ones flourished especially where spraying was heaviest. Rogers presents Soper as an absolutist who rejected using DDT sparingly as one tool among several.

### Equality in the Consequences of Innovations (pdf 556–557)
- Change agents shape consequences through whom they work with. The missionaries gave steel axes to women, children and young men, unaware of the emphasis on respect for elderly males. Contacting the poor and less educated would make benefits more equal, but change agents usually contact the better educated and higher status, so they tend to widen gaps (pdf 556).
- Consequences can also be classed as increasing or decreasing equality (pdf 556).
- Rogers: diffusion *generally* widens gaps within an audience, for three reasons (pdf 557):
  1. Innovators and early adopters have favourable attitudes, search more actively and have resources for higher-cost innovations.
  2. Change agents concentrate on them, hoping opinion leaders will pass ideas on. But network links mostly join people similar in adopter category and status, so innovations "trickle across, rather than down".
  3. Adopting sooner brings windfall profits, so early adopters get richer and later adopters' economic gain is comparatively less.
- Widening need not occur if special strategies are used. The snowmobile among the Skolt Lapps shows two dimensions: faster travel for everyone (higher average Good) and reindeer ownership concentrating in a few hands (unequal distribution) (Figure 11-1) (pdf 557).
- Figure 11-1 (pie charts): in the upper pair the total Good grows while the 10%/90% split stays the same (equality unchanged); in the lower pair the total grows and the split changes to 30%/70%, and the caption says the wealthy minority's proportion increases, "Hence there is less equality" (pdf 558). The labels alone do not make clear which slice is the wealthy minority's.

### The Communication Effects Gap (pdf 557–560)
- **First dimension:** the average change in knowledge, attitudes or adoption. Figure 11-2a: all members rise from 3 at $t_1$ to 7 at $t_2$, an average gain of 4 units (pdf 558–559).
- **Second dimension:** did the communication activity affect some individuals more than others? Figure 11-2b: Downs rise from 2 to 4 (+2); Ups rise from 4 to 10 (+6); the gap grows from 2 to 6. The Downs are absolutely better off but relatively worse off (pdf 559).
- Tichenor and colleagues (1970) proposed the paradigm: collect data at two or more times, before and after the intervention, and measure whether gaps in socioeconomic status or knowledge grew or shrank. The audience may be split by socioeconomic status, adopter category or information level ("information-rich" vs "information-poor") (pdf 559–560).

*Intuition note:* An average can rise while the gap widens. To see who gains, split the audience into segments and measure each before and after.

### Gap-Widening Consequences (pdf 560–562)
- **Generalization 11-4:** consequences usually widen the socioeconomic gap between earlier and later adopting categories. **11-5:** they usually widen the gap between segments previously high and low in socioeconomic status (pdf 560).
- Colombian coffee (Havens and Flinn, 1974; Támesis, 1963–1970): of 56 growers, 17 adopted new varieties plus fertilizers and herbicides (pdf 561).

| | Net income 1963 → 1970 (pesos) | Gain |
|---|---|---|
| Adopters (17) | 6,700 → 21,000 | 14,300 (213%) |
| Nonadopters | 4,500 → 12,000 | 7,500 (166%) |

  The gap grew from 2,200 to 9,000 pesos (pdf 561).
- Per acre (controlling for farm size): adopters 290 → 1,642 pesos (+1,352); nonadopters 222 → 632 (text says +415). Rogers concludes much of the inequality was due to the innovation (pdf 561).
- Adopters' farms grew from 19 to 33 acres; nonadopters' shrank from 8 to 6. Eleven nonadopters left farming (pdf 562).
- Why didn't others adopt? New trees take three years to produce, so credit was needed. Small campesinos lacked collateral and could not borrow. Rogers calls this a vicious circle and says the innovation worked "like a lever" widening the rich–poor gap (pdf 562).

### Social Structure and the Equality of Consequences (pdf 562–564)
- Gotsch (1972): irrigation wells cost about the same and watered 50–80 acres in both countries (pdf 562).
- Pakistan: 70% of wells bought by farmers with 25+ acres; only 4% of villagers with under 13 acres adopted. With irrigation water plus fertilizers and other chemicals, a farmer could typically raise net farm income by about 45% (pdf 563).
- Bangladesh: farms of 1–2 acres, so village cooperatives bought pumps and gave water to all members. Incomes doubled via a winter rice crop. Adoption was slower (collective rather than individual-optional decision) but benefits were much more equal (pdf 563).
- **Generalization 11-6:** social structure *partly* determines equality of consequences. When structure is already very unequal, a high-cost innovation is likely to produce even wider gaps (pdf 563).
- System-level factors (existing co-ops in Bangladesh; no credit for small growers in Colombia) largely determined who adopted. Structure is not static: a rural development agency had organized the Bangladesh co-ops just before the wells arrived, so that small farmers could adopt high-cost innovations. Organized groups can achieve collective efficacy (pdf 563–564).

*Intuition note:* The same machine gave different distributions because the surrounding social arrangements differed. Organizing people can change who gets the benefit.

### Strategies for Narrowing Gaps (pdf 564–567)
Gaps usually widen unless an agency makes special efforts. Rogers lists strategies by cause (pdf 564):

**(a) "Ups" have greater access to awareness information** (pdf 564–566):
1. Provide messages redundant or of less interest to "ups" (the *ceiling effect* strategy), so "downs" catch up. Example: Indian TV (Shingi and Mody, 1976).
2. Tailor messages to "downs" (education, beliefs, habits), e.g. more visuals. Formative research (testing while the activity is ongoing) helps. Health Web sites need at least high-school education, yet a New Mexico site for low-income rural Hispanics worked using formative research.
3. Use channels that reach "downs": in the U.S. heavy TV viewing but less print; in developing nations radio rather than print or TV.
4. Organize "downs" into small groups to build self-efficacy and collective efficacy.
5. Shift change-agent contact to late majority and laggards. They trust peers more than agents, but when contacted directly and the innovations suit them, response has often been encouraging (Röling et al., 1976).

Rogers' arithmetic: one farmer with 100 acres and 100 other farmers averaging one acre each. A ten-bushel-per-acre gain on the elite farm has the same total consequence as a ten-bushel average gain per acre won with all 100 smaller farmers (both about 1,000 bushels) (added step), but takes much less agent effort. This is why contact concentrates on early adopters (pdf 566).

**(b) "Ups" have greater access to peer evaluation** (pdf 566–567). **Trickle-down theory** says "downs" quickly follow "ups"; but "ups" talk mainly to "ups" (Röling et al.). Strategies: concentrate on opinion leaders among the disadvantaged; select change-agent aides from among the "downs" who can reach homophilous peers; form groups among "downs".

**(c) "Ups" have greater slack resources** (pdf 567). Strategies: recommend innovations appropriate for "downs" (R&D aimed at their problems); create organizations (co-ops) giving them command of resources; let them take part in planning diffusion programs; set up special agencies for "downs". With such an agency Colombian small farmers might have received credit.

### Wider Gaps Are Not Inevitable (pdf 568)
- **Generalization 11-7:** with special efforts it is possible to narrow, or at least not widen, gaps (from Shingi and Mody, 1976, and Röling et al., 1976) (pdf 568).
- Shingi and Mody (India): agricultural TV programs on wheat and potatoes were content-analysed before broadcast for 21 main information items; the programs were designed to be useful to small farmers and redundant with much of what large farmers already knew. Large farmers watched few programs, "turned off" by what they knew; small farmers watched eagerly. All had unlimited access to a community set. Interviews before and after showed the gap narrowed because of the ceiling effect. The authors concluded the gap is "by no means inevitable" (pdf 568).

### The Digital Divide (pdf 569–570)
- Definition: the gap between people advantaged and relatively disadvantaged by the Internet, within nations and between rich and developing regions (pdf 569).
- 2001 users per 1,000: North America 479; Western Europe 218; Latin America 21; Asia 17; Middle East/Africa 7; worldwide 52 (about 450 million users) (pdf 569).
- Causes: lack of money, of electricity and telecommunications, and government policies (China regulates all traffic, though this policy may be changing) (pdf 569).
- U.S. (NTIA survey, 48,000 people, 2000): 34% of Hispanics and 33% of African Americans used the Internet at home or work, versus 56% of Euro-Americans. Users resemble earlier adopters. About half of households own a computer; 94% of Americans have a telephone (pdf 569–570).
- In 2002 about 71% of U.S. adults were Internet users. As diffusion nears saturation, the divide "as we know it today" will disappear, but inequality may continue as a learning-divide, content-divide or others (pdf 570).
- Strategies: public access (cybercafés, telecenters); Web sites written at about eighth-grade level; tailored messages to late adopters and laggards, so far used only on a very limited scale (pdf 570).

### Summary (pdf 570–573)
Rogers restates: consequences are understudied because agencies stress adoption and assume positive effects, survey methods may be unsuitable, and consequences are hard to measure (pdf 571). He recaps the classification and Generalizations 11-1 to 11-3 (pdf 571–572). Three equilibrium states:
- *Stable equilibrium:* almost no change.
- *Dynamic equilibrium:* change commensurate with the system's ability to cope.
- *Disequilibrium:* change too fast to adjust.

Agents generally want dynamic equilibrium (pdf 572). Generalizations 11-4 to 11-7 are restated (pdf 572).

## Toy example (ours)
Knowledge scale 0–10; two equal-sized groups, Ups and Downs. Before: Ups 6, Downs 2, mean 4, gap 4.
- **Program A (general content):** Ups 6→9 (+3), Downs 2→3 (+1). Mean $(9+3)/2=6$, up 2. Gap $9-3=6$ (wider).
- **Program B (ceiling-effect content, mostly already known to Ups):** Ups 6→7 (+1), Downs 2→5 (+3). Mean $(7+5)/2=6$, up 2. Gap $7-5=2$ (narrower).

Both raise the average by 2 (first dimension), but they differ in the second dimension. Only a split by group shows this.

## Questions this raises
- How can we separate the effect of the innovation from pre-existing differences? Rogers' per-acre control is one try; are there better ones?
- Does the ceiling-effect strategy work where "ups" do not tune out redundant content?
- Is the digital divide really replaced by learning and content divides, as Rogers suggests may happen?
- When agencies prioritise "downs", what happens to adoption speed, as with the slower Bangladesh adoption?
