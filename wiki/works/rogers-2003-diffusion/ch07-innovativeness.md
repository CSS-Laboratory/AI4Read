# Innovativeness and Adopter Categories

## L0 — One line
Because adoption over time usually approaches a normal curve, the mean and standard deviation of adoption time can be used to split the adopters of an innovation into five standard categories, and earlier adopters generally differ from later ones in socioeconomic, personality and communication variables.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
People in a system adopt a new idea at different times. How can we describe them in a standard way? Rogers argues that adoption over time usually follows a bell-shaped curve, or an S-shaped curve when cumulated. Laying off standard deviations from the mean adoption time gives five categories: innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%). Earlier adopters generally have higher socioeconomic status, some distinctive personality traits, and more cosmopolite (outward-looking), active communication. This supports audience segmentation (different messages for each subaudience). It also shows an "innovativeness/needs paradox": those who most need an innovation tend to adopt last. The categories are ideal types (tools for comparison), not sharp groups.

**Prerequisites:** mean, standard deviation, normal curve; cumulative vs frequency distribution; basic diffusion terms (innovation-decision, change agent, opinion leader).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Innovativeness (how early a unit adopts relative to others) became the main dependent variable in diffusion research (the outcome most studies try to explain), because it is a main objective of many change agencies and indicates overt behavior change (pdf 340).
- Adoption over time is usually bell-shaped (frequency) or S-shaped (cumulative). The S-curve is innovation-specific and system-specific, and describes only successful innovations. Its shape should be tested empirically, not assumed (pdf 347–352).
- The standard classification cuts the normal curve at the mean and at one and two standard deviations. This gives five categories with 2.5, 13.5, 34, 34 and 16 percent (pdf 355–356).
- The categories are ideal types on a continuous variable. Rogers says past research shows no support for a "chasm" between categories (pdf 357).
- Each category has a dominant value and a communication role. Innovators are gatekeepers (they bring ideas in from outside), early adopters have the most opinion leadership (people ask them for advice) in most systems, and laggards are traditional, with many near isolates (pdf 357–360).
- Generalizations 7-2 to 7-26 link earlier adoption to socioeconomic, personality and communication variables; the evidence for one (7-9, dogmatism) is called not strong. Age is the exception: earlier adopters are stated to be no different from later ones, and the evidence is inconsistent (pdf 364–368).
- Different adopter categories suggest audience segmentation. Change agents' "least resistance" strategy contributes to the innovativeness/needs paradox and to widening gaps (pdf 369, 372–373).
- Networks matter: individuals with more adopters among their network partners were more likely to adopt, and a previously adopting neighbor state can facilitate a state's adoption (pdf 373–374).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pdf 340–341).** Individuals adopt in an over-time sequence. Adopter categories classify members by when they first use an idea. This is more efficient than describing each adopter separately (pdf 340). *Innovativeness* is the degree to which a unit is relatively earlier in adopting than other members. It is the bottom-line behavior of diffusion, and the main dependent variable (pdf 340).

**Case: farm innovations in a Colombian village (Saucío) (pdf 342–346).**
- Setting: the study was done in 1962 by Deutschmann and Fals Borda. It was the first diffusion study in a peasant village in Latin America, Africa or Asia. About 500 earlier studies were all from North America and Europe (pdf 342). Saucío had 71 farmers. Forty-two percent were illiterate, and 14 percent of households owned a radio (pdf 343).
- Six innovations were studied. All followed an S-curve: few adopters at first, then a critical mass, then leveling off (pdf 343). Two (chemical fertilizer, spray guns) reached almost 100 percent adoption over 30 years. Two more (concentrated feed, potato fungicide) reached 75 percent in ten years (pdf 343).
- Method: the six innovations were combined into a composite innovativeness score. Earlier adoption got greater weight. The cumulative scores were S-shaped and near normal (pdf 343).
- Categories: innovators 2 farmers, early adopters 10, early majority 23, late majority 23, laggards 13 (pdf 344).
- Findings: in Saucío and in Ohio, farm size, education, mass media exposure and opinion leadership were most highly related to innovativeness (pdf 344). Ohio average farm size ran from 339 to 128 acres, innovators to laggards. In Saucío the top innovator had 100 times the land of the least innovative farmer (pdf 344).
- Channels: diffusion in Saucío went mainly through interpersonal channels within the village. At the knowledge stage, 43 percent of reported channels were face-to-face contacts with other farmers. This seemed to slow diffusion, giving long left "tails" of five to ten years for the first two innovations (pdf 344–345).
- Trial: unlike Iowa farmers, most Saucío villagers did not try an innovation first. Recent innovations were more often tried on trial, suggesting learning (pdf 345).
- Innovators: they had higher mass media exposure than laggards (score 26 vs 4). They used mass media channels more at the knowledge stage (11% vs 2%) and used more outside-village sources (33% vs 17%) (pdf 345). They launched each innovation; then interpersonal channels could make diffusion self-sustaining (pdf 345).
- Conclusion: diffusion seemed a general model of human behavior, not limited to the U.S. and Western Europe (pdf 344).

*Intuition note.* The same variables that separate early from late adopters in Ohio also did so in a poor Andean village. But the extremes were larger in Saucío, and diffusion there ran mainly through interpersonal channels within the village (although innovators still used mass media and outside sources more than laggards did). Rogers says the study demonstrated the usefulness of the concepts of innovativeness and adopter categories (pdf 344–346).

**Classifying adopter categories on the basis of innovativeness (pdf 346).** Early researchers used many labels ("progressists", "drones", "diehards" and others). Standardization was needed to compare studies. One method based on the S-curve became dominant in the early 1960s (pdf 346).

**The S-shaped curve of adoption and normality (pdf 346–350).**
- The frequency of adopters over time is usually bell-shaped. The cumulative number is S-shaped. These are two displays of the same data (Figure 7-1, Iowa hybrid corn) (pdf 346–347).
- Why normal? Many human traits are normally distributed, so innovativeness is expected to be as well. If a social system replaces the individual in a learning curve, each adoption acts like a learning trial (pdf 347).
- Network argument: peer-network influence accumulates as knowledge and adoption spread. In a hypothetical example, the first adopter tells two others, and each of them tells two peers. The result follows a binomial expansion, which is normal-shaped over successive generations. This is similar to an unchecked epidemic (pdf 348–349).
- Limits of the example: members do not have free access to everyone (status, geography). Leveling off after half have adopted happens because non-knowers become scarce (pdf 349).
- The take-off comes when interpersonal networks activate. The part from about 10 to 20 percent adoption is "the heart of the diffusion process". After it, further diffusion is often impossible to stop (pdf 349).
- Test: Ryan and Gross used a chi-square goodness-of-fit test. Adoption deviated from the normal curve in 1935–36 (fewer adopters than expected) and in 1937 (more). This suggests early resistance, perhaps overcome once a critical mass was reached. Deviations tended to cancel over the whole process (pdf 349).
- **Generalization 7-1:** Adopter distributions follow a bell-shaped curve over time and approach normality (pdf 349–350).
- Caveats: the S-curve is innovation-specific and system-specific. It describes only successful innovations. Many fail, and an innovation adopted by few may be rejected and nose-dive through discontinuance (pdf 350).

*Intuition note.* In the author's network account (a hypothetical "each tells two" model whose assumptions are seldom found in real life), early on few people have heard of the idea, so adoption is slow. In the middle, many adopters can tell many non-adopters, so it speeds up. Near the end, almost everyone already knows, so it slows again.

**Measuring organizational innovativeness (pdf 350–352).** Organizations also show S-curves. Example: U.S. hate crime laws. California adopted first in 1978. Three years later Oregon and Washington followed, and then seven more states in the next two years. By 1995, seventeen states had not adopted (pdf 350–351; Figure 7-2). More innovative states were larger, richer and more liberal and progressive. They also tended to have neighbors that had adopted earlier (pdf 351). Spatial networks therefore explain part of state innovativeness. The laws were re-invented, as their details differed (pdf 351–352). Some innovations do not give S-curves. Possible reasons, which the text offers with "perhaps", are that the innovation is taboo or suits only some groups, such as "safe sex" for high-risk groups; the curve may then be S-shaped only for a population segment. So the shape is "an open question, to be determined empirically" (pdf 352).

**Who adopts? (pdf 352–354).** Early studies assumed that individuals, or the top official of an organization, were the adopters. A school principal's traits may have little relation to the school's innovativeness (pdf 352). In an eastern Nigeria study, the male household head knew little about the farm's innovations. His wives each ran a plot and made the decisions. So the unit of analysis became the farm and whoever decides for it (pdf 353). In David's (1998) hedgerow intercropping study in Africa, roles were split. Igbo men decided on tree planting, while women pruned the trees for fodder and killed many. In western Kenya, many farms are headed by women, who do not trim the trees because they see it as men's work. The text asks, "So who is the adopter?" (pdf 353–354).

**Adopter categorization (pdf 354–357).**
- Rogers developed the method as a doctoral student using the mean and standard deviation, and published it in 1958 (pdf 354).
- Three decisions are needed: the number of categories, the share of members in each, and the defining method (pdf 354).
- Innovativeness is relative and continuous. Partitioning it is a conceptual device, like dividing social status into classes. It simplifies but loses some information (pdf 354–355).
- Ideal categories are exhaustive, mutually exclusive, and derived from one principle (pdf 355).
- The mean ($\bar{x}$) and standard deviation (sd) of adoption time cut the normal curve (Figure 7-3, pdf 356):

| Category | Region of adoption time | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Innovators | below $\bar{x}-2\,\mathrm{sd}$ | 2.5% |
| Early adopters | $\bar{x}-2\,\mathrm{sd}$ to $\bar{x}-\mathrm{sd}$ | 13.5% |
| Early majority | $\bar{x}-\mathrm{sd}$ to $\bar{x}$ | 34% |
| Late majority | $\bar{x}$ to $\bar{x}+\mathrm{sd}$ | 34% |
| Laggards | above $\bar{x}+\mathrm{sd}$ | 16% |

(added step) The shares sum to $2.5+13.5+34+34+16=100$.
- The scheme is asymmetric: three categories left of the mean, two right. Rogers keeps it because laggards look fairly homogeneous and innovators differ from early adopters (pdf 356).
- Incomplete adoption (innovations below 100 percent use) breaks exhaustiveness. Example: 2 of 259 Iowa farmers had not adopted. A composite scale of several innovations avoids this (pdf 356).
- Rogers states that this is the most widely used method and "essentially the only" one (pdf 357).

**Adopter categories as ideal types (pdf 357).** Ideal types are abstractions designed to allow comparison. They are not averages, and exceptions exist. **Moore's claim (as reported):** some scholars claimed a discontinuity between innovators and early adopters on one side and the other categories on the other. **Rogers's reply:** past research shows no support for such a "chasm". Innovativeness, properly measured, is continuous, though there are important differences between categories (pdf 357).

**The five categories (pdf 357–360).**
- Innovators (venturesome): cosmopolite, often connected to other innovators far away. They need financial resources, technical understanding and tolerance of uncertainty and setbacks. They may not be respected locally, but they import the idea across the system's boundary (a gatekeeping role) (pdf 357–358).
- Early adopters (respect): localites, with the highest opinion leadership in most systems. They are role models and the "individual to check with". They help trigger critical mass. They adopt judiciously to keep their esteem, and their adoption works as a "stamp of approval" (pdf 358).
- Early majority (deliberate): adopt just before the average member and interact frequently with peers, but seldom lead. They are about one third of the system, with a longer innovation-decision period, and link early and late adopters (pdf 359).
- Late majority (skeptical): adopt just after the average member. Adoption may be both an economic necessity and a result of increasing peer pressure, and they wait until system norms favor the idea and uncertainty is mostly removed (pdf 359).
- Laggards (traditional): the last to adopt, with almost no opinion leadership, many of them near isolates, oriented to the past. They are suspicious of change agents. Their resistance "may be entirely rational" given limited resources (pdf 360). The label carries a negative connotation because of the pro-innovation bias. Any other term would acquire it, and "system-blame" may describe their situation more accurately (pdf 360).

**Case: Old Order Amish (pdf 361–363).**
- The Amish reject tractors, automobiles, TV, radio and schooling beyond eighth grade. Shunning punishes deviants, and about 20 percent defect. Their high birthrates more than offset this loss (pdf 361–362). Tractors are rejected to keep work for their children. Escalating land prices have threatened their way of life, and about half of adult Amish in Lancaster County now work in nonfarm jobs (pdf 362).
- They say "no" to most consumer and many agricultural innovations. But Sommers and Napier (1993; 366 farmers, three Ohio counties) found that the Amish were concerned about groundwater pollution. Their norms favor conservation, so using less fertilizer and pesticide is encouraged by the same pressures that oppose machinery (pdf 363).
- Conclusion: "relatively uninnovative in a general sense", but very innovative with ideas that fit their cultural values (pdf 363).

**Characteristics of adopter categories (pdf 363–368).** Generalizations are grouped in three headings. Most are stated in the form "earlier adopters have more X than later adopters"; exceptions (age, dogmatism, fatalism, and the hedged "may be" in 7-9) are noted.
- *Socioeconomic.*
  - 7-2: earlier adopters are stated to be no different from later adopters in age, though the evidence is inconsistent. About half the studies show none; a few show earlier adopters younger, some older (pdf 364).
  - 7-3: more years of formal education.
  - 7-4: more likely to be literate.
  - 7-5: higher social status.
  - 7-6: greater upward social mobility. They may be using adoption as one means of getting there.
  - 7-7: larger units (farms, schools, companies) (pdf 364).
  - Direction of cause: whether innovators innovate because they are richer, or are richer because they innovate, "cannot be answered solely on the basis of available cross-sectional data". Some ideas need large capital outlays, and the first adopters usually gain the greatest profits. Innovators must also absorb failures (pdf 364–365). Economics does not fully explain innovativeness, since many rich farmers are not innovators (pdf 365).
- *Personality* (little research, partly because of measurement difficulty) (pdf 365).
  - 7-8: greater empathy. This is the ability to project oneself into another's role.
  - 7-9: *may be* less dogmatic. The evidence is not strong, from only several studies.
  - 7-10: greater ability to deal with abstractions.
  - 7-11: greater rationality.
  - 7-12: more intelligence.
  - 7-13: more favorable attitude toward change.
  - 7-14: better able to cope with uncertainty and risk.
  - 7-15: more favorable attitude toward science.
  - 7-16: less fatalistic.
  - 7-17: higher aspirations (pdf 365–366).
- *Communication behavior.*
  - 7-18: more social participation.
  - 7-19: more interconnected through interpersonal networks.
  - 7-20: more cosmopolite. Examples: Iowa corn innovators traveled to Des Moines more often. Doctors who innovated attended more out-of-town meetings (pdf 366–367).
  - 7-21: more contact with change agents.
  - 7-22: more mass media exposure.
  - 7-23: more interpersonal channel exposure.
  - 7-24: more active information seeking.
  - 7-25: greater knowledge of innovations.
  - 7-26: higher opinion leadership. The strength of this link depends in part on system norms, and opinion leaders are more innovative where norms favor change (pdf 368).
- Simmel's "stranger": a member of a system who is not strongly attached to it, with a special perspective and relative freedom from its norms. Rogers says the innovator has much in common with the stranger. The innovator is a cosmopolite with weak ties to local members, which frees him or her to try untried ideas (pdf 367).
- Mini-case (Figure 7-4, 14 farmers in Collins, Iowa): the innovator adopted a weed spray in 1948 and got one opinion-leader vote, from an early adopter. That early adopter adopted in 1950 and was named by eight of the other thirteen farmers. The innovator was indirectly influential through the opinion leader (pdf 368, 370).

*Intuition note.* Early adoption goes with resources, outside contacts and openness. Innovators are well placed to bring ideas in. Early adopters, who are better connected locally, are better placed to spread them.

**Audience segmentation and adopter categories (pdf 368–369).** Most correlates are positive. Dogmatism and fatalism are negative. In traditional Colombian villages, innovators averaged thirty trips a year to cities and laggards 0.3 (pdf 368–369). *Audience segmentation* means using different channels or messages for each subaudience. It breaks a heterophilous audience into more homophilous ones. Scientific credibility may appeal to innovators but not to the late majority and laggards. Later adopters trust peers' subjective experience, passed on through interpersonal networks (pdf 369).

**Case: cell phone laggards in Hong Kong (pdf 371–372).** Few studies examine laggards. Wei and Leung's 1998 and 2000 surveys included 590 non-owners; cell phone adoption was 77 percent in late 2000 (pdf 371). First adopters were well-educated, higher-income businesspeople. As phones became cheaper, they spread to blue-collar workers, homemakers and students. Socioeconomic gaps between adopters and non-adopters shrank. Later users used phones mostly for emotional rather than business purposes (pdf 371). Laggards cited complexity, incompatibility with values (public phones are everywhere) and relative disadvantage (pdf 372).

**The innovativeness/needs paradox and the strategy of least resistance (pdf 372–373).**
- Those who most need an innovation's benefits (the less educated, less wealthy) are generally the last to adopt. This tends to widen socioeconomic gaps (pdf 372).
- Example: contraception in Third World nations. Elite families average two or three children and adopt first. Lower-status families average five or six and mostly do not adopt (pdf 372). Two reasons: poor families see many children (especially sons) as an economic asset, and change agents follow a *strategy of least resistance*, contacting the most receptive elites. Contraceptive methods also require resources and skills (pdf 372–373).
- The paradox "need not occur". A *strategy of greatest resistance* would concentrate effort on the lowest-status groups. Neglecting late majority and laggards widens gaps between the information-rich and information-poor (pdf 373).
- Rogers suggests the Internet may offer a way to overcome the paradox, through targeted messages. It has been used to reach lower-income, less-educated people with health messages (pdf 373).

**Network influences on innovativeness (pdf 373–374).** Recent work looks at network partners. Foster and Rozenzweig (1995, Indian farmers) found that neighbors' experience contributed to a farmer's innovativeness. Mohammed (2001, Tanzania, discussed in the next chapter) found that individuals with more adopters of family planning among their partners were more likely to adopt it (pdf 374). Similar results have been reported for U.S. state policies (hate crime laws, lotteries). Politicians facing uncertainty look to states with experience (pdf 374). In Putnam's Italy study, twelve laws were scored across 21 regional governments, and earlier adoption earned more points. The most innovative regions tended to influence their neighbors (pdf 374). More research on network influences is needed (pdf 374).

**Summary (pdf 375–376).** The chapter restates Generalizations 7-1 to 7-26 and the five categories with their dominant attributes: venturesome, respect, deliberate, skeptical, traditional. It concludes that the categories can be used for audience segmentation (pdf 375–376).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A system has 100 adopters. Their adoption times are roughly normal with mean $\bar{x}=10$ months and sd $=2$ months.

1. Cut points: $\bar{x}-2\,\mathrm{sd}=10-4=6$; $\bar{x}-\mathrm{sd}=10-2=8$; $\bar{x}=10$; $\bar{x}+\mathrm{sd}=10+2=12$.
2. Expected counts, using the percentages on pdf 355:
   - innovators (before month 6): 2.5
   - early adopters (months 6–8): 13.5
   - early majority (months 8–10): 34
   - late majority (months 10–12): 34
   - laggards (after month 12): 16
3. Check: $2.5+13.5+34+34+16=100$.
4. Classifying individuals: a farmer who adopts in month 7 is an early adopter (6 < 7 < 8). One who adopts in month 13 is a laggard (13 > 12).

## Concepts
- [[innovativeness]] (new) — the degree to which a unit is relatively earlier than other members in adopting new ideas; continuous and relative (pdf 340, 354–355).
- [[adopter-categories]] — five classes (innovators to laggards) defined by cutting the normal adoption-time curve with the mean and sd (pdf 355–356).
- [[diffusion-s-curve]] — cumulative adoption over time is S-shaped, with a bell-shaped frequency curve. It is innovation- and system-specific and describes only successful innovations (pdf 347, 350).
- [[critical-mass]] — the number of adopters after which, in the Saucío example, the cumulative adoption rate speeded up, and after which, perhaps, early resistance was overcome in the Iowa corn data; early adopters help trigger it (pdf 343, 349, 358).
- [[opinion-leader]] — a person others consult for advice and information. Early adopters have the most opinion leadership in most systems (pdf 358, 368).
- [[homophily-heterophily]] — segmentation turns a heterophilous audience into more homophilous subaudiences (pdf 369).
- [[ideal-type]] (new) — an abstraction from empirical observation, designed for comparison. Not an average, and exceptions exist (pdf 357).
- [[cosmopoliteness]] (new) — the degree to which an individual is oriented outside a social system (pdf 367).
- [[audience-segmentation]] (new) — using different channels or messages for each subaudience (pdf 369).
- [[innovativeness-needs-paradox]] (new) — those who most need an innovation's benefits tend to adopt last (pdf 372).
- [[knowledge-gap-hypothesis]] — the paradox and elite-focused strategies tend to widen gaps between information-rich and information-poor. Chapter 11 is cited, not stated here (pdf 372–373).

## Canonical instances
- [[saucio-colombia-study]] — diffusion model applied in a peasant village. Composite score gives five categories (pdf 342–346).
- [[ryan-gross-hybrid-corn]] — S-curve, with chi-square test and deviations from normality (pdf 347–349).
- [[hate-crime-laws-diffusion]] — S-curve of state adoption, with neighbor influence and re-invention (pdf 350–352).
- [[old-order-amish]] — rejection of most consumer and many agricultural innovations, but adoption of values-consistent ones (pdf 361–363).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Normal distribution of adoption times ↔ statistics: standard normal tail areas (2.5%, 13.5%, 34%) — categories are just fixed areas under a normal curve cut at integer sd values.
- [structural-analogy] Binomial "each tells two" spread ↔ epidemiology: unchecked infectious epidemic — the text itself makes this comparison (pdf 349).
- [structural-analogy] Innovator as Simmel's stranger ↔ network science: weak ties linking a system to outside information — the text speaks of the innovator's "weak ties" (pdf 367).
- [metaphor] Laggards' resistance as rational ↔ risk and resource constraints in economics — resistance reflects limited resources and low tolerance for failure (pdf 360).

## Questions this raises
- If the S-curve describes only successful innovations, how would one study diffusion samples that include failures or discontinuance?
- Do the 2.5/13.5/34/34/16 shares fit real data, or are they a convention imposed on continuous scores (as in Saucío, 2/10/23/23/13)?
- Why is age unrelated to innovativeness while education and status are related?
- How could change agents operate a "greatest resistance" strategy in practice, and at what cost?
