# Diffusion Networks (Rogers, ch. 8, part 2): from opinion leaders to critical mass

## L0 — One line
Rogers argues that adoption of innovations is shaped by interpersonal network links: connected people adopt earlier, neighbours cluster, weak ties bring new information, and interactive innovations need a critical mass.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers asks how network structure explains who adopts an innovation and when. He ends the opinion-leader evidence by saying that the strategy generally has robust effects in health programs. Then he uses Coleman's tetracycline study, whose findings later scholars reanalysed with somewhat different results. Further evidence comes from clustered adopters (Korean "pill villages", air conditioners, copycat crimes). He then introduces communication network analysis, Granovetter's strength-of-weak-ties theory, Bandura's social learning theory, and the critical mass for interactive technologies. The point is that diffusion is a social process: information about innovations flows especially through weak, often heterophilous (dissimilar) ties, while close ties have greater potential for influence.

**Prerequisites:** the S-shaped adoption curve; the idea of opinion leaders; homophily/heterophily; the idea of a "unit of analysis".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Across ten experiments, opinion-leader strategies generally have robust health effects, however leaders are chosen or trained. Leaders need continued retraining (pdf 403–404).
- Coleman's tetracycline study used network links as units of analysis. Network interconnectedness predicted innovativeness better than personal traits (pdf 405–407). Later reanalyses reached somewhat different conclusions (pdf 409–410).
- Interconnected doctors followed a snowballing S-curve. Isolated doctors followed a nearly straight line (pdf 407–408).
- Adopters cluster: Korean "pill/IUD villages", air conditioners, towers, copycat crimes and hijackings. This suggests network influence among neighbours (pdf 413–415).
- Weak, low-proximity ties, which are often heterophilous, carry new information (jobs, innovations). Close ties may carry stronger influence (pdf 420–421).
- People tend to link with near and similar others. This is low-effort but usually of limited value for information about innovations (pdf 422).
- Social learning theory (observational modeling) has much in common with diffusion theory. Both seek to explain behaviour change as a result of communication (pdf 422–423).
- Interactive innovations show reciprocal interdependence, and their adoption often displays a critical mass after which it self-sustains (pdf 424–425).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opinion leaders in health programs (end of the section; Table 8-2)
- Many opinion leaders had personal experience of the health problem. Examples: many of the 170 leaders in Earp's North Carolina mammography program had recovered from breast cancer; many of the 805 North Karelia leaders had had a heart attack or lost a close male relative to heart disease. Most also showed altruism (pdf 402).
- Table 8-2 lists experiments with their designs, ways of identifying leaders, and effects. The text says experiments show robust effects (pdf 402).
- Rogers says the identification procedure "undoubtedly" shapes impact "at least to a certain extent". Rogers says leaders must be constantly retrained and encouraged to keep up their influence activities: after four years, half of the 805 North Karelia leaders were inactive (pdf 403).
- Roles varied. In gay-bar experiments, leaders were role models for safer sex and wore small traffic-light buttons, and patrons approached them. In North Carolina, leaders were expected to contact women in churches, beauty parlours and similar places (pdf 403).
- Valente et al.'s school experiment compared three methods of choosing leaders. The network-partner method beat the teacher method, which beat the sociometric method, for smoking attitudes and intentions (pdf 403–404).
- **Conclusion (Rogers):** however leaders are identified or trained, the strategy generally has robust health effects (pdf 404).

*Intuition note:* Using opinion leaders generally works across many designs. Rogers says they must be retrained and encouraged to keep up their influence activities; half of the North Karelia leaders had become inactive after four years.

### Networks in the diffusion of a medical drug (Coleman et al., 1966)
- Early studies counted each person's network choices, so the individual was the unit of analysis. Coleman's team used network links as units. Rogers calls this a profound shift (pdf 405).
- Network variables were the most important predictors of innovativeness, more than age, cosmopoliteness or socioeconomic status (pdf 405).
- Tetracycline had evident relative advantage. Doctors faced uncertainty and relied on others. In the study, 15% tried it after 2 months, 50% after 6 months, and almost all after 17 months. Practically no discontinuance occurred (pdf 406).
- Innovativeness was associated with seven measures of **network interconnectedness** (degree to which units are linked by interpersonal networks) (pdf 406–407):
  1. regular hospital staff membership;
  2. more frequent staff meetings;
  3. sharing an office practice;
  4. being named as a source of information and advice;
  5. being named as a patient-case discussion partner;
  6. being named as a best friend;
  7. reciprocating links.
- Doctors with more links adopted earlier. Isolates (no sociometric choices received) adopted later. Between-doctor variables beat within-doctor variables. Friendship was the best predictor (pdf 407).
- More than half of the 46 isolate doctors had not adopted after ten months. Almost all interconnected doctors had (pdf 407).
- Coleman et al. explained this by a chain-reaction contagion process that seemed to take place in the early months. An early adopter tells two or more peers, who each may adopt and tell two or more others, and so on (pdf 407). Interconnected doctors "took off", then levelled off as nearly all adopted. Isolates followed an almost straight line, curving slightly because new adopters per period were a constant percentage of non-adopters (Figure 8-3; pdf 408).
- Coleman et al. wrote that the network impact was quick and strong for integrated doctors, slower and weaker but not absent for isolates. Valente (1995) found mean adoption times of 9.5 months for isolates and 7.9 for connected doctors (pdf 409).
- Meaning was socially constructed. Closely linked doctors interpreted the drug similarly. Three important opinion leaders influenced followers, apparently early (pdf 409).
- **Generalization 8-10:** network interconnectedness is positively related to innovativeness (pdf 409).
- **Reanalyses** (pdf 409–410):
  - Burt: similar adoption times reflect "structural equivalence" (occupying the same position in the network structure), not mainly a direct link.
  - Marsden and Podolny (event history analysis): network variables had little influence.
  - Valente (threshold model, each doctor having a resistance level that network influence gradually overcomes): journals (cosmopolite sources) plus interconnectedness explained innovativeness best. Innovativeness correlated .23 with opinion leadership.
  - Van den Bulte and Lilien: aggressive drug-company marketing, rather than networks, may explain the rapid spread.
  - Rogers' conclusion: different models and tools led to somewhat different conclusions (pdf 410).
- **Diffusion Networks (opening):** Rogers restates that the heart of diffusion is modeling and imitation of near peers' experiences. People depend mainly on the communicated experience of similar others, and these subjective evaluations flow mainly through interpersonal networks, so networks must be understood (pdf 410).

*Intuition note:* Doctors who talked to many peers heard early experiences and adopted sooner, giving a snowball curve. The reanalyses question parts of this causal story.

### Photovoltaics in the Dominican Republic (boxed case)
- In 1984 Lesnick installed a solar pump at a Jamaican school. Fifteen years later it was vandalised and the principal had left. In the Dominican Republic there were more than 8,000 adopters, among poor rural residents (pdf 411).
- Hansen lent about $600 each to three neighbours and trained them as installers. They diffused it to 27 more households. The adopters formed the Association for the Expansion of Solar Energy. It trained 150 installers, gave loans (down payment $65), and lobbied to cut the import tax from 100% to 18% (pdf 411–412).
- The association expanded to Honduras in 1999, reaching over 1,000 adopters within a year (pdf 412).
- Rogers: one main reason for success was that adopters organised a network that facilitated spread (pdf 412).

### Cluster studies
- Rogers and Kincaid studied 25 Korean villages. Some were "pill villages", others "IUD villages", one a "vasectomy village", although the national program promoted a standard menu. Such homogeneity could hardly be chance. Example: a dozen women of one village went together to get IUDs (pdf 413).
- Similar findings came from reanalysis by Kohler, Egyptian, Thai and Taiwanese studies (pdf 413).
- Village adoption rates ranged from over 50% to 10–15%. Rogers traces these differences to intravillage networks (pdf 413–414).
- Kohler: the average woman knew the method used by seven other women, mostly best friends. Of IUD users' network partners, 4.3 used the IUD, 2 the pill, and 1.7 condoms or vasectomy. Since contraceptive use is not observable, interpersonal communication must be the main means by which a woman knew of others' methods. A worry: some women undoubtedly used a method unsuited to their situation just because their partners did (pdf 414).
- Whyte: air-conditioner adopters clustered in a Philadelphia suburb. Neighbouring adopters seldom bought the identical brand; adopters told partners about air-conditioning but did not push their brand, saying all brands were pretty much alike (pdf 414).
- San Gimignano: neighbours kept building taller stone towers (pdf 415).
- Copycat crimes: 19 similar school-violence incidents in 22 months after Columbine. Here influence runs through mass media (pdf 415).
- Holden: 137 US hijacking attempts in 1968–72. Each successful extortion hijacking generated two more attempts within 45 days. This explained 85% of extortion hijackings (pdf 415).

### John Snow and cholera (boxed case)
- Rogers says diffusion and epidemics have much in common and similar mathematical models have been used for both (pdf 416).
- Snow mapped cholera deaths in Golden Square, London, 1854. Of 83 victims, all but ten lived closer to the Broad Street pump than to any other. He concluded cholera was carried by sewage-polluted water. He removed the pump handle on September 8, 1854, and deaths stopped (pdf 416–417).
- Rogers says the dot-maps "remind one" of the Korean "pill village" and adds "Perhaps" Snow should also be honoured for detecting effects of network behaviour (pdf 417).

### Communication network analysis
- A communication network is interconnected individuals linked by patterned information flows. This gives structure and predictability (pdf 418).
- Possible links grow with size: 100 members give 4,950 links, 200 give 19,900, and 1,000 give almost half a million. The source's formula is lost in the text; the numbers fit $\binom{N}{2}=N(N-1)/2$ (added step: $100\cdot 99/2=4950$). A computer is needed (pdf 418).
- Network analysis assigns people to cliques by communication proximity. This corresponds to Simmel's social distance. Example: Erdős numbers. The 485 coauthors of Erdős have number 1; Einstein had 2 (pdf 418–419).
- **Communication proximity:** degree to which two linked individuals' personal networks overlap (pdf 419).
- **Interlocking personal networks:** everyone interacts with everyone. **Radial personal networks:** contacts do not interact with each other. Radial networks are less dense and more open, so they matter for diffusion. Valente reanalysed three data sets and found radiality positively related to innovativeness (pdf 419).

### The strength-of-weak-ties theory
- Granovetter surveyed 282 Newton job changers. Most heard of jobs from heterophilous, not-close contacts. Only 17% used close friends or relatives. Example: an accountant who shared a taxi with a businessman (pdf 419–420).
- Reason: close friends seldom know much that you do not, and they usually form a clique of each other's friends. Weak ties are often bridge links between cliques. Without them, cliques would be disconnected (pdf 420).
- Weak ties are low in proximity and often heterophilous. Filipino housewives' family-planning information spread quickly within small cliques but did not reach the whole community until weak ties carried it from one clique to another (pdf 421).
- **Generalization 8-11:** information-exchange potential of links is negatively related to communication proximity and homophily. Rogers adds that perhaps there is a "strength-of-strong-ties" for interpersonal influence, and that the influence potential of ties with intimate friends is certainly stronger than that of weak ties. Closely linked peers in interlocking networks seldom exert this potential, because such networks are seldom activated by information about an innovation; information must flow in from outside to provide energy for further exchange (pdf 421).

*Intuition note:* Close friends tell you what you already know. Acquaintances connect you to other groups, so they bring new things.

### Who is linked to whom?
- **Generalization 8-12:** individuals tend to link to those close in physical distance and relatively homophilous. These are low-effort links but usually of limited informational value. Rogers says that individuals who wish to receive information effectively should break out of comfortable close links and form more heterophilous, spatially distant ones. The Internet makes this easier (pdf 422).

### Social learning theory
- Bandura's theory looks outside the individual. Observational modeling extracts essential elements of observed behaviour, so the learner can adapt it (like re-invention) (pdf 422–423).
- Verbal exchange is not necessarily required (though it often accompanies modeling). Modeling can occur through networks or media. Ideally, one learns more if the model is rewarded rather than punished (pdf 423).
- Both theories explain overt behaviour change through communication and network links (pdf 423).
- Arizona sociologists applied it to hijackings. Hamblin et al. said decisions rest partly on observed or talked-about experiences of others (pdf 423).

### Critical mass and interactive innovations
- **Critical mass:** the point after which further diffusion becomes self-sustaining. It comes from social-movement scholars (pdf 424).
- Interactive technologies (e-mail, telephone, fax) are of little use to an adopter unless the others with whom he or she wishes to communicate also adopt. The first telephone had no utility until a second adopter. Adoption is slow until critical mass, then accelerates (Figure 8-5) (pdf 424).
- **Interactivity:** degree to which participants can exchange roles and control mutual discourse (pdf 424–425).
- Noninteractive innovations show sequential interdependence. Interactive ones show reciprocal interdependence: benefits rise for all earlier and later adopters. Example: e-mail value rises with each Internet adopter (pdf 425).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
100 doctors, 10 adopters at time 0. Interconnected group (chain reaction, simplified: each adopter converts 2 non-adopters per period, capped at 100): 10 → 10 + 2×10 = 30 → 30 + 2×30 = 90 → 90 + 10 remaining = 100. Isolated group (10% of non-adopters adopt per period): non-adopters 90 → 0.10×90 = 9 new, adopters 19; 81 → 8.1, adopters 27.1; 72.9 → 7.29, adopters 34.39. The network group is at 100% after 3 periods while the isolated group has about 34%. The isolated curve is nearly straight and slows slightly, as Rogers describes (pdf 407–408).

## Questions this raises
- If reanalyses disagree (Burt, Marsden & Podolny, Van den Bulte & Lilien), what data would distinguish network influence from marketing or shared position?
- Can an easy, homophilous link carry influence but not information, and how would we measure that?
- Does mass-media "contagion" (hijackings, copycat crimes) count as network influence in the same sense as face-to-face ties?
- How is the critical mass identified in practice, other than by hindsight on the curve?
