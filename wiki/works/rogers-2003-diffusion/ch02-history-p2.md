# A History of Diffusion Research, part 2: education, public health, communication

## L0 — One line
In these studies of schools, doctors, family planning and news, Rogers reports that peer networks and opinion leaders play an important part in adoption, that mass media created awareness in the drug and Taichung studies, and that for news events the balance between media and personal channels depends on salience and situation.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part of Rogers's history asks what research in the education, public health and communication traditions found. It ends the education tradition, then covers public health (drug study, KAP surveys, Taichung, STOP AIDS) and communication (news-event diffusion). In several studies, interpersonal networks and opinion leaders (people many others name as social friends) seem to help the S-curve of cumulative adoption take off. In the drug and Taichung studies, mass media created awareness but personal contact motivated adoption. Preventive innovations generally diffuse slowly; news events spread much faster than technologies. The studies also show diffusion theory being used to plan and evaluate health interventions.

**Prerequisites:** the S-shaped adoption curve; the idea of an opinion leader; the difference between a survey and an experiment.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Carlson's Pittsburgh study added sociometric (friendship-network) data. The first adopter was an isolate (nobody talked with him), and the curve took off only after a clique (a small friend group) containing the opinion leaders adopted (pdf 105).
- Rogers judges that kindergartens spread worldwide helped by the high degree of re-invention (local adaptation) to fit national values (pdf 107–108).
- The Columbia drug study established that diffusion is a social process. Scientific evaluations were not sufficient to persuade the average doctor; peers' subjective evaluations were key (pdf 110, 113).
- Family-planning diffusion studies, including KAP surveys (surveys of knowledge, attitudes and practice), were intellectually "disappointing" (pdf 114).
- Preventive innovations (ideas adopted to lower the chance of a future unwanted event) have an unclear advantage and unobservable results, so they generally adopt slowly (pdf 114–115). KAP surveys often find a "KAP-gap": high knowledge and favorable attitudes but low practice (pdf 115).
- The Taichung field experiment (a before-and-after experiment in realistic conditions) succeeded spectacularly: home visits were essential, and interpersonal diffusion spilled into the control area (pdf 116–117).
- STOP AIDS applied opinion-leader and small-group logic, and HIV infections dropped sharply, though not all of the drop was due to it (pdf 119–120).
- News events diffuse within hours. Salience (how important people judge an event to be) and situation shape how far media or interpersonal channels dominate (pdf 121–126).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the education tradition (pdf 104)
After Paul Mort died in 1959, Teachers College at Columbia lost its dominance in educational diffusion research. More recent studies used teachers rather than school administrators as respondents. They also studied within-school as well as school-to-school diffusion. Some evaluated government diffusion initiatives, and others were graduate students' dissertations (pdf 104).

### The Diffusion of Modern Math in Pittsburgh (pdf 105–106)
- Carlson (1965) studied modern math among school administrators in Pennsylvania and West Virginia: opinion leaders in the networks, variables related to innovativeness, perceived characteristics of innovations and their rate of adoption, and the consequences of programmed instruction (pdf 105).
- In Allegheny County he interviewed each of the 38 school superintendents. He asked the year each adopted modern math and who their best friends were (pdf 105).
- The first adopter (1958) was a cosmopolite (an outward-looking traveller) and a sociometric isolate: none of the other 37 talked with him (pdf 105).
- The S-curve did not take off until 1959–1960, after a clique of six superintendents adopted. These six included the three most influential opinion leaders (pdf 105).
- Cumulative adoptions: 1 (1958), 5 (end 1959), 15 (1960), 27 (1961), 35 (1962), 38 (end 1963). That is about five years, versus fifty for kindergartens (Mort) (pdf 105).
- The initial adopter was too innovative to be an appropriate role model. Others waited until the opinion leaders in the six-member clique favored the innovation (pdf 105).
- Carlson's network focus was a step forward from Ryan and Gross, who gathered no sociometric data. Rogers says the study reminds one of the drug study (pdf 105–106).

*Intuition note:* In Carlson's data, an early adopter whom nobody talked to did not start the wave. The curve took off after a clique including the opinion leaders adopted.

### Worldwide Diffusion of the Kindergarten (pdf 107–108)
- Froebel created the kindergarten around 1850. His disciples spread it: to Western Europe and the United States by the 1870s, and everywhere, including Africa and Asia, by World War I (pdf 107).
- Diffusion was sometimes direct (from Berlin) and sometimes indirect (Japanese teachers studied in St. Louis and Chicago; China borrowed the Japanese model) (pdf 107).
- Educators "recontextualized" it (Wollons). The American version stressed patriotism and individualism, the Polish one was used to transmit Polish language and culture, and the Israeli one taught Zionist philosophy and Hebrew (pdf 107). In the U.S. women usually taught; in Japan and China men dominated (pdf 107–108).
- Rogers's judgement: the high degree of re-invention "undoubtedly" greatly aided diffusion (pdf 108).

### Public Health and Medical Sociology (pdf 108–109)
- Innovations studied are new drugs adopted by doctors, or family-planning, HIV/AIDS and other health ideas adopted by the public (pdf 108).
- Share of diffusion publications: 36 papers (7%) in 1961, 76 (7%) in 1968, 226 (7%) in 1981, 227 (7%) in 1994, about 10% in 2002 (pdf 108).
- Health gains have come from intervention programs and also, to a good degree, from spontaneous diffusion. When enough people in someone's network stop smoking, the person feels uncomfortable smoking around them. Rogers links Gladwell's "tipping point" to what he calls critical mass (pdf 108). The diffusion model is centrally involved in both spontaneous diffusion and planned interventions (pdf 109).

### The Columbia University Drug Diffusion Study (pdf 110–113)
- **Standing.** Rogers calls it the classic study of this tradition and perhaps second only to Ryan and Gross in its contribution to the diffusion paradigm. Its most noted impact was to orient research toward interpersonal networks through which subjective evaluations are exchanged. It suggested the role opinion leaders play in the takeoff of the S-curve (pdf 110).
- **Design.** Katz, Menzel and Coleman got $40,000 from Pfizer, which wanted to know whether its journal ads were influential. They turned this into a network study (pdf 110). Pilot: 33 doctors in a New England town. Main study: late 1954, four Illinois cities, on tetracycline (pseudonym "gammanym"). It had fewer side effects than earlier antibiotics (pdf 110–111).
- **Sample.** 125 doctors (85% of those in relevant specialties) named 103 more as network partners. The 228 total were 64% of all doctors in the four cities (pdf 111).
- **Measurement.** Adoption time came from prescription records, not recall. Many doctors reported adopting earlier than the records showed, possibly because only a 10% sample of records was gathered (pdf 111).
- **Parallels with hybrid corn.** When the data were gathered the researchers were not aware of the Ryan and Gross study. Innovators attended more out-of-town meetings (cosmopoliteness) and served richer patients, so socioeconomic status was positively related to innovativeness (pdf 111).
- **Opinion leaders.** Defined as doctors with three or more sociometric choices as social friends. Almost all of these opinion leaders had adopted by month 8 of 17, and about then the followers' S-curve took off. Rogers calls this point, when a critical mass adopted, a key factor in diffusion (pdf 112).
- **Social system as learning system.** Earlier adopters' experiences, passed through networks, set followers' rate. If the drug had failed, curves would have been slower or declined. Rogers compares the system to a participatory democracy casting a "consensus vote" (pdf 112).
- **Isolates.** Doctors not connected to anyone else took on average 9.5 months to adopt; nonisolates took 7.9. Rogers: being connected meant being innovative (pdf 112).
- **Information vs. persuasion.** Journals and detail men created awareness-knowledge, but scientific evaluations were not sufficient to persuade the average doctor. Peers' subjective evaluations, based on personal experience, were key in convincing the typical doctor (pdf 113).
- **Who talks to whom.** Religion and age were the main determinants of friendship links, with hometown and medical school of some importance. Professional affiliations (same hospital, clinic, office partnership) were the main reasons for links (pdf 113).
- **Conclusion.** The study showed the important role of interpersonal networks. "More than anything else," Rogers says, the social power of peers talking to peers led to adoption (pdf 113).

*Intuition note:* The doctors had plentiful scientific information. According to Rogers, what was key in convincing the typical doctor was peers' personal experience, such as a colleague saying "it works for my patients".

### Family-planning diffusion and KAP surveys (pdf 113–115)
- KAP surveys (from the 1960s) sample knowledge, attitudes and practice of family planning. Hundreds were done (pdf 114).
- Rogers's verdict: their contribution to understanding behaviour change "has been dismal" (Rogers, 1973), except Taichung. Practically they showed parents wanted fewer children, that most of the public wanted a government program, and that programs were feasible (pdf 114).
- Modifications of the classical model: incentive payments, nonprofessional change agent aides, strategies to overcome taboos (pdf 114).
- **Preventive innovation:** adopted now to lower the probability of a future unwanted event (pdf 114). Its relative advantage is not clear-cut. Prevented events cannot be observed or counted; officials estimate contraception's consequences as "births averted", which adopters cannot perceive. For these reasons such innovations generally have a relatively slow rate of adoption (pdf 114–115).
- **KAP-gap:** high K and A but low P. Mass media campaigns raised K and A, but most parents did not share the government's wish for slower population growth. They wanted children for farm labor, retirement support and family name (pdf 115). Rogers notes the gap is consistent with the hierarchy-of-effects model (Chapter 5) (pdf 115).

### The Taichung Field Experiment (pdf 116–118)
- A **field experiment** measures a dependent variable in realistic conditions at two times (benchmark and follow-up survey), with a treatment between. It lets the researcher determine the time order of treatment and outcome (pdf 116).
- Berelson and Freedman (1964) used about 2,400 neighborhoods (20–30 families each) in four conditions: (1) meetings; (2) meetings plus mailed information; (3) meetings plus a female change agent's home visit; (4) meetings plus visits to both husband and wife. All received posters (pdf 116).
- **Results:** 40% of about 10,000 eligible women adopted. Pregnancy rates fell by about 20%. 78% of contraceptives were IUDs (pdf 116–117).
- **Lessons:** home visits were essential. Posters created awareness-knowledge, while interpersonal communication motivated adoption (pdf 117).
- Interpersonal diffusion leaked into the rest of the city, the control group. This spoiled the design but may have been the most important finding (pdf 117).
- Later programs could not match Taiwan's rates. Rogers suggests the experiment perhaps bred unrealistic optimism and that experience suggests contraceptives are particularly hard to diffuse (pdf 117).
- Methodological point: a field experiment lets researchers use diffusion theory to plan interventions and evaluate them with benchmark and follow-up surveys. Chapter 8 discusses field experiments showing opinion leaders can speed up diffusion (pdf 117).
- Few diffusion studies are field experiments, because of cost and time (pdf 118).

### STOP AIDS in San Francisco (pdf 119–120)
- Rogers presents it as showing that when the diffusion model is used effectively in public health it can save lives (pdf 119).
- Mid-1980s, organized by and for gay men. It used diffusion theory and Lewin's small-group strategy, with gay outreach workers recruiting men to meetings of 10–12 (pdf 119).
- Planners assumed that reaching a critical mass of opinion leaders would let the idea spread spontaneously. Meeting attendees were asked whether they would organize and lead a future meeting (pdf 119).
- It trained 7,000 people, who reached another 30,000 of 142,000 gay men. Attendance fell in the late 1980s and the program closed (pdf 119).
- New infections per year fell from 8,000 (1983) to 650 (1985), "not all of which was due to STOP AIDS". Unprotected anal intercourse fell from 71% (1983) to 27% (1987). Deaths per year fell from 1,600 in the mid-1980s to 250 in recent years, with much of the very recent decrease since the mid-1990s due to anti-retroviral drugs (pdf 119–120).
- It began too late for almost half the population, who were already infected (pdf 120).
- Replication: Kelly and colleagues had bartenders in gay bars identify opinion leaders to train. This strategy is being evaluated in five developing nations (pdf 120).

### Communication tradition (pdf 120–126)
- Communication scholars wrote 1% of diffusion publications at the time of Rogers's 1962 book, when he did not count communication as a tradition, and about 15% now. Communication study got under way around 1960, after Shannon and Weaver (1949), as departments spread (pdf 120–121).
- **News-event diffusion:** more than sixty studies. News events have no material basis but diffuse in a generally similar fashion: an S-shaped distribution of knowers over time and comparable roles for interpersonal and mass media channels. News events spread much more rapidly (pdf 121).
- Examples: 92% of U.S. adults knew of Kennedy's shooting within an hour. Within a few hours of Challenger the stock price of Morton Thiokol fell 20%. Within three hours of September 11, almost all of 127 sampled Albuquerque adults knew (pdf 121).
- Rogers explains the speed this way: news requires only awareness-knowledge, while adopting a technology involves knowledge, persuasion, decision and implementation stages (pdf 121).
- **Deutschmann and Danielson (1960)** set the pattern. They were Stanford communication PhDs and former reporters, and they did not cite diffusion research in any other tradition (pdf 122). 70–90% of the public knew within half a day of events such as Eisenhower's stroke (pdf 123).
- **Firehouse research:** questionnaire and trained interviewers ready in advance, phone interviews starting about 24 hours after the event, samples of several hundred in three cities. Rogers likens news events to fruit flies for geneticists (pdf 123).
- **Findings:** radio, television and newspapers were each cited as first source by more respondents than interpersonal networks. Two-thirds talked about the news, and many then turned to media (especially television) to confirm it. Early knowers had more education and higher-status jobs (pdf 123–124). They concluded the diffusion process was "far more regular than we suspected" (pdf 124).
- **Salience** is the degree to which individuals see an event as important. Media signal it by bulletin status, headlines and airtime. Audiences judge celebrity, direct effect and local implications (pdf 124–125). Everyday front-page news spreads mainly via mass media; low-news-value events mainly spread by interpersonal channels among a few interested people (pdf 124).
- High salience prompts people to seek more information and tell others, often strangers. Rogers says this heterophilous talk explains why high-salience news spreads more rapidly and particularly via interpersonal channels (pdf 125).
- Salience varies by person (Catholics and the pope; Hindus and the milk-drinking statues). **Parasocial interaction** is the perceived personal relationship with a media personality. Rogers cites it as one explanation for grief over Diana, with younger women showing more (pdf 125).
- **Situation:** people at home were three times as likely to hear of Challenger from the media than people at work. Women, more often at home on weekdays, were less exposed to interpersonal communication about it (pdf 126).

*Intuition note:* The more important an event feels, the more people tell others, even strangers, so personal channels grow. Everyday front-page news mainly spreads through the media. Items of low news value mainly spread among the few people who are interested.

## Toy example (ours)
A system of 10 people: one isolate I, two leaders L1 and L2, and seven followers who each copy their leader after seeing the leader adopt.
- Year 1: only I adopts. Nobody follows him. Cumulative 1/10 = 10%.
- Year 2: L1 and L2 adopt. Cumulative 3/10 = 30%.
- Year 3: five followers adopt (they see leaders using it). Cumulative 8/10 = 80%.
- Year 4: the last two adopt. Cumulative 10/10 = 100%.

New adopters per year are 1, 2, 5, 2. The cumulative curve is slow, then steep, then flat, so it is S-shaped. The takeoff follows leader adoption, not the isolate's earlier adoption, as in Carlson's data.

## Concepts
- [[diffusion-s-curve]] — cumulative adoption over time; in Carlson's and the drug study it took off after opinion leaders adopted (pdf 105, 112)
- [[opinion-leader]] — in the drug study, a doctor with three or more sociometric friend choices (pdf 112)
- [[critical-mass]] — Rogers calls the tipping-point process critical mass (pdf 108); in the drug study, the point when a critical mass adopted and the curve took off (pdf 112)
- [[homophily-heterophily]] — Rogers says heterophilous conversations (with strangers) explain fast spread of high-salience news (pdf 125)
- [[sociometric-isolate]] (new) — person not connected to others in the network: nobody talked with the Pittsburgh first adopter (pdf 105); in the drug study, doctors not connected to anyone else adopted more slowly (pdf 112)
- [[re-invention]] (new) — local adaptation of an innovation, as with kindergartens (pdf 107)
- [[preventive-innovation]] (new) — adopted to lower the chance of a future event; generally slow adoption (pdf 114–115)
- [[kap-gap]] (new) — knowledge and attitude advance more than practice (pdf 115)
- [[field-experiment]] (new) — pre/post measurement with a treatment in a natural setting (pdf 116)
- [[firehouse-research]] (new) — pre-planned rapid data collection after breaking news (pdf 123)
- [[news-salience]] (new) — perceived importance of a news event; shapes channel use and speed (pdf 124)
- [[parasocial-interaction]] (new) — perceived personal relationship with a media personality (pdf 125)

## Questions this raises
- Taichung found that home visits were essential and that diffusion spread to the control area; how can the effect of peers be separated from the effect of the program?
- Can STOP AIDS's effect be separated from other causes of the 1983–87 decline?
- Does re-invention always help diffusion, or only when it fits local values?
- How would the news-diffusion findings change in today's media environment?
