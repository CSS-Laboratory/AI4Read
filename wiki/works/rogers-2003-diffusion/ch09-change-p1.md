# The Change Agent (part 1 of 2)

## L0 — One line
Change agents link experts and clients, and their success is positively related to effort, client orientation, empathy and homophily, and campaigns can succeed if they are well designed. Yet they tend to reach the clients who need them least.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers asks what change agents do and why some succeed. A change agent influences clients' innovation-decisions in a direction a change agency considers desirable. Agents sit between two worlds, so they face marginality and role conflict. Rogers gives an ideal sequence of seven roles and says reality may be quite different, as the Norplant case in Indonesia suggests. Success is linked to contact effort, client orientation, fit with felt needs, empathy and homophily. Cases show how targeting and campaigns work (Pumwari, St. Lucia, Egypt) and fail (Cincinnati). Agents contact high-status clients most, so they help least those who most need help.

**Prerequisites:** the Chapter 5 innovation-decision stages; homophily/heterophily; opinion leaders; the S-curve of adoption; adopter categories.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A change agent influences clients' innovation-decisions in a direction the change agency wants. Agents usually promote adoption but may also slow it (pdf 451–452).
- Targeting means customizing a program to its audience. Tailoring is the extreme case, with one message fitted to one individual. A targeted Nairobi program prevented an estimated 6,000–10,000 infections a year. Rogers says similar services to 1,000 randomly chosen men, with the same condom use, would have averted only 80 (pdf 451, 453).
- Agents bridge expert and client systems and may be relatively heterophilous from both. This heterophily gap creates role conflict and marginality. Agents must also deal with information overload (pdf 454–455).
- Rogers gives seven ideal roles, from creating a need to a terminal relationship. Norplant in Indonesia shows a trade-off between the number of adopters and the quality of their decisions (pdf 455–460).
- Rogers states Generalizations 9-1 to 9-4 and 9-9. Success is positively related to effort, client orientation, compatibility with needs, empathy and homophily (pdf 460–472).
- Campaigns can work if they use formative research, specific goals, audience segmentation and messages that trigger peer talk. Cincinnati failed, and St. Lucia and Egypt succeeded (pdf 466–470).
- Contact with agents rises with clients' status, participation, education and cosmopoliteness. The result is a circle in which change agents help least those most in need (pdf 470–471).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening case and definition
**Pumwari, Nairobi.**
- The program served about 1,000 commercial sex workers (CSWs). About 80% were HIV-positive in 1985, when it began (pdf 451).
- Peer educators did outreach through one-to-one contacts and baraza (group meetings). Free condoms, a free STD clinic, counseling and six-monthly checkups were provided (pdf 451).
- It prevented an estimated 6,000–10,000 infections a year, at about $10 per infection prevented (pdf 451).
- If 1,000 randomly chosen Nairobi men had received similar services with the same condom use, only 80 infections a year would have been averted (Altman, 1997) (pdf 451).
- Rogers draws the lesson that intervening "upstream" is advantageous. Prevention is also cheaper and more humane than treating the infected (pdf 451).

**Definition.** A *change agent* influences clients' innovation-decisions in a direction a change agency considers desirable (pdf 451–452).

*Intuition note:* The same services give more benefit where risk is concentrated. Rogers's comparison suggests that choosing the audience can change a program's impact greatly.

### Targeting
- In 1993 Rogers and colleagues studied San Francisco's 212 HIV prevention programs. Each was aimed at a specific group, such as Thai massage-parlor CSWs or the roughly 150 Deadheads. Members of the target group usually ran them (pdf 452).
- An *intervention* is actions with a coherent objective to bring about behavior change and produce identifiable outcomes (pdf 452).
- The CDC provided at least part of the funding for many programs. Rogers grants that there was some duplication and waste. The benefit was cultural sensitivity (pdf 452–453).
- *Targeting* is customizing the design and delivery of a communication program to the characteristics of its intended audience (pdf 453).
- Cultural sensitivity is helped by formative research, peer educators and audience advice. STOP AIDS was designed and run by gay men for gay men (pdf 453).
- *Tailoring* is the ultimate targeting. A message goes to one individual, often chosen by computer to fit that person's stage of change (pdf 453).
- *Effectiveness* is the degree to which a program fulfills its objectives. *Cost-effectiveness* is the cost per unit of behavior change. For peer-educator programs with CSWs it is estimated at $8–12 per infection prevented (Jha et al., 2001) (pdf 453).
- *Uniqueness* is the degree to which a homophilous audience differs from the larger population. High-risk groups are perceived as unique and may be stigmatized, for example in India (pdf 453–454).
- Peer outreach workers are homophilous with these groups. Being nonjudgmental is an essential quality (pdf 454).

### Change agents as linkers
- Teachers, consultants, extension agents and salespeople all link a resource system (expertise) to a client system. Feedback must flow back through the agent to the agency (pdf 454).
- Agents are needed because of social and technical chasms. Their expertise is also a barrier to direct communication (pdf 454).
- Agents are heterophilous from clients and may be relatively heterophilous from the agency too. This creates role conflict, and the agent is "a marginal figure with one foot in each of two worlds" (pdf 454).
- *Information overload* is a state in which excessive communication inputs cannot be processed, leading to breakdown. By understanding clients' needs, the agent can pass on only relevant information (pdf 455).

### The sequence of change agent roles
Rogers lists seven roles (pdf 455–456):
1. Develop a need for change. The agent points out alternatives, dramatizes problems, and may help create needs.
2. Establish an information exchange relationship. The agent needs credibility, competence, trustworthiness and empathy. Clients often accept the agent before the innovation. Peer outreach workers first build a personal relationship, which may take weeks or months. In HIV peer outreach, repeated contacts (perhaps a dozen or more) are usually necessary to change an individual's behavior (pdf 456).
3. Diagnose problems, viewing the situation from the client's perspective (pdf 456).
4. Create an intent to change (pdf 456).
5. Translate intent into action. Near-peer networks matter most at the persuasion and decision stages. The agent usually works indirectly, through opinion leaders (pdf 456).
6. Stabilize adoption and prevent discontinuance, using reinforcing messages at the implementation or confirmation stage (pdf 456).
7. Achieve a terminal relationship. Clients move from reliance to self-reliance, so the agent puts himself or herself "out of business" (pdf 456).

Rogers calls this sequence an ideal, and reality may differ (pdf 457).

### Case: coercion in Norplant safaris, Indonesia
- By the usual measure, rate of adoption, the campaign was a huge success. Indonesia had 3.6 million adopters in 1998, more than any other country. Officials from 86 nations visited (pdf 458).
- At the micro level, Rogers reports some coercion, little counseling, and active discouragement of discontinuance (pdf 458).
- Norplant is six tubelets inserted in the forearm. They release progestin and prevent pregnancy for five years (pdf 458).
- *Safaris* were intensive local campaigns that usually lasted about six weeks, with police, politicians and military taking part. They rested on the minister's idea of "guided democracy". Of West Java adopters, 73% adopted during a safari (pdf 459).
- Providers justified this by saying they knew more than potential adopters. Rogers says this top-down approach contradicts several recommended agent rules (pdf 459).
- Counseling was shortchanged. Women were not told about side effects or that removal was possible. Norplant discontinuation was one fourth the rate for IUDs and pills. Discontinuance was lower among women who did not know it was possible, and many removal requests were denied (pdf 459).
- Weak women's organizations did not counterbalance the "superenthusiast" officials. The Population Council, which developed Norplant, acted as a champion rather than as a change agency concerned with quality (pdf 459–460).
- Rogers draws a general lesson. Every agent and agency faces a trade-off between the number of adopters and the quality of adoption decisions (pdf 460).

*Intuition note:* A high adoption count can hide poor decisions. Rogers therefore suggests a need for micro-level study of agent–client interaction at the time of adoption.

### Factors in change agent success
**Effort (Generalization 9-1).** Success is positively related to the extent of agent effort in contacting clients. Success is usually measured as rate of adoption. Alternatives are the desired consequences of adoption, or the quality of decisions (pdf 460–461).
- Evidence comes from North Karelia, Finland (Korhonen et al., 1999). Doctors' and nurses' advice to stop smoking was effective and credible. Their influence was limited because they spent little time on it, since preventive work paid less than treating "more serious" problems (pdf 461).
- Contact amount is not the sole explanation. In Stone's (1952) Michigan study, adoption followed agent days early on. After about 30% adoption, agents' effort fell but farmers kept adopting at an almost constant rate. Rogers explains this by opinion leaders adopting and a critical mass being reached. The agent can then begin to retire for that innovation (pdf 461–462).

**Client orientation (9-2).** Success is positively related to a client orientation rather than a change agency orientation. Client-oriented agents are more feedback-minded and have closer rapport, higher credibility and a basis in clients' needs (pdf 462).

**Compatibility with needs (9-3).** Success is positively related to the degree to which a program is compatible with clients' needs (pdf 462).
- An Indian village got funds for an irrigation well, but wanted drinking water. It built the well in the village center and did not irrigate (pdf 462–463).
- In a Southeast Asian self-help program (Niehoff, 1964), more than half the projects were Buddhist temples, which the government had not expected or desired (pdf 463).
- Agents should adapt to felt needs but not give up their role in shaping those needs (pdf 463).

**Sustainability.** This is the degree to which a program continues after the agency's initial resources end. For a program to continue over the long term, the innovation must be highly compatible with clients' needs and resources, and clients must feel it is "theirs" (pdf 464).
- "Chicken" Davis introduced caged Western chickens, with imported feed and chicks, in eastern Nigeria over three years. The program spread rapidly and farmers profited. A few weeks after his departure an epidemic killed every imported bird. The wild village chickens were immune. Rogers asks what mistakes Davis made and whether he should have attended to indigenous knowledge (pdf 464–465).

**Empathy (9-4).** *Empathy* is the degree to which a person can put himself or herself in another's role. Success is positively related to empathy with clients (pdf 465).
- A three-day Nigerian course taught clinic staff to greet, listen, make eye contact and smile. It was checked with clinic records and "mystery clients". Quality of adoption rose, and adopters' satisfaction was passed on to others (Kim et al., 1992) (pdf 465).
- A Ghana field experiment also used mystery clients. More empathy and a more positive relationship contributed to greater success (Huntington et al., 1990) (pdf 465–466).
- Rogers contrasts these cases with coercive Indonesia (pdf 466).

### Communication campaigns
**Cincinnati UN campaign (Star and Hughes, 1950).**
- The campaign used 60,000 pieces of literature, 2,800 speeches and 60 radio spots a week for six months. It targeted groups known to be poorly informed (pdf 466).
- It reached the younger and better educated instead. People who knew anything about the UN rose only from 70% to 72% (pdf 466).
- *Selective exposure* (attending to messages consistent with prior attitudes) is Rogers's explanation for the missed audience. The slogan was also abstract. A local resident said he or she never found out what it means (pdf 466).

**Conditions for success.** After years of believing campaigns had only minimal effects, scholars began to realize that campaigns could succeed if carried out more effectively (Mendelsohn, 1973). Four strategies are listed (pdf 467):
1. Formative research, meaning study of the audience and messages to plan the campaign.
2. Specific but reasonable goals.
3. Audience segmentation, meaning division of a heterogeneous mass audience into homogeneous segments.
4. Mass media messages designed to trigger interpersonal network communication.

A campaign is purposive, aimed at a large audience, time-limited and an organized set of activities (Rogers and Storey, 1988) (pdf 467).

**St. Lucia, *Apwe Plezi*.**
- This was a radio soap opera using entertainment-education (an educational idea inside an entertainment message). It was planned with focus groups and written by St. Lucian scriptwriters. It encouraged marriage, family planning and protecting the parrot (pdf 467–468).
- It reached about one third of adults. Of listeners, 14% adopted a family-planning method because of it. Even nonlisteners knew "Catapult" condoms, which were introduced only through the program. Rogers reads this as listeners talking to nonlisteners (pdf 468).
- Rogers says this peer communication was decisive in clinching decisions. The campaign worked through formative research, pretested lively messages and peer talk (pdf 468).

### The ORT campaign in Egypt
- Before the 1983 launch, 130,000 infants a year died from diarrhea-related dehydration, 70 per 1,000 under age two. The goal was a 25% reduction over five years (pdf 469).
- Results: mortality halved within two years and fell 70% in five. Awareness reached 100% and oral rehydration salt (ORS) adoption reached 96%, from 1% before (pdf 469).
- The audience was rural women, 80% illiterate, so print was ruled out. Fifteen one-minute TV spots built on an earlier entertainment-education approach. Formative research chose the Arabic word *gafaf* for dehydration (pdf 469).
- Pretesting led to replacing a male comedian with the actress Karima Moktar, who was perceived as credible. Each spot ended with a jingle, and the expression *Edilo mahloul* ("Don't worry, there is a solution") became part of everyday life (pdf 469–470).
- 80% of respondents named the spots as main source. ORS cost 6 cents, or was free from 3,000 depot holders, each under a respected opinion leader (pdf 470).
- The campaign ran 1983–1991. Rogers attributes its success to long duration, formative research, and entertainment-education spots combined with opinion leader/depot holders (pdf 470).

### Homophily and change agent contact
- Agents differ from clients but have most contact with clients like themselves (pdf 470).
- Generalizations 9-5 to 9-8: contact with change agents is positively related to clients' higher socioeconomic status (9-5), greater social participation (9-6), higher formal education (9-7) and cosmopoliteness (9-8) (pdf 470–471).
- Rogers reads these as meaning that communication is more effective, and more rewarding for the agent, when homophily is higher (pdf 471).
- Brazilian farmers (n = 1,307), agent contacts per year (Rogers et al., 1970) (pdf 471):

| Adopter category | Contacts per year |
|---|---|
| Innovators | 20 |
| Early adopters | 15 |
| Early majority | 12 |
| Late majority | 5 |
| Laggards | 3 |

- Across fifteen variables and about 4,000 farmers in three countries, innovativeness was the variable most highly related to agent contact, even with other variables controlled. Socioeconomic status was highly related to contact in Brazil, Nigeria and India (pdf 471).
- The text names three related variables: socioeconomic status, change agent contact and innovativeness. Rogers concludes that change agents help least those who most need help (pdf 471).

*Intuition note:* Easier, more rewarding communication with similar (higher-status) clients encourages agents to contact them. Rogers calls the interrelated variables a circle.

### Contact with lower-status clients
- Why agents neglect needier clients (pdf 472):
  - The innovativeness/needs paradox: communication is easier with homophilous elite clients.
  - The heterophily gap impedes communication.
  - Lower-status clients may distrust agents from establishment institutions.
  - They often lack the resources to adopt.
  - Agents hold a self-fulfilling stereotype that lower-status clients do not respond.
- Proposed remedy: choose agents as much like clients as possible, for example para-professional aides. **Generalization 9-9:** success is positively related to the agent's homophily with clients (pdf 472).
- STOP AIDS speakers were young gay opinion leaders who matched their audience. They were usually HIV-positive and so seen as highly credible. The text ends mid-sentence here (pdf 472).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two groups of 1,000 people get the same program, costing $10,000 each. It cuts infections by the same 50% in both groups.
- Group A (high risk): 20% baseline yearly risk gives 0.20 × 1,000 = 200 infections. The program prevents 0.5 × 200 = 100, so the cost is $10,000 / 100 = $100 per infection prevented.
- Group B (low risk): 2% baseline gives 0.02 × 1,000 = 20 infections. The program prevents 0.5 × 20 = 10, so the cost is $10,000 / 10 = $1,000 per infection prevented.

The same service is ten times more cost-effective when aimed at the high-risk group. This is the logic of targeting.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Status → contact → innovativeness circle ↔ feedback: a loop of mutually reinforcing variables keeps inequality in place.
- [structural-analogy] Cincinnati's missed audience ↔ selective exposure: self-selection of who hears a message defeats targeting by need.
- [metaphor] Agent as "marginal figure with one foot in each of two worlds" ↔ a bridge or boundary role between systems.

## Questions this raises
- How can agencies measure the quality of adoption decisions, not only counts, and make agents accountable for it?
- Is the 30% adoption point in Stone's study a general threshold for withdrawing agents, or specific to that case?
- How do agents balance shaping needs against respecting felt needs, as with the Indian well?
- Do para-professional aides solve the heterophily gap, or do they create new role conflicts?
