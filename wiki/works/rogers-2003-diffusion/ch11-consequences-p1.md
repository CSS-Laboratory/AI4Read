# Consequences of Innovations (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
Adopting an innovation brings consequences, desirable and undesirable, that are often indirect and unanticipated; change agents seldom predict them and usually cannot separate the undesirable from the desirable ones.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rogers asks what happens after adoption. Diffusion research has mostly studied who adopts and has neglected consequences. Consequences are hard to study and to predict. Rogers shows this with cases: snowmobiles among the Skolt Lapps, steel axes among the Yir Yoront, oral rehydration therapy (ORT) and the Amish. He classifies consequences on three dimensions: desirable/undesirable, direct/indirect, anticipated/unanticipated. Generalizations 11-1 to 11-3 say that effects usually cannot be separated, that the "bad" and the "good" types usually cluster together, and that change agents more easily foresee form and function than meaning. Most change agents aim for dynamic equilibrium, a rate of change the system can absorb. So adoption alone cannot always be called "success".

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an innovation, adopters, and change agents (people who promote an innovation to a client system); no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Consequences are the ultimate end of diffusion, yet little studied. Evidence is mostly "soft" case studies, so generalizing is hard (pdf 532, 536–537).
- The pro-innovation bias: change agents often, and change agencies tacitly, assume that adoption brings mainly benefits. That assumption is not always valid (pdf 532, 536).
- Measuring consequences is hard. They usually unfold over extended periods, users are often not fully aware of them, judgments are almost unavoidably subjective and value-laden, and effects are often mixed up (confounded) with other causes (pdf 537–538).
- Consequences vary on three dimensions: desirable/undesirable, direct/indirect, anticipated/unanticipated (pdf 539).
- Generalization 11-1: effects usually cannot be managed so as to separate desirable from undesirable consequences. The Amish act on this principle (pdf 541–542).
- Generalization 11-2: undesirable, indirect and unanticipated consequences usually go together, as do desirable, direct and anticipated ones (pdf 547, 549).
- Generalization 11-3: change agents more easily anticipate an innovation's form and function than its meaning (form = physical appearance, function = contribution to the way of life, meaning = users' subjective perception) (pdf 549–550).
- Early adopters often gain windfall profits (special economic gains of the first adopters), and new ideas usually widen the gap between earlier and later adopters (pdf 540–541).
- The long-range goal of most change agents is dynamic equilibrium (change at a rate the system can cope with), not disequilibrium (change too fast to adjust to). The optimum rate of change is hard to judge (pdf 552).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pdf 532)
Consequences are changes in an individual or social system resulting from adopting or rejecting an innovation. Rogers can classify them but cannot predict when and how they occur. Long-term unpredictability is one type of uncertainty in diffusion. Change agents often assume mainly beneficial results (pro-innovation bias). Ideally, they should be able to predict advantages and disadvantages beforehand, but this is seldom done and often cannot be done.

### Case: Snowmobile revolution in the Arctic (pdf 533–536)
- **Method.** Anthropologist Pertti Pelto lived among the Skolt Lapps from 1958, before snowmobiles arrived in 1962. He returned repeatedly over a decade, using participant observation, interviews and a key informant. He argued that snowmobiles belong to a class of innovations that shift energy from local, autonomous sources to dependence on external ones (pdf 533).
- **Before.** Herding reindeer was the livelihood. Society was egalitarian, with about equal herds, and reindeer were central to the culture (pdf 533–534).
- **Adoption.** The first Ski-Doo was displayed in 1961. Adoption was very fast: 3 snowmobiles in year two, 5 more next, then 8, then 16 in 1966–67. By 1971 almost all 72 households had at least one (pdf 534).
- **Direct gain.** The round trip to buy supplies in Norway fell from three days by sled to five hours (pdf 534).
- **Consequences.** Noise and smell drove the reindeer wild and fewer calves were born. Many animals were also slaughtered to pay for machines. A machine cost about $1,000, and gas and repairs about $425 a year. The average herd per household fell from 52 to 12 in 1971. About two thirds of households quit herding, and most became unemployed. One early-adopting family owned one third of all reindeer by 1971 (pdf 534–535).
- **Why no resistance?** Pelto suggests that the Lapps could not foresee the outcomes at any point. Society was individualistic, and the first adopters (wealthier, younger) gained advantages, so initial adoption could not be prevented (pdf 535).
- **Later.** Motorcycles and helicopters followed. More slaughtered reindeer have stomach ulcers (pdf 536).

*Intuition note:* A useful innovation (speed) badly damaged the reindeer herding that the Lapps valued most. Pelto suggests they could not foresee this, and that once the first adopters gained, initial adoption could not be prevented.

### Studying consequences (pdf 536–538)
- Future research should ask "What are the effects of adopting innovations?" Innovativeness then becomes a predictor of consequences (pdf 536).
- Reasons for few studies: (1) change agencies, which sponsor research, overemphasize adoption and assume positive consequences; (2) diffusion researchers rely on one-shot surveys, which suit consequences poorly (pdf 536).
- Case studies give idiosyncratic data that are hard to generalize. Consequences unfold over years, so long-range designs are needed (pdf 537).
- Panel studies (interviews before and after) and pilot field experiments can give quantitative data. That data can support predictive generalizations, not just a "postmortem" (pdf 537).
- Measurement problems:
  - Users are often unaware of all consequences, so reports from respondents often lead to incomplete and misleading conclusions (pdf 537).
  - Judgments are subjective. Cultural relativism says each culture should be judged by its own circumstances, as with India's sacred cows, whose manure is useful (pdf 537–538).
  - Effects are confounded with other causes, such as droughts. Unanticipated consequences are hard to classify because the original aims may be concealed by later rationalizations (pdf 538).

### Classification of consequences (pdf 539)
Three dimensions: (1) desirable vs undesirable, (2) direct vs indirect, (3) anticipated vs unanticipated.

#### Desirable vs undesirable (pdf 539–542)
- Desirable consequences are functional effects. Undesirable ones are dysfunctional effects. Rejecters can also be affected, for example by a widening gap (the Internet's digital divide). Snowmobiles hurt almost everyone, though a few Lapps got rich. Some system qualities, such as family bonds, should not be destroyed (pdf 539).
- An innovation may be functional for a system but not for certain individuals. The Green Revolution brought higher yields, incomes and lower food prices. It also brought fewer farmers, migration to slums, unemployment, and in some countries political instability. Many individuals profited, yet the Green Revolution led to unequal conditions for the system as a whole, so whether the consequences are desirable depends on whether individuals or the whole system is the point of reference (pdf 540).
- **Windfall profits** are a special advantage of first adopters. Their unit costs are lower and their added output barely moves the price. When everyone adopts, the price falls. Innovators also risk "windfall losses" (pdf 540).
  - Pocket calculators: the 1971 model cost $249 and did four functions. A year later a four-function model cost $100, then $50, and within a decade under $10 (pdf 540).
  - The author's VCR cost $2,000 in 1980 and cost $100 by 1990. It was also a Betamax, later replaced by another standard (pdf 540–541).
  - Iowa hybrid corn (Gross 1942, reanalyzed by Rogers 1962): innovators of the late 1920s earned almost $2,500 more than 1941 laggards. The reasons were a temporarily higher price, more acreage (124 vs 70 acres) and more years of higher yields (pdf 541).
  - Usually new ideas make the rich richer and the poor poorer (pdf 541).
- **Generalization 11-1:** the effects of an innovation usually cannot be managed so as to separate the desirable from the undesirable consequences (pdf 541).
- The Old Order Amish forgo tractors, cars and electricity to avoid dependence on outsiders, less farm labor and pressure for bigger farms. In Lancaster County, high land prices now push young people into urban jobs, and some leave. Their future is uncertain, but Rogers says adherence to inseparability has served them well (pdf 541–542).

*Intuition note:* Benefits and harms often come in one package (Generalization 11-1 says they usually cannot be separated). The Amish refuse the package and, Rogers says, this has served them well, although their future is uncertain.

#### Direct vs indirect (pdf 542–546)
- Direct consequences occur in immediate response to adoption. Indirect consequences result from the direct ones ("consequences of consequences") (pdf 542).
- Madagascar wet rice (Linton and Kardiner 1952): a nomadic dry-rice tribe moved to irrigated rice. Land ownership, status differences, nuclear families replacing clans, and changes in tribal government followed (pdf 543).
- ORT case (pdf 544–546):
  - Before the 1980s about 5 million children a year died of diarrhea-related causes, about 30 percent of infant deaths. Powdered formula, not used as directed, contributed (pdf 544).
  - Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) is one bottle cap of salt and eight of sugar in three bottles of clean water. Reversing the ratio can kill, and ORT does not cure the infection (pdf 544).
  - Campaign problems: a wordless poster was sometimes misread, so some programs used foil packets. Traditional beliefs in Honduras and The Gambia clashed with the concept of dehydration. Most users did not understand how ORT worked (pdf 545).
  - By the mid-1990s almost every developing nation had an effective ORT campaign. Infant mortality fell and populations rose. Family planning was relatively ineffective in many of the poorest countries (pdf 545–546).
  - Rogers suggests that indirect consequences are often especially hard to plan for, as they are often unanticipated (pdf 546).

#### Anticipated vs unanticipated (pdf 546–547)
- Anticipated consequences are recognized and intended (manifest). Unanticipated ones are neither intended nor recognized (latent). Snowmobile speed was anticipated, the reindeer decline was not (pdf 546).
- A system is "like a bowl of marbles": move one element and the others change (pdf 546).
- Uncertainty about consequences motivates information seeking, especially through peers. It can be reduced but never completely removed. Adopters are often able to get adequate information from peers about desirable, direct, anticipated consequences. Unanticipated ones are by definition unknown at adoption (pdf 547).
- **Generalization 11-2:** the undesirable, indirect and unanticipated consequences usually go together, as do the desirable, direct and anticipated (pdf 547).

### Case: Steel axes for the Yir Yoront (pdf 548–549)
- Sharp (1952) studied them by participant observation. The stone ax symbolized masculinity and respect for elders. Only men owned stone axes, though women and children were the principal users. Heads were obtained through trade at seasonal fiestas (pdf 548).
- Missionaries gave out steel axes equally to men, women and children. Young men adopted more readily than elders, who distrusted the missionaries. Elders had to borrow from social inferiors. Status relationships and trade ties broke down, and interest in the fiestas declined (pdf 548–549).
- Men prostituted daughters and wives for the use of others' axes. Spare time went to sleep (pdf 548–549).

### Form, function and meaning (pdf 549–550)
- Form: directly observable physical appearance and substance. Function: contribution to the system's way of life. Meaning: subjective, often unconscious perception by members (pdf 549–550).
- **Linton (1936):** meaning is much less susceptible to diffusion than form or function. A receiving culture attaches new meanings to borrowed elements (pdf 550).
- Missionaries grasped form and function but not meaning. Change agents are especially likely to err when they are heterophilous (different from) with clients and do not empathize (pdf 550).
- **Generalization 11-3:** change agents more easily anticipate form and function than meaning (pdf 550).

### The Irish potato famine (pdf 551)
The potato from North America let Ireland's population grow from 2 million (1700s) to 8 million (1845), mostly in poverty. In 1845 the fungus *Phytophthora infestans* destroyed the crop. Faster clipper ships (12–14 days) let infected potatoes survive the crossing. Weather was cool and rainy. Rogers calls it one of the worst famines in history (at least one million dead, 2 million migrants to the United States). Rogers ends with questions about who or what was responsible and gives no answer in these pages (pdf 551).

### Achieving a dynamic equilibrium (pdf 552)
Perhaps the missionaries introduced too many axes too fast. Three types of equilibrium:
1. **Stable:** almost no change, as with the Yir Yoront before the missionaries.
2. **Dynamic:** change at a rate commensurate with the system's ability to cope.
3. **Disequilibrium:** change too rapid to adjust to. Rogers compares it to a traffic circle with one car too many, where movement stops.

Most change agents aim for dynamic equilibrium. The optimum rate is hard to gauge, and the missionaries misjudged it (pdf 552).

*Intuition note:* The speed of change matters as well as its direction. A system can absorb only so much change at a time.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A village of 10 farmers each sells 100 units of grain. A new seed cuts cost from $8 to $5 per unit. The price is $10 while only the 1st farmer has adopted, and it falls to $6 once 9 of the 10 have adopted.

- Innovator: profit per unit = 10 − 5 = $5, so 100 × 5 = $500. This is a windfall profit.
- Late adopter at $6: profit per unit = 6 − 5 = $1, so $100.
- Non-adopter at $6: profit per unit = 6 − 8 = −$2, so −$200. Rejecters are affected too.
- Classification: higher income for the innovator is direct, desirable and anticipated. A later effect, such as rejecters selling land and moving to town, is indirect and probably unanticipated (an added illustration).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] indirect and unanticipated consequences ↔ complex systems: a change in one element shifts others (the "bowl of marbles").
- [metaphor] windfall profits and widening gaps ↔ first-mover advantage and cumulative advantage in economics and sociology.
- [structural-analogy] dynamic equilibrium vs disequilibrium ↔ systems stability: change faster than adjustment capacity breaks the system.
- [metaphor] meaning vs form and function ↔ the encoding/decoding idea: receivers attach their own meanings.

## Questions this raises
- If consequences cannot be predicted, how can change agents be held responsible for them?
- Could panel studies and pilot field experiments reveal unanticipated consequences before wide diffusion?
- How can one measure "the rate a system can absorb", and who judges what is desirable?
- Is Generalization 11-1 strong enough to justify the Amish approach for other groups?
