# The Generation of Innovations

## L0 — One line
Much that influences diffusion happens before the first adopter: problem recognition, research, development and commercialization can help shape which innovation spreads and who gains from it (pdf 189, 219).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Past diffusion studies typically start at the first adopter. This chapter asks where innovations come from and how that earlier history affects diffusion and its consequences. Rogers describes six loosely ordered stages: problem recognition, research, development, commercialization, diffusion/adoption, consequences. Cases illustrate that technology is socially shaped. Serendipity (accidental discovery), lead users (users who prototype ahead of the market), skunkworks (small, often hidden R&D units), corporate profit motives, government policy and failed technology transfer all matter. Early design choices, such as machine size and cost, affect who adopts and who benefits. Retrospective "tracer" studies document the early stages but have clear limits. Agricultural extension is presented as an unusually integrated system linking research to users.

**Prerequisites:** the S-shaped diffusion curve and the definition of "innovation" (Ch. 1, 3); basic idea of an agenda of social problems.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Diffusion is only one phase of a larger innovation-development process. Studying only the part from the first adopter onward can miss decisions that often strongly influence diffusion (pdf 189, 225).
- The six stages are a rough guide. They may occur in another order, and some may be skipped (pdf 191, 214, 216).
- Innovations often come from outside planned R&D: serendipity (Post-its, Rogaine, penicillin, warfarin) and lead users (von Hippel: 77% of scientific-instrument innovations) (pdf 193–194, 215).
- Technology is socially constructed, not autonomous. Profit, military demand and regulation can shape which technology reaches the public (refrigerator hum, Segway classification) (pdf 201–203).
- Technology transfer is usually a two-way communication process, and it often fails. Examples are Ampex's VCR and Xerox PARC (pdf 205–211).
- Decisions made during R&D can help determine later diffusion and its consequences, including inequality. The California tomato harvester is the main case (pdf 216, 218–219).
- Tracer studies show that innovation clusters (sets of linked innovations), long lags (often ~20 years) and unplanned research are common. They are retrospective and data-limited (pdf 220–223).
- The agricultural extension model succeeded as an integrated research–specialist–agent system with roughly equal funding for diffusion (Rogers gives this as one reason for its success). Copies in other fields mostly failed (pdf 224–225).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction
Past diffusion studies began at the left tail of the S-curve, with the first adopters (pdf 189). Earlier activities usually came first: a perceived problem, R&D funding, invention, development, commercialization, a decision to diffuse, transfer to a diffusion agency, and communication to potential adopters (pdf 189). This chapter reviews research on these pre-diffusion aspects.

### The innovation-development process
An *innovation* is an idea, practice or object perceived as new by an adopting unit (Ch. 1). The *innovation-development process* is all decisions, activities and impacts from recognizing a need or problem, through research, development and commercialization, through diffusion and adoption, to consequences (pdf 190). Figure 4-1 shows six stages. In the figure, the scope of the usual past tracer study spans roughly stages 2–4 (research to commercialization), and the scope of the usual past diffusion study covers roughly stage 5 (diffusion and adoption); a longer bar underneath spans the whole sequence (pdf 191, figure). The figure note says the stages are "somewhat arbitrary": they do not always occur in this order, and some may be skipped (pdf 191).

### 1. Recognizing a problem or need
- Scientists may foresee a future problem. Example: a UC Davis scientist foresaw a labor shortage when the bracero (Mexican farmworker) program ended, and began breeding hard tomatoes for machine picking (pdf 190).
- Alternatively, a problem may rise on the social agenda through *agenda setting*. Example: auto safety. R&D existed for years but was not put into practice until the mid-1960s, after congressional hearings and Nader's book. Vehicle deaths had reached 50,000 a year in the early 1960s. Agenda setting took several years (pdf 190–191).
- Havelock (1972) surveyed hundreds of auto-safety researchers and hundreds of decision-makers in highway-safety organizations. Decision-makers shared the public's individual-blame view ("the nut behind the wheel"). Researchers held a system-blame view: redesign cars and roads (pdf 191).
- The researchers' invisible college had opinion-leader professors with much contact with government officials and insurers. The perception gradually shifted to system blame. A federal law followed, requiring safer cars and safer roads (pdf 191).
- Rogers' conclusion: the problem was socially constructed, through both scientific expertise and political forces (pdf 192).

*Intuition note:* A "need" is not simply there. It becomes a priority through a process of attention and persuasion. In the auto-safety case, how the problem was defined helped steer which research and policies followed.

### 2. Basic and applied research
- *Technology* = a design for instrumental action that reduces uncertainty in cause–effect relations needed to reach a desired outcome. It has a *hardware* aspect (material equipment) and a *software* aspect (knowledge, skills, procedures). Software is usually less visible (pdf 192).
- *Basic research*: original investigation to advance knowledge, with no specific practical objective. *Applied research*: scientific investigation intended to solve practical problems. Applied researchers are the main users of basic research. An *invention* may result from basic research → applied research → development (pdf 192).
- A *patent* protects the inventor's rights for a period of years. To get a U.S. patent, the inventor must prove to the Patent Office that the idea is genuinely original and does not overlap with existing knowledge. A license is usually sold for an initial fee plus a royalty (pdf 192–193).
- *Serendipity* = accidental discovery of a new idea (pdf 193). Cases in the text:
  - 3M's weak bonding agent, judged worthless, became Post-its. Pads were given to the president's secretary and then mailed to the secretaries of Fortune 500 presidents (pdf 193).
  - Upjohn's blood-pressure drug minoxidil caused hair growth in researchers. It became Rogaine after the researchers persuaded the president (pdf 193).
  - Penicillin (Fleming) and DDT (Müller, searching for a moth protection for wool) (pdf 194).
  - SMS, thought to have been found by Japanese teenagers in the mid-1990s (pdf 194).
- **Lead users.** Von Hippel (1988) found that the assumption "manufacturers develop innovations" is often wrong. In some fields, lead users (whose needs run well ahead of the market) develop a prototype and then convince a manufacturer to make it (pdf 194–195).
  - Lead users developed 77% of innovations in scientific instruments and 67% in semiconductors and printed circuit boards (pdf 194).
  - Examples: Arnold Beckman (Caltech) spun off Beckman Instruments after colleagues kept asking for copies of his devices (pdf 195). Busicom, as lead user, together with Intel's Ted Hoff produced the microprocessor (pdf 196).
  - In tractor shovels, engineering plastics and plastic additives, manufacturers mostly develop the innovations and lead users matter little (pdf 196).
  - 3M finds lead users through sociometric questions and a two-day workshop. Sony's Web site for PlayStation hackers drew ten thousand responses (pdf 196).
- **Case: Toshiba laptop (1986).** Mizoguchi envisioned a portable PC in 1983, compatible with IBM's desktop (pdf 197).
  - Headquarters vetoed the project twice. He worked "underground" at the Ome factory, diverted funds and moved ten engineers from military projects. A prototype took 24 months (pdf 197).
  - Executives called it a fad and barred sales in Japan (pdf 198). Nishida of Toshiba Europe promised 10,000 sales in a year, and they sold in 14 months (pdf 198).
  - Market shares: 38% Europe and 21% U.S. in 1988; 46% Japan a year later. Production rose from 5,000 a month (1986) to 100,000 (1989) (pdf 198).
  - Rogers states a general principle (citing Day, 1994): the lower an innovation champion is in the hierarchy, the more innovative the new product (pdf 198).

### 3. Development
- *Development* = putting a new idea into a form expected to meet the needs of potential adopters. It usually follows research, though the two are hard to separate. Example: UC Davis researchers designed the tomato harvester and built a prototype, then contracted a machinery maker (pdf 199).
- **Uncertainty.** The inventor-developer must anticipate the problems of the eventual adopters; the behavior of others in the R&D organization, competitors, government policy makers and other factors may also affect success. They work hard to get information: performance data, competitors' innovations, patents, policies, users' problems (pdf 199–200). Rogers concludes the process is "most of all" driven by technical-information exchange under high uncertainty (pdf 200).
- **Case: refrigerator.** Gas refrigerators have no moving parts and no noise. By about 1930 both types existed as prototypes, yet the electric type won. Large firms (GE, GM, Kelvinator, Westinghouse) invested heavily in R&D and promotion for it, because they expected larger profits (pdf 201). The technology was shaped by corporate profitability, not consumer choice (pdf 201).
- **Social construction of technology.** *Technological determinism* = the belief that technology causes changes in society (a view that implies technology is somehow autonomous). Rogers says technology is obviously not outside society. The opposite view, *social determinism / social construction*, holds that social factors shape technology (pdf 201). Other shaping forces: military demand (nuclear power, jet aircraft, the Internet) and regulation (health, safety, pollution, antitrust) (pdf 202).
- **Case: Segway** (late 2001). Whether it counted as a scooter or a motor vehicle would determine where it could be driven. Kamen lobbied agencies. NHTSA ruled it similar to a motorized wheelchair, and the CPSC called it a consumer product. Safety experts wanted more regulation. Rogers: classification is decided through a social construction process that will affect the adoption rate (pdf 203).
- **Skunkworks** = small, often subversive units inside a larger organization, created to pioneer a technology by escaping routine procedures. Staff are highly selected and work on a crash basis (pdf 204).
  - The name comes from Lockheed's P-80 Shooting Star unit in WWII, which was housed in a circus tent (pdf 204).
  - Jobs led the Macintosh skunkworks (about fifty young designers). Mac was shown January 1984 (pdf 204).
  - Reason given: most R&D organizations are bureaucracies built for stability, not flexible enough for innovation. A skunkworks gets "the best of both" (pdf 204–205).
- **Technology transfer** = application of information to use. The conventional view is one-way, from university researchers to firms, with technology as hardware. Because technology includes software and is composed of information, transfer is a communication process, and usually a two-way back-and-forth (pdf 205).
  - U.S. policy concern: Japanese firms turned U.S.-led R&D into higher-quality, cheaper products and gained market share (cars, VCRs, memory chips) (pdf 205).
  - VCR: Ampex invented it in the late 1950s. Its R&D staff proposed a home version, but management said it was not in that market and sold the rights to Sony. No American company makes VCRs now (pdf 205–206).
  - After 1989, weapons labs (Los Alamos, Sandia, Oak Ridge) were told to transfer technology. Their culture, built on preventing leakage, changed slowly. Several years passed (pdf 206).
  - The Bayh-Dole Act (1980) encouraged university technology transfer. Most research universities now have a transfer office (pdf 206).
  - Three levels of transfer (Gibson and Rogers, 1994): (1) knowledge, (2) use, (3) commercialization. Their confusion is one reason transfer often fails. Rogers says the effort needed has been underestimated (pdf 207).

*Intuition note:* Moving a technology is moving information, not just a machine. In the source's account, both sides usually keep exchanging information while they try to reach a mutual understanding of what it means.

### 4. Commercialization
- *Commercialization* = production, manufacturing, packaging, marketing and distribution of a product that embodies an innovation; converting a research idea into a product or service for sale (pdf 207). Not all innovations come from R&D. Some come from practitioners such as lead users (pdf 208).
- A *technology cluster* (also "innovation package") = elements of technology perceived as closely interrelated. The basic argument for packaging is that diffusion is more rapid (pdf 208).
- **Case: Xerox PARC.** Founded in 1970, it produced the Alto personal computer, the mouse, icons and pull-down menus, laser printing and local area networks. Xerox invested $150 million in 14 years. Of the PARC computing technologies, only laser printing was commercialized by Xerox into a useful product (pdf 209).
  - Reasons for PARC's success: (1) outstanding staff, including people from Engelbart's SRI group who brought the mouse; (2) Taylor's management style: free information exchange, little hierarchy, plentiful resources; (3) staff used their own inventions daily; (4) timing: the microprocessor from Intel and falling semiconductor prices (pdf 210).
  - Reasons for failure to transfer: (1) Xerox saw itself in the copier business only; (2) no mechanism to move technology to manufacturing and marketing, and Palo Alto was far from Stamford, Connecticut, and distance reduces personal contact; (3) culture clash between headquarters and PARC (pdf 211).
  - Jobs visited PARC in November 1979, hired key staff and started the Macintosh skunkworks. Transfer to Apple was rapid, but commercialization took five years (1979–1984). Xerox failed to take the final step (pdf 211–212).

### 5. Diffusion and adoption
- *Gatekeeping* = controlling message flow in a channel. One of the most crucial choices is the decision to begin diffusing. There is usually pressure to approve early, while a change agency's credibility depends on recommending only beneficial innovations (pdf 212).
- Agriculture: experiment stations develop innovations, and extension services recommend them, sometimes only for certain climate or soil conditions. This is an organizational interface between R&D and a diffusion agency (pdf 212).
- Medicine: NIH *consensus development* brings together scientists, practitioners, consumers and others to agree whether an innovation is safe and effective, ending in a published consensus statement (pdf 213). Before this there was no formal gatekeeping. There was concern that unevaluated technologies might diffuse while validated ones diffused too slowly. Now practitioners face information overload from guidelines (pdf 213).
- *Clinical trials* = experiments determining prospectively an innovation's efficacy, safety and other factors under real-life (rather than laboratory) conditions, as the basis for a go/no-go decision. The FDA requires extensive trials before approving a drug (pdf 213).

### 6. Consequences
*Consequences* = changes to an individual or social system resulting from adoption or rejection (see Ch. 11). The stages need not occur in linear order, and some may not occur. The stage idea is still useful for understanding where innovations come from (pdf 214).

### Case: warfarin
- Link (Wisconsin, 1934) studied "sweet clover disease" in cattle and isolated the anticoagulant coumarin. A dozen years later he found coumarin was a very effective rat killer (pdf 215).
- Derivative: warfarin (named after WARF, the university's transfer office). Royalties support research at Madison (pdf 215).
- Rats seek water and die outside their burrows, so effectiveness is observable, which helps adoption (pdf 216). Rogers concludes that the six-phase model is only a general guide from which many innovations may deviate (pdf 216).

### Socioeconomic status, equality, and innovation-development
- Status is highly related to change-agent contact, which is highly related to innovativeness (Ch. 9). So change agencies often increase inequality (pdf 216).
- Design choices set who can adopt: a hybrid car as low-priced sedan or high-end model, or research for larger versus smaller farmers (pdf 216). Whether technologies can be developed for greater equality depends on how status factors affect each stage (pdf 216–217).

### Case: hard tomatoes in California
- Before the harvester (1962): about 4,000 growers and 50,000 mostly Mexican farmworkers. Nine years later: 600 growers. Replaced by 1,152 machines (about $65,000 each) and about 18,000 sorters, roughly 80% women (pdf 218).
- Production moved to Yolo and Fresno Counties. Scientists bred hard tomatoes, though consumers preferred soft; taste was the same, with somewhat fewer vitamins (pdf 218).
- **Friedland and Barton's view:** the scientists did not anticipate these effects. They labelled the scientists "social sleepwalkers" (pdf 218). Hightower's book title: *Hard Tomatoes, Hard Times* (pdf 219).
- Research used more than $1 million of public funds. Hanna bred the tomato, Lorenzen designed the machine, and Blackwelder produced it under contract (pdf 219).
- 1964: 224 machines harvested 25% of tomatoes, a jump caused by the end of the bracero program. 1970: 1,521 machines harvested 99.9% and 32,000 hand pickers lost work (pdf 219).
- Rogers asks counterfactual questions: a smaller machine? less rush? prior social and economic research? (pdf 219). Conclusion: R&D decisions directly affect the later diffusion stage (pdf 219).
- Parallel: precision farming (GPS plus onboard computer) costs $50,000–$70,000, and only large commercial farmers can justify the cost. Rogers asks whether it could have been designed for smaller farmers (pdf 219–220).

*Intuition note:* Size, price and design are choices made before diffusion. They help determine who can afford to adopt, so they can affect who gains from the innovation.

### Tracing the innovation-development process
- *Retrospective tracer studies* reconstruct key events and decisions, using interviews, publications and archival records (pdf 220).
- **Project Hindsight** (Isenson, 1969): 20 weapons systems, about 35 events each. Most contributing research was highly applied and funded for the specific system. Usually interpreted as showing that applied research contributes more directly than basic research (pdf 220–221). Follow-ups: TRACES I–III, Project Sappho, Minnesota Innovation Project (pdf 221).
- Findings (pdf 221–222):
  - Major advances need a cluster of innovations, often up to a dozen (heart pacemaker: transistors, compact batteries). Diffusion scholars often overlook this interdependence.
  - Long lags, often up to twenty years, from basic research to application. Conception to first realization: oral contraceptives 9 years (1951–1960); hybrid corn 25 years (1908–1933); insecticides 13 years (1934–1947); TRACES II average 19 years.
  - Research is often done without a practical application in mind. Comroe (1977): of about 500 key articles behind ten cardiopulmonary technologies, 41% reported research that had no relation to the eventual disease when it was done. So the process does not always begin with a perceived need.
- **Shortcomings.** Studies are retrospective. They covered very important innovations, so results may not hold for less significant ones. Data come almost entirely from publications, so they say little on diffusion and almost nothing on consequences. They imply R&D is rational and planned, while serendipity is unlikely to be fully reported (pdf 222–223).
- **Future research.** Open questions: how research agendas are set and users' needs communicated; the role of change agencies as translators; the effect on credibility when an agency reverses a recommendation (2,4-D example, pdf 223); how common lead-user innovation is; consequences for equality of size and cost set early; links among organizations at the launch decision (pdf 223–224).

### The agricultural extension model
- Three components: (1) research subsystem (professors at state experiment stations and USDA), (2) county extension agents, (3) state extension specialists linking them. Researchers and specialists are in state universities and have similar expertise. It is an integrated system for innovation-development (pdf 224).
- History: land-grant universities from 1862, the Hatch Act (1887) for research funding, the Smith-Lever Act (1914) for extension (pdf 224–225).
- Extension funding is approximately equal to public agricultural research investment, and Rogers gives this fifty-fifty level as one reason for its success. No other federal mission agency spends more than a few percent on diffusion (pdf 225).
- Copies in other fields (education, public transportation, vocational rehabilitation, energy conservation, family planning) were not very successful. Some omitted local-level agents, and others ignored the many years needed to build a reputation. Agricultural research is also highly applied (pdf 225).

### Summary
Diffusion research should be broadened to the whole generation process. The six stages may be non-linear, and stages may be absent (pdf 225–226).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An early design choice sets who can adopt. A harvester saves $40 per acre per year. A farmer adopts only if the machine pays back in 5 years or less (our assumed rule).
- Design A costs $60,000. A 1,000-acre farm saves 1,000 × $40 = $40,000/yr, so payback = 60,000 / 40,000 = 1.5 years → adopts. A 100-acre farm saves 100 × 40 = $4,000/yr, so payback = 60,000 / 4,000 = 15 years → does not adopt.
- Design B costs $20,000. The 100-acre farm has payback 20,000 / 4,000 = 5 years → adopts.

Changing one R&D-stage choice (cost and size) moves the small farm from non-adopter to adopter. The S-curve and its consequences then differ, even though nothing in "diffusion" changed.

## Questions this raises
- Do lead users and serendipity account for a large share of innovations outside the studied fields, or only in a few (Rogers asks this too, pdf 223)?
- How can the likely consequences of a design be anticipated before the first adopter, so that harm to groups such as farmworkers is mitigated?
- Why did the extension model's integration work, and which elements are essential for a copy (Rogers names several that were omitted, pdf 225)?
- Would prospective studies of ordinary, less important innovations confirm the tracer-study findings?
