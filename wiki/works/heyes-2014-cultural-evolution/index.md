# Heyes, C. M., & Frith, C. D. (2014). The cultural evolution of mind reading. *Science*, 344(6190), 1243091. doi:10.1126/science.1243091

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[heyes-2014-cultural-evolution/main]].

## In one line
Explicit mind reading (deliberating and talking about mental states) is argued to be a culturally inherited skill passed on by verbal instruction, like print reading, while the early "implicit" mind reading of infants is a separate, possibly genetically based, process.

## The gist
Mind reading seems both early (infants appear to track false beliefs) and slow (children need years and conversation). The authors resolve this by separating two kinds. Implicit mind reading gives fast, accurate expectations about behaviour and does not tax executive function. Explicit mind reading is argued to be like literacy. It is built from general-purpose, genetically evolved mechanisms and depends on tuition. The evidence comes from structural parallels with print reading, a twin study, a new sign language, Samoan children and studies of maternal talk. This matters because it predicts cross-cultural variation and points research to the genetic "start-up kit" that makes cultural inheritance possible.

**Prerequisites:** the false-belief idea; what "executive function" means (working memory, inhibitory control); heritability from twin studies; basic ideas of social learning.
