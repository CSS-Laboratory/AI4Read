# The cultural evolution of mind reading (Heyes & Frith, 2014)

## L0 — One line
Explicit mind reading (deliberating and talking about mental states) is argued to be a culturally inherited skill passed on by verbal instruction, like print reading, while the early "implicit" mind reading of infants is a separate, possibly genetically based, process.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Mind reading seems both early (infants appear to track false beliefs) and slow (children need years and conversation). The authors resolve this by separating two kinds. Implicit mind reading gives fast, accurate expectations about behaviour and does not tax executive function. Explicit mind reading is argued to be like literacy. It is built from general-purpose, genetically evolved mechanisms and depends on tuition. The evidence comes from structural parallels with print reading, a twin study, a new sign language, Samoan children and studies of maternal talk. This matters because it predicts cross-cultural variation and points research to the genetic "start-up kit" that makes cultural inheritance possible.

**Prerequisites:** the false-belief idea; what "executive function" means (working memory, inhibitory control); heritability from twin studies; basic ideas of social learning.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Mind reading and print reading share many features (Table 1): meaning from signs, dedicated cortical areas, heritable disorders, cultural variation around a common core, and both interpretive and regulative roles. Because print reading is known to be culturally inherited, these shared features do not, by themselves, show that mind reading is genetically inherited, and they clear the way to consider that mind reading is also culturally inherited (p. 1).
- Infant looking-time studies suggest "implicit" mind reading. The paper compares three readings of it: continuity (the same mechanisms underlie implicit and explicit mind reading), two-systems (favoured by Frith: separate mechanisms, both specialized for mental states) and submentalizing (favoured by Heyes: implicit mind reading rests on domain-general mechanisms) (p. 3).
- Implicit and explicit mind reading appear to dissociate. Executive-function load impairs explicit but not implicit mind reading, and in autism explicit mind reading can succeed despite implicit problems (p. 3).
- Explicit mind reading develops slowly and effortfully, and neural systems for it mature late (p. 4).
- A twin study found substantial shared-environment and negligible genetic influence on individual differences. Natural experiments (Nicaraguan Sign Language, Samoa) and maternal-talk studies point to conversation about the mind as the key input (p. 4).
- The authors' view resembles social constructivism (accounts that stress conversation and cultural variation) and, like theory-theory (children build explicit theories like scientists), assumes broadly rational learning that yields conceptual knowledge. It contrasts with nativist theories (mechanisms specialized for mind reading are genetically inherited). Unlike theory-theory, it holds that novices' ideas come mainly from instruction, not from their own observation and hypothesis testing (p. 4).
- Mind reading also has a regulative role: believing in mental causation shapes behaviour, so beliefs about the mind can differ across cultures and change behaviour (pp. 2–3, 4).
- The authors assume that genetically evolved mechanisms provide much of the raw material for explicit mind reading (a "start-up kit"; components might include newborns' preference for faces making eye contact and a preference for contingent objects). Identifying it, and finding out how implicit outputs feed explicit mind reading, is the research priority (pp. 4–5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 1)
- "Mind reading" = theory of mind, mentalizing, folk psychology: ascribing beliefs, desires and intentions to explain and predict behaviour (p. 1).
- In print reading the signs are marks on paper and meanings relate to the world. In mind reading the signs are facial expressions, body movements and utterances, and meanings relate to mental states (p. 1).
- Literacy is only 5–6 thousand years old, so its specialized mechanisms are thought to be products of cultural inheritance, not direct targets of gene-based natural selection. Cultural evolution means change through heritable variation in fitness. Inheritance is by social learning, and fitness is measured by how many individuals or groups adopt the system (p. 1).
- Literacy mechanisms are "recycled" from genetically evolved ones during training (p. 1).
- Plan: (1) parallels with print reading; (2) infant evidence; (3) direct evidence for cultural inheritance of explicit mind reading (p. 1).

### Mind reading and print reading (pp. 1–3; Table 1, p. 2)
**Neural bases.** Medial prefrontal cortex, temporo-parietal junction and precuneus are more active for mental-state thinking than for similar tasks without it (p. 1; Fig. 2, p. 3). Print reading has comparable specialization: the occipito-temporal "visual word form area" responds more to words than to comparable nonwords (p. 1).

**Developmental disorders.** Many people with autism spectrum conditions (ASCs) have a specific mind-reading impairment compared with IQ- and language-matched controls. ASC heritability is substantial, which *might suggest* inherited mind-reading mechanisms. But dyslexia is heritable and disrupts a culturally inherited skill, so this is only one of many candidate explanations (p. 1).

**Cultural variation.** Print reading looks diverse. Scripts map whole words (Kanji), syllables (Kana) or phonemes (alphabets) (p. 1). Mind reading looks universal. Recent work suggests print reading is more, and mind reading less, uniform across cultures than was supposed (p. 2):
- A featural analysis of 115 writing systems found most characters are formed by three strokes (p. 2).
- Children in Australia and the US grasp diverse beliefs before knowledge access. Children in China and Iran, where individualism and self-expression are less important, show the reverse order (p. 2).
- Ethnography suggests cultures vary in how much they treat mental states as causes of behaviour, whether they place them inside or outside the body, and whether they see them as subject to natural or supernatural laws (p. 2). Western ideas about mind and mental disorder have also changed since ancient times (p. 2).

**Interpretation and regulation.** Literacy has a reading (interpretive) and a writing (regulative) side. Likewise "mental literacy" has a regulative role: novices learn that behaviour can be, and should be, produced by rational interactions among beliefs and desires, and are encouraged to behave that way (p. 2). Evidence: adults discouraged from believing in free will (told that deterministic neural processes, not mental states, control behaviour) show weaker neural signals associated with action planning, and more cheating and aggression (pp. 2–3).

> *Intuition note.* Each feature that might be thought to show a genetic basis (brain specialization, heritable disorder, cultural universals) also appears in print reading, which is known to be culturally inherited. So these features do not, by themselves, show a genetic basis.

### Implicit mind reading in infants and adults (pp. 3–4)
- Print reading appears at 5–6 years in Western children. Infants as young as 7 months seem able to mind read, notably by ascribing false beliefs (p. 3).
- Typical experiment (18): an infant watches an adult place a toy in the green box (not the yellow) and reach for it repeatedly. The toy then moves to the yellow box while the adult is present (true belief) or absent (false belief). In the final phase, true-belief infants look longer (more surprise) when the adult reaches for the green box. False-belief infants look longer when she reaches for the yellow box. So the infant "expects" a reach toward where the adult believes the toy is (p. 3).
- Inferring mind reading from nonverbal behaviour like looking time is called "implicit" mind reading. Three interpretations (p. 3):
  1. **Continuity:** the same mechanisms control implicit mind reading and adult explicit mind reading (19).
  2. **Two-systems (favoured by Frith):** different mechanisms, but both specialized for mental states. The implicit system is early, fast and efficient. The explicit system is later, slower and makes heavier demands on executive functions such as working memory and inhibitory control (20, 21). Autism, with specific mind-reading failures and other domains intact, is cited in support (22).
  3. **Submentalizing (favoured by Heyes):** explicit mind reading depends on specialized, late, slow, effortful mechanisms, but implicit mind reading depends on domain-general mechanisms (23, 24).
- Dissociation evidence: in autism, explicit mind reading can be achieved despite continuing problems with implicit mind reading (25). In neurotypical adults, a concurrent executive-function task impairs the accuracy of verbal judgments about others' thoughts and feelings (26) but does not interfere with implicit mind reading (27). This is hard to reconcile with continuity but compatible with the other two (p. 3).
- **Dot perspective task** (Fig. 3): participants count dots in a room with a central avatar. Responses are slower and less accurate when some dots are behind the avatar than when all are in front (28) (p. 3). The two-systems account explains this as automatic calculation, by a specialized system, of the number of dots the avatar can see, which conflicts with the participant's own count (p. 3). The submentalizing account explains it as automatic attentional orienting to the avatar's gaze direction, the same domain-general process by which arrows direct attention (29, 30) (p. 4). The effect also occurs when the central object is an arrow (Fig. 3 caption, p. 3).
- Both accounts agree that explicit mind reading is like print reading: slow and cognitively demanding. Reading-related event-related potentials in 16-year-olds are not quite as fast as in adults (31). In Western cultures, the neural systems for explicit mind reading are among the last to mature (32). Explicit performance (perspective-taking, emotion recognition, detecting pretence and irony) keeps improving from adolescence to adulthood (33, 34) (p. 4).

> *Intuition note.* "Early" mind reading in infants and "slow" mind reading in children need not conflict if they come from different processes. A test that taxes executive function separates the two kinds in adults.

### Learning to read minds (p. 4)
**Twin study (35).** Over 1,000 twin pairs took explicit mind-reading tests at age 5. Within-pair correlation was 0.53 for nonidentical twins (about 50% of genes shared) and also 0.53 for identical twins (all genes shared). The authors quote the study: "substantial shared environmental influence but negligible genetic influence on individual differences in theory of mind" (p. 4). The study alone does not say what kind of environmental influence or learning is involved (p. 4).

**Theory-theory vs simulation.** It is now widely accepted that they are complementary: theory-theory for explicit high-level mind reading, simulation theory for implicit low-level mind reading (36). Theory-theory says children, like scientists, generate and test theories by observation (37). The authors' view is that the novice's ideas come primarily from instruction, from what expert mind readers tell her (p. 4).

**Natural experiment: Nicaraguan Sign Language (NSL).** NSL emerged in the 1970s among deaf people. Pyers and Senghas (38) compared two adult cohorts. The first learned NSL when it was rudimentary. The second learned it about 10 years later, when it had many more mental-state signs. The first cohort was worse at false-belief understanding although 10 years older. The authors infer that lacking mental-state vocabulary, they could not receive instruction about the mind (p. 4).

**Samoa.** Where talking about mental states is considered improper, children understand false belief at about 8 years, 4 or 5 years later than children in Europe and North America (39) (p. 4).

**Conversation.** Together with other studies (40, 41), these results indicate we learn about the mind through conversation about the mind. In principle this could come from eavesdropping, but experts, especially mothers, tailor ("epistemically engineer") their talk (42–45) (p. 4):
- At 15 months, mothers make proportionally more references to desires and emotions than to thoughts and knowledge. Desire/emotion talk selectively predicts children's explicit mind reading at 24 months. At 24 months, mothers talk proportionally more about thoughts/knowledge, and this predicts explicit mind reading at 33 months (43).
- Desires and emotions are easier to understand because children constantly try to fulfil desires and emotions are often reflected in distinctive facial expressions. This parallels teaching easy words before hard words (p. 4).
- Mothers' causal-explanatory statements (e.g. "She is smiling because she is happy") also predict development (41).
- Lohmann and Tomasello (46): training 3- and 4-year-olds by conversing about deceptive objects (e.g. a pen that looks like a flower) was especially effective for false belief when the adult used sentential-complement syntax ("What do you think it is?"). The authors read this (47) as teaching mental-state concepts and a format for representing them, not just labels. Children culturally inherit mechanisms specialized for representing mental states (48) (p. 4).

**Relation to other views.** The view resembles social constructivism (49) in stressing conversation and variation. Like theory-theory, it assumes the developmental processes are broadly rational and yield conceptual knowledge. Some elements (e.g. those relating to knowing or primary emotions) are likely to vary less, but substantial variation is allowed even in the "heartland". For example, looking is likely predictive of behaviour in all cultures, yet beliefs about seeing and knowing could still vary (50). Because mind reading is also regulative, distinctive beliefs about the mind can lead to different observable behaviour (p. 4).

> *Intuition note.* The central claim is that children get their theory of mind mostly by being told, in conversation, how situations, mental states and behaviour connect. They do not discover it alone. The twin result (identical twins were no more alike than nonidentical twins), the NSL cohorts and Samoa all fit this.

### Start-up kit (pp. 4–5)
- Against nativism, the authors hold that humans do not genetically inherit mechanisms specialized for developing explicit mind reading. Genetically evolved mechanisms supply much of the raw material, including mechanisms that become specialized for mental-state representation and those that make cultural inheritance possible (p. 4).
- Priority: identify the genetic "start-up kit" (22). No one has made a concerted attempt to train chimpanzees to read minds, but few researchers would expect success; why? Which inherited resources let humans, but not chimpanzees, culturally inherit mind reading? (pp. 4–5).

### Implicit mind reading: the infant's resources (p. 5)
- From birth infants attend to face-like stimuli. By the end of year one they use faces to recognize identity, race, emotion and gaze direction (51). They prefer moving limbs (52) and by 12 months show predictive eye movements for others' actions (53). Attending to their own movements links action observation and production (motor resonance, linked to simulation theory) (54).
- Analogy: infants learn statistical regularities in speech (55) before mapping sounds to letters. Body movements also have regularities (56), and infants learn to categorize actions by success and efficiency (57). This provides a basis for expectations about outcomes (58) and "predetermined targets" for later instruction about how mental states relate to actions (p. 5).

### Explicit mind reading (p. 5)
- Implicit mind reading is a one-way process: the learner observes an actor who need not know they are watched. Explicit mind reading emerges from deliberately instructive behaviour of others, an essentially two-way communicative process (p. 5).
- Components of a start-up kit might include newborns' preference for faces making eye contact and very young infants' preference for objects that respond contingently. Related findings: at 4 months, gaze following is much more likely after direct eye contact (59); at 12–18 months, eye contact and pointing are used to separate communicative from noncommunicative interactions (60, 61). Joint attention seems distinctly human (62) (p. 5).

### Conclusions and future directions (p. 5)
- Explicit mind-reading mechanisms are constructed ("recycled") from mechanisms that evolved for more general functions (parsing and predicting event sequences, getting information from others). Construction depends on tuition (p. 5).
- Most, possibly all, human neurocognitive skills are shaped by culture and many are culturally inherited, but the mind reading–print parallels are "extraordinary". Colour perception contrasts: it rests on specialized, genetically inherited mechanisms shared with other species, and culture adjusts them without making a new system (p. 5).
- The analogy is imperfect. In "sound symbolism" inscription–sound relations depend partly on nervous-system structure, but they are likely more arbitrary than behaviour–mental-state relations. Numeracy may be a better analogue. Explicit mind reading is comparable to print reading in weak dependence on specialized inherited mechanisms, strong dependence on tuition, and the legacy: representing relations between mental states, behaviour and the world, and regulating and interpreting a limitless range of contents. Both legacies promote inheritance of other skills; mind reading helps experts represent what novices know (p. 5).
- Implicit mind-reading mechanisms continue to operate throughout the life cycle, allowing swift coordination when time is short and demands are heavy. Their outputs may help explicit development, e.g. by segmenting behaviour into units that can be aligned with mental categories. Still, implicit mind reading is "radically insufficient" for explicit development, and no amount of individual learning would suffice. If a group of human infants managed to survive on a desert island, they would be no more likely to develop a theory of mind and become explicit mind readers than to develop a writing system (p. 5).
- Open question: are implicit mechanisms specialized for mind reading or domain-general (p. 5).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Logic of the twin study. Suppose differences in a trait were due only to additive genetic effects (no shared environment). Identical twins share 100% of genes, nonidentical about 50%. Then a hypothetical identical-twin correlation of 0.80 would fall to about 0.80 × 0.5 = 0.40 for nonidentical twins. The gap is 0.80 − 0.40 = 0.40. The real result for explicit mind reading at age 5 was 0.53 and 0.53 (p. 4). The gap is 0.53 − 0.53 = 0. A zero gap fits a shared environment (like family conversation) much better than inherited differences. (The 0.80 is invented, and the halving rule holds only when genes alone, acting additively, explain the similarity.)
