# The Financial Instability Hypothesis (Minsky, 1992)

## L0 — One line
In a capitalist economy with debt-financed capital assets, long good times tend to shift finance from "hedge" units toward "speculative" and "Ponzi" units, so the system's instability can be explained without relying on outside shocks, by internal dynamics together with the interventions meant to contain them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Minsky asks why capitalist economies sometimes suffer inflations and debt deflations that feed on themselves. His answer: firms finance capital assets with debt, and this debt is a schedule of payment commitments. Whether the commitments can be met depends on profits, and profits depend on investment. Units fall into three types: hedge, speculative and Ponzi. If hedge finance dominates, the economy may be stable. The greater the weight of speculative and Ponzi finance, the more likely it is that the economy amplifies deviations. Prolonged prosperity tends to move the economy from the first state to the second. So business cycles come from internal dynamics together with the policies designed to contain them. This challenges the view that the economy always seeks equilibrium.

**Prerequisites:** balance sheets (assets, liabilities, equity); interest versus principal; cash flow; basic idea of an equilibrium-seeking system.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Empirical claim: inflations and debt deflations seem able to spin out of control by amplifying themselves; Minsky treats this as evidence against the view of the economy as always equilibrium-seeking (p. 1).
- The economic problem is the "capital development" of the economy. Capital assets are financed by liabilities, which fix prior payment commitments, while the assets generate conjectured receipts (pp. 2–3).
- Keynes's "veil of money" links money to financing through time and to bank–business negotiation. The Quantity Theory's veil does not (pp. 3–4).
- Profits determine whether debts are validated. On the Kalecki–Levy view, profits come from investment (plus the government deficit) (pp. 5–6).
- Bankers are profit-seeking and strive to innovate, which invalidates the presupposition of an unchanging "money" item with near-constant velocity (p. 6).
- Three income–debt relations are defined: hedge, speculative and Ponzi (p. 7).
- Theorem 1: some financing regimes are stable and some unstable. Theorem 2: prolonged prosperity moves the economy from stable to unstable regimes (pp. 7–8).
- If an economy with many speculative units is inflating and the authorities try to end inflation by monetary constraint, speculative units will become Ponzi units, and a collapse of asset values is likely (p. 8).
- Cycles are generated by internal dynamics plus the system of interventions and regulations; the model does not rely on exogenous shocks (p. 8).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: empirical aspect (p. 1).** From time to time capitalist economies show inflations and debt deflations that "seem to have the potential" to spin out of control. The system's reactions amplify the movement. Government interventions seem to have been inept in some historical crises. Minsky takes this as evidence against the view, which he attributes to Smith and Walras, that the economy is constantly an equilibrium-seeking and sustaining system. He cites Fisher (1933) for debt deflation, Kindleberger (1978) for self-sustaining disequilibrating processes, and Wolfson (1986) for data and a review of crisis theories (p. 1).

**Theoretical roots (pp. 1–2).** As theory, the hypothesis is an interpretation of Keynes's *General Theory*, placed in history (the 1930s contraction was part of the evidence to be explained). It also draws on Schumpeter's credit view of money. Minsky's own key works are the 1975 and 1986 books (pp. 1–2).

**Capitalist economy and finance (pp. 2–4).**
- The problem is Keynes's "capital development of the economy," not the Knightian "allocation of given resources" (p. 2).
- Investment exchanges present money for future money. Present money pays for resources used to produce investment output. Future money is the profits of firms that own the assets (p. 2).
- Control of capital assets is financed by liabilities, which are commitments to pay money. Each unit's liabilities fix a time series of payment commitments, while its assets give a time series of conjectured cash receipts (pp. 2–3).
- Keynes (1972, p. 151) is quoted: owners of assets have often borrowed, and banks stand between depositors and borrowers. This is the "veil of money" (p. 3).
- **Contrast.** In the Quantity Theory the veil means goods are really exchanged for goods. In Keynes's version money is tied to financing through time. Money flows first from depositors to banks to firms, and later from firms to banks to depositors (p. 3).
- Money flows to firms in response to expected profits. Money flows from firms are paid out of realized profits (pp. 3–4).
- In negotiations between "generic" bankers and businessmen, businessmen are enthusiasts and bankers are skeptics (p. 4).
- Expectations of profits determine both new financing contracts and market prices of existing ones. Realized profits determine whether commitments are met (p. 4).
- Institutional complexity may result in several layers of intermediation between the ultimate owners of the community's wealth and the units that control and operate it (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* Every debt is a promise of future payments, written today. Whether the promise is kept depends on realized profits, while the assets only give conjectured receipts when the debt is taken on.

**Wider units and modern changes (pp. 4–5).** Households, governments and international units also have liability structures that current performance validates or invalidates (pp. 4–5). Greater complexity and larger government roles as refinancing agents "may" make the system behave differently than before. Government participation has much diminished the downside vulnerability of aggregate profits. The same interventions "may well" induce a greater upside (inflationary) bias (p. 5).

**Profits (pp. 5–6).** The hypothesis uses the Kalecki–Levy view that the structure of aggregate demand determines profits (p. 5).
- Skeletal model, with highly simplified consumption behavior by receivers of profit incomes and wages: aggregate profits = aggregate investment in each period.
- More complex (though still highly abstract) structure: aggregate profits = aggregate investment + government deficit (pp. 5–6).
- So whether liabilities are validated depends on investment. Investment happens now because businessmen and bankers expect investment in the future (p. 6).

**Banking (p. 6).** The hypothesis is a theory of debt's impact and of how debt is validated. Unlike the orthodox Quantity Theory, it treats banking as profit-seeking. Bankers (meaning all finance intermediaries) are "merchants of debt" whether brokers or dealers, who strive to innovate in the assets they acquire and the liabilities they market. This invalidates the Quantity Theory presupposition of an unchanging "money" item with near-constant velocity, and hence a linear proportional link between money supply and the price level (p. 6).

**Three financing types (p. 7).**
- **Hedge:** can meet all contractual payments from cash flows. More equity in the liability structure makes a hedge unit more likely.
- **Speculative:** can pay the income-account commitments (interest) but not repay principal from income. It needs to roll over, e.g. issue new debt to meet maturing debt. The page as printed then says governments with floating debts, corporations with floating issues of commercial paper and banks "are typically hedge units" (see Reading notes).
- **Ponzi:** operating cash flow covers neither principal nor interest. It can sell assets or borrow. Doing so to pay interest lowers equity, raises liabilities and prior commitments, and lowers the margin of safety for creditors.

**Theorems (pp. 7–8).** "It can be shown" that if hedge finance dominates, the economy "may well" be equilibrium seeking and containing. The greater the weight of speculative and Ponzi finance, the greater the likelihood that it is deviation amplifying (p. 7).
- Theorem 1: there are financing regimes in which the economy is stable and regimes in which it is unstable.
- Theorem 2: over prolonged prosperity the economy moves from stable to unstable financial relations (p. 8).
- In particular, over a protracted period of good times, capitalist economies tend to move from a financial structure dominated by hedge units to one in which speculative and Ponzi units have large weight (p. 8).

*Intuition note:* The paper says that over a long stretch of good times the mix of financing tends to move from hedge toward speculative and Ponzi units, and that some financing regimes are stable and others unstable. On these pages it does not explain why the mix drifts.

**Mechanism of collapse and conclusion (p. 8).** If an economy with many speculative units is inflating and the authorities try to end inflation by monetary constraint, speculative units become Ponzi units and the net worth of previously Ponzi units quickly evaporates. Units with cash shortfalls try to "make position by selling out position," which is likely to collapse asset values. The model does not rely on exogenous shocks. Historical cycles are compounded of internal dynamics and the system of interventions and regulations (p. 8).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three firms each owe interest of 10 and principal of 40 this year.
- Firm A: operating cash flow 60. Needs 10 + 40 = 50, and 60 ≥ 50 → **hedge**.
- Firm B: cash flow 15. Covers interest (15 ≥ 10) but not 50 → **speculative**. It must roll over the 40 principal with new debt.
- Firm C: cash flow 6 < 10 → **Ponzi**. Shortfall on interest = 10 − 6 = 4. Say its debt is 200. If it borrows 4 and rolls over 40, debt = 200 − 40 + 40 + 4 = 204, so debt rises and its safety margin falls.
- Tightening: if interest on B rises from 10 to 16, then 15 < 16 and B becomes Ponzi.

## Concepts
- [[financial instability hypothesis (hedge/speculative/Ponzi)]] — hedge: pays all commitments from cash flow. Speculative: pays interest but must roll over principal. Ponzi: cash flow misses even interest (p. 7)
- [[equilibrium]] — the classic view of the economy as always seeking and sustaining equilibrium, which Minsky disputes (p. 1)
- [[debt deflation]] — a self-feeding contraction, with Fisher cited as the classic description (p. 1)
- [[endogenous-cycles]] (new) — cycles generated by internal financial dynamics plus interventions, without relying on exogenous shocks (p. 8)
- [[veil-of-money]] (new) — Keynes's sense: money linked to financing through time via bank intermediation (p. 3)
- [[profits-validate-debt]] (new) — Kalecki–Levy view: aggregate profits equal investment in the skeletal model (plus the government deficit in a more complex one), and they decide whether liabilities are validated (pp. 5–6)
- [[bankers-as-innovators]] (new) — profit-seeking intermediaries innovate in assets and liabilities, so the presupposition of an unchanging "money" with near-constant velocity fails (p. 6)
- [[Knightian uncertainty]] — the paper does not use the word "uncertainty"; it only contrasts the Knightian problem of allocating given resources with Keynes's capital development (p. 2)

## Canonical instances
- [[great-contraction-1929-33]] — evidence the General Theory aimed to explain. Modern government participation is said to prevent such degeneration (pp. 1–2, 5)
- [[roll-over-financing]] — governments with floating debts, corporations with floating commercial paper and banks, which the page as printed calls "typically hedge units" although roll-over is the mark of speculative units (p. 7; see Reading notes)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Hedge → speculative → Ponzi drift ↔ economics-finance: bank run equilibrium — both treat fragile financing structures as sources of self-reinforcing collapse.
- [structural-analogy] Deviation-amplifying system ↔ complex systems: positive feedback — reactions reinforce, not damp, a movement.
- [metaphor] "Making position by selling out position" ↔ fire sales — forced asset sales push prices down, hurting other units.

## Questions this raises
- What measurable indicators would show an economy moving from hedge toward speculative and Ponzi finance?
- Minsky says "it can be shown" that hedge dominance may give stability. What model would show this, and under what conditions?
- If interventions reduce downside risk but add upside (inflationary) bias, how does the cycle change?
- How would the profit identity (profits = investment + deficit) change in an open economy?
