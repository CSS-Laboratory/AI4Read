# Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind? (Premack & Woodruff, 1978)

## L0 — One line
The authors suggest that a chimpanzee's choices of "solution" photographs for videotaped human problems can be understood as imputing purpose (and perhaps knowledge) to the actor, and they lay out experiments to test this.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The paper asks whether a chimpanzee imputes (attributes) unobservable mental states, such as purpose and knowledge, to others. The authors showed the chimpanzee Sarah videotaped human problems and let her pick a solution photograph. She did well, in some cases even when the solution did not look like the scene or the problem was not about food. The authors favour a theory-of-mind reading. They rule out physical matching and treat associationism (learning familiar sequences) and empathy (predicting what she herself would do) as rivals to be tested. They admit the first experiment cannot decide this alone, so most of the paper is a programme of further tests: knowledge, guessing, pretending, lying and self-knowledge.

**Prerequisites:** binomial "chance" reasoning (a two-choice test gives 50% by luck); the idea of controls in animal experiments; basic notions of association versus rule-based learning.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A "theory of mind" means imputing mental states to self and others. It counts as a theory because the states are not directly observable and the system is used to predict the behaviour of others (p. 515).
- The method tests *comprehension* of a problem, not problem-solving. Sarah watches a human actor struggle, then picks the photograph that solves the problem (p. 516).
- Sarah was correct on 21 of 24 trials, with all errors on one problem. Physical matching cannot explain this: every element appears in every scene, and posture separates only one problem from three (p. 516).
- Associationism (completing familiar sequences) and empathy (predicting what she would do in the actor's place) are rivals that the first experiment cannot separate from theory of mind, so further experiments target them (pp. 516–518).
- She also did well on non-food problems and on finely graded alternatives (no errors in the first series, one in the second). Her choices varied with the actor's identity, which gives no support to simple empathy (pp. 520–521).
- Planned tests (embedded videotape, guess versus know, pretend versus real, deception) aim at knowledge-related states such as knowing, guessing and pretending (pp. 522–525).
- Sarah's test on adult versus child competence was indeterminate (10 of 12 correct), so the test does not show that she imputes knowledge (p. 522).
- The authors argue that imputing mental states is primitive, and that behaviourism requires suppressing it (pp. 525–526).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 515–516)
- Köhler's apes, in nearly all cases, used cage materials to get inaccessible food. The ape Elizabeth pulled in a blanket to recover a ball, with the "right" force (p. 515). The authors say they care about the ape as psychologist rather than as physicist; they are interested in what he knows about the physical world only insofar as it affects what he knows about what someone else knows (p. 515).
- **Definition.** An individual has a theory of mind if he imputes mental states to himself and others. Such a system counts as a theory because the states are not directly observable and the system can be used to predict the behaviour of others (p. 515).
- For now they consider whether the chimpanzee imputes mental states to others at all. Whether his theory is good, complete or accurate is, for the moment, too difficult to test (p. 515).
- Mental states humans infer: purpose or intention (the authors say it seems beyond question that this is the state we impute most widely), believing, thinking, knowing, guessing, doubting, pretending, liking. Promise and trust are not on the list (p. 515). Embedded inferences, in which one person's state is about another person's state, are set aside. The authors say human limits on embedding are not impressive: only about four steps make our species uncomfortable (pp. 515–516).

### Basic approach: problem comprehension, not problem-solving (p. 516)
- Four 30-second videotapes show a human actor in a cage struggling to get bananas that are (1) overhead, (2) outside the cage, (3) outside with a box blocking reach, (4) outside with a box laden with cement blocks (p. 516).
- Photographs show the solutions: stepping onto a box, reaching out with a rod, moving the box aside, removing the blocks (p. 516; Figure 1, p. 517).
- Procedure: show the tape, hold the last 5 seconds, and offer a pair of photographs, one a solution. The manner of testing was designed to control for inadvertent social cues: the trainer puts the alternatives in a box and leaves the room. Sarah picks one, places it by the TV, rings a bell, and is then told "right" or "wrong" (p. 516).
- Sarah is a 14-year-old African-born chimpanzee, in cognitive testing five days a week for ten years, and was taught a simplified visual language between ages 4.6 and 6.5. She had no formal experience with these tests but much experience of TV (p. 516).
- **Result.** She was correct on 21 of 24 trials (p < .001). All errors were on one problem, the cement blocks. The authors say it is probably not a coincidence that this was also the problem on which Köhler's apes largely failed. She made three errors in a row and then succeeded on the last three trials; the authors say she could have learned the solution during testing but call this an unlikely reading of her overall performance. On the other three problems she was 6/6 each (p = .013, binomial test) (p. 516).

*Intuition note:* Sarah never solves the problem herself. On the authors' reading, she picks the picture that fits what the actor is trying to do, so her choices can be read as evidence about what she understands of the actor. This is the authors' interpretation, not yet a settled result.

### Three interpretations (pp. 516–518)
1. **Physical matching.** Sarah matches physical elements between tape and photograph. The authors rule this out quite directly: every element (actor, bananas, blocks, stick, rope, box) appears in every scene (Figure 1). The actor's posture was more upright in one problem only, so "upright" can separate one problem from three but cannot account for three of four correct. They say matching is too weak a device to explain the results (p. 516).
2. **Associationism (rival view).** One completes a familiar but incomplete sequence, or resumes an interrupted act. Applied here, one need only assume Sarah is familiar with such problems (p. 516). The authors grant she has probably met similar problems in daily life, though with different items, and that she was the one seeking the objects, and they say there was probably enough similarity to grant the associationist argument (pp. 516–518). The usual associationist reply for unfamiliar sequences is generalization. The authors call generalization a vacuous proposal for well-defined sequences such as language, where rules rather than gradients explain productivity. For most of the present problem sequences a well-formed structural description is not yet possible, so they cannot convincingly rule generalization out here. They say there are other grounds, to be pointed out later (p. 518).
3. **Theory of mind (the authors' recommended view).** She imputes at least intention or purpose and knowledge or belief. On this view she assumes the actor wants the banana and knows how to get it, and so picks the solution (p. 518).

- **Empathy (a further alternative).** She puts herself in the actor's place and predicts what she would do, not what the actor will do (p. 518). It is testable: the empathy view is supported if the actor's identity has no effect on her choices, and cannot be correct if it does (p. 518). The authors say empathy and theory of mind are not radically different; they are in part identical. Empathy does not grant any inferences about knowledge, so it is a theory of mind restricted to purpose (p. 518). Footnote 1: the version "if I were a three-year-old child" presupposes knowledge of what such a child knows, and so seems equivalent to making inferences about another's knowledge (p. 518).
- Associationism and theory of mind are not mutually exclusive. The authors object to associationism only as "a sufficient and exclusive mechanism". They think novel situations generate expectancies from theories. They suggest children and lower species may form expectancies by association, and adults and higher species largely from theory (p. 518).

*Intuition note:* The first experiment fits several explanations, so the authors say it cannot decide among them by itself (p. 518). They describe further experiments with more resolving power and say that the proper combination of results could decide the issue (p. 518).

### Problems not restricted to physical inaccessibility (pp. 518–520)
- Human "problems" are open-ended (a car that will not start, a recalcitrant spouse). The authors ask whether the chimpanzee's concept of problem is really as narrow as the experiments suggest, or whether the narrowness comes from experimenters not testing the ape in ways that would show its abstractness (p. 518).
- Four new problems: an actor locked in a cage; a malfunctioning heater (the actor shivers and glances wryly at it); an unplugged phonograph; a hose not attached to the faucet (p. 520; Figure 2, p. 519).
- **Series 1 (gross alternatives):** key, attached hose, plugged-in cord, lit paper cone. Sarah made no errors. Two cases (hose, cord) could be physical matches. The other two could not: a key does not resemble a caged actor, and a flame does not resemble a shivering actor (p. 520).
- **Series 2 (refined alternatives):** three versions each: key intact, twisted or broken; hose or cord attached, not attached, or attached but cut; paper unlit, lit or burnt out. She made one error in twelve choices (as printed; see Reading notes), picking the twisted key. The authors attribute it to photograph quality: the photographs had been reduced from 8×10 to 3×4 inches (p. 520).
- She had to pick the correct item in the correct state. The authors argue she could not have solved this by choosing familiar over unfamiliar scenes: they say disconnected hoses and unlit or burnt-out wicks must have been as familiar to her as a lit one (p. 520).
- Sarah had often seen trainers perform these acts but had never done them herself. The acts were never segmented for her; the authors say she evidently segmented them herself by watching (p. 520).
- They conclude that the chimpanzee's concept of problem is not the restricted one suggested by earlier tests (p. 520).

### Modalities: "would," "should," "would like" (pp. 520–521)
- Her choice might answer "What would the actor do?", "What should he do?" or "What would I like to see him do?", or simply express a preference. The authors note she picks a given picture only when it is the solution to the tape shown, so they think it makes sense to treat her choices as answers to a question rather than as plain preference (p. 520).
- A human adult can shift among these modalities with evident ease (the authors say this is an impression, not a study). Whether the chimpanzee can is not answered by the tests so far, which are noncommittal; markers might be introduced later (p. 520).
- **Keith/Bill test** (as printed; part of this passage was changed in proof, see Reading notes). The problems were redone with Keith, Sarah's favourite trainer, and Bill, an acquaintance for whom she displayed no affection. "Bad" alternatives showed mishaps, such as stepping through the box, falling over it, or lying under the cement blocks, or an inappropriate object (a rod too short). The trainer approved all choices (p. 521).
  - Keith: "good" alternative 8/8. Bill: "good" only 2/8. A run of ten consecutive "appropriate" responses in thirteen trials gave p < .01 (Grant's test) (p. 521).
  - The authors say her choices could be interpreted as answering what she would like to see happen to agents she likes and does not like. They cannot rule out that she sees Bill as less able, though they find this even less likely, or that she simply prefers the pictures (p. 521).
  - **Control 1** (a third, irrelevant "bad" alternative): Keith 7/8 "good", Bill 1/8. Three of the seven "bad" picks for Bill were irrelevant, which suggests that the photographs' own content also matters (p. 521).
  - **Control 2** (preference matched; only Bill's tapes): she chose a "bad" alternative 10 of 12 times, nine relevant and one irrelevant (p < .05). The authors say even this relatively crude control was enough to reveal an effect of the tape–photograph relation (p. 521).
- The authors add that preference also affects answers to linguistic questions, and that the role of nonverbal factors deserves wider recognition (p. 521).
- These results give no support to empathy: her choice depends on who the actor is. They do not show that she would inflict such outcomes herself (p. 521).

*Intuition note:* Sarah's choices change with the actor she is thought to like or dislike. A plain "what would I do?" rule would give the same answer for both actors.

### Agent-specific knowledge and belief (pp. 521–523)
- **Proposed test:** show an adult and a child facing a complex device such as an unlit heater. If Sarah imputes knowledge, and her inferences about knowledge are at least approximately correct, her photograph choices should differ appreciably between them. In one sense her success would be of limited interest if she had merely watched adults and children succeed and fail (pp. 521–522).
- **Adult/child test.** A four-year-old girl and an adult woman, both unknown to Sarah, each in six tasks: object and shape match-to-sample, memory, completion, causal inference, spatial matching. Each task had a correct and an incorrect photograph. Sarah chose the correct one on 10 of 12 trials (5 for the adult and 5 for the child) (p. 522).
- The authors call this negative outcome indeterminate. Sarah had little experience with children, and the six tasks are not well graded in difficulty. Four-and-a-half-year-old children can, they recalled in retrospect, do all six. So, if Sarah's two errors are ignored, her choices could be read as an accurate assessment of both adult and child; the authors propose this facetiously and say they will correct for the indeterminacy in future tests (p. 522).
- **Embedded videotape (not yet used).** A tape shows observer O watching participant P on tape. P is locked in a cage picking keys while a lion approaches. O gets two photographs: P escapes, or P does not. Sarah watches O and must choose the photograph O would choose (p. 522).
  - Sarah is told whether O likes or dislikes P, using anonymous actors. If she picks O1 giving untoward outcomes to P1 (disliked) and O2 giving benign ones to P2, the authors say "we can conclude" she believes O1 dislikes P1 and O2 likes P2, so that she imputes attitudes (p. 522).
  - Conservation version: Sarah must decide not merely whether children or adults are capable of conservation, but whether children or adults *know* that children or adults are capable of it (pp. 522–523).
- **The chimpanzee as learning theorist.** An animal that ascribes different knowledge to naive and experienced individuals must know something about learning or maturation. The authors list questions: what process does the animal infer, and does it see the adult as a transformed child, or the human as a transformed chimpanzee? All remain to be answered (p. 523).

*Intuition note:* The embedded tape makes clear that the question is not merely what Sarah knows about the world but what she knows about what someone else knows or believes (p. 522).

### Epistemic states: a programme (pp. 523–525)
- Distinguishing know from guess, and truth from deceit, matters in human communication, for example when judging an informant. Whether it plays any role in other species' communication is still largely unknown (p. 523). The authors do not yet know whether Sarah distinguishes guess from know or pretend from real (p. 523).
- **Guess versus know.** Three contrast sets exemplify the two states: a naive versus an experienced rat at a choice point; a human drawing marbles from an opaque versus a transparent urn and predicting the colour; a judge identifying faces seen briefly versus for long (p. 523). Evidence of grasp would come from transfer, by sorting new cases or applying markers (p. 523).
- The recognition experiment bears on whether Sarah tracks guess/know rather than success/failure. The authors can require her to evaluate only cases where the judge was successful. If she can still separate brief from long viewing, she cannot be discriminating merely between successful and unsuccessful prediction. In that case she also cannot be sorting by viewing time, because viewing time has nothing in common with the urns or rats (p. 523).
- **Pretend versus real: deception.** The authors report laboratory evidence that points toward the chimpanzee ascribing pretending to himself and others. They say it is not definitive and more than one test is needed (p. 524).
  - Setup: a mesh partition and two containers, one baited. The animal knows where the bait is; the trainer does not. If the trainer judges from the animal's behaviour which container is baited and chooses correctly, the chimpanzee is given the bait. Trainers are benevolent (reward the animal and give correct guidance) or selfish (keep the bait, and mislead as sender). They wear distinct costumes. Roles were also reversed, so the animal was both receiver and sender (p. 524).
  - Four young chimpanzees were tested over nearly two years. All possible outcomes occurred: the oldest lies in both production and comprehension, the youngest in comprehension only, the male in production only, and the animal described as a social isolate in neither. "Lying" here means false positives (directing to the wrong container) (p. 524).
  - The authors say "lying" is used for convenience. To justify it they would need to know that the animal believes the trainer knows where the bait is and misleads on purpose (p. 524).
  - **Follow-up test (100 containers):** liar L (red hat) is present at baiting and gives bad advice; fool F (green hat) is absent and guesses. Both give bad advice. Does the animal tell them apart, as a human sees L as guilty by intent and F by ignorance? The experiment is under way (p. 524).
  - After two or three experiences with the selfish trainer, Sarah threw objects at him, aggressively enough that the authors judged continuing dangerous. This might be a response to a liar or simply to anyone who misguides her. The authors also ask whether Sarah knew the selfish trainer was a liar, since she never saw him watch the baiting. They note she had always met trainers who knew the answer, and say the 100-container experiment will distinguish the alternatives (p. 524).
- **Pretend-problems.** A Köhler-type problem has a physical factor (food out of reach) and a psychological one (the actor is trying to get it). Crossing them gives four cases. Pretend versions can simulate the psychological factor, for example an actor jumps for an apple to fool the escape-artist chimpanzee Burt. A tape showing both viewpoints could test whether Sarah separates genuine from simulated problem solving (p. 525).
- **Self-knowledge.** The same method can ask whether Sarah applies markers such as truth/lie and know/guess to her own acts, which bears on how self-knowledge develops (p. 525).

### Positivists and young children (p. 525)
- The authors name two groups that might not read the scenes mentally. They think highly trained "positivists" first read the scene inferentially, then inhibit the inference and describe movements only. Very young children, shown scenes out of sequence, give disjointed descriptions, a bare list of elements such as a man, a dog and a tree. Children younger than about four could not make sense of out-of-order picture stories (p. 525).
- The authors hold that assigning mental states is primitive, not sophisticated. They say the inferences are absent only when understanding is too poor or when they are deliberately suppressed (p. 525).
- **Analogy with causal inference:** the authors think "A precedes B, so A causes B" is the primitive belief. The sophisticated adult blocks it and asks four questions: does A occur without B, does B occur without A, and so on; the source prints four questions, two of them repeating the others. If the answers are appropriate, the adult lets the inference through. The content of a sophisticated inference may not differ importantly from the primitive one; the process does (p. 525).

### Concluding remarks (pp. 525–526)
- Theory-of-mind inferences are, to the authors' knowledge, universal in human adults. They say acquisition is more reminiscent of walking or speech than of reading or arithmetic, and the only direct impact of pedagogy would appear to be suppressive (p. 525).
- Comparative studies use the same tapes, with a few exceptions, on normal and retarded children. They ask whether chimpanzees infer at all, whether at least some retarded children lack this form of theory building, and how it develops (p. 526).
- **Speculation.** Of all possible guesses the authors find this the most compelling: inferences about motivation precede those about knowledge, across species and developmental stages. They say not even the chimpanzee will fail tests that require imputing wants, purposes or affective attitudes, but he may fail when required to impute knowledge. If he distinguishes guess from know but judges adult versus child acts poorly, this cannot be simply because he does not impute knowledge; it would rather be a deficiency in the accuracy of knowledge content. If he cannot tell guess from know, it may make more sense to consider that he makes no inferences outside the motivational realm (p. 526).
- **Behaviourism.** If mental theories are natural, behaviourism must be unnatural, since it requires suppressing primitive inferences. The authors say there is no way to answer whether mentalism is therefore preferable and more likely to yield valid theories; naturalness does not guarantee validity (they cite Tversky & Kahneman, 1977) (p. 526). If a behaviourist replied that apes' theories are wrong, the authors think this would not save behaviourism, since admitting that animals are mentalists compels the admission that behaviourist accounts of animals are "at best profoundly incomplete". They add, with more than facetious intent, that the ape "could only be a mentalist", and that, unless they are badly mistaken, he is not intelligent enough to be a behaviourist (p. 526).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show why posture matching cannot explain Sarah's score. Suppose four problems, each with a two-photograph choice, and a learner who picks only by matching the actor's posture. Posture separates solution from foil in only 1 of the 4 problems (as in the paper, where the actor was more upright in one problem).
- Matcher: 1 problem solved, plus 3 problems at chance (0.5 each): $1 + 3\times 0.5 = 2.5$ correct out of 4 expected.
- A learner who picks the photograph serving the actor's purpose: 4 of 4.
- Observed in the paper: 21/24 overall, with 6/6 on each of three problems (p. 516). The matcher would expect 2.5/4 = 62.5% against the 21/24 = 87.5% observed. A chance guesser would get 6/6 on one problem only with probability $(1/2)^6 = 1/64$.

- [structural-analogy] Sarah's choices answer "what would the actor do?" ↔ theory of mind: predicting action from imputed goals and knowledge — the paper uses goal imputation as its core inference.

## Questions this raises
- If Sarah tracks purposes, does she also track *false* beliefs? (The paper plans guess/know tests; these pages do not mention false belief.)
- Could any of the planned tests separate "knowing" from "being accurate" in a way that a purely associative learner would fail?
- Is the proposed order, motivation before knowledge, borne out across species and development?
- Does hostility to the selfish trainer reflect an inference about intent, or only about outcomes (p. 524)?
