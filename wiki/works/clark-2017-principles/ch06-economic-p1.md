# The Economic Determinants of Democracy and Dictatorship (part 1/2): modernization theory vs. the survival story

## L0 — One line
Rich countries are mostly democratic, but this fact fits two theories; the authors argue that conditional transition probabilities in the 1950–1990 data favour modernization theory (income helps democracy emerge and survive) over Przeworski's survival story (income helps survival only).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do some states become democratic and others not, and why does democracy survive in some states but not others? Classic modernization theory says economic development promotes both the emergence and the survival of democracy. Przeworski's "survival story" says income helps democracy survive but does not help it emerge. Both predict that democracy is more common in rich countries, so correlation alone cannot decide between them. The authors derive implications and test them with data from Przeworski et al. (2000), using probabilities of transition rather than raw counts. The data appear to contradict the survival story's claims about emergence and about total transitions, and are consistent with modernization theory. This part covers the first half of the chapter.

**Prerequisites:** reading line graphs; conditional probability as a ratio of counts; the idea of competing theories with shared and divergent implications.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Classic modernization theory says development (smaller agriculture, larger industry and services) moves societies from dictatorship toward democracy, and helps democracy survive (pp. 172–173).
- Income is measured as GDP per capita (value of all goods and services produced in a year, divided by population) in 1985 PPP (purchasing power parity) U.S. dollars. PPP exchange rates compare the price of a bundle of goods across countries; ordinary exchange rates do not reflect price differences well (pp. 174–175).
- Democracy is far more common at high income (Figs 6.2, 6.4). This is consistent with modernization theory and with Przeworski's survival story, so it cannot separate them (pp. 174, 176–177, 179–180).
- Przeworski's survival story: rich people lose from gambling on dictatorship, poor people have little to lose. So income helps democracy survive, while emergence may be unrelated to income (pp. 176–177).
- Two stories that explain the same fact must share some implications and differ on others. Researchers should find the divergent ones and design a critical test, that is, a test of an implication on which the two stories differ (pp. 177–178).
- Raw transition counts can mislead, because poor country-years (one country observed for one year) are more numerous. Probabilities conditional on income level are needed (pp. 180–181).
- Probability of any transition shows no strong relation to income, which would seem to falsify implication 4b (transitions become less likely as income rises). Probability of transition to democracy rises with income, in direct contradiction to the survival story and consistent with modernization theory (pp. 181–183).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter opening (pp. 171–172)
The chapter overview lists claims developed in the chapter. This part covers the first: classic modernization theory and its evidence (p. 171). Later claims (a variant theory, natural resources, aid, inequality, performance) are announced on p. 171 but fall outside these pages.

Two questions: why do some states become democratic and others not, and why does democracy survive in some states but fail in others? This chapter looks at "economic arguments", meaning economic development and the structure of the economy. The authors say this illustrates how the predatory view of the state (Chapter 4) can explain regime type. Cultural arguments come in the next chapter (p. 172).

### Classic modernization theory (pp. 172–174)
- A *paradigm* is a family of explanations. Modernization theory says all societies pass through the same historical stages of economic development (p. 172).
- After decolonization, poorer countries were seen as an earlier stage of this linear process. Rostow (1960) and Gerschenkron (1962) are cited. "Immature" societies have large agriculture and small industry and services. "Mature" ones have small agriculture, large industry and services, more urbanization and education, and more societal "complexity" (p. 172).
- Footnote 1: scholars later believed countries could jump stages by copying others, which helped explain Soviet advance (p. 172).
- Political scientists, most famously Lipset (1959, 1960), took the theory up. Dictatorships might be sustainable in immature societies, but not in mature ones (p. 172).
- Przeworski et al. (2000, 88) summarize it. As a country develops, its social structure becomes complex, new groups organize, and workers gain autonomy and private information. Command-style control loses effectiveness. The bourgeoisie, workers or "civil society" rise against the dictatorship and it falls (p. 173).
- Democracy is thus "secreted" out of dictatorship by development. The authors say Lipset (1959, 75) argues the theory also implies survival: "the more well-to-do a nation, the greater the chances that it will sustain democracy" (p. 173).
- Classic modernization theory therefore predicts that development helps (a) the emergence and (b) the survival of democracy (Figure 6.1: traditional society = large agriculture, small industry, small service, dictatorship; modern = the reverse, democracy) (p. 173).
- The authors note that the terminology ("primitive", "backward", "third world", "underdeveloped", "developing") has changed because of discomfort with its implications. They say discomfort is no reason to reject a theory; one must ask whether the evidence falsifies it (pp. 173–174).

**Intuition note:** The theory has two parts: richer countries become democratic (emergence) and stay democratic (survival). Keeping these parts separate matters for the rest of the unit.

### Evidence: income and democracy (pp. 174–176)
- A central implication is a strong relationship between development and democracy. Figure 6.2 plots the proportion of democracies at different income levels, using Przeworski et al. (2000) data, 1950–1990 (p. 174).
- Result: countries are much more likely to be democratic at high income. Virtually all with GDP per capita above $8,000 are democracies. Only 12 percent of those below $1,500 are, so 88 percent are dictatorships (pp. 174–175).
- **GDP per capita** = value of all goods and services produced in a country in a period (usually a year), divided by population (p. 174).
- **Box 6.1 (comparing income):** income is the common indicator of development because countries tend to become richer as they develop (p. 175).
  - GDP per capita is normally measured in local currency, so to compare countries it must be converted to a common currency. Using one country's actual exchange rate is considered problematic because exchange rates do not reflect price differences across countries (p. 175).
  - The standard method is purchasing power parity (PPP). It prices a bundle of goods in each country's currency and takes the ratio of the two prices as the exchange rate (p. 175).
  - Example: if a Big Mac costs US$4 in the U.S. and £3 in Britain, the PPP rate is £3 for $4 (p. 175).
  - The usual PPP rate compares goods with equivalent U.S. goods ("PPP U.S. dollars") and is fixed at one date (here 1985) because inflation changes currency values over time (p. 175).

### The survival story (pp. 176–177)
- The data fit modernization theory, but also a "survival story": increasing income promotes the survival of democracy but does not affect whether a country becomes democratic in the first place (p. 176).
- **Przeworski's reasoning (as reported by the authors):** the choice between democracy and dictatorship depends on the outcomes citizens expect. In democracy, resources are spread relatively broadly, so at least a moderate level of consumption is expected. In dictatorship, people do extremely well inside the dictator's circle and extremely poorly outside it, and the chance of being inside is small (p. 176).
- A wealthy person would face a small chance of getting richer and a large chance of losing everything, so switching is a gamble. According to Przeworski, most wealthy people therefore prefer to stay in democracy (p. 176).
- A poor person has "nothing to lose" and may take the gamble. So democracy tends to be unstable in poor countries, and survival is more likely in rich countries (p. 176).
- The survival story takes the standpoint of actors already in a democracy. Przeworski and coauthors argue that the emergence of democracy may be entirely unrelated to income, and the process by which countries become democratic may be unknowable (p. 177).
- **Flip argument:** suppose a country flips between dictatorship and democracy at random. Flips at high income are helped to stay democratic by that income. Flips at low income likely collapse back. If this story is correct, the result is a world in which nearly all rich countries are democratic, while poor countries keep alternating between democratic and dictatorial episodes (p. 177).
- Unless countries for some unknown reason flip to democracy more often than to dictatorship, democracies will on average be richer than dictatorships (p. 177).

**Intuition note:** Imagine regimes flipping at random. Rich democracies would tend to stay democratic and poor ones would likely collapse back, so over time rich countries pile up as democracies even though wealth never "caused" the switch to democracy.

### Competing theories and the critical test (pp. 177–179)
- Both stories predict that democracy is more likely in high-income countries, so the pattern in Figure 6.2 cannot decide between them (p. 177).
- Competing explanations of the same phenomenon must share some implications (otherwise they would not explain the same thing) and differ on others (otherwise they would not be different). The task is to find divergent implications and a critical test (pp. 177–178; refers back to Chapter 2).
- Boix and Stokes (2003) plot expected probabilities of transition to dictatorship and to democracy against income (Figure 6.3). Both stories: transition to dictatorship falls with income. Modernization theory: transition to democracy rises with income. Survival story: it is flat (p. 178).
- The probability of any transition is the sum of the probabilities of the two kinds of transition, each weighted by how frequent that kind is (p. 178).
- Under the survival story, the probability of any transition falls with income. Democracies become more stable and dictatorships' stability is unaffected (p. 179).
- Under modernization theory the effect on any transition is ambiguous. Higher income stabilizes democracy but destabilizes dictatorship, and the theory does not say which effect is stronger (p. 179).
- Footnote 2: Przeworski et al. (2000, 88–92) are clear that, as income rises, the probability of transition to democracy stays constant while the probability of transition to dictatorship falls (p. 179).

**Table 6.1 (p. 179)**

| | Modernization theory | Survival story |
|---|---|---|
| Shared 1 | Democracy is more common in rich than poor countries | same |
| Shared 2 | Transitions to dictatorship become less likely as income rises | same |
| 3a / 3b | Transitions to democracy become more likely with income | Transitions to democracy are unaffected by income |
| 4a / 4b | Regime transitions may or may not become less likely as countries get richer | Regime transitions become less likely as countries get richer |

### Evaluating the implications (pp. 179–183)
**Implication 1.** Democracy is more common at high income. This is confirmed by Figure 6.2 and by Figure 6.4, which plots country-years under each regime by income (p. 179).
- Below $2,000: about 900 country-years of dictatorship and 100 of democracy (almost 9 in 10 under dictatorship).
- Above $8,000: virtually all country-years are democratic.
- Between $4,000 and $6,000: about equal numbers (p. 179).

**Implication 4 (first attempt, raw counts).** The authors name two critical implications that can separate the theories: (a) the frequency of regime transitions in general and (b) the effect of income on transitions to democracy (p. 180). Figure 6.5 plots the number of transitions to democracy, to dictatorship and in total, by income (p. 180).
- Poor countries seem to have more transitions, which seems to support the survival story (p. 180).
- But raw counts are misleading. The number of possible transitions depends on how many countries are democracies or dictatorships at each income level, and the world has had more experience with poverty than with wealth. Even with equal probability, more poor countries mean more transitions. So Figure 6.5 is not sufficient to show that transitions become less likely with income (p. 180).

**Probability of a transition at income level.** The authors use (p. 181):

$$\Pr(\text{Regime Transition} \mid \text{Income Level}) = \frac{\text{Number of Transitions to Democracy or Dictatorship}}{\text{Number of Country Years}}\bigg|_{\text{Income Level}}$$

- It is the total number of transitions at that income level divided by the number of country-years at that level (p. 181).
- Figure 6.6: there is no strong relationship between income and this probability. It does not appear to decline linearly with income as the survival story predicts. The authors say this "would seem to falsify" implication 4b (p. 181).
- The rise at low incomes fits modernization theory, because a certain amount of resources may be necessary for any change. The fall at high incomes also fits it, because by then democracy should have emerged in nearly all countries and has no reason not to survive (p. 181).

**Intuition note:** To compare risk across groups of different size, divide events by the number of cases that could have had the event. Counting events alone mixes up "risky" with "common".

**Implications 2 and 3 (direction-specific probabilities).** The authors say the evidence suggests the survival story is wrong in predicting that transitions decline linearly with income, but the key implication separating the theories is whether higher income makes transitions to democracy more likely. Figure 6.6 pooled both directions, so they compute separate probabilities with different denominators (p. 182):

$$\Pr(\text{Transition to Democracy} \mid \text{Income Level}) = \frac{\text{Number of Transitions to Democracy}}{\text{Number of Autocratic Country Years}}\bigg|_{\text{Income Level}}$$

$$\Pr(\text{Transition to Dictatorship} \mid \text{Income Level}) = \frac{\text{Number of Transitions to Dictatorship}}{\text{Number of Democratic Country Years}}\bigg|_{\text{Income Level}}$$

(Denominator: only country-years in the regime one could leave. Added step: this follows from the formulas on p. 182.)

Figure 6.7 results (pp. 182–183):
- Although Figure 6.6 showed little consistent effect of income on transitions in general, Figure 6.7 clearly shows that the kind of transition depends on income (p. 183).
- As both stories predict, the probability of transition to dictatorship declines as income rises, so high income encourages democratic survival (Implication 2) (p. 183).
- Contradicting the survival story and consistent with modernization theory, the probability of transition to democracy increases with income (Implication 3a). Countries seem more likely to become democratic as income increases (p. 183).
- Odds are shown as multiples in the figure. Below $2,000, transition to dictatorship is 18 times more likely than transition to democracy. Above $6,000, transition to democracy is six times more likely than transition to dictatorship (p. 183). Another label: at $4,000, dictatorship is twice as likely as democracy (figure note, p. 183).

**Intuition note:** Total change is not consistently less likely in rich countries (Fig. 6.6 shows no strong relationship). What differs more clearly is the direction: at low income transitions to dictatorship are more likely, at high income transitions to democracy (p. 183).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Invented counts for two income groups, to show why raw counts mislead and why direction-specific probabilities matter.

| | Poor | Rich |
|---|---|---|
| Autocratic country-years | 900 | 40 |
| Democratic country-years | 100 | 60 |
| Total country-years | 1000 | 100 |
| Transitions to democracy | 18 | 2 |
| Transitions to dictatorship | 10 | 1 |
| Total transitions | 28 | 3 |

- Raw counts: 28 vs 3, so poor countries seem far less stable.
- Pr(transition | income): poor 28/1000 = 0.028; rich 3/100 = 0.03. Almost equal, so the raw gap was caused by many more poor country-years.
- Pr(to democracy): poor 18/900 = 0.02; rich 2/40 = 0.05. It rises with income.
- Pr(to dictatorship): poor 10/100 = 0.10; rich 1/60 ≈ 0.0167. It falls with income.
- Odds: poor 0.10/0.02 = 5 times more likely toward dictatorship; rich 0.05/0.0167 = 3 times more likely toward democracy.

This pattern (flat total, rising democracy probability, falling dictatorship probability) resembles what the chapter reports as supporting modernization theory. A survival-story world would instead keep Pr(to democracy) equal across the two groups.

## Concepts
- [[modernization-theory]] (new) — family of explanations: all societies pass through the same stages of development, and development (less agriculture, more industry and services) promotes democracy's emergence and survival (pp. 172–173)
- [[survival-story]] (new) — Przeworski's view: income helps democracy survive but does not affect whether a country becomes democratic (p. 176)
- [[gdp-per-capita-ppp]] (new) — GDP divided by population, converted into common currency by purchasing-power-parity rates, here 1985 U.S. dollars (pp. 174–175)
- [[country-year]] (new) — one country observed for one year. It is the unit for counting regimes and transitions (p. 179)
- [[conditional-transition-probability]] (new) — transitions divided by country-years at risk, at a given income level; separate denominators for each direction (pp. 181–182)
- [[critical-test]] (new) — a test of an implication on which two competing theories differ, used to choose between them (pp. 177–178)
- [[observable-implications]] — testable consequences deduced from each theory (Table 6.1) (pp. 177–179)
