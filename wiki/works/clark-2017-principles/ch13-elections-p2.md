# Proportional Electoral Systems: List PR and the Single Transferable Vote

## L0 — One line
Proportional electoral systems use multimember districts and a quota or divisor to turn votes into seats, and their proportionality, party-versus-candidate focus and incentives depend on the formula, district magnitude, tiers, thresholds and ballot type.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part of Chapter 13 asks how proportional representation (PR) electoral systems work and how good they are. PR systems use multimember districts and a quota or a divisor to turn votes into seats. The authors give worked numbers for quotas, divisors and remainder rules. They then show how district magnitude, higher tiers, thresholds and list types affect proportionality and the link between voters and representatives. Finally they explain the single transferable vote (STV), the one PR system without party lists, and weigh its advantages and criticisms. It matters because the formula and the rules change who wins seats, how many votes are wasted, and how parties and candidates behave.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and division; the idea of a multimember district and of majoritarian systems (from part 1 of the chapter); the alternative vote (ranking candidates).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- PR systems are defined by their mechanics (multimember districts plus a quota or divisor), not by the proportional outcomes they produce, to avoid circular reasoning (p. 564).
- Advocates say PR gives a more accurate votes-to-seats translation and better minority representation. Critics point to coalition governments, extremist and powerful small parties, and a weak constituency link (pp. 565–566).
- Whether PR or preferential voting (voters rank candidates) suits divided societies is disputed. The PR camp stresses power-sharing. The preferential camp stresses incentives for cross-group appeals. The authors add that these are only likely to work if groups are not geographically concentrated (pp. 565–566).
- A quota is the number of votes that guarantees a seat. Quota systems (Hare, Hagenbach-Bischoff, Imperiali, Reinforced Imperiali, Droop) give automatic seats plus remainder seats. In divisor systems (d'Hondt, Sainte-Laguë) each party's votes are divided by a series of numbers, and no remainder seats are left. Different formulas give different seat totals from the same votes (pp. 567–570).
- District magnitude (seats per district) is generally seen as the most important influence on proportionality. Higher electoral tiers (levels at which votes become seats), natural and formal thresholds (minimum support needed to win any seat), and apparentement (parties pooling their votes) also shape who gets seats (pp. 571–574).
- Closed, open and free party lists decide which candidates fill a party's seats. They shift power between party leaders and voters and change incentives for a personal vote (pp. 574–578).
- STV is a candidate-centered PR system with ranked ballots. Votes pass from eliminated candidates and from the surplus (votes above what an elected candidate needs) of elected candidates (pp. 578–581).
- STV's claimed advantages: rich preference information, few wasted votes, no need for parties, a strong local link. Its criticisms: intraparty factionalism and difficulty in large districts (pp. 581–582).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Proportional electoral systems (pp. 564–566)

**Link to the previous section.** The unit opens in the middle of the discussion of the party block vote (PBV). The first words are the end of a sentence begun on the previous page, which refers to "highly disproportional results that are harmful to minority groups". As of 2004, countries that use the PBV for all or significant portions of their legislatures include Cameroon and Chad (p. 564).

**Definition.** The rationale behind most PR systems is to reduce the gap between a party's vote share and its seat share. The goal is proportional outcomes, both within districts and in the nation as a whole. A party with 10% of the vote should get 10% of the seats (p. 564).
- The authors do not want to define PR by its outcome (as some scholars, e.g. Cox 1997, do). First, PR systems differ in how proportional they are, and it is even possible, under some circumstances, for a majoritarian system to regularly produce more proportional outcomes than a PR system (Barkan 1995). Second, if we want to explain proportionality by system type, an outcome-based definition would be circular (p. 564).
- All PR systems share two features: multimember districts, because a single seat cannot be divided proportionally, and a quota or divisor that fixes the votes needed to win a seat (p. 564).
- Definition used: a *proportional electoral system is a quota- or divisor-based system employed in multimember districts* (p. 564).
- Footnote 14: some majoritarian systems are quota-based (majority-runoff, AV), but all but one of them are used in single-member districts. The exception is Mali, which uses a party block vote with an absolute-majority requirement in multimember districts. To avoid this ambiguity, PR could be defined as "nonmajoritarian" quota- or divisor-based systems in multimember districts (p. 564).
- The two main types (Figure 13.1) are list PR and the single transferable vote (STV) (p. 564).

**Claimed advantages (many scholars, e.g. Lijphart 1999; p. 565).**
- PR tends to translate votes into seats more accurately. This avoids a party winning many votes but few seats, one of the possible anomalies of majoritarian systems (p. 565).
- Small parties win seats in proportion to their size, so minorities are likely to be better represented (p. 565).
- Small parties have a greater chance of winning, so voters face weaker incentives to vote strategically. Outcomes should therefore better reflect sincere preferences. Arguably, turnout is also higher, because votes are less likely to be wasted (Blais and Carty 1990; Blais and Dobrzynska 1998) (p. 565).

**Divided societies: two camps (p. 565).**
- **PR camp (Lijphart; Norris):** some have argued that PR is "all but essential" for ethnically and religiously divided societies. Their claim is that stakes are often high in a bitterly divided society and the risks of state instability are too great for parties to treat elections as winner-take-all (Reynolds 2011). PR lets groups form ethnic or religious parties with representation in proportion to their size, which gives legislatures that reflect society and coalition governments built on power-sharing (p. 565). The authors note that this implicitly assumes the groups will ultimately choose to work together (p. 565).
- **Preferential-voting camp (Horowitz; Reilly):** PR may simply replicate societal divisions in the legislature without incentives for cooperation. Preferential systems (AV, supplementary vote) push parties toward broadly based centrist appeals, because their success is likely to depend on preference transfers from other groups (p. 565).
- The authors add a condition. This is only likely if ethnic groups are not geographically concentrated in such a way that parties can win a majority by appealing to a single group (p. 565).
- Footnote 15 (Fiji): the AV was introduced in 1997 to encourage cross-ethnic appeals. Things "did not work out entirely as planned": small districts and the geographic concentration of indigenous and Indo-Fijians left too few mixed constituencies. Some have argued that the AV precipitated the collapse of Fijian democracy following a coup in 2000 (Reynolds 2011) (p. 565).
- The authors frame the choice as replicating ethnic divisions in the legislature and hoping leaders cooperate afterwards, versus institutional incentives to weaken the salience of ethnicity (p. 566).
- A common complaint against AV and SV is that they are majoritarian and disproportional (Lijphart 1997). The STV, which works in multimember districts and is relatively proportional, is an alternative (p. 566).

**Criticisms of PR (p. 566).**
1. PR tends to produce coalition governments. Accountability is hard because responsibility for policy is unclear. Parties that lose many votes often return to coalitions anyway. Coalitions are also more unstable than single-party majority governments (Chapter 12 evidence).
2. PR lets small extremist parties win seats. Some argue that such parties undermine democracy, for example the Nazi Party in Weimar Germany.
3. Small parties in government formation can get concessions out of proportion to their support. Seat majorities are rare, so large parties often need a small partner. In Israel, ultrareligious parties have won policies by threatening to leave the government.
4. The link between constituents and representatives is weak, because no single representative is responsible for policy in a district. Voters might also wonder which representative represents them.

### List PR systems (pp. 566–578)

In a *list PR system* each party presents a list of candidates in each multimember district, and parties get seats in proportion to their overall vote share (p. 566). List systems differ in four ways: (a) the formula for allocating seats to parties, (b) district magnitude and higher tiers, (c) thresholds, (d) the type of party list (pp. 566–567).

#### Electoral formulas: quotas and divisors (pp. 567–571)

A *quota* is the number of votes that guarantees a party a seat in a district (p. 567). Five quotas are in common use: Hare, Hagenbach-Bischoff, Imperiali, Reinforced Imperiali, Droop (p. 567).

$$Q(n)=\frac{V_d}{M_d+n}$$

Here $V_d$ is the total number of valid votes in district $d$, $M_d$ is the number of seats in $d$, and $n$ is the modifier (p. 567).
- $n=0$: Hare. $n=1$: Hagenbach-Bischoff (H-B). $n=2$: Imperiali. $n=3$: Reinforced Imperiali (p. 567).
- The Droop quota is the H-B quota plus one, with any decimal part removed (p. 567). Footnote 16 says many scholars wrongly call the H-B quota "Droop" because the two are so similar (p. 567).
- Worked numbers, a district with 10 seats and 100,000 valid votes (p. 567):
  - Hare: $100{,}000/10=10{,}000$.
  - H-B: $100{,}000/(10+1)=9{,}090.9$.
  - Imperiali: $100{,}000/(10+2)=8{,}333$.
  - Reinforced Imperiali: $100{,}000/(10+3)=7{,}692$.
  - Droop: $9{,}090.9+1=9{,}091.9$, then remove the decimal part, giving $9{,}091$.

**Hare quota example (Table 13.7, p. 568).** Ten seats, 100,000 votes, quota 10,000. Votes are A 47,000; B 16,000; C 15,800; D 12,000; E 6,100; F 3,100. Votes/quota: 4.7, 1.6, 1.58, 1.2, 0.61, 0.31 (p. 568). Full quotas give automatic seats: A 4, B 1, C 1, D 1, E 0, F 0. That is 7 seats, leaving 3 *remainder seats* (pp. 567–568).

Three methods allocate remainder seats: largest remainder (LR), highest average (HA) and modified highest average (mHA). LR is by far the most common (pp. 567–568).

**Largest remainder (Table 13.8, p. 568).** The remainder is the fraction of a Hare quota left unused. Remainders are A 0.7, B 0.6, C 0.58, D 0.2, E 0.61, F 0.31. Remainder seats go in order of largest remainder: A, then E (0.61), then B (0.6). Totals: A 5, B 2, C 1, D 1, E 1, F 0 (10 seats). Hare with LR was used as of 2004 in Colombia, Honduras, Namibia and Sierra Leone (p. 568).

**Highest average (Table 13.9, p. 569).** Divide each party's votes by its automatic seats, which gives the average votes "paid" per seat: A 11,750; B 16,000; C 15,800; D 12,000. Remainder seats go to the highest averages: B, C, D. Totals: A 4, B 2, C 2, D 2, E 0, F 0. Benin and Brazil used Hare with HA as of 2004 (p. 569).
- Footnote 17: mHA divides by automatic seats plus one. No country uses it with the Hare quota. Luxembourg uses it with the Droop quota (p. 569).

**Divisor (highest average) systems (p. 569).** These do not use quotas. Each party's votes are divided by a series of divisors to give quotients, and seats go to the highest quotients. Three are common: d'Hondt, Sainte-Laguë and modified Sainte-Laguë (p. 569). Footnote 18: d'Hondt was known in the United States as the Jefferson method and Sainte-Laguë as the Webster method (p. 569).

**d'Hondt example (Table 13.10, p. 570).** Divide votes by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, … The ten largest quotients win. Seat order is in brackets:
- A: 47,000 (1), 23,500 (2), 15,666 (5), 11,750 (7), 9,400 (8).
- B: 16,000 (3), 8,000 (9).
- C: 15,800 (4), 7,900 (10).
- D: 12,000 (6).
- E and F win no seats (quotients such as 6,100 and 3,100 are too small).
- Totals: A 5, B 2, C 2, D 1, E 0, F 0 (p. 570).

Divisor systems leave no remainder seats (p. 570). d'Hondt is the most common divisor system (Argentina, Bulgaria, Portugal, Turkey, others) (p. 570).

Footnote 19 gives the general d'Hondt rule. The quotient is $V/(S+1)$, where $V$ is a list's valid votes and $S$ is the seats it has been allocated so far, starting at 0. The party with the highest quotient gets the next seat, and its quotient is recalculated (p. 570).

Sainte-Laguë uses divisors 1, 3, 5, 7, …, and is used in Latvia. The general quotient is $V/(2S+1)$ (footnote 20) (p. 570). The modified Sainte-Laguë, used in Sweden, uses 1.4, 3, 5, 7, 9, … (p. 570).

**Proportionality of formulas (Table 13.11, pp. 570–571).** Formulas differ in proportionality, and some help small parties more than others. Proportionality also depends to some extent on how votes are distributed among parties. The "widely accepted overall ranking", from most to least proportional, is: Hare LR, Droop LR, Sainte-Laguë, Imperiali LR, modified Sainte-Laguë, d'Hondt, Imperiali HA. All are more proportional than SMDP systems (p. 571).

*Intuition note.* A quota says how many votes guarantee one seat. With quotas, leftover votes then need a rule for the last seats. A divisor system gives out all seats by quotients and leaves no remainder seats. Different rules can give different seat totals from the same votes, so the formula matters, as the Hare LR and d'Hondt tables above show for the same district.

#### District magnitude and higher electoral tiers (pp. 571–572)

District magnitude (seats per district) is generally recognized as the most important factor for proportionality (Cox 1997; Lijphart 1994; Rae 1967; Taagepera and Shugart 1989) (p. 571).
- Larger magnitude means more proportionality, because small parties are much more likely to win seats. A party needs more than 25% of the vote to be guaranteed a seat in a three-seat district, but only a little over 10% in a nine-seat district (p. 571).
- With small magnitude, the outcome is likely to be disproportional whatever formula is used. This is why magnitude is seen as the most important factor (p. 571).
- Average magnitude varies widely among PR countries. The Netherlands and Slovakia elect all 150 legislators in one national district. Chile elects all its legislators in sixty 2-seat districts (p. 572). Low averages: Chile 2.00, Benin 3.50, Cape Verde 4.50. High: Netherlands 150, Slovakia 150, Israel 120, Brazil 19 (p. 572).
- As magnitude and the geographical size of the district increase, the voter–representative link is likely to weaken (p. 572).

**Electoral tiers.** An *electoral tier* is a level at which votes are translated into seats. The lowest tier is the constituency, and higher tiers group constituencies (usually regional or national) (p. 572). Some countries use two tiers to combine a strong link with proportionality (p. 572).
- Some seats are filled in small constituencies. Some supplemental seats are kept for a higher tier and go to parties that are underrepresented at the lower tier (p. 572).
- Each party's votes not used to win constituency seats are pooled in the higher tier, and the supplemental seats are allocated on these "unused votes" (p. 572).
- Examples: about 11% of Swedish seats are allocated nationally to parties and cartels whose constituency seat share is below their vote share. Venezuelan parties underrepresented at the constituency level can get limited compensatory seats (p. 572).
- Footnote 21: these tiers are "linked", meaning the higher-tier allocation depends on lower-tier results. This is by far the most common arrangement. A few countries (Poland, Guatemala) have unlinked tiers. These do not usually reach as much proportionality, because they ignore unused lower-tier votes (p. 572).

#### Electoral thresholds (pp. 572–574)

An *electoral threshold* is the minimum support a party needs for representation, nationally or in a district. It can be formal (written in law) or natural (a mathematical by-product of the system, such as district magnitude) (pp. 572–573). The size of the threshold has a strong effect on proportionality (p. 573). Footnote 22: majoritarian systems also have thresholds (for example, the AV majority requirement), but thresholds matter more in PR because its goal is proportional outcomes (p. 573).
- **Natural threshold.** All electoral systems have one (p. 573). Netherlands: 150 seats in one district gives $100\%/150=0.67\%$, so a party must win more than this to gain a seat (p. 573). Israel's natural threshold is only 0.83% (p. 573).
- **Formal thresholds.** Israel: 2% nationally since 2003. Turkey: more than 10%. Poland: 5% for parties and 8% for coalitions. Germany: 5% of the national vote or three constituency seats (p. 573). The authors state that formal thresholds always increase disproportionality, because votes for parties that might otherwise have won are wasted (p. 573).
- **Purpose.** Formal thresholds are often meant to reduce party-system fragmentation. Germany's 5% threshold was largely a response to the fractious Weimar party system. Many Eastern European countries set high thresholds to consolidate stable party systems (p. 573).
- **Apparentement.** Some countries want to block only small extremist parties. One potential way is a formal threshold plus *apparentement*, which lets two or more parties agree that their votes are combined for seat allocation. Cartel parties stay separate on the ballot and campaign independently, but their votes count together for passing the threshold (p. 573). The authors add a condition. To the extent that extremist parties find it harder to get partners, this helps non-extremist small parties and makes it hard for extremists to win seats (pp. 573–574). It is used in Israel and some Latin American and European countries (p. 574).
- **Effects.** In Turkey in 2002, 46% of votes were wasted because many parties missed the 10% threshold. In Poland in 1993, 34% were wasted under the 5%/8% thresholds. These wasted votes were crucial in letting the former Communists return to power (Kaminski, Lissowski and Swistak 1998) (p. 574). The authors ask whether wasted votes and disproportionality are more or less acceptable than the fragmentation that thresholds are meant to fix (p. 574).

*Intuition note.* A natural threshold follows from arithmetic: with 150 seats, a party must win more than 1/150 of the vote to get a seat. A formal threshold adds a legal bar on top. Votes for parties below the bar are wasted, so they do not count toward seats.

#### Types of party list (pp. 574–578)

The party list type decides which candidates get the seats a party has won (p. 574).

**Closed list** (nonpreferential or blocked): the party fixes the order, and voters cannot express a candidate preference. Seats go from the top of the list down; with four seats, the top four candidates win (p. 574). Ballots may show candidate names and list positions. More often they show only party names and symbols, perhaps a leader's photograph, as on South Africa's 1994 ballot (Figure 13.4) (p. 574).
- Potential advantage: parties can more easily include candidates who might have trouble being elected, such as ethnic or linguistic minorities or women (p. 574).
- Potential disadvantage: voters cannot choose their preferred candidates, and may have to elect unpopular ones to support their party (pp. 574, 576).
- Party leaders often prefer closed lists, because placement toward the top or bottom lets them reward or discipline candidates. Parties therefore tend to matter more than individual candidates (p. 576).

**Open list** (preferential or unblocked): voters can indicate a preferred candidate within the party as well as the party (p. 576).
- In most open list systems the voter decides whether to name a candidate. Over 25% of Swedish voters regularly choose a candidate, and many of these candidates would not have been elected under a closed list (p. 576).
- Denmark (Figure 13.5): a voter casts a single vote for a party (party vote) or for a candidate on its list (personal vote). Both types count equally in deciding the party's seats. Each candidate is credited with all his personal votes plus a share of the party votes, and the party's seats go to candidates in order of total votes credited (p. 576).
- In countries like Brazil, Finland and the Netherlands the voter must vote for a candidate. Party seats follow total candidate votes, and candidates are ordered by their individual votes (p. 576).
- Compared with closed lists, open lists give voters more freedom and weaken party leaders' control (p. 576). A frequent consequence is internal party fighting, because co-partisans compete for the same votes. Candidates then have incentives to cultivate a personal vote rather than a party vote (Carey and Shugart 1995) (p. 576). A *personal vote* is a vote based on the candidate's characteristics rather than the party's (p. 576). The authors compare it to US legislators bringing pork-barrel projects to their districts, and note that similar incentives exist in multimember open list districts (pp. 576–577).
- Some scholars worry that open lists make minority candidates less likely to be elected. In Sri Lanka, majority Sinhalese parties put Tamil candidates in winnable list positions. Many voters deliberately voted for lower-placed Sinhalese candidates, making this ineffective (p. 577).

**Free list:** voters have multiple votes, which they can give to candidates within one list or across lists (p. 578).
- *Panachage* is voting for candidates from different party lists (p. 578). *Cumulation* is giving more than one vote to a single candidate (p. 578).
- Luxembourg: a voter either votes for a party and accepts its ranking, or votes for as many individual candidates as there are seats, whatever their party (p. 578).
- Switzerland is similar, and voters may give up to two votes to the same candidate (p. 578). Parties list names in no particular order, often alphabetical. A voter may (a) pick a candidate, (b) pick one twice, (c) drop a candidate, (d) write in candidates from another list (up to twice each), or (e) vote for the party without naming individuals. The sole condition is that a voter has only as many votes as district seats (p. 578). Party seats follow the party's total votes. Individuals get seats by the number of times their name appears on party lists, including write-ins on other lists (p. 578).

### Single transferable vote (STV) (pp. 578–582)

The STV is the only proportional system without a party list (p. 578). It is used in multimember districts, and voters rank at least one candidate (1st, 2nd, 3rd, …). Like AV, SV and the Borda count, it is a preferential voting system (p. 578).

**Mechanism (p. 578).**
1. A candidate must reach a quota. Votes initially go to each voter's first choice.
2. If too few candidates reach the quota to fill all seats, the candidate with the fewest first-choice votes is eliminated.
3. His votes, and the surplus votes of already elected candidates, are reallocated to remaining candidates.
4. This continues until enough candidates meet the quota to fill all seats.
- The exact transfer process and quota size vary by country. The Hare quota is sometimes used, but the Droop quota is the most common (pp. 578–579).
- The authors say STV is essentially the AV applied in multimember districts (p. 579).

**Worked example (Tables 13.12 and 13.13, pp. 579–581).** Five candidates (Bruce, Shane, Sheila, Glen, Ella) compete for three seats among twenty voters. The Droop quota is

$$\frac{20}{3+1}+1=6$$

so six votes are needed (p. 579). The surplus method is the Clarke method, as used for the Australian Senate (p. 579). Footnote 23 lists other surplus methods: Hare's, Cincinnati, senatorial rules, Meek's (p. 579). Footnote 24: in actual Australian Senate elections, voters must rank all candidates for their vote to count (p. 579).

Ballot profiles (read from the icons in Table 13.12; the text layer does not give them): 4 ballots Bruce > Shane; 2 Shane > Bruce; 8 Sheila > Glen > Ella; 4 Sheila > Ella > Glen; 1 Glen only; 1 Ella only. That is 20 ballots (pp. 579–580). Not everyone ranks all candidates (p. 579).

- **Count 1.** Sheila has 12 first-choice votes and is elected, with 6 surplus votes (p. 579).
- **Surplus transfer (Clarke method).** Sheila's ballots are bundled by second choice. The 8 Glen-second ballots are two-thirds of her 12, so two-thirds of the surplus (4 votes) go to Glen. The 4 Ella-second ballots are one-third, so one-third of the surplus (2 votes) go to Ella (pp. 579–580).
- **Count 2.** No one else has reached 6 (Bruce 4, Shane 2, Glen 1+4=5, Ella 1+2=3; the sums for Glen and Ella follow the text's transfers). Shane has the fewest votes and is eliminated. His two ballots rank Bruce second, so Bruce gets them (p. 580).
- **Count 3.** Bruce has 6 votes and is elected. He has no surplus (pp. 580–581).
- **Count 4.** Two seats are filled and no one else has the quota. Ella is eliminated and only Glen remains, so no further recount is needed. Glen is elected (p. 581).
- **Result:** Sheila, Bruce and Glen win the three seats (p. 581).

*Intuition note.* STV keeps asking whom a vote should help next if its first choice cannot use it. A candidate who has more votes than needed passes the extra on. A candidate with too few is eliminated and his voters' next choices count. The authors say STV minimizes wasted votes, and in the example the three seats are filled one by one.

**Advantages of STV (pp. 581–582).**
- Voters can convey a lot of preference information by ranking all candidates (Bowler and Grofman 2000), unlike yes/no voting for one or more candidates in most majoritarian and list PR systems (p. 581).
- It minimizes wasted votes, because preferences are reallocated when a candidate is elected or eliminated (p. 581).
- Voters can vote across parties. Most list PR systems, except those allowing panachage, do not allow this. It can be useful for issues that cut across party lines, such as abortion (p. 581).
- It is a proportional system that does not require parties, which could matter where parties are yet to organize or elites do not wish to allow them (see Box 13.4) (p. 581).
- Voters keep total control over their vote. As Gallagher, Laver and Mair (2006) note, STV is set apart from list systems, where a preference given to one candidate might end up helping another candidate of the same party, perhaps one the voter does not like. Under STV, a preference for a candidate can never help that person against a candidate the voter ranked higher (p. 581).
- Like other preferential systems, it creates incentives for candidates to appeal beyond their core supporters and run on centrist platforms, because election may well depend on transfers from other social groups. Some scholars therefore advocate preferential systems for divided societies (p. 581).
- Unlike AV and SV, which are majoritarian and can be highly disproportional, STV works in multimember districts and typically produces more proportional outcomes than majoritarian systems. STV thus holds out the possibility of combining relative proportionality with cross-cleavage incentives (pp. 581–582).
- Supporters claim a strong constituency link, because STV is candidate-centered and candidates build personal votes and do constituency service. Katz (1980) is cited for evidence that STV in Ireland leads to local campaigning, district work and little emphasis on ideology and national issues. Sinnott (1992) compares this to the first-past-the-post (SMDP) notion of representation, not to the party representation of list systems (p. 582).
- Strategic incentives are reduced, because votes are less likely to be wasted. They are never entirely absent: Irish parties hand out "candidate cards" to channel transfers, like how-to-vote cards under the Australian AV (p. 582).

**Criticisms of STV (p. 582).**
- It tends to weaken party unity. Co-partisans have incentives to criticize and campaign against each other, and Farrell and McAllister (2000) attribute Irish factionalism and localism to this competition on ranked ballots (p. 582). The incentive is weaker than under the single nontransferable vote, because candidates can expect votes from eliminated fellow party members and so do not want to harm each other too much (p. 582).
- It is hard to operate in large districts, so outcomes tend to be less proportional than under list PR (p. 582). Practically, it is hard where magnitude exceeds ten, since the ballot could carry fifty or more names. The 1995 Australian Senate ballot in New South Wales had 99 candidates and was several feet long (p. 582). The authors find it difficult to believe that voters can rank beyond the first ten or so names. District sizes are therefore small: the largest magnitude in Ireland and Malta is five (p. 582).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
One five-seat district, 100,000 valid votes: X 46,000; Y 30,000; Z 24,000.

**Hare quota with largest remainders.**
- Quota $=100{,}000/5=20{,}000$.
- Votes/quota: X 2.3, Y 1.5, Z 1.2. Automatic seats: X 2, Y 1, Z 1 = 4 seats.
- Remainders: X 0.3, Y 0.5, Z 0.2. One seat is left and goes to Y (0.5).
- Seats: X 2, Y 2, Z 1.

**d'Hondt.** Quotients $V/(S+1)$ with divisors 1, 2, 3:
- X: 46,000; 23,000; 15,333.
- Y: 30,000; 15,000.
- Z: 24,000; 12,000.
- The five largest quotients are 46,000 (X), 30,000 (Y), 24,000 (Z), 23,000 (X), 15,333 (X). The next, Y's 15,000, is sixth.
- Seats: X 3, Y 1, Z 1.

Same votes, different seats: X gets 40% of seats under Hare LR but 60% under d'Hondt, against 46% of votes. This matches the book's ranking, which puts Hare LR above d'Hondt in proportionality (p. 571), though the ranking is a general one and this is one district.

## Concepts
- [[proportional-representation]] (new) — quota- or divisor-based system in multimember districts, defined by mechanics, not outcome (p. 564)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Closed lists let party leaders discipline candidates ↔ principal–agent problem / delegation: leaders control candidates' prospects, as principals use incentives over agents.
- [structural-analogy] Preferential voting encourages centrist, cross-group appeals ↔ spatial model of voting / median voter: candidates move toward the voters they need for transfers.
- [structural-analogy] Fewer wasted votes under PR/STV, so weaker strategic incentives ↔ strategic (sophisticated) voting and paradox of voting: voters' choices depend on whether a vote can matter.
- [structural-analogy] Small parties gaining leverage in coalition formation ↔ veto player: parties whose consent is needed can extract concessions.

## Questions this raises
- If district magnitude drives proportionality most, how large is its effect compared with formula and thresholds, and how would one test this?
- Do the claimed STV effects on cross-ethnic appeals and factionalism hold outside Ireland and Australia?
- Under which geographic distributions of groups do preferential systems encourage cross-group appeals, and when do they fail as in Fiji?
- Do high thresholds reduce extremism, or mainly waste votes, as the Turkey and Poland figures suggest?
