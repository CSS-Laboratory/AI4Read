# Federalism's critics, Bicameralism and Constitutionalism

## L0 — One line
A second legislative chamber and constitutional review by judges can both constrain legislative majorities, and how much they matter depends on the chamber's composition and power, and on how constitutional justice is organised.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part asks how two institutions, a second legislative chamber and judicial constitutional review, limit legislative majorities. It first finishes the critics' case against federalism. Bicameral systems are then classified by congruence (similar composition) and symmetry (equal or near-equal power). All the "strong" examples in Figure 15.3 (different composition plus near-equal power) are federal states. Most states now have higher-law constitutions (entrenched, with a bill of rights and constitutional review), run on an American (decentralised) or European (constitutional court) model. Courts' real power depends on public support and on voters monitoring compliance.

**Prerequisites:** the federal/unitary distinction; parliamentary vs presidential government (Ch. 12); the idea of a status quo that actors can block or change; basic percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Critics of federalism argue it may widen regional inequality and blur who is responsible for outcomes. They also argue it may raise corruption, citing Treisman's finding (p. 691).
- Bicameralism does not change the legislature–executive relationship, but a second chamber can still have a significant effect on the legislative process, even when the upper house is weak. Delay, for example, may in effect kill a bill before an election (pp. 692–693).
- Bicameral systems vary on congruence (how similar the chambers' political composition is) and symmetry (how equal their constitutional power is). "Strong" bicameralism needs incongruence plus symmetry, and all the strong examples in Figure 15.3 are federal (pp. 693, 699–700, 703).
- Malapportionment means some votes weigh more than others because seats are not based on population. It arises in territorial upper chambers where units get equal weight regardless of population, and its level varies widely. In the U.S., the smallest states holding 10% of the population hold 39.7% of Senate seats (pp. 697–698).
- The justification of bicameralism moved from representing social classes, to representing territories in federal states, to improving legislative quality in unitary states (pp. 702–705).
- Virtually all new constitutions are "higher law": entrenched (changeable only by a special amendment procedure), with a bill of rights and constitutional review. Legislative supremacy constitutions (none of the three) are now relatively rare (pp. 707–709).
- Judicial power in practice is often constrained by the "implementation problem": courts rely on others to carry out their rulings. Courts need public support and voter monitoring, so they publicise selectively (pp. 711–712).
- Constitutional justice follows two models. The American model is decentralised, concrete (within a specific case) and a posteriori (after a law is enacted). The European model is a centralised constitutional court; most such courts can also do abstract review (no specific case), which is a priori (before enactment) (pp. 713, 716–717).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Federalism: the critics' case (continued from the previous part) (p. 691)
The page opens mid-argument. The visible part says that regional governments that attract residents and enlarge their tax base will likely prosper relative to others (p. 691).
- **Regional inequality.** In this part of the critics' case, the expectation of greater regional inequality is likely to create conflict and instability. Advantaged communities would demand more autonomy, and disadvantaged ones would try to reverse the asymmetries (p. 691).
- **Accountability.** Critics (not the authors' own claim) argue federalism is just as likely to undermine accountability as to enhance it, as supporters claim. More layers and shared responsibility make blame shifting and credit claiming easier (p. 691).
  - Citizens may not know which level is responsible. If the regional economy does badly, neither level wants responsibility and each will likely try to blame the other (p. 691).
- **Corruption.** If federalism does lower accountability, as critics maintain, it may actually raise corruption. Treisman (2002) finds that more decentralised countries have higher corruption and lower public-goods provision (p. 691).

> **Intuition note.** Dividing power across levels can make it harder for voters to know whom to reward or punish. That is why critics argue federalism may undermine accountability rather than enhance it (p. 691).

### Bicameralism
**Definitions.** A *unicameral* legislature deliberates in one assembly. A *bicameral* legislature deliberates in two distinct assemblies (p. 691). In 2011, 79 of 194 independent states (40.9%) had one (p. 691). Norway is an odd case: legislators are elected as one body and then pick a quarter of themselves to form a second chamber. Disagreements go to a joint session (fn. 10, p. 691).

**Effects on the executive and on legislating.**
- Bicameralism does not affect the legislature–executive relationship. In almost every non-presidential country, "legislative responsibility" refers exclusively to having a majority in the popularly elected lower house (p. 692). Germany and Italy are exceptions, with confidence also linked to the upper house (fn. 11, p. 692).
- A second chamber can still have a significant effect on the legislative process (p. 692).
  - **United States.** The two chambers are equal partners, and each can veto the other's bills and amendments. Legislators can sometimes vote for a popular but ideologically unpalatable version if they know the other chamber will reject it. This position-taking is frequently not possible in a unicameral legislature, where votes are "more often than not legislating" (p. 692). A presidential veto can allow the same behaviour in a unicameral system (fn. 12, p. 692).
  - **United Kingdom.** Since the 1911 and 1949 Parliament Acts, the Lords' only real power is delaying nonfinancial bills for two parliamentary sessions or one calendar year. Money bills can be delayed one month. The Salisbury Convention rules out opposing manifesto promises (fn. 13, p. 692).
    - Delay takes on a whole new light in the year before an election, when it may in effect kill a bill. Thatcher had to postpone dissolving the Greater London Council in 1984 after the Lords rejected her legislation (pp. 692–693).
    - Reversal of a floor vote like this is impossible in a unicameral legislature (p. 693).

**Types of bicameralism.** Two dimensions are used. Congruence/incongruence concerns the membership and the citizens represented. Symmetry/asymmetry concerns the relative power of the chambers (p. 693).

#### Congruent and incongruent bicameralism (pp. 693–699)
- *Congruent* means a similar political composition. *Incongruent* means a different one. It depends on how members are selected and whom they represent. With the same selection method and the same citizens represented, the chambers' preferences are often assumed identical or very similar (p. 693).
- Lower chambers are almost always directly elected with equal weight. Upper chambers use four main methods: heredity, appointment, indirect election and direct election. Since the 1999 House of Lords Act, no contemporary bicameral system predominantly uses heredity (p. 693).

**Box 15.2: Hereditary peers in the British House of Lords (pp. 694–696).**
- As of 2011 the Commons had 650 members and the Lords 830. Hereditary peers hold titles passed from generation to generation (p. 694).
- The Lords was once small: 29 hereditary peers in the fifteenth century under Henry VII. Peerages were sometimes created to secure a pro-monarch majority. Queen Anne created twelve in one day. By 1999 there were 759 hereditary peers (Table 15.2: 742 male, 17 female; Baron/Lord 419, Earl 175, Viscount 102, Marquess 34, Duke 28, Prince 1) (p. 694).
- The 1958 Life Peerages Act allowed unlimited life baronies (non-inheritable, lifelong). In practice all are granted after prime-ministerial proposal, so it increased the prime minister's ability to change the Lords' composition. It also allowed female peers (pp. 694–695).
  - Table 15.3 lists 1,244 life peers created from 1958 to 2011: Thatcher 200, Blair 374, Macmillan 48, Wilson 123 + 80, Heath 56, Callaghan 57, Major 141, Brown 34, Cameron 117, Douglas-Home 14 (p. 695).
- Labour promised in 1997 to remove all hereditary peers. The 1999 House of Lords Act was a compromise: ninety-two hereditary peers stay on an interim basis, so the Lords is now predominantly appointed (p. 695).
- Further reform has stalled (pp. 695–696):
  - 2000: the Wakeham Commission proposed about 20% elected, rest appointed. It was quickly rejected.
  - 2003: a committee offered seven options (fully appointed up to fully elected). All were rejected in votes.
  - 2005: a proposal for 70% elected was rejected.
  - 2007: the Commons backed 80% or 100% elected, but the Lords rejected this and preferred a fully appointed chamber.
  - May 2011: a draft bill proposed 15-year terms, cutting membership by more than half, and 80% or 100% elected. The outcome was undecided.

**Other selection methods (pp. 696–697).**
- *Appointment*: Belize, Germany, Ireland, Jordan, Madagascar, Russia and Thailand have fully or partly appointed upper chambers. Monarchs appoint in Jordan, Malaysia and Thailand, but usually an elected government does. In Germany, regional governments appoint. A rationale is that it brings in skills, knowledge or experience (p. 696).
- Appointment is, like heredity, often seen as undemocratic. Indirect elections are used in, for example, Austria, France, India and the Netherlands. Direct elections are more common, as in Argentina, Australia, Japan, Mexico and the U.S. Spain mixes methods: 208 of 264 senators are directly elected and 56 are appointed by regional legislatures (pp. 696–697).
- Congruence also depends on whom members represent (p. 697).
  - In Italy and Japan the upper chamber members are also supposed to represent all citizens equally ("one person, one vote"), so the upper house tends to mimic the lower (p. 697).
  - In the vast majority of systems the upper chamber does not represent the population equally. The Irish Senate is mostly elected by five vocational panels. Venezuela's upper house represents minorities and Belgium's linguistic communities (p. 697).
  - Most commonly it represents subnational units, always in federal systems and in some unitary ones (Bolivia, Netherlands, Spain) (p. 697).

**Malapportionment** means representation is not based on constituency population, so some votes weigh more than others (p. 697). Where every unit gets equal representation (Australia, Switzerland, Venezuela, U.S.), the "one person, one vote" principle is clearly violated. Each U.S. state has two senators, so California (about 37 million) equals Wyoming (half a million). Canada and Germany overrepresent less populous regions without equalising. Austria is roughly proportional (p. 697).

**Summary on congruence.** It depends on similar selection methods and on representing the same citizens. Congruent bicameralism is relatively rare. Lijphart (1999, 212) lists as congruent at the end of the 1990s: Austria, Bahamas, Barbados, Ireland, Italy, Jamaica, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, the Philippines, and Trinidad and Tobago (pp. 698–699).

#### Symmetric and asymmetric bicameralism (p. 699)
- *Symmetric* means equal or near-equal constitutional power. Total symmetry exists when both chambers must agree to enact a law. *Asymmetric* means unequal power. Total asymmetry exists when one chamber has ultimate decision power. In all asymmetric cases the lower house is the stronger (p. 699).
- Symmetry in practice is extremely rare. Formally equal: Colombia, Italy, Switzerland, U.S. Similar but not equal: Australia, Germany, Japan, Netherlands (p. 699). Belgium, Denmark and Sweden used to be symmetric (fn. 14, p. 699).
- Upper chambers are much more likely to have coequal power when citizens play a direct role in electing them (Mastias and Grangé). The authors say this is "likely due to" the democratic legitimacy of elected chambers (p. 699).

**Figure 15.3 (Lijphart 1999, 212) (pp. 699–700).**

| | Congruent | Incongruent |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetric | Weak: Italy, Japan | **Strong**: Australia, Germany, Switzerland, U.S. |
| Asymmetric | Insignificant: Austria, Bahamas, Jamaica | Weak: Canada, France, India, U.K. |

- **Strong:** the upper house is likely to be an important actor, with similar powers and different preferences (p. 699).
- **Insignificant:** the upper house is unlikely to matter, with extremely weak powers and a composition that mirrors the lower house (p. 700).
- **Weak (two ways):**
  - *Congruent but symmetric*: powerful, but not expected to affect policy much because it resembles the lower chamber.
  - *Incongruent but asymmetric*: likely to conflict with the lower chamber, but its constitutional powers are weak (p. 700).

> **Intuition note.** An upper chamber is expected to be an important actor when two things combine. It has power to block (symmetry), and it is likely to want something different (incongruence). In the text's account, either one alone is not expected to be enough (p. 700).

**Box 15.3: Resolving intercameral conflict (pp. 701–702).** The five mechanisms are the navette, conference committee, joint session, decision by one chamber, and new elections.
- *Navette (shuttle)*: a bill goes back and forth with amendments until agreed. It is used by almost all bicameral systems (p. 701).
  - France, Madagascar and Mauritania let shuttling continue indefinitely unless the government intervenes. Italy, Liberia and the Philippines abort a bill that keeps failing after a time (p. 701).
  - Most countries add mechanisms after a set number of failed shuttles. Belgium uses two readings. In South Africa and Pakistan, second-chamber disagreement in the first round triggers a joint session (p. 701).
- *Conference committee*: equal numbers of delegates from each chamber, usually deciding by majority. The compromise gets an up-or-down vote with no amendment. Used in, for example, Chile, France, Germany, Japan and the U.S. (p. 701).
- *Joint session*: the lower chamber nearly always has more members, so this tends to favour it. Used in, for example, Australia, Brazil, India and Norway (p. 701).
- *Decision by one chamber*: in France the government can ask the lower chamber to decide. Elsewhere, ultimate power usually reverts automatically to the lower chamber (Austria, Ireland, Japan, U.K.) or to the initiating or reviewing chamber (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico) (p. 702).
- *New elections* are extremely rare, as in Australia (p. 702).

#### Why bicameralism? (pp. 702–705)
- **Greek origins.** Aristotle and Plato valued "mixed government", with all social classes represented, over "simple government" of the one, few or many. Sparta, Crete and Carthage had separate assemblies for different classes. The authors say these are not bicameral legislatures in the modern sense, because their functions were not clearly separated (p. 702, fn. 15). Mixed government was thought to prevent aristocracy degenerating into oligarchy and to be efficient and stable through checks and balances (p. 702).
- **Eighteenth-century view.** Most theorists read mixed government as a chamber for the aristocracy and one for the common people, plus a monarch. The aristocracy's supposed knowledge, age and wisdom would improve the lower chamber's legislation. For roughly two thousand years, bicameralism meant separate representation of social classes (p. 703).
- **Republicanism and territory.** Republicanism (late eighteenth century) stressed the people as a whole, so bicameralism was increasingly justified as representing the territorial units of federal states. In the 1787 "Great Compromise", the House is elected by population and the Senate gives each state equal representation, to protect small states and win their signatures (p. 703).
  - This incongruent bicameralism was justified by states' distinct needs (*Federalist* No. 62). To protect them, the founders gave the upper house enough power that the lower house could not override it. They promoted symmetry, so both chambers must agree to change the status quo (p. 703). Australia, Germany and Switzerland later adopted it (p. 703).
  - Class-based upper chambers were increasingly repudiated, but territory gave a competing legitimacy to powerful upper chambers (Tsebelis and Money 1997, 33). The authors add that it is "perhaps no surprise" that all "strong" examples in Figure 15.3 are federal (p. 703).
- **Unitary states.** Strong bicameralism was harder to justify there, with no territorial units to represent (pp. 703–704).
  - Some curtailed the upper house. The U.K.'s 1911 Act asserted Commons supremacy, and the 1949 Act cut the Lords' maximum delay to one year (p. 704). The Acts were used to pass legislation against the Lords on only seven occasions since 1911, but the mere threat has forced the Lords to accept bills on several other occasions (fn. 16, p. 704).
  - The French Senate was coequal under the Third Republic (1870–1940). The Fourth Republic (1946–1958) limited it to delay. The Fifth (1958–) lets the government give the National Assembly the final say (p. 704).
  - The case for keeping weak upper chambers rests on a belief that upper members have valuable traits (wisdom, age, knowledge, training), and on the belief that delay reduces hasty "bad" laws. This argument requires incongruence, often achieved by appointment for skills (p. 704).
  - Denmark and Sweden instead "democratised" upper-chamber selection, which made it congruent with the lower. That raised the question of purpose, summed up in Sieyès: "If the second chamber agrees with the first, it is useless; if it disagrees it is dangerous." Both abolished their upper chambers (pp. 704–705).
- **Summary.** There are two basic defences. In federal countries bicameralism protects the federal system and the distinct preferences of territories. In unitary countries it improves legislative quality (p. 705).

> **Intuition note.** The argument for keeping a weak upper chamber requires it to differ from the lower house (otherwise, in Sieyès's words, it is "useless"). Strong ones kept their legitimacy where territory gave them a competing legitimacy that social class now lacked (pp. 703–705).

### Constitutionalism (pp. 705–717)
**Definitions.** *Constitutionalism* is the commitment of governments to accept the legitimacy of, and be governed by, authoritative rules in a constitution. *Systems of constitutional justice* are the institutions and procedures that protect those rules (p. 705).
- Historically few countries had constitutional justice. None existed when the first fully codified written constitution emerged in the U.S. in 1787. The norm was *legislative supremacy*: laws made by elected representatives should not be constrained by other authorities such as a constitution (p. 705).
- Shapiro and Stone Sweet call today's situation a "new constitutionalism": virtually all new constitutions contain a charter of rights protected by institutions such as constitutional courts. These can use *constitutional review* to invalidate legislation that violates individual rights (p. 705).
- There has been a shift since 1945, but states vary in how far they delegated control to judges. This has led some to worry about a "government of judges" (pp. 705–706; fn. 17, p. 706).

#### The shift to a new constitutionalism (pp. 706–710)
**Constitutions in general.** A constitution is the formal source of state authority. It establishes institutions and their powers, and says how they interact. Recent constitutions (about the last sixty years) also list rights that are substantive constraints on government (p. 706).
- *Codified* means written in a single document. *Uncodified* means several sources, written or unwritten. As of 2007 only Israel, New Zealand and the U.K. had uncodified constitutions (p. 706). The U.K.'s written part includes the 1998 Scotland Act and Human Rights Act. The unwritten part includes conventions, royal prerogatives, customs and precedents (p. 706, fn. 18).
- *Entrenched* means it can be modified only by a constitutional amendment procedure requiring more than a legislative majority (p. 706). *Unentrenched* means any legislative majority can change it, like any law (p. 707).
  - Examples of amendment rules: federal systems often need a majority of regional legislatures. Australia and Switzerland need a national referendum majority plus a majority in every state or canton. Japan needs a two-thirds supermajority in both chambers plus a referendum majority. Some rules vary by provision (pp. 706–707).
  - Entrenchment implicitly recognises constitutional law as higher than statute law. In unentrenched systems like the U.K., "constitutional amendment" makes no sense. Almost all contemporary constitutions are entrenched (p. 707).

**Two ideal types** (pp. 707–709; summary in Table 15.5):

| Feature | Legislative supremacy | Higher law |
|---|---|---|
| Entrenched | No | Yes |
| Constitutional review | No | Yes |
| Bill of rights | No | Yes |

- **Legislative supremacy constitution.** The principle is that "elections legitimize legislative authority, and legislative majorities legitimize statutory authority" (Stone Sweet 2000, 20). There is explicit recognition that legislatures can do no legal wrong. It has three features (p. 707):
  1. It is not entrenched and can be changed by simple majority.
  2. No institution reviews the constitutional legality of statutes. Judges ignore any conflict or rule for the statute.
  3. It has no bill of rights. Some such countries protect rights, but the source should be the legislature, not the constitution (p. 708).
  - Examples: the U.K., New Zealand, and the French Third and Fourth Republics (p. 708).
- **Higher law constitution.** It starts from the premise that the state *can* do legal wrong and that individual and minority rights must be protected from it (p. 708).
  - A bill of rights gives nonstate actors a basis to claim statutes invalid if they violate protected rights (p. 708).
  - *Constitutional review* is the authority of an institution to invalidate acts of government, such as legislation, administrative decisions and judicial rulings, that violate constitutional rules. When ordinary judges of the regular system do it, it is called *judicial review*. Special tribunals (constitutional courts) do it in most European countries, ordinary judges in the U.S. (p. 708).
  - Review lets judges intervene in lawmaking and even draft legislation's terms. Higher law constitutions are entrenched, with special amendment procedures (p. 708).

**How common?** Legislative supremacy constitutions were once common, especially in Europe, with deep hostility toward judges, but are now relatively rare. Virtually all new constitutions, democratic and authoritarian, are higher law (p. 708). All 106 constitutions adopted since 1985 with reliable data have a rights catalogue. All but five (Iraq 1990, Laos, North Korea, Saudi Arabia, Vietnam) provide for constitutional review (Stone Sweet 2008) (p. 709). Rights can be extensive, including social rights such as health care or housing, and duties (fn. 19, p. 709).

**Why the shift?** (pp. 709–710)
- It began in Europe after 1945, with constitutional courts in Austria (1945), Italy (1948) and West Germany (1949). The authors say it was *partly* a response to interwar fascism, which showed that states could do wrong. It can *also* be traced to U.S. troops and U.S. pressure for a bill of rights and review in Germany and Italy (p. 709).
- Interwar higher law constitutions with review (e.g., Czechoslovakia 1920, Spain 1931, Ireland 1937) were largely ineffective, except Ireland's court, the only one to survive World War II (fn. 20, p. 709).
- To a large extent, later adoption has coincided with democratic transitions. Examples are Portugal (1976), Spain (1978), post-1989 Central and Eastern Europe, and Latin America and Asia in the 1980s–90s. In all, adoption can "in part" be seen as an attempt to prevent repetition of recent state abuses (p. 710).

> **Intuition note.** Rights-based constitutions with judges to enforce them spread, in part, as an attempt to prevent a repeat of recent state abuses (p. 710). The text also links them to U.S. pressure after 1945 and to democratic transitions (pp. 709–710).

#### Box 15.4: Judicial power and the judicialization of politics (pp. 710–712)
- **Two approaches.** (1) *De jure* power: a court is powerful if it has wide legal authority (jurisdiction). The problem: courts may not use it, or their decisions may be ignored. Examples are local governments in the U.S. and Germany refusing to implement constitutional decisions, and courts avoiding rulings they expect other branches to oppose (p. 710). In Venezuela in 1999, Supreme Court president Cecilia Sosa Gomez resigned after her Court approved a state of emergency allowing judges to be fired. She said the Court had "committed suicide to avoid being assassinated" (p. 710). (2) *De facto* power. Most political scientists now focus on it, defining judicial power as the ability of courts to bring about policy outcomes they prefer (Cameron 2002). So courts are not always impartial interpreters, and judges have policy preferences like other actors (p. 711).
- **Implementation problem.** Courts rely on others to implement and enforce decisions. Hamilton (*Federalist* No. 78) noted a court has no financial or physical coercive powers of its own. This can be particularly constraining when a court rules against those who must implement. Judges sometimes rule policies constitutional to avoid conflict even when they know the policies are constitutionally suspect (p. 711).
- **Yet courts sometimes act powerfully.** The Israeli Supreme Court limited interrogation techniques even under security threats (p. 711). Per Vanberg (2001, 2005), *two conditions* must be met (pp. 711–712):
  1. Courts must enjoy public support. Then elected officials expect an electoral backlash if they do not comply.
  2. Voters must be able to monitor responses to rulings. At minimum, decisions must be transparent and public. The "electoral connection" works only if voters can identify noncompliance.
- **Strategic publicity.** Courts gain support if decisions show them to be impartial. Publicising decisions that show judges imposing personal preferences will probably not raise support, so courts have an incentive to publicise only the impartial-looking ones (p. 712). Constitutional courts run websites, and judges give lectures and interviews. This is puzzling for unelected judges but understandable if public support is necessary for judicial power (p. 712). Staton (2010) supports this with a study of the Mexican Supreme Court and a cross-national analysis (p. 712).

> **Intuition note.** On the authors' account, a court can be expected to influence policy effectively only when politicians fear voters' reaction to ignoring it. Voters must therefore be able to see what the court said, and courts have an incentive to publicise selectively (pp. 711–712).

#### Different systems of constitutional justice (pp. 712–717)
Systems vary on many dimensions. The text focuses on (a) type, (b) timing and (c) jurisdiction of review (p. 712).
- **Type.** *Abstract* review examines legislation without a concrete case. *Concrete* review examines it in a specific case before the court. A country can allow both (p. 713).
- **Timing.** *A priori* means before a law is formally enacted. *A posteriori* means after enactment. A country can allow both (p. 713).
- **Jurisdiction.** *Centralised* means only one court conducts review. This single constitutional court sits outside the normal judiciary and settles only constitutional disputes. The authors suggest we might think of it "more as a special tribunal than a court". *Decentralised* means more than one court can interpret the constitution. These are part of the regular system, and the top court (Supreme Court) has general jurisdiction (p. 713).
- These three dimensions give eight combinations (2 × 2 × 2 = 8, added step; Navia and Ríos-Figueroa 2005). Only four exist (p. 713), as shown in Table 15.6 (p. 716).
  - Two are impossible: concrete review cannot be a priori, since it needs a legal case challenging an existing statute (p. 713).
  - Two are not observed nationally: abstract review with decentralised jurisdiction. Local judges would nullify laws in the abstract, so laws would vanish in some districts. This would create legal insecurity and "legal holes" (pp. 713, 716).
  - Concrete review with decentralised jurisdiction is somewhat less problematic, since local rulings apply only to the case (fn. 21, p. 716).

| | Concrete a priori | Concrete a posteriori | Abstract a priori | Abstract a posteriori |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Centralised (European) | Not possible | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Decentralised (American) | Not possible | Yes | Not observed | Not observed |

Most political scientists focus on centralised vs decentralised, calling these the European and American models (p. 716). The European model is sometimes called Kelsenian (fn. 22, p. 716).

**Box 15.5: Other dimensions (pp. 714–715).**
- *Who can challenge a law?* In abstract review, usually designated elected politicians, such as executive or legislative figures (France, Germany, Spain) or regional governments (Germany, Italy, Spain) (p. 714).
  - French referrals have been possible since 1974 by sixty representatives. Laws must be referred within fifteen days of adoption (p. 714).
  - Most challenges come from the opposition, often as their last chance to delay or block a bill. The authors say this encourages majorities to consider opposition interests and foster compromise (Stone Sweet 1992; Vanberg 1998) (p. 714).
  - In concrete review the judiciary usually initiates. In centralised systems the judge refers the question to the constitutional court and suspends the case. In decentralised systems the judge rules, and challenges go up the hierarchy (p. 714).
  - Some countries (Germany, Spain) allow individuals to complain directly after exhausting other remedies (p. 714).
- *Selecting constitutional judges.* By nomination, election, or both (p. 715).
  - France: nine judges, nonrenewable nine-year terms, one-third every three years, with each of three presidents appointing one. Former presidents may also sit, so there were eleven members in 2011 (p. 715).
  - Germany: two senates of eight judges each, twelve-year terms. Bundestag and Bundesrat each elect four per senate by two-thirds majority (p. 715).
  - Spain: twelve judges, nine-year terms. Congress and Senate each elect four with 60% support. Two are nominated by the government and two by the General Council of the Judiciary (p. 715).
- *Qualifications.* Most countries require legal training and professional experience, and some have quotas from the ordinary courts (Germany at least six federal judges of sixteen; Italy one-third of fifteen elected by the judiciary). Law professors are the largest group. France requires no legal training, and its council has always had a majority of former ministers and parliamentarians (p. 715).

**The American model (pp. 716–717).**
- "any judge of any court, in any case, at any time, at the behest of any litigating party, has the power to declare a law unconstitutional" (Shapiro and Stone Sweet, p. 716). It is decentralised review by ordinary judges, i.e., judicial review (p. 716).
- Separation of powers marks a "judicial function" (resolving cases) from a "political function" (legislating). Constitutional issues can arise in a case, so ordinary judges have *concrete a posteriori* review as part of their job. Courts are prohibited from abstract review and advisory opinions on bills, which would usurp the legislature's role (pp. 716–717).

**The European model (p. 717).**
- The judiciary was traditionally subordinate to the legislature, not coequal. Judicial review was explicitly prohibited in French and German constitutions since the end of the eighteenth century. From a European view, American-style review "establishes a permanent confusion of powers" (Stone Sweet 2000, 33). So Europeans invented the constitutional court (p. 717).
- Four defining characteristics (p. 717):
  1. A monopoly on constitutional review. Ordinary courts cannot do it or appeal the court's decisions.
  2. Formally detached from the regular judicial system, so it is not a "judicial" institution. Judicial review remains prohibited.
  3. Jurisdiction only over constitutional matters, not ordinary litigation.
  4. Most can do abstract review, evaluating bills before they harm anyone. This is the only type allowed in France. Many also do concrete review.
- Table 15.7 (model characteristics) is referred to but is not on these pages. For 2010, Table 15.8 is described: the European model is more popular than the American, which predominates in Asia, North America, and Central and South America. The European model predominates elsewhere (p. 717).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Malapportionment index, Table 15.4 method.** Country X is unitary but has a Senate representing five regions, with two senators each (10 seats). Populations in millions: R1 = 10, R2 = 15, R3 = 20, R4 = 25, R5 = 30. The total is 100.
- Sorted smallest to largest, cumulative population shares are 10%, 25%, 45%, 70%, 100%.
- The smallest unit(s) holding 10% of the population is R1 only. Its seats are 2/10 = 20%. The ratio 20% / 10% = 2.0, so R1 gets twice its population share.
- The smallest units holding 25% are R1 + R2 (10 + 15 = 25). Their seats are 2 + 2 = 4, and 4/10 = 40%. The ratio is 40% / 25% = 1.6.
- With proportional seats, the entries would be 10% and 25%. The excess (20 vs 10, and 40 vs 25) shows the Senate favours small regions.

**Classification.** Suppose X's Senate is chosen by regions (a different basis from the lower house, so incongruent). If a law needs Senate consent (symmetric), X is "strong" in Figure 15.3. If the lower house can override the Senate (asymmetric), X would be "weak" (pp. 698–700).
