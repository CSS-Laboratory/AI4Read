# Democracy and Dictatorship: Conceptualization and Measurement

## L0 — One line
Turning an abstract concept like democracy into a measure always means simplifying and choosing, so measures are commonly evaluated by validity, reliability and replicability, and whether a measure is appropriate depends on the research question.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How should social scientists turn abstract concepts such as democracy into measures that can test theories? The chapter traces how democracy's meaning changed over time. It then presents Dahl's minimalist two-dimension view (contestation, inclusion). It compares three measures (DD, Polity IV, Freedom House) on two choices: minimalist (rules and procedures only) or substantive (outcomes too), and dichotomous (two categories) or continuous (a scale). The measures agree on easy cases and disagree on mixed regimes. Measures are judged by validity, reliability and replicability. The authors argue that simplification is unavoidable and that the best measure depends on the research question. Results may differ by measure. Scholars should be transparent and try to improve measures rather than dismiss them.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a theory and an empirical test; correlation as "overlap"; simple addition and subtraction of scores.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Democracy was long seen as a bad, obsolete form of rule (mob or class rule, choice by lot). It became synonymous with representative government only after the French and American revolutions, and gained a highly positive connotation in the second half of the twentieth century (pp. 144–149).
- State, government and regime are different things. The regime is the set of rules that determines how the government is formed and how big decisions are made (p. 146).
- Dahl favoured a minimalist (procedural: rules and institutions only) view over a substantive (outcome-based) one. He described regimes by two dimensions: contestation (freedom to organize and compete for office and policies) and inclusion (who may take part) (pp. 150–151).
- The Democracy-Dictatorship (DD) measure is dichotomous (two categories) and procedural, and uses four rules that together are necessary and sufficient for democracy. Polity IV and Freedom House are continuous (a scale of values) and combine scores over several attributes. Freedom House is substantive (pp. 152–158, 163).
- The three measures agree on easy cases but disagree on mixed regimes (the middle of the Polity IV and Freedom House scales). Empirical results may depend on which measure is used (p. 158).
- Substantive measures risk circular reasoning when the research question concerns the same outcomes. Whether a dichotomous or continuous view fits depends partly on the question (pp. 161–162).
- Validity raises several issues; the chapter treats three: which attributes to include, how to aggregate them, and the measurement level (nominal, ordinal, interval) (pp. 162–165).
- Reliable does not imply valid. Measures based on observable facts (DD) are likely more reliable than ones based on subjective judgment (pp. 165–166).
- Replicability at a minimum requires clear coding rules and disaggregated data (the scores on each attribute). Freedom House is weakest of the three here (pp. 166–167).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction and overview (pp. 143–145)
- There is now strong agreement that democracy is desirable. Historically this was not so. Hanson is quoted: for two millennia politicians and philosophers saw democracy as inferior, dominated by mob rule and class warfare (p. 144).
- Macpherson: "Democracy used to be a bad word." Within about fifty years it became a good thing (p. 144).
- Why measurement matters: the next chapters test economic and cultural explanations of how democracy emerges and survives. Those tests presuppose that we can tell which countries are democracies. The chapter asks what makes a democracy a democracy (p. 144).
- Measurement issues apply to any abstract concept (representation, culture, identity, accountability, etc.). Good measures are a prerequisite for good theory testing (p. 145).

### Democracy and dictatorship in historical perspective (pp. 145–149)
- Democracy gained a positive connotation in the second half of the twentieth century. Even countries widely seen as dictatorships have professed support for democracy. Many refer to the people or democracy in their names and often adopt seemingly democratic institutions such as elections, legislatures and parties (p. 145).
- Until the mid-nineteenth century, democracy was commonly seen as obsolete, dangerous and unstable. The debate took place among elites (p. 145).
- Demokratia means "rule by the demos". In Plato's and Aristotle's time, demos meant the common people: those with little economic independence who were politically uneducated (p. 145; p. 147).
- **Plato:** decisions should rest on expertise, like trained pilots flying planes. Democracy would be rule by the poor and uneducated against the rich and educated. The masses would be open to demagoguery and would quickly surrender power to a tyrant (pp. 145, 147).
- **Aristotle:** the will of the many could, under some conditions, be equal to or wiser than that of the few. Yet he did not think highly of democracy (p. 147).
- Aristotle classified regimes by number of rulers and by whether rulers govern for the good of all or only for themselves (Table 5.1, p. 147):

| Number of rulers | Good form (for the good of all) | Bad form (for the good of the rulers) |
|---|---|---|
| One | Monarchy | Tyranny |
| Few | Aristocracy | Oligarchy |
| Many | Politeia | Democracy |

- Each good form can be corrupted into its bad neighbour. Aristotle says we should choose the form whose corrupt version is least dangerous, which for him is aristocracy. Like Plato, he saw democracy as the most dangerous form because it is class rule (p. 147).
- Similar fears of class warfare and expropriation of the rich recurred in the 18th and 19th centuries in debates over suffrage. Marx stated that universal suffrage and democracy would inevitably set off class struggle. Such fears also motivated twentieth-century efforts to restrict voting in the US (p. 147).
- Old democracy differed from today's. It had nothing to do with elections. In aristocratic regimes leaders were elected, while in democracies they were chosen by lot. This view lasted into the eighteenth century, partly explaining why many theorists favoured monarchy. Those arguing for representative government then did not see themselves as democrats. Democracy meant direct legislation by the people, possible only in ancient city-states, so it seemed obsolete (p. 148).
- Only with the French and American revolutions, when a fault line formed between democracy and aristocracy, did representative government and democracy become synonymous (p. 148). Earlier opposition targeted unjust rulers, not monarchy as such, and nobles often sided with commoners against them (pp. 148–149).
- After 1789 the three-way split (one, few, many) was replaced by a dichotomy: democracy (many) versus autocracy/dictatorship (one or few) (p. 149).
- **Box 5.1 (p. 146).** A *state* uses coercion and the threat of force to rule a territory, and is generally stable. A *government* is the set of people who run the state at a given time, and governments come and go. A *regime* is the set of rules, norms or institutions that determine how the government is constituted, organized and takes major decisions. Regimes are classed as democratic or dictatorial.
  - Tunisia: state from 1956; civilian dictatorial regime 1956–1987 and military dictatorial regime 1987–2011. The government was led by Bourguiba (1956–1987) and Ben Ali (1987–2011), then by successive governments in 2011.
  - Australia: state since 1901, always a parliamentary democratic regime, with many governments.
- **Box 5.2 (p. 148).** "Dictator" was originally an extraordinary Roman magistrate appointed for emergencies. Machiavelli said dictatorial authority did the Roman republic good. Rousseau suggested dictators might be needed in emergencies. The positive connotation has disappeared. Dictatorship is now seen as largely synonymous with autocracy, tyranny and despotism.

*Intuition note:* Words such as "democracy" change meaning over time, so a modern classification should not assume that older sources meant the same thing by it.

### Classifying democracies and dictatorships (pp. 149–158)

**Measures and indicators (p. 149).** We never observe abstract concepts directly. We only evaluate indicators or measures, which are quantifications of what interests us. Tests are good only to the extent that the indicators mirror the concept. Footnote 2 (p. 149) distinguishes the two. A *measure* directly quantifies the concept (years measure age). An *indicator* quantifies something known or believed to be correlated with the concept (corruption or transparency indicate political-system characteristics).

**Dahl's view (pp. 149–151).**
- The core idea of democracy is that "the people", not a subset, should rule. Translating this into criteria varies enormously (p. 149).
- A *substantive* view classifies regimes by the outcomes they produce, not just their institutions. Aristotle's view can be seen as substantive. A *minimalist (procedural)* view classifies regimes only by institutions and procedures (p. 150).
- **Dahl's argument:** if scholars define "true" democracy by outcomes such as economic justice or accountability, they may find few real-world examples. So he advised a minimalist view (p. 150).
- Dahl's two dimensions (p. 150):
  - *Contestation:* how far citizens are free to organize into competing blocs to press for policies. It covers freedom to form parties, free speech and assembly, and free and fair elections.
  - *Inclusion:* who gets to participate. Low barriers to naturalization and universal adult suffrage mean high inclusion. Property requirements, or denial of the vote by birthplace, ethnicity or gender, mean lower inclusion.
- Examples (Figure 5.1, pp. 150–151):
  - Soviet Union: high inclusion, low contestation (one party).
  - China: low on both (one party, no elections above municipal level).
  - Apartheid South Africa and the US before 1830: multiparty elections but low inclusion.
  - The US franchise expanded in the 1830s, but barriers remained until at least 1964 (Voting Rights Act).
  - Liechtenstein pre-1984, Switzerland pre-1971 and France pre-1945: high contestation, moderate inclusion because suffrage was male only.
  - Today's recognizable democracies sit in the top right (high on both).
- Dahl conceded that contestation and inclusion are only two aspects of what people mean by democracy. He therefore used the term *polyarchy* for a regime with high levels of both. He also did not believe any large country could be a true democracy: countries can be closer or farther from the ideal type, never at it (p. 151).
- The emphasis on the two dimensions has stuck, but the term polyarchy has not. The authors keep using "democracy" even for procedural definitions inspired by Dahl (p. 151).

*Intuition note:* Dahl splits "who can compete" (contestation) from "who can take part" (inclusion). A regime can be high on one and low on the other, so a single label can hide this difference.

**Three measures (p. 152).** DD, Polity IV and Freedom House are commonly used, and all build on Dahl to differing extents (p. 152).

**The DD (Democracy–Dictatorship) measure (pp. 152–154).**
- Cheibub, Gandhi and Vreeland (2010) provide annual data for 199 countries, 1946 (or independence) to 2008. It revises the earlier PACL measure (p. 152, fn. 3).
- **Conceptualization:** democracies are regimes whose governmental offices are filled as a result of contested elections. The chief executive and the legislature must both be elected. Contestation requires an opposition with some chance of winning, and has three elements (p. 152):
  - (a) *ex ante uncertainty:* the outcome is unknown before the election (rules out Iraq under Saddam Hussein);
  - (b) *ex post irreversibility:* the winner takes office (rules out Algeria in 1991, when the army blocked the Islamic Salvation Front);
  - (c) *repeatability:* elections occur at regular, known intervals (rules out Weimar Germany in the 1930s, where the Nazis won and then cancelled further contests).
- **Operational rules (p. 152).** A country is a democracy if all four hold:
  1. The chief executive is elected.
  2. The legislature is elected.
  3. More than one party competes.
  4. An alternation in power under identical electoral rules has taken place.

  A country is a dictatorship if any of the four fails (p. 153).
- Rule 3: voters need at least two alternatives, so single-party lists (Syria, Turkmenistan, Vietnam) do not count as contested (p. 153).
- Rules 3 and 4 recognize that elections alone do not separate regimes, because virtually every country has held legislative or presidential elections at some time (p. 153).
- Rule 4: alternation means the chief executive is replaced through elections by someone else. **The DD authors' view:** until an incumbent has shown willingness to give up power after losing, we cannot truly know whether a country is a democracy. Popular incumbents who would leave if beaten and incumbents who hold elections only because they cannot lose are *observationally equivalent* (they look the same) (p. 153).
- **Box 5.3 (p. 154).** Botswana, Japan, Malaysia and Mexico have had one party in power for long periods.
  - Until the incumbents lost and gave up power in Japan (LDP) and Mexico (PRI), their status was unclear.
  - In Malaysia the incumbent won the first two multiparty elections (three were held between 1957 and 1969). After losing the third, it declared a state of emergency, closed parliament and rewrote the constitution so it never lost again. This suggests it would not have given up power after the earlier elections either.
  - Botswana's incumbents have never lost since 1966. Many scholars call it a democracy, but DD codes it a dictatorship because we have never seen the incumbent lose.
  - The DD authors prefer to risk mistaking a democracy for a dictatorship rather than the reverse; the authors add that every scholar has to make a choice of this kind.
- **Relation to Dahl (p. 153).** DD is purely procedural and focuses on contestation. It ignores inclusion, justified by the lack of variation in inclusion in 1946–2008, since by 1946 all but a handful of countries had universal suffrage (fn. 4: it would presumably take inclusion into account for earlier periods). The biggest difference is that DD treats regime type as a dichotomy, whereas Dahl treats it as a continuum.
- A *dichotomous* measure has two discrete values. A *continuous* measure can take any value in a range (p. 154).
- **DD authors' view:** there is a qualitative difference between democracy and dictatorship. Uncontested offices (China, North Korea) are not even partly democratic, and no point exists at which a regime is equally democratic and dictatorial (Figure 5.2a vs 5.2b). They choose a dichotomous measure because their concept is dichotomous. They do not think it impossible to measure whether some regimes are more democratic than others, as some have implied (Elkins 2000) (pp. 154–155).

*Intuition note:* DD asks a sharp, observable question: have elections actually been contested and has power actually changed hands? That makes classification easy and cautious, at the risk of coding as dictatorships cases that many scholars call democracies, such as Botswana.

**Polity IV (pp. 155–156).**
- Marshall, Gurr and Jaggers (2010) give annual scores for 190 polities from 1800 to the present. The Democracy score and the Autocracy score each range from 0 to 10 (p. 155).
- $$\text{Polity Score} = \text{Democracy Score} - \text{Autocracy Score}$$ This ranges from $-10$ (as autocratic as possible) to $+10$ (as democratic as possible) (p. 155).
- Polity IV follows Dahl in using a continuum (Figure 5.2b) and a largely minimalist measure (p. 155).
- **Common practice:** many scholars code $+6$ to $+10$ as democracy, $-6$ to $-10$ as dictatorship, and $-5$ to $5$ as "anocracy" or "mixed regime". Cut-points differ across scholars, are rarely justified theoretically, and there is reason to believe the choice matters in empirical tests (p. 155, fn. 5).
- **Five attributes** (p. 155): (a) competitiveness of executive recruitment, (b) openness of executive recruitment, (c) constraints on the executive, (d) regulation of political participation, (e) competitiveness of political participation.
  - Together they capture contestation and inclusion. The attribute of constraints on the executive adds a dimension to Dahl: democratic governments must be limited (p. 156).
  - Fn. 6 notes that most of the variation in Polity Scores across countries comes from this extra dimension (Gleditsch and Ward 1997).
- Each attribute contributes different numbers of points to the Democracy and Autocracy scores. The contributions of two attributes are shown below (Tables 5.2 and 5.3, pp. 156–157). The scores from each dimension are added to get the overall Democracy, Autocracy and Polity scores (p. 156).

| Competitiveness of participation | Democracy | Autocracy | Polity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | 3 | 0 | 3 |
| Transitional | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Factional | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Restricted | 0 | 1 | −1 |
| Suppressed | 0 | 2 | −2 |

| Regulation of participation | Democracy | Autocracy | Polity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regulated | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Multiple Identity | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| Sectarian | 0 | 1 | −1 |
| Restricted | 0 | 2 | −2 |
| Unregulated | 0 | 0 | 0 |

- The weights differ across the two dimensions (p. 156).

**Freedom House (pp. 156–158).**
- An independent nongovernmental organization that has provided an annual "global freedom" measure since 1972. The 2011 survey covers 194 countries and 14 related and disputed territories (p. 156).
- It is not technically a measure of democracy, but many scholars use it as one, presumably assuming democracy and freedom are synonymous. The authors leave it to the reader to judge whether this is reasonable (p. 157).
- **Political rights dimension:** ten questions, each worth 0–4 points, in three categories (electoral process; political pluralism and participation; functioning of government). The score out of 40 is converted to a 7-point scale (1–7) (p. 157).
- **Civil rights dimension:** fifteen questions, each 0–4, in four categories (freedom of expression and belief; associational and organizational rights; rule of law; personal autonomy and individual rights). The score out of 60 is converted to a 7-point scale (p. 157).
- The overall score is the average of the two dimension scores (p. 157). Fn. 9: the coding rules have changed slightly from year to year, and past scores are not updated, so research over time is potentially problematic.
- It follows Dahl in covering contestation and inclusion, and conceives regime type as a continuum (p. 158).
- **Common coding practice:** Free (Democratic) 1–2.5; Partly Free (Mixed) 3–5.5; Not Free (Dictatorship) 5.5–7 (p. 158). Table 5.4 codes 5.5 as dictatorship.
- Unlike DD, Polity IV and Dahl, Freedom House uses a *substantive* view. It holds that particular institutions are necessary but not sufficient for democracy. It takes into account outcomes such as academic freedom, freedom from war and freedom from socioeconomic inequalities (p. 158).

**Comparing the three (pp. 158–160).**
- Several scholars have noted that the measures are highly correlated (large overlap); the authors say this is largely driven by easy cases. All three classify the US, Australia, Sweden and Denmark as democracies and China, Saudi Arabia and Burma as dictatorships (p. 158).
- There is considerable disagreement on mixed regimes, in the middle of the Polity IV and Freedom House distributions. Map 5.1 shows this for Africa and South America (p. 158).
- Examples from Table 5.4 (2008, p. 160):
  - Egypt, Pakistan, China and Iran: all three say dictatorship.
  - Russia (DD dictatorship; Polity IV mixed, 4; FH dictatorship, 5.5) and Tunisia (DD dictatorship; Polity IV mixed, −4; FH dictatorship, 6) are split.
  - Botswana: DD dictatorship, Polity IV democracy (8), FH democracy (2).
  - Nigeria: DD democracy, Polity IV mixed (4), FH mixed (4).
  - Sierra Leone: DD democracy, Polity IV democracy (7), FH mixed (3).
- Growing evidence suggests results of tests on democracy and dictatorship do depend on which measure is used (Casper and Tufts 2003; Cheibub et al. 2010; Elkins 2000) (p. 158).

### Evaluating measures of democracy and dictatorship (pp. 160–167)
- Operationalizing always involves some simplification or loss of meaning, but simplification is essential to science. Not all measures are equally good. Researchers must justify both the construction and the use of a measure. Appropriateness depends on the research question. Measures are evaluated by conceptualization, validity, reliability and replicability (p. 160).

**Conceptualization (pp. 160–162).** *Conceptualization* is creating mental categories that capture the meaning of objects, events or ideas (p. 160). The three measures differ on (a) minimalist vs substantive and (b) dichotomous vs continuous (p. 161).

- *Minimalist vs substantive* (p. 161):
  - If democracy is defined by accountability, equality or freedom from war (as Freedom House does), then using it to test whether democracy affects those same things is **circular reasoning**. Tests will always seem to confirm the effect because of how the measure is built.
  - Substantive measures are arguably at least as appropriate as minimalist ones when the research question is unrelated to the attributes in the measure. Examples are joining international organizations or signing treaties.
  - Minimalist views have potential advantages for isolating causal processes. If a Freedom House study finds democracy raises development, one cannot tell which of the 25 attributes (10 + 15) drives it. Many substantive measures conflate institutions and procedures with outcomes.
  - Minimalist views allow that democracies can be organized in many ways.
  - **Authors' doubt:** in many ways a free and democratic country by Freedom House looks remarkably similar to an idealized United States. Is this an accident, or normatively appealing traits colouring an ostensibly objective measure? Are judicial independence and property rights part of what makes a democracy, or traits that vary among democracies and need explaining? (p. 161)
- *Dichotomous vs continuous* (p. 162):
  - **Bollen and Jackman:** democracy is always a matter of degree, and treating it as a dichotomy is a "flawed" practice.
  - **Alvarez et al.:** Bollen and Jackman are "confused", since regimes cannot be half-democratic and there is a natural zero point. The textbook authors summarise this as a pregnancy analogy: you cannot be half pregnant.
  - The debate hinges on whether Figure 5.2a or 5.2b fits. In the authors' opinion, scholars may reasonably disagree.
  - Fit depends partly on the question. Democratic transitions suggest a dichotomy (one regime to another), so the authors use DD in Chapters 6 and 7. Studies of parliamentary vs presidential regimes and stability seem to assume a dichotomy implicitly. Studies of foreign intervention on the level of democracy, or of veto players and democratic stability, do not necessarily imply a dichotomy.

*Intuition note:* How appropriate a measure is depends heavily on the research question. A measure that defines democracy by outcomes runs into circular reasoning when used to test whether democracy produces those outcomes, and whether a two-category or a scale view fits depends partly on what you ask.

**Validity (pp. 162–165).** *Validity* is the extent to which measures correspond to the concepts they intend to reflect. Several issues arise; the authors briefly consider three: attributes, aggregation and measurement level (p. 162).

- *Attributes* (p. 163): there are no hard-and-fast rules for which to include.
  - Too many attributes (substantive measures such as Freedom House): Dahl worried no country would qualify, and such a measure limits the research questions it can answer.
  - Too few (DD and Polity IV): they may not capture all we think of as democracy. Some criticize DD for ignoring inclusion.
- *Aggregation* (pp. 163–164):
  - DD needs no aggregation rules, since a country passes or fails a set of necessary and sufficient conditions.
  - Freedom House and Polity IV require aggregation rules. No single attribute is explicitly necessary. A country counts as democratic when it scores enough points overall.
  - Consequently, many combinations yield the same score. Fully seventeen distinct combinations of the five Polity IV dimensions give a Polity score of 0 (Cheibub et al. 2010, 93). Hundreds of combinations can yield a "mixed regime", leading some scholars to ask what mixed regimes conceptually have in common (fn. 10).
  - **Questions the authors pose (not answered):** Is equal weighting of the 25 Freedom House questions justified? Is academic freedom as important as judicial independence? Does Polity IV assume a move from 1 to 2 on one attribute equals a move from 3 to 4, or equals a 1-to-2 move on another attribute? Can attributes be aggregated along one dimension, given Dahl's claim that democracy is inherently multidimensional?
  - Treier and Jackman (2008) examined aggregation in Polity IV and conclude that scepticism about the precision of the Polity scale "is well-founded".
- *Measurement level* (p. 164):
  - *Nominal:* discrete categories that are mutually exclusive (no case in two categories) and collectively exhaustive (every case fits one). It is essentially naming. DD is nominal, a valid way to measure a dichotomous concept.
  - *Ordinal:* rank-orders cases (more or less), but not by how much.
  - *Interval:* a standard unit, so statements like "two more units of democracy" are meaningful.
  - Polity IV and Freedom House are widely considered interval measures, valid given their continuum conceptualization (p. 165).
  - Fn. 11: technically they are categorical (Polity IV has only 21 values, each Freedom House dimension 7). Arguably they should be seen as (polychotomous) nominal rather than interval, given their aggregation rules.

**Reliability (pp. 165–166).**
- A *reliable* measure repeatedly and consistently produces the same score for a case under the same process, so several coders with the same rules would give the same score (p. 165).
- Reliable does not imply valid. A measure can consistently give the same "poor" score (Figure 5.3, left). A measure can be valid on average but unreliable, with large differences between attempts (centre). We want both (right) (p. 165).
- DD is likely highly reliable because it uses only observables (elections, parties, alternation). Freedom House and Polity IV are likely less reliable because they depend on coders' subjective judgments (for example, "fair electoral rules" or "reasonable self-determination") (pp. 165–166).
- *Interobserver reliability* can be assessed empirically by checking whether different observers give consistent estimates. Polity IV has conducted some checks in recent years. The authors know of none from Freedom House (p. 166).

*Intuition note:* Think of darts. Reliability is a tight cluster of throws, and validity means the throws are centred on the target on average. A tight cluster in the wrong place is reliable but invalid.

**Replicability (pp. 166–167).**
- *Replicability* is the ability of third parties to reproduce the process by which a measure is created. King argues that enough information should be available for a third party to understand, analyze, replicate and evaluate the results without further information from the author. It lets outsiders evaluate reliability and validity (p. 166).
- At a minimum: clear coding rules and disaggregated data (scores on each attribute) (p. 166).
- DD and Polity IV give much more detailed and clear coding rules than Freedom House, which gives no rules for why a country gets, say, a 3 rather than another score on one of its 25 questions (pp. 166–167).
- Polity IV releases disaggregated data on its five attributes. Freedom House has not historically released its 25 question scores. Since 2006 it has provided some information, but not all 25 scores (p. 167, fn. 12).
- Munck and Verkuilen conclude that Freedom House's aggregate data has to be taken largely on trust (p. 167).

### Conclusion (pp. 167–168)
- No single measure can capture all meanings of democracy, and we would not necessarily want one to. Measurement implies simplification, which implies choices and trade-offs. Researchers should consider the research question first. The most multifaceted measure is not always best, and minimalist measures may answer more, and more interesting, questions (p. 167).
- Political science measures often need subjective evaluation, which risks poor validity and reliability. Without such measures, many important claims could not be tested (p. 167).
- Scholars should know the limits of their measures, avoid overstating generality, and publish construction details so others can replicate (p. 167).
- Readers should not reject a measure just because it seems imperfect. They should ask whether they can build a better one, and whether measure quality affects the author's conclusions. Just as we do not reject a theory until a better one comes along, the authors say we should not immediately reject a measure until a better one exists. They add that it may be better to spend a little more time developing better measures first (pp. 167–168).

## Toy example
*(Our own illustration, not from the text.)* Take an invented country, Alpha. Its president and its legislature are both elected, and three parties compete. The same party has won every election since the first one.

**1. DD rules (p. 152).** Rule 1 (executive elected): pass. Rule 2 (legislature elected): pass. Rule 3 (more than one party): pass. Rule 4 (alternation under identical rules): **fail**, since no alternation has occurred. Because a failure on any rule means dictatorship, DD codes Alpha a dictatorship. This mirrors the Botswana logic in Box 5.3.

**2. Polity IV partial score (Tables 5.2 and 5.3).** Suppose coders judge participation "Competitive" and regulation "Regulated".
- Competitive: Democracy +3, Autocracy +0, Polity contribution $3 - 0 = 3$.
- Regulated: +0 and +0, contribution $0 - 0 = 0$.
- Total from these two attributes: $3 + 0 = 3$.

Alpha therefore scores positively on these two Polity IV attributes (three more are not scored here) but is a dictatorship under DD. This shows that classification depends on the chosen concept.

**3. Aggregation.** Take Country P: participation "Competitive" ($+3$), regulation "Restricted" ($-2$), total $+1$. Take Country Q: participation "Factional" ($+1$), regulation "Regulated" ($0$), total $+1$. Two quite different profiles give the same sum of 1, which is the many-ways-to-one-score problem (p. 163).

## Canonical instances

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Validity vs reliability (darts on a target, Figure 5.3) ↔ statistics: bias vs variance of an estimator — a consistent but off-target measure resembles a low-variance, biased estimate.
- [structural-analogy] Circularity of substantive democracy measures ↔ research design: including the outcome in the definition of the predictor makes the test uninformative.
- [metaphor] Polity IV's many combinations giving one score ↔ composite indices in other fields: different underlying profiles collapse into the same number.
- [structural-analogy] DD's four necessary-and-sufficient rules ↔ logic: a conjunction of conditions, where failing any one gives the "dictatorship" label.

## Questions this raises
- How large are the differences in results when the same theory is tested with DD, Polity IV and Freedom House? The chapter cites such evidence without presenting it.
- Is it better to wrongly code a democracy as a dictatorship (DD's choice) or the reverse? On what research questions would this matter?
- Can a measure of democracy be built that is both minimalist and multidimensional, without the aggregation problems of Polity IV?
- How should one choose cut-points on a continuous score (such as +6 on Polity IV) when they are rarely justified theoretically?
