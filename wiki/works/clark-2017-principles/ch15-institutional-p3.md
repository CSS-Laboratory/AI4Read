# Veto Player Theory: Federalism, Bicameralism and Constitutional Review as One Thing

## L0 — One line
Federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review can all be treated as institutional veto players, and adding veto players never enlarges, and moving them further apart shrinks, the set of policy changes that can win unanimous support, so policy is expected to be more stable.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The part asks how to compare political institutions across countries in one framework, not through separate dichotomies. Veto player theory answers that every actor whose agreement is needed to change the status quo (current policy) is a veto player. Using circles in a policy space, the authors show that the "winset" (policy changes all veto players prefer to the status quo) shrinks, or stays equal, as veto players are added, and shrinks as they move apart. A small winset means policy stability, smaller and less varied policy shifts, and weaker agenda setters (actors who make take-it-or-leave-it proposals). Stability may bring government or regime instability and more judicial and bureaucratic activism. The authors link this to Arrow's stability–responsiveness trade-off.

**Prerequisites:** the spatial model (ideal points, indifference curves, median voter, winset, agenda setter) from Chapter 11; basic ideas of federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review from earlier in this chapter.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Constitutional review comes in an American (decentralized, a posteriori, concrete) and a European (centralized, often abstract) model. Table 15.8 counts 54 American, 89 European, 14 mixed, 22 other and 4 "none" systems in 2010 (pp. 718–719).
- Veto player theory suggests we might usefully view federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review as types of the same thing. Each adds actors whose agreement is needed to change the status quo (pp. 718–720).
- Institutional veto players come from the constitution and are fixed while it stays the same. Partisan veto players come from political competition and change with it (pp. 719–720).
- The winset under unanimity is the overlap of all veto players' indifference circles. A smaller winset means more policy stability, smaller and less varied shifts, and less agenda-setting power (p. 722).
- More veto players never enlarge the winset, but may leave it unchanged ("absorption"). Greater ideological distance between them shrinks it (p. 724).
- The authors apply this to the Obama presidency, and they link policy stability to government instability (parliamentary), regime instability (presidential) and judicial/bureaucratic activism (pp. 725–727).
- Many veto players may, in some sense, overcome instability without a single agenda setter, but they grant something like dictatorial power to past policymakers. The authors say there appears to be some stability–responsiveness trade-off (p. 728).
- The problems build spatial models of bicameralism, judicial review and the unanimity core (pp. 729–739).

### Veto players: the definition (pp. 718–720)
- Political scientists have sometimes sorted democracies into dichotomies: federal or unitary, bicameral or unicameral, constitutionalist or not. Comparative scholars have begun to move away from this. They see these institutions as conceptually the same, because all act as checks and balances and affect how easily the status quo can be changed. This approach is **veto player theory** (Tsebelis) (p. 718).
- A **veto player** is an individual (e.g. a president) or collective actor (e.g. a legislative chamber) whose agreement is necessary to change the political status quo (pp. 718–719).
- The theory is not restricted to democracies. In dictatorships, veto players might include the military or religious leaders. What matters is which actors can block change, and this varies across countries, time and possibly policy areas (fn. 23, p. 719).
- **Institutional veto player:** generated by the constitution. The U.S. Constitution makes the president, Congress and the Senate veto players by giving them the right to block legislative change (p. 719).
- **Partisan veto player:** generated by the political game, not by a constitution. For example, particular parties in a legislature or coalition government may be partisan veto players if they can block change. This depends on how citizens vote, how votes become seats, how governments form, and so on (pp. 719–720).
- Institutional veto players are essentially fixed while the constitution stays the same. The identity of partisan veto players changes with political competition (p. 720).
- Fn. 24: the type of government and the size of the party system can likewise be recast in terms of partisan veto players. Coalition governments have more partisan veto players than single-party majority governments (p. 720).

**Intuition note.** Instead of asking "is this country federal?", ask "who can say no, and how far apart are they?" The same question works for a second chamber, a regional government or a court (pp. 718–720).

### Federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review as veto players (p. 720)
- Veto player theory suggests viewing the three institutions as different types of institutional veto player. All place hurdles on changing the status quo (p. 720).
- Bicameralism: a majority in the lower chamber may need a powerful upper chamber's support. In the U.S., both chambers must agree (p. 720).
- Courts are not veto players when they interpret ordinary statutes, because later legislation can override them. They are veto players when they engage in constitutional review (Tsebelis 2002, 226). The French Constitutional Council can invalidate laws before they are applied, so such courts are in many ways like a third legislative chamber (Stone Sweet 1992) (p. 720).
- Federalism: subnational government support may be needed, especially in policy areas constitutionally delegated to subnational governments (p. 720).
- Hence adopting federalism, bicameralism or constitutional review can be thought of as equivalent to increasing the number of institutional veto players (p. 720).
- **Claimed result.** Countries with many veto players with conflicting preferences are *likely* to have (a) greater policy stability, (b) smaller shifts in policy, (c) less variation in the size of shifts, (d) weaker agenda-setting powers. This in turn matters for judicial and bureaucratic activism, government stability and regime stability (p. 720).

### The spatial building blocks (pp. 720–722; Figure 15.4)
- Veto players are represented by their ideal points in an issue space. In Figure 15.4, A, B and C sit in two dimensions: Issue 1 is state intervention in the economy, Issue 2 is education spending. The status quo is SQ (pp. 720–721).
- Each veto player's **indifference curve** is a circle around his ideal point passing through SQ. It contains all outcomes equally distant from his ideal point as SQ. He is indifferent between any point on it and SQ, and prefers any point inside it, because such a point is closer to his ideal point (p. 721).
- The **winset** is the set of alternatives that would beat the status quo in a pairwise contest under the voting rule in use. By the definition of a veto player, unanimity is required. So the winset is the set of alternatives inside the indifference circle of every veto player. In Figure 15.4 it is the shaded petal-shaped area starting at SQ (pp. 721–722).

**Intuition note.** A proposal beats SQ only if every veto player prefers it to SQ (the problems later accept ties). Geometrically that is the overlap of all the circles. A small overlap leaves little room for change (pp. 721–722).

### Four effects of winset size (p. 722)
1. **Stability.** A large winset means many alternatives can defeat the status quo, so policy is less stable. A small winset means it is more stable.
2. **Size of shifts.** With a small winset, shifts must be small. With a large winset, more radical shifts are possible. If every alternative in the winset is equally possible, the average size of shifts rises with the winset's size.
3. **Variation in shifts.** Shifts must be small when the winset is small. When it is large they may be small or large. So we are likely to see more variation the larger the winset.
4. **Agenda setting.** An agenda setter makes take-it-or-leave-it proposals to the other veto players. He treats the winset as his constraint and picks the point in it closest to his ideal point. If C were the agenda setter in Figure 15.4, he would propose $c_{AS}$. When the winset is small, the agenda setter cannot move policy far from where the other veto players would want to move it if they were the agenda setter. When it is large, agenda setting matters much more.

### Number of veto players and the winset (pp. 723–724; Figure 15.5)
- Panel (a): two veto players, A and B. Panel (b): three veto players, A, B and C. The winset in (b) is smaller than in (a) (p. 723–724).
- Panel (c): two veto players, A and C. Its winset is *exactly the same size* as in (b). The new veto player B lies on the line connecting A and C, so A and C cannot jointly prefer anything to SQ that B will not also prefer. B is "absorbed" (p. 724).
- Fn. 26: the line connecting A and C is the "unanimity core". Adding a veto player to the unanimity core leaves the winset unchanged. This is discussed in problems 7 and 8 (p. 724).
- **Conclusion:** more veto players decrease the winset or leave it the same, and never increase it (p. 724).

### Ideological distance and the winset (pp. 724–725; Figure 15.6)
- Panel (a) has two veto players with similar ideal points. Panel (b) has two with more distant ideal points. The winset in (b) is smaller. The authors call this a general result that "always holds" (p. 724).
- Combined: the winset is determined jointly by the number of veto players and the distance between them. Generally we expect it to shrink as either or both increase (p. 724).
- For countries with federalism, bicameralism or constitutional review, expect (a) policy stability, (b) small shifts, (c) little variation in shifts, (d) weak agenda setting. These are likely to be especially strong if the institutional veto players have dissimilar preferences (p. 724).
- Examples the authors expect: lower and less radical policy output in divided government or cohabitation, and when coalition governments are ideologically diverse. Also greater stability when the two chambers, or central and subnational governments, are controlled by different parties (p. 724).

**Intuition note.** Adding a person who already agrees with whatever the others would accept changes nothing. Adding a person whose acceptable range cuts into the others' range, or moving veto players apart, narrows the overlap. Veto players are not a headcount: their positions matter (p. 724).

### Illustration: the United States and Obama (pp. 725–726)
- Many on the left were disappointed with the legislative achievements of President Obama after he took office in January 2009, despite his "hope" and "change" platform. He had proposed radical changes: a single-payer health system, strong financial regulation, closing Guantanamo, ending the Bush-era tax cuts for the wealthy, and a large stimulus. In all these cases change, if any, did not produce dramatic shifts (pp. 725–726).
- **Authors' explanation:** political scientists familiar with veto player theory were not surprised. The U.S. system, with constitutionalism, bicameralism and federalism, is full of veto players. The ideological distance between them increased significantly after the 2010 midterms, when the Republicans took Congress. So radical change was always going to be extremely unlikely (p. 726).
- This is not unique to Obama. Candidates often promise to shake things up, but the U.S. institutional structure "all but guarantees" this will not happen. The authors say that to a large extent this is what the Founding Fathers intended, since they designed institutions to protect the status quo and, with it, their privileges and power (p. 726).

### Consequences of policy stability (pp. 726–727)
- There is nothing inherently good or bad about policy stability. It is typically viewed as good by those who like the status quo and bad by those who do not (p. 726).
- **Government and regime stability.** Suppose a government promises radical reform but the veto-player configuration prevents or limits change. It will likely look ineffective. In a parliamentary democracy, actors wanting to resolve the crisis will likely push a no-confidence vote. So the theory predicts a link between policy stability and government instability in parliamentary systems (p. 727).
- In a presidential democracy there is no such mechanism. Actors may look to extraconstitutional means, such as a coup. So policy stability is predicted to increase the likelihood of regime instability in presidential democracies. This is taken up again in Chapter 16 (p. 727).
- **Judicial and bureaucratic activism.** Veto player theory suggests policy stability leads to high levels of both (Tsebelis 2002, 222–247). Judges make policy by interpreting statutes, and bureaucrats by implementing them. The legislature can overrule them with new legislation. But when policy is stable because many legislative veto players have dissimilar preferences, judges and bureaucrats can act close to their own ideal points, knowing the legislature cannot agree to override them. So expect more activism in federal and bicameral countries than in unitary and unicameral ones (p. 727).

**Intuition note.** The same blocking power that stops a reforming government from changing the law also stops the legislature from correcting judges and officials, who can then go their own way (p. 727).

### Conclusion (pp. 727–728)
- **Link to Chapter 11.** Arrow's theorem demonstrates that stable outcomes (group transitivity) can be guaranteed only if the freedom to form preferences (universal admissibility) or the ability of preferences to influence group decisions (nondictatorship) is compromised. If universal admissibility is held inalienable, the basic tension is between instability and dictatorship. Increased stability is likely to result from someone having been given agenda power (p. 727). Fn. 27: this "institutional trilemma" assumes independence from irrelevant alternatives and unanimity are met (p. 727).
- **Recap.** Chapters 12–14 covered executive–legislative relations, elections and party systems. This chapter added federalism, bicameralism and constitutionalism, which share one feature: each creates actors able to block change in the status quo. Federalism can create regional actors able to block national law. Bicameralism creates a second chamber. Constitutionalism lets judges overturn laws the legislature approves. Each creates an institutional veto player (p. 728). Fn. 28: presidentialism also creates one, the president, who can check the legislature (p. 728).
- Veto player theory can be seen as a modern version of arguments about mixed government and checks and balances that go back to theorists such as Aristotle (p. 728).
- Systems with many veto players "may, in some sense" overcome the instability of democratic institutions without creating a single powerful agenda setter (p. 728).
- **But** this does not fully overcome Arrow's dilemmas. Complex veto structures mean current decisions are profoundly influenced by the status quo. This grants something like dictatorial power to those who chose the past policies. If circumstances or tastes change, a large share of the populace might want change but be unable to get it. So there appears to be a trade-off between policy stability and the responsiveness of decisions to voters' preferences (p. 728).
- Next chapter: how institutions shape the translation of preferences into policy, in four areas (quality of representation, size of the welfare state, ethnic conflict, survival of democracy). It also revisits how policy stability may come at the expense of regime instability in presidential democracies (p. 728).

**Intuition note.** Many veto players can give stability without a single powerful agenda setter, but they let past policy decisions bind the present. If tastes change, many voters may want change and not be able to get it (p. 728).

### End-of-chapter problems (pp. 729–739)
Rule for all problems: a policy that is not worse than the status quo is acceptable. If an actor is indifferent, assume she chooses the new policy (p. 729).

**Problem 1: spatial model of bicameralism (pp. 729–730).**
- Setup, unicameral: median voter MV at 6, SQ at 3 on a 0–10 left–right line, with single-peaked preferences (Fig. 15.7). Part (a) asks for the set of policies MV prefers to SQ, what she would propose as agenda setter, and the outcome (pp. 729–730).
- Setup, bicameral: SQ = 3, upper-chamber median UC = 4, lower-chamber median LC = 6. Both chambers must agree (Fig. 15.8). Parts (b)–(c) ask for each chamber's preferred range, the overlap, and the proposal if the lower or the upper chamber is the agenda setter (p. 730).
- Part (d): repeat with SQ at 5 and the same ideal points (p. 730).

**Problem 2: spatial model of judicial review (pp. 731–734).** Loosely based on Ferejohn and Shipan (1990). The problem reports the Ferejohn–Shipan argument (not the authors' own claim) that agency policymaking is the routine practice in modern government and that statutes mostly constrain bureaucrats rather than give detailed directives; the passage then says a model putting agency actions first "seems likely" to be more relevant most of the time (p. 731).
- *Agency Policymaking Model:* the agency chooses a policy first. The legislative committee, which supervises the agency, may initiate legislation. It goes to the floor, where it can be amended freely. If the committee does not act, the agency's policy stands. Ideal points in Fig. 15.9: A = 2, LC = 5, L = 7. All actors have single-peaked preferences and vote sincerely. Parts (a)–(d) ask where the legislature would set policy if it can amend, the committee's preferred range, when the committee acts, and where the agency should set policy so it is not overturned (pp. 731–732).
- *Statutory Review Model:* a court can strike down the agency's policy, and policy then reverts to SQ. The committee may then legislate (p. 732). Scenario 1 (Fig. 15.10): SQ = 1, A = 2, C = 4, LC = 5, L = 7. Part (e) asks what happens if the court strikes the policy down; parts (f)–(h) use an agency policy of 3.1 (pp. 732–733).
- Scenario 2 (Fig. 15.11): C moves to 6. Scenario 3 (Fig. 15.12): C moves to 8. Parts (i)–(l) ask where the agency should set policy to avoid being struck down, and whether that is the final outcome (pp. 733–734).
- Parts (m)–(o) ask whether the court affects outcomes, whether it has to make rulings to do so, whether it moves policy toward or away from the legislature's median, and whether judicial activism is antidemocratic in this model (p. 734).

**Problems 3–4 (p. 734).** Use the "Constitution Finder" (Univ. of Richmond) to compare rights and duties in three constitutions. Use the Venice Commission links to study three constitutional or supreme courts.

**Problems 5–6: veto player theory (pp. 735–736).** Given figures with SQ and two (A, B) or three (A, B, C) veto players in two dimensions, draw indifference curves, shade the winset, and say what a winset is and whether the three-player winset is smaller (p. 735–736).

**Problem 7: the unanimity core (pp. 736–738).**
- Definition: the **unanimity core** is the set of policy alternatives that cannot be defeated in a pairwise contest by unanimity rule, a region of policy stability. If policy gets there, it cannot be moved (p. 736).
- Its size is directly related to the winset's size: whatever shrinks the winset enlarges the core, and vice versa. So policy stability goes with a small winset and a large core (p. 736).
- How to find it: imagine the ideal points as pins in a board and wrap string around the outside. In Fig. 15.15 it is the triangle connecting A, B, C (p. 737).
- Fn. 29: it is also called the "Pareto set" (Tsebelis 2002, 21). Hinich and Munger (1997, 61) are quoted: for points inside it, any change makes at least one veto player worse off (p. 737).
- Parts (a)–(f): show a point inside cannot be defeated and a point outside can; effect of moving ideal points apart; effect of adding a veto player inside or outside the triangle; and whether more veto players always increase stability by enlarging the core (pp. 737–738).

**Problem 8: activist judges with agenda-setting power (pp. 738–739).** Judges move first by interpreting a law. The legislature then decides whether to overrule. Fig. 15.16 shows legislative veto players L1, L2, L3 and judges J1, J2 in two dimensions. Parts (a)–(e): draw the unanimity core, check whether each judge setting policy at her ideal point can be overturned, consider more distant legislators and the core's size versus judicial discretion, and ask where more activism is expected, federal/bicameral or unitary/unicameral (pp. 738–739).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
One policy dimension, SQ = 0. Under unanimity, each veto player accepts any point at least as close to his ideal point as SQ is (ties accepted, as in the problems rule, p. 729). In one dimension the circles become intervals.

1. Veto players A at 4 and B at 6.
   - A accepts points within distance 4 of 4: [0, 8].
   - B accepts points within distance 6 of 6: [0, 12].
   - Winset = [0, 8], length 8.
   - Agenda setter A proposes 4. Agenda setter B proposes 6. The gap between them is 2.
   - Unanimity core = segment between ideal points = [4, 6].
2. Add C at 5, which lies between A and B ("absorbed"). C accepts points within distance 5 of 5: [0, 10]. Winset = [0,8] ∩ [0,10] = [0, 8], unchanged. Core is still [4, 6].
3. Instead add D at −1, on the other side of SQ. D's distance to SQ is 1, so D accepts [−2, 0]. Winset = [0, 8] ∩ [−2, 0] = {0}, just SQ. No change is possible, and any agenda setter must propose 0, so agenda power vanishes. The core is now [−1, 6], bigger than before.

This shows the chapter's pattern: adding a veto player never enlarges the winset (steps 2 and 3). A smaller winset goes with a bigger core and weaker agenda setters.

## Concepts
- [[veto-player]] — actor whose agreement is necessary to change the status quo. Individual or collective, in democracies or not (pp. 718–719, fn. 23)
- [[veto-player-theory]] (new) — approach that describes a country's institutions by its configuration of veto players (p. 718)
- [[institutional-veto-player]] (new) — veto player created by the constitution, fixed while the constitution is unchanged (pp. 719–720)
- [[partisan-veto-player]] (new) — veto player created by political competition (votes, seats, government formation). Its identity changes (pp. 719–720)
- [[winset]] (new) — set of alternatives that beat the status quo. Under veto players, the intersection of all indifference circles (pp. 721–722)
- [[unanimity-core]] (new) — set of policies that cannot be defeated by unanimity rule. Also called the Pareto set (p. 736; fn. 29, p. 737)
- [[agenda-control]] — a veto player who makes take-it-or-leave-it proposals picks the winset point closest to his ideal point. Its importance grows with the winset (p. 722)
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — veto players as ideal points, with indifference circles, in an issue space (pp. 720–721)
- [[institutions]] — here, federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review, viewed as checks and balances on the status quo (p. 718)
- [[arrow-impossibility-theorem]] — recalled in the conclusion as the source of the trade-off between stability and nondictatorship or universal admissibility (p. 727)
- [[constitutional-review-models]] (new) — American (decentralized, a posteriori, concrete) versus European (centralized, abstract and/or concrete, broad standing) (p. 718)
- [[judicial-bureaucratic-activism]] (new) — judges and bureaucrats making policy close to their own ideal points. Veto player theory expects more of it when the legislature cannot agree to override (p. 727)
