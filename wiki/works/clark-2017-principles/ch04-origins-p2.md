# The Origins of the Modern State (part 2/2): the predatory view, normal-form games, and cooperation without a state

## L0 — One line
If rulers are self-interested actors who fight rivals, the predatory view explains the modern state as a by-product of war-making and resource extraction; and game theory shows that cooperation fails without a state in a one-shot game but can be sustained in an infinitely repeated one if players value the future enough.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks why a state that protects citizens might also prey on them, and where states come from. Answer: the predatory view treats rulers as self-interested actors who face rivals. To survive they make war, which requires extracting resources, and this unintentionally builds states. Rulers who win citizens' quasi-voluntary compliance may do better than those who simply seize assets. The unit then teaches normal-form games (simultaneous choices): best replies, Nash equilibrium (no one gains by switching alone), dominant strategies, the Prisoner's Dilemma and other games. A box shows that in an infinitely repeated game, cooperation without a state can be an equilibrium if players value the future enough, but it is not guaranteed.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a game with payoffs; reading a 2×2 table; sums of a geometric series; basic inequalities.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Surrendering control of violence to the state may create a new problem between citizens and the state (who will guard the guardian?), so solutions to political problems can erase their own benefits (p. 114).
- The predatory view asks when a state will enforce rules rather than use its advantage in violence to prey on citizens. It sees rulers as threatened by rivals and as extracting resources for security. Tilly compares states to extortion rackets (pp. 114–115).
- Population growth and new military technology raised the economies of scale of protection (unit costs fall as output rises). This favoured bigger rulers, and European political geography is, in the authors' words, essentially an unintended result of rulers' survival strategies (p. 116).
- Tilly lists four activities of rulers (war making, state making, protection, extraction); the authors sum up the argument as "war makes states". They add that today's states exist only because they outcompeted rivals who are now gone (pp. 116–117).
- Rulers could seize assets outright or win quasi-voluntary compliance (subjects pay because they feel they get something back), which may raise net extraction. Herbst applies the scale logic to weak states in sub-Saharan Africa (p. 118).
- Philosophers of science would call the predatory view a "progressive problem shift" over contract theory, say the authors: it seems to explain what contract theory explains and answers some of its open questions. It is almost exclusively positive (it describes behaviour rather than saying what people ought to do) and treats rulers as egoistic rational actors like the rest of us (p. 119).
- The unit gives a four-step method for normal-form games (payoff table, best replies, Nash equilibria, dominant strategies) and applies it to the Prisoner's Dilemma and the arms race (pp. 120–124).
- Problems 1–10 present further games: Chicken, Stag Hunt, coordination games, Rock–Paper–Scissors, football, Mafia, counterterrorism, free trade. No solutions are printed (pp. 125–137).
- Box 4.1: in an infinitely repeated State of Nature Game, if both players use grim trigger (refrain until the other steals, then steal forever), (Refrain; Refrain) is a Nash equilibrium when the discount factor $d$ (how much the future counts) exceeds $1/2$. (Steal; Steal) stays an equilibrium too, so cooperation is possible but not guaranteed (pp. 138–142).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the contractarian discussion (pp. 114)
- This part opens mid-argument. It contrasts Hobbes with Jefferson. Jefferson, borrowing from Locke and other social-contract theorists, held that we have a natural right (what we could obtain in the state of nature) to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. He also believed that our commitment to the state was so conditional that we should probably engage in revolution, or at least rewrite the Constitution, every couple of decades (p. 114).
- The authors say current disputes over cutting civil liberties to fight terrorism echo this debate. Politicians who say freedom means little without security take, in the authors' words, a distinctly Hobbesian view (p. 114).
- The authors' point: creating the state may solve the problem between individuals, but it may also create a potential new problem between us and the state. If we give up control of violence, what stops the state using it against us? Some put this as the question of who will guard the guardian (p. 114).
- Once the state has a "comparative advantage" in violence, the authors "would expect" a renegotiation of the social contract. At a minimum it would set the tax rate so high that the citizen is indifferent between the state of nature and civil society (p. 114).
- Many game-theoretic models imply that solutions to political problems often change behaviour in ways that erase the solutions' benefits (p. 114).

*Intuition note.* Giving someone the power to stop others from hurting you also gives that someone the power to hurt you. The next section builds a theory of the state around that worry.

### The predatory view of the state (pp. 114–119)
**Definition.** The contractarian view focuses on conflicts among individuals. The predatory view focuses on conflicts between citizens and the state. It asks when a state enforces rules and fosters cooperation rather than using its comparative advantage in violence to prey on citizens (p. 114). A state that effectively controls violence can threaten the citizens it, in the social-contract view, has a duty to protect (p. 114).

**Rulers face their own security dilemma.** Rulers resemble individuals in the state of nature because rivals constantly vie to replace them. They extract resources both to ensure their survival and because leaving resources with rivals is dangerous (pp. 114–115).

**Tilly's extortion-racket image.** Like the contract view, the predatory view sees the state as trading security for revenue. The difference is that the seller of security also threatens the buyer's security (p. 115).
- The state demands tribute (taxes, obedience) in return for protection that includes protection from the state itself (p. 115).
- The authors illustrate with a Monty Python skit. Gangsters tell an army colonel it would be a shame if something happened to his operation, a thinly veiled demand for payment for "protection". On the predatory view, the state plays the gangsters' role toward its own citizens (p. 115).
- Proponents of the predatory view argue: if individuals are not trustworthy and public-spirited (otherwise no state would be needed), why assume state officials with a near monopoly on force are? They say Rousseau's advice to take men as they are and laws as they might be applies at least as much to rulers as to the ruled (p. 115).

**Origins in feudal Europe (Tilly 1985).** Tilly argues that predation varied across early modern Europe because rulers faced cross-pressures (p. 115).
- After Rome's decline, Europe was a hodgepodge of local lords who gave peasants protection in exchange for rents in kind or service. Like crime bosses, lords constantly tried to put down external rivals and internal challengers (p. 115).
- Changes in military technology favoured lords who controlled more peasants and land. Holdings were consolidated under feudal kings, who at first coexisted uneasily with local lords. The balance then tilted toward kings and the lords became subjects (pp. 115–116).

**Economies of scale in violence (p. 116).**
- *Definition:* a production process has economies of scale if unit costs fall as the number of units produced rises. Big producers of violence then beat small ones (p. 116).
- In the "high middle ages" (tenth to thirteenth century), population grew while agricultural land was held constant. The marginal productivity of labor (extra output from one more worker) therefore fell. It paid less to add serfs on the same land (p. 116).
- New military technology (armored cavalry, crossbows, better fortification, siege weapons) raised the fixed costs of protection (Bartlett 1993). Together these raised the economies of scale of protection (p. 116).
- Lords could restore profitability by controlling more land, since falling labor productivity could be offset by cultivating new land. Investments in protecting one manor could also be redeployed to others (p. 116).

**Bargaining with serfs.** A serf who found his lord's demands unreasonable could flee to a neighbouring manor. Lords were tempted to keep runaways, so competition for serfs could erode a lord's bargaining power. This would be harder if one lord centralized coercive authority over many manors (North and Thomas 1971) (p. 116).

**Result.** The economies of scale in protection gave entrepreneurial rulers incentives to conquer neighbours. Underused arable land gave them incentives to settle new lands. Fierce, costly competition pushed rulers to administer efficiently and extract revenue efficiently. The authors, following Tilly, call Europe's political geography essentially an unintended consequence of rulers' strategies to keep power (p. 116).

**Tilly's four activities (p. 116).**
1. War making: eliminating or neutralizing rivals outside the territory where the ruler has clear and continuous priority as wielder of force.
2. State making: doing the same to rivals inside that territory.
3. Protection: doing the same to the enemies of one's clients.
4. Extraction: acquiring the means to carry out the first three.

**Tilly's argument (quotations, pp. 117).**
- Pursuing war drew power holders "willy-nilly" into extracting resources and promoting capital accumulation. War making, extraction and capital accumulation interacted to shape state making (p. 117).
- Power holders did not intend to create national states (centralized, differentiated, autonomous, extensive political organizations, in Tilly's description) and did not ordinarily foresee them. They warred to overcome competitors and enjoy power within a secure or expanding territory (p. 117).
- In the short run they got capital by conquest, selling assets, or coercing accumulators of capital. In the long run they needed regular access to capitalists for credit and some form of regular taxation (p. 117).
- Tilly (1985, 183): extraction meant eliminating, neutralizing or coopting the great lord's internal rivals, and created tax agencies, police, courts and exchequers. War making expanded military organization (standing army, war industries, bureaucracies, and "rather later" schools) (p. 117).
- The authors' summary: the modern state arose as a by-product of leaders trying to survive. "War makes states" (Tilly 1985, 170). The kill-or-be-killed logic behind the predatory view makes clear, the authors say, that today's states exist only because they outcompeted rivals now gone (p. 117).

*Intuition note.* Nobody set out to build a modern state. Rulers wanted to beat rivals, that needed money, and collecting money required tax offices, police and courts. Those organizations were the state.

**Limits on predation: quasi-voluntary compliance (pp. 117–118).**
- Rulers needing resources against geopolitical pressure had two options. They could seize assets outright (the predatory state's strategy). Or they could rely on what Levi (1989, 52) calls "quasi-voluntary compliance" (the limited state's strategy) (pp. 117–118).
- *Definition:* the subject feels he gets something, such as policy concessions or limits on future state behaviour, in return for taxes (p. 118).
- Advantages over outright predation: rulers spend fewer resources on coercion, and subjects may invest and innovate more, expanding the tax base. Leaders can then meet external challenges without, in the authors' image, killing the goose that laid the golden egg, taking a smaller portion of a larger pie (p. 118).
- Not all states succeeded in limiting predation, and as a result the character and consequences of rule varied widely across early modern Europe. Chapter 6 returns to why some leaders limited predation more than others (p. 118).

**Africa (Herbst 2000, inspired by Tilly).** The modern state developed later and less completely in sub-Saharan Africa. Reasons given: modest returns from agriculture, rough geography that makes administering large holdings difficult, large distances between populated areas, and outside interference limiting geopolitical competition. In effect the payoff to effective state projection was not always worth the cost. Large areas nominally controlled by states created at the end of the colonial period appear "stateless". State failure "may occur" when a state's nominal size exceeds, for historical reasons such as colonization and decolonization, the "economy of scale" of protection implied by political, economic and geographic conditions (p. 118).

### Conclusion (pp. 118–119)
- The contract theory explains how the state solves conflict among society's members. Evolution-of-cooperation work (Box 4.1) points out that a state is not strictly necessary for this, but does not conclusively show that decentralized cooperation is sufficient for optimal outcomes (p. 118).
- Contract theory says little about conflicts between rulers and ruled. The predatory theory assumes such conflicts and tries to explain why states do not always run roughshod over citizens (pp. 118–119).
- Because the predatory view recognizes and explains exchange between rulers and ruled, philosophers of science would call it a "progressive problem shift". It seems to explain all that contract theory explains and answers some questions that theory raises (p. 119).
- Caveat: the predatory theory is almost exclusively "positive" (explaining behaviour, not what people ought to do). The contract theory arose largely to explore citizens' moral responsibilities to the state (p. 119).
- Further advantages (p. 119):
  - It views rulers as egoistic, maximizing, rational actors, so leader behaviour can be integrated with other models of human behaviour.
  - It shows how goal-oriented behaviour changes the institutional environment and how institutional change alters leader behaviour. For example, political units grew large to benefit from economies of scale in violence.
  - It has the potential to explain why some rulers share power or limit extraction. Chapter 6 takes this up; Chapter 5 deals with conceptualizing and measuring democracy.
- The page also lists the chapter's Key Concepts (p. 119).

### Preparation for the problems (pp. 120–121)
Normal-form (strategic-form) games model *simultaneous* choices. The previous chapter used extensive-form games for sequential choices (p. 120). The author's procedure:
1. **Draw the payoff table** (pp. 120). Identify the players. Draw the table showing each player's choices. Determine payoffs for each outcome. Write payoffs in each cell, row player first and column player second, separated by a comma (p. 120).
2. **Solve the game** (p. 120). Taking the row player's role, find the best choice against each possible choice of the column player (the *best replies*) and underline or circle those payoffs. Repeat for the column player (p. 120).
3. **Identify Nash equilibria** (pp. 120–121). A Nash equilibrium is a cell where both players' payoffs are underlined (both play best replies). List actions as (row action; column action). There may be several equilibria, or none (pp. 120–121).
4. **Dominant strategy** (p. 121). The authors say a player has one if she makes the same choice whatever the other player does. If both players have one, there is a dominant-strategy Nash equilibrium (p. 121).

### Worked example: the Prisoner's Dilemma (pp. 121–124)
**Story (p. 121).** Two suspects are held in separate cells. Evidence suffices for a minor offence only, unless one talks. Both Quiet: each convicted of the minor offence. Exactly one Talks: that one goes free and testifies, and the other is convicted of the major crime. Both Talk: each convicted of the major crime with some leniency.

**Preferences (p. 122).** Written with suspect 1's action first:
- Suspect 1: (Talk; Quiet) > (Quiet; Quiet) > (Talk; Talk) > (Quiet; Talk).
- Suspect 2: (Quiet; Talk) > (Quiet; Quiet) > (Talk; Talk) > (Talk; Quiet).
- Ordinal payoffs (numbers that only rank outcomes) are 4 for best, 3, 2, and 1 for worst.

**Payoff table (Fig. 4.10, p. 122).** Rows are suspect 1, columns suspect 2:

| | Quiet | Talk |
|---|---|---|
| **Quiet** | 3, 3 | 1, 4 |
| **Talk** | 4, 1 | 2, 2 |

**Solution (pp. 122–123).**
- Suspect 1's best replies: against Quiet, Talk (4 > 3). Against Talk, Talk (2 > 1).
- Suspect 2's best replies are the same: Talk against either choice (4 > 3; 2 > 1).
- Only the cell (Talk; Talk) has both payoffs underlined. It is the unique Nash equilibrium, with payoff 2 each.
- Both suspects always talk, so both have a dominant strategy and (Talk; Talk) is a dominant-strategy Nash equilibrium.
- Both would be better off with (Quiet; Quiet), 3 each. It is not an equilibrium because each would talk if he expected the other to keep quiet (4 > 3).
- Promises made before the crime are not credible once the suspects are caught. One of the central characteristics of the game is that the Nash equilibrium is suboptimal from the players' perspective, since both prefer another outcome (p. 123).

*Intuition note.* Each player gains by betraying whatever the other does, so both betray and both end up worse off than if they had trusted each other. Better outcomes can exist that no individual can safely reach alone.

**Wider use (p. 124).** The authors say the State of Nature Game has the same basic structure as the Prisoner's Dilemma. The game has also been used for arms races (Powell 1999). In the Cold War game the US and the Soviet Union arguably each rank (they build up, the other reduces) best and (they reduce, the other builds up) worst. Both-reduce ranks above both-build-up (lower cost, security maintained versus higher cost, no added security) (p. 124).

**Fig. 4.11 (p. 124).**

| US \ USSR | Reduce Arms | Continue Buildup |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce Arms** | 3, 3 | 1, 4 |
| **Continue Buildup** | 4, 1 | 2, 2 |

The Nash equilibrium is (Continue Buildup; Continue Buildup), and the expected outcome is an arms race (p. 124).

### Box 4.1: Can cooperation occur without the state? (pp. 138–142)
**Setup (p. 138).** Hobbes: life in the state of nature is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". In the State of Nature Game both players have a dominant strategy to steal. In the Civil Society Game a large enough state punishment makes refraining dominant. This is Hobbes's justification for the state. Axelrod (1981, 1984) and Taylor (1976) argue cooperation can emerge in a decentralized way if individuals interact repeatedly and care enough about future benefits (p. 138).

**Discount factor (p. 138).**
- It tells how much future benefits are discounted compared with today's; high means people value the future a lot.
- If $1,000 now or in a month is equal to you, $d=1$. If the future payment is worthless to you, $d=0$. In between, $0<d<1$. So $0\le d\le 1$ (p. 138).
- Note: the text also calls $d$ the "discount rate" in places.

**Present value (p. 139).** The present value of a stream of benefits is what the stream is worth today. For $1 a day, tomorrow's dollar is worth $1d$ today, two days ahead $1d^2$, and so on (p. 139):

$$\text{PV(Promise)} = 1 + 1d + 1d^2 + 1d^3 + 1d^4 + 1d^5 + \dots + 1d^{\infty} \tag{4-1}$$

$$\text{PV(Promise)} = 1 + d + d^2 + d^3 + d^4 + d^5 + \dots + d^{\infty} \tag{4-2}$$

$$1 + d + d^2 + d^3 + \dots + d^{\infty} = \frac{1}{1-d} \tag{4-3}$$

The authors state (4-3) without proof. (added step) Let $S = 1 + d + d^2 + \dots$. Then $S = 1 + dS$, so $S(1-d)=1$ and $S=1/(1-d)$ when $d<1$. For $5$ a day the present value is $5/(1-d)$; for $3$, $3/(1-d)$ (p. 139).

**Repeated State of Nature Game (Fig. 4.23, p. 140).** Payoffs for (A; B): (Refrain, Refrain) = 3,3; (Refrain, Steal) = 1,4; (Steal, Refrain) = 4,1; (Steal, Steal) = 2,2 (p. 140). Each round players choose again.

**Grim trigger strategy (p. 139).** Refrain as long as the other refrains. If the other ever steals, steal in the next round and in all future rounds. If both use it and both refrain, the payoff is 3 per period, discounted:

$$\text{PV(Refrain)} = 3 + 3d + 3d^2 + 3d^3 + 3d^4 + 3d^5 + \dots + 3d^{\infty} = \frac{3}{1-d} \tag{4-4}$$

The printed last term of (4-4) reads "$d^{\infty}$" without the 3; the image shows it so, and the context requires $3d^{\infty}$ (p. 139).

**Deviation (p. 140).** A Nash equilibrium requires that no player has an incentive to deviate on their own, given what the other player is doing. Suppose A starts stealing while B plays grim trigger. A gets 4 in the first period. B then steals forever, so A's best reply is to keep stealing and get 2 each period afterwards:

$$\text{PV(Steal)} = 4 + 2d + 2d^2 + 2d^3 + 2d^4 + 2d^5 + \dots + 2d^{\infty} \tag{4-5}$$

$$\text{PV(Steal)} = 4 + 2d(1 + d + d^2 + d^3 + d^4 + d^5 + \dots + d^{\infty}) \tag{4-6}$$

Using (4-3):

$$\text{PV(Steal)} = 4 + \frac{2d}{1-d} \tag{4-7}$$

**Condition for refraining (p. 141).** A does not deviate if PV(Refrain) > PV(Steal):

$$\frac{3}{1-d} > 4 + \frac{2d}{1-d} \tag{4-8}$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{3}{1-d} > \frac{4-4d+2d}{1-d} \tag{4-9}$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{3}{1-d} > \frac{4-2d}{1-d} \tag{4-10}$$

$$\Rightarrow 3 > 4-2d \tag{4-11}$$

$$\Rightarrow 2d > 1 \tag{4-12}$$

$$\Rightarrow d > \tfrac{1}{2} \tag{4-13}$$

(added step) (4-9) puts $4$ over $(1-d)$ as $4(1-d)=4-4d$. (4-11) follows because $1-d>0$ for $d<1$. B has the same payoffs, so he also refrains when $d>1/2$ (p. 141).

**Conclusions of the box (pp. 141–142).**
- (Refrain; Refrain) can be a Nash equilibrium of the infinitely repeated game when both use grim trigger and $d>1/2$. The threshold $1/2$ is specific to these payoffs. The general result is that grim-trigger players can sustain cooperation in equilibrium as long as the discount factor is sufficiently high (p. 141).
- Footnote 21: the infinite horizon (or not knowing when the game ends) matters. With a known, finite number of periods, cooperation cannot be sustained. Both steal in the last period, so both steal in the one before it, and so on back to period one (p. 141).
- Tit-for-tat (TFT): start by cooperating, then copy the other player's last move. With a sufficiently high discount factor, TFT can also sustain cooperation (p. 141). The authors conclude cooperation can occur in the state of nature without a state. This runs counter to Hobbes and supports anarchists who think society can thrive without a state (pp. 141–142).
- Caveats (p. 142):
  - (Steal; Steal) is also a Nash equilibrium of the repeated game.
  - Game theory cannot say which equilibrium occurs, so there is no reason to believe cooperation is more likely than the others.
  - Sustaining cooperation takes effort. Everyone must monitor everyone and the group must punish those caught stealing.
  - So decentralized cooperation may not be the best thing to rely on; the authors suggest a state may be a more preferable and reliable route to cooperation.

*Intuition note.* If the game goes on and the future matters enough, a threat of endless stealing makes one-time cheating not worth the quick gain. But the same game also allows endless stealing as an equilibrium, so patience makes cooperation possible, not certain.

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text.* It applies the Box's present-value logic to the "goose that laid the golden egg" point (p. 118), with invented numbers.

A ruler picks one of two policies.
- **Limited:** take 30 per period forever.
- **Seize:** take 80 now. Subjects then stop investing, and the ruler takes only 12 per period thereafter.

Formulas, using $1/(1-d)$ from (4-3):
- $PV(\text{Limited}) = \dfrac{30}{1-d}$.
- $PV(\text{Seize}) = 80 + \dfrac{12d}{1-d}$.

Check at $d=0.9$:
- Limited: $30/0.1 = 300$.
- Seize: $80 + 12(0.9)/0.1 = 80 + 10.8/0.1 = 80 + 108 = 188$.

Limited is better. At $d=0.5$:
- Limited: $30/0.5 = 60$.
- Seize: $80 + 6/0.5 = 80 + 12 = 92$.

Seizing is better.

Threshold: Limited is better when $\frac{30}{1-d} > 80 + \frac{12d}{1-d}$. Multiply by $(1-d)>0$: $30 > 80(1-d) + 12d = 80 - 68d$. So $68d > 50$ and $d > 50/68 \approx 0.735$.
- At $d=0.75$: $30/0.25 = 120$ against $80 + 9/0.25 = 116$, so Limited wins.
- At $d=0.7$: $100$ against $80 + 8.4/0.3 = 108$, so Seize wins.

A ruler who cares enough about the future prefers to limit predation.

## Concepts
- [[contractarian-view-of-state]] (new) — the state arises to solve conflicts of interest among individuals; like the predatory view, it sees the state as trading security for revenue (pp. 114–115, 118).
- [[predatory-view-of-state]] (new) — states with effective control of violence can threaten and exploit citizens; asks when they instead enforce rules (p. 114).
- [[economies-of-scale-in-violence]] (new) — unit costs of violence fall as more is produced, so big producers of protection have the advantage (p. 116).
