# Majoritarian vs consensus democracy: how institutions combine and what each type delivers

## L0 — One line
Democracies tend to cluster into a majoritarian type that concentrates power and a consensus type that disperses it, and each type does better on a different set of democratic goals (accountability and mandates versus representation).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Democratic institutions vary enormously. Yet they tend to come in two bundles: majoritarian (power concentrated) and consensus (power dispersed). The chapter asks why institutions cluster and how each bundle performs. Clark, Golder and Golder argue that many institutions are causally linked and that the two types rest on different visions of elections. Majoritarian systems score higher on accountability (voters can reward or punish parties) and mandates (policies a government is authorized to carry out). Consensus systems mirror society in the legislature more closely, and most studies find more ideological congruence (government views match citizens'). The authors report a scholarly consensus that constitution writers face a trade-off, and suggest the two types probably need different criteria.

**Prerequisites:** how electoral systems, party systems, parliamentary and presidential regimes, federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review work (Chs. 12–15); Arrow's theorem and the median voter idea (Ch. 11, only referred to here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Arrow's theorem (no decision rule can have all three of: coherent group choices, free individual preferences, no dictator) implies that designers must make trade-offs. In practice they frequently make one overall trade-off: concentrate power (majoritarian) or disperse it (consensus) (p. 743).
- The two types rest on different visions of elections. The majoritarian vision treats elections as choosing a government that is then judged. The consensus vision treats them as choosing representatives who will bargain (pp. 744–746).
- Institutions are classed along a majoritarian–consensus dimension (Table 16.1). Many of them are causally linked, so choosing certain majoritarian institutions can virtually guarantee others. This helps explain why democracies tend to come in two main types (pp. 747–751).
- Lijphart's data on 36 democracies (1945–1996) show that majoritarian institutions tend to go together. The authors read the star-plots (one star-shaped chart per country, longer lines meaning more majoritarian) as showing mostly large (majoritarian) or small (consensus) shapes (pp. 751–753).
- Majoritarian systems tend to give more accountability (voters can reward or punish parties) and clarity of responsibility (voters can tell who is responsible for policy). Government identifiability (voters can tell which government they are voting for) is higher in them too, but mandate claims (authority to carry out campaign policies) are open to challenge because single-party majorities are typically manufactured majorities: seat majorities without a vote majority (pp. 755–761).
- Responsiveness (how well representatives respond to changes in voters' preferences) is understood differently in the two visions (Figure 16.3). Both types come close to their ideals but fall short (pp. 761–763).
- Congruence (how closely representatives' preferences match citizens') between government and citizens is, by most studies, higher in consensus democracies. Three recent studies challenge this. One study finds that legislatures under proportional rules reflect society's ideological diversity more accurately (pp. 764–765).
- The authors suggest we should probably use different criteria for the two types, so consensus democracies are not clearly failing when accountability is weak (p. 759). Hybrids such as the United States raise the question of whether mixing is best, and scholars do not agree (p. 765).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter introduction (pp. 741–743)
- The chapter opens with an epigraph from Rustow on proportional representation and an overview. The overview lists four literatures: how institutions reflect voters' interests, economic effects, ethnic conflict, and democratic survival (p. 741). This part covers only the first.
- Democratic institutions vary widely: presidential, parliamentary or semi-presidential; majoritarian, proportional or mixed electoral rules; many or few parties; federal or unitary; bicameral or unicameral; with or without constitutional review. Government types also differ (p. 742).
- Choosing one institution opens further choices, for example investiture votes, constructive no-confidence votes, district magnitude, thresholds, and the single transferable vote versus list systems (p. 742).
- Because of Arrow's theorem there is no ideal decision mechanism. Institutional choice is "second bests" and likely depends on context. Designers do not mix institutions "with reckless abandon", since particular sets recur (p. 742).
- Designers choose institutions to get outcomes they want. This presumes they know how institutions affect outcomes (p. 742).

### Combining institutions: majoritarian or consensus democracy? (p. 743)
- Arrow's theorem creates an institutional "trilemma" (Figure 11.10). Designers can satisfy at most two of three attributes: group transitivity (coherent, stable group choices), universal admissibility (freedom to form any preferences), and nondictatorship (preferences influence group decisions) (p. 743).
- Designers could trade off differently for each institution. In practice they frequently make one trade-off for the whole system of government (p. 743).
- They either disperse power or concentrate it. Concentrated-power democracies are **majoritarian**. Dispersed-power democracies are **consensus** (Lijphart 1984) (p. 743).
- Footnote 1 says these types are sometimes called "Westminster" and "proportional" democracies. The authors prefer "majoritarian" and "consensus" as more indicative of how the models are meant to work (p. 743).

**Intuition note.** Arrow's result means no rule gives everything. Real constitutions often pick one side of the trade-off for the whole system.

### Majoritarian and consensus visions of democracy (pp. 743–747)
Because policy is made by elected representatives rather than by citizens themselves, elections are, to the extent that they let citizens take part in policymaking, the primary "instruments of democracy". Two visions, each with a long tradition, see their role differently (pp. 743–744).

**Majoritarian vision** (p. 744)
- Elections let citizens choose between two competing teams. The winning team forms the government and should implement the policies it campaigned on.
- Citizens judge the incumbents' record. They reward by voting for the incumbent and punish by voting for the opposition.
- Citizens influence policy only at election time, and not between elections.
- Policy should follow what the majority wants. Minority preferences should have no influence.
- **Tocqueville's view** (an early proponent): "The very essence of democratic government consists in the absolute sovereignty of the majority" (p. 744).
- This vision essentially requires all policymaking power to be concentrated in a majority government. Dispersing power would almost certainly involve the minority opposition, which this vision sees as illegitimate (p. 744).

**Consensus vision** (pp. 745–746)
- Elections choose representatives from as wide a range of social groups as possible. These representatives then bargain over policy in the legislature (p. 745).
- Elections are not a referendum on the government's record. A central goal is a legislature that is a "miniature reflection" of society (p. 745).
- The tradition is old. **Burke** wrote that a legislature's virtue lies in being an express image of the nation's feelings. **Mill** wrote of an arena where the opinion of "every section" of the nation can show itself (p. 745).
- Representatives are elected to bargain, not to enact a precise set of policies. This frequently means shifting legislative majorities. Citizens can therefore influence policy between elections too (p. 745).
- Majority preferences get no privileged status. All groups, including minorities, should influence policy in direct proportion to their electoral size. **Dahl** is cited for equal consideration of every person's interests (p. 745).
- To protect minorities, power should be dispersed so the minority has some valuable policymaking influence. If power is too concentrated, the majority may capture it and use it against the minority, which this vision sees as illegitimate (pp. 745–746).

**Summaries by others** (p. 746)
- **Mueller:** citizens either elect "a government" (the party whose policies they prefer) or elect a truly representative body. That body votes as the citizens would have in a grand "town meeting".
- **Lijphart:** the visions differ in how they answer who will govern and to whose interests the government should be responsive. Majoritarian: a majority of the people. Consensus: as many people as possible.
- **Powell's view:** the majoritarian vision might be preferred when issues are clear-cut and citizens united. Consensus representation may be preferred when issues are complex, citizens are divided, or unforeseen problems arise. Citizens who fear being in the minority on issues that dominate one election but expect to be in the majority on others may especially prefer representative agents (p. 746).
- The authors add that a democracy needs relatively stable rules. A choice between the types must therefore be made, and it is likely to endure (p. 746).

**Intuition note.** In the majoritarian picture, voters hire one team and fire it later. In the consensus picture, voters send a faithful sample of society to the bargaining table. The two pictures lead to opposite designs.

### Majoritarian and consensus institutions (pp. 747–754)
- A constitution that encourages single-party legislative majorities that control the executive, with power concentrated in a single-party government, is majoritarian. One that encourages equitable representation of multiple parties and dispersal of power among them is consensual (Powell) (p. 747).
- The two visions are the end points of a majoritarian–consensus dimension. The institutions of Chs. 12–15 are placed on it by whether they concentrate or disperse power (p. 747).
- Footnote 2 notes that the dimension is a continuum, not a dichotomy. For example, some forms of bicameralism disperse power more than others, and some electoral systems are more proportional than others (p. 747).

**Table 16.1 — Institutions and the majoritarian–consensus dimension** (p. 748)

| Institution | Majoritarian | Consensus |
|---|---|---|
| Electoral system | Majoritarian | Proportional |
| Party system | Two parties | Many parties |
| Government type | Single-party majority | Coalition/minority |
| Federalism | Unitary | Federal |
| Bicameralism | Unicameral | Bicameral |
| Constitutionalism | Legislative supremacy constitution | Higher law constitution |
| Regime type | Parliamentary | Presidential |
| Interest-group relations | Pluralism | Corporatism |
| Prototypical examples | Barbados, New Zealand (prior to 1996), United Kingdom | Belgium, Netherlands, Switzerland |

**Why institutions come in bundles.** Many of these institutions are causally related. Choosing certain majoritarian institutions can virtually guarantee others, and likewise for consensus ones (p. 747).

*Electoral system → party system → government type* (pp. 748–749)
- In majoritarian electoral systems only the candidates or parties with the most votes win, and usually only one candidate wins. Proportional systems give power in proportion to votes. The more proportional the system, the more it approximates the consensus vision (p. 748).
- Two-party systems concentrate power. Multiparty systems disperse it, and the more parties, the more dispersed (p. 748).
- Single-party majority governments concentrate power. Coalition and minority governments disperse it (p. 748).
- Footnote 3: even single-party minority governments, at least in parliamentary democracies, disperse power. Some "support" parties must keep the government in power, and their ability to bring it down means the government has to share policymaking power with them if it wants to stay in office (p. 748).
- From Duverger's theory (Ch. 14): majoritarian electoral systems tend to go with small party systems. Proportional systems tend to go with large party systems, at least where social heterogeneity is sufficiently high (p. 749).
- From Ch. 12: few parties make single-party majority governments much more likely. Many parties make coalition or minority governments much more likely (p. 749).
- So choosing an electoral system tends also to choose a party system and government type. We see either (a) majoritarian electoral system, small party system, frequent single-party majorities, or (b) proportional system, large party system, frequent coalition or minority governments (p. 749).

*Federalism, bicameralism, constitutionalism* (pp. 749–750)
- Federal states disperse power across territorial levels. Bicameral states disperse it between two chambers. Higher-law constitutions give bodies such as constitutional courts authority to invalidate government acts. Legislative supremacy constitutions concentrate power in the legislature (p. 749).
- The authors give a designer's logic for why these three go together. A designer wants a stable, credible decentralized government, which requires that the centre not usurp regional power. Otherwise regions might withdraw (p. 749).
- Writing federalism into the constitution helps, but it is not clear why actors would comply. A constitutional court could punish violations (p. 750).
- A court alone is "not necessarily" sufficient, because a national majority may amend the constitution to centralize. Checks and balances make such majorities harder, for example a bicameral legislature whose upper chamber represents the regions (p. 750).
- Combining the three can enhance the stability and credibility of decentralization (Bednar and others) (p. 750).
- Footnote 4 says a court's independence matters, since it relies on other institutions to enforce its decisions. Footnote 5 lists other ways to fragment national power, such as open party lists or regionally based parties. Riker is cited for the claim that a decentralized party system is crucial for a healthy federal system (p. 750).

*Regime type* (pp. 750–751)
- Presidential systems (separation of powers) fall at the consensus end, because power is divided between executive and legislature. Parliamentary systems fall at the majoritarian end, because power is concentrated in an executive backed by a legislative majority (p. 750).
- Premier-presidential democracies fall at the majoritarian end. President-parliamentary democracies depend on cohabitation (president and prime minister from different blocs). With cohabitation they are consensus, because power is divided. Without it they are majoritarian, because power is concentrated in the president (pp. 750–751).

*Interest groups* (p. 751)
- Corporatism brings labor, business and agriculture into formal policymaking and so disperses power. Pluralism keeps groups outside and concentrates power. Corporatism tends to encourage minority governments, another consensus institution (p. 751).

**Evidence: Lijphart's data and Figure 16.1** (pp. 751–753)
- Lijphart (1999) codes 36 democracies, 1945–1996. The figure shows a star-plot per country for the eight institutions (p. 751).
- Executive-parties dimension: electoral system, party system, regime type, government type, interest-group relations. Federal-unitary dimension: federalism, bicameralism, constitutionalism (p. 751).
- Each line's length shows how majoritarian an institution is, and the area of each star-plot is a good visual guide to how majoritarian the country's institutions are as a whole. The authors standardize Lijphart's measures to make the scales comparable (fn. 6) (p. 751).
- Executive-parties dimension: most majoritarian are Jamaica, the Bahamas, Trinidad and Tobago. Least majoritarian are Switzerland, Finland, Israel (p. 753).
- Federal-unitary dimension: most majoritarian are Luxembourg, New Zealand, Finland. Least majoritarian are the United States, Germany, Australia (p. 753).
- Most countries have either a relatively large or a relatively small star-plot (p. 753).

**Prototypes** (pp. 753–754)
- The United Kingdom is the historical prototype of the majoritarian ("Westminster") democracy. According to Lijphart, New Zealand came closest to the ideal type in 1945–1996. Two large parties dominated, one always had a legislative majority, and third parties barely won seats. It was also pluralist, unitary and unicameral (p. 753).
- Footnote 7: New Zealand deviates in having larger districts reserved for the Maori (about 12 percent of the population) to ensure minority representation (p. 753).
- In 1996 New Zealand replaced single-member plurality with a more proportional mixed system. The party system grew and coalition governments became more frequent. The authors add that the UK is also less Westminster than before (devolution, stronger third parties, the 2010 Conservative–Liberal coalition). The most majoritarian today are Caribbean (p. 753).
- Belgium is the consensus prototype: federal (since 1993), bicameral (since 1995), constitutional review (since 1984). Proportional representation gives a large party system and broad coalitions. Cabinets must have equal numbers of French- and Flemish-speaking ministers, which increases cabinet parties (p. 753).
- Broad coalitions bring compromise and attention to minority views. The negative side is difficulty reaching agreement (p. 754).
- After the June 2007 election, eleven parties won seats. The government took office in December, eight months later. The authors report protests, EU reports of deleterious economic effects, and media reports that some Belgians considered splitting the country (p. 754).
- The government collapsed less than two and a half years later. After the June 2010 election the same eleven parties were returned. A government was sworn in on December 5, 2011, 540 days after the election, a record (p. 754).
- The authors note that a country with many parties should expect delays in forming a government, and that its coalitions tend to be unstable and short lived. Broad representation may cost stability, and a stable government may cost voters' choice (p. 754).

**Intuition note.** Many of these institutions are causally linked and tend to push in the same direction. Proportional rules tend to go with many parties, and many parties make coalition or minority governments more likely. A designer who wants a decentralized state has reasons to add federalism, a constitutional court and a regional upper chamber. Choosing certain institutions can therefore virtually guarantee others.

### Evaluating the two visions (pp. 755–765)
Preferring one vision depends on how you value representation and meaningful choice versus efficiency and accountability (pp. 754–755). The authors look at accountability, mandates and representation. Majoritarian democracies value accountability and mandates over representation, and consensus democracies embody the opposite trade-off (p. 755).

#### Accountability and mandates (pp. 755–761)
**Accountability** is the extent to which voters can reward or punish parties for the policies they introduce. It rests on **retrospective voting**, in which voters judge past performance (p. 755).

*Why consensus democracies have lower accountability* (pp. 755–756)
- Voters typically do not choose the government directly. Single-party majorities are rare with many parties. Elections start bargaining over who governs, and party leaders' choices may or may not reflect citizens' preferences (pp. 755–756).
- Governments are often removed between elections without voters being consulted (p. 756).
- Party leaders may act as the voters' surrogates if they anticipate punishment at the polls (p. 756).
- A party that lost votes may still be in the next coalition. The authors single out Switzerland as perhaps the most extreme case. Its four main parties have shared government since 1959 irrespective of how citizens vote (the "magic formula"; fn. 10: revised in December 2003) (p. 756).
- In majoritarian democracies with two parties and single-party majorities, voters simply vote for the opposition (p. 756).

**Clarity of responsibility** is the ability to identify who is responsible for policy. The authors call it a necessary condition for accountability (p. 756).
- It rises with concentration of power. It is very high with a single-party majority, lower in coalitions where blame is unclear, and lower still in minority governments with shifting support parties (pp. 756–757).
- Federalism and bicameralism reduce clarity. Federalism allows blame shifting and credit claiming. Bicameral systems have a similar effect, particularly if the chambers are equally powerful and controlled by opposing sides (p. 757).
- Short government tenure, more likely with many diverse parties, blurs responsibility. So does a larger opposition role in the legislature (p. 757).
- Footnote 11: weak party discipline or factions make it unclear whom to blame within a party (p. 757).

**Figure 16.2** (p. 758)
- Dictatorships have very high clarity but essentially no accountability, since citizens cannot remove the dictator by electoral means. Fn. 12 notes the selectorate theory of Ch. 10: dictators are accountable to a winning coalition, but almost never through elections (p. 758).
- Majoritarian democracies: high clarity and high accountability. Consensus democracies: relatively low on both (p. 758).
- Because clarity is necessary for accountability, no system combines low clarity with high accountability (p. 758).

**Authors' judgement.** Those who value accountability should prefer majoritarian institutions. This does not mean consensus democracies do badly. Accountability is not particularly important in the consensus vision. Different criteria should probably be used for the two types (p. 759).

**Mandates** (pp. 759–761)
- A **mandate** is a policy that the government is both authorized and obligated to carry out. In the majoritarian vision elections give mandates, so voters engage in **prospective voting** (looking at promises) as well as retrospective voting. The consensus vision regards mandates as bad because they mean ignoring the minority's preferences (p. 759).
- Presumably, a government should be able to claim a mandate only if voters can meaningfully be said to have voted for it. This requires **government identifiability**, meaning voters can tell which government alternatives they are choosing (p. 759).
- Table 16.2 gives identifiability from high to low (p. 760):
  1. Few parties; single-party majority likely (high).
  2. Many parties, preelectoral coalitions.
  3. No preelectoral coalitions, but certain government coalitions likely.
  4. Many parties; coalitions known only after the election (low).
- Examples: single-party government nearly always in Greece, Spain, the UK. Very low in Belgium and Finland, where formation starts after the election. Preelectoral coalitions are common in Australia, France and Germany. Czech Republic 2006: voters expected a government from the winning bloc, but the blocs won exactly the same number of seats and were deadlocked for months (fn. 13) (p. 760).
- In general, identifiability is higher in majoritarian than in consensus democracies (p. 760).
- Identifiability is necessary but not sufficient for a mandate. Most people believe a government should also win at least a majority of votes cast (p. 760).
- It is extremely rare for a single party to win over 50 percent of the vote in majoritarian democracies. Single-party majority governments typically enjoy only "manufactured majorities": a party without a vote majority wins a seat majority because of disproportional vote-to-seat translation (pp. 760–761). The authors ask whether such a mandate claim is legitimate (p. 761).
- Dispersed power makes mandates hard to implement. Veto institutions such as bicameralism and federalism may block platforms, and in consensus democracies other actors can influence or block legislation. In majoritarian democracies such difficulties are minimized (p. 761).
- Summary: majoritarian democracies have high identifiability and easy implementation, but rarely a majority of votes. Consensus democracies have poor identifiability and veto players, but mandates are not a goal of that vision (p. 761).

**Intuition note.** Accountability needs two things: voters must know whom to blame, and they must be able to remove them. Concentrated power tends to give both. A mandate claim in a majoritarian democracy is open to challenge, because the winning party typically has less than half of the votes.

#### Representation (pp. 761–765)
The authors split representation into **responsiveness** (dynamic: how well representatives respond to changes in voter preferences) and **congruence** (static: how well representatives match voter preferences) (p. 761).

**Responsiveness** (pp. 761–763)
- Many scholars measure responsiveness by whether policies shift with public opinion. The authors instead ask whether a party's policymaking power tracks changes in its electoral support (pp. 761–762).
- Majoritarian ideal: a party with under 50 percent of the vote has no policymaking power, and one with over 50 percent has all of it. Consensus ideal: power in direct proportion to vote share. Figure 16.3 (adapted from Powell 2000) plots both. The majoritarian curve is flat at near zero until 50, then rises steeply to 100. The consensus line is the diagonal (p. 762).
- Majoritarian practice: most governments are single-party majorities, but many lack a majority of votes. The party with the most votes almost always governs. Powell notes that analysts and defenders of the majoritarian vision are seldom inhibited from replacing expectations about majorities with expectations about pluralities. The authors leave this judgement to the reader (p. 762).
- The authors suggest that majoritarian democracies are fully responsive when the majority-vote party governs, and only partially responsive when a mere plurality party governs (p. 762).
- Footnote 15: sometimes the party with the most votes is not the party with the most seats. Powell estimates this in about 20 percent of 45 elections in the six majoritarian democracies he examines (p. 762).
- Consensus practice: proportional rules give a close votes–seats link. The complicated government formation process gives only a weak link between seats and share of government power. For example, the largest party is not always in government (p. 763).
- Consensus democracies also disperse power to non-governmental parties through committees and corporatism. Powell finds they come close to their ideal if one looks at actual policymaking power (p. 763).
- Bottom line: both types come close to, but fall short of, their ideals (p. 763).

**Congruence** (pp. 764–765)
- Congruence is the proximity between citizen and representative. Most scholars measure the ideological distance between the government and the median citizen (p. 764).
- In theory both types could do well. In ideal majoritarian democracy two parties converge on the median voter (Box 11.1). In a consensus democracy the party closest to the median is likely to play an important role in government formation (p. 764).
- Empirically, most studies suggest governments are more congruent with citizens in consensus than in majoritarian democracies (Huber and Powell; McDonald and Budge; Powell and others). Footnote 17 says three new studies have challenged what appeared to be a growing empirical consensus (p. 764).
- Footnote 16 distinguishes descriptive representation (mirroring gender or race) from substantive representation (mirroring ideological makeup). The authors focus on substantive (p. 764).
- Legislature-level congruence: do seat shares match the distribution of citizen preferences (the authors' example is a 10 percent Communist preference and seats)? M. Golder and Stramski (2010) find that legislatures under proportional rules more accurately reflect the diversity of ideological opinion than those under majoritarian rules (pp. 764–765).

**Conclusions** (p. 765)
- A "consensus has emerged" that majoritarian institutions are better at mandates, accountability, identifiability and clarity of responsibility. Consensus institutions are superior at dispersing power, providing choice and generating ideological congruence. This consensus holds that constitution writers face an explicit trade-off (p. 765).
- Hybrids exist. The most common departure involves parliamentarism versus presidentialism. Some majoritarian democracies are presidential. It is not unusual for primarily consensus countries, such as Belgium, to be parliamentary, though parliamentarism is the more majoritarian regime type (p. 765).
- The United States is probably the best hybrid. It is extremely majoritarian in electoral system, party system, government type and interest-group relations. It is extremely consensus-oriented in constitutional review, presidentialism, federalism and bicameralism (p. 765).
- The authors say one could argue that combining some of each optimizes the accountability–representation trade-off, but scholars do not agree. Some scholars welcome such combinations as combining the strengths of both types, while others are frustrated by them (p. 765).
- The authors close by pointing to the next three sections: effects on economic policy, ethnic conflict and democratic stability (p. 765).

## Toy example (ours; not from the text)
Three single-seat districts, 100 voters each, three parties, plurality wins each seat.

| | A | B | C | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| District 1 | 40 | 35 | 25 | A |
| District 2 | 40 | 30 | 30 | A |
| District 3 | 20 | 45 | 35 | B |
| Total votes | 100 | 110 | 90 | |

- Votes of 300: A = 100/300 = 33.3%, B = 110/300 = 36.7%, C = 90/300 = 30.0%.
- Seats: A wins 2 of 3 (66.7%), B wins 1 (33.3%), C wins 0.
- A holds a seat majority without a vote majority, so this is a "manufactured majority". A can claim a mandate, but 66.7% of voters chose other parties.
- B has the most votes but not the most seats. This is the situation in footnote 15.
- Under strictly proportional seats, shares would be about 33%, 37% and 30%, close to the consensus ideal of power in proportion to votes.
- Under the Figure 16.3 majoritarian ideal, a party with under 50% of the vote should have no policymaking power, yet A holds the seat majority. A does not even have the most votes, which the authors call a more troubling case for the majoritarian vision (fn. 15).

## Concepts
- [[majoritarian-democracy]] (new) — democracy that concentrates power in a majority government. Elections choose a team to be judged at the next election (pp. 743–744).
- [[consensus-democracy]] (new) — democracy that disperses power. Elections choose representatives who bargain, and power is shared in proportion to electoral size (pp. 743, 745).
- [[majoritarian-consensus-dimension]] (new) — continuum of how widely institutions disperse power. Many institutions on it are causally related (p. 747).
- [[arrow-s-impossibility-theorem]] — here used as the "trilemma": at most two of transitivity, universal admissibility and nondictatorship (p. 743).
- [[accountability]] (new) — extent to which voters can reward or punish parties for office-holding policies (p. 755).
- [[retrospective-voting]] — voting on incumbents' past performance (p. 755).
- [[clarity-of-responsibility]] (new) — voters' ability to identify who is responsible for policy. Necessary for accountability (p. 756).
- [[mandate]] (new) — policy the government is authorized and obligated to carry out (p. 759).
- [[prospective-voting]] (new) — voting on parties' stated plans and expected performance (p. 759).
- [[government-identifiability]] (new) — how well voters can identify the government alternatives they vote for. Necessary, not sufficient, for a mandate (pp. 759–760).
- [[manufactured-majority]] (new) — seat majority without a vote majority, produced by disproportional vote–seat translation (pp. 760–761).
- [[responsiveness]] (new) — dynamic representation. Here: how well parties' policymaking power tracks changes in electoral support (pp. 761–762).
- [[congruence]] (new) — static representation: match between representatives' and citizens' preferences (pp. 761–762, 764).
- [[veto-player]] — veto institutions such as bicameralism and federalism that can block a government's platform (p. 761).
- [[median-voter-theorem]] — referred to for the ideal majoritarian two-party convergence (p. 764).

## Questions this raises
