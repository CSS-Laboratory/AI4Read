# Introduction: why study comparative politics, and why across countries

## L0 — One line
Comparative politics studies politics predominantly within countries, and the authors adopt explicit cross-national comparison to introduce questions about the causes and consequences of democracy, dictatorship and their institutions, because they judge a series of single-country studies to be a less useful approach for this purpose.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what comparative politics is, which questions drive it, and how an introduction should be organised. It opens with the 2011 Arab Spring and the 1848 revolutions, mostly followed by reaction, to show that political change is hard to predict, though not impossible to understand. It defines comparative politics as the study of political phenomena predominantly within countries. It then previews the book's questions: state failure, economic and cultural causes of democracy, transitions, democracy's benefits, and institutional design. It argues for a cross-national approach. Few single countries vary enough over time, a handful of country studies can at most give confirming cases, and scholars mostly explain rather than describe. Good tools matter if people try to engineer democracy.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school civics (what a constitution, a legislature and an election are).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The authors define comparative politics as the study of political phenomena predominantly within countries, and international politics as the study predominantly between countries. The line is often blurred, for example for political economy and civil war (p. 5).
- Political change is hard to predict. The 1848 revolts looked like a democratic spring and mostly ended in reaction, so quick generalisations from the 2011 Arab Spring can be misleading, though the authors do not say it must end the same way (pp. 3–4).
- The field's focus seems to shift with each generation's urgent problem (depression and fascism, development, oil shocks, post-communist transitions, poverty and weak democracy) (pp. 4–8).
- Claims used in policy debates, such as President Bush's 2003 claims about Iraq, need theoretical and empirical evaluation. The book aims to give readers the knowledge and tools to do that (pp. 8–12).
- The book's questions are why democracy or dictatorship, whether it matters, which institutional forms exist, and what they cause. The authors suggest that institutional effects are likely to depend on local conditions (pp. 8–16).
- Cross-national comparison supplies variation that single countries rarely offer, and a small set of country studies can at most yield "confirming cases" (pp. 16–17).
- Scholars mostly build and test explanations. The authors argue that students can follow this work and even contribute (pp. 17–18).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter opening (p. 1)
- Summary bullet: political science is the study of politics in a scientific manner. International politics is the study of politics predominantly between countries; comparative politics is the study of politics predominantly within countries (p. 1).
- Overview: the chapter outlines the central questions of the book. They all relate to the causes and consequences of democracy and dictatorship and to the variety of democratic and dictatorial institutions. The authors argue that attempts to engineer democracy, should they occur, should rest on foundations provided by the study of comparative politics. They also say why they use an explicitly cross-national approach to introduce students to the field (p. 1).

### Opening: the Arab Spring (pp. 2–3)
- Mohamed Bouazizi, a street produce seller in Sidi Bouzid, Tunisia, set himself on fire on 17 December 2010 after officials confiscated his goods. He had been harassed, apparently to extract bribes, and his appeal to the governor's office was ignored. He died on 5 January 2011 (p. 2).
- Mass protests spread to Tunis. President Ben Ali, in power almost a quarter of a century, fled on 15 January, the first Arab leader in generations to leave office in response to public protests (p. 2).
- Discontent then spread: Egypt and Algeria (self-immolations), then Yemen, Egypt (protests organised via Facebook and Twitter; Mubarak resigned 11 February), Libya (protests became an insurrection aided by NATO airstrikes), and crackdowns in Saudi Arabia and Syria. By the end of March 2011, significant protests had occurred in seventeen Middle Eastern and North African countries (p. 2).
- Because some long-standing dictators were removed, the authors say the prospect was raised that this might be the start of a wave of liberalization, perhaps of democratization, in a region long dominated by resilient authoritarian regimes (pp. 2–3).

### The 1848 comparison (p. 3)
- A wave of nearly fifty revolts began in Palermo in January 1848 and spread across Europe. As in 2011, some leaders were removed, some went into exile when armies refused to fire, and some crushed the rebellions (p. 3).
- Shared causes: the Industrial Revolution shifted social structure and gave middle-class groups new power. They often allied with working-class groups mobilised by economic crisis. Rulers tried to placate people with sackings, promises of constitutional reform and universal male suffrage. The Frankfurt National Assembly wrote a constitution for a German Empire (p. 3).
- By autumn, hope had turned to disappointment. In Vienna the army put down the "October Rising" by bombardment and occupation. The emperor was replaced, but little else changed (p. 3).
- Tsar Nicholas I had asked "What remains standing in Europe?" and, by April 1849, the answer was "almost everything" (p. 3). Revolution was followed swiftly by reaction, and power stayed largely in the same hands (p. 3).
- **Authors' point:** they do not say the Arab Spring will necessarily end like 1848. Many twentieth-century European transitions can be traced partly to 1848. The point is that social and political change is hard to predict and occurs in complex, context-dependent ways. This makes it difficult to understand, "but not impossible" (p. 4).
- The book offers tools and a comparative framework: economic and cultural determinants of democracy, strategic interaction between rulers and oppositions, institutional determinants of policy outcomes, and the effects of constitutional design. The authors do not claim that mastering the book will make it easy to know what will become of the Arab Spring, but they believe it gives tools for understanding contemporary developments (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* Two events can look alike (revolt, fleeing ruler, new constitution) but a resemblance alone is a weak basis for a forecast. The authors warn that rapid generalizations can mislead and say the Arab Spring need not end as 1848 did. They offer tools and a comparative framework for understanding, not predictions.

### Generations of motivation (pp. 4–8)
- Great Depression and rise of fascism: scholars asked what governments can do against market instability, and how to design electoral institutions so that anti-democratic extremists like the Nazis in Weimar Germany are less likely to be elected. Both remain central (p. 4).
- After World War II, decolonization and the cold war led to "political development": what could reduce instability in poor countries. The cold war heightened interest in the causes and consequences of communist revolutions in China and Cuba and turmoil in Vietnam and Chile (pp. 4, 6).
- 1970s oil crisis: scholars revisited interwar questions in Western Europe, now assuming the "post-war settlement" or "class compromise". Workers accepted capitalism and free trade in return for the welfare state. Interest turned to how a country's variety of capitalism affects its capacity to weather economic storms (pp. 6–7).
- After the cold war: Eastern and Central Europe faced "twin transitions" (planned to market economy; one-party dictatorship to democracy). In the twenty-first century, the authors say, attention appears to be turning back to development: why poverty is widespread, and why government "by, for, and of" the people seems tenuous for so many (p. 8).

### Box 1.1: What is comparative politics? (pp. 5–7)
- **Definition 1 (adopted):** comparative politics is the study of political phenomena that occur predominantly within countries. International politics is the study of phenomena predominantly between countries. The labels follow LaPalombara (*Politics Within Nations*) and Morgenthau (*Politics Among Nations*) (p. 5).
- A good scientific typology (classification scheme) is **logically exhaustive** (every case has a category) and **mutually exclusive** (no case fits more than one category). The within/between typology is exhaustive but not mutually exclusive. US trade policy toward China, EU economic policy, environmental issues, and revolution, terrorism and civil war (often foreign-supported) mix both kinds of factors (p. 5).
- So the authors say "predominantly". Figure 1.1 shows two overlapping circles (p. 6):
  - International only: conflict, foreign policy, international organizations.
  - Overlap: revolution, political economy, environmental politics.
  - Comparative only: elections, party systems, executive–legislative relations, interest groups, legislatures.
- Political economy topics such as trade, central bank independence and exchange-rate policy cross the divide (p. 5).
- American politics is a separate subfield in most US departments. Whether it belongs to comparative politics is hotly debated, and the authors leave the decision to the reader (pp. 6–7).
- **Definition 2 (rejected):** comparative politics as the study of every country except one's own. The authors say it "seems rather silly" to them: Nigerian politics would count as comparative politics unless studied in Nigeria (pp. 6–7).
- **Definition 3 (rejected):** comparative politics as the study of politics using the comparative method, a tradition going back at least to Aristotle's classification of constitutional forms. The authors say it is, to some extent, descriptively accurate but not particularly useful. They say comparison is central to all science (shown in Chapter 2), so this definition would make it synonymous with political science (p. 7).
- **Authors' approach:** they compare national-level attributes across countries. Example: ask why some countries have two parties (like the United States) and others many (like France), instead of describing each system separately. They do not say single-country study falls outside the field, nor that cross-national comparison is more worthy. They say it is a reasonable introduction and sets a framework for advanced study (p. 7).

### Why this matters: Iraq 2003 and the book's goal (p. 8)
- In February 2003, just before ordering the invasion of Iraq, President George W. Bush said that all Iraqis must have a voice and all citizens must have their rights protected. **Bush's claim:** Iraqi democracy was desirable and achievable (p. 8).
- The authors ask what the theoretical basis is, what the evidence says, and how one would evaluate such claims. One central goal of the book is to give substantive knowledge and methodological tools to do so (p. 8).

### Overview of the book (p. 8)
- Political science is the study of politics in a scientific manner. This is not very informative, so Chapters 2 and 3 (Part I) ask what politics is and what science is (p. 8).
- Part II contrasts democracies and dictatorships: origins of the modern state; why some countries are democracies and others dictatorships; and whether it matters. Part III covers the types of democracy and dictatorship and the institutional forms. Part IV asks how types of democracy affect government performance and democratic survival (p. 8).
- The authors want answers that are relevant to current problems and reliable, built on best practices of contemporary political scientists (pp. 8–9).

### State failure (p. 9)
- The 11 September 2001 attacks were planned from Afghanistan, a failed state where the Taliban gave al-Qaida sanctuary. Chapter 4 defines the "state" and describes life in a failed state through Somalia since 1991. There al-Shabab, an Islamic group, competes with the Transitional Federal Government and other militias and warlords. The rest of Chapter 4 asks what distinguishes the modern state and what led to its development (p. 9).

### Economic determinants of democracy (pp. 9–10)
- The US invaded Afghanistan in October 2001 and stated a goal of replacing the Taliban with a more democratic government. The authors say that, to establish democracy intentionally and successfully, it is important first to understand what encourages or discourages its emergence and survival (p. 9).
- **Bush's claim (2003):** Iraq's "abundant resources" and "skilled and educated people" make democracy feasible (p. 9).
- Iraq facts given: oil-rich (only Saudi Arabia has larger reserves); 2005 GDP per capita $3,400; 95 percent of foreign exchange earnings from oil. Rapid modernization between 1950 and 1980 was followed, over the next quarter-century, by war, sanctions and inefficiencies encouraged by central planning, which undid many of these gains. Since independence in 1932, Iraq has been ruled by a monarchy and dictatorships (p. 10).
- Chapter 6 asks how development and economic structure affect the chance a country becomes and remains democratic (p. 10).
  - **Some scholars:** transitions to democracy become more likely as economies modernize (less reliant on resource exports, more productive, industrial, educated).
  - **Other scholars:** modernization helps democracies survive but does not help dictatorships become democratic.
  - Either way, most Arab Spring countries do not fulfil many of the basic requirements of modernization, so both sides would reach essentially the same conclusion: the prospects are poor (p. 10).
- Many political scientists argue democracy is unlikely where the economy depends on resource extraction, especially capital-intensive extraction with huge economies of scale, as with oil. If readers find this persuasive, "abundant resources" in Iraq and Libya are a cause for concern rather than hope (p. 10).

*Intuition note:* The authors do not say that oil causes dictatorship. They say that scholars have argued so, and that readers can weigh the arguments after Chapter 6.

### Cultural determinants of democracy (pp. 10–12)
- Many scholars have argued that democracy is incompatible with particular cultures. Which cultures count as bad for democracy changes over time, depending on which countries are democratic. Catholicism was seen as inimical to democracy in the 1950s–60s. This view waned as Catholic countries in southern Europe and Latin America democratized in the 1970s–80s. Today Islam is deemed most antithetical, mainly because few contemporary Islamic democracies are seen (p. 11).
- Chapter 7 examines the evidence. The authors say that after-the-fact (post hoc) theorizing should be treated with considerable skepticism. Their example is concluding that something about Islam discourages democracy just because there are few predominantly Muslim democracies today (p. 11).
- The authors say readers should still be suspicious of Bush's claim that, because Japan and Germany overcame alleged cultural barriers, Iraq can too. They compare it to arguing that, because two elderly relatives smoked all their lives without lung cancer, one can safely smoke (p. 11).
- Chapter 8 covers transitions: bottom-up (popular revolution) and top-down (elites introduce liberalization). It aims to explain why dictatorships often look stable, why popular revolutions are rare, and why, when they happen, they nearly always come as a surprise though they often seem inevitable in hindsight. It stresses the role of information, beliefs and uncertainty in elite–mass strategic interaction (p. 11). The book does not examine foreign-imposed democracy by force in any great detail (p. 11).

### What's so good about democracy anyway? (pp. 12–13)
- **Bush's claim:** people everywhere want good things for themselves and their children. The authors say that this is hard to establish scientifically, but most of us would probably accept it. Whether democracy actually produces these good things is a question for social science (p. 12).
- Chapters 9–11 ask whether there is a sound basis for pursuing democracy. Chapter 9 compares policy performance. The picture is more nuanced than politicians' rhetoric: democracies seldom perform poorly on material well-being, but they frequently fail to outperform a substantial number of dictatorships (p. 12).
- Chapter 10 treats variation among dictatorships (personalistic, as in Libya under Qaddafi; hereditary monarchies of the Gulf states; military juntas such as Myanmar, Burkina Faso and Chad). It covers effects on economic performance, regime stability and the likelihood of democratic transitions (pp. 12–13).
- Chapter 11 asks whether the process of democracy is morally or normatively attractive beyond its material benefits. The authors report the bottom line: no support for the idea that there is an ideal form of political organization, democracy included (p. 13).

### Institutional design (pp. 13–16)
- **Bush's claim:** the US left "constitutions and parliaments, not occupying armies" in Germany and Japan. The authors call this not entirely accurate. The US Army occupied Japan until 1952. West Germany was created in 1949, and Allied occupation ended in 1955. East Germany was Soviet-controlled, and some date the end of that occupation to 1990 (p. 13).
- There is considerable truth in the broader idea that the US encouraged democracy there. It gave billions in aid, and US officials and scholars influenced the constitutions, which differ from each other and from the US one (p. 13).
- The authors suggest that designers may have been aware that the effects and suitability of institutions are likely to depend on local conditions (social structure, politics, geography, economy, history). There are "many, many" ways to structure a democracy. If one were convinced democracy was best for a country, the next question is how to design it, which presumes knowing how institutions work and what they cause (pp. 13–14).
- Chapters 12–15 cover how institutions work (p. 14):
  - Ch. 12: parliamentary versus presidential democracy (US presidential; Germany, Japan parliamentary), and how governments form and survive.
  - Ch. 13: electoral systems. The US uses single-member district plurality (SMDP) for its national legislature. Japan used the single non-transferable vote (SNTV) in multimember districts from 1948 to 1993. Germany uses a mixed system combining proportional representation with SMDP. Criteria: proportionality, ethnic accommodation, accountability, minority representation, revelation of sincere preferences.
  - Ch. 14: electoral laws help shape the party system. The type of party system, unlike government type and electoral system, is typically not written into law but arises from evolving competition. Electoral system and social structure together are likely to determine the number and types of parties. In Iraq, Arabs (about 75%) and Kurds (about 25%), and Shia (about 60%) and Sunni (about 40%) are the main divisions. The electoral law is likely to play an important role in determining what parties a democratic Iraq has.
  - Ch. 15: federal versus unitary; bicameral versus unicameral; judicial independence (p. 14).
- Federal systems divide government activity constitutionally between regional and central governments (Germany, US). Japan is unitary. Germany, Japan and the US are bicameral, but roughly 60 percent of the world's democracies are unicameral (pp. 14–15).
- A written constitution may mislead on judicial independence. The US Constitution does not mention judicial review, yet it developed. Japan's constitution says judges are independent, yet a recent study suggests they respond to political pressure in practice (Ramseyer and Rasmusen 2003) (p. 15).
- The authors say these three choices can be thought of as forms of checks and balances that create institutional veto players. Their causes and consequences are therefore closely related, so the book treats them in one chapter (p. 15).
- **Authors vs. Bush:** Bush appeared to see institutional consequences as straightforward. The authors do not. Chapter 16 (Part IV) asks about the normative and material consequences of institutional combinations. Examples: are some democracies more accountable, representative and responsive? What are the economic consequences? (p. 15)
- Chapter 16 also reviews how institutions affect democratic survival. **Many scholars** argue that ethnic and religious diversity like Iraq's destabilises democracies. The authors ask whether such divisions make stability impossible or whether institutions can mitigate them. They note considerable evidence that parliamentary democracies survive significantly longer than presidential ones, and raise the puzzle of why democracy persists in the United States (pp. 15–16).

### The approach taken in this book (pp. 16–18)
- Many textbooks are sequences of country studies (Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Japan, India, Brazil, Nigeria, sometimes China and Mexico). Eight countries are little more than 4 percent of the world's 193 widely recognised states. The authors find the claim that they are the most important countries displeasing and the claim that they are the most representative questionable (p. 16).
- Goals of an introductory class: stimulate interest, introduce principal concerns and findings, and show where there is consensus or debate. The authors focus on questions long considered vital and on those with growing consensus, including a growing, less obvious consensus on the causes and consequences of particular autocratic and democratic institutions (p. 16).
- Reasons for a cross-national design (p. 17). The authors say a traditional series of country studies would not be the most useful approach for the questions they want to address:
  1. Very few countries vary enough over time in democracy, or in institutions, to answer questions about causes and consequences from one country. Countries tend to keep presidentialism or an electoral law for long periods, and often choose the same again when they must choose anew. Cross-country comparison supplies the variation (p. 17).
  2. The authors say they lack the memory and attentiveness to compare details (for example the Russian Duma, the Japanese Diet and the British House of Commons) across a semester, and, perhaps incorrectly, do not expect their students to. Even if they could, eight or ten countries may not give enough variety of experiences to evaluate the field's hypotheses. For factors that stay fairly constant within countries, a small sample at most gives "a collection of confirming cases", which is problematic for the scientific method (Chapter 2) (p. 17).
  3. The country-by-country approach creates a gap between what scholars teach and what they do. They mostly construct and test theories (explaining why politics is organised on ethnic lines in some countries and class lines in others, or why some countries are democracies), rather than describing laws, institutions and power (p. 17).
- The authors say comparative political science "is not rocket science". The authors say that the scientific method has been applied to politics only relatively recently, which suggests to them that students should be able to engage the literature with relative ease. They believe that, compared with physics or mathematics, there is unusual room for students to contribute. Footnote 2 notes that undergraduates took significant roles in published research (pp. 17–18).

*Intuition note:* If a factor hardly changes inside one country, that country gives little leverage for judging its effect. Comparing countries supplies the missing variation. The authors call a mere collection of confirming cases problematic for the scientific method (explained in Chapter 2).

## Toy example (ours, invented numbers)
Suppose a theory says "oil dependence discourages democracy". Take eight imaginary countries (invented, not real data).

| Country | Oil-dependent? | Democracy? |
|---|---|---|
| A | yes | no |
| B | yes | no |
| C | yes | no |
| D | yes | yes |
| E | no | yes |
| F | no | yes |
| G | no | yes |
| H | no | no |

- A textbook with only A, B, C has three oil-dependent dictatorships. These are three "confirming cases" and show no variation on democracy.
- With all eight countries: oil-dependent group has 1 democracy out of 4, so 1/4 = 25%. Non-oil group has 3 out of 4, so 3/4 = 75%. The gap is 75% − 25% = 50 percentage points.
- Cases D and H show that the pattern is not perfect. Only the full comparison reveals this.
- Within-country version: if country E kept one electoral law for all 40 years, its 40 years have the same value of the "cause". There is no contrast to learn from inside E (the point on p. 17).

## Concepts
- [[comparative-politics]] (new) — study of political phenomena predominantly within countries; the authors' preferred definition (p. 5)
- [[international-politics]] (new) — study of political phenomena predominantly between countries (p. 5)
- [[scientific-typology]] (new) — classification that should be logically exhaustive and mutually exclusive; the within/between typology fails exclusivity (p. 5)
- [[cross-national-comparison]] (new) — comparing national-level attributes across many countries to get variation single cases lack (pp. 7, 17)
- [[confirming-cases]] (new) — a collection of cases that fit a theory, which is the most a small sample can give; the authors call this problematic for the scientific method (reasons in Chapter 2) (p. 17)
- [[post-hoc-theorizing]] (new) — building a theory after the fact from the observed pattern; treated with skepticism (p. 11)
- [[state-failure]] (new) — the authors speak of failed states, such as Somalia and Afghanistan under the Taliban, as places with a power vacuum; Chapter 4 defines the "state" (p. 9)
- [[modernization-and-democracy]] (new) — debated claim: some scholars say modernization makes transitions to democracy more likely, others that it only helps democracies survive (p. 10)
- [[democratic-transition-modes]] (new) — bottom-up (popular revolution) and top-down (elite liberalization) paths to democracy (p. 11)
- [[institutions]] — here the constitutional and electoral rules (government type, electoral system, federalism, bicameralism, judicial independence) whose design and effects the book studies (pp. 13–15)
- [[veto player]] — federalism, bicameralism and judicial independence can be thought of as checks and balances that create institutional veto players (p. 15)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Arab Spring contagion (p. 2) ↔ political-science canon: revolutionary threshold / cascade — the unit speaks of contagion across countries but does not state a threshold model.
- [structural-analogy] "Confirming cases" and post hoc theorizing (pp. 11, 17) ↔ political-science canon: observable implications — a theory needs varied tests, not only fitting cases.
- [metaphor] Smoking-relatives argument (p. 11) ↔ evidence logic: a few non-fitting individual cases do not settle a probabilistic claim.

## Questions this raises
- Does cross-national comparison risk hiding what is distinctive in each country, given that the authors say single-country study is not excluded and that cross-national comparison sets a framework for closer study of individual polities at an advanced level (p. 7)?
- How should one test a claim like "culture X blocks democracy" if the observed pattern was used to invent it (p. 11)?
- Why does democracy persist in the United States if presidential democracies survive less (p. 16)?
- What counts as "enough variation" across time within a country (p. 17)?
