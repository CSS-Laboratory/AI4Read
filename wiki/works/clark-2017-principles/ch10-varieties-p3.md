# Selectorate Theory: Why Dictatorships Differ in Performance (Clark, Golder & Golder, Ch. 10, part 3)

## L0 — One line
Selectorate theory says that if all leaders want to stay in office, the sizes of the winning coalition (W) and the selectorate (S) largely determine whether leaders are pushed to serve the public or only a few insiders.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do some dictatorships perform well and others badly, when all leaders want to gain and keep power? Selectorate theory points to institutions. A leader needs a winning coalition W (supporters he needs) drawn from a selectorate S (people with a say in choosing him). A small W/S (the chance a member of S is in any coalition) gives a strong loyalty norm, so the leader can pay supporters little, steal, and ignore public goods. A large W pushes leaders towards public goods. It predicts good performance in democracies (large W, large W/S), middling in monarchies and juntas, and poor in dominant-party and personalistic regimes. A table links W and W/S to better growth, wealth, spending and health.

**Prerequisites:** expected value (probability × payoff); public vs private goods; reading regression coefficients and significance stars; basic game-theory vocabulary (for the problems); the regime types monarchy, military junta, dominant-party and personalistic dictatorship (introduced earlier in the chapter).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Dictators with the "appropriate" or "over" level of institutions (legislatures, multiparty elections) survived longer than under-institutionalized ones. The authors suggest such institutions are therefore not necessarily signs that a democratic transition is under way (p. 384).
- Selectorate theory assumes all leaders act as if they want to gain and keep office, and always face a rival. The theory explains differing outcomes by institutions, since leaders are assumed to share the same goal (pp. 384–385).
- Institutions are summarised by W (people whose support the leader needs) and S (people with a say in choosing the leader). W separates democracies (large) from dictatorships (small); S separates types of dictatorship (pp. 388–390).
- W/S is a "loyalty norm". It is the chance a defector joins the next coalition. A small W/S makes coalition members loyal and cheap to keep, so the leader can skim revenue (pp. 391–394).
- Leaders prefer private goods, but as W grows, each member's private share shrinks. Large-W leaders therefore rely on public goods (p. 395).
- Predicted performance: good if W and W/S are large, middling if W is small and W/S large, poor if both are small. This is consistent with the uneven performance of dictatorships, and is offered as a potential explanation (pp. 396–397).
- A civic-minded leader is neither necessary nor sufficient for good policy. Institutions matter, as in the cases of Leopold II and Chiang Kai-shek (pp. 397–399).
- Cross-national regressions show W and W/S positively associated with growth, wealth and spending, and negatively with infant mortality (pp. 400–402).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the durability section (p. 384)
- Estimated survival of dictators, as reported here:

| Level of institutionalization | Years survived |
|---|---|
| Under-institutionalized | 3.3 |
| Appropriate | 6.88 (twice as long) |
| Over-institutionalized | 9.36 (almost three times as long as under) |

  (p. 384). The terms are not defined on these pages.
- The authors say the results suggest that allowing legislatures and multiparty elections can extend dictators' rule, even after accounting for the conditions that push them to adopt such institutions (p. 384).
- They say legal opposition parties and parliaments therefore have substantial consequences in dictatorships. Such institutions are "not necessarily" signs that a transition to democracy is under way (p. 384).

> **Intuition note.** Dictatorships that look more "democratic" on paper may last longer, not shorter. Dictatorial rule appears able to persist in many forms.

### Selectorate theory: basic assumption (p. 384)
- Chapter 9 ended with large variation in dictatorships' economic experience. Democracies all seem to do well, but some dictatorships perform poorly while others appear to perform as well as, if not better than, the average democracy (p. 384).
- Selectorate theory claims that material well-being depends less on democracy vs dictatorship than on the size of the winning coalition and the selectorate (p. 384).
- Basic assumption: all leaders are motivated by gaining and keeping office. Leaders may have other goals, but competition forces them to behave "as if" they want office. Those who do not try are replaced by rivals who do (p. 384).
- A rival is always ready to replace the incumbent. In dictatorships rivals are harder to see because they keep a low profile (p. 384, n. 27).

### The puzzle (p. 385)
- **Puzzle (BDM2S2 = Bueno de Mesquita, Smith, Siverson and Morrow, 2003):** if all leaders have the same induced goals, why do outcomes vary? Why do some leaders provide public goods, avoid corruption and kleptocracy, and bring peace and prosperity, while others bring war and ruin? (p. 385)
- Good performance does not necessarily bring long tenure. For 1955–2002, BDM2S2 list the 25 "best" leaders (top-quartile growth, no wars, high Freedom House scores; n. 28) and the 25 longest-ruling leaders. The lists have no overlap (p. 385).
- The best leaders averaged 7% annual growth and lasted six years on average. Fourteen of the 25 longest-ruling leaders (the rest have no data) averaged 4.4% growth, and the group averaged 35.1 years in office (p. 385).
- The data "would seem to suggest" that good performance goes with short tenure and poor performance with long tenure (p. 385).

### Institutions (pp. 385–388)
- Each country has rules defining three nested groups (Figure 10.6, p. 386):
  - **Disenfranchised:** residents with no legal right to take part in choosing the government.
  - **Selectorate (S):** people with a legitimate say, if they choose, in selecting the leader. This need not be voting, so S is not always an electorate (p. 386).
  - **Winning coalition (W):** members of S whose support is necessary for the leader to stay in power. If he cannot keep it loyal, he loses office to a challenger (p. 388).
- Examples of S:
  - In King John's England (1199), 197 lay barons and 39 ecclesiastical barons had a say, so S was 236 (p. 386).
  - In monarchies such as Saudi Arabia and Oman, S is typically the royal family, perhaps the wider nobility and certain religious leaders (p. 386).
  - In a junta such as Pinochet's Chile, S is usually the armed forces or the heads of the branches (p. 386).
  - In dominant-party dictatorships that hold elections (Suharto's Indonesia, Saddam's Iraq, Marcos's Philippines, the USSR), S arguably is all adult citizens with the vote (pp. 386–387).
  - In democracies S is nearly always large: all eligible voters (p. 387).
- Examples of W:
  - In a democracy with two candidates, W is as large as a majority of the electorate (p. 388).
  - In dictatorships W is "always quite small": for example, in a junta it might be a majority of officers or a few colonels and generals; in Communist states often a small subset of the party; perhaps a majority of the nobility in a monarchy (p. 388).
  - North Korea's W was estimated at 250–2,500 out of 20 million, and more recently as low as 73 (p. 388).
  - W and S are measured as proportions of residents, to allow comparison across countries (p. 388, n. 29).
- The "support coalitions" used earlier to define dictatorship types are, in many respects, the same as selectorate theory's winning coalitions (p. 388).

**Box 10.3, Kenya 1989 (p. 387)** (account from BDM2S2):
- Kenya's 1986 queue voting system: voters lined up publicly behind a candidate's representative. A candidate judged to have 70% or more won. Otherwise there was a secret-ballot runoff. Many of these elections were rigged by lying about the counts (p. 387).
- In the 1 February 1989 Kiharu by-election, a local reporter photographed a chalkboard tally of 9,566 (92.46%) for Dr. Julius Kiano and 780 (7.53%) for Mweru. Kiano was well regarded locally but distrusted by the ruling party (p. 387).
- The returning officer reported 2,000 for Kiano and 9,000 for Mweru, and Mweru won. The winning coalition "appears to have been just one man", the returning officer (p. 387).
- Nationally, W was larger: surely at least one person per constituency and probably many more whose loyalty was not in doubt (p. 387).

> **Intuition note.** W is the group a leader cannot do without. S is the pool from which that group could be drawn. A tiny W means a few insiders decide who rules.

### Mapping W and S onto regime types (pp. 388–390)
- Theory (Figure 10.7a): democracies have large W and large S. Dominant-party and personalistic dictatorships ("rigged election systems") have small W and large S. Most monarchies and military juntas have small W and small S (pp. 388–389).
- The key factor separating democracies from dictatorships is W. The key factor separating types of dictatorship is S (p. 388).
- Measurement: BDM2S2 call their measures of W and S "crude and primitive" and "in [their] infancy". The authors say the measures "appear to have some face validity" (p. 390).
- Figure 10.7b plots average W and S scores for 1946–2000 by regime type, using BDM2S2 data and Geddes's classification (p. 389). BDM2S2 do not use regime type to build the scores (p. 390).
- The authors report that the pattern is "entirely consistent" with the theory. Democracies' average W is much larger than that of any dictatorship type. Monarchies and military regimes have small S, and personalistic and dominant-party regimes have large S (p. 390).
- Read off the graph (approximate): democracies at W ≈ 0.78, S ≈ 0.96; dominant-party at W ≈ 0.5, S ≈ 0.94; military at W ≈ 0.23, S ≈ 0.45 (p. 389).

### Government performance (pp. 390–391)
- Leaders keep their winning coalition happy with public goods, private goods, or both. Public goods benefit everyone and are nonexcludable and nonrival. Private goods (licenses, jets, villas) go only to coalition members (p. 390).
- The leader also picks a tax rate. It sets his budget and how residents split time between productive work and leisure (pp. 390–391).
- A challenger simultaneously offers the selectorate a package (public goods, private goods, tax rate). The "political entrepreneur" who best meets the needs of the relevant actors wins (p. 391).

### Loyalty norm (pp. 391–394)
- A coalition member thinking of defecting has no guarantee of a place in the next coalition. A challenger's promise of a place is "not credible", so defectors risk losing their private goods (p. 391).
- That risk is captured by W/S, the probability that a selectorate member is in any winning coalition. It is also the probability that a defector joins the next one (p. 391).
- If W/S is small, members are likely to be intensely loyal, since they are lucky to be in W and unlikely to be in anyone else's. As W/S rises, loyalty declines. W/S thus acts as a **loyalty norm**: strong if small, weak if large (pp. 391–392).
- Note 32 links this to exit, voice and loyalty: a large W/S means members have credible exit threats (p. 392).
- **Example, societies A and B (p. 392).** Each leader has $1 billion for the 1,000 members of W plus himself, so the most each member could get is $1 billion / 1,000 = $1 million. Societies differ only in S:

| | S | W/S | Defector's expected value | Leader must pay each member |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 100,000 | 1,000/100,000 = 0.01 | $1,000,000 × 0.01 + $0 × 0.99 = $10,000 | slightly more than $10,000 |
| B | 10,000 | 1,000/10,000 = 0.1 | $1,000,000 × 0.1 + $0 × 0.9 = $100,000 | slightly more than $100,000 |

  (pp. 392–393). The leader also has to come close to matching any public goods a challenger promises (p. 392).
- The leader keeps the difference as a discretionary "slush fund". It can be used to buy loyalty if a challenger offers attractive public goods (pp. 392–393). (added step) Total skimmed: roughly $1,000 million − 1,000 × $10,000 = about $990 million in A, and about $900 million in B, a little less in each case since he must pay "slightly more".
- Conclusion: leaders in small W/S systems have greater opportunities to steal than leaders in large W/S systems (p. 393).
- Illustrations: Mobutu's Zaire (1965–1997) reportedly put up to a third of the budget under personal control, skimmed a quarter of copper profits, and is estimated to have stolen $4 billion. Marcos (1965–1986) is thought to have stolen $5–10 billion (p. 393).
- Strong loyalty norms also give poor policy incentives more generally. Members are loyal because the leader gives them more private goods than any challenger and because they fear being cut out of the next coalition. So long as they are "bribed" enough, they do not care about citizens' welfare (p. 393).
- Such leaders have no incentive to produce good public policy. The authors cite Saddam Hussein holding on after a trade embargo wrecked Iraq's economy, as long as he paid the military and his clansmen (p. 394).
- Good public policy may even get a small-W/S leader ousted. Spending on broad public goods lets a challenger credibly promise more private goods to the coalition than the incumbent gives (p. 394).
- In large W/S systems such as democracies, voters would not lose their private benefits by switching support. Leaders must work harder and cannot skim much. They have a strong incentive for good overall economic performance and not to over-tax, because over-taxed citizens work less and shrink "the economic pie" (p. 394).
- Predicted: in large W/S systems kleptocracy and taxation lower, growth higher (p. 394).
- Caveat (n. 34): good performance does not guarantee longevity. Leaders in large W/S systems tend to survive less long than those in small W/S systems, even if they perform better. This is said to explain why democratic leaders rarely last as long as even poorly performing dominant-party or personalistic dictators (p. 394).

> **Intuition note.** A defector loses a sure benefit and gets only a W/S chance at a new one. When W/S is tiny, loyalty is cheap, so the leader has money left over to keep.

### The size of the winning coalition (pp. 394–396)
- Leaders always prefer private goods to public goods for the coalition. If competition were only over private goods, the incumbent would always beat the challenger, because challengers cannot credibly promise defectors a place in their coalition (p. 394).
- So challengers stress public goods, and criticise corruption and poor services. When they win, they often keep the old corruption. The example is Jomo Kenyatta, who railed against corruption before 1963 and did little to stop it once in office. Kenya consistently ranks at the bottom of Transparency International's list (p. 395).
- Selectorate theory suggests foreign supporters of public-minded opposition leaders should not necessarily expect improved performance if those leaders come to power (p. 395).
- As W grows, each member's private share shrinks. With $1 billion: W = 1,000 gives at most $1 million each, while W = 1 million gives at most $1,000 each (p. 395).
- So the incumbent's advantage in private goods shrinks as W grows. At some point private goods are less valuable to members than public goods would be. Small-W leaders (dictatorships) therefore tend to use private goods, large-W leaders (democracies) primarily public goods (p. 395).
- This is said to explain why democratic competition is nearly always over public goods, such as education and health plans (p. 395).

### Predictions: Figure 10.8 (pp. 395–397)
- **Good performance:** W and W/S both large (democracies). Leaders provide public goods (large W), and the weak loyalty norm forces hard work (p. 396).
- **Poor performance:** W and W/S both small (dominant-party and personalistic dictatorships). Leaders give small private payments to the coalition and are highly corrupt. The only brake on predation is residents' refusal to work, which leaves nothing to prey on. This brake is much weaker with natural resources (oil, minerals) or large foreign aid (A. Smith 2007) (p. 396).
- **Middling performance:** W small, W/S large (monarchies, military juntas). They provide few public goods but need good economic performance to pay a not-so-loyal coalition. This implies some care for the workforce and some basic public goods (p. 397).
- The authors say this fits Figure 9.4 (democracies tend to perform well, dictatorships vary widely). Selectorate theory is offered as a "potential explanation" (p. 397).
- **Leader type:** a civic-minded leader is "neither necessary nor sufficient" for good policy. What is needed is institutions with large W and large W/S. There, only leaders who perform well can stay in power, whether they care about performance for its own sake or only for staying in office (p. 397).
- Under such conditions residents have incentives to invest, and the economy is expected to grow (p. 397).
- Even if civic-minded and non-civic-minded leaders both exist, all are forced to govern well in large-W, large-W/S systems and poorly in small-W, small-W/S systems if they want to stay in power (pp. 397–398).

> **Intuition note.** The map puts the pressure on institutions, not personalities. The same person governs well or badly depending on who he needs to please and how replaceable those people are.

**Box 10.4, the two Leopolds (pp. 398–399)** (BDM2S2's account):
- Leopold II, king of Belgium (1865–1909), gave Belgians public goods and reforms: the right to strike, wider suffrage, child-labor limits, education improvements and public works (p. 398).
- In the Congo Free State (1885–1908), which he ruled as personal property, he set up the Force Publique. Soldiers earned low pay plus commissions on rubber and ivory. They used slave labor, torture and murder to meet quotas, and were rewarded for killing "rebels", often villagers who refused forced labor. Hands or heads served as proof (pp. 398–399).
- Revenue was not returned as public goods. The only goods exported to the Congo were weapons (p. 399).
- Belgium was a constitutional monarchy relying on an elected government, so W was reasonably large. In the Congo W was just Leopold and the Force Publique (p. 399).
- BDM2S2 conclude the "real" Leopold was the Congo ruler, because he inherited Belgium's institutions but freely designed the Congo's. His behaviour in both fits the institutional incentives (p. 399).
- Chiang Kai-shek ruled China (small W, small W/S) for twenty years and Taiwan (large W, large W/S) for twenty-five. He gave more public goods and better performance in Taiwan (p. 398).

**Who prefers which institutions (pp. 398–400):**
- Leaders prefer small W and large S, which help them stay in power and enrich themselves.
- Coalition members prefer small W with large W/S. Small W means private goods, and large W/S forces the leader to pay a lot to counter weak loyalty.
- Selectorate members and the disenfranchised prefer large W and large W/S.
- So leaders prefer dominant-party or personalistic dictatorships, coalition members prefer monarchies or juntas, and everyone else prefers democracies (pp. 399–400).

- The authors read this as follows. Larger W and larger W/S go with higher growth, wealth, education and health spending and life expectancy, and lower infant mortality. They say this gives "a great deal of support" for two key implications, and that political institutions clearly affect policies important for citizens' material well-being (p. 400).

> **Intuition note.** Countries where more people must be satisfied (larger W) or where defection is easier (larger W/S) show better average outcomes. The pattern holds across six indicators, which is why the authors count it as support. The growth model explains very little variance (R² under 0.01).

### Conclusion (pp. 402–403)
- Dictatorship types by "support coalitions": monarchies, military and civilian dictatorships, the last split into personalistic and dominant-party (p. 402).
- Durability: monarchs last longest, then dominant-party, then personalistic, then military dictators. Creating multiparty legislatures "might be able to" extend rule (p. 402).
- Selectorate theory, starting from the assumption that all leaders want to win and keep power, offers an institutional explanation of performance variation. Leaders in large-W, weak-loyalty systems must provide public goods. Small-W, strong-loyalty leaders are "forced" to provide private goods and perform poorly. Monarchies and juntas are in between (p. 403).
- The key concepts list (p. 403) includes: civilian dictatorship, competitive authoritarian regime, corruption, dictator's dilemma, disenfranchised, dominant-party dictatorship, electoral authoritarian regime, hegemonic electoral regime, kleptocracy, loyalty norm, monarchy, military dictatorship, personalistic dictatorship, politically closed authoritarian regimes, selectorate, selectorate theory, winning coalition.

### Problems (pp. 403–411)
- **1. Military Intervention Game (pp. 403–405).** After Geddes (2003), a military split between an Intervener Faction and a Professional Faction forms an asymmetric coordination (Battle of the Sexes) game. The authors assume that a coup fails if only one faction takes part, and that both factions prefer a cohesive military above all. Tasks: rank outcomes, fill the Figure 10.9 payoff table with 4–1, find Nash equilibria, then redraw as an extensive form with the Intervener moving first and find the subgame perfect equilibrium (pp. 404–405).
  - Geddes's point: a first-mover strategy must be credible. In Spain's 1981 coup attempt, King Juan Carlos phoned garrison commanders and went on television, which made the first move lose credibility. One plotter reportedly said "Next time, cut the king's phone line" (p. 405).
- **2. Dictatorship Party Game (pp. 405–408).** Geddes (2005) argues dictators form parties and hold elections to ward off coups from within the elite. The Regime chooses "Do Nothing" or "Launch Party". The Challenger is weak with probability p or strong with 1 − p, and the Regime does not know which. Assumptions: a party makes coups by either type fail, and without a party only weak Challengers fail (p. 406). Figure 10.10 gives the cardinal payoffs. Tasks: preference orders, subgame equilibria, expected payoffs, the critical probability, and the value of information (pp. 406–408).
- **3. Classifying regimes (pp. 408–409).** Place nine governments (Cuba, Saddam's Iraq, the US in 1776, the UAE, Pinochet's Chile, Argentina, apartheid South Africa, Taiwan, Jordan) in the W–S space of Figure 10.11. The bottom-right cell is empty because W cannot exceed S (p. 408).
- **4. Public and private goods (p. 409).** Give three examples of each that leaders might use.
- **5. Size of W (p. 409).** $1 billion revenue; public goods worth $2,000 per person if all is spent publicly; compare W = 250,000 and W = 750,000.
- **6. Loyalty norm (pp. 410–411).** $1 billion; W = 250,000; S = 50 million, then S = 1 million. Compute the defector's expected value, the amount needed for loyalty, and the amount left for the leader.
- **7. International organizations (p. 411).** A description of Kenya after 2002, where aid was largely stolen under Kibaki. Asks what selectorate theory implies for the IMF and World Bank in small-W countries.

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text.* A leader raises $900 for coalition members and himself.

**Case 1: W = 3, S = 30.**
- W/S = 3/30 = 0.1.
- The most each member could get is $900 / 3 = $300.
- A defector's expected value: 0.1 × $300 + 0.9 × $0 = $30.
- The leader must pay each a little over $30, about 3 × $30 = $90 in total.
- He can keep about $900 − $90 = $810.

**Case 2: W = 3, S = 9.**
- W/S = 3/9 ≈ 0.33.
- The maximum is still $300 each.
- The defector's expected value is about 0.33 × $300 = $100.
- The leader pays about 3 × $100 = $300 and can keep about $600.

**Case 3: W = 300 (large), same $900.**
- Each member's maximum private share is $900 / 300 = $3.
- If a challenger can offer a public good worth more than $3 per person, private goods alone no longer win support (assumed numbers; the text says this happens at some size of W, p. 395).

Same revenue and same W, yet a smaller S (a weaker loyalty norm) cuts the leader's skim from about $810 to about $600 in the first two cases. A larger W shrinks private shares, as in the text (p. 395).
