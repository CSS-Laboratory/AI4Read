# Presidentialism and Democratic Survival (end of Chapter 16)

## L0 — One line
The authors find considerable support for the argument that presidential constitutions make democratic consolidation harder than parliamentary ones, particularly when the legislature is highly fragmented.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Do institutions affect whether new democracies survive? These pages review evidence that democracy has lasted less often under presidentialism than under parliamentarism. The authors report considerable support for Mainwaring's argument that the gap is larger when the legislature is highly fragmented. His reasoning: fragmentation makes president–legislature deadlock more likely; presidential systems lack neat constitutional ways to break it, so deadlock is more likely to lead to democratic instability, while in parliamentary systems it is more likely to lead to cabinet instability. The authors also suggest that institutional choice may matter more in poor countries, and that permissive electoral laws plus presidentialism are likely to cause problems.

**Prerequisites:** Parliamentary vs presidential government (confidence votes, dissolution, fixed terms); proportional representation (PR); reading regression coefficients and standard errors (recapped on pp. 814–815); veto players (more of them make the status quo harder to change, p. 823). "Incongruent/asymmetric federalism" is defined earlier in the chapter, not in these pages.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Historical evidence suggests democracy is less stable under presidentialism: expected democratic life of 21 years vs 73 under parliamentarism (Przeworski et al.) (p. 805).
- Stepan and Skach explain this by mutual independence (presidential) vs mutual dependence (parliamentary). Deadlock in presidential systems has no constitutional exit, so politicians and citizens may look to the military (p. 809).
- Their tables fit this: survival rates 61% vs 20%, coup rates 18% vs 40%, more overachievers among parliamentary systems (overachievers = countries more democratic than their development level predicts). The authors say the first two comparisons (survival rates) say very little about the causal link; the coup and over/underachiever tables then probe a mechanism and a confounder (pp. 810–813).
- The authors' own probit model (a statistical model of the probability that a democracy survives) finds a negative, significant presidentialism coefficient even with wealth, growth and oil controls (pp. 814–815).
- The hospital worry: presidentialism may be adopted in difficult circumstances, so Stepan and Skach's comparisons may overstate its effects. Cheibub's military-tradition argument is one case (pp. 815–816).
- Mainwaring: fragmentation (a legislature split among many parties) raises the likelihood of instability in both systems, but of democratic instability under presidentialism and of cabinet instability under parliamentarism. Data support a "difficult combination" of presidentialism and multipartism (pp. 816–820).
- The authors' interaction model: presidentialism's negative effect is significant once the effective number of legislative parties (a measure of party-system size) exceeds about 2 and grows with party-system size (pp. 821–822).
- Veto-player reading and wealth: institutions are likely to matter more in poor countries. If a presidential constitution is chosen, permissive electoral laws are likely to cause problems (pp. 823–825).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the federalism section (p. 805)
The visible text starts mid-argument; the antecedent of "they" is on the previous page, which is not given here. The first sentence says that "they" are likely to discourage the emergence and success of parties that campaign on one regional or ethnic identity group (p. 805).
- **Alternative strategy:** cross-regional vote requirements. Parties must compete in a certain number of regions and win a certain percentage of the vote there to be elected to the federal government (p. 805).
- Russia, Indonesia and Nigeria adopted such laws in an attempt to prevent identity-based parties from forming (Brancati 2004) (p. 805).
- **Conclusion:** incongruent and asymmetric federalism can help reduce ethnic conflict and stabilize democracy in countries with geographically concentrated ethnic groups. This might hold only if combined with mechanisms that prevent regionally based identity parties from dominating subnational governments (p. 805).

*Intuition note:* The authors' claim is conditional. The authors say incongruent and asymmetric federalism can help in countries with geographically concentrated ethnic groups, but this might be so only alongside rules that stop regional identity parties from dominating subnational governments (p. 805).

### Presidentialism and democratic survival: framing (pp. 805–806)
- This returns to Part II's question of why some countries are democratic. Chapters 6–7 covered economic and cultural explanations. Institutionalist approaches ask: if democracy emerges, do some institutions make survival more or less likely? The focus here is presidential vs parliamentary democracy (p. 805).
- Przeworski et al. (2000, 129): expected life of democracy is 21 years under presidentialism, about three to five times shorter than the 73 years under parliamentarism. This led some to speak of the "perils of presidentialism" (Linz 1990a) (p. 805).
- Scholars debate whether this fragility is due to something inherent in presidentialism, and if so what. The question matters because the share of democracies with presidential constitutions doubled from 16% (1978) to 32% (2008) (pp. 805–806).
- The authors' preview: some presidential democracies, specifically those with multiparty systems, are more unstable than others. Ongoing economic development is making all presidential democracies more stable than in the past (p. 806).

### The perils of presidentialism (pp. 806–809)
- Presidents fuse the symbolic role of head of state with the powers of head of government (Linz). Supporters of presidentialism say presidents check legislators, who are elected by a subset of voters and may have particularistic interests (p. 806).
- **Shively's five commonly thought consequences** (p. 806–807):
  1. Policymaking power is concentrated in the president. Through direct appeals to voters and fused roles, he can wield power unavailable to most prime ministers, who are "first among equals" (p. 806).
  2. Low clarity of responsibility: each branch can blame the other for bad outcomes and claim credit for good ones (p. 806).
  3. Policy is hard to make quickly. A presidential government can face a legislature controlled by parties that do not support the president's goals; a parliamentary government cannot survive without a legislative majority (pp. 806–807).
  4. Executive recruitment differs. Prime ministers tend to come from the party's legislative leadership after working up from the backbenches. Presidential candidates are often outsiders, so frequently lack legislative experience and policy expertise (p. 807). Fn. 45: Alberto Fujimori won Peru's 1990 election as a complete outsider; such a candidate becoming prime minister is "almost unthinkable" (p. 807).
  5. Comprehensive policy is hard to produce. Executive–legislative bargaining means some policies pass only to gain another branch's acquiescence on an unrelated matter. A parliamentary cabinet has the legislature's confidence by definition, so is thought to have a better chance of comprehensive programmes (p. 807).
- **Caveat:** many of these consequences are not unique to presidentialism. Parliamentary democracies with coalition governments are also likely to have difficulty making policy quickly, locating responsibility, and making comprehensive policy. Some scholars therefore speak of the "problems of parliamentarism" (p. 807).
- **Immobilism:** in parliamentary democracies, government coalitions so weak and unstable that they cannot agree on new policy (p. 807).
- **French Fourth Republic (1946–1958):** 24 governments under 16 prime ministers in 12 years. Governments could not deal with main issues such as Indochina and Algeria (pp. 807–808).
  - The PR system helped to produce a highly fragmented, polarized legislature. Roughly 30% of Assembly seats regularly went to extreme-right (Gaullists, Poujadists) and extreme-left (Communists) parties not seen as viable partners (p. 808).
  - So five or six small centre parties had to build majorities from the other 70%, which proved extremely difficult. Governments typically chose between immobility and pushing their programme, which normally led to quick removal (p. 808).
  - In May 1958 a majority of legislators voted themselves and the constitution out of existence and delegated power to de Gaulle. One goal of his constitution was a strong president who could override stalemates (p. 808).
- **Postwar Italy:** average cabinet lasted less than a year. Many scholars blame a PR system that helped create fragmented, polarized legislatures. Rustow (1950, 116) wrote that PR tends to prevent any exercise of power, by facilitating cabinet crises, stalemates and legislative inaction (p. 808).
- **Government instability can overstate political instability** (pp. 808–809):
  - Cabinet instability need not imply a lack of ministerial experience (fn. 46 defines political experience as experience in any portfolio and portfolio experience as experience in a specific one) (p. 808).
  - Italy: the Christian Democrats were in every cabinet 1948–1992, with rotating junior partners (Liberal, Republican, Social Democratic, Socialist). Several scholars note that each new cabinet was essentially the previous cabinet's people (pp. 808–809). Scholars of the Fourth Republic argue almost identically (p. 809).
  - The authors conclude that cabinet instability need not imply ministerial instability and, as they say they will show, does not imply democratic instability (p. 809).
- Illustrative cases are not enough: "ransacking history" for cases supporting one's favourite or most-hated institution is not good scientific practice. They turn to systematic analysis (p. 809).

*Intuition note:* Both systems can fail to act. On the views reported here, parliamentary systems can fail through weak coalitions (immobilism) and presidential ones through protracted executive–legislative bargaining. The open question is which failure threatens democracy itself (pp. 807–809).

### Stepan and Skach's argument and evidence (pp. 809–815)
- **Theory:** Stepan and Skach (1993) claim democratic survival prospects are worse under presidentialism. The essence of parliamentarism is mutual dependence: the government needs legislative-majority support, and it can dissolve the legislature. In presidentialism the two branches are mutually independent, each with its own legitimacy and fixed electoral mandate; the legislature cannot remove the president and he cannot remove it (p. 809).
- Mutual dependence encourages reconciliation; mutual independence encourages antagonism. This can lead to deadlock when the president faces an opposition-dominated legislature. With no constitutional mechanism, politicians and citizens may look to the military. In parliamentary systems, a no-confidence vote or dissolution and new elections are constitutional ways out. This is thought to be at the heart of why stability is greater under parliamentarism (p. 809).
- **Evidence 1 (Table 16.6, p. 811):** 80 countries became independent democracies 1945–1979: 41 parliamentary, 36 presidential, 3 semi-presidential (p. 810). Only 15 were continuously democratic through the 1980s. All 15 were parliamentary; none of the 36 presidential ones sustained democracy (p. 810).
- **Evidence 2 (Table 16.7, p. 812):** 53 non-OECD countries democratic for at least one year in 1973–1989 (28 parliamentary, 25 presidential). Democratic for ten consecutive years: 17 parliamentary, 5 presidential. Survival rate 61% vs 20%, so parliamentary is three times presidential (pp. 810, 812).
  - Fn. 47: non-OECD countries are the focus because democracy is most unstable in poor countries and institutional choice is arguably most important there (p. 810).
- The authors say these simple comparisons say very little about the causal link between regime type and survival (p. 810).
- **Evidence 3 (observable implication, Table 16.8):** if deadlock invites extraconstitutional behaviour, military coups should be more common in presidential democracies. Same 53 countries: coups in 5 of 28 parliamentary (18%) and 10 of 25 presidential (40%), more than twice as likely under presidentialism, "exactly as" Stepan and Skach predict (pp. 810, 812).
- **Confounding worry:** the authors say it is probably the case that some factors that cause democracies to fail are also associated with choosing parliamentarism or presidentialism. If so, ignoring them might lead us to overestimate the effect of regime type (p. 812).
- **Evidence 4 (Table 16.9, p. 813):** Vanhanen (1991) built an index of democratization and an index of power resources (societal development; fn. 48 lists six components). The two are strongly but not perfectly associated. Stepan and Skach call countries scoring surprisingly high on democratization "overachievers" and surprisingly low "underachievers" (p. 813).

| | Parliamentary | Presidential |
|---|---|---|
| Overachievers | 31 | 10 |
| Underachievers | 6 | 12 |
| Ratio over/under | 5.17 | 0.83 |

  - Stepan and Skach read this as: after accounting for modernization variables, parliamentary systems are five times more likely to be overachievers than underachievers. Presidential systems are slightly more likely to be underachievers (p. 813).
  - Equivalently, overachievers are about three times more likely to be parliamentary than presidential, and underachievers about twice as likely to be presidential than parliamentary. The authors call this strong support for lower survival under presidentialism even after controlling for other factors (pp. 813–814).
- **The authors' own test (Table 16.10, p. 814):** Przeworski et al.'s data on 135 countries, 1950–1990, dynamic probit; dependent variable is the probability a country is a democracy this year if it was one last year (p. 814).

| Variable | Model 1 | Model 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Presidentialism | −0.58*** (0.14) | −0.32* (0.16) |
| GDP per capita | | 0.0002*** (0.00005) |
| Growth in GDP per capita | | 0.04*** (0.01) |
| Oil producer | | −0.12 (0.28) |
| Constant | 2.22*** (0.10) | 1.29*** (0.18) |

  - Reading the table: a positive coefficient raises the probability of democratic survival, a negative one lowers it. As a rule of thumb, we can be 95% confident of the sign if the coefficient is bigger than twice its standard error. Stars: * over 90%, ** over 95%, *** over 99%. No star means we cannot be confident the variable has any effect (pp. 814–815).
  - Result: presidentialism's coefficient is negative and significant. Presidential democracies are less likely to remain democratic than nonpresidential ones, even with the economic factors from Chapter 6 taken into account (p. 815).

*Intuition note:* Each test adds one check (coups as a mechanism, then modernization level, then wealth/growth/oil). The survival, over/underachiever and probit results all point the same way, and the coup table fits the predicted mechanism. But none settles whether regime type itself is the cause: the authors next raise the hospital worry (pp. 810–816).

### The difficult combination: presidentialism and multipartism (pp. 815–823)
- **The hospital metaphor:** de Gaulle-style belief in a "strong man" in crisis is widespread. If presidentialism is adopted in moments of crisis, presidential regimes may fail more often not because presidentialism is inherently problematic but because it is adopted in difficult circumstances. Like hospitals with high death rates, because patients are sicker, not because hospitals kill (p. 815). Fn. 49: Shugart (1999) similarly argues presidentialism tends to be adopted in large, complex societies with unequal incomes and regional disparities; in his argument it is these conditions, rather than presidentialism itself, that make democracy hard to sustain (p. 815).
- Until we are convinced the two groups of countries are alike, studies like Stepan and Skach's risk overstating the harm of presidentialism (p. 816).
- The standard fix is a "poor health" control variable. With one exception the authors know of no such analysis. Fn. 50: Cheibub (2007) argues presidential regimes are more likely to emerge where the military has traditionally had a strong political role; any democracy is more likely to fail there; so presidential democracies fail more because they are adopted in difficult circumstances, not because they are presidential (p. 816).
- **Complication:** some factors may be dangerous only for parliamentary or only for presidential regimes. If the two process factors such as legislative fragmentation in fundamentally different ways, it may prove extremely difficult to measure a regime's underlying health independently of its type (p. 816).
- **Mainwaring's work suggests** the two do process fragmentation differently. Fragmentation always increases the likelihood of instability, but it increases cabinet instability in parliamentary systems and democratic instability in presidential ones. In the authors' reading: if fragmentation is an ailment in parliamentary democracy (as de Gaulle claimed), presidentialism could well be a medicine worse than the disease (p. 816).
- **Why fragmentation differs in effect** (pp. 816–817):
  - *Deadlock* in a presidential democracy: a legislative majority opposed to the president is large enough to pass bills but not to override his veto. Fragmentation raises the chance that the president's party lacks a majority (p. 816).
  - A parliamentary prime minister serves at the legislature's pleasure and must form a cabinet of a coalition that commands a majority. Presidents do sometimes form coalitions (fn. 51: but coalition governments are less common and minority governments more common in presidential democracies) (p. 817).
  - Key difference: legislators voting against a president's bill block it. Voting against a prime minister's bill gives a good chance the government falls. A parliamentary coalition government implies a legislative coalition; this presumption does not hold in presidential systems (fn. 52: a cabinet post does not necessarily imply party support for the president) (p. 817).
  - Result: fragmentation is much more likely to produce deadlock under presidentialism than immobilism under parliamentarism (p. 817).
- **Recruitment makes it worse:** presidents are often outsiders with little policy experience or legislative contacts, partly because they are directly elected. So they may lack the skills to build coalitions to end deadlock (p. 817).
  - It has been argued that coalition partners in presidential regimes have acute incentives to distance themselves from the president's goals. One reason is that they expect to run against him in a winner-take-all contest (Coppedge 1994) (p. 817).
  - Prime ministers have typically spent years in the legislature and learned coalition building. According to Mainwaring, presidential democracies are more apt to have executives whose programmes are consistently blocked, and executives less capable of dealing with problems (p. 817).
- **Deadlock leads to different instability** (p. 818):
  - Parliamentary: no-confidence vote or dissolution and new elections. Presidential: per Mainwaring, no neat means of replacing a president who is very unpopular and has lost legislative support (p. 818).
  - No no-confidence vote may ensure the head of government's stability but introduces rigidity. It can threaten democracy by encouraging frustrated elites or masses to call for the president's removal or parliament's dissolution (Linz 1994) (p. 818).
  - Some presidential constitutions allow actors to do this within bounds, but the solution is more likely to be extraconstitutional in presidential regimes. So deadlock is more likely to produce democratic instability there, while immobilism in parliamentary democracies is more likely to produce cabinet instability (p. 818). A photo caption shows the 1973 coup in Chile (p. 818).
- **Implication:** democracy should be more fragile in some presidential regimes than others, specifically (perhaps only) those with high fragmentation. When fragmentation is low, deadlock and democratic instability should also be low (p. 818).
- **Mainwaring's evidence** (pp. 819–820):
  - Of countries with uninterrupted democracy 1967–1992 (25 years), 24 were parliamentary and only four presidential: Colombia, Costa Rica, the United States, Venezuela. All effectively had two-party systems (Table 16.11: effective number of legislative parties 2.45, 2.21, 1.95, 2.42) (p. 819).
  - At the time of writing in 1993, Mainwaring argued that only one multiparty presidential regime had sustained democracy for 25 years: Chile (democracy from 1932; 1973 coup against Allende). Allende was elected in 1970 with 35.3% and faced a highly fragmented, polarized legislature. Fn. 53: the authors are reluctant to invoke "exception that proves the rule" but think it fits here (p. 819).
  - Table 16.12, success = 25 uninterrupted years at any time 1945–1992 (p. 820):

| Regime type | Success rate |
|---|---|
| Multiparty presidentialism | 1/15 = 0.07 |
| Two-party presidentialism | 5/10 = 0.50 |
| Parliamentarism | 25/44 = 0.57 |

  - The authors read this as showing consolidation is possible in two-party presidential regimes but not in multiparty ones. The two-party presidential rate (0.50) is almost as high as the parliamentary rate (0.57). They call this strong evidence that the combination of presidentialism and multipartism, not just presidentialism, is inimical to democracy; Mainwaring calls it "the difficult combination" (p. 819).
  - Table 16.13 (Stepan and Skach; countries independent after 1945 that sustained democracy 1979–1989): parliamentary 23 with fewer than three effective parties and 11 with three or more; semi-presidential 0 and 2; presidential 5 and 0. Long-lived multiparty parliamentary regimes are not particularly rare; long-lived multiparty presidential regimes are (p. 820).
- **Two concerns remain** (pp. 820–821):
  - Arbitrary cutoffs: 25 years, ten years in the 1980s, and "multiparty" meaning an effective number of legislative parties above 2.9 (pp. 820–821).
  - No account taken of other factors associated with democracy, regime type and party-system size (p. 821).
- **Conditional model** (p. 821): the Table 16.10 model is modified so that presidentialism's effect on survival can vary with party-system size (Brambor, Clark and Golder 2006). Mainwaring's hypothesis: presidentialism has a stronger negative effect with a large party system than with a small one, and may have no negative effect at all when the system is sufficiently small (p. 821). Fn. 54: dynamic probit with GDP per worker, its growth and oil-producer status; 135 countries, 1950–1990. The plotted case is the "average democracy": non-oil producer, GDP per worker $14,468, growth 2.41% (p. 821).
- **Reading Figure 16.11** (p. 822): the vertical gap between the gray zero line and the solid line is the estimated effect of presidentialism at each effective number of legislative parties. The solid line starts close to but below zero and becomes more negative as the number rises, so the effect is always negative and grows with party-system size (p. 821). The dashed lines are the 95% confidence interval. If the interval spans zero, we cannot reject no effect (like a coefficient with no stars). When both bounds are below zero, as when the effective number of parties is above 2, we can be 95% confident the effect is negative (p. 822).
- **Result:** presidentialism has a significant negative effect once the effective number of legislative parties exceeds about 2, and this grows with the number, "exactly as Mainwaring predicts" (p. 822). Fn. 55: the effect would stop being significant slightly above 6.1 parties, but only about 3% of presidential observations have a larger system (p. 822).

*Intuition note:* The test asks whether presidentialism's effect depends on a second variable, so we read the whole line and its interval, not one coefficient. The effect is estimated as negative at every party-system size, but is significant only above about 2 effective parties (and, per fn. 55, no longer beyond about 6.1) (pp. 821–822).

### Summary (pp. 823–825)
- The authors found considerable support for an argument that presidential constitutions make consolidation more difficult than parliamentary ones, particularly with a highly fragmented legislature. The key weakness of presidentialism appears to be its inability to find legal ways out of executive–legislative deadlock, which is more likely when the legislature is highly fragmented (p. 823).
- **Veto-player link:** from Chapter 15, all else equal, more veto players are expected to make it more difficult to change the status quo, particularly if they hold diverse preferences. Policy stability from veto players is equivalent in many ways to this chapter's deadlock or immobilism (p. 823). Tsebelis conjectured that many diverse veto players may encourage policy stability but also political instability (1995, 322) (p. 823).
- If Tsebelis is correct that more veto players lead to policy stability (deadlock in its extreme form), presidentialism and parliamentarism should affect the type of instability that ensues. Under a presidential constitution deadlock is likely to encourage a coup or other democratic instability; under a parliamentary one, immobilism is likely to lead to a no-confidence vote, reshuffle or elections, i.e. cabinet instability (Figure 16.12) (pp. 823–824). Fn. 56: semi-presidential democracies are ignored (p. 823).
- **Wealth and institutions:** Przeworski et al. argue wealth is essentially a sufficient condition for survival. Countries above about $6,055 per capita (1985 PPP US$) are likely to stay democratic forever, if they become democratic (pp. 823–824). So institutional choice, e.g. multipartism plus presidentialism, is likely to matter more in poor countries than rich ones, since democracy is likely to survive in rich countries whether or not their institutions are felicitous (p. 824).
  - Traditional approaches predict the opposite: formal institutions matter more in rich countries, where rule of law is more likely (p. 824; fn. 58 says the importance of rule of law and legal institutions itself varies with development) (p. 824).
  - Ferrer's (2003) undergraduate thesis found the probability of democratic regime failure rises with the number of veto players, but only in poor countries (fn. 59: poor = below $6,055 per capita). The authors call this suggestive enough to warrant future work (p. 824).
  - Fn. 57: Boix (2003, 1850–1990) finds presidential democracies more likely than parliamentary ones to fail only in underdeveloped countries, with no difference in developed ones (p. 824).
- **Policy implication:** constitution writers typically choose between presidentialism and parliamentarism explicitly, but party-system size is not entirely under their direct control. A country with high social heterogeneity can be expected to produce a highly fragmented legislature unless majoritarian electoral institutions are adopted. So if a presidential constitution is chosen, permissive electoral laws such as PR with high district magnitude are likely to cause problems (p. 825).
- Przeworski et al.'s result may suggest richer countries have a greater margin of error. That may explain why some multiparty presidential regimes in Latin America have stayed democratic since the mid-1980s, as many have exceeded the "magical" wealth threshold (p. 825).

### Conclusion (p. 825)
Four summarized results: (1) patterned differences in how majoritarian and consensus democracies represent voters; (2) electoral laws influence who rules and how they wield power, at least for redistributive fiscal policy; (3) constitutional elements such as electoral laws and federalism are likely to affect the probability of ethnic conflict; (4) the choice of electoral laws and of presidentialism vs parliamentarism is likely to strongly influence new democracies' survival, especially if wealth is not high enough by itself to make survival likely. Many questions remain about how these results interact (p. 825).

### Key Concepts and Problems (p. 826)
The chapter's key-concepts list includes accountability, immobilism (p. 807), retrospective voting, consociationalism and others (p. 826). Two synthesis problems follow: (1) design a constitution maximizing a new democracy's survival, with ranked recommendations and evidence; (2) advise Burma's interim leadership on parliamentarism vs presidentialism vs semi-presidentialism (p. 826).

## Toy example
*Our own invented numbers, not data from the book.* Suppose 40 democracies, 10 in each cell:

| | Two-party legislature | Multiparty legislature |
|---|---|---|
| Presidential: survivors out of 10 | 6 | 1 |
| Parliamentary: survivors out of 10 | 6 | 5 |

- Pooled presidential rate = (6 + 1)/20 = 35%. Pooled parliamentary rate = (6 + 5)/20 = 55%. A pooled comparison shows a 20-point gap.
- Within two-party legislatures: 60% vs 60%, a gap of 0.
- Within multiparty legislatures: 10% vs 50%, a gap of 40 points.
- The pooled 20 points averages a zero gap and a 40-point gap: (0 + 40)/2 = 20. So the pooled gap hides that, in this toy, the difference sits entirely in multiparty systems. This is the logic of a conditional hypothesis such as Mainwaring's (pp. 819–822).

## Concepts
- [[institutions]] — here, constitutional choices (presidentialism vs parliamentarism, electoral laws, federalism) whose effects on democratic survival are studied (p. 805)
- [[mutual-dependence-vs-independence]] (new) — parliamentarism: government and legislature each can end the other; presidentialism: each has own fixed mandate and neither can remove the other (p. 809)
- [[legislative-deadlock]] (new) — in presidentialism, an opposition legislative majority can pass bills but cannot override a veto (p. 816)
- [[immobilism]] (new) — in parliamentary democracies, coalitions so weak and unstable they cannot agree on new policy (p. 807)
- [[difficult-combination]] (new) — Mainwaring's name for presidentialism plus multipartism, as a threat to democratic consolidation (p. 819)
- [[effective-number-of-legislative-parties]] (new) — measure of party-system size; "multiparty" here means above 2.9 (pp. 819, 821)
- [[selection-into-regime-type]] (new) — hospital worry: presidentialism may be adopted in difficult circumstances, so simple comparisons may overstate its effects (p. 815)
- [[conditional-effect-interaction]] (new) — the effect of presidentialism varies with party-system size; read from a plot with a confidence interval (pp. 821–822)
- [[democratic-overachievers-underachievers]] (new) — countries more/less democratic than their power-resource level predicts (Stepan and Skach, using Vanhanen) (p. 813)
- [[observable implications]] — the coup-rate prediction that follows from Stepan and Skach's theory (p. 810)
- [[veto player]] — more, and more diverse, veto players make status-quo change harder; in extreme form this is deadlock (p. 823)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Deadlock without an exit in presidential systems ↔ veto-player theory: more veto players, more policy stability; this chapter links that to deadlock (p. 823).
- [structural-analogy] Hospital metaphor ↔ causal inference: selection into treatment confounds the comparison of outcomes.
- [same-math] Conditional effect of presidentialism across party-system size ↔ interaction terms in regression: same model-and-interval logic as other conditional hypotheses.

## Questions this raises
- If Cheibub's military-tradition argument is right, how much of the presidentialism effect remains after controlling for it?
- Why would presidential systems with few parties (such as the four in Table 16.11) avoid deadlock?
- Do semi-presidential systems, ignored here (fn. 56), combine the benefits or the problems of both?
- If wealth is nearly sufficient, why would institutions matter more in poor countries, and how robust is Ferrer's thesis result?
