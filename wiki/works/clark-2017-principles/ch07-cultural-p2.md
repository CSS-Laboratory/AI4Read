# Experiments, Culture and Democracy (Ch. 7, second half: experiments, conclusion, problems)

## L0 — One line
Ultimatum- and Dictator-Game experiments in fifteen small-scale societies suggest that culture might be considered a shared way (evolved over many years) of playing everyday "games", which may make some societies better suited than others to the "game" of democracy.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks whether culture affects democracy, using experiments as well as surveys. In the Ultimatum Game a proposer offers part of a sum; the responder accepts or rejects (rejection: both get nothing). Students almost everywhere make fairly generous offers, unlike the self-interested Homo economicus. Fifteen small-scale societies show much more variation. Two features of their economic life (payoffs to cooperation and market integration) explain 68 percent of the variance (spread) in offers. One interpretation: people apply analogies from everyday life, so some societies' habits may fit democracy better. For majority Muslim countries, the hurdle to democracy has less to do with being Muslim than with poverty. A box explains experimental method; problems include a coordination game.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a payoff matrix or game tree; correlation versus causation; mean versus mode; the idea of a rational self-interested actor.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The Ultimatum Game separates two motives for generosity: fairness and fear of rejection. The Dictator Game removes rejection, so any positive offer there must come from fairness (p. 248).
- A purely self-interested proposer (Homo economicus) is predicted to offer almost nothing in the Ultimatum Game and nothing in the Dictator Game. A substantial portion of the student subjects do not approximate this (p. 249).
- Across fifteen small-scale societies, offers and rejections vary far more than among students. Mean offers range from 26 to 58 percent (pp. 250–251, 256).
- Two group-level variables, payoffs to cooperation and market integration, explain 68 percent of the variance in offers. Individual traits such as sex, age, education and wealth could not explain the variation (pp. 256–257).
- One interpretation offered is that people drew on analogies from daily life. The results suggest culture might be considered a shared way of playing everyday games, and some societies' ways of playing may be less compatible with democracy than others' (pp. 257–258).
- Box 7.5: experiments deliberately vary the data-generating process, which helps with causal inference. They can also test the assumptions behind theories, such as whether ethnic identity is easy to identify (pp. 252–255).
- The authors' own reanalysis suggests a possible virtuous cycle among development, cultural change and democracy. Doctrinal elements that seem incompatible with democracy are hardly a firm basis for concluding that a religion is incompatible with it; for majority Muslim countries, the authors' analyses indicate that, to a large extent, the hurdle has less to do with being Muslim and more to do with being poor (pp. 258–259).

### Experiments and Culture: the games (pp. 248–249)
**Ultimatum Game.** Two anonymous players are paired (p. 248).
- The proposer gets a divisible pie, usually money, and offers part of it to the responder.
- The responder sees the offer and the pie size, and accepts or rejects.
- If the responder accepts, the responder gets the offer and the proposer keeps the rest. If the responder rejects, both get nothing (p. 248).
- Example: pie $100, offer $40. If accepted, the responder keeps $40 and the proposer $60. If rejected, both get $0 (p. 248).

**Dictator Game.** It is essentially the same, except that the responder cannot reject (p. 248).

**Why compare them.** A positive offer in the Ultimatum Game could reflect fairness or fear of rejection. In the Dictator Game there is no fear of rejection, so any positive offer there must be from fairness (p. 248).

**Homo economicus** is the label for someone who cares only about their own share (p. 249). The predictions are:
- Ultimatum Game: the proposer offers $\varepsilon$, slightly above 0, and the responder accepts because $\varepsilon > 0$. The payoffs are $\$100-\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon$ (p. 249).
- Dictator Game: the proposer offers nothing and keeps all $100 (p. 249).

**Student results.** These games were played in virtually all industrialized democracies. Typically, anonymous students are paired in a computer lab. The results are similar across countries (p. 249).
- Ultimatum Game: the modal offer (the most common one) is almost always 50 percent. The mean is 40–45 percent. Offers under 20 percent are rejected about half the time (p. 249).
- Dictator Game: the modal offer is normally 0 percent, but the mean is still 20–30 percent (p. 249).
- The authors infer two things. The gap between the modes suggests many Ultimatum offers come from fear of rejection. Yet the positive Dictator offers show prosocial behavior, so many students do not approximate Homo economicus (p. 249).

*Intuition note.* A purely self-interested player would offer almost nothing, or nothing in the Dictator Game. Yet some students make large offers even when nobody can reject them. So a substantial portion of people are not only calculators of their own gain, and the next question is whether that varies by culture.

### Small-scale societies (pp. 249–251)
- Student populations differ culturally only a little compared with the world's range, so these questions cannot be answered with any satisfaction simply by looking at student results (p. 250).
- Anthropologists and economists therefore ran both games in fifteen small-scale societies, in twelve countries on five continents (Gintis 2003; Henrich et al. 2001, 2004). They include foragers, slash-and-burn horticulturalists, nomadic herders and sedentary small-scale farmers (p. 250).
- Table 7.4 lists the groups with country, environment and economic base. Examples: the Machiguenga (Peru, horticulture), the Hadza (Tanzania, foraging), the Orma (Kenya, pastoralism) and the Lamelara (Indonesia, foraging-trade) (p. 250).
- Figure 7.4 is a bubble plot of Ultimatum offers. Bubble size shows the share of the sample making that offer. The right edge of the gray bar is the group mean. University of Pittsburgh students are the benchmark (p. 251).
- Worked reading of the figure: the Machiguenga have mode 0.15, secondary mode 0.25 and mean 0.26 (p. 251).

### Box 7.5: Experiments in political science (pp. 252–255)
This box interrupts the main argument. Experiments are increasingly used in political science, for example on turnout, ethnic diversity and public goods, and campaign messages (p. 252).

**Definitions (p. 252).**
- Experimental research: the analyst deliberately introduces variation into the data-generating process, the process that produces the data (p. 252).
- Example: a medical researcher gives one group a new drug and another a placebo (p. 252).
- Nonexperimental research: the researcher observes the data but has nothing to do with how they are generated. The data are observational data (p. 252).
- Natural experiment: nature, not the analyst, intervenes exogenously. The analyst acts as if she had intervened. It uses observational data, but the key causal variables are argued to be randomly distributed (p. 252).
- Example: Hurricane Katrina and how congressional representatives adapt to changing constituencies (p. 252).

**Settings (pp. 252–253).**
- A laboratory experiment is run in a common location, such as a computer lab, where the researcher controls everything but the subjects' behavior (pp. 252–253).
- Field and Internet experiments interact with subjects in the context of their everyday lives. Internet experiments run over the Internet (p. 253).
- The box says an Internet experiment gives less control than a lab but to some extent more control than a traditional field experiment (Morton and Williams 2010) (p. 253).

**Causal inference.** Experiments give control over data generation, which makes it easier to identify causal processes and rule out spurious relationships (p. 253).
- Example: a survey may show that TV habits correlate with beliefs. It cannot show that TV caused the beliefs, because heavy TV users may differ in many other ways (Kinder and Palfrey 1992) (p. 253).
- An experiment can randomly assign citizens to different newscasts. Random assignment tries to remove all systematic differences other than the newscasts, so later opinion differences can be attributed to the programs (p. 253).

**Testing assumptions.** Other experiments check the assumptions behind game-theoretic models. An experimenter recreates the payoffs, voting rules and voter types, and sees whether subjects behave as predicted. The results can inform more "reasonable" models (p. 254). The Ultimatum and Dictator experiments are of this kind: results differ from the equilibrium predictions, which forces an explanation (p. 254).

**Habyarimana et al. (2005) on ethnic identifiability (pp. 254–255).**
- Many theories of ethnicity assume people can tell who is in their own group, whether identity is primordial or constructed. This is tested by having subjects guess ethnicity from pictures or video clips in which people give varying true or false information (p. 254).
- Subjects were UCLA and USC undergraduates who self-identified with one of seven groups: African American, Arab, Asian, Caucasian, Indian, Latino, Persian (p. 254).
- Result: there is significant variability within and among groups in how identifiable members are. The distinctiveness of any two groups varies across individuals and groups (p. 254).
- The authors suggest implications for theories of coalition building, in-group policing, mobilization and violence (pp. 254–255).
- Anecdotal support: Mali (1990–1995) versus Senegal (1982–present), both with minority-led separatism (p. 255).
  - In Mali the Tuaregs and Maures are considered easily identifiable. Pressuring co-ethnics to join, and targeting civilians for reprisals, were easier. Positions polarized swiftly and communal violence followed (p. 255).
  - In Senegal's Casamance region the Diola are harder to identify, mobilization and targeting were harder, and violence was much lower (p. 255).
- The authors suggest that the contrasting identifiability corresponds to a sharp difference in group mobilization and the scope of violence, a difference that would be hard to account for if one assumed, erroneously, that all ethnic groups were equally identifiable (p. 255).

**fMRI.** Political scientists also use fMRI scans. Evidence suggests that local changes in brain activity cause local changes in oxygen, and fMRI measures these changes. This lets researchers see which brain areas respond, and in what order, instead of asking subjects why (p. 255).

### Results from the small-scale societies (p. 256)
- No society conforms easily to Homo economicus (p. 256).
- Variation in offers is much larger than among students. Mean offers range from 26 percent (Machiguenga) to 58 percent (Lamelara). Modal offers range from 15 to 50 percent (p. 256).
- Rejections also vary (the authors show no evidence for this). Offers below 20 percent in industrial democracies are rejected with probability 0.4–0.6 (p. 256).
  - The Machiguenga rejected only one offer, although 75 percent of offers were below 30 percent (p. 256).
  - The Au and Gnau (Papua New Guinea) were equally likely to reject offers below or above 50 percent (p. 256).
- Dictator Game variation: students have a mode at zero and a secondary mode at 50 percent. The Orma have a mode at 50 percent and the Hadza at 10 percent. The Tsimané made no zero offers, with mean 32 percent and mode 25 percent (p. 256).

### Explaining the variation (pp. 256–257)
- **Experimenters' claim:** group-specific conditions, such as social institutions or cultural fairness norms, affect preferences or expectations. They tested individual-level traits (sex, age, education, relative wealth), and statistical analyses indicated these could not explain the variation (p. 256, n. 23).
- They ranked the societies on two dimensions (p. 256):
  - Payoffs to cooperation: how important and large a group's payoff is from cooperation in production with nonimmediate kin. Low: Machiguenga and Tsimané, who are almost totally economically independent at family level and rarely cooperate in production beyond the family. High: the Lamelara, whose whale-hunting canoes carry twelve or more people (p. 256).
  - Market integration: how much people rely on market exchange. Low: the Hadza. High: the Orma, who work for wages and sell livestock (pp. 256–257).
- Expectation and result: higher payoffs to cooperation, and greater market integration, should go with higher Ultimatum offers. Market experience should give experience of abstract sharing principles toward strangers (p. 257). A statistical analysis found higher values on both dimensions associated with higher mean offers. The two variables alone explained 68 percent of the variance in offers (p. 257).

*Intuition note.* Individual traits such as sex, age, education and wealth could not explain the differences between societies. What was associated with them was how groups make a living: groups with higher payoffs to cooperation or more market integration made higher mean offers.

### Interpretation: analogues (p. 257)
One interpretation is that subjects asked themselves which familiar situation the novel game resembles, and acted as that situation requires (p. 257). Examples (the authors' accounts):
- Au and Gnau: hyperfair offers and frequent rejections fit a gift-giving culture. Accepting a gift commits you to reciprocate later, and very generous gifts put you in a subordinate position. Large offers were rejected because they caused anxiety about unspoken strings (p. 257).
- Hadza: low offers and high rejection fit hunters who avoid sharing meat. One ethnographer calls this reluctance "tolerated theft" (p. 257).
- Lamelara: equal or slightly more-than-fair offers fit the real-life division of a whale catch into predesignated parts. They may have seen the pie as like a whale (p. 257).
- Similar stories could be told for the other societies (p. 257).

### From experiments to democracy (pp. 257–258)
- Results suggest culture may be seen as a shared way of playing everyday games that evolved over many years (Bednar and Page 2007) (p. 257).
- Reasoning (hedged): choices are shaped by everyday interactions, and people seem to look for analogous situations in new settings. If so, it seems reasonable that some societies' shared way of playing may be less compatible with the game of democracy (pp. 257–258).
- Democracy often requires cooperation, competition and compromise. Societies whose everyday games already require this should find it easier to adopt and support democratic institutions. Societies without such games will find consolidation harder (p. 258).

### Conclusion (pp. 258–259)
- The idea that democracy or dictatorship suits some cultures is old. Montesquieu's and Mill's vague claims were later taken up systematically by cultural modernization theory (p. 258).
- That theory argues economic development produces cultural changes that lead to democracy (Inglehart and Welzel 2005). A key change is a "civic culture", seen by many as a prerequisite for democracy's emergence and survival, and by some as important for government performance (p. 258).
- Civic culture is conceptualized as a shared cluster of attitudes that typically includes things like high interpersonal trust, preference for gradual change, high support for the existing system and high life satisfaction (p. 258).
- Empirical support for the claim that democracy requires civic culture is "only partially supported" (p. 258).
- The authors' reanalysis of Inglehart and Welzel data: development fosters democratization through cultural change but also through other channels. This suggests development can be expected to raise the likelihood of democracy irrespective of political culture. Experience with democracy can help produce a democratic culture that reinforces later practice. They suggest there may be a virtuous cycle among social development, cultural change and democratic outcomes (p. 258).
- Religion:
  - Some scholars claimed as recently as the 1970s that Catholicism was antithetical to democracy. Few do now, given democracy's firm establishment in many Catholic countries (p. 259).
  - Virtually all religions, Islam included, have doctrinal elements that seem incompatible with democracy and others that do not (Przeworski et al. 1998) (p. 259).
  - There is growing evidence that religions' stance toward political institutions often depends less on doctrine and more on leaders' interests and strategic concerns (Kalyvas). Culture is also constructed and malleable rather than primordial (p. 259).
  - Authors' conclusion from the chapter's analyses: there is little reason to believe majority Muslim countries cannot become and remain democratic once wealth is accounted for. To a large extent the hurdle has less to do with being Muslim and more with being poor (p. 259).
- The next chapter turns to how democratic transitions actually play out (p. 259).

  - Payoffs (S, A, B) in Figure 7.5 (p. 262):

| Outcome | (S, A, B) |
|---|---|
| Not transgress (status quo) | (2, 8, 8) |
| Transgress, both acquiesce | (8, 2, 2) |
| Transgress, A acquiesces, B challenges | (8, 2, 1) |
| Transgress, A challenges, B acquiesces | (8, 1, 2) |

## Toy example (ours, not from the source)
Pie = $100. A proposer cares only about expected money. We compare two imaginary societies. We assume a responder always accepts offers of $40.

| Offer | Society X: acceptance probability | Proposer's expected money in X | Society Y: acceptance probability | Proposer's expected money in Y |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10 | 0.5 | $0.5 \times 90 = \$45$ | 0.95 | $0.95 \times 90 = \$85.50$ |
| $40 | 1.0 | $1.0 \times 60 = \$60$ | 1.0 | $1.0 \times 60 = \$60$ |

- In X, where low offers are often rejected, the best offer of these two is $40, because $60 > $45.
- In Y, where rejection is rare, the best of these two is $10, because $85.50 > $60.
- In a Dictator Game nobody can reject, so a purely self-interested proposer offers $0 in both societies, keeping $100.
- A positive Dictator offer, or a high offer in Y, therefore signals fairness norms, not fear of rejection. The same game produces different behavior when the responders' shared norms differ, which is the logic behind reading the fifteen-society results as culture.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Ultimatum Game rejection of low offers ↔ social-choice/bargaining theory: a credible threat of costly refusal shapes the first mover's offer.
- [structural-analogy] Randomly assigned newscasts (Box 7.5) ↔ any treatment-control design: randomization removes systematic differences between groups.
- [structural-analogy] Coordination and Democracy Game ↔ collective action: citizens must coordinate to act against a transgressing state (compare the revolutionary threshold concept).
- [metaphor] Culture as a "shared way of playing everyday games" ↔ institutions as "rules of the game".

## Questions this raises
- If the experiments suggest that people use analogies from daily life, how can one tell which everyday game a subject has in mind, rather than inventing one after the fact?
- Fifteen societies is a small sample. Could the 68 percent variance explained reflect only a few societies?
- How do offers in these games connect to actual democratic outcomes? The text offers only a hedged link ("it seems reasonable").
- If democracy itself produces a democratic culture, how can one separate cause from effect in the observational data?
