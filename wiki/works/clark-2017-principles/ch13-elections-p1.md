# Elections and Electoral Systems (part 1/3): overview and majoritarian systems

## L0 — One line
Most political scientists sort electoral systems by the formula that turns votes into seats, and the majoritarian family (where those with the most votes win) contains many systems whose trade-offs differ in proportionality, accountability, strategic voting and minority representation.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part asks how electoral rules translate votes into seats, and why systems are grouped by electoral formula into majoritarian, proportional and mixed families. It first gives an overview of democratic elections and of who may vote. It then walks through the majoritarian systems used nationally: single-member district plurality, alternative vote, Borda count, two-round systems, supplementary vote, single nontransferable vote, block vote and party block vote. Each is illustrated with real elections and weighed for proportionality, accountability, strategic voting, minority representation and effects on parties. The proportional and mixed families are announced but not covered in these pages.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and simple fractions; the difference between a plurality (most votes) and an absolute majority (more than half); the idea of rank-ordering options.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An electoral system is a set of rules; its main parts are the formula, the ballot structure and the district magnitude. Most political scientists group systems by formula, which the authors call an established convention with 'no really good answer', though they report that the formula strongly influences proportionality and government type (pp. 536, 538, 542).
- "Majoritarian" means the candidates or parties with the most votes win; it does not always mean an absolute majority. These systems tend to help the largest party (p. 543).
- Single-member district plurality (SMDP) is simple and gives clear accountability. It can elect winners on very small vote shares and give very disproportional national results (pp. 543–545).
- Compared with SMDP, the alternative vote (AV) and two-round systems give voters less incentive to vote strategically (to back a likely winner instead of a true favourite), but the authors say the incentive does not disappear entirely. AV, two-round systems and the supplementary vote (SV, a one-round system with a first and second choice) push candidates to seek second preferences or later-round support (pp. 546–553, 557–559).
- The authors, citing Gibbard and Satterthwaite, say scholars have shown that all reasonable systems give some incentive to act strategically (none is "strategy-proof"), but that SMDP creates stronger incentives than some others (p. 545).
- The Borda count (ranked ballots scored in equal steps) tends to favour candidates with broad support, but still invites strategic misranking called "compromising" and "burying" (pp. 547–548).
- Multimember majoritarian systems behave differently: the single nontransferable vote (SNTV) can improve minority representation but can weaken parties and encourage clientelism (targeted bribes and patronage). The block vote (BV) and party block vote (PBV) can be extremely disproportional (pp. 560–563).
- Outcomes such as proportionality or minority representation are not what defines a family. The authors classify SNTV as majoritarian by its mechanics (pp. 560–561).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter opening and introduction (pp. 535–536)
- The chapter overview says elections are a defining feature of democracies and the main way governments obtain authority to rule (p. 535).
- An **electoral system** is a set of laws and regulations governing electoral competition between candidates or parties or both. It includes:
  - the **electoral formula** (how votes become seats);
  - the **ballot structure** (candidates or parties or both; one vote or a series of preferences);
  - the **district magnitude** (number of representatives elected in a district);
  - administrative rules on nominations, voter registration and polling places (p. 536).
- Most political scientists sort systems into three families by formula: majoritarian, proportional, mixed. This is partly why some speak of majoritarian and proportional democracies (p. 536).
- The chapter focuses on national legislative and presidential elections (p. 536).

### Elections: an overview (pp. 536–542)
- **Role of elections.** All modern democracies hold regular elections. Dictatorships also hold them, but elections are not defining of authoritarian regimes as they are of democracies (pp. 536–537). Footnote 1 recalls that ancient Athens chose representatives by lot (p. 536).
- *Practical role:* elections let citizens select representatives, influence government formation, reward or punish politicians, and shape future policy (p. 537).
- *Symbolic role:* legitimacy comes from being chosen through a process giving citizens an equal, relatively low-cost chance to take part (p. 537).
- The authors say democracies recognize that governments' authority to rule comes solely from the consent of the governed, and that elections translate that consent into authority (p. 537).
- **Statistics.** In 1946–2000, 125 countries had at least one democratic election (p. 537).
  - Table 13.1 (decades 1950s→1990s): average number of democracies 36.5, 42.3, 42.7, 58.6, 100.7; legislative elections 111, 121, 127, 162, 281; presidential 33, 37, 35, 48, 114. Roughly one-third of elections in 1950–2000 fell in the 1990s, driven by the Soviet collapse and the return of multiparty elections in Africa (p. 537).
  - Table 13.2: totals of 867 legislative and 294 presidential elections in 1946–2000. Almost half of presidential elections were in Latin America (133 of 294); a third of legislative elections were in Western Europe (285 of 867) (pp. 537–538).
- **Rules vary.** The authors say all contemporary democracies allow for **universal suffrage** (the right to vote is not restricted by race, gender, belief or social status), yet they place differing restrictions on who can vote (p. 538). Box 13.1 softens this to "virtually all" and says the US does not quite have it because of the District of Columbia (p. 539). Systems also differ in whether voters pick candidates or parties, cast one vote or several, vote in one round or more, and elect one or many representatives per district (p. 538).
- **Box 13.1: Who can vote?** Dahl's two dimensions of democracy are contestation and inclusion. Dahl (as quoted) requires that "practically all adults" have the right to vote; the box adds, quoting Katz, that "no country allows all adults to vote" (p. 539). Examples:
  - *History:* women got unrestricted voting rights first in New Zealand (1893) and last (in the box's examples) in Liechtenstein (1984). Property qualifications lasted in most countries until the nineteenth century. Race exclusions ended in Australia in 1967, in the southern United States effectively in 1964, and in South Africa in 1993 (p. 539). Citizens of the District of Columbia lack a voting member of Congress (p. 539).
  - *Age:* 59 of 61 sampled democracies exclude under-18s. Across all countries the minimum ranges from 16 (Brazil) to 21 (Malaysia, Maldives, Pakistan, Singapore) (pp. 539–540).
  - *Mental health:* only Canada, Ireland, Italy and Sweden have no mental-health requirement (p. 540).
  - *Citizenship:* 48 of 61 democracies restrict voting to citizens; a few allow some noncitizens, for example Chile after five years' residence (p. 540).
  - *Residency:* district requirements range from one month (Australia, New Zealand) to six months (France, Mali, Philippines). Country residency ranges from three months (Germany) to seven years (Malawi, St. Lucia) (pp. 540–541).
  - *Citizens abroad:* 40 of 61 democracies let them vote; 30 of those do so indefinitely. Some require a return home to vote, so the right is "more symbolic than practical" (p. 541).
  - *Prisoners:* from no vote for any convicted prisoner (e.g. Brazil, India, UK) to all prisoners voting (Germany, Namibia, Sweden). Some places remove the vote beyond the sentence (Belgium five years; lifetime for felons in Iowa, Kentucky, Virginia unless the governor or legislature intervenes) (p. 541).
  - *Registration:* needed everywhere in democracies. It is done by government in many countries, compulsory for voters in the UK and Australia, and voluntary in the United States (p. 541).
- **Why classify by formula?** The authors admit there is "no really good answer"; it is an established convention. The closest reason: the formula strongly influences proportionality and the type of government that forms (pp. 538, 542).
  - Majoritarian systems tend to produce more disproportional results and raise the probability of single-party majority governments.
  - Proportional systems tend to produce more proportional results and lower that probability (p. 542).
- **Figure 13.1** (all systems used nationally for legislative and presidential elections in independent countries) (p. 542):
  - Majoritarian: SMDP, alternative vote, two-round system, supplementary vote, block vote, party block vote, single nontransferable vote, Borda count.
  - Proportional: list PR, single transferable vote.
  - Mixed: dependent, independent.
  - Footnote 2 notes that other systems exist, such as approval voting, used for other kinds of elections.

*Intuition note:* The three families are a filing system based on one feature, how votes become seats. The authors admit there is "no really good answer" for choosing this feature; the closest they come is that the formula strongly influences proportionality and government type. They do not claim it is the only feature that matters.

### Majoritarian electoral systems (pp. 542–563)
- A **majoritarian electoral system** is one in which the candidates or parties that receive the most votes win. The word is somewhat misleading: some systems need an absolute majority, others only a relative majority (plurality) (pp. 542–543).
- The label probably persists because these systems often give the largest party an absolute majority of seats without an absolute majority of votes (p. 543).
- Eight varieties are used nationally: SMDP, AV, BC, TRS, SNTV, BV, PBV, SV (p. 543).

#### Single-member district plurality (SMDP) (pp. 543–546)
- **Rule.** Each voter casts one vote for one candidate in a single-member district; the candidate with the most votes wins, even without a majority. It is the simplest and most common majoritarian system, used mainly in the UK and former British colonies (Belize, Canada, India, Nepal, Nigeria, Trinidad and Tobago, US) (p. 543).
- "First-past-the-post" is misleading because there is no post to pass. In theory a candidate can win with two votes if all others get one each (p. 543).
- **Example (Table 13.3, Corby, UK 2010):**

| Candidate | Party | Votes | % |
|---|---|---|---|
| Louise Bagshawe (won) | Conservative | 22,886 | 42.2 |
| Phil Hope | Labour | 20,935 | 38.6 |
| Portia Wilson | Liberal Democrat | 7,834 | 14.5 |
| Roy Davies | British National Party | 2,525 | 3.6 (as printed) |

  (pp. 543–544).
- **Advantages (as the authors present them):**
  - Simplicity: easy to explain, understand and administer, so cheap (p. 543).
  - One representative per district makes responsibility clear and accountability easy. Political scientists therefore often link SMDP with high levels of constituency service and close constituent–representative bonds (pp. 543–544).
  - For some scholars, SMDP is associated with single-party majority governments (Blais and Carty 1987). Such governments help voters identify who is responsible for national policy, and they are more stable than other forms of government (p. 544).
- **Criticism 1: unrepresentative districts.** 57.8% of Corby voters did not back the winner. In Kerowagi, Papua New Guinea (1987), the winner took just 7.9%, so 92.1% voted for someone else (p. 544).
- **Criticism 2: unrepresentative nationally.** In the 1983 UK election the Alliance (Social Democratic–Liberal) won 25.4% of votes but 3.5% of seats. It had 675,985 fewer votes than Labour (out of 30,661,309) but 186 fewer seats. The Conservatives won 61.1% of seats with 42.4% of votes (pp. 544–545).
  - Disproportionality can be lower: if parties win some districts and lose others, national results may be fairly proportional. Malawi 1994: UDF 48% of seats from 46% of votes; Malawi Congress Party 32% from 34%; Alliance for Democracy 20% from 19% (p. 545).
- **Criticism 3: strategic voting.**
  - *Sincere voting* is voting for your most preferred candidate or party. *Strategic voting* is voting for your most preferred candidate who has a realistic chance of winning (p. 545).
  - Example: a voter with LD > L > C in Corby may see the Liberal Democrat finishing third. A vote for LD is likely "wasted" (it does not affect the outcome). She may therefore vote Labour to stop the Conservative (p. 545).
  - Authors' preference: "we prefer" systems that encourage sincere expression. But they say scholars have shown that all reasonable systems create incentives for strategic action; there are no strategy-proof systems (Gibbard 1973; Satterthwaite 1975). SMDP creates stronger incentives than some others (p. 545).
- **Criticism 4: ethnic or clan parties.** Where ethnic groups or clans are regionally concentrated, SMDP can encourage ethnic or clan-based parties. The result can be regional strongholds with little competition and minorities with little say over policy or private goods. The authors cite Malawi and Kenya (pp. 545–546). They add that SMDP "probably helped" segregationist Democrats keep single-party dominance in the US South for almost a century (p. 546).

*Intuition note:* SMDP is cheap and gives a clear person to blame or reward. The price is that a plurality winner can represent a small slice of voters, and seats can diverge widely from vote shares.

#### Alternative vote (AV) (pp. 546–553)
- **Rule.** Used in single-member districts. **Preference (preferential) voting** means voters rank candidates. In AV voters rank at least one candidate:
  - "Full preferential" means all candidates must be ranked; "optional preferential" means only some (p. 546).
  - A candidate with an absolute majority of first preferences wins at once. Otherwise the candidate with the fewest first preferences is eliminated and her ballots are reallocated by their next preferences. This repeats until one candidate has an absolute majority of votes cast (full) or of valid votes remaining (optional) (p. 546).
  - AV is also called the instant-runoff vote (IRV) (p. 546).
- **Box 13.2: Borda count (BC) and modified BC** (pp. 547–548). BC is a majoritarian system with preference voting:
  - It is candidate-centred, used in single- or multimember districts. Voters number all candidates; preferences get values in equal steps and the values are summed. With ten candidates the values run 1, 0.9, 0.8, … 0.1. The candidate(s) with the highest total win (p. 547).
  - The authors give a history (a French designer, Borda, in the eighteenth century; a variant in the Roman Senate after 105 AD). Current uses: choosing presidential candidates from MPs in Kiribati, electing two ethnic-minority members in Slovenia, and Major League Baseball's Most Valuable Player (p. 547).
  - It is "often advocated" by voting theorists. It tends to favour candidates with broad support over candidates backed by a majority (the "least-unpopular"), and to elect candidates near the centre of citizen preferences (Cox 1987). Some think this encourages consensus politics in divided societies (Emerson 1998) (p. 547).
  - Strategic incentives remain. With sincere ranking A > B > C > D > E and polls showing only B and C can win, you may do two things:
    - *Compromising:* rank B first, above your sincere ordering, to stop C.
    - *Burying:* rank C last to help B (pp. 547–548).
  - *Modified BC* (Nauru since 1971): values use divisors 1, 2, 3, …, so with ten candidates the values are 1, 0.5, 0.33, 0.25 … 0.1. Voters are assumed to care more about higher ranks, which makes the system more majoritarian because candidates need more lower-order preferences to overtake a leader (p. 548).
  - Table 13.4 (Buada, Nauru 2004; two seats): the highest vote values were Roland Kun 145.324 (elected first) and Lyn Terangi Adam 131.967 (elected second); the others ranged from 84.890 to 123.243 (p. 548).
- **History.** AV began in Queensland in 1893, spread to other Australian colonies after 1901, and has elected the Australian House of Representatives since 1919. Australia uses full preferential voting (p. 549).
- **Worked example (Table 13.5, Richmond, NSW 1990):**
  - First count: Blunt 28,257 (40.9%), Newell 18,423 (26.7%), Caldicott 16,072 (23.3%), Gibbs 4,346 (6.3%), Sims 1,032, Paterson 445, Leggett 279, Baillie 187 (0.3%) (p. 550).
  - No one has a majority, so Baillie is eliminated and his 187 ballots are reallocated. Paterson's rise from 445 to 480 means 35 of those ballots ranked Paterson second (p. 549).
  - Elimination continues (Leggett next). In the sixth count: Blunt 29,778, Newell 20,238, Caldicott 18,903 (pp. 549–550).
  - Seventh count: Newell 34,664 (50.5%) beats Blunt 33,980 (49.5%). Blunt led every count until the last (pp. 549–550).
- Because reallocated preferences can decide the result, Australian parties hand out "how-to-vote" cards (Figure 13.2) (p. 549).
- **Fiji** (Figure 13.3): full-preferential AV with "default preferences" set by parties and "custom preferences" set by the voter.
  - Above the black line the voter ticks one party box and accepts that party's ranking. Below it she ranks all candidates.
  - In 2001 under 10% filled in custom preferences. In Tailevu, several parties on the ballot had no candidate in the district. Parties have an incentive to tell supporters how to rank even without a candidate, to influence the result (pp. 549, 551).
- Other users: Ireland's presidential election (full-preferential; footnote 6 notes the Irish constitution calls it single transferable vote, which the authors call technically incorrect); Papua New Guinea (AV 1964–1975, reintroduced 2003, optional-preferential top three in 2007) (p. 552).
- **Advantages:**
  - It keeps SMDP's accountability, with high constituency service expected (p. 553).
  - Voters convey more information through rankings (p. 553).
  - There is less incentive to vote strategically, since a vote for an unpopular favourite transfers instead of being wasted. Incentives do not disappear: voters may misrank to influence the order of elimination, which is one explanation for how-to-vote cards (p. 553).
  - Candidates seek second preferences, so they have incentives for broadly centrist appeals. Evidence: "preference swapping" deals between Australian major and minor parties. AV is therefore advocated for deeply divided societies (Bosnia and Herzegovina, Fiji, Papua New Guinea, South Africa; Horowitz 1991, Reilly 2001) (p. 553).
  - It addresses SMDP's problem of winners without majority support, since candidates are eliminated one at a time until one has an overall majority (p. 553).
- **Disadvantages:**
  - *Critics claim* the winner need not have a "genuine" majority: a candidate who would win every head-to-head race can be eliminated early for lack of first-place votes, so a majority may prefer someone else (p. 553).
  - It is complicated, needing literacy and numeracy from voters and costly, slow counting by authorities (p. 553).

*Intuition note:* AV simulates a series of run-offs on one ballot, so a voter's second choice can matter. Its "majority" is a majority among the candidates still standing, which is why the winner may not beat every rival one-to-one.

#### Two-round systems (TRS) (pp. 554–560)
- **Rule.** Candidates or parties are elected in round one if they reach a specified vote level, nearly always an absolute majority. Otherwise a second round, normally one or two weeks later, is held and those with the most votes win (p. 554). Footnote 7 notes some TRSs can have more than two rounds (p. 554).
- **Majority-runoff TRS.** Usually single-member, candidate-centred, one vote per voter. If nobody wins an absolute majority, the top two meet in a runoff, and the winner then has an absolute majority (barring an exact tie) (p. 554).
  - It is the most common method for electing presidents worldwide (France, Mali, most of Latin America). It is also used for legislatures in several countries (Central African Republic, Comoros, Haiti, Kyrgyzstan, Ukraine) (p. 554).
  - Rarely it is used in multimember districts: in Mali a party list with an absolute majority wins all seats; otherwise the top two lists run off, and the winner takes all district seats (p. 554).
- **Majority-plurality TRS.** Almost always single-member. If no one has a majority, all candidates above a preset threshold may enter round two, where the plurality winner is elected (p. 555).
  - *France:* a candidate needs 12.5% of the *registered electorate*, often about 17% of actual votes (footnote 8) (p. 555).
  - *Table 13.6* (Drôme 3rd district, 2007): Round 1: Mariton (Union for a Presidential Majority) 44.0%, Gregoire (Socialist) 27.4%, Pegon 7.4%, and ten others between 5.63% and 0.2%. Round 2: Mariton 52.6%, Gregoire 47.4% (p. 556).
  - Parties on the left or right often withdraw weaker eligible candidates to avoid splitting their side. In 2007 no French second round had more than two candidates (p. 555).
  - *Kiribati* uses a majority-plurality TRS in multimember districts. Voters have as many votes as seats but at most one per candidate. A candidate on over 50% of ballots wins in round one. Otherwise the top five (three-seat districts) or top four (two-seat) go to round two (p. 555).
- **Qualified-majority TRS:** a candidate can win in round one without an absolute majority. Thresholds named: Peru 1956 and 1963, first place with more than 33%; Sierra Leone, more than 55%; Argentina, first place with more than 45%, or 40% plus 10 points over the runner-up; Mongolia 2000 legislature, 25% of the district vote. Most such systems run off the top two (p. 556).
- **Advantages (vs SMDP):**
  - More choice: voters whose candidate loses in round one vote again, and may change their minds in light of new information, which preferential voting does not allow (p. 557).
  - Less incentive to vote strategically, since there is a second chance. But sincere voting in round one can backfire. In France 2002 the left split its vote among many candidates, none reached round two, and left voters faced Chirac versus Le Pen; the authors say it is arguable that strategic voting would have served them better (p. 557).
  - Round-two candidates are pushed to reach out to eliminated parties' supporters (p. 557).
  - Voters need not rank with numbers, so some have argued TRS suits countries with widespread illiteracy (Reynolds, Reilly and Ellis 2005) (p. 557).
- **Disadvantages:**
  - Two elections cost more for administrators, which led Sri Lanka to choose differently. Voters bear costs, and there is considerable drop-off in turnout between rounds (p. 557).
  - Like SMDP, TRS produces disproportional results, with some evidence that it is the most disproportional in Western democracies (p. 560).
  - According to some, it hurts minority representation. Guinier (1994) argues several US southern states adopted majority-runoff after African Americans gained the vote to reduce their chances. Laboratory elections by Morton and Rietz (2008) are cited as evidence that it hurts minority candidates (p. 560).

#### Box 13.3: Supplementary vote (SV) and Sri Lanka (pp. 558–559)
- **Rule.** SV is a candidate-centred single-member system. Voters mark one first choice and, optionally, one second choice. A candidate with an absolute majority of first preferences wins. Otherwise all but the top two are eliminated and second preferences of their voters are reallocated (p. 558).
- It resembles majority-runoff TRS but with one round, so it costs less. It is a special case of AV limited to two choices and at most two counts. Voters whose chosen candidates all finish outside the top two have wasted their votes (p. 558).
- Uses: Queensland 1892–1942 (as the "contingent vote"), Alabama 1915–1931, and current mayoral elections including London (p. 558).
- **Sri Lanka's variant:** voters mark up to three preferences for president. Sri Lanka originally used SMDP with a parliamentary system. Concerns about minority representation led to an elected president in 1978, amid a history of conflict between Sinhalese and Tamils (p. 558).
  - Goals: a meaningful role for Tamils in electing the president, and explicit majority support for the winner (p. 559).
  - A France-style majority runoff was dropped because of the "extreme costs and security issues" of two elections within two weeks during civil war (p. 559).
  - In six elections (1982–2010) the winner has had an absolute majority in the first round (if only just). Some scholars argue that the possibility of needing preference transfers made candidates attend more to minorities. Kumaratunga (1994) formed a coalition with the major Muslim party and drew Tamil-party support (p. 559).

#### Majoritarian systems in multimember districts (pp. 560–563)
- **Single nontransferable vote (SNTV)** is essentially SMDP in multimember districts: one candidate-centred vote; the top vote-getters fill the seats (p. 560).
  - *Safe threshold:* with $n$ seats, a candidate is guaranteed election with more than
$$\frac{1}{n+1}$$
of the votes, because $n$ other candidates cannot all beat her. In a four-seat district that is more than 20% (p. 560).
  - Users: Japan (to 1994), Taiwan (to 2005), and currently Afghanistan, Jordan and Vanuatu (p. 560).
  - *Advantage:* tends to give more proportional outcomes than SMDP and to improve representation of small parties and minority groups (p. 560).
  - *Classification:* some (Lijphart 1994) call SNTV "semi-proportional". The authors say this confuses outcomes with mechanics. Since the winners are those with the most votes, SNTV is majoritarian. Footnote 9 criticises IDEA's residual "other" category (pp. 560–561).
  - *Disadvantage 1: weak parties.* Candidates compete against their own party colleagues, so campaigns centre on personal reputations. In Afghanistan (2005) Karzai adopted SNTV without party labels on the ballot. Kabul had about 400 candidates for 33 seats; over two-thirds of votes were wasted; the legislature had over thirty shifting factions (p. 561). Footnote 10 says the lack of need for parties was one reason for adoption (p. 561).
  - *Disadvantage 2: clientelism.* A small safe threshold encourages targeted electoral bribes and patronage. Japan's LDP was notorious for pork and used districts of three to five seats. Afghan magnitudes run from 2 to 33, so a seat can be won with under 3% (at 33 seats, $1/34$). Some 2010 winners got a few hundred votes, votes sold for roughly $1–$18, against seats paying about $2,200 a month (pp. 561–562).
  - *Disadvantage 3: tends to favour incumbent and well-organized parties.* Parties must choose carefully how many candidates to run, and supporters must judge which candidate needs their vote, or votes split or are wasted; well-organized parties coordinate this better. In an extreme, a party with an absolute majority of votes could win no seats. Taiwan's Kuomintang assigned candidates to zones and wrote to members (p. 562).
  - *Disadvantage 4: no incentive to build broad coalitions.* Candidates can win with a small share and use extremist rhetoric; some believe this may elect extremists and destabilize the system (pp. 562–563).
- **Block vote (BV):** like SNTV but voters have as many votes as seats and can give at most one per candidate; the top vote-getters win. Used in UK two-member districts until the 1950s, and as of 2004 in Kuwait, Laos, Lebanon, Mauritius, Syria and Tonga (p. 563). Footnote 12: fewer votes than seats is the limited vote (Gibraltar). Footnote 13: BV is also called plurality-at-large (p. 563).
  - A well-known disadvantage is that BV often produces extremely disproportional outcomes, especially if voters give their votes to candidates of the same party. Mauritius 1982 and 1995: the opposition won all seats with 64% and 65% of votes (p. 563).
- **Party block vote (PBV):** voters have one vote for a party list; the top party wins all district seats (p. 563).
  - Singapore: People's Action Party won 95–98% of seats with 61–75% of votes in 1991–2006 (p. 563).
  - Djibouti: lists must include different ethnic groups by law, so voters are essentially forced to elect minority candidates they might never have chosen otherwise. The authors stress that this effect is not inherent to PBV but comes from the accompanying law (p. 563).
  - The text breaks off in the middle of the sentence "Without such a law, the PBV is, as we've noted, likely to produce…" (p. 563).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
100 voters, three candidates A, B, C, with these sincere rankings:

| Group | Size | Ranking |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 | A > B > C |
| 2 | 25 | B > C > A |
| 3 | 35 | C > B > A |

- **SMDP (sincere):** A 40, B 25, C 35. A has a plurality (not a majority) and wins.
- **AV:** nobody exceeds 50. B has the fewest (25) and is eliminated. Group 2's second choice is C, so C gets 35 + 25 = 60 and A keeps 40. C wins.
- **TRS (sincere in both rounds):** round 1 as in SMDP, so A (40) and C (35) go through. In round 2, group 2 prefers C to A, so C = 35 + 25 = 60, A = 40. C wins.
- **Strategy in SMDP:** group 2 (B > C > A) sees B is last. If its 25 voters vote C, then C = 35 + 25 = 60 and A = 40; they prevent their worst outcome, A (a "wasted vote" logic, p. 545).
- **The "genuine majority" worry (p. 553):** head-to-head, B beats A (25 + 35 = 60 vs 40) and B beats C (40 + 25 = 65 vs 35). B is preferred to each rival by a majority, yet AV eliminates B first.
- **SNTV threshold (added step):** 100 voters, 4 seats, $\frac{1}{n+1} = 20\%$. Suppose A gets 21 votes. For A to miss out, all 4 rivals would each need at least 22 votes, so at least $4 \times 22 = 88$; with A's 21 that is $109 > 100$. Impossible, so A is safe.

## Concepts
- [[electoral-system]] (new) — laws and regulations governing electoral competition, including formula, ballot structure and district magnitude (p. 536)
- [[electoral-formula]] (new) — rule translating votes into seats; used to define the three families (pp. 536, 542)
- [[district-magnitude]] (new) — number of representatives elected in a district; it affects the SNTV safe threshold (pp. 536, 560)
- [[ballot-structure]] (new) — how choices appear: candidates or parties, one vote or rankings (p. 536)
- [[universal-suffrage]] (new) — right to vote not limited by race, gender, belief or social status; still compatible with other restrictions (p. 538)
- [[majoritarian-electoral-system]] (new) — system where those with the most votes win, whether by absolute or relative majority (pp. 542–543)
- [[smdp-plurality]] (new) — one vote, single-member district, most votes wins (p. 543)
- [[alternative-vote]] (new) — ranked ballots with sequential elimination until an absolute majority (p. 546)
- [[borda-count]] (new) — ranked ballots scored in equal steps and summed; the modified version uses divisors (pp. 547–548)
- [[two-round-system]] (new) — a second round if no candidate reaches the first-round threshold; majority-runoff, majority-plurality and qualified-majority types (p. 554)
- [[supplementary-vote]] (new) — first and optional second choice; all but the top two eliminated and second preferences reallocated (p. 558)
- [[single-nontransferable-vote]] (new) — one candidate-centred vote in a multimember district (p. 560)
- [[block-vote]] (new) and [[party-block-vote]] (new) — multiple candidate votes per voter; a single list vote with winner-take-all (p. 563)
- strategic (sincere vs strategic) voting — here, strategic means voting for one's most preferred *viable* candidate (p. 545). Canon id: [[strategic-sophisticated-voting]].
- [[no-strategy-proof-system]] (new) — claim that all reasonable systems give incentives for strategic action (Gibbard; Satterthwaite) (p. 545)
- [[wasted-vote]] (new) — a vote that does not affect the outcome (p. 545)
- [[disproportionality]] (new) — gap between vote shares and seat shares; tends to be larger in majoritarian systems (pp. 542, 545)
- [[compromising-and-burying]] (new) — strategic misranking under Borda: raise a viable compromise candidate, or rank a viable rival last (pp. 547–548)
- [[clientelism]] (new) — targeted benefits or bribes to defined groups; encouraged by low safe thresholds under SNTV (p. 561)

## Questions this raises
- If no system is strategy-proof, how can we measure which systems create *weaker* incentives, and how much strategic voting actually occurs?
- Does the Borda count's centrist bias come at the cost of electing candidates no one strongly supports?
- How far do the claimed effects (accountability, ethnic outcomes, clientelism) come from the formula itself and how far from other rules such as district magnitude or ethnic-list laws, as the Djibouti case suggests?
- How do the proportional and mixed families, announced on pp. 535–542, compare on the same criteria?
