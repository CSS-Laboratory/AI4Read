# Mixed Electoral Systems, Systems Around the World, and Why Countries Choose Them

## L0 — One line
Mixed systems combine majoritarian and proportional formulas (more proportional when the list tier "compensates"), and the authors discuss several explanations of why countries choose their systems (party self-interest, general interest, colonial or historical precedent, accident); because those who won under the current rules usually decide on reform, major reform is unlikely unless they face an impending threat, though reform can happen "by accident".

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part ends Chapter 13. It asks how mixed systems work, which systems countries use, and why they pick them. Mixed systems are *independent* (the two tiers run separately) or *dependent* (the list tier compensates for district results). List PR is used by the most countries, but SMDP covers the most people. Dictatorships use majoritarian systems more often than democracies. Explanations of choice point to party self-interest, general-interest goals, colonial or historical legacies, and accident. Poland 1989 and New Zealand 1993 illustrate miscalculation and accident. No system is best; each has trade-offs, and those who win under the old rules usually control reform.

**Prerequisites:** SMDP (single-member district plurality), list PR, STV (single transferable vote), TRS (two-round system), district magnitude and proportionality (earlier in Chapter 13); the idea that parties want seats or policy.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A mixed system combines a majoritarian and a proportional formula in the same election. Several tiers are not required (p. 583).
- Independent (parallel) mixed systems run the two formulas separately. Dependent systems (MMP) use the list tier to compensate for district disproportionality. In the same vote example, the dependent system gives a much more proportional result (pp. 583–86).
- In dependent systems, a party winning more district seats than its list entitlement keeps them; this creates "overhang seats", so legislature size is not fixed (p. 586).
- By 2004, list PR was used in the most countries (65), SMDP by the most people. Regions differ in how varied their systems are (pp. 587–89).
- Dictatorships are much more likely than democracies to use majoritarian systems. One conjecture is that these are easier to manipulate (p. 589).
- Explanations of choice: party self-interest (policy- or office-seeking), general interest, colonial or historical precedent, accident (pp. 589–96).
- Poland 1989 shows strategic miscalculation: overestimated support, and apparent unawareness of STV (pp. 592–93).
- There is no perfect system. Major reform is unlikely unless the incumbent winners face an impending threat, though reform can happen "by accident" (pp. 596–97).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Mixed electoral systems (pp. 583–587)
**Definition.** A *mixed electoral system* is one in which voters elect representatives through two systems, one majoritarian and one proportional (p. 583). Many have more than one tier. The authors say multiple tiers are *not necessary*, against some claims (Shugart and Wattenberg 2001). Mixed systems also exist with one tier. What defines them is combining the two formula types in the same election (p. 583). There are two main types: independent and dependent (p. 583).

**Independent mixed systems (mixed parallel).** The majoritarian and proportional components are implemented independently (p. 583).
- Most common form: two tiers. Ukraine in 1998 and 2002 elected half of its legislators by SMDP in constituencies and half by list PR in one national district. It then switched to list PR for 2006 and was scheduled to use a similar system for 2012 (p. 583).
- Russia used a similar system until Putin switched to closed-list PR in a single national district in 2007. The aim was mainly to reduce the power of regional politicians with their own bases in the constituencies (fn. 25, p. 583).
- The balance of seats varies. Only Andorra and Ukraine have a 50-50 split. South Korea gives voters one vote for both parts. Japan and Lithuania give two votes (p. 583).
- Rarer forms: different formulas in one tier (Madagascar: SMDP in some districts, list PR in others). Rarest: different formulas in one constituency. Turkey 1987–94 had a "contingency mandate": the first seat in a constituency went to the largest party, the rest by list PR. In effect, this gave the largest party a bonus seat (p. 584).

**Worked example (Table 13.14, p. 584).** Ten seats: five SMDP, five from one national list PR district. Votes: Party A 3,000 in each of 5 districts (15,000, 60%); Party B 2,000 in each (10,000, 40%).
- A wins all five constituencies (first in each). It gets 60% of 5 list seats = 3. Total 8 seats (p. 584).
- B wins no constituencies. It gets 40% of 5 = 2 list seats. Total 2 seats (p. 584).

**Dependent mixed systems (sometimes called MMP).** The proportional formula depends on the seats or votes the majoritarian formula produced. The list tier is used to compensate for disproportionality at constituency level (p. 584).
- Examples: Mexico (300 SMDP seats; 200 list PR seats in five 40-member regional districts), Albania, Germany, New Zealand (p. 585).
- In Germany and New Zealand, voters have two votes: a candidate vote (constituency) and a party vote (higher tier). *Split-ticket voting* means giving the two votes to different parties. With one vote, the constituency vote also counts for the candidate's party (p. 585).

**Same votes, dependent rules (Table 13.15, p. 585).**
1. Each party gets seats in proportion to its national vote: A 60% → 6, B 40% → 4 (p. 585).
2. Subtract constituency seats won. A has 5, so it keeps only 1 of its 6 list seats. B has 0, so it keeps all 4 (p. 585).
3. Result: A 6 (5 + 1), B 4 (0 + 4) (pp. 585–86).

The party vote fixes how many seats a party gets; the candidate vote fixes whether they are constituency or list seats. This version is used in Germany and New Zealand (p. 586). With the same votes, the dependent result is much more proportional (6–4 versus 8–2). This is expected, because the list tier is designed to reduce majoritarian disproportionality (p. 586).

> *Intuition note.* In a dependent system, the list tier first works out each party's fair total from the national vote. The district wins then count toward that total, and the list seats fill the gap. In an independent system, the two tiers are added together without correction, so district distortions remain.

**Two complications (p. 586).**
- A candidate who wins a constituency and is also high on the list keeps the constituency seat, and her name is crossed off the list (p. 586).
- *Overhang seats* (Table 13.16, p. 587). Three parties compete for ten seats. A gets 40% (15,000 of 37,500) and wins all five constituencies. B and C get 30% each (11,250) and win none. B and C each get 3 list seats. A is entitled to 4 in total, but it already has 5. It loses all list seats and keeps 5. Total seats: 5 + 3 + 3 = 11. The extra seat is an overhang seat, so legislature size depends on the outcome (p. 586).
- New Zealand 2005: the Maori Party won 2.1% of the party vote, which entitled it to three seats. It won four constituencies, so it got four. The legislature had 121 seats instead of 120 (p. 586).

**Assessment (pp. 586–87).**
- Mixed systems try to combine good features of both families. They help produce proportional outcomes while some representatives stay linked to geographic districts (p. 586).
- Dependent systems are "likely to be more proportional" than independent systems (p. 586). Another passage says dependent systems produce outcomes "as proportional as" pure list PR, so they share many advantages and disadvantages of list PR (p. 587).
- The vast majority of new Eastern European democracies chose independent, not dependent, mixed systems, contrary to what is often assumed (p. 587).
- In independent systems, proportionality is likely affected by the percentage of list PR seats, the district magnitude of the proportional tier, and the proportional formula (M. Golder 2005) (p. 587).
- Issues specific to dependent systems: (i) two classes of legislators, one accountable to a constituency and one more beholden to the party, which can influence party cohesiveness (Reynolds, Reilly, and Ellis 2005); (ii) concern that voters do not realize that the party vote, not the candidate vote, determines seat totals (p. 587).

### Electoral systems around the world (pp. 587–591)
**Figure 13.6 (p. 588; data for 177 countries in 2004; population data 2007).** Countries per system: SMDP 41; BV 7; PBV 1; AV 3; TRS 21; SNTV 3; BC 1; list PR 65; STV 2; dependent mixed 11; independent mixed 22. Population (millions): SMDP 2,253; BV 34; PBV 0.5; AV 27; TRS 421; SNTV 38; BC 0.01; list PR 1,197; STV 4.5; dependent 332; independent 818 (p. 588).
- List PR is used by the most countries (65), SMDP second (41). Few countries use BV, PBV, AV, SNTV, BC or STV (17 together) (p. 587).
- More people live under SMDP than any other system. The authors say "fully two-thirds" of the world's population lives in countries using SMDP (2.2 billion) or list PR (1.2 billion) (p. 589).

**Table 13.17: regions (p. 590).**
- Homogeneous regions: 81% of Caribbean and North American countries use SMDP (13 of 16; only SMDP, TRS and list PR appear). 74% of Latin American countries use list PR. 70% of Western European countries do (p. 589).
- Diverse regions: Oceana, North Africa and the Middle East, Sub-Saharan Africa. The thirteen Oceana countries use seven systems, many rarely used elsewhere (p. 589).
- Majoritarian systems are largely absent in Latin America and in Western and Eastern Europe. They are more common in Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean and North America (p. 589).

**Dictatorships vs. democracies (Figure 13.7, p. 591).** Using Freedom House (Not Free, Partially Free, Free), the percentage of countries with majoritarian systems declines from the Not Free to the Free end. Dictatorships are "much more likely" to use majoritarian systems (p. 589). Little work examines system choice under dictatorships. The authors' *conjecture* is that dictatorships prefer majoritarian systems because they are easier to manipulate. Some evidence comes from Birch (2007): across 24 former Communist countries, SMDP elections were much more likely to be manipulated than list PR elections (p. 589).

### Why countries have the systems they do (pp. 589–596)
Explanations point to party self-interest, general interest, historical precedent, external pressures and idiosyncratic occurrences (Benoit 2004, 2007) (p. 589).

**Self-interest (pp. 589, 594).** Electoral systems are distribution mechanisms rewarding one party at others' expense. Parties therefore have conflicting preferences, and adoption results from a struggle between them (p. 594). Parties may be *policy-seeking* or *office-seeking*.
- Policy-seeking: Bawn (1993) explains why Germany used PR in 1949 and a mixed system in 1953 (p. 594).
- Office-seeking: Boix (1999) explains the spread of PR early in the twentieth century. **Boix argues** that ruling elites, previously elected under majoritarian rules, adopted PR when suffrage was extended to the poor and socialist parties were growing. The incentive was strongest where the conservative right was fragmented and strong socialists threatened its rule. PR was a preemptive strategy to guarantee strong representation even if divided. Similar office-seeking stories cover Hungary, Poland, Russia and Taiwan (p. 594).

**Box 13.4: Poland 1989 (pp. 592–93).** The authors present this as an instance of parties' frequent strategic miscalculations, following Kaminski (1999). In the negotiations over the 1989 electoral law (first semifree elections in the Soviet bloc), the Communist PUWP made two mistakes:
1. *It overestimated its support* (p. 592). Its polling centre (CBOS, set up after martial law in 1981) was hampered by preference falsification. About 30% of respondents refused to complete the surveys, and much opposition stayed underground. The PUWP entered talks too optimistic (p. 592).
2. *It did not adopt a proportional system* (p. 592). The 1989 elections used a majority-runoff TRS. Solidarity won all of the 35% of seats it could contest. Solidarity's roughly 70% of the vote became 100% of seats; the PUWP's roughly 25% became zero seats. Under PR the outcome would have been "very different" (p. 592).

Why no PR? The PUWP believed it had enough support to win seats under a majoritarian system. It also refused to legalize additional parties (Solidarity was to be legal only as a trade union) and so rejected any system requiring votes for parties. It believed all PR systems need parties. STV is proportional and uses votes for candidates, and the PUWP seems to have been unaware STV existed (pp. 592–93). **Kaminski indicates** STV would have been acceptable to both sides. It would have guaranteed Solidarity significant representation and given the PUWP a greater margin of safety (p. 593). The authors say it "seems likely" the PUWP might then have kept power. They raise, as a *counterfactual question*, whether communism in Eastern Europe could have been saved, noting that the collapse of communism in Poland had a snowball effect "to a large extent" on the rest of the Eastern bloc (p. 593).

Reactions Kaminski received (p. 593), each in its speaker's voice:
- **Urban** (former Communist spokesman): they did not read the surveys properly and were ignorant of electoral laws.
- **Kubiak** (former Politburo member): the "negative heroes" are the communist experts; the regime might have survived with STV.
- **Reykowski** (designer of the 1989 law): the value of the work is in showing that technical decisions may have fundamental historical importance.
- **Kiszczak's wife**: communists did not really want to keep power. She says her husband resisted a temptation to cancel the 1989 elections.

> *Intuition note.* A party can pick the wrong rule if it misjudges its own popularity or does not know the options. Here, a system that gave the opposition everything was chosen by a regime that expected to win.

**General interest (p. 594).** Some claim systems are chosen for legitimacy, fairness, ethnic accommodation, participation, accountability or sincere preference revelation.
- Sri Lanka's supplementary vote for presidents: let the Tamil minority play a meaningful role, ensure majority support, and keep administrative costs low (Reilly 2001).
- Eastern Europe: some argue leaders sought legitimacy and fairness by promoting proportionality and party development (Birch et al. 2002).
- Uncertainty about voting in new democracies (Andrews and Jackman 2005) makes it harder for parties to know their interest. Partly for this reason, **Birch (2003) claims** East European leaders often chose systems less to maximize seats than to promote general-interest goals and minimize risk (p. 594).

**External pressure and historical precedent (p. 595).**
- Colonial rulers matter (Blais and Massicotte 1997). Nearly every African country with SMDP is a former British colony. The Central African Republic, Comoros and Mali use the French Fifth Republic's TRS. Former Portuguese colonies (Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe) and Italian Somalia use PR (p. 595).
- Previous experience: Czechoslovakia chose PR in 1990, "there is some evidence", because it used a similar system in the interwar period. France's 1958 TRS can "perhaps" be traced to the Second Empire and much of the Third Republic (p. 595).

**Accident: New Zealand 1993 (pp. 595–96; Benoit 2007).** SMDP had produced a Labour–National duopoly and single-party majority governments since 1914. Discontent grew from the 1970s. In 1978 and 1981 Labour won a plurality of votes, but National won a majority of seats. Labour pledged a Royal Commission, which recommended MMP, though most Labour MPs opposed it. Politicians of both parties tried to put reform "back in the bottle". It succeeded for six years until Prime Minister Lange inadvertently promised a binding referendum in a televised debate. National exploited the broken promise, and both parties promised a referendum. In 1992, 84.7% rejected the existing system and 70.5% favoured MMP. The change came "not so much [from] a revolution from below [as from] an accident from above" (p. 596).

### Conclusion (pp. 596–597)
- Political scientists mostly classify systems into three families: majoritarian, proportional, mixed (p. 596).
- Each system has advantages and disadvantages. Some promote proportionality but weaken accountability. Others convey sincere preferences more accurately but are complex and costly. "There is no perfect electoral system" (p. 596).
- For adoption, the question shifts from "which is best?" to "which is politically feasible given the actors' preferences?", then choose the best within that set (p. 596).
- Because systems reward some at others' expense, those who won under the current rules usually decide on reform. They are unlikely to reform except in ways that secure future wins. The authors say that only when there is some impending threat to their continued electoral success, as for conservatives when suffrage extended to the working class, are these actors likely to consider major reform (p. 597).
- US example: the authors suggest reformers "not hold their breath" and ask rhetorically why either the Democratic or the Republican party would adopt a more proportional system that would hurt its chances of re-election. Reform "by accident" remains possible (p. 597).
- Fn. 26 (p. 596): Carey and Hix (2011) find there *may be* an "electoral sweet spot". PR with low magnitude (about six seats) gives proportionality similar to large-magnitude PR, without legislative fragmentation.
- The next chapter links electoral systems to the number of parties, which depends on proportionality and on which social and ethnic differences are politicized (p. 597).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ten seats: five constituency seats and a list tier. National votes: X 60%, Y 20%, Z 20%. Constituency winners: X 4, Y 1, Z 0.
- **Independent:** the list tier shares 5 list seats by vote share: X 0.6 × 5 = 3, Y 0.2 × 5 = 1, Z 0.2 × 5 = 1. Totals: X 4 + 3 = 7, Y 1 + 1 = 2, Z 0 + 1 = 1. Z has 10% of seats for 20% of votes.
- **Dependent:** total entitlement from national vote: X 6, Y 2, Z 2. List seats = entitlement − constituency seats: X 6 − 4 = 2, Y 2 − 1 = 1, Z 2 − 0 = 2. Sum of list seats = 5 ✓. Totals 6, 2, 2 are exactly proportional.
- **Overhang variant:** national votes X 20%, Y 50%, Z 30%. Constituency wins: X 3, Y 2, Z 0. Entitlements: X 2, Y 5, Z 3. X has 3 > 2, so it gets 0 list seats and keeps 3. Y gets 5 − 2 = 3 list seats, Z gets 3. Total = 3 + 5 + 3 = 11 seats, one more than 10. Same mechanism as Table 13.16.

## Questions this raises
- Why do dependent mixed systems give outcomes as proportional as list PR, and under what conditions would they not (e.g., many overhang seats)?
- How strong is the evidence that dictatorships choose majoritarian systems because they are easier to manipulate, beyond Birch's 24 countries?
- Can the self-interest and general-interest explanations be tested against each other?
- Does the "sweet spot" of about six seats (Carey and Hix 2011) hold across contexts?
