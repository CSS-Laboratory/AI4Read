# Classifying democracies and forming governments in parliamentary systems

## L0 — One line
Democracies are classed as parliamentary, presidential or semi-presidential by whether the legislature can remove the government and whether the head of state is popularly elected for a fixed term, and in parliamentary systems the type of government that forms depends on many factors, including whether the actors are office seeking or policy seeking.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do we tell democracies apart, and how do governments form in parliamentary ones? Two questions sort democracies: can the legislature remove the government without cause, and, if so, is the head of state popularly elected for a fixed term? Parliamentary governments need the implicit support of a legislative majority; in practice a few party leaders tend to bargain over who governs. Using West Germany in 1987, the authors show that purely office-seeking motives predict a least minimal winning coalition and purely policy-seeking motives a connected least minimal winning coalition. They argue that real politicians probably care about both. Cabinet posts go roughly in proportion to seats (Gamson's law). Minority and surplus-majority governments also occur.

**Prerequisites:** what a legislature, cabinet and head of state are; majority and percentage arithmetic; reading a left–right policy line. Terms used in L1–L2: a no-confidence vote forces a government to resign if it loses; a formateur is the person asked to form a government; an investiture vote tests whether a proposed government may take office.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Form of government is defined by rules.** It is the rules for who the government is, how it gets into power and how it stays there. Two yes/no questions give three types (pp. 458–459, 462).
- **Legislative responsibility is the key criterion.** If a majority can remove the government without cause, the system is parliamentary or semi-presidential. If not, it is presidential. A president's mere presence settles nothing (pp. 460–461).
- **A parliamentary government always needs an implicit legislative majority.** A no-confidence vote (a legislative vote that forces the government to resign if it loses) can end it at any time, even where there is no formal investiture vote (a vote on whether a proposed government may take office) (p. 468).
- **Formation tends to be bargaining among party leaders.** The head of state's role varies: choosing a formateur (the person asked to form the government), appointing an informateur (an adviser who examines feasible coalitions and nominates formateurs), or only swearing in the government. The largest party's leader is usually the first formateur (pp. 469–473).
- **Office-seeking and policy-seeking logics give different predictions.** In 1987 Germany: a least minimal winning coalition (no unneeded party, fewest surplus seats) with the Greens (office seeking) versus a connected one (parties next to each other on the policy line) with the FDP (policy seeking). The authors think real actors weigh both (pp. 476–480).
- **Gamson's law fits data strongly but not perfectly.** Small parties tend to get slightly more than proportional shares, which is a puzzle with proposed explanations (pp. 477–478).
- **Minority governments need an implicit majority too.** They survive only while the opposition chooses not to bring them down (p. 481).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter opening and overview (pp. 457–458)
- The epigraphs say pure parliamentarism is mutual dependence and pure presidentialism is mutual independence (Stepan and Skach), and that a cabinet is a "hyphen" or "buckle" joining legislature and executive (Bagehot) (p. 457).
- The chapter overview announces five points. (1) Democracies are classified as parliamentary, presidential or semi-presidential. (2) Parliamentary government formation is complicated and can produce several government types; which one forms depends on many factors, including whether actors are office seeking or policy seeking. (3) Presidential democracies differ: more minority governments, fewer coalition governments, more nonpartisan ministers, less proportional allocation of posts, and more parliamentary-like governments if the president is weak. (4) Semi-presidential government formation is relatively understudied, but there is evidence that it shares features of both. (5) To a large extent, the three systems can be viewed as different systems of delegation (pp. 457–458).
- This unit covers the classification and the parliamentary case. Points 3–5 are announced here but are not treated in these pages.
- **Form of government** = the rules that define who the government is, how it comes to power and how it stays in power (Cheibub 2007) (p. 458). The authors use the scheme of Cheibub, Gandhi and Vreeland (2010); other scholars use slightly different criteria (fn. 2, p. 458).

### Classifying democracies (pp. 458–462)
The **government** = the political chief executive plus the ministers who head government departments. Classification depends on the relationship between government, legislature and the president (if there is one) (p. 458). Figure 12.1 gives two questions (p. 459).

**Question 1: Is the government responsible to the elected legislature? (pp. 459–461)**
- **Legislative responsibility**: a legislative majority has the constitutional power to remove the government from office *without cause* (p. 459).
- The tool is the **vote of no confidence**: the legislature votes on whether the government stays. If a majority votes against, the government must resign (p. 459).
- Footnote 3: Germany is a slight exception. Article 81 lets a government that lost a no-confidence vote in the Bundestag keep power for six months if it still has a majority in the Bundesrat (p. 459).
- **Constructive vote of no confidence** (for example Belgium, Germany, Israel, Spain): opponents must also say who replaces the government. A success replaces one government with another in one step (pp. 459–460).
  - It tends to reduce government instability, because it is often easier to unite against a government than behind an alternative (p. 460).
  - In interwar Weimar Germany, anti-government majorities were easy to build but pro-alternative majorities were very hard, so governments were very short-lived. The postwar constitution adopted the constructive vote in response (p. 460).
- **Vote of confidence**: started by the *government*; if it fails to win a majority, it must resign (p. 460).
  - Reasons to call one: attach it to a bill, so that legislators who dislike the bill may vote for it anyway, particularly if bringing the government down means new elections and possible seat loss; unite a divided party; humiliate critics unwilling to vote the government out (p. 460).
  - These tactics can backfire if the government misjudges opponents' willingness to call its bluff (p. 460).
- Conclusion: legislatures can remove governments by passing a no-confidence vote or defeating a confidence vote. Presidential democracies are defined by the *absence* of legislative responsibility, not by the presence of a president (pp. 460–461).
- Footnote 4: legislatures in presidential democracies can sometimes remove individual members of the government, but only "for cause" (typically incapacity or crime) (p. 461).

**Question 2: Is the head of state popularly elected for a fixed term? (p. 461)**
- Among democracies with legislative responsibility: yes → semi-presidential; no → parliamentary (p. 461).
- *Popularly elected* means voters either (i) vote directly for the candidate (Benin, Mexico, South Korea) or (ii) elect an assembly whose sole role is to elect the head of state (Czech Republic, Italy, United States). The German head of state is elected by regional legislatures, so is not popularly elected (p. 461).
- *Fixed term* means serving a fixed period before reappointment and not being removable in the meantime (Irish presidents: seven years). Impeachment (removal from office by the legislature for wrongdoing) or incapacity procedures in the US and Ireland are described as extraordinary and costly (p. 461, fn. 5).
- The head of state is either a monarch or a president. A presidential head of state is neither necessary nor sufficient for telling the types apart, since presidents can exist in all three. A monarch automatically means parliamentary, because monarchs are not popularly elected and serve no fixed term (p. 461).
- Footnote 6: a title is not enough. In Kiribati, the Marshall Islands and South Africa the "presidents" can be removed by a no-confidence vote, so they are really prime ministers (p. 461).

**The three definitions (p. 462)**
- *Presidential*: the government does not depend on a legislative majority to exist.
- *Parliamentary*: the government depends on a legislative majority and the head of state is not popularly elected for a fixed term.
- *Semi-presidential*: the government depends on a legislative majority and the head of state is popularly elected for a fixed term.

*Intuition note.* The first question asks whether the legislature can remove the government without cause. The second asks whether the head of state has a fixed popular mandate. In this scheme only these two answers matter, not the title "president".

**An overview of the world (pp. 462–465)**
- Map 12.1 (2008) shows presidential democracies tending to predominate in the Americas, particularly South America. In Europe they are entirely absent except for Switzerland; the rest of Europe is a mixture of parliamentary and semi-presidential democracies. African democracies tend to be presidential or semi-presidential (p. 462). The map image was not supplied; this follows the text.
- Figure 12.2 and text: in 2008, 51 of 118 democracies (just over 43%) were parliamentary, 39 (about a third) presidential, 28 (close to a quarter, 23.7%) semi-presidential. Parliamentary democracies were 74% of democracies in 1978. Of 34 democracies in 1946 only Austria, Finland, Ireland and Iceland (11.8%) were mixed, i.e. semi-presidential (fn. 1, p. 458) (p. 462).

### Making and breaking governments: parliamentary democracies (pp. 465–482)

**The government (pp. 465–467)**
- The government is essentially a **prime minister** (political chief executive and head of government; titles vary: "chancellor" in Germany, "president" in the Marshall Islands) and a **cabinet**. The executive branch and the government are the same thing here (pp. 465–466). Table 12.2 shows the British government of May 2010 (Cameron as PM, Clegg as Deputy PM, and ministers by department) (p. 466).
- The cabinet is in many ways the equivalent of a country's board of directors (Gallagher, Laver and Mair 2006) (p. 466). Each minister heads a department (the *portfolio*) and is responsible to the cabinet for it: **ministerial responsibility** (p. 466).
- **Collective cabinet responsibility**: ministers are typically bound by it; they may air disagreement in cabinet, but once a decision is made each must defend it publicly or resign. Example: Robin Cook resigned as UK foreign minister in 2003 over the decision to go to war in Iraq (p. 467).
- Contrast: in presidential democracies cabinet members run particular policy areas and are not responsible for, or expected to influence, overall policy direction. That belongs to the president and his staff (p. 467).

**Government formation process (pp. 467–474)**
- In parliamentary democracies citizens elect only legislators. The 1987 West German result (Table 12.3) was CDU/CSU 223 seats (44.9%), SPD 186 (37.4%), FDP 46 (9.3%), Greens 42 (8.5%), total 497. The resulting government is not obvious from the table (p. 467).
- Any proposed government must have the "confidence" of the legislature to come to power and stay in power (pp. 467–468).
  - Some countries require an **investiture vote**: a formal vote on whether a proposed government can take office (Box 12.1).
  - Even without one, the government needs implicit majority support at all times, because a no-confidence vote can remove it whenever a majority wishes (p. 468).
- **Box 12.1 (investiture rules vary).** Some constitutions require an absolute majority (more than half of all members), others a plurality (more votes for than against). Germany and Spain: absolute majority in the first vote, plurality in a second. Belgium and Italy: plurality only. Abstentions may count for or against depending on the country; in Italy they count in favour. Andreotti's 1976 government had at most 258 of 630 deputies' support but took office, since all but 44 of the rest abstained (p. 468).
- **Single-party majority.** If one party holds a majority of seats, a single-party majority government typically forms. CPD data: such a party governed alone in 40 of 47 cases (85%) in eleven West European parliamentary democracies, 1945–1998 (pp. 468–469).
- No majority party is the normal situation: 219 of 266 governments (82%) formed in that sample without a majority party. Proportional representation (seats shared out in proportion to votes) helps explain why few parties win majorities. Only disproportional rules such as single-member district plurality (one seat per district, won by the candidate with most votes; e.g. the UK) regularly produce single-party majorities (p. 469).
- Nothing in the rules says who must be in the implicit majority. In practice, where party discipline is tight (as in many countries), the business of forming a government tends to be done by a small group of senior politicians (party leaders and potential ministers) bargaining. A government forms as soon as enough leaders commit their parties' support to reach a majority (p. 469).
- **Table 12.4** lists 15 possible governments for 1987, with seats, percentage and surplus seats. A majority is 249 of 497 seats, so surplus = seats − 249 (the 249 is an added step, consistent with the table). Examples:
  - SPD + FDP + Greens: 274 seats, 55.1%, +25.
  - SPD + Greens: 228 seats, −21, so 21 short.
  - CDU/CSU + FDP: 269, +20.
  - CDU/CSU + Greens: 265, +16.
  - CDU/CSU + SPD: 409, +160.
  - All four parties: 497, +248 (pp. 469–470).

**Figure 12.3: the formation process (pp. 470–474)**
- The head of state (monarch or president) presides and formally invests the government. Involvement in bargaining varies. In some countries the head of state just swears in the elites' proposal. If there is an investiture vote, the proposal must show a majority, and then the head of state appoints it. The government stays until the next election, a lost no-confidence vote or its resignation (pp. 470, 472).
- Figure 12.3 is a flow chart: government resigns → election or not → whether the head of state is active (if not, freestyle bargaining between elites until a formateur emerges with a proposed cabinet) → formateur (or informateur) → can a cabinet be formed → investiture vote if required → appointment → government remains in office until an election is due or it loses a vote, when the cycle starts again (p. 471).
- **Formateur** = person designated to form the government, often the PM designate (p. 472).
  - *Constrained heads of state.* In Greece and Bulgaria the constitution requires the leader of the largest party to be formateur first, then the second largest, and so on. The head of state has little discretion (p. 472).
  - *Discretionary heads of state.* Czech Republic, June 2006: a 100–100 seat tie between left and right blocs. President Václav Klaus (ODS, right wing) appointed his party colleague Mirek Topolánek as formateur. The first investiture vote failed (100 for, 100 against). Klaus renominated the same person with the same proposed government. In what the authors call somewhat dubious circumstances, Topolánek won the second vote 100 to 98 when two left-wing legislators surprisingly abstained. The authors say Klaus influenced formation in a starkly partisan way through his power to appoint the formateur (p. 472).
  - *Informateur.* Where partisan activity by the head of state is seen as inappropriate (typically monarchies), the head of state may only appoint an informateur: someone supposed to lack personal political ambition, who examines politically feasible coalitions and nominates formateurs. The head of state is then "at least theoretically" one step removed. Monarchs such as the Dutch Queen or Belgian King have still sometimes intervened more directly (p. 472).
- Despite this discretion, the first formateur is usually the leader of the largest legislative party (Bäck & Dumont 2008), and almost always also the PM designate. This is expected because that leader can often credibly threaten to veto other formateurs' proposals (p. 473). Fn. 9: the rare exception is Sonia Gandhi, India 2004, who declined the prime ministership (p. 473).
- *Choosing ministers.*
  - In single-party majority cabinets the PM has enormous discretion, constrained only by internal party politics. Appointments may reward loyalty, represent a faction or reflect administrative skill. Thatcher in 1979 felt compelled to appoint a cabinet where her supporters were a distinct minority. Even so she later recalled making sure that the key economic ministers would be true believers in her economic strategy (p. 473).
  - In coalition cabinets, party leaders nominate ministers for the portfolios allocated to their party. Vetoes of nominations are rare, but this does not prove full discretion, since objectionable nominees may simply never be proposed (Indriðason and Kam 2005). Leaders bargain hard first over the number of portfolios and then over who gets them (p. 473).
- If an investiture vote fails, formation starts again, with or without a new election. If it succeeds (or none is needed), the head of state appoints the cabinet (pp. 473–474).
- **Caretaker government.** After an election is called, or when a government resigns or loses a no-confidence vote, the incumbent government runs the country until a new one is ready (p. 474).
  - There is a strong convention in most countries that it makes no important policy changes. This is problematic if formation takes long, especially after a crisis (p. 474).
  - Fn. 10: Germany has no caretaker period after a constructive no-confidence vote, since the replacement is named in the motion (p. 474).

*Intuition note.* Since voters do not choose the government directly, it tends to be created by bargaining among a few party leaders. The head of state's job varies by country: it is sometimes only procedural, but where the head of state may pick the formateur it can matter, even in a starkly partisan way, as in the Czech case.

**A simple model of government formation (pp. 474–475)**
- Take Helmut Kohl (CDU/CSU leader, appointed formateur because his was the largest party) in 1987. He would not propose a government without his own party, so all options lacking the CDU/CSU are dropped (p. 474).
- The authors suggest formation may be easier if the proposed government controls a majority, so options with non-positive surplus seats are also dropped. Fn. 11: governments without an explicit majority do sometimes form (p. 474).
- Seven options remain (bold in **Table 12.5**): all four parties; CDU/CSU + SPD + Greens; CDU/CSU + SPD + FDP; CDU/CSU + FDP + Greens; CDU/CSU + SPD; CDU/CSU + FDP; CDU/CSU + Greens (p. 475).
- To choose among them we need the actors' goals. An **office-seeking** politician wants the intrinsic benefits of office (as many portfolios as possible). A **policy-seeking** politician wants portfolios only to influence policy (pp. 475–476).

**A purely office-seeking world (p. 476)**
- In a purely office-seeking world, and with only a minority of seats, Kohl can win other leaders' support only by offering them ministerial posts in exchange for legislative support (p. 476). He wants to give as few as possible, and will probably have to give more to parties with more seats.
- **Gamson's law**: cabinet portfolios are distributed among government parties in strict proportion to the seats each contributes to the government's legislative majority. The authors cite strong empirical evidence for this regularity (p. 476; Box 12.2).
- Implication 1: no parties beyond those needed for a majority → a **minimal winning coalition (MWC)**, where no member can be removed without losing the majority. Of the seven options, three are MWCs: CDU/CSU + SPD, CDU/CSU + FDP, CDU/CSU + Greens (p. 476).
- Implication 2: choose the **least MWC**, the one with the fewest surplus seats, since Kohl does not want to "buy" more seats with office than necessary. Hypothesis: if the world is purely office seeking we should observe least MWCs. For 1987 that predicts CDU/CSU + Greens (surplus 16) (p. 476).

**Box 12.2: Gamson's law (pp. 477–478)**
- Example: parties A (80 seats) and B (40 seats) form a government. A gets 80/120 = two-thirds of portfolios and B gets 40/120 = one-third (p. 477).
- Figure 12.4 plots portfolio share against share of the government's seats for 14 Western European countries, 1945–2000. There is a strong positive relationship, called "one of the most robust empirical patterns in all of political science." But it is not perfectly proportional: small parties tend to sit above the 1:1 line and large parties below it. There is some evidence that smaller parties tend to be slightly over-represented and larger ones slightly under-represented (p. 477).
- This is a puzzle because most bargaining models (Austen-Smith and Banks 1988; Baron and Ferejohn 1989) predict large parties will do better (p. 478). Two possible answers:
  1. Large parties get the more powerful portfolios (PM, finance, foreign, defence); there is some evidence (Warwick and Druckman 2001) (p. 478).
  2. Because of the no-confidence vote, large formateur parties may give small partners slightly more so they are less likely to leave and trigger a no-confidence vote later. This is a slightly smaller share for a longer time (Indriðason 2010; Penn 2009) (p. 478).
  - Support for answer 2: Buttard and Golder (2011) found in France that small parties got a greater portfolio share at the national level (no-confidence vote exists) than at the regional level (none exists) (p. 478).

*Intuition note.* Gamson's law says that your share of posts follows your share of the majority. Data suggest the pattern is strong but leans slightly towards small partners. One suggested reason is that keeping partners happy helps the government last.

**A purely policy-seeking world (pp. 478–479)**
- Kohl must now pay with policy concessions: the coalition policy lies somewhere between the partners' ideal points (each party's preferred policy position, p. 478). He is likely to concede more to parties with more seats, so large parties tend to pull policy more towards their ideal point (pp. 478–479).
- Figure 12.5 puts the 1987 parties on a left–right economic line in this order: Greens (42 seats), SPD (186), CDU/CSU (223), FDP (46) (p. 479).
- He prefers partners close in policy space: **connected coalition** = members directly next to each other in policy space. CDU/CSU + FDP is connected. CDU/CSU + Greens is not, because the SPD lies between them (p. 479).
- Five of the seven options are connected: all four; CDU/CSU + SPD + Greens; CDU/CSU + SPD + FDP; CDU/CSU + SPD; CDU/CSU + FDP (p. 479).
- Implication: choose the connected least MWC. Hypothesis: a purely policy-seeking world produces connected least MWCs. For 1987 that predicts CDU/CSU + FDP (p. 479).

**The trade-off between office and policy (pp. 479–480)**
- Kohl actually formed CDU/CSU + FDP, the policy-seeking prediction. The authors do not conclude that policy seeking dominates. They believe such questions are hard, if not impossible, to answer empirically (p. 479).
- Here the CDU/CSU gave up only slightly more office (four extra surplus seats, 20 vs 16) for a coalition policy likely much closer to its ideal point than with the Greens (pp. 479–480).
- The authors' view: politicians probably care about both office and policy and trade them off, so, they say, it probably makes little sense to classify them as purely one type (p. 480).
- The authors believe that even a pure type would have to act as if it cared about both. A policy seeker needs office to change policy. An office seeker needs to win elections, so must care about policy. The authors therefore see no way to tell the types apart by observation (p. 480).
- Their conclusion: it probably makes slightly more sense to expect connected least MWCs than just least MWCs (p. 480).

*Intuition note.* Office-seeking predicts buying the cheapest majority in seats. Policy-seeking predicts buying the cheapest majority among neighbours on the policy line. In 1987 the two logics pointed to different partners, and the actual choice matched the second. The authors say it is hard to tell from this case which motive dominates.

**Different types of government (pp. 480–482)**
- So far we assumed governments explicitly control a majority and contain no more parties than needed. In fact we also see minority and surplus-majority governments (p. 480).
- Table 12.6 and Figure 12.6 cover 266 cabinets in eleven West European parliamentary democracies, 1945–1998 (excluding caretaker and nonpartisan governments).

| Type | Number of governments | % of governments | % of time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-party majority | 40 | 15.0 | 19.8 |
| Minimal winning coalition | 73 | 27.4 | 35.8 |
| Single-party minority | 70 | 26.3 | 22.6 |
| Minority coalition | 33 | 12.4 | 8.0 |
| Surplus majority coalition | 50 | 18.8 | 13.9 |

  (pp. 481–482). The table also gives counts by country, e.g. the UK had 19 single-party majorities out of 20 cabinets, Denmark had none out of 31, and Italy had 22 surplus majorities out of 48 (p. 481).
- **Minority government** = the governing party or parties do not explicitly command a majority of seats; it can be a single party or a coalition (pp. 480–481).
  - The opposition could remove it by a no-confidence vote at any time, so it exists only while the opposition chooses not to. Hence there must be an implicit legislative majority supporting it (p. 481).
  - In some countries the implicit majority is known: "support parties" publicly promise to sustain the government without being in the cabinet, usually in exchange for being consulted on policy. Example: the Liberals supported Labour in Britain in 1977 after Labour lost its majority (p. 481).
  - The text then starts a further example from after the 2010 legislative elections; the sentence breaks off at the end of p. 481, and p. 482 holds only Figure 12.6.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A 100-seat parliament; a majority needs 51 seats. Parties: A 40, B 30, C 20, D 10. Left-to-right order: C, B, A, D. A is the largest party, so A's leader is formateur.

1. *Options containing A.* A + D = 50 seats (one short, so excluded). A + B = 70 (surplus 70 − 51 = 19). A + C = 60 (surplus 9). A + B + C = 90, A + C + D = 70, A + B + D = 80 and A + B + C + D = 100 are not minimal: dropping C (or D) leaves at least 60 seats, still a majority.
2. *MWCs:* A + B and A + C (removing either partner leaves 30 or 40 seats, no majority).
3. *Office-seeking prediction:* the least MWC is A + C (surplus 9 < 19).
4. *Policy-seeking prediction:* A + C is not connected, since B lies between them. A + B is connected (neighbours). So the connected least MWC is A + B.
5. *Gamson's law with 21 portfolios.* Under A + C, A provides 40/60, so gets 21 × 40/60 = 14 and C gets 21 × 20/60 = 7. Under A + B, A gets 21 × 40/70 = 12 and B gets 21 × 30/70 = 9.
6. *Trade-off.* A pays 10 extra surplus seats (19 − 9) and gives up 2 portfolios (14 − 12) to get a partner next to it on the policy line. This is the same kind of trade as CDU/CSU choosing the FDP over the Greens. Whether A does so depends on how much it values policy relative to office.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Constructive vote of no confidence ↔ veto players: removing the status quo needs agreement on an alternative, which tends to make governments more stable.
- [structural-analogy] Office vs policy seeking with ideal points on a line ↔ spatial model of voting: parties and coalition policies are located in a one-dimensional policy space.
- [structural-analogy] Gamson's law ↔ bargaining models in which a larger share of the needed votes brings a larger share of the spoils; the data deviate in favour of small parties.
- [structural-analogy] A government attaching confidence to a bill ↔ agenda control: the choice is reframed so that rejecting the bill costs legislators something else they care about (their seats).

## Questions this raises
- If Gamson's law holds so strongly, why do small parties get slightly more than proportional shares? Is "keeping partners from leaving" the best explanation (p. 478)?
- If office and policy seeking cannot be separated by observation (p. 480), what data would distinguish them?
- How do minority governments and surplus-majority governments fit the office/policy logic, since they break the "minimal winning" assumption (p. 480)?
- How does government formation differ when the president, not parliament, picks the government? (The chapter announces this, p. 458.)
