# The Origins of the Modern State (part 1: what a state is, failed states, and the contractarian view)

## L0 — One line
A state is an organization that rules a territory by coercion; in the contractarian view people create it to escape a Hobbesian "state of nature" (life with no state, modelled as a game in which each person's best choice leaves both worse off), but this works only if its punishments are high enough and its taxes low enough.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what a state is and where it comes from. Part 1 defines the state as a coercive territorial organization and uses Somalia to show what a failed state looks like. States and stateness vary along a continuum. It then presents the contractarian view (the state as a deal among citizens). Without a state, a simple two-person game has "steal" as a dominant strategy (best whatever the other does), so both end up worse off. A state that punishes theft hard enough (p > 1, in the chapter's numbers) fixes this. Citizens accept the state only if its tax is also small enough (t < 1). This sets up the next problem: what stops the state from preying on citizens?

**Prerequisites:** reading a 2×2 payoff table; simple inequalities; the idea of a rational player maximizing a payoff.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A state is an entity that uses coercion and the threat of force to rule a territory. Later definitions (Tilly, North) dropped Weber's "legitimate" and "monopoly" (pp. 89–91).
- Using coercion does not make a state all-powerful: all states tolerate some noncompliance, because at some point the extra cost of enforcing laws becomes too great (p. 90).
- A failed state cannot coerce or control the people in its territory. Somalia after 1991 is the illustrating case (pp. 91–97).
- Stateness (how effective a state is at controlling its territory) is a continuum, from Somalia, Chad and Sudan to Finland, Norway and Sweden (p. 98).
- The contractarian view explains the state as a solution to the structure of the state of nature, not to human moral failings (pp. 100–101).
- In the State of Nature Game (a two-person steal-or-refrain game), (Steal; Steal) is the unique Nash equilibrium (neither player gains by changing his choice alone). Both players have a dominant strategy to steal (it is best whatever the other does), yet both prefer (Refrain; Refrain) (pp. 106–108).
- A third-party enforcer (here the state, which punishes theft) with punishment p > 1, in the chapter's numbers, makes refraining the best choice whatever the other does; the outcome is (Refrain; Refrain) (pp. 109–111).
- Citizens prefer civil society only if the tax is small enough, t < 1 here. So the state works as a solution only if p > 1 and t < 1 (pp. 112–113).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter opening and overview (pp. 87–88)
- **Epigraph (Madison):** a government must first control the governed, and then be obliged to control itself (p. 87).
- The overview bullets define a state as an entity relying on coercion and the threat of force to rule in a given territory. A failed state is a state-like entity that cannot coerce or control the inhabitants of a territory (p. 87).
- **Two views of the state** (p. 87):
  - *Contractarian view:* the state helps solve disputes among citizens. But it creates a new problem. A state strong enough to stop conflict between citizens might use its power against them.
  - *Predatory view:* it focuses on conflicts of interest between citizens and state. States emerge as an unintended consequence of strategies used by lords and kings to seize and keep power. A state can be seen as an "extortion racket" that threatens citizens and then sells them protection from itself.
- Politics is the subset of behaviour involving power or influence (defined in the previous chapter). Comparative politics tends to focus on political behaviour at state level (p. 88).
- The authors will use the predatory view as a lens on the origins of the state in early modern Europe (p. 88). That part is not in these pages.

### What is a state? (pp. 88–91)
**Weber's definition:** the state is "a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory" (p. 88, quoting Weber 1918/1958). The authors split it into components:
1. **Territory.** In some sense, this separates states from nations. A nation is a group sharing an identity (language, religion, ethnicity, or shared history); it need not be tied to a place. A nation-state is a state in which a single nation predominates and state boundaries are connected to that nation (p. 88). Example: many Jews believed they belonged to a nation long before Israel was established, and many Jews in interwar Europe advocated an extraterritorial view of the Jewish nation (p. 88). Nations and states remain distinct: there are "stateless nations" (Kurds in Iraq) and "diasporic nations" (Roma) (p. 89). All later definitions keep the territory requirement (p. 89).
2. **"Legitimate".** It is hard to say what use of force is legitimate. The authors cite violence against 1960s US civil rights protesters, and Martin Luther King Jr.'s 1967 criticism of the US state (p. 89). So later scholars largely dropped legitimacy (p. 89).
3. **"Monopoly".** Non-state actors (IRA, Hamas, Islamic Jihad, al-Qaida, the Afghan mujahideen against the Soviets) can use force, and some people consider this legitimate. Whether you agree that their violence is legitimate will probably depend on which side of the conflict you are on (pp. 89–90). So later scholars have tended to shy away from "monopoly" (p. 90).

**Later definitions** (p. 90):
- **Tilly (1985, 170):** relatively centralized, differentiated organizations whose officials, more or less successfully, claim control over the chief concentrated means of violence in a population inhabiting a large contiguous territory.
- **North (1981, 21):** an organization with a comparative advantage in violence, extending over a geographic area whose boundaries are determined by its power to tax constituents.

**Points the authors draw from these** (pp. 90–91):
- These definitions drop legitimacy and monopoly but keep territory and reliance on the threat of force. Lane (1958) calls the state a "violence-producing enterprise" (p. 90).
- All states at least threaten force, democracies included. Example: few would pay taxes in full without an implicit threat of imprisonment (p. 90).
- Being coercive does not make a state all-powerful. States never perfectly monopolize force. All tolerate some noncompliance (red-light running, underage drinking), because at some point the marginal cost of enforcement is so great that the state prefers to tolerate some noncompliance (p. 90).
- States differ in how they justify coercion, what they use it for, and its effects, but all rely on it (pp. 90–91).
- **Failed states** cannot coerce or control people in their territory. Examples named for the 1990s: Afghanistan, Bosnia, DR Congo, Haiti, Liberia, Sierra Leone, Somalia, Yemen. They "failed" not by missing a policy goal but by being unable to provide the functions that define a state: in effect they "failed to exist" (p. 91).

*Intuition note:* The authors define the state by what it does (rules by force over a territory), not by whether it is liked or lawful. This lets them say that harsh dictatorships and laudable democracies alike rest on coercion, and lets "state failure" mean a loss of that basic ability.

### A brief history of a failed state: Somalia (pp. 91–98)
**The state's absence in 1993** (p. 91): an aid worker (Nest 2002) describes Baidoa in 1993. There was no army, bureaucracy, police, courts, or public services. He could enter the country with no immigration stamp. Armed gangs guarded water wells and "technicals" (4WD vehicles with mounted machine guns) cruised the streets. Since 1991 there have been at least fourteen attempts to set up a national government, none successful. As of March 2011 the internationally recognized Transitional Federal Government (TFG) controlled only a small part of Mogadishu, with African Union troops, and had no national military (pp. 91–92; Map 4.1, p. 92).

**Origins of collapse** (p. 93):
- The state failure was precipitated by a civil war in 1991, which can be traced to an on-and-off war with Ethiopia from the late 1970s into the 1980s. One might trace it further back to arbitrary colonial borders. There is still no internationally recognized Somalia–Ethiopia border, only a "provisional administrative line" (p. 93).
- Somalia was formed in 1960 by merging British Somaliland and Italian-run Somalia. Independence came on June 26 and June 30. The two legislatures met on July 1 and elected a president. Elections followed in 1964 and 1969 (p. 93).
- Charges of fraud accompanied the 1969 elections. The army and police saw the government as nepotistic and corrupt. A military coup in October 1969 created the Supreme Revolutionary Council under General Siad Barre. It banned parties, abolished the National Assembly, suspended the constitution, and set up a socialist dictatorship (p. 93).
- Siad Barre wanted a "Greater Somalia" uniting ethnic Somalis in neighbouring states. In 1977, after border incidents and an attempt to gain control of Ethiopia's Ogaden region, Somalia went to war with Ethiopia (p. 93).
- The war was a Cold War proxy conflict. Soviet military aid had given Somalia an edge in the early 1970s. Ethiopia was in turmoil; when a Marxist-Leninist-leaning government appeared, the USSR negotiated with it and Ethiopia broke with the US. The USSR found itself supplying military aid to both sides, then turned its back on Somalia and gave additional aid to Ethiopia. The war ended in 1978 with a Somali defeat and refugees fleeing to Somalia (pp. 93–94).

**Consequences of the Ogaden War** (p. 94):
- The Somali military was weakened and dissatisfaction with Siad Barre grew.
- Dissident groups gained access to weapons from international sources.
- Somalia became a US client state in exchange for US military bases.

**Collapse** (pp. 94–95):
- Dissident movements grew in the 1980s, some encouraged by Ethiopia. Siad Barre answered with brutal repression and refugee flows. Donors would not fund refugee relief given the cause of the flows.
- After the 1989 "July massacres" the US began distancing itself.
- By 1990 he was mocked as the "mayor of Mogadishu" because he dared not travel elsewhere. In January 1991 he was driven out of Mogadishu by troops led by General Aideed.
- The authors call this a significant turning point because it marked the collapse of the Somali state (p. 94).
- Aideed and Ali Mahdi Mohamed could not agree on power-sharing, and Mogadishu split into two armed camps. One source estimates 14,000 killed there; the UN estimated as many as 300,000 died and nearly a million fled abroad (pp. 94–95).

**Intervention and factions** (pp. 95–97):
- A UN humanitarian mission began in March 1992. Later that year a UN-backed coalition, largely American, entered under "Operation Restore Hope."
- Factions attacked it. In the 1993 Battle of Mogadishu, eighteen US soldiers were killed and seventy-nine injured. The US public demanded withdrawal, and the other UN forces left too (p. 95).
- Footnote 7 says the battle changed US foreign policy toward reluctance to intervene (p. 95).
- Footnote 5: in the north, Somaliland proclaimed independence in 1991. It is relatively peaceful and has held elections. Puntland has been autonomous since 1998. Neither was recognized by any foreign government as of August 2011 (p. 95).
- The Union of Islamic Courts (UIC) began as local courts set up by businessmen and religious leaders who needed a minimum of law and order to deal with thieves and to enforce contracts. The courts joined together and became a major player in the south (pp. 95–96).
- Talks in Arta (2000) elected a transitional president; other clan leaders kept fighting. The TFG's 275 MPs were sworn in only in August 2004, in Nairobi because of the danger (p. 96).
- In 2006 the US backed secular Mogadishu warlords against the UIC. The warlords lost, and by June 2006 the UIC controlled much of the south and all of Mogadishu. Order improved: schools opened, garbage was collected, and businesses started. "In many ways," the UIC had begun re-establishing a state (p. 96).
- Ethiopia invaded in December 2006 to support the TFG, with US air strikes. The UIC was driven out but the TFG and Ethiopians could not exert control. Clan conflicts returned and militias extorted motorists at roadblocks. Radical movements, above all al-Shabab, emerged from the UIC and used guerrilla war. By January 2009 they had forced Ethiopian troops out. The African Union force (AMISOM) was left to protect a 2008 UN-sponsored power-sharing deal (pp. 96–97).

**Situation as of August 2011** (p. 97):
- Al-Shabab controls much of southern and central Somalia, including parts of Mogadishu, and imposes harsh Sharia law. It has been designated a terrorist organization by several countries. In July 2010 it carried out suicide bombings in Kampala.
- The TFG regained some towns. Pirates operate from Puntland (the city of Eyl).
- Businessmen pay private security firms and make payoffs to rival factions in order to trade, though the economy has "performed surprisingly well." A severe drought is under way (the worst in over sixty years). The authors conclude that establishing a Somali state is still a long way off.

**State failure and terrorism** (pp. 97–98):
- Rotberg (2005, quoted) argues that lack of a state does not on its own make Somalia a bastion of terror. Rotberg adds that the implanting of terrorist cells and the free movement of terrorists is always easier when a weak central government emerges from collapse, because it and its leaders become more susceptible to terrorists' blandishments and intimidation. The authors note a parallel with Afghanistan under the Taliban from 1995 (p. 97).
- **Authors' point:** if such states had well-functioning central governments, the United States might be able to punish them or give them incentives to restrain terrorists. With no strong central government, sanctions are unlikely to be effective, so outsiders often have little leverage except direct intervention (pp. 97–98).

### How typical is Somalia? The continuum of stateness (p. 98; Map 4.2, p. 99)
- The 2011 Failed States Index (Fund for Peace) lists as the ten most unstable: Somalia, Chad, Sudan, DR Congo, Haiti, Zimbabwe, Afghanistan, Central African Republic, Iraq, Ivory Coast (p. 98).
- It sorts countries into Alert, Warning, Moderate, Sustainable using twelve indicators. The text lists eight: refugee movements, vengeance-seeking group grievances, uneven development along group lines, criminalization or delegitimization of the state, deteriorating public services, arbitrary rule of law and human-rights violations, a security apparatus acting as a state within a state, and intervention by other states (p. 98).
- The cut-offs between categories are "not entirely obvious," and country experts will presumably disagree. What is clear is a **continuum** of state effectiveness (p. 98).
  - Near the stateless end: Somalia, Chad, Sudan.
  - At the strong end: Finland, Norway, Sweden.
  - In between: Colombia (drug cartels), Russia (separatists, e.g. Chechnya), Turkey (Kurdish separatists). Their governments are somewhat effective but leave areas ungoverned.
- Huntington (1968, 1): the most important political distinction among countries is "not their form of government but their degree of government" (p. 98). The authors say that in many ways it is hard to disagree.

*Intuition note:* "State" is not all-or-nothing. A country can have a flag, a capital and a government and still fail to control parts of its territory. The text's point is that this degree of control varies along a continuum; it quotes Huntington's claim that degree of government matters more than form of government, and says that in many ways this is hard to disagree with.

### The contractarian view of the state (pp. 100–113)
Somalia illustrates life without a state and why the origin of the state matters (p. 100). The **contractarian view** sees the state as arising from a social contract among individuals in a state of nature, in which the state provides security in exchange for obedience (p. 100). Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau used thought experiments: what would life be like with no government? (p. 100). Footnote 10 notes that some claim these theorists thought people had really lived in such a state.

#### The state of nature (pp. 100–108)
- **Hobbes:** life in the state of nature is a "war of every man against every man," and "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" (p. 100). With rough equality, each person could gain by attacking a neighbour in a moment of vulnerability, and knows the neighbour thinks the same. Even the weakest could overcome the strongest by trickery or by allying with others. All would be better off if all refrained, but if an attack happens it is far better to be attacker than victim. Without a "common power to keep them all in awe," this is the dilemma (p. 100).
- Footnote 11: Hobbes's "state of nature" resembles the realist international relations notion of "anarchy" (no world government, a security dilemma) (p. 100).
- **Structure, not morality:** the contract theorists did not say the state of nature was bad because of any moral failing. Rousseau even held romantic notions of "noble savages." They argued that the structure of the situation makes good behaviour hard (p. 101).
- **Hobbes's own words** (p. 101): two people desire the same thing they cannot both enjoy. Someone who plants, sows or builds may expect others to come with united forces to dispossess him of the fruit of his labour, and even of life or liberty. Under these conditions there is no place for industry, because the industrious cannot be confident of keeping what they produce.
- **Two kinds of game form** (pp. 101–102):
  - *Extensive form* (the previous chapter): a game tree, players take turns.
  - *Normal (strategic) form*: a payoff table, players choose simultaneously.
- **The game** (p. 102; Figure 4.1). Players A (rows) and B (columns) each choose **Steal** or **Refrain**. Footnote 12 notes that "steal" presupposes property, which Hobbes denied could exist in the state of nature, so "dispossess" would be more accurate (p. 101).
- **Preference orderings from Hobbes** (p. 103), written (A's action; B's action), ">" meaning strictly preferred. Each player's best outcome is stealing while the other refrains, and the worst is the reverse. Both-refrain is preferred to both-steal, because war prevents productive activity and investment (p. 102).
  - A: (Steal; Refrain) > (Refrain; Refrain) > (Steal; Steal) > (Refrain; Steal)
  - B: (Refrain; Steal) > (Refrain; Refrain) > (Steal; Steal) > (Steal; Refrain)
- **Ordinal payoffs:** 4, 3, 2, 1 for most to least preferred. They give only the ranking, not how much more one outcome is liked. Any numbers with the same order (e.g. 50, 12, 1, –10) would do (p. 103 and fn. 13).

| | B: Refrain | B: Steal |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Refrain** | 3, 3 | 1, 4 |
| **A: Steal** | 4, 1 | 2, 2 |

(Figure 4.2, p. 104; A's payoff first.)

- **Definitions** (pp. 103–106):
  - A *strategy* is a complete plan of action saying what a player does in every possible circumstance (p. 103).
  - A *Nash equilibrium* is a set of strategies, one per player, such that no player has an incentive to change unilaterally given what the others do. Equivalently, each plays a *best reply*, the action with the highest payoff given what the other does (pp. 103–104).
  - A player has a *dominant strategy* if it is a best reply to all of the other player's strategies. A *dominant-strategy Nash equilibrium* occurs when both players have one (p. 106).
- **Solving it by best replies** (pp. 104–106; Figures 4.3–4.6):
  - A's best reply to B-Refrain: Steal gives 4, Refrain gives 3, so Steal. To B-Steal: Steal gives 2, Refrain gives 1, so Steal.
  - By symmetry, B's best reply to A-Refrain is Steal (4 vs 3), and to A-Steal is Steal (2 vs 1).
  - The only cell where both payoffs are best replies is (Steal; Steal). That is the unique Nash equilibrium, with payoff 2 each.
  - Both players have the dominant strategy Steal. Footnote 15: other games may have no equilibrium or several; uniqueness is not a general rule (p. 106).
- **Result:** the expected outcome is that refraining is unlikely and theft is endemic, which matches Hobbes's description (p. 107). Footnote 16 says repeated play is treated in Box 4.1 (p. 138), which is not in this unit.
- **Caveats** (p. 107): the model simplifies. In reality even the weak can sometimes fend off attacks, and attacks between equals succeed only in moments of temporary vulnerability. Still, without someone to keep actors in awe, attacks come when the opportunity arises, and people live in persistent fear.
- **Real-world resemblances** (pp. 107–108): commentators describe Somalia as a modern Hobbesian state of nature. Similar descriptions might fit Iraq during the US occupation, Darfur, south central Los Angeles and New York in the 1980s, post-Katrina New Orleans, and the world of *The Sopranos*. Fogel (2004): Hobbes's world fairly accurately describes most of human history. Life expectancy of hunter-gatherers before about 10,000 BC was estimated at no more than 25 years, and in England in 1700 only 37 (p. 108).
- **The dilemma** (p. 108): (Steal; Steal) is the second-worst outcome for both. Both would get 3 instead of 2 by refraining. Individual rationality leads to an outcome both see as inferior to an alternative.
- **Why promises are not enough** (p. 108):
  - If the other steals, your best reply is to steal too (payoff 1 → 2).
  - Even if you know the other will refrain, you gain by stealing (3 → 4).
  - So a mutual promise does not make people stop.
  - Hardin (1968): in a world like this, people swayed by moral appeals may not survive to pass such ideas on. Hardin suggests that relying on moral suasion risks eliminating moral people.

*Intuition note:* Each player reasons that, whatever the other does, stealing pays more. Both reason alike, so both steal and both lose compared with cooperation. The trouble lies in the incentives, not in anyone's character, and agreeing that cooperation is better does not by itself change the incentives.

#### Civil society and the social contract (pp. 109–113)
- **Hobbes's solution** (p. 109): create a "Sovereign" with enough force that people stand in awe of it. Hobbes says there must be a coercive power to compel performance of covenants by "the terror of some punishment greater than the benefit they expect by breach" (p. 109).
- **Definitions** (p. 109):
  - *Natural right*: universal, inherent in living beings; can exist in the state of nature.
  - *Civil right*: created by the state through laws; cannot exist in the state of nature.
  - *Social contract*: an implicit agreement among individuals in the state of nature to create and empower the state, outlining the rights and responsibilities of state and citizens.
- Individuals give up natural rights in exchange for civil rights protected by the sovereign, in effect giving up what they had to the sovereign in return for protection (p. 109).
- **Differences among theorists** (p. 109):
  - Hobbes thought life in the state of nature was bad enough that people would, and should, transfer everything to the sovereign.
  - The authors say this helps explain why many Afghans and Somalis welcomed the "law and order" of the Taliban and the UIC even if they disagreed with the ideology.
  - Rousseau and Locke were more hopeful that limited cooperation was possible. For them, how far to delegate authority to a third-party enforcer should depend on conditions.
  - All see the state as a third-party enforcer who punishes socially destructive behaviour; these punishments were to be structured so that "steal" would no longer be a dominant strategy.
- Footnote 17: the social contract lacks some defining features of ordinary contracts. Agreement may not be universal or voluntary, and there is no third-party enforcer because the sovereign does not exist until the contract is accepted (p. 109).

**The Civil Society Game** (p. 110; Figure 4.7):
- Same players and choices, plus a passive player, the state. The state punishes anyone who steals with a penalty of value $p$; the authors assume, for simplicity, that it sees every infraction and always punishes.
- Payoffs are now treated as cardinal, meaning they say exactly how much more one outcome is valued than another: an outcome worth 4 is valued four times one worth 1 (p. 110).

| | B: Refrain | B: Steal |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Refrain** | 3, 3 | 1, 4 − p |
| **A: Steal** | 4 − p, 1 | 2 − p, 2 − p |

**Solving for A** (p. 111):
- If B refrains: A refrains if $3 > 4 - p$, i.e. $p > 1$. Here $1$ is the difference between 4 and 3.
- If B steals: A refrains if $1 > 2 - p$, i.e. $p > 1$. Here $1$ is the difference between 2 and 1.
- By symmetry B refrains under the same condition. When $p > 1$ the unique Nash equilibrium is (Refrain; Refrain), payoff 3 each, and refraining is dominant for both (Figure 4.8).

*Intuition note:* The punishment must exceed the gain from stealing. Here that gain is 1 point, whichever way the other player acts. Once it does, "refrain" is best for each player whatever the other does, so the bad equilibrium disappears.

**Why a state is not automatically chosen** (p. 112):
- The authors ask why anyone would want to be sovereign. One common answer sees citizens in an exchange relationship: the sovereign polices in return for taxes (fn. 18: "taxes" may be conceived broadly, e.g. required religious attendance or dress codes).
- Part of the tax revenue will build the state's comparative advantage in violence. Because a sovereign demands tax revenue, it is not immediately obvious that citizens will leave the state of nature; much depends on the tax rate. The authors conclude that citizens will not always choose to create a state.
- **Figure 4.9** (printed on p. 113). The Civil Society Game now subtracts a tax $t$ from every payoff of each player:

| | B: Refrain | B: Steal |
|---|---|---|
| **A: Refrain** | 3 − t, 3 − t | 1 − t, 4 − p − t |
| **A: Steal** | 4 − p − t, 1 − t | 2 − p − t, 2 − p − t |

  - Because the tax applies in every cell, the expected outcome is unchanged: both refrain (assuming $p > 1$).
- **Comparison** (p. 112):
  - A citizen gets 2 in the state of nature and $3 - t$ in civil society, so she prefers civil society if $3 - t > 2$, i.e. $t < 1$ (the difference between 3 and 2).
- **Conclusion** (pp. 112–113): for the state to solve the state of nature, (a) punishment must be large enough that people prefer to refrain, and (b) the tax must not be so large that people prefer the state of nature. With these cardinal payoffs, $p > 1$ and $t < 1$ (p. 113).

**Authors' wider point** (p. 113):
- The comparison between what the state demands of citizens (taxation) and what citizens gain from civil society is "central to the very nature of politics."
- Thinkers who see the state of nature as dire will expect citizens to accept draconian responsibilities. Those who see civil society as a mere convenience will limit what the state may ask.
- Hobbes wrote at the end of long religious wars in Europe and civil war in his home country, so for him the difference between civil society and the state of nature was effectively infinite. The authors introduce this with a hedge (it is perhaps not an accident) and then say it was because he had seen such war firsthand that he held this view.
- For Hobbes almost any level of taxation looked like a good deal.
- Footnote 19: Leeson (2006) argues that taxation under Siad Barre was so heavy that many Somalis' welfare was lower with a state than now. He cites life expectancy, immunization, child mortality and access to health facilities. The authors say that, to the extent he is right, Somalis may not rush to rebuild a state (p. 113).
- The page ends mid-sentence ("You …").

*Intuition note:* For the state to be a solution, the authors say it must meet two conditions together: it must punish enough, and it must not tax so much that citizens prefer the state of nature. In this model the tax threshold depends on how bad the alternative is: the worse the state of nature, the more a state can demand. The authors say that thinkers who see the state of nature as dire expect citizens to accept heavy responsibilities.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take a different cardinal payoff set with the same ranking structure as the text's game: (Steal; Refrain) = 6 for the stealer and 0 for the victim, (Refrain; Refrain) = 4 each, (Steal; Steal) = 2 each.

*State of nature.* A's best reply to B-Refrain: Steal 6 > Refrain 4. To B-Steal: Steal 2 > Refrain 0. So Steal is dominant, and by symmetry the equilibrium is (Steal; Steal) with 2 each.

*Civil society with punishment p.* A refrains against a refrainer if $4 > 6 - p \Rightarrow p > 2$. A refrains against a stealer if $0 > 2 - p \Rightarrow p > 2$. So we need $p > 2$ (the stealing gain is 2).
- With $p = 1.5$: stealing against a refrainer gives $6 - 1.5 = 4.5 > 4$, and against a stealer $2 - 1.5 = 0.5 > 0$. Steal is still dominant, so the state fails.
- With $p = 3$: $6 - 3 = 3 < 4$ and $2 - 3 = -1 < 0$. Refrain is dominant, and the equilibrium payoff is 4 each.

*Tax t.* Citizens prefer civil society if $4 - t > 2 \Rightarrow t < 2$. With $t = 1$: $4 - 1 = 3 > 2$, so they accept the state. With $t = 2.5$: $4 - 2.5 = 1.5 < 2$, so they stay in the state of nature. Condition: $p > 2$ and $t < 2$, the analogue of the text's $p > 1$, $t < 1$.

- [[natural-vs-civil-rights]] (new) — natural rights can exist without a state; civil rights are created by state law (p. 109)
- [[normal-form-game]] (new) — a game with a payoff table and simultaneous choices (pp. 101–102)
- [[ordinal-vs-cardinal-payoffs]] (new) — ordinal payoffs give only ranking; cardinal payoffs give how much more one outcome is valued (pp. 103, 110)
- [[nash-equilibrium]] (new) — a strategy set in which no player gains by changing unilaterally; each plays a best reply (pp. 103–104)
- [[dominant-strategy]] (new) — a strategy that is a best reply to all of the other player's strategies (p. 106)
- [[third-party-enforcer]] (new) — the state as an actor that punishes violations of the social contract (p. 109)

## Canonical instances

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] State of Nature Game ↔ game theory: the text says (fn. 14, p. 103) that games with this payoff structure are more familiarly known as Prisoner's Dilemma games — same ranking 4/3/2/1.
- [structural-analogy] Punishment $p$ changing the dominant strategy ↔ collective action: selective incentives that reward or punish individuals so contributing becomes individually rational — both change payoffs to overcome free riding.
- [structural-analogy] Hobbes's state of nature ↔ international relations: anarchy and the security dilemma (fn. 11, p. 100) — the text itself draws the parallel.
- [structural-analogy] Tax threshold $t < 1$ ↔ exit option: citizens compare a state's terms with their outside option (state of nature) — loosely like the exit option in the previous chapter's exit–voice–loyalty framework.

## Questions this raises
- If the state must be strong enough to punish citizens, what limits its use of that power against them (Madison's problem; the predatory view)?
- How does repeated play change the State of Nature Game (Box 4.1, referred to on p. 107)?
- Is Leeson's claim about welfare in Somalia without a state well supported, and what are its limits?
- Does the Civil Society Game's assumption that the state sees every infraction and always punishes hold in real states, given that states tolerate noncompliance?
