# Electoral laws, federalism, and ethnic conflict

## L0 — One line
Ethnic diversity does not by itself clearly cause violence, and the effects of proportional representation (PR), the alternative vote and federalism on ethnic conflict are conditional and disputed, so institutional design involves trade-offs rather than a single fix.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Are ethnically diverse societies prone to conflict, and can electoral laws or federalism reduce it? The authors argue that ethnic conflict is rare compared with what could happen, and that diversity alone is not clearly a cause. Which institutions help is disputed. Lijphart favours PR and consociationalism (power sharing with guaranteed group representation). Horowitz favours the alternative vote (ranked-ballot instant runoff). The little systematic evidence does not clearly support PR. Federalism seems to help in some countries and fail in others. Brancati argues that the key is whether decentralization strengthens regional parties. The lesson is that institutional effects are conditional and involve trade-offs, so comparative politics cannot offer an unqualified recommendation.

**Prerequisites:** SMDP, PR and district magnitude (Ch. 13–14); federalism, incongruent and asymmetric federalism (Ch. 15); consensus vs majoritarian vision of democracy; ratios and base rates; causal diagrams with + and − signs.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Counting famous ethnic wars ignores the many "nonevents" and the question "compared with what?". Fearon and Laitin's actual-to-potential ratios show communal violence in sub-Saharan Africa is rare (pp. 789–791).
- After controlling for wealth, countries with civil wars are not more ethnically diverse than others. Diversity may have no direct effect but an indirect one, through poverty (pp. 791–792).
- Authors differ on what matters: group number, polarization, a permanently excluded minority, or interethnic civic ties. Easterly argues good institutions can help reduce diversity's costs (pp. 793–794).
- Chandra argues that conflict predictions assume fixed ethnic identities. If identities are malleable, outbidding spirals (ever more extreme ethnic parties) need not occur, but her model covers only single-member district plurality (SMDP) (pp. 794–795).
- Electoral rules interact with group size: whether groups back ethnic or broad parties depends on how thresholds compare with perceived group size (pp. 795–796).
- Lijphart (PR, consociationalism) and Horowitz (alternative vote) clash; consociationalism means power sharing through guaranteed group representation. Systematic evidence is thin, and Selway and Templeman find PR does not weaken the diversity–violence link (pp. 796–801).
- Federalism is seen as a stabilizer but also as an intensifier of ethnic conflict. Brancati makes its effect conditional on regional-party strength, which presidential elections and preferential voting may limit (pp. 801–804).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Lead-in: macroeconomic trade-offs (p. 788)
The preceding section ended with the point that macroeconomic outcomes are distributional: they help some citizens and harm others. So the literature cannot name the best institutions for macroeconomic performance. The authors only want to show that trade-offs exist and that comparative politics can help evaluate them (p. 788). They then ask whether ethnic conflict will allow "unqualified recommendations", and signal that reality is more complicated (p. 788).

### Electoral Laws, Federalism, and Ethnic Conflict — framing (p. 788)
Political scientists have long debated whether institutional choice (electoral laws, federalism) affects ethnic conflict. The debate matters for nation building (Iraq, Afghanistan) and for constitution writing after the 2011 Arab Spring (p. 788). Before asking whether an "institutional fix" exists, the authors ask a prior question: are ethnically diverse societies inclined toward conflict at all? (p. 788)

### Ethnic Diversity and Conflict (pp. 788–795)

**Rarity of conflict.** Large-scale ethnic conflict is the exception, and interethnic peace is the rule (p. 788). Listing conflicts (Rwanda, Yugoslavia, Sudan, Iraq and others) has two problems (p. 789):
1. It ignores the longer list of "nonevents" of peaceful coexistence.
2. It does not ask "Compared with what?". Saying diverse groups are conflict prone implies they are *more* prone than relations within a group or between nonethnic groups, and a list cannot show that (p. 789).

**Fearon and Laitin (1996).** They use data on 36 sub-Saharan countries, 1960–1979, from Morrison, Mitchell and Paden (1989). The data cover four forms of communal violence (p. 789):
- *Ethnic violence*: a short event in which two communal groups fight each other for a short-term goal.
- *Irredentism*: a group tries to move its allegiance to a government run by people who share its identity.
- *Rebellion*: violence to gain greater autonomy.
- *Civil war*: violence to form a new political system based on ethnic-community boundaries.

The last three are conflicts between a group and the state (p. 789). Table 16.5 compares actual incidents with potential incidents (p. 790).

| Type | Incidents | Mean/yr | Potential incidents | Potential mean/yr | Actual ÷ potential |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ethnic violence | 20 | 0.03 | 38,383 | 59 | 0.0005 |
| Irredentism | 29 | 0.04 | 18,757 | 26 | 0.0015 |
| Rebellion | 27 | 0.04 | 18,757 | 26 | 0.0014 |
| Civil war | 52 | 0.10 | 18,757 | 26 | 0.0028 |

How the potential counts are built (Table 16.5 notes, p. 790):
- For irredentism, rebellion and civil war, potential cases per year are $N-1$, where $N$ is the number of ethnic groups. This assumes one group holds power and the others can challenge it.
- For ethnic violence, the conservative estimate is the smaller of $2N$ and $N(N-1)/2$. Here $N(N-1)/2$ is the total number of pairs of groups (dyads).
- These numbers are summed over countries and years.

Reading the table:
- 36 countries over 19 years give about 680 country-years. Twenty incidents give $20/680\approx 0.03$, or three per 100 country-years (p. 789–790).
- This overstates frequency, because the typical country has more than two dozen groups and so many potential conflicts per year. The actual-to-potential ratio for ethnic violence is lower: about five per 10,000 potential incidents (p. 790).
- Violence aimed at the state is more frequent but still rare. Irredentism and rebellion occur about 1.4–1.5 times per 1,000 potential incidents (Table 16.5; the text says "about 1–5"), and civil war about 2.8 per 1,000 (pp. 790–791).
- Combining all three state-directed types gives $108/18{,}757=0.0058$, or 5.8 per 1,000 potential incidents (p. 791, n. 36).

*Intuition note:* A conflict count means little until it is divided by the number of conflicts that could have happened. When many groups could fight and few do, the notable fact is how rare conflict is.

The authors conclude that even where states are weak and colonial borders were imposed, ethnic heterogeneity "does not lead inexorably" to conflict, and that popular notions about the link should be questioned (p. 791).

**Diversity and civil war.** Fearon and Laitin (2003) study 161 countries and 127 civil wars, 1945–1999. After accounting for wealth and other variables, countries with civil wars are no more ethnically or religiously diverse than those without. What matters most are factors favouring insurgency: poverty, oil-dependent exports, political instability and rough terrain. These raise the probability of civil war by creating weak states and settings favourable to rebel recruitment (p. 791). Footnote 38: poverty, instability and rough terrain make a challenge more likely to succeed. Oil raises the value of controlling the state (p. 791).

**Possible indirect effect (Fig. 16.9).** Diversity might still raise civil-war risk indirectly. Using Fearon and Laitin's data, (a) diversity is positively associated with civil-war onset when GDP per capita is dropped from the model, and (b) diversity is negatively associated with wealth (p. 792). Taken together, these results *suggest* that diversity may have no direct effect but an indirect one: it lowers wealth, and low wealth raises civil-war risk. Figure 16.9 shows heterogeneity → poverty (+), poverty → civil war (+), and heterogeneity → civil war (?) (p. 792).

**Disputed link to growth.** Some economists deny any causal link between diversity and growth, and those who accept it disagree on the mechanism (p. 792).
- **Easterly and Levine (1997):** ethnically diverse African countries grow more slowly. They argue that governments there are often unstable and choose policies leading to low schooling, weak finance, distorted exchange markets and poor infrastructure. Groups with different preferences may also get less satisfaction from public goods, for example a school whose language, location or curriculum they cannot agree on (p. 792).
- Members of one group may not want to pay for goods that other groups consume (p. 793).
- Support: Alesina, Baqir and Easterly (1999) find that more diverse U.S. cities provide fewer public goods (p. 793).
- **Easterly (2001):** "good institutions" (less bureaucratic delay, contract enforcement, lower risk of nationalization, sound infrastructure) can reduce diversity's negative effects. The authors add: if institutions are good enough, diversity may have no effect on public goods or conflict (p. 793, n. 39).

**Polarization and permanent minorities.** Several scholars argue that distribution matters, not group number (p. 793):
- *Polarization literature* (Esteban and Ray; Montalvo and Reynal-Querol and others): civil-war risk is higher with a few large groups with opposing interests than with many small groups.
- *Chandra and Boulet (2005):* democracy is endangered by a permanently excluded minority, not by heterogeneity. A minority with no democratic route to power is more likely to break with democracy. Many evenly sized groups can stabilize democracy by allowing shifting coalitions.
- *Collier and Hoeffler (2004):* civil-war risk is highest when one group makes up 45–90 percent of the population. The authors read this as "presumably" reflecting a permanent majority and thus a potentially disgruntled permanent minority (pp. 793–794).

The electoral-law debate is closely tied to permanent minorities. One side wants guaranteed representation. The other wants minorities encouraged to form coalitions so they avoid permanent opposition (p. 794).

**Interethnic ties (Varshney 2001, 2002).** Peace is promoted by interethnic civic engagement, and its "absence or weakness opens up space for ethnic violence" (p. 794). Formal associations (business associations, sports teams, clubs, unions) work better than everyday contact (eating together, children playing). Both promote peace, but the associational form is sturdier when politicians try to polarize people. The more networks cut across ethnic lines, the harder it is for politicians to polarize voters (p. 794).

**Constructed identities (Chandra).** Most studies take identities as given. Chandra (2005) argues this is one reason why many studies find an inherent tension between diversity and democratic stability. Her model of SMDP competition lets party leaders define ethnic groups strategically. The result: there are conditions under which leaders redefine identity to attract voters across ethnic lines, so leaders do not always mobilize along ethnic lines (p. 794).

*Ethnic outbidding* (Horowitz 1985; Rabushka and Shepsle 1972) is the claimed spiral in which one ethnic party leads to a more extreme opposing one, which pushes the first to become more extreme, until nonethnic competitive politics is destroyed (p. 795). Chandra's result: outbidding need not occur if identities are not fixed. Diversity may even help stability, by increasing the dimensions along which alliances can form. A majority built on one dimension is then unlikely to be permanent, because politicians in a minority on that dimension can mobilize on another (p. 795).

Caveat: she analyses only SMDP, so more analysis is needed for her claim that some institutional contexts yield benign and others malign ethnic politics (p. 795).

*Intuition note:* If groups are not fixed, a loser on one issue can become part of a winning coalition on another. That makes permanent exclusion, the danger identified above, less likely.

### Electoral Laws and Ethnic Conflict (pp. 795–801)

**Thresholds and group size.** Ch. 14: high-magnitude PR lets cleavages enter the party system, while less permissive rules such as SMDP push entrepreneurs and voters to set differences aside (p. 795). Members of a group do an "ethnic head count" (Chandra 2004) (p. 795):
- If the group is large enough given the rules, they back a group-specific party.
- If the perceived threshold exceeds their estimate of group size, they back a broader party, either nonethnic or an ethnic party with a more inclusive definition.

Political entrepreneurs may try to shape perceptions of group size by organizing cultural, religious or sporting events. Elite appeals and voter responses are a function of the interaction between group size and electoral laws (pp. 795–796). Footnote 40 notes that the idea that actors condition behaviour on chances of success is controversial for some. An alternative view holds that people sometimes act because it is the "right thing to do" (Varshney 2004). The authors reply that this is to some extent true, but that it is hard to say ex ante when people make such sacrifices, and that suicide bombing has been explained within a rational-actor framework (Berman and Laitin 2006) (p. 796, n. 40).

**The real dispute.** Scholars agree that electoral laws shape whether many ethnically homogeneous parties or fewer broad parties form. They disagree on whether stability is best secured by taking groups as given and guaranteeing minority representation, or by assuming identities are malleable and channelling them toward regime-supporting behaviour (p. 796).

**Lijphart and consociationalism.** *Consociationalism* is power sharing through guaranteed group representation (p. 796).
- Lijphart (2004): minorities excluded from formal institutions are an acute danger. Minorities included in institutions that reflect as broad a population as possible have a stake in the system's survival. He also wants multiple checks on government to limit abuse of minority rights (p. 796).
- The authors call it a strategy for implementing the consensus vision of democracy (p. 796).
- *Netherlands:* conflicts in the late 19th and early 20th centuries produced four "pillars" (Calvinist, Catholic, Socialist, liberal). Each had its own schools, hospitals, university, newspapers, parties and representation guarantees (p. 797).
- *Lebanon:* the 1943 National Pact set the president as a Maronite Christian, the prime minister as a Sunni Muslim and the speaker as a Shia Muslim. Parliament had a six-to-five Christian-to-Muslim ratio, based on the 1932 census. The 54 Christian seats were split Maronite 30, Orthodox 11, Catholic 6, Armenian 5, other 2. The 45 Muslim seats were split Sunni 20, Shia 19, Druze 6. Ministries and many posts were also allocated by religion (p. 797).
- Lebanese *confessionalism* (consociationalism applied to religious groups) was at first thought highly successful. Demographic change, Palestinian refugees from 1948 and regional conflicts led to a civil war from 1975 to 1990. The 1989 Taif Agreement changed the ratio to fifty-fifty (p. 797).
- Chandra argues against institutionalizing groups because it tends to "impose an artificial fixity on ethnic identities" (p. 797). The authors then focus on two typical consociational choices, PR and federalism (p. 797).

**Lijphart's case for PR.** He calls the choice of a legislative electoral system the most important one facing constitution writers, says a broadly representative legislature should be the crucial consideration, and says PR is "undoubtedly the optimal way" (p. 797). He claims a scholarly consensus against majoritarian laws in deeply divided societies, because they can exclude groups indefinitely (pp. 797–798). On the other options (p. 798):
- Mixed systems yield minority representation to the extent that their PR part is compensatory. If it is not, results are "less than fully proportional". If it does override the majoritarian part, they are essentially proportional.
- Guaranteed seats (Colombia, Croatia, Pakistan) are inferior to PR. They force governments to decide which minorities deserve guarantees, while PR treats all groups the same (p. 798, n. 41: Pakistan after 1985 reserved 10 seats for four religious minorities).

His underlying assumption is that conflicts can be mitigated if all relevant parties get adequate legislative representation and PR is the best route (p. 798).

**Drawbacks of PR (Ch. 13).** PR can help small antisystem parties win seats; Weimar Germany is the dramatic example. PR can also give small parties disproportionate leverage in forming governments, as with Israeli ultra-religious parties threatening to quit (p. 798). Carey and Hix (2011) study 609 elections in 81 countries, 1945–2006. They find that low-magnitude PR (about six seats per district) gives similar proportionality as large-magnitude PR without high fragmentation, the "electoral sweet spot" (pp. 798–799).

**Horowitz and the alternative vote (AV).** Critics (Barry 1975) say group representation is neither necessary nor sufficient for peace. Horowitz (1991) argues that winning seats is not the same as minority interests receiving attention (p. 799). Why copy social divisions into the legislature without incentives for cooperation? These critics want a mechanism that encourages compromise, and many believe majoritarian laws supply it (p. 799). Horowitz's alternative is AV, an instant-runoff system (p. 799):
1. One legislator is elected per district, and voters rank candidates.
2. A candidate with an absolute majority of first preferences wins.
3. Otherwise the lowest candidate is eliminated and her ballots go to the next preferences. This repeats until someone has a majority.

Under AV, voters have little incentive to vote strategically, since a vote for a likely loser is transferred rather than wasted. Ethnic voters can rank a co-ethnic first and the least unsavoury outsider second. In diverse districts, candidates need second preferences from other groups, so winners tend to make centrist appeals across ethnic lines (p. 799). Evidence: Australian parties bargain for second preferences. Papua New Guinea and, to some extent, Fiji show cross-ethnic coalition building (p. 799).

Summary: PR copies ethnic divisions into the legislature and hopes leaders cooperate after the election. AV creates incentives to weaken or even transcend ethnicity's political salience (p. 799).

*Intuition note:* The two sides disagree on timing. PR gives groups a voice first and hopes for cooperation later. AV makes candidates seek other groups' votes during the election.

**Weak evidence.** Few studies test Lijphart against Horowitz systematically. The literature mostly offers case evidence from partisans (pp. 799–800). Some studies find PR reduces violence (F. Cohen 1997; Reynal-Querol 2002; Saideman et al. 2002), but the authors give three reasons for caution (p. 800):
1. The studies rarely compare PR democracies with majoritarian democracies directly. Many mix in dictatorships, and Reynal-Querol's comparison appears to include presidential and mixed democracies as well.
2. None tests whether PR changes how heterogeneity affects conflict. That is Lijphart's actual claim: heterogeneity leads to conflict in majoritarian democracies but less or not at all in proportional ones. (Footnote 42: Reynal-Querol is a partial exception.)
3. None distinguishes among majoritarian systems. So there is little evidence on AV, which few countries use.

**Selway and Templeman (forthcoming).** They test whether PR weakens the link between ethnic fractionalization and political violence. They find that it does not. If anything, PR appears to *worsen* violence when fractionalization is high. Parliamentary rule, compared with presidential, gives similar results. The authors say this casts "serious doubt" on the benefits of consociationalism. They expect future studies and note that recommendations will likely have to take account of interactions with other institutions (pp. 800–801).

### Federalism and Ethnic Conflict (pp. 801–804)

**Traditional view.** Incongruent and asymmetric federalism (subunit borders aligned with group boundaries, and some units holding more power; p. 801, n. 43) has been seen as appealing where policy preferences differ across geographically concentrated groups. It brings government closer to the people, increases participation and gives groups discretion over their affairs. This is thought to reduce conflict and secessionism. It lets groups protect interests (language, education, culture, security, development) regionally and shifts power to minorities, making them content to be permanent national minorities (p. 801). It is commonly accepted that federalism has stabilized democracy in Belgium, Canada, India, Spain and Switzerland (p. 801). Several scholars called for federalism in Iraq. As one example, Brancati (2004) states that federalism offers the "only viable possibility" for preventing ethnic conflict and secessionism and establishing a stable democracy there (pp. 801–802).

**Challenges.** Several studies suggest federalism may intensify conflict (p. 802):
1. It reinforces regional ethnic identities by officially recognizing groups (Hardgrave 1994; Kymlicka 1998).
2. It gives ethnic leaders resources to pressure the state: regional legislatures, police and media. Leaders in Sri Lanka and Indonesia opposed federalism for this reason, as a slippery slope.
3. It makes it easier for subnational majorities to pass discriminatory laws against regional minorities.

Cross-national evidence is "suggestive". Federalism tends to decrease outright rebellion but increases minority protest (Hechter 2000; Lustik et al. 2004). Another study finds no effect on attachment to the state (Elkins and Sides 2007) (p. 802).

To a large extent, scholars have treated the effect in black-and-white terms, as always reducing or always increasing conflict. The authors believe most would accept that decentralization helped in Belgium and India but failed in Nigeria and the former Yugoslavia. The real question is why it helps in some contexts but not others (pp. 802–803).

**Brancati (2006): regional parties.** Decentralization reduces ethnic conflict when regional parties are weak but can increase it when they are strong. Figure 16.10 shows decentralization → conflict and secessionism (−), decentralization → regional parties (+), and regional parties → conflict (+) (p. 803). The negative path works through closeness, participation and discretion. Decentralization strengthens regional parties by giving them chances to win regional elections and influence policy (Ch. 14, Box 14.4; Chhibber and Kollman) (p. 803). Strong regional parties tend to (pp. 803–804):
- reinforce regional identities, for example the Northern League in Italy, which makes northerners think of themselves as "Northern Italians" and issues identity cards for residents of what it calls the Free Republic of Padania;
- pass discriminatory laws, for example Moldova in 2004, closing schools that did not use the Cyrillic alphabet and so keeping Romanians in Transnistria from being educated in their language;
- help violent insurgents, for example Sinn Féin and Herri Batasuna with the IRA and ETA. Batasuna collected ETA's "revolutionary tax", used Basque media for ETA and stored its weapons.

**Policy implication.** If Brancati is correct, policymakers should combine incongruent and asymmetric federalism with features that make regional parties less likely to form and succeed (p. 804). Two are named:
- *Presidential elections* (Golder 2006; Hicken and Stoll): the presidency is the main prize. Parties without a viable presidential candidate are abandoned by voters and entrepreneurs, which nationalizes the party system. This works better if the president is powerful relative to others and if presidential and regional elections coincide (p. 804).
- *Electoral laws*: preferential systems such as AV should encourage broad centrist appeals and attempt to weaken or transcend regional as well as ethnic identities (Horowitz; Reilly) (p. 804).

*Intuition note:* Federalism can both calm and inflame ethnic conflict. Brancati's account says the outcome depends on a middle link, whether decentralization strengthens regional parties.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take a made-up country with $N=5$ ethnic groups, observed for 10 years, with 1 civil-war incident.
- Naive rate: $1/10 = 0.10$ incidents per country-year.
- Following Table 16.5's rule for civil war, potential incidents per year are $N-1 = 4$, since one group holds power and four can challenge it. Over 10 years, potential incidents are $4\times 10 = 40$.
- Actual ÷ potential $= 1/40 = 0.025$, which is 25 per 1,000 potential incidents.

The same single incident looks four times rarer once potential conflicts are counted. This is the "compared with what?" move (p. 789). Adding more groups raises the denominator and lowers the ratio, unless incidents rise too.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Fig. 16.9 indirect effect (diversity → poverty → civil war) ↔ causal inference: mediation, where a variable's effect passes through an intermediate variable.
- [structural-analogy] Actual ÷ potential incidents ↔ statistics: rates per exposure (base rates).
- [structural-analogy] Varshney's interethnic associations ↔ social capital: networks that constrain polarization.
- [structural-analogy] Brancati's conflicting paths in Fig. 16.10 ↔ suppressor or opposing-effects models, where two paths have opposite signs and the net effect depends on their relative strength.
- [metaphor] Ethnic outbidding as a "downward spiral" ↔ positive feedback loops in dynamical systems.

## Questions this raises
- If wealth is dropped from the model and diversity becomes positively associated with civil war, how can one tell mediation through poverty from omitted-variable confusion?
- How would a test of Lijphart's claim look (PR changing the effect of heterogeneity on conflict), given that few countries use AV?
- Does the choice among institutions depend on whether identities are fixed or constructed, and who decides which they are?
- Does Brancati's regional-party mechanism hold outside her cases, and can presidential timing really offset it?
