# The Economic Determinants of Democracy and Dictatorship (part 2/2): the mobile-asset variant of modernization theory

## L0 — One line
Rulers are expected to accept limits on their power, and so democracy can emerge and survive, when they depend on elites whose mobile assets give them a credible exit threat. Income appears to raise the chance of democracy, while oil and other fixed assets (assets that cannot easily be turned into cash) appear to lower it.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Part 2 of Chapter 6 asks why income goes with democracy and why oil-rich countries tend to stay dictatorships. The authors present a variant of modernization theory: rulers are expected to accept limits on their power when they depend on elites whose assets are mobile. A small Exit, Voice, and Loyalty game, applied to England and France, formalises this. The argument is extended to the resource curse (resource wealth linked to dictatorship), foreign aid, inequality, and dictators' economic performance. Probit regressions on 1946–1990 data suggest that income raises both democratic transition and survival, while oil significantly lowers only transition. This matters because, if regime change needs structural change, democratization is likely to be slow.

**Prerequisites:** game trees and backward induction (from Chapters 3–4); the idea of a regression coefficient and its standard error; the "classic modernization theory" and "survival story" from part 1 of the chapter.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The evidence favours classic modernization theory over the survival story (the rival view that income does not affect transitions to democracy): more income appears to raise both the chance that democracy emerges and the chance that it survives (p. 184).
- The variant of modernization theory says that structural change, not income itself, matters. Rulers who depend on elites with mobile (liquid) assets must bargain with them and are expected to accept limits on their power (pp. 185, 195).
- A credible commitment problem arises when a promiser may want to renege later and holds the power. Contracts and repeated play often fail in politics. Institutions that shift power to the beneficiary can solve it (pp. 187–189).
- In the Exit, Voice, and Loyalty game (elites can keep investing, petition for limits, or withdraw assets), a dependent Crown (one that needs the elites' money) facing elites with a credible exit threat accepts limits. If the elites have no exit threat, it rejects them (pp. 192–195).
- Fixed assets that produce quasi-rents (large gaps between an asset's best and second-best use) are unlikely to give a credible exit threat. This helps explain the resource curse and rich dictatorships such as Saudi Arabia (pp. 196–197).
- The same logic suggests why foreign aid can hinder democracy, why inequality need not harm it, and why dictatorships vary more in growth than democracies (pp. 200–203).
- Probit estimates: higher income goes with more transitions to democracy and more survival; growth goes with fewer transitions but more survival; oil goes with fewer transitions, with no significant effect on survival (pp. 205–207).
- The authors say that if structural economic change is a prerequisite for democratization, change is likely to come slowly. All else equal, oil-rich Arab countries are expected to be less likely to democratize (pp. 207–208).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of "Evidence" section: summary of part 1 (p. 184)
- The authors say the observed world looks more like the one in modernization theory than the one in the survival story. Additional income appears to increase both the likelihood that democracy emerges and the likelihood that it survives (p. 184).
- They promise to show later that this holds even when other factors are taken into account (p. 184).
- Table 6.2 summarises the results (p. 184):
  - 1. Democracy is more common in rich countries: YES (supported).
  - 2. Transitions to dictatorship become less likely as income rises: YES.
  - 3a. Transitions to democracy become more likely as income rises (modernization theory): YES. 3b. Transitions to democracy are unaffected by income (survival story): NO.
  - 4a. Regime transitions may or may not become less likely as countries get richer (modernization theory): YES. 4b. Regime transitions become less likely as countries get richer (survival story): NO.
- Footnote 3: many empirical studies support classic modernization theory. The authors say Przeworski et al.'s claim that income does not raise the probability of transitions is contradicted by results from their own fully specified model (p. 184).

> **Intuition note.** Rich countries are democratic partly because democracies last longer there. But the data also suggest that income helps dictatorships turn into democracies, which the survival story denies.

### A variant of modernization theory (pp. 184–185)
- **Criticism of the classic version:** it lacks a strong causal mechanism and relies on a correlation between income and democracy (Acemoglu and Robinson 2006; Rueschemeyer et al. 1992) (p. 184).
- **The variant:** it is not income per se but the changes in socioeconomic structure accompanying development that encourage democracy. It adds a predatory view of the state, in which rulers may take assets, to explain why some rulers limit their extraction (democracy) and others do not (dictatorship) (p. 185).
- The authors claim the variant also explains why resource-abundant countries tend to be dictatorships. It offers explanations for why foreign aid may harm democratization, why inequality need not harm democracy, and why dictatorships' economic performance is more heterogeneous (p. 185).

### Economic development, natural resources, and democracy (pp. 185–199)

**Sector change (pp. 185–186).**
- Modernization theorists see all societies passing through similar stages. A key structural change is the relative size of the traditional (agricultural) and modern (manufacturing and services) sectors (p. 185).
- Early on, agriculture is large. As agriculture becomes more efficient, resources move to manufacturing and services, which grow relative to agriculture (p. 185).
- Footnote 4: the "maturation" metaphor was understandably offensive to many in the developing world (p. 185).
- Many scholars argue this happened in early modern Europe. Fewer peasants were needed, and feudal bonds broke. People moved to towns, and in England the gentry shifted from grain to sheep for the wool trade (Moore 1966) (pp. 185–186).

**Bates and Lien (1985) on England (p. 186).**
- By the seventeenth century, economic power had shifted from traditional agricultural elites to wool producers, merchants and financiers. Their assets were harder for the state to count and tax (p. 186).
- Fields cannot be hidden from tax collectors, but sheep can be moved and profits hidden. So the Crown, needing money at home and for wars, could no longer rely on predation and had to negotiate (p. 186).
- The growth of towns and the wool trade increased the number of economic decision makers whose actions determined the Crown's revenue. It was "perhaps natural" for them to strengthen Parliament, which aggregated their interests (p. 186).
- The authors say greater mobility of assets can be thought of as equivalent to a higher value of the elites' "exit option" (p. 186).

**North and Weingast (1989) on credible commitment (pp. 186–187).**
- The king needs temporary extra funds (for example, for a foreign war). Because the new gentry can hide assets, he is likely to have to borrow and promise to repay with interest (p. 186).
- The king controls violence, so lenders fear default, and no authority can force repayment (pp. 186–187).
- **Sovereign debt** is debt accrued by the government. It creates a **credible commitment problem**: (a) a promiser may later want to renege, and (b) power is held by the promiser, not the beneficiary (p. 187).
- Lenders know they cannot force repayment, so they are unwilling to lend. One solution is to strengthen Parliament against the king, so that reneging brings punishment from Parliament. Many scholars use this to explain the reforms of the Glorious Revolution of 1688 (p. 187).

**Box 6.2: credible commitment problems (pp. 188–189).**
- At least two features, which the box says always occur: a temporal dimension (what you promise today may not be in your interest later, hence "time inconsistency") and power held by the promiser, not the beneficiary (p. 188).
- Non-political example: an employer promises to pay at month's end and may not want to pay after the work is done (p. 188).
- Three ways to deal with the problem (Acemoglu and Robinson 2006, 134) (p. 188):
  1. **Enforceable contracts.** A court can force the employer to pay. This is weaker in politics, because the enforcer (the king or state) may be one of the actors. The king would have had to enforce his own debt (p. 188).
  2. **Repeated interactions.** Reputation concerns can deter reneging, but only if the promiser cares enough about the future. A king under financial stress in wartime may discount the future so steeply that he cares little about reputation, so his promise is not particularly credible. The authors add that we mainly care about reputation when we are fairly sure we have a future (p. 189).
  3. **Institutions that alter the distribution of power.** An example is a trade union that can strike against an employer who reneges. The English king did this by transferring power to Parliament, which could realistically punish him (p. 189).

**Three political examples (pp. 190–191).** The authors say the difficulty of solving credible commitment problems in politics "should not be underestimated" (p. 190).
- *Iraq after 2003:* a Sunni militia that hands in weapons has no guarantee the Shia-dominated government will not repress it. Promises of inclusion and oil-revenue sharing are not credible (p. 190).
- *Northern Ireland:* the IRA fears losing leverage if it disarms first. Attempts to solve the problem have used small incremental steps to build reputation. The IRA allowed an Independent Monitoring Commission to verify that weapons were put beyond use, and the British government removed army posts or made reforms to the police. The authors say these steps help explain the slow progress towards peace (p. 190).
- *South Africa:* whites might accept democracy, but an ANC promise not to redistribute too much is not credible once the ANC has a large majority. The authors say this problem may well help explain why apartheid lasted so long, and that it may have prolonged apartheid even if both sides preferred low-redistribution democracy. They report one answer (Wood 2000): globalization in the 1980s and early 1990s made whites' assets more mobile. Whites could now remove assets if the ANC reneged, and the economy depended on those assets. This made the ANC's promise credible and helps explain the timing of the 1994 transition (pp. 190–191).

> **Intuition note.** A promise is more believable if the promiser would still want to keep it later, or if someone can punish a broken promise. Mobile assets turn elites into people who can punish by leaving.

**The Exit, Voice, and Loyalty (EVL) game for England (pp. 187, 191–193).**
- *Setup* (p. 187): the Crown has confiscated some elite assets. The Parliamentarians (a segment of the elite) have three options:
  - exit: shield remaining assets and take them out of production, so the economy is likely to stagnate;
  - voice: petition the Crown for limits on future predation (for example, a Parliament veto on tax rises or an independent judiciary) in exchange for continued investment;
  - loyalty: keep investing as before.
- If they use voice, the Crown can accept or reject the limits. After a rejection, they choose between investing (loyalty) and withdrawing assets (exit) (p. 191).
- Outcomes (Figure 6.8, p. 192): O1 and O5 are unlimited government with a stagnant economy. O2 and O4 are unlimited government with a growing economy. O3 is limited government with a growing economy.
- Payoffs are the same as in Chapter 3's EVL game (p. 191). Parliamentarians' payoff is listed first, Crown's second (Figure 6.9, p. 193):

| Terminal outcome | Payoffs (P, C) |
|---|---|
| Demand limits → Crown accepts | $(1-c,\ L)$ |
| Pay taxes/produce (first move) | $(0,\ 1+L)$ |
| Disinvest (first move) | $(E,\ 1)$ |
| Reject → pay taxes/produce | $(0-c,\ 1+L)$ |
| Reject → disinvest | $(E-c,\ 1)$ |

- Meanings (p. 193): $E$ = Parliamentarians' exit payoff; $1$ = benefit the Crown takes from them; $L$ = Crown's value from loyal Parliamentarians who do not exit; $c>0$ = cost of using voice. Assumed throughout: $E<1-c$. Dependent Crown: $L>1$ (p. 192).
- **Credible exit** ($E>0$) means mobile assets: hiding them is worth more than paying taxes in a confiscatory environment (p. 192).
- **Case 1 (Figure 6.9): dependent Crown, $E>0$.** The subgame perfect equilibrium (the solution found by backward induction, as Problem 3 asks; the text does not define the term on these pages) is (Demand limits, Disinvest; Accept limits). The outcome is limited government with a growing economy (pp. 192–193).
  - The Crown accepts because it needs the money and knows that, if it rejects, the Parliamentarians will disinvest. Knowing the petition works, they demand limits. This is the authors' account of England (p. 192).
  - (added step) After a rejection, $E-c>0-c$, so P disinvests and C gets $1<L$, so C accepts. At the first move, $1-c>E$ (the assumption $E<1-c$) and $1-c>0$ (since $E>0$ and $E<1-c$), so P demands limits.
- **Case 2 (Figure 6.10): dependent Crown, $E<0$.** The equilibrium is (Pay taxes, Pay taxes; Reject limits). The outcome is unlimited government with a growing economy (p. 193).
  - The Crown rejects because the Parliamentarians will keep investing anyway. Knowing the petition would be ignored, they simply pay and produce at the start (p. 193).
  - Bates and Lien's account of France: less modernized agriculture, a traditional oligarchy with quasi-feudal production that was easy to observe and tax, so no credible exit ($E<0$). The Crown was as dependent as in England (pp. 192–193).
  - The Estates General did not meet between 1614 and May 1789, and French elites had to work through court politics (pp. 193–194).
- **Autonomous Crown ($L<1$)** (the authors describe the results and leave the solving to the reader) (p. 194):
  - Without credible exit, the Parliamentarians keep investing and paying, since the Crown ignores petitions. The economy grows (p. 194).
  - With credible exit, they exit, since petitions are pointless. The economy stagnates (pp. 194–195).

**Table 6.3: summary of outcomes (p. 195).**

| | Crown autonomous ($L<1$) | Crown dependent ($L>1$) |
|---|---|---|
| Credible exit ($E>0$, mobile assets) | Poor dictatorship (unlimited, stagnant) | Rich democracy (limited, growing) |
| No credible exit ($E<0$, fixed assets) | Rich dictatorship (unlimited, growing) | Rich dictatorship (unlimited, growing) |

- The authors say democracy (limited government) is expected to emerge and survive "only when" the state depends on economic elites with credible exit options. This was the situation in the Glorious Revolution (p. 195).
- General statement: representative government is more likely to emerge and survive when rulers depend on a segment of society with a relatively large number of people holding liquid, mobile assets. The authors link this to Moore's phrase "No bourgeois, no democracy" (p. 195).
- Link to Hobbes and Locke: Locke worried that a state strong enough to end the state of nature could prey on citizens. The authors say the model illustrates some conditions under which a state will voluntarily limit itself: when it depends on segments of society with mobile assets (pp. 195–196).

> **Intuition note.** In this model, a ruler restrains himself only if he needs the money and the payers can walk away. Need without exit leaves the payers at the ruler's mercy. Exit without need means the ruler can let them leave.

**Quasi-rents, fixed and liquid assets (p. 196).**
- A **quasi-rent** is the difference between an asset's value in its best use and its short-run opportunity cost, that is, its second-best use. A copper mine's second-best use might be as a swimming pool, so it earns large quasi-rents (p. 196).
- A **fixed asset** cannot easily be converted into cash. A **liquid asset** (cash, bank deposits) can easily be turned into other assets (p. 196).
- Owners of fixed assets (oil wells, copper mines) are unlikely to have credible exit options. Owners of liquid assets do (p. 196).
- According to the EVL analysis, the state tends to attend to liquid asset holders and be unresponsive to fixed asset holders, even if it depends equally on both. So states that depend on liquid asset holders are more likely to accept limits on predation (p. 196).

**Empirical support (p. 197).**
- Democracy is less likely to emerge and survive where oil is central (Barro 1999; Ross 2001). Scholars speak of the **rentier state** (revenue mainly from rents on natural resources sold to outside clients). It is also less common and stable with other primary resources (minerals, diamonds, copper) or where large landowners dominate (p. 197).
- This is the **resource curse**: resource-rich countries tend to have poor governance, low development, civil war and dictatorship (p. 197).
- Rich resource-based dictatorships (Saudi Arabia, Oman) contradict classic modernization theory's claim that income produces democracy. They are compatible with the variant, since they rely on fixed assets (p. 197).
- Footnote 7 notes that in Figure 6.7 the probability of democratic transition declines slightly above about $7,000 GDP per capita. The authors say the variant explains this as the effect of resource-rich dictatorships (p. 197).
- Human capital is the "quintessential liquid asset." Bates (1991) and Rogowski (1998) argue that when it drives growth, states must bargain, making democracy "almost unavoidable." If globalization raises capital mobility and reliance on human capital, the EVL framework suggests it should aid democratization. Huntington's first and third waves coincide with periods of increased globalization, which the authors call "suggestive evidence" (p. 197).
- Footnote 8: Haber, Razo, and Maurer (2003) argue that more sophisticated production technology makes states less predatory. The authors read this as dependence on actors with high human capital and credible exit threats (p. 197).

**Box 6.3: natural resources and dictatorships (pp. 198–199).** Besides the fixed-asset argument, Ross (2012) gives at least three further reasons why resources help dictators stay in power (p. 198):
1. *Revenue without taxation.* Dictators can buy off citizens without high taxes. There is often a close link between taxation and representation. Ross (2011) notes that oil-funded leaders answer calls for accountability with handouts or tax cuts, and this usually works. After the 2011 Arab Spring, Algeria, Saudi Arabia and Kuwait announced such measures (p. 198).
2. *Low-information environment.* Control of national companies lets dictators hide finances. Ross (2011) notes that citizens are satisfied with low taxes and seemingly generous benefits only when they do not realize how much is lost to theft, corruption and incompetence. A 2010 survey found that oil-rich Algeria and Saudi Arabia disclose almost nothing, while oil-poor Egypt, Jordan and Morocco release some information (p. 198).
3. *Rewarding the military.* Resource revenue buys the armed forces' loyalty. Some of the biggest oil producers (Oman, Saudi Arabia, the UAE) are also among the biggest military spenders, and when citizens of Oman and Saudi Arabia protested in 2011, their armies proved relatively willing and able to suppress the protests (p. 199).
- *Field experiment (Paler 2011, Indonesia)* on mechanisms 1 and 2. 1,863 randomly sampled adults in 93 villages around oil-rich Blora were randomly assigned to four groups: primed that the district budget comes mainly from windfall resource revenue or from taxes, and given spending-performance information or not (pp. 198–199).
  - Citizens were more motivated to monitor and punish incumbents when revenue came from taxes. The main reason was a greater sense of ownership of government funds (p. 199).
  - More information encouraged closer monitoring. There was no conditional relationship: the effect of information was no different in the windfall group than in the tax group (p. 199).
- *Libya versus Egypt* on mechanism 3. Qaddafi used oil money to hire mercenaries. It is widely believed that without NATO intervention they and the army would have defeated the rebels (p. 199).
  - Egypt's per capita oil and gas revenue was considerably lower. Mubarak kept military loyalty through US aid, an average of about $2bn a year since 1979, much of it military aid, and through military control of parts of the economy. Estimates of the share range from 5% to 45% (p. 199).
  - The authors conclude that the military, with US aid under threat and with the protesters seen as "customers" as much as protesters, was reluctant to fire on the crowd and distanced itself from Mubarak (p. 199).
- Summing up: the variant explains both the link between development and democracy and the link between resource abundance and dictatorship (p. 200).

### Foreign aid, inequality, and economic performance (pp. 200–203)

**Foreign aid (pp. 200–201).**
- Democracy is unlikely when the state is autonomous (Table 6.3). So anything that reduces a state's dependence on its citizens harms democracy's prospects. Aid arguably does this, and may also reduce the incentive for good economic performance (p. 200).
- Implication: aid to dictatorships, at least when it reduces their dependence on citizens, may inhibit democracy and prolong citizens' suffering. Many studies are consistent with this (p. 200).
- More recent work is more complicated. Wright (2010): aid hurts growth when institutions encourage a "personal vote" and helps when they do not. Wright (2009): aid helps dictators with a small winning coalition stay in power, but it can encourage some dictators with larger support bases and a good chance of winning fair elections to democratize or liberalize (p. 200).
- Decolonization in Africa (Herbst 2000): colonial powers handed power to elites and supported them, reducing their dependence on citizens. This helps explain why almost all of the fledgling democracies that colonial powers established in Africa before leaving in the 1950s and 1960s soon collapsed into dictatorships (p. 201).

**Inequality (pp. 201–202).**
- *The standard argument* (Acemoglu and Robinson, Boix, and others; back to Tocqueville): in unequal societies democracy produces class cleavages and pressure for redistribution. Elites fear expropriation through the ballot box, so they block democratization or stage coups (p. 201).
  - Evidence offered: coups in Argentina 1976, Chile 1973, Guatemala 1954, and the U.S. framers' limiting of suffrage to male property holders (p. 201).
- *Problem:* the authors say the empirical support is mixed and not especially convincing. Boix (2003) finds inequality promotes democratic survival once wealth is accounted for. Others find no clear effect. Universal suffrage has not historically led to the poor expropriating the rich (p. 201).
- *Possible explanation 1:* poor or limited inequality data (p. 201).
- *Possible explanation 2, from the variant:* elites with credible exit options can withdraw their investment. The poor "depend" on them for growth, so the poor have incentives to curb redistribution demands (pp. 201–202).
  - Implication: inequality should be bad for democracy "only" where elites lack credible exit options. This may help explain why the South African whites, with increasingly mobile assets, accepted democracy despite high inequality (p. 202).
- *Ansell and Samuels (2010):* the type of inequality matters. Income inequality rises with a large middle class of liquid asset holders, who do not fear redistribution. Land inequality signals a landed aristocracy with immobile assets, which does fear democracy. They find that income inequality is positively associated with democratization and land inequality negatively (p. 202).

**Economic performance (p. 203).**
- The EVL outcomes (the text cites "Table 6.2", but the relevant table is Table 6.3) predict that democracies tend to do well while dictatorships vary (p. 203).
  - Dictatorships whose citizens lack exit options should do relatively well, because citizens keep investing.
  - Dictatorships whose citizens have exit options should do badly, because they redeploy assets.
- Support: Przeworski et al. (2000, 176) report a standard deviation of growth rates of 7.04 for dictatorships and 4.85 for democracies (1950–1990). Their "miracles and disasters" lists are populated almost exclusively by dictatorships (p. 203).
- This may help explain why studies often fail to find that democracies routinely outperform dictatorships. The authors say regime type should have little impact on growth when fixed asset holders dominate the economy. Chapter 10 covers why some dictatorships have incentives for good performance (p. 203).

### Some more empirical evidence (pp. 203–207)

**Method (pp. 203–205).**
- A **dynamic probit model** is used on Przeworski et al. (2000) data for 1946–1990. The **dependent variable** is the outcome to be explained. The **independent (explanatory) variables** are what we think explains it (pp. 203–204).
- Reading a table: a positive coefficient means an increase in the variable is associated with a higher probability of the outcome, and a negative one with a lower probability. The smaller the standard error relative to the coefficient, the more confident we can be. As a rule of thumb, 95% confidence requires the coefficient to be bigger than twice its standard error. Stars: \* above 90%, \*\* above 95%, \*\*\* above 99% (pp. 204–205).

- *Income:* positive and highly significant. A dictatorship's probability of becoming a democracy rises with income, consistent with classic modernization theory and the literature (p. 205).
- *Growth:* negative and significant. Dictatorships that produce growth are less likely to become democracies, which implies dictatorships have an incentive to perform well (p. 205).
- *Oil:* negative and significant. Oil producers are less likely to democratize, consistent with the fixed-asset argument. Footnote 10: a few scholars question the resource curse (p. 205).
- *Size of effects (Burkina Faso, 1987):* a dictatorship with GDP per capita $500, not an oil producer, growth –2.15%. The world average GDP per capita in 1987 was $4,022 (p. 205).
  - With $4,022 instead of $500, it would have been 3.07 times more likely to become a democracy in 1988, a rise "by fully 307 percent" (p. 206).
  - With US growth of 2.55% instead of –2.15%, it would be 23% less likely to transition (p. 206).
  - As an oil producer it would be 66% less likely to transition (p. 206).

> **Intuition note.** In these estimates, higher income goes with a higher chance that a dictatorship becomes a democracy, and oil goes with a lower chance. Growth also goes with a lower chance of transition.

- *Burkina Faso as a democracy in 1987:* raising GDP per capita from $500 to $4,022 increases the probability of survival by 12%. Raising growth from –2.15% to 2.55% increases it by 4% (p. 207).

### Conclusion (pp. 207–208)
- Considerable evidence supports classic modernization theory: higher income encourages both emergence and survival of democracy. The structural changes accompanying income also matter. Limited government was more likely to arise in early modern Europe where the Crown depended on elites with mobile assets. The authors say this generalises: states reliant on fixed, quasi-rent assets are less likely to sustain democracy (pp. 207–208).
- Their statistics show that income and growth remain important after taking account of oil (p. 208).
- Ross (2001) shows that increased income affects democratization by bringing about changes such as occupational differentiation, improved education and a growing service economy. The authors say these can be expected to increase citizens' "exit options" (p. 208).
- The authors say such structural arguments are unpopular with democratic activists: if democracy in Britain resulted from centuries of economic change, what hope is there for people under authoritarian rule now? For the 2011 protests in the Middle East and North Africa, Ross (2011) argues successful transition in oil-rich countries is not impossible but highly unlikely. He notes that no country with more oil wealth than Venezuela had in 1958 has successfully democratized (p. 208).
- **Table 6.6** (Ross 2012) gives 2009 oil and gas income per capita. Qatar $24,940, Kuwait $19,500, UAE $14,100, Oman $7,950, Saudi Arabia $7,800, Libya $6,420, Bahrain $3,720, Algeria $1,930, Iraq $1,780, Iran $1,600, Syria $450, Yemen $270, Egypt $260, Tunisia $250 (p. 209).
- The authors say that, if economic structure constrains democratization, then all else equal countries near the top are expected to be much less likely to democratize. It is, perhaps, telling that the rulers of Tunisia and Egypt, at the bottom, were the first to lose power. Removing autocrats is necessary but not sufficient for democratization. As of Nov. 2011, Ross says only Qaddafi was seriously threatened among oil-funded rulers, and only because of NATO's intervention (p. 208).
- If structural economic change is a prerequisite, change is likely to come slowly. Culture, the topic of the next chapter, also changes slowly (p. 208).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take the EVL game with $c=0.2$, $L=2$ (dependent Crown, $L>1$). Check that $E<1-c=0.8$.

**Case A: $E=0.5$ (mobile assets).**
1. After "Reject limits", P compares Pay taxes $=0-0.2=-0.2$ with Disinvest $=0.5-0.2=0.3$. P disinvests.
2. Crown: Accept gives $L=2$. Reject, then disinvest, gives $1$. Since $2>1$, it accepts.
3. First move, P compares Demand limits $=1-0.2=0.8$ (Crown accepts), Pay taxes $=0$, Disinvest $=0.5$. P demands limits.
4. Result: (Demand limits, Disinvest; Accept limits). Limited government, growing economy, as in England.

**Case B: $E=-0.5$ (fixed assets).**
1. After "Reject limits", P compares Pay taxes $=-0.2$ with Disinvest $=-0.5-0.2=-0.7$. P pays taxes.
2. Crown: Accept gives $2$. Reject, then pay, gives $1+L=3$. It rejects.
3. First move: Demand limits $=-0.2$ (Crown rejects, P pays), Pay taxes $=0$, Disinvest $=-0.5$. P pays taxes.
4. Result: (Pay taxes, Pay taxes; Reject limits). Unlimited government, growing economy, as in France.

The only change is the sign of $E$ (whether exit is credible). This flips the outcome between limited and unlimited government.

## Concepts
- [[modernization-theory]] (new) — as used here, countries tend to become democratic and stay democratic as they become wealthier and more "modern" (pp. 184, 207).
- [[survival-story]] (new) — rival view in which income does not affect transitions to democracy, only the survival of democracy (p. 184, Table 6.2).
- [[credible-commitment-problem]] (new; close to canon "commitment problem") — a promiser may later want to renege and holds the power, not the beneficiary. The authors give a temporal dimension and power asymmetry as at least two basic characteristics (pp. 187–188).
- [[exit-voice-loyalty]] — here a game between Crown and Parliamentarians where exit means disinvesting, voice means petitioning for limits, and loyalty means continuing to invest (pp. 187, 191).
- [[subgame-perfect-equilibrium]] (new) — the solution reached by backward induction in the EVL game (pp. 192–193, 210–211).
- [[quasi-rent]] (new) — an asset's value minus its short-run opportunity cost (p. 196).
- [[fixed-vs-liquid-assets]] (new) — fixed assets cannot easily be converted into cash and are unlikely to give a credible exit option. Liquid assets can be redeployed and give credible exit (p. 196).
- [[resource-curse]] (new) — countries with abundant natural resources tend toward poor governance, low development, civil war and dictatorship (p. 197).
- [[rentier-state]] (new) — a state deriving most revenue from rents on natural resources sold to outside clients (p. 197).
- [[state-dependence]] (new) — the Crown is dependent ($L>1$) if it needs elites' resources, and autonomous ($L<1$) if not (pp. 192–195).
- [[dependent-independent-variable]] (new) — the outcome to explain versus the explanatory factor (p. 204).
- [[institutions]] — here, institutions such as Parliament can transfer power to a promise's beneficiary (p. 189).

## Canonical instances
- [[glorious-revolution-1688]] — a dependent Crown with mobile-asset elites accepts parliamentary limits (pp. 187, 195).
- [[france-estates-general]] — fixed-asset elites, an absolutist Crown, no Estates General meeting 1614–1789 (pp. 193–194).
- [[south-africa-1994]] — mobile white assets made the ANC's promise credible, helping end apartheid (pp. 190–191, 202).
- [[northern-ireland-iraq-commitment]] — disarmament commitment problems (p. 190).
- [[arab-spring-2011]] — oil wealth and the loyalty of the military (pp. 198–199, 208).
- [[paler-indonesia-experiment]] — taxation versus windfall revenue and monitoring (pp. 198–199).
- [[burkina-faso-1987]] — worked effect sizes from probit tables (pp. 205–207).
- [[latin-american-coups]] — elite coups blocking redistribution (Argentina 1976, Chile 1973, Guatemala 1954) (p. 201).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] EVL game with exit threats ↔ political-science canon: exit/voice/loyalty — here the leverage of voice comes from a credible exit option, with the Crown as the organization.
- [structural-analogy] Credible commitment problem ↔ canon: commitment problem in the bargaining model of war — both involve a party that cannot promise not to exploit its future power.
- [structural-analogy] Parliament's veto over taxes ↔ canon: veto player — an actor whose consent constrains the ruler.
- [structural-analogy] Low-information environment in resource-rich autocracies ↔ canon: principal–agent problem — citizens cannot monitor rulers.
- [metaphor] Fixed-asset elites as "captive" payers ↔ canon: extractive institutions — both describe rulers whom payers cannot discipline.

## Questions this raises
- If the Crown's dependence ($L>1$) and elite exit are both needed, how can the two be measured separately across modern countries?
- Does the oil coefficient capture fixed assets specifically, or other oil-related channels such as Ross's three mechanisms?
- Why does growth lower transitions from dictatorship but raise democratic survival, and does this support the incentive reading?
- How would results change if credible exit were measured directly, for example by asset mobility, rather than by oil production?
