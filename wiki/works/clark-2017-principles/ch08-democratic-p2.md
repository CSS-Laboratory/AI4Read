# Top-Down Transitions to Democracy: The Transition Game and Poland 1989

## L0 — One line
In the authors' Transition Game, a top-down transition to democracy can occur only when regime soft-liners wrongly believe the opposition is weak, so that, in effect, it happens through a mistake under incomplete information.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do some dictatorships democratize from above? The authors build a two-player game between authoritarian soft-liners (regime insiders who may prefer to liberalize) and the democratic opposition. With complete information, a weak opposition enters a "broadened dictatorship" (a liberalized but still authoritarian regime), and a strong one leads soft-liners to do nothing. Neither case ends in democracy. In the authors' version of the game, a transition arises only if soft-liners liberalize because they wrongly think the opposition is weak. Poland in 1989 fits: Communist leaders misjudged Solidarity's strength, and the partly open election ended in a defeat for them. The model also suggests that institutions such as elections may speed a dictatorship's collapse when beliefs are wrong.

**Prerequisites:** extensive-form games, backward induction and subgame perfect equilibrium (Ch. 3); ordinal payoffs (Ch. 4); preference falsification.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Preference falsification (people hiding their real views in public) is said in the quoted passage to conceal "the forces that were working against it" after a revolution. This makes it even less comprehensible why the revolution was unforeseen, so revolution will often seem inevitable in hindsight (p. 290).
- Some transitions come mainly from liberalization by authoritarian elites. The policy is often meant to stabilize the dictatorship, and the aim is typically to co-opt the opposition (bring it into the regime's institutions), not to reach democracy (pp. 290–291).
- Broadened dictatorships (elections, legislatures) are not necessarily on the way to democracy. It appears that liberalization can, under many circumstances, significantly strengthen dictatorial stability (p. 292).
- Liberalization is inherently unstable for soft-liners. The opposition may enter the institutions or use its new freedoms to mobilize further. If the opening fails, soft-liners can repress (which may succeed or end in an insurgency) or allow democratic institutions (pp. 292–293).
- Transition Game, complete information: with a weak opposition the outcome is a broadened dictatorship; with a strong one it is the status quo. No democracy results (pp. 298–300).
- With incomplete information, soft-liners who wrongly believe the opposition is weak open up, then find that democratizing is their best option. Top-down transitions occur only under such uncertainty (p. 301).
- Two implications: if a broadened dictatorship counts as an institutionalized one, institutionalization is likely only when soft-liners think the opposition is weak enough, and institutions may accelerate collapse if that belief is wrong. Also, soft-liners may want reforms yet do nothing because they cannot control the process (pp. 301–302).
- Poland 1989 illustrates the model. The Communists misjudged Solidarity's strength; polls were poor indicators of support (pp. 303–304).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Preference falsification and hindsight (p. 290, end of the preceding section)
The unit begins mid-sentence in a quotation (author not named on these pages): "... revolution as highly unlikely, preference falsification now conceals the forces that were working against it." The quote adds that post-revolutionary preference falsification makes it even less comprehensible why the revolution was unforeseen. The authors conclude that revolution will often seem inevitable in hindsight, even though this is far from the case at the time (p. 290).

*Intuition note:* Even after a revolution, preference falsification can keep hiding part of the picture. Looking back, the revolution often seems inevitable, although it was far from certain at the time.

### Top-down transitions to democracy (p. 290)
- Some transitions come mainly from **liberalization** by authoritarian elites. The policy is often meant to stabilize the dictatorship but sometimes inadvertently leads to democracy (p. 290).
- Examples. Brazil 1982–85: Geisel and Figueiredo's *distençao* and *abertura* ended in an implicit pact naming Tancredo Neves president in 1985. Uruguay 1983–84: talks produced the Naval Club Accord and presidential elections in 1984. Chile: a 1980 constitution provided for a transfer of power within eight years. Pinochet lost a plebiscite in October 1988, and presidential elections followed. Poland 1989 is treated later (p. 290).

### A Game-Theoretic Model of Top-Down Transitions

**The Story (pp. 290–293)**
- Such transitions frequently follow a split between **soft-liners** and **hard-liners**. The dictatorship is under pressure, often from economic decline, and soft-liners have come to prominence. Hard-liners tend to be satisfied with the status quo. Soft-liners may prefer to liberalize in order to gain allies, strengthen their position against hard-liners, and manage opposition groups (pp. 290–291).
- Liberalization is a controlled opening: parties, elections, a constitution, a judiciary, a legislature. Its goal is to incorporate the opposition into authoritarian institutions, not to bring democracy. The intended result is a "broadened dictatorship" (p. 291).
- Lust-Okar (2005; Egypt, Jordan, Morocco) shows that dictators have been able to influence when opponents unite or divide, by creating rules and institutions that admit some groups and not others. In this way they were able to lower the opposition's willingness and ability to challenge them. The events of 2011 show that even skilful authoritarians cannot always contain opposition (p. 291).
- Footnote 14: the decision to liberalize often follows pressure from society, so bottom-up and top-down processes are hard to separate (p. 291, n. 14).
- Some scholars read seemingly democratic institutions as signs of gradual movement to democracy ("hybrid", "partly free"). The authors cite strong and growing evidence that broadened dictatorships are not necessarily in a prolonged transition. It appears that liberalization and institutionalization can, under many circumstances, significantly enhance the stability of dictatorial rule (pp. 291–292).
- Why not always liberalize? Soft-liners cannot guarantee success. After opening, the opposition can (a) enter the institutions and accept the dictatorial rules, which soft-liners count as success, or (b) use its freedoms to organize further. Example: after the Communists allowed Solidarity in September 1980, it had three million members within two weeks and became a direct threat (p. 292).
- If the opposition keeps mobilizing, the opening has failed and the soft-liners' position is likely to be undermined. They then have two choices (pp. 292–293):
  - *Repress.* If successful, the result is a "narrow dictatorship" in which soft-liners are replaced by hard-liners. Examples: King Hussein of Jordan in the 1950s and King Hassan II of Morocco in the 1960s imposed martial law, removed the liberalizers and excluded all opposition. The 1968 Prague Spring ended in repression (Box 8.6). If repression fails, the dictatorship is likely to face an insurgency, so elites must weigh its chance of success (p. 293).
  - *Accept the opposition's demands.* South Korea's pro-democracy rallies in the 1980s led to free elections in 1987. The authors say this is "arguably" also what happened under Gorbachev, and it is what happened in Poland in 1989 (p. 293).
- Przeworski (1991, 60), quoted: failed liberalizations are reversed ("normalization") or continue to democratization (p. 293).

**Box 8.6: Prague Spring 1968 (p. 294)**
- Soft-liner Dubček replaced hard-liner Novotný in January 1968. From March to August he pursued the "Action Program", aiming at "socialism with a human face", with less censorship and more participation (p. 294).
- These limited reforms encouraged further opposition mobilization: anti-Soviet statements, a separate Social Democratic party, political clubs. The Soviets demanded a halt. Dubček refused, and about 400,000 Soviet and 80,000 other Warsaw Pact troops invaded. Hard-liner Husák then reversed the reforms, purged soft-liners and repressed dissidents ("normalization") (p. 294).
- Gorbachev in 1987 said the difference between the Prague Spring and his reforms was "Nineteen years" (p. 294). The box adds that current setbacks in MENA may mask long-run democratizing effects (p. 294).

**The Model (pp. 293–299)**
- Each actor's choice depends on what it expects the other to do, so the authors use game theory. The Transition Game (Figure 8.2) is based on a similar game outlined by Przeworski (1991) (pp. 293–295, n. 17).
- *Sequence* (Figure 8.2, p. 295):
  1. Soft-liners choose **Do nothing** (O1, status quo) or **Open**.
  2. If they open, the opposition chooses **Enter** (O2, broadened dictatorship) or **Organize**.
  3. If the opposition organizes, soft-liners choose **Repress** or **Democratize** (O5, democratic transition).
  4. Repression gives O3 (narrow dictatorship, hard-liners return) or O4 (insurgency). The authors assume repression succeeds (O3) if the opposition is weak and fails (O4) if it is strong (pp. 295–296).
- *Preferences* (">" = strictly preferred to; p. 296):
  - Soft-liners: Broadened dictatorship > Status quo > Narrow dictatorship > Democratic transition > Insurgency.
  - Opposition: Democratic transition > Broadened dictatorship > Status quo > Insurgency > Narrow dictatorship.
  - The authors say the results do not depend on how soft-liners rank democratic transition versus insurgency, or how the opposition ranks insurgency versus narrow dictatorship (p. 296).
- *Payoffs* (Table 8.2, p. 297; soft-liners; opposition). These are **ordinal**: they give rank order, not how much more one outcome is preferred (p. 297).

| Outcome | Soft-liners | Opposition |
|---|---|---|
| O1 Status quo | 4 | 3 |
| O2 Broadened dictatorship | 5 | 4 |
| O3 Narrow dictatorship | 3 | 1 |
| O4 Insurgency | 1 | 2 |
| O5 Democratic transition | 2 | 5 |

- *Weak opposition* (Figure 8.3a; solved by backward induction, pp. 297, 299):
  - Last node: Repress (3) beats Democratize (2), so soft-liners repress.
  - Opposition: Enter gives 4, and Organize leads to repression and payoff 1, so it enters.
  - First node: Do nothing gives 4, and Open leads to Enter and 5, so soft-liners open.
  - SPE: (Open, Repress; Enter). Outcome: broadened dictatorship (5; 4) (p. 299).
- *Strong opposition* (Figure 8.3b, p. 299):
  - Last node: Repress gives 1 (insurgency), Democratize gives 2, so soft-liners democratize.
  - Opposition: Enter gives 4, and Organize leads to democratization and 5, so it organizes.
  - First node: Do nothing gives 4, and Open leads to Organize, then Democratize, and 2, so soft-liners do nothing.
  - SPE: (Do nothing, Democratize; Organize). Outcome: status quo (4; 3) (p. 299).

*Intuition note:* A weak opposition knows it cannot resist repression, so it accepts a modest deal. A strong opposition is strong enough that repression would fail, so opening would force soft-liners to democratize. Under complete information they therefore do nothing.

**What can be learned (pp. 300–302)**
- The key insight: a transition to democracy is not possible "as things stand". With complete information, only a broadened dictatorship (weak opposition) or the status quo (strong opposition) occurs (p. 300).
- Footnote 19: if strength means being unified or divided, this agrees with Lust-Okar's finding that liberalization is more likely to succeed and be stable when the opposition is divided (p. 300, n. 19).
- A **complete information game** is one in which each player knows everything about the game: players' identities and types, choices, order, outcomes, preferences. All models in the book so far are of this kind, as is Figure 8.3 (p. 300). Here the soft-liners are assumed to know whether the opposition is weak or strong (p. 300).
- Given preference falsification, the authors say it seems almost certain that soft-liners will not always have complete information and will often be uncertain or misinformed about opposition strength. Citizens may support the regime publicly and oppose it privately. Underground networks nearly always exist (p. 301).
- Suppose soft-liners believe the opposition is weak, but it is strong. They open up. The strong opposition organizes. Seeing this, soft-liners realize that only a strong opposition would do so, and that repression would fail. They democratize, and a top-down transition results (p. 301).
- Conclusion: transitions from above are not possible under complete information. They can occur only under incomplete information, in effect when someone makes a mistake (p. 301). Box 8.7 (not in this unit) gives a more formal version (p. 301, n. 20).

*Intuition note:* In this model the regime does not choose democracy. It stumbles into a situation where it must concede, because it misjudged who it faced.

- *Implication 1.* If a broadened dictatorship counts as an institutionalized one, institutionalization is likely only when soft-liners think the opposition is sufficiently weak. The game gives no firm prediction on whether institutions help dictators survive. That depends on whether elite beliefs are correct. If they are mistaken, elections and legislatures "may, in fact, accelerate" collapse (pp. 301–302).
- *Implication 2.* Soft-liners may want reforms yet do nothing because they cannot control the process once started. This suggests that some people living in dictatorships are actually under more repression than necessary. If the opposition could commit not to exploiting liberalization, soft-liners might introduce some reforms, giving a less repressive broadened dictatorship but not democracy (p. 302).

### Applying the Transition Game to Poland (pp. 302–304)
- *1970s–1981.* The Communist government borrowed from the West to subsidize prices. Foreign debt rose from $700 million to $23 billion between 1975 and 1981. When repayment came due, party leader Gierek raised prices, and strikes began in Gdansk. The strikers' twenty-one demands included an independent trade union. Unsure the army would fire, Gierek backed down, and Solidarity was formed in September 1980. It had three million members in two weeks and ten million by 1981, and it opposed the regime directly (p. 302).
- *Backlash.* Hard-liner Jaruzelski had replaced Gierek. He declared martial law in December 1981: mass arrests, strikes smashed, Solidarity banned. He implied he prevented a Soviet invasion ("lesser evil"). Opposition groups said it was a desperate attempt to keep power. Released Soviet documents suggest the Soviets did not intend to invade and had rejected his request for military help in 1981. Martial law lasted until 1983. The authors read this as a liberalization that got out of control and ended in repression by hard-liners (p. 303).
- *1988–89.* The economy did not improve, and new strikes threatened in mid-1988. The Communist Party (PUWP) tried a new liberalization, the "big thaw" (footnote 21 mentions a "little thaw" in 1986). The Roundtable Talks (February–April 1989) agreed to legalize Solidarity and hold legislative elections in June. The aims were to defuse unrest, co-opt the opposition without major change in power, and borrow Solidarity's moral authority. Jaruzelski: "the game is about absorbing the opposition into our system" (p. 303).
- *Election design.* All 100 Senate seats were freely contested. 65 percent of Sejm seats were reserved for Communists and allies, so they could expect to appoint the prime minister, and the presidency was guaranteed. The Party expected to do well. CBOS, a public-opinion centre set up in 1981, aimed to give accurate information on regime support. Polls since the mid-1980s showed more confidence in the Party than in Solidarity. The election was set for June 4, 1989, two months after the talks, to deny Solidarity time to strengthen (pp. 303–304).
- *Result.* Solidarity won all 35 percent of Sejm seats it could contest and 99 of 100 Senate seats. An actress announced on TV: "Friends, this is the end of communism in our country!" The reserved seats were split between the Party (37.6 percent) and allied "deaf and speechless" parties (26.9 percent). Two of those parties joined Solidarity, giving it over 50 percent of the Sejm and the first non-Communist prime minister in Eastern Europe in forty years. The Soviets, unlike in 1981, made clear they would not intervene, so the Communists could not feasibly reverse the liberalization. Events in Poland encouraged oppositions elsewhere in Eastern Europe (p. 304).
- *Link to the model.* A key element was the Party's incorrect belief about Solidarity's strength. The CBOS surveys were careful but deeply flawed. About 30 percent of respondents refused, largely because Solidarity supporters did not want to deal with Communist institutions. Many who answered would have falsified their preferences out of fear (p. 304). The unit ends mid-sentence, on a comparison with 1981 Solidarity membership (p. 304).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Question: how sure must soft-liners be that the opposition is weak before opening is worth it?

The book gives only ordinal payoffs, so this example treats the Table 8.2 numbers as if they were cardinal. That is our assumption, not the authors'.

- Let $p$ be the soft-liners' belief that the opposition is weak.
- If weak (Fig. 8.3a): Open leads to Enter and payoff 5.
- If strong (Fig. 8.3b): Open leads to Organize, then Democratize, payoff 2.
- Expected payoff of Open: $5p + 2(1-p) = 2 + 3p$. Do nothing gives 4.
- Open is better when $2 + 3p > 4$, that is $p > 2/3$.
- With $p = 0.8$: $2 + 2.4 = 4.4 > 4$, so they open.
- If the opposition is in fact strong (probability 0.2), the outcome is a democratic transition (2; 5). The soft-liners get 2, less than the 4 from doing nothing.

This shows the main idea: the democratic outcome comes from an opening that was a good bet but lost.

## Concepts
- [[liberalization]] (new) — controlled opening of political space by authoritarian elites, aiming to co-opt the opposition, not to democratize (p. 291)
- [[soft-liners-hard-liners]] (new) — soft-liners may prefer to liberalize and broaden the regime's base. Hard-liners tend to be satisfied with the status quo (pp. 290–291)
- [[broadened-dictatorship]] (new) — authoritarian regime with institutions (elections, legislature) that has co-opted opposition. It is not necessarily in transition (pp. 291–292)
- [[narrow-dictatorship]] (new) — hard-liner regime after successful repression of failed liberalization (p. 293)
- [[transition-game]] (new) — Soft-liners–Opposition extensive-form game whose outcomes include status quo, broadened dictatorship and democratic transition (pp. 295–299)
- [[complete-information-game]] (new) — each player knows everything about the game, including types and preferences (p. 300)
- [[incomplete-information-game]] (new) — a player is uncertain of the other's type, here whether the opposition is weak or strong (pp. 300–301)
- [[subgame-perfect-equilibrium]] (new; defined in Ch. 3) — the action each player takes at every choice node. It is found by backward induction (p. 299)
- [[ordinal-payoffs]] (new; defined in Ch. 4) — payoffs showing rank order only (p. 297)
- [[preference-falsification]] — hiding private preferences in public. Here it helps explain why elites are likely to be uncertain about opposition strength (pp. 290, 301)
- [[institutions]] — here, seemingly democratic ones in dictatorships (elections, legislatures), which may stabilize or hasten collapse depending on elite beliefs (pp. 292, 302)

## Questions this raises
- How would the result change if the opposition's strength were drawn from a probability and soft-liners updated their beliefs as the game unfolds (cf. Box 8.7, not in this unit)?
- If broadened dictatorships often stabilize rule, what separates cases where co-optation works from those where it backfires?
- How could an opposition credibly commit not to push beyond liberalization?
- Does the model need soft-liners to hold wrong beliefs, or could other kinds of uncertainty also produce transitions?
