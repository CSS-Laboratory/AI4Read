# Institutional Veto Players (part 1/3): Federalism

## L0 — One line
Federalism (a constitutional structure) should be kept conceptually separate from decentralization (where policymaking power actually sits), and the authors present federalism as one of three "institutional veto players", alongside bicameralism and constitutionalism.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter treats federalism, bicameralism (two legislative chambers) and constitutionalism (government bound by a constitution) as checks and balances that affect how easily the political status quo can change. This part covers only the overview and federalism. It defines a federal state by three structural criteria and separates federalism (structure) from decentralization (practice, measured by tax-revenue shares). It also covers federal types (congruent/incongruent, symmetric/asymmetric), devolution in unitary states, two origins of federalism (coming-together, holding-together), and the arguments of supporters and critics. Federalism is relatively rare, and structure and practice can diverge.

**Prerequisites:** what a constitution is; federal vs unitary states at high-school level; reading percentages and simple scatter-style figures.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The chapter groups federalism, bicameralism and constitutionalism as "institutional veto players" that shape how easily the status quo changes; this is called veto player theory (p. 674).
- A state is federal only if it meets three structural criteria: geopolitical division, independence and direct governance. Only 24 countries met them in 1990–2000 (pp. 675–677).
- Devolution is not federalism: a unitary state may delegate a lot of power but can unilaterally recall it (p. 680).
- Federalism (structure, a dichotomy) differs from decentralization (practice, a continuum), usually measured by the central government's share of tax revenue. Federal states are on average less centralized, but there is much overlap (pp. 683–687).
- Federal types are classified as congruent (units resemble each other and the country demographically) or incongruent, and symmetric (units have equal powers relative to the centre) or asymmetric (pp. 679–683).
- Federalism arises "coming together" (bottom-up bargaining) or "holding together" (top-down, against secession). These origins tend to go with different federal forms (pp. 687–688).
- Supporters claim better preference matching, accountability, competition, experimentation and protection against tyranny. Critics claim duplication, collective-action problems (regional politicians undermining national policy goals), races to the bottom (regions cutting regulation, welfare or taxes to compete) and amplified inequalities (pp. 688–690).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and introduction (pp. 673–674)
- The chapter examines the origins and consequences of federalism, bicameralism and constitutionalism (p. 673).
- **Bicameral** means legislative deliberation happens in two distinct assemblies. Such legislatures were first designed to represent social classes. They are now more closely tied to representing territorial units (p. 674).
- **Constitutionalism** is a government's commitment to be bound by authoritative rules in a constitution. It requires a codified constitution, a bill of rights and constitutional review (p. 674).
- Scholars increasingly see these institutions as conceptually the same: all act as checks and balances and affect how easy it is to change the status quo. This is **veto player theory** (p. 674).
- Earlier chapters distinguished democracies by type of government, electoral system and party system. This chapter adds other institutional dimensions. Because the three institutions are "institutional veto players", their causes and consequences are closely related (p. 674).
- The rest of this part covers federalism only. Bicameralism and constitutionalism are not developed in these pages.

### Federalism (pp. 674–675)
- Scholars disagree on what makes a state federal, so they produce different lists. Argentina, Canada, Germany and the US are widely agreed to be federal. Others (China, India, Italy, Russia, Spain, UK, Venezuela) are called federal by some scholars and not others (pp. 674–675).
- The authors trace much of the confusion to whether a country must be federal both in structure (*de jure*) and in practice (*de facto*). They keep the two apart, following Bednar (2009). *De jure* federalism is called **federalism**; *de facto* federalism is called **decentralization** (p. 675).

### Federalism in structure (pp. 675–679)
- **Three structural criteria** (Bednar 2009), usually checkable in the constitution (p. 675):
  1. *Geopolitical division*: mutually exclusive regional governments, constitutionally recognized and not unilaterally abolishable by the centre.
  2. *Independence*: regional and national governments have independent bases of authority, typically by being elected independently.
  3. *Direct governance*: authority is shared, each level governs citizens directly (each citizen has at least two authorities), and each level can act independently in at least one policy realm, declared constitutionally.
- **Definition:** a federal state is one in which sovereignty is constitutionally split between at least two territorial levels, so that independent governmental units at each level have final authority in at least one policy realm. Non-federal states are **unitary** (p. 675).
- Regional units go by different names: states, provinces, Länder, cantons, regions (p. 675).
- **Table 15.1** lists 24 federal countries in 1990–2000, with regime type from Polity IV. Examples are Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Germany, Switzerland, the US, the UAE and Venezuela. The EU meets the criteria but is not an independent state, so it is excluded (p. 676).
- By definition all countries not in the table are unitary. As of 2000, only about 10% of independent countries were federal, and slightly under 20% of world population lived in one. Federal countries tend to be either large (Australia, Brazil, USSR, US) or heterogeneous (Belgium, Ethiopia, Malaysia, Switzerland, Yugoslavia) (p. 677).
- *Intuition note:* "Federal" is a yes/no test about the written constitution. The authors say it can typically be checked in the constitution. Three criteria decide it: are regions protected from unilateral abolition, independent in their authority (typically through separate elections), and governing citizens directly, with constitutionally declared sovereignty in at least one policy realm?

**Example: UAE, a federal dictatorship (pp. 677–678).**
- The seven emirates were the Trucial States under a British protectorate. After Britain's 1968 decision to withdraw, they negotiated with Bahrain and Qatar, who went their own way. Six formed the UAE in December 1971 and Ra's al-Khaimah joined in February 1972 (p. 678).
- The provisional constitution became permanent in 1996. Articles 116 and 122 leave all powers not explicitly given to the federal authorities with the emirates (p. 678).
- The UAE is the only current Middle Eastern federal state and the only successful Arab political union. The Egypt–Syria United Arab Republic (1958) collapsed in 1961. The 1990 North–South Yemen merger ended in failure when the South tried to secede militarily in 1994 (p. 678).

**Example: Brazil, a federal democracy (pp. 678–679).**
- The current arrangements date to the 1988 constitution. Brazil has three levels: federal, 26 states plus a federal district, and 5,560 municipalities, each with an "organic law" (p. 678).
- Article 1 declares an "indissoluble union". States may legislate on anything not explicitly prohibited (Art. 25). Municipalities may legislate on anything not contradicting state or national constitutions (Art. 30) (pp. 678–679).
- Brazil is one of four Latin American federal states, with Argentina, Mexico and Venezuela (p. 679).

### Box 15.1: Devolution versus federalism (pp. 680–682)
- Several countries satisfied two of the three criteria in the 1990s (Comoros, India, Italy, Spain, Sudan, St. Kitts and Nevis, Tanzania, Ukraine, UK). Most failed *geopolitical division* (p. 680).
- **Devolution** occurs when a unitary state grants powers to subnational governments but keeps the right to unilaterally recall or reshape them (p. 680).
- *UK:* After 1997 referenda, Scotland and Wales elected bodies in 1999. A Northern Ireland assembly was set up in 1999 under the 1998 Good Friday Agreement. The UK meets independence and direct governance but not geopolitical division. London suspended the Northern Ireland Assembly four times; the fourth suspension ran from 14 October 2002 to 7 May 2007 (p. 680).
- *India:* It has 28 states with their own governments and 7 union territories governed directly by the centre. Article 246 splits authority between a Union List and state matters. India meets independence and direct governance but not geopolitical division (p. 680).
  - Article 3 lets the national legislature change state boundaries and create new states (p. 681).
  - Article 356, "President's Rule", lets the president take over a state's executive via a governor. The state legislature may be dismissed or suspended. Rule lasts six months to three years, or longer by constitutional amendment (as in Punjab and Jammu and Kashmir) (pp. 681–682).
  - Meant for unmanageable civil unrest, it has been used frequently against state governments of political opponents, and has been invoked more than 100 times since 1950. Examples: Kerala's elected Communist government in 1959, and Karnataka in 2007 (p. 682).
- Both countries "remain unitary states in structure" despite devolving power (p. 682).
- *Intuition note:* One key test is who can take a power back. If the centre can unilaterally recall or reshape it, the authors call this devolution and not federalism, however much power has been handed over.

### Types of federalism (pp. 679, 682–683)
- Scholars classify federal systems as congruent or incongruent, and as symmetric or asymmetric (Lijphart 1999) (p. 679).
- **Congruent:** territorial units share a similar demographic (ethnic, cultural, linguistic, religious) makeup with each other and the country. Examples: US, Brazil. **Incongruent:** makeup differs among units and the country. Examples: Switzerland, Belgium (pp. 679, 682).
- In incongruent systems, political boundaries tend to line up with the geography of social groups. In congruent systems they tend to cut across them (Lijphart 1999) (p. 682).
- A purported advantage of incongruent federalism is that it turns a diverse country with geographically concentrated groups into a federation of fairly homogeneous units. In Switzerland, with four official languages, 22 of 26 cantons have one language, 3 (Bern, Fribourg, Valais) are bilingual and 1 (Graubünden) is trilingual (p. 682).
- **Symmetric:** units have equal powers relative to the centre (US). **Asymmetric:** some units have more extensive powers. Asymmetries usually aim to meet ethnic, linguistic, demographic or cultural differences. Examples: Belgium, Canada, Malaysia, Russia, Switzerland (p. 683).
- In Canada, Quebec has much more autonomy than the other nine English-speaking provinces: special powers for French-Canadian culture, authority over employment and immigration, and its own pension plan (p. 683).
- Unitary states can also devolve asymmetrically (footnote 3: Spanish autonomous communities; Scotland vs Wales) (p. 683).

### Decentralization: federalism in practice (pp. 683–687)
- **Decentralization** is the extent to which actual policymaking power lies with central or regional governments. Most scholars treat it as a revenue issue: the greater the central share of all tax revenue, the less decentralized the state (p. 683).
- Being federal says little about where policy is made. The definition fixes no ratio of policy realms and does not guarantee that regions have the resources to act. Being unitary does not necessarily mean all power is central (pp. 683–684).
- Constitutions can mislead: having authority is different from being able to act. Without their own tax revenue, regions with health or education powers may be constrained (p. 684).
- **Fiscal centralization measure:** the percentage of all tax revenue collected by the central government. The assumption is that governments need revenue to implement policy. Higher central share means more centralized (p. 684).
- Federalism is seen as constitutional, decentralization as budgetary (p. 685).
- **Figure 15.1** covers 53 countries in 1997 (13 federal, 40 unitary), except Japan (1990). Revenue legally mandated to go to regions through revenue sharing counts as regional, even if the centre collects it (p. 685).
  - Example: roughly 28% of Argentina's central revenue was automatically transferred in 1997 (pp. 685–686).
  - Nonmandatory transfers count as central, because many are conditional on central priorities (footnote 7) (p. 686).
  - Central governments do not always comply with mandated sharing. Diaz-Cayeros (2006) finds they comply much more closely when democratic. Where they do not comply, the figure overstates decentralization, mainly in federal states (footnote 6) (p. 685).
- **Results:** average revenue centralization is 74.6% in federal states and 87.95% in unitary states, so federalism and decentralization tend to go together (p. 686).
  - There is substantial variation within both groups. Unitary China, Denmark, Finland, India, Japan and Sweden are more decentralized than the average federal state. China (48.6% central) is the most decentralized in the sample (p. 686).
  - Federal Belgium, Malaysia and South Africa are much more centralized than the average unitary state. Malaysia's central share is 97.6% (p. 686).
- **Figure 15.2** is a two-by-two matrix of structure (unitary/federal) by practice (centralized/decentralized). Examples are federal and decentralized (Brazil, Canada, Germany), unitary and centralized (Botswana, Dominican Republic, Kenya), unitary but decentralized (China, India, Sweden), and federal but centralized (Belgium, Malaysia, Venezuela). The authors say they "simplify the world somewhat" (pp. 686–687).
- Structure is a dichotomy, and decentralization is best thought of as a continuum (p. 686).
- *Intuition note:* "Federal or not" is about what the constitution says. "Decentralized or not" is about where policymaking power actually sits, which scholars usually proxy by who collects the tax money. The two can disagree, so the authors keep them conceptually separate.

### Why federalism? (pp. 686–688)
- The authors use "federalism" broadly here, covering both structure and practice (footnote 8, p. 686).
- **Coming-together federalism** is bottom-up. Previously sovereign polities voluntarily give up part of their sovereignty to pool resources, for collective security or other, typically economic, goals such as a common currency and freer trade (Riker 1964). Examples: Australia, Switzerland, US. Such federations are typically symmetric, though there is some variation (p. 687).
- **Holding-together federalism** is top-down. A central government in a multiethnic state, fearing that territorially based ethnic groups may secede, decentralizes power to units where the aggrieved group dominates (pp. 687–688).
  - Example: Belgium adopted federal arrangements in the 1990s to placate its linguistic groups. India, Spain and the UK are unitary in structure but have devolved power for the same reason (p. 688).
  - Such federations are generally both incongruent (their purpose is to decentralize to territorial ethnic groups) and asymmetric (they meet differing group needs) (p. 688).
- *Intuition note:* Coming-together federations are built from separate units, while holding-together ones are carved out of a unit that might otherwise split. This is why the second kind tends to give different regions different powers.

### Claimed advantages of federalism (pp. 688–690)
All of the following are presented as claims by supporters (the text calls them "purported" advantages), not as established findings.
- **Preference matching** (Alesina and Spolaore; Buchanan and Tullock; Tiebout; Tullock) (p. 688). The authors' example:
  - *Unitary state:* 60 prefer A, 40 prefer B. A is adopted and 40 are unhappy.
  - *Federal state, two regions:* Region 1 has 50 for A and 10 for B. Region 2 has 30 for B and 10 for A. A is adopted in Region 1 and B in Region 2, so 10 + 10 = 20 are unhappy.
  - Free movement between regions could raise satisfaction further. The authors introduce this with "it seems reasonable to expect" and say, as the example suggests, that policy is more likely to match preferences in a federal state, particularly in large countries with diverse preferences (p. 688).
- **Information and accountability:** closer government may mean more information for citizens and governments. Subnational governments can tailor policy, and citizens can better hold them to account (Hayek; Oates; Lijphart). Federalism is also thought to raise participation and perceived legitimacy. Hamilton (Federalist 17) argued that people feel stronger "affection, esteem, and reverence" toward visible subnational government (pp. 688–689).
- **Competition and exit:** if citizens and investors can move, subnational governments have incentives to perform well. This is "voting with one's feet" (Tiebout 1956), an example of the exit option from earlier chapters. Competition is expected to give smaller, more efficient, less corrupt government (Buchanan 1995) and is central to arguments that federalism supports markets and growth (p. 689).
- **Experimentation:** regions can try policies and learn quickly without risking the whole country. Justice Brandeis (1932) called them "laboratories". The 1990s US welfare reform is a much-cited example (p. 689).
- **Against tyranny:** Madison (Federalist 10) argued that a harmful project is less likely to spread through the whole union than through one member. Hamilton (Federalist 9) argued that interlocking arrangements protect individual rights at both levels. Others argue federalism protects territorial minorities from majority decisions (Horowitz 1985), minimizing coercion (pp. 689–690).

### Critics' arguments (p. 690)
- Critics (e.g. Rose-Ackerman 2000) claim layers cause unnecessary duplication and overlapping, possibly contradictory, policies (p. 690).
- **Collective-action problems:** Rodden and Wibbels (2002) argue federalism empowers regional politicians who have incentives to undermine macroeconomic management, market reforms and other policies with national public-good characteristics, either locally or by blocking the centre (p. 690).
  - Regional governments may block painful fiscal reform and overspend if they expect a central bailout.
  - Several scholars find federal systems more prone to economic mismanagement and crises than unitary ones (Rodden; Treisman; Wibbels) (p. 690).
- **Race to the bottom** ("downward harmonization", Hallerberg 1996): competing for investment and residents may lower regulation, welfare, taxes and trade barriers. Local redistributive taxes become hard to apply because the wealthy move to low-tax regions. The poor may move to regions that keep welfare, which are then forced to cut it (p. 690).
- **Amplified inequality:** in asymmetric federations, competition may amplify inequalities in population, wealth and political power (Congleton; Peterson). The page ends mid-sentence ("If favored regional governments can take advantage of their additional authority to attract…") (p. 690).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two countries each have 100 units of total tax revenue.
- **Country F** is federal in structure. Its central government collects 95. It has no mandated sharing. Centralization = 95/100 = 95%.
- **Country U** is unitary. Its central government collects 70, regions collect 30. Centralization = 70/100 = 70%.
- On the revenue measure, unitary U is more decentralized in practice than federal F. This is the pattern the text describes for China vs Malaysia (p. 686).
- Now suppose F's law mandates that 30 of its 95 go to regions. By the Figure 15.1 rule, mandated transfers count as regional. Central revenue = 95 − 30 = 65, so centralization = 65/100 = 65%, and F now ranks as more decentralized than U (65% < 70%).
- If the centre does not actually pay the 30, the measure overstates decentralization (footnote 6, p. 685).
