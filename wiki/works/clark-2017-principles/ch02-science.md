# What Is Science?

## L0 — One line
Science is a critically minded method of deducing testable implications from a theory and trying hard to show them false (to falsify them), so all scientific knowledge stays provisional.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what "science" means, so that comparative politics can be called scientific. Its answer: science is a method, not a body of facts. A theory explains a puzzle, implies further testable predictions, and is tested against the world. Logic explains why this works. A confirming observation cannot prove a theory (concluding P from "If P then Q" and Q, called affirming the consequent, is invalid). A disconfirming observation can count against it (concluding not-P from "If P then Q" and not-Q, called denying the consequent, is valid). So theories stay tentative, and falsifiability separates science from non-science. Mill's comparative method, which starts from observations, is criticised partly for this reason (and for others). The chapter also rejects four myths, including the claim that politics cannot be studied scientifically.

**Prerequisites:** "if–then" statements; sets and subsets; the idea of a cause; the difference between a necessary and a sufficient condition (re-explained in the text).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Science is defined by a method, not by a stock of facts: a collection of statements would make Newton "unscientific" and the definition circular (p. 21).
- A statement is scientific if it is falsifiable: some imaginable observation could refute it. Tautologies (statements true by definition) and claims about unobservables are not falsifiable, but they may still be true or meaningful (pp. 22–23).
- The method has five steps: question, theory/model, implications, observation, evaluation. Good models are simple, explain by mechanism, and yield many, partly surprising implications (pp. 23–26).
- Competing theories are separated by critical tests (observations on which the rival theories predict differently). Box 2.1 shows this with three theories of why female athletes do well academically (pp. 26–29).
- Logic gives an asymmetry (in "If P, then Q", P is the antecedent and Q the consequent): affirming the antecedent and denying the consequent are valid; denying the antecedent and affirming the consequent are not. So confirmation never proves a theory, while disconfirmation can count against it (pp. 34–37).
- Corroboration (a theory's survival of attempts to falsify it) depends on the severity of tests more than their number. In practice one failed test is probably not enough to discard a theory (p. 30).
- Mill's comparative method (comparing cases to find necessary and sufficient causes) starts from observations, so it relies on affirming the consequent, needs restrictive assumptions, and describes co-occurrence without explaining (pp. 39–42).
- Four myths are rejected: science proves; science needs experiments; science is value-neutral; politics cannot be studied scientifically (p. 43).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and Introduction (pp. 19–21)
- The overview says science is a strategy for understanding the social and natural world, using statements that can be examined to see whether they are wrong. Explanations should clear up puzzling facts, be logically consistent and yield many falsifiable predictions. All of them are tentative (p. 19).
- The introduction lists five statements and says that all are, in some sense, wrong (p. 20):
  1. Science is a collection of facts about what we know.
  2. A scientific theory is one that has been proven.
  3. The claim that the sun revolves around the earth is not scientific.
  4. If my theory is correct, rich countries are more likely to be democracies; I observe that they are; therefore my theory is correct.
  5. Politics cannot be studied scientifically.
- The authors' corrections: theories cannot be proven. The sun statement is scientific though false. Statement 4 is logically invalid. Politics can be studied scientifically (p. 20).
- The authors say it is as much a mistake to blame science for harms done in its name as to blame religion for what some believers do (p. 20).
- Science is a method and also a culture. The epigraphs capture the culture: Popper (knowledge is tentative), Socrates (awareness of ignorance), Paul (test everything). Scientists who use a critical method are, the authors say, more likely to be humbled than emboldened (p. 20).
- Science is about asking tough questions, giving answers that invite criticism, and using the best available logic and evidence while accepting that answers may be overturned (pp. 20–21).
- "Political science" is called a tautology at this point. The next chapter defines politics. This chapter defines science, aiming at how most practising scientists would answer (p. 21).

### What Is Science? (pp. 21–23)
- Science is not a body of knowledge. If it were, Newtonian physics would have become unscientific once Einstein's theory replaced it. Justifying knowledge by "it's scientific" would also be circular (p. 21).
- Scientific knowledge may be a product of science, but it is not science itself. Science is a method for *provisionally* understanding the world. It answers the epistemological question (epistemology is the study of knowledge) of how we know what we know with: because we subjected our ideas to the scientific method (p. 21).
- Meditation, reading scripture and gazing at sunsets may be ways of seeking knowledge, but the authors say none is scientific. Science is the "recklessly critical" pursuit of knowledge (p. 21).
- The source of an idea is open. Kekulé's benzene structure came in a half-asleep vision by the fire. It became science only after its implications survived critical testing in daylight. Science begins only when one asks what else ought to be true if the insight is true. It ends, if ever, when researchers are satisfied that they have taken every reasonable pain to show the implications false and have failed (p. 22).
- **Falsifiability:** scientific statements must be falsifiable. There must be some imaginable observation that could refute them. This does not mean they ever will be falsified, and a statement need not have been tested yet (p. 22; footnote 1: a statement can be scientific even if we lack the data or equipment to test it).
- Non-falsifiable statements include:
  - Tautologies (true by definition). The example is the statement that strong states are able to overcome special interests in order to implement policies best for the nation. It may be true, but it is just a definition unless a strong state can be identified without reference to that ability (p. 22).
  - Claims about inherently unobservable things, such as "God exists." These may be true and meaningful. Science simply has nothing to say about their truth, and "nonscience" is not "nonsense" (pp. 22–23).

*Intuition note.* The test of a scientific statement is not whether it is true but whether we can imagine evidence against it. A claim that nothing could ever contradict gives science nothing to work with.

### The Scientific Method (pp. 23–26)
The authors say there is no single written-down method followed by all scientists, but the basic features can be described in five steps (p. 23).

**Step 1: Question.** Observe the world and find a puzzle. A puzzle is an observation that surprises us, so it conflicts with some prior expectation or theory. We therefore always hold a prior theory; without one, nothing could surprise us (p. 23).

**Step 2: Theory or model.**
- A theory (also "model" or "explanation") is a set of logically consistent statements that tell us why what we observe occurs. Consistency is needed because otherwise we cannot work out its empirical predictions or what would falsify it (p. 23).
- Uniformity of nature: if X causes Y today, it will cause Y tomorrow. If it does not, X is not a cause. The principle says the laws of nature do not change, not that nature does not change. Rejecting it means accepting that things "just happen" (pp. 23–24). In social science one often separates systematic from unsystematic behaviour and explains only the systematic part (p. 24).
- Method of building a model: treat the puzzle as the end result of an unknown process. Imagine a prior world that would have produced it. If we can deduce the puzzle from that world, we have a theory (Lave and March 1975) (p. 24).
- Imagination is free here. Feynman describes science as imagination in a straightjacket, meaning imagination constrained by what we already know (p. 24). We get "a" theory, not necessarily "the" theory, which is why we keep testing (p. 24).
- A model is a simplified picture. It should contain only what is needed to explain the puzzle. Its purpose is to explain, not to describe, so descriptive accuracy is not a core value. The aircraft example: LCD screens on seat backs do not matter for a turbulence model (p. 25).
- Simple models invite falsification because they make clear what we should not observe. Many amendments and conditions make contradictory evidence easier to dismiss (p. 25). Footnote 5 (Theil): "models should be used, not believed."

**Step 3: Implications (hypotheses).**
- Deduce implications other than the observations used to build the model. A model cannot be falsified by only the observations it was built to explain (p. 25).
- Good models yield many different implications. Each is another chance to fail, and a model that survives earns more confidence. Fertile models also link apparently unrelated events. Good models give some surprising implications, but if every implication surprises, either everything we thought is wrong or the model is (p. 25).

**Step 4: Observe the world (test hypotheses).**
- The aim is to try to falsify, not to defend. Test as many implications as possible, especially those most likely to fail, and submit a model to the harshest test one can devise (p. 26).
- Ask whether other models could explain the same phenomenon. Competing models share some implications but differ on others. A **critical test** uses observation to separate competing explanations. If one is possible, observation is decisive, because there is only one world and the models contradict each other about it (p. 26).

**Step 5: Evaluation.**
- If implications are observed, the theory is *corroborated*. It is not verified or proven. All we can conclude is that it has not yet been falsified (p. 26).
- The method is most cautious when it "succeeds" (p. 26). Footnote 7: scientists often slip into verification language, e.g. claiming that wealth causes democracies to live longer, when they can only say they failed to falsify that claim (p. 26).

### Box 2.1: The Case of Smart Female Athletes (pp. 27–29)
- Puzzle: a professor notices through casual observation that women in athletic competition often do better academically than the average student, despite missing classes (p. 27).
- Candidate explanations, improved step by step (p. 27):
  - The conjecture that female athletes are smart nearly restates the observation.
  - The conjecture that athletes are smart is more general, but it has no process, only pushes the question back, and comes close to being a tautology.
  - A process model: hard work in athletics transfers to studies. Generalised, this becomes **Work Ethic Theory**: activities that reward hard work build a habit of working hard, which brings success elsewhere (pp. 27–28).
- Two rivals (p. 28):
  - **Excellence Theory:** success in one area (perhaps based on talent) creates a "taste" for success, which motivates success elsewhere.
  - **Gender Theory:** women are often treated differently and infer that some activities are "not for them." Gender-specific athletics let them develop free of gender bias, and the resulting efficacy carries over to the classroom.
- Critical tests (pp. 28–29):
  - Does athletics help women more than men? Gender Theory predicts yes (male athletes get no advantage). Work Ethic and Excellence both predict it helps both sexes equally.
  - Work Ethic and Excellence both predict that academic success goes with success in other areas of life, so with accomplished musicians it is hard to tell them apart. To separate them, the authors extend Excellence Theory: a taste for excellence also builds competitiveness. Excellence then predicts athletes do better even at frivolous board games. Work Ethic predicts no difference, since athletes do not train for board games (p. 29).
- Table 2.1 (p. 29), predictions (Gender / Excellence / Work ethic):

| Question | Gender | Excellence | Work ethic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will athletics help women more than men? | Yes | No | No |
| Is academic success associated with success in other areas of life? | No | Yes | Yes |
| Are female athletes more successful at board games than non-athlete women? | Yes | Yes | No |

- Overlap among predictions is common. What matters is that theories have enough distinct predictions to choose among them, however tentatively (p. 29).

*Intuition note.* Rivals always agree on the original puzzle. You find the question where their answers differ and then collect data on that question. Sometimes a theory has to be developed further first, as with the board-game extension.

### Corroboration and rejection in practice (p. 30)
- More tests do not always mean better corroboration. Repeating the same implication adds little. What matters is the severity of tests, which depends on how falsifiable the theory is. A theory that survived a few harsh tests deserves more confidence than one that survived many easy ones (p. 30).
- Bold statements are meant as invitations to criticism, not hubris (p. 30).
- If an implication is not observed, can we conclude the theory is wrong? "Probably not": measurement may be flawed, and if behaviour is probabilistic one observation need not reject a theory. One should weigh the number, severity and quality of tests, and think about what would replace the theory. "It takes a theory to kill a theory" (p. 30).

### An Introduction to Logic (pp. 30–35)
**Valid and invalid arguments (pp. 31–34).**
- An argument is a set of logically connected statements: premises plus a conclusion. It is *valid* if accepting the premises compels us to accept the conclusion, and *invalid* if we are free to accept or reject the conclusion (p. 31).
- A categorical syllogism has a major premise (typically "If P, then Q"), a minor premise and a conclusion. "If P" is the antecedent and "then Q" the consequent (p. 31).
- Set picture (Figure 2.1): the major premise means the cases where P occurs form a subset of the cases where Q occurs (p. 32). Four forms:
  1. **Affirm the antecedent** (P; therefore Q): valid. A case in P must be in Q (Table 2.2, p. 31; p. 32).
  2. **Deny the antecedent** (not P; therefore not Q): invalid. A case outside P can still be in Q. India is a nonwealthy democracy, but the argument is invalid because the conclusion is not compelled, not because of India. The major premise says only what happens if a country is wealthy, not that wealth is the only route (Table 2.3, p. 33; pp. 32–33).
  3. **Affirm the consequent** (Q; therefore P): invalid. A case in Q need not be in P. This confuses necessity and sufficiency: the premise makes wealth sufficient for democracy, not necessary (Table 2.4, p. 33; p. 34).
  4. **Deny the consequent** (not Q; therefore not P): valid. A case outside Q cannot be in P (Table 2.5, p. 34).
- Validity and factual truth are separate. A valid argument can have a false conclusion, and an invalid one a true conclusion. To show invalidity one need only show the conclusion need not be true (pp. 33–34).
- Table 2.6 (p. 34): affirm antecedent valid; deny antecedent invalid; affirm consequent invalid; deny consequent valid. This classifies all arguments that can be broken into such syllogisms (p. 34).

**Testing theories (pp. 35–38).**
- Example theory (simplified from Przeworski 2001, to be discussed in Chapter 6) (p. 35):
  1. Dictatorship is risky: friends of the dictator do extremely well, others extremely poorly.
  2. Democracy is less risky, since leaders spread gains and pain more evenly.
  3. Rich people are less willing to take risks because they have more to lose, so countries with many rich people are more likely to be democracies.
- A testable implication: rich democracies should live longer than poor democracies (p. 35).
- Implications are often easier to observe than the theory's elements, because risk propensity is internal and psychological (p. 35; footnote 9 notes experimental work on measuring it). So scientists use as major premise: if my theory is true, then its implications will be true (p. 35).
- **If the implication is observed:** concluding "the theory is true" is affirming the consequent (Table 2.7, p. 36), which is invalid. Other processes may produce the same observation (p. 36).
- **If it is not observed:** concluding "the theory is incorrect" is denying the consequent (Table 2.8, p. 37), which is valid (p. 36).
- Asymmetry: confirmation shows only that the theory may be correct. Disconfirmation compels the conclusion that it is false. So we are justified in more confidence when we reject a theory than when we do not. Unrejected theories stay tentative and can never be proven (pp. 36–37).
- **Popper's conclusion** (voice: Popper, quoted from [1959] 2003, 280–81): the ideal of absolutely certain knowledge turned out to be an idol; every scientific statement must remain tentative for ever; worship of certainty hampers the boldness of questions and the rigour of tests (p. 37).
- Confirmations still matter. If we start from the theory and then observe, the theory risked being contradicted. Surviving that risk justifies greater confidence, though not "verified" or "confirmed" status (pp. 37–38).

*Intuition note.* "If P then Q" lets you move from P to Q and from not-Q to not-P. It does not let you move from Q to P. A prediction that comes true is compatible with the theory but also with other theories. A prediction that fails cannot happen if the theory is true and the inference chain holds.

### Falsificationism; deduction versus induction (p. 38)
- **Falsificationism:** scientists deduce testable hypotheses from theories, and a theory's merit lies only in how well it withstands rigorous tests. It is the view of science used in the book (p. 38).
- **Deductive** learning: formulate an expectation from a theory, then check observation (theory precedes observation). **Inductive** learning: start from observations, find a pattern, build an explanation (observation precedes theory) (p. 38).
- The authors say induction rests, at some point, on affirming the consequent, so the theory is never exposed to falsification (p. 38).
- Popper ([1959] 2003): the biggest problem with induction is not that it is wrong but that it is impossible. Observers choose which facts to notice, and that choice reflects a hunch, which is a theory. So scholars who claim to be inductive are implicitly deductive. If we are all deductivists, as Popper claims, the case for deduction is that it is better to use theory explicitly than implicitly (p. 38).

### Box 2.2: The Comparative Method — Overview and Critique (pp. 39–42)
- The **comparative method** (Mill's methods, from John Stuart Mill's *A System of Logic*, 1872) is the most common inductive method in comparative politics. It collects observations and uses them to develop general laws, aiming to find necessary and sufficient causes. Footnote 10 lists users: Weber, Moore, Skocpol, Katznelson, Kalyvas (p. 39).
- Definitions (p. 40): a **necessary condition** is a circumstance whose absence means the phenomenon cannot occur. A **sufficient condition** is one whose presence means the phenomenon must occur.
- **Method of Agreement** compares cases that "agree" on the outcome. Table 2.9 (p. 39), columns Democracy / Wealth / Ethnically homogeneous / Parliamentary:

| Country | Democracy | Wealth | Ethn. homog. | Parliamentary |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Belgium | Yes | Yes | No | Yes |
| US | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |

  - Belgium shows ethnic homogeneity is not necessary; the US shows a parliamentary system is not necessary. Wealth alone survives as a possible necessary condition. A user of the method would conclude that democracy is caused by wealth (p. 40).
  - The method cannot show sufficiency. That needs wealthy non-democracies, so outcomes would not all "agree" (p. 40).
- **Method of Difference** compares cases that differ on the outcome. Table 2.10 (p. 41) adds Mexico before 1990: Democracy No, Wealth Yes, Ethnically homogeneous Yes, Parliamentary No. Conclusions (pp. 40–41):
  - Wealth is not sufficient (Mexico) but may be necessary.
  - Ethnic homogeneity is neither necessary (Belgium) nor sufficient (Mexico).
  - A parliamentary system is not necessary (US) but may be sufficient (Belgium and UK).
  - The method can check both sufficiency and necessity, so it is "stronger" (p. 40). To learn what suffices for democracy you must study non-democracies (footnote 13).
- These methods underlie "most similar systems" and "most different systems" designs. Footnote 14: most similar = Method of Difference; most different = Method of Agreement (p. 41).
- Criticism (authors' view, citing Lieberson and Sekhon) (pp. 41–42):
  - Valid inference needs restrictive assumptions: only one cause, deterministic, all potential causes identified, causal factors independent. The method does not say when these hold. The authors think at least one is likely violated in almost any social science application.
  - Mill himself warned against using his methods for affairs of nations, calling arguments of that kind ludicrous parodies of experimental reasoning (p. 41).
  - A deeper problem: even if the assumptions held, the method only shows what occurs together, not why. This is like saying the sun came up because the rooster crowed. A theory needs a process story (p. 42).
  - Because it starts with observations, the method relies entirely on affirming the consequent. Its "theory" restates the patterns, so disconfirmation is impossible, and no number of confirming cases justifies saying a conclusion is verified (p. 42). Footnote 15: the method is, at most, suitable for developing theories, not testing them.
  - The remedy is to start from the theory and then observe, i.e. falsificationism (p. 42).

### Myths About Science (p. 43)
1. **Science proves things and leads to certain truth.** The authors say the best science offers tentative statements. It can speak with more confidence about what we do not know than what we know, so scientific accumulation can be thought of as the evolution of our ignorance. We keep beliefs that survive the most stringent tests we can devise (p. 43).
2. **Science needs experimental manipulation.** False: theories need only be falsifiable. Research on dinosaurs cannot manipulate. A theory need not have been tested either. Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905), which the text says implied curved space, was tested with a solar eclipse fourteen years later and was not unscientific meanwhile (p. 43).
3. **Science is value-neutral.** Pursuit of knowledge is entangled with attempts to change the world, and subjects may read and act on social science. We should be clear about the limits of our knowledge and not push action on poorly corroborated knowledge. We should not resist conclusions because we hoped otherwise, but there is nothing wrong with raising the evidentiary bar for results that could lead to regrettable policies (p. 43).
4. **Politics cannot be studied scientifically.** False: the study of politics generates falsifiable hypotheses. Subjects who can read and react make the job harder than in natural science (p. 43).

### Conclusion (p. 44)
- The authors' view of science is "fairly minimalist." What unites scientists is presenting ideas so that they invite refutation. The scientist should ask what she ought to observe if what she claims to be true about the world is false (p. 44).
- Knowledge is tentative and should be objective. Prejudices motivate work and may creep in, but the goal is to present conclusions so that others who do not share those biases can judge whether they are reasonable (p. 44).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Set version.** Let theory T say: "If a case is in P, it is in Q." Take seven cases numbered 1–7. Let P = {1, 2} and Q = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. P is a subset of Q, so the major premise is true.
- Affirm the antecedent: case 2 is in P, so it is in Q. Valid.
- Affirm the consequent: case 4 is in Q. Is it in P? No, 4 is not in {1, 2}. Concluding "P" would be wrong, so the argument is invalid.
- Deny the antecedent: case 5 is not in P. Is it outside Q? No, 5 is in Q. So "not P, therefore not Q" is invalid.
- Deny the consequent: case 7 is not in Q = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. It cannot be in P, since every member of P is in Q. Valid.

**Coin version.** Theory T: "The coin is two-headed." Implication I: "five flips give five heads." Major premise: if T, then I.
- We see 5 heads. A fair coin also gives 5 heads with probability $(1/2)^5 = 1/32 \approx 0.031$ (added step: five independent flips, each probability 1/2). So we cannot conclude T. The theory is only corroborated.
- We see one tail. A two-headed coin can never show a tail, so not I, hence not T. The theory is falsified.

One tail does more logical work than five heads, which is the chapter's asymmetry.

## Concepts
- [[falsifiability]] (new) — a statement is scientific if some imaginable observation could refute it; it need not have been tested (p. 22).
- [[falsificationism]] (new) — approach that deduces testable hypotheses and judges theories only by how well they survive rigorous tests (p. 38).
- [[tautology]] (new) — statement true by definition, so not falsifiable and not scientific (p. 22).
- [[theory-model]] (new) — set of logically consistent statements telling why observed things occur; a simplified picture built to explain, not describe (pp. 23–25).
- [[observable implications]] — consequences deduced from a model beyond the data it was built on. More, varied and surprising ones are better (p. 25).
- [[critical-test]] (new) — an observation that distinguishes between competing explanations because they predict differently (p. 26).
- [[corroboration]] (new) — a theory's survival of attempts to falsify it. It is not proof, and it depends more on severity than number of tests (pp. 26, 30).
- [[uniformity-of-nature]] (new) — laws of nature do not change: if X causes Y today it will tomorrow (pp. 23–24).
- [[valid-vs-invalid-argument]] (new) — valid: premises compel the conclusion. Invalid: we may accept or reject it (p. 31).
- [[conditional-argument-forms]] (new) — affirming the antecedent and denying the consequent are valid; denying the antecedent and affirming the consequent are invalid (p. 34).
- [[necessary-sufficient-condition]] (new) — necessary: absence rules the outcome out; sufficient: presence guarantees it (p. 40).
- [[deduction-vs-induction]] (new) — deduction: theory precedes observation. Induction: pattern-finding from observations first (p. 38).
- [[comparative-method]] (new) — Mill's methods of Agreement and Difference: systematic search for necessary and sufficient causes (pp. 39–40).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Denying the consequent vs affirming it ↔ statistics: rejecting versus "failing to reject" a hypothesis — both are asymmetric in what a result allows you to conclude.
- [structural-analogy] Critical test between rival models ↔ comparative politics: the "most similar / most different systems" designs, which choose cases by what rivals predict (the text links the designs to Mill's methods, not to critical tests).
- [metaphor] Popper's "recklessly critical quest" ↔ the unit's culture of science: willingness to be wrong as a norm.
- [structural-analogy] Necessary vs sufficient condition (p. 40) ↔ veto-player or threshold arguments: a condition may be needed for change without guaranteeing it.

## Questions this raises
- If one disconfirming observation is logically decisive but the authors say it is "probably not" enough in practice (p. 30), how many failed tests should lead a researcher to drop a theory?
- How can rival theories be extended so they make distinct predictions without becoming unfalsifiable add-ons (cf. the board-game extension, p. 29)?
- If Mill's methods are suited at most to developing theories (footnote 15), how should the theory-building and theory-testing data be kept apart?
- Is the claim "induction is impossible" (p. 38) too strong for large-data pattern searches?
