# Varieties of Dictatorship: Elections, Regime Type and Survival

## L0 — One line
How long a dictatorship survives depends on its type, through the interests and exit options of its insiders, and on its institutions, but the effect of institutions is hard to estimate.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks why some dictatorships last longer than others. First it finishes the list of reasons dictators hold elections. Then it argues that regime type shapes survival. Military regimes are short-lived, and more so after the cold war. Dominant-party regimes are long-lived, and personalistic regimes become unstable only with economic catastrophe, security-force defection or the leader's death. Monarchies last longest. A second approach stresses institutions such as parties and legislatures, which dictators sometimes allow in order to win cooperation. The authors warn that the effect of liberalizing reforms (such as multiparty elections) depends on opposition strength, which is hard to observe. This matters for judging whether elections and legislatures stabilize or undermine dictators.

**Prerequisites:** the typology of dictatorships (military, dominant-party, personalistic, monarchy); the Chapter 8 "Transition Game"; the idea of a support coalition and rents; basic percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Authoritarian elections can serve further purposes: information on local officials, legitimacy at home and abroad, and a controlled outlet for discontent such as spoiled ballots (p. 374).
- Military regimes can face factionalism, and their exit option (the value of giving up power) is likely high, since they can threaten to return. On this approach they are therefore expected to be shorter-lived and more likely to end in competitive government, and the evidence supports this (p. 375).
- Goemans and Marinov: elections followed coups within five years far more often after the cold war (74%) than during it (25%). They explain this by changed Western pressure through aid (pp. 375–377).
- Dominant-party regimes are held together by the shared interest in staying united, so their average duration is longest among the three types Geddes compares. Majority factions tend to co-opt (buy off or include) minorities, and fraud can signal strength (pp. 377–379).
- Personalistic regimes rarely share spoils with rivals, because defection is very risky and repression makes success unlikely. They tend to become unstable only with economic catastrophe, security-force defection or the leader's death, and they are more likely than other types to end in violence (pp. 380–381).
- Monarchies are the longest-lived dictatorships. Menaldo suggests a "monarchical culture" makes monarchs' rent-sharing promises more credible than in other dictatorships (pp. 378–379).
- Gandhi and Przeworski argue that dictators sometimes allow parties and legislatures in order to win citizens' cooperation. Empirically, it seems civilian regimes allow them more; the authors also argue that regimes dominated by mobile, hard-to-tax assets need them more, while resource-rich regimes allow them less (pp. 381–382).
- Liberalizing reforms are expected to stave off breakdown only when the opposition is of moderate strength. Their causal effect is hard to estimate because it depends on opposition strength, which is hard to observe. Gandhi and Przeworski predict institutions from opposition strength and classify regimes as over- or underinstitutionalized (pp. 382–383).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Authoritarian elections (end of the list; part starts mid-sentence)
- Dictators may use elections to gather information about local officials' performance (Blaydes 2011). Example: Chinese elites sometimes read low support in local elections as a sign of incompetent officials (Birney 2007) (p. 374).
- **Fourth point.** Elections can build legitimacy at home and abroad. Some regimes hold them to signal to the United States or the IMF that they are moving toward democracy (Schedler 2006). They may merely create an appearance of competition to keep goodwill and funds flowing (p. 374).
- **Final point.** Elections give a controlled outlet for discontent. Abstention and ballot nullification can signal dissatisfaction (Blaydes 2011). Examples: nonvoting in the former Soviet Union was read as protest by educated people who spoiled ballots (Karklins 1986; Roeder 1989). Blank and spoiled ballots were read as protest against military rule in Brazil, 1964–1985 (Powers and Roberts 1995) (p. 374).

*Intuition note:* An election is not only a contest. It can also work as a thermometer for the regime and as a safety valve for citizens.

### Does the type of dictatorial regime influence regime survival?
The authors say there is growing evidence that regime type has a strong effect on survival. The first approach looks at dictators' interests and intra-regime politics (Geddes 2003) (p. 374).

**Military dictatorships.**
- Professional soldiers often value military unity and efficacy above all. Their corporate interests include discipline and cohesion, autonomy from civilian intervention, and budgets large enough to recruit and buy weapons (Geddes 2003) (p. 374).
- There is evidence that officers tend to join coups only when civilian government threatens these interests or the military's existence (Nordlinger 1977; Stepan 1971). The authors read Egypt's military ending loyalty to Mubarak in 2011 in this light (p. 374).
- Militaries that come to power often carry "the seeds of their own destruction" (Geddes 2003, 63) (p. 375). Disputes over economic policy or office benefits can cause factionalism, and officers may prefer to return to the barracks rather than risk unity (p. 375).
- The exit option (the value of giving up power) is likely much higher for military regimes. They hold the guns and keep a credible threat of re-intervention. They often negotiate the handover to protect their interests (p. 375).
- Predictions: shorter durations, more endings by negotiation, and more transitions to competitive government. Considerable evidence supports these (Geddes 2003) (p. 375).
- Table 10.1 (1946–1996): 29.1% of military dictatorships ended in democratic transition, against 15.6% of civilian dictatorships and 4.5% of monarchies (p. 375).

**Cold war vs post–cold war (Goemans and Marinov 2011).**
- Figure 10.5: of 167 coups in 1960–1990, 25% were followed by competitive elections within five years. Of 43 coups in 1991–2004, 74% were followed by competitive elections within five years (the pie chart shows 26% with no election) (p. 375; p. 376).
- Their explanation is international reaction. Coup leaders who depend on foreign aid are sensitive to it. Examples: the Greek colonels of 1967 lobbied for US recognition, and Niger's Major Wanke in 1999 promised the EU early elections to secure aid (pp. 375–376).
- During the cold war the world was a "chessboard of West vs. East". Western states often preferred anti-Communist juntas to elections that might yield left-leaning governments (p. 376). Footnote 23: the Nixon administration and CIA helped the 1973 coup against Allende in Chile (p. 376).
- Goemans and Marinov argue that after the Soviet collapse the West felt freer to press for democracy. Since 1997 US law requires suspending aid after a coup. The EU made a similar commitment in 1991 and suspended aid after coups in Burundi, Comoros, The Gambia, Haiti, Niger, Pakistan and Sierra Leone (pp. 376–377).
- Finding: greater dependence on Western aid strongly increases the probability of a post-coup election, but only after the cold war (p. 377).
- **Authors' caveat:** they find the evidence quite compelling but wonder whether the apparent post–cold war break is somewhat overstated. Western influence is still challenged in some regions, for example by China, al-Qaida or the Taliban. It is not clear to them whether the West will back elections there rather than prop up friendly dictators (p. 377).

*Intuition note:* Soldiers can step down with more assurance because they can threaten to return. Whether they must hold elections depends partly on whether foreign donors punish coups.

**Dominant-party dictatorships.**
- Party cadres, like democratic politicians, want office, for policy or for spoils. The best way to keep access is to stay united. Splits risk an opposition victory or a weakened ruling faction (Geddes 2003, 59) (p. 377).
- So these regimes should be long-lived. Geddes (1946–1999): average rule lasted 19.6 years for dominant-party, 15.5 for personalistic and 9.5 for military dictatorships. Brownlee (2009) offers further evidence (p. 377).
- Majority factions therefore tend to try to co-opt minorities rather than exclude them. In crises they grant modest participation, more opposition seats and some institutional demands (Geddes 2003, 68) (pp. 377–378).
- **Electoral fraud.** The traditional view is that fraud happens when elections are close. This does not fit the record (Simpser 2008). Example: Shevardnadze in Georgia, 2000, was polled at 52% against 19% for his closest rival. He manipulated the vote and won with close to 80% (p. 378).
- One reason: lopsided wins deter defection within the party, discourage future opponents and reduce protests. They signal regime strength and the futility of challenge (pp. 378–379).
- Footnote 24 (p. 379):
  - Hyde (2011) shows that inviting election monitors began with states seeking international support. Other authoritarian states then imitated them.
  - Little (2011a) suggests monitors can change citizens' beliefs about fraud, because inviting them is costly for the regime. Monitoring can lower fraud. If it is imperfect, low-level manipulation remains.
- Co-optation needs resources, so economic downturns threaten these regimes. A close election can signal weakness. Combined with a downturn or protests, it can trigger mass defection (p. 380).
- Way (2011) describes a bandwagoning effect (politicians defecting en masse). He quotes a defecting Zambian UNIP member: "only a stupid fly . . . follows a dead body to the grave." The authors say this arguably preceded many colored revolutions (p. 380).

**Personalistic dictatorships.**
- The leader keeps personal control of policy and personnel, and spoils come only through loyalty. The security apparatus is often highly repressive (p. 380).
- The authors describe intra-regime politics here as the leader's faction deciding how much to share with a minority rival faction (Geddes 2003, 60). It rarely shares much: in effect it gives just enough to prevent defection. Defection risks life, liberty and property, and repression lowers the chance of success (p. 380).
- Economic crises destabilize these regimes too, but, generally, the crisis has to be deeper and longer than in dominant-party regimes. Three reasons (p. 381):
  1. Benefits are concentrated, so the dictator can ride out downturns.
  2. Repression makes overthrow unlikely.
  3. Exit options are less valuable. Elites are tied to the leader, find it very hard to defect, and typically have to go into exile if the regime is threatened.
- So elites often fight to the very end, and personalistic regimes are more likely to end in violence. They tend to become unstable only with economic catastrophe, defection of the security apparatus and military, or the leader's death, which collapses the patronage system (p. 381).

*Intuition note:* Insiders in a personalistic regime have few good exits, so they often fight on. This helps explain why such a regime needs a bigger shock to become unstable and is more likely than others to end in violence.

### Box 10.2 — Monarchies and regime stability (pp. 378–379)
- Monarchies are the longest-lived dictatorships, though little cross-national research explains why (p. 378).
- Menaldo (2011) suggests that monarchies developed a political culture that allows their leaders to solve credible commitment problems toward their support coalitions (p. 378).
- Evidence: in the 2011 Arab Spring, MENA monarchies (Morocco, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, UAE, Oman) were largely spared the violence that hit Tunisia, Egypt, Syria, Yemen and Libya. This stability holds for most of the postwar period. Some monarchies did spend heavily or repress, for example Saudi spending and Bahrain's crackdown (p. 378).
- Monarchies, like other dictatorships, distribute rents. In Menaldo's view, a monarch's promise to do so is more credible, because "monarchical culture" has three parts (p. 379):
  1. Clear insider and outsider rules, resting on tight family structures reinforced by intermarriage.
  2. Norms on sharing rents within the royal family, for example alternating succession between two branches of the Sabah family in Kuwait.
  3. Institutions, such as royal courts or appointed legislatures, that let the family monitor the monarch and enforce the norms. They act as commitment devices.
- The authors compare these courts to the early modern European legislatures of Chapter 6. They call this an open area of research (p. 379).

### Institutions and regime survival
- Chapter 8 recap: "soft-line" leaders liberalize (multiparty elections, legislatures) only if they think the opposition is weak enough to co-opt. In the model, a democratic transition occurs when they guess wrong and the opposition reveals (or discovers) its true strength and resists co-optation (p. 381).
- Another outcome is a "broadened dictatorship", now also called an "electoral authoritarian regime". Gandhi and Przeworski (2007) argue that leaders sometimes allow parties and legislatures in order to elicit cooperation, so they need not spend all their resources on coercion. Levi (1988) calls this "quasi-voluntary compliance" (pp. 381–382).
- Empirical patterns (p. 382):
  - Empirically, it seems civilian dictatorships are more likely to allow multiparty legislatures than military juntas and monarchies (Gandhi and Przeworski 2007). The authors link this to civilian regimes lacking an immediate institutional base.
  - Dictatorships with natural resource revenue (e.g., oil) are less likely to allow multiparty elections, since they can buy off their coalition. They survive longer even after controlling for repression (B. Smith 2004).
  - Where mobile, hard-to-tax assets dominate, repression is less likely to be effective and dictators have to try to obtain compliance through liberalizing reforms. Escribà Folch (2003) models parliament creation rising with the share of mobile capital. The authors link this to Chapter 6 on liquid asset holders in early modern Britain.
- **Causal effect of reforms.** It is hard to identify, because the choice to liberalize results from strategic interaction between leaders and opposition (p. 382). Reforms lead to a broadened dictatorship only if the opposition is weak enough to co-opt. They are expected to stave off breakdown only when opposition strength is moderate: the opposition must be strong enough to worry the regime into trying to control it, but not so strong that it is uncontrollable. If elites miscalculate and the opposition is too strong to co-opt, a transition to democracy becomes more likely (pp. 382–383):
  - If the opposition is weaker than expected, reforms have little effect, because the regime would have survived anyway.
  - If it is stronger than expected, reforms speed up breakdown.
- The estimated effect therefore depends on opposition strength, which is hard to observe. According to Chapter 8, it is precisely because leaders have difficulty gauging opposition strength, perhaps due to preference falsification (hiding true preferences), that reforms sometimes lead to transition (p. 383).
- **Authors' question** on Brownlee's (2009) finding that "competitive authoritarianism" encourages transitions. Is it the competitive elections that push toward democracy? Or does opposition strength lead the regime to hold elections, make them competitive and weaken the dictator's hold (p. 383)?

*Intuition note:* Reforms can look harmful or harmless partly because we usually do not see how strong the opposition really was. The same reform can save one regime and sink another.

**Gandhi and Przeworski's test (p. 383).**
- Institutions are scored 0–2: 0 = no parties allowed; 1 = one party only, even without a legislature; 2 = more than one autonomous party in the legislature.
- They first predict the institutions a dictator adopts from three measures of opposition strength:
  1. Whether the dictator inherited a multiparty legislature (the opposition is likely stronger if it was part of the previous system).
  2. The cumulative number of leaders in the authoritarian spell, excluding natural deaths (little turnover suggests a stabler regime and weaker opposition).
  3. The percentage of democracies in the world (more democracies mean more external pressure to liberalize).
- Overinstitutionalized means more institutions than predicted; underinstitutionalized means fewer. Results: 47% overinstitutionalized, 22% under, 31% appropriately institutionalized.
- They then study how the degree of institutionalization affects leaders' survival. The unit's last sentence breaks off: "They find that, on average," (p. 383).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrate "reforms help only when opposition is of moderate strength" with invented numbers. Suppose a regime can co-opt any opposition of strength up to 6, and can survive without reforms if strength is below 2. Strengths run from 0 to 10.
- True strength 1 (below 2): the regime would survive anyway, so reforms have little effect.
- True strength 4 (between 2 and 6): reforms co-opt the opposition, so breakdown is delayed. This is the moderate range.
- True strength 8 (above 6): the opposition resists co-optation, so reforms speed up breakdown.

Now suppose the regime believes strength is 4 and liberalizes. If the truth is 1, 4 or 8, the outcomes are no effect, delay and speed-up. An analyst who sees only "liberalized" and "fell" cannot say whether reforms caused the fall without knowing the true strength. This is the identification problem on pp. 382–383.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Military exit option and re-intervention threat ↔ political-science: commitment problem — a group that keeps its power can give up office because its future influence is protected.
- [structural-analogy] Royal courts as commitment devices ↔ political-science: early modern legislatures that bound monarchs (Chapter 6) — an institution that constrains the ruler makes promises credible. The authors make this comparison themselves.
- [structural-analogy] Mass defection from a regime party ↔ political-science: revolutionary threshold cascade — each insider leaves as others leave.
- [structural-analogy] Election monitors as a costly signal ↔ political-science: credible signalling under hidden information — a costly act makes a regime's claim of low fraud more believable.

## Questions this raises
- If the effect of reforms depends on unobserved opposition strength, what data or research design could separate the effect of institutions from selection into institutions?
- Do the post–cold war results on aid and post-coup elections hold when Western interests are challenged by China or Islamist groups, given that the authors say it is not clear that the West will back elections there?
- Why do monarchies' commitment devices work when similar legislatures or courts in other dictatorships do not?
- Does the shorter duration of military rule mean it is less stable, or only that soldiers choose to exit more easily?
