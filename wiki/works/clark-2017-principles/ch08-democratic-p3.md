# Democratic Transitions (part 3/3): Conclusion, Problems and the Incomplete Information Transition Game

## L0 — One line
Bottom-up transitions are rare because of free riding and hidden preferences, and top-down transitions seem to need mistaken beliefs. Games of incomplete information show how such mistakes can arise and be analysed with expected payoffs and critical probabilities.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part closes the chapter. It asks why democratic transitions happen. Collective action theory helps explain why bottom-up transitions are rare: democracy is a public good, so people free ride and hide their dislike of the regime. The authors' analysis suggests top-down transitions can happen only when someone makes a mistake. It also teaches a four-step method for games where one player does not know another's type: write one game per possible world, solve each, join them with an information set, and compare expected payoffs. In Box 8.7, soft-liners (regime elites) open the regime if and only if they think the opposition is weak with probability above 2/3. Practice problems cover collective action, consolidation in Iraq, and religious parties.

**Prerequisites:** game trees and backward induction; ordinal vs cardinal payoffs; probability and expected value; simple linear inequalities; the free-rider problem and public goods (chapter 8, earlier parts).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Democracy is a public good, so collective action to obtain it suffers from free riding. Public opposition is therefore rare, and dictators usually look stable on the surface (p. 305).
- Preference falsification (publicly supporting a regime you dislike) helps explain why popular revolutions nearly always come as a surprise yet look inevitable in hindsight (pp. 305–306).
- The authors' analysis suggests top-down transitions can happen only when someone makes a mistake. In their Transition Game (a game between regime elites and the opposition) they will not occur under complete information (everyone knows everything about the others), but can occur under incomplete information, where a player does not know another's type (p. 306, p. 320).
- To some extent, the top-down/bottom-up split is "an analytical convenience". In Egypt 2011, Mubarak's response to bottom-up pressure could be thought of as the first node of the Transition Game (pp. 306–307).
- Way (2011), as reported, argues there are reasons to be pessimistic about successful transitions in many countries of the Middle East and North Africa (MENA). He cites structural factors and the absence of external factors that helped Eastern Europe (p. 307).
- Incomplete information games (a player does not know another's type) are solved in four steps: separate games, solve each, join with an information set (a dashed line joining nodes the player cannot tell apart), compare expected payoffs (probability-weighted payoffs) (p. 312).
- An uncertain actor's choice flips at a critical probability, the belief value at which she switches: 1/2 in the Terrorism Game and 2/3 in the Transition Game (pp. 315–316, 322).
- Actors have incentives to influence others' beliefs, e.g. a strong opposition acting weak until the regime has opened (p. 323).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the Poland case (p. 305)
- The page opens mid-quotation, mentioning expected membership "calculated on the basis of available statistics" (Kaminski 1999). Much of Solidarity stayed underground, out of the Communist Party's sight (p. 305).
- **Kaminski (a former colleague, who also was a political prisoner) reports:** in 1985 he ran the underground publishing house STOP, with about 20 full-time workers and up to 100 moonlighters. It published about 35 titles, over 100,000 books in total, in 1982–1989. It belonged to a decentralized network of about 100 publishing houses, hundreds of periodicals, thousands of trade union organizations and even theaters and galleries. They called it an "independent society" (p. 305).
- The authors conclude that the Party might have seriously overestimated its own strength relative to Solidarity. Mistaken beliefs were crucial to Poland's 1989 transition and are "central to top-down transitions … more generally" (p. 305).

### Conclusion (pp. 305–307)
- **Two routes.** Bottom-up: the people overthrow a regime in a popular revolution. Top-down: dictatorial elites introduce liberalizing reforms that inadvertently lead to democracy. The chapter used Eastern Europe as evidence (p. 305).
- **Collective action.** Collective action theory helps explain why bottom-up transitions (East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Romania, 1989) are rare and why dictatorships often look stable. Democracy is a public good, so attempts to produce it face a free-rider problem. Individuals have strong incentives not to join protests and to free ride. Public opposition is therefore very rare, and elites usually seem, at least on the surface, to hold power firmly (p. 305).
- **Preference falsification.** Opposing a dictatorship publicly is dangerous, so people who dislike it tend to hide their preferences and support it in public (pp. 305–306).
  - Revolutions come as a surprise because nobody can be sure how much opposition exists. Societies can reach the brink without anyone noticing (p. 306).
  - They look inevitable in hindsight because few people openly admit supporting the old regime once it falls. The danger then comes from opposing the new democracy (p. 306).
- **Tipping models.** They explain why revolutions are unpredictable and why small preference changes can quickly turn subservient people into protesters. Societies have different distributions of revolutionary thresholds. An action that triggers a cascade in one dictatorship may have little or apparently little effect in another (p. 306).
- **Top-down transitions.** The authors' analysis of Poland "suggests that they can happen only when someone makes a mistake" (p. 306).
  - Liberalization is typically meant to strengthen and stabilize the dictatorship by broadening its social base (p. 306).
  - According to the Transition Game, top-down transitions will not occur if the elite and the opposition know everything about each other. They can occur under incomplete information, when actors have mistaken beliefs about the other's type. For example, the elite wrongly thinks the opposition is weak when it is strong (p. 306).
- **Analytical convenience.** The authors say the top-down/bottom-up distinction is partly an analytical convenience. Bottom-up transitions may start with autonomous mass action, but opposition groups and elites then often interact strategically. Elites may respond with reforms or promises of reform (p. 306).
- **Egypt 2011.**
  - Mubarak first promised a crackdown. When his military seemed unwilling to repress widely, he promised to step down in six months after an election (p. 306).
  - The authors say this could be seen as the first node of the "top-down" Transition Game. Because the offer came from weakness, it did not lead to a broadened dictatorship (p. 307).
  - The opposition organized more protests. Mubarak stepped down a few weeks later. In fall 2011 new protests suggested the military, now cast as the "soft-line" alternative, might itself struggle to manage a shift to a broadened dictatorship (p. 307).
- **Outlook (Way 2011, as reported).**
  - The authors say it is "too early to tell" whether MENA pressures will bring transitions or authoritarian reactions like the "return to normalcy" after the Prague Spring (p. 307).
  - Way argues that a decade after communism, economic development and natural resources were the best predictors of which Eastern European countries consolidated democracy. The authors say (referring to Chapter 6) these factors give little basis for optimism in most MENA countries (p. 307).
  - Way also argues that external factors helping Eastern Europe are largely absent in MENA. These were the Soviet hands-off approach and the wish to join the European Union. The authors add that concerns about radical Islam and oil have historically led the United States to support authoritarian regimes there (p. 307).
- The next chapter asks whether democracy makes a material difference to people's lives. The three chapters so far implicitly assumed that democracy is good (p. 307).

> **Intuition note.** Hidden preferences help explain both why dictatorships look safe and why revolutions come as a surprise. Nobody can be exactly sure about the true level of opposition. For top-down transitions, the authors treat mistaken beliefs as central (pp. 305–306).

### Preparation: incomplete information games (pp. 311–316)
- The authors recommend reading Box 8.7 first. The overview draws on Powner and Bennett (2005) (p. 311).
- An **incomplete information game** is one in which a player does not know all relevant information about another player's characteristics or type. The soft-liners, for example, are unsure whether the opposition is weak or strong. This makes it hard to choose at their decision nodes (p. 311).
- The key is to combine multiple games, one per possible type. The reasoning runs roughly: if the world is like this, I would choose X; if it is like that, I would choose Y; I believe the world is probably like this, so I will do X or Y (Powner and Bennett 2005, paraphrased, p. 311).
- **Four steps** (p. 312):
  1. Write separate games for the different possible worlds.
  2. Solve the separate games.
  3. Join them with an information set showing uncertainty about which game is played.
  4. Compare the expected payoffs of the uncertain actor's choices in the combined game.

**Example: the Terrorism Game revisited (pp. 312–316).** It comes from the problems of Chapter 3 (p. 312).
- *Players and types.* Reluctant Terrorists prefer negotiation but turn to terrorist acts if repressed. The Government is either Responsive (willing to negotiate in good faith) or Repressive (wishes to repress all opposing groups) (p. 312).
- *Moves.* Reluctant Terrorists choose Violence or Negotiate (requesting a concession). Violence ends the game with a terrorist act. After Negotiate, the Government chooses to repress or to negotiate in good faith. The three outcomes are Terrorist act, Repression, Good faith negotiations (p. 312).
- *Preference orderings (p. 312).*
  - Reluctant Terrorists: Good faith negotiations > Terrorist act > Repression.
  - Responsive Government: Good faith > Repression > Terrorist act.
  - Repressive Government: Repression > Good faith > Terrorist act.
- *Step 1–2: separate games (Figure 8.5, cardinal payoffs, Terrorists first; p. 313).*
  - Responsive Government: Terrorist act (2, 1); Repression (1, 2); Good faith (3, 3). The SPE is (Negotiate; Negotiate), with outcome Good faith negotiations (p. 313).
  - Repressive Government: Terrorist act (2, 1); Repression (1, 3); Good faith (3, 2). The SPE is (Violence; Repress), with outcome Terrorist act (p. 313).
  - The Terrorists do not know which game they are in (p. 313).
- *Step 3: information set (Figure 8.6).* Nature moves first. With probability $p$ the Government is Responsive (top subgame), with probability $1-p$ Repressive (bottom subgame). A dashed line joins the Terrorists' two choice nodes, so they do not know which node they are at (p. 314).
- *Step 4: expected payoffs (p. 315).*
$$E[\text{Violence}] = (p\times 2) + [(1-p)\times 2] = 2p + 2 - 2p = 2$$
$$E[\text{Negotiate}] = (p\times 3) + [(1-p)\times 1] = 3p + 1 - p = 2p+1$$
  - Violence pays 2 in either game, so it does not matter which game is played (p. 315).
- *Critical probability.* Violence is chosen if $2 > 2p+1$, i.e. $1 > 2p$, i.e. $\tfrac12 > p$ (p. 315).
  - If $p<\tfrac12$ the Terrorists choose violence. In words: if they think the chance of a responsive government is below 0.5 (given these payoffs), they use violence (p. 316).
  - The result is a critical value of $p$. It is not the Terrorists' actual or perceived belief (p. 316).
- *Lessons (p. 316).*
  - A terrorist act can occur even against a responsive government, for instance if the Terrorists mistakenly think it is repressive. Under complete information this cannot happen.
  - Responsive governments dealing with reluctant terrorists should therefore avoid actions that might make them look repressive.
- Footnote 23 says the book shows how to find the critical probability but not how to find and write down the equilibrium of such games (p. 316).

> **Intuition note.** An uncertain player compares a sure payoff with a weighted average of possible payoffs. The weights are her beliefs, so her choice flips at a belief threshold. A wrong belief can then produce an outcome that complete information would rule out (pp. 315–316).

### Problem 3: Religious Party Game (pp. 316–319)
- *Background.* Kalyvas (2000) concludes that religious parties may be compatible with democracy as long as they can credibly commit not to impose a theocratic dictatorship if they win. He argues that some religions can provide such commitments better than others (p. 316).
- *Players and moves.* A Regime that has begun democratization decides to hold or cancel elections. The Religious Party is expected to win (assumed). If elections are held, it chooses to pursue a moderate agenda (democratic consolidation) or a radical policy (religious regime). The Party is moderate or radical, as determined by its dominant faction. Moderates prefer consolidation to theocracy, and radicals the opposite. The Regime does not know the Party's type (p. 317).
- *Figure 8.7 payoffs (Regime, Party; p. 317).*
  - Continued dictatorship (20, 5) after Cancel, in both types.
  - If moderate (probability $p$): new religious dictatorship (5, 15); democratic consolidation (25, 25).
  - If radical (probability $1-p$): new religious dictatorship (5, 30); democratic consolidation (25, 10).
- *Questions.*
  - (a)–(c): write preference orderings, and solve each subgame as if there were no uncertainty (p. 317).
  - (d)–(f): expected payoffs of Cancel and Hold, and the critical probability at which the Regime holds elections (p. 318).
  - (g)–(i): the Regime's choice at $p$ = .75, .8 and .5 (p. 318).
  - (j)–(k): what each type of party would want the Regime to believe (p. 318).
  - (l): why a moderate party's simple announcement that it is moderate may not suffice (p. 318).
- *Kalyvas's cases (as reported; p. 318).*
  - He claims only two cases in which religious parties won or were expected to win an electoral mandate: Belgium (1870–1884) and Algeria (1988–1992). Footnote 24 excludes parties that were always in opposition, only in coalition, or that came to power by revolution (p. 318).
  - Algeria: in January 1992 the military aborted the electoral victory of the FIS, and a bloody civil war followed.
  - Belgium: a Catholic party won in 1884 on a religious program. Incumbents accepted the result although they could have aborted it. The party did not apply its religious program in full and did not challenge secular, liberal institutions.
  - Both parties had moderate and radical factions, but Kalyvas presents evidence that moderates were dominant in each (p. 318).
- *Link to the game.* The authors say that if their game is correct, the reason the Belgian Catholic party was allowed to take power while the Algerian Islamic party was not must have been that only the Catholic party's promises of moderation were seen as credible (pp. 318–319).
- *Kalyvas's explanation (p. 319).* Commitments to moderate policy are more credible from hierarchical religions like Catholicism than from decentralized ones like Islam. A leader at the top can control members. Pope Leo XIII in 1879 told Belgian Catholics to stop attacking the constitution and to purge prominent radical leaders. In Islam, different leaders compete, and each claims to speak for the masses.
- *Turkey questions (m)–(p; p. 319).* Readers use outside sources on Turkey's regime type and its religious parties (Refah, Fazilet, Saadet, Justice and Development). They then test Kalyvas's 2000 claim that no contemporary case of compliance with a religious party's mandate victory exists. They also test the implication that Islamic parties find it harder to be incorporated. Finally they ask whether repeated electoral interaction can help overcome the commitment problem (p. 319).

### Box 8.7: Transition Game with Incomplete Information (pp. 320–323)
- *Setup.* Earlier, the main text analysed two complete information Transition Games (Figure 8.3): the soft-liners know the opposition is strong, or know it is weak. The main-text analysis suggested top-down transitions cannot occur under complete information. The box examines more formally the informal claim that they are possible under incomplete information (p. 320).
- *New element: Nature.* Nature picks the game with a weak opposition with probability $p$ and with a strong opposition with probability $1-p$. The soft-liners do not know which is chosen (p. 320).
- *Information set.* A dashed line joins the soft-liners' first two decision nodes, "Do nothing" or "Open". It shows that the player does not know which node she is at (Figure 8.8; p. 320–321).
- *Cardinal payoffs.* Incomplete information games need cardinal payoffs, which show exactly how much more one outcome is valued. A payoff of 4 is four times a payoff of 1. The box keeps the earlier numbers, now read as cardinal (p. 320).
- *Figure 8.8 payoffs (Soft-liners, Opposition; p. 321).*

| Outcome | Payoffs |
|---|---|
| Status quo (both sides of tree) | (4, 3) |
| Broadened dictatorship (both sides) | (5, 4) |
| Insurgency, weak opposition | (3, 1) |
| Insurgency, strong opposition | (1, 2) |
| Democratic transition (both sides) | (2, 5) |

- *Backward induction (p. 321).*
  - Final nodes: soft-liners repress when the opposition is weak (left) and democratize when it is strong (right). *(Added step, from Figure 8.8: repress gives 3 vs 2 on the left; democratize gives 2 vs 1 on the right.)*
  - Opposition nodes: a weak opposition enters (broadened dictatorship); a strong opposition organizes (the text cites "our earlier analysis"). *(Added step: weak: 4 > 1; strong: 5 > 4.)*
- *Why backward induction stops.* At the first node the soft-liners do not know which side of the tree they are on (p. 321).
  - If they open up and the opposition is weak, the outcome is a broadened dictatorship (payoff 5).
  - If they open up and it is strong, the outcome is a democratic transition (payoff 2).
  - "Do nothing" always gives 4.
- *Expected payoff* of a choice is the sum of each outcome's payoff times its probability. It tells players their average payoff if they could choose repeatedly (p. 322).
$$E[\text{Choice}] = (\Pr_1 \times \text{Payoff}_1) + (\Pr_2 \times \text{Payoff}_2)$$
$$E[\text{Open}] = (p\times 5) + [(1-p)\times 2] = 5p + 2 - 2p = 3p+2 \tag{p. 322}$$
- *Critical probability (p. 322).* The soft-liners open when $E[\text{Open}] > \text{Payoff(Do nothing)}$:
$$3p+2>4 \;\Rightarrow\; 3p>2 \;\Rightarrow\; p>\tfrac23$$
  - They open and liberalize "if and only if" they believe the probability that the opposition is weak is greater than 2/3. This is the "critical probability". Below it they do nothing and the outcome is the status quo (pp. 322–323).
  - Footnote 25: the 2/3 value depends on the particular payoffs used (p. 323).
- *How a top-down transition arises (p. 323).*
  - Soft-liners open whenever they are sufficiently confident the opposition is weak. Their belief may be wrong.
  - If the opposition is strong, it keeps organizing. Only a strong opposition would organize, so the soft-liners realize their mistake and update to $p=0$.
  - They then democratize, because repression would fail and cause an unwanted insurgency. The soft-liners become democratizers inadvertently, because of mistaken beliefs.
- *Strategic implications (p. 323).*
  - Actors have incentives to influence others' beliefs. A strong opposition, if it thinks the soft-liners might liberalize, will try to avoid signalling strength and act like a weak one. Once the regime opens, it reveals its strength and pushes for full transition.
  - The game could be adapted so that the opposition is unsure about the dictatorship's type (repressive or liberal), or both are uncertain. Footnote 26 says Blaydes and Lo (2012) do this, combining the Transition Game with the Religious Party Game (p. 323).
- The box refers readers to problem 3 here and to the Dictatorship Party Game in Chapter 10 for practice (p. 323).

> **Intuition note.** Opening the regime is a gamble. It pays well (5) if the opposition is weak and badly (2) if it is strong, against a sure 4. In this game the gamble is worth taking only if you believe the opposition is weak with probability above 2/3, so a mistaken belief that the opposition is weak can start a transition (pp. 322–323).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An elite can "Hold" a risky reform or "Pass". Passing gives a sure 3. If the rival group is weak (probability $p$), Hold gives 6. If it is strong (probability $1-p$), Hold gives 1.

- $E[\text{Hold}] = 6p + 1(1-p) = 6p + 1 - p = 5p+1$.
- Hold is better when $5p+1>3$, so $5p>2$, so $p>0.4$.
- If the elite believes $p=0.5$: $E[\text{Hold}]=5(0.5)+1=3.5>3$, so it holds.
- If the rival is actually strong, the realized payoff is 1, below the sure 3. The choice was reasonable given beliefs but turned out badly, which is the "mistake" pattern of Box 8.7.
- If the elite believes $p=0.3$: $E[\text{Hold}]=5(0.3)+1=2.5<3$, so it passes.
