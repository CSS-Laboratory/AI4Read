# Social Cleavages and Party Systems (part 2/3): Types of Parties and the Politicization of Cleavages

## L0 — One line
Parties arise around social cleavages, but which latent cleavage becomes a politicized one depends on the interaction between how attributes are distributed in society and electoral institutions such as the winning threshold.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The section asks which social divisions end up defining party systems. It first describes the British Whig–Tory origins and the main cleavages: urban–rural, confessional, secular–clerical, class, post-material, and ethnic/linguistic. It then presents a "thinly constructivist" approach (Chandra and Boulet). Individuals have fairly fixed attributes. Identity categories built from those attributes are socially constructed. Which categories get politicized is likely to depend on how attributes are distributed and correlated, and on electoral rules. Hypothetical countries A–D and Posner's Malawi/Zambia study illustrate this. The authors say comparative politics has no fully worked-out theory of politicized cleavages yet. The part ends mid-sentence on p. 640.

**Prerequisites:** minimal winning coalitions (Chapter 12 logic); electoral thresholds and plurality rules; reading simple two-way tables of shares. Terms used early: cleavage = social division; latent = only potential; politicized = activated in politics; patronage = use of public resources to reward supporters; SMDP = single-member district plurality (one seat per district, most votes wins).

## L2 — Key ideas
- A party is by definition a "part" of society. Parties form when office seekers pursue goals shared by part, not all, of the population; Whigs and Tories grouped views across several dimensions (pp. 620–621).
- Parties became competing office-seeking teams once the monarch drew a ministry from a single party. That raised the value of office and the use of patronage (pp. 621–622).
- The chapter lists pre-industrial cleavages (urban–rural, confessional, secular–clerical) and the class cleavage. It stresses that class and ethnic membership each have an objective side and a subjective, consciousness side (pp. 622–629, 632–633).
- Marxist expectations of socialist majorities were inhibited by three factors: workers not voting by class, workers not being a majority, and structural limits plus organizational oligarchy (pp. 628–629).
- Lipset and Rokkan's freezing hypothesis was used to explain stable European party systems. Left-libertarian and populist extreme-right parties challenge it, but their average vote shares were small (pp. 629–632).
- Chandra and Boulet's approach takes attributes as given and sticky, and identity categories as constructed. Cross-cutting versus reinforcing attributes shape which categories are likely to be activated (pp. 634–636).
- The same attribute distribution can yield different politicized cleavages under different electoral thresholds, and the same threshold can yield different cleavages under different distributions (pp. 637–638).
- Posner's Chewa–Tumbuka case illustrates this. Posner argues that group size relative to the chance of winning SMDP elections explains why the cleavage is politicized in Malawi but not in Zambia (pp. 639–640).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Types of parties: social cleavages and political identity formation (p. 620)
Perhaps the central function of a party in a democracy is to represent, articulate and champion the interests of its membership. These interests are shared by only part of the population. The dictionary defines a party as "a division of a whole; a part, portion, or share." So parties arise when officials or would-be officials seek office for goals shared by part of society (p. 620).

**Origins of the British party system (pp. 620–622).**
- The system emerged in the seventeenth century from a parliamentary conflict over church and state. In 1679 Shaftesbury introduced an Exclusion Bill to stop the Catholic James, Duke of York, succeeding Charles II. Supporters were "Whigs," opponents "Tories." Both names began as insults (p. 620).
- The leaders embraced the terms, probably because the insults gave a sense of shared offense to mobilize group loyalty. The labels outlived the Exclusion Crisis (1681) partly because they helped like-minded parliamentarians organize their work. They were loose groupings compared with modern parties (p. 620).
- Following Bulmer-Thomas (1965), the deepest division was attitude to change. Tories stood for the status quo, Whigs for "progress." Attitudes were not fully determined by material position, but not unrelated to it. Tories tended to come from the landed agricultural elite; Whigs were more likely to come from the rising commercial elite (p. 620).
- Tories tended to back the Church of England's monopoly on public office. Whigs tended to back toleration, though only to a limit (the 1689 Act of Toleration did not cover Catholics or Unitarians). Whigs were more internationalist and willing to finance Queen Anne's military efforts to protect commerce. Tories were more isolationist (pp. 620–621).
- The mapping across dimensions was imperfect (nonconformist Tories; rural Whigs; some Tories invited William of Orange and George of Hanover). Still, on the whole members shared views across dimensions, and it is (only) at this point that they began to resemble a party (p. 621). Table 14.3 lists the pairs: Progress/Tradition, Limited government/Monarchy, Gentry/Nobility, Nonconformity and toleration/High Church orthodoxy, Commerce/Agriculture, Urban/Rural, Internationalism/Isolationism (p. 621).
- George I (1714–1727) was the first king to draw his inner circle from a single party. This "party ministry" gave a party more control over policy and public resources and shifted power from the Crown to the parliamentary elite. It raised the benefit of office, so teams had incentives to help co-partisans get elected and to use patronage (pp. 621–622).

*Intuition note:* In the Whig–Tory case, a party-like group appeared once many separate disagreements lined up, so that people on one side tended to agree on several issues at once. Control over office and patronage then gives the group a reason to act as a team (pp. 621–622).

### Social cleavages (pp. 622–633)
These cleavages (lines of political division in society) help analyze party systems; the text lists common ones and their changing salience (p. 622).

**Urban–rural (p. 622).**
- It is one of the oldest conflicts and has economic and cultural sides. Town dwellers are food consumers and gain when food prices fall. Rural dwellers are more likely producers and gain when prices rise.
- Rural exchange was often barter, with credibility resting on reputation tied to kinship. Town exchange was monetized and anonymous. Urban commitment problems concerned the credibility of money, weights and measures, so contract law, property rights and financial innovation mattered more in towns. Rural actors tended to value tradition, townspeople change.

**Confessional (pp. 622–624).**
- Religious conflict emerged with the sixteenth-century Reformation. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the Thirty Years' War and the Eighty Years' War and reinvigorated *cuius regio, eius religio* ("whose region, his religion"). It may have reduced conflict between jurisdictions, but it did little to resolve conflict within states and all but dictated intolerance (p. 623).
- Britain was cleaved on at least two fronts: Church of England versus Nonconformists, and Church of England versus Catholic Jacobites. There was also Protestant–Catholic conflict in Ireland (p. 623).
- The continued salience of the Protestant–Catholic cleavage depends largely on whether one side established dominance. Outcomes differ: two countries (Netherlands/Belgium), an uneasy truce (Germany), one church dominant (Sweden, France), or no significant Reformation (Italy, Spain) (p. 623).
- Non-European cases: Hindu–Muslim partition of India (1947) and Kashmir; Sunni–Shia divisions in the Middle East, exacerbated in Iraq by the 2003 removal of Saddam Hussein; Nigeria and Sudan. In Sudan, 98 percent of southerners voted in a January 2011 referendum to break away (South Sudan, July 2011) (pp. 623–624).
- Lebanon institutionalizes the cleavage. The president, prime minister, deputy prime minister and speaker must be a Maronite Christian, Sunni Muslim, Orthodox Christian and Shia Muslim respectively (p. 624).

**Secular–clerical (pp. 624–627).**
- Over the last two centuries, religious competition in European democracies has run mainly along a church–state axis (Lipset and Rokkan 1967) (p. 624).
- France: the Catholic Church was the largest landowner and could levy *la dîme* (a 10 percent tax). The 1789 Revolution was aimed at the church as much as the monarchy. Laws in 1790 abolished the dîme, confiscated property, and made the clergy state employees (Civil Constitution of the Clergy). Napoleon restored some church power (Concordat of 1801) and the church regained more after 1814 (p. 624).
- Anticlericalism grew again late in the nineteenth century (Jules Ferry laws for free, lay education). The Dreyfus Affair divided France into Dreyfus supporters (republicans, socialists, anticlericalists) and opponents (royalists, conservatives, the Catholic Church) (p. 625).
- The 1905 law separated church and state. It is the backbone of *laïcité*, the separation of private (religious) from public life. Laïcité rests on equal treatment of citizens. It differs from anticlericalism because it need not imply hostility to religion (p. 625).
- Religion still causes conflict: the Headscarf Debate (2005 law against "conspicuous" religious symbols in schools), the April 2011 face-covering ban, and the September 2011 street-prayer ban. Marine Le Pen's criticism of street prayers is reported. Debate continues on whether these laws integrate or alienate Muslims (pp. 625–626).
- Beyond France, there was a persistent retreat of religious institutions from the public square, though secularization of individual piety is debated. The church was often associated with the unpopular ancien régime (p. 626).
- Church officials resisted separation, and religious parties formed. The Tories rallied around "For Church and King." In the Netherlands, Catholic and Protestant parties came to make common cause against secular parties, and the Christian Democratic Appeal is explicitly nondenominational (p. 626).
- In many countries in Europe and Latin America, Christian Democratic parties combine conservative social positions with an activist economic position, and many take an organic or corporatist view of the economy. Liberal parties defend individual rights, are secular, and favor free markets (p. 627).

**Class (pp. 627–629).**
- Lipset and Rokkan call the preceding ones "pre-industrial cleavages"; class became salient with the Industrial Revolution. Urban–rural conflict is horizontal (between sectors). Class conflict is vertical (within sectors: labor versus property or capital). It typically involves using the state to redistribute from rich to poor. Capitalists favor the free market, a small state and a restricted franchise; workers favor more state intervention and a wider franchise (p. 627).
- Salience rose in the nineteenth century with franchise demands. The 1832 Great Reform Act still let only about one in five male adults vote. The 1867 Act extended the vote to all working-class males (p. 627). Workers became relevant at the ballot box (p. 628).
- "Left" and "right" come from 1791 seating in the French National Assembly (monarchists on the right, bourgeois reformers on the left). The center of gravity moved left over the nineteenth century. By the twentieth, the Left meant Communist, Social Democratic and Labor parties, and the Right meant Christian Democratic, Conservative and Liberal parties (p. 628).
- **Marxist expectation:** industrial working class expands, franchise expands, so large Socialist majorities legislate a transition to socialism (Przeworski and Sprague 1988) (p. 628). Three factors inhibited this:
  1. *Not all actors voted by class.* Marx distinguished a class "in itself" (objective relation to the means of production) from a class "for itself" (conscious of that status). The socialist project is class formation. Marxist orthodoxy held it inevitable; individual Marxists differed on how far leaders' organization could speed or delay it. Class consciousness took longer than expected, and scholars labelled continued bourgeois voting "false consciousness" (Engels). Whatever the explanation, a nontrivial portion of manual laborers voted against left parties and a substantial share of managers and professionals voted for them (pp. 628–629).
  2. *Workers were never a numerical majority* (Przeworski and Sprague). Socialists broadened their appeals or governed in coalition, which ultimately diluted class salience in some countries (p. 629).
  3. *Structural and organizational limits.* Investors discipline radical policy (the "structural dependence of the state on capital," Chapters 6 and 9). Michels argued that in all sufficiently complex organizations a division of labor must arise between rank-and-file members and professional managers. Leaders develop different lifestyles, skills and interests, so the organization comes to pursue goals different from its original ones. This is **Michels's iron law of oligarchy** (p. 629). The authors say it has been used to explain trade unions, parties, religious denominations, corporations and nonprofits (p. 629).

*Intuition note:* Having an objective class position is not the same as acting on it. The text treats a distance between "class in itself" and "class for itself" as an explanation for why class voting was weaker than Marxist theory predicted (pp. 628–629).

**Post-material (pp. 629–632).**
- Lipset and Rokkan observed that European party systems were remarkably stable in the twentieth century. The older cleavages were activated when new groups were being mobilized (new elites against the nobility and clergy; workers and later women when the franchise expanded) (pp. 629–630).
- **Freezing hypothesis:** party systems "froze" with universal suffrage in the 1920s. Barriers to entry for new parties became too high, because there was no untapped electoral base. Unrepresented positions would stay unrepresented, or existing parties would adapt. It was used to explain why European party systems had similar ideological dimensions and why 1960s parties were those of the 1920s–1930s. The modal system had two left parties (Socialist, Communist), a Conservative (often Christian Democratic) and a Liberal party on the right (p. 630).
- **Challenge 1: left-libertarian parties** (Kitschelt 1988), 1960s–70s. They are less tied to the working class and emphasize issues like environmentalism and immigration. Inglehart argues they respond to a value shift from "materialist" to "post-materialist" values, held by generations raised in plenty who prioritize human freedom (multiculturalism, gender and racial equality, reproductive choice) (p. 630–631). Kitschelt describes them as critical of growth priority, elite bargaining and the bureaucratic welfare state, linking libertarian commitments to a leftist concern for equality. He reports a strong correlation between development and the success of parties like the Greens. His survey evidence suggests they disproportionately draw younger, well-educated middle-class voters, employed in human services (p. 631).
- **Challenge 2: populist extreme right** (1980s–90s). Several scholars link it to the same post-material cleavage, as a reaction to the libertarian left. These parties stress traditional values and portray immigration as a threat to national identity and to native workers' jobs. They also exploit the shift to a postindustrial economy, in which, for example, the uneducated feel alienated (p. 631). Slogans are given: Le Pen in 1984; the German Republicans (p. 631).
- **Debate on real change:** left-libertarian parties polled about 5 percent on average in Western Europe in the 1990s, and populist extreme-right parties about the same. The authors say these averages hide variation but it is hard to argue for large-scale change in aggregate alignments. Left and right as wholes show "extraordinary persistence" (Gallagher, Laver and Mair 2006). Dominant parties are largely those of the 1920s (pp. 631–632).

**Ethnic and linguistic (pp. 632–633).**
- Definitions of "ethnic" vary, but a common feature is descent. Members share some attribute with each other more closely than with non-members, believed to be related to descent. Common ancestry claims are often myths. Chandra lists genetic, cultural-historical, and lifetime markers of descent (names, tribal markings) (p. 632).
- Eligibility is not the same as membership. There is a tension between groups "in themselves" and "for themselves." Individuals may choose how much to identify (Clark's Irish American example). Others, such as gatekeepers or officials, may classify them. Ethnic identity is not always easily identified by others, even by co-ethnics (Habyarimana et al. 2005) (pp. 632–633).
- *Primordialists* see ethnic attachments as transmitted automatically and group composition as naturally, externally determined. *Constructivists* see attachments as socially constructed, the result of past choices. *Instrumentalism* might be thought of as a subcategory of constructivism: instrumentalists see group attachments as serving some other purpose, such as political survival or access to state resources (p. 633).
- **Ethnic party** (Chandra 2005): appeals to voters as champion of one ethnic category or set of categories to the exclusion of others, centrally to its mobilizing strategy. The key aspect is exclusion. It may champion several categories only by identifying a common ethnic enemy (p. 633). Probably the first were Jewish parties in the Russian and Austro-Hungarian empires and the Swedish Party in Finland. Ethnic parties are found in many regions. Latin America historically had few, but indigenous ethnic parties have recently emerged in Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador and Venezuela (p. 633).

### Theorizing about politicized cleavages (pp. 633–640)
Why are some systems divided mainly along ethnic lines and others along class, religion, language or region? Comparative political scientists "have not developed a fully worked-out theory." Recent research suggests that the distribution of individual attributes and electoral institutions are likely key parts (pp. 633–634). The authors draw heavily on Chandra and Boulet (2003).

**Attributes and identity categories (p. 634).**
- Individuals have a repertoire of **attributes** (religion, language, class, gender, skin color...). Attributes make them eligible for membership in an **identity category**, a social group where one can place oneself. Table 14.4 lists examples (class, skin color, nationality, profession, region, origin, height) (p. 634).
- Chandra and Boulet take attributes as given, self-evident and sticky. Identity categories are socially constructed, by the choices of social actors over time and the institutional context. This is a "thinly constructivist" approach (p. 634).

**Latent categories (p. 635).** Assignment to a category rests on a shared understanding of how attributes map to groups, built over long periods. Example: a country with region (North/South) and language (French/Dutch). Table 14.5 gives cell shares: Northerner–French $a$, Northerner–Dutch $b$, Southerner–French $c$, Southerner–Dutch $d$. Table 14.6 lists nine potential (latent) categories with sizes:
- Northerner $a+b$
- Southerner $c+d$
- French speaker $a+c$
- Dutch speaker $b+d$
- the four single cells $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$
- Everyone $a+b+c+d$

Which ones are activated or politicized is "not immediately obvious" (p. 635).

**Cross-cutting versus reinforcing attributes (pp. 636–637).**
- If attributes are uncorrelated and fairly evenly distributed, there may be a propensity for each combination to be thought of as a separate group. This is **cross-cutting** attributes (Table 14.7: 0.25 in each cell). All else equal, North vs. South, French vs. Dutch, and the four-way split are presumably equally likely to be activated (p. 636).
- If attributes are highly correlated, they are **reinforcing** (Table 14.8: N-French 0.03, N-Dutch 0.57, S-French 0.36, S-Dutch 0.04). The effective number of attribute repertoires is likely smaller: knowing a person is from the North predicts Dutch with fair confidence. It seems plausible, all else equal, that the activated categories will be "French-speaking southerners" and "Dutch-speaking northerners" (p. 636).
- A similar distribution is found in contemporary Belgium: three constitutional communities (French-speaking Wallonia, Dutch-speaking Flanders, German-speaking East). The Wallonia–Flanders line is said to mark the northern reach of Roman Gaul in the fourth century. It is "centuries older" than the Mason-Dixon Line (pp. 636–637).

*Intuition note:* When two attributes are strongly correlated, knowing one attribute lets you predict the other with fair confidence, so the effective number of attribute repertoires is likely to be smaller. When they are uncorrelated and fairly evenly distributed, each combination may be thought of as a separate group, and the groupings are presumably about equally likely to be activated (p. 636).

**Electoral institutions (pp. 637–638).** Salience of such boundaries rises and falls across times and places. This suggests looking for other factors that might matter; electoral rules are one such factor (p. 637).
- **Countries A and B**, identical attributes (Table 14.9: all four cells N-French 0.40, N-Dutch 0.10, S-French 0.40, S-Dutch 0.10). Office needs 50 percent in A, 60 percent in B (p. 637).
- *Country A:* using the logic of least minimal winning coalitions (Chapter 12), N-French and N-Dutch have incentives to ally ($0.40+0.10=0.50$), and likewise S-French and S-Dutch ($0.40+0.10=0.50$). Each reaches 50 percent, so elections are effectively coin flips. Whoever builds better ties to Dutch speakers wins. Parties compete on interlinguistic cooperation, reinforcing regional divisions. The main politicized cleavage is likely regional, with regional parties (p. 638). Footnote 7: a French-speaker coalition across regions (80 percent) would split spoils among more people, so lower average payoffs (p. 638, n. 7).
- *Country B:* a coalition of northern and southern French speakers could expect 80 percent ($0.40+0.40$). Dutch speakers would be a permanent minority. The politicized cleavage and party system would be linguistic. Only the threshold changed (p. 638).
- **Countries C and D**, both at 60 percent. C has Table 14.9, D has Table 14.10 (N-French 0.25, N-Dutch 0.35, S-French 0.15, S-Dutch 0.15). We might expect the activated cleavage to be linguistic in C and regional in D (p. 638).

*Intuition note:* In the text's account, entrepreneurs seeking office choose groups that are the right size to build a winning coalition. A change in the threshold or in the population shares changes which building blocks work best (pp. 638, 640).

**Box 14.3 — Posner (2004): Chewas and Tumbukas in Zambia and Malawi (pp. 639–640).**
- The Malawi–Zambia border was arbitrarily drawn in 1891 by the British South Africa Company. About two-thirds of Chewas and Tumbukas ended up in Malawi, one-third in Zambia (p. 639).
- Posner studied four border villages (a Chewa and a Tumbuka village on each side). The groups are culturally distinct in both countries (language, dance, bridewealth: seven cows versus one chicken), with equally strong differences on both sides (p. 639).
- Salience differs. Would not vote for a presidential candidate from the other group: 61 percent in Malawi, 21 percent in Zambia. Would not marry a member of the other group: 55 percent in Malawi, 24 percent in Zambia (p. 639).
- Both countries use single-member district plurality (SMDP), are former British colonies, and have experienced one-party and multiparty rule. So Posner looks elsewhere (p. 639).
- Population shares: Malawi, Chewas about 57 percent and Tumbukas about 12 percent. These groups have a realistic chance in SMDP elections, so the cleavage is politicized (Malawi Congress Party as Chewa party, AFORD as Tumbuka party). Zambia, Chewas about 7 percent and Tumbukas 4 percent. They have little realistic chance of winning SMDP elections, so entrepreneurs politicize a regional cleavage: easterners (Chewas and Tumbukas together) versus others. Chewas and Tumbukas (easterners) have to work together, rather than against one another, if they are to have any hope of winning political power (p. 640).
- Conclusion given: political actors choose divisions that define the most usefully sized building blocks; politicization "will ultimately depend on the interaction" between institutions and the distribution of latent cleavages (p. 640).

**Summary of the causal story (p. 640).** Each country has latent cleavages determined by its distribution of individual attributes. Which become politicized is influenced by electoral institutions (Figure 14.1, not on these pages). An implication begins on p. 640: the politicized cleavages, and hence party types, can change either (sentence cut off at the end of this part).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A country has two attributes: zone (Hill/Coast) and occupation (Farmer/Trader). Shares: Hill-Farmer 0.30, Hill-Trader 0.20, Coast-Farmer 0.30, Coast-Trader 0.20 (sum $=1.00$).

Possible two-cell building blocks: Hill $=0.30+0.20=0.50$; Coast $=0.30+0.20=0.50$; Farmers $=0.30+0.30=0.60$; Traders $=0.20+0.20=0.40$.

- **Threshold 50%:** Hill (0.50) and Coast (0.50) just win. Following the author's "smallest winning coalition" logic, they are the least costly winners. So a regional cleavage is the likely politicized one.
- **Threshold 55%:** Hill and Coast (0.50) fail. Traders (0.40) fail. Only Farmers (0.60) wins among these blocks. The likely politicized cleavage is occupational, and Traders would be a permanent minority.

The attribute distribution is identical, yet the winning threshold changes the likely cleavage, as in countries A and B.

## Concepts
- [[party-as-part-of-society]] (new) — a party represents a part, not the whole, of society; formed when like-minded office seekers pursue shared goals (p. 620)
- [[social-cleavage]] (new) — a social division (urban–rural, confessional, class, ethnic...) along which political conflict and parties can form (p. 622)
- [[latent-vs-politicized-cleavage]] (new) — potential identity categories exist ("latent"); only some are activated or politicized (p. 635)
- [[laicite]] (new) — separation of private (religious) and public life, without necessarily implying hostility to religion (p. 625)
- [[class-in-itself-for-itself]] (new) — class as objective position versus class as conscious membership; moving from the first to the second is class formation (p. 628)
- [[iron-law-of-oligarchy]] (new) — Michels: organizational leaders develop distinct goals that become dominant (p. 629)
- [[freezing-hypothesis]] (new) — Lipset–Rokkan: Western European party systems froze after universal suffrage in the 1920s (p. 630)
- [[post-materialism]] (new) — Inglehart's claimed value shift from materialist to post-materialist values (expanding human freedom) in advanced industrial democracies, linked to new parties (pp. 630–631)
- [[ethnic-group-and-party]] (new) — descent-based attributes; an ethnic party champions some categories to the exclusion of others (pp. 632–633)
- [[primordialism-constructivism-instrumentalism]] (new) — ethnic identity as given and external versus socially constructed versus constructed for a purpose (p. 633)
- [[attribute-and-identity-category]] (new) — attribute: characteristic qualifying one for a category; identity category: social group where one can place oneself (p. 634)
- [[cross-cutting-vs-reinforcing-cleavages]] (new) — uncorrelated attributes cross-cut; correlated attributes reinforce (p. 636)
- [[institutions]] — here, electoral thresholds and SMDP rules that shape which cleavages are politicized (pp. 637–640)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Cleavage politicization via winning thresholds ↔ social choice: coalition building to a required majority — the threshold determines which groups are usable building blocks
- [structural-analogy] Freezing hypothesis ↔ path dependence: early mobilization patterns persist and constrain later options
- [metaphor] Michels's iron law of oligarchy ↔ principal–agent problem: leaders pursue goals distinct from those of the rank and file
- [structural-analogy] Class "for itself" ↔ collective action problem: a shared objective position does not by itself produce coordinated action

## Questions this raises
- If a country's attribute distribution changes (migration, birth rates) or its electoral threshold changes, how quickly would politicized cleavages and parties change?
- How would the threshold argument work under proportional representation, where there is no single winning threshold?
- Why do the authors expect coalitions to form at the "least" minimal winning size, and how well does that fit real parties?
- Are the left-libertarian and populist-right parties best seen as cleavage parties or as responses by existing parties?
