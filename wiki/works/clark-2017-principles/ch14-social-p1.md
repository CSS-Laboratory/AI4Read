# Social Cleavages and Party Systems (part 1): What parties do, party-system types, counting parties, and where parties come from

## L0 — One line
Parties structure politics, recruit elites, mobilize voters and link rulers to citizens. Party systems are classified by the number and size of parties (measured by the "effective number of parties"). Their origins are explained by an interplay of social demands and institutional channelling.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks why democracies have the party systems they do: few or many parties, and divided by ethnicity, class, religion, language or region. This part first defines a party and lists its four functions. It then sets out five party-system types, and introduces the effective number of parties, which counts parties weighted by vote or seat share. It ends by opening the question of origins: a "bottom-up" primordial view versus a "top-down" instrumental view. The authors say both matter: social demand largely drives party formation, but political institutions channel it. Party systems are not intentionally designed by particular individuals; they arise from the interplay of decisions by leaders and voters.

**Prerequisites:** basic idea of elections, legislatures and parliamentary versus presidential government; squares and reciprocals; Chapters 10–13 are referred to (dictatorships, chaos/Arrow theorem, government formation, electoral systems).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A party is a group of officials or would-be officials linked to a sizeable group of citizens, with winning or keeping power as a chief aim. This goal separates parties from interest groups. Parties also exist in dictatorships (pp. 604–605).
- Parties perform four functions: structuring the political world, recruiting and socializing elites, mobilizing the masses, and linking rulers and ruled (p. 605).
- To some extent, the recruiting and socializing role is more important in parliamentary than in presidential democracies, where primaries and outsider presidents weaken party gatekeeping (pp. 606–607).
- The link between rulers and ruled runs both ways: voters use parties to hold officials accountable, while leaders use parties and whips to discipline legislators (pp. 608–610).
- Five party-system types exist: nonpartisan, single-party, one-party dominant, two-party, multiparty. Single-party systems occur only in dictatorships (p. 611).
- Simple party counts can hide big differences between systems. The effective number of parties $=1/\sum v_i^2$ weights parties by size. A common rule treats a value below three as two-party and three or more as multiparty (pp. 616–617).
- Two views of party origins, primordial (bottom-up) and instrumental (top-down), resemble demand and supply. The authors hold that both interact (p. 619).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter overview and introduction (pp. 603–604)
- Epigraph: "Party is organized opinion" (Disraeli) (p. 603).
- Political scientists often classify democracies by party system, mainly by the number and size of the parties. Example: the United States is a two-party system and the Netherlands a multiparty one (p. 604).
- Government type and electoral system are usually written into a constitution or legal document. The party system is not (p. 604).
- A party system arises from the evolving nature of political competition. Like an economic market or the grammar of a language, it is not intentionally designed by particular individuals. It arises from the interplay of decisions by actors such as party leaders and voters (p. 604).
- Thesis: socioeconomic forces largely drive the structure of the party system. They are channelled in powerful ways by political institutions (those of the previous two chapters). Hence the structure results from the interplay of social and institutional forces (p. 604).
- Plan: what a party is and does; types of party system; social and institutional factors behind the number and types of parties (p. 604).

### Political parties: what are they and what do they do? (pp. 604–610)
**Definition.** Shively's definition (quoted by the authors): a party is a group of officials or would-be officials linked with a sizeable group of citizens in an organization. A chief object of the organization is to ensure that its officials attain or keep power (p. 604).
- A party includes actors from officeholders to regular voters to people who give money or time (p. 604).
- The goal of attaining power distinguishes parties from interest groups. Interest groups (e.g. the National Rifle Association, American Medical Association, Greenpeace) try to influence policy without taking power (pp. 604–605).
- Parties are not restricted to democracies or elections. Communist parties in China and Cuba control power without holding national-level elections. So parties can serve purposes beyond getting officials elected (p. 605).
- Four functions are discussed: (a) structuring the political world, (b) recruiting and socializing elites, (c) mobilizing the masses, (d) linking rulers and ruled (p. 605).

**(a) Structuring the political world (pp. 605–606).**
- *For elites.* Imagine no parties: every legislator is an independent, with no institutionalized link to others. With many issues and disagreement, policymaking would probably be chaotic and unstable. This is the prediction of the chaos theorem and Arrow's impossibility theorem (Chapter 11) (p. 605).
- Parties help by coordinating individual legislators and simplifying the issue space (p. 605).
- Aldrich (1995) argues that the need to coordinate votes among representatives with similar but not identical preferences explains why parties formed in the eighteenth-century United States (p. 605).
- *For voters.* Parties give "information shortcuts" (Downs; Fiorina). Voters rarely have the time, energy or inclination to gather information. Party labels and attachments tell them how to feel about issues. A voter may oppose a bill simply because their party dislikes it (p. 605).
- Party identification, quoting Campbell et al.: an attachment to a party that helps citizens locate themselves and others on the political landscape. Saying "Democrat" or "Labour" conveys a lot about likely stances. This helps explain why many people see politics as the fortunes of parties rather than the fate of issues (p. 606).

*Intuition note.* For elites, parties coordinate legislators and simplify the issue space. For voters, party labels give a shortcut instead of reading up on each issue. So parties give both groups some order in politics.

**(b) Recruitment and socialization of elites (pp. 606–607).**
- In many countries it is extremely difficult to be elected as an independent. Being chosen as a party's candidate is often a necessary condition for a successful run. Cabinet posts are often restricted to senior party members (p. 606).
- Gallagher, Laver and Mair (quoted): access to power requires being accepted by a party. Ministers have usually spent years as party members, learning to see politics from the party's perspective (p. 606).
- Shively's British example: an ambitious young woman works for a party (e.g. the Conservatives), is nominated by a local selection committee, probably in a hopeless district first, then a decent one. In the House she is "molded and guided by the party" and rises if she is a faithful party voter. The prime minister is chosen in an internal vote of the party's MPs. According to Shively this is essentially the only way to make a political career in Great Britain (p. 606).
- This role is, to some extent, more important in parliamentary than in presidential democracies. Parties are not monopolistic gatekeepers in presidential systems (p. 607).
  - Presidents are much more likely to appoint nonpartisan ministers (Chapter 12), because there is no vote of no confidence and so less need to negotiate with legislative parties (p. 607).
  - Primaries (the United States and, increasingly, Latin America) weaken party control. Candidates can appeal directly to voters, as illustrated by Tea Party candidates in Republican primaries. Primaries are often posited (Aldrich 1995) as the key reason for weak party organization and candidate-centred campaigns in the United States (p. 607).
  - Complete outsiders can win the presidency: Alberto Fujimori, an academic and TV presenter, won in Peru in 1990. Such an election to the equivalent of prime minister is "almost unthinkable" (p. 607).
  - Inference (citing Gallagher, Laver and Mair): this suggests that leaders' political direction in presidential democracies is inclined to be less stable and more unpredictable than in parliamentary ones (p. 607).

**(c) Mobilization of the masses (pp. 607–608).**
- Voting is costly (time, alternatives, weather), and the chance that one vote decides the election is incredibly small. So people will not necessarily vote. Organizations such as parties therefore have a significant role in getting people to the polls (p. 607).
- Parties are well placed: membership and organization extend deeply into each constituency, and they have a strong incentive to get (at least particular) people to the polls, because they want to win (p. 607).
- Mobilization matters outside elections too. In 1968 a student-led protest in France grew as police used tear gas, water cannons, mass arrests and beatings. The trade unions joined, and as many as ten million workers went on strike in two weeks. De Gaulle then spoke on radio, played on fears of a Communist revolution, said he would dissolve the National Assembly and hold elections, and called on people to defend the republic. Hitchcock is quoted: perhaps half a million people marched up the Champs-Elysées behind Gaullist and right-wing leaders. About a month later the electorate gave the Gaullist candidates a huge majority (pp. 607–608).
- Parties can also mobilize against a regime. Examples: Our Ukraine in the 2004 Orange Revolution; the Congress Party in India and the National Front for Liberation in Algeria, originally set up to mobilize against British and French colonial rule (p. 608).

**(d) The link between rulers and ruled (p. 608).**
- Democratic theorists say governments should reflect citizens' preferences. The party is the primary vehicle ensuring this (Chapter 12). The authors say it is "probably" the central function of a party in a democracy to represent, articulate and champion its membership's interests (p. 608).
- Parties are the main means by which democracies can be induced to be responsive. Schattschneider is quoted as saying that "modern democracy is unthinkable save in terms of parties" (p. 608).
- Accountability: policy results from the collective action of many officeholders. The party, as a collective group, is the main means by which voters can hold them collectively responsible. Fiorina: collective responsibility exists only through the party (p. 608).

**The link runs both ways (p. 610).**
- Leaders can use promotion, campaign resources, threats of expulsion and the like to induce legislators to vote the "right" way (p. 610).
- A **whip** ensures that members attend legislative sessions and vote as the leadership wants. Whips are frequently some of the most important political actors in a country, though the public rarely sees them as whips (p. 610).
- Parties can also control citizens, most egregiously under authoritarian regimes (Chapter 10) (p. 610).

**Box 14.1: Party whips in the United Kingdom (pp. 609–610).**
- The term came into use in the English Parliament in the 1880s, from the "whipper-in" at a fox hunt who keeps the pack together. Whips matter most when government and opposition voting strengths are close, as in 1992–1997 (p. 609).
- Duties: (a) keep legislators informed of coming business; (b) keep voting strength up by ensuring attendance and voting as the leadership wants; (c) pass legislators' opinions up to the leadership (p. 609).
- A weekly circular, also called a whip, marks each debate in which there will be a vote with "Your attendance is absolutely essential", underlined once (routine, attendance optional), twice (required unless "paired" with an absent Opposition member) or three times (highly important, pairing not normally allowed). This gives one-, two- and three-line whips. Three-line whips apply to votes such as no confidence and second readings of significant bills (p. 609).
- Sanctions for a backbencher (not in the cabinet or shadow cabinet): being overlooked for promotion, little support in reelection, deselection or a less safe seat, or expulsion. Cabinet ministers who defy the whips are immediately dismissed. Nine Conservative MPs were suspended in 1994 for voting against John Major's government on the European Union (p. 609).
- Whips can be forceful, even using "blackmail, verbal intimidation, sexual harassment and physical aggression" (Dixon 1996). They sometimes bring very sick MPs to vote. In the Spriggs case under Callaghan, as told by Joe Ashton, a vote was tied 310–310. Spriggs was brought by ambulance after a severe heart attack; after the whips looked in, one of them turned the knob on the heart machine and said "There, you've lost—it's 311" (pp. 609–610).

### Party systems (pp. 611–617)
**Five types** (classified by the number and size of parties) (p. 611):
- *Nonpartisan democracy*: no official political parties.
- *Single-party system*: only one party is legally allowed to hold power.
- *One-party dominant system*: several parties may legally operate, but only one has a realistic chance of power.
- *Two-party system*: only two major parties have a realistic chance of power.
- *Multiparty system*: more than two parties have a realistic chance of power.
Single-party systems exist only in dictatorships. Nonpartisan and one-party dominant systems are relatively rare in democracies. So scholars usually distinguish two-party from multiparty democracies (p. 611).

**Nonpartisan democracy (p. 611).**
- Parties are often called a necessary condition of modern democracy (Lipset, Schattschneider, Katz are cited). Yet a small handful of democracies are nonpartisan (p. 611).
- Absence may be due to a legal ban or because parties have not yet formed. George Washington's administration and the first sessions of Congress were nonpartisan (p. 611).
- Today: Kiribati, Marshall Islands, Micronesia, Nauru, Palau, Tuvalu, all tiny. Nauru, Palau and Tuvalu have under 15,000 residents. Footnote 1: Micronesia, Palau and Tuvalu are widely accepted as nonpartisan, but for the others, especially Kiribati, there is disagreement over whether parties existed in certain periods. Much depends on how one defines a party (p. 611).
- Subnational examples: Nebraska's unicameral legislature, some Swiss cantons, some Canadian territories (p. 611).

**Single-party system (pp. 611–612).**
- Liberia is generally considered the first: the True Whig Party ruled 1878–1980, until a military coup. Current examples: China, Cuba, Eritrea, Laos, North Korea, Syria, Turkmenistan, Vietnam (p. 611).
- Minor parties are sometimes allowed, but are always legally required to accept the dominant party's leadership. In Communist Poland, the PUWP let the ZSL, SD and a Catholic group coexist under separate labels. In elections their candidates had to be on a single PUWP list, so the PUWP approved them in advance (pp. 611–612).
- All single-party systems occur in dictatorships (p. 612).

**One-party dominant system (p. 612; Box 14.2, pp. 613–614).**
- Many occur in dictatorships, which allow some opposition parties but use various means to keep them from power (Lust-Okar). Examples: MPS in Chad, CPDM in Cameroon (p. 612).
- Not all are necessarily undemocratic. In democracies, long dominance is normally attributed to long-running popularity, a divided opposition, efficient patronage and, occasionally, electoral fraud. Examples cited: ANC in South Africa since 1994, Botswana Democratic Party since 1966, the Democratic Party in the US South (1880s–1960s), the LDP in Japan (1955–1993), Congress in India (1947–1977) (p. 612).
- Unlike a single-party system, the availability of other active parties guarantees fairly open debate and provides for possible long-term flexibility (Shively) (p. 612).

*Box 14.2, the LDP in Japan (pp. 613–614).*
- The LDP regularly won over 50 percent of the vote and formed single-party majority governments from 1955 to 1993. It was formed in 1955 by a merger of the Liberal Party and the Japan Democratic Party against the Japan Socialist Party. The merger was strongly supported by the United States, and the CIA spent millions helping the LDP over two decades (p. 613).
- It has had no well-defined ideology. Between six and thirteen factions have existed, each led by a senior figure who offers money and contacts. Each LDP MP is typically in one. The desire to win elections and share spoils held the factions together. Much of the real power struggle was between factions inside the LDP (p. 613).
- Under the single nontransferable vote (SNTV) (Chapter 13), candidates of one party compete with each other. LDP legislators therefore cultivated personal reputations, and Japan was seen as the most candidate-centred democracy of the period. They gave constituency service (koenkai networks, events, favours) and pork (pp. 613–614).
- Hirano's example: Tanaka Kakuei took 11,000 people to a hot spring at a cost of $1.4 million, and brought the high-speed rail line to his home prefecture, with four of eight stops there (p. 614).
- The resulting multitiered patronage structure gave many people incentives to preserve the LDP's dominance. It depended on the "three bans": jiban (organized constituency), kaban (briefcase of money), kanban (prestigious appointments). It led to scandals, e.g. the 1976 Lockheed bribery scandal, with Tanaka arrested over $3 million in bribes (p. 614).
- End of dominance: after scandals and economic downturn, over fifty LDP members left (forming Shinseito and Sakigake). The LDP lost its majority and an opposition coalition took office in 1993 (p. 614).
- A 1994 reform package created a new electoral system, public party subsidies and stricter donation rules. Yet in 1994 the LDP was back in government with the Socialists. It was the largest party in 1996, 2000 and 2003, and regained a majority in 2005. It lost its upper-house majority in July 2007. In August 2009 its share of lower-house seats fell from over 60 percent to a quarter (p. 614).

**Two-party system (pp. 614–615).**
- Nearly all elected offices are held by candidates of one of two major parties. Success under a third label is difficult but not impossible (p. 614).
- Examples: Jamaica, the United Kingdom, the United States. In 2010 all 435 US House seats and 98 of 100 Senate seats were held by Democrats or Republicans. In Jamaica in 2007 all sixty seats went to the two main parties. In the UK in 2010 Conservatives and Labour won 87 percent of seats (p. 615).
- Despite the name, two-party systems usually have many more parties competing: 419 registered in the UK in 2011 and at least 50 national-level parties in the US. They count as two-party because only the two major ones usually expect to win (p. 615).

**Multiparty system (p. 615).**
- More than two parties have a realistic chance of power, separately or in a coalition. Most democracies are multiparty (examples: France, Israel, the Netherlands) (p. 615).
- Table 14.1, Knesset seats after 2009 (total 120): Kadima 28, Likud 27, Yisrael Beiteinu 15, Labor 13, Shas 11, United Torah Judaism 5, Ra'am-Ta'al 4, Ichud Leumi 4, Hadash 4, New Movement–Meretz 3, Habayit Hayehudi 3, National Democratic Assembly–Balad 3. No party came close to a majority. Netanyahu's coalition included Likud, Yisrael Beiteinu, Labor, Shas, United Torah Judaism and Habayit Hayehudi (p. 615).

### Counting parties: the effective number of parties (pp. 615–617)
**Problem.** Counting every party that contests elections would include joke parties (the "Monster Raving Loony Party" in the UK, the "Beer Drinkers Party" in Russia and others) (p. 616). Also, ten parties with equal vote shares are quite different from two parties with 90 percent and eight sharing the rest. Calling both "ten-party" loses this (p. 616).

**Measure** (Laakso and Taagepera 1979). Each party that wins votes or seats counts, but with a weight related to its share. For four parties with vote shares $v_1,\dots,v_4$:
$$\text{effective number of parties}=\frac{1}{v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2+v_4^2}.$$
- Four equal parties ($v_i=0.25$) (p. 616):
$$\frac{1}{0.25^2+0.25^2+0.25^2+0.25^2}=\frac{1}{0.25}=4.$$
- Two parties with 0.45 each, two with 0.05 each (p. 616):
$$\frac{1}{0.45^2+0.45^2+0.05^2+0.05^2}=\frac{1}{0.41}=2.44.$$
- The measure is desirable if one thinks the second situation is more like a two-party than a four-party system (p. 616).

*Intuition note.* Squaring shares makes big parties count much more than small ones. So the result is "how many equally sized parties would give this concentration". The same four parties can give 4 or about 2.4, depending on their sizes.

**Variants (p. 617).** Weighting by vote shares gives the *effective number of electoral parties*. Weighting by seat shares gives the *effective number of legislative parties*. A common rule: two-party system if the effective number is below three; multiparty system if three or more (p. 617).

- Ecuador has the highest effective number of electoral parties, and Brazil the highest effective number of legislative parties. In both, votes and seats were shared fairly evenly among very many parties (p. 619).

### Where do parties come from? (opening, p. 619)
- Two views of why democracies have the party systems they do (p. 619).
- **Primordial view ("bottom up").** Parties are the natural representatives of people with common interests. Society has "natural" divisions, or cleavages. Groups form around them, and parties emerge and evolve to represent them (p. 619).
- **Instrumental view ("top down").** Parties are teams of office seekers. Elites and entrepreneurs, perhaps with informational advantages and resources, see an opportunity to represent a previously unrepresented interest. They may even help citizens become aware of an interest, so they can "create" cleavages (p. 619).
- Analogy to supply and demand: the primordial view takes social demand as given. The instrumental view holds that "supply creates its own demand", as advertisers shape tastes (p. 619).
- Authors' position: understanding origins requires both. Social demands largely drive party formation, but political institutions, which structure the environment of would-be entrepreneurs and voters, channel them. The next sections look at social (primordial) and institutional (instrumental) forces and begin with types of parties, through cleavages and identity formation (p. 619).

*Intuition note.* Think of a market. Voters' interests are the demand and entrepreneurs' offers are the supply. The authors say that understanding where parties come from involves both sides interacting, because institutions channel social demands and structure the environment of entrepreneurs and voters.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Country X: three parties with vote shares 0.5, 0.3, 0.2.
- Squares: $0.5^2=0.25$, $0.3^2=0.09$, $0.2^2=0.04$. Sum $=0.38$.
- Effective number $=1/0.38\approx 2.63$.
- Under the common rule (below three is two-party) this is a "two-party" system, though three parties win votes.
- Compare Country Y: shares 0.4, 0.3, 0.3. Squares $0.16+0.09+0.09=0.34$, so $1/0.34\approx 2.94$. Still below three, but closer to it.
- Country Z: shares 1/3 each. Squares $=3\times(1/9)=1/3$, so the effective number is exactly 3 (multiparty by the rule). The more equal the shares, the higher the number.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Effective number of parties $1/\sum v_i^2$ ↔ economics: inverse of a concentration index (sum of squared market shares) — same formula, shares are of votes or seats instead of sales.
- [structural-analogy] Primordial versus instrumental party formation ↔ economics: demand-driven versus supply-driven markets — the text itself draws this analogy (p. 619).
- [structural-analogy] Parties coordinating legislators ↔ Arrow/chaos results in social choice: a party reduces the issue space and stabilizes outcomes (p. 605).
- [structural-analogy] Parties mobilizing voters ↔ paradox of voting: organizations pay the cost of turnout that individuals have little incentive to pay (p. 607).

## Questions this raises
- If the effective number of parties is a better measure than a raw count, which institutions (electoral rules, Chapter 13) push it down or up?
- Do social divisions create parties, or do parties create the divisions? What evidence would distinguish the primordial from the instrumental view?
- Is the "below three" threshold for two-party systems robust, or does it depend on the vote or seat measure used? (Cf. India 1984: 3.98 electoral, 1.69 legislative, p. 618.)
- How far does the one-party dominant democracy (LDP, ANC) differ in accountability from a dictatorship allowing weak opposition parties?
