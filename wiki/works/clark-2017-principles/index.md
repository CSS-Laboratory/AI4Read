# Clark, W. R., Golder, M., & Golder, S. N. (2013). *Principles of Comparative Politics* (2nd ed.). Washington, DC: CQ Press.

*Edition used in AI4Read: the library copy above. The catalog lists 3rd ed., CQ Press / SAGE, 2017; page numbers follow the library copy.*

## In one paragraph
This textbook introduces comparative politics as a cumulative science. It builds simple theories, often small strategic games, and tests their implications on cross-national evidence. It first explains what science is and what politics is. It then asks where states come from, why countries are democracies or dictatorships, how transitions happen, and whether regime type matters. Next it turns to the variety of dictatorships and the problems of group decision making (Condorcet and Arrow). It then covers how governments form and survive, how electoral rules shape party systems, and how federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review act as veto players. It ends with the consequences of democratic institutions. The recurring message is that outcomes depend on institutions and on the incentives of actors who use them. Institutional design involves trade-offs, not one best solution.

## The path through the work
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch01-introduction]] — Comparative politics studies politics predominantly within countries, and the authors adopt explicit cross-national comparison to introduce questions about the causes and consequences of democracy, dictatorship and their institutions, because they judge a series of single-country studies to be a less useful approach for this purpose.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch02-science]] — Science is a critically minded method of deducing testable implications from a theory and trying hard to show them false (to falsify them), so all scientific knowledge stays provisional.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch03-politics]] — Politics is behavior involving power or influence, and a simple sequential game of exit, voice and loyalty shows that a citizen can influence a state only if she has a credible exit threat and the state depends on her.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch04-origins-p1]] — A state is an organization that rules a territory by coercion; in the contractarian view people create it to escape a Hobbesian "state of nature" (life with no state, modelled as a game in which each person's best choice leaves both worse off), but this works only if its punishments are high enough and its taxes low enough.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch04-origins-p2]] — If rulers are self-interested actors who fight rivals, the predatory view explains the modern state as a by-product of war-making and resource extraction; and game theory shows that cooperation fails without a state in a one-shot game but can be sustained in an infinitely repeated one if players value the future enough.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch05-democracy]] — Turning an abstract concept like democracy into a measure always means simplifying and choosing, so measures are commonly evaluated by validity, reliability and replicability, and whether a measure is appropriate depends on the research question.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch06-economic-p1]] — Rich countries are mostly democratic, but this fact fits two theories; the authors argue that conditional transition probabilities in the 1950–1990 data favour modernization theory (income helps democracy emerge and survive) over Przeworski's survival story (income helps survival only).
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch06-economic-p2]] — Rulers are expected to accept limits on their power, and so democracy can emerge and survive, when they depend on elites whose mobile assets give them a credible exit threat. Income appears to raise the chance of democracy, while oil and other fixed assets (assets that cannot easily be turned into cash) appear to lower it.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch07-cultural-p1]] — The authors argue that cultural explanations of democracy are often vague, rest on one-time snapshots and have unclear causal direction, and that their own tests find no significant effect of a Muslim majority on democracy once wealth is taken into account.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch07-cultural-p2]] — Ultimatum- and Dictator-Game experiments in fifteen small-scale societies suggest that culture might be considered a shared way (evolved over many years) of playing everyday "games", which may make some societies better suited than others to the "game" of democracy.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch08-democratic-p1]] — Collective action theory helps explain why popular revolutions against dictatorships are rare, and preference falsification plus threshold ("tipping") models help explain why, when they come, they can arrive rapidly and as a surprise.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch08-democratic-p2]] — In the authors' Transition Game, a top-down transition to democracy can occur only when regime soft-liners wrongly believe the opposition is weak, so that, in effect, it happens through a mistake under incomplete information.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch08-democratic-p3]] — Bottom-up transitions are rare because of free riding and hidden preferences, and top-down transitions seem to need mistaken beliefs. Games of incomplete information show how such mistakes can arise and be analysed with expected payoffs and critical probabilities.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch09-democracy]] — Theory gives conflicting predictions about whether democracy raises growth, and the data show that democracies rarely do badly but do not beat all dictatorships, so the democracy–dictatorship split may not be the best way to explain differences in performance.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch10-varieties-p1]] — One useful way to classify dictatorships is by who forms the ruler's support coalition (the royal family, the military, or neither, which is civilian), and the authors argue that electoral competition is a second dimension along which dictatorships can be placed, not a separate type.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch10-varieties-p2]] — How long a dictatorship survives depends on its type, through the interests and exit options of its insiders, and on its institutions, but the effect of institutions is hard to estimate.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch10-varieties-p3]] — Selectorate theory says that if all leaders want to stay in office, the sizes of the winning coalition (W) and the selectorate (S) largely determine whether leaders are pushed to serve the public or only a few insiders.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch11-problems-p1]] — Majority rule, the core of democracy, can fail to give a stable group decision even when every voter is rational, and the fixes studied so far (other counting rules, agendas, restricted preferences) each cost something.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch11-problems-p2]] — Arrow's theorem shows that every group decision rule that guarantees a transitive (stable) group preference must give up at least one minimal fairness condition, so no perfect democratic institution exists.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch12-parliamentary-p1]] — Democracies are classed as parliamentary, presidential or semi-presidential by whether the legislature can remove the government and whether the head of state is popularly elected for a fixed term, and in parliamentary systems the type of government that forms depends on many factors, including whether the actors are office seeking or policy seeking.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch12-parliamentary-p2]] — Because politicians care about policy and not only office, "too small" and "too large" cabinets are common and understandable, and presidential cabinets tend to differ from parliamentary ones because presidents need no legislative majority to stay in office.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch12-parliamentary-p3]] — Semi-presidential systems split into premier-presidential and president-parliamentary types (cohabitation is discussed mainly for the latter), and, to a large extent, all three regime types can be viewed as systems of delegation whose problems (agency loss, adverse selection, moral hazard) can be mitigated by screening, selection and monitoring.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch13-elections-p1]] — Most political scientists sort electoral systems by the formula that turns votes into seats, and the majoritarian family (where those with the most votes win) contains many systems whose trade-offs differ in proportionality, accountability, strategic voting and minority representation.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch13-elections-p2]] — Proportional electoral systems use multimember districts and a quota or divisor to turn votes into seats, and their proportionality, party-versus-candidate focus and incentives depend on the formula, district magnitude, tiers, thresholds and ballot type.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch13-elections-p3]] — Mixed systems combine majoritarian and proportional formulas (more proportional when the list tier "compensates"), and the authors discuss several explanations of why countries choose their systems (party self-interest, general interest, colonial or historical precedent, accident); because those who won under the current rules usually decide on reform, major reform is unlikely unless they face an impending threat, though reform can happen "by accident".
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch14-social-p1]] — Parties structure politics, recruit elites, mobilize voters and link rulers to citizens. Party systems are classified by the number and size of parties (measured by the "effective number of parties"). Their origins are explained by an interplay of social demands and institutional channelling.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch14-social-p2]] — Parties arise around social cleavages, but which latent cleavage becomes a politicized one depends on the interaction between how attributes are distributed in society and electoral institutions such as the winning threshold.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch14-social-p3]] — The size of a party system depends on the interaction of social heterogeneity (the demand for parties) and the permissiveness of electoral rules (how much of that demand is allowed through).
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch15-institutional-p1]] — Federalism (a constitutional structure) should be kept conceptually separate from decentralization (where policymaking power actually sits), and the authors present federalism as one of three "institutional veto players", alongside bicameralism and constitutionalism.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch15-institutional-p2]] — A second legislative chamber and constitutional review by judges can both constrain legislative majorities, and how much they matter depends on the chamber's composition and power, and on how constitutional justice is organised.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch15-institutional-p3]] — Federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review can all be treated as institutional veto players, and adding veto players never enlarges, and moving them further apart shrinks, the set of policy changes that can win unanimous support, so policy is expected to be more stable.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch16-consequences-p1]] — Democracies tend to cluster into a majoritarian type that concentrates power and a consensus type that disperses it, and each type does better on a different set of democratic goals (accountability and mandates versus representation).
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch16-consequences-p2]] — Voters' demands over taxing and spending are likely to be related to income, but these demands affect policy only after institutions filter them; the authors focus on electoral rules (PR versus majoritarian). Scholars disagree about the exact mechanisms.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch16-consequences-p3]] — Ethnic diversity does not by itself clearly cause violence, and the effects of proportional representation (PR), the alternative vote and federalism on ethnic conflict are conditional and disputed, so institutional design involves trade-offs rather than a single fix.
- [[clark-2017-principles/ch16-consequences-p4]] — The authors find considerable support for the argument that presidential constitutions make democratic consolidation harder than parliamentary ones, particularly when the legislature is highly fragmented.

## Big ideas across units
- **Politics as strategy, solved with simple games.** Situations are modelled as games and solved by backward induction or equilibrium: exit-voice-loyalty, the state of nature, the Transition Game, the Ultimatum Game, principal–agent delegation, and Duverger-style party entry. Units: ch03-politics, ch04-origins-p1, ch04-origins-p2, ch06-economic-p2, ch07-cultural-p2, ch08-democratic-p1, ch08-democratic-p2, ch08-democratic-p3, ch12-parliamentary-p3, ch14-social-p3.
- **Science as falsification and critical tests.** Theories are judged by testable implications, and rival theories are separated by critical tests. Units: ch01-introduction, ch02-science, ch05-democracy (measurement), ch06-economic-p1, ch07-cultural-p1, ch14-social-p3.
- **Credible exit threats and dependence.** Those who control mobile assets and can leave are able to bargain with rulers who depend on them. This helps explain responsive states, the resource curse, redistribution limits and aid. Units: ch03-politics, ch06-economic-p2, ch09-democracy, ch10-varieties-p2, ch15-institutional-p1.
- **Hidden preferences, collective action and surprise.** Free riding and preference falsification make revolutions rare and surprising yet look inevitable afterwards. Mistaken beliefs under incomplete information drive top-down transitions. Units: ch08-democratic-p1, ch08-democratic-p2, ch08-democratic-p3, ch10-varieties-p1, ch10-varieties-p2, ch13-elections-p3.
- **Dictatorships differ, and the support coalition matters.** Type, insiders' exit options, institutions and the sizes of W and S shape survival and performance. Units: ch08-democratic-p2, ch09-democracy, ch10-varieties-p1, ch10-varieties-p2, ch10-varieties-p3.
- **Majority rule is unstable, so design means trade-offs.** Cycling, the chaos theorem and Arrow's theorem imply no perfect institution. The same stability-versus-responsiveness trade-off returns in veto player theory and in majoritarian versus consensus democracy. Units: ch11-problems-p1, ch11-problems-p2, ch15-institutional-p3, ch16-consequences-p1, ch16-consequences-p3.
- **Electoral rules channel social demand into party systems and policy.** Formulas, district magnitude and thresholds shape proportionality, the number of parties, and fiscal and ethnic-conflict outcomes. Units: ch13-elections-p1, ch13-elections-p2, ch13-elections-p3, ch14-social-p1, ch14-social-p2, ch14-social-p3, ch16-consequences-p2, ch16-consequences-p3.
- **Veto players, delegation and regime type shape how governments act and survive.** Units: ch12-parliamentary-p1, ch12-parliamentary-p2, ch12-parliamentary-p3, ch15-institutional-p1, ch15-institutional-p2, ch15-institutional-p3, ch16-consequences-p4.
