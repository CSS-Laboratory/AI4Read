# The Effect of Political Institutions on Fiscal Policy

## L0 — One line
Voters' demands over taxing and spending are likely to be related to income, but these demands affect policy only after institutions filter them; the authors focus on electoral rules (PR versus majoritarian). Scholars disagree about the exact mechanisms.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why does fiscal policy (taxing and spending) differ so much across rich democracies? The authors first present economic and cultural explanations: Wagner's law, the Meltzer-Richard model, the partisan model, and attitudes toward the poor. They then argue that these preferences are filtered through institutions. PR systems are associated with larger government and more redistribution. Three explanations are given: PR helps left-wing parties win and act. Large PR districts are expected to reduce pork-barrel spending. PR is linked to coalition governments, which are expected to spend more and borrow more. The authors stress that mechanisms remain debated, while institutional choices clearly matter for economic management.

**Prerequisites:** median voter idea; plurality (SMDP) versus PR electoral systems; coalition government; Prisoner's Dilemma; reading a scatter plot.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Political economy approach.** Economic policy is typically made by officials who may have goals other than stable growth, and it tends to have distributional effects. So the "benevolent social planner" assumption is rejected (p. 766).
- **Fiscal activity varies widely across OECD countries and rose over 1947–1997, and so did the variance between countries.** Wagner's law does not straightforwardly explain the continued, growing variation (pp. 767–769).
- **Meltzer-Richard logic.** Richer citizens are net contributors and poorer ones net recipients. Greater inequality implies a larger median-voter demand for redistribution. Unequal turnout is likely to weaken this link in practice (pp. 769–773).
- **Partisan and cultural explanations.** Evidence for the partisan model is mixed. Cross-national differences in left-wing strength and in attitudes toward the poor go with differences in government size (pp. 774–778).
- **Argument 1: PR facilitates left-wing governments.** Rodden uses geography and wasted votes. Iversen and Soskice use coalition bargaining. Left-wing governments are in office far more often under PR (pp. 778–783).
- **Argument 2: district size and the "law of 1/n".** Many small districts in SMDP are expected to encourage costly local projects. PR is expected to favour broader public goods (pp. 784–785).
- **Argument 3: composition of government.** This literature argues that more parties in government mean worse common-pool problems, so we would expect more spending and debt. Tsebelis offers a veto-player rejoinder (pp. 785–787).
- **Summary claim.** Institutions matter for macroeconomic policy, though mechanisms are debated (p. 787).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Setting up (p. 766)
- **Fiscal policy** = government policies on raising revenue (taxation) and on spending (p. 766). The authors say a full review of the institutions–economy literature would cover at least a whole book, so they cover only the institutions of Chapters 12–15 and fiscal policy (p. 766).
- **Political economy approach** rests on two assumptions: (a) economic policy is made by elected officials, or technocrats they appoint, who may have goals other than "stable growth"; (b) policies have distributional consequences (p. 766).
- Contrast with welfare economics: it assumes a "benevolent social planner" who weighs everyone's interests. Because this planner is one person, group-decision problems (Chapter 11) are assumed away. The authors instead see conflicts of interest between government and citizens, among citizens, and within governments. They focus on fiscal policy because it is used for redistribution, because raising revenue is central to being a state, and because it measures the size of government (p. 766).

### Cross-national variation (pp. 766–768)
- Figure 16.4 (Franzese 2002) shows, for 21 OECD countries in 1947–1997, central government revenues and expenditures as a % of GDP. The dot is the country mean, the box is ±1 standard deviation, and the whiskers are the minimum and maximum (pp. 766–767).
- The US and Switzerland vary little over time. Belgium, the Netherlands and Sweden vary a lot (p. 766).
- Fiscal activity is low (near or below 50% of GDP) in the US, Japan, Canada, Spain and Switzerland. It is high (above 75%) in France, Belgium and the Netherlands (p. 767).

### Economic and cultural determinants (pp. 767–778)

**Wagner's law.** Government grows as countries become more industrialized, often read as "as they become wealthier" (pp. 767–768). Figure 16.5 shows the OECD yearly mean rising over the postwar period. The boxes and whiskers also lengthen, so cross-national variance rose too (p. 768). Franzese suggests this variance reflects different responses to the 1970s oil crises (n. 18, p. 768). Wagner's law gives no straightforward explanation for the continued variation among countries that were probably becoming more similar in wealth and industrialization (pp. 768–769).

**The Meltzer-Richard setup** (p. 769):
- Every citizen is taxed at the same rate $0<t<1$ and receives an equal subsidy $s$. The subsidy stands for the value the citizen puts on state-provided public goods.
- Tax bill (increasing in gross income $y_i$):
$$T = y_i t$$
- Overall benefit to citizen $i$:
$$B_i = y_i + s - y_i t$$
- If taxes are non-distortionary (they do not change income by altering the work–leisure trade-off), the citizen cares only about the net effect $s - y_i t$ (p. 769).
- With a balanced budget in the medium run, total subsidies equal total taxes. Some citizens are then net recipients and some net contributors (p. 769).
- Figure 16.6 uses $t = 20\%$ and $s = \$4{,}100$ (n. 19). The citizen with income slightly above $20,000, the average, breaks even. This is because of the balanced-budget assumption (n. 20). Below-average earners are net recipients and above-average earners are net contributors (pp. 769–770).
- Because high-income citizens are net contributors, they would prefer a zero tax rate and no subsidies. Low-income citizens can be expected to support even higher rates, getting the full benefit $B$ but paying only a fraction of the cost (p. 770).
- A higher $t$ steepens the tax-bill line. Net benefits to below-average earners rise and net contributions from above-average earners rise (p. 770).
- Footnote 19: any numbers give a similar figure, unless subsidies are so high that everyone is a net recipient (unsustainable deficits) or rates so high that everyone is a net contributor (which might lead to a tax revolt or costly repression) (n. 19, p. 769).

*Intuition note.* When everyone pays the same share and gets the same subsidy, the poor gain and the rich lose. So what people want from fiscal policy is likely to be related to their income.

**Robustness to realism** (p. 771):
- Real systems are not flat-rate or equal-subsidy. Taxes tend to be progressive, which would bend the tax-bill line upward. Spending tends to target the below-average-income group, which would tilt the subsidy line downward (p. 771).
- The authors say neither change alters the central result that tax-and-spend systems split people into net contributors and net recipients, with the rich mostly in the first group. The only systems without this feature would be a regressive tax system, subsidies going disproportionately to the rich, or no budget constraint (so subsidies could be so large that everyone is a net recipient). They say nothing like these is observed in modern polities (p. 771).
- Occupy Wall Street objection: loopholes may lower the rich's effective tax rate. The authors think it unlikely that they pay less in total than middle-income earners. The top 1% still provide a very large share of revenue. Governments may not close loopholes partly because these actors hold very liquid assets that governments want invested (p. 771). Oil-rich countries with large universal subsidies would shift the intersection of the lines up and to the right, but the pattern would probably remain (n. 21, p. 771).

**Meltzer-Richard model** (pp. 772–773):
- If different income groups could dictate policy, the average earner is indifferent to the existing system versus zero taxes and subsidies. Richer voters want lower taxes and subsidies. Poorer voters want higher ones (p. 772).
- The model assumes the median voter, the citizen with median income, dictates policy. Because every country has some inequality (a few very rich, many poor), the author says the median voter always has less than average income and is always a net recipient. More inequality means a larger median-to-average gap and more enthusiasm for a large tax-and-transfer system. So total fiscal activity is predicted to increase with income inequality (p. 772).
- Problem: the model implicitly assumes all earners vote, but numerous empirical studies show that high-income earners are much more likely to vote than low-income earners. This makes the average-to-median-voter gap smaller than the average-to-median-earner gap, so real demand for redistribution is likely smaller than predicted (p. 772).
- Iversen and Soskice (2006) found little evidence for the inequality–size link. The authors suggest this may be because of unequal turnout and cross-country turnout differences (p. 772).
- The model should fit better in high-turnout countries. Franzese (2002) finds the effect of inequality on the size of the tax-and-transfer system is largest when voter participation is high (p. 773).
- This suggests that turnout-affecting institutions (compulsory voting, registration rules, proportionality) also influence fiscal policy (p. 773). SMDP rewards large parties, so supporters of unpopular local parties vote strategically or abstain. Turnout tends to be higher under PR (Powell 1982). So fiscal activity may be larger in PR countries. The authors note little agreement on why (p. 773).

**The partisan model** (pp. 773–775):
- The Meltzer-Richard approach implicitly assumes preferences are translated frictionlessly into policy. The partisan model adds parties: left-wing parties represent low-income voters and right-wing parties high-income voters. It predicts that a change in partisan control leads to predictable fiscal changes: left-wing governments are expected to be associated with higher total fiscal activity and more "expansionary" policy (Hibbs 1977) (pp. 773–774).
- Evidence is mixed. Some find left-wing governments more expansionary, some find no relationship, and some find right-wing governments more expansionary if anything (p. 774).
- Absence of a difference is not itself evidence that the parties want the same policies. Party competition for the median voter, or the "structural dependence of the state on capital," may prevent them from acting on their wishes (p. 774).
- There is a cross-national version: if the median voter is further left in some countries, left-wing parties would govern more often there. Cameron (1978), Figure 16.7, shows a positive association (1960–1975) between the share of governments' electoral base made up of Social Democratic or Labor parties and the growth of government revenues as a share of GDP (pp. 774–775).
- Sweden (left rule throughout) had a 20-percentage-point increase. The US (right rule, "center-right" in Cameron's comparison) had almost none (pp. 774–775, n. 24). Between the extremes the relationship is fairly close. Canada, Ireland and the Netherlands grew more than predicted (n. 25, p. 775).

**Cultural factors** (pp. 775–778):
- If voter preferences differ across countries, why? Alesina, Glaeser and Sacerdote (2001, World Values Survey 1981–1997), Table 16.3 (EU vs US, %): believe the poor are trapped in poverty 60 vs 29; believe luck determines income 54 vs 30; believe the poor are lazy 26 vs 60; identify as left 30 vs 17 (p. 776). The authors say these data suggest that attitudes toward the poor might drive left-right differences between the US and Europe (p. 776).
- At the individual level the link is looser. About twice as many Europeans hold "charitable" views as identify as leftists (p. 776).
- Two possible explanations: a large left may shift the terms of debate so that some non-leftists accept its views, or leftists do not monopolize these attitudes. Christian democrats combine interventionist welfare views with moral conservatism, so the non-leftists with charitable views may be Christian democrats (p. 776). Careful studies therefore use three categories (right, left, Christian democratic) and expect the last two to act alike (E. Huber, Ragin and Stephens 1993) (p. 777).
- Figure 16.8: countries where few believe luck determines income have substantially lower social spending. Apart from Portugal and Brazil, countries where most believe it have substantially higher social spending. Variation around the pattern is considerable (p. 777). The authors say the data suggest that fiscal policy is influenced by cultural factors (attitudes toward the poor, beliefs about luck) as well as economic ones such as inequality (pp. 777–778).

### Electoral laws and fiscal policy (pp. 778–787)
Scholarship seeks to explain why PR is associated with more public goods, larger and more redistributive welfare programs, and bigger government than majoritarian systems (Iversen and Soskice 2006; Persson and Tabellini 2004). The consensus is growing but the "why" is disputed. Three sets of arguments follow (p. 778).

#### 1. PR facilitates left-wing governments (pp. 778–783)
**Rodden (2006)** (pp. 778–780):
- Left-wing support is geographically concentrated (urban industrial and mining areas), a legacy of industrialization when high transport costs kept workers near workplaces. The pattern persists. Right-wing support is spread over a broader area (p. 778).
- In SMDP, a party needs only more votes than any other party in a district. Votes beyond that are "surplus" and wasted. Concentrated left support means the left is competitive in fewer districts and wastes more votes. Under PR, seats track support, so this bias shrinks or disappears (p. 779).
- Table 16.4 (Iversen and Soskice 2006; 17 democracies, 1945–1998; centrist governments excluded): PR has 342 left-years and 120 right-years (proportion left 0.74). Majoritarian systems have 86 left and 256 right (0.25) (p. 780).
- The authors say they are claiming not necessarily that the electoral system causes left-wing success, but merely that the two go together. Boix (1999) notes that ruling elites where the left was strong replaced majoritarian systems with PR. Causation may run both ways (p. 779).
- Behavior in office: Rodden argues left parties press less aggressively for redistribution under SMDP. Parties court the national median voter under PR but the median voter of marginal constituencies (evenly split between the two biggest parties) under SMDP. Because of left concentration, the marginal-constituency median voter is likely far to the right of the average left-party voter (pp. 779–780).

**Iversen and Soskice (2006)** give a different causal logic: coalition bargaining, not geography (p. 780).
- Model: three equal-size income groups (Low, Middle, High). Low prefers taxing High and Middle at the highest rate and redistributing to itself. Middle prefers taxing High and dividing the proceeds with Low. High prefers zero taxes and no redistribution (p. 780). These preferences are derived from the model, using a larger lump-sum tax on richer groups and a nonregressive system (n. 27, p. 780).
- *SMDP*: Iversen and Soskice assume only two parties, Center-Left and Center-Right. Middle-income voters split between them, so they are a minority faction in each. Both campaign on Middle's policies but cannot credibly commit to them (p. 781).
- With probability $p$ a party keeps its promise. With probability $1-p$ it defects: Center-Right implements High's policy and Center-Left implements Low's policy. Let $L, M, H$ be Middle's valuation of Low's, Middle's and High's preferred policies (p. 781).
- Expected payoffs for Middle voters: Center-Left $pM+(1-p)L$, Center-Right $pM+(1-p)H$. Middle prefers Center-Left if
$$pM + (1-p)L > pM + (1-p)H$$
which, cancelling $pM$, comes down to
$$(1-p)L > (1-p)H.$$
- If $1-p=0$, they are indifferent. Otherwise the choice depends on whether $L$ or $H$ is larger. In the model, Middle prefers High's policy to Low's ($H>L$), so Center-Right has the higher expected payoff. Middle voters tend to vote center-right in SMDP systems (pp. 781–782).
- *PR*: three parties (Left, Center, Right). No party has a majority, so a coalition forms. Middle voters can trust the Center Party. Iversen and Soskice predict Center will more often join Left. Policy is a compromise weighted by bargaining strength (Rubinstein bargaining, n. 30). Because the system is nonregressive, Center can join Left to redistribute away from the rich, but a coalition with Right that redistributes away from the poor is not politically feasible (p. 782).
- Center gets roughly half the spending pie with Left but about one-third with Right. So center-left coalitions are more likely under PR (pp. 782–783). The authors quote Iversen and Soskice that Center has "a common interest with [the Left Party] in soaking the rich," whereas under majoritarianism Middle's concern is to avoid being "soaked by the poor" (p. 783).
- Evidence (Luxembourg Income Study, 14 democracies): redistribution is measured as the gap between inequality in gross and net incomes. Right-wing governments are associated with less redistribution, also when partisanship is measured relative to the median voter's orientation. Fiscal policy is more redistributive under PR even controlling for government partisanship. A left government elected under SMDP likely redistributes less than a left government elected under PR (p. 783).

*Intuition note.* Both arguments say PR helps the left get power. Rodden stresses geography and wasted votes. Iversen and Soskice stress who the middle group can trust as a partner. In both, SMDP also makes a left government less aggressive.

#### 2. PR shrinks the incentive for targeted spending: district size (pp. 784–785)
- **Persson and Tabellini** (1999, 2000, 2004) argue competition is fiercer in SMDP. Leaders target transfers at ideologically flexible swing voters, leaving fewer resources for broader public goods, which they assume are more redistributive (p. 784).
- This is the "law of 1/n" (Weingast, Shepsle and Johnsen 1981). If legislators care only about their own district's projects and taxes are not linked to projects, taxing and spending become a **common pool resource problem**. With $n$ districts, a district gets the full benefit of a project but pays only $1/n$ of the cost. It has the same strategic structure as an N-person Prisoner's Dilemma (n. 31) (p. 784).
- SMDP tends to have many small districts and PR a few large ones. Under SMDP, legislators can be expected to vote for lavish local projects. With a "universalism" norm (support for others' projects in return for support for one's own), national spending exceeds what each legislator wants (Weingast 1979) (p. 784).
- A single actor answerable to a national constituency would bear the full cost and approve only projects whose national benefit exceeds cost. PR legislators bear a larger share and refuse some low-benefit projects. So we might expect PR to yield fewer costly concentrated projects and more broad-benefit ones. PR is more redistributive to the extent that broad-benefit projects are more redistributive (p. 785).
- McGillivray (2004): whether spending concentrates in marginal or safe districts, and whether redistribution is greater in SMDP or PR, depends on party discipline (n. 32, p. 784).
- **Selectorate link** (Chapter 10): in a UK-style SMDP system, winning needs a little over half the votes in a little over half the districts, a winning coalition of roughly 25% of the electorate. In a one-district PR system such as the Netherlands it is roughly 50%. So PR should give more public goods and less narrow targeting (p. 785).

#### 3. PR affects spending and debt through the composition of governments (pp. 785–787)
- **Personal vote**: voting on the candidate's characteristics rather than the party's. Closed lists give party leaders control over who fills seats, so candidates court the leadership. Open lists let voters choose candidates, giving candidates an incentive to cultivate a personal vote and to distribute resources to their own districts (pp. 785–786).
- Hallerberg and Marier (2004): the common pool problem is most severe when candidates have strong personal-vote incentives, but "centralizing" fiscal institutions can mitigate it. In 25 Latin American and Caribbean countries (1988–1997), a higher personal-vote index goes with larger deficits when executive influence over the budget is minimal. The effect disappears as executive influence grows (p. 786).
- **Cabinet common-pool argument** (Bawn and Rosenbluth 2006; Hallerberg and von Hagen 1999): each minister maximizes their own budget and shifts the costs onto the whole government. Fiscal centralization (considering full cost) should lower spending, deficits and debt (p. 786).
- This literature assumes parties discipline only their own ministers, so the problem worsens with the number of parties in government. Single-party majority governments often empower a strong finance minister. Coalition governments find it harder to restrain ministers from other parties. Some coalition countries use "fiscal contracts" or "negotiated targets" (n. 33). So more parties in government should mean higher spending, larger deficits and more debt (p. 786).
- SMDP reduces the number of parties (Ch. 14) and favors single-party governments (Ch. 12), so this is an argument about electoral laws. Persson, Roland and Tabellini (2007) find positive associations between PR and party fragmentation, between fragmentation and coalition government, and between coalition government and higher spending (p. 786).
- **Tsebelis's rejoinder** (2002, ch. 8) (p. 787): veto player theory predicts that policies requiring legislative approval change more slowly with more veto players. Each cabinet party is a veto player, so fiscal policy should change more slowly when more parties govern. Almost all the countries in these studies were greatly affected by the 1970s oil crises, which gave them high spending and debt that were effectively exogenous. Countries with few veto players cut debt faster afterwards. So the association may be a historical accident. The authors add that this suggests analysts examining a period without the 1970s oil crises might not find any association between the number of parties and spending or debt (p. 787).

*Intuition note.* Several arguments share one logic: when the people who decide a spending project do not bear its full cost, spending rises. Disagreement is about where that happens (legislature or cabinet) and whether the evidence reflects design or history.

### Summary (p. 787)
PR laws are associated with coalition governments and more frequent left-wing government. Both are shown to be associated with more redistribution and with higher spending, deficits and debt. Debate remains about mechanisms, but the authors say there is little question that constitutional choices affect how governments manage their economies (p. 787). Footnote 34 adds that this is "the tip of the iceberg": presidents may have incentives to curb spending; Goodhart (1999) and Keefer and Stasavage (2002) study monetary policy; others link electoral laws to trade policy and to favoring producers over consumers (n. 34, p. 787).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Meltzer-Richard logic with three citizens. Incomes (in $1,000s): 10, 20, 90. Mean = (10+20+90)/3 = 40; median = 20.
- At $t=0.2$: taxes are 2, 4, 18. Total = 24. Balanced budget gives equal subsidy $s = 24/3 = 8$. Net $s - y_i t$: $+6$, $+4$, $-10$.
- At $t=0.3$: taxes 3, 6, 27. Total = 36, so $s=12$. Net: $+9$, $+6$, $-15$. The median citizen gains more from the higher rate (+4 → +6).
- Contrast: incomes 10, 20, 30 (mean = median = 20). At $t=0.2$: taxes 2, 4, 6, $s=4$, net $+2$, $0$, $-2$. The median is exactly indifferent, so the model gives the median no reason to push the rate up. A larger gap between mean and median therefore means a stronger median demand for redistribution.
- Turnout twist: take five earners with incomes 10, 20, 30, 40, 100 (mean 40, median earner 30, gap 10). If the two poorest do not vote, the voters are 30, 40, 100. The median voter then has income 40, equal to the mean, so is indifferent and the gap is 0. Unequal turnout shrinks the median-voter gap, so the predicted demand for redistribution falls (the point made on p. 772).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] law of 1/n in legislatures ↔ collective-action problem: costs shared, benefits local — one pays 1/n of the cost of what one fully enjoys, as in an N-person Prisoner's Dilemma (p. 784, n. 31).
- [structural-analogy] Meltzer-Richard median voter ↔ median voter theorem: the median voter's preferred tax rate wins when they dictate policy (p. 772).
- [structural-analogy] Iversen–Soskice credibility problem ↔ commitment problem in the bargaining model of war: parties cannot credibly promise policies after they are in power (p. 781).
- [structural-analogy] number of parties in government and slower fiscal change ↔ veto player theory: more veto players, more stability (p. 787).

## Questions this raises
- If PR countries and strong left parties go together partly because elites adopted PR when the left was strong, how can researchers separate cause from reverse causation (p. 779)?
- Do the three electoral-law mechanisms (left wins, district size, coalition composition) work together or compete, and could data tell them apart (p. 778)?
- If Tsebelis is right about the oil crises, would the party–spending link reappear in other time periods (p. 787)?
- How far do unequal turnout and institutions explain why Iversen and Soskice found little inequality–size link (p. 772)?
