# Cultural Determinants of Democracy and Dictatorship (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
The authors argue that cultural explanations of democracy are often vague, rest on one-time snapshots and have unclear causal direction, and that their own tests find no significant effect of a Muslim majority on democracy once wealth is taken into account.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Does democracy need a particular culture, and are some religions incompatible with it? The chapter reviews classical and modern cultural arguments. It notes that they are often vague or leave the causal direction open. It raises validity doubts about survey measures of "civic culture" (attitudes like trust). It then tests religion and diversity on 1950–2000 data. A Muslim majority is not significantly linked to becoming or staying democratic once wealth is controlled. A Catholic majority raises the chance of becoming democratic. With controls, it lowers the chance of survival. The text links ethnic and cultural diversity to survival, not emergence. This matters because such claims shape policy.

**Prerequisites:** correlation vs causation; independent/dependent variable; regression coefficient and standard error; idea of a mediating variable; Chapter 6's wealth–democracy findings.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Cultural arguments are either **primordialist** (culture is fixed and inherited, so democracy is "not for everyone") or **constructivist** (culture is made and changeable, so it is not necessarily an impenetrable barrier). Primordialist arguments imply that democracy may not suit every culture; constructivist ones do not treat culture as an impenetrable barrier. (pp. 214–215)
- Mill and Montesquieu tie institutions to culture but do not say exactly which cultural trait matters. The authors say such claims are so vague that they become nonfalsifiable (no evidence could count against them). Mill is read as a constructivist. (pp. 215–217)
- Almond–Verba and Inglehart treat a "civic culture" (trust, support for gradual change, etc.) as a requirement for stable democracy. The causal arrow is disputed: a "values story" (culture drives democracy) versus an "institutional story" (democracy drives culture). (pp. 217–221)
- The authors' reanalysis of Inglehart and Welzel data tends to support the values story more than the institutional story. Self-expression values (values that put self-expression above security) only partly mediate the development→democracy link (carry only part of its effect), and both stories may operate. (pp. 222–223)
- Cross-national survey items have limits: they are poorly suited to studying the emergence of democracy, respondents in dictatorships may hide their preferences, the same question can be understood differently, and two World Values Survey (WVS) items raise face-validity doubts (the answers do not match a common-sense ranking of stable democracies). (pp. 224–229)
- The authors find doctrinal arguments that a religion is incompatible with democracy doubtful. Virtually all religions have doctrines that can be read both ways, and considerable evidence suggests that a religion's stance often depends less on doctrine and more on leaders' interests and organization. (pp. 233–236)
- Single-time snapshots can mislead. Catholicism looked bad for democracy in 1976 but not in 2004, and the authors say the argument that Islam is antidemocratic is the same type of reasoning from one point in time; explanations with no testable implications beyond the observation that prompted them do not invite falsification. (pp. 238–239)
- Emergence tests (1950–2000): the Muslim-majority coefficient loses significance once wealth is controlled. A Catholic majority raises the probability of becoming democratic. Diversity variables are insignificant. (pp. 242–245)
- Survival tests: no Protestant-majority democracy collapsed. A Muslim majority is not significant once economic controls are added. With controls, a Catholic majority lowers survival. Ethnic diversity is starred in Model 3, and the text also reports cultural diversity as significant. (pp. 245–247)
- Wealth and growth keep their effects when culture is controlled, so Huntington's claim about Islam appears wrong on these data. (p. 247)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and introduction (pp. 213–215)
The chapter asks whether democracy needs a "democratic culture", whether some cultures are incompatible with it, and how culture affects emergence and survival (p. 214). The overview previews these themes (pp. 213–214):
- cultural modernization theory;
- the Islam question;
- evidence on Islam and wealth;
- experiments (the experiments are not covered in these pages).

**Cultural modernization theory:** economic development produces cultural changes such as a civic culture, and these changes lead to democracy. The overview calls the empirical support "somewhat mixed" (p. 213).

Two families of cultural argument (p. 214):
- **Primordialist:** culture is objective, inherited, fixed since "primordial" times. It exists prior to political interaction and is unchanged by it. It shapes politics by giving ideological guidelines for collective action. Hence democracy may not be compatible with all cultures; primordialist arguments imply that democracy is not for everyone. Geertz is cited for primordial attachments (bloodlines, language, race, religion, customs) (p. 214).
- **Constructivist:** culture is constructed or invented, not inherited. Like primordialists, constructivists say culture has a causal effect and a democratic culture is required. But cultures are malleable and can change in response to social, economic and political actors. So they are not impenetrable barriers. The speed of change likely varies, so some cultures adopt democracy more easily (pp. 214–215).

### Classical cultural arguments: Mill and Montesquieu (pp. 215–217)
- **Montesquieu** (18th century) is called the first to write in detail on culture and institutions. He claimed that monarchy suits Europe, despotism the Orient and democracy the ancient world. He held that the best government is the one that suits the temperament and character of the people for whom it is set up. Institutions should relate to climate, soil, size, occupation, religion, riches, manners and customs. So exporting institutions can succeed only by chance (p. 215). (Aeschylus's contrast of Asian authoritarianism and Athenian democracy is mentioned as an early vague precedent; p. 215.)
- **Mill** said no one believes every people can work every sort of institution. Necessary characteristics included "moral" or "mental habits" (e.g., willingness to cooperate with the law against wrongdoers). They also included development, such as a press that can spread public opinion and a tax system able to enforce obedience (p. 215). Legislators should take account of the habits and feelings a people already has (p. 216).
- The authors stress that Mill is **not** claiming that some cultures are incompatible with democracy. He thinks people can learn new things, because dwelling on an idea makes it familiar even if it seemed strange at first. He does not treat particular cultural traits as "necessary conditions" for democracy and thinks culture is malleable. The speed of change varies across countries, but he does not say what affects that speed. The authors call this a constructivist argument (p. 216).
- Both thinkers' arguments were later incorporated into strands of **cultural modernization theory.** Development moves "immature" societies (large agricultural sectors, authoritarian institutions) to "mature" ones (large industry and service sectors, democratic institutions). Development does not directly cause democracy. It produces cultural changes, and these produce democratic reform. Inglehart and Welzel (2005, 15): development brings fairly predictable cultural changes that make democracy more likely where absent and stronger where present (p. 216).

**Two problems the authors draw from these classics** (pp. 216–217):
1. *Vagueness.* Neither says exactly what about culture matters (religion, customs, morals, manners, marital institutions). They also cite non-cultural factors (climate, soil, economy). Cultural arguments must specify what matters; otherwise one can never conclude that culture does not matter. Such arguments become nonfalsifiable ("nonscientific" in the Chapter 2 sense) (p. 217).
2. *Causal direction.* Does culture cause democracy, or economic development, or do institutions and development cause culture? If culture is a cause, is it necessary or sufficient, and does it affect emergence or only survival? Figure 7.1 shows seven candidate structures (p. 217; figure p. 218):
   - (a) culture → democracy and culture → economic development;
   - (b) culture → democracy and economic development → democracy;
   - (c) culture → economic development → democracy;
   - (d) economic development → culture → democracy;
   - (e) economic development → democracy, with culture acting on that link (arrow from culture pointing at it, as the image shows);
   - (f) economic development → democracy → culture;
   - (g) all three linked by two-way arrows.

   Mill and Montesquieu held that both development and culture matter, but their exact causal view is hard to discern (p. 217).

*Intuition note:* The authors say a cultural argument must specify exactly what about culture matters; if it is too vague, it becomes nonfalsifiable. They also ask which way the causal arrow runs, a separate problem that Mill and Montesquieu leave unclear.

### Does democracy require a civic culture? (pp. 217–224)
**Almond and Verba (1963).** They accepted that development matters but believed that only culture could supply the psychological basis of democratization, and that without it the prospects for democratic survival were slim (p. 217). They distinguish three types of political culture (p. 217):
- parochial (compatible with traditional African tribal systems);
- subject (congruent with centralized authoritarian systems, such as Soviet-dominated Eastern Europe);
- participant or "civic" (the only type compatible with democracy).

Culture is how individuals think and feel about the political system. It is studied by surveys, and a nation's culture is the distribution of responses (pp. 217–218). The civic culture is a cluster of four attitudes (p. 220):
- (a) belief that one can influence political decisions;
- (b) positive feelings toward the political system;
- (c) high interpersonal trust;
- (d) preference for gradual change.

In five countries (Germany, Italy, Mexico, US, UK), the US and UK were the most stable democracies and closest to the civic ideal. They concluded a civic culture was necessary for democratic stability (p. 220).

**Inglehart (1990).** He used surveys from 25 industrial nations in the 1980s. Culture includes life satisfaction, interpersonal trust and support for gradual change. Countries high on these were more likely to be stable democracies (p. 220). Box 7.2 lists both lists of elements. Inglehart's longer list adds support for the current social order, postmaterialist values, political discussion and percentage Protestant (Box 7.2, p. 221; footnote 2, p. 220). Footnote 3 says Inglehart and Welzel (2005) later stressed self-expression (postmaterialist) values. Using 81 countries of the World Values Survey 1981–2001, they find that self-expression countries are more likely to become and remain democratic (p. 220, n. 3).

**Box 7.1 (p. 219): Putnam on Italy.** Putnam (1993) argues that cultural norms explain why northern Italian regional governments performed better than southern ones.
- He measured performance with twelve indicators (e.g., cabinet stability, day-care centers, responsiveness to information requests).
- Institutions were similar across regions, so he argued they could not explain the variation.
- He focused on social capital or civic culture: "norms of reciprocity and networks of civic engagement". It was measured with voluntary associations, newspaper readership, referendum turnout and (lack of) personalized preference voting.
- Regions with a civic culture performed better. The north had a "good" civic culture traced to medieval communal republics. The south had "amoral familism" traced to a monarchical past, with reciprocity limited to family and self-interest dominant.
- Putnam describes these as vicious and virtuous circles that lead to contrasting path-dependent social equilibria.
- *Bowling Alone* (2000) argues that US participation in voluntary associations has fallen, which has been seen as a possible threat to US democracy.

*Intuition note:* In this tradition, "culture" means a measurable bundle of attitudes (trust, support for gradual change, civic engagement). A country's culture is the distribution of what its citizens report.

#### Debate over the direction of causality (pp. 220–223)
- **Values story** (Figure 7.1d; Inglehart; Inglehart and Welzel): development → cultural change → democratization. Cultural values act on political institutions (pp. 220–221).
- **Institutional story** (Figure 7.1f; Barry 1970; Muller and Seligson 1994; Seligson 2002): development → democratization → cultural change. Institutions act on values (p. 221).

**Inglehart and Welzel's (2005) test** (pp. 221–222):
- Self-expression is measured by five survey questions on life satisfaction, interpersonal trust, postmaterialism, homosexuality and petition signing.
- Societal development is a "power resource" index (size of agriculture, education, urbanization).
- Democracy is measured by Freedom House scores.
- They ask whether the change in democracy score from 1987 to 1996 is explained by development and self-expression.
- They claim considerable support for cultural modernization. They found causal effects from democratic institutions to self-expression "negligible" (p. 222).

**The authors' reanalysis, using mediation (pp. 222–223).** In the values story, culture is a *mediating variable*: it lies between development and democratization in the causal chain (p. 222).
- *Procedure* (Baron and Kenny 1986): estimate the effect of development on democratization, then control for cultural values and see whether the effect shrinks substantially. If it does, there is reason to believe development operates through the mediator. If the effect disappears entirely, mediation is complete. If not, it is partial (p. 222).
- *Result:* some support for self-expression as a mechanism. But development still has a significant effect after controlling for values, so mediation is only partial. At least some of the effect works through other mechanisms (p. 222).
- Footnote 6 notes two departures from Inglehart and Welzel: the power-resource values predate the values data, and the authors use an "effective democracy" measure (p. 222).
- *Alternative mechanism:* the Chapter 6 bargaining story. States depend more on citizens with mobile assets as societies develop, and these citizens use the stronger bargaining position to gain limited, representative government (pp. 222–223).
- *Institutional story test:* the authors estimate the effect of development on values, then control for democratic experience (years as a democracy 1850–1990 or since independence; footnote 7). Development does raise democratic experience, but only a small portion of its effect on values operates through that route (p. 223).
- *Conclusion:* the reanalysis tends to support the values story more than the institutional story, yet there is some evidence for both. Nothing rules out both being true, perhaps as a "virtuous cycle". Inglehart and Welzel's data cover too short a period to estimate such a dynamic model. The results are called "suggestive" (p. 223).

*Intuition note:* Mediation asks whether a cause works "through" a middle variable. If a significant effect of development remains after controlling for culture, then at least some of its effect on democracy works through other routes.

#### Surveys and cross-national research (pp. 223–229)
The World Values Survey (run by Inglehart, interviews in more than eighty societies) is a main data source (p. 223). A much-used item asks whether democracy, despite its problems, is better than any other form of government (answers run from strongly agree to strongly disagree) (p. 224). Many believe mass support gives legitimacy, so low support is often seen as a harbinger of instability. The authors say that whether such answers really predict stability has been "insufficiently examined" (p. 224).

**Limitations** (pp. 224–226):
1. Surveys are suited to how culture affects *stability* and are not well suited to asking whether it *produces* democracy. Studying emergence needs surveys in dictatorships, where *preference falsification* (hiding true preferences in public for fear of punishment) makes inference hard (p. 224).
2. Comparability: individuals can understand the "same" question in very different ways (Brady, quoted p. 224). Sen's example (p. 225): Kerala has the highest literacy and longevity in India but the highest reported morbidity among Indian states. Bihar, with low longevity, reports the lowest. The US reports even higher rates than Kerala for the same illnesses. Self-reported morbidity is thus negatively correlated with actual health. Respondents understood the questions or scales differently (p. 225).
3. People conceive of democracy differently (equality vs competitive elections). Experts themselves disagree (minimalist vs substantive definitions, Chapter 5). National history matters: e.g., Sri Lankans who endured civil war may judge democracy by peace, while South Africans may judge it by social freedom and economic equality (p. 225).

**Face validity** means that responses match common-sense understanding of which countries are stable democracies (p. 226). Two WVS 1995 items for 37 countries were checked (pp. 226–229):
- *Democracy-is-best item* (Figure 7.2, scale 1 strongly agree to 4 strongly disagree; weighted national means; p. 227). Lower mean means higher predicted stability. Bangladesh is top. Democracies in Bangladesh, Croatia, the Dominican Republic, Nigeria, Argentina and Montenegro would be more stable than the US (p. 226). The authors suspect few would claim the US is less stable than these (p. 226).
- *Gradual-change item* (Figure 7.3; 1 = radical change by revolution, 2 = gradual reform, 3 = defend society against subversion; p. 228). A higher mean means more support for gradual change, and thus predicted stability. Peru, Venezuela, South Korea, Taiwan, Bangladesh and Mexico should be more stable than the US (p. 229). Again the authors suspect that few people would say the US is less stable.
- Nigeria ranks near the top on the first item and near the bottom on the second. The correlation between the two items is only 0.38. The authors infer that at least some attitudes seen as parts of civic culture do not form a coherent cluster. Jackman and Miller (1996) and Muller and Seligson (1994) support this (p. 229).
- The authors do **not** say surveys are inappropriate. They urge care in drawing strong inferences (p. 229).

*Intuition note:* If a survey question is read differently across countries, comparing averages can mislead. The authors suspect that the two WVS items rank the United States below several countries that few would call more stable, and the items only weakly agree with each other (0.38), so the authors urge care in drawing strong inferences about civic culture from them.

### Religion and democracy (pp. 229–247)
Recent cultural arguments focus on religion and have shaped public discourse. Huntington's *Clash of Civilizations* was reportedly recommended reading for many soldiers heading to Iraq in 2003. The authors say the long history of such claims should perhaps make us wary of accepting them (p. 229).

#### Are some religions incompatible with democracy? (pp. 229–235)
**Protestantism.**
- Lipset (1994b, 5) says scholars have assumed that Catholicism, Orthodoxy, Islam and Confucianism are negatively linked to democracy and Protestantism positively linked (pp. 229–230).
- Weber is commonly credited with the first link. Lipset says Protestant emphasis on individual responsibility furthered democratic values. The causal story: Protestantism → economic development → bourgeoisie → democracy, matching Figure 7.1c (p. 230).
- Footnote 14 notes that Lipset gives no specific passage and Weber appears to have said little about political institutions (p. 232).
- Stark: capitalist features existed in Italian city-states before the Reformation, so Christianity in general, not Protestantism, mattered (orthodoxy and a rational personal God supported science and self-rule) (p. 230).
- Woodberry: Protestant missionary activity in colonial periods spread mass education and printing, which brought literacy, equality and a larger middle class, helping explain democracy today (p. 230).

**Catholicism.** Seen as antithetical to democracy. Lipset argues that its claim of one church and one truth clashes with the acceptance of competing ideologies. Church hierarchy and the clergy–laity divide are said to hinder egalitarian institutions. The Church supported Mussolini's Italy and Franco's Spain and several dictatorships in South America and Asia (pp. 230–232).

**Box 7.3 (Clash of Civilizations, p. 231).** Fukuyama declared the "end of history" after the Cold War. Huntington (1993, 1996) disagreed but agreed that conflict would no longer be ideological:
- Conflict will be cultural, between civilizations, defined as the broadest cultural grouping (p. 231).
- The authors say the number of civilizations is ambiguous, and they seem to be coded mainly by religion (p. 231).
- Western universalism and democratization efforts will antagonize others. Cultural conflicts are less mutable.
- He says Islamic and Confucian countries cannot sustain democracy and Catholic countries will find it hard. He also says Muslims are prone to violence.
- The authors note that Huntington does not systematically test his claims and relies on anecdote (p. 231).

**Confucianism** (p. 232):
- Huntington (1993): Western ideas often have little resonance in Islamic, Confucian and other cultures; "Confucian democracy" is a contradiction in terms.
- Asian values debate: respect for authority and communalism supposedly make Confucianism incompatible with democracy (Kim, Pye, Scalapino). The Bangkok Declaration (April 1993; China, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore) was a catalyst.
- Lee Kuan Yew made similar claims. Authoritarian leaders used the argument to justify their regimes.

**Islam** (pp. 232–233). Three proposed reasons for incompatibility:
- Montesquieu's claim that Islam "speaks only by the sword", with Huntington repeating that Muslims are prone to violence.
- Islam's supposed inability to separate religion and politics, since God is sovereign lawgiver (Lewis: theocracy; Huntington: God is Caesar in Islam).
- Unequal treatment of women (Fish; Norris and Inglehart): patriarchy in the family replicates in society, or the marginalization of women leaves society open to domination.

**The authors' critique** (pp. 233–235):
- Arguments built on religious doctrine face a problem: virtually all religions have doctrinal elements compatible and incompatible with democracy (Stepan 2000). Even Protestantism has them: Przeworski et al. say its legitimization of inequality and self-interest ethic provide a poor moral basis for living together peacefully (p. 233).
- *Confucianism:* meritocracy, education and tolerance (Fukuyama); Lee Teng-hui says it calls for limited government. Others cite the right of rebellion against rulers who deviate from the "Way" (Leys). A public sphere existed in Joseon Korea (1392–1910). So there seems to be nothing explicit in Confucianism that necessitates authoritarian government (pp. 233–234).
- *Islam:* many scholars dispute doctrinal incompatibility. They find a basis for democracy in *shura* (consultation), and some Islamic scholars say elections and a parliament serve it. Others see *ijma* (consensus), *ijtihad* (reinterpretation) and *maslaha* (public welfare) as supporting parliamentary governance and reform (p. 234).
- *Law of God vs law of man:* sovereignty lies in different places (God vs the people), but in practice the state exercises it in Islam. Both systems aim to limit the power of rulers, in Islam by making God primary lawgiver, in democracy by elections and checks and balances. Footnote 16 gives Lee Kuan Yew's counter-claim that individual freedom comes at the expense of order (p. 234).
- Democratic theory does not require a secular state. Until the eighteenth century, many Christian church leaders vehemently opposed democracy and secularism; only in the nineteenth century did democracy and secularism become broadly acceptable in Western Christian society. Islamic doctrine, like others, contains elements compatible with many traditional aspects of democracy (pp. 234–235).
- Footnote 15: Tocqueville said Catholics were the most republican and democratic class in the US and that Catholicism was not a natural enemy of democracy (p. 233).

#### Some empirical evidence (pp. 235–240)
Since doctrines cut both ways, it is "an empirical question" whether some religions pose particular difficulties (p. 235).

**Culture is malleable and interest-driven** (pp. 235–237):
- Eickelman and Piscatori show that Islamic doctrine has been interpreted to justify many types of government. Lee Kuan Yew and Lee Teng-hui drew opposite conclusions from Confucianism. Przeworski et al.: "Cultures are made of cloth but the fabric of culture drapes differently in the hands of different tailors." (p. 235)
- Religious stances toward political institutions depend less on doctrine and more on leaders' interests. Kalyvas shows that Catholicism's relation to democracy in Europe had less to do with the content of Catholic faith and more to do with the strategic considerations of Church elites. Balmer makes a similar point about US Protestants. Kalyvas also explains Belgium (19th century, Catholic fundamentalism compatible with democracy) vs Algeria (1990s, Islamic fundamentalism not) by different organizational structures more than by doctrine (pp. 235–236).
- "Cultural entrepreneurs" (Laitin, Posner) suggest that conflicts over culture tend to be matters of interest and strategy rather than primordially given content. The authors say a vast amount of empirical evidence undermines Huntington's implication that the antidemocratic tendencies of some religions are given once and for all (p. 236).
- The authors say all religions have historically been compatible with a broad range of political institutions. Confucian societies exist in democracies (Japan, South Korea, Taiwan), semi-authoritarian Singapore and authoritarian Burma, China and North Korea. China's imperial system was abolished in 1911 without loss of societal coherence. The lasting legacy is a personal ethic about family, work and education (p. 236).
- Islamic countries: apart from Iran since 1979 and Taliban-era Afghanistan (1990s), there have been few precedents for religious leaders holding political power. On the whole, secular elites have ruled for about 1,400 years since Muhammad's death in 632 A.D. Hundreds of millions of Muslims live in democracies. Several majority-Muslim countries are considered democracies (Albania, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Mali, Niger, Senegal, Turkey) (pp. 236–237).
- Quasi-democratic historical practices: the Afghan Loya Jirga (functioning since at least 1709, a consultative council), and the Constitution of Medina (622 A.D.; Box 7.4). It was signed by Muslim, Jewish, Arab and other tribal communities and granted religious tolerance and equality to Jews who stayed loyal. Muhammad sought written consent instead of ruling only through revelation (pp. 237–238).

**Snapshot problem** (pp. 238–239):
- Studies finding Islam bad for democracy (Barro; Fish; Karatnycky). Karatnycky (2001): Islamic countries 1 Free, 18 Partly Free, 28 Not Free. Non-Islamic: 85 Free, 39 Partly Free, 21 Not Free (p. 238).
- Fish's mechanism is the treatment of women: wider literacy gap, fewer women in government, lower gender empowerment, each of which reduces Freedom House scores (p. 238).
- The authors counter that these studies use a single point in time. The same logic applied to Catholicism gives opposite conclusions in different years (p. 238):

| Year | Catholic-majority countries | Protestant-majority countries |
|---|---|---|
| 1976 | 47 countries: 14 Free, 16 Not Free | 16 countries: 11 Free, 1 Not Free |
| 2004 | 57 countries: 40 Free, 3 Not Free | 23 countries: 17 Free, 1 Not Free |

- Likewise, Western civilization looked democratic-compatible today but not to someone in 1930s Europe (Mazower: democracy, triumphant in 1918, was almost extinct in Europe twenty years later) (p. 239).
- Many religion–democracy arguments rest implicitly on observations at one point in time, and theory construction came after the observation. The authors say there is nothing inherently wrong with that, but an explanation that suggests no testable implications beyond those that led to it is an *ex post* (ad hoc) explanation, and such explanations violate scientific norms because they do not invite falsification. The same type of argument once made about Catholicism is now frequently made about Islam, and the history of arguments about Catholicism (and Confucianism) should make us wary (p. 239).
- Footnote 19: it is misleading to speak of a single Confucian culture in Asia, and Dalton and Ong (2004) find that Asians do not share a single coherent set of values and that Asian values do not differ significantly from Western attitudes on the Pacific Rim (p. 239).
- On Fish's women-treatment evidence, the authors argue that Freedom House scores implicitly include women's status, so the correlation is partly built in by construction. The question stays "open" (p. 240).

*Intuition note:* A fact like most Muslim countries being dictatorships today does not show that being Muslim causes dictatorship. It describes one date, and the same style of argument about Catholicism lost most of its force once Catholic countries became democratic.

#### A new test (pp. 240–247)
**Hypotheses** (pp. 240, 242):
- *Catholic:* majority-Catholic countries are less likely to become and stay democratic.
- *Protestant:* majority-Protestant countries are more likely to become and stay democratic.
- *Islamic:* majority-Muslim countries are less likely to become and stay democratic.
- *Ethnic, religious and cultural group hypotheses:* countries with many such groups are less likely to become and stay democratic. The motivation is Weingast's argument that democracy requires citizens to coordinate beliefs about when government has transgressed. Others argue that ethnic diversity makes compromise hard and raises the risk of intercommunal violence (p. 242).

Table 7.1 (p. 241) lists majority-Muslim, Protestant and Catholic countries, from Przeworski et al. (2000), with "Catholic" including Orthodox. Footnote 21 notes that arguing from observed patterns resembles Mill's Method of Difference. It adds that many Catholic dictatorships do not logically show that Catholicism causes dictatorship (p. 241).

**Method.** The authors use the same dynamic probit model as in Chapter 6 (our gloss: a model for a yes/no outcome; the text gives no further definition), on 199 countries, 1950–2000 (p. 242).
- *Emergence:* the dependent variable is the probability of being a democracy this year given dictatorship last year.
- *Survival:* the dependent variable is the probability of being a democracy this year given democracy last year (p. 245).
- A positive coefficient raises the probability and a negative one lowers it. As a rule of thumb, we are 95 percent confident of the sign if the coefficient exceeds twice its standard error. Stars: * above 90%, ** above 95%, *** above 99% (pp. 242–244).
- *(added step)* Examples: Muslim majority, Model 1: $0.28/0.12 \approx 2.33 > 2$ (starred **). Model 2: $0.18/0.16 \approx 1.13 < 2$ (no star).

**Table 7.2: emergence** (coefficient, standard error in parentheses; p. 243):

Reading of the results (pp. 244–245):
- *Model 1:* the Muslim coefficient is negative and significant. The Protestant coefficient is negative and almost significant at 90%, with no evidence of a positive effect. The Catholic coefficient is positive and significant, so there is little evidence that Catholicism is a barrier to emergence.
- Muslim countries tend to be poorer, and poor countries are less likely to become democratic (Chapter 6). *Model 2* adds GDP per capita, growth and oil production. The Muslim coefficient stays negative but loses significance. The authors say the evidence "strongly suggests" that Muslim countries are less likely to democratize not because they are Muslim but because they are poor. If they became wealthier, nothing here indicates that a Muslim majority would be more of a barrier than a Protestant majority. The Catholic effect remains significant.
- *Models 3–5:* none of the diversity coefficients is anywhere near significant, so there is no reason to suspect that diversity inhibits or promotes emergence.
- *Summary of emergence* (p. 245): wealth makes transitions more likely, and high growth makes them less likely. A Catholic majority makes them significantly more likely. Protestant and Muslim majorities have no significant effect. Diversity does not destabilize dictatorships.

Reading of the results (p. 245, continuing on p. 247):
- *Model 1:* no Protestant-majority democracy collapsed, which the authors take to show a strong association between Protestant majority and survival. The Muslim coefficient is negative and highly significant. There is no evidence that Catholicism hurts survival.
- *Model 2* adds the economic variables. The Muslim coefficient stays negative but is no longer significant. The authors say the evidence strongly suggests that Muslim countries are less likely to stay democratic because they are poor, not because they are Muslim (pp. 245–247).
- Diversity (Models 3–5): the text says that countries with many ethnic or cultural groups are less likely to stay democratic, because the coefficients are negative and significant. It says there is no evidence that the number of religious groups matters (p. 247). In the table, the ethnic coefficient has a star in Model 3, and the cultural coefficient has none (see Reading notes). *(Added step:* $0.23/0.12 \approx 1.9 < 2$.)

*Intuition note:* "Muslim majority" looks harmful in the simple models. It stops being significant once wealth, growth and oil are held constant, which suggests that poverty, not religion, is behind the raw gap. Note that the table shows no star, which the authors read as no evidence of an effect.

**Conclusions of the analysis** (p. 247):
1. No evidence that predominantly Muslim countries are less likely to become or stay democratic. They typically have authoritarian governments now, and the authors believe this has more to do with poverty than with being Muslim. They say Huntington appears to be wrong in his claim that Islam has antidemocratic proclivities.
2. Majority Protestant countries are not more likely to become democratic. If one does become democratic, it is likely to stay so (none collapsed in 1950–2000). It is hard to say whether this is religion or wealth.
3. Majority Catholic countries are significantly more likely to become democratic. This runs counter to the traditional argument, and the authors say it is unclear why. They add that these countries find it hard to stay democratic (with economic controls they are significantly less likely to stay democratic).
4. Diversity does not destabilize dictatorships. Ethnic and cultural diversity do seem to destabilize democracies, while religious diversity does not matter. One interpretation (Weingast 1997): shared values or beliefs are required for democracy, but not for authoritarianism, to persist.
5. Wealth and growth keep their effects. Richer countries are more likely to become and stay democratic. Growth reduces both the likelihood of a transition and of a collapse. A difference from Chapter 6 is that oil production now has no evidence of an effect (the page breaks off mid-sentence; p. 247).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Purpose: show why "Muslim-majority countries are less often democratic" can vanish once wealth is held fixed (the logic of Models 1 → 2). The numbers are invented for counting, not a probit.

Suppose 30 countries:
- **Poor:** 8 Muslim-majority (2 democratic), 4 other (1 democratic).
- **Rich:** 2 Muslim-majority (1 democratic), 16 other (8 democratic).

Raw comparison (no control):
- Muslim-majority: 10 countries, $2 + 1 = 3$ democratic → $3/10 = 30\%$.
- Other: 20 countries, $1 + 8 = 9$ democratic → $9/20 = 45\%$.
- Gap: $45 - 30 = 15$ points.

Within each wealth group:
- Poor: Muslim $2/8 = 25\%$, other $1/4 = 25\%$ → gap 0.
- Rich: Muslim $1/2 = 50\%$, other $8/16 = 50\%$ → gap 0.

The raw gap arises because 8 of 10 Muslim-majority countries are poor, against 4 of 20 others. Poverty, not religion, carries the difference. Model 2's drop in the Muslim coefficient is the same kind of effect (confounding by wealth).

## Concepts
- [[primordialist-culture]] (new) — culture as objective, inherited and fixed; democracy may not fit all cultures (p. 214)
- [[constructivist-culture]] (new) — culture as invented and malleable; not necessarily an impenetrable barrier, and the speed of change likely varies (pp. 214–215)
- [[cultural-modernization-theory]] (new) — development produces cultural change (e.g., civic culture), which produces democracy (pp. 216, 213)
- [[civic-culture]] (new) — cluster of attitudes (efficacy, system support, trust, gradual change; Inglehart adds life satisfaction etc.) taken as supporting democracy (pp. 220–221)
- [[social-capital]] — reciprocity norms and civic networks, which Putnam treats as civic culture (p. 219)
- [[path-dependence]] — Putnam's "path-dependent social equilibria": history locks in vicious or virtuous circles (p. 219)
- [[mediation-analysis]] (new) — test whether a cause works through a middle variable by seeing if its effect shrinks after controlling for it (p. 222)
- [[values-story-vs-institutional-story]] (new) — rival causal directions: culture→democracy vs democracy→culture (pp. 220–221)
- [[preference-falsification]] — hiding true preferences in public for fear of punishment; limits surveys in dictatorships (p. 224)
- [[face-validity]] (new) — whether measured responses match a common-sense picture of reality (p. 226)
- [[ex-post-explanation]] (new) — explanation built after the observation, with no testable implications beyond it; not falsifiable (p. 239)
- [[observable-implications]] — the missing requirement for ex post explanations (p. 239)
- [[institutions]] — political rules such as democracy, seen here as shaping or being shaped by culture (pp. 221, 223)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Mediation analysis (development → culture → democracy) ↔ causal inference: an effect "through" a middle variable is judged by whether controlling for it shrinks the effect — compares total effect with the remaining effect.
- [structural-analogy] Confounding of "Muslim majority" by wealth ↔ omitted-variable bias: the raw coefficient absorbs poverty's effect until wealth is added.
- [structural-analogy] Cross-national survey non-comparability (Kerala) ↔ measurement validity/text-as-data: scales mean different things across groups, so validation is needed.
- [structural-analogy] Ex post explanations that suggest no new testable implications ↔ observable implications (KKV): a theory is useful only if it implies things beyond the facts that suggested it.
- [metaphor] Weingast's coordination of beliefs about government transgression as "democratic culture" ↔ coordination games/focal points: a shared belief that lets citizens act together.

## Questions this raises
- If democracy is more likely to emerge in Catholic-majority countries but survives less often there, what mechanism could explain both results (the authors say it is unclear)?
- If shared values matter for democratic survival but not for dictatorship (Weingast interpretation), how could we test that directly rather than infer it from diversity counts?
- How could "civic culture" be measured in a way that is comparable across countries and passes face validity?
- If the values and institutional stories form a virtuous cycle, what data and model would be needed to estimate it, given that the available data cover too short a period?
