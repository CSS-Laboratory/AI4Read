# Democracy or Dictatorship: Does It Make a Difference?

## L0 — One line
Theory gives conflicting predictions about whether democracy raises growth, and the data show that democracies rarely do badly but do not beat all dictatorships, so the democracy–dictatorship split may not be the best way to explain differences in performance.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether regime type changes citizens' material well-being. It first examines three theoretical links to economic growth: property rights, consumption versus investment, and dictatorial autonomy. Together they point in different directions. The property-rights link is weak because it is not clear that democracies protect property better. Two models show how redistribution might hurt investment, or be deterred by capital flight (owners moving or consuming their capital). Studies of growth split three ways. Plots of six well-being indicators are "triangular": democracies seldom do badly, but some dictatorships do as well. Democracy looks sufficient for some success, not necessary. Comparisons that ignore the variation among dictatorships are likely to mislead.

**Prerequisites:** taxes and transfers; investment versus consumption; discounting the future; reading scatterplots; the idea of sufficient versus necessary conditions.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The chapter focuses on material outcomes (growth, health, wealth) rather than on normative arguments for democracy (p. 326).
- Property-rights argument: democracy → rule of law → stable property rights → investment → growth (Figure 9.1). Barro finds the first link apparently missing: electoral rights have no predictive content for the rule of law. Other scholars disagree (pp. 327–330).
- Meltzer-Richard: with equal transfers and a balanced budget, below-average earners want high taxes. If democracy represents them, democracy means more redistribution, which might reduce investment (pp. 331–333).
- Two criticisms of that prediction: poor people vote less, and, in the structural-dependence argument (Przeworski's model), redistribution lowers output because capitalists may withdraw capital, and governments depend on their investment; structural Marxists therefore argue it is likely to be self-defeating (pp. 334–337).
- Whether democracy leads to aggressive redistribution depends on capitalists' credible exit threats (a believable ability to move their assets away). Where they are weak, as with immobile oil assets, redistribution is more likely (pp. 338–339).
- The consumption-versus-investment and autonomy arguments each rest on implicit assumptions (including about what rulers want), so they give no clear prediction (pp. 340–342).
- Empirically, studies split: 8 find dictatorships grow faster, 8 democracies, 5 no effect (p. 342).
- Six well-being indicators show triangular scatter (democracies seldom score badly, dictatorships range widely): democracy looks sufficient for some success, not necessary. Understanding the variation among dictatorships is crucial (pp. 342–345).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 325–326)
- Epigraph: Sen (1999) says no substantial famine has occurred in an independent, democratic country with a relatively free press (p. 325).
- The chapter sets aside normative arguments (freedoms, fairness, justice). It asks whether democracy makes a *material* difference, such as growth, longevity, health, education and wealth (p. 326).
- The answers are "not as straightforward as you might imagine". Examples: child mortality (Ross 2006) and growth (Przeworski and Limongi 1993) (p. 326).
- Claim of the chapter: democracy is often *sufficient* for high material well-being, but certainly not *necessary*, since some dictatorships do well (p. 326).

### The effect of regime type on economic growth (p. 326)
Scholars say democracy promotes growth (Barro, North, Olson), hinders it (Huntington and others), or makes no difference (Przeworski and Limongi and others). Theory focuses on three factors: (a) property rights, (b) incentives to consume rather than invest, (c) dictatorial autonomy (p. 326).

#### Property rights (pp. 327–340)
**The argument.** Many scholars argue that democracies are characterised by the rule of law and the protection of property rights. On this view, democracy limits arbitrary seizure, so it encourages investment and growth (p. 327). The authors link this to Chapter 6: democratic institutions can be a credible commitment mechanism for asset holders. They cite England (limited government) versus France in the 17th century as a potential explanation for faster English growth (p. 327). The causal chain is shown in Figure 9.1: democracy → rule of law → stable property rights → investment → growth (pp. 327–328).

**Barro's evidence.** Using about 100 countries, 1960–1995, Barro finds that the rule of law significantly encourages growth and is related to the investment/GDP ratio (p. 328). The problem is that he does not find democracy strongly associated with the rule of law. The first link appears to be missing (p. 328).
- Table 9.1 lists country-years where the rule-of-law and electoral-rights indexes (both 0–1, from Political Risk Services) differ by at least 0.5 (pp. 328–330).
- High rule of law but low electoral rights, 1982 and 1998: for example Singapore (1.00 vs 0.50 in 1982; 1.00 vs 0.33 in 1998), Chile 1982 (0.83 vs 0.17), China 1998 (0.83 vs 0.00) (p. 329).
- Low rule of law but high electoral rights, 1982: for example Bolivia (0.17 vs 0.83), Honduras (0.17 vs 0.83), Colombia (0.33 vs 0.83) (p. 330).
- Dictators who supported reliable law include Pinochet (Chile 1973–1990), Lee Kuan Yew (Singapore 1959–1990) and the Shah (Iran 1941–1979). Their law was reliable (consistently applied), not necessarily fair. Investors are typically more concerned with reliability, because it lets them predict returns (p. 328).
- Barro concludes that the electoral rights index "has no predictive content" for the rule of law, so encouraging democracy for growth "sounds pleasant, but is simply false" (p. 330).
- **Other scholars disagree:** Leblang, and Rigobon and Rodrik, find that more democratic countries are more likely to protect property rights. Leblang finds higher growth as a result (p. 330).
- Footnote 2: Haber, Razo and Maurer report that Mexico (1876–1929) failed to protect property in many sectors but protected it in sectors where the government depended on actors with credible exit threats. Growth occurred in those sectors despite disorder (p. 330).

> *Intuition note.* The "democracy → growth" chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Barro finds that the rule of law helps growth, partly by encouraging investment, but that democracy is not strongly associated with the rule of law. Others dispute this, so the question is open (pp. 328–330).

**Why democracy may not protect property: the Meltzer-Richard model** (pp. 331–333).
- Setup: everyone pays a share $t$ of income as tax. Revenue is divided equally among all. The budget is balanced, so redistribution is limited by revenue raised (p. 331).
- Anyone with above-average income pays above-average tax but gets only the average benefit, so is a net contributor. Below-average earners are net beneficiaries. Net recipients prefer high taxes and transfers, and net contributors prefer low ones (p. 331).
- Footnote 3: redistribution occurs even though the rich and poor pay the same rate. All that is needed is total tax increasing with income and benefits independent of income (p. 331).
- Income is tied to productivity (Figure 9.2). Below productivity $x_0$, people do not work and live on transfers, so they prefer the maximum rate $t_{max}$. Above the societal average $\bar{x}$, people prefer a tax rate of zero. Between $x_0$ and $\bar{x}$, the desired rate falls along a downward slope. Those close to $\bar{x}$ want rates near zero, and those with low productivity want rates almost as high as $t_{max}$ (pp. 331–332).
- Policy choice: democracies represent a wider portion of society than dictatorships. **Assumption:** dictatorships set tax to match above-average earners' preferences, and democracies match below-average earners'. Then a shift to democracy can be expected to raise taxation and redistribution, likely by a lot if inequality is widespread (p. 332).
- **Link to growth:** investors always choose between consuming and investing. If they judge the tax rate too high, they consume, and growth can be expected to stop without investment (p. 333).
- Box 9.1 (numbers): an investor has 100 units. She expects 10% return per year, so 110 units next year. She is indifferent between 100 today and 105 next year, so she invests. A 55% tax on profits takes 5.5 of the 10 units, leaving 104.5 units. Since $104.5 < 105$, she now prefers to consume today (p. 333).
- Democracy may also give the poor a way to "seize" the property of the rich through taxes. The text cites coups against left-wing democracies in Argentina (1976), Chile (1973) and Guatemala (1954) as a commonly told story, and the limited suffrage of the US founders (pp. 333–334).

> *Intuition note.* In this model, below-average earners gain from taxes. If policy follows their preferences, taxes rise, and high taxes can cut the rich's incentive to invest. Whether this happens in practice is the point of the next two criticisms (pp. 331–334).

**Two criticisms of Meltzer-Richard**, both suggesting it may overstate redistribution and disinvestment (p. 334).
1. Participation falls with income: the poor vote less. If the "decisive" voter in both regime types has above-average productivity ($x > \bar{x}$), the tax-transfer system would not differ between regimes (p. 334).
2. Structural dependence of the state on capital (Marxist argument). Przeworski's (1991) model (pp. 334–336):
 - Two groups: profit takers (P) and wage earners (W). The technological possibility frontier (Figure 9.3) shows all feasible divisions of maximum output. At $P^*$ profit takers get everything, at $W^*$ wage earners get everything, and $E$ is the equal split (p. 334).
 - Maximum output is achieved only if each group receives the entire return on what it invests. Only one point, $M$, does this. In this society $M$ is assumed to lie left of $E$, so profit takers get more (p. 335).
 - Footnote 5: the position of $M$ depends on the relative rates of return on capital and labor. If capital's return is higher, $M$ lies left of $E$ (p. 335).
 - Reason: people can consume capital rather than invest it, or take leisure rather than work. If either group gets less than its full return, resources are underused. This gives the **capitalist possibility frontier** (the curved line), inside the technological frontier except at $M$ (p. 336).
 - Example: an autocracy has found that output is maximised at $M$. It is replaced by a democracy with at least as many wage earners as profit takers. This presumably pushes toward $E$. As in Meltzer-Richard, there is pressure to redistribute (p. 336).
 - If the government actually redistributes, profit takers withdraw capital by consuming it or by capital flight. Output falls from $E$ to point $C$ on the capitalist frontier. The distance $E$–$C$ is the output lost. There is a potential trade-off between equality and growth (p. 336).
 - **Structural Marxist claim:** governments recognise this trade-off and choose growth over equality, whoever controls them (p. 337). Block (1977): state managers depend on economic activity because (1) revenue depends on it and (2) public support falls if it drops. Capitalists "have a veto over state policies" through their investment decisions (p. 337).
 - Even actors who want redistribution probably will not introduce it, because of a smaller future tax base and higher unemployment. The poor suffer first from a downturn (Hibbs 1987), so strongly redistributive systems may be self-defeating. If structural Marxists are right, a change in "who governs" is not likely to have much effect on the tax-transfer system, so a shift to democracy would not have the negative growth effect predicted by Meltzer-Richard (p. 337).

> *Intuition note.* Any attempt to move the split away from $M$ shrinks the "pie". If rulers know this, even a pro-worker government will probably avoid big redistribution. Through this mechanism, regime type then matters little for redistribution and growth (pp. 336–337).

**Assumptions behind Przeworski's predictions** (pp. 337–339).
- (1) If $M$ were right of $E$, a move toward equality would still cut output, because workers would work less. This is structural dependence on labor, and the trade-off remains (p. 338).
- (2) Wage earners get a larger share of a smaller pie. Aggressive redistribution is deterred only if capital flight shrinks output enough to leave wage earners worse off than with no redistribution (p. 338).
- This depends on capitalists' alternatives. With credible exit threats (easy shift to consumption or foreign investment), democracy is less likely to lead to aggressive redistribution and lower growth (p. 338). Democracy is much more likely to lead to it when there are constraints on international capital markets or immobile asset holders dominate (p. 338).
- Evidence offered: rapid expansion of redistribution when international capital markets collapsed (1918–1939), and aggressive redistribution in oil-dominated economies (Persian Gulf states, Norway, Venezuela) (p. 339).
- Footnote 6: inequality need not harm democratic stability in all situations. The poor may not vote to expropriate if they foresee capital flight. The authors' argument suggests inequality should harm democracy only where capitalist elites lack sufficiently credible exit threats (p. 338).
- Any move away from $M$, including toward $P^*$, also cuts output, since workers then take leisure or, below subsistence, are too sick to work. Fogel (2004) estimates most people through history ate enough to work about two hours a day. So even dictators are likely to try to move toward $M$, lest they kill the goose of Aesop's fable (Box 9.2) (p. 339).
- **Conclusion on property rights:** property rights and rule of law seem to promote growth, but it is unclear that democracies protect them better. If the state is the main threat to investment, democracies should grow faster. If the poor are the main threat through redistribution, dictatorships should. If structural Marxists are right, regime matters little through this mechanism (pp. 339–340).

#### Consumption versus investment (pp. 340–341)
- **Argument** (de Schweinitz, Galenson): workers tend to consume rather than invest, as they need to eat and pay rent today. In democracies they can organise in parties and unions, push wages up and cut profits and investment. Governments that need their votes direct money toward them. Governments that push saving too hard risk losing office (p. 340).
- Dictators who care about the future can force saving. Rao (1984) argues that investment programs need strong measures that governments must enforce firmly, and that no party can win a democratic election on current sacrifice for a bright future (paraphrased, p. 340).
- **Implicit assumptions:** the poor consume more than the rich, and growth is driven mainly by capital investment. Even then, it does not follow that dictatorships do better unless we also assume that dictators care more about the future than democratic leaders. Why that would hold is not obvious, so no clear prediction results (p. 341).

#### Autonomy from special interests (pp. 341–342)
- Claim: dictators face less pressure from special interests because democratic leaders can easily be voted out (p. 341).
- *Story 1:* insulated dictators can make hard short-term choices and avoid wasteful spending, so dictatorships grow faster. Key assumption: the dictator chooses to promote growth. The authors ask why he would, if satisfied with his share of revenue (p. 341).
- *Story 2:* autonomous dictators can be predatory, so elites invest less and growth is lower. Concluding that democracies grow faster requires assuming democracies protect property better, which the authors call extremely hard to defend theoretically and empirically (pp. 341–342).
- Overall: the arguments reach quite different predictions (p. 342).

#### The empirical record on growth (p. 342)
Przeworski and Limongi (1993) reviewed 18 studies (21 findings): 8 find dictatorships grow faster, 8 democracies, 5 no effect. They conclude "we do not know whether democracy fosters or hinders economic growth", and suspect that politics matters but "regimes" do not capture the relevant differences (p. 342).

### Empirical evidence on government performance (pp. 342–345)
- **Data (Figure 9.4):** 88 countries, 1960–1990. The horizontal axis is average Polity IV democracy (−10 most dictatorial to +10 most democratic). The vertical axes are six indicators: (a) log GDP per capita, (b) births attended by a physician, (c) prenatal care, (d) vaccination, (e) infant/child (under 5) mortality per thousand, (f) life expectancy. Data come from McGuire (2002) (pp. 342–343).
- **Why expect democracy to do better, especially for the poor** (p. 344):
 1. The poor can vote out leaders who ignore them.
 2. Freer press means governments and voters know about the poor's problems, whereas dictatorships can censor (the text suggests, as an illustration, they might have suppressed images like those from New Orleans after Katrina).
 3. Democratic leaders provide more public goods and redistribution, either to win elections (Lake and Baum; McGuire and Olson) or because they have a broader set of supporters to appease (Bueno de Mesquita et al.; Ghobarah et al.).
 - Footnote 7: Ross (2006) argues that such policies may go to the middle class, not the poor (p. 344).
- **Reading the plots.** At first glance there is little relationship. But the data are "triangular" in every plot (p. 344). Democracies seldom perform poorly, but often fail to outperform many dictatorships. Some dictatorships do as well as democracies. So democracy appears sufficient for some success in these areas, but obviously not necessary (p. 345).
- Triangular data are often a sign of complex causation. Here they suggest greater variability in dictatorships' performance. Comparisons that ignore this variation are likely "misleading at best and wrong at worst" (p. 345).
- One should not simply drop high-performing dictatorships as "different". The task is to understand why some dictatorships perform poorly and others do not (p. 345).
- Footnote 8: oil explains only some, and far from most, of the dictatorial overachievers. Many OPEC members perform poorly (p. 345).
- **Authors' conclusion:** it would be too pessimistic to say that institutions do not matter. They matter for the type of performance governments have incentives to provide, but the democracy/dictatorship split is not always the most useful way to see this. Next chapter: varieties of dictatorship (p. 345).

> *Intuition note.* A "triangle" means democracies seldom score badly, while dictatorships spread from very bad to very good. Comparisons that ignore this spread are likely to mislead, so the task is to understand why some dictatorships do well and others do not (pp. 344–345).

### Conclusion (p. 346)
Often there is no compelling theoretical reason to expect democracies to outperform dictatorships. Empirically, democracies appear to produce relatively high material well-being, but they do not regularly outperform all dictatorships. The authors say that thinking purely in terms of a democracy–dictatorship dichotomy may not be the best way to explain performance variation (p. 346).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Meltzer-Richard with five people, incomes 10, 20, 30, 40, 100. Total = 200, mean = 40.
- Flat tax $t = 0.2$. Revenue = $0.2 \times 200 = 40$. Equal transfer = $40/5 = 8$ each.
- Net gain = transfer − tax paid: 10: $8-2=+6$. 20: $8-4=+4$. 30: $8-6=+2$. 40: $8-8=0$. 100: $8-20=-12$.
- Check: $6+4+2+0-12=0$ (balanced budget).
- Only the person at the average (40) breaks even, and only the richest loses, so three of five (incomes 10, 20, 30) gain and want high taxes. This matches the model: below-average earners are net beneficiaries.
- The median income (30) is below the mean (40). If the decisive voter is the median person, a democracy would favour a high tax. If the decisive voter earned 100 (as when the poor do not vote, or under a dictatorship favouring the rich), the preferred tax would be zero.
- Our toy ignores work and investment choices. These are what the structural-dependence argument adds.

## Questions this raises
- If the empirical results are driven by dictatorships' variability, which institutional features separate good from bad dictatorships (the topic of the next chapter, p. 345)?
- Does the threat of capital flight actually deter redistribution, given that the authors list conditions (exit options) under which it would not (pp. 338–339)?
- If democracies spend on the middle class rather than the poor (Ross 2006), do the well-being gains in Figure 9.4 reach the poorest (p. 344)?
- Could the triangular shape arise from measurement choices such as the Polity IV scale rather than from the causal process the authors suggest (pp. 342, 345)?
