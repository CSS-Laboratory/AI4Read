# Problems with Group Decision Making (part 2/2): the chaos-theorem lesson, Arrow's theorem, conclusion, problems, Box 11.2

## L0 — One line
Arrow's theorem shows that every group decision rule that guarantees a transitive (stable) group preference must give up at least one minimal fairness condition, so no perfect democratic institution exists.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether majority rule, which many people equate with democracy, reliably gives stable and fair group decisions. When preferences are not single-peaked, or issues have several dimensions, majorities may cycle, or whoever controls the agenda decides. Arrow's theorem generalises this beyond majority rule. A rule that must guarantee transitive group preferences has to sacrifice at least one of nondictatorship, universal admissibility, unanimity, or independence from irrelevant alternatives. So no perfect set of institutions exists. Institutional design trades stability against fairness and is political, not technocratic. Systems of government may resolve the trade-off differently from mechanism to mechanism, and the authors say the variety of democratic institutions is itself implied by the theorem.

**Prerequisites:** transitive and complete preference orderings; majority cycling (Condorcet's paradox); agenda setting; single-peaked preferences and the median voter; ideal points and indifference curves in a policy space.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Chaos-theorem lesson.** If politics cannot be reduced to one dimension, then in a wide set of circumstances either stable outcomes do not occur or an agenda controller imposes them. The authors say it is likely that institutions play a significant role in alleviating the "chaos" (p. 439).
- **Arrow's four minimal fairness conditions.** These are nondictatorship, universal admissibility, unanimity (Pareto) and independence from irrelevant alternatives (IIA). Each ties to problems already met under majority rule (pp. 440–442).
- **Arrow's theorem.** Any decision process that guarantees group transitivity, and hence stable outcomes, must sacrifice at least one of the four conditions (p. 442).
- **Institutional trilemma.** If unanimity and IIA are taken as given, an institution can have at most two of: stable outcomes, universal admissibility, nondictatorship (pp. 442–443).
- **Clear outcomes are not necessarily the "will of the group".** They may reflect excluded preferences or agenda control. Choosing a decisive institution amounts to choosing which minority controls the agenda (pp. 443–444).
- **Blair and Pollack extension.** Weakening transitivity to acyclicality allows rules meeting Arrow's other conditions, but if alternatives outnumber individuals, some individual can veto some proposals, which the authors read as an "oligarchy" protecting the status quo. Institutional design is therefore political (p. 444).
- **Constitutions are sets of mechanisms.** Arrow's theorem applies to each mechanism separately. Systems of government may differ in how their mechanisms resolve the trade-off; the authors say the variety of democratic institutions is itself implied by the theorem (pp. 444–446).
- **Box 11.2.** Radial symmetry (Plott 1967) is sufficient for a stable outcome in two-dimensional majority voting. Such conditions are very restrictive, so the authors judge it reasonable to conclude that majority rule is unstable in multidimensional situations except under very unusual circumstances (pp. 453–455).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the chaos theorem discussion (p. 439)
- The set of preferences that prevents majority cycling in two or more dimensions is "extremely rare and special". Box 11.2 gives details (p. 439).
- Lesson: if politics cannot be reduced to a single issue dimension, then in a wide set of circumstances either (a) stable outcomes will not occur or (b) stable outcomes are imposed by whoever controls the agenda (p. 439).
- The authors cite McCarty and Meirowitz (2007) for the importance of studying the institutions within which choices are made. They say it is *likely* that these institutions play a significant role in alleviating the "chaos" (p. 439).

*Intuition note.* With several issues at once, stability occurs only in rare circumstances, so a majority can often find something better than the current outcome. Otherwise stability is imposed by whoever controls the agenda, which is why the authors point to the role of institutions.

### Arrow's theorem (pp. 439–444)

**Recap of the problems with majority rule (pp. 439–440).**
- Condorcet's paradox: rational individuals can form a group that cannot choose rationally in round-robin tournaments, because majorities can cycle indefinitely (p. 439).
- The Borda count lets clear winners emerge under some conditions, but its results are not robust. Changing rankings of irrelevant alternatives can change the outcome (p. 439).
- "Single elimination" tournaments forming an agenda can avoid cycles. But whoever controls the agenda could dictate the outcome (p. 439).
- The median voter theorem gives stability when there is a single issue dimension, voters have single-peaked preferences, and other conditions are met. The authors call it "cold comfort" for two reasons (pp. 439–440):
  1. It requires restrictions on preferences. If some voters have non-single-peaked preferences, majority cycles can arise. It seems to the authors that a "democratic" process that works reliably only when participation is restricted to those with the "right kind" of preferences loses much of its ethical appeal (p. 440).
  2. Many crucial political questions have more than one dimension. The three-group distributional bargaining example was unstable ("chaotic"). Stability in two or more dimensions occurs only in exceedingly rare circumstances, and outcomes can vacillate (p. 440).
- Hence it is hard to guarantee a stable group choice without either granting someone agenda-setting power or restricting preferences (p. 440).

**The theorem (p. 440).**
- Arrow (1963) shows that these problems are in some ways special cases of a more fundamental problem. It is impossible to design *any* decision system, not just majority rule, that aggregates individual preferences, guarantees a rational outcome, and meets what Arrow argued was a minimal standard of fairness (p. 440).
- Arrow chose a minimal set of conditions, so that violating them would produce a procedure unfair in a way evident to all (p. 440).
- Footnote 19: the theorem is often called the "impossibility" theorem. Arrow himself labelled it the "possibility theorem" (p. 440).

**Arrow's four fairness conditions (pp. 440–442).**
1. *Nondictatorship.* No individual fully determines the group outcome in disregard of the others' preferences. Very minimal: it only rules out an individual who, if she prefers x to y, forces the group to choose x irrespective of everyone else. A dictator might be benevolent, but the mechanism is still inherently unfair (pp. 440–441).
2. *Universal admissibility.* The rule must work with any logically possible set of individual preference orderings. Individuals may adopt any rational ordering. It is tied to the doctrine of individualism: one may prohibit acting on desires but should not judge the social desirability of the desires themselves (p. 441). Riker (1982, 117), as quoted: if outcomes rest on individual judgments, restricting those judgments means the outcome rests as much on the restriction (p. 441). In voting, every voter may vote as she pleases (p. 441).
3. *Unanimity (Pareto optimality).* If all individuals prefer x to y, the group preference must also be x over y. Failing this is "perverse", like a vending machine giving Sprite when you press Coke (p. 441).
4. *Independence from irrelevant alternatives (IIA).* The group choice among alternatives in a subset should depend only on the rankings of those alternatives. Example: a group prefers x to y when choosing among x, y, z. If someone changes her ranking of z, the group must still prefer x to y (pp. 441–442). Varian (1993): the group's x–y preference should depend only on how people rank x versus y (p. 442).
   - A rule respects IIA whenever the group's x–y ranking depends only on each member's relative ranking of x and y (Geanakoplos 2005). The Borda count can violate this (p. 442).
   - Some scholars read it as reliability: the rule returns the same decision if rankings of the relevant alternatives are unchanged (Riker 1982). So it concerns reliability of the aggregation technology as much as fairness (p. 442).
- Summary: a fair mechanism must prevent dictatorship, must not restrict preference types, and must link group choice to individual preferences at least rudimentarily (unanimity, IIA) (p. 442).

**Statement of Arrow's theorem as given (p. 442).**
- Group transitivity is necessary for stable outcomes in the absence of manipulation by powerful minorities acting as agenda setters (p. 442).
- Every decision process we could design, including any majority-rule one, must sacrifice at least one of Arrow's four conditions if it is to guarantee group transitivity and hence stable outcomes (p. 442).
- Put differently: if all four conditions are insisted on, the possibility of group intransitivity must be accepted (p. 442).

*Intuition note.* You can get a stable, always-transitive group preference only by paying with something else: giving one person control, banning some preferences, or ignoring the link to individual rankings. The theorem says this holds for every rule, not just for voting by majority.

**The institutional trilemma (pp. 442–443, Figure 11.10).**
- Take unanimity and IIA as uncontroversial and given. Then there is a "trilemma" between stable outcomes, universal admissibility and nondictatorship. Institutions can have at most two of the three (p. 442).
- Figure 11.10 is a triangle: vertex A = group transitivity (stable outcomes), B = nondictatorship, C = universal admissibility (p. 443).
- Reading the triangle (p. 443):
  - Guarantee transitivity (A): give up B or C.
  - Avoid dictatorship (B): give up A or C.
  - Keep individual preferences inviolable (C): give up A or B.
- Designers must choose their poison among restrictions on individual preferences, dictatorship, and possible group intransitivity (p. 443).

*Intuition note.* Think of three goods where you can pick only two. The text's own examples of what each choice costs are in the Conclusion below.

**What a clear outcome tells us (pp. 443–444).**
- Following Shepsle (1992), the theorem shows it is at least difficult to read any group outcome as *necessarily* reflecting the will of the group (p. 443).
- A clear decision *may* mean preferences lined up so that the outcome represents a large portion of the group. But it may also mean that people with inconvenient preferences were excluded, or that some actor exercised agenda control. Then the outcome may reflect a powerful subset, not the group, or even a majority (pp. 443–444).
- So when choosing among decisive (non-intransitive) institutions, we are in effect choosing which minority may influence outcomes through agenda control (p. 444).

**Blair and Pollack (p. 444).**
- Blair and Pollack (1982a, 1982b) replace transitivity with the weaker *acyclicality* (no cycles based on strict preferences). Rules meeting Arrow's other conditions then exist (p. 444).
- But if the number of alternatives exceeds the number of individuals, some individual can veto some proposals. As alternatives increase without limit, the share an individual can veto approaches 100 percent (p. 444).
- The authors say this suggests that stability under otherwise fair rules occurs because some subset of actors, which Blair and Pollack call an "oligarchy", is able to veto challenges to the status quo that a majority prefers. Choosing decisive institutions means choosing which oligarchy to empower. Institutional design is a political, not technocratic, exercise (p. 444).

*Intuition note.* Allowing the group preference to be a bit less tidy means rules meeting Arrow's other conditions exist. But if alternatives outnumber individuals, stability then depends on some actors who can veto change.

### Conclusion (pp. 444–446)
- Majority rule is the usual picture of democracy. There is a fundamental tension between guaranteeing coherent, stable group choices and guaranteeing individuals' freedom to form preferences that influence decisions (p. 444).
- Arrow's theorem extends this tension to a wide set of minimally fair methods. Most conceptions of democracy are probably more ambitious than Arrow's criteria, so the implications are profound (p. 444).
- The theorem's most direct implications concern particular decision mechanisms, not collections of procedures. A constitution stipulates a whole set of procedures: choice of the executive, of legislators, how laws are made, how branches interact, judicial review, national versus local jurisdiction (p. 444).
- The theorem applies to each mechanism. The trilemma may be resolved differently from mechanism to mechanism within one constitution (p. 445). Footnote 20: it also applies to student organisations, unions, boards, families and friends choosing a film (p. 445).
- Examples of how mechanisms resolve it (p. 445):
  - Reducing the number of alternatives: the two-party system for electing US presidents can be seen this way.
  - Granting agenda-setting power to an individual: a large literature on the US Congress argues committee chairs play this role. Cabinet ministers play a similar role in many parliamentary systems.
- Every mechanism faces the trade-offs and every system of government is a collection of mechanisms. We can ask which fairness criterion is sacrificed where stability is privileged. Is stability produced by strong agenda setters or by restrictions on preferences? If the latter, are some actors excluded, or are participants socialised to hold the "right" preferences? (p. 445)
- Most basic implication: there is no perfect set of decision-making institutions. Every set risks intransitivity or compromises a fairness condition, so democracy must "in some sense" be imperfect (p. 445).
- The authors say perhaps this inspired Churchill's remark that democracy is the worst form of government except for all the others tried (footnote 21: House of Commons, 11 November 1947) (p. 445).
- Preview (pp. 445–446): Ch. 12 on choosing the head of government and its relation to the legislature. Ch. 13 on electing legislators. Ch. 14 on how group preferences and electoral laws shape party systems. The authors say party systems can be seen as encouraging stability by reducing the number of alternatives. Ch. 15 on federalism, bicameralism and constitutional review, seen as attempts to limit powerful agenda setters by pitting them against each other.
- Institutional variety is itself implied by Arrow's theorem. Institutional choice is a choice of "second bests", dictated by context. Some situations make instability threatening enough to make sacrificing fairness conditions reasonable, whereas others may permit accepting some instability to protect fairness (p. 446).
- Caveat: the idea that society "chooses" optimal institutions is itself in tension with the theorem. Today's institutions may come from past agenda setters' choices, or from times when people with certain preferences were barred from constitutional deliberations (p. 446).

### Box 11.2: Stability in two-dimensional majority-rule voting (pp. 453–455)
- Purpose: informal evidence that stability in multidimensional voting is rare. The authors treat two dimensions, and say the logic applies to more dimensions (p. 453).
- **Radial symmetry** (Plott 1967): one individual is the median voter on both dimensions, and the other voters are aligned symmetrically around that person (p. 453).
- Figure 11.17: voters A, B, C on issue 1 (education) and issue 2 (health care). B is median on both and lies on the line between A and C (the dashed line) (pp. 453–454).
  - If B's ideal point were the status quo, no majority could displace it. It is an equilibrium (p. 453).
  - The circles around A and C are indifference curves through B. Each shows policies A or C is indifferent about versus B. Points inside are preferred to B by that voter (p. 453).
  - B has no circle, since by definition nothing is preferred to her ideal point (p. 453, footnote 22).
  - To replace B, two of three voters must prefer an alternative. The circles do not overlap, so A and C cannot agree on one. Any alternative to B gets at most one vote and loses (pp. 453–454).
- A small shift makes this fail. In Figure 11.18, C's ideal point moves "an arbitrary amount" southeast off the line (p. 454).
  - The circles of A and C now overlap in a lens-shaped area, the **winset** of B: all alternatives that both A and C prefer to B (p. 454).
  - A or C can propose a point in it, say $P_1$, and it wins a majority vote. New indifference curves through $P_1$ again produce a winset, so the process continues and chaos-theorem instability ensues (p. 454).
- Plott (1967) proved radial symmetry is *sufficient* for a stable outcome in two dimensions. It is not strictly necessary. Other sufficient conditions have been found, but they are also quite restrictive (p. 455, footnote 23).
- Conclusion: it is reasonable to conclude majority rule is unstable in multidimensional situations except under very unusual circumstances (p. 455).

*Intuition note.* In this example B is safe because A and C are aligned on a line through B and block each other (the authors say radial symmetry is sufficient, not strictly necessary). Tilt one voter slightly, and A and C share a region they both prefer, so they can outvote B.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three voters rank x, y, z (1 = best):
- Voter 1: x > y > z
- Voter 2: y > z > x
- Voter 3: z > x > y

Pairwise majority votes:
- x vs y: voters 1 and 3 prefer x → x wins 2–1.
- y vs z: voters 1 and 2 prefer y → y wins 2–1.
- z vs x: voters 2 and 3 prefer z → z wins 2–1.

The group prefers x > y, y > z, and z > x, which is intransitive (a cycle). Here no pair is ranked the same way by all three voters, so unanimity is never triggered. Any ordering of the three voters' rankings is allowed (universal admissibility), and no single voter decides (nondictatorship). Arrow's theorem says that any rule meeting universal admissibility, unanimity and IIA that never produces a cycle must be a dictatorship.

Make voter 1 a dictator: the group ranking is simply x > y > z. It is transitive, but we paid with nondictatorship. This is one corner of the trilemma.

## Concepts
- [[arrow-impossibility-theorem]] — every decision process guaranteeing group transitivity must sacrifice at least one of four fairness conditions; Arrow himself called it the "possibility theorem" (pp. 440, 442)
- [[nondictatorship]] (new) — no individual fully determines the outcome regardless of others' preferences (p. 440)
- [[universal-admissibility]] (new) — the rule must work for any rational preference ordering individuals might hold (p. 441)
- [[unanimity-pareto-condition]] (new) — if all prefer x to y, the group must prefer x to y (p. 441)
- [[independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives]] — group x–y ranking depends only on individuals' x–y rankings; also read as reliability (pp. 441–442)
- [[institutional-trilemma]] (new) — with unanimity and IIA given, institutions have at most two of: stability, universal admissibility, nondictatorship (pp. 442–443)
- [[cycling-intransitivity]] — group preferences may be intransitive; transitivity is needed for stability without agenda manipulation (p. 442)
- [[acyclicality]] (new) — Blair and Pollack's weaker condition: no cycles based on strict preferences (p. 444)
- [[oligarchy-blair-pollack]] (new) — the subset able to veto majority-preferred changes under acyclic fair rules (p. 444)
- [[agenda-control]] — controlling order or menu of votes can dictate outcomes; chairs and ministers as examples (pp. 439, 445)
- [[chaos-theorem]] (new) — in more than one dimension, stability needs rare conditions or agenda control (p. 439)
- [[radial-symmetry]] (new) — Plott's sufficient condition: a median voter on both dimensions, others aligned symmetrically around her (pp. 453, 455)
- [[winset]] — set of alternatives that beat the status quo by majority; here the overlap of indifference circles (p. 454)
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — actors' preferences as points in policy space, nearer is better (p. 450)
- [[median-voter-theorem]] — stability with one dimension and single-peaked preferences; "cold comfort" (pp. 439–440)
- [[institutions]] — here, decision mechanisms whose design resolves the trade-offs; institutional design is political (pp. 444–446)

## Questions this raises
- If the stability of a decisive institution always comes from some privileged minority, how do we judge which minority is least objectionable?
- How could one tell empirically whether a mechanism privileges stability through agenda setters or through restrictions on preferences?
- Do institutions such as bicameralism or federalism really reduce agenda control, as Chapter 15 will claim?
- How often do real voter preferences approach radial symmetry or single-peakedness, so that cycling is not a practical problem?
