# Varieties of Dictatorship (part 1/3): typology, civilian subtypes, electoral authoritarianism

## L0 — One line
One useful way to classify dictatorships is by who forms the ruler's support coalition (the royal family, the military, or neither, which is civilian), and the authors argue that electoral competition is a second dimension along which dictatorships can be placed, not a separate type.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The authors ask how dictatorships differ. They sort them by the ruler's support coalition (the group he needs to stay in power): monarchy (family), military, or civilian. Civilian regimes split into dominant-party ones (one party controls office) and personalistic ones (the leader controls policy). Fallen dictators are likely to be replaced from their own coalition. The authors reject "electoral authoritarianism" as a separate type (elections plus systematic breaches of democratic norms). They argue that electoral competition is a second dimension, shaped largely by strategic interaction between dictator and opposition. A reported consensus is that dictatorial elections help rulers stabilise. This part also previews regime durability and selectorate theory.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a regime (democracy vs dictatorship); reading simple tables and percentages; preference falsification (a signalling idea from an earlier chapter).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Cheibub, Gandhi and Vreeland note that dictators are nearly always removed by insiders, so classifying regimes by the identity of the "support coalition" is useful. This gives three types: monarchy, military, civilian (p. 351).
- Classification follows a three-step rule: find the effective head of government, then ask two questions. Is the head a "king" with a hereditary predecessor or successor? Is the head a current or past member of the armed forces? Otherwise the regime is civilian (pp. 354–355).
- In 1946–1996 data, a dictator was replaced by a dictator of the same type about half the time. If democratic successors are ignored, this rises to 70% (military) and 65% (civilian). This is why one can speak of dictatorial regimes, not just leaders (p. 356).
- Civilian dictators lack a ready-made base, so they build one. Dominant-party regimes use a party to distribute perks and sort loyalists. Personalistic rulers often undermine parties and militaries, and many cultivate personality cults (glorification of the leader) (pp. 359–362).
- On Marquez's account (which the authors follow), personality cults are best understood less as indoctrination and more as costly signals of loyalty: praise that is costly to give, and so credible. They also help the dictator gauge support and make it hard for opposition groups to coordinate, and some people may come to believe (pp. 363–364).
- Electoral authoritarianism (regimes with elections but systematic breaches of democratic norms; hegemonic electoral = the leader's party routinely wins overwhelming majorities, competitive authoritarian = opposition parties win substantial minorities) is, the authors argue, not a separate dictatorship type. The authors give three doubts, and data showing every type varies on it (pp. 367–369).
- Electoral competition can change within one regime, as under Marcos. The authors suggest it is perhaps best seen as an outcome or policy choice, shaped in large part by strategic interaction between dictator and opposition (pp. 369–370).
- Box 10.1: about half of the legislative and presidential elections in 1946–2000 took place in dictatorships. These elections vary widely, and they are thought to stabilise dictators, not to hasten democratisation (pp. 371–373).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter overview and preview (pp. 349–351)
- The chapter overview says dictatorships can be distinguished by their "support coalitions", giving three types. Civilian ones split into personalistic and dominant-party (p. 349).
- **Preview claims, argued in later parts (not covered here):** monarchies are the most stable form, dominant-party regimes outlast personalistic ones, and personalistic ones outlast military ones. Allowing multiparty legislatures ("institutionalizing") might extend rule (p. 349).
- **Preview of selectorate theory.** Selectorate = those with a say in selecting the leader. Winning coalition = those in the selectorate whose support the leader needs to stay in office (p. 350).
  - Large winning coalition and large selectorate (democracies): incentives to produce public goods, so good performance.
  - Small winning coalition and large selectorate (dominant-party dictatorships): private rewards to the coalition, so poor performance.
  - Small winning coalition and small selectorate (monarchies, military dictatorships): middling performance (p. 350).
- According to selectorate theory, the key to material well-being has less to do with democracy vs dictatorship than with the sizes of winning coalition and selectorate (p. 351).

### Toward a typology of authoritarian regimes (pp. 351–355)
- Many classifications exist. Cheibub, Gandhi and Vreeland (2010) classify by how rulers are removed. Dictators are nearly always deposed by fellow regime members, so the classification looks at the "inner sanctum" or support coalition (p. 351).
- Footnote 2: some parliamentary democracies have monarchs as symbolic heads of state, so a monarch alone does not mean dictatorship (p. 351).
- **Monarchy:** the executive comes to and keeps power through family and kin networks (p. 354).
  - Succession need not follow primogeniture (first-born inherits). Even if the procedure is broken, the new leader typically must have royal-family elite support (p. 352).
  - Examples: the emir of Qatar in 1992 installed sons, a grandson and nephews in key posts. In Kuwait the family "elects" the ruler by consensus. In Swaziland the Royal Council (of the royal family) plays the key role in choosing the successor, and the king cannot name his own (p. 352).
  - Swazi king Mswati III has fourteen wives and twenty-three children. The king is expected to consolidate his position by marrying from every clan (p. 352).
- **Military dictatorship:** the executive relies on the armed forces. Rule is usually by a "junta" (committee). High-ranking officers have small juntas. Lower-ranked officers, perhaps after a coup, often have larger ones to build support (p. 352).
  - Juntas often claim to be "guardians of the national interest" (Cheibub et al.). The authors say it is not clear that military rulers actually have such altruistic motives. Some scholars see coups as driven by class conflict or corporate interests. Examples given are the Latin American coups in Argentina 1976, Chile 1973 and Guatemala 1954. The authors speculate that Egypt's military (SCAF) moved against Mubarak in 2011 to protect its economic interests (pp. 352–353).
  - The main threat to military rule comes from within the military. In Guinea there were coups and countercoups in 1984, 1985, 1996, 2008 and 2009. In Guatemala power changed hands thirteen times between military factions after 1945 (pp. 353–354).
- **Civilian dictatorship:** all others. Civilian rulers have no immediate institutional base, so many create regime parties or personality cults (p. 354).
- **Coding rules (Fig. 10.1, p. 355):**
  1. Identify the effective head of government. Sometimes an "eminence grise" stands behind a figurehead (p. 354).
  2. Does he bear the title "king" and have a hereditary successor or predecessor? If yes: monarchy.
  3. If no: is he a current or past member of the armed forces? If yes: military. If no: civilian (pp. 354–355).
  - Guerrilla leaders (Castro, Museveni, Kagame) count as civilian, since they cannot rely on the military as members can. The military is often a main threat to them (p. 355).
- All dictators, like democratic leaders, rely on a coalition and must keep it happy (p. 355).

**Intuition note.** On this approach, the type of dictatorship is read from the group the ruler depends on to gain and keep power, not from how powerful he looks. Even a "one-man" regime depends on a group behind him.

### Succession evidence (p. 356)
- Implication: a removed dictator is likely replaced by a defector from his own coalition, so by a similar type (p. 356).
- Data (Gandhi and Przeworski 2007): 388 leaders who took power after 1945 and left before 1996 for reasons other than natural death (p. 356).

| Current \ Successor | Monarchy | Military | Civilian | Democrat | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monarchy | 11 | 6 | 4 | 1 | 22 |
| Military | 0 | 89 | 38 | 52 | 179 |
| Civilian | 2 | 53 | 103 | 29 | 187 |
| Total | 13 | 148 | 145 | 82 | 388 |

- Same-type replacement is about 50% on average: 11 of 22 monarchs, 89 of 179 military leaders, 103 of 187 civilians (p. 356).
- Ignoring democratic successors, 70% of military and 65% of civilian leaders were followed by the same type (p. 356). (added step: 89/(179−52) = 89/127 ≈ 70%; 103/(187−29) = 103/158 ≈ 65%.)
- This persistence is why one can speak of regimes, not only leaders (p. 356).

### An overview across time (pp. 356–359)
- Map 10.1 shows regime types for 2008, 1998, 1988, 1978 and 1968. Figure 10.2 shows counts and percentages by year, 1946–2008 (p. 356). The civilian form has always been the most common (p. 356).
- In 2008 there were 74 dictatorships: 38 civilian (51.4%), 24 military (32.4%), 12 monarchies (16.2%) (pp. 356, 359).
- Civilian and military counts changed a lot over time, monarchy counts much less. The authors say this suggests monarchies are a particularly stable form (p. 359).
- Table 10.2 lists the 2008 classification (p. 359).

### Subcategories of civilian dictatorships (pp. 359–364)
**Dominant-party dictatorship:** one party dominates access to office and control over policy, though other parties may exist and compete as minor players (Geddes 2003) (p. 359). Footnote 7: the authors prefer this label to emphasise that opposition parties are sometimes allowed (p. 359).
- Parties recruit and socialise the elite, as in democracies. In the USSR, CPSU membership was, for all intents and purposes, a necessary condition for joining the ruling class (the nomenklatura). Perks rose with rank: shops, foreign goods, foreign travel (p. 360).
- **Poland (Gandhi and Przeworski):** after the 1948 "merger" forming the PUWP, the communists tolerated other parties. These ran as a single approved list, so elections only ratified seat distribution (p. 360).
  - **Gandhi and Przeworski's reading:** this "multipartism" was a menu of contracts. People with different attitudes sorted themselves into parties with different degrees of identification with the regime and different perks (highest in the PUWP, lowest in the Catholic groups). This maximised support and visibly isolated those who would make no gesture of support (pp. 360–361).
- **Hough's view:** the Soviet government tied individuals' fate to the regime's by admitting many educated people to the party. They could advance if they avoided dissidence, and the managerial class had reason to wonder what an anti-Communist revolution would mean for them personally (p. 361).
- **Mexico's PRI (Magaloni 2006):** policies kept peasants poor and dependent on PRI patronage. Citizens' choices were free but constrained by "strategic dilemmas" (the "tragic brilliance" of the regime). Term limits made legislators depend on the party for later jobs and kept ambitious politicians waiting their turn (p. 361).

**Personalistic dictatorship:** the leader, though often supported by a party or military, keeps personal control of policy and of selecting personnel, because these organisations are not developed or autonomous enough to stop him (Geddes 2003) (p. 361).
- Such leaders often undermine parties and militaries to deny rivals a base, for example by frequent rotation of personnel. Typical features: weak press, strong secret police, arbitrary violence (p. 361).
- **Personality cults:** Niyazov's *Ruhnama* in Turkmenistan was the chief textbook at all levels, and all citizens seeking a driving licence had to take a sixteen-hour course on it. His successor replaced it with his own books. Kim Jong-il inherited a cult from Kim Il-sung and claimed to control the weather and to teleport. Qaddafi's *Green Book* was widely studied. Qaddafi gave up any official title yet was seen as the all-powerful ruler (pp. 362–363).
- **Standard story:** cults alter beliefs by indoctrination and by removing alternative information, so they produce false beliefs and loyalty. **Authors' objection:** cults are often "ridiculously unbelievable" (p. 363).
- **Marquez's alternative (2011):** cults are best understood as dictators harnessing "signaling" for social control. This rests on preference falsification (an earlier-chapter idea: lying about one's preferences under pressure) (pp. 363–364).
  - **Dictator's dilemma (Wintrobe 2001):** repression keeps him in power but gives everyone reason to lie, so he never learns his true support. He can limit repression and learn it, which often happens when he cannot afford a reliable spy network. Or he can repress and risk a surprise later (p. 364).
  - To be credible a signal must be costly: you must praise the leader as superhuman and act, for example by denouncing others or displaying his image. Raising how "over the top" the claims are lets the dictator find where public acceptance stops (p. 364).
  - Three benefits for the dictator: (1) opposition groups struggle to organise because citizens hide preferences for fear of denunciation; (2) he gauges societal support; (3) some people become true believers (p. 364).
  - **Marquez:** once in full swing, a cult "practically runs itself", an equilibrium of lies that can be hard to disrupt unless people get a credible signal that others also hate the dictator and will act (p. 364).
- Fig. 10.3: three types, with civilian split into dominant-party and personalistic (pp. 364–365).

**Intuition note.** On this account, an absurd claim is useful because people must say it publicly. Saying it costs them something, so it reveals who is willing to show loyalty.

### Electoral authoritarianism (pp. 365–370)
- **Definition (Schedler):** leaders hold elections and tolerate some pluralism and interparty competition, but violate minimal democratic norms so severely and systematically that the regime cannot be classed as democratic. Outright fraud is often unnecessary, since the violations may be subtle yet broad (p. 365).
- Contrast: a **politically closed** regime grants no opposition party legal space (China, Cuba, Turkmenistan, North Korea, Burma). Electoral authoritarian regimes allow some parties, often on an unequal playing field (p. 365).
- Two subtypes (Diamond 2002) (p. 366):
  - **Hegemonic electoral:** the leader's party routinely wins overwhelming majorities, with no meaningful contest for power (Egypt, Singapore, Burkina Faso, Tunisia, Angola, Uzbekistan in the 1990s).
  - **Competitive authoritarian:** opposition parties win substantial minorities in presidential or legislative elections (Croatia under Tudjman, Russia under Putin, Peru under Fujimori, and others) (p. 366).
- Fig. 10.4 shows the alternative typology: civilian regimes split into politically closed (personalistic, dominant-party) and electoral authoritarian (hegemonic, competitive) (p. 366).

**The authors' three doubts** (author's voice) (pp. 367–368):
1. **Coding.** It is hard to give precise rules, and there are almost as many classifications as scholars. Levitsky and Way code a regime hegemonic if presidents are reelected with more than 70% of the vote. Brownlee does so if the largest party wins more than 75% in legislative elections. Subjective rules raise miscoding and lack one authoritative data set. So it is difficult to know whether differing conclusions reflect theory or classification (p. 367).
2. **Research questions.** It would be problematic to test whether electoral fraud, competition or regime stability vary across types if the types are themselves defined by these traits (a similar problem to Freedom House). A fix is a minimalist measure. Hyde and Marinov's NELDA calls an election competitive if (i) opposition is allowed, (ii) multiple parties are legal, and (iii) more than one candidate competes (pp. 367–368).
3. **Two dimensions confused (the biggest concern).** Support coalition is one dimension. Electoral competition is a second one, not a separate category (p. 368).

**Evidence (Table 10.3, Brownlee 2009; country-years, 1975–2004)** (p. 369):

| Regime type | Closed | Hegemonic | Competitive | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Military | 178 | 47 | 61 | 286 (14.2%) |
| Personalistic | 296 | 86 | 155 | 537 (26.7%) |
| Dominant party | 586 | 165 | 187 | 938 (46.6%) |
| Monarchy | 203 | 51 | 0 | 254 (12.6%) |
| Total | 1,263 (62.6%) | 349 (17.3%) | 403 (20.0%) | 2,015 |

- Brownlee's coding (fn. 17): a 7-point World Bank index recoded so scores 1–4 = politically closed, 5–6 = hegemonic electoral, 7 = competitive authoritarian (p. 368).
- Every type has both closed and open variants. Closed is more common overall (about 63% of the time) and within each type. Military, personalistic and dominant-party regimes are fairly evenly split between hegemonic and competitive. Monarchies are strongly closed: one year in five is electoral authoritarian, and none is competitive (pp. 368–369).
- **Authors' conclusion:** electoral authoritarianism is "not a separate category of dictatorship, but a second dimension" (p. 369).
- **Marcos case (Philippines, 1972–1986)** (pp. 369–370):
  - He was elected in 1965 and 1969 (the 1969 election saw fraud and violence). Martial law came in 1972 and a new constitution in 1973 that let him go beyond the two-term limit. The regime is generally seen as personalistic (p. 369).
  - Brownlee's coding: closed before 1979; hegemonic electoral 1979–1983. This included the 1981 presidential election, held six months after martial law was lifted and boycotted by major opposition parties, which Marcos won in a landslide. Competitive authoritarian 1984–1986 (p. 369).
  - In 1986 the opposition united behind Corazon Aquino. Her husband, Benigno Aquino Jr., had been jailed for 7 years from 1973 and was assassinated in 1983 on his return, by his military escort. When the National Assembly declared Marcos the winner, the "People's Power Revolution" removed him (p. 370).
  - Few scholars would say there were three regimes. What changed were the strategies of dictator and opposition (p. 370).
- **Conclusion:** electoral competitiveness is perhaps best seen as an outcome or policy choice that varies within and across regime types, dictated in large part by strategic interaction. The authors prefer Fig. 10.3 (p. 370).

**Intuition note.** The "support coalition" says who the regime rests on. The "level of electoral competition" says what the ruler and opposition are doing this year. The second can change while the first stays the same.

### Box 10.1: Elections in dictatorships (pp. 371–373)
- About half of the legislative and presidential elections in 1946–2000 took place in dictatorships. As of 2011, only Brunei, China, Eritrea, Qatar and Saudi Arabia had held no national-level elections in the postwar period. The authors say there has arguably been experience of, or interest in, elections even in these five (local elections, a referendum, scheduled elections), and conclude that only Brunei has no electoral roots at all (p. 371).
- **Variation (Gandhi and Lust-Okar 2009):**
  - Candidate choice: Iraq under Saddam had yes/no referenda. Tanzania in the 1960s had two ruling-party candidates per district. Kenya and Zambia held similar elections under one-party rule. Russia under Putin and some other states had multiparty competition (p. 371).
  - Franchise: the USSR let basically all adults vote. In the UAE's first legislative elections in 2006, only 6,689 people (1% of the population) could vote, all handpicked (pp. 371–372).
  - Competitiveness: outcomes are often predetermined by coercion, rigging or invented totals. In Iran in 2004 the Council of Guardians deemed thousands of would-be candidates unfit, including virtually all reformist ones. Saddam won 100% with 100% turnout in 2002 and 99.96% in 1995 (p. 372).
- **Electoral rules:**
  - Dictatorships are much more likely than democracies to use majoritarian systems (shown in Ch. 13). One conjecture is that these are easier to manipulate. A study of twenty-four former communist countries found majoritarian elections more often manipulated (Birch 2007) (p. 372).
  - **Lust-Okar and Jamal's claim:** dominant-party dictatorships prefer majoritarian systems, which favour their large party. Monarchs, as political arbitrators whose legitimacy comes from outside popular support, prefer proportional systems that keep rivals divided. The authors say empirical evidence from the Middle East supports this (pp. 372–373).
- **Two older views, now shown wrong:** elections as window dressing (for hegemonic regimes) and as a prelude to democratisation (for competitive ones). The overall consensus is that authoritarian elections have significant consequences and help dictators stabilise their rule (p. 373).
- **Gandhi and Lust-Okar's three functions** (p. 373):
  1. Co-opt elites, party members and societal groups. Elections serve as an arena for patronage and a more "efficient" way to manage competition inside the regime. They can also deter defection, since one-sided results hide the true level of opposition.
  2. Co-opt, divide or control opposition groups by letting only some compete.
  3. Give the dictator information on support and opposition strongholds, which he can then target. The text breaks off here, mid-quotation (p. 373).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three invented regimes classified with Fig. 10.1 (p. 355), then placed on the second dimension with Brownlee's recoding (fn. 17, p. 368).

| Regime | Q2: king + hereditary link? | Q3: current or past armed-forces member? | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | Yes | (not asked) | Monarchy |
| Y | No | Yes (ex-colonel) | Military |
| Z | No | No (rebel leader, never served) | Civilian |

Regime Z over three years has index scores 3, 5, 7.
- Year 1: score 3, in 1–4, so politically closed.
- Year 2: score 5, in 5–6, so hegemonic electoral.
- Year 3: score 7, so competitive authoritarian.

Z's type is the same in all three years (civilian), while its electoral category changes. This is the authors' point, as with Marcos: electoral competition moves within a regime type.

Succession arithmetic with Table 10.1 numbers: if 10 military leaders fall, of whom 3 are replaced by democrats and 5 by military leaders, the same-type share is 5/10 = 50%. Ignoring democrats it is 5/(10−3) = 5/7 ≈ 71%.

## Concepts
- [[support-coalition]] (new) — the group whose backing a dictator needs to stay in power. It defines regime type here (p. 351).
- [[monarchy-dictatorship]] (new) — executive holds power through family and kin networks (p. 354).
- [[military-dictatorship]] (new) — executive relies on the armed forces. Often a junta (pp. 352, 354).
- [[civilian-dictatorship]] (new) — any autocracy that is neither monarchy nor military (p. 354).
- [[dominant-party-dictatorship]] (new) — one party dominates access to office and control of policy. Minor parties may compete (p. 359).
- [[personalistic-dictatorship]] (new) — leader keeps personal control of policy and personnel. Weak party and military autonomy (p. 361).
- [[personality-cult]] (new) — glorification of the leader. Standard view: indoctrination. Marquez's view: costly signalling (pp. 362–364).
- [[preference-falsification]] — publicly hiding true preferences. Underlies the dictator's dilemma (p. 364).
- [[dictators-dilemma]] (new) — repression keeps the ruler in power but hides his true support (p. 364).
- [[electoral-authoritarianism]] (new) — elections and some pluralism, but systematic violation of democratic norms. Hegemonic or competitive (pp. 365–366).
- [[politically-closed-regime]] (new) — no legal opposition party (p. 365).
- [[selectorate]] and [[winning-coalition]] (new) — previewed only; defined as in L3 (p. 350).
- [[institutions]] — partly relevant: regime parties and legislatures as tools of rule (pp. 349, 361).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Personality cult as costly signal ↔ preference falsification and revolutionary threshold: hidden private opinion plus public conformity gives sudden collapse once a credible signal arrives.
- [structural-analogy] Dictator's dilemma ↔ principal–agent problem: the ruler cannot observe supporters' true loyalty.
- [structural-analogy] Dictators' reliance on a winning coalition ↔ veto players: the group whose consent the ruler needs.
