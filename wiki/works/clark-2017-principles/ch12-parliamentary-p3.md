# Semi-presidential democracies, delegation as a unifying framework, and chapter wrap-up

## L0 — One line
Semi-presidential systems split into premier-presidential and president-parliamentary types (cohabitation is discussed mainly for the latter), and, to a large extent, all three regime types can be viewed as systems of delegation whose problems (agency loss, adverse selection, moral hazard) can be mitigated by screening, selection and monitoring.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do semi-presidential democracies make and break governments, and is there one framework that covers parliamentary, presidential and semi-presidential systems? The chapter splits semi-presidentialism by whether the government is also responsible to the president. It discusses cohabitation (president and PM from different blocs), with France and Ukraine as cases. It then argues that, to a large extent, regimes can be viewed as delegation from voters through agents. A simple Principal-Agent Game shows how far an agent can move policy. Hidden types (adverse selection) and hidden actions (moral hazard) can be mitigated by screening, selection, police patrols and fire alarms. Delegation problems are thought to be worse in presidential systems. Coalition cabinets face the same problems internally.

**Prerequisites:** single-peaked preferences on a line; the idea of a status quo; parliamentary vs presidential responsibility of the government; cabinet and coalition basics from earlier in the chapter.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Semi-presidential systems have a government responsible to the legislature and a popularly elected, fixed-term head of state. They come in two types, depending on whether the government is also responsible to the president (pp. 506–507).
- Premier-presidential systems work much like parliamentary ones. Debates about semi-presidentialism are often about the president-parliamentary type (p. 507).
- Cohabitation (president and PM from different blocs) arises from the "duality" of the executive. France shows that presidential dominance depends on a legislative majority; Ukraine shows cohabitation can be bitterly conflictual (pp. 508–511).
- Cabinets in president-parliamentary systems should lie between parliamentary and presidential ones, and Amorim Neto and Strøm find this. Stability findings are mixed (p. 511–512).
- Regimes can largely be viewed as chains of delegation: a single chain in parliamentary systems, multiple chains in presidential ones (pp. 512–514).
- In the Principal-Agent Game the agent proposes and the principal accepts or keeps the status quo. In the examples, the agent gets his ideal point only if it lies inside the principal's acceptable region; otherwise he proposes the closest acceptable point, and agency loss is the distance between the principal's ideal point and the outcome (pp. 515–517).
- Adverse selection (hidden type) can be mitigated by ex ante screening and selection. Moral hazard (hidden action) can be mitigated by ex post police patrols and fire alarms (pp. 517–520).
- In coalition cabinets, junior ministers and legislative committees monitor partners and appear to be substitutes (pp. 521–522).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**(Start of the unit, p. 506.)** The page opens with the end of the discussion of presidential democracies. Cabinets there are very partisan and highly proportional in Costa Rica, Mexico and the United States, but much less so in Brazil, Peru and Venezuela. Amorim Neto (2006) shows this variation is systematically related to presidents' need to negotiate with opposition parties (p. 506).

### Making and breaking governments: semi-presidential democracies (pp. 506–512)

**Definition and types.** A semi-presidential democracy is one where the government depends on the legislature to stay in power and the head of state is popularly elected for a fixed term (p. 506). Many Eastern European countries adopted it after the transitions of the late 1980s and early 1990s (p. 506). There are two types (Elgie 2011; Shugart and Carey 1992). In both, the government (PM plus cabinet) is responsible to the legislature. They differ on whether it is also responsible to the president (p. 506).

- **Premier-presidential:** the president cannot remove the government ("no presidential responsibility"). Such systems look and function like parliamentary democracies with a popularly elected head of state. Some scholars (Cheibub 2007) classify them as parliamentary. The president is not part of the executive; the executive is the PM and cabinet. Examples: Ireland, Armenia, Portugal, Mali, Mongolia, Cape Verde (p. 506).
- **President-parliamentary:** the president can remove the government, so there is both legislative and presidential responsibility. The president is more powerful and the government must take more account of her preferences. The president is not in the government but is part of the executive (government plus president). Executive power is divided differently across countries. Often the president has more say in foreign policy and the PM in domestic policy. In France a convention has evolved to some extent; elsewhere it is written in the constitution. Examples: France, Taiwan, Senegal, Kyrgyzstan (pp. 506–507).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legislature | yes | yes | yes | no |
| President | no | no | yes | yes |

(Table 12.13, p. 507.)

The authors then focus on president-parliamentary systems. Scholars often equate semi-presidentialism with this version, so debates about its virtues and vices are mostly about it (p. 507).

> **Intuition note.** What defines the split between the two semi-presidential types is whether the government is also responsible to the president (can the president remove it?). If not, the PM holds the reins and, to a large extent, the system looks and functions like a parliamentary one.

**Cohabitation.** A popularly elected president need not share a party with the PM. Periods in which they come from different parties or blocs are called cohabitation. The president's party is then in legislative opposition, not the cabinet (p. 508). The president nearly always appoints the PM. He may still appoint an opponent because the government needs a legislative majority when the president's bloc lacks one. Cohabitation results from the duality of the executive: a president not elected by the legislature, and a PM who needs its majority (p. 508).

- **Cohabitation is not divided government.** In presidential systems a president can appoint whomever she likes to the cabinet. The authors' example: after the Democrats won the House in 2006, Speaker Nancy Pelosi could not replace President Bush's cabinet. The US had divided government, not cohabitation (p. 508).
- **Footnote 18.** Presidents are also elected in premier-presidential systems, so differing parties are possible there. Scholars rarely call this cohabitation. One possibility the authors give is that the word suggests president-PM conflict, which is largely absent where the president is weak (p. 508).

**France (pp. 508–510).** There have been three periods of cohabitation since 1986.
- 1981: the Socialist Mitterrand faced a right-wing legislature but dissolved it. The Socialists won an absolute majority, which preempted cohabitation (p. 508).
- 1986: a right-wing coalition won a two-seat majority. Mitterrand had to appoint Jacques Chirac (RPR) as PM. The two-year period was uneasy, with each leader constrained by the other. It ended in 1988 when Mitterrand defeated Chirac, dissolved the legislature and, with some centrist help, appointed a left-wing PM (pp. 508–509).
- 1993: a right-wing coalition won 80 percent of the seats. Mitterrand appointed Édouard Balladur. This period ended when Chirac became president in 1995. He appointed Alain Juppé, as the right already had a majority (p. 509).
- 1997: Chirac dissolved the legislature early in a strategic attempt to build support for reforms. It backfired: the left won, and he appointed the Socialist Lionel Jospin. When Chirac was reelected in 2002 he dissolved the legislature. His UMP won an overwhelming majority and he appointed Jean-Pierre Raffarin, ending the third period after five years (p. 509).

The constitution gives the president powers: appointing the PM, dissolving the legislature (not more than once a year), and emergency powers if territory is threatened (p. 509). From 1958 to 1986 the president seemed to dominate domestic and foreign policy, and France seemed to function like a presidential democracy. Cohabitation showed that this dominance was contingent on the president controlling a legislative majority. Without one, the president defers on domestic policy and to some extent on foreign policy (p. 509). During cohabitation France functions very much like a parliamentary democracy (pp. 509–510).
- **Duverger (1980), as reported:** for France, president-parliamentary semi-presidentialism is just an alternation between presidential and parliamentary government, depending on whether the president has a majority (p. 510).
- **Footnote 19:** the constitution is ambiguous on foreign policy. The president is commander in chief and negotiates and ratifies treaties; the PM is responsible for defence; only the legislature declares war. A convention of foreign policy as a presidential "reserved domain" is occasionally challenged. At the 1986 G7 in Tokyo both Mitterrand and Chirac attended, but protocol let only the head of state into the most important meetings (p. 509).

**Ukraine (pp. 510–511).** In 2006 President Yushchenko had to appoint his rival Yanukovych as PM.
- The rivalry dates at least to the 2004 presidential election. In round one Yushchenko got 39.87% and Yanukovych 39.32%. The runoff was marred by fraud and Yanukovych was declared winner. After thirteen days of protest (the Orange Revolution), the Supreme Court ordered a rerun, which Yushchenko won with 51.99% (p. 510).
- In the 2006 legislative elections Yanukovych's Party of Regions won the most seats. Despite their hostility, the result forced a compromise, a coalition government including both men's parties, with the PM post going to Yanukovych (p. 510).
- Relations were not smooth. In April 2007 Yushchenko dissolved parliament and called elections he hoped would reduce Yanukovych's power. Legislators called it a coup d'état until the constitutional court ruled it legal. Both eventually agreed to new elections to end weeks of deadlock (p. 511).
- Conclusion: for many, cohabitation can be an effective system of checks and balances. Ukraine shows it can also bring bitter, violent conflict when actors have starkly different ideologies and goals (p. 511).

**Cabinet composition (p. 511).** Few studies cover president-parliamentary cabinets. Amorim Neto and Strøm (2006) argue it is perhaps appropriate to think that president and PM both hold de facto vetoes over cabinet appointments. So the president is weaker than in a presidential system and the PM weaker than in a parliamentary one. Expected implication: fewer partisan ministers and lower proportionality than in parliamentary regimes, but more of both than in presidential regimes. They find this "precisely" in twelve parliamentary and twelve semi-presidential European democracies in the 1990s. Variation within the type is expected. When the president's party has a legislative majority, cabinets should look more presidential. Facing an opposition-dominated legislature, they should look more parliamentary. France's history "seems to bear this out" (p. 511).

**Stability (pp. 511–512).** Conventional wisdom (Elgie 2011) holds president-parliamentary arrangements to be a dangerous choice for new democracies, and Ukraine's struggles help explain why. Schleiter and Morgan-Jones (2009) find that once government attributes and the constitutional rules on dissolution are taken into account, governments in president-parliamentary democracies are no less stable than parliamentary ones. The authors say we lack a complete understanding and more research is needed (p. 512).

> **Intuition note.** In president-parliamentary systems, who really governs depends on election results. In France, the president's dominance depended on his side controlling the legislature; without that, the PM took the lead. Cabinet composition is expected to shift accordingly.

### A unifying framework: principal-agent and delegation problems (pp. 512–522)

**Delegation.** Delegation occurs when a principal relies on an agent to act on the principal's behalf. Examples are patient and doctor, or customer and mechanic (p. 512). In democracies voters are the ultimate principals and delegate policymaking to representatives. This shift from direct to representative democracy is needed because citizens lack the time and expertise to decide everything (p. 512).

**Parliamentary democracy: single chain (Fig. 12.10a).** Voters → legislators → prime minister → cabinet ministers → civil servants. Each principal delegates to an agent it conditionally authorizes (Strøm, Müller, and Bergman 2003) (pp. 512–513). An agent is accountable to the extent the principal controls it. Examples are voters refusing to reelect a poor legislator, a no-confidence vote, and ministerial responsibility forcing a resignation. The chain is indirect: only legislators are chosen directly, so the other agents are only indirectly accountable to voters (p. 513).

**Presidential democracy: multiple chains (Fig. 12.10b).** Voters delegate to a legislature and a president, who must bargain ("transactional" relations, versus the "hierarchical" ones of parliamentary systems; Shugart 2006). Power then flows to the president's secretaries and to civil servants. In the US the Senate's role in confirming cabinet members links the Upper House to the secretaries, but afterwards ministers answer only to the president. The chain is much more complex than in a parliamentary system (p. 513).

**Benefits and risks.** Delegation lets the principal use others' expertise and achieve goals at lower personal cost. But power is transferred, so the agent may not do what the principal wants. This is "shirking" (Lupia 2003). Reasons include incapacity or conflicting preferences. These difficulties are principal-agent (delegation) problems: the agent (a) potentially has different goals and (b) cannot be perfectly monitored (p. 513). Madison, *Federalist* 51: first enable the government to control the governed, then "oblige it to control itself" (p. 514).

**Outcome measures (pp. 514–515).**
- *Perfect agent:* does what the principal would have done had she been the agent.
- *Agency loss:* the difference between the actual consequence of delegation and what it would have been with a perfect agent. It describes the outcome from the principal's viewpoint.
- *Success:* delegation is successful if it improves the principal's welfare relative to not delegating. The no-delegation outcome is the status quo, or reversion point.

**Principal-Agent Game (p. 515).** Two actors have single-peaked preferences on a 0–10 policy line, with ideal points A (agent) and P (principal), plus a status quo SQ. The agent moves first and proposes a policy. The principal accepts it, which replaces SQ, or rejects it, so SQ stays. Footnote 20 simplifies: the principal accepts any proposal that does not make her worse off than SQ (p. 515). The principal's *region of acceptability* is the set of policies she prefers to SQ. The agent proposes the policy in that region closest to his own ideal point (pp. 515–517).

Figure 12.11 scenarios, with the author's numbers:
- **(a)** SQ = 5, A = P = 3. SQ is 2 from P, so the region runs from 1 to 5. A's ideal point lies inside, so he proposes 3 and the principal accepts. There is no agency loss (p. 515).
- **(b)** SQ = 1, A = 3, P = 7. SQ is 6 from P, so the region runs "from 1 to 13" (the text's figure; the scale ends at 10). A's ideal point is inside, so the outcome is 3. The agency loss is 7 − 3 = 4. Delegation is "successful" (principal better off than at SQ) but loses something (p. 515).
- **(c)** SQ = 4, P = 5, A = 9. SQ is 1 from P, so the region is 4 to 6. A's ideal point is outside, so he proposes 6, the point in the region closest to 9. The agency loss is 1, and the text calls delegation successful (pp. 516–517).

Lessons drawn: the principal may suffer varying agency loss. She is often better off delegating than keeping the status quo. The agent's power is not unconditional: sometimes he gets his ideal point, sometimes not (p. 517).

> **Intuition note.** The principal can veto, so the agent can only propose things the principal prefers to the status quo. An agent whose ideal point is far away is squeezed to the edge of what she will accept.

**Information problems (pp. 517–518).** Principal-agent problems often come from incomplete and asymmetric information, since principals delegate because they lack skills the agents have.
- **Adverse selection** (Akerlof 1970): the agent has attributes hidden from the principal, such as her "type" (preferences, skill, motivation). Voters cannot always tell public-spirited candidates from those who pretend while planning personal gain (Lupia 2003), or know whether a candidate is competent. This helps explain why voters sometimes elect corrupt or inept politicians (p. 517).
- **Moral hazard:** the agent can take actions hidden from the principal. The standard example is insurance: insured clients may behave more riskily. The authors also mention debates over bailouts of Euro-zone countries (Ireland, Portugal, Greece) and US financial guarantees. Politically it arises when voters lack information about what the government does. Widely reported issues constrain agents. Issues that are unnoticed, complicated, or affected by events outside the agent's control leave her free to follow her own preferences, if they are not perfectly aligned with the principal's (pp. 517–518).

**Mitigation.** Principals gather information through two kinds of mechanism (pp. 518–519).
- *Ex ante mechanisms* teach the principal about agents before they act or are chosen. They help against adverse selection. They are of two kinds (p. 519):
  - *Screening* sets up a competition among candidates. Campaigns and debates force candidates to reveal something about their type (p. 519). In presidential systems the skills for a nationwide campaign differ from those for governing, so voters know more about campaigning than governing. In parliamentary systems politicians climb the party hierarchy over decades and are screened by the elite, who know more than ordinary voters. Chapter 16 returns to this (p. 519).
  - *Selection* puts agents in a position to choose an action that reveals their type. Spence's (1974) model: if skilled applicants find a high level of education easier to attain, and skill correlates with education, employers can use education as a signal of skill (pp. 519–520). Applied to portfolio allocation: some ministries go to people with distinguished backgrounds, for example finance ministers with economics or finance backgrounds, since delegating the economy to an unskilled agent is costly. Candidates may also signal their policies through the party they join (p. 520).
- *Ex post mechanisms* teach the principal about actions after the fact. They help against moral hazard and come in two types (McCubbins and Schwartz 1984) (p. 520):
  - *Police patrol:* the principal monitors directly, for example public hearings with interest groups or experts about bureaucratic agencies.
  - *Fire alarm:* the principal relies on others' information, for example rules letting citizens and groups charge agencies with violating policy and seek remedies. A free and vibrant media can be a fire alarm for voters.

Delegation problems are generally thought to be greater in presidential than in parliamentary democracies. Presidential systems have multiple chains and transactional relations. Parliamentary systems have a simpler, more transparent single chain and hierarchical relations (p. 520).

### Box 12.5: Delegation problems in coalition cabinets (pp. 521–522)
Each coalition party (principal) delegates a ministry to a minister (agent). Parties have distinct preferences, and a minister with more information than the rest of the cabinet might implement her own party's policy instead of the agreed coalition policy (p. 521).
- **Müller and Strøm (2000):** these problems can be resolved simply by writing very detailed coalition agreements. **Authors' reply (rhetorical questions):** what makes such promises credible, and why would parties keep them because they are written in detail? The implied answer is that writing alone does not guarantee it. Parties instead devised institutional monitoring solutions (p. 521).
- **Junior ministers** (a police-patrol system). Thies (2001): parties often place their own junior ministers in portfolios held by partners, even though vetoing others' nominations is rare. Junior ministers generally do not share power with the minister, do not vote in cabinet, and only occasionally attend meetings, but the minister cannot dismiss them unilaterally. Thies finds this in Italy, Japan and the Netherlands but not Germany (pp. 521–522).
- **Legislative committees** resolve Thies's "German anomaly." Martin and Vanberg (2004): Dutch and German committees scrutinize bills more when coalition partners' ideological divergence on the bill's issues is large. Kim and Loewenberg (2005): in the Bundestag (1961–1998), parties chaired committees overseeing ministries held by their partners (p. 522).
- **Lipsmeyer and Pierce (2011)** (twelve parliamentary and semi-presidential countries) "seem to confirm" that junior ministers and committees are substitutes. Junior ministers are more likely in important ministries when partners' policy differences are large and other oversight, such as strong committees, is unavailable (p. 522).

### Conclusion (pp. 522–524)
Regimes are classified by the relations among president, government and legislature, which are three ways of structuring executive-legislative relations (p. 522).
- **Presidentialism:** the government serves at the president's pleasure, with no legislative responsibility. Symbolic no-confidence votes are rare and non-binding. Examples are a planned 2007 Senate vote on Attorney General Gonzales, which failed (53–38 for holding it, short of the 60 needed against a filibuster), and a 1950 Senate vote on Dean Acheson, who kept his post (pp. 522–523, nn. 21–22). This demonstrates a key feature of presidential systems: a separation of powers between executive and legislature (p. 523).
- **Parliamentary:** the legislature directly controls the government. The PM plays a central role in choosing ministers, but all, including the PM, serve at the legislature's pleasure (p. 523). Thatcher (PM three times, most recently in 1987 with a 102-seat Conservative majority) lost party support in 1990. After Howe's resignation Heseltine challenged her. She won more votes but fell short of the supermajority required to avoid a second round, and stepped down, avoiding a no-confidence vote Kinnock had proposed. She was removed by a party vote of 204 of 362 valid votes, oftentimes without recourse to the voting public (pp. 523–524). A rough analogy for US students: parliamentary government is a US government in which the Speaker, not the president, heads the government (p. 524).
- **Semi-presidential (president-parliamentary):** the government can be formed and reformed by presidential or legislative initiative. Mitterrand's 1981 dissolution and the 1986 change of PM illustrate this. The government has "two masters," and which one dictates depends on electoral fortunes (p. 524).

Some scholars see presidentialism as division of executive and legislative powers and parliamentarism as fusion. Stepan and Skach (1993): parliamentarism is "mutual dependence," presidentialism "mutual independence." Chapter 16 will examine how this choice affects democratic survival (p. 524).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Policy line 0–10; the principal accepts any proposal not worse for her than the status quo, as in footnote 20.

1. SQ = 2, P = 6, A = 4. SQ is 4 from P, so the region is 6 − 4 = 2 to 6 + 4 = 10. A = 4 lies inside, so the agent proposes 4. Agency loss = |6 − 4| = 2. At SQ the distance was 4, so the principal is better off.
2. SQ = 5, P = 3, A = 9. SQ is 2 from P, so the region is 1 to 5. The point closest to 9 inside it is 5, which is SQ itself. Nothing changes, and the agent gets no power. Loss = |3 − 5| = 2.
3. SQ = 8, P = 6, A = 1. SQ is 2 from P, so the region is 4 to 8. The agent proposes 4. The loss is |6 − 4| = 2, equal to the distance at SQ, so under footnote 20 she is only indifferent.
