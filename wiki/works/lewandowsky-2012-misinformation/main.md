# Misinformation and Its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing

## L0 — One line
Retracting a false claim usually does not remove its influence, but warnings, repeated retractions and especially a plausible alternative explanation can reduce it, while worldview can block correction and sometimes make it backfire (strengthen the false belief).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The paper asks why false beliefs persist after clear retractions, and what reduces their influence. The authors first review how misinformation spreads in society (rumors, fiction, politicians, vested interests, media, the Internet). They then review cognitive mechanisms: people judge truth by compatibility, coherence, source and social consensus, and proposed explanations say retractions may leave a gap in the story, be misremembered, or repeat the myth. Three techniques are identified as helpful: preexposure warnings, repeated retractions, and alternative explanations. Worldview can block correction and even cause backfire. The authors end with practitioner recommendations, ethical limits, and nudging as an alternative.

**Prerequisites:** basic memory ideas (recall vs. familiarity); the idea of attitudes and ideology; reading simple experimental designs (control vs. treatment groups, percentages). No mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Misinformation may be more damaging than ignorance, because it is often held strongly, whereas ignorance tends to bring low-confidence decisions based on simple rules of thumb (heuristics) (p. 107–108).
- It spreads inadvertently (piecemeal news, fiction, rumors) and deliberately (politicians, vested interests); media balance norms and a fractionated Internet landscape (split into like-minded niches) help it persist (p. 108–111).
- By default, people accept information; when they do evaluate it, they use compatibility, coherence, source credibility and perceived consensus, and fluency (ease of processing) and familiarity can stand in for these (p. 111–113).
- Continued influence effect: retractions rarely eliminate reliance on misinformation, even when people remember the retraction. Explanations proposed: mental models (causal pictures of an event, with a gap left by the retraction), retrieval failure, fluency/familiarity, and reactance (resisting being told what to think) (p. 113–116).
- To date, only three factors have been identified that improve retractions: preexposure warnings, repetition of the retraction, and a plausible alternative account that fills the gap. The last is described as particularly effective, but is not always possible (p. 116–118).
- Worldview-dissonant corrections can fail or backfire. Affirming worldview or self-identity, and skepticism at the time of exposure, can help (p. 118–121).
- Practical rules: fill gaps, stress facts over myths, keep rebuttals simple, warn first, respect audience values (p. 122–123).
- Debiasing has ethical and efficiency limits. Nudging (choice architecture) may work where debiasing cannot (p. 124).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Summary and opening cases (p. 106–107)
The paper first examines how misinformation is disseminated, then individual-level cognition that makes it resistant to correction, then successful debiasing, then recommendations (p. 106).

Three cases open the paper:
- **"Birthers."** Claims that Obama was born abroad persisted despite overwhelming evidence. They were widely believed, including by a majority of voters in 2011 Republican primaries (p. 106).
- **MMR–autism.** A 1998 UK study suggested a vaccine–autism link. Health bodies pointed out the lack of evidence, and the claims were later found unsubstantiated. The first author had an undisclosed conflict of interest, the journal retracted the paper, and he was found guilty of misconduct. Yet in 2002, 20–25% of the public still believed the link, and a further 39–53% believed there was equal evidence on both sides. A substantial number of health professionals also kept believing it (p. 106–107).
- **Listerine.** Ads falsely claimed for over 50 years that it helped prevent or reduce colds. The US FTC mandated corrective advertising. For 16 months in 1978–1980, 5-second retractions ran midway through 30-second TV spots with a $10 million budget, and the campaign was only moderately successful. A telephone survey (Armstrong et al., 1983) found a significant drop in belief that Listerine alleviates colds, but acceptance stayed high. 42% of Listerine users still believed it was promoted as an effective cold remedy, and 57% said its presumed medicinal effects were a key factor in buying it (15% among consumers of a competing product) (p. 107).

Two distinct difficulties: cognitive variables that make misinformation "sticky" (the paper's focus), and the pragmatic problem of reaching the target audience (p. 107).

### The societal cost of misinformation (p. 107–108)
A functioning democracy relies on an informed populace. If a majority believes something false, the belief may drive societal decisions against its interest. Individuals may also decide badly for themselves. The authors cite the MMR case: reduced immunization, more vaccine-preventable disease, and wasted research and campaign money (p. 107).

*Misinformation vs. ignorance.* The authors define ignorance as absence of relevant knowledge. Reliance on misinformation differs from it, and may be worse. Ignorant people often turn to simple heuristics (e.g., familiarity can permit accurate guesses), and have low confidence in such decisions. False beliefs are often held strongly and perhaps "infectiously." Example: those who most vigorously reject climate science also believe they are best informed (p. 107–108).

*Intuition note.* Not knowing tends to bring low-confidence guesses based on simple rules. A false belief is often held with strong, perhaps contagious, conviction, which is why it can lead to strong support for a cause.

### Origins of misinformation (p. 108–111)
Some misinformation is unintentional and unavoidable, e.g., piecemeal news such as revised disaster death tolls, and the provisional nature of science (p. 108). Sources discussed: rumors and fiction, governments and politicians, vested interests, media (p. 108).

**Rumors and fiction (p. 108–109).**
- In 2006 a majority of Democrats believed the Bush administration assisted in or failed to stop 9/11, though the theory had little mainstream media traction (p. 108).
- People seem to pass on mainly information that evokes emotion, irrespective of truth. Emotional arousal increases willingness to pass information on. Disgust, fear or happiness content spreads more than neutral content (p. 108).
- People extract knowledge even from sources labelled fiction. This is often adaptive, but fiction need not stick to facts. Marsh, Meade and Roediger (2003): people used misinformation from clearly fictitious stories on later quizzes, even against common knowledge. Source attribution was mostly intact, but reading also increased illusory belief of prior knowledge (p. 108–109).
- Marsh and Fazio (2006): prior warnings were ineffective. Acquisition was only reduced, not eliminated, under active on-line monitoring (pressing a key at each error). Few people read fiction so alertly (p. 109).
- Crichton's *State of Fear* misrepresented climate science but was introduced as "scientific" evidence in a US Senate committee (p. 109).

**Governments and politicians (p. 109).**
- Iraq: officials claimed no doubt about WMDs, juxtaposed Iraq with the 9/11 attacks, and implied intelligence linking Iraq to al-Qaida. Corrections followed (published corrections of tentative WMD reports; the Duelfer report). Even so, years later some 20–30% of Americans believed WMDs had been found, and around half endorsed an Iraq–al-Qaida link (p. 109).
- "Death panels": within 5 weeks 86% of Americans had heard the claim. Of those who heard it, fully half believed it or were unsure (p. 109).
- Seemingly systematic efforts to misinform about climate change are described, including political interference and a 2008 NASA inspector-general finding (p. 109).
- The public is somewhat aware of political misinformation, but when asked to identify instances, people often cannot tell false from correct information. Awareness is therefore no barrier to confusion (p. 109).

**Vested interests and NGOs (p. 109–110).**
- "Agnogenesis" is the willful manufacture of mistaken beliefs. The authors see evidence in industry responses on smoking (a 2006 US federal court found cigarette makers guilty of conspiring to deny, distort and minimize hazards) and climate change (industry groups' campaigns from the early 1990s, alliances with conservative think tanks). More than 90% of books published 1972–2005 expressing skepticism on environmental issues have been linked to such think tanks (p. 109–110).
- Not all misinformation serves industry. The vaccine myth arguably cut pharmaceutical revenue. Opposition to GM foods seems disproportionate to risks as portrayed by expert bodies, and people appear to rely on NGOs critical of peer-reviewed science (p. 110).

**Media (p. 110–111).**
- Systemic reasons for error: oversimplification or overdramatization of science (e.g., a *Nature* extinction study misreported as more catastrophic and faster); and misplaced "balance." Balance is appropriate in many stories, but not when it features nonexpert contrarians against more than 95% of actively publishing climate scientists, as in the Australian TV "expert" with a book on cat palmistry. This asymmetric choice of experts creates the perception of a debate resolved long ago (p. 110).
- Quality varies by outlet and time. Kull and colleagues show belief in misinformation varies dramatically with preferred news outlet, from Fox News viewers (most misinformed on most issues) to NPR listeners (least misinformed overall) (p. 110).
- *Internet.* It bypasses gate-keeping such as editors, especially with Web 2.0 user-generated content (p. 110). Blog users find blogs fairer and more credible (p. 110–111). Online health information varies: 61% of US adults looked online for health information in 2009. Of the first 50 "weight loss diets" sites, only 3 gave sound advice. 75% of depression sites were completely accurate, and 86% of obesity sites at least partially accurate (p. 111). Of 153 YouTube vaccination videos, about half were not explicitly supportive of immunization. For H1N1 videos, 61.3% had useful information and 23% were misleading (p. 111). Hoax sites pose as official sources, e.g., martinlutherking.org (p. 111).
- *Fractionation.* Cable, talk radio and the Internet make selective exposure (seeking sources that support one's views) easier. More than half of blog readers seek supportive blogs and 22% seek opposing ones, creating "echo chambers" and "cyber-ghettos," linked to political polarization (p. 111). The authors say fractionation is a precondition for "strategic extremism" by politicians (extremism gaining net votes), and may prolong misinformation, especially when it leaks out of cyber-ghettos into the mainstream (p. 111).

### From individual cognition to debiasing strategies (p. 111)
Roadmap: how people assess truth; why corrections fail and can backfire; successful techniques; worldview; recommendations and ethical implications (p. 111).

### Assessing the truth of a statement (p. 111–113)
*Default acceptance.* Misleading information rarely carries a warning label. Conversation carries a "guarantee of relevance" (Sperber & Wilson); listeners assume speakers are truthful, relevant and clear (Grice). Some research (Gilbert, 1991) suggests comprehension requires at least temporary acceptance. Suspension of belief is possible but seems to need high attention, considerable implausibility, or distrust. If the topic is unimportant to you or you are distracted, misinformation will likely slip in (p. 111–112).

When people evaluate truth thoughtfully, they ask four questions, answerable from knowledge or from feelings of familiarity and fluency (ease of processing) (p. 112):

1. **Compatible with what I believe?** Information is more likely accepted when consistent with other beliefs. Once accepted, knowledge-consistent information is highly resistant to change, more so the larger the compatible knowledge base. Compatibility therefore raises the chance that misinformation is accepted and lowers the chance it is corrected. A cheaper cue is the feeling of fluency: inconsistent messages are processed less fluently, and disfluency prompts closer scrutiny. Fluency effects arise even from superficial features: high color contrast, rhyme, familiar accent, easy-to-read font. Analytic and intuitive processing therefore both favor messages compatible with prior beliefs (p. 112).
2. **Coherent story?** A story is compelling if it organizes information without internal contradiction, consistent with assumptions about human motivation. Gaps are filled with story-consistent intrusions. Once formed, a coherent story resists change, since each element is supported by the others and altering one would cause downstream inconsistencies. Coherent stories are also easier to process (p. 112–113).
3. **Credible source?** Persuasiveness increases with perceived credibility, but untrustworthy sources are often influential. People are insensitive to contextual cues (expert testimony under oath is equally persuasive; climate-denial messages were similarly influential whether "funded by Exxon" or by "donations by people like you"). The gist is often more memorable than the source ("sleeper effects"). Repeating an unknown name can make it seem familiar and thus credible (p. 113).
4. **Do others believe it?** Repetition increases acceptance. In wartime rumors, simple repetition was the strongest predictor of belief (Allport & Lepkin). Familiarity can mislead about consensus: in Weaver et al., hearing the same statement repeatedly from one source raised estimated consensus: "a single repetitive voice can sound like a chorus" (p. 113). Echo chambers make this repetition influential (p. 113).
   - *Pluralistic ignorance*: a divergence between the actual prevalence of a belief and what people think others believe. In the run-up to Iraq, prominent unilateralist voices made the multilateralist majority feel like a minority. The flip side is the *false-consensus effect* (p. 113).
   - Extreme case (Pedersen et al., 2008): people with particularly negative attitudes toward Aboriginal Australians or asylum seekers overestimated support by 67% and 80%. Only 1.8% held strongly negative attitudes toward Aboriginals, yet they thought 69% of all Australians (79% of their friends) shared their views (p. 113).

From the truth-assessment view, a correction is a competition between the perceived truth of misinformation and of correct information. Corrections often fail, and this failure is the continued influence effect, "the central conundrum" of the field (p. 113).

*Intuition note.* Feeling that something is easy, familiar, consistent and widely believed is a cheap signal of truth. Misinformation can score well on all four without being true.

### The continued influence effect (p. 113–116)
**Paradigm** (Wilkes & Leatherbarrow, 1988; Johnson & Seifert, 1994). Participants read a fictitious event report containing a target piece of information. For some readers it is later retracted, for controls it is not. A questionnaire then counts clear references to the target (mis)information (p. 114). The typical story is a warehouse fire initially attributed to gas cylinders and oil paints in a closet. The retraction says the closet was in fact empty. Indirect questions (e.g., one asking what caused the black smoke) are counted. Participants also recall facts and say whether they noticed the retraction (p. 114).

**Findings.**
- Retractions rarely, if ever, eliminate reliance, even if people believe, understand and remember them. A retraction will at most halve references to misinformation, and in some studies did not reduce reliance at all (p. 114).
- Immediate retraction within the same narrative is still ineffective (p. 114).
- Seifert (2002): a strengthened negation (stating that paint and gas were never on the premises) backfired, increasing reliance. Giving a rationale for how the misinformation arose (e.g., a truckers' strike) reduced references somewhat, but influence remained (p. 114).
- Overall it is extremely difficult to return exposed people to the baseline of never-exposed people (p. 114).

*Intuition note.* "Retraction" does not delete the earlier claim. A plain retraction reduces its use by at most half (and in some studies not at all), so much of it can remain available for inferences.

**Proposed explanations** (p. 114–116):

*Mental models (p. 114).* People build causal models of events (A led to B; B plus C caused outcome X). A retraction that removes a central element (B) leaves a gap, and the event representation "doesn't make sense," so people may keep using the false element in answers while knowing of the correction. Consistent with this, misinformation is more resilient when people are asked to explain why it might be true (Anderson et al., 1980). People fill episodic gaps with schema-congruent information. The authors say continued use of discredited models "remains poorly understood." People may prefer an incorrect model to an incomplete one, or resolve the conflict between a plausible answer and a known retraction by ignoring the retraction (p. 114).

*Retrieval failure (p. 114–115).* Three versions:
1. Source confusion or misattribution: e.g., remembering the oil-paints claim but attributing it to the final police report instead of a retracted initial one (p. 115).
2. Failure of strategic monitoring (Ayers & Reder, 1998): valid and invalid memory entries compete for automatic activation, but contextual integration needs strategic processing. A plausible misinformation item is activated automatically, and monitoring is needed to judge its validity (p. 115).
3. The "negation tag": retraction is like attaching "—NOT" to a memory entry (Gilbert et al.; Johnson & Seifert, 1998). In Johnson and Seifert (1998), after reading that John played hockey for New York but actually played for Boston, reading "the team" activates both cities. The tag can be lost, especially when strategic processing is impaired (old age, high cognitive load). Negations should work better when encodable as an affirmation of an alternative attribute (Mayo et al., 2004): "not messy" can be recoded "tidy" (rarely misremembered as "messy"), whereas "not charismatic" has no polar opposite and is more often misremembered as "charismatic" (p. 115).

*Fluency and familiarity (p. 115–116).* This account focuses on the experience of processing rather than what is recalled. Misinformation raises the perceived familiarity and coherence of later related material. A retraction that repeats the myth (by saying no oil paints and gas cylinders were present) can strengthen it, since repetition strengthens information in memory and belief. Retractions may therefore fail or backfire, because people may feel they have heard the claim before and conclude there is probably something to it (p. 115).
- *Myth-versus-fact handouts* (e.g., CDC flu-vaccine handouts). Immediately after reading, recipients distinguish myths from facts correctly and report consistent intentions. After 30 minutes, readers identify more "myths" as "facts" than people who never saw a handout, and report fewer vaccination intentions than people who were not exposed to it (Schwarz et al., 2007) (p. 115).
- Older adults (and potentially children) are especially vulnerable, since recollection declines with age more than familiarity does. They are more likely to forget the details of a retraction and retain only a sense of familiarity (p. 115–116).
- A similar effect in corporate "ambushing": publicly exposing a company's feigned sponsorship association may make people remember the feigned association even better (p. 116).

*Reactance (p. 116).* People dislike being told what to think, so may reject authoritative retractions (Brehm & Brehm, 1981). In mock-jury studies, conviction rates were higher when an "inadmissible" ruling came with extensive legal explanation than when unexplained (p. 116).

### Reducing the impact of misinformation (p. 116–118)
Unsuccessful: clarifying the retraction (Seifert, 2002; van Oostendorp, 1996) and placing it immediately after the misinformation. To date, only three factors have been identified that increase retraction effectiveness: (a) warnings at initial exposure, (b) repetition of the retraction, (c) corrections that tell an alternative story filling the coherence gap (p. 116).

**Preexposure warnings (p. 116).**
- Warning people up front reduces misinformation effects. Ecker et al. (2010) found warnings must specifically explain the ongoing effects of misinformation, not just say misinformation may be present (as in Marsh & Fazio, 2006). Warnings could routinely be given in advertising, fiction with historical or pseudoscientific content, or courts (p. 116).
- Warnings seem more effective before encoding than after. This is interpreted via Gricean expectations: an a priori warning changes the default expectation of validity, so recipients can "tag" input as suspect. Schul (1993): people took longer to process warned misinformation, suggesting they considered it within an alternative mental model rather than dismissing it (p. 116).
- Warnings may induce a temporary skepticism that maximizes discrimination between true and false information (p. 116).
- Warnings are still somewhat effective after encoding. This supports a dual-process view in which strategic monitoring assesses automatically retrieved misinformation. Monitoring takes effort, so warnings may prompt vigilance (p. 116).

**Repeated retractions (p. 116–117).**
- Ecker et al. (2011): if misinformation was encoded repeatedly, repeating the retraction helped alleviate (not eliminate) the effect. If misinformation was encoded only once, one retraction and three retractions gave the same persistence. Weakly encoded misinformation therefore seems to have a floor of persistence regardless of retraction strength (p. 116).
- Possible reasons: automatic processing that strategic control counters only if people are aware of it; a sampling model in which each memory token is offset by one retraction, and further retractions do not strengthen the "retracted" marker (weak encoding gives one token, strong encoding gives several tokens that can benefit from several retractions); a "protest-too-much" effect (Bush et al., 1994); and repeated retractions repeating the myth (p. 116–117).
- Ecker et al. (2011) suggest that repetition of the initial misinformation has a stronger and more reliable (negative) effect on later inferences than repetition of its retraction does. This is unfortunate for social media, where information spreads fast, with little fact-checking, and is taken from worldview-consonant sources (p. 117).

**Filling the gap: an alternative narrative (p. 117).**
- A retraction can create a coherence gap (people may think: not the oil and gas, but then what else could it be?). Providing an alternative causal explanation can fill it. Studies show continued influence can be eliminated by an alternative account explaining why the information was wrong (e.g., saying that arson materials have been found) (p. 117).
- To work, the alternative must be plausible, account for the important causal qualities of the initial report, and ideally explain why the misinformation was thought correct. Example: "grain silos" does not explain the initial WMD report, but "a chemical factory" is more plausible since it may contain components that also occur in WMDs. Another option: WMDs were present but destroyed before 2003 (p. 117).
- Explaining the source's motivation can help (e.g., government officials were "trigger-happy"). Reagan's "There you go again!" in 1980 framed Carter's attack as "expected" from its source and discredited it (Cialdini, 2001) (p. 117).
- Boundary conditions: merely mentioning or self-generating alternatives is insufficient. The alternative must be integrated into the existing information from the same source. People prefer simple explanations, so a much more complex alternative may be rejected for a simpler account that keeps the misinformation. An "overkill" backfire effect can follow from too many counterarguments (Sanna et al., 2002), but asking for only a few self-generated counterarguments can help (Sanna & Schwarz, 2006). For corrections of political misinformation, suspicion about the rationale behind the correction, and behind the initial presentation of the misinformation, may be particularly important (p. 117).

Summary: three established techniques; the last (alternative explanation) is "particularly effective" but not always possible, since an alternative may not be available. Worldview adds complications (p. 117–118).

*Intuition note.* A retraction takes something out of a story. People accept this more readily if something else goes in to explain what happened, and if that replacement is plausible and not too complicated.

### Corrections in the face of existing belief systems: worldview and skepticism (p. 118–121)

**Worldview (p. 118).**
- Real-world evidence: Republicans are more likely than Democrats to keep believing the "birthers" and WMD claims despite retractions. Liberals are less likely than conservatives to recognize the risks of "peak oil" (p. 118).
- Open question: do retractions (a) fail specifically among people whose belief they violate, or (b) work equally for all, with post-retraction differences mirroring pre-retraction ones? Both are consistent with truth-assessment theory. Worldview-congruent misinformation is more familiar, easier to process, more coherent and better supported. Retractions that contradict worldview are the reverse. The evidence is "sparse and mixed" (p. 118).
- Evidence for (b): Ecker et al. (2012) used a robbery story with an indigenous Australian as suspect (one experiment) or hero (another). Referencing the racial information covaried with prejudice, but this held regardless of retraction, so the retraction was equally effective in low- and high-prejudice participants. A plane-crash study (bomb retracted; faulty fuel tank) found the same with Islamophobia (unpublished analysis) (p. 118).
- Evidence for (a): Nyhan and Reifler (2010) found retractions of real-world misperceptions (tax cuts increasing revenue; WMDs) effective only among people whose political orientation the retraction supported. Worldview-dissonant corrections (here for Republicans) produced a backfire effect, increasing commitment to the misinformation. Hart and Nisbet (2011) found a similar backfire with climate-health messages: Democrats' support for mitigation rose and Republicans' fell versus control (p. 118).
- Polarization examples: Gollust et al. (2009) found that evidence that social circumstances can cause Type 2 diabetes reduced policy endorsement among Republicans and raised it among Democrats. Berinsky (2012) found similar polarization when the death-panel myth was rebutted. Brand connection: highly connected people given negative brand information lowered self-esteem but kept the brand image (p. 118–119).
- **Authors' suggested boundary** (their inference): worldview affects retraction effectiveness when the misinformation concerns a real-world event relevant to preexisting beliefs (the fictitious events in Ecker et al. versus real events and politicians in Nyhan & Reifler). Political-science studies find sensitivity to corrective information on issues lacking salience and emotiveness, so not all political issues necessarily polarize (p. 119).

**Backfire effects (p. 119–120).**
- Mechanism: people implicitly counterargue against worldview-challenging information. Prasad et al. (2009), "challenge interview": participants produced counterarguments or stayed unmovable. Taber and Lodge (2006), "motivated skepticism": people uncritically accepted arguments for their own position and were highly skeptical of opposing ones (p. 119).
- Backfire ("boomerang") effects also occur in other communication, e.g., health campaigns. Rejecting a message may predispose people to reject future messages from its source; if this occurs for misinformation corrections, it is "cause for concern" (p. 119).
- *Belief polarization* (Lord, Ross & Lepper, 1979): the same information produces further divergence between people with opposing views. Believers' belief increased after a fictitious report disproving the resurrection, while nonbelievers became more skeptical (Batson, 1975). Nuclear-power supporters and opponents read the same accident description oppositely (Plous, 1991). Techniques for reducing belief polarization are highly similar to those for overcoming worldview resistance (p. 119).
- Source affiliation: among Republicans, corrections of the death-panel myth worked mainly when issued by a Republican politician. Because people judge a source more credible if they believe its statement (Fragale & Heath), "epistemic circularity" can arise, where no opposing information is credible enough to overturn dearly held beliefs. Munro (2010): belief-threatening science led people to discount the scientific method itself (p. 119).
- Education does not protect: higher education made Democrats more likely, and Republicans less likely, to see global warming as a threat (Hamilton, 2011). Among Republicans, more education went with a larger increase in the belief that Obama is a Muslim between 2009 and 2010 (p. 120).
- Summary: personal beliefs can facilitate acquisition of attitude-consonant misinformation, increase reliance on it, and inoculate against correction (p. 120).
- Emotion alone is not sufficient: retracted plane-crash causes persisted equally ("terrorist attack," "bad weather," "technical fault") although the first was more emotionally affecting (p. 120).
- Limitation: worldview is not a process explanation. The authors suggest it may serve as a frame for deciding whether to assimilate or accommodate information (Piaget), or as a schema (Bartlett), so that factual information is discarded or misinformation kept (p. 120).

**Taming worldview by affirming it (p. 120).**
- Tailor corrections to the audience, preferably worldview-consonant. Cultural-cognition work (Kahan): framing solutions in worldview-consonant terms can raise acceptance. Examples: nanotechnology framed as protecting the environment for those with an "eco-centric" outlook; Republicans accept an identical charge more as a "carbon offset" than as a "tax" (Hardisty et al., 2010) (p. 120).
- *Self-affirmation*: writing a few sentences about a time one acted on an important value made recipients more receptive to threatening messages. The authors say it may help the facts get a fair hearing (Cohen et al., 2007; Nyhan & Reifler, 2011). It also let highly brand-connected people lower brand evaluation instead of their own self-esteem. Distancing from a self-focused perspective may help (p. 120).

**Skepticism: a key to accuracy (p. 120–121).**
- Skepticism can reduce susceptibility if it prompts questioning of origins. People who questioned the official Iraq-war rationale processed war-related information more accurately overall, and also recognized correct information more accurately (not cynicism) (Lewandowsky et al., 2005). Fein et al. (1997): mock jurors told to disregard inadmissible evidence were still influenced unless made suspicious of the prosecutor's motives (p. 120–121).
- Distrust can be useful: participants shown an "untrustworthy" face solved more nonroutine problems later (Schul et al., 2008); distrust priming can enhance creativity in some circumstances (Mayer & Mussweiler). The authors attribute this to nonroutine, "lateral" processing (p. 121).
- Limits: skepticism is most likely to help when experienced at message exposure. Effects can persist even when misinformation is identified as intentionally deceptive or from an unreliable source. In Green and Donahue (2011), participants wanted to undo attitude changes, especially in the deception condition, but could not in either (error or deception) condition (p. 121).

**Using misinformation to inform (p. 121).** Unlike brief myth-vs-fact, prolonged dissection of incorrect arguments may help. Kowalski and Taylor (2009): lectures explicitly refuting 17 psychology misconceptions reduced misconceptions more than nonrefutational provision of the same information. Osborne (2010) argues for argumentation in science education. Jerit (2008), analyzing more than 40 opinion polls, ran contrary to the conventional wisdom that political actors should not engage opponents. Bedford (2010): students learned climate science by analyzing "denialist" literature (p. 121).

### Debiasing in an open society (p. 121–122)
Large-scale precedent: Paluck (2009) in Rwanda, a controlled yearlong field experiment. A radio soap opera on reducing intergroup prejudice, violence and survivors' trauma altered listeners' perceptions of social norms and their behavior, but not their beliefs, relative to a health-focused soap opera. Delivery was via tape recorders for experimental control, mimicking traditional radio listening (p. 122).

### Concise recommendations for practitioners (p. 122–123)
Figure 1 pairs problems with solutions (p. 122): Continued Influence → Alternative Account; Repeated Retraction (without reinforcing the myth); Familiarity Backfire → Emphasis on Facts; Preexposure Warning; Overkill Backfire → Simple, Brief Rebuttal; Foster Healthy Skepticism; Worldview Backfire → Affirm Worldview; Affirm Identity. The list (p. 123):
- Consider what gaps debunking creates and fill them with an alternative explanation.
- Use repeated retractions, but backfire risk rises when the original misinformation is repeated in them.
- To avoid a familiarity backfire, emphasize the facts rather than the myth.
- Give an explicit warning before mentioning a myth.
- Keep material simple and brief. If the myth is simpler and more compelling than the debunking, risk an overkill backfire effect.
- Consider whether content threatens audience worldview. If so, risk a worldview backfire effect, strongest among those with firmly held beliefs. The most receptive are those not strongly fixed in their views.
- If you must present threatening evidence, the authors say you may be able to reduce the worldview backfire effect by presenting it in a worldview-affirming way (opportunities and benefits rather than risks) and/or encouraging self-affirmation.
- Alternatively, circumvent worldview with behavioral techniques such as choice architecture.

### Future directions (p. 123)
Little is known about: emotion (emotiveness does not drive persistence, but affects what is passed on, and worldview threats elicit emotional defense); individual differences besides worldview (intelligence, memory capacity, memory-updating ability, tolerance for ambiguity are candidates); and social networks (processes of misinformation spread and persistence through complex networks) (p. 123).

### Concluding remarks: psychosocial, ethical and practical implications (p. 123–124)
- **Rationality.** The authors caution against concluding people are irrational. Jern, Chang and Kemp (2009) modeled belief polarization in a Bayesian network, which can include hidden variables such as trust in a source. With such variables, it can capture behavior (such as backfire) that looks irrational. The authors call the research "suggestive" and say rejecting corrections may "arguably" be a normatively rational integration of prior biases with new information (p. 123–124).
- **Ethics and efficiency.** Correcting misinformation is cognitively indistinguishable from misinforming people to replace correct beliefs, so public understanding of misinformation effects matters ("throw mud" that will "stick"). Debiasing may be inadvisable for strongly ideological believers, where a retraction can do more harm than good, particularly when it cannot be framed worldview-congruently (p. 124).
- **Nudging** (Thaler & Sunstein): choice architecture encouraging decisions without preventing free choice. Example: organ-donation rates about 15–20% in opt-in countries versus over 90% in opt-out countries. Advantages: it does not depend on a delivery vehicle reaching the audience, and design can be transparent and open to scrutiny. Three situations where it is especially applicable: fast population-wide change (e.g., CFC phase-out), ideology blocking debiasing, and organized programmatic misinformation (p. 124). Nudges may also be rendered ineffective by worldview according to some suggestions, though the authors see "considerable promise" (p. 124).
- **Closing example.** After decades denying the smoking–cancer link, tobacco industry experts argued in court that after 1964 there was still space for responsible disagreement. The authors read this as an attempt to replace one myth with another: that the industry did not know. Spreading doubt about scientific uncertainty is a popular strategy, and for laypeople the magnitude of uncertainty matters little as long as it is believed meaningful. Researchers should monitor such sociopolitical developments (p. 124).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the continued influence effect with the paper's paradigm (p. 114). Suppose 4 inference questions per person (e.g., "What caused the black smoke?"), and we count references to the retracted "gas and paint" cause. Invented group means:
- Never told about gas and paint (baseline): 0.2 references.
- Told about gas and paint, no retraction: 3.0 references.
- Told, then retracted (the closet is declared empty): 1.8 references.

Arithmetic: retraction reduction = (3.0 − 1.8) / 3.0 = 1.2 / 3.0 = 0.40, i.e., 40%. This is within the paper's "at most halve" pattern (p. 114). Residual influence = 1.8 − 0.2 = 1.6 references above baseline. Adding an alternative cause (arson materials were found) would, per the paper, be the technique that can eliminate the residual (p. 117). In the toy numbers, that corresponds to about 0.2 references. The retraction removes some use of the claim, but baseline is not restored until the gap is filled.

## Concepts
- [[continued-influence-effect]] — reliance on misinformation in inferences after a clear retraction; retractions at most halve references (p. 113–114)
- [[misinformation]] (new) — information initially processed as valid and later retracted or corrected; distinct from post-event (witness) misinformation (p. 124–125, note 1)
- [[misinformation-vs-ignorance]] (new) — ignorance = absence of knowledge, whose effects may be less severe than those of strongly held false belief (p. 107)
- [[agnogenesis]] (new) — willful manufacture of mistaken beliefs (p. 109)
- [[selective-exposure]] — choosing news sources that support existing views; fostered by fractionated media (p. 111)
- [[echo-chamber-cyber-ghetto]] (new) — blogs linking mainly to like-minded blogs; linked to polarization (p. 111)
- [[processing-fluency]] (new) — ease of processing; fluently processed information feels more familiar and is more likely to be accepted as true (p. 112)
- [[pluralistic-ignorance]] (new) — gap between actual belief prevalence and perceived prevalence; its flip side is the false-consensus effect (p. 113)
- [[mental-model-account]] (new) — retractions leave causal gaps, so people may keep relying on the false element (p. 114)
- [[familiarity-backfire-effect]] (new) — repeating the myth in a correction raises its familiarity and acceptance (p. 115)
- [[preexposure-warning]] (new) — warning before exposure; to be effective it needs to specifically explain the ongoing effects of misinformation (p. 116)
- [[alternative-account]] (new) — plausible alternative causal explanation filling the gap left by a retraction (p. 117)
- [[overkill-backfire-effect]] (new) — too many or too complex counterarguments make the simple myth more attractive (p. 117)
- [[worldview-backfire-effect]] (new) — worldview-threatening corrections can strengthen the initial belief (p. 118)
- [[belief-polarization]] (new) — same information increases divergence between opposing views (p. 119)
- [[motivated-skepticism]] (new) — accepting supporting arguments uncritically and attacking opposing ones (p. 119)
- [[self-affirmation]] (new) — affirming one's values to increase receptivity to threatening messages (p. 120)
- [[nudging]] (new) — choice architecture steering behavior without misinformation correction (p. 124)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Pluralistic ignorance / false consensus ↔ spiral of silence: misperceived opinion climate shapes expression and belief — both rest on people's estimates of what others think.
- [structural-analogy] Worldview-affirming framing (cultural cognition) ↔ Framing (Entman): selecting how an issue is presented changes acceptance of the same facts.
- [structural-analogy] Selective exposure and echo chambers ↔ limited-effects/selective-exposure tradition: media choice reinforces existing attitudes.
- [metaphor] Familiarity backfire ↔ accessibility in agenda setting/priming (canon link; not stated in this unit): repeated exposure makes content easier to retrieve and use.
- [structural-analogy] Jern et al.'s Bayesian network with hidden trust variable ↔ Bayesian updating: an apparently irrational reaction can follow from conditioning on source trust (paper gives no equations).

## Questions this raises
- When is the worldview-moderation of corrections real (Nyhan & Reifler's backfire) and when is it only a pre-existing difference that corrections do not change (Ecker et al.)? Which features of the issue decide this?
- Why does the strength of encoding set a floor on persistence, and can that floor be shown to be due to sampled memory tokens rather than automatic processing?
- How can practitioners mention the myth enough to be understood without raising its familiarity, given the "myth vs fact" results?
- What exactly makes nudging more or less ethically acceptable than debiasing, and does worldview reduce nudges' effects as some studies suggest?
