# Arguments

## L0 — One line
Logic evaluates arguments, meaning premises plus a conclusion, and in logic only sentences that can be true or false are counted as premises or conclusions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what logic means by "argument" and which sentences can appear in one. An argument is a collection of premises together with a conclusion. Logic restricts these to sentences that can be true or false. The conclusion can come first, in the middle or last. Indicator words such as "so" and "since" often help to find the conclusion. Questions, commands phrased as imperatives and exclamations are neither true nor false, so they do not count as logical sentences. Answers to questions do count. Whether a sentence is a fact or an opinion does not matter. Sorting good arguments from bad ones starts with this clear picture of what an argument is.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond ordinary reading of English sentences.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Logic's "argument" is not a shouting match. It is premises plus a conclusion (pp. 2–3).
- "Valid" is given a short gloss: the argument is valid if you think the conclusion follows from the premises. If you also believe the premises, this (perhaps) gives you a reason to believe the conclusion (p. 2).
- One sentence can contain a whole argument, and the conclusion can come first, middle or last. The first task is to separate conclusion from premises (p. 3).
- Conclusion indicator words and premise indicator words help with this, but judgement is still needed (pp. 3–4).
- A logical sentence is one that can be true or false (p. 4).
- Fact versus opinion is a different distinction. Unknown truth value or opinion does not disqualify a sentence (p. 4).
- Questions, imperatives and exclamations are not logical sentences. Answers to questions, and commands not phrased as imperatives, are (p. 5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter introduction (pp. 2–3)
- Logic has many uses. The focus here is evaluating arguments, that is, sorting good ones from bad ones (p. 2).
- "Argument" in everyday speech can mean a belligerent shouting match. Logic calls that a disagreement, not an argument (p. 2).
- Example (p. 2):
  - Either the butler or the gardener did it.
  - The butler didn't do it.
  - ∴ The gardener did it.
- "∴" is read "therefore". The last sentence is the **conclusion**. The earlier sentences are the **premises** (p. 2).
- If you believe the premises and think the conclusion follows from them (the argument is then called **valid**), this (perhaps) gives you a reason to believe the conclusion (p. 2).
- Definition: an argument is any collection of premises, together with a conclusion (p. 3).
- Natural-language arguments come first. Later they are represented in a formal language (p. 3).

*Intuition note:* An argument is a small package: some claims offered as support, and one claim they are meant to support. "Valid" is glossed here as: you think the conclusion follows from the premises. The reason to believe the conclusion (perhaps) comes from believing the premises as well (p. 2).

### Forms of presentation (p. 3)
- One sentence can hold a whole argument: "The butler has an alibi; so they cannot have done it." (one premise, then the conclusion) (p. 3).
- The butler/gardener argument can be reordered. The conclusion can come first (the gardener sentence, then "after all" and the premises) or in the middle (the butler sentence, then "accordingly" and the gardener sentence, then "given that" and the either-or premise) (p. 3).
- So the first step is to separate the conclusion from the premises (p. 3).

### Indicator words (pp. 3–4)
- Conclusion indicator words: so, therefore, hence, thus, accordingly, consequently (p. 3).
- Premise indicator words: since, because, given that. They often indicate a premise (p. 4).
- The authors add that in analysing an argument "there is no substitute for a good nose" (p. 4). The source presents the words as a guide that is often used this way.

### 1.1 Sentences (pp. 4–5)
- An argument can be defined generally as a series of sentences. The early ones are premises and the final one is the conclusion. If the premises are true and the argument is a good one, you have a reason to accept the conclusion (p. 4).
- In logic we are only interested in sentences that can be true or false. A **sentence** is defined as a sentence that can be true or false (p. 4).
- This is not the fact/opinion distinction. "Rudolf Carnap was born in Ronsdorf" is a fact. "Rhubarb is tasty" is something you might think of as a matter of opinion. Both can be premises or conclusions. Unknown truth value or opinion does not disqualify a sentence (p. 4).
- Some grammatical "sentences" are excluded (p. 5):
  - **Questions.** "Are you sleepy yet?" is neither true nor false. The answer "I am not sleepy" is, so it counts. "What is this course about?" does not count, but the statement that no one knows what the course is about does (p. 5).
  - **Imperatives.** "Wake up!" is neither true nor false. Commands are not always imperatives: "You will respect my authority" is true or false, so it counts (p. 5).
  - **Exclamations.** "Ouch!" is neither true nor false. "Ouch, I hurt my toe!" is treated as meaning the same as the plain statement that I hurt my toe. The "ouch" adds nothing that could be true or false (p. 5).

*Intuition note:* The test is whether the sentence could be true or false. Questions and imperatives fail it, but answers and non-imperative commands such as "You will respect my authority" pass. Unknown truth value or opinion does not matter.

### Practice exercises (pp. 5–6)
- The text says learning logic is more about developing a way of thinking than memorising facts (p. 5). The first exercise is to highlight the conclusion in four arguments (p. 6).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Argument: *I will get wet. After all, it is raining, and I have no umbrella.*
1. "After all" signals premises (a premise indicator in spirit). So the premises are *it is raining* and *I have no umbrella*.
2. The remaining sentence, *I will get wet*, is the conclusion. It comes first, which is allowed.
3. Sentence test on related items:
   - "Is it raining?" is a question, so it is not a logical sentence.
   - "Take an umbrella!" is an imperative, so it is not a logical sentence.
   - *You will take an umbrella* can be true or false, so it is a logical sentence.
4. Count: 2 premises + 1 conclusion = 3 sentences in the argument.

## Concepts
- [[argument]] (new) — a collection of premises together with a conclusion; a series of sentences (pp. 3–4)
- [[premise]] (new) — a sentence offered in support of the conclusion (p. 2)
- [[conclusion]] (new) — the sentence the premises are meant to support; marked by "therefore" (p. 2)
- [[validity / soundness]] — only validity is glossed here: you think the conclusion follows from the premises; soundness is not discussed in this unit (p. 2)
- [[indicator-words]] (new) — conclusion indicators (so, hence…) and premise indicators (since, because…); fallible guides (pp. 3–4)
- [[logical-sentence]] (new) — a sentence that can be true or false; excludes questions, imperatives and exclamations (pp. 4–5)

## Canonical instances
- [[butler-gardener-argument]] (new) — an "either…or" argument with a negated premise, reordered in several ways (pp. 2–3)
