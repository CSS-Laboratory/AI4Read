# Magnus, P. D., Button, T., Trueman, R., & Zach, R. (with J. R. Loftis & A. Thomas-Bolduc) (2025). *forall x: Calgary. An Introduction to Formal Logic* (Fall 2025 ed.). Open Logic Project, CC BY 4.0.

## In one paragraph
The book teaches formal logic as a way to see an argument's form. It starts by defining an argument as premises plus a conclusion. It defines validity as "no case makes the premises true and the conclusion not true". It then builds a formal language for sentence logic (TFL) with five connectives, truth tables and natural deduction. It next extends this to first-order logic (FOL), which adds names, predicates, quantifiers, identity and descriptions, and shows how to test arguments with interpretations. Throughout, it asks how English is symbolized, what is lost, and how semantic (truth-based) and proof-based methods relate. It sketches soundness and completeness, and closes with modal logic, normal forms and functional completeness. Along the way it separates using a word from mentioning it, which is useful for anyone writing about language.

## The path through the work
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch01-arguments]] — Logic evaluates arguments, meaning premises plus a conclusion, and in logic only sentences that can be true or false are counted as premises or conclusions.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch02-scope]] — An argument is valid if no possible case makes all its premises true and its conclusion not true, and logic wants to choose cases so that validity depends only on an argument's logical form, not on its content or on whether the sentences are actually true.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch03-other]] — Like validity, the notions of joint possibility, necessary truth, necessary falsehood, contingency and necessary equivalence are all defined by asking which sentences are true in which cases (conceivable scenarios).
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch04-first]] — Many arguments are valid because of their form, so we replace whole sentences with letters to show that form; TFL does this, but at best it helps us understand arguments that are valid because of their form.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch05-connectives]] — Many English sentences built with not, and, or, if…then and if and only if can be paraphrased and symbolized with the five connectives of TFL (¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔), at the cost of nuance such as tone and contrast, causal connection and time order.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch06-sentences]] — TFL defines "sentence" exactly, by an inductive definition: sentence letters are sentences, and connectives build bigger sentences from smaller ones.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch07-ambiguity]] — English sentences can have several meanings, but every sentence of TFL (the book's formal language) has exactly one reading, so TFL can be used to separate the readings of an ambiguous English sentence.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch08-use]] — To talk about a word or symbol rather than with it, you usually have to mark it as mentioned, and this matters for how we talk about the formal language TFL.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch03-truth-p1]] — In TFL the truth value of a compound sentence depends only on the truth values of its parts, so a table listing every assignment of True/False to the sentence letters shows when any sentence is true.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch03-truth-p2]] — Truth tables turn logical notions (tautology, equivalence, entailment, satisfiability) into checks over valuations, and for each notion either a complete table or one line suffices, depending on the question.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch17-basic]] — Instead of checking every truth-table line, we can show an argument is valid by chaining a few simple introduction and elimination rules, tracking extra assumptions in subproofs.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch18-constructing]] — Proofs can be found with rules of thumb: work backward from the goal's main operator, work forward from premises and assumptions, and use indirect proof (IP) as a last resort.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch19-additional]] — The chapter adds extra rules (DS, MT, DNE, LEM, De Morgan) to the TFL proof system, which makes it "a bit easier to work with"; chapter 21 will show they are not strictly speaking necessary (p. 162).
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch20-proof]] — The single turnstile "⊢" (there is a proof) defines theorem, interderivable and jointly inconsistent; for theorem, inconsistent and interderivable, showing "yes" needs only one or two proofs, but showing "no" needs reasoning about all possible proofs; for consistent, the two are reversed.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch21-derived]] — The chapter aims to show that the extra rules of chapter 19 are not strictly necessary, and it does so for several of them: any use can be replaced by extra lines using only the basic rules of chapter 17.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch22-soundness]] — For TFL (truth-functional logic, the book's logic of sentence letters and connectives), the truth-table notion of validity and the derivation notion of validity coincide (⊢ implies ⊨, and ⊨ implies ⊢), so we may use whichever method suits the task.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch23-building]] — Because TFL cannot look inside a sentence, we build first-order logic (FOL) from names, predicates, quantifiers and a domain, so that structure like "all logicians…" is kept (pp. 190–198).
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch24-sentences]] — To symbolize English in first-order logic (FOL), paraphrase it, pick the domain (the things the quantifiers range over) and the predicates carefully, and usually pair ∀ with → and ∃ with ∧; the scope of a quantifier (the part of the sentence it governs) matters.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch25-multiple]] — With many-place predicates and several quantifiers, the order of the gaps and the order of the quantifiers change what a sentence says.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch26-identity]] — Adding the symbol '=' (one and the same object as) to first-order logic lets us symbolize many sentences with *else*, *other than*, *only* and *except*, and also say how many things of a kind there are.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch27-sentences]] — A sentence of FOL is a formula, built by recursive rules from terms, predicates, connectives and quantifiers, in which no variable is free.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch28-definite]] — Russell's analysis lets FOL symbolize *the F is G* with quantifiers and identity alone: there is exactly one F, and it is G.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch29-ambiguity]] — An ambiguous English sentence can have several readings; different FOL symbolizations can separate them, and in FOL every connective and quantifier has a well-determined scope.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch30-extensionality]] — FOL cares only about what its predicates are true of (their extensions), not what they mean, so we *symbolize* English in FOL instead of translating it, and an interpretation is just a domain plus truth values, referents and extensions.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch31-truth]] — A first-order sentence is true in an interpretation clause by clause: atomic sentences by what the interpretation says, connectives by truth tables, and quantifiers by asking whether all (or some) objects satisfy the formula, using a temporary new name.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch32-semantic]] — In FOL, the logical notions (entailment, validity, equivalence, satisfiability) are defined by quantifying over interpretations (all of them, or some), and formulas with free variables can express relations that have no predicate symbol of their own.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch33-using]] — To show that a first-order sentence or argument fails a logical test, build one interpretation (a counter-model) in which the right sentences are true or false.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch34-reasoning]] — To show that a sentence of FOL is a validity (or that an entailment holds), one must reason in the metalanguage about all interpretations, while one counter-interpretation is enough to refute the claim.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch36-basic]] — FOL proofs get four quantifier rules (∀E, ∃I, ∀I, ∃E); ∀I and ∃E need side-conditions on the names they use, so that the reasoning is general (for ∀I the name must be "arbitrary").
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch37-proofs]] — Quantifier proofs are built with the same backward/forward strategy as TFL proofs; the key choice is which name to use: a new name for ∀I and for the ∃E assumption, a well-chosen name for ∃I (in general one already in the proof) and for ∀E.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch39-rules]] — The proof system gets rules linking quantifiers with negation (CQ), plus an introduction rule (=I) and an elimination rule (=E, Leibniz's Law) for identity.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch41-proofs]] — Provability (⊢) and semantic entailment (⊨) are different notions, but they agree, so for a given task you may use whichever method is easier.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch42-introducing]] — Modal logic adds the operators □ and ◇ to sentential logic; their readings vary (necessity, knowledge, obligation…), and rules are added to fit each reading.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch43-natural]] — Modal logic can be given proof rules in stages: system K handles □ with strict subproofs, and T, S4 and S5 each add one rule that makes □ behave more like necessity.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch44-semantics]] — ML sentences are true or false *at possible worlds*, and □ and ◇ quantify over the worlds accessible from the current one, so each modal system (K, T, S4, S5) matches a property of the accessibility relation.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch45-normal]] — Every TFL sentence is logically equivalent to a sentence in disjunctive normal form (an ∨ of ∧s of atomic or negated atomic sentences) and to one in conjunctive normal form (an ∧ of ∨s), and both can be read off the truth table.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch46-functional]] — Every possible truth table can be expressed using only ¬ with ∨ (or ¬ with ∧, or ¬ with →), and even a single connective, ↑ or ↓, is enough, so TFL never needs new connectives.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch47-proving]] — Because equivalent parts can be swapped inside a sentence and sentence letters can be replaced by any sentence, a small table of basic equivalences lets you prove equivalences, and find normal forms, by chains of rewriting steps.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch48-soundness]] — If a sentence C can be proved in TFL from assumptions Γ, then Γ really entails C, because every proof line is "shiny" and each rule keeps shininess.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch43-symbolic]] — Other logic texts use different names and symbols for the same ideas, and Polish notation writes connectives first so that no parentheses are needed.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch44-b]] — Which natural-deduction rules count as basic and which as derived is a choice, and the authors justify their choices by cleanliness, ease of checking proofs, an intuitive treatment of the quantifiers, and by keeping rules that philosophers discuss, such as IP and explosion, visible as separate rules.
- [[magnus-2025-forallx/ch100-quick]] — Appendix C is a lookup sheet of the book's formal toolkit: truth tables, English-to-symbol patterns, numerical quantifiers with identity, deduction rules (basic and derived), citation rules and equivalence laws.

## Big ideas across units
- **Validity as "no counterexample", and as form.** Consequence means no case with true premises and a non-true conclusion. Logic wants this to depend on form, not content. The same idea returns for TFL valuations and FOL interpretations, and a counterexample or counter-model shows invalidity. Units: ch01-arguments, ch02-scope, ch03-other, ch04-first, ch03-truth-p2, ch32-semantic, ch33-using, ch34-reasoning.
- **Symbolizing English costs something.** The book repeatedly asks what a formal symbolization keeps and what it loses. Examples are tone, causation and time order, subjunctive conditionals, borderline cases, empty names, ambiguous predicates, and the contested analysis of *except*. Predicates are only extensions. Units: ch04-first, ch05-connectives, ch03-truth-p1, ch03-truth-p2, ch24-sentences, ch26-identity, ch28-definite, ch30-extensionality, ch33-using.
- **Syntax defined inductively, with scope.** Sentences are defined by a base clause, construction clauses and a closing clause, for TFL and for FOL. The main logical operator and the scope of connectives and quantifiers fall out of this. Units: ch06-sentences, ch27-sentences, ch05-connectives, ch24-sentences, ch45-normal, ch42-introducing.
- **Ambiguity is mostly scope, and formal languages separate readings.** English leaves scope unclear, while TFL and FOL sentences have one reading. Units: ch07-ambiguity, ch25-multiple, ch28-definite, ch29-ambiguity, ch24-sentences.
- **Use vs. mention, object language vs. metalanguage.** Talking about a formal language needs quotation or metavariables. This also marks the difference between → and ⊨, and between ⊢ and ⊨. It shows up again when reasoning about all interpretations. Units: ch08-use, ch03-truth-p2, ch20-proof, ch34-reasoning, ch43-symbolic.
- **Two methods, semantic and proof-theoretic, that agree.** Truth tables or interpretations give a semantic test. Natural deduction gives a proof test. Soundness and completeness say they match for TFL, and the book states that ⊢ and ⊨ agree for FOL. Which method is easier depends on whether you want to show "yes" or "no". Units: ch03-truth-p2, ch17-basic, ch20-proof, ch22-soundness, ch33-using, ch34-reasoning, ch41-proofs, ch48-soundness.
- **Proof systems are designed, with strategies and choices.** Rules come in intro/elim pairs and use subproofs. There are strategies for finding proofs, derived rules that add convenience but no power, and contested rules (IP, explosion). Quantifier rules need name restrictions. Units: ch17-basic, ch18-constructing, ch19-additional, ch21-derived, ch36-basic, ch37-proofs, ch39-rules, ch44-b, ch100-quick.
- **Extending the framework: from TFL to FOL, then modal logic, with expressive limits.** TFL cannot see inside sentences, so FOL adds structure, and modal logic adds □ and ◇ with proof rules and possible-world semantics. TFL's connectives are shown to be expressively complete. Units: ch23-building, ch42-introducing, ch43-natural, ch44-semantics, ch45-normal, ch46-functional.

## How to read it
- **Essential first pass:** ch01-arguments to ch08-use, then ch03-truth-p1 and ch03-truth-p2. This matches the catalog's focus on Parts I–III. ch08-use is the use/mention chapter the catalog highlights. After that, the proof core is ch17-basic and ch18-constructing. For first-order logic, read ch23-building to ch27-sentences and ch31-truth to ch33-using.
- **Dependencies:** ch02-scope uses ch01. ch03-other uses ch02's "case". ch04-first to ch06-sentences build TFL step by step. ch03-truth-p1 needs the connectives and main operator (ch05, ch06). ch17-basic needs truth-table validity. ch18, ch19 and ch21 depend on ch17. ch20 and ch22 need proofs plus entailment. ch23 to ch29 need TFL. ch25 needs ch24, and ch29 needs ch24, ch25 and ch28. ch31 to ch34 need ch30 (interpretations). ch36 and ch37 need the TFL rules plus ch31's ideas. ch39 and ch41 need ch36. ch43 needs ch17, and ch44 needs ch43. ch45 to ch47 need truth tables, and ch46 needs ch45.
- **Can be skipped or deferred on a first pass:** ch19-additional and ch21-derived (the extra rules are, in the book's words, not strictly necessary). Also ch22-soundness and ch48-soundness (the book says full proofs are beyond it, and ch48 proves the TFL soundness theorem), ch41-proofs, ch44-b, and ch42 to ch47 (modal logic and normal forms are extensions). ch43-symbolic is a notation reference for reading other books. ch100-quick is a lookup sheet that explains nothing.
- **FOL extras as needed:** ch26-identity and ch28-definite are optional for basic quantifier work. ch28 uses ch26.
