# A Cultural Approach to Communication

## L0 — One line
Carey argues that communication should be studied less as the transmission of messages across space and more as a ritual, symbolic process by which reality is produced, maintained, repaired and transformed.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey asks what John Dewey meant by calling communication "wonderful." His tentative answer ("I think") is that Dewey used two senses of the word: a transmission view (sending messages over distance for control) and a ritual view (sharing beliefs and sustaining community through time). Carey says American thought has favoured transmission and has neglected ritual. He then defines communication as "a symbolic process whereby reality is produced, maintained, repaired, and transformed." Symbols, models and maps are both "of" reality and "for" reality. So our models of communication also shape the communication they describe. He suggests that today's power and anxiety models help produce the troubles we now see in communication and community.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday familiarity with newspapers, maps and the idea of "culture"; the names Dewey, Durkheim, Geertz and Kuhn appear as Carey's sources.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Dewey's puzzling remarks on communication make sense once two senses are separated: transmission and ritual. Carey uses their tension as a tool for research (pp. 11–12).
- Transmission view: messages are moved through space to control distance and people. Its metaphor comes from transportation, and its roots lie in religious ideas about movement in space (pp. 12–14).
- Ritual view: communication maintains society in time and represents shared beliefs. Its archetype is the sacred ceremony, and it offers confirmation, not information (p. 15).
- The same object looks different under each view. A newspaper is either an instrument with effects and functions, or a drama like attending a mass (pp. 16–17).
- Carey's definition: communication is a symbolic process that produces, maintains, repairs and transforms reality. This inverts the common-sense order of "reality first, language after" (pp. 19–21, 23–24).
- Symbolic forms such as maps, dances and poems have displacement (they can stand for absent things) and productivity (finite elements yield infinitely many representations). Thought is mainly public and social, and symbols work both as "of" and "for" reality (pp. 22–23).
- Models of communication are themselves "of" and "for." Social science is therefore reflexive: its models of communication, such as the power model (people seek power) and the anxiety model (people flee anxiety), help create the behaviour they describe (pp. 24–26).
- Carey says widespread worry about communication reflects a derangement in our models of communication and community, caused by commitment to transmission. He recommends a ritual model (pp. 26–27).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Dewey's two senses of communication (pp. 11–18)

**The puzzle (pp. 11–12).** A literal reading of Dewey's claim "of all things communication is the most wonderful" makes it false or mundane. Carey says most mass-media news and entertainment is trivial, and most encounters with others are wonderful only in moments of "excessive masochism" (p. 11). Dewey also wrote that society exists "not only by transmission... but... in transmission" (Dewey 1916: 5). Carey asks what the shift in prepositions means. The obvious reading, that societies distribute information, is reasonable but hardly needs scholars to say it (pp. 11–12).

**Carey's thesis (p. 12).** The complexity in Dewey comes from two contrasting definitions of communication in Western thought. Dewey used their conflict as a creative tension, and it also led him to some characteristic errors. Carey proposes to use the same contradiction to "vivify" communication studies. Both conceptions have been alive in American culture since the term entered common discourse in the nineteenth century, and both derive from religious origins. He labels them the transmission view and the ritual view (p. 12).

**Transmission view (pp. 12–15).**
- It is the commonest view in our culture, "perhaps in all industrial cultures," and dominates dictionary entries. It is defined by "imparting," "sending," "transmitting," "giving information to others" (p. 12).
- It is a metaphor of geography or transportation. In the nineteenth century, moving goods or people and moving information were seen as essentially identical processes, both called "communication" (p. 12).
- Its centre is the transmission of signals or messages over distance for control. Carey traces it to the old wish to speed up messages in space, from Egypt's First Dynasty to the telegraph (p. 12).
- The telegraph ended the identity of transport and communication but not the metaphor. Carey's summary: communication is "a process whereby messages are transmitted and distributed in space for the control of distance and people" (p. 13).
- Religious roots (Carey's own illustration, which he calls a "devious though, in detail, inadequate" path, p. 13). The modern transmission view arises with the age of exploration. Motives were political and mercantile, but also religious, for example the Dutch Reformed Church in South Africa and the Puritans in New England. Carey says the migration expressed a belief that movement in space could be a redemptive act (p. 13).
- Transportation that brought Christian Europe into contact with the "heathen" Americas was seen as a form of communication with "profoundly religious implications," an attempt to extend the kingdom of God. The moral meaning of communication was the same (p. 13).
- Sources cited:
  - Gardner Spring called thought's travel by "steam and magnetic wires" a border of a "spiritual harvest" (p. 13).
  - Batchelder said the railroad was built for missionary purposes (p. 13).
  - Morse's first telegraph message, "What Hath God Wrought?", is read as showing the invention was not meant "to spread the price of pork" (pp. 13–14).
  - Perry Miller describes a "Christianized technology" at the 1858 awakening (p. 14).
- Later the religious metaphors fell away and the technology moved to the centre. The telegraph was valued as "transmission of thought," not only "modification of matter" (p. 14).
- Dissenters were few: Thoreau, and Calhoun, who saw electricity's subjugation as "the last era in human civilization" (p. 14).
- The religious undercurrent persists. From telegraph to computer, machines are invoked with a sense of "possibility for moral improvement." Improved communication is called the talisman of our troubles (pp. 14–15).
- Carey says he can "only assert here rather than demonstrate" that the same root attitudes operate in most scientifically sophisticated views of communication (p. 15).

**Ritual view (p. 15).**
- It is a "minor thread" in national thought but "by far the older"; dictionaries list it as "Archaic" (p. 15).
- Linked terms: "sharing," "participation," "association," "fellowship," "possession of a common faith." It uses the common roots of "commonness," "communion," "community" and "communication" (p. 15).
- It is directed "not toward the extension of messages in space but toward the maintenance of society in time; not the act of imparting information but the representation of shared beliefs" (p. 15).
- The archetype is the sacred ceremony that draws persons into fellowship. This view stresses prayer, chant and ceremony over sermon and instruction (p. 15).
- On this view the original or highest manifestation of communication is the construction and maintenance of "an ordered, meaningful cultural world" that serves as "a control and container for human action" (p. 15).
- Writers in this tradition often trace their heritage partly to Durkheim, for whom society projects the community's ideals as a different world (1953: 95). Dance, plays, architecture, news stories and speech embody this projection. It creates a symbolic order, "artificial though nonetheless real," giving "not information but confirmation," not changing minds but representing "an underlying order of things" (p. 15).

**Why Americans neglected ritual (pp. 15–16).** Transmission fits the "wellsprings" of American culture. Carey says "culture" is a weak notion in American social thought, a concept we readily apply to other peoples but which dissolves into a residual category when applied to American culture. He says this aversion derives in part from:
- obsessive individualism, which makes psychological life paramount;
- Puritan disdain for activity that is not practical;
- the isolation of science (culture-free truth) from culture (ethnocentric error) (p. 16).

So scholarship on ritual comes largely from European sources or Americans they influenced (p. 16).

**The newspaper under each view (pp. 16–17).**
- *Transmission:* the paper disseminates news in larger packages over greater distances. Questions concern effects (enlightening or obscuring, changing or hardening attitudes) and functions (integration or maladaptation of society, stability or instability of personalities). Carey calls this a "mechanical analysis" (p. 16).
- *Ritual:* reading a paper is less like gaining information and more like "attending a mass." Nothing new is learned, but a view of the world is portrayed and confirmed (p. 16).
- News is "dramatic": the reader sees contending forces and shifts roles. A monetary-crisis story casts one as an American patriot, a women's caucus story as supporter or opponent, a campus-violence story evokes class antagonisms (pp. 16–17).
- The question is then about presentation and involvement in structuring "the reader's life and time," not about effects or functions. News changes little yet is satisfying; it performs few functions yet is habitually consumed. The paper is seen as a text, like a Balinese cockfight, a Dickens novel or a student rally (p. 17).
- News is also a historic form, invented by the middle class largely in the eighteenth century. It reflects a "hunger for experience" for the novel over the epic and traditional. This hunger is not universal, and Carey says the form will dissolve when the sponsoring class and its significance for us evaporate (p. 17).
- Summary: under a ritual view "news is not information but drama." It portrays an arena of forces and invites participation by assuming social roles, often vicariously (p. 17).

**The views are not strict opposites (p. 17).** A ritual view does not exclude information transmission or attitude change. It says these cannot be understood aright except within a ritualistic view of communication and social order. Transmission writers also need something like Malinowski's phatic communion. Carey adds that "origins determine endings": where one starts largely fixes the path of analysis (p. 17).

**Dewey's emphasis (p. 18).** Carey reads Dewey's "the most wonderful" as meaning that communication is the basis of human fellowship, producing the social bonds that make associated life possible. In Dewey (1916: 5–6), men live in community "in virtue of the things which they have in common." These are aims, beliefs, aspirations and knowledge, which cannot be passed like bricks or divided like a pie. "Consensus demands communication" (p. 18). Dewey was often untrue to his own thought: he came to overvalue scientific information and communication technology as a solvent of social problems (p. 18).

**Carey's stance (p. 18).** Transmission has dominated American thought since the 1920s. When Carey entered the field he felt this view, in behavioural and functional terms, was exhausted and academic. He wanted to reopen it using Dewey's tension and material from biology, theology, anthropology and literature (p. 18).

> *Intuition note.* Two pictures of "communication" compete. In one, a message is moved from A to B. In the other, people take part in something together, and that keeps their world in place. Carey says American thought has been "glued" to the first, and that the second has not been explored.

### II. Communication as the symbolic production of reality (pp. 18–28)

**Sources and definition (p. 19).** Carey draws on Weber, Durkheim, de Tocqueville, Huizinga, Burke, Duncan, Portmann, Kuhn, Berger and Geertz. The most viable tradition he finds is the Chicago School descended from Dewey: Mead and Cooley, then Park, then Goffman (p. 19). His definition: "communication is a symbolic process whereby reality is produced, maintained, repaired, and transformed" (p. 19).

**Making the familiar strange (pp. 19–20).** Communication is as mundane as water to a fish, so it is hard to notice, and we tend to study its trivial aspects (p. 19). Art makes the phenomenon strange: Fitzgerald's Daisy has "a voice full of money." Social science can likewise force background facts into the foreground. Carey reads Dewey's "most wonderful" remark as trying to induce this wonder. Carey says Dewey knew that knowledge grew most effectively when things became problematic, when an "information gap" opened between what circumstances push us to do and what we need to know to act (pp. 19–20).

**Inverting realism (p. 20).**
- Common sense and scientific realism say there is first a real world of objects and events, then symbols that name it, then "after the fact" accounts. Language is to reality as secondary is to primary, in the "old Galilean paradigm." Carey says we rarely dispute this realism (p. 20).
- Carey inverts this order. He does not make "large metaphysical claims" but wants to make communication more problematic (p. 20).
- Playing on St. John, "in the beginning was the word." Following Burke, "things are the signs of words." Reality "is brought into existence, is produced, by communication"—construction, apprehension and use of symbolic forms. It is not a mere function of symbolic forms, but is produced by terministic systems that focus it in specific terms (p. 20).
- The world is "entropic—that is, not strictly ordered," though constrained enough that the mind can grasp its outline and impose order. Carey's example: nature has no lines of latitude and longitude, but laying them over the globe imposes order for limited purposes (p. 20). (Carey glosses "entropic" himself as "not strictly ordered"; no formula is given on the page.)
- For us, reality is "a vast production, a staged creation." J. Z. Young: the brain "does literally create his or her own world." Vogelin: the order of history is "the history of order" (p. 21).
- Carey invokes Cassirer: man lives in a symbolic reality. Carey says this is often said but rarely investigated. Carey reads Cassirer as contending that communication, even scientific and mathematical, must be examined as primary phenomena of experience, not as something "softer" and derivative from a "realer" nature (p. 21).
- Footnote 5 qualifies this. He does not say language constitutes the real world, as Cassirer "often seems to argue." His claim is that the world is apprehensible for humans only through language or another symbolic form (p. 28).

**The map example (pp. 21–23).**
- Task: teach a child of six or seven to get from home to school, some six or seven blocks away. The child recognises the school but not the relation between it and home (p. 21).
- Options: trial and error as in a conditioning experiment; following an adult (Carey says the Apaches do this, "I'm told"); or the ordinary method, a drawn map. A map turns "vacant space into a featured environment," represents an environment and guides behaviour (p. 21).
- The same space can be mapped as poetry or song (the mnemonic aids memory) or as danced ritual (p. 22).
- The three modes are visual, oral and kinesthetic symbolic forms. Each has two characteristics (p. 22):
  - **Displacement:** representing something when it is not present (p. 22).
  - **Productivity:** infinitely many representations from a finite set of elements. Example: finite words or phonemes yield infinite sentences by grammar (p. 22).
- A map simplifies. Space is made manageable by "the reduction of information." Different maps bring the same environment alive differently, producing different realities. Carey concludes that living within different maps is living within different realities, so "maps... constitute nature itself" (p. 22).
- **Thought is public and social** (pp. 22–23). We think of thought as private, as in Rodin's "The Thinker." Carey says instead that it occurs on blackboards, in dances and recited poems. Private thought is "derived and secondary," later in the person and in the species. Thought is public in two senses (p. 22):
  - it depends on a public stock of symbols;
  - in a stronger sense, thinking is building maps of environments, models run "faster than the environment" to see if nature can be coerced to behave like the model (pp. 22–23).
- In the school example, the finger tracing the map stands for the walking child. "Running" the map is faster than walking and constitutes the "experiment" (p. 23).
- Other maps and models: football plays on a blackboard, equations, ritual dances, prose like his own (p. 23).

**Models "of" and "for" reality (p. 23).** The "miracle" of producing reality and then living in it rests on a quality of symbols: they are representations "of" and "for" reality.
- A blueprint is "for" reality when a house is built under its guidance. It is "of" reality when someone asks about a particular house and one points at it and says "That's the house." These are "two sides of the same coin." As "symbols of" they present reality; as "symbols for" they create the reality they present (p. 23).
- The map is "of" when pointed to for the home–school relation. It is "for" when the child walks under its guidance and experiences space as the map formulates it, including its "blinders" (p. 23).
- Religious ritual represents human life in one mode and in its "for" mode "induces the dispositions it pretends merely to portray" (p. 23).
- Carey says all human activity is "squaring the circle": we produce the world by symbolic work and then reside in it. "There is magic in our self deceptions" (p. 23).
- Footnote 6 credits Clifford Geertz heavily for this formulation (p. 28).

> *Intuition note.* A map tells you what the place is like (of), and it also shapes how you move through the place and what you notice (for). Carey says this double role holds for blueprints, rituals and models, so they are not neutral copies of reality.

**Maintenance, repair, transformation (pp. 23–24).**
- Production alone is not enough. New generations need reality regenerated and made authoritative (pp. 23–24).
- Reality must be repaired because it breaks down: people get lost, experiments fail, counter-evidence appears, mental derangement sets in (p. 24).
- Eventually we may discard authoritative representations and build anew. Carey's example: going to bed convinced behaviourists (Skinner, language as operant conditioning) and waking convinced rationalists (Chomsky, deep structures). These are two intellectual worlds, and anomalies in one may lead us to transform it into another (p. 24).
- Footnote 8 reads Kuhn's *Structure of Scientific Revolutions* as describing production (paradigm creation), maintenance (articulation, training new scientists through exemplars), repair (dismissing anomalies) and transformation (revolutions and their institutionalisation) of a scientific world (p. 28).
- Footnote 7 adds that humans produce multiple worlds and live in transit between them (Schutz's "multiple realities"). Carey says some such perspective is necessary to make sense of communicative "effects" (p. 28).

**What studying communication means (p. 24).**
- It means examining the social process in which significant symbolic forms are created, apprehended and used (p. 24).
- **Objection:** some scholars would dismiss this as insufficiently empirical. **Carey replies:** the opposite; these activities are publicly observable, occur in historical time, and he wants to sweep away notions that "devitalize our data" (p. 24).
- Questions he lists:
  - How are symbol systems (art, science, journalism, religion, common sense, mythology) built?
  - How do they differ?
  - How do historical and comparative variations arise?
  - How does technology change what we can create and apprehend?
  - How do groups struggle over the definition of the real? (p. 24)

**Reflexivity and ethics (pp. 24–25).**
- Communication about communication doubles back. There is no "pure" communication to be revealed by a method free of culture. We understand it only through models, and models have an "of" and a "for" aspect (p. 24).
- Several models may be empirically adequate, but they have different ethical implications because they produce different social relations (pp. 24–25).
- Nothing in our genes tells us how to communicate. We take models from culture: common sense, law, religion and, increasingly, science. Religion once supplied models for uses of language, forms of contact, ends and motives (p. 25).
- Today, within an overarching transmission view, American social science has generally represented communication as either a **power model** (information theory, learning theory, influence theory) or an **anxiety model** (dissonance, balance theory, functionalism or uses and gratifications). Carey says these reduce communication's diversity to people pursuing power or fleeing anxiety. He says institutions' policies and programs create the abstract motives and relations these models portray (p. 25).
- Models are therefore "of" and "for." To study communication is to study the construction and use of models, in encounters such as parent–child, advertiser–consumer, welfare worker–supplicant and teacher–student. Our models "create what we disingenuously pretend they merely describe," so our science is reflexive (Gouldner's term) (p. 25).

**Williams and the conclusion (pp. 26–27).**
- Williams (1966) is quoted: communication models are rules or conventions we change or extend when inadequate. Many become social institutions that "teach the models from which they start" (p. 26).
- Carey notes the habit of calling every human problem a communication failure. He calls this a way of coating reality with cliches but thinks it has a "kernel of truth" (p. 26).
- Following Dewey, problems of communication are linked to problems of community. Society seen as communication is a process in which reality is "created, shared, modified, and preserved." When this becomes opaque, we face problems of communication "in their most potent form" (p. 26).
- Carey's diagnosis: the social interest in communication derives from a derangement in our models of communication and community. That derangement derives from an obsessive commitment to the transmission view and its complementary power and anxiety models (pp. 26–27).
- As a result we see society as a political order (power, administration, control) or an economic order (property, production, trade). Following Williams, social life also includes shared aesthetic experience, religious ideas, values and sentiments, and intellectual notions: a **ritual order** (p. 27).
- Three examples of abuse in the service of politics, trade and therapy (p. 27):
  - Communications technology was devoted almost exclusively to government and trade, not to expanding people's powers to learn and exchange.
  - Education became citizenship, professionalism, consumerism and increasingly therapy.
  - Cities became residences of technology and bureaucracy: streets for cars, sidewalks for trade.
- Aim: recasting communication studies in ritual terms is meant both to grasp the "wonderful" process more firmly and to rebuild a model "of and for" communication "of some restorative value" for our common culture (p. 27).

**Other footnotes.** Fn 4: Carey believes the two views link to contrasting views of language, thought and symbolism. The transmission view favours language as an instrument of practical action and discursive reasoning, thought as conceptual and individual, and symbolism as analytic. The ritual view sees language as dramatic action, thought as situational and social, and symbolism as "fiduciary" (pp. 27–28). Fn 9 quotes Kuhn: the choice between competing paradigms is a choice between "incompatible modes of community life" (p. 28).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town has 6 features: streets, bakery, park, dog yard, bus stop, church. Two maps of the walk from home to school:
- Map A marks 2 features: streets, dog yard. It keeps 2/6 ≈ 33% of the features.
- Map B marks 3 features: streets, bakery, park. It keeps 3/6 = 50%.

Both maps reduce information (Carey, p. 22), but they differ.
- **"Of" use:** asked "how are home and school related?", one points at Map A. The answer is "along these streets, avoiding the dog yard."
- **"For" use:** a child who walks with Map A notices streets and the dog yard. A child who walks with Map B notices the bakery and the park. Each child has been guided by the map's "blinders" (p. 23).

The same town yields two experienced realities. This is Carey's claim that different maps "produce quite different realities" (p. 22), and the child experiences space as the map formulates it.

## Questions this raises
- Can the transmission and ritual views be tested against each other empirically, or does the starting point ("origins determine endings," p. 17) decide the results?
- If models of communication shape behaviour, how could a researcher check which model is "empirically adequate" yet ethically different (pp. 24–25)?
- Carey says the religious root of the transmission view operates in "most" scientific theories (p. 15), but only asserts it. What evidence would support or refute this?
- Does a ritual view of news explain digital platforms where people choose and share content?
