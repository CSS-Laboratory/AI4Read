# The Mythos of the Electronic Revolution

## L0 — One line
Carey and Quirk argue that the claim that electronics will bring decentralized, harmonious community is the latest version of an old American "sublime" rhetoric that repeatedly disguised centralizing technology, so intellectuals should demythologize it and work through politics.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Futurists such as Brzezinski and McLuhan say electronics will bring community, decentralization and harmony. The authors ask how to judge this claim. They trace the same promise through American history: steam, then electricity, in Carey, Geddes, Giant Power, the New Deal and the TVA (a federal power agency). The authors say the promise was repeatedly left undelivered, and that the rhetoric helped the firms and institutions that controlled the technology. Following Harold Innis, they say modern media widen reception but narrow distribution (audiences cannot respond directly), so they create the potential for control over space. They reject both celebration and neo-Luddite (anti-technology) protest. Intellectuals should demythologize the rhetoric and press political questions of democratization.

**Prerequisites:** basic US history (Civil War, Depression, New Deal); the idea that technologies are discussed in persuasive language; no math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Prophets of an "electronic revolution" from many camps share one faith: electricity is benign and will bring community, decentralization and harmony. The authors call this a secular theology (pp. 88–89).
- The authors say recent electronic technology has in fact been biased toward recentralization of power and the erosion of regional cultures (pp. 89–90).
- The authors link the faith to the "machine in the garden" idea (blending industry with rural nature) and the "technological sublime" (awe-filled praise of steam and machinery). When mechanization disappointed, the hope moved to electricity: the "electrical sublime" (pp. 91–94).
- The electrical sublime recurs from Carey through Geddes, Giant Power (a 1920s campaign for public electric power), Roosevelt and the TVA (the Tennessee Valley Authority, a New Deal power agency). The authors argue it mainly benefited the power companies and the institutions in charge (pp. 99–102).
- The TVA is the authors' test case. They say it shows the folly of equating technical projects with democratic community (p. 102).
- Innis: media bias societies toward time or space. Print and electricity are space-binding (tied to expansion and control over territory), so they favor commerce, empire and technocracy (p. 103).
- Authors' own claim: modern media widen reception but narrow distribution (large audiences cannot respond directly), so they enable control of large spaces and populations. Only politics can counter this (p. 105).
- Neo-Luddite protest (symbolic resistance to technology), dystopian fiction and utopian enclaves are rejected as mirror images of the myth (pp. 106–107).
- Their prescription: demythologize (strip away the myth), treat technology as only a means of communication and transportation over space, and ask political questions about democratization (pp. 107–108).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I.1 The "eighth day" and the futurist mood (pp. 87–88)
- **Authors:** Wilder's *The Eighth Day* shows Americans around 1900 hoping for rebirth through science. In the novel, a community leader's speech pictures the new century as an "eighth day" after Genesis and its people as a new breed, free from the past (p. 87).
- Today's "eighth day" is the Year 2000 projects and the "electronic revolution". Agencies of prophecy include the Commission on the Year 2000, the World Future Society and Rand. The authors say the language of contemporary futurology contains an orientation of "secular religiosity" that surfaces whenever the name of technology is invoked (p. 87).
- The coming era is alternatively termed "postindustrial society", "post-civilization", "technetronic society" and "global village". The new breed of human is characterized as "post-modern man", "the protean personality" and "post-literate-electronic man" (p. 88).
- The electronic-revolution view links electricity, cybernetics, computers and information with community, decentralization, ecological balance and harmony. McLuhan is the best-known voice, but it is a school of thought with many speakers: Fuller, Cage, Toffler, Brzezinski, parts of the New Left, Teilhardian theologians, Feigenbaum, and advertisers and executives (p. 88).
- **Authors:** these people act as "secular theologians" writing theodicies (defenses of a power's goodness) for electricity. Their shared belief is that electricity will overcome the historical and political obstacles that blocked earlier utopias (p. 88).

### I.2 The rhetoric, and the record (pp. 88–90)
- **Brzezinski:** the technetronic revolution will "affect the essence" of existence, unlike earlier revolutions. He expects devolution of authority. Technetronics eliminates the "insulants" of time and space. Social scientists, above faction, can reduce conflict to measurable dimensions (pp. 88–89).
- **McLuhan:** the artist, not the scientist, is heir. The electronic age, "if given its own unheeded leeway", will drift quite naturally into modes of "cosmic humanism". Electricity can "abolish space and time alike", and the computer promises a "Pentecostal condition of universal understanding" (p. 89).
- **Authors:** sacred or secular, the rhetoric gives electricity intrinsically benign properties. High-tech firms are promised to bring jobs, ecological harmony and an end to alienation between people and machines (p. 89).
- **Authors:** this contrasts with the record. Electronic use has been biased toward recentralization (computer centers, grids, the Pentagon, NASA, GE). It brings pollution from generation and erodes regional cultures through national networks (pp. 89–90).
- High tech demands lenient legislation and weak unions. States compete to supply this. Educated elites say the competitive failing results from widespread scientific illiteracy and propose a new definition of the liberal arts around mathematics, computer science and technological expertise; anxious parents send children to computer camps. Computer advertising turns "priesthood of all believers" into "priesthood of all computers" (p. 90).
- **Dilemma (authors):** either the "electricians" see a future we cannot, or the revolution is wishful thinking or, worse, a new legitimation of the status quo. History may supply terms to judge it (p. 90).

*Intuition note:* The authors compare the promise with what electronics has done, then turn to the history of earlier technologies and their social use for terms to judge it.

### I.3 The American background: machine in the garden and the technological sublime (pp. 90–93)
- **Authors:** Europeans dreamed America as redemption from European history, with Nature as the controlling metaphor. Machinery was to be humanized by a rural landscape: "the factory without the factory system" (pp. 90–91).
- Scholars (Miller, Smith, Marx, Trachtenberg) trace the "machine in the garden". It imagined a *middle landscape* between rural life and industrial city. America was "exempt from history": wealth from machines, peace from nature (p. 91).
- Condorcet thought America was insulated and would "double the progress of the race". This view moved Jefferson to accept manufactures, because American manufacturers would stay moral while vacant land remained (pp. 91–92).
- Particular technologies received special praise. Jefferson tied print and press freedom to literacy and liberty. Steam received essays on its "Moral Influence", and Fraser spoke of steam uniting the land. Emerson wrote that "Machinery and transcendentalism agree well" (p. 92).
- Leo Marx called this the **technological sublime**. The authors call it the "false consciousness" of the pre-Civil War decades (p. 92).
- After the Civil War, industrial slums, class and racial conflict, depression and ravaged land showed America was not exempt. **Authors:** reality could not reverse rhetoric. A new school held that electricity differed qualitatively from machinery and could redeem what machines betrayed (pp. 92–93).

### I.4 Henry Charles Carey and the electrical sublime (pp. 93–95)
- Henry C. Carey held that industry in America would avoid the wage slavery of Europe. His 1848 *Past, Present and Future* proposed "regional associationism": regional links between industry and agriculture, in a cooperative, decentralized economy (p. 93).
- His plan went unrealized as centralization grew. In *The Unity of Law* he recast it in the language of electricity. He identified the laws of electricity with the laws of society, and electricity as the bond between nature and society (p. 93).
- In the quoted passage, society is "a great electric battery" in which each member contributes "his pair of plates". Each is alternately giver and receiver, and perfect circulation produces prosperity and "improved mental and moral action" (p. 94).
- **Authors:** positive and negative are used as a unity of opposites, so conflict is mere appearance. This rhetoric tried to merge two desires. One was the freedom and decentralization mechanization had promised. The other was power and productivity (p. 94).
- In the same decade Edison and Bell built the technology, Gould and Vanderbilt fought over it, and the base of firms like GE was laid. **Authors:** inventors became symbols while "new empires" arose behind an "electrical mystique" (pp. 94–95).
- The authors say intellectuals could devise nothing more effective than a purely literary strategy: inverting the sublime. Clemens's *Connecticut Yankee* is called probably America's first dystopia: Hank Morgan is trapped by his own electric fence. Henry Adams was obsessed with thermodynamics and the specter of "entropic disaster", and located the shift to "sheer power" in 1844 (p. 95).
- Reformers (Bellamy, Howells) instead blamed the corporation and kept the optimism. They saw electric communication and transport spreading culture and decentralizing control (p. 95).

### I.5 European roots and Geddes (pp. 95–99)
- Mercier (1770) wrote the first electrical utopia, in which electricity was a material and a moral instrument (p. 96).
- Mumford, as cited, credits Kropotkin with seeing that electric power could restore communal conditions through "industrial villages" (p. 96). Siemens hoped power stations would help small workshops compete with steam factories. Howard assured readers that in his Garden City "all machinery is driven by electric energy" (p. 96).
- Wallas wrote *The Great Society*. He observed that the first developers of steam and electricity expected wholly good results, and that people now doubt the future happiness of individuals and the permanence of the Great Society. He advocated revived dialogue and discussion (p. 97).
- Geddes is described as the first to build a systematic utopia on a theory that electrical and mechanical technology differ qualitatively. He distinguished "paleotechnic" (steam) from "neotechnic" (electric) ages and expected a "Geotechnic" phase of regional "Eutopia". His disciples included Mumford (pp. 97–99).
- The Regional Planning Association launched a "giant power" crusade combining electricity with conservation and "democratic localism". Pinchot said steam centralized, while Giant Power "may" decentralize, "if we control it" (p. 99).

### I.6 Who benefited: the power companies, the New Deal and the TVA (pp. 99–102)
- **Authors:** the real beneficiaries were the light and power companies. Insull's public relations invoked the new civilization until the Depression ended their favor (p. 99).
- In the 1930s the crusade moved to government. Stuart Chase's 1933 "A Vision in Kilowatts" described electricity as yoking a continental economy into one "organic whole" with small, flexible parts (pp. 99–100).
- Roosevelt (1936): electricity is flexible and could be sent anywhere, but "sheer inertia" keeps the over-centralization of the steam era. He called a revolution possibly "already under way" (p. 100).
- The TVA was to be a showcase linking electricity, decentralization and citizen participation. Roosevelt called it a corporation with government power and private flexibility (pp. 100–101). Lilienthal promised "an end to drudgery, congestion, waste" (p. 101).
- Admirers abroad included Madame Keun, who saw the TVA as a "happy balance" between Jefferson's dream and the power age. Henry Wallace proposed many TVAs ("valleys are much the same everywhere"). Schlesinger saw it as a Cold War weapon (p. 101).
- **Authors' verdict:** the authors call the TVA "an ambiguous legacy at best". It tied itself to electrical goods industries, was accused of strip mining and rate fixing, bureaucratized its interests and elevated narrow efficiency. Exported, it fed political misadventures, such as the Mekong Delta Authority and the McNamara DMZ project, and ecological disasters (pp. 101–102).
- **Conclusion (authors):** the TVA shows "the folly" of identifying technical projects with democratic community. TVA rhetoric tied electrical sublimity to contact with nature, and as a result the TVA "mesmerized liberals" and prevented a serious evaluation of its failures. Only Tugwell seems to have kept detachment. By midcentury Mumford blamed pragmatic liberals of the TVA type, but had no strategy for dealing with the reversal of his hopes, and his own rhetoric had, if anything, contributed to the situation (p. 102).

*Intuition note:* The authors trace a recurring pattern: when mechanization disappointed, electricity was hailed as redeeming what machines had betrayed (p. 93), and the real beneficiaries of the electrical rhetoric were the power companies (p. 99).

### I.7 Innis's critique (pp. 102–105)
- Innis (Toronto, McLuhan's colleague) disputed the claim that electricity would replace centralization with decentralization and democracy. He placed the "tragedy of modern culture" in the tendency of print and electronic media to serve commercialism and expansion (pp. 102–103).
- **Innis's theory (as given):** communication and transportation systems "bias" relations of time and space. *Space-binding* media (print, electricity) favor territorial control, commercialism, empire, technocracy. *Time-binding* media (manuscript, speech) favor close communities, metaphysical speculation, traditional authority (p. 103).
- Modern technology, Innis argued, undermines both time and space. Industrialism cuts time into fragments for engineers and accountants, and present-mindedness destroys the sense of duration (p. 103).
- In "The Penetrative Powers of the Price System", Innis said apparent decentralization from electric power was superimposed on a larger trend of expansion and control. Electronic media let imperial powers bring satellite areas under control. Only politics and scholarship, and a deliberately maintained counterculture rooted in oral tradition, offered a way out (p. 104).
- **Authors' own claim:** each modern medium has increased the capacity to control space by reducing *signaling time* (the gap between sending and receiving a message). Print solved standardized production but not distribution. Telegraph, radio and television solved both (pp. 104–105).
- **Authors:** modern media "widen the range of reception while narrowing the range of distribution". Large audiences receive but cannot respond directly, so media create the potential for simultaneous administration of huge spaces and populations. "No amount of rhetoric" will remove this, and only politics can control the bias (p. 105).
- In his last years Innis feared cultural imperialism, and he saw scholars themselves becoming "hot gospellers of truth". The authors say Innis saw most clearly that the main meaning of electronics was the enlarged possible scale of social organization, not entertainment and information. They add, as a possibility, that the final meaning of electronics may be the colonization and political control of "outer space" (p. 105).

*Intuition note:* The authors, following Innis, say the main meaning of electronics lies not in the entertainment and information it provides but in its effect on the scale of organization. A medium that sends fast to many but receives little back suits a center that rules from a distance.

### I.8 What intellectuals should do (pp. 105–108)
- Since the 1960s, prophets such as Toffler and Naisbitt see "technological solutions" to what the authors say are in fact "persistent political problems". **Authors:** their vision is loosely shared by engineering societies, corporations, universities seeking funds, the military and the State Department (pp. 105–106).
- **Authors' answer** to the question "What, then, is the responsibility of intellectuals concerning the electronic revolution?": it is not to hold more futurist congresses. Planning attempts failed because the bias of electronic power is "antithetical to dispersed use and small-scale control". Promoting the illusion of an "electronic revolution" borders on complicity by intellectuals in the myth-making of the electrical complex (p. 106).
- The remedy is to counter and direct rather than disguise the bias: use cultural and qualitative checks, and decouple the humanistic from the technological rather than imagine "humanized technology" (p. 106).
- **Rejected alternatives (authors):** purely literary strategies (antisublime, black humor, Orwellian science fiction), confrontation-protest ("neo-Luddite"), and utopian enclaves such as Skinner's *Walden II* or Huxley's *Island*. The anti-technologists "reverse the mythology" and also assume a new age free of history. Their powerlessness breeds more powerlessness. Vonnegut's *Player Piano* is cited as predicting their defeat (pp. 106–107).
- **First task (authors):** demythologize the "electronic sublime". "Technology is technology; it is a means for communication and transportation over space, and nothing more." Also dismantle the fetishes of communication and of decentralization or participation "without reference to content or context" (p. 107).
- **Authors:** the real political questions are democratization, an adequate curriculum, and reconciling power and wealth with liberty and equality. These matter more than centralism versus decentralism, book versus computer, or representative versus participatory institutions. Real control over electronic media "might" need more formal centralism (p. 108).
- Intellectuals should produce critical studies that reach the public in sympathetic terms, in favor of the arts, ethics and politics. They quote Perry Miller on the duty to secure a hearing from the audience. They say that hearing must be secured in a demythologized language of democracy, and that at least this seems to be a responsibility for forming a "party of the mind" (p. 108).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the claim that media "widen reception while narrowing distribution" (p. 105). Take a town of 1,000 people.
- **Broadcast pattern:** 1 station sends, 999 receive, and replies go only by slow mail. In one hour: 1 message produces 999 receptions, i.e. $999/1 = 999$ receptions per sender. Direct return flow in that hour is 0.
- **Two-way pattern:** every pair can talk directly. Possible pairs are $\binom{1000}{2} = \frac{1000 \times 999}{2} = 499{,}500$.
- Same electricity, very different structure. The first pattern gives one center a "voice" over 999 listeners. This shows what the authors mean by structure biasing control. It does not test their claim that electronic media as built follow the first pattern.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Space-binding vs time-binding media ↔ communication-theory: Craig's ritual view (community maintained over time) vs transmission view (signals sent over space) — each pair opposes spatial reach to community continuity.
- [metaphor] "Electrical sublime" ↔ diffusion research: pro-innovation bias — both are over-optimism about a technology's effects.
- [structural-analogy] Widened reception, narrowed response ↔ media effects: mass-media one-way flow versus feedback in cybernetics.

## Questions this raises
- If the bias lies in the technology, why do the authors expect politics to be able to control it (p. 105)?
- Does the authors' claim that "technology is technology … nothing more" fit their own stress on technology's centralizing bias (pp. 105, 107)?
- How would one test the claim that electronic media have centralized power more than they have decentralized it?
- Is "more formal centralism" (p. 108) compatible with the chapter's praise of local community?
