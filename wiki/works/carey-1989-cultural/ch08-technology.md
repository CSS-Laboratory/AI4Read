# Technology and Ideology: The Case of the Telegraph

## L0 — One line
By separating communication from transportation, the telegraph let information outrun goods, and this reshaped commerce, language, empire and the ordinary experience of time and space (and supplied the transmission model of communication).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey asks how one technology, the telegraph, changed ideas as well as business. His answer: it ended the old identity of "communication" and "transportation," so symbols could move faster than people and goods. He traces the effects through three links to ideology (monopoly capitalism, religious "electrical sublime" rhetoric, and ordinary ideas) and then through news language, empire, commodity markets (arbitrage, buying cheap and selling dear between places, gave way to futures, trading between times) and standard time. The telegraph also became a model for thinking about communication: the transmission model. Carey's case shows how a communication technology can shape practical consciousness, the everyday common sense of a society. He also says the telegraph prefigured the computer.

**Prerequisites:** basic supply and demand; the idea of a price difference between two places; longitude and time zones; the idea of ideology as justifying ideas.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The telegraph's most important fact is the "effective separation of communication from transportation." Symbols could move independently of, and faster than, transportation, and communication could also control physical processes (p. 157).
- The telegraph was a "thing to think with." It supplied what Carey calls the "transmission model" (his name for the model of communication that the telegraph provided) and displaced older religious views of communication, though it was described in religious language (p. 157).
- Three telegraph–ideology links are listed: monopoly capitalism, popular religious imagery (the "electrical sublime," a rhetoric of religious aspiration), and effects on ordinary ideas. Carey focuses on the third (pp. 158–62).
- Wire services and costly words pushed news toward standardized, "objective," stripped language. The stringer, who supplied bare facts, replaced the letter-writing correspondent; the reporter displaced the editor as archetypal journalist; and news was treated like a commodity (pp. 162–63).
- The cable helped turn colonialism into imperialism, because the centre could now dictate to the margin and secure overseas investment (pp. 163–64).
- Telegraphed prices evened out markets in space. This largely ended arbitrage (buying cheap in one place and selling dear in another) and moved speculation to time (futures, that is, trading between times). Futures needed markets cut off from local conditions, trade in paper receipts instead of goods, and standard grades (pp. 167–70).
- Carey ties this to Marx's "commodity fetish" (the abstraction of the product out of the real conditions of its production) and to the growing divorce of signs from the things they stand for (pp. 170–71).
- Standard time zones (1883) were established, Carey says, because of the telegraph's power to send time faster than a railroad car could move. They laid a grid of time over space and extended commercial control to time itself (pp. 171–75).
- After space was filled, time became the "new frontier" (the sabbath, the night, the microsecond). The telegraph prefigures the computer (pp. 175–76).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Why the telegraph deserves study (pp. 155–157)
- Carey opens with Henry Adams. Adams dated Boston's entry into industrial America to May 1844 by the railroad, the Cunard steamers and telegraph news of the presidential nominations (p. 155).
- Carey says the telegraph is the least studied communications technology. Thompson's *Wiring a Continent* (1947) is old, ends in 1866 and concentrates on Western Union (p. 155).
- He gives four reasons the neglect is unfortunate (pp. 155–56):
  1. The telegraph was dominated by the first great industrial monopoly, Western Union. With the railroad, it was where modern management of complex enterprises was worked out. It also produced a major patent fight, the "telegraph war" over the quadruplex system, which Carey says Gould rightly prized as the "nerve of industry" (pp. 155–56).
  2. It was the foundation of the electrical goods industry, the first of the science- and engineering-based industries. As the first electrical engineering technology, it was the first to focus on the central problem of modern engineering: "the economy of a signal" (p. 156).
  3. It changed language, ordinary knowledge and "structures of awareness." Contemporaries argued about it in euphoric and pessimistic terms. Adams saw a demonic device, Thoreau a trivializer, and many others a force of spiritual, moral, economic and political improvement (p. 156).
  4. It was a watershed. Carey warns against overstating this: the telegraph "twisted and altered but did not displace" patterns set by geography. Yet it can stand "metaphorically" for the innovations that began modern American communication (p. 156).
- **Main thesis.** The most important fact is "at once the most obvious and innocent": it separated communication from transportation (p. 157).
  - Messages no longer needed physical movement. Communication could also control physical processes, as in railroad signaling (p. 157).
  - The telegraph reached the West Coast eight years before a transcontinental railroad, so the identity of the two ended "in both fact and symbol" (p. 157).
  - Before it, "communication" named both transport and message-sending. Afterwards, symbols moved independently of, and faster than, transportation (p. 157).
  - It also changed how communication was thought about: it gave Carey's "transmission model" and displaced older religious views, while being described in religious language. It was "a thing to think with" (p. 157).

*Intuition note.* Once a message no longer has to travel with a person or a cart, speed and distance are separate problems. Carey calls this split the simplest and most important point about the telegraph (p. 164).

### II. Three links between telegraph and ideology (pp. 157–62)
Carey would ideally show effects on space, time, language, concepts and a national commercial middle class. These effects were also displacements: older language, interactions and "city-state capitalism" declined (p. 157). He will concentrate on ideas, "broadly, ideology" (pp. 157–58).

**Link 1: monopoly capitalism (p. 158).**
- The telegraph was a new force of production. It demanded new law, economic theory, politics, management techniques and organization, plus rationales to justify a private monopoly (p. 158).
- Carey says it is poorly studied here, partly because "technology as such" is too abstract a category and is only sorted into stages of capitalism (p. 158).
- Before the telegraph, business was personal, coordinated by the market's "invisible hand." Volume and speed then required impersonal relations run through organizations. Chandler: "the visible hand of management" replaced the market's invisible hand where new technology and larger markets allowed (p. 158).
- Theory, law, common sense and religion carried these relations into explicit consciousness and "naturalized" them, making them seem merely the order of things (pp. 158–59).

**Link 2: popular religious imagery (pp. 159–62).**
- The telegraph was hailed as the "noiseless tenant of the wilderness." Its language is Leo Marx's "rhetoric of the technological sublime"; Carey and Quirk call it the "rhetoric of the electrical sublime" (p. 159).
- Carey's explanation: electricity was invisible yet powerful. This encouraged idealist thought, located vital energy in the nonmaterial realm, and seemed to connect the spiritual and material (p. 159).
- Voices from the period:
  - Rev. Gannett called electricity both a "swift winged messenger of destruction" and the "vital energy of material creation," brought under control "like a very slave" (p. 159).
  - Gardner Spring said we were on the "border of a spiritual harvest" (p. 159).
  - Morse (1838) foresaw channels of nerves making "one neighborhood of the whole country" (p. 160).
- Carey's reading: the telegraph fused the two poles of the electrical sublime, namely peace, harmony and self-sufficiency on one side and power, profit and productivity on the other. It promised to bind the country as the Civil War loomed (p. 159). Organic metaphors entered American thought here (p. 160). A doggerel poem to Morse (1856) mixes science, commerce, politics and religion (p. 160).
- Carey, citing Schivelbusch, finds a central tenet of middle-class ideology: communication and motion bring progress, and isolation is barbarism (p. 160). The eighteenth-century universalist ideal (the Brotherhood of Man) was powered by communication (p. 160).
- **Briggs and Maverick (1858):** the telegraph binds nations "by a vital cord," and old prejudices and hostilities cannot continue (p. 161).
- A quoted writer in Andrews (1857) is said by Carey to have "got the whole matter rather more correctly." The telegraph link to India would be a commercial success and would "consolidate British power" (p. 161).
- Carey's conclusion: an essentially religious view of communication (or one cloaked in religious metaphors) works as a "progressively vanishing mediator" between middle-class aspiration and capitalist, increasingly imperial, development. A vanishing mediator (from Jameson) carries change and can disappear once institutions ratify it (p. 161; n. 5, p. 177). Franklin, Weber's Protestant archetype, reappears as "the first electrician" (p. 161).
- Carey adds that technology came to be housed in engineering societies, universities and labs. Justifying ideologies became more consciously planned by the same groups (pp. 161–62).

*Intuition note.* On Carey's account, religious talk about universal brotherhood acted as a mediator between middle-class aspiration and capitalist, increasingly imperial, development, and a vanishing mediator disappears once institutions ratify the change (p. 161).

### III. Effects on ordinary ideas: language, journalism, empire, business (pp. 162–71)
Carey's method: study a few sites where effects are clearest, later to be tied to general theory (p. 162).

**Site 1: language and journalism (pp. 162–63).**
- Carey calls it an "old saw," one he has repeated himself, that the wire services broke the tradition of partisan journalism by forcing "objective" news usable by papers of any political stripe (p. 162). He says the issue is deeper.
- The services needed language stripped of the local, regional and colloquial: a near-"scientific" language of strict denotation, with connotation under control. For the same story to be understood from Maine to California, language "had to be flattened out and standardized" (p. 162).
- Hence, in Carey's account, the tall story, the hoax, much humor, irony and satire declined. They depended on the "fiduciary" use of symbols (p. 162). "The origins of objectivity may be sought, therefore, in the necessity of stretching language in space" (p. 162).
- The relation between writer and reader changed from fiduciary to "coordinated," like the mechanical coordination of buyer and seller (p. 162).
- Because words were expensive, the correspondent who analysed events gave way to the stringer who supplied bare facts. The story was reassembled at the far end of the wire (pp. 162–63).
- Every constraint was also an opportunity: "cablese" influenced Hemingway's spare style (p. 163).
- The flood of terse copy forced routinized news judgment and factory-like newsrooms. The reporter displaced the editor as archetypal journalist, and news was treated like a commodity, subject to "rates, contracts, franchising, discounts and thefts" (p. 163; Carey quotes Birkhead, n. 8).

**Site 2: empire (pp. 163–64).**
- Carey advises studying specific cases (naval coordination, war correspondents, early multinational business), not a grand theory of imperialism (p. 163).
- He says little attention has been paid to telegraphy as a ground condition of imperialism. The words "empire" and "imperialism" entered the language in 1870, soon after the transatlantic cable: "probably no accident" (p. 163).
- Colonies could be held by print, letters and sail, but loosely, since the margin had as much power as the centre (pp. 163–64). The cable and telegraph, "backed, of course, by sea power," turned colonialism into imperialism, a system where the centre can "dictate rather than merely respond" (p. 164).
- Heavy overseas investment waited for this control. The telegraph supplied "if not the absolute impetus" for expansion, at least the means to make it tenable. It also created a tension between the capability to expand and the capacity to rule (p. 164).
- Railroad, steam and telegraph formed a coherent communication system, with the telegraph as the "coordinating, regulating device" (p. 164).

**Site 3: humble telegraph shacks (p. 164).**
- Many commercial empires began in telegraph operators' shacks (Sears, Edison, Carnegie; Gould merged telegraph and railroad). The operator could monopolize information for a few moments. This mattered for trading, speculation, gambling, credit and mail-order merchandising (p. 164).

**Restating the thesis; pre-electric telegraphy (pp. 164–66).**
- Carey says "decisive separation" because there were earlier premonitions: line-of-sight (optical) telegraphs, such as telegraph hills (p. 165).
- Chappe's system came into practical use at the end of the eighteenth century in post-Revolutionary France. It was adopted as a national utility. It answered theorists who held a large country too big for a republic, and tied the departments to the centre (p. 165). In America a line opened between Martha's Vineyard and Boston in 1800, and the first practical use was reporting arriving ships (p. 165). Information was already being separated from cargo by shipping changes (p. 165).
- The theoretical significance is the telegraph as "both a model of and a mechanism for control" of physical movement. Information can be "a simulation of and control mechanism" for what it leaves behind (pp. 165–66).
- Railroad dispatching by telegraph began in England in 1844 and in the U.S. in 1849. Before it, the Boston and Worcester Railroad kept horses every five miles to warn engineers of collisions (p. 166). Carey draws a link to systems theory: "the system is the solution." The integrated switched system matters more than its parts (p. 166).
- The telegraph made possible a "thoroughly encephalated social nervous system," with signaling divorced from musculature. Carey argues that actual practice (telegraph and railroad), more than German idealism, explains the nineteenth-century obsession with organic metaphors (p. 166).
- Like most communication innovations down to the computer, the telegraph was first a producer good (for commerce, government and military), then a consumer good (p. 166).

**Commodity markets (pp. 166–71).**
- *Before.* Early nineteenth-century Americans expected prices to differ from city to city, since markets were largely independent (pp. 166–67).
- *Arbitrage* (buying cheap and selling dear by moving goods) is then the main trading method. If it is widely practiced, prices should settle so that the price difference equals the transport cost. This presumes classical economics' "perfect information," rarely approached before the telegraph (p. 167).
- *Evidence.* Cole's data for 1816–1842 (to the eve of the telegraph): average annual price disparity fell from 9.3 to 4.8 and average monthly disparity from 15.4 to 4.8 (p. 167). Carey reads this as the effect of canals and turnpikes, probably understated by Cole's grouping of years. "The railroad and canal regionalized markets; the telegraph nationalized them" (p. 167).
- *Effect.* The telegraph evens out markets in space and "puts everyone in the same place for purposes of trade." Apart from marginal exceptions, it eliminates arbitrage by realizing perfect information (pp. 167–68).
- *Shift.* Speculation moves "from space to time, from arbitrage to futures." The arbitrageur trades Cincinnati for St. Louis; the futures trader sells August against October (p. 168). Chicago's exchange opened in 1848, the year the telegraph reached the city, "not, then, mere historic accident." Carey says that "in a certain sense the telegraph invented the future as a new zone of uncertainty" (p. 168).
- *Retreat (Carey's qualifications).* He says he overdrew the case. (1) Arbitrage is never fully eliminated, because information is always imperfect, as at auctions. (2) Time contracts came in between. An approximation of futures trading occurred as early as 1733, when the East India Company began trading warrants, which transferred ownership without physical transfer. Warrant trading advanced when information moved by steamer ahead of cotton shipped by sail; on news of the crop and samples, "to-arrive" contracts were executed, used mainly for transatlantic sales and widely used in Chicago in the 1840s (p. 168).
- *Telegraph effect.* By spreading price and crop information, it drew markets together. Cincinnati lagged Eastern markets by two years in the 1820s, four months by 1840, and was instantaneous by 1857 "and probably much earlier." Once space was "annihilated," time opened to commerce (p. 169). Carey admits there are no good before-and-after measures (p. 169).
- *Telephone parallel.* In 1894 the telephone made information time identical in major cities and business drifted from New York to Boston. The New York exchange banned telephones on the floor, relaying prices by messenger. This gave a thirty-second knowledge monopoly and drew business back (p. 169).
- *Three consequences of futures trading (pp. 169–70):*
  1. Markets were *decontextualized*: unresponsive to local supply and demand. Carey says they became "everywhere" and "everytime" markets, "less apprehensible at the very moment they became more powerful" (p. 169).
  2. The commodity was *sundered from its representation.* Warehouse receipts were traded independently of goods. The trade is "time against price," and the receipt "has no intrinsic relation to the real product" (p. 170).
  3. Goods are sold by *grade,* not inspection. Products must be mixed and standardized, so not all products suit futures markets. Carey quotes Chandler: "high volume sales required impersonalized standards" (p. 170).
- Futures required: information faster than goods, uniform prices in space, decontextualized markets, receipts separate from commodities, and uniform grades (p. 170).
- *Marx and Benjamin.* Carey says these conditions underlay Marx's commodity fetish (developed in the late 1850s, when futures dominated agricultural pricing). Its elements are decontextualized markets, use value separated from exchange value, and abstraction by grading (p. 170). Marx (Grundrisse) calls the movement of the product to market its transformation into a commodity (pp. 170–71). Carey links this to Benjamin's "loss of aura." The commodity form masks real conditions of production from the buyer. The receipt–product divorce is part of the divorce of signifier from signified (p. 171).
- *Summary.* Communication in the nineteenth century diminished space as a differentiating criterion. Innis's "penetrative powers of the price system" spread a uniform price system, with the telegraph the critical instrument. The correlate is Stravinsky's "statisticalization of mind": the world turned into quantity (p. 171).

*Intuition note.* If everyone knows prices everywhere at once, there is little profit in carrying goods between towns. Speculation then moves to time, as Carey describes. This shifts trade onto paper claims and standard grades rather than real goods.

### IV. Standard time (pp. 171–75)
- Standard time in the U.S. began on November 18, 1883. Before that, communities set noon by the sun (p. 171). Carey cites local time-keeping by church, courthouse, jeweler or stationmaster, and the dropped ball in Kansas City (p. 172).
- Island communities had little cause to worry about differences. As commerce and politics spread, "temporal chaos" came (p. 172). Noon came four minutes later for each degree westward; 13 miles is about one minute (p. 172). Examples: when it was noon in Boston it was 11:48 in Albany. When it was 12:00 in Washington, it was 11:30 in Atlanta, 12:24 in Boston and 12:41 in Eastport (p. 172). Michigan had 27 local times, Wisconsin 39 (p. 172).
- Railroads used fifty-eight local times, keyed to different cities, causing scheduling confusion, accidents and irritation (p. 172).
- Standard time came earlier in Europe, where pressure was less (England has only half an hour of sun-time spread). But England needed it for empire, since time control allows coordination and "effective social control." Examples: ships on Greenwich time; Irish clocks set by Big Ben via rail and ferry (p. 173).
- Charles Dowd (1870) proposed 24 one-hour zones centred 15° apart from Greenwich (15° = one hour). Carey calls it "crackpot realism" and "the grid is the geometry of empire." Maine and Florida would be in separate zones (p. 173).
- Railroads adopted Dowd's plan provisionally but Congress would not, for what Carey calls "an obvious reason": religious offense at violating the natural order. Religious language was again "a vanishing mediator" for political feeling. Standard time was "Vanderbilt's time," and protest was part of populist protest against banks, telegraph and railroad (p. 173).
- William F. Allen's plan (1881–83): zones adjustable up to 100 miles for economy and ecology, and no minute index. The U.S. was divided into four zones centred on the 75th, 90th, 105th and 120th meridians, each extending 7.5° either side (pp. 173–74).
- Changeover: Chicago was tied by telegraph to an Allegheny observatory. Its clocks were held for 9 min 32 s until the sun reached the 90th meridian (p. 174). Mass meetings, anger and religious protest "to no avail." Within a few months, local communities' switches to it were well under way. It became official only in World War I (p. 174). The U.S. did not adopt 24-hour time (p. 174).
- Boundaries have since been adjusted, and Carey suggests the television networks would prefer three zones (p. 174).
- Conclusion: the zones were established through the telegraph's power. Time could be sent faster than a railcar moved, so the whole system could be coordinated. This let industry and government coordinate activity, enter practical consciousness and "uproot older notions of rhythm and temporality" (pp. 174–75). The time grid is like the surveyor's grid of space and is used to control activity within it (p. 175).

### V. Time as the new frontier (pp. 175–76)
- Once space was filled as an arena of commerce, time became the frontier, "a continuation of space in another dimension" (p. 175).
- *Sabbath.* Carey uses it in a "fully secular sense": a region free of state and commerce, a major resistance to state and market power. It was penetrated by the Sunday newspaper in the 1880s, with Hearst's *New York Sunday World* creating a "sabbath market" (p. 175).
- *Night.* After the spatial frontier was officially closed in 1890, night became the frontier. Carey cites Melbin (1987), and cites the growth of all-night broadcasting. In Boston there were no 24-hour radio stations from 1918 through 1954, and now half operate all night (p. 175). Carey says "night as frontier" is a metaphor but more than that. Melbin lists shared features of spatial and temporal frontiers: stages of advance, sparse and homogeneous population, solitude, fewer social constraints, isolated settlement, decentralized government, more lawlessness and more friendliness, new styles of behavior (pp. 175–76). The same dialectic of centralization and decentralization appears. Communication is more privatized at night, and constraints relax (p. 176).
- *Computer.* Computers extend this: time is now an "ecological niche" filled to the nanosecond and picosecond, "a level at which time can be pictured but not experienced." The telegraph prefigures this. It built a "simulacrum of complex systems," an analogue model of the railroad and a digital model of language, coordinating activity "often behind the backs of those subject to it" (p. 176).
- *Close.* E. P. Thompson finds it ominous that the young Henry Ford made a watch with two dials (local and railroad time), and says attention to time in labour depends largely on the need for synchronization. The retiree receiving a watch is "grotesque and comic," since he gets it when the need to tell time is over. The watch coordinated the factory; the telegraph, through the time grid, coordinated the industrial nation. The microcomputer is replacing the watch as the retiree gift. Carey sees here "the deeper relationship between technology and ideology" (p. 176).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Wheat price in Town A is 90 cents/bushel and in Town B 100 cents. Moving a bushel costs 4 cents.
1. *Arbitrage, imperfect information.* A trader buys at 90, ships for 4, sells at 100. Profit = 100 − 90 − 4 = 6 cents per bushel.
2. As traders do this, A's price rises and B's falls. Profit vanishes when the gap equals 4. For example, A = 94 and B = 98 give 98 − 94 − 4 = 0.
3. *With a telegraph.* Both towns see each other's prices at once. The gap is held to 4 quickly, so spatial profit is at most about 0 and geography matters little (Carey's "everyone in the same place for purposes of trade").
4. *Time.* Now a trader sees August wheat at 98 and October wheat at 105 (assumed prices). The trader sells October at 105 and buys August at 98. The "price gap" is between times (7 cents), not places. This is Carey's shift from space to time.
5. *Time zones.* Two towns 3° apart in longitude differ by 3 × 4 = 12 minutes in local noon (Carey's 4 minutes per degree, p. 172). A shared telegraphed time lets railroad timetables agree.

## Concepts

- [[separation-of-communication-from-transportation]] (new) — telegraph's key effect: symbols move independently of, and faster than, goods and people (p. 157).
- [[electrical-sublime]] (new) — religious and utopian rhetoric that greeted telegraph and electricity; it joins peace and harmony with power and profit (p. 159).

- [[fiduciary-use-of-language]] (new) — trust-based, traditional symbolic use (hoax, irony, satire) that "objective" wire language displaced; Carey points to chapter 1 for it, and these pages do not define it (p. 162; n. 6).

- [[standard-time]] (new) — a grid of time zones that coordinates activity across space; adopted by railroads in 1883 (p. 174).
- [[practical-consciousness]] (new) — Carey's term for ordinary ideas and common sense, where technology's ideological effects are sought (p. 162).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Telegraph as control of railroad ↔ Feedback (cybernetics): information steering a physical process; Carey himself links it to servo mechanisms and systems theory (p. 166).
- [structural-analogy] Transmission model from telegraph ↔ Transmission view: Carey says the telegraph supplied this model of communication (p. 157).
- [metaphor] "Economy of a signal" ↔ Information (Shannon): both concern efficient use of a channel, but Carey gives no mathematics (p. 156).

## Questions this raises
- Carey says the telegraph made time a new frontier, yet he calls the telegraph's role a "watershed" and warns against overemphasis. How far can a technology's role be claimed without a before-and-after measurement (p. 169)?
- If objectivity partly came from the cost and needs of wire language, how much of journalistic "objectivity" is technology and how much is professional ideology (p. 162)?
- Carey's religious "vanishing mediator" is said to fade once institutions take over. What replaces religious language for later technologies, such as the computer (pp. 161, 176)?
- Which other communication technologies, in Carey's terms, separate signals from objects, and what do they make into a "frontier"?
