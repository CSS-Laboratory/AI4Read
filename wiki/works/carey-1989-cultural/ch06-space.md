# Space, Time, and Communications: A Tribute to Harold Innis

## L0 — One line
Carey argues that Harold Innis, by showing that every communication medium is biased toward controlling space or time, corrected the Chicago School's romantic faith in communication technology and gave a historical, critical theory of communication.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey asks what Harold Innis contributed to North American communication theory. His answer: Innis corrected the Chicago School's hope that technology would turn the "great society" into a "great community." Innis argued historically that each medium has a bias toward space or toward time, and that biases harden into monopolies of knowledge. In Carey's account, US policy aimed solely at spreading messages further in space. This centralized power in New York, privatized the public, and weakened the oral tradition. Carey offers Innis's historical, critical approach as an alternative to the behaviorist and therapy models. He applies it to electronics and satellites, where Innis's remedy is to support oral tradition, time and a public sphere.

**Prerequisites:** basic US and Canadian history (frontier, railroads, newspapers); the idea of a monopoly (control of supply by one source); the idea of "public opinion."

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Carey reads the Chicago School (Dewey, Mead, Park, Cooley) as the origin of US communication research. Its strengths were a wide view of communication as culture-making and a concern for the public. Its weakness was a romantic belief in technology as a unifying force (pp. 110–111).
- Later US research split into a behaviorist "power" model (mind plays no part in behavior) and a "therapy" model drawn from the Hawthorn factory experiments. Carey says these were also models *for* social practice, and that social scientists thereby became a "priestly" class (pp. 112–113).
- Innis worked historically, interdisciplinarily and critically. He distrusted universal theory because scholarship reflects its place of origin (pp. 113–115).
- Innis argued that every frontier has a "back tier", an outside centre whose economy and strategic position the frontier's products serve: first Europe, later North American metropolitan centres. Communication in the US built a New York-centred pattern of central city and dependent hinterland ("metropole–hinterland") that Carey says persists (pp. 115–119).
- Media have a bias toward controlling space (light, portable media) or time (heavy, durable or oral media). Cultures can be space-binding (interested in land, expansion, control) or time-binding (interested in history and continuity), and more of one means less of the other (pp. 122–124).
- Innis read the First Amendment as protecting a technology and its monopoly. Carey reports that, in this line of thought, commercial print media privatized reading, turned the public into an audience, and wore away elements of permanence (pp. 124–129).
- Monopolies of knowledge can grow only where some division of labor makes people dependent on an outside supply of knowledge. Advancing "knowledge" can go with declining "knowing" (pp. 128–129).
- Electronics repeats the imperial pattern of early print. Innis's remedy is to support the oral tradition, time and a public sphere against the spatial bias (pp. 130–132).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 109)
- Carey says that, in the third quarter of the century, North American communication theory (or at least its most interesting part) could be described as an arc from Innis to McLuhan. He adds Oscar Wilde's joke that it would be "more impressive" if it ran the other way (p. 109).
- Carey calls Innis's work, despite its obscure and elliptical style, "the great achievement in communications on this continent." It offers "permanent riddles rather than easy formulas" and is "things to think with" (p. 109).
- Innis also had a moral stance: he opposed the cold war and defended the university against use as an instrument of state or market power (p. 109).
- Carey says his interest is Innis's place in North American, especially US, communication theory (p. 109).

### I. The Chicago School, Innis, and the shape of American communication
**The Chicago School (pp. 110–111).**
- Communication research began as a cumulative tradition in the late 1880s at Ann Arbor with five people: Dewey, Mead, Park, Cooley, and the journalist Franklin Ford. Ford shared with Dewey the belief that "a proper daily newspaper would be the only possible social science" (p. 110).
- Spencer's organic view of society, with its nerves and arteries, matched the parallel growth of telegraph and railroad. In this picture communication control was divorced from the physical movement of people and things (p. 110).
- The group hoped technology could turn, in Dewey's terms, the "great society" made by industry into a "great community." Carey says this belief belongs to an unbroken tradition of thought on communications technology that Leo Marx named, and Carey appropriated, the "rhetoric of the technological sublime" (p. 110).
- Three features of the School (p. 110–111):
  1. Methodologically they revolted against formalism and treated social study as a branch of history.
  2. They held a version of the frontier hypothesis. For them the frontier mattered because strangers built community institutions *de novo* in small western towns. With no inherited tradition, communication had to be the source of social order (pp. 110–111).
  3. They stressed public life. They conceived communication as the whole process by which a culture is created, maintained in time, and sedimented into institutions, "in the envelope of" art, architecture, custom, ritual and politics. They took the public to be created by the printing press (agreeing with Tarde). They went on to ask when it yields rational, critical discourse (p. 111).
- In the 1920s this produced worry about the "vanishing" or "eclipse" of the public (Dewey, 1927). Many came to see that mass media brought the public into existence but later threatened public life (p. 111).

**Innis and the School (p. 111).**
- Innis studied at Chicago when Park and Mead taught there. Carey calls McLuhan's claim that Innis was the most eminent member of Park's group "an absurdity." Park had no direct influence on Innis, whose courses were narrow, mostly political economy (p. 111).
- **Carey's claim:** Innis took up the School's concerns, corrected and completed them, widened their range, and purged their romanticism. The result was a historically grounded theory and a more adequate view of communications in American life (p. 111).

**What followed in US research (pp. 111–113).**
- Chicago sociology became symbolic interactionism (a social psychology of self and others from Mead). Carey says this was tucked away from questions of politics, power and social change (p. 112).
- *Influence 1: behaviorism* (Watson, drawing on Thorndike). Mind played no part in organizing behavior. Communication became a branch of learning theory, governed by conditioning and reinforcement. Carey says the price was removing the possibility of rational action. With World War II research, this yielded a power or domination model of communication (p. 112).
- *Influence 2: the Hawthorn experiments.* The "Hawthorn effect" was that productivity rose because of the experiments themselves. The experiments presumably tested a Durkheim-derived view of the factory as an integrated system. Their lesson was communication as therapy: counselors resocialized workers to their grievances (p. 112).
- Lazarsfeld and his students joined these strands. Communication study became a subdiscipline of social psychology, with two models: communication as domination (people seek power) and as therapy (people flee anxiety) (pp. 112–113).
- **Carey's point:** these were models *of* communication and also models *for* its enactment, that is, of actual social practice. He says social scientists shifted from a prophetic to a priestly class, absorbed into "the apparatus of rule" (p. 113).
- A recurrent cultural pattern in North America reads technology as part of a narrative of progress: more information, moving faster, ends ignorance and strife and brings a beneficent future. Carey calls this account a sketch and presents it as the background against which Innis should be assessed (p. 113).

**Intuition note.** Carey is saying that how a field models communication also shapes how people practise it. Carey presents Innis's historical and critical approach (p. 115) as an alternative to the "progress" story (see p. 128).

**Innis's approach (pp. 113–115).**
- Carey sets out a general point: scholarship is adapted to its time and place, either unconsciously or openly. He quotes Marx's description of North American society (as seen through the economist H. C. Carey). Marx describes a country where bourgeois society did not grow out of feudalism, where the state was subordinate to that society from the start, and where even its antitheses appear "only as vanishing moments" (pp. 113–114).
- Innis accepted the ethnocentric bias of social science. He held that American and British scholarship pretended to find Universal Truth, Law and Man. He judged that these resembled the Cambridge (Massachusetts or England) type, what Congress and Parliament found useful, and English and American accents. Carey adds that imperial powers, "so it seems", seek intellectual clients as well as economic ones (p. 114).
- He worked as geographer, historian, economist and political scientist at once. He freed communications from social psychology and natural-science models and sought to restore a historical model of analysis. His terms were "made-in-the-kitchen" concepts for the historical record, not a verification model: technological limits, spatial and temporal bias, monopolies of knowledge, social change, selective advantage, cultural stability and collapse (pp. 114–115).
- Like Geddes, he held that intellectual universals can be reached only through radical particularities of history and geography. His polarities were metropole/hinterland, center/margin, capital/periphery, or, most abstractly, time and space (p. 115).
- Carey calls his work historical (testing the limits of theory), empirical (the actual record rather than "ironclad laws of development"), interpretive (people's varying definitions of experience) and critical (a standpoint from which to judge society by humane values) (p. 115).

**The frontier has a back tier (pp. 115–116).**
- Innis argued against frontier hypotheses "so gratifyingly isolationist" that the source of action was at the periphery rather than the centre. Carey sums up: "Every frontier, in short, has a back tier." The back tier's interest was determined by how far frontier products strengthened its economy, supplemented rather than competed with its products, and enhanced its strategic position (Heaton, 1966) (p. 115).
- The first back tier was Europe. Later the back tier moved to North American metropolitan centers, with effective control shifting toward New York and Washington. Innis's studies of paper and pulp showed that mechanized communication creates new types of empire (p. 116).
- He wrote that US mechanized communication and organized force sponsored "a new type of imperialism" in which sovereignty is kept *de jure* and used to expand imperialism *de facto* (Innis, 1950: 215). Carey says Innis thus founded media-imperialism studies, but more subtly, since he knew of tensions and accommodations between partners (p. 116).
- This let him pierce the Chicago School's organic metaphors. Carey says that even if society were like an organism, there would be some centralized brain, some group collecting the power to direct the nerves of communication and the arteries of transportation. So technology would not transform the great society into a great community, only enable monopoly of knowledge by particular groups (p. 116).

**The New York pattern (pp. 116–118).**
- Innis saw a dialectic of decentralization and recentralization: hinterland communities try to outrun the metropolis and are later absorbed (p. 116).
- Before the Revolution, messages moved east–west between London and the colonies, which communicated with each other via London. After the Revolution news was mostly European and port cities spoke more with England than with their hinterland (pp. 116–117).
- After 1812, "internal improvements" were presented as binding the nation. In fact the colonial pattern returned with New York replacing London. Carey, citing Schlesinger, calls it city-state imperialism (p. 117).
- New York's hegemony rested on the Hudson, the Erie Canal and the Great Lakes route to Chicago, which let it "service and drain" the Mississippi Valley. Philadelphia (canals, which brought Pennsylvania near bankruptcy), Baltimore (national highway, B&O railroad) and Boston (railroads, though blocked by New York) also tried to win the interior (p. 117).
- Pred's studies document that New York drew hinterland cities into its information field and isolated other East Coast cities. The Illinois Central ("great St. Louis cut-off") and the northern transcontinental route strengthened it. The South, apart from New Orleans, was isolated and routed communication through New York (pp. 117–118).
- Carey says the outline persists despite telegraph, wire services, films, broadcasting and jets. The dominant eastern corridor has since 1800 produced an "effective monopoly of knowledge in news and entertainment." It undercut local culture and hid how provincial the "national" culture was. Local issues matter only if turned into national issues, and news and entertainment grow more "slick and abstract" (p. 118).

**Resistance, and the same pattern at smaller scales (pp. 118–119).**
- Innis also saw that localities resisted: conflicts over standard time, mail-order houses, parcel post, rural free delivery, and department stores. Western newspapers resisted the metropolis, and the telegraph at first strengthened local presses until wire services and chains undercut them. Spread of the spatial bias was uneven, "a complicated interplay of resistance and acceptance" (pp. 118–119).
- Carey says the pattern recurs in cities and counties (Mandelbaum on New York City; Carey's own studies at regional and county level) (p. 119).

**The "high communications policy" (p. 119).**
- **Carey's term:** a policy aimed solely at spreading messages further in space and cutting transmission costs. Communication was seen only in the "envelope of space and power." The idea of it as a container of interaction that lets culture persist never entered policy. This power/container distinction parallels Innis's space/time distinction (p. 119).
- Carey says universities were also infected: economics, political science, planning, sociology and physical science chart problems in space. Social science made the future a frontier to conquer, and historians used time as a container for a story of progress (p. 119).
- Summary: effective units of culture shifted from local to national and international. Individuals were linked without local structures, and local communication mattered less. Long-distance communication cultivated new structures of thought (national classes and professions), new things thought about (speed, space, movement), and new things to think with (more abstract, manipulative symbols) (p. 119).

**Intuition note.** In Carey's reading, a communication system built only for speed and reach is not neutral. It routes power through a few centers and weakens local life.

### II. Staples, print, and the space–time bias
**The fur trade and empire (pp. 120–121).**
- Innis's dissertation on the Canadian Pacific railroad found that it overlay old fur-trade routes. This led to staples (fish, furs, timber, pulp) and to *The Fur Trade in Canada* (1930) (p. 120).
- There he rejected German formal stages and Turner's frontier hypothesis. He argued that North America was an extension of European power and politics. He named three bands: the Canadian North, the American South tied to England by staples, and the mixed American North between them (p. 120).
- Controlling factors were "harder facts": the biology of beavers, staples in trade, and unused capacity on trade routes. Contact between oral Indian cultures and literate Europe shattered Indian culture through dependence on European goods. Carey says the book is more a portrait of the Europeanization of North America than of its particularism (p. 120).
- Two ideas followed: (1) what let European power move beyond Europe, and (2) that communication might be the basic staple in the growth of empire (pp. 120–121).
- On the first: the central impulse was improved communications: fast ships, navigation, and above all printing. Writing and printing were first used in administration, tax and war (Clanchy) (p. 121).
- Print did two things. It centralized authority (uniform law, standard vernacular, uniform education, central administration). It also permitted decentralization through portable, reproducible documents, so that trading and settlement companies could be directed from a distance. Print plus navigation "burst the bonds of geography" (p. 121).
- Print had limits. The French empire was thin and held by force. Weak communication let federalism develop in the American colonies. Control from London became possible only in the nineteenth century, "but by then history had turned a corner" (p. 121).

**Pulp and paper; the "Canadian double bind" (p. 122).**
- American newspaper growth (the "penny press") raised demand for Canadian pulp and paper. Canada was a staple economy, with decisions made in London, New York and Washington (p. 122).
- The US imported raw material under "freedom of trade," then exported finished newspapers, books, magazines and advertising back under "freedom of information." Canada was caught "between the scissors" of American demand for paper and American supply of printed goods (p. 122).

**Space-binding and time-binding (pp. 122–123).**
- Innis described modern Western history as running from temporal to spatial organization. Oral and manuscript tradition, with community, morals and metaphysics, gave way to print and electronics with a bias toward space (p. 122).
- Changes in communication technology alter interests (things thought about), symbols (things thought with) and community (the arena of thought) (p. 122).
- *Space-binding culture:* interests in land, voyage, expansion, empire and control. Symbols were the physics of space, navigation, the price system and "affectless, rational" symbols. Communities were mobile, connected across distance (pp. 122–123).
- *Time-binding culture:* interests in history, continuity and permanence. Symbols were fiduciary (oral, mythopoetic, religious, ritual). Communities were rooted in place (p. 123).
- Innis thought good social policy serves both, "to use historicism to check the dreams of reason and … reason to control the passions of memory." The two are reciprocal: more time-binding means less space-binding, and conversely (p. 123).
- Space-binding media are light and portable. Time-binding media are heavy and durable, or, like oral tradition, persistent. Carey's summary: "structures of consciousness parallel structures of communication" (p. 123).

**Beard and horizontal cleavage (pp. 123–124).**
- Beard picked 1896 because the party conventions added horizontal (class) cleavages to existing vertical (sectional) ones (Beard, 1914: 164 quoted). Carey notes this is when a national communication system (news services, national magazines, rural free delivery, mail order) emerged (p. 123).
- Local communities had a class structure, but it showed class variations on a common culture (vertical divisions within communities). Long-distance communication created national class-factions. Progressivism produced not Dewey's great community but Wiebe's "segmented society" of horizontal communities that Riesman called "veto groups." Carey says there was little relation among these segments except the exercise of power and manipulation (p. 124).
- **Expectation (Carey, after Beard):** if communication is effective over short distances and weak over long ones, we would expect units of culture and politics to be grounded in place, with small spatial differences producing great differences in culture and interests, and larger units to be federal rather than national. As long-distance communication improves, local and federal relations "evaporate" into a stratified national community. As long-distance communication improves and short-distance deteriorates, we would expect relationships to shift to a horizontal dimension (p. 124).

**Intuition note.** The better messages travel far, the more people tie themselves to distant groups of their own class or profession, and the less to neighbors of other classes.

### III. Freedom of the press, the oral tradition, and monopolies of knowledge
**The First Amendment (pp. 124–125).**
- Innis stressed paradox. He argued that the First Amendment protected technology more than free speech, so it restricted freedom (p. 125). In his words, press freedom "provided bulwarks for monopolies which have emphasized control over space," leaving duration "neglected, indeed obliterated"; time was cut "into pieces the length of a day's newspaper" (Innis, 1954) (p. 125).
- Carey reports that it consolidated newspaper monopoly and, through dependence on advertising and news, a "one-day world." It replaced the right of people to speak to and inform each other with an abstract right to be spoken to and informed, especially by professional classes (p. 125).
- Innis refused the notion that democracy rises with capital invested in communication, and hoped to restore "the political power of the foot and the tongue" (p. 125).

**Plato and the oral tradition (pp. 125–127).**
- Innis identified the oral tradition with the Greeks and Plato's attack on writing in the *Phaedrus* (writing gives a "recipe not for memory but for reminder") (pp. 125–126).
- Carey glosses two objections. Writing is inherently shallow in its effects, because reading books may give a specious sense of knowledge that can really be attained only by oral question and answer; and essential principles of truth can be arrived at only dialectically. Knowledge goes deep only when "written in the soul of the learner" (p. 126).
- **Innis:** improved communication makes understanding harder: cable widened the gap between English and American, and cinema and radio created classes of readers with little prospect of communication between them. Mechanization of knowledge brings "monopolies of language which prevent understanding" (Innis, 1951) (p. 126).
- Phillpotts (1931, quoted): print makes knowledge easy to avoid. An Icelandic shepherd could not avoid the farmer's evening literature, giving a "really national culture" (p. 126). Carey's gloss: literate culture is easily avoided and its effects may be shallow, so we fall prey to experts who "do our knowing for us" (pp. 126–127).

**Privatization and the public (p. 127).**
- **Carey:** Innis held that democracy presupposes a public sphere and an oral tradition of public discourse as a counterweight to print. The First Amendment instead put educational weight on the written tradition (p. 127).
- Commercially driven media created an essentially private system. Private reading and reading audiences replaced the reading public. Carey says it was privatization more than the Bill of Rights that led to the decline of censorship, quoting Innis on the decline of reading aloud (p. 127).
- Carey says the public becomes "a mere statistical artifact," public taste a measure of private opinion "not realized in discourse." Thus the public sphere goes into eclipse (p. 127).
- Innis's later project was to restore the oral tradition: skills of memory, speech and argument, and an institutional home for them. He agreed with Dewey that speech is the agency of creative thought and printing of dissemination, and he sought to correct the imbalance (pp. 127–128).
- **Carey:** the purpose was modern, to show that mechanical communication does not necessarily expand freedom and knowledge. "Freedom of the press could suppress freedom of expression" (p. 128).

**Bias and monopoly (pp. 128–129).**
- Any form has a bias: it is best at reducing signaling time and controlling space, or at strengthening collective memory and controlling time. A bias hardens into monopoly when groups control the form and identify their interests with it (p. 128).
- Carey states the economic logic: monopoly is control of supply by a single source. Knowledge can be monopolized only with some division of labor, because people must depend on an outside supply. In Innis's view, commercialism transferred control from person and community to the price system (p. 128).
- Speech is a natural capacity and hard to monopolize. With writing, mathematics, printing and photography, there are producers and consumers of knowledge, and knowledge moves into special institutions. In extreme form there is a "knowledge industry" certifying what counts as knowledge (p. 128).
- Innis argued that modern communication enlarges the range of reception while narrowing the points of distribution. He wrote that monopolies of communication involve "a continuous, systematic, ruthless destruction of elements of permanence" (1952: 15) (p. 129).
- Carey says Innis claimed more than increasing media concentration. "Information," "entertainment" and the knowledge to produce them concentrate in elites and institutions, dividing the civic landscape into knowledgeable elites and ignorant masses. People become dependent consumers of communication (p. 129).
- Result, in Carey's wording: if "monopolistic" is too strong, oligopolistic structures expropriate widespread skills. Given a network of such monopolies backed by corporate economic and political power, there is simultaneously "advancing knowledge and declining knowing." The apparatus generates change and obsolescence, "time is destroyed, the right to tradition is lost" (p. 129).

**Intuition note.** The worry is not just that a few own the media. Many people lose the habit of producing knowledge in conversation and wait to be informed.

### IV. Electronics, satellites, and Innis's remedy
- Carey says satellites, cable, computer utilities and multinationals pose Innis's questions anew, but responses lack Innis's scope. He lists dystopian tracts, mechanical-versus-electrical analyses, socialist-versus-capitalist analyses, technocrats who claim technology will solve every problem, and utopians of the "global village" and "spaceship earth." Most of this, in his words, is "pleasant if not dangerous nonsense" (pp. 129–130).
- **Carey's alternative:** we are seeing another increase in scale of social organization, the imperial struggle of early print again but with messages at the limits of physics. Larger federations (Soviet bloc, Common Market, North America) and multinationals, which could not exist without jets, computers and electronic communication, are building a new culture. Sampson's ITT managers are quoted: phones "abolish distance," and company differences matter more than nationality (p. 130).
- There is also decentralization. Satellites push cultures into new regions of space, and direct broadcasting lets the two largest electronic powers enlarge their influence (p. 131).
- The US–Soviet rivalry extends to space itself. Carey says the delay in space exploration was not rocket thrust but the lack of communication to control travel from earth. "As printing went with seagoing navigation and the telegraph with the railway, electronic and computer-based communication go with the space ship" (p. 131).
- The meaning of electronic communication is "not in the news that informs us or the entertainment that distracts us" but in making space a domain of political competition (p. 131).
- Carey says electronics, like early print, is biased toward a "powerhouse society" of wealth, power, productivity, "technical perfectionism and ethical nihilism." Only politics and daily maintenance of a contradictory pattern of life, thought and scholarship would reverse this, not rhetoric (p. 131).
- Innis's remedy: cut back the influence of modern technics and cultivate art, ethics and politics. The oral tradition (dialogue, dialectics, ethics, metaphysics) is the counterweight. This needs stability, controlled mobility, and communities freed from "the blinding obsolescence of technical change" (pp. 131–132).
- Growth, empire and technology instead favor media that foster administrative efficiency, print and electronics. Only by supporting "substantive rationality, democracy, and time" would technology's bias be controlled. Innis summarized this in "A Plea for Time" (p. 132).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Five cities: one hub H (like New York) and four others A, B, C, D. Each pair of cities exchanges one message. Compare two networks.

- **Hub-and-spoke (colonial pattern: all via the hub).** Links: H–A, H–B, H–C, H–D = 4 links. Pairs: $\binom{5}{2} = 10$.
  - Pairs including H: 4 (travel 1 hop).
  - Pairs among A–D: $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ (travel 2 hops, via H).
  - Every one of the 10 messages passes through H, so the hub sees 10 out of 10.
  - Total hops: $4\times 1 + 6\times 2 = 16$.
- **Fully connected.** Links: 10. Total hops: $10\times 1 = 10$. Each non-hub city can bypass H, so H sees only its own 4 messages (4/10).

The hub network is cheaper to build (4 links against 10) but concentrates all information with H. This illustrates, in miniature, the kind of centralization Carey describes in the New York pattern (pp. 116–118); the numbers are ours.

## Concepts

- [[public-sphere]] — the space of discussion and argument that Innis said democracy presupposes; eclipsed by privatized reading and audiences (p. 127).
- [[bias-of-communication]] (new) — each medium is best at either controlling space or strengthening time and memory (p. 128).

- [[monopoly-of-knowledge]] (new) — a bias hardens into monopoly when groups control the medium and identify their interests with it (p. 128).

- [[high-communications-policy]] (new) — Carey's term for policy aimed only at spreading messages further and cheaper (p. 119).

- [[segmented-society]] (new) — Wiebe's term: horizontal communities tied across space as "veto groups" (p. 124).
- [[technological-sublime]] (new) — Leo Marx's term, adopted by Carey: rhetoric that technology will unify a continental community (p. 110).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Innis's space-binding vs. time-binding ↔ communication-theory: transmission view vs. ritual view — Carey ties his power/container distinction to Innis's space/time (p. 119).
- [structural-analogy] Innis's monopolies of knowledge and "audience" replacing "public" ↔ communication-theory: public sphere and its decline — both concern a public that is reduced to a consuming audience.
- [metaphor] The hub-and-spoke New York pattern ↔ network science: centralization of information flow — the toy example is only a metaphor for Carey's historical account.
- [structural-analogy] Carey's "models of and for" communication ↔ social science generally: theories that shape the practice they describe.

## Questions this raises
- Is Carey right that the 1896 and later horizontal "veto groups" came mainly from long-distance communication, or could other causes explain them?
- Carey says Innis's New York hub pattern persists. How could one measure this today with message-flow data?
- What would a modern institutional "home" for the oral tradition look like, as Innis's remedy requires (p. 127)?
- Does Carey's attack on the "global village" literature (p. 130) still apply to current media?
