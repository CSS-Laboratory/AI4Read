# Reconceiving “Mass” and “Media”

## L0 — One line
Carey argues that Dewey's view of communication as a conversation that constitutes a shared reality should replace Lippmann's view of communication as the transmission of accurate pictures, and that reality is a scarce resource, so the power to define and display it is a basic form of power.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey asks how scholars should think about the mass media. He argues that the deepest divide is between objectivism (reality is neutral fact to be pictured) and expressivism (reality is made by human action). He reads the 1920s Lippmann–Dewey exchange through this divide. Lippmann treats communication as the transmission of correct pictures; Dewey treats it as conversation in which a public forms. Carey sides with Dewey, with reservations. He proposes a cultural approach: communication constitutes, maintains and transforms reality, and reality is a scarce resource, so the power to define and display it is the fundamental form of power. The aim is to move media theory from effects and information toward meaning, practice and power.

**Prerequisites:** Only a loose sense of the Enlightenment/science-versus-humanities contrast and of 1920s American democracy debates; no math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Theory of the mass media has resisted adequate formulation. Simple models and methods that are at least unembarrassing for interpersonal communication fail there, because questions of political power and institutional change are inescapable (p. 53).
- Carey says the basic divide among communication scholars is objectivism versus expressivism, not administrative versus critical research (p. 56).
- Carey's own claim is historical, not metaphysical: human activity has progressively remade reality, so there are no given starting points or privileged methods (p. 56).
- Lippmann recast the press problem from morals and politics to epistemology (the theory of knowledge). Carey says this played down state and class power and contributed to depoliticizing the public sphere (the shared arena of public life). On Lippmann's account news cannot picture reality, so he proposed an official intelligence bureau (pp. 58–59).
- Dewey replaced seeing with hearing and the spectator with the participant. In Dewey's view, public opinion forms only in discussion, and science is one strand of the conversation (pp. 60–63).
- Carey says the crisis of public life stems partly from models that make communication an instrument of influence; he calls for treating communication as action that constitutes the world (pp. 63–64).
- Carey's cultural studies takes meaning as its central problem and a "ritual view" (communication as producing a coherent world that is then presumed to exist) as its stance. Symbols have an "of" and a "for" side: they portray a world and also help produce it (p. 65).
- Reality is a scarce resource. The power to define and display it is the basic power, and conflict over communication is conflict over practices, with class and status both involved (pp. 66–67).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 53–54)
- **Rorty's hermeneutic ideal.** Scholars are strands of a conversation, united by civility, not by a common goal or ground (p. 53). Carey says this is needed most in theory of the mass media. That area resists theory most, and methods that look merely "unembarrassing" for interpersonal communication look "hapless" there (p. 53).
- Reason: interpersonal communication can often be "encysted" from the wider world. The mass media cannot, because political power and institutional change are inescapable (p. 53).
- **Anticipated conclusion** from Carey's telegraph essay (chapter 8): the telegraph drove the mass press and extended communication's spatial limits. It opened the future as a zone of interaction, helped create a futures market and standard time, ended forms like the hoax and tall tale, and brought a national commercial middle class into being (pp. 53–54). Here he wants a theory that supports such historical-empirical work, and the route to it needs "detours" (p. 54).

### I. Descartes, Vico, and objectivism versus expressivism (pp. 54–57)
- Carey says the Enlightenment/Counter-Enlightenment ridge (Descartes versus Vico) resurfaces in media studies as critical versus administrative research (p. 54).
- **Descartes' three "peaks"** (p. 54): (1) noncontingency of starting points; (2) indubitability (universal concepts and methods, else no knowledge claim); (3) identity (the world of problems is independent of, and accessible to, the knowing observer). Together they made science the paradigm for all culture (p. 54).
- **Vico's reaction**, which Carey admits he twists somewhat to his purpose (pp. 54–55):
  1. the world has no essence and no real independence; the "real" is continuously remade for human purposes;
  2. Cartesian science is one form of human expression, "a new suburb of the language" (Wittgenstein's phrase), one voice in the human conversation;
  3. there are no timeless invariant methods or concepts, only culturally bounded symbols through which the world is intelligible.
- Carey warns that this portrait is "two-dimensional". Scholars straddle the ridge, assimilate one side to the other, or walk away, as William James did (p. 55).
- **Taylor's terms** (p. 55). *Objectivism* (Descartes): humans are subjects with their own picture of the world, and the world is a neutral, contingent domain of fact, mapped by correlations and manipulated for human purposes. Later this was extended to human life and society. *Expressivism*: the view that reality expresses something. Descartes discredited the spiritual form of it (pp. 55–56).
- Expressivism returned in romanticism and in Hegel (the Geist, "growth of rational freedom"). Marx "anthropologized" it (Taylor's phrase, quoted by Carey): reality expresses humans because it is a product of human activity, and technology, social relations and artifacts are "social hieroglyphics" (p. 56).
- Carey's claim: this distinction, not administrative versus critical, is the fundamental divide among communication scholars (p. 56). He accepts it "only as a prelude to modifying it" (p. 56).
- **Carey's modification.** He agrees, "at least to a limited extent", that reality is a product of human activity. The claim is "simply historical", not philosophical (p. 56). Nature has been turned into culture (Lévi-Strauss: vegetation into cuisine, animals into totems), and culture has penetrated nature (mind ulcerating the stomach, an equation splitting the atom) (p. 56). So reality is not objective, contingent and neutral. Consequence: no given starting points, Archimedean points, indubitable concepts or privileged methods; the only basing point is "the historically varying nature of human purposes" (p. 56).
- Carey glosses over the materialist/idealist fault line inside expressivism. Both readings can agree that the associative, cooperative mind, extended in culture and realized in technique, is the most important means of production, and its most important product is a produced and sustained reality (pp. 56–57).

> **Intuition note.** Carey says his claim is historical, not philosophical or metascientific (p. 56). His argument is that human activity has refigured virtually every reach of space, so reality is not neutral and given, and the only basing point left is the historically varying nature of human purposes (p. 56). Later he adds that this view involves no commitment to idealism or subjectivism (p. 66).

### II. Lippmann versus Dewey (pp. 57–63)
- **Framing.** Carey says the critical/administrative split is usually tied to Adorno and Lazarsfeld. He says "critical" did not so much describe a position as serve as a cover under which Marxism might hide during a hostile period in exile (p. 57). He instead relocates the distinction in an American exchange of the 1920s. Rationale: we cannot grasp a conversation elsewhere until we understand the one at home (p. 57).
- **Lippmann's *Public Opinion* (1922).** Carey believes it is the founding book of American media studies, with the news media as its real subject (pp. 57–58).
- **Old problematic: freedom.** Utilitarian view as Carey reports it: ends are arbitrary; reason judges only means. Freedom was said to guarantee perfect information, which guaranteed rational means, which in a manner "never quite explained" yielded the social good (p. 58). The threats to freedom were seen as political and institutional (p. 58).
- **Lippmann's change.** A free system does not guarantee perfect information. The enemies are now the nature of news and newsgathering, audience psychology and the scale of modern life. Carey: this moved the press problem from morals and politics to epistemology, played down state and class power, and contributed "paradoxically" to depoliticizing the public sphere (p. 58).
- **Lippmann's position (Carey's account)** (p. 59):
  - truth is matching an independent, picturable reality with corresponding language;
  - news cannot picture reality. It is like a sonar blip signalling that something is happening, or it gives "degenerate photographs" or a pseudo-reality of stereotypes;
  - news approximates truth only where reality reduces to a statistical table (scores, stock reports, births, deaths, elections). Where there is good machinery of record, news works with precision; where not, it spreads stereotypes;
  - remedy: an official, quasi-governmental intelligence bureau (p. 59).
- **Carey's critique.** This is the "classic fallacy of the Cartesian tradition": assuming that vision/mapping metaphors that fit small routine assertions ("the Cubs lost 7–5") fit large debatable ones. Numbers may picture the stock market but not what is happening in Central America (p. 59).
- **Subsidiary doctrines (Carey's reading).** The basic metaphor of communication is vision. Communication is transmission of a secured truth independent of power, so cadres of scientists are needed. Scientists are a potential "new priesthood". Public opinion is the statistical aggregation of private opinions informed by news. Effects of mass communication come from the inadequacy of news plus audience stereotypes and selective perceptions. The ground of discussion shifts from public, power and freedom to knowledge, truth and stereotypes (pp. 59–60).
- **Dewey's response.** He called the book the greatest indictment of democracy yet written (review, 3 May 1922) and replied in *The Public and Its Problems* (1927) (p. 60). Carey quotes the last pages: diffused intelligence requires real local communal life; print research is a tool whose "final actuality" lies in face-to-face exchange; logic returns to "dialogue"; "Vision is a spectator: hearing is a participator"; public opinion is real only when meanings pass "from mouth to mouth" (pp. 60–61).
- **Carey's reading of Dewey** (pp. 61–62):
  - Dewey attacks representation in both political and epistemological forms. Language is activity, not a system of representations, so it cannot serve a representative function;
  - truth is what "it is better for us to believe" (James); a proposition is tested by its adequacy to our purposes. Words get meaning from other words and from practice;
  - science is part of the cultural conversation, valued for its habits (full disclosure, reasons, openness to experience, systematic criticism), not for privileged representations. Dewey wanted "a science in society", not a science presided over by a priesthood; Carey adds that "in this sense everyone is a scientist";
  - news is "another voice to be heard", an occasion for discussion and action, not degenerate science;
  - if reality is what we will to believe in support of shared purposes, then (Carey's inference) it is constituted by human action, especially symbolic and associative action. The public is the instrument of democratic order, so an atomized or "phantom" public makes democracy impossible (p. 62).
- **Carey's side-by-side summary** (pp. 62–63):
  - **Lippmann:** effective public opinion = individuals holding correct representations, aggregated statistically. Newspapers transmit them. Social-science cadres in quasi-public bureaucracies (model: the Bureau of Standards) produce them (p. 62).
  - **Dewey:** opinion forms only in discussion in community life. News's main fault is not failing to represent. The line between image and stereotype cannot be drawn. News should signal, tell a story and activate inquiry, and inquiry is a systematic form of conversation (p. 62). What is lacking is institutions and habits (following an argument, taking another's view) (pp. 62–63). A press that sees its role as informing the public abandons conversation (pp. 62–63).
- **Deep conflict.** Dewey sees in Lippmann the "spectator theory of knowledge". In Carey's words, Lippmann views the public as "a spectator of the spectator", who receives the representations that scientists have made. Knowledge and politics tied to vision deny the public any role beyond ratifying a world the experts already decided. For Dewey we are participants in making the world, depending on our purposes and foresight (p. 63).

> **Intuition note.** For Lippmann the model is a picture sent to a viewer; for Dewey it is a discussion among participants. Carey's point is that the two models imply different roles for press, science and public (pp. 62–63).

### III. Extending Dewey: communication as cultural action (pp. 63–67)
- **Carey's evaluation.** Dewey was flawed (optimism, small-town romance, simple view of technology), but "had the best of this argument" (p. 63).
- **Diagnosis.** Analysis begins from a crisis of community and public life. Carey traces it to models of communication dominating everyday life, from a science of society that casts communication as people pursuing influence (causal models) or fleeing anxiety (functionalist models). The model in *Public Opinion* is part of the cause (pp. 63–64).
- Language is increasingly defined as an instrument for manipulating objects. Carey cites Camus ("dialogue… replaced by propaganda or polemic") and Arendt (speech makes humans political; "public space") (p. 64). Convergence: the divorce of truth from discourse and action has not only increased propaganda; it has disrupted the notion of truth and so our bearings (p. 64).
- **Reformulation.** If communication is a "form of life" (Wittgenstein), changing its models can change that form of life (p. 64). Discard language as reference, correspondence and representation. Substitute language as action or interaction that molds or constitutes the world (p. 64).
- Communication "has no essence"; it is historically varying practices and reflections on them, joining human conceptions and purposes with technological forms in sedimented social relations (pp. 64–65). Carey calls this approach **cultural studies**, with meaning as its central problem (p. 65).
- **Meaning** is a constituting activity by which humans give an "elastic though resistant world" enough coherence to support their purposes. Symbols have an "of" and a "for" side: they portray what they pretend to describe, so we can produce the world symbolically and then "take up residence" in it (p. 65).
- Carey calls this a **ritual view**: the production of a coherent world presumed to exist. It stresses paradigms over experiments, presuppositions over propositions, "the frame, not the picture" (p. 65).
- **Goal:** hermeneutic: find out what others are up to, make their concepts and purposes transparent, and extend the human conversation. Understanding a culture is akin to understanding a scientific theory: look at practices, the concepts they presuppose, and the social relations they manifest (p. 65).
- **Communication as ensemble.** It is a structure of action, an ensemble of expressive forms, and a structured and structuring set of social relations. Each moment "coactualizes" conceptions, expressive forms and social relations. Technique is "vectoral", not neutral. A building anticipates the social relations it permits, and so does a television signal (pp. 65–66).
- **Geertz caveat.** This commits nobody to idealism, subjectivism, or the belief that people act in circumstances of their own choosing (p. 66). Reality is not private property or taste, and not an eternal given awaiting representation. It is a product of collective, associated work and action. Carey says this locates social conflict and makes it intelligible (p. 66).
- **Reality as scarce resource.** The "fundamental form of power" is the power to define, allocate and display it. The site where artists, writers and broadcasters work is also the site of conflict over the real. The conflict is over the codetermination of ideas, technique and social relations, and over "acts and practices that are themselves the effects", not over the effects of communication (p. 66).
- Conflict occurs at the level of paradigms, formulas and stereotypes, and at points of exclusion where forms of thought are cast into "unintelligibility, subversion, and disgrace". Reality is scarce because few command the machinery that determines it (pp. 66–67). Carey adds that there is status conflict as well as class conflict, including among intellectuals' access to classrooms, journals and media (p. 67).
- **Closing verdict.** Dewey is naive about class, status and power. Lippmann seems sophisticated because he understood and accepted the new media and the class power they embodied (p. 67). Dewey's community of equals ("We can all talk") is hard to believe now. Modern thought about communication, affirming or critical, shows "lust" for power. A critical theory must affirm what is before our eyes and transcend it by imagining "a world more desirable" (p. 67).

> **Intuition note.** Carey's shift (our paraphrase) is from asking what communication does to people to asking what world communication practices make and who has access to making it. Meaning and access become central (pp. 65–67).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town of 5 residents votes on a footbridge.
- **Lippmann-style model.** A bureau issues one table to everyone: "cost 100; 20 crossings per day." Each resident holds the same correct facts, and opinion is the aggregate: 3 yes, 2 no, so 3/5 = 60% in favour. The table settles the factual part, but not what the bridge is for.
- **Dewey-style model.** The 5 meet. Two "no" voters say they fear the bridge will bring traffic. A third resident says it is mainly for 12 of the 20 daily crossings, which are school children. One "no" voter changes sides, giving 4 yes, 1 no = 80%. The change came through discussion of purposes, not through a new number.
- **Point.** In the first model opinion is a sum of pre-formed pictures. In the second, opinion is formed in the exchange. Carey says this is the difference between a spectator and a participant public (pp. 62–63).

## Questions this raises
- If reality is made by action, what separates better from worse claims in the "conversation"? Carey, describing Dewey's view, says only that "objective truth" is the best idea we currently have for explaining what is going on (p. 61).
- How could Dewey's habits of full disclosure and criticism be built into mass-media institutions, given Carey's point on unequal access (pp. 62–63, 67)?
- Does the cultural approach lose the ability to study effects, when Carey says conflict is over practices, "not… the effects of communication" (p. 66)?
- Which historical studies follow from this theory? Carey points to the telegraph study as one (pp. 53–54).
