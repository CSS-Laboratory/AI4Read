# Mass Communication and Cultural Studies

## L0 — One line
Carey argues that communication research should go beyond explaining symbolic forms by causes or functions and also treat human action as a text whose meanings it interprets, following Clifford Geertz.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey asks why American communication research, named "mass communication", handles culture so poorly. Following Williams, Hall and Geertz, he argues that it rests on a transmission view (messages sent for control). It explains symbolic forms by causes or functions, and this dissolves their content. He proposes a cultural science. It treats human action as a text and interprets the meanings people build into experience, without trying to predict behavior. Culture is a set of practices that creates multiple realities, and none of them is a final court for the others. The older mass-culture debate, he says, asked the right question: what is the significance of living in the world of popular art?

**Prerequisites:** the idea of correlation (a statistical link between two variables); a rough sense of what sociology and anthropology study; no mathematics needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The 1950s mass-culture debate (MacDonald, Mills, Shils) was left unresolved and then absorbed into effects research (studies of how media change attitudes or behavior) and later into questions of power. Carey says it still asked the right question about popular art's significance (pp. 29–31).
- Williams and Hall objected to the labels "mass communications" and "communication". The labels limit study to a few media, ignore the practices and forms of communication, and cut it off from interpretive methods (pp. 31–32).
- Two metaphors organise the field. A transmission view treats communication as sending messages across space for control. A ritual view treats it as creating and maintaining a shared culture in time (p. 33).
- Social science faces Mannheim's Paradox: if laws of behavior govern the scientist too, his work is "nonsense"; if not, "what kind of sense" can be made of it? Carey reads the behavioral, formal and cultural strategies as responses to it (pp. 35–37).
- Causal explanations of symbolic forms predict poorly, and functional ones rely on vague "latent functions" (hidden effects said to link a motive to an unintended result). In Carey's account, neither seriously examines the content of the symbols (pp. 40–43).
- Cultural science seeks to understand, not predict. It reads action as a text and "interprets the interpretations". Carey uses the death scene to suggest that laws, functions and deep structures (hidden mental structures that formal theorists posit) are unpromising for understanding it (pp. 43–47).
- Human thought is not science versus error. It builds multiple realities through many symbolic forms, so Carey calls for a "theory of fictions" (pp. 48–49, 50–51).
- Culture is first a set of practices that creates reality (p. 50). Popular art is one cultural world among several (p. 51). Advances in understanding culture cannot be secured unless they are tied to a vivid sense of technology and social structure (p. 49).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the lost popular-culture debate (pp. 29–31)
- **Carey's account.** During and after World War II and the Korean War, American intellectuals debated popular culture. The debate was really about popular entertainment: songs, films, stories (p. 29).
- **The three leaders:**
  - MacDonald attacked popular culture from the conservative side, in the name of folk and elite culture.
  - Mills attacked it from the left, in the name of authentic democratic community. He opposed manipulation by elites who controlled industrial cultural production.
  - Shils defended liberal belief. For him taste was not debased, artists were freer, audiences better entertained, and creativity as high as ever (p. 29).
- **Carey's verdict.** All three were right: tradition was evaporating, things were better in many ways, and ordinary people faced shallow, manipulative culture controlled by a "power elite" (p. 30). The prudent conclusion cost the field, temporarily at least, its subject matter (p. 30).
- **What followed.**
  - In the 1960s the topic was absorbed into functional sociology and behaviorist psychology, the "effects" tradition.
  - In the 1970s it returned inside one problematic, power and domination (p. 30).
- **The lost question.** What is the significance of conceiving the world on the terms laid down by popular art? How does this form of consciousness relate to scientific, aesthetic, religious, ethnic and mythological ones? (p. 30)
- **Carey's position.** He resists the fashion of dismissing the debate. He holds that the debate's protagonists collectively grasped how modern societies were put together, and that mass society theory (Mills, extended by Kornhauser) has not been superseded by critical theory, postmodernism or effects research. He calls North American conditions "Tocquevillian" (pp. 30–31).
- **The weakness.** The debate assumed a relatively crude conception of culture. Carey will review recent advances through commentary on Geertz's *The Interpretation of Cultures*, a collection written over fifteen years. Read chronologically, it becomes "progressively" a theory of communication as well (p. 31).
- **Plan.** First a dilemma of communication studies, then a contradiction of social science, then a simplified unpacking of Geertz (p. 31).

### I. Williams, Hall and two views of communication (pp. 31–35)
- **Williams's three reasons** for burying the term "mass communications" (pp. 31–32):
  - It limits study to a few areas such as broadcasting and film, when "the whole common area of discourse in speech and writing" needs attention.
  - "Mass" is lodged in language in its weakest sense, the mass audience, which blocks analysis of specific forms and situations.
  - Because the audience was a mass, the only question was how and whether media influence or corrupt people. Impact studies were therefore the easiest to fund.
- **Carey's reading of Williams.** The term overlooks that communication is first a set of practices, conventions and forms. In "mass situations" these are assumed but never investigated. It also excludes speech and writing. Carey says this emphasis derives partly from European Marxism but is shared by American pragmatism (p. 32).
- **Hall's objection** ("somewhat more opaque", Carey thinks he had a similar intention):
  - "Communication" narrows study to products delivered by mass media, separate from literature, art, and everyday ritual forms such as religion, conversation and sport.
  - "Culture", in the anthropological sense, points to a whole way of life. "Communication" points to one isolated segment.
  - Methodologically, it cuts study off from interpretive and comparative methods in anthropology, literary study and modern Marxism (p. 32).
- Carey notes the possible dismissals: only departments' names are at issue, or the limits buy scientific method. He suspends judgment (p. 33).
- **Two metaphors** (Carey calls this a generalization with exceptions; p. 33):
  - **Transmission or transportation view.** Communication is transmitting messages at a distance for control. The archetype is persuasion, attitude change, behavior modification or individual choice. The term echoes the nineteenth-century use of "communication" for "transportation" and the wish to extend influence over wider populations.
  - **Ritual view.** Communication is a process through which a shared culture is created, modified and transformed. The archetype is ritual and mythology (anthropology) or art and literature (literary criticism). It aims at the maintenance of society in time, even if some find that maintenance dominating and illegitimate. It aims at the creation, representation and celebration of shared, even illusory, beliefs (p. 33).
- The views have different problematics: the basic questions of one do not connect with those of the other (pp. 33–34).
- **The culture–society question.** For most American scholars, the relation of expressive form to social order is "not even seen as a problem". Much European work treats it as a principal task (p. 34).
- **Burns** (as Carey reports him): art makes sense out of life, and social science makes sense out of the senses we make out of life. The social scientist stands to religion, ideology and journalism as the critic stands to a novel (p. 34). On this reading, culture is the meaning people find in experience through art, religion and so on. Studying culture can equally be called studying communication (p. 34).
- **American studies**, simplified by Carey: they found the conditions of persuasion or social control most problematic. They sought the psychological and sociological conditions under which attitudes change, or the functions that hold society together (pp. 34–35). Cultural forms enter only insofar as they bear on "states and rates". Their relation to social order, their history, their place in a subjective world of meaning and their role in creating a way of life are never seriously entertained (p. 35).

> **Intuition note.** The transmission view asks "how do messages move and change people?" The ritual view asks "how do people make and share a world of meaning?" Carey says the basic questions of one tradition do not connect with those of the other (pp. 33–34).

### II. Mannheim's Paradox and three strategies (pp. 35–51)

**The dilemma (pp. 35–37)**
- **Bendix's formulation (reported by Carey).** North American intellectuals are committed to reason as the instrument of political action. Yet the social sciences model action as individual preference or as governed by intrinsic or environmental laws: conditioning, prelogical functions, preconscious urges (p. 35).
- **The question.** Where do these laws come from? They are either authored by the scientist for his purposes as a member of a controlling class, or they are part of nature and govern the scientist too. If they govern him, his work is "nonsense". If not, "what kind of sense" can be made of it? (pp. 35–36)
- **Ideology as the test case.** Intellectuals contrast "political science", which captures truth, with "ideology", a tissue of error. But political theory might be one more ideology (p. 36). Geertz calls this dilemma **Mannheim's Paradox**, because Karl Mannheim wrestled with it without resolution. Carey quotes Geertz (1973: 194): where, "if anywhere", ideology leaves off and science begins has been the "Sphinx's Riddle" of much of modern sociological thought (p. 36).
- **Carey's two claims.** The study of communication begins when this dilemma is faced directly, and its principal strategies can be seen as devices for escaping the paradox. He names three: a behavioral science of laws, a formal science of structures, a cultural science of meaning (pp. 36–37).

**Strategy 1: behavioral science (pp. 37–38, 40–43)**
- **Two explanations of ideology** (Geertz: interest theory and strain theory; Carey prefers causal and functional) (p. 37):
  - *Causal.* Ideology is derived from group interests, especially class, and predicted from class membership. Class and ideology correlate only weakly, so "one class, one ideology" breaks down.
  - *Functional.* Ideology satisfies needs of personality or society and restores equilibrium. Carey's example: a shopkeeper's anti-Semitism is explained by class in the causal model, and as catharsis, the displacement of tension onto symbolic enemies, in the functional model.
- **Weaknesses** (p. 37). Causal laws are usually weak at prediction. Functional laws are usually obscure about what the functions are. Both link data to concepts such as "interest" or "catharsis" by questionable operational definitions.
- **Escapes from the paradox** (p. 38):
  - The laws are only statistically true, so they apply "to everyone in general" and "no one in particular". They explain only part of the variance, so they need not govern the scientist.
  - The scientist escapes the law by comprehending it, and can then unmask others' ideologies.
- Carey calls neither particularly effective. Perhaps the greatest disservice is that they dispose of the phenomenon in the act of naming it. They assume flat, disinterested prose is the only vehicle of truth, and "what they object to in ideology is hyperbole" (p. 38).
- **Mass communication research parallels this** (pp. 39–41):
  - The earlier work, inspired by behaviorism and information theory, produced a **power model**: an environment acts on a relatively passive receiver. Causes included psychological variables (source credibility, appeal) and structural ones (class, status, religion, income) (p. 40).
  - Benefit: it rooted culture in social structure or conditioning. Cost: ambiguous predictions. Correlations were modest ("usually less than 0.5"), and success was often bought by screening test populations (p. 40).
  - Results come down to "some do, some don't". Carey says the data force an antiessentialism, citing Morley's audience studies. The imagery of culture as power (opiate, hypodermic needle) reduces subjects to trivial machines (p. 40).
  - The **functional model** arises in response (p. 41). It shifts from source to audience, from power to anxiety release, and from antecedents to consequences for maintaining a system. Carey's three "theories": religion, "We're all in this together"; ideology, "Even paranoids have enemies"; mass communication, "Everything we do is useful" (p. 41).
  - Functionalism explains phenomena by the future states they maintain or erode. Carey says this leaves the equivocal notion that mass communication may upset or confirm consensus, relieve or exacerbate tensions, and so on. Anecdote supports all of these, but nothing specifies when each happens (p. 41).
- **Uses and gratifications** (Carey's critique). It sometimes comes close to motives, but its link to consequences is vague. A person watches TV to be entertained and "by some mysterious process" ends up restoring morale or solidarity (pp. 41–42).
- **Geertz's summary of the dilemma** (p. 42): primitives pray for rain and strengthen solidarity; a ward politician seeks the trough and mediates between immigrants and bureaucracy; an ideologist airs grievances and sustains the system that grieves him (Geertz 1973: 206).
- **Latent functions and false consciousness** close the gap between intention and consequence. Carey says this was inherited from Malinowski. Apparently irrational action is linked by "hidden indirection" to rational meaning, leaving two options: the action is primitive and irrational, or it is secretly utilitarian (p. 42).
- **The charge.** Either option dissolves the content of an experience (a ritual, a movie, a news story) into something pre- or protological without examining it as an ordered system of symbols. Mass communication research has only a rudimentary conception of symbolic processes. Studies of entertainment rarely analyse "the voice in which films speak" (p. 42). Content analyses refer back to needs or forward to systems, not to other themes or a semantic theory (p. 43).

> **Intuition note.** Both explanations skip over the symbols. In Carey's account they go from the source or cause straight to an effect without seriously examining the symbols, so the meaning of the film, prayer or news story is not examined. Carey's complaint is that the connecting step is left as a vague "latent function".

**Strategy 2: formal science (pp. 38–39)**
- Carey says formal theories are rare in American communication work but have influenced it imaginatively. Examples: linguistics, systems theory, cybernetics, cognitive psychology, structuralism after Lévi-Strauss (p. 38).
- They avoid the paradox by turning away from behavior, as in the competence/performance or language/speech distinctions. Linguists explain competence, the abstract ability to produce grammatical sentences, not performance (p. 38).
- Starting points are "irrefragable" universals, such as novel utterances or systems of symbolic opposition (up/down, stop/go). Theorists then build structures that could produce them: "the deep structure of mind or culture out of the fugitive materials of everyday acts" (pp. 38–39).
- **Symbolic forms treated as force or function.** Without a successful formal analysis, one searches for effects or for uses and gratifications. This is a shift from persons as "trivial machines" to persons as complex systems, reflecting the general history of the social sciences (p. 39).
- **Carey's diagnosis** (p. 39): it is useful to separate the symbolic from the artifactual (house/blueprint, making love/poem; borrowed from Geertz, who borrows from Burke). When the symbolic is present they reduce it to social structure or psychological needs. They "seem incapable" of handling culture "as an ordered though contradictory and heterogeneous system of symbols". The switch from causal to functional explanation mirrors a switch from a power model to an anxiety model and from passive to active, utilitarian images of behavior (p. 39).

**Strategy 3: cultural science (pp. 43–51)**
- **Aim** (Carey). Cultural studies embraces Mannheim's Paradox and risks "vicious relativism", though Geertz does not see that as a problem. It does not explain behavior by laws or dissolve it into structures. It seeks to understand and "diagnose human meanings" and to descend "deeper into the empirical world". Its goals are more modest but "more human" (p. 43).
- **Geertz's definition** (p. 43): man is an animal "suspended in webs of significance he himself has spun". Culture is those webs, and its analysis is "not an experimental science in search of law but an interpretive one in search of meaning" (Geertz 1973: 5).

**The death scene (Carey's own example; pp. 43–46)**

| Speaker | When death occurs | Notes given by Carey |
|---|---|---|
| Contemporary physician | cessation of brain waves | empirically testable; organs available for transplant |
| Typical middle American | cessation of heartbeat | empirical; heart symbolizes emotion and community |
| Irish peasant | three days after heartbeat stops | days can be counted; person treated as alive (wake); death is social death |
| Member of Turnbull's Mountain People | seven days before heartbeat stops | life ceases when food can no longer be gathered |

(pp. 43–44)
- **Conclusion on choice.** We cannot choose among the definitions on scientific grounds. Death is not given unequivocally by biological and social markers. We can show how definitions point to different values (prolonging life, continuity of community). About choosing among them on any presumed scientific grounds, we must, "at least at this point, remain agnostics" (p. 44).
- **Why the usual tools fail** (pp. 44–45):
  - *Laws.* Carey cannot imagine a "lawlike" statement other than a tautology. The only causal statement he can imagine is historical, and it "seems intuitively obvious" that every people "demands a separate history".
  - *Functions.* There is no a priori reason these definitions serve any function. Geertz: a latent function "names the phenomenon … rather than explains it", leaving functional analyses "hopelessly equivocal" (Geertz 1973: 206, quoted on p. 45).
  - *Deep structure* (Lévi-Strauss). It verges on "a charade". Geertz concludes that particular scenes make the doctrine of a changeless "structure of the mind" seem "merely quaint" (1973: 359).
- **Why this example.** Death is about as universal as a phenomenon gets, yet resistant to reduction to laws, functions or interests. Our speechlessness comes from failing to understand "the imaginative universe in which the acts of our actors are signs" (p. 45). The challenge is to untangle "a multiplicity of complex conceptual structures", then grasp and render them (p. 46, quoting Geertz 1973: 10).
- **Verstehen, as Carey uses the word.** Social scientists do assign meanings, but these have "no necessary relation" to the subjects' intentions. The first task is to understand the meaningful structure of symbols in terms of which people bury their dead. Carey says verstehen is "no long-distance mind reading" but deciphering interpretations that are public (p. 46).

**Action as text (pp. 46–47)**
- A cultural science views action "as a text" and constructs a "reading". The text is a sequence of symbols (speech, writing, gesture) containing interpretations. The task is to "interpret the interpretations" (p. 46).
- **Geertz on the Balinese cockfight** (p. 46): culture is an "ensemble of texts" the anthropologist reads "over the shoulders" of their owners. The cockfight is one face of Balinese temperament, not unqualified or unchallenged by other cultural statements. Racine and Molière were contemporaries, and the same people arrange chrysanthemums and cast swords (Geertz 1973: 452–53).
- Carey says the text metaphor makes the task closer to that of a literary critic or scriptural scholar than to that of a behavioral scientist, though "not the same". Texts are not only printed. Doing ethnography is, in Geertz's words, like reading a foreign, faded manuscript written in "transient examples of shaped behavior" (p. 47, quoting Geertz 1973: 10).
- **Deverbalizing the death scene.** Remove the words and we see wakes, brain-wave measuring, "rolling relatives into ravines". We must read these gestures in relation to social structure without reducing them to it. Carey admits this is not easy (p. 47).
- **Why construct readings?** The objective is "to make available to us answers that others guarding other sheep in other valleys have given" (Geertz, p. 30), enlarging "the human conversation" (p. 47). Carey: the failure to converse is the "imperative failure" of modern social science, which imposes meanings. Carey says social scientists treat their own views as political theories and their subjects' as political ideologies, seeing themselves as free and rationally informed and their subjects as conditioned and ruled by habit and superstition: "not good intellectual soil for a working democracy" (pp. 47–48).

> **Intuition note.** Instead of asking "what makes people believe this?", the interpretive scientist asks "what do they mean by it, and how does it fit their other meanings?". The result is a better understanding of others' answers, not a law.

**A theory of fictions (pp. 48–49)**
- Carey says Geertz's suggestion is that cultural studies can uniquely produce a theory of fictions, using *fictio* in the sense of a "making" or construction (p. 48).
- Recent theory has eroded the supposed irreducible difference between "primitive" and modern minds. Carey says that difference "appears now to have taught us more" about social scientists' "political purposes and personal conceits" than about the nature of human thought (p. 48).
- **The lesson** is not that we are both primitive and modern, reasoning and superstitious. It is that thought is not "irrefragable maps" of the world (science) versus errors. Thought is interpretations applied to experience. Their verification "cannot be exhausted by the methods of science". People create multiple realities, and no single symbolic form exhausts reality (p. 48).
- Genius lies in building models of reality with many symbol types (verbal, written, mathematical, gestural) and forms (art, science, journalism, ideology, ordinary speech, religion, myth). A theory of fictions must explain how they operate, what devices they use and what meanings they sustain (pp. 48–49).
- **Method** (Geertz, 1973: 453). One may start anywhere in a culture's forms and end anywhere. One can stay within one form, move between forms, or compare cultures. The guiding principle is that societies, like lives, "contain their own interpretations" (p. 49).
- Carey: the work is "making large claims from small matters". For communication students, further questions press in. How does technology change the constructions placed on experience and the forms of community? What is thought about, thought with, and to whom is it expressed? Advances in understanding culture need "a vivid sense of technology and social structure" (p. 49).

**Culture as practice, and multiple realities (pp. 49–51)**
- A theory of popular culture needs persons conceived as cultural, not psychological or sociological. The model assumes a basic disposition to cast up experience in symbolic form. These forms are "aesthetically right and conceptually veridical", and supply identities along with an apprehended world (p. 49).
- Human activity, "by the very nature of the human nervous system" (Geertz 1973: 68), is cultural. It builds a symbolic container for whatever needs or dispositions exist (pp. 49–50). Weber, writing of religion, says belief rests on a stand toward something experienced as "senseless", and the demand that the world order be a meaningful "cosmos" (p. 50).
- In communication research, culture usually appears as an environment or a power. Carey allows that there is truth in these views. But culture must first be seen as practices, a process in which reality is "created, maintained, and transformed", though it may later be reified into an independent force (citing Berger and Luckmann). A theory of meaning (semantics or semiotics) is needed to remove culture from the status of power or environment (p. 50).
- The theory is usually avoided by placing needs and motives outside history, the eighteenth-century view that human nature is everywhere the same beneath cultural trappings. Research gains precision only when placed within history and culture (p. 50).
- **Multiple realities** (following Schutz). Mass communication research typically assumes a hard reality beyond culture to validate imaginative productions. Carey finds it "comic" that popular music analyses define love songs as fantasy and blues or protest songs as reality (Carey 1972; Hayakawa 1957). The forms differ in the audience's taste for modes of casting up experience, not in real versus fantastic (pp. 50–51).
- People live in qualitatively distinct zones of experience. Few are satisfied with common sense alone. The "scientific conceit" is the presumption that scientific frames are unequivocally superior, which blinds us to the realms of discourse in which people live (p. 51).
- **Popular art.** It is first an "immediate experience" (Warshow) that must be apprehended in its own terms. It is one of several cultural worlds, with little relation among them except when questionnaires force a merger. It creates moods and motives, but whether these cross into laboratories, street corners and churches is "radically problematic. Usually they do not" (p. 51). The analysis of mass communication must examine the several worlds people inhabit, their tensions and interpenetration, and must drop the assumption that needs found in scientific worlds are a final court (p. 51).

### III. Conclusion (pp. 51–52)
- Mentioning cultural studies to communication researchers revives the old mass/popular culture arguments. That was part of the disaster Williams meant. Carey repeats that those who worked on popular culture were on the right track: what is the significance of living in the world of meanings of popular art, and how does it relate to science, religion and ordinary speech? (pp. 51–52)
- Geertz's work and work in phenomenology, hermeneutics and literary criticism have clarified a cultural science and defined an interpretive science of society (p. 52).
- **The task now:** to turn these advances on contemporary products: news stories, bureaucratic language, love songs, political rhetoric, daytime serials, scientific reports, TV drama, talk shows, leisure, ritual and information. These are the conventions Williams felt had slipped by when the field was named "mass communications" (p. 52).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Twenty people are asked how they will vote on a local issue X. Ten are working-class and ten are middle-class. Suppose 6 of 10 working-class and 4 of 10 middle-class people vote yes.
- **Predicting by class** (the causal strategy). Rule: working-class → yes, middle-class → no. Correct guesses: 6 (working-class yes) + 6 (middle-class no) = 12 of 20 = 60%.
- **Baseline.** Total yes = 6 + 4 = 10, so guessing "yes" for everyone is right 10 of 20 = 50% of the time. The class rule gains only 10 percentage points. This is Carey's "some do, some don't" pattern (p. 40).
- **Still unanswered.** The numbers say nothing about what "yes" means to each voter, for instance community pride for one and fear of change for another.
- **Cultural strategy.** Record each person's own words about X and read them as a text. Compare how meanings fit with one another and with the speaker's other worlds. The aim is understanding what each means, not a better prediction (pp. 43, 46).

## Questions this raises
- If cultural studies "embraces" Mannheim's Paradox and risks "vicious relativism" (p. 43), what checks can separate better readings from worse ones?
- How can interpretive readings be tied to "technology and social structure" (p. 49), and to the structural conditions that Carey says mass-society theory caught well (pp. 30–31)?
- If experience is split into multiple worlds with little traffic between them (p. 51), how should effects findings be re-read?
- Why does Carey call his reading of American studies a simplification (p. 34), and which exceptions would test it?
