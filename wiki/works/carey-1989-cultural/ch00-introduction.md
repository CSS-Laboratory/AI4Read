# Introduction (to *Communication as Culture*)

## L0 — One line
Carey argues that American talk of a "communications revolution" repeats an old story: from the start, the nation tried to bind space with transport and print technology while also relying on older, oral ways of binding time and character, and American discourse has held these two contradictory notions of communication from the outset.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey starts from Boorstin's claim that multiplying forms of communication is perhaps the most important change in recent human, and especially American, consciousness. He accepts part of it but rejects the melodramatic "revolution" story. The United States was created, in significant ways, as an attempt to move culture across distance by literacy, print and transport. That attempt met an older oral ideal of small republics held together by speech and memory. The "transmission or transportation solution" promised to rebuild the Greek city-state's closeness on a continental scale. It also carried a fear: the same lines could carry demagogic enthusiasm. Carey treats technology as cultural from the outset, and he uses these contradictions as a resource.

**Prerequisites:** basic US history (Constitution, Federalist Papers, Jefferson, Madison); Greek city-state democracy; the difference between oral and written culture.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Boorstin's thesis that communication has multiplied is, in Carey's words, common but "largely uninvestigated". Carey finds the melodramatic revolution-or-rupture version of it not particularly helpful (pp. 1–2).
- Talk of a communications revolution, and the hopes and fears about media, repeat so predictably that they suggest "undeviating corridors of thought". Carey says we are dealing with "an old story rather than a new one" (p. 2).
- The US was created, in significant ways, by an attempt to move culture across distance with literacy, cheap paper, transport and print. It still had to meet the need to ritualize and stabilize experience with resources carried from elsewhere (p. 2).
- Classical political thought limited democracy by geography and population: "the range of the foot and the power of the tongue". In Bruce Smith's account, republics are built on memory. Carey expresses this in the roles of citizen (a relation in space) and patriot (a relation in time) (pp. 3–4).
- The "transmission or transportation solution" (Madison, Jefferson, Gallatin) was to bind a continental republic by roads, canals and print. This is space-binding (joining distant places) by technology. Republican character and virtue were to come from time-binding (joining past and present) by oral speech and discourse (pp. 4–6).
- Communication technology brought hope of unity and fear of demagogic contagion (mass enthusiasm stirred by leaders who play on the crowd). Carey calls these "the systolic and diastolic beats of the culture". Madison labored to show that the hope could exist without the fear (pp. 6–7).
- Carey aims to show that technology is thoroughly cultural and that media are "definite forms of life", not merely instruments. He uses the contradictions rather than trying to resolve them (p. 7).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The introduction has no numbered sections. Headings below follow its order.)

**Boorstin's claim and Carey's first response (pp. 1–2).**
- **Boorstin's view:** the multiplying of the means and forms of "communication" is perhaps the most important single change in human consciousness in the last century, and especially in the American consciousness (p. 1).
- **Carey:** this conclusion is common but remains largely uninvestigated. He asks "But is it true?" (p. 1).
- **Carey's answer.** Until recent times ordinary people stood outside mechanically reproduced communication beyond the circle of village and kin. To reach them one had to work through networks of personal relations such as churches, parties, neighborhoods and ethnic societies (p. 1). The impression is too often left in histories that 19th-century people sat waiting for news from Washington (p. 1). Today the media are inescapable. The Joe Buck example (*Midnight Cowboy*) has "necessary license", but its insight is sound: modern communications have changed the ordinary terms of experience and consciousness (p. 1).
- Still, a "melodramatic modernism or postmodernism" stressing revolutions and ruptures is not particularly helpful and is "pretty much based on an illusion" (p. 2). In many of the essays that follow, Carey attempts to puncture the "satanic and angelic" images of the media (p. 2).
- The computer and satellite are "simply the latest chapter in an old tale". The talk of a communications revolution and the exalted hopes and exaggerated fears of the media are repetitions so predictable that they suggest "undeviating corridors of thought" (p. 2).

*Intuition note:* Carey grants that media changed daily life but treats the revolution story as unhelpful. The way people talk about new media follows an old pattern.

**The American beginning: space, time, orality and print (pp. 2–3).**
- Carey says that "if we yield to a useful ethnocentrism", the multiplying of the means and forms of communication can be seen as central to US history from the outset. The US was a creation, in significant ways, of an attempt to change how culture was made and spread: to move it out of villages, resettle it at a distance and adapt it to "uncongenial surroundings" (p. 2).
- This could never unload the instincts and necessities of an ancient past. "We remained possessed by that which we no longer quite possessed": rituals and narratives that are "anthropological" (p. 2).
- In "more deterministic language", the US was the product of literacy, cheap paper, fast transport and mechanical reproduction of words. This was understood as eclipsing time and space, but neither could be eclipsed (p. 2). The result was "scar tissue", and the need to ritualize and stabilize experience had to be met with resources carried from elsewhere (p. 2).
- A second description: the US arose in a "historical void" between oral and written traditions (p. 2). The oral tradition did not travel well unless stabilized by writing and printing. The written tradition brought habits at odds with it: solitude, distant conversation, private composition and keeping up with the news (pp. 2–3). Both traditions were "substantively empty" until each filled the other: oral tales translated into print, and print's habits filtered through speech (p. 3).

**Political theory: limits of democracy (pp. 3–4).**
- Until the end of the 18th century, a broad consensus in political philosophy ran from Plato and Aristotle to Rousseau and Montesquieu: democracy has natural limits of geography and population (p. 3).
- *Geography:* Greek city-states ranged, "according to some estimates", from 10×10 to 70×70 km. A citizen could travel on foot from the most remote point to the political center and back in one day (p. 3).
- *Population:* Dahl is cited for Plato's optimum of 5,040 citizens. Carey says the number displays the "fallacy of misplaced concreteness" but expresses the democratic wish for universal participation (p. 3).
- Democracies were thus limited by "the range of the foot and the power of the tongue". Carey adds that Greek democracy turned limits into virtues. Democracies, "or so the theory goes", had to be large enough to be self-sufficient but small enough for citizens to know one another's character and share the roles of self-government (p. 3).
- It was an oral democracy of assembly, debate and talk, which also cultivated rhetoric and feats of memory (p. 3).
- **Bruce Smith (as cited):** republics are mnemonic structures (built on memory), erected "upon the injunction: remember" (p. 3). The primordial memory is that republics have concrete beginnings and therefore can have concrete endings (p. 3).
- Aristotle's "political animal" meant a place in the polis defined through speech and remembrance (pp. 3–4).
- The citizen has a relation in space to contemporaries. The patriot has a relation in time to predecessors. Both are marked by "radical finitude" (Pocock's phrase) (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* In this account, how big a republic could be depended on how far a walker and a voice could reach. Remembrance was the way republican politics was carried through time.

**The federal union and the transmission solution (pp. 4–6).**
- The Constitution and Federalist Papers affirmed the republican tradition and tried to transcend and contradict it. They proposed a republic continental in geography and virtually unlimited in population (p. 4). Jefferson's "perpetual revolution" isolated each generation and shrank time to a lifetime (p. 4). Terrain and rivers threatened unity, and "some thought" geography might overwhelm the republic (p. 4).
- **The question:** how to "cement the union" (p. 4). Carey simplifies the answer: "the word and the wheel", meaning print and civil engineering. The less optimistic called the result cultural hegemony (domination) (p. 4). This stressed literacy, the press and education, and set local life somewhat apart from national life, a balance problem "ever since" (p. 4).
- Carey's contrast: republican unity was to be achieved technologically by space-binding communication. Republican character and virtue were to come from the time-binding power of oral speech and discourse (p. 4).
- American society did, here and there, approximate the Greek ideal, hence the pull of the New England town meeting. The eastern states were organized around the horse rather than the foot, and Jefferson's "democracy of the middle landscape" was local in emphasis (pp. 4–5).
- Carey's central point: from the outset, American discourse has held two contradictory notions of communication, one from print and transport and one from the voice. The First Amendment's juxtaposition of assembly, speech and press symbolizes this without resolving it (p. 5).
- **Madison (Federalist 10 and 14):** improved communications (canals and the press) would efface distance and facilitate continental democracy. Geography would assist rather than hinder union (p. 5). A constitutionally protected technology would amplify democratic debate and serve as a check on government (p. 5). Carey calls this the "transmission or transportation solution", embodied in the phrase "the communication between east and west" (p. 5).
- **Jefferson (via Trachtenberg, paraphrased):** in 1806 he announced a "progress of improvement" with roads and public higher education. Henry Adams pointed out that this plan contained the crown of Jefferson's hopes. Jefferson expected new channels of communication to make the lines of separation disappear and cement the union (pp. 5–6).
- **Gallatin (1808 report):** federal control of internal improvements, because the benefits were national. He held that good roads and canals shorten distances and unite remote quarters through a community of interests (p. 6).
- **Carey's assessment:** the policy's consequences were ambiguous. It secured some benefits but frustrated some precious hopes, especially the agrarian republic (p. 6). The episode established a creed recited at each new advance in technology: technology would make it possible to erect the vivid democracy of the Greek city-state on a continental scale (p. 6).
- Carey writes that in North America technology is "not only artifact but actor", and that "machines… have teleological insight" (p. 6). The latest technology is always the occasion of "voyages outward in space but backward in time": restoration as much as progress (p. 6).

*Intuition note:* In Carey's account, the creed was that transport and communication technology would let the vivid, Greek-style democracy exist on a continental scale.

**Hope and fear (pp. 6–7).**
- Technology and communication created hope of economic, political and cultural unity: small markets, political units and cultural enclaves would be collected into one (p. 6).
- The same technology and geography stimulated fear: factionalism or "contagions of demagogic enthusiasm". The lines could "run backward", like "a nervous system in reverse", collecting antidemocratic energies in the provinces and concentrating them in the capitals (p. 6).
- Carey calls hope and fear "the systolic and diastolic beats of the culture" (p. 6).
- **Madison's argument:** Madison labored to show that "extent of territory" would allow us to have the hope without the fear. A larger sphere takes in more parties and interests. A common majority motive to invade others' rights becomes less probable. If one exists, it is harder for those who feel it to discover their own strength and act in unison (pp. 6–7).
- **Wills (as cited):** he warns against reading Madison's "extent of territory" too literally, as if bad roads gave both political unity and virtue. He suggests that reading would expose Madison's argument to refutation by the telegraph (p. 7). Carey comments that this is the refutation many read into electronic communication and the "great illiterate media" of film and broadcasting (p. 7).

**Carey's stance and plan of the book (p. 7).**
- Carey shares the hopes and fears and lives with the contradictions. Following Shaw: "if you can't get the skeleton out of the closet you might as well make it dance" (p. 7).
- The essays use contradictions and ambiguities as a resource, to "increase the precision with which we vex one another" (Geertz's phrase) (p. 7).
- *Part I:* culture, cultural studies, and attitudes to communication and technology that Carey hopes go beyond "happy pastorals of progress or grim narratives of power and domination". *Part II:* narratives we tell about ourselves; technology is "thoroughly cultural from the outset" (p. 7).
- Aim: to show that media are not merely instruments of will and purpose but "definite forms of life" that reproduce contradictions in thought, action and social relations (p. 7).
- The essays began as speeches and seminars over eighteen years. They are episodic, sometimes in "irritable counterpoint", and more a running argument than a neat structure (p. 7).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates Madison's "extend the sphere" argument and Carey's hope/fear reading.
- Five towns, 100 voters each, so 500 in all. Interest X has 60 voters in town 1 and 10 in each of towns 2–5.
- Separate towns: in town 1, X is 60/100 = 60%, a local majority that could invade others' rights.
- Joined as one republic: X has 60 + 4×10 = 100 voters, and 100/500 = 20%, below 50%. This is Madison's hope: a larger sphere makes a common majority motive less probable.
- The fear, "lines run backward": suppose the lines let a demagogue recruit X to 260 voters. Then 260/500 = 52%, a national majority. The same lines that cemented the union now help those who share a motive to find their strength and act in unison.

## Questions this raises
- If media change is an "old story", what evidence would show a real break, and what counts as continuity?
- How can print and oral discourse each "fill" the other, and how would we observe this today?
- Is Wills's "refutation by the telegraph" a refutation of Madison, or only of a literal reading of him?
- How does the space-binding/time-binding contrast relate to the need to "ritualize and stabilize experience" (p. 2)?
