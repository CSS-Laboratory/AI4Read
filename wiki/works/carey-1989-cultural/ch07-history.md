# The History of the Future

## L0 — One line
Talk of "the future" works in Anglo-American public life as exhortation, prophecy and participation ritual, and, the authors argue, its futurist forms often promise that new technology will dissolve politics and power, which in practice it has not done.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey and Quirk ask what job the idea of "the future" does in American and British public life. They answer that it works in three ways: as exhortation to keep faith (fairs, speeches), as prophecy (futurist literature), and as a participation ritual (computer-based planning, polling, "games"). In the futurist forms they examine, new communication technology is offered as the cure for social conflict, while politics, elites and power drop out of view. Predictions fail, but new ones replace them. The authors call this the "futurean mirage." An afterword adds that the future now behaves like the past in pastoral writing, a receding horizon. They call this "nostalgia for the future."

**Prerequisites:** basic 19th–20th-century Anglo-American history (Industrial Revolution, telegraph, radio, computers); the idea of an ideology; no math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The authors claim modern scientific forecasters occupy the role of ancient astrologer-priests: a caste with special knowledge of the future and a claim to order human affairs (p. 133).
- They distinguish three ways "the future" speaks: exhortation, prophecy, and ritual of participation. The afterword calls them transpositions of one another (pp. 134, 152–153).
- Exhortation (e.g. the 1876 Centennial) invites people to "discount the present for the future," and so to stay confident in existing institutions while real conflicts go unmentioned (pp. 134–138).
- Futurist literature repeatedly pictures the newest communication or transport technology as a lasting solution and a sublime landscape of harmony. Its record of failure is long (Garvey, Birkenhead, Geddes, Mumford, Toffler, Reich) (pp. 139–145).
- Each "communications revolution" has been expected to bring community and democracy. The authors say these hopes have repeatedly miscarried, and that participation systems risk coopting citizens into a technical apparatus (pp. 146–148).
- "Monopoly of knowledge" has two senses: control of data, and control of the paradigm that decides what counts as a fact. The authors say futurists confuse information with knowledge (pp. 148–150).
- The "futurean mirage" treats the future as already out there and reaching back into the present. The authors say past projections have foreclosed real open futures (pp. 150–151).
- The afterword says the future is now a receding horizon like the pastoral past, and "nostalgia for the future" seems the more pernicious pastoral because it is less self-conscious (pp. 152–154).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 133–134)
- Polak traces the human concern with the future to Delphic oracles and astrological priesthoods. The authors say the *modern* history of the future begins with science and the age of exploration. Precise clocks and telescopes let a "secular priesthood" seize the idea of a perfect future, a zone beyond ordinary history and geography (p. 133).
- Continuity claim (authors): ancient astrologers and modern scientists are both elevated castes that profess special knowledge of the future and claim "eminent domain" over it. Astrologers used predictable planets to order the calendar and agriculture. Modern scientists use their ability to predict narrow, closed systems to claim the right to predict and order all human futures (p. 133).
- The future as a *prophetic form* has a long history. As a predictable region of experience it "never appears": it is always awaited (p. 133).
- Guiding question, borrowed from "Does the past have a future?": "What sort of a past has the future had?" (p. 134).
- Three functions in American and British life in the last two centuries (p. 134):
  1. **Exhortation**: the future as reason to renew optimism ("keep faith"), found in expositions, world fairs, speeches and national goals.
  2. **Prophecy**: in "the politics of literary prophecy," the future fulfils a particular ideology. Past and present are rewritten to show that certain policies and technologies will bring a new age.
  3. **Participation ritual**: with information-processing and decision technologies, collecting data and letting the public extrapolate trends and make choices is treated as a way to cleanse confusion and human fallibility ("technological exorcism").

### I. Exhortation and prophecy (pp. 134–145)

**The Centennial Exhibition, Philadelphia 1876 (pp. 134–136).**
- Its stated purpose was to show American unity eleven years after the Civil War. The main attraction was the Hall of Machinery: thirteen acres of machines driven by pulleys, shafts and belts from a giant Corliss engine. President Grant opened the fair by turning the levers, assisted by Dom Pedro of Brazil (pp. 134–135).
- Grant said progress so far had gone into subduing nature and building industry, and that the nation would soon rival older nations in science, arts and law. The fair looked ahead to 1976 (p. 135).
- Counter-record (authors): a worldwide depression after 1873. The "communications revolution" of the 1830s had integrated markets, so failures spread. Benson is cited for an international agrarian market, depression and discontent. Further events: the Molly Maguires hanged in 1877, troops sent to the South in 1876, Susan Anthony's Women's Declaration of Independence on 4 July, Frederick Douglass's difficulty reaching the stand, the spread of Jim Crow, and news of Custer's defeat nine days before 4 July (p. 135).
- Race and ethnic relations, democracy, the industrial proletariat and depression did not enter the official rhetoric, which looked at "Tomorrow" (p. 136).
- The authors say the same style continues. Commissions on national goals and the year 2000 appear, and the same problems "marred" the bicentennial. Only the symbols changed (satellites, computers, information utilities in place of steam engines and dynamos). They quote Kallen: "the doctrine and discipline of pioneering made art" (p. 136).

**Why America (authors' explanation, pp. 136–137).**
- The creation of the United States was an attempt to outrun history. Discovering America moved utopia "from literature" into life (p. 136).
- Belief in a dispensation from European experience first appeared in religion. In the nineteenth century, technology was treated as analogous to the spread of American religion, and canals, railways and the telegraph became "missionary activity." America's mission became winning the world to an absolute truth, first religious, then technical (p. 136).
- When the future failed, a *new* future was invoked to patch the old one. Leaders appealed to Americans to keep faith in the future "as such," as a solvent that would justify present sacrifice (pp. 136–137).

**Examples of exhortation (pp. 137–138).**
- The Beards, writing in 1940, saw social destiny in electricity, the internal combustion engine and radio. Electricity would emancipate people and integrate city and country (p. 137).
- Roosevelt in the Depression: "We say that we are a people of the future…" (p. 137). At the 1933 Chicago exposition he spoke of a "Century of even greater progress" (p. 137).
- Mills (cited by the authors): the more antagonisms must be suffered, the more the future is drawn on "as a source of pseudo-unity and synthetic morale" (p. 137).
- **Nevins' view:** American unity comes from shared expectations, not shared doctrines. Hope for the future is "the great unifying sentiment," and it must be maintained (p. 138).
- **Authors' reply:** this ideology can serve as "false consciousness," deflecting attention from conflicts over wealth, status and control of technology to a future where such problems "cannot exist." Nixon's National Goals Research Staff is an example (p. 138).
- The future functions much like the "invisible Hand of Providence" in earlier dreams of heavenly cities. Huxley, whom the authors cite, compared it to the Methodist sermon of hard times now and reward later, which worked as an alternative to political revolution. The salvation is now terrestrial (pp. 138–139).

> **Intuition note.** The authors read speeches and fairs about "the future" as work done in the present: they keep people confident while real conflicts go unaddressed. The case rests on contrast between rhetoric and the record, such as the 1876 events.

**Prophecy: futurist literature (pp. 139–145).**
- Common themes (authors): the newest communication and transport technologies are seen as a lasting solution and a break with history, and the future landscape is one of balance, harmony and peace (p. 139).
- *The Reign of George VI, 1900–25* (1763; first major technological forecast in English, per Clarke): the future as a perfected George III order and Pax Britannica, built on better communication and transport (p. 139).
- After the 1851 Great Exhibition, optimism imagined a global community of interests as a by-product of trade and empire. Some dissenters saw that industrialization would dwarf man and nature. Promised gifts included freedom from drudgery and an end to war, "for which we are still waiting" (pp. 139–140).
- Garvey, *The Silent Revolution* (1852): the world as the Great Exhibition writ large. The railway is "master of ceremonies" introducing classes to each other. The authors say Garvey shared the mistaken notion of most futurists that social conflict results from insufficient communication and isolation. He foresaw "a perfect network of electric filaments" analogous to a nervous system. The authors read his ulterior motive as loyalty to the regime and silence about reform: the "silent revolution" substitutes for social revolution (p. 140).
- The authors say American futurism such as Brzezinski's "Technetronic Society" draws on the British Pax Americana tradition (p. 140).
- Birkenhead, *The World in 2030* (1930): the "death of a society" would be settled by technology, not politics. Electronic communication would revive Greek city-state democracy through broadcast debates and instant polling. But in his analysis government should probably go to expert specialists, with electronic consultation a formality, "a guise of democracy" (p. 141). The authors sum up his world as an 1830 Tory mentality given technocratic expression (p. 141).
- The British intelligentsia, even Liberals, Fabians and Haldane, conceived the future solely in terms of empire. H. G. Wells was initially in a circle that viewed the Empire as "the pacific precursor of a practical World State" and the royal military, equipped with the latest communication and transport technology, as forerunners of a "world police" (p. 141).
- **Alternative strand:** Kropotkin's "industrial village" (electricity replacing the huge steam engine) and Geddes, who split the machine age into a *paleotechnic* age (smoke, steam) and a *neotechnic* age (electricity). Geddes aimed at a "Eutopia" and expected politics, parties and ideologies to pass, with "peace armies" of "university militants" (pp. 141–142). Mumford credits Geddes with introducing the future as a "legally bounded terrain" in social thought (p. 142).
- Mumford evaluated Geddes's neglect of political facts as "the critical oversight" in his view of the future. The authors add that Geddes's future rested on other errors: the new technology as applied increased centralization and concentration, and extended the control of imperial power centers over indigenous cultures (p. 142).
- Mumford, *Technics and Civilization* (1934): he attributed "revolutionary changes" to electricity itself, including a cleaned landscape and a radio-based political unity "as close as … in the tiniest cities of Attica" (p. 143).
- Irony (authors): dams eroded the environment, the megalopolis grew, automation was predicted more than realized, the C.I.O. drive began as Mumford wrote of the end of the working class, and it was an age of dictators. Mumford later admitted a "miscarriage of the machine" and that belief in electricity as a revolutionary force was mistaken (p. 143).
- Toffler, *Future Shock* (1971) revives these themes: a society that has "broken irretrievably with the past," automation "more important than the industrial revolution," clean silent industries, a "new organization man" replacing class conflict. In "town halls of the future," dissident minorities are coopted into future-planning games and dissent is funneled into support for technology (pp. 143–144).
- **TVA vs. Reich.** The authors compare the 1930s celebration of the TVA with Reich's *Greening of America* (1970) (p. 144). Hutchinson (1937) called the TVA a "Revolution by Electricity" with an "absence of politics." The authors say its record reversed this: technocratic structures, company-town psychology, strip-mining, alignment with the military-industrial complex. "The machine again became the real counterrevolutionary" (pp. 144–145).
- Reich: "the machine itself has begun to do the work of revolution." The authors judge its manifestations "illusory or ephemeral": the counterculture extends the entertainment industries, and the record industry collects "the real cultural power." Reich cites Woodstock and is silent on Altamont (p. 145).
- Ickes (1944) mocked TVA enthusiasts for thinking they could breathe life into democracy by chanting "TVA, TVA, TVA." The authors call the "greening of America" a similar chanting exercise. The shaping of the future still follows past lines toward concentration and centralization (p. 145).

> **Intuition note.** The authors read these forecasts as repeating one pattern, in which a new technology is said to end the old problems. They say the old problems were still present when the promises were later checked (e.g. the TVA, Mumford's own admission).

### II. The third communications revolution and participation (pp. 145–151)

- Wiener (quoted): this is "the age of communication and control." Engineering now concerns "the accurate reproduction of a signal" (pp. 145–146).
- The authors' three revolutions: (1) printing (mechanized information, spread literacy, enlarged empire); (2) electricity joining production and transmission (telegraph and telephone to television); (3) linking storage and retrieval with telephone, TV and computer, making "information utilities" (p. 146).
- Their claim: the revolutionary appeal does not lie in the prosaic facts (more information, faster, farther). It lies in the supposed power to create new community, new politics and a "new man." Printing was expected to end ignorance and provincialism, and telegraph and radio to bind peoples in peace (p. 146). Westin (quoted): the "cybernetic revolution" would make policy options clear, outcomes predicted and "the feedback mechanism from society" so effective that man could solve society's central problems (p. 146).
- Futurists portray the computer-telephone-TV system as the ultimate communications machine, combining the speed and intimacy of dialogue, the memory of history and a fully informed electorate (p. 146).
- **Machines as agents (authors):** industrialists, technocrats and scientists are portrayed as "self-abnegating servants of power" merely accommodating themselves to the truth and the future as determined by the advance of science and technology, not as an elite usurping power. "It is the machines that possess teleological insight" (p. 147). The public is invited into a "technical ritual" of planning through World Games and electronic Delphic techniques (p. 147).
- Democracy by electricity is old. Lowell (1860s) saw newspapers and telegraph making the nation a "vast town-meeting" (p. 147). Fuller (1963) proposed daily secret nationwide voting by machine so that democracy would be saved (pp. 147–148).
- **Authors' assessment:** such demands would coopt citizens into the technical apparatus "with only the illusion of control" (p. 148). Michael proposes republicanism with specialists as mediators. The authors argue that, given engineered complexity, public involvement becomes "a mere ritual" legitimating a new scientific elite. They add: if either of these modes of participation is seriously entertained, then whatever form of government results, "it will be called democracy." Futurist writing does not consider the polity itself, because political community is near collapse under rationalization, centralization and uniformity. Mills at least recognized elitism. Technocratic elites presume the atomization of society (p. 148).
- **Two senses of "monopoly of knowledge"** (authors, p. 148 onward):
  1. *Control of factual information.* Codes and storage let groups deny access to others. If information flows at unequal rates, what is future for one group is already past for another. **Pool's view:** computers can decentralize by giving every part of an organization the information now at the center (p. 149).
  2. *Control of the whole system of thought* (paradigm) that decides what can be factual and what standards judge truth. Computer enthusiasts may share data. What they monopolize is "the approved, certified, sanctioned, official mode of thought," the definition of reasonable (p. 149).
- This is possible, the authors say, because of a "persistent confusion between information and knowledge" (p. 149). Futurists assume reality consists of recordable bits, so a receiver could in principle know everything. The authors reply that knowledge is paradigmatic and not "given in experience as data." There is no information about the world "devoid of conceptual systems." Paradigms sit inside information systems as metainformation (programs, statistical devices, retrieval codes, technical theories, binary opposition) (pp. 149–150).
- Paradigms are not independent of outside biases and purposes. Computer information systems are "emanations of attitudes and hopes," vested in institutions such as the Rand Corporation. Class, status and power cannot be dissolved into a future where they are presumed absent (p. 150).

> **Intuition note.** The authors say there is no information about the world apart from conceptual systems (paradigms). A system predefines what counts as a fact, so controlling the system is a more stringent monopoly than controlling the data.

- **Summary (authors):** the third revolution has the "same seeds of miscarriage" as earlier ones. It invites a "ritual of control" in which fascination with technology masks politics. Futurist literature is a "cultural strategy" to mobilize people toward predefined ends by prescribed means. Past and present facts that contradict the plan are proclaimed "obsolete" or examples of cultural lag. The futurist can rewrite the future because there is no record to check it against, and can postpone the day of consummation (p. 150).
- **"Futurean mirage":** the illusion that the future is already out there, converging with the last stage of history and acting as an agent reaching back to remove obstacles. The authors say this future is "a carefully prepared predestination" set by technological imperatives, not by human needs (pp. 150–151).
- The "great irony": no real future is left open. Past projections have "foreclosed" earlier open futures and exhausted resources. Emphasis on speed over space has eclipsed local community. The "mythology of the powerhouse" promised decentralized economies but has tended toward commercial empires and "cosmetically treated landscapes" (p. 151). The authors close with a question: is it not time to rejoin the future "to the real past and the realities of the present"? (p. 151)

### III. Afterthought and afterword (pp. 151–154)
- The future has become a school subject (a 1979 study found 280 courses; the number later estimated to have doubled by the mid-1980s). It has also become a "subject" in a psychoanalytic sense: a speaker "interpellated into discourse," telling us where we are going (p. 151).
- Early images of the future were utopian: out of space and generally out of time. With the Enlightenment, "nowhere became somewhere" and "no time became sometime," the Future (p. 151).
- Now the future has joined the pastoral tradition, in inverted form. Raymond Williams found the pastoral past a "continuously receding horizon," always two generations back (1920s looked to 1880s, 1880s to 1830s). The authors say the same holds for the future: never actualized, a "perils of Pauline" future (p. 152).
- Hence "nostalgia for the future." Intellectuals guard against nostalgia for the past, but the future "has merely displaced the past" without changing the social import of the images (p. 152).
- The three enunciations (restated): exhortation (world fairs, commissions, computer festivals), prophecy (forecasts, science fiction; what is "inscribed in the DNA of history" will arrive effortlessly), and ritual of participation (the "Future Game": Delphi techniques, computer games, polls, commissions), where "everything of significance … has already been decided" and participation is "ritualistic in the debased sense" (pp. 152–153).
- Donoghue on Whitman (quoted): disengaging from the past, "all surface, no depth." The authors link this to postmodernism as a futurism of "politically displaced intellectuals" responding to new technology. They grant that computers and satellites have real effects, such as simulating complex environments. But postmodernism too often "evacuates the present" into such a landscape (p. 153).
- Mumford's "fallacy of systems" (reducing life to one set of principles) needs a landscape outside history where machines alone have teleological insight. Technology and politics are then removed from democratic control, and "morbid or titillated resignation" follows (pp. 153–154).
- **Conclusion:** the subtext of futurist literature is the loss of an effective public sphere, Dewey's "conjoint life of the polity." Left or Right, postmodern or postindustrial, the future evades the role our imaginings of past and future play in controlling the present. Nostalgia for the future seems more pernicious because it is less self-conscious (p. 154).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the "receding horizon" (pp. 150–152). Suppose a forecaster publishes, every ten years, "full automation in ten years."

| Year of forecast | Target date | Has target date arrived by 2010? | Fulfilled? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 2000 | yes (2000 ≤ 2010) | no |
| 2000 | 2010 | yes (2010 ≤ 2010) | no |
| 2010 | 2020 | no (2020 > 2010) | not yet |

- Distance from forecast year to target: 2000 − 1990 = 10, 2010 − 2000 = 10, 2020 − 2010 = 10. It is always 10, so the horizon never gets closer.
- Checkable forecasts by 2010: 2. Fulfilled: 0. The score is 0 of 2.
- Yet the newest forecast still looks ten years away. If nobody compares forecasts across years, no failure shows. This matches the authors' remark that "there is no record to compare it with" (p. 150).

## Questions this raises
- How can we tell a forecast that is "exhortation" from one that is a sincere, checkable prediction?
- Does the authors' claim that futurists confuse information and knowledge apply to current data-driven social science?
- If participation in planning is "ritualistic," what features would make public participation real?
- Does the case against 1876 and 1930s rhetoric carry over to present claims about new media and democracy?
