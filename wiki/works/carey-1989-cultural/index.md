# Carey, J. W. (1989). A Cultural Approach to Communication. In *Communication as Culture: Essays on Media and Society*. Unwin Hyman / Routledge (essay orig. 1975). Reprinted as ch. 4 in Craig & Muller (2007).

## In one paragraph
Carey argues that communication should not be studied only as the transmission of messages across space for control. It should also be seen as a ritual, symbolic process that sustains shared beliefs and community through time, and that produces, maintains, repairs and transforms reality. Seen this way, American communication research and its "effects" tradition hide a utilitarian view of people, and a cultural approach that interprets meaning, practice and power should replace them. The book also shows how technologies such as the telegraph, electricity and computers carry ideology. Talk of an "electronic revolution" or "the future" promises community and harmony, while centralizing power and hiding politics. Carey draws on Dewey, Geertz and Innis to make this case.

## The path through the work
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch00-introduction]] — Carey argues that American talk of a "communications revolution" repeats an old story: from the start, the nation tried to bind space with transport and print technology while also relying on older, oral ways of binding time and character, and American discourse has held these two contradictory notions of communication from the outset.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch01-cultural]] — Carey argues that communication should be studied less as the transmission of messages across space and more as a ritual, symbolic process by which reality is produced, maintained, repaired and transformed.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch02-mass]] — Carey argues that communication research should go beyond explaining symbolic forms by causes or functions and also treat human action as a text whose meanings it interprets, following Clifford Geertz.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch03-reconceiving]] — Carey argues that Dewey's view of communication as a conversation that constitutes a shared reality should replace Lippmann's view of communication as the transmission of accurate pictures, and that reality is a scarce resource, so the power to define and display it is a basic form of power.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch04-overcoming]] — Carey argues that mass communication research should drop the "effects tradition" and its hidden utilitarian model of society, and adopt cultural studies, which treats science and ideology as parts of culture and keeps power and authority in view, with communication as the process by which societies are made and remade.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch05-mythos]] — Carey and Quirk argue that the claim that electronics will bring decentralized, harmonious community is the latest version of an old American "sublime" rhetoric that repeatedly disguised centralizing technology, so intellectuals should demythologize it and work through politics.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch06-space]] — Carey argues that Harold Innis, by showing that every communication medium is biased toward controlling space or time, corrected the Chicago School's romantic faith in communication technology and gave a historical, critical theory of communication.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch07-history]] — Talk of "the future" works in Anglo-American public life as exhortation, prophecy and participation ritual, and, the authors argue, its futurist forms often promise that new technology will dissolve politics and power, which in practice it has not done.
- [[carey-1989-cultural/ch08-technology]] — By separating communication from transportation, the telegraph let information outrun goods, and this reshaped commerce, language, empire and the ordinary experience of time and space (and supplied the transmission model of communication).

## Big ideas across units
- **Two views of communication: transmission and ritual.** Transmission means sending messages across space for control. Ritual means sustaining shared beliefs and community in time. The contrast is introduced, defined, applied to research traditions and then traced through technology. Units: ch00, ch01, ch02, ch03, ch05, ch06, ch08.
- **Communication produces reality.** Symbols and models are both *of* and *for* reality. Social science is therefore reflexive, there are multiple realities, and reality is a scarce resource whose definition is a form of power. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch04.
- **Binding space versus binding time.** Media and cultures lean toward controlling space or toward continuity in time. This runs from the early republic through Innis's "bias" of media to the telegraph's effects on markets and standard time. Units: ch00, ch05, ch06, ch08.
- **Technology is cultural and carries ideology.** Media are "forms of life", not just instruments. The "electrical sublime" and the telegraph as a "thing to think with" show how technology shapes everyday consciousness. Units: ch00, ch05, ch07, ch08.
- **Technological promise hides power.** Hopes of community and harmony from steam, electricity, electronics and futurism recur. They disguise centralization and monopolies of knowledge, and politics drops out of view. Units: ch00, ch05, ch06, ch07.
- **A critique of mainstream communication research.** Transmission-based research dissolves the content of symbols. It rests on causal or functional explanation, a hidden utilitarianism and objectivism, and it casts people as controlled objects. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch04.
- **An interpretive alternative: cultural studies.** It treats action as a text, is interested in meaning, practice, conflict and power, and keeps the field's methods while changing its vocabulary. Units: ch02, ch03, ch04.
- **Democracy and the public.** The question runs from republics held together by speech and memory, through Dewey's conversation against Lippmann's pictures, to Innis's call for oral tradition and a public sphere. Units: ch00, ch03, ch04, ch06, ch07.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch01 holds the core transmission/ritual distinction and Carey's definition of communication. ch00 frames the contradiction that drives the book, and is short. ch03 states the cultural approach most compactly: reality as a scarce resource, and Dewey against Lippmann.
- **Dependencies:** ch02 and ch04 develop the critique and the case for cultural studies that ch01 and ch03 open. ch02 and ch03 both restate the transmission/ritual contrast. ch05 and ch06 belong together: ch05 draws on Innis's space/time argument, and ch06 gives that argument fully. ch08 returns to the "electrical sublime" from ch05 and gives a detailed historical case of the transmission model. ch07 takes up the futurist rhetoric and "monopoly of knowledge" themes from ch05 and ch06.
- **Can wait on a first pass:** ch04 mostly addresses resistance inside the research field (positivists, phenomenologists, the effects tradition), so a reader mainly after the transmission/ritual idea can skim it. The detailed historical cases in ch07 and the market and time-zone sections of ch08 can also be skimmed. The units on technology can be read in any order once ch01 is understood.
