# Overcoming Resistance to Cultural Studies

## L0 — One line
Carey argues that mass communication research should drop the "effects tradition" and its hidden utilitarian model of society, and adopt cultural studies, which treats science and ideology as parts of culture and keeps power and authority in view, with communication as the process by which societies are made and remade.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Carey considers why American communication research resists cultural studies and argues that it should not. He says the effects tradition carries a twisted utilitarianism. It has failed on its own terms, and it casts people as controlled objects, which is a problem for democracy. He proposes cultural studies: keep the effects tradition's methods and findings, but re-read them in a vocabulary of experience, interaction, conflict, authority and power. He answers two kinds of resistance. Positivists defend science as a support for democracy. Phenomenologists fear that stressing ideology and power flattens the diversity of experience. Carey rejects both objections. His closing question is how societies produce and reproduce themselves, and his answer is through communication.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a "paradigm" (a shared framework of a research field); basic familiarity with behaviorism, functionalism and Marxism by name; utilitarianism (the view that people act to maximize pleasure or happiness); "positivist" (here: a defender of a law-seeking positive science); "phenomenologist" (here: someone who studies people's lived experience and consciousness); the "effects tradition" (research on what media do to audiences); the mass-communication "effects" question.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Hidden utilitarianism.** Media research inherits utility theory but moves "utility" out of the individual's head into genes, environment or society. Behaviorism and functionalism are the main routes (pp. 68–69).
- **No neutral small studies.** Empirical studies borrow concepts from wider social theories and serve as evidence for them. Carey says this is unavoidable (p. 69).
- **Failure and democratic cost.** Carey says effects research is intellectually stagnant. Where it succeeds, it presents people as law-governed objects, which is antidemocratic (pp. 70–71).
- **Reform, not demolition.** Methods and the classic literature stay. The field's self-image changes, with less talk of paradigms and rigor and more of achievement and originality (pp. 71–72).
- **Lineage of cultural studies.** Carey names Williams, Hoggart, Weber, Dewey, Park and symbolic interactionism, plus Riesman, Mills, Innis and Burke. He also discusses the British (Hall) turn to ideology, noting both similarities and differences (pp. 72–75).
- **Positivist resistance.** Science once protected democracy. Carey replies that democracy needs no scientific guarantor and that every vocabulary, science included, carries moral and political commitments (pp. 76–79).
- **Phenomenological resistance.** Carey warns that cultural studies could become a "dismal science" if it reduced everything to power, but says power need not be excluded (pp. 79–81).
- **Conflict is irremovable.** Contradictions persist in any society. Culture should not be treated as unconscious, automatic or coercive (pp. 81–83).
- **Conclusion.** Cultural studies uses the mass media as a site for the question of how societies are produced and reproduced. Communication is the answer (pp. 83–84).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The chapter has no printed subheadings. The subheadings below are mine, in the author's order.)

### A. The framework to be dropped (pp. 68–71)
**Aim.** Carey recalls his earlier argument that students of mass media should abandon the "positive science" of communication, which seeks laws of behavior and universal functions of media. He quotes Dewey's phrase "neurotic quest for certainty". He wants to "unload" the "effects tradition" and its "power and anxiety model" of communication (p. 68).

**Utilitarianism.** On this theory:
- Desires are individual and subjective, so they are unknowable or exogenous (given from outside the theory).
- Action tries to maximize utility, meaning pleasure or happiness (pp. 68–69).
- Economics is built on this principle (p. 69).

The rest of the social sciences dislike how utility theory assumes away social order. They "desubjectivize" utility and relocate it in our genes (Social Darwinism, sociobiology), our environment (behaviorism) or our society (functionalism) (p. 69). Behaviorism and functionalism underpin mass communication research. One big exception to the lack of economic or biological influence is that assumptions about language and communication back the belief that the quest for utility can produce progressive order. Carey's examples are the theory of representation and the self-righting "free market of ideas" (p. 69). Communication research thus carries an *objective* utilitarianism: the utilities of ecology, population survival or the given social order (p. 69).

**Studies "articulate" with wider theory.** Small empirical studies are not detached. They articulate *out* by borrowing language and assumptions from the wider intellectual environment. They articulate *in* by serving as evidence for or against social theories. Carey's examples: "attitude", "effect", "uses" and "gratifications" come from utility theory, and effects evidence supports liberal, utilitarian or pluralist mass-society theory (p. 69). "There are no neutral positions" (p. 69).

*Intuition note:* Even a narrow survey rests on a picture of what people and society are. Carey says that picture is hidden utilitarianism, and that there are no neutral positions on such questions.

**Critique.** Carey says a large philosophical literature attacks the behavioral and functional sciences, but he wants to argue "in the small" (p. 70). His claims:
- The field has not withered away, contrary to Berelson's prediction. It is institutionally successful but intellectually stagnant (p. 70).
- Effects research produced no agreed laws or functions of sufficient power, and has become "the solemn repetition of the indubitable". Its retreat into method suggests that "doing it right guarantees getting it right" (p. 70).
- It would be a greater social failure if it were a greater intellectual success. Utility theory leaves democracy a dilemma: if desires are separate and subjective, how does cooperation arise? This is usually assumed away, for example with the "invisible hand" (p. 70).
- Objective utility theorists answer that genes, environment or social norms make us democrats. Carey calls his own phrasing here "a bit of burlesque", thinks they are "a little too optimistic", and says the price is losing the self-governing subject (pp. 70–71).
- Lippmann's *Public Opinion* and Lazarsfeld's work express this dilemma. *The People's Choice* "turns out not to be the people's choice at all but the choice of an index of socioeconomic status". Laws of behavior are antidemocratic either because they show a subject unfit for democracy or because they can be used to control subjects (p. 71).

Carey says philosophical criticism is met with silence, and waiting for "our Newton" is like waiting for Godot (p. 71).

*Intuition note:* If behavior follows laws that people do not control, democratic self-government looks like an illusion. That is why Carey calls the framework antidemocratic.

### B. Reform, not book-burning (pp. 71–72)
- Abandoning the effects tradition does not mean abandoning methods or counting, or ignoring the classics. The literature must be "deconstructed" and reinterpreted, and the methods redeployed (p. 71).
- Carey says there will be no progress that does not engage the effects tradition, which, at least for its major practitioners, tried to deal with serious problems of American politics and culture, and is now part of that culture (p. 71).
- What must change is the field's self-image and its claims about its knowledge. Following Rorty, Carey would have the field talk less about paradigms, methods and rigor, and more about concrete achievements and originality. It would use more the vocabulary of poetry and politics, and feel more solidarity with society and fellow students (p. 72).
- The field would also drop the alternating self-image of "neutral discoverers" and "new priesthood", and treat truth as, in William James's phrase, what is "good by way of belief" (p. 72).
- Cultural studies is the vehicle for this. At minimum it means recentering the concept of culture relative to the media and disposing of "effect" and "function" (p. 72).
- Carey admits there is no good map yet. He only offers an alternative vocabulary and literature (p. 72).

### C. Lineage of cultural studies (pp. 72–75)
- **Mass-culture debate.** Williams and Hoggart applied the anthropological conception of culture to industrial society (Williams's "long revolution") (pp. 72–73).
- **Weber.** He sought a phenomenology of industrial societies (their subjective life and ends of action) and an analysis of dominance and authority. He called this "cultural science" (p. 73).
- **"Studies" over "science."** Carey prefers "studies" to "science" because "science" took on an honorific sense that matches Plato's knowledge/opinion distinction (citing Kuhn, 1983). Cultural studies aims first to dissolve that distinction, along with objective/subjective and primary/secondary. "Studies" is modest and "keeps us from confusing the fish story with the fish" (p. 73).
- **American strand.** Dewey, symbolic interactionism and Park's analysis of mass culture. The Chicago School transplanted Weber within pragmatism but lost his emphasis on authority, conflict and domination, which Carey wants restored (p. 73).
- **Concepts.** Carey lists experience, subjectivity, interaction, conflict, authority, domination, class, status and power (p. 73). The minor 1940s–50s tradition of Riesman, Mills, Innis and Burke was historic, interpretive and critical. Cultural studies tries to reclaim it (pp. 73–74).
- **Ethnocentrism.** Social analysis is formulated within national traditions, and importing analysis wholesale is "a pose". Habermas, Foucault, Giddens and Bourdieu can contribute only if embedded in native lines of argument (p. 74).

**Birmingham and Hall (pp. 74–75).** The Centre's work draws on Marxism and structuralism, though not on Frankfurt-style critical theory. Carey says British cultural studies could be called "ideological studies": ideology stands for culture as a whole (synecdoche) (p. 74). Hall's phrase is "the return of the repressed in media studies". On this reading ideology was always the hidden subtext of effects research, and psychological attitude scales masked structural political divisions. "Consensus" was achieved by reading "deviants" out. The positive sciences were part of the social process that masked and sustained the order (p. 74). The "administrative vs critical" contrast is stale, and the real difference is not simply supporting or criticizing the status quo (p. 75).

British and American cultural studies share these features (p. 75):
- Both trace a founding moment to the early 1950s.
- Both draw on the mass-culture debate and on Williams, Hoggart and Thompson.
- Both use symbolic interactionism. In Britain it serves subcultures and deviance. In the US it is a general model of social action.
- Both use Weber. In Britain mainly his concept of legitimation.
- Britain made a detour through French structuralism. Structuralism has made little headway in the US, where information theory and transformational linguistics are stronger.

Carey admires Hall's balance, but he thinks the work is "likely" to strengthen rather than reduce US resistance. He calls that resistance "understandable" but "short-sighted" (p. 75).

### D. Positivist resistance and Carey's reply (pp. 75–79)
**The resistance (as Carey describes it).** Positive science grew up with capitalism and parliamentary democracy. It gave democracy an epistemological ground (truth can transcend opinion) and a model of uncoerced communication. It shored up democracy in the Depression and World War II. So an attack on its self-understanding seems politically insensitive (p. 76).

**Carey's reply.**
- Past service does not guarantee permanent service. After World War II the behavioral and functional sciences took on an antidemocratic character. Laws of behavior and functions of society make subjective life look like epiphenomena (illusory by-products). Only the ruling groups and those who do their bidding (scientific and technical elites) grasp the laws, so that people can be governed, "albeit unconsciously" (pp. 76–77).
- Lippmann first proposed substituting positive science for uncoerced communication. Dewey called *Public Opinion* the greatest indictment of democracy yet written. Carey says the behavioral sciences were central to the Vietnam disaster (p. 77).
- Democracy can be defended by Rorty's "criterionless muddling through": compare societies with tolerance, free inquiry and undistorted communication to those without. No methods need to be named guarantors of Truth (p. 77).
- Cultural studies is a "thinly disguised moral and political vocabulary", but so is every vocabulary, positive science included (Burke: vocabularies carry a rhetoric of attitudes and motives). The fear that cultural studies commits one in advance to a wholly negative verdict on Western democracies and to revolution is "real" but "a little silly" (p. 77).
- The task is not to separate science from morals and politics but to make the connections explicit and defensible, not to hide behind "science says" (p. 77).
- Cultural studies treats ideology and theory as varying forms of expression within one culture. They differ in style, semantics and conditions of expression and reception, not in an a priori contrast of truth and error (p. 78).
- The objective sciences get us into a worse dilemma than utility theory did: a "ruling class of social scientists" managing society on "uncontaminated truth". Following Weber, Carey suspects that such credentials work as class closure. Preferred jobs are Hirsch's "positional goods", valued because scarce and carrying power to monopolize cultural resources. Carey says no one knows how to get out of this fix, but we must first recognize it (p. 78).
- The way out is "plunging science more deeply into culture and ideology" (p. 78).

**Bell and Kristol's *The Public Interest* (1965).** Carey says the founders did not defend positivist theory. They gathered social scientists, used intelligence "irrespective of method and theory" for what Hall would call a hegemonic project (Carey prefers "project of social reconstruction"), and openly admitted the link between ideology and science, despite their "end of ideology" essays. Carey stresses that he is not applauding them (pp. 78–79). The lesson: critics of cultural studies should make explicit the commitments in their own work and analyze the articulations of theory, practice and ideology in the effects tradition. Their answer is to move toward cultural studies (p. 79).

*Intuition note:* Carey says no vocabulary is value-free. So the honest course is to state your values and defend them, not to claim the facts decide.

### E. Phenomenological resistance and Carey's reply (pp. 79–81)
**The resistance (as Carey describes it).** Phenomenologists accept giving up the positivist framework, or at least a division of labor between sciences and humanities. They will write a phenomenology of modern consciousness in relation to the media (Williams's "structure of feeling"), using *verstehen* or hermeneutics. They stop at the early Williams and Hoggart and avoid French structuralism. They resist putting power, conflict, domination or sociostructural elements at the centre (pp. 79–80).

**Carey's reply.** He is "not at all unsympathetic" but calls the resistance "misplaced" (p. 80).
- Ideological analysis can become "another entry of the Platonic", where knowledge/opinion is replaced by knowledge/ideology. If ideology names a whole way of life, diversity is flattened into class and faction conflict (p. 80).
- **Dismal-science analogy.** Utilitarian economics reduced the person to "economic man", and Malthusian logic made acquisition endless. Cultural studies could become a dismal science if it reduces society to the quest for power. That pursuit would be as exhausting and inexhaustible as the pursuit of wealth (pp. 80–81).
- But only a positivist reason keeps a phenomenology from engaging power. Power may enter "the household" but need not "occupy every room" (p. 81).
- Stripping away diversity to find a "univocal structure of ideology and politics" would steamroll subjective consciousness as behaviorism did: do not trade the "Skinner box" for the "Althusserian box" (p. 81).
- The tensions arising from diversity develop in particular relation to the media (p. 81).

### F. Conflict, power and Durkheim (pp. 81–84)
- Carey disagrees with both resistances. Democracy needs no objectivist prop, and we need not abandon ideology or power to respect diversity (pp. 81–82).
- Conflicts and contradictions are integral and irremovable. His examples:
  - Competition among student majors for "positional goods."
  - Parents buying their children places through tuition.
  - Jobs treated as entitlements.
  - Automation widening the gap between mechanical work and glamorous professions.
- Removing one contradiction produces others. Removing the master contradictions of race, class and gender would create "a new master class" (p. 82).
- Ideology and power are central but "less than the whole cloth". Ideology matters more because coercion matters less (p. 82).
- No adequate analysis of power exists. Weber tied power to action and subjectivity. Now power is more often derived from Durkheim, for whom it was invisible, so the analysis is "constantly slipping into a functionalism" (p. 82).
- Durkheim played down power and conflict, using "collective representations" and "collective conscience". He argued that capitalism rests on a precapitalist base ("precontractual elements of contract") and that *Gesellschaft* needs *Gemeinschaft*. In effect he inverted base and superstructure (p. 83).
- Carey calls this useful. But from "culture supports contract" it does not follow that culture is unconscious, irrational, coercive or automatic. To argue that makes one an objective utility theorist or a mechanical Marxist. That leap makes it hard to tell Marxism from functionalism, except that they evaluate quite differently the order being silently integrated (p. 83).

**Conclusion.** Cultural studies need not reduce culture to ideology, social conflict to class conflict, consent to compliance, action to reproduction, or communication to coercion (p. 83). Its advantage is that it abandons an outmoded philosophy of science and centres the mass media as "a site (not a subject or a discipline)" for the question of how societies produce and reproduce themselves. That production is never guaranteed, automatic or mechanical, and its problems are often best revealed in conflict, coercive violence and disorder (pp. 83–84). Societies "at least those with which we are most familiar" are created, maintained and transformed through communication, "the intergraded relations of symbols and social structure" (p. 84).

*Intuition note:* Carey says social order is never guaranteed, automatic or mechanical: people live its reproduction "in all its turmoil and ambiguity" rather than it running silently behind their backs, and he says communication is how societies are created, maintained and transformed (pp. 83–84).

## Toy example
(Ours, not from the text.) Suppose a small survey of 100 voters:

| Group | Voters | Voted A | % A |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-SES | 60 | 45 | 45/60 = 75% |
| Low-SES | 40 | 10 | 10/40 = 25% |

Overall, A gets 45 + 10 = 55 of 100 votes, or 55%.

An "SES index" rule predicts A for high-SES and not-A for low-SES. It is right for 45 high-SES voters and for 40 − 10 = 30 low-SES voters. That is 75 of 100, or 75%.

An effects-tradition reading says: "SES explains the vote". The voter becomes a case of a law, which is the "people's choice as index of SES" worry (p. 71). A cultural-studies reading keeps the numbers but asks different questions. What did voters think they were doing, and how did the media, authority and status shape their view? And which social theory does the claim "SES explains the vote" support? This is the "articulation" Carey describes (p. 69).

## Questions this raises
- If methods stay, what would a cultural-studies reading of an effects study look like in practice? Carey admits there is no map yet (p. 72).

- Is Carey's "ethnocentric" argument about national traditions (p. 74) consistent with borrowing Weber, Durkheim and Rorty?
