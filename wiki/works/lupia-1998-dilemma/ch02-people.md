# How People Learn

## L0 — One line
People who know few political facts can still make reasoned choices, because learning is active and goal-oriented, and because a few cheap, reliable cues plus learned connections can stand in for encyclopedic information.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Many scholars infer from survey results that poorly informed citizens cannot choose in a reasoned way. Lupia and McCubbins reject this. They define a choice as reasoned if it rests on accurate predictions of its consequences. They model attention as a scarce resource with expected benefits and costs. They model memory as a competition among learned "connections" (links between observations and past pleasure or pain). From this they conclude that information is valuable only if it prevents costly mistakes, so ignoring most stimuli is rational. Cheap, reliable cues such as party labels can replace detailed facts. Political trivia questions alone therefore cannot show whether someone chooses in a reasoned way.

**Prerequisites:** expected value (probability times size of loss); the idea of costs and benefits at the margin; the survey finding that citizens know few political facts.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The target view, held by pundits and scholars: citizens who lack information are incapable of reasoned choice (p. 17). The authors say it rests on an erroneous assumption: that reliable prediction is possible only if one knows a detailed set of facts. That would make only "ambulatory encyclopedias" (people who store and quickly retrieve detailed facts about every decision) capable of reasoned choice (p. 18).
- Definitions: knowledge is the ability to predict accurately the consequences of choices. Information is data. Knowledge requires information, but more information does not ensure knowledge (pp. 20, 24–25).
- Calculus of attention: E(Return) = E(Benefit) − E(Opportunity Costs) − E(Transactions Costs). Opportunity costs are the returns forgone by not attending to other stimuli. Transaction costs are the energy needed to turn a stimulus into a useful inference (pp. 25–26). Benefit is zero if a stimulus changes no action, positive if it prevents a costly mistake, and negative if it causes one (p. 26). Additional information is beneficial only if it prevents a costly mistake (or, alternatively, if it causes a reasoned choice) (p. 28).
- Theorem 2.1: more information can have zero or negative benefit or return, and is neither necessary nor sufficient for reasoned choice (p. 27).
- Theorem 2.2: when the environment is complex, people have an incentive to ignore many stimuli. People should use simple, cheap, effective cues when these are available and suffice (p. 29).
- The "cognitive stock market": connections compete in the mind, and those linked to pleasure or pain gain value. Common experience produces shared winners and individual differences produce divergent ones (pp. 32–34).
- A cue's usefulness depends on context. Party labels work in partisan presidential contests, less in primaries or nonpartisan ballots (pp. 35–36).
- Judging capacity for reasoned choice requires more than political trivia questions, and failing them may reveal nothing about decision quality (pp. 20, 36).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction: the claim under attack (pp. 17–21)
**Target view.** Survey research shows citizens cannot recall basic facts, lack consistent ideological understanding and often cannot name their representatives (p. 17). Pundits and scholars conclude that citizens who lack information are incapable of reasoned choice. Lane and Sears are cited for this (p. 17). Similar arguments are made about legislators, who have limited time and so cannot be well informed about most proposals. Many scholars add that this lets expert bureaucrats dominate policymaking (p. 18).

**The authors' definition and reply.** A choice is *reasoned* if it is based on an accurate prediction about its consequence (p. 18). The prevailing conclusion rests on the assumption that reliable prediction is possible *only if* one knows a detailed set of facts. If that were true, only "ambulatory encyclopedias" could choose in a reasoned way. If it is false, people who fail survey questions may still choose in a reasoned way (p. 18).

**Two replacement assumptions.**
1. Learning is active and goal-oriented. Humans usually choose when and what to learn (p. 18).
2. *Connectionism* is the process of connecting current observations to feedback from past experience (p. 19). Example: dark cloud → rain is coming → seek cover if you want to stay dry. This needs no weather report (p. 19).

Without such a process, reasoned choice requires encyclopedic information. With it, reasoned choice requires less (p. 19).

**Sufficiency of simpler fact sets.** Suppose one set of facts is sufficient for a reasoned choice, such as Clinton's position on 100 issues. If there exists another, perhaps simpler, set of facts or connections that is sufficient for the same choice, such as an NEA endorsement, then the first set is not a prerequisite (pp. 19–20).

**Survey implication.** People are generally poor at verbally describing what they discriminate well (Churchland). So even people who choose in a reasoned way should be unable to answer detailed questions. Failing such questions "may reveal nothing" about decision quality (p. 20).

**Four points** (p. 20):
1. Learning is active.
2. Knowledge is the ability to predict accurately the consequences of choices. Information is data from which knowledge may be derived. Knowledge requires information, but large amounts of information do not ensure knowledge.
3. Information is valuable only when it improves the accuracy of predictions about consequences.
4. Reasoned choice requires only accurate predictions about the consequences of choices.

Footnote 7 separates this view from "rational ignorance" (Downs). The authors agree that the costs of acquiring information often outweigh its benefits. They add that the benefit itself may be zero or negative (p. 20).

*Intuition note:* The authors do not deny that citizens know little. They deny that this shows poor choosing. What matters is whether the little a person uses predicts consequences well (pp. 18–20).

### Attention: this is how we learn (pp. 21–30)
**Learning is active.** Learning seems automatic because routine tasks (walking to the office, tying shoelaces) no longer need thought. But each was once new, and we formed connections through trial-like experience (p. 21). The authors give three facts: learning requires effort, effort is scarce, so people choose what and when to learn (p. 22). Attention is a prerequisite for learning and is scarce. Attending to one stimulus is generally at the expense of others (p. 22). Footnote 8 says instincts are automatic reactions such as a blink. It uses the new-glasses example to show that apparently automatic abilities are at least partly learned (p. 21).

**Purpose of attention.** The authors assume scarcity and the capacity to feel pain and pleasure are ubiquitous. If pain and pleasure correlate with environmental factors, people have an incentive to learn about the environment. The purpose of directing attention is to avoid future pain and gain future pleasure, that is, to make reasoned choices (pp. 22–23).

**Rationality (authors' definition).** Rationality is all behaviour directed toward pursuing pleasure and avoiding pain. This includes reflexes (which footnote 11 says are learned too) and complicated choices (p. 23). It does not equate rationality with omniscience. Concern for others is compatible with it (fn. 10). The authors read Kahneman and Tversky's results as showing systematic responses to complexity, which they say are consistent with their definition (fn. 12, p. 23). They agree with Simon that models must include human limits. They deny that bounded rationality implies nonmaximizing behaviour. They cite Jensen and Meckling: maximization is "subject to costs of information and decision making" (p. 24).

**Knowledge.** Reasoned choice needs beliefs of the form "If A, then B" that generate accurate predictions. It does not need fundamental truths about causal processes. A prediction is accurate if the ex ante belief matches the ex post observation (p. 24). Example: correctly predicting the 1948 election winner, or the incentive effects of a tax policy (p. 24). Information is data. One can hold information without knowledge, but not knowledge without information (pp. 24–25). The chain is: reasoned choice requires knowledge, knowledge requires information, information requires attention. Reasoned choice need not require full information or unlimited attention (p. 25).

**Calculus of attention** (p. 25):
$$E(\text{Return}) = E(\text{Benefit}) - E(\text{Opportunity Costs}) - E(\text{Transactions Costs})$$
- *Cognitive opportunity cost*: the returns forgone by not attending to other stimuli (p. 25).
- *Cognitive transaction cost*: the energy needed to process a stimulus into a useful inference. It varies with the stimulus (mother's voice vs. Sumerian) and with perceptual ability (p. 26).
- Benefit: if a stimulus does not change one's action, the benefit is zero. If it changes action and prevents a painful mistake, the benefit is positive. If it changes action and causes a painful mistake, the benefit is negative (p. 26).

$$E(\text{Benefit}) = \sum_m \big(P_{prev}\,E(\text{Mistake}) - P_{cause}\,E(\text{Mistake})\big)$$
Here $E(\text{Mistake})$ is the expected pain from a mistaken choice. $P_{prev}$ is the perceived probability that attending prevents such a mistake. $P_{cause}$ is the perceived probability that attending causes it. The sum runs over all possible mistakes $m$ (p. 26). Attention that lowers the likelihood of a costly mistake raises expected benefit. Attention that raises it lowers expected benefit (p. 26). Expected benefit may be short-run or discounted long-run (fn. 14, p. 25).

**Theorem 2.1** (p. 27). The authors say this conclusion "can be proven true in our model" with or without reference to the costs of attention. These pages contain no proof; the chapter Appendix, which gives "a more technical version of our model" (p. 25), is not included here.
- The benefit of more information can be zero or negative.
- The return from more information can be zero or negative.
- More information is neither necessary nor sufficient for reasoned choice.

**Supporting arguments.**
- Expectant parents may ask not to be told the baby's sex.
- People ignore medication warnings. Footnote 18 cites at least 33 percent of patients not adhering to their regimens (p. 27).
- Information that does not prevent a costly mistake is a squandered resource ex post (p. 28).
- Freeway example: a neighbour's tip leads you to a slower route, so more information made you worse off (p. 28).

The belief that more information is always better usually rests on equating information with knowledge (p. 28). So people reject many chances to acquire political information. Even if people could acquire "complete information", the fact that information is valuable only if it prevents costly mistakes would dampen their desire to do so (p. 28). The authors write that additional information is beneficial only if it prevents a costly mistake (or, alternatively, if it causes a reasoned choice) (p. 28).

**Corollaries to Theorem 2.1** (pp. 28–29):
- A rise in the expected cost of an avoidable mistake either induces attention to the stimulus or has no effect.
- A rise in the expected probability that attention prevents a painful mistake either induces attention or has no effect.
- A rise in the expected probability that attention causes a painful mistake either reduces attention or has no effect.

**Theorem 2.2** (p. 29). The authors add two premises that they call reasonable for many political contexts. There are thousands of stimuli, and the opportunity cost of attending to a given stimulus is then substantially higher than with one or two stimuli. Then: **People have an incentive to ignore many stimuli.** The normal response is to ignore. If we assume that the availability of many stimuli implies high enough opportunity costs, then Theorem 2.2 implies a corollary: a person who can attend to only one stimulus will attend to the one whose expected benefits are extremely high relative to expected costs (p. 29). Put another way, people pay attention only to stimuli that are easy to process and strongly associated with the greatest avoidance of pain or production of pleasure (p. 29).

**Cues.** If cognitively cheap, pain-reducing information suffices for reasoned choice, there is little incentive to get more detail (p. 29). The authors say the idea of shortcuts is not new (Popkin; Sniderman et al.). Their addition is deriving when shortcuts are used and which are chosen when many exist (fn. 22). If more shortcuts are available than a person can attend to, the cheapest and most highly correlated one is attended to. A shortcut's effectiveness is "not automatic or constant", but varies with individuals and contexts (fn. 22, p. 29).

Drivers use traffic signals, street signs and warning lights. These cease to be useful if they turn out to be unreliable (pp. 29–30). Voters use party label, work experience, scandals, sound bites, interest-group ratings, endorsements or appearance. Partisanship promises more return when candidates of different parties compete than in nonpartisan or primary elections. The reverse holds for interest-group and elite endorsements if they correlate with schisms within the party. This claim is subject to the caveat that the endorsement is credible (fn. 23, p. 30). *In sum* (p. 30): reasoned choice requires knowledge but not necessarily detailed information, and it requires that people direct their attention in systematic ways. People are wise to attend to certain types of stimuli while ignoring most others.

*Intuition note (our gloss):* Attention is like a budget. In the authors' calculus, a stimulus is worth attending to when its expected benefit (avoiding costly mistakes) exceeds its opportunity and transaction costs. Because there are thousands of stimuli, the normal response to any given one is to ignore it (pp. 25–29).

### The cognitive stock market (pp. 30–35)
**The problem.** A stimulus (breaking glass) allows many theories. People must quickly draw one welfare-enhancing inference. The safari example: running from a lion, he needs a safe tree quickly, not the tallest (p. 31). The authors argue that people regularly generate a wide range of welfare-enhancing choices from a small store of information (p. 31).

**Connectionism** (pp. 31–32). Knowledge is represented as networks of neurons. Connections that lead to success are used more, become stronger, and so are used still more (p. 32). Holland et al. say the "knowledge is in the connections". Learning is the revision of connection strengths (p. 32). Researchers usually simulate such networks on computers (p. 32).

**The analogy.** Connections compete in a "mental tournament", like stocks in a stock market. A connection's value is the capitalized value of experience with causal relations (p. 32). Values start the day from past experience. They rise or fall with the new information we attend to, depending on whether a connection correlates with pleasant outcomes or with costly mistakes (p. 32). Footnote 25 says connections gain value by generating images that help predict consequences.

**Requirements for hosting the market** (p. 33):
1. Observe outcomes and new information.
2. Remember some events that preceded the outcome.
3. Believe that the future will resemble the past.

If these hold, the competition lets you make a wide range of reasoned choices with a limited store of encyclopedic knowledge (p. 33). Footnote 26 gives the T-maze rat: it goes left with increasing frequency once left yields food. Similar experiments gave equivalent results in many other species, including bees. Variants suggest hamsters are sensitive to changes in the probability that venues hold food (p. 33).

**Which connections win** (p. 33). External regularities, life path and perceptive capabilities determine winners. Winners must correlate with extreme pleasure or pain in a few trials, or some degree of it in many trials. Representations not associated with pain or pleasure are less useful and are relied on less.

**Common winners.** Some connections have high value in all brains (fire → pain) (p. 33). The authors argue that communication depends on shared beliefs, since metaphor and analogy would otherwise be impossible. Because we can train animals (or they can train us), the authors say we must share common representations with them (p. 34). Commonalities in environment and in cognitive and perceptive abilities lead to systematic winners: "some stocks are always better investments than others", no matter who you are. The authors say this implies that people *should* respond to some stimuli in systematic ways (p. 34). Example: everyone believes the sun will rise, though no two people's experience of it is the same. If enough correspondences are similar for all of us, people should adapt to complexity in common and systematic ways (p. 34). Footnote 28 treats such shared correspondences as a population's culture.

**Divergent winners.** Different physical makeup or socialization can give different connections different values. This holds as long as multiple ways of conceptualizing the world permit survival. Even identical twins or clones with many of the same experiences can draw different cause-effect associations (pp. 34–35; fn. 29).

### Attention and connections (pp. 35–36)
Through trial and error, people form simple attention strategies. As a rule they ignore most available information. They depart from the rule if they observe, or expect to observe, a string of sufficiently costly errors (p. 35).

**Parable: party identification.** Most voters vote along party lines in presidential elections. Partisanship matters less in primaries and in initiatives and referenda (p. 35). In presidential elections, parties, presidents and policy outcomes have strong, consistent historical associations. So party-performance connections should be highly valued and often used. People who know the party differences, and see them tied to what causes pain and pleasure, may stop searching once they know the party (p. 35). Where parties do not compete, as in primaries or nonpartisan initiatives, the connection is weaker and the cue less effective and less attended to (p. 35).

**Brand analogy.** Brand names (Mercedes-Benz, McDonald's) and party labels are valuable only if they have strong and consistent connections to outcomes. A Republican-run car plant would probably not signal quality. The authors write "probably not" (p. 36).

**Summary.** Attention and connection strategies let people substitute simple cues for detailed information. When no substitutes exist, the information required for reasoned choice rises. Judging capacity for reasoned choice requires more than political trivia questions (p. 36).

### Conclusion (pp. 36–38)
- People lack the ability and the incentive to attend to most things. For now, the authors assert that politics falls in the "most things" category (p. 36).
- They presume most people choose to have little information on most political issues. They conclude that their attention strategies "do not necessarily prevent" them from making reasoned choices (pp. 36–37).
- Demanding detailed facts for reasoned choice is like requiring toothpaste buyers to recall the ingredients (p. 37).
- The authors concur with earlier work (Popkin; Sniderman et al.) that simple cues can substitute. They add when simple substitutes suffice and why some cues are used and others ignored. Cues likely to be attended to are those that prevented costly mistakes in analogous past circumstances (p. 37).
- People make mistakes, as consumers do. But they lack detailed information about cars, flights and treatments and are not generally said to fail there. It is "wrong to conclude" that lacking detailed political information means being unable to choose in a reasoned way (p. 37).
- Chapter 3 turns to learning from others' testimony. This is hard when both sides are strategic and possibly at cross-purposes (pp. 37–38).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* A voter can avoid one costly mistake: voting for a candidate whose policies hurt her. Pain units: $E(\text{Mistake}) = 10$. One mistake type, so the sum has one term.
- **Cue A, party label.** $P_{prev}=0.5$, $P_{cause}=0.1$. $E(\text{Benefit}) = 0.5\cdot10 - 0.1\cdot10 = 5-1 = 4$. Opportunity cost 1, transaction cost 0.5. $E(\text{Return}) = 4-1-0.5 = 2.5$.
- **Cue B, full voting record.** $P_{prev}=0.6$, $P_{cause}=0.1$. $E(\text{Benefit}) = 6-1 = 5$. Opportunity cost 1, transaction cost 4. $E(\text{Return}) = 5-1-4 = 0$.
- Cue A has the higher return, so she attends to the label and ignores the record, even though B is slightly more informative.
- If the label no longer correlates with outcomes ($P_{prev}=P_{cause}=0.3$), the benefit is $3-3=0$. The return is $0-1-0.5=-1.5$, so the cue is dropped. This mirrors the primary-election case.

- [structural-analogy] Calculus of attention ↔ decision theory: the benefit formula is an expected loss avoided, like a value-of-information calculation.
- [structural-analogy] Cognitive stock market ↔ learning from reward: connections strengthened by success resemble reinforcement.
- [structural-analogy] Rational ignorance (Downs) ↔ this chapter: both hold that costs deter information gathering; the authors add that benefits can be zero or negative.
- [metaphor] Brand names and party labels as cues ↔ credibility and signalling: the chapter says cue value depends on consistent outcomes and, in a footnote, credibility (fn. 23, p. 30).

## Questions this raises
- The proofs of Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 are not in these pages: which assumptions about perceived probabilities do they need?
- Can accuracy of predictions be tested directly in surveys, rather than inferred from trivia scores?
- When two cues conflict (party vs endorsement), how does a person judge which is more reliable? Chapter 3 is announced as treating credibility.
- If people choose their own shortcuts, can a shortcut be wrong in a systematic way without people noticing?
