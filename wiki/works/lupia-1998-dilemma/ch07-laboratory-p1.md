# Laboratory Experiments on Information, Persuasion, and Choice (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
The authors test their theory of persuasion with a coin-toss game between a "principal" and a "speaker", and report that behavior largely matches what the theory predicts under its conditions for persuasion, enlightenment and deception.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter 7 asks whether the Part I theory of persuasion and enlightenment predicts behavior in the laboratory. A principal predicts an unseen coin toss, and a speaker sends a message about it. The authors vary what the speaker knows, whether interests are common or conflicting, and external forces (verification, lying penalties, costly effort). Where the theory's conditions for persuasion hold, principals follow the speaker about 90% of the time. Otherwise they match at about chance. Where the conditions for enlightenment hold, 93% of persuaded principals are correct. Where persuasion holds but the enlightenment conditions fail, half of the persuaded principals are deceived. This part gives the summary results and the design.

**Prerequisites:** Part I model: principal, speaker, signal, "better/worse"; conditions for persuasion and enlightenment; the idea of a control and a treatment condition.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The experiment is built as a close analogy to the model: a principal chooses between two options, a speaker sends one of two messages (p. 101).
- The theory says perceived speaker attributes and external forces decide persuasion (Thm 3.3). Actual speaker attributes and external forces decide whether persuasion enlightens (Thm 4.1) or deceives (Thm 4.2) (p. 102).
- Persuasion is measured as the rate at which the principal's prediction matches the speaker's statement. Enlightenment (persuaded and correct) and deception (persuaded and incorrect) are defined only among persuaded cases (p. 103).
- Reported results (Table 7.1): persuasion 89% and enlightenment 93% when the conditions held. About chance (the authors' wording; observed persuasion was 58%) when persuasion conditions failed. Persuasion 90% but deception 50% when only the enlightenment conditions failed (pp. 103–104).
- Four analogies are constant across all experiments. Six groups of analogies vary, to create or remove the conditions (pp. 105–107).
- Treatment and control differ in only one respect (p. 108).
- Subjects could not see or speak to each other, and got no feedback during the session. This limits repeated-play effects, because the theory has a single interaction (p. 111).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter introduction (pp. 101–104)
**Where the chapter starts.** Part I argued that reasoned choice does not require complete information. It argued that people can learn from others they need not know well. It argued that communication enlightens only if the principal accurately assesses the speaker's incentives and knowledge (p. 101).

**Setup.** The experiments are "a close analogy to our theory". The principal predicts whether an unobserved coin landed heads or tails. The speaker tells the principal whether heads or tails would be the better prediction (p. 101).
- The principal is paid a fixed amount, usually $1.00, for a correct prediction (p. 101).
- The speaker's pay varies. Common interests: he earns $1.00 when the principal is correct. Conflicting interests: he earns $1.00 when the principal is wrong. In some cases the principal is uncertain which applies (pp. 101–102).
- The authors vary perceived speaker attributes, actual speaker attributes and external forces. They set conditions for three outcomes: persuasive and enlightening, persuasive and deceptive, or unpersuasive (p. 102).
- Examples. Persuade and enlighten: the speaker saw the toss and had an incentive to reveal it, through common interests or external forces. Persuade and deceive: the speaker saw the toss and the principal wrongly believed interests were common. Unpersuasive: the principal knew the speaker did not see the toss (p. 102).
- Footnote 1: the instructions stated only the rules (how people earn money, whether the speaker saw the toss). They did not say how to behave to maximise payoff (p. 102).

**Measures.**
- *Persuasion* = the number of times the principal's prediction matched the speaker's statement. It is strong evidence when significantly above chance (usually 50%) (pp. 102–103).
- *Enlightened* = persuaded and correct. *Deceived* = persuaded and incorrect. Both are defined only for persuasive statements (p. 103).
- *Reasoned choice* = correctly predicting the toss (p. 104).

**Table 7.1 (percent; counts in brackets).**

(p. 103)

**Reading the table (author's account).**
- Top: persuasion occurred in almost 90% and enlightenment in more than 90% of cases (p. 103).
- Middle: the author expects the principal's predictions to be independent of the speaker's statement, with about half correct. Persuasion, enlightenment and reasoned choice occurred "at approximately the level of chance" (pp. 103–104).
- Footnote 2: the model predicts chance, not 0% persuasion. 0% would mean the principal systematically does the opposite of the speaker. That would itself condition her action on the statement, which the model denies in these cases (p. 104).
- Bottom: the theory predicts high persuasion and far more deception than the 7% at the top. Persuasion was almost 90%, and the principal was deceived in half of these trials (p. 104).
- Overall: persuasion about 90% when the conditions held, otherwise about 50%. Over 90% of persuasive statements enlightened and 7% deceived when the enlightenment conditions held (p. 104).

*Intuition note:* The theory does not say "speakers persuade". It says when they do. Where the listener has no reason to rely on the speaker, the theory expects her predictions to be independent of the message (observed persuasion was 58%, which the authors call approximately chance). Where persuasion conditions held but enlightenment conditions did not, principals followed the message in almost 90% of trials and half were deceived.

### Experimental design (pp. 104–105)
- Subjects were UCSD undergraduates, recruited by flyers in 1996 (p. 104).
- Each was paid a nominal amount (usually $2.00) for showing up and signed a consent form about an experiment on "decision making". All signed and none left early (p. 105).
- Footnote 3: callers had to be at least 18 and undergraduates. About 20% no-shows were expected. Extra arrivals were paid $5.00 and invited to another session. No one took part more than once (p. 105).
- Footnote 4: some details are omitted. The authors found no evidence that the variations affected the results (p. 105).
- Validity requires that the analogies be faithful to the theory. Table 7.2 lists the premise–analogy pairs, split into constants and variables (p. 105).

### The Constants (pp. 105–108)
1. **Participants.** Each subject was a principal or a speaker (p. 105).
2. **Principal's choice.** The principal predicts heads or tails (the text layer reads "beads", read as "heads"). The coin toss gives a simple way to introduce uncertainty (p. 108).
3. **Consequences.** In the theory, choosing the better alternative earns positive utility. In the experiment the principal is paid a fixed positive amount for each correct prediction and nothing for an incorrect one. Example: outcome heads, $1.00 for predicting heads, nothing for tails (pp. 106, 108).
4. **Speaker's choices.** The speaker signals "heads" or "tails" and need not tell the truth. If the toss was tails, "tails" corresponds to "better" and "heads" to "worse", because the principal is paid for being correct (p. 108).
   - Footnote 5: the payment schemes follow induced value theory (Smith 1976, 1982). It requires that reward depend on actions, that subjects prefer more reward, that utility is based mainly on the reward, and that each subject has private information about his or her reward (p. 108).

### The Variables (pp. 108–110)
**Control and treatment.** The authors set up a control, where no persuasion is expected. The treatment differs in only one respect, usually a stimulus expected to lead to persuasion. Example (the authors recall their earlier conclusion): perceived speaker knowledge is a necessary condition for persuasion. In the control the speaker does not see the toss. In the treatment he does (pp. 108–109).

**Principal characteristics (Table 7.2, pp. 106, 109).**
- The theory has nature choose "better" with probability $b\in[0,1]$. The experiment uses fair coins (p. 106).
- Variable: the principal either observes the toss before predicting, or does not observe it but knows the coins are fair (pp. 106, 109).

**Speaker characteristics, three variables (pp. 106–107, 109).**
- *Interests* (theory: common or conflicting). Common: the speaker is paid only if the principal is correct. Conflicting: the speaker is paid only if the principal is incorrect (p. 109).
- *Principal's knowledge of interests* (theory: nature picks common with probability $c\in[0,1]$). A die roll decides common or conflicting. Everyone knows a die is used, but only the speaker sees the roll. The principal has beliefs only (p. 109).
- *Speaker's knowledge* (theory: the speaker knows with probability $k\in[0,1]$). The speaker sees the toss, does not, or a die roll decides. Only the speaker knows the roll (pp. 107, 109).

**External forces, three variables (pp. 107, 110).**
- *Verification*: the true outcome is revealed to the principal with probability $v$ after the speaker speaks (p. 107).
- *Penalty for lying*: a speaker who makes a false statement pays a penalty ($pen \ge 0$ in the theory) (pp. 107, 110).
- *Costly effort*: the speaker pays a fixed amount to make a statement ($cost \ge 0$ in the theory) (pp. 107, 110).

**Summary.** The ten analogies in Table 7.2 are the basic design. The authors say the design presents subjects with simple, familiar situations (p. 110).

*Intuition note:* The varied model parameters map to things the experimenters control, such as who sees the coin, how the speaker is paid, and what the principal is told. A die roll with a hidden result lets the principal be uncertain about the speaker while the experimenters still know the truth.

**Instructions and quizzes (p. 110).** Each session began with simple explanations and examples, and the authors did not suggest behaviors. A quiz followed, and most subjects scored perfectly. The authors take this as evidence that subjects understood their situation, as the model's players do (p. 110).
- Footnote 6: subjects were paid a small amount (e.g. 10 cents) per correct answer. Those who failed a predetermined number of questions were omitted from the data for that set of trials, and were never told they failed. Including them does not alter the results (p. 110).
- **The authors' view of rationality tests:** they did not use them, unlike some economic experiments (for example Cooper et al. 1993, which requires players to choose the dominant strategy 85% of the time). They call this procedure analogous to excluding outliers and of "questionable scientific value". They never selected subjects on past behavior (p. 110).

### Other design elements (p. 111)
- *Limiting what subjects learn about each other.* Partitions blocked sight. Subjects could not speak. Letting them speak would reduce control over whether the conditions for persuasion and enlightenment held, which the tests need. The speaker therefore checked a "heads" or "tails" box, and an experimenter read the message (p. 111).
- *Repeated interaction.* In the theory, principal and speaker interact once. Because a one-decision session was impractical, they interacted many times (p. 111).
- *Reducing repeated-play effects.* Subjects got no information about others' past actions. They learned only their total earnings after the session. They never learned which predictions were correct, and the principal never learned when the speaker lied. They thus had an incentive to treat each interaction as the only one (p. 111).
- This part ends here, at the bottom of p. 111.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Suppose a principal makes 20 predictions with a speaker present.
- She matches the speaker's statement in 18 trials, so persuasion is 18/20 = 90%. Chance would be 10/20 = 50% (a principal who ignores the speaker matches about half the time).
- Among the 18 matches, 15 are correct and 3 are wrong. Enlightenment = 15/18 ≈ 83%. Deception = 3/18 ≈ 17%. (They sum to 100% because both are counted only among matches.)
- In the 2 non-matching trials, suppose 1 is correct. Reasoned choice = (15 + 1)/20 = 80%.
- If the speaker had conflicting interests but the principal believed they were common, the same 90% persuasion could come with many more wrong predictions. This is the pattern in Table 7.1's bottom section.

## Concepts
- [[conditions-for-persuasion]] — in the theory, persuasion is determined by external forces and perceived speaker attributes; perceived speaker knowledge is a necessary condition. Here these are set by design in the lab (pp. 102, 108–109)
- [[persuasion-enlightenment-deception]] (new) — persuasion = principal's prediction matches the statement. Enlightened = matched and correct. Deceived = matched and incorrect (p. 103)
- [[reasoned-choice]] (new) — here, operationalised as a correct prediction of the coin toss (p. 104)
- [[external-forces]] (new) — verification, penalties for lying, and costly effort, which in the theory (with perceived speaker attributes) determine persuasion (pp. 101–102, 107, 110)
- [[experimental-analogy]] (new) — a lab feature paired with a theoretical premise, so the experiment tests the theory (pp. 105–107)
- [[control-and-treatment]] (new) — two conditions differing in one respect, usually one stimulus expected to produce persuasion (p. 108)
- [[induced-value-theory]] (new) — the conditions that enable the experimenter to influence subjects' incentives by linking rewards to actions (Smith 1976, 1982) (p. 108)
