# Laboratory Experiments on Delegation

## L0 — One line
In coin-toss experiments, agents proposed 50–100% of the time where proposals were predicted (chiefly when an ill-informed principal could be "enlightened" by her speaker and the agent shared her interests) and 6–18% of the time where none were predicted. The authors take this to support their theory of delegation.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can delegation succeed when the principal is ill-informed and the agent's interests may conflict with hers? The authors' theory says yes if the knowledge and incentive conditions hold: an enlightened principal rejects bad proposals, so the agent has reason to propose only good ones. They test this in coin-toss experiments with an agent, a speaker and a principal. They vary speaker knowledge, speaker interests, penalties for lying and verification. Agents proposed 50–100% of the time when proposals were predicted, and 6–18% when none were predicted. The verification results were weakest; risk aversion and complexity are offered as possible reasons. The authors suggest that institutions designed to help learning can make delegation work.

**Prerequisites:** the persuasion experiments of Chapter 7 (speaker, principal, coin toss); expected value; the book's theory of delegation (Chapter 5); basic comparison of treatment and control.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Delegation becomes abdication when the agent's and principal's interests conflict and the principal cannot tell whether proposals help her. The theory says both problems can be overcome if the knowledge and incentive conditions hold (p. 149). (Knowledge condition: the principal can correctly infer whether a proposal beats the status quo for her. Incentive condition: the agent is motivated to propose something better than the status quo for her.)
- Principal enlightenment can also produce the incentive condition: an agent who expects rejection of bad proposals has reason to make only good ones (p. 149).
- The design maps each theoretical premise to a coin-toss analogy (Table 8.1). The agent gives up a sure payoff to propose; the principal predicts a hidden coin toss; the speaker advises (pp. 150–155).
- Repeated play and many agents per principal were used for cost reasons. Feedback was withheld so that each trial acts like a one-shot interaction (pp. 156–157).
- Speaker-knowledge and speaker-interest experiments: agents proposed far more often where proposals were predicted than where none were predicted. One treatment cell (common interests, Fig. 8.2h) was ambiguous, and risk aversion is offered as an explanation (pp. 158–174).
- Penalty-for-lying experiment: results closely matched expectations, with a 65% mean proposal rate in the predicted cell against 12–18% elsewhere (pp. 174–178).
- Verification experiment: the weakest results (50% vs 10–18%). The authors call them "some affirmation" and speculate about complexity and risk aversion (pp. 178–182).
- Conclusion: institutional design that facilitates learning can be an effective way of making delegation successful (p. 182).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 149–150)
- Delegation can become abdication, where the agent acts without regard to the principal's welfare. This happens when two circumstances hold: (1) conflicting interests, so the agent wants proposals that lower the principal's welfare; (2) the principal lacks the information to judge whether a proposal helps her (p. 149).
- The theory's central lesson: these circumstances can be overcome when the knowledge and incentive conditions hold (Theorem 5.1). The knowledge condition holds iff the principal can correctly infer whether the proposal makes her better or worse off than the status quo. The incentive condition holds only if the agent is motivated to propose something better than the status quo for her (p. 149).
- Theorem 5.3: enlightenment can produce the incentive condition as well. If the principal can tell good proposals from bad, the agent knows bad ones are rejected, so he has an incentive to make only proposals better than the status quo for her (p. 149).
- The experiments add an agent to the persuasion design (ill-informed principal, advising speaker). The authors expected agents to benefit principals when the conditions for enlightenment held, and report this is what they saw. They conclude that if those conditions hold, delegation can succeed even when the principal lacks information and interests conflict (p. 150).

*Intuition note:* If a principal cannot be fooled, bad proposals are wasted effort, so even a self-interested agent has reason to send only good ones. The experiments check whether real subjects behave this way.

### Experimental design (pp. 150–157)
**Trial sequence (p. 150).**
1. The agent decides whether to propose.
2. If not, the trial ends and agent and principal each get a status quo payoff.
3. If yes, the speaker advises "heads" or "tails". The principal then predicts a hidden coin toss, which effectively accepts or rejects the proposal. Payoffs depend on her accuracy.

The experiments ran in the first six months of 1996, with the subject pool and recruitment of Chapter 7. Payoff variations across sessions showed no evidence of affecting results (p. 150, n. 1).

**Table 8.1 (pp. 151–152): premise → analogy.**
- *Constants:*
  - Three roles: principal, speaker, agent.
  - Proposing is costly, and not proposing ends the game at the status quo.
  - The principal is uncertain which alternative is better: she does not see the coin toss.
  - She is goal-oriented: a small fixed amount if no proposal, more if she predicts correctly, nothing if wrongly.
  - The speaker signals heads/tails and need not tell the truth.
- *Agent interest variable:* the experimenters choose common or conflicting interests. Under common interests the agent earns more than the fixed amount if the principal is correct and nothing otherwise. Under conflict the payoffs are reversed.
- *Speaker characteristic variables:*
  - Speaker–principal interests common (speaker paid when principal correct) or conflicting (paid when she is wrong).
  - Speaker knowledge: the speaker observes the coin toss or not.
- *External force variables:*
  - Penalty for lying: the speaker pays a penalty for a false statement.
  - Verification: the true outcome is revealed to the principal with probability $v$.

**Constant analogies (pp. 153–154).**
- The cost of proposing is the forgone guaranteed status quo payoff (p. 153).
- The principal never observed the coin toss in these experiments (p. 154).
- Example payoffs: principal 70 cents without a proposal, $1.00 if correct, nothing if wrong (p. 154).
- The agent always saw the coin-toss outcome; the principal never did (p. 154).
- In most common-interest trials the agent got $1.40 for no proposal, $2.00 if proposing and the principal was correct, and nothing if wrong. Under conflict the last two payoffs were switched (p. 154, n. 5).

**Treatment and control (p. 155).**
- *Treatment condition:* the speaker is knowledgeable and either shares the principal's interests or faces sufficient external forces, so the principal is expected to be enlightened. The agent should then propose only under common interests, since only then does the proposal pay more than the status quo (p. 155).
  - Footnote 7 payoffs: common interests give $2.00 against $1.40 fixed; conflict gives $0 (p. 155, n. 7).
- *Control condition:* enlightenment is not expected, so the principal is expected to be correct about half the time. The agent's expected return is then $.5(\$2.00)+.5(\$0)=\$1.00$, below the $1.40 fixed amount, so he should not propose (p. 155, n. 8).
- No costly-effort experiment was run. Costly effort affects the principal's beliefs equally whoever pays, so it would have been redundant. There were also no experiments with principal uncertainty about speaker interests (p. 155, n. 6).
- Subjects took quizzes on the instructions, and most scored perfectly (pp. 155–156).

**Other design elements (pp. 156–157).**
- Partitions and a ban on talking prevented any subject from identifying another, now including the agent (p. 156).
- *Repeated play:* the theory is one-shot, but subjects interacted many times for cost reasons. Subjects learned only their total earnings after the session. They never learned which predictions were right or when the speaker lied, so they had incentives to treat each interaction as the only one (p. 156).
- The principal also got no information on whether the agent proposed. She predicted in every trial, but was paid for accuracy only if the agent proposed. If he did not, she got the status quo amount regardless (pp. 156–157).
- *Many agents:* one speaker, one principal, 8–10 agents. Each agent was paid only for his own decisions, saw nothing of the others, and faced identical incentives. So the principal and speaker could not discriminate among agents (p. 157). In some treatments the principal was paid $1.00 per correct prediction per proposing agent (p. 157, n. 10).

### Experiments on delegation (pp. 158–182)
Sessions had two experimenters, 2–4 assistants and 8–12 subjects. Each began with the Chapter 7 trials (p. 158). Four experiments each varied one variable analogy (p. 158).

**Speaker knowledge experiment (pp. 158–166).**
- *Hypothesis:* if principal and speaker have common interests, making the speaker knowledgeable will induce an agent to make proposals favorable to the principal. *Control:* speaker does not observe the toss, common interests, no external forces. *Treatment:* speaker observes the toss (p. 158).
- *Payoffs:* the agent got 65 cents for no proposal and $1.00 for a proposal with a correct prediction, and the reverse under conflict. The principal got 65 cents status quo and $1.00 if correct. The speaker got $1.00 per correct principal prediction. The principal also got about 10 cents for choosing heads. This isolates the effect of speaker knowledge, because with common interests following advice is weakly dominant anyway (pp. 158–159, n. 12).
- *Expectations (Table 8.2, p. 160):* agents propose in cells 1 and 4 and in the speaker-sees cells 5 and 6 (common interests); agents do not propose in cells 2, 3, 7 and 8.
  - Without speaker knowledge, the principal predicts heads because of the 10-cent bonus. So the agent proposes when the coin is heads and interests are common (cell 1), and when it is tails and interests conflict (cell 4) (p. 159).
- *Results (Fig. 8.1, pp. 159–161):*
  - Control cells: 14 of 18 agents always proposed in cell 1; 16 of 18 never proposed in cell 2; 16 of 18 made no proposals in cell 3; 15 of 18 always proposed in cell 4. For cell 1 (Fig. 8.1a), the authors say the likelihood of such strings under random proposing (as defined in Chapter 7) is very close to zero (p. 159).
  - Treatment, common interests: 23 of 24 always proposed.
  - Treatment, conflicting interests: 18 of 24 made no proposals.
  - Matched comparisons differ only in speaker knowledge. Tails, common interests: Fig. 8.1b (16 of 18 never proposed) against Fig. 8.1f (12 of 12 always proposed). Tails, conflicting interests: Fig. 8.1d (15 of 18 always proposed) against Fig. 8.1h (8 of 12 never proposed).
- *Table 8.3 (mean % proposals, p. 167).*

| Speaker | Coin | Common interests | Conflicting interests |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does not see toss | heads | 83% (n=18) | 6 (18) |
| Does not see toss | tails | 8 (18) | 89 (18) |
| Sees toss | heads | 96 (12) | 8 (12) |
| Sees toss | tails | 100 (12) | 17 (12) |

  - Where proposals were expected, they ranged from 83 to 100%. Where none were expected, from 6 to 17% (p. 166).

*Intuition note:* With an uninformed speaker, the authors expected the principal to predict heads (because of the small bonus), and agents proposed accordingly. With an informed, aligned speaker the principal was expected to be right, and agents mostly proposed when their interests matched hers (23 of 24 under common interests; 18 of 24 made no proposals under conflict).

**Speaker interest experiment (pp. 166–174).**
- *Hypothesis:* if the speaker is knowledgeable and there are no external forces, then giving speaker and principal common interests will induce an agent with common interests to make proposals favorable to the principal. *Control:* speaker knows the toss, interests with the principal conflict. *Treatment:* common interests (p. 166).
- The speaker earned 50 cents per incorrect (control) or correct (treatment) principal prediction. Expectation: agents propose only if the speaker, principal and agent all have common interests (Table 8.4, p. 168).
- *Control, agent–principal conflict (Figs. 8.2a, b):* about 65% of agents never proposed; the rest proposed in few trials (pp. 166, 168).
- *Control, agent–principal common interests (Figs. 8.2c, d):* the agent got $1.20 for no proposal, $2.00 for a correct prediction, nothing otherwise. So he should propose only if the probability of a correct prediction is at least 60% (p. 168). (added step: $2.00p \ge \$1.20 \iff p \ge 0.6$.) Enlightenment was not expected, so the principal was expected to predict randomly. The median agent never proposed (p. 168).
- *Treatment, agent–principal conflict (Figs. 8.2e, f):* a majority, and the median agent, never proposed (p. 168).
- *Treatment, common interests (Figs. 8.2g, h):*
  - Fig. 8.2g: the modal agent always proposed.
  - Fig. 8.2h: the results are "more ambiguous". Agents mostly chose not to propose, and the modal agent proposed 25% of the time, contrary to the hypothesis. Still, proposals were significantly higher than in Figs. 8.2a–f (p. 169).
- *Authors' explanation:* risk aversion. Their theory assumes risk-neutral agents. Risk-averse agents would value the sure $1.20 more, so, in the authors' words, it would not take much risk aversion to deter proposals even under enlightenment (p. 169). Post-experimental questionnaires suggested the experiment's complexity facilitated this. The agent moves first and must anticipate the speaker and principal; he should propose only if he has common interests and believes the principal is correct with probability at least 60% (p. 169, n. 15).
- *Table 8.5 (mean % proposals, SD, n; p. 174).*

| | Common interests | Conflicting interests |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment | 65 (.33), n=18 | 12 (.15), n=18 |
| Control | 16 (.28), n=48 | 18 (.30), n=48 |

  - Percentages standardize the differing number of trials per condition. Each agent's percentage is averaged over agents (p. 174, n. 16).
  - Other cells are significantly lower (12–18%). The authors say the principal's ability to become enlightened induced agents to act as they otherwise would not (p. 174).

**Penalties-for-lying experiment (pp. 174–178).**
- *Hypothesis:* if the speaker is knowledgeable and has interests conflicting with the principal's, a sufficient penalty for lying will induce an agent with common interests to make proposals favorable to the principal. *Control:* as in the interest experiment (p. 174).
- The speaker got $5.00 per incorrect principal prediction. In the treatment, he was penalized $10.00 for a false statement. The authors expected the principal to ignore the speaker in the control and follow him in the treatment, with truthful signals (p. 175).
- Footnote 17 payoffs: principal 0 if the agent took the fixed amount, $1.00 if proposal and correct. Agent: $1.40 fixed, $2.00 if correct, 0 if wrong (p. 175, n. 17).
- *Results (Fig. 8.3):*
  - Treatment, conflicting interests: almost no proposals.
  - Treatment, common interests, Fig. 8.3c (five trials, eight agents): only two always proposed, five proposed more than half the time, and one never proposed.
  - Fig. 8.3d (three trials): the median agent proposed two times out of three. The text says agents proposed 88% of the time (see Reading notes) (p. 175).
- *Table 8.6 (p. 178):* treatment, common interests 65 (.32), n=16; treatment, conflicting 12 (.24), n=16; control cells as in Table 8.5 (16 and 18, n=48 each). The predicted cell is substantially higher than each of the others (pp. 175, 178).

**Verification experiment (pp. 178–182).**
- *Hypothesis:* if the speaker is knowledgeable and in conflict with the principal, a sufficient threat of verification will induce an agent with common interests to make proposals favorable to the principal. *Control:* as before. *Treatment:* verification introduced (p. 178).
- *Procedure:*
  - After the agents decided, the speaker chose whether to pay $2.00 for the privilege of signalling.
  - He earned $5.00 if the principal erred and nothing if she was correct.
  - A ten-sided die was rolled: on 1–7 the statement was replaced by the true outcome; on 8–10 the speaker's statement was reported.
  - The principal knew only that there was a 70% chance her signal was the true outcome (pp. 178–179).
- *Expected agent earnings (p. 179, n. 18):*
  - Common interests: $(.7)(\$2)+(.3)[(.5)(\$2)+(.5)(\$0)]=\$1.70>\$1.40$.
  - Conflicting interests: $(.7)(\$0)+(.3)[(.5)(\$0)+(.5)(\$2)]=\$0.30<\$1.40$.
- *Results (Fig. 8.4):*
  - Treatment, conflicting interests: very few proposals; the median agent never proposed.
  - Treatment, common interests, Fig. 8.4c: more than half of agents proposed fewer than three of six times, and the modal agent proposed once in six.
  - Fig. 8.4d: closer to expectations, with half of agents proposing at least four of five times.
  - The authors say "these results do not support our expectations". They can only speculate that complexity and risk aversion played a role (p. 179).
- *Table 8.7 (p. 182):* treatment, common interests 50 (.39), n=18; treatment, conflicting 10 (.16), n=18; control 16 and 18 (n=48 each). It is the weakest result, but the pattern is as expected, with substantial differences from the other cells. The authors call this "some affirmation" given the experiment's complexity (p. 179).

### Conclusion (p. 182)
Where proposals were expected, agents made them 50–100% of the time; where none were expected, 6–18%. The conditions for enlightenment induced agents to make proposals favorable to the principal and dissuaded unfavorable ones. The authors suggest institutional design that facilitates learning can effectively make delegation successful (p. 182).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A hypothetical agent gets $1.50 for no proposal. If he proposes, he gets $2.50 when the principal predicts correctly and $0 otherwise (common interests); under conflict the two payoffs swap. Let $p$ be the probability that the principal is correct.
- *Threshold:* common interests: propose iff $2.50p \ge 1.50$, so $p \ge 0.6$. Conflicting interests: expected value $2.50(1-p)$.
- *Enlightened principal* ($p=1$): common interests give $2.50 > 1.50$, so he proposes. Conflicting interests give $2.50(0)=0 < 1.50$, so he does not.
- *Unenlightened principal* ($p=.5$): either type expects $2.50 \times .5 = 1.25 < 1.50$, so neither proposes.
- *Verification (assumed here, as in n. 18: verified outcome always correct, otherwise random guess):* $p = v + .5(1-v) = .5+.5v$. The common-interest agent proposes iff $.5+.5v \ge .6$, so $v \ge .2$. At $v=.7$: $p=.85$, giving $2.125 > 1.50$ (common) and $2.50(.15)=0.375 < 1.50$ (conflict).

## Concepts
- [[principal-agent-problem-delegation]] — a principal delegates to an agent with possibly conflicting interests; here the agent chooses whether to propose, and the principal accepts or rejects (p. 149).
- [[abdication]] (new) — delegation in which the agent acts without regard to the principal's welfare. The authors say it happens when interests conflict and the principal lacks the information to judge proposals (p. 149).
- [[knowledge-condition]] (new) — holds iff the principal can correctly infer whether the proposal beats the status quo for her (p. 149).
- [[incentive-condition]] (new) — holds only if the agent is motivated to propose something better than the status quo for the principal (p. 149).
- [[enlightenment]] (new) — the principal's learning from the speaker. In the experiments it is expected when the speaker is knowledgeable and either shares her interests or faces sufficient external forces (p. 155).
- [[conditions-for-persuasion-lupia-mccubbins]] — named in Table 8.8 but not defined in this unit; here treatment and control are defined by the speaker's knowledge, interests and external forces (p. 155).
- [[experimental-analogy]] (new) — a lab feature mapped to a theoretical premise (Table 8.1). The authors say the design is a valid test only if the analogies are faithful to the theory (p. 150).
- [[risk-aversion-explanation]] (new) — the authors' proposed ("ready") explanation for fewer proposals than predicted in one cell; their theory assumes risk-neutral agents (p. 169).

## Canonical instances
- [[coin-toss-delegation-experiment]] (new) — agent, speaker and principal around a hidden coin toss, with proposal-versus-fixed-payoff choice (pp. 150–157).
- [[speaker-knowledge-experiment]] (new) — varies whether the speaker sees the toss; the cell-by-cell prediction table (Table 8.2) is its core (pp. 158–166).
- [[speaker-interest-experiment]] (new) — speaker–principal common versus conflicting interests; 65% proposals in the predicted cell (pp. 166–174).
- [[penalty-and-verification-experiments]] (new) — external forces; 65% and 50% in the predicted cells (pp. 174–182).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] The agent anticipates how an enlightened principal will respond and so proposes only acceptable options ↔ political-science (veto player): a proposer shapes proposals to what the veto player will accept.
- [structural-analogy] Verification and penalties for lying as external forces ↔ political-science (principal–agent monitoring and sanctions; Ostrom's monitoring and graduated sanctions): costly detection or punishment disciplines agents.
- [metaphor] An informed principal disciplining agents ↔ retrospective voting: voters who can judge outcomes discipline incumbents.

## Questions this raises
- How large would risk aversion have to be to explain the weaker results in Figs. 8.2h and 8.4c?
- How well does the coin-toss analogy transfer to real delegation, where the principal's "better or worse" judgement is not a binary hidden outcome?
- Would results change with one agent per principal and truly one-shot play rather than repeated play with withheld feedback?
- What explains the modest agent proposal rates in the verification treatment (50%), and would a simpler design show a larger effect?
