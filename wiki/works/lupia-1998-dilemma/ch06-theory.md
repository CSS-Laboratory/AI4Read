# Theory, Predictions, and the Scientific Method

## L0 — One line
The authors treat their theory as an explanation with unobservable premises, so they turn its theorems into eleven testable predictions and test them with experiments that must be faithful analogies of the theory.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Part I argued that limited information need not block reasoned choice or successful delegation. Part II aims to persuade readers that those theories are suitable representations of real decision making. Because some premises (brain processes) cannot be observed, the authors say direct evidence that all the premises suit the situation is impossible. Instead they derive eleven predictions (Table 6.1), test them in laboratory and survey experiments, and examine democratic institutions. They say every empirical test needs an "inductive leap": the method must be a faithful analogy of the theory. They argue that social scientists often leave this assumption unstated, and that it is often false.

**Prerequisites:** the Part I vocabulary (principal, speaker, persuasion, enlightenment, deception, delegation); the idea of a testable hypothesis; basic regression.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Part I aimed to show that the conclusions follow from the premises. Part II aims to persuade readers that the premises and conclusions suit real decision making (p. 97).
- An argument goes from observable premises to an unobservable conclusion. An explanation goes from unobservable premises to an observable conclusion (p. 97).
- Some premises cannot be observed directly, as in quantum mechanics. The authors say this holds for brain processes their theory relies on (p. 97).
- Strategy: turn theorems into eleven empirical predictions (Table 6.1) and test them (pp. 97–99).
- Tests: laboratory experiments on persuasion (Ch. 7) and delegation (Ch. 8), a survey experiment (Ch. 9), and an institutional analysis (Ch. 10) (pp. 99–100).
- Every empirical test needs an inductive leap: the method must be a faithful analogy of the theory. The authors say researchers often do not state this assumption, and it is often false (p. 99).
- The authors claim their lab setup, with coin tosses, gives faithful analogies. They claim this is sufficient for powerful claims about the predictions' reliability (p. 100).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Purpose of Part II (p. 97).** The persuasiveness of the book depends on two things: the conclusions must follow from the premises, and the premises and conclusions must suitably represent real decision making. The Appendix proofs address the first. Part II addresses the second.

**Argument vs. explanation (p. 97).** The authors cite Schwartz (1980: 202).
- In an argument, the goal is to show that a conclusion is true. Its premises are observable starting points, and its conclusion is unobservable.
- In an explanation, the goal is to say why a conclusion is true. It builds from unobservable premises to an observable conclusion.
- In Part II the authors treat their theories of communication and delegation as explanations.

**The dilemma (p. 97).** If some premises are unobservable, direct evidence that all of them suit the situation is impossible. The authors call this a common scientific dilemma and give quantum mechanics as an example. Their own theories rely in part on systematic features of physical and chemical processes in the brain, which social scientists cannot at present observe.

*Intuition note:* You cannot check the hidden premises directly. So you check what they imply about things you can see. The authors' route is to convert the theory's conclusions into predictions and test those, to persuade readers that the theory suits real decision making.

**Strategy and Table 6.1 (pp. 97–99).** First, convert theoretical conclusions into empirical predictions. Table 6.1 lists eleven, each tied to its theorem (p. 98). The five persuasion predictions have the form "if … then an increase in X leads to an increase in Y"; 4.1 and 4.2 state the same kind of increase-leads-to-increase link under "in the presence/absence of" conditions; the delegation predictions say that a change in conditions "will induce" favorable proposals.
- *Persuasion:*
  - 3.1: if the principal believes the speaker is knowledgeable, a higher assessed probability of common interests raises the incidence of persuasion.
  - 3.2: if she believes they have common interests, a higher assessed probability of knowledge raises persuasion.
  - 3.3 (three predictions): if she believes the speaker is knowledgeable, more observable costly effort, a larger penalty for lying, or a higher probability of verification each raises persuasion.
- *Enlightenment, 4.1:* with common interests or external forces that induce truth telling, more persuasion leads to more enlightenment.
- *Deception, 4.2:* without common interests or such forces, more persuasion leads to more deception.
- *Delegation (each prediction says a change will induce an agent to make proposals favorable to the principal; three of the four specify an agent who has common interests with the principal):*
  - 5.1 and 3.1: if the speaker is knowledgeable and there are no external forces, giving speaker and principal common interests induces such proposals.
  - 5.1 and 3.2: if speaker and principal have common interests, making the speaker knowledgeable induces them.
  - 5.1 and 3.3 (two predictions): if the speaker is knowledgeable but has conflicting interests, a sufficient penalty for lying, or a sufficient threat of verification, induces such proposals.

Second, evaluate the predictions in laboratory and survey experiments: Ch. 7 (lab, persuasion), Ch. 8 (lab, delegation), Ch. 9 (survey experiment, persuasion) (p. 99). The lab designs draw on experimental psychology and experimental economics (citations on p. 99).

**The inductive leap (p. 99).** All empirical science requires an assumption that the method of observation is a faithful analogy to the theory. The authors say all scientists make this leap, though many do not realize it.

Footnote 1 gives the authors' criticism of common practice (p. 99):
- Researchers often fail to state, question or discuss such assumptions, which are often false.
- Example: to test "education does not affect racial tolerance," one must accept the measures as reasonable analogies. Education is usually self-reported years of schooling. Tolerance is often measured by answers on school integration and public accommodation. The authors say neither measures its concept. Years of schooling ignores classroom hours, class level, school quality and performance. They say it would make Dexter Manley, a former lineman who admitted illiteracy, at least as educated as Bill Gates.
- Statistical techniques also carry assumptions (e.g., Gauss-Markov) that should be discussed.
- Seven-point party or ideology scales are often entered in regressions as linear. This implies a "strong democrat" (3) is three times as partisan as a "weak democrat" (1). The authors call this an extremely strong assumption, likely a bad analogy. Depending on the regression technique, coefficients can depend heavily on it, and if it fails the analysis should not be compelling.

*Intuition note:* A test is only as convincing as the link between the abstract concept and what was actually measured. The authors say researchers should state, question and discuss such assumptions and use suitable analogies, rather than leave them implicit.

**Experiments (p. 100).**
- *Laboratory.* Control of the environment lets them build analogies. The principal's binary choice becomes subjects predicting coin-toss outcomes. A principal learning from a speaker becomes some subjects (speakers) sending messages about coin tosses to others (principals). The authors argue these analogies are faithful and sufficient for powerful claims about the predictions' reliability.
- *Survey experiment.* Survey methods have advanced since the 1930s (Kalton 1983: 6) in sampling design, data analysis and instrumentation. Using computer-assisted telephone interviewing (CATI), they gave more than 1,400 subjects complex binary choices, then messages from speakers. They varied speaker attributes and measured subjects' perceptions of the speakers' attributes and incentives. They say the links between subjects' issue positions and perceptions allow powerful inferences about who can persuade whom.
- *Institutions (Ch. 10).* They examine the incentives created by electoral, legislative, bureaucratic and judicial institutions. They say they show that democratic institutions can also be institutions of knowledge: their incentive effects can create the conditions for enlightenment, reasoned choice and successful delegation.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Test prediction 3.1 with invented data. The principal is assumed to believe the speaker is knowledgeable.
- Condition A: principals told the speaker probably has opposed interests. Condition B: told the speaker probably has common interests.
- Ten principals per condition. Persuaded: A = 3, B = 7.
- Incidence of persuasion: A = 3/10 = 0.30. B = 7/10 = 0.70. Difference = 0.70 − 0.30 = +0.40, in the predicted direction.
- The inductive leap: this supports the theory only if "told the speaker probably has common interests" is a faithful analogy of "the principal's assessed probability of common interests," and "changed their coin guess" is a faithful analogy of "persuaded." If either link fails, +0.40 says little about the theory.

## Questions this raises
- Can the lab coin-toss analogy be defended for real political choices, where stakes and information differ?
- Which test would count against the theory if a prediction fails: the premises, or the analogy?
- Does testing predictions separately validate the unobservable brain-process premises, or only their implications?
- How should one defend the linearity assumption in survey scales, or avoid it?
