# Knowledge and the Foundation of Democracy

## L0 — One line
Limited information need not prevent citizens from making reasoned choices, because reasoned choice requires knowledge (the ability to predict consequences) rather than complete information, and advice from others can supply that knowledge when conditions let listeners judge speakers' knowledge and incentives.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Many scholars hold that citizens, legislators and jurors know too little to control the people they delegate to. The worry that the people called on to make reasoned choices may be unable to do so is the "democratic dilemma". Lupia and McCubbins accept the ignorance but reject the conclusion that democracy must fail. Reasoned choice needs knowledge, meaning the ability to predict the consequences of actions, not complete information. People can borrow knowledge from others, but this opens the door to deception. The authors preview theories of attention, persuasion and delegation. These are meant to say when advice enlightens and when it deceives, and how institutions can help. The chapter ends with a map of the book's three parts.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of principal and agent (delegation); the idea of costly signals; no math is used in this chapter.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The democratic dilemma: a mismatch between what democratic delegation demands and what citizens can do; if it is too prevalent, effective self-governance is impossible (p. 1, p. 12).
- The authors concede citizen ignorance but define reasoned choice as needing *knowledge* (ability to predict consequences), not full information (p. 2).
- Information is useful only if it helps avoid costly mistakes. Information that would not change a decision should be ignored (p. 6–7).
- Knowledge comes from personal experience or from others. In much of politics only the second is available (p. 7).
- Learning from others ends in enlightenment, deception, or nothing. Persuasion needs the listener to see the speaker as knowledgeable and trustworthy (p. 8–9).
- External forces (costly effort, penalties for lying) can substitute for a speaker's character. Heuristics such as party or ideology help only if they convey knowledge and trust (p. 9–11).
- Delegation succeeds if a knowledge condition and an incentive condition hold. The principal need not match the agent's expertise (p. 12).
- Institutions that clarify advice-givers' incentives can support enlightenment and successful delegation (p. 12).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 1–2)
- Epigraph (Madison): popular government without popular information is "but a Prologue to a Farce or a Tragedy" (p. 1).
- The founders believed that democracy requires reasoned choice, and that reasoned choice requires knowing the consequences of one's actions (p. 1).
- Many observers conclude that voters, legislators and jurors cannot choose reasonably. Studies document broad and deep ignorance, and many people get information from short news pieces, ads and sound bites (p. 1).
- **Democratic dilemma:** the people called on to make reasoned choices may not be capable of it (p. 1). It is widely believed that there is a mismatch between democracy's requirements and most people's ability to meet them; if this mismatch is too prevalent, effective self-governance is impossible (p. 1).
- The authors concede ignorance and the risk of deception by people "of sinister designs". They do not concede that democracy must succumb (pp. 1–2).
- Their claims (p. 2):
  - Reasoned choice needs the ability to predict consequences of actions. They call this **knowledge**.
  - People ignore most available information.
  - People often substitute others' advice for missing information. This lowers cost but allows deception.
  - People choose whose advice to follow in systematic, predictable ways.
  - Institutions can help when they clarify advice-givers' incentives.
- Footnote 2: the book does not try to settle what democracy *should* do. The definition of reasoned choice is precise about information, knowledge and choice, and deliberately vague on normative debates. Readers can set their own required level of knowledge (p. 2).

### Democracy, Delegation, and Reasoned Choice (pp. 3–4)
- Modern democracies rest on delegation: people elect or appoint legislatures, executives, commissions, judges and juries (p. 3).
- **Dahl's worry:** by delegating, may I not end up with an "aristocracy of experts, or even false experts"? (p. 3). Many observers answer yes, because delegators seem less informed than those they delegate to (p. 3).
- Critics, by delegation (views of the cited critics, except where marked as the authors' own description):
  - Voters: the authors themselves call Cicero's remark that the common people lack wisdom, penetration and judgment an apt summary of modern voting studies. Iyengar says low knowledge lends credence to charges that popular control is "illusory" (p. 3).
  - Legislators: Weber says the bureaucracy's power position is overtowering, with the political master a "dilettante" facing the expert. Niskanen says officials are at the mercy of special interests and bureaucrats. Lowi says policy emerges from tripartite bargaining among administrators, members of Congress and organized interests (pp. 3–4).
  - Jurors: Posner says the jury's cognitive limits will become more costly. Abramson speaks of "trial by ignorance" (p. 4).
- **Common conclusion of the critics:** reasoned choice does not govern delegation. Schumpeter says the typical citizen becomes "a primitive again" in politics (p. 4).
- **Authors' reply:** this possibility is alarming, but capabilities and requirements are "not as mismatched as many critics would have us believe". They will identify when the mismatch does and does not exist (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* Many critiques implicitly assume that people who lack information cannot choose reasonably (p. 6). The authors challenge this by asking how much information reasoned choice really needs, and answer that it needs knowledge, not complete information (pp. 2, 4, 6).

### A Preview of Our Theory (pp. 4–5)
- Earlier work argued that voters use opinion leaders, parties and other cues to overcome information shortfalls (Berelson et al., Downs, and a later generation of scholars) (pp. 4–5). The authors agree that people can use substitutes for encyclopedic information (p. 5).
- They want more: *specific conditions* under which limited information supports reasoned choice. The questions they list (p. 5):
  - When do people use cues, and when do they seek more detail?
  - When are cues sufficient for reasoned choice?
  - When can cue-givers deceive?
  - Why do people rely on some cues and not others?
  - How do institutions affect cue use and effectiveness?
- Method: theories of attention, persuasion and delegation. Lineage: games of incomplete information, signaling and strategic communication models. They also use premises from cognitive science and social-psychological persuasion theory, so the theory bears on debates in cognitive science, economics, political science and psychology (pp. 5–6).

### Knowledge and Information (Chapter 2 preview) (pp. 6–7)
- Chapter 2 is a theory of attention: how humans cope with complexity and scarcity (p. 6).
- For many political issues, information is not scarce. The *cognitive resources* to process it are (p. 6). So people often have incomplete information.
- Implicit in many critiques is the claim that people who lack information cannot choose reasonably. The authors argue instead that such people solve enormously complex problems every day by using the information available and sorting useful from useless (p. 6).
- Reasoned choice requires knowledge, not complete information. Footnote 3: knowing which of two products is better is often enough to choose as if fully informed (p. 6).
- Information is useful only if it helps people avoid costly mistakes. If more information would not change a decision, it has no instrumental benefit and should be ignored (p. 6).
- Examples of ignoring information: taking medication without knowing all harmful conditions, buying a house with little information about neighbours. People do this because the cost of attending exceeds the value of use (pp. 6–7).
- Footnote 4: some information can make people choose worse. A newspaper endorsement of Jones may lead to a vote for Jones, who later opposes a policy both she and Smith support (p. 7).
- **Traffic-signal example:** advocates of complete information might argue that safe navigation needs as much information as possible about other drivers' intentions and their cars' speed, acceleration, direction and mass. At many intersections a traffic signal is a simple substitute for all of it. Where there is no signal or similar cue, reasoned choice needs more information. So limited information precludes reasoned choice only if people face complex political intersections and lack effective "political traffic signals" (p. 7).

*Intuition note:* Information is judged by whether it would change your decision. A cue is good if it lets you decide as you would with the full facts (p. 6–7).

### Persuasion, Enlightenment, and Deception (Chapters 3–4 preview) (pp. 7–11)
- Two ways to get knowledge: personal experience, or learning from others. Politics is often abstract and its consequences remote, so often only the second is available (p. 7).
- Many heuristics (simple means of generating information substitutes) have been proposed: opinion leaders, party identification, biased providers, campaign events, history, polls, costly action, fire alarms, similar-interest people, demographics, competition, endorsements, media (p. 8).
- **Problem:** quoting Sniderman, Brody and Tetlock, "the most serious risk" is that every correlation is taken as evidence of a new shortcut. The authors agree. They need a theory of *how people choose whom to believe* (p. 8).
- Chapter 3: who can learn from whom. Chapter 4: what people learn, and when learning from others substitutes for experience or prevents reasoned choice (p. 8).
- Three outcomes of trying to learn from others (p. 8):
  - **Enlightenment:** someone furnishes us with knowledge.
  - **Deception:** testimony reduces our ability to predict consequences, e.g. we believe a lie.
  - **Nothing learned:** beliefs unchanged.
- **Persuasion** is a successful attempt to change another's beliefs. Both enlightenment and deception require it (p. 9).
- **Contrast with tradition:** most scholars of communication and politics, dating back to Aristotle, treat a speaker's internal character (honesty, ideology, reputation) as a necessary condition for persuasion. The authors give different necessary and sufficient conditions (Chapter 3): persuasion requires that a listener perceive the speaker as both knowledgeable and trustworthy. Character can produce perceived trust, but *external forces can substitute for character* (p. 9).
- **Costly-effort example (p. 9), built from four suppositions:**
  1. The listener knows the speaker is one of three types: conservative-intense, conservative-non-intense, liberal-non-intense.
  2. The listener does not know which.
  3. The listener can choose reasonably only if she knows whether the speaker is liberal or conservative.
  4. Suppose that, if the listener observes that the speaker paid a quarter of his or her income to affect a policy outcome, she can conclude the speaker has intense preferences.

  If all four hold, the costly effort persuades, and she can infer the speaker is conservative.
- **Penalty for lying** (fines for perjury, loss of reputation) can also generate trust. The presence of a penalty may lead the listener to believe that certain types of lies are prohibitively costly, rendering certain statements very likely to be true (pp. 9–10). Footnote 6: a third force is some chance that statements are externally verified (p. 10).
- Implications (p. 10):
  - You do not necessarily learn more from people like you or people you like. Example: financial advisors for mutual funds, Mom for child rearing.
  - The conditions identify when a speaker can deceive (lies and is believed). Deception requires several factors that are "not trivially satisfied". Chapters 4 and 10 use this to show how institutions can be redesigned to reduce deception.
  - Context matters: two people making the same statement may differ in persuasiveness if only one faces penalties for lying.
  - Not everyone can persuade, and the theory is meant to explain whom people listen to.
- **Bounds on heuristics:** when ideology correlates highly with a speaker's knowledge and trustworthiness, people are likely to find ideological cues useful; when there is no clear correlation, ideology is useless. Reputation, party and ideology are useful heuristics only if they convey information about knowledge and trust. The converse is not true: knowledge and trust are the fundamental factors, the others are not (pp. 10–11).
- Chapter 4 (institutions): the key to enlightenment is that the listener has accurate beliefs about the speaker's knowledge and incentives; the key to deception is inaccurate beliefs about these (p. 11). Footnote 7: if you know a falsehood is coming, ignoring the speaker is optimal, so you can be deceived only if you mistake a false statement for a true one (p. 11).
- Where nature, cultural norms or political institutions give listeners "a window" to the speaker's interests, knowledge and incentives, the context is ripe for enlightenment. Otherwise people are likely to be deceived. The authors conclude Chapter 4 by arguing that reasoned choice is impossible only when information is limited *and* the conditions for enlightenment do not exist and cannot be created (p. 11).

*Intuition note:* Liking or resembling a speaker does not by itself make you learn more from them (p. 10). What the authors treat as fundamental is whether you perceive the speaker as knowledgeable and trustworthy. Costly effort and penalties for lying are external forces that can help create that trust. A heuristic is useful only if it conveys information about knowledge and trust (pp. 9–11).

### Successful Delegation and the Institutions of Knowledge (Chapter 5 preview) (pp. 11–12)
- Delegation has three possible consequences. It succeeds if the agent enhances the principal's welfare. It fails if the agent reduces it. It has no effect if the agent's actions do not affect the principal's welfare (p. 11).
- Two commonly cited reasons for failure: conflicting interests, and agent expertise the principal lacks. Under both conditions agents can act as they like and the principal cannot cause otherwise (pp. 11–12).
- **Delegation succeeds if two conditions are satisfied (p. 12):**
  - *Knowledge condition:* the principal can tell beneficial from detrimental agent actions, either through personal experience or by obtaining this knowledge from others. She need not know everything the agent knows.
  - *Incentive condition:* agent and principal have at least some goals in common. In many cases, satisfying the knowledge condition is sufficient for the incentive condition, since an enlightened principal can motivate the agent or reject its actions.
- The outcome is not determined by whether the principal matches the agent's technical expertise. It is determined by her ability to use others' testimony effectively. If she lacks information about the agent and lacks that ability, delegation is doomed (p. 12).
- Institutions (administrative procedure, rules of evidence, statutory law) provide the context in which principals learn about agents' actions. Poorly designed or opaque institutions make failed delegation likely. Institutions that clearly structure incentives facilitate enlightenment, reasoned choice and successful delegation, even in complex circumstances (p. 12).

### Conclusion (pp. 12–13)
- The democratic dilemma is the mismatch between what delegation demands and citizens' capabilities (pp. 12–13).
- Reform-minded critics: if they are right, effective self-governance may indeed require reform. If wrong, reforms may restrain the competent and do more harm than good (p. 13).
- Optimists, who see heuristics as sufficient: if right, no reform is needed and the critics are "Chicken Littles". If wrong, the optimists are "Pollyannas" who defend harmful systems (p. 13).
- Both sides accept ignorance of details and the existence of cues. Missing is an understanding of when ignorance prevents reasoned choice, how people choose among heuristics, and when heuristics are effective substitutes. Only then can the two camps be separated and solutions built (p. 13).

### Plan of the Book (pp. 13–14)
- Part I (Chapters 2–5): theory. Part II (Chapters 6–9): tests. Chapter 6 sets standards for empirical research. Chapter 7 is lab experiments on persuasion, enlightenment and deception. Chapter 8 is lab experiments on delegation. Chapter 9 is a survey experiment on persuasion (p. 13).
- Part III (Chapter 10 and Afterword): implications for the effect and design of institutions. Chapter 10 examines U.S. and other institutions and how far they support successful delegation. The end of Chapter 10 and the Afterword discuss institutional reform (pp. 13–14).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A listener must decide how to vote on a bill and needs to know only one thing: is a speaker conservative or liberal? Assume three equally likely speaker types:
- A: conservative, intense. Willing to pay up to \$8,000 to lobby.
- B: conservative, non-intense. Willing to pay up to \$1,000.
- C: liberal, non-intense. Willing to pay up to \$1,000.

Assume each type pays for lobbying only if the cost is within its willingness.
- Before any observation: P(conservative) = 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3. She cannot be sure, so (by the setup) she cannot choose reasonably.
- She observes the speaker pay \$5,000. Only A is willing at \$5,000 (8,000 ≥ 5,000; 1,000 < 5,000).
- Posterior: P(A) = (1/3)/(1/3) = 1, so P(conservative) = 1. She is enlightened without trusting the speaker's character and without knowing every detail of the bill.

This mirrors the logic of the costly-effort example (p. 9): the cost reveals the speaker's incentives.

## Questions this raises
- How exactly are "knowledgeable" and "trustworthy" defined, and are they jointly sufficient for persuasion (Chapter 3)?
- How would one test that people choose cues "in systematic and predictable ways"? (Part II is said to do this.)
- Can institutions that clarify incentives be built cheaply for voters, not only for juries and legislators?
- If knowledge is defined relative to a chosen standard of reasoned choice, how much does the conclusion depend on that standard (fn. 2)?
