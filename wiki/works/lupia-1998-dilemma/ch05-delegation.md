# Delegation and Democracy

## L0 — One line
Delegation succeeds when the agent has an incentive to propose something good for the principal and the principal can tell good proposals from bad ones. Persuasive, enlightening testimony from others can supply that knowledge, so ignorance does not doom democratic delegation.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Critics hold that democratic delegation fails because agents have conflicting interests and know more than principals. The authors accept both premises but deny that principals are helpless. They build a spatial model with a principal, an agent and a speaker. Delegation depends on a knowledge condition (the principal can tell better proposals from worse ones) and an incentive condition (the agent offers proposals the principal prefers to the status quo). If both hold, delegation succeeds. If neither holds, it fails. If only one holds, the worst outcome is the status quo. The conditions for persuasion and enlightenment from earlier chapters can satisfy the knowledge condition. Many routes to success therefore exist, and some need little principal information.

**Prerequisites:** ideal points and single-peaked utility on a line; sequential games with a take-it-or-leave-it offer; Chapters 3–4 of this book (persuasion, enlightenment, speaker types).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Democracy requires delegation: from people to representatives, from representatives to leaders and bureaucracy, and from people to judges and jurors. The same actor can be agent in one relation and principal in another (p. 79).
- The authors define success as an agent's actions improving the principal's welfare, and failure as reducing it. Critics' pessimism rests on assuming principals cannot adapt to limited information (pp. 79, 81).
- A principal has three information sources: direct monitoring, the agent's self-report, and third-party testimony. Each has drawbacks, and relying on a third party is itself delegation (pp. 81–82).
- The model adds a speaker who may lie. The speaker is either persuasive (principal believes the signal) or not (signal ignored) (pp. 83–84).
- Theorem 5.1: both knowledge and incentive conditions give success. Neither gives failure. The text adds that with only one, the worst case for the principal is the status quo (pp. 85–86).
- Theorems 5.2 and 5.3 list the routes to each condition. When a proposal exists that both prefer to $sq$, a knowledgeable principal also gives the agent an incentive to propose something good for her (pp. 86–89).
- The principal need not be expert. The possibility of adapting by many means supports success (pp. 86, 89).
- With a tougher standard (gain must exceed a cost $K$), success still does not seem as improbable as often believed, but it gets harder as $K$ grows (pp. 91–92).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pp. 79–80).** Democracy requires delegation: people to representatives, representatives to leaders and bureaucracy, people to judges and jurors. Each delegation has a *principal* (who delegates) and an *agent* (who receives authority) (p. 79). Roles shift: jurors are agents of the people but principals for the attorneys. Legislators are agents of voters but principals of the bureaucracy (p. 79). The two dilemmas are conflicting interests and agents' information advantages. *Success* = the agent's actions improve the principal's welfare. *Failure* = they reduce it (p. 79).

**Critics' view.** Scholars of opinion and voting say the people-to-government delegation is ripe for systematic failure because of unprincipled officeholders and uninformed voters. Niskanen (1971) and Lowi (1979) say officials are at the mercy of special interests, lobbyists and bureaucrats (pp. 79–80). The authors accept that interests may conflict and that agents may have information advantages. They challenge the claim that principals lack means to cope (p. 80).

**Claim of the chapter.** If a principal has access to others' testimony, and either the ability to identify enlightening testimony or access to institutions that give her this ability, then delegation can succeed even when the dilemmas are present (p. 80).

### The Dilemma of Delegation (pp. 80–82)
- **Basic model (Fig. 5.1).** Nature gives information; unless otherwise stated, all is common knowledge. The agent proposes one alternative $x\in[0,1]$ to the status quo $sq$. The principal accepts $x$ or keeps $sq$. The one exception to common knowledge: the principal is uncertain whether $x$ is better or worse for her than $sq$ (pp. 80–81). Repeating the game over time models long-run delegation (fn. 4, p. 81).
- **Critics' assumption.** The authors argue that Lowi, Schumpeter and Weber, who say important delegations fail, reach that conclusion by assuming principals cannot adapt. Like the basic model, they assume no alternative to ignorance, so the conclusion is "not surprising" (p. 81).
- **Three sources.** (1) *Direct monitoring*: very costly. Voters, legislators and jurors have many duties, so monitoring all agents is prohibitively costly (pp. 81–82). (2) *Agent's self-report*: with conflicting interests the agent has no incentive to share expertise (p. 82). (3) *Third-party testimony*: no direct monitoring cost, and the speaker may lack the agent's conflict. But it is "itself an act of delegation". If interests conflict, the speaker has expertise, and the principal cannot adapt, she cannot learn from him (p. 82).
- If interests conflict, principals know too little, monitoring is too costly and they cannot learn from others, failure is inevitable. The authors look for conditions where it is not (p. 82).

### A Theory of Delegation with Communication (pp. 82–85)
Three players: principal, speaker, agent. Motivation (fn. 5, citing McCubbins and Schwartz 1984): when much is at stake, knowledgeable people have reason to speak up. The principal's problem is whose advice to follow. The model also covers an agent who speaks about his own proposal (agent and speaker are the same player) (p. 82).

- **A5.1 Goal-oriented.** Each player has an ideal point on $[0,1]$ and a single-peaked utility. Each prefers that the principal choose whichever of $x$, $sq$ is closer to his or her ideal point (p. 83). Results extend to N dimensions for binary choice, and to utilities that fall monotonically with distance (fn. 6).
- **A5.2 Uncertainty.** The principal knows $sq$ and her ideal point $p$, but is uncertain about the location of $x$ (p. 83).
- **A5.3 Speaker can lie.** The speaker says "better" or "worse" for the principal, and need not be truthful (p. 83).
- **A5.4 Sequence (Fig. 5.2).** Nature gives the common knowledge. The agent either pays an exogenous cost $C\ge 0$ and proposes $x$, or makes no proposal and the game ends at $sq$. Then the speaker speaks and the principal chooses $x$ or $sq$. Payoffs fall with distance from ideal point. The agent's payoff is reduced by $C$ if he proposes (pp. 83–84). Examples of $C$: ballot signatures, the collective action needed to place a bill on the agenda (pp. 83–84).
- **A5.5 Speaker type.** The speaker is *persuasive* (principal believes the signal and may learn) or *not persuasive* (signal ignored, as if no speaker; Fig. 5.1). Underlying the types are the conditions for persuasion of Chapter 3: the perceived relationship between speaker and principal interests, perceived knowledge, penalties for lying, verification, observable costly effort (pp. 84–85).

#### The general conditions (p. 85)
- **Knowledge condition:** satisfied if and only if the principal can correctly infer whether the agent's proposal is better or worse for her than $sq$.
- **Incentive condition:** the agent has an incentive to propose something better for the principal than $sq$. This holds if interests are common, or if external forces motivate the agent to propose something better than $sq$ for both.

**Theorem 5.1.** If both conditions are satisfied, delegation succeeds. If neither is, delegation fails (p. 85).

*Argument.* With both, the agent proposes something that helps the principal and she knows enough to accept. With neither, she cannot hold him accountable and he has no incentive to help her (p. 85). With only the knowledge condition, she can reject harmful proposals, so the worst case is $sq$. With only the incentive condition, the agent's proposal improves her welfare, so rejecting gives $sq$ and accepting does better (pp. 85–86).

*Intuition note.* Either an agent who has an incentive to propose well or a principal who can tell good proposals from bad protects her from loss. Both together produce a gain. Only when both are missing does she lose.

#### How to satisfy the knowledge condition (p. 86)
**Theorem 5.2.** The knowledge condition is satisfied only if (i) the principal's prior knowledge suffices to separate better from worse proposals, or (ii) she can learn enough to do so. When she initially lacks knowledge, the conditions for persuasion and enlightenment are necessary for the knowledge condition (p. 86).

Implications (authors' statements): if the incentive condition and both routes of 5.2 fail, delegation fails. If either route of 5.2 or the incentive condition holds, the result is no worse than $sq$. If the incentive condition and either route hold, delegation succeeds (p. 86). The principal need not know much. For example, the incentive condition plus prior knowledge, or knowledge gained from the agent's costly effort, or enlightenment by the speaker, means delegation can succeed (p. 86).

#### How to satisfy the incentive condition (pp. 86–89)
**Theorem 5.3.** The incentive condition is satisfied only if:
- the principal's ideal point is closer to the agent's ideal point than to $sq$, and the agent gains more than $C$ if the principal picks his proposal over $sq$; or
- the knowledge condition is satisfied and some point exists that both principal and agent (after paying $C$) prefer to $sq$ (p. 86).

*Why (p. 87).* Proposing nothing is costless, so it is always better for the agent to propose nothing than to propose something worse for him than $sq$. A necessary condition is therefore an alternative that the principal prefers to $sq$ and the agent prefers enough to pay $C$. If none exists, anything the agent prefers leaves her worse off, and vice versa. If the agent's ideal point is better for her than $sq$ and recovers $C$, he proposes it in equilibrium. Otherwise a knowledgeable principal is needed, for example one who learns from a persuasive speaker (p. 87).

**Example (Fig. 5.3, pp. 87–88).** $sq=.7$, principal's ideal point $p=.4$, agent's ideal point $0$ (unknown to the principal), $C=0$, uniform prior on $x$ over $[0,1]$.
- *No persuasive speaker.* The principal accepts and the agent proposes $0$. This is worse for her than $sq$, so the incentive and knowledge conditions fail and delegation fails (pp. 87–88).
- *Persuasive speaker, enlightenment holds.* The speaker says "better" if and only if $x$ really is better. The principal accepts only after "better". Proposing $0$ gives $sq$. Proposing a point between $.1$ and $.7$ gets "better" and acceptance. So the agent has an incentive to propose something good for her, and delegation succeeds (p. 88).
- *Deception holds instead.* The agent proposes his ideal point and the principal accepts it. Delegation fails (p. 88).

(added step) Why $.1$ to $.7$: the principal prefers $x$ to $sq$ when $|x-.4|<|.7-.4|=.3$, i.e. $.1<x<.7$.

**Cost as information (p. 88).** If $C$ is known (e.g. $1 million or a filibuster risk), the principal can infer that the proposal and $sq$ differ enough to make the agent pay. This parallels the speaker's costly effort in Chapter 3 (p. 88).

**Knowledge helps incentives (pp. 88–89).** A principal who can tell better from worse makes any proposal worse for her than $sq$ (including the agent's ideal point) lead to $sq$. So the agent should make only a proposal that leaves both better off. If she cannot distinguish, he has no reason to help her. Hence when a proposal exists that both prefer to $sq$, satisfying the knowledge condition is also sufficient for the incentive condition (pp. 88–89). Combining with 5.1: if knowledge and both of 5.3's conditions fail, delegation fails. If at least one holds, the result is no worse than $sq$. If knowledge and any 5.3 condition hold, delegation succeeds (p. 89).

### What It All Means (pp. 89–91)
Fig. 5.4a (a causal diagram, arrows left to right) links the persuasion/enlightenment conditions to the knowledge condition. Example path: penalties for lying → speaker perceived trustworthy → persuasive → enlightenment → knowledge condition → delegation succeeds. So penalties for lying can cause successful delegation (p. 89). Fig. 5.4b shows that Interest (alternatives both prefer to $sq$) supports the incentive condition, which together with the knowledge condition (AND) gives success (p. 91). Institutions can be compared by how far the left-hand factors (e.g. observable costly effort, speaker knowledge) hold in each. Chapter 10 does this (p. 89).

The authors read Theorems 5.1–5.3 as showing many routes to success. Delegation fails only if all of 5.2's and 5.3's conditions fail. It succeeds if any 5.2 condition and any 5.3 condition hold. Some routes need little principal information, so the possibility of adapting "by any number of means", not expert principals, supports success (pp. 89, 91).

*Intuition note.* Success does not need a principal who knows everything. It needs some route by which she can sort good proposals from bad, and an agent who gains from offering good ones.

### A Tougher Standard (pp. 91–92)
Delegation is costly (Alchian and Demsetz; Jensen and Meckling; fn. 9 mentions Kiewiet and McCubbins and Spence). Let setup and maintenance costs be a constant $K>0$. Tougher definition: delegation succeeds only if the agent's actions raise the principal's welfare by more than $K$. Otherwise it fails (p. 91–92).

**Theorem 5.1′.** If both conditions are satisfied, delegation *can* succeed. If either condition is not satisfied, delegation fails (p. 92).

Theorems 5.2 and 5.3 are unchanged. Under the tough definition delegation fails only if both of 5.2's conditions fail or both of 5.3's fail. It can succeed if any 5.2 condition and any 5.3 condition hold. Success depends on $K$: the larger it is, the harder success (fn. 10, p. 92). The authors say success does not seem as improbable as often believed (p. 92).

### Conclusion (pp. 92–93)
Delegation's outcome depends on the incentive and knowledge conditions. Both turn on whether the principal can become enlightened, which depends on the conditions for persuasion and enlightenment. Only when these fail do limited information and conflicting interests imply that delegation must fail (p. 92). The authors recap Chapters 2–4 (focused attention and connections let few facts support reasoned choices, learning from others, institutional features promoting persuasion). They conclude that people can adapt to limited information in many ways, so seemingly ill-informed people can be capable of reasoned self-governance (pp. 92–93). If actors cannot or do not adapt, they will fail to choose reasonably and cannot delegate successfully (p. 93).

## Toy example (our own, not from the text)
Take $sq=.8$, principal ideal point $p=.5$, agent ideal point $.1$, $C=0$, uniform prior on $x$.
- Distance of $sq$ from $p$: $|.8-.5|=.3$. The principal prefers $x$ iff $|x-.5|<.3$, i.e. $.2<x<.8$.
- Without a persuasive speaker, her expected distance is $E|x-.5|=(.5^2+.5^2)/2=.25<.3$, so she accepts. The agent proposes $.1$. Its distance is $|.1-.5|=.4>.3$, so she is worse off. Delegation fails.
- With an enlightening speaker she accepts only "better". Proposing $.1$ gives $sq$ (agent's distance $|.8-.1|=.7$). Proposing $.25$ is accepted (her distance $.25<.3$). The agent's distance is $|.25-.1|=.15<.7$, so he gains. Both are better off than at $sq$, and delegation succeeds.
