# How People Learn from Others

## L0 — One line
People believe a speaker when they perceive him as knowledgeable and as having a reason to tell the truth, and that reason can come from external forces (verification, penalties, costly effort) instead of from his personal character.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how people decide whom to believe when they lack personal experience and do not know the speaker well. Aristotelian views make persuasion depend on the speaker's personal character. The authors build a model in which a principal hears a "better"/"worse" message from a speaker whose knowledge and interests she is unsure about. Absent external forces, persuasion requires perceived common interests and perceived speaker knowledge, not actual ones. External forces (verification, penalties for lying, costly effort) can substitute for common interests. Cues like party or ideology help only if they signal knowledge or trust. So, given a speaker she perceives as knowledgeable and trustworthy, limited information need not block reasoned choice.

**Prerequisites:** Bayes' rule and expected utility; basic game-theory vocabulary (players, nature's moves, equilibrium, signalling); necessary vs. sufficient conditions.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Reasoned choice often requires learning from others, so persuasion (one person's successful attempt to change another's beliefs) is a necessary condition for it in those settings (pp. 39–40).
- Aristotle treats a speaker's character (good sense, excellence, good will) as almost the most effective means of persuasion, alongside the content of the speech. The authors reject two premises behind this: that people know one another well, and that character assessment is the only basis for trust (pp. 41–43).
- Basic model: a principal chooses x or y after a cheap-talk message. She is uncertain about which option is better (b), about the speaker's knowledge (k) and about common interests (c) (pp. 45–49).
- Theorems 3.1–3.2: absent external forces, perceived common interests and perceived knowledge are each necessary, neither sufficient. Their corollaries: the actual ones are neither necessary nor sufficient (pp. 50–51).
- Verification, penalties for lying and observable costly effort give the principal a window on the speaker's incentives. Theorem 3.3: they can substitute for perceived common interests and for each other (pp. 53–60).
- Further results: an observer who believes her interests conflict with both speaker and principal can be persuaded to do the opposite of what the speaker advises; reputation, ideology, partisanship and affect are neither necessary nor sufficient; the authors call the jury-theorem assumption that people believe everything they hear unreasonable in elections, legislatures and courtrooms (pp. 60–64).
- Cues such as party or ideology are useful only if they convey information about knowledge and trust (p. 64).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 39–40)
- Reasoned choice requires knowledge: people must predict the consequences of their actions. Knowledge comes from personal experience or from what others say, write or do (p. 39).
- In many political settings only the second source is available, because politics creates problems unfamiliar to people's own experience. Then a person needs the opportunity and the ability to learn from others (p. 39).
- Plan: this chapter explains *how* people learn from others (who can learn from whom; whose advice they follow). Chapter 4 explains *what* they learn, i.e. when learning from others substitutes for personal experience (pp. 39–40).
- Existing answers, each a "judgmental shortcut", include party identification, known issue biases, likability, observed behaviour, competition, shared policy interests, elite status, and aggregate actions (polls, media, campaign spending, protests, public mood) (p. 40).
- The authors agree with Sniderman, Brody and Tetlock that the risk is treating every correlation as a new shortcut. No one can use all shortcuts all the time, so each person must choose whom to believe (p. 40).
- They define **persuasion** as one person's successful attempt to change another's beliefs. Where reasoned choice needs learning from others, persuasion is necessary for reasoned choice. "How do people choose whom to believe?" is equivalent to "Who can persuade whom?" (pp. 40–41).

### The Aristotelian theories of persuasion (pp. 41–43)
- **Aristotle's view (Rhetoric, as quoted):** persuasion works through the speaker's character, the audience's frame of mind, and the proof in the speech. Character is almost the most effective means, especially where exact certainty is impossible and opinions are divided (p. 41).
- Character means good sense, excellence and good will. False statements come from lack of sense, moral badness, or lack of good will toward hearers. Someone thought to have all three inspires trust (p. 41). The authors gloss these in footnote 3 as germane knowledge, moral fortitude to act on what is good for the other, and wanting what is good for the other (p. 41).
- The authors argue that most modern political issues are of this uncertain, divided kind, so Aristotle would expect character judgments to decide whom to believe. Their own conclusion is that persuasion *need not* depend on such judgments (p. 41).
- **Two premises separate them from Aristotle (p. 42):**
  1. Aristotle assumes the small Athenian city-state, where citizens know one another well. In modern society we are often uncertain about others' knowledge and motives. People are influenced by the *New York Times* or CNN while knowing little about the character of those who write or speak there.
  2. Persuasion does not require positive or affective character assessments of any kind. Incentive-altering **external forces** (culture, norms, markets, political and legal institutions) offer other ways to judge credibility. Example: millions of market transactions between strangers rest on laws and customs that realign incentives. The authors argue that analogous substitutes make advanced democracies possible.
- They claim the Aristotelian view pervades current work. Game-theoretic models assume actors know one another's ideal points, and psychologists use speaker-attribute ratings (honesty, fairness). Ideology, likability, partisanship, known biases and elite status are all personal-characteristic explanations (p. 43). Footnote 5 says that in Crawford–Sobel and Gilligan–Krehbiel models, proximate interests are both necessary and sufficient for persuasion and for communication to lead to an increase in knowledge (p. 43).
- Their alternative rests on two premises: "people may not know one another well" and "incentives matter". They derive necessary and sufficient conditions for persuasion in a simple setting (p. 43).

**Intuition note.** Aristotle assumes a village-like society where everyone knows everyone's character. Where people are strangers, the authors say, another basis for trust is needed, and rules and incentives can provide it.

### Our theory of persuasion: setting (pp. 43–45)
- The theory descends from games of incomplete information, signalling and strategic-communication models. The authors say they quarrel not with Crawford–Sobel or Spence, which suit bargaining where parties know each other well, but with importing those models into politics. Proofs are in the Chapter 3 Appendix (pp. 43–44, fn. 8).
- Dual-process theories (central vs. peripheral route) are, in the authors' view, hard to compare because they address how much attention is paid, not how people choose among heuristics. They regard attention as a prerequisite for filtering advice and say the two accounts are consistent where they overlap (fn. 9, p. 44).
- Learning is active (Chapter 2). Whether many advise one or one advises many, persuasion rests on individual decisions about what to say and whom to believe (pp. 44–45).
- The decision maker is badly informed and lacks two kinds of information: the advice giver's intentions and his expertise (p. 45).

### The basic model (pp. 45–49)
**Players and choice.** A principal and a speaker. At the end the principal chooses one of two alternatives, x or y. Binary choice is common in politics: legislative agendas, juries, regulatory decisions, vetoes, many elections (p. 45, fn. 10). The speaker first provides information about the relative attributes of x and y. Unless otherwise stated, everything about the interaction is common knowledge (pp. 45–46). The basic model modifies the Crawford–Sobel cheap talk model by adding uncertainty about knowledge and about interests (p. 46).

**Nature's three probabilistic moves** (order irrelevant to results; p. 46):
1. *Which alternative is better.* Nature chooses "better" with probability $b\in[0,1]$ and "worse" with $1-b$. If the principal chooses x she earns $U\ge 0$ when x is better and $\underline U\le 0$ when x is worse. If she chooses y she earns 0 (without loss of generality). Note $U$ need not equal $-\underline U$. The prior $b$ is her belief about which alternative is better, and the key assumption is that she is uncertain about this (p. 46, fns. 12–13).
2. *Whether the speaker knows.* "Speaker knows whether x is better or worse than y" with probability $k\in[0,1]$, "does not know" with $1-k$. The principal knows $k$ but not how much the speaker actually knows. In the standard cheap talk model it is common knowledge that $k=1$ (pp. 46–48, fn. 14).
3. *Interests.* With probability $c\in[0,1]$ the interests are **common**: the speaker gets $Z\ge 0$ when $U\ge 0$ and $\underline Z\le 0$ when $\underline U\le 0$. With probability $1-c$ they **conflict**: the speaker gets $\underline Z\le 0$ when $U\ge 0$ and $Z\ge 0$ when $\underline U\le 0$. The speaker earns 0 when the principal chooses y (p. 48). The possibility of conflict makes this *strategic communication* in Goffman's sense (fn. 15). Aristotelian theories assume the principal knows the speaker's interests, but the authors say political complexity makes this unlikely (p. 48).

The authors avoid the term "type". They note that the speaker can be one of six types in the extensive form, but they treat uncertainty about interests and about knowledge as separable (fn. 11, p. 46).

**Play (p. 49).** (1) The speaker sends "better" ("I assert x is better for the principal") or "worse". He need not tell the truth. (2) The principal chooses x or y, and payoffs are received. Two-valued messages are a simplifying assumption. The authors say both messages contain knowledge sufficient for reasoned choice here (fn. 16, p. 49).

**Two equilibria (Proposition 3.1 in the Appendix; p. 49):**
- *Persuasive equilibrium:* the principal bases her choice on what the speaker says. The speaker tells the truth only if he knows or believes himself to have common interests with the principal. Otherwise he lies.
- *Nonpersuasive equilibrium:* the principal ignores the speaker.
- The principal's priors about the speaker's interests and knowledge decide which occurs. The persuasive one can occur when she assesses a high probability of common interests and of speaker knowledge (p. 49).
- Deception can occur in the first equilibrium because people do not know each other well. In the standard cheap talk model people cannot deceive one another in equilibrium, since they see lies coming (p. 49).

### Results of the basic model (pp. 50–51)
- **Theorem 3.1:** Absent external forces, perceived common interests are a necessary condition for persuasion, but not sufficient (p. 50).
- **Theorem 3.2:** Absent external forces, perceived speaker knowledge ($k>0$) is a necessary condition for persuasion, but not sufficient (p. 50).
- Reasoning given: persuasion requires the principal to believe the statement will help her avoid costly mistakes. If she is certain the speaker lacks the knowledge, or likely has conflicting interests, she ignores him. Even certainty of common interests does not help if she thinks the speaker lacks knowledge (p. 50).
- Beliefs that conservatives are necessarily more persuasive to conservatives, or African-Americans to African-Americans, are therefore only conditionally reliable. Whatever his attributes, a speaker perceived to lack knowledge cannot persuade (p. 50).
- Footnote 18 (voice: authors on Crawford–Sobel): CS say equilibrium signalling is more informative when preferences are more similar. The authors note that CS's Corollary 1 shows the number of partitions falls to one (an uninformative message) even for relatively small interest conflicts. A similar result arises in the authors' model by restricting $c$ to 0 or 1 (p. 50).
- Knowledge alone is not enough: even a seemingly knowledgeable speaker persuades only if trusted. Examples given: Nixon on Watergate, Clinton on marijuana use (p. 51).
- **Corollary to 3.1:** Actual common interests are neither necessary nor sufficient for persuasion. **Corollary to 3.2:** Actual speaker knowledge is neither necessary nor sufficient (p. 51).
- So a speaker with common interests and the knowledge can fail to persuade. A speaker who knows nothing and has conflicting interests can persuade (p. 51).

**Intuition note.** What counts is what the principal *believes* about the speaker, because people do not know one another well. Absent external forces, beliefs about knowledge and about interests are two separate gates, and both must be open.

*Example 1, case 1* (p. 51–52).
- Prior $b=.35$ gives expected utility of x of $-.3$, below the 0 from y, so absent persuasion she chooses y.
- After "better" her posterior that x is better is about .51 and her expected utility from x is about .03 > 0, so she chooses x.
- The numerator of the expected utility given "better" has six terms: $kcbU + k(1-c)(1-b)\underline U + (1-k)(1-c)bU + (1-k)(1-c)(1-b)\underline U + (1-k)cbU + (1-k)c(1-b)\underline U$ (fn. 20, p. 52). Reading the six terms as the ways a speaker says "better": an informed speaker with common interests says it when x is better, an informed speaker with conflicting interests says it when x is worse, and an uninformed speaker says it in either state *(added step: my reading of the terms)*.
- Cases 2–4 each change one factor: lower $c$, lower $k$ (case 3: posterior equals prior), and a prior closer to 0 or 1. In all three the speaker is not credible enough to change her beliefs, so she ignores him (p. 52).

*Example 2.*
- Case 1: $c=1$, $b=.4$. Prior expected utility of x is $-.2$. After "better" the posterior is about .57 and the expected utility about .14, so she chooses x (pp. 52–53).
- Cases 2–4: lower confidence in interests or in knowledge, or a more certain prior ($b=.1$), remove persuasion (p. 53).
- Deception is possible only in Example 1, case 1. Common interests there have probability .8, so with probability .2 nature draws conflicting interests, and then the speaker lies and successfully deceives. With common interests he tells the truth (p. 53).

### Three extensions: external forces (pp. 53–60)
Three forces, each common in politics, represent classes of forces that alter speaker and principal incentives (p. 53).
1. **Verification:** after the speaker speaks and before the principal chooses, nature reveals to her whether x is better or worse, with probability $0<v<1$. $v=0$ is the basic model and $v=1$ is trivial (p. 53, fn. 21).
2. **Penalty for lying:** a cost $pen>0$ is deducted from the speaker's utility whenever he sends a false signal. Truth-telling payoffs are as in the basic model. This is a *statement-specific* cost, motivated by perjury fines and reputational loss for dishonesty. The O. J. Simpson/Fuhrman example is used to illustrate statement-specific costs (pp. 53–54, fn. 22).
3. **Observable costly effort:** a cost $cost\ge 0$ must be paid to send any signal at all. Without payment no signal is received (p. 54).

Verification changes how the signal is received. The other two change the speaker's costs. Together, the authors say, they cover the range of effects external forces can have on communication (p. 54).

**Theorem 3.3 (Conditions for Persuasion; p. 54–55).** The following are individually necessary and collectively sufficient for persuasion: the principal must perceive the speaker to be trustworthy, and must perceive him to have the knowledge she desires. Absent external forces, this requires perceived common interests and perceived speaker knowledge. With external forces the requirements can be reduced: as the likelihood of verification, the magnitude of the penalty, or the magnitude of costly effort increases, the extent to which perceived common interests are required decreases. External forces can be substitutes for common interests and for each other (p. 55).

Consequences:
- Persuasion occurs *only if* the principal is initially uncertain which alternative is better, believes the speaker may have the knowledge, and believes he has an incentive to reveal it. If one fails, persuasion cannot occur (p. 55).
- The principal's perceptions of the speaker's incentives and knowledge are what psychologists call the fundamental source effects. Other source effects (party, ideology, reputation) work *because* they shape those perceptions (p. 55).
- **Corollary to 3.3:** Perceived common interests are not necessary for persuasion (p. 55).
- When incentive-altering forces are present, ideology, partisanship, reputation, actual knowledge or affect may, the authors say, have no bearing at all on persuasiveness (p. 55). In a court, perjury threats and cross-examination can give the principal a reason to believe a speaker with conflicting interests (p. 56). A speaker she finds "perfectly disgusting" can be believed if external forces make him truthful and she thinks him knowledgeable (p. 56).

**Intuition note.** Trust needs a *basis*. The basis can be a shared interest, or it can be a rule that makes lying costly for the speaker.

**Verification (pp. 56–57).**
- The same two equilibria as in the basic model (Proposition 3.2).
- It works by threatening that the principal can tell true signals from false ones. As $v$ rises, the chance the speaker gains from a false signal falls. Only a speaker with conflicting interests can gain from lying, so the direct effect falls on him. Under common interests the speaker is indifferent whether the principal learns from him or by verification (p. 56).
- Limit: verification alone does not make a would-be liar tell the truth. It only makes his false signal ineffective, so it lowers the expected value of communication for speakers who gain from lying (p. 57).
- Competition is neither necessary nor sufficient for persuasion. It is not the only route to trust, and added competitors need not be knowledgeable and trustworthy. A competitor induces persuasion only by creating one of the Theorem 3.3 conditions (p. 57).

**Penalties for lying (pp. 57–58).**
- In the basic equilibrium the principal does not follow a speaker she knows would lie. Lying happens when the speaker expects to be mistaken for a truth teller (p. 57). Standard cheap talk rules deception out by assuming rational expectations about the truth value of signals (p. 57).
- Penalties help when they let a principal who would otherwise expect lies believe she can tell truth tellers from liars (Proposition 3.3). Small penalties leave the equilibrium as before. If penalties are large enough, the speaker never deceives, but they need not be that large (p. 58).
- **Authors' example:** the speaker knows the truth and has conflicting interests. If x is better for the principal, the speaker loses \$20 when she chooses x. If x is worse, he earns \$75 when she chooses x. Choosing y pays him nothing, and the lying penalty is \$50. When x is better, lying costs \$50 to avoid a \$20 loss, so he tells the truth ("better"). When x is worse, lying nets $75-50>0$ *(added step: arithmetic)*, so he may say "better" or "worse". So on hearing "worse" the principal can infer it is true (p. 58).
- General inference after a statement: either it is true, or it is false and the value of lying to the speaker exceeds the expected penalty. This gives a "window" on the speaker's incentives (p. 58).

**Observable costly effort (pp. 58–59).**
- Logic: "actions speak louder than words", equivalent to Spence's costly signalling (Proposition 3.4). A speaker who pays \$100 for the chance to persuade reveals that the difference in expected value to him between the principal's action after hearing him and without hearing him is at least \$100. This holds even if he speaks in a language we do not understand (p. 58).
- **Figure 3.2.** Let $y=.5$, let $C$ be the speaker's cost to get a chance to say "better" or "worse". Assume the speaker knows where x lies when he pays, the principal knows this, and only a policy change of distance $.2$ or more makes paying $C$ worthwhile. Then on seeing payment, she infers that x is not within $.2$ of y. Her belief about x (uniform on $[0,1]$ before) now excludes the interval $(.3,.7)$ (p. 59).

### Dynamic implications (pp. 59–60)
- External forces substitute for each other: verification lowers the perceived common interests *and* perceived knowledge needed for persuasion, and also the penalty or effort needed. The authors say this is because each gives the speaker incentives and the principal a window on them. Together they work in complementary ways (pp. 59–60).
- Theorems 3.1–3.3 give *minimum* requirements. If the principal is very sure of her prior, e.g. $b=.98$, she cannot be talked out of it unless $c$ and $k$ are very high (p. 60).
- All else constant, as $b$ approaches 0 or 1 the magnitude of verification, costly effort or penalty required is nondecreasing. External forces work best when the principal lacks strong priors. At the extreme, only the strongest force can make her believe the speaker (p. 60).

### More than one principal (pp. 60–61)
- Puzzle: sometimes we are persuaded *because* a speaker has conflicting interests. Example: we can imagine Mr. Colin, who ardently opposes environmental regulation, being persuaded by a pro-environment group's endorsement; he may take what they say and do the opposite. The basic model and Aristotelian theories cannot produce this (p. 60).
- Extension: add **observers**, players who cannot directly affect the speaker's utility (people at a rally or watching a televised speech) (p. 61).
- The authors state these as sufficient bases for an observer's trust: she believes the speaker has an incentive to be truthful to her; or she believes her interests conflict with both the principal's and the speaker's, and that the speaker has an incentive to be truthful to the principal (p. 61).
- If the observer shares interests with both speaker and principal, she can be persuaded as a spillover. If her interests conflict with both, she should take the advice and do the opposite. Example: a Democrat watching Newt Gingrich address Republican supporters, who believes he is knowledgeable and trying to win the group's favour, and that she conflicts with both (p. 61).
- Observers can be persuaded by speakers perceived to have conflicting interests *only if* the speakers face external forces or have common interests with the principal addressed (p. 61).

### More than one speaker (pp. 61–62)
Added speakers can matter in two ways. They may have different characteristics or face different external forces, so they may persuade where the first did not. Or their presence may create external forces on the first speaker (making him easier to verify, penalize, or impose effort costs on) (pp. 61–62).

### Persuasive implications (pp. 62–64)
Three implications: all statements are not equally informative, all speakers are not equally persuasive, and you do not necessarily learn more from people who are like you (p. 62).
- *Statements* vary in content and in context. The same person saying the same thing to the same audience under different external forces need not be equally persuasive (p. 62).
- *Speakers:* persuasiveness depends on how a speaker is perceived. Condorcet Jury Theorem variants (Feddersen–Pesendorfer, Grofman–Feld) assume people believe everything they hear. The authors say that in elections, legislatures and courtrooms this is unreasonable, and Theorem 3.3 implies believing everything is warranted only under extraordinary circumstances. They judge the relevance of jury-theorem results there "tenuous at best". Ladha's error-tolerant variants are an exception, and they want such models to go further (p. 62, fn. 32).
- "Knowledge is power" fails unless the knowledgeable can persuade. Footnote 33: since we know little about the speakers we rely on, we must use subjective beliefs about them. A speaker who actually has the knowledge but is believed ignorant gives us no grounds to follow him (p. 63).
- *Like you / liked by you:* you follow your pediatrician rather than what your children want. With high lying penalties, people can be persuaded by speakers they know to have conflicting interests (p. 63).
- **Reputation** is neither necessary nor sufficient. If sufficient, two interactions between the same principal and speaker would necessarily make him trustworthy (a simple model extension shows this false). If necessary, the principal in the model would never believe the speaker. Reputation generates persuasiveness only if it generates the conditions for persuasion. Footnote 34 adds that learning from others does not require repeated interaction (p. 63).
- **Ideology, affect, partisanship** are likewise neither necessary nor sufficient. Example: Mr. A is liked and conservative like you but known to know nothing of policy B, so do not follow him. If instead he is knowledgeable, nonconservative and unlikable but has a strong incentive to reveal what he knows, follow him (p. 63).
- **Analyst's test (p. 64).** For the hypothesis "factor F makes the listener change her mind on T", ask whether the listener perceives F as correlated with (a) the speaker's knowledge of T and (b) the speaker's interests or incentives to reveal what he knows. If "No" to both, F cannot be a cause of persuasion in this model's sense and the hypothesis should be discarded. If "Yes" to either, continue.
- Where ideology correlates highly with the factors behind the conditions (common interests, penalties for certain lies), it can be a good trust indicator. Where it does not, ideology and party are useless cues. Authors' summary: reputation, party or ideology are useful heuristics only if they convey information about knowledge and trust. The converse is not true (p. 64).

**Intuition note.** A cue is only a clue to the two things that matter. If the listener does not link it to knowledge or incentives, it does no persuading.

### Conclusion (pp. 64–65)
- The authors' theorems suggest that people suffer the consequences of limited information less often than many scholars and pundits proclaim (p. 64).
- If a principal has access to at least one speaker she perceives as knowledgeable and trustworthy, limited information need not preclude reasoned choice. Trust need not derive from character assessment, since external forces that clarify incentives can serve as its basis. Reasoned choice then requires neither encyclopedic information nor relevant personal experience (p. 64).
- Critics who think people are easily deceived overlook that learning is active. If it were passive, every heavily advertised product would win universally rave reviews, and it does not (p. 65).

### Food for thought: two parables (pp. 65–67)
- **Gritz (pp. 65–66).** The U.S. government sent retired Colonel Bo Gritz as negotiator at Ruby Ridge and with the Montana Freemen, though he held views hostile to government (details on p. 66). *Authors' reading:* compromise requires negotiation, which requires persuasion, which requires perceived knowledge and trust. Militias premise the illegitimacy of government, so government-friendly negotiators (the authors name Powell and Schwarzkopf) were less likely to be trusted. Gritz had "militia credentials". The standoffs ended as the government wished, and Gritz was widely credited with a role. He was an "effective extremist" (p. 66).
- **Tamales (p. 67).** From Popkin's *The Reasoning Voter*: in 1976 President Ford bit into a tamale with the corn husk on. Readers who know Mexican food see ignorance. Others do not initially get the point. Even those who see it may not link it to his credibility on issues. The authors conclude it is unwise to treat simple cues (party, ideology, reputation) as affecting a populace uniformly, and repeat that persuasion is a function of perception, context and choice (p. 67).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take the book's setting with $U=1$, $\underline U=-1$. Let $b=.4$, $c=.9$, $k=.8$. Assume the speaker behaves as in the persuasive equilibrium, reading the six terms of fn. 20 (p. 52): informed with common interests says "better" iff x is better; informed with conflict says "better" iff x is worse; uninformed always says "better". This computes a posterior and does not prove an equilibrium.

Prior expected utility of x: $.4(1)+.6(-1)=-.2<0$, so absent persuasion she chooses y.

*x better (prob .4), speaker says "better" in these branches:*
- informed and common: $kc=.8\times.9=.72$
- uninformed: $1-k=.2$
- Total: $.4\times(.72+.2)=.4\times.92=.368$.

*x worse (prob .6), speaker says "better" in these branches:*
- informed and conflict: $k(1-c)=.8\times.1=.08$
- uninformed: $.2$
- Total: $.6\times(.08+.2)=.6\times.28=.168$.

$P(\text{"better"})=.368+.168=.536$. Posterior: $.368/.536\approx .687$. Expected utility of x: $.687-.313\approx .373>0$, so she chooses x. Equivalently $(.368-.168)/.536=.2/.536\approx.373$.

*Lower trust in interests:* set $c=.5$, keeping $k=.8$ and $b=.4$.
- x better: $.4\times(.8\times.5+.2)=.4\times.6=.24$.
- x worse: $.6\times(.8\times.5+.2)=.6\times.6=.36$.
- Posterior: $.24/.60=.4$, equal to the prior, so expected utility stays $-.2$ and she ignores the speaker. This mirrors Table 3.1 (a lower $c$ removes persuasion).

## Concepts
- [[persuasion]] (new) — one person's successful attempt to change another's beliefs. Its conditions are the topic of the chapter (p. 40).
- [[conditions-for-persuasion-lupia-mccubbins]] — the principal must perceive the speaker as knowledgeable and trustworthy. Absent external forces this means perceived knowledge and perceived common interests (Theorem 3.3; p. 55).
- [[aristotelian-theories-of-persuasion]] (new) — authors' label for views that make persuasion depend on assessments of the speaker's personal character (pp. 41–43).
- [[cheap-talk-model]] (new) — Crawford–Sobel-style costless messages. Here modified so that the principal is uncertain about the speaker's knowledge ($k$) and interests ($c$) (pp. 46–48).
- [[external-forces]] (new) — culture, norms, markets, political and legal institutions that alter what people say and believe. In the model, verification, penalties for lying and costly effort (pp. 42, 53–54).
- [[verification]] (new) — nature reveals to the principal whether x is better, with probability $v$. It makes false signals ineffective but does not penalize them (pp. 53, 56–57).
- [[penalty-for-lying]] (new) — a statement-specific cost $pen$ paid by a lying speaker. It lets the principal infer that a statement is either true, or false with a value to the speaker greater than the expected penalty (pp. 53–54, 58).
- [[costly-signaling]] (new) — observable costly effort reveals how much the speaker values the outcome (Spence-style logic) (pp. 54, 58–59).
- [[heuristics-cues]] — party, ideology and reputation are useful cues only if they convey information about knowledge and trust (p. 64).
- [[condorcet-jury-theorem]] — mentioned as assuming people believe everything they hear. The authors say its relevance to politics is tenuous (p. 62).

## Canonical instances
- [[ford-tamale-1976]] — a cue (a gaffe) affects people non-uniformly, depending on what they perceive and connect to credibility (p. 67).
- [[bo-gritz-negotiation]] — a government-hostile negotiator whom, on the authors' suggestion, the government could expect militias to perceive as knowledgeable and trustworthy (pp. 65–66).
- [[mr-colin-reverse-persuasion]] — a hypothetical listener who may do the opposite of what a speaker with conflicting interests advises, which the observer extension explains (pp. 60–61).
- [[witness-perjury-court]] — a court's perjury threat gives a basis to trust a speaker whose interests conflict (p. 56).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Costly effort as a signal ↔ economics: Spence job-market signalling. The author says the logic is equivalent (fn. 30, p. 58).
- [same-math] Posterior belief after hearing "better" ↔ statistics: Bayes' rule over speaker types (fn. 19–20).
- [structural-analogy] Principal who needs advice from a speaker she cannot monitor ↔ principal–agent / delegation: incentives and monitoring replace knowledge of the agent's character.
- [structural-analogy] Perceived knowledge and interests as conditions for believing a speaker ↔ Lupia–McCubbins conditions for persuasion (the canon concept).
- [metaphor] External forces as substitutes for trust ↔ institutions as rules of the game: laws realign incentives among strangers.

## Questions this raises
- The proofs are in the Appendix, which is not in this unit. Under what exact conditions on $b$, $c$, $k$, $U$, $Z$ does the persuasive equilibrium exist?
- Chapter 4 asks when learning from others can substitute for personal experience. Can persuaded principals actually choose as if fully informed?
- The speaker's costs are common knowledge here. How robust is the result when the principal is also unsure about them? (The authors say it is robust, fn. 23, p. 54.)
- How would one measure *perceived* knowledge and incentives empirically (the analyst's test, p. 64)?
