# The Institutions of Knowledge

## L0 — One line
Electoral, legislative, bureaucratic and legal institutions can help poorly informed principals decide whom to believe, by clarifying speakers' interests, penalising lies, threatening verification and requiring costly effort, but only if their effects really give speakers an incentive to be trustworthy and principals widely believe they do.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The question: can voters, legislators and jurors who lack detailed knowledge still choose well by deciding whom to trust? The authors answer that institutions can supply the conditions for persuasion and enlightenment. They do this by clarifying speakers' interests, imposing penalties for lying, creating a threat of verification, or requiring costly effort. They must also be widely believed to have this effect. The chapter walks through electoral, legislative, bureaucratic and courtroom examples. It then argues that nonpartisan elections and term limits seem likely to hinder reasoned choice, and that deliberation is far more likely to help when these conditions are present. This matters because it ties institutional design to the democratic dilemma: reasoned choice without full information.

**Prerequisites:** principal–agent delegation; the book's earlier "conditions for persuasion" (speaker perceived as knowledgeable and trustworthy); basic knowledge of US/Japanese/UK legislative organisation. Terms used below: principal = the person or group that delegates (voter, legislator, juror); agent = whoever acts for them; enlightenment and reasoned choice are earlier-chapter terms this chapter uses without defining; ex post = after the fact, ex ante = beforehand; closed-list PR = voters choose parties, not individual candidates (p. 207); abdication = delegation where the people's will neither constrains nor motivates policy (p. 215); NEPA = the US National Environment Policy Act of 1969 (p. 216); fire alarm oversight = citizens and groups bring agency violations to the legislature (p. 219).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Institutions help only if their incentive effects make speakers trustworthy and principals perceive this. This is the criterion the chapter applies when asking which institutions promote enlightenment (pp. 205–206).
- Electoral rules can clarify interests: plurality and closed-list PR systems give parties reasons to build reliable "brand names". A cue works only when voters see it as a better indicator of knowledge and trust than other cues (pp. 207–208).
- Competition, a free press, disclosure laws and observable campaign spending can give voters verification and costly-effort signals. Subsidies and spending limits weaken the cost signal (pp. 208–210).
- Legislatures use common interests, penalties for lying, checks between committees and leaders, and costly procedures. This can help members judge advice they cannot evaluate directly (pp. 210–215).
- Delegation to bureaucrats risks abdication. Legislators can reduce this by stacking procedures (NEPA, burden of proof), overlapping jurisdictions, ex post sanctions and "fire alarm" oversight, which verifies agencies (pp. 215–222).
- Trials supply perjury penalties, evidence rules, visible motives and cross-examination, so, the authors say, judicial institutions can help relatively uninformed jurors make reasoned choices (pp. 223–225).
- Nonpartisan elections and term limits seem likely to hinder reasoned choice; deliberation is far more likely to be beneficial where conditions for enlightenment hold. Persuasion is not enlightenment (pp. 225–227).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 205–206)
Democracy delegates sovereign authority to elected or appointed agents. Institutions define these delegations and shape the incentives of principals and agents (p. 205). Altering agents' incentives is not enough. The effects must be "salutary" (give agents and speakers an incentive to be trustworthy) and must be widely believed to have this effect. Then institutions can create the conditions for persuasion, enlightenment and reasoned choice, and so help resolve the democratic dilemma (p. 205). The chapter examines electoral, legislative, bureaucratic and judicial institutions to see when they do this (pp. 205–206).

Recurring themes: *clarifying interests/common interests*, *penalties for lying*, *verification*, *costly effort*.

### Electoral institutions (pp. 206–210)
Voters know little detail, but that does not by itself imply they cannot choose reasonably. The question is whether institutions help them use substitutes for knowledge (p. 206).

**Clarifying interests.** No one can attend to all cues, so voters follow some and ignore others (p. 206). Party identification is a useful cue only if it conveys information about knowledge and trust. If voters are to be influenced by partisan appeals, they must perceive co-partisan speakers as knowledgeable and have some basis for trusting them. That basis can be forged from perceived common interests or from an external force making speakers trustworthy (p. 206).
- Single-member plurality districts reward large parties with disproportionate seat shares (Rae, Lijphart) and tend to support two large parties (Cox, Duverger). Candidates thus have an incentive to coordinate positions, building a reliable party brand name (p. 207).
- Closed-list PR works similarly: voters choose parties, often with a legal threshold (e.g. 5 percent), so parties link policy to the party. In closed-list PR systems party is the only widely used voting cue (p. 207).
- Broad "peak associations" have similar incentives (p. 207).
- Forming a party or group does not guarantee persuasive power. Obscure groups are ignored, and electoral changes that make parties or groups take inconsistent positions can weaken the value of their cues (pp. 207–208).
- Footnote 2: if voters know candidates are supervised by party leaders, statements may face verification or penalty for lying (p. 207).

**General lesson:** any cue (party, ideology, gender, race) is effective only when voters perceive it as a better indicator of knowledge and trust than other available cues. To explain votes, we must explain how voters choose among cues and how institutions affect that choice (p. 208).

*Intuition note:* A party label is a shortcut that is useful only if voters see it as conveying knowledge and trust. Electoral rules that give parties reasons to keep a consistent, reliable label can make the shortcut more useful.

**Verification.** Competition among candidates, news organisations, interest groups and citizens can generate conditions for verification. Electoral institutions promote it through equal TV time, advertising limits, low barriers to the ballot (nominal fees or minimal signatures), and sometimes campaign-expense reimbursement (p. 208). A free press raises the likelihood that statements are verified and gives verifiers public avenues, enabling a competition of ideas. By facilitating competition, these features can increase the threat of verification and so the likelihood that voters are capable of reasoned choice (pp. 208–209).

**Costly effort.** Disclosure laws require committees and lobbyists to identify significant contributors and amounts (p. 209).
- Example: a voter reasonably happy with the status quo sees a ballot-initiative sponsor spend several million dollars. The voter can infer, with no further information, that the initiative proposes a very large change and should vote against it (p. 209).
- Example: in a primary, where party cannot be a cue, a very expensive challenger campaign against an incumbent lets voters infer that many knowledgeable contributors saw substantial differences (p. 209).
- Principle: a voter who sees a knowledgeable person give up something valuable to obtain an outcome can draw an inference about how to vote (p. 209).
- Caveats: voters must be sure costs are not subsidised or limited. If the state pays all campaign costs it may be misleading to distinguish candidates by cost, and under strict limits cost may not reflect the size of differences. US presidential elections (public subsidy, limited spending) make costly effort less useful (p. 210). Footnotes: pseudonyms can hide contributors (n. 8); incumbents' free resources make their effort hard to gauge (n. 9) (p. 209).

*Intuition note:* Money spent is a signal. If someone who knows the issue pays heavily to win, the voter can read the size of the stake from the size of the spending. The signal can mislead, or be less useful, when the state pays the costs or spending is limited.

### Legislative institutions (pp. 210–215)
Legislators have many information sources (colleagues, executive officials, staff, subpoenaed experts). Testimony faces perjury penalties, but the basic problem is sorting trustworthy from untrustworthy advisors (p. 210).

**Common interests.** Legislators invest heavily in identifying whom to trust: choosing cabinet ministers, leaders and committee members. They screen those who control the agenda and align committees and leaders with the majority party's interests (p. 210). On unfamiliar issues they follow like-minded colleagues or whips (pp. 210–211).
- In some instances committee interests differ from the majority party's (US House Agriculture Committee). Absent external forces, the authors would not expect such committee endorsements to be persuasive (as Krehbiel argued). Differences between party leaders and backbenchers may likewise render leadership stands unpersuasive (Japan's LDP). Where common interests are absent, persuasion and trust require external forces (p. 211).

**Penalties for lying.** Trust-breaking penalties underlie many US Congress norms and are often enforced by party leaders, not the chamber. In parliamentary democracies, where parties control members' electoral prospects more, penalties can include loss of office and expulsion. Sanction threats are also thought to hold lobbyists in line (p. 211). If the penalty or its enforcement likelihood is small, it dissuades few liars and may not generate trust. Scientists' opinions, expert testimony and floor remarks during recess are not greatly affected. This may be one reason legislators frequently ignore them (p. 211).

**Verification.** The constitutional structure determines how many competing information sources legislators have. Open legislatures make verification much more likely than closed ones (p. 212).
- Fig. 10.1 (US House process): the process sets up dual agenda control shared by committees and party leaders, with many points where backbenchers can check leaders. Ambition is pitted against ambition (p. 212).
- Bills pass through introduction, committee, subcommittee, Rules Committee, floor, then budget/reconciliation and appropriations. Substantive committees (e.g. Agriculture) are checked by the Appropriations, Rules and Budget "control" committees. Members with expertise can thus verify others' claims (pp. 212, 214).
- This generates the context for verification to the extent that actors' ambitions are adversarial, or other features make some actors trustworthy (p. 214).
- Japan: for most of the postwar period the LDP's Policy Affairs Research Council (PARC) reviewed proposals before they reached the Diet, giving backbenchers information to counter cabinet reports. "Coordinating Councils" put interest-group representatives at the table with bureaucrats and scholars (p. 214).
- The British Parliament lacks intraparty equivalents of PARC, and its committees are impermanent and subject to leadership whims. The authors conclude that in numbers of potential verifiers, US legislators seem best off among their three examples (US, Japan, UK) and British backbenchers worst off (p. 214).

**Costly effort.** Drafting, hearings, reports, deals and whipping all use scarce resources (p. 215). In many countries, funding a new program takes more work than extra funding for an existing one. In the US, large changes in appropriations require a written explanation or new legislation. Legislators can thus infer that proposals showing such effort represent a large policy change. This inference alone "may be sufficient" for reasoned choice (p. 215).

### Bureaucratic institutions (pp. 215–222)
Delegation to bureaucrats may bring expertise, but an agent with expertise and authority can leave legislators worse off than no delegation. Then delegation equals **abdication**. The legislator's dilemma is to gain delegation's benefits without abdicating control (p. 215).

**Clarifying interests.** Legislators appoint agents. All else constant, they have an incentive to appoint agents whose interests are common to their own; the authors say this leads to satisfaction of the Incentive Condition (defined in Chapter 5) (p. 216). Screening is difficult, so legislators also use structure and process (p. 216).

An agency's incentives are determined by three factors: the interests of its personnel, its decision rules (whose vote counts, how votes aggregate), and its political environment. Structure and process can affect the last two (p. 216).
- *NEPA (1969):* environmental groups had become better organised. NEPA required environmental impact statements, gave these groups early participation and a greater ability to sue. Many federal projects were stopped, delayed or altered (p. 216).
- *Burden of proof:* it matters most under great uncertainty, where proving anything can be hard or impossible. In that case, a rigorous burden on one side essentially guarantees that side cannot get its preferred outcome (p. 217).
  - Drugs: the 1938 Food, Drug and Cosmetics Act requires firms to prove a new drug safe and efficacious (p. 217).
  - Chemicals: under the 1976 TSCA, the EPA must prove a new chemical hazardous before regulating. New chemicals can then be marketed. The authors state that few new drugs are marketed in the US, while the EPA has regulated none of 50,000 chemicals under TSCA (p. 217).
  - Airlines: the 1938 Civil Aeronautics Act put the burden on entrants to show they would not harm existing carriers. The Kennedy amendments at the end of the 1970s shifted it to existing carriers to show that competition would harm social welfare (p. 217).
- Purpose: this "deck stacking" is not to preselect policy but to cope with uncertainty. It ensures that winners of the legislative battle also win at implementation, so the agency can run on "autopilot" without close supervision (p. 217).
- Agencies copy this logic internally. A separately elected executive also tries to mirror his or her political forces in rules for the bureaucracy (p. 218).
- The enabling statute can fix the reversionary policy (entitlement spending, "sunset" provisions) (p. 218).
- Footnote 17: courts can enforce administrative procedures. This works only if judicial remedy is highly likely when an agency breaks its rules (p. 218).
- Environment: assign agenda control to several agencies. They then compete for budgets and authority, which increases their incentive to please political leaders. Examples: Japan's electricity-price working group of several ministries; US NIOSH must identify a hazard before OSHA can issue a rule (pp. 218–219).
- Ex post sanctions: Weingast's "big club behind the door". Ex post sanctions "create ex ante incentives for bureaucrats to serve congressmen", the law of anticipated reactions (p. 219).

*Intuition note:* Because screening appointees is difficult, legislators can also set the rules of the game: who must prove what, who may participate, who competes. A rule that gives the agency interests in common with the legislators lets legislators come to trust its statements.

**Verification.** McCubbins and Schwartz distinguish two oversight modes (p. 219):
- *Police patrol*: centralised, active, direct. The legislature examines a sample of agency activities and discourages violations by surveillance.
- *Fire alarm*: less centralised. The legislature sets up rules and practices that let citizens and groups examine decisions, charge agencies with violations and seek remedies from agencies, courts or the legislature. Fire alarms in essence verify bureaucratic statements and actions.

Fire alarms save leaders search effort and surface problems constituents care about. They let leaders advertise, claim credit and take issue-specific positions. The authors say leaders are therefore likely to prefer this low-risk, high-reward strategy (pp. 219–220). Footnote 18: surveys show fire alarm is the modal type of congressional oversight, but police patrol is more comprehensive and effective than commonly believed (p. 220).

Problem: on any issue there are many alarms, and legislators must sort credible from noncredible ones (p. 220). Credible alarms need rules governing information about agency activity. The 1946 Administrative Procedure Act (APA) requires (p. 220):
1. public notice without bias toward any action;
2. solicitation of comments from all interested parties;
3. participation in decision making (extent often mandated by the organic statute and the courts); if hearings are held, parties may be allowed to bring testimony and evidence and often to cross-examine other witnesses;
4. explicit treatment of the evidence, with a rationalizable link to the decision;
5. availability of a record of each member's final vote.

These facilitate credible alarms in at least three ways (pp. 220–221). Agencies cannot secretly conspire. Agencies must solicit political information, learning which interests are at stake and the costs and benefits of actions. And proceedings are public, with rules against *ex parte* contact, so many groups can give and acquire knowledge. Participation need not be universal. The authors say diffuse non-participating groups are much less likely to become an electoral force than participating ones (p. 221).

Adversarial competition also builds credibility (Lupia and McCubbins 1994b). Legislators' time is finite and thousands of groups lobby. The wise group guards its access by giving accurate, succinct information, since broken trust may not be regained (Bauer, Pool and Dexter). Groups may compete to be verifiers. For verification to work, only some groups, the verifiers, must satisfy the conditions for persuasion and enlightenment (p. 221).

Verifier agencies: the OMB (1921 Budget and Accounting Act) helps the president compile a budget. The GAO is a special agent of Congress, acting as auditor and accountant (year-end books), comptroller (flow of funds against authorisations) and special investigator. Together, structure and process ensure that knowledgeable, trustworthy groups verify bureaucratic statements and actions (pp. 221–222).

**Costly effort.** All agency actions use scarce resources, so legislators may be able to infer much from observed costs: choosing an action signals that bureaucrats believe its benefits outweigh its costs (p. 222). Actions are either general rule making or application to specific cases. The APA imposes costs on rule making: notice, comments, public criticism of the draft, and evidence that criticism was not arbitrarily rejected. Each stage consumes time and resources (p. 222).
- TSCA: to regulate a chemical the EPA must design a test rule and write an implementation regulation. Producers need not prove non-toxicity and face very low costs (pp. 222–223).
- Drugs: the firm bears the cost of proving safety, so introduction is costly. These costs enable legislators and regulators to evaluate the potential change in the status quo (p. 223).
- Evidence law distinguishes the burden of producing evidence (who bears costs to proceed) from the burden of persuasion (the test to carry an issue). Example: OIRA, created under the 1980 Paperwork Reduction Act. Reagan read the Act as a mandate for cost–benefit analysis of major regulations (Exec. Order 12291) (p. 223).

### Legal institutions (pp. 223–225)
Trial procedure governs how evidence is introduced and challenged. The best-known rule is sanctions for perjury, equivalent to penalties for lying. The authors say that showing such procedures exist is sufficient to show that judicial institutions can help relatively uninformed jurors make reasoned choices (p. 223).

**Knowledge.** The California code allows testimony only from those with first-hand knowledge or special knowledge or experience. Scientific evidence must use methods generally accepted by the relevant community. An opposing attorney can question a witness's recollection or perceptive ability. The rules help assure that speakers are knowledgeable on their topic (p. 224).

**Common interests.** Jurors know what each side wants. Some witness interests are transparent: police are presumed to enforce the law, experts are often thought impartial (though paid experts are suspect), and friends, family, ex-spouses and victims have obvious axes to grind. Attorneys point out hidden motives. Rules of evidence assure dismissal of a witness shown to have poor character, a reputation for lying, or biases (p. 224).

**Verification.** Almost every statement is subject to challenge. A witness caught in false statements by cross-examination faces perjury penalties (p. 225). Hearsay, which cannot be verified, is inadmissible. Depositions are admissible because cross-examination is possible (p. 225, n. 24). Jurors often believe easily verified, unchallenged evidence. They tend to discount evidence that is challenged, especially if refuted, and are less likely to believe evidence that is hard to verify (p. 225).

### Unenlightening democratic institutions (pp. 225–227)
**Nonpartisan elections** seem to the authors "a classic example" of reform that hinders reasoned choice. They remove party brand-name incentives and cues, weaken collective responsibility, and leave no central partisan authority to hold legislators accountable (p. 225).

**Term limits.** Proponents' argument (Petracca, Will) has three premises: term limits remove long-term incumbents; this empowers challengers and raises competition; competition yields more responsive policy. Conclusion: term limits yield more responsive policy (p. 226). Even granting the first two premises, Gerber and Lupia (1996) find that term limits need not produce responsive legislation or competition. Adding new candidates facilitates neither persuasion nor enlightenment. The authors say Gerber and Lupia thereby show that term limits are neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for successful electoral delegation. For example, a challenger who is not persuasive enough to influence voters cannot change other candidates' incentives, and so term limits may have no effect on whether any candidate satisfies the incentive condition (p. 226).

Further: term limits remove informative stimuli. Incumbents have public histories, and people who face reelection and believe their principals may hold them accountable have an incentive to improve the principals' welfare. Those barred from reelection have no such incentive. The authors say it is "at a minimum" hard to reconcile term limits with outcomes based on enlightened voters' decisions (p. 226).

**Deliberation** (Fishkin, Habermas): the proposal is that deliberating citizens enlighten one another. Were persuasion and enlightenment the same, deliberation would be an ideal solution, but they are not the same (p. 227). Deliberation differs from enlightenment when the most persuasive people are not knowledgeable, or have an incentive to mislead. Deliberation can be beneficial, but is far more likely to be so in a context that generates the conditions for enlightenment (p. 227).

*Intuition note:* More candidates, fewer incumbents or more talk do not by themselves produce knowledge. What matters is whether listeners can tell who is knowledgeable and trustworthy.

### Conclusion (p. 227)
Voters, legislators and jurors delegate and tend to lack information about consequences. Whether they can choose well depends on whether their opportunities to gain knowledge produce knowledge. People often cannot discern others' interests and expertise because they do not know one another well. Institutions that clarify interests, penalise lying, threaten verification or require costly effort enable more accurate predictions of consequences (p. 227).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the "penalty plus threat of verification" idea. It is a simple expected-value sketch, not the book's formal model.

A lobbyist can tell the truth and gain 4. A lie, if never caught, gains 10. If a verifier catches it (probability $v$), the lobbyist loses 12.
- Expected payoff of lying: $(1-v)\cdot 10 + v\cdot(-12) = 10 - 22v$.
- Lying is less attractive than truth-telling when $10 - 22v < 4$, i.e. $v > 6/22 \approx 0.27$.
- Check, $v = 0.2$: $0.8\cdot 10 + 0.2\cdot(-12) = 8 - 2.4 = 5.6 > 4$, so the lobbyist lies.
- Check, $v = 0.5$: $0.5\cdot 10 + 0.5\cdot(-12) = 5 - 6 = -1 < 4$, so the lobbyist tells the truth.

Lesson: competing verifiers (higher $v$) or a heavier penalty make truth-telling the better choice for the speaker. The chapter adds that principals must also *perceive* this, or the speaker's statements will not persuade them.

## Concepts
- [[conditions-for-persuasion-lupia-mccubbins]] — on these pages, a cue or speaker is effective when voters perceive the speaker as knowledgeable and trustworthy; institutions can supply or support these conditions (pp. 205–206, 208)
- [[principal-agent-problem-delegation]] — voters, legislators and jurors delegate to agents who may not share their interests. Bad delegation becomes "abdication" (pp. 205, 215)
- [[institutions]] — constitutions, statutes, rules, norms and customs that define delegations and shape principals' and agents' incentives (p. 205)
- [[heuristics-cues]] — party and other cues are useful only if they signal knowledge and trust, and only relative to other available cues (pp. 206, 208)
- [[verification]] (new) — the threat that a statement will be checked, which competition, a free press, cross-examination and fire alarms can create (pp. 208–209, 219, 225)
- [[costly-effort-signal]] (new) — observable resources spent by a speaker can let principals infer the size of the stake or change (pp. 209, 215, 222)
- [[penalties-for-lying]] (new) — sanctions (perjury, expulsion, loss of office) that give speakers an incentive to be truthful, weak if either the penalty or its likelihood of enforcement is small (pp. 211, 223)
- [[fire-alarm-oversight]] (new) — decentralised oversight in which citizens and groups bring agency violations to the legislature, contrasted with police-patrol oversight (p. 219)
- [[burden-of-proof-deck-stacking]] (new) — procedures set up so that the legislative winners also win at implementation, especially under uncertainty (pp. 216–217)
- [[abdication]] (new) — delegation in which the will of the people neither constrains nor motivates policy (p. 215)
- [[law-of-anticipated-reactions]] (new) — ex post sanctions create ex ante incentives for agents to serve principals (p. 219)
- [[enlightenment-vs-persuasion]] (new) — persuasion and enlightenment are not the same, so deliberation is far more likely to be beneficial where conditions for enlightenment hold (p. 227)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Costly effort as an inference signal ↔ economics of signaling: observable expenditure reveals a sender's private information — both read cost as evidence of type or stake.
- [structural-analogy] Fire-alarm vs police-patrol oversight ↔ monitoring design in organisations: sampling by the principal versus complaints from affected third parties.
- [structural-analogy] Burden of proof as a default rule ↔ status-quo/reversion point in agenda models: whoever must overcome the default faces higher costs.
- [metaphor] Verification threat ↔ peer review in science: the possibility of being checked disciplines claims.

## Questions this raises
- How can a principal tell whether an institution really makes a speaker trustworthy, given that the effect must also be perceived and believed?
- Do the authors' cross-country comparisons (US, Japan, UK) rest on enough evidence to rank the number of potential verifiers?
- If fire alarms are many and uneven, which groups end up as the "verifiers", and could they be captured by the legislators' own coalition?
- When might "costly effort" mislead, for example when wealthy actors can afford costs that others cannot?
