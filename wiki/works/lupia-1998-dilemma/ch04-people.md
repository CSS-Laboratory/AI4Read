# What People Learn from Others

## L0 — One line
When a persuasive speaker alone holds the knowledge a listener needs, his testimony enlightens if common interests or external forces really induce him to reveal it, and it deceives if no such inducement exists.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what people learn when they rely on others for political knowledge. Learning can end in enlightenment, deception, or nothing. Two theorems give necessary and sufficient conditions for enlightenment (a better ability to predict the results of one's actions) and deception (a worse one). Both need a persuasive speaker who alone holds the needed knowledge. They differ in one thing: whether common interests or external forces actually induce the speaker to reveal it. Institutions such as courtrooms can supply such forces. The authors end with a nine-question checklist for judging whether a person can make a reasoned choice. They argue that lacking such trivia is not enough to call citizens incapable.

**Prerequisites:** necessary vs sufficient conditions; Chapters 2–3 ideas (knowledge = ability to predict accurately; persuasion needs perceived knowledge and a basis for trust).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Learning from another person has three mutually exclusive outcomes: enlightenment, deception, or learning nothing (pp. 68–69).
- Theorem 4.1: enlightenment occurs if and only if the speaker is persuasive, only he holds the knowledge, and common interests or external forces make him reveal it (p. 69).
- Theorem 4.2: deception occurs if and only if the first two conditions hold and no such inducement exists. So the real, not just perceived, basis for trust separates enlightenment from deception (pp. 70–72).
- The claim that decision makers are regularly or easily deceived has limits. Not everyone is persuasive, and a persuasive speaker can deceive only a principal (the listener) who cannot see it coming (p. 74).
- Aristotelian theories of communication (the authors' label) assume people know each other well. The authors say this blurs persuasion and enlightenment, and makes more information look always better (p. 74).
- Institutions can impose verification threats, costly effort or penalties for lying, making enlightenment possible where it otherwise would not be. The courtroom is the example (pp. 75–76).
- A nine-question checklist assesses whether a person can make a reasoned choice (p. 77).
- Not knowing facts like senators' names is insufficient grounds for calling people incapable, unless those facts are necessary for reasoned choice (p. 78).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 68–69)
- Politics is unfamiliar to most people, so they often learn from others. Chapter 3 showed that persuasion needs a perception of knowledge and a basis for trust. That basis can come from external forces or from perceived common interests (p. 68).
- Three categories, "mutually exclusive, and collectively exhaustive" (p. 68):
  - **Enlightenment:** after observing or hearing someone, our ability to predict the consequences of our actions improves. If we lack knowledge sufficient for reasoned choice and can get it only from others, we can choose reasonably only if they enlighten us (p. 68).
  - **Deception:** our ability to predict accurately falls. "We are deceived when someone lies to us and we believe that person" (p. 69).
  - **Learning nothing:** our predictive ability is unchanged (p. 69).
- Footnote 1: enlightenment need not mean learning the truth, and deception need not mean being lied to. Knowledge was defined as the ability to predict accurately, so enlightenment requires only improved knowledge (p. 68).
- The debate: do political communications (e.g. campaigns) enlighten or deceive? If they enlighten, citizens can choose reasonably. If they regularly fail (deceive or are uninformative), citizens are less capable (p. 69).
- The chapter derives necessary and sufficient conditions for enlightenment and deception (p. 69).

### The conditions for enlightenment (pp. 69–70)
**Theorem 4.1.** Communication leads to enlightenment if and only if (p. 69):
1. the speaker is persuasive;
2. only the speaker initially possesses the knowledge the speaker needs [the text says "the speaker needs"; later text says the principal needs];
3. common interests or external forces induce the speaker to reveal what he knows.

Footnote 2: the analysis covers purposeful enlightenment, where the speaker intends to enlighten (p. 69).
- Condition 1 requires the principal to perceive the speaker as knowledgeable and trustworthy. Conditions 1 and 2 together make both actual and perceived knowledge necessary. Conditions 1 and 3 together make both an actual incentive to reveal and the principal's perception of it necessary (pp. 69–70). Figure 4.1 relates the conditions for persuasion to those for enlightenment (p. 70; the figure is on p. 71, and only its caption is in the supplied text).
- **Aristotelian explanations** (as the authors describe them): they focus solely on speaker–principal incentive compatibility and assume the principal can tell knowledgeable, trustworthy speakers from others (p. 70). The authors say Theorem 4.1 contradicts these.
- In many contexts people do not know who is knowledgeable and trustworthy. So incentive-altering contracts or institutions "need not be sufficient." Even with compatible incentives, enlightenment will not occur unless the principal's beliefs about them are accurate (p. 70).
- Hence institutional solutions must introduce external forces that act on both the speaker's incentives and the principal's beliefs (p. 70).

*Intuition note:* A good adviser is not enough. The adviser must know the thing, have a reason to tell it, and be believed because the principal's perception of his incentive is accurate. If any link is missing, you are not enlightened.

### The conditions for deception (pp. 70–74)
- The authors focus on willful deception: the speaker lies with intent to harm the principal. They call it simply "deception" from here (p. 70).

**Theorem 4.2.** Communication leads to deception if and only if (p. 70):
1. the speaker is persuasive;
2. only the speaker initially possesses the knowledge the principal needs;
3. neither common interests nor external forces induce the speaker to reveal what he knows.

- Conditions 1–2 are identical to those for enlightenment. The two sets differ in one way: whether such inducements act on the speaker (pp. 70, 72).
- So the factor deciding whether persuasive testimony enlightens or deceives is whether the perceived basis for trust actually exists. If it does, persuasive testimony enlightens. Otherwise it deceives (p. 72).
- Theorem 4.2 implies that the speaker can deceive only if he is correct that the deception will not be anticipated or discovered. A principal who anticipates or discovers it has reason to disregard him. So willful deception requires a principal who "does not have and cannot obtain knowledge about the speaker's incentives" (p. 72; Figure 4.2 is on p. 73, and only its caption is in the supplied text).
- Both accidental deception (a speaker lacks the knowledge we thought he had) and willful deception (a speaker lacks the common interests we thought he had) require a misperception on our part (p. 72).
- **Example, Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (7 Aug 1964):** the resolution, which formed the basis for U.S. military expansion in Vietnam, allowed the president to take all necessary measures to repel armed attacks on U.S. forces. Democratic majorities relied on President Johnson's information, but he lacked knowledge about the incident. Many members later felt misled. Senator Fulbright said the affair had been marked by a lack of candor. Congress's misperception about the administration's knowledge enabled the president to obtain powers "out of proportion with the incident" (p. 72). Footnote 3: Congress passed the War Powers Act in 1973 (p. 72).
- **Two limits on the claim that decision makers are easily deceived (p. 74):**
  - Not everyone is persuasive: people follow some hosts, programs, endorsers and friends but not others.
  - Not everyone persuasive can deceive. Where lying risks verification, penalties, or costly observable effort, likely liars can be branded as such.
- If people know enough about one another, deception is impossible. A theory that assumes people know each other well therefore cannot state the conditions under which deception occurs. A theory that does not assume this can (p. 74).
- Aristotelian theories, the authors say, reinforce two mistaken impressions (p. 74):
  - That persuasion and enlightenment are equivalent. Persuasion is necessary for enlightenment, and enlightenment is sufficient for persuasion. The converses are false.
  - Because deception is impossible in these theories, that more information is necessarily better, which the authors say earlier chapters refuted.

*Intuition note:* Enlightening and deceiving speakers can sound equally convincing. What differs is whether the speaker really has a reason to reveal what he knows. Deception works only when the listener cannot check this.

### Discussion: how we choose whom to believe (pp. 74–76)
- People use simple strategies: they ignore speakers they think lack knowledge and judge others' character and incentives from their background and circumstances. So they rely more on family, friends and colleagues than on strangers. Car shoppers prefer *Consumer Reports* or *Road and Track* to *Mad* (pp. 74–75).
- Political elites also act in line with the conditions. Candidates know rivals and commentators wait to expose false statements, and for many of them verification of false statements may bring career-ending costs. Legislators draw on competing sources: some perceived to share interests, others paying substantial costs or risking reputation. Competition-induced verification, observable costly effort and penalties for lying substitute for detailed policy information (p. 75). Footnote 4 cites Matthews and Stimson (1970, 1975) and Kingdon (1977) on learning in Congress (p. 75).
- **Two arguments (p. 75).** (a) Reasoned choice requires knowledge, knowledge usually requires others' testimony, and that requires persuasion, so reasoned choice usually requires persuasion. (b) People can design institutions; institutions can contain incentive-altering external forces; these affect what speakers say and help listeners judge credibility; this can favor the conditions for enlightenment over those for deception. So institutions can make enlightenment possible where it otherwise would not occur.
- Combined: people can design institutions to facilitate reasoned choice. This is possible when they can impose verification threats, costly effort requirements or statement-specific costs such as penalties for lying, which give speakers incentives to reveal and principals the ability to perceive those incentives accurately (p. 75). When reasoned choice requires enlightenment and such structures cannot be created, people may be hard pressed to choose reasonably (pp. 75–76).
- **Courtroom example (p. 76).** The authors call courtrooms perhaps the clearest example of institutional design generating the conditions for persuasion and enlightenment where neither would otherwise exist. Jurors' personal experience is usually insufficient for reasoned choice. Witnesses and attorneys are strangers they likely never meet again. They know prosecution and defense interests conflict, so they are likely to be uncertain who shares their interests. But all testimony is subject to immediate verification (cross-examination, evidence) and penalties for lying (perjury). Footnote 5: the judge may share jurors' interests, but it is unclear he or she knows the evidence, and judges are often more concerned with procedure than justice (p. 76).

*Intuition note:* Institutions can supply a basis for trust where personal acquaintance cannot. In court, jurors can rely on the fact that testimony is subject to verification and penalties for lying.

### Conclusion (pp. 76–78)
- Part I recap: limited information prevents reasoned choice under identified conditions. Chapter 2: reasoned choice rarely requires detailed information, and people have incentives to be very selective about what to attend to. Chapter 3: politics often forces reliance on others' testimony. Chapters 3–4: how people sort testimony. Institutions matter also because they affect who can learn from whom (p. 76).
- The goal is more reliable judgments of whether a given limited-information principal can choose reasonably. The authors propose these questions (pp. 76–77):
  1. What choice must the person make?
  2. What information suffices for a reasoned choice? (Q1–2 discourage judging people by ad hoc political-trivia questions, since people rarely need encyclopedic recall, p. 77.)
  3. Does the person already have it? If so, stop: reasoned choice is possible without learning from others.
  4. Is there a cost-effective way to get it without relying on others? If so, stop: possible, no learning from others needed.
  5. Can someone provide it? If not, stop: reasoned choice is impossible.
  6. Does the person ignore all who could provide it? If so, stop: impossible.
  7. Does the person perceive the speaker as knowledgeable? If not, stop: impossible.
  8. Does the person perceive the speaker as having common interests or an incentive to reveal what he knows? If not, stop: impossible.
  9. Do the conditions for enlightenment exist? If so, reasoned choice is possible; if not, impossible.
- The authors say their theory challenges critiques of democracy that assume ordinary people cannot choose reasonably. They argue democracy need not be threatened by the fact that relatively few Americans have memorized their senators' names or can recall the seventh amendment's content without looking. Unless such knowledge is a necessary condition for reasoned choice, lacking it is insufficient grounds for calling people incapable. Hence, they say, it is wrong to assume that citizens unable to recite civics-text passages are less competent to vote (pp. 77–78).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A tenant, Rosa, must decide whether to back a bill that may raise her rent. She predicts the effect correctly about 50% of the time (invented number). Three speakers each know the bill's effect. Ann and Bob persuade her; Cy does not.
- **Ann**, a city analyst, is cross-examined at a public hearing and punished for false statements. She faces a real inducement to reveal, so conditions 1–3 of Theorem 4.1 hold. Rosa's accuracy rises to 90%: enlightenment.
- **Bob**, a landlord lobbyist, has opposite interests and no penalty, but Rosa wrongly believes he shares her interests. Conditions 1–2 hold, 3 fails, so Theorem 4.2 applies. He says "no rent rise." Her accuracy falls to 20%: deception.
- **Cy** is a stranger Rosa distrusts. She ignores him: condition 1 fails, her accuracy stays 50%. She learns nothing.
Ann and Bob differ only in condition 3.

## Concepts
- [[conditions-for-persuasion]] — persuasion requires that the listener perceive the speaker as knowledgeable and trustworthy; the basis for trust can come from external forces or perceived common interests (pp. 68–69)
- [[enlightenment]] (new) — a speaker's words or actions raise the listener's ability to predict accurately; no truth is required (p. 68, fn. 1)
- [[deception]] (new) — the listener's predictive ability falls; here the focus is willful deception (pp. 69–70)
- [[external-forces]] (new) — verification threats, penalties for lying, costly observable effort, competition; they can give the speaker an incentive to reveal and help the listener judge credibility (pp. 70, 74–75)
- [[reasoned-choice]] (new) — a choice made with knowledge sufficient for reasoned choice (p. 68); knowledge here means the ability to predict accurately (fn. 1, p. 68). The authors say reasoned choice rarely requires detailed information and does not often need encyclopedic recall (pp. 76–77)
- [[institutions]] — here, designed structures that can carry external forces and so can affect who can learn from whom (pp. 75–76)
- [[aristotelian-theories-of-communication]] (new) — as the authors describe them: theories that assume people know each other well and make deception impossible (p. 74)

## Canonical instances
- [[gulf-of-tonkin-resolution]] — Congress misperceived the president's knowledge and was deceived (p. 72)
- [[courtroom-jurors]] — institutional design (cross-examination, perjury) creates conditions for enlightenment among strangers (p. 76)
- [[legislators-information-sources]] — competing advisers, reputation costs and costly effort give effective substitutes for the detailed policy information legislators often lack (p. 75)
- [[senators-names-seventh-amendment]] — lacking such trivia is insufficient grounds for calling people incapable of reasoned choice, unless that knowledge is necessary for it (p. 78)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Theorems 4.1/4.2 ↔ principal–agent problem/delegation: a principal cannot observe an agent's incentives, and external forces align them.
- [structural-analogy] Designing verification and penalties ↔ institutions: rules that change incentives and information, not just preferences.
- [metaphor] Choosing advisers by perceived knowledge and trust ↔ heuristics/cues: low-information shortcuts that stand in for detailed knowledge.

## Questions this raises

- Does the theory treat knowledge as a binary "only the speaker has it"? What changes if the speaker's knowledge is partial or shared?
- Which real institutions meet the conditions the authors list, and which merely look like they do?
- How could the nine-question checklist be tested empirically?
