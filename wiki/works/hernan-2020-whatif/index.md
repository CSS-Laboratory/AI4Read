# Hernán, M. A., & Robins, J. M. (2020). *Causal Inference: What If*. Boca Raton: Chapman & Hall/CRC.

## In one paragraph
The book teaches how to ask causal questions precisely and when observational data can answer them. A causal effect is defined as a contrast between counterfactual outcomes under different treatments. It can be identified from data when exchangeability, positivity and consistency hold. Causal diagrams show the structural sources of bias: confounding, selection and measurement error. Methods such as standardization, IP weighting, g-estimation and instrumental variables then estimate the effect, each with its own modelling assumptions. For treatments that change over time, treatment-confounder feedback defeats ordinary adjustment, so the g-methods are needed. The unifying advice is to state the protocol of a hypothetical "target trial" and analyze observational data to emulate it. This makes assumptions explicit and open to criticism.

## The path through the work
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch01-definition]] — A causal effect is a contrast between outcomes in the same population under different treatments (counterfactual worlds), and this is a different quantity from an association between treatment and outcome among those who actually received each treatment.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch02-randomized]] — In an ideal randomized experiment the treated and untreated are expected to be exchangeable, so association is causation; when randomization depends on a covariate $L$, standardization or inverse probability weighting recovers the same population-wide causal effect.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch03-observational]] — An observational study can be treated as a conditionally randomized experiment, so that causal effects can be identified, if three conditions hold (exchangeability, positivity and consistency), conditions the authors call "often heroic" and of which exchangeability can never be tested.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch04-effect]] — There is no single "the causal effect" of a treatment: the average effect depends on the population's mix of effect modifiers, and whether modification exists depends on the effect measure, so you must say which population and which measure you mean.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch05-interaction]] — Two treatments interact when the effect of one differs depending on the level the other is set to by a joint intervention; this is a counterfactual contrast, and it is a different concept from sufficient-cause "mechanistic" interaction.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch06-graphical]] — A causal diagram (DAG) records our assumptions about who causes whom, and simple path rules then tell us which variables we should expect to be associated, marginally or after conditioning.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch07-confounding]] — Confounding is the bias from common causes of treatment and outcome, shown as open backdoor paths in a causal diagram; if the causal diagram is known, whether adjusting for a set of variables L removes it can be decided by the backdoor criterion (or by a SWIG), rather than by the traditional, mostly association-based rules.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch08-selection]] — Selection bias is a treatment–outcome association that can appear when the analysis is restricted by conditioning on a common effect of two variables (one tied to treatment, one to outcome), and it can arise even in a randomized trial if the selection happens after randomization.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch09-measurement]] — Even without confounding or selection bias, measuring variables with error can bias effect estimates, and a DAG arrow only has causal meaning if a well-defined intervention exists for it.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch10-random]] — Even with no systematic bias, finite samples make causal estimates vary by chance. Confidence intervals quantify this only in a frequentist, super-population sense, and the tempting rule "condition on observed covariate imbalance" breaks down when there are many covariates.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch11-model]] — When data are too thin to "speak for themselves", a model adds an a priori restriction that lets us borrow information, and the price is possible bias if the restriction is wrong (versus larger variance if we restrict less).
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch12-ip]] — With a model for the probability of treatment given confounders, IP (inverse-probability) weighting builds a pseudo-population without confounding, and a marginal structural model (a model for the mean of the counterfactual outcome) can then be fitted by weighted regression.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch13-standardization]] — Standardization with a fitted outcome model (the parametric g-formula) estimates an average causal effect under the same identifiability conditions as IP weighting, but with different modeling assumptions, so comparing the two methods is a useful check.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch14-g]] — G-estimation finds the causal-effect parameter as the trial value that turns the observed outcome into a "candidate untreated outcome" that, under conditional exchangeability, no longer predicts treatment given the covariates.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch15-outcome]] — Outcome regression and propensity-score methods can estimate causal effects under exchangeability and positivity given covariates $L$, but each depends on a correctly specified model, and variable selection built for prediction can harm causal estimates.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch16-instrumental]] — A variable that affects the outcome only through the treatment can replace the need to measure all confounders, but only under extra untestable assumptions (a fourth condition such as homogeneity or monotonicity), and the method is fragile when the instrument is weak.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch17-causal]] — For a time-fixed treatment, causal survival curves can be built from estimated discrete-time hazards, chained into survival by a product and adjusted for confounding with IP weighting or the parametric g-formula; structural AFT models are fitted here by g-estimation modified with "artificial censoring".
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch18-variable]] — Choosing adjustment variables for a causal effect needs subject-matter knowledge, not just prediction algorithms; once a sound set is chosen, doubly robust estimators with sample splitting and cross-fitting let machine learning handle high-dimensional adjustment, and under stated conditions the confidence intervals stay valid.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch19-time]] — With treatments that change over time, a causal effect is only defined as a contrast between named treatment strategies, and estimating it validly requires some form of sequential exchangeability (plus sequential positivity and consistency).
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch20-treatment]] — When a time-varying confounder is itself affected by earlier treatment, ordinary adjustment (stratification, regression, matching) can create bias even though all the needed data are measured.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch21-g]] — With treatment-confounder feedback, the g-formula, IP weighting and g-estimation (and their doubly robust versions) can give correct effects for sustained treatment strategies under sequential exchangeability, positivity and consistency, where traditional adjustment fails.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch22-target]] — Causal questions about sustained treatment strategies are clarified by stating the protocol of a (hypothetical) target randomized trial; for per-protocol effects, the analysis, which generally needs g-methods, should then be the same whether the data come from a trial or from observational records.
- [[hernan-2020-whatif/ch23-causal]] — The standard "pure direct / total indirect" mediation effects are cross-world quantities that no experiment can verify, so the authors prefer to reframe mediation as the effect of interventions on separable components of treatment, whose assumptions can in principle be tested in a randomized trial.

## Big ideas across units
- **Causal effects are counterfactual contrasts, not associations.** Individual effects are never observed, and population contrasts differ from conditional associations. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch04.
- **Identifiability conditions: exchangeability, positivity, consistency.** Randomization guarantees them by design. In observational data they are assumptions, and exchangeability is untestable. They are extended to sequential versions for time-varying treatments and to censoring. Units: ch02, ch03, ch05, ch07, ch08, ch12, ch19, ch21.
- **Causal diagrams show where bias comes from.** Common causes give confounding, conditioning on common effects gives selection bias, and mismeasured variables give measurement bias. Naive adjustment (colliders, descendants of treatment, mediators, instruments) can make things worse. Units: ch06, ch07, ch08, ch09, ch18, ch20.
- **Well-defined interventions give causal meaning.** Consistency needs them, arrows for non-intervention variables like obesity have no clear causal meaning, and mediation is reframed via separable components. Units: ch03, ch06, ch09, ch23.
- **One estimand, several estimators with different assumptions.** Standardization, IP weighting, g-estimation, outcome regression, propensity scores and instrumental variables each rely on different correct models or assumptions. Agreement or disagreement between them is informative, and doubly robust estimators combine two models. Units: ch02, ch12, ch13, ch14, ch15, ch16, ch18, ch21.
- **Random error and modelling choices are separate from bias.** Identification is distinguished from estimation. Models borrow information at the risk of misspecification bias, and many covariates cause trouble for conditioning and for confidence intervals. Units: ch10, ch11, ch12, ch13, ch18.
- **Time-varying treatments need g-methods.** Effects are contrasts between treatment strategies. Treatment-confounder feedback makes stratification-based methods fail, and the g-formula, IP weighting and g-estimation succeed. Survival analysis uses the same tools. Units: ch17, ch19, ch20, ch21.
- **Emulate a target trial.** Stating the hypothetical trial's protocol makes the causal question precise and shows ill-defined or extreme interventions. It also aligns trial and observational analyses. Units: ch03, ch12, ch22.

## How to read it
- **Core path (also the catalog's focus):** ch01–ch03 give the definitions and identifiability conditions. Then ch06–ch08 on graphs, confounding and selection bias, ch12 and ch13 on IP weighting and the g-formula, and ch22 on target trial emulation.
- **Foundation for Part I:** ch02 builds on ch01, and ch03 builds on ch02 (standardization and IP weighting). ch04 builds on ch01–ch03. ch07 needs ch06 (graphs) as well as ch02 and ch04. ch08 and ch09 need ch06 and ch07, and ch09 also draws on ch03 (well-defined interventions).
- **Bridge to Part II:** ch10 (random error) and ch11 (why model) come before the estimation chapters. ch12 needs ch02–ch03. ch13 needs ch12 and ch03. ch14 needs ch12–ch13. ch15 needs ch12–ch14.
- **Can be deferred on a first pass:** ch05 (interaction, including the sufficient-cause material), ch16 (instrumental variables), ch17 (survival), ch18 (variable selection and high-dimensional data), and ch23 (mediation). This is a judgement based on the catalog's focus list, which omits them. ch05 is independent of the main path after ch04. ch17 needs ch12–ch14 and ch08, and ch18 needs the g-formula, IP weighting and the DAG chapters.
- **Time-varying sequence (read in order):** ch19 needs SWIGs from ch07. ch20 needs ch19. ch21 needs ch12–ch15 and ch19–ch20. ch22 needs ch19–ch21 and the confounding/selection-bias chapters. ch23 needs the g-formula, SWIGs and ch09's front-door formula.
