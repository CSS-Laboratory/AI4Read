# Thinking, imagination, the self and consciousness (PI §§313–426)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein argues that we do not find out what words such as "think", "understand", "imagine", "I" and "conscious" mean by inspecting an inner process or object, but by looking at how the words are used (their "grammar").

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Wittgenstein asks how words for mental life work. His answer: we do not learn their meaning by inspecting an inner process or object. We have to look at their use, their "grammar", in human practices, criteria and the language we are taught. Pictures tempt us: thought as a process behind speech, the "visual room", consciousness as a mysterious inner something, a mind located in the head. The real question is how such pictures are applied. The unit shows his method. He replaces "what is it?" questions with "how is the word used?" questions. He treats many philosophical puzzles as confusions about forms of expression.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a word's meaning; the contrast between a sensation and the word for it; the law of excluded middle (p or not-p); basic idea of solipsism (only my own mind is certain). Terms as used here: *grammar* = how a word is used and what kind of thing it is said of (§373); *criterion* = what we go by in applying a word, e.g. what someone tells us plus the rest of his behaviour (§344); *visual room* = the "room" of what I see, which Wittgenstein says has no owner (§398).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Watching ourselves think is not how the concept "think" is used; the comparison is with trying to make out "checkmate" from the last move of a game without knowing chess (§316, p. 111e).
- In his examples, what constitutes thought is not a process that must accompany the words; intending is embedded in a setting of customs and institutions (§§330, 332, 337, p. 114e–115e).
- Wittgenstein suggests that inner speech, the idea of pain "from my own case", and "the same sensation as mine" have no clear application once we move beyond ordinary cases (§§344–351, p. 117e–119e).
- A picture such as excluded middle applied to inner states seems to fix a sense, but it gives only a picture whose application is not clear (§352, p. 119e).
- One ought to ask how the word "imagination" is used rather than what images are; for my own image there is no criterion of sameness (§§370, 376–382, p. 123e–125e).
- The "visual room" is described as a new way of speaking taken for the discovery of a new object; disputes between solipsists, idealists and realists are likened to attacks and defences of a form of expression as if it were an assertion (§§398–402, p. 127e–129e).
- "I" does not name a person; in "I am in pain" I name no one, as when I groan (§§404, 410, p. 129e–130e).
- Having a picture is not the same as understanding its application; the final remarks say this about the soul, blindness and consciousness (§§422–426, p. 134e).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §§313–315 — Pain as an example
Pain can be exhibited as red, straight and crooked, trees and stones can; that is what we call "exhibiting" (§313, p. 111e). If I am inclined to study my current headache in order to get clear about the philosophical problem of sensation, that indicates a fundamental misunderstanding (§314, p. 111e). He asks whether someone who never felt pain could understand the word "pain", whether experience is to teach this, and, if we say one could not imagine pain without having felt it, how we know and how it could be decided (§315, p. 111e). These are posed as questions only.

### §§316–326 — Thinking, sudden understanding, certainty
- The idea that, to get clear about "think", we watch ourselves thinking (what we observe will be what the word means) is not how this concept is used. It would be like trying to find out, without knowing chess, what "checkmate" means by close observation of the last move of a game (§316, p. 111e).
- Misleading parallel: a cry expresses pain, a sentence expresses a thought. It suggests that the purpose of a sentence is to tell one person how it is with another, only in his "thinking apparatus" and not in his stomach (§317, p. 111e).
- When we speak or write with thought we would not, by and large, say that we think faster than we talk; the thought seems not detached from the expression. Yet we also speak of the speed of thought, of thought like lightning, of problems becoming clear at a stroke. So it is natural to ask whether the same happens in lightning-like thought as in speech that is not thoughtless, only extremely accelerated: the clockwork running down all at once in one case, bit by bit and braked by words in the other (§318, p. 111e).
- I can see or understand a thought complete in a flash in the same sense in which I can note it in a few words or pencilled dashes. He asks what makes the note an epitome of the thought (§319, p. 111e).
- A lightning-like thought may stand to a spoken thought as an algebraic formula to the sequence of numbers developed from it. Given an algebraic function, I am certain I can work out its values for 1, 2, 3 … up to 10. This certainty will be called well-grounded, for I have learnt to compute such functions. In other cases there will be no grounds, but it will be justified by success (§320, p. 112e).
- The question what happens when a man suddenly understands is badly framed. If it asks what the expression "sudden understanding" means, the answer is not to point to a process we give this name. The question might instead mean: what are the symptoms of sudden understanding, its characteristic mental accompaniments (§321, p. 112e)? Such a description does not answer the question about meaning, and this tempts us to conclude that understanding is a specific, indefinable experience. But the question that should concern us is how we compare these experiences and what criterion of identity we stipulate for their occurrence (§322, p. 112e).
- The exclamation that one now knows how to go on corresponds to an instinctive sound, a glad start. It does not follow from my feeling that I will not be stuck when I try to go on. There are cases in which I would say that when I said I knew how to go on, I did know, for example after an unforeseen interruption; but what is unforeseen must not simply be that I get stuck. One can also imagine someone to whom light constantly seems to dawn, who exclaims that he has it and then can never substantiate this in practice; it might seem to him as if he forgot again the meaning of the picture that occurred to him (§323, p. 112e).
- **Interlocutor (put as a question):** is this a matter of induction, so that I am as certain I can continue the series as I am that a book will drop when I let it go, and as astonished to get stuck as to see the book hang in the air? **Reply:** we need no grounds for this certainty either; what could justify it better than success (§324, p. 112e–113e)?
- **Interlocutor:** the certainty of going on after seeing a formula is simply based on induction. Wittgenstein asks what this means: do I reason that fire has always burned me so it will now? Or is the earlier experience the cause of my certainty, not its reason? Whether it is the cause depends on the system of hypotheses, of natural laws, in terms of which we consider certainty. He asks whether such confidence is justified and says that what people accept as a justification shows how they think and live (§325, p. 113e). We expect this and are surprised at that, but the chain of reasons has an end (§326, p. 113e).

*Intuition note:* In these remarks "understanding" is not shown to be a special inner event whose look defines it. The questions that matter are what criterion of identity we stipulate for such experiences, and that certainty is justified by success and by what people accept as justification.

### §§327–343 — Thinking and speaking
- Asked whether one can think without speaking, a tempting voice answers: don't you ever think; can't you observe yourself and see what is going on? It should be quite simple (§327, p. 113e). Wittgenstein asks instead what one calls thinking, what one has learnt to use the word for, whether, if I say I have thought, I must always be right, and what kind of mistake is possible. He asks whether someone who measures in the middle of a train of thought has interrupted the thinking if he says nothing to himself while measuring (§328, p. 113e).
- When I think in words I do not have meanings in my mind in addition to the verbal expressions; language itself is the vehicle of thought (§329, p. 113e).
- Is thinking a kind of speaking? One would like to say it distinguishes speech with thought from talking without thought, and so it seems an accompaniment of speech, a process that may accompany something else or go on by itself (§330, p. 113e–114e). Example: say "Yes, this pen is blunt. Oh well, it'll do", first with thought, then without, then just think the thought without the words. While writing I might test the point of my pen, make a face and write on with a gesture of resignation. Or, while taking measurements, I might act so that an onlooker would say I wordlessly thought that if two magnitudes equal a third they equal one another. But what constitutes thought here is not some process which has to accompany the words if they are not to be spoken without thought (§330, p. 114e).
- Imagine people who could think only aloud, as there are people who can read only aloud (§331, p. 114e). We sometimes call accompanying a sentence by a mental process "thinking", but that accompaniment is not what we call a "thought". Utter a sentence with understanding, then do only what accompanied it; likewise sing a song with expression, then repeat only the expression (swaying, slower and faster breathing) (§332, p. 114e).
- **Interlocutor:** only someone who is convinced can say that. Wittgenstein asks how the conviction helps him when he says it, and whether it is present alongside the spoken expression or masked by it as a soft sound by a loud one. He adds the parallel of someone saying that to sing a tune from memory one must hear it in one's mind and sing from that (§333, p. 114e).
- We use the phrase "So you really wanted to say…" to lead someone from one form of expression to another. One is tempted to picture that what he meant was already in his mind before we articulated it. To understand this, Wittgenstein suggests considering how solutions of mathematical problems relate to their occasion, for example trisecting an angle with ruler and compass while people are trying to do it and after it has been proved impossible (§334, p. 114e–115e).
- When we make an effort to find the right expression, the way of speaking compares the process to translating or describing, with thoughts already there. This picture is more or less appropriate in different cases, but many other things happen: I surrender to a mood and the expression comes; I have a picture and try to describe it; an English expression occurs and I try to recollect the German one; I make a gesture and ask which words correspond. He then asks what one would have to reply if asked whether one has the thought before finding the expression, and what the thought consisted in before its expression; the text leaves these as questions (§335, p. 115e). The idea that one could not think a sentence with German or Latin word order as it stands is similar (§336, p. 115e).
- **Objector:** didn't I already intend the whole construction of the sentence at its beginning? Wittgenstein: if it was in my mind it would still not normally be there in some different word order; but here we form a misleading picture of "intending", that is, of the use of this word. An intention is embedded in a setting, in human customs and institutions. Without the technique of chess I could not intend to play chess. To the extent that I intend an English sentence in advance, that is made possible by the fact that I can speak English (§337, p. 115e). One can want to speak without speaking, as one can want to dance without dancing, and then the mind reaches for the idea of dancing or speaking (§338, p. 115e–116e).
- Thinking is not an incorporeal process which lends life and sense to speaking and could be detached from it, as the Devil took Schlemihl's shadow. But in what way not an incorporeal process? He says that, trying to explain the word "thinking" in a primitive way, he helped himself to that expression. One could say "Thinking is an incorporeal process" if using it to distinguish the grammar of "think" from that of "eat"; only that makes the difference between the meanings look too slight. An inappropriate expression is a sure means of remaining stuck in confusion (§339, p. 116e).
- One cannot guess how a word functions; one has to look at its application and learn from that. The difficulty is to remove the prejudice that stands in the way, and it is not a stupid prejudice (§340, p. 116e). Speech with and without thought is to be compared to playing a piece of music with and without thought (§341, p. 116e).
- William James, to show that thought is possible without speech, quotes the reminiscences of the deaf-mute Mr Ballard, who wrote that in early youth, even before he could speak, he had had thoughts about God and the world. Wittgenstein asks what that could mean, whether this is the correct translation of wordless thoughts into words, and why this question, which otherwise seems not to exist, arises here. Does he want to say the writer's memory deceives him? He does not even know whether he would say that; the recollections are a strange memory phenomenon, and he does not know what conclusions can be drawn about the narrator's past (§342, p. 116e). The words with which I express my memory are my memory reaction (§343, p. 117e).

*Intuition note:* Thought is not presented as a ghostly layer behind words. Whether speech is with thought is not settled by finding a separate inner process; Wittgenstein points to the setting, customs and abilities in which we speak and intend (§§337, 330).

### §§344–351 — Inner speech and others' sensations
- He asks whether it is conceivable that people never speak an audible language but talk to themselves inwardly in the imagination. **Interlocutor:** they would merely be doing always what they now do sometimes. The next sentences (speaker not marked in the text) say it is then quite easy to imagine, one need only make the easy transition from some to all; similarly, an infinitely long row of trees is simply one that does not come to an end. Our criterion for someone's saying something to himself is what he tells us and the rest of his behaviour; and we say someone talks to himself only if, in the ordinary sense, he can talk. We do not say it of a parrot or a gramophone (§344, p. 117e).
- He asks what kind of proposition it is that what sometimes happens might always happen; it is similar to: if F(a) makes sense, (x).F(x) makes sense. Likewise: if one can make a false move in some game, then everybody could make nothing but false moves in every game. So we are tempted to misunderstand the logic of our expressions and to give an incorrect account of the use of our words. Orders are sometimes not obeyed, but if no order were ever obeyed the concept of an order would have lost its purpose (§345, p. 117e).
- Could we imagine God suddenly giving a parrot reason, so that it says things to itself? He notes that it is important that, to arrive at this idea, I had recourse to the notion of a deity (§346, p. 117e).
- **Interlocutor:** at least I know from my own case what it means to say things to oneself, and I could do so even without the organs of speech. **Reply:** if I know it only from my own case, then I know only what I call that, not what anyone else does (§347, p. 117e).
- **Interlocutor:** deaf-mutes have learned only a sign-language, but each talks to himself inwardly in a vocal language. Wittgenstein asks what he can do with this information and says the whole idea of understanding smells fishy here; it is an English sentence, apparently in order until one wants to do something with it (§348, p. 117e–118e). Interlocutor: this assumption surely makes good sense. In ordinary circumstances these words and this picture have an application we know; if we suppose a case where this application does not exist, we become aware of their nakedness (§349, p. 118e).
- **Interlocutor:** supposing someone has a pain is supposing he has just what I have so often had. **Reply:** that gets us no further, like saying that because you know what 5 o'clock here means you know what 5 o'clock on the sun means, namely the same time as here. The explanation by sameness does not work, for I do not know in which cases to speak of the same time here and there. Likewise the supposition that he has a pain is no explanation if given as the supposition that he has the same as I. What is clear to him is this part of grammar: one will say the stove has the same experience as I if one says it is in pain and I am in pain (§350, p. 118e).
- **Interlocutor:** a sensation of pain is a sensation of pain, whether he or I have it. Wittgenstein says he might go along with that. Asked whether he does not know what he means by saying the stove is in pain, he replies that the words may lead him to imagine all sorts of things, but their usefulness goes no further. A better example is the application of "above" and "beneath" to the globe: only reflection shows that in this case they cannot be used in the customary way (§351, p. 118e–119e).

*Intuition note:* "Same as mine" borrows a familiar use. Where we have not fixed the cases for applying it, the phrase looks meaningful but has not been given a use.

### §§352–358 — Excluded middle, criteria, "meaning it"
- The temptation to say that either an image floats before his mind or not, with no third possibility; the same with the group 7777 in the infinite expansion of π. That means: God sees, but we don't know. The picture is of a visible series which one person can survey and another cannot, and the law of excluded middle says it must look either like this or like that. So it says nothing at all but gives us a picture, which seems to determine what to do and look for, but does not, because we do not know how it is to be applied. The same holds for saying that either he has this sensation or he doesn't (§352, p. 119e).
- Asking whether and how a proposition can be verified is only a special form of the question how you mean it; the answer contributes to the grammar of the proposition (§353, p. 119e).
- The fluctuation in grammar between criteria and symptoms makes it look as if there were nothing but symptoms. That sense impressions can deceive us about rain rests on a definition (§354, p. 119e–120e). The point is that we understand their language, which like any other rests on convention (§355, p. 120e). Wittgenstein asks whether it is not a misleading metaphor to say my eyes send me the message that there is a chair over there (§356, p. 120e).
- We do not say that possibly a dog talks to itself. One might say that if one sees the behaviour of a living being, one sees its mind. I do not say of myself that I talk to myself from observation of my behaviour, yet it makes sense only because I behave so (§357, p. 120e). **Interlocutor:** surely our meaning it gives the sentence sense; meaning lies in the mind, is private, an intangible Something comparable only to consciousness. Wittgenstein asks how one could find this ludicrous and says that it is, as it were, "a dream of our language" (§358, p. 120e).

### §§359–366 — Machines, silent talk, calculating in the head
- Could a machine think; could it be in pain? He asks whether the human body is to be called such a machine; it surely comes as close as possible to being one (§359, p. 120e). **Interlocutor:** surely a machine cannot think. Wittgenstein asks whether that is an empirical statement and says no; we say it only of a human being and what is like one, also of dolls, and perhaps even of ghosts. Regard the word "to think" as an instrument! (§360, p. 120e–121e).
- The chair is thinking to itself: where? The question seems senseless for a man but to demand an answer for a chair, because we want to know how the chair is supposed to be like a human being. What is it like to talk silently to oneself? Only by teaching someone the meaning of the expression can I explain it, and we learn it as children; but no one will say the teacher tells us what goes on here (§361, p. 121e). It seems to us that the instructor conveyed the meaning without telling the pupil directly and that in the end the pupil gives himself the correct ostensive definition; this is where the illusion lies (§362, p. 121e).
- **Interlocutor:** when I imagine something, something goes on. Wittgenstein: and then I make a noise, presumably to communicate what went on; but what is the language-game of communicating? We are so used to communicating in speech that it looks as if the whole point lay in someone else's grasping the mental sense of my words; whatever he does further would be no part of the immediate purpose. The remark ends with an analogy: as if one said the clock shows the time, what time is is not yet settled, and the point of telling the time doesn't come in here (§363, p. 121e–122e).
- A man calculates in his head and uses the result for building a bridge. Do you want to say he did not really calculate? Wittgenstein asks whether calculating in the imagination is in some sense less real than calculating on paper, and says it is real calculating-in-the-head. Whether it is similar to calculating on paper, he does not know: is a bit of white paper with black lines similar to a human body (§364, p. 122e)? Adelheid and the Bishop play a real game of chess; they are not merely pretending (§365, p. 122e). One might be inclined to say calculating in the head is less real, but one can think the opposite too. The mistake is wanting to say there was a mental process corresponding to the multiplication on paper, so that one could speak of a method of projection from the mental image to the sign (§366, p. 122e).

### §§367–374 — Imagination and grammar
- A mental image is the image described when someone describes what he imagines (§367, p. 122e). In the room example, chairs I described as green are painted dark red and where I said yellow he paints blue; I may still say: quite right, that is what it looks like (§368, p. 123e).
- Answering what goes on when one calculates in the head with "First I add 17 and 18, then I subtract 39…" is not the answer to the question; what is called calculating in the head is not explained this way (§369, p. 123e).
- One ought to ask not what images are or what goes on when one imagines something, but how the word "imagination" is used. This does not mean he wants to talk only about words, since the question what imagination essentially is concerns the word as much as his question does. The question is not clarified, for the person imagining or for anyone else, by pointing or by describing a process; it too asks for the clarification of a word but makes us expect a wrong kind of answer (§370, p. 123e).
- "Essence is expressed in grammar" (§371, p. 123e). §372 asks us to consider, in quotation marks, the claim that the only correlate in language to an objective necessity is an arbitrary rule (p. 123e). Grammar tells what kind of object anything is; theology as grammar (§373, p. 123e).
- The great difficulty is not to present the matter as if there were something one could not do, as if there were an object from which I extract a description that I cannot show anyone. The best he can propose is to yield to the temptation to use this picture and then investigate what its application looks like (§374, p. 123e).

*Intuition note:* Asking what imagining really is expects a thing to point at. Wittgenstein redirects us to how the word is used.

### §§375–386 — Comparing images; private recognition
- How does one teach someone to read silently, and how does one know when he can (§375, p. 123e)? What is the criterion that I am doing the same as someone else who silently says the ABC? It might be found that the same thing goes on in our larynxes. He asks whether we learnt the use of such words by someone pointing to a process in the larynx or brain, and notes it is possible that my auditory image and his correspond to different physiological processes. The question is how one compares images (§376, p. 124e).
- A logician may think that the same is the same and that how someone satisfies himself of sameness is a psychological question. Wittgenstein asks what the criterion is for the sameness of two images, and for the redness of an image: for someone else's image, what he says and does; for my own, nothing. What goes for "red" goes for "same" (§377, p. 124e).
- **Interlocutor:** before I judge that two images are the same I must recognize them as the same. How do I then know "same" describes what I recognize? Only if I can express my recognition in some other way and someone else can teach me that "same" is the correct word. For if I need a warrant for using a word, it must also be a warrant for someone else (§378, p. 124e).
- **Interlocutor:** first I recognize it as this, then I remember what it is called. He asks in which cases this can rightly be said (§379, p. 124e). How do I recognize that this is red? "I see that it is this and then I know it is called red": what kind of answer to "This?" makes sense? He remarks that the interlocutor keeps steering towards an inner ostensive explanation. He could not apply rules to a private transition from what is seen to words; the rules would hang in the air, for the institution of their application is lacking (§380, p. 124e). One answer to how I recognize this colour as red is: I have learnt English (§381, p. 124e). He asks how he can justify forming this image on hearing this word, whether anyone showed him the image of blue and told him it is blue, and how one points at an image, or twice at the same one (§382, p. 125e).
- We analyse a concept, not a phenomenon, and hence the application of a word. So it may look like nominalism, which wrongly interprets all words as names (§383, p. 125e). You learned the concept of pain in learning language (§384, p. 125e). He asks whether one could learn to calculate in the head without ever calculating aloud or on paper, and what would count as a criterion for being able to. A tribe acquainted only with head-calculation would have to be depicted as a limiting case, and then it arises whether we still want to apply the concept or whether it has lost its purpose (§385, p. 125e).
- **Interlocutor:** you know what calculating and red are elsewhere, so if you say you have imagined red then it is red, and you don't always rely on agreement with others. **Reply:** I do have confidence in myself. The difficulty is not doubt whether I imagined anything red; it is that we should be able, just like that, to point out or describe the colour imagined, so that mapping the image into reality presents no difficulty. He asks whether I can ask what an actual mental image of the colour looks like. In a parenthesis he says he cannot accept this testimony, because it is not testimony; it tells him only what the person is inclined to say (§386, p. 125e–126e).

### §§387–397 — Images and imaginability
- The deep aspect readily eludes us (§387, p. 126e). He asks how one can know one can show violet with a paint box, how I know from my image what the colour looks like, and how I know that I'll be able to do something (§388, p. 126e).
- **Interlocutor:** a mental image must be more like its object than any picture, since it is intrinsically the image of this and nothing else. That is how one might come to regard a mental image as a super-likeness (§389, p. 126e).
- He asks whether one could imagine a stone's having consciousness and why, if someone can, that should not prove merely that such image-mongery is of no interest to us (§390, p. 126e). He can perhaps even imagine (not easily) that each person in the street is in frightful pain but adroitly concealing it; it is important that adroit concealment must be imagined, and I as it were play a part and act as if the others were in pain (§391, p. 126e).
- The analysis "when I imagine he's in pain, all that really goes on in me is…" oscillates between natural science and grammar (§392, p. 127e). Where outside philosophy do we use "I can imagine that he is in pain"? To an actor we say he must imagine it and give no further directions, so the suggested analysis is not to the point (§393, p. 127e). He asks when we would ask what actually went on in someone as he imagined, and what answer we expect (§394, p. 127e).
- There is a lack of clarity about the role of imaginability, about the extent to which it ensures that a sentence makes sense (§395, p. 127e). It is no more essential to understanding a sentence that one imagine something than that one make a sketch from it (§396, p. 127e). Imaginability can be called representability in a particular medium, which may point a way to further use of a sentence; but a picture may obtrude and be of no use (§397, p. 127e).

### §§398–413 — Visual room, the "I", consciousness
- **Interlocutor:** when I imagine or see I have something my neighbour has not. Wittgenstein understands the wish to say "at any rate only I have this", but asks what these words are for: they serve no purpose. If you logically exclude other people's having something, it loses its sense to say you have it. He thinks one can say that the speaker is talking of the visual room, which has no owner; it cannot belong to anyone else and so not to me. He adds an imaginary landscape with a house: asked whose house it is, one might say the farmer sitting in front of it, but he cannot step into his house (§398, p. 127e–128e). One could also say that the owner of the visual room would have to be of the same nature as it, but he isn't inside it, and there is no outside (§399, p. 128e).
- The visual room seemed like a discovery, but what its discoverer found was a new way of speaking, a new comparison, and, one could even say, a new experience (§400, p. 128e). You interpret the new conception as the seeing of a new object, and a grammatical movement you have made as a quasi-physical phenomenon you observe; but Wittgenstein adds that his own expression "grammatical movement" is not unobjectionable: you have found a new conception, as if you had invented a new way of painting or a new kind of song (§401, p. 128e).
- **Interlocutor:** the words "I have" are merely a sign for others; the visual world is described completely by the description of the image. Wittgenstein: you mean they are like "Attention please!" When one disapproves of ordinary expressions we have a picture in our heads which conflicts with the picture of ordinary speaking. Disputes between idealists, solipsists and realists look like this: one party attacks the normal form of expression as if attacking an assertion, the other defends it as if stating facts every reasonable person recognizes (§402, p. 129e).
- If I reserved the word "pain" for what I had called "my pain" and others called it "L.W.'s pain", I would do others no injustice so long as a notation made good the loss of the word elsewhere. But I would gain nothing, and the solipsist does not want any practical advantage either (§403, p. 129e).
- **Interlocutor:** when I say "I am in pain" I don't point to a person, since in a certain sense I don't know who is. Wittgenstein says this can be given a justification: in saying it I name no person, as when I groan, though someone else sees from the groan who is in pain. Knowing who is in pain means, for example, knowing which man in the room; there is a great variety of criteria for the identity of a person, and none leads me to say I am in pain (§404, p. 129e–130e). I just want to draw attention to myself (§405). He asks whether this is true in every case, even when I merely groan (§406, p. 130e). Someone could groan out that someone is in pain, he doesn't know who, and people would help him, the one who groaned (§407, p. 130e). "I don't know whether I or someone else is in pain" would be a logical product, one factor of which, "I don't know whether I am in pain or not", is not a significant sentence (§408, p. 130e).
- In a circle with a hidden electrostatic generator I can say, when I see which of us was given a shock, that now I know who it is, it's me; I could also say I now know who is feeling the shocks, which would be an odd way of speaking. But if I suppose I can feel a shock when someone else is given one, the form of expression becomes quite inappropriate: it does not belong to this game (§409, p. 130e). "I" doesn't name a person, nor "here" a place, and "this" is not a name, but they are connected with names, which are explained by means of them. It is characteristic of physics not to use these words (§410, p. 130e).
- The questions whether these books, this foot, this body, this sensation are mine each have practical uses; for the sensation one goes astray by imagining that directing attention to a sensation is pointing at it (§411, p. 131e).
- The feeling of an unbridgeable gulf between consciousness and brain process is accompanied by slight giddiness. It occurs when I turn my attention in a particular way on my own consciousness and say astonished that this is produced by a brain process. What I described with the words about turning my attention was an act of gazing, vacant, not at any point or object. The sentence itself has nothing paradoxical about it: I could say it in an experiment showing that an effect of light is produced by stimulation of a part of the brain (§412, p. 131e–132e). James's introspection, which suggested that the self consists mainly of peculiar motions in the head and throat, showed not the meaning of "self" but a philosopher's state of attention when he says the word to himself (§413, p. 132e).

### §§414–421 — Natural history and consciousness
- You think you must be weaving because you sit at a loom, even if it is empty, and go through the motions of weaving (§414, p. 132e). What we supply are remarks on the natural history of human beings: not curiosities, but facts that no one has doubted, which have escaped notice only because they are always before our eyes (§415, p. 132e).
- **Interlocutor:** human beings agree in saying they see, hear, feel, so they are their own witnesses that they have consciousness. Wittgenstein asks whom I inform by "I have consciousness" and how another person can understand me. Sentences like "I see" or "I am conscious again" do have uses, for example to a doctor or to someone who thinks I am in a faint (§416, p. 132e). He asks whether I observe myself and perceive that I am conscious. If the words "I perceive" show that I am attending to my consciousness, which is ordinarily not the case, then the sentence does not say that I am conscious but that my attention is focused in such-and-such a way. He asks what experience occasions saying "I am conscious again" (§417, p. 132e–133e).
- He asks whether my having consciousness is a fact of experience and what it would be like if it were otherwise (§418, p. 133e). He asks in what circumstances we shall say a tribe has a chief; an objector replies that the chief must surely have consciousness (§419, p. 133e).
- He asks whether I can imagine that people around me are automata. Alone in my room the idea is perhaps a little uncanny; but trying to hold it in ordinary intercourse, the words either become quite empty or produce an uncanny feeling. Seeing a living human being as an automaton is analogous to seeing one figure as a limiting case or variant of another (§420, p. 133e). A single report mixing physical and conscious states seems paradoxical to us because we want to say the sentence is about both tangibles and intangibles. He asks whether it worries us to say three struts give a building stability, and whether three and stability are tangible. Regard the sentence as an instrument and its sense as its employment (§421, p. 133e).

### §§422–426 — Picture versus application
- In believing that man has a soul, or that a substance contains two carbon rings, there is a picture in the foreground but the sense lies far in the background: the application of the picture is not easy to survey (§422, p. 134e). I do not dispute the picture's validity or correctness; only let me understand its application, for example for blindness pictured as darkness in the mind or head of a blind person (§§423–424, p. 134e).
- Usually we exert ourselves to find a picture, and once found, the application comes about automatically. Here a picture obtrudes at every turn but does not help with the difficulty, which begins only now. A drawing reduced in scale may show how a mechanism fits a casing; saying "it is the same there" no longer explains anything but only invites me to apply the picture (§425, p. 134e).
- A picture seems to fix the sense unambiguously, and the actual use looks muddied. As in set theory, the form of expression seems tailored for a god who sees infinite series and into human consciousness. For us these forms are like vestments we may put on but cannot do much with. In actual use we make detours and go by side roads; we see the straight highway but cannot use it, because it is permanently closed (§426, p. 134e).

*Intuition note:* We often reach for a picture (inner theatre, darkness in the head) as if it settled meaning. Wittgenstein's remarks here say the work lies in finding how, in practice, such words are applied.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A teacher writes 2, 4, 6, 8 and asks a pupil to go on. Over a session the pupil says "I know how to go on!" five times, with five different series, and then continues. Four times he continues correctly; once he is stuck at the next term.
- Share of exclamations followed by correct continuation: 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8, i.e. 80%.
- Our reading: the teacher's check (continuing correctly) is what she can use to say the pupil did understand, not any inner flash. This fits §§321–324 (success as justification). The fifth case does not show the feeling was absent; it fits the point that the feeling does not guarantee one will not be stuck (§323).

## Concepts

- [[private language argument]] — related passages: no private warrant, no private ostensive definition of colour words (§§378–380, p. 124e); the argument is not stated as a thesis here
- [[analysis]] — Wittgenstein says we analyse concepts (the application of words), not phenomena (§383, p. 125e)
- [[rule-following problem]] — rules for a private transition from the seen to the word would hang in the air, since the institution of their application is lacking (§380, p. 124e)
- grammar (new) — what grammar tells: what kind of object anything is (§373); essence is said to be expressed in grammar (§371, p. 123e)
- criterion vs. symptom (new) — the fluctuation in grammar between criteria and symptoms makes it look as if there were only symptoms; sense impressions' deceiving us about rain rests on a definition (§354, p. 119e–120e)
- picture vs. application (new) — a picture may seem to fix the sense, but the sense lies far in the background, in the application (§§422–426, p. 134e)
- visual room (new) — what has no owner (§398); what its discoverer found was a new way of speaking (§400, p. 127e–128e)
- natural history of human beings (new) — remarks on facts no one has doubted, unnoticed because always before our eyes (§415, p. 132e)
