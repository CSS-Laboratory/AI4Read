# Family resemblance, exactness, philosophy, and the general form of the proposition (§§65–142)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein says that "game", "number" and "language" have no single common essence, only overlapping similarities. He concludes that philosophy should describe everyday use, not hunt for hidden essences.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
An objector complains that Wittgenstein never says what all language has in common. He replies that there is no one common feature. Instances of "game", "number" or "language" are linked by overlapping similarities, which he calls family resemblances. Such concepts can have blurred edges and still be usable. "Exact" depends on the purpose at hand. The longing for a pure, hidden essence of propositions and logic comes from pictures built into our forms of expression. Philosophy should only describe and survey the everyday use of words, not explain or justify it; reform is not his aim here. The unit ends by arguing that a mental picture, such as a cube, does not force one use of a word.

**Prerequisites:** the language-game idea from earlier sections; what an "ordinary-language" explanation by examples is; the Tractatus claim that propositions have a general form (only named here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Family resemblance.** Games form a network of overlapping, criss-crossing similarities with no feature common to all. This answers the demand for an essence of "language" (§§65–67, pp. 35e–36e).
- **Blurred concepts are still concepts.** No boundary has been drawn for "game", and none is needed to make the concept usable, except perhaps for a special purpose. A boundary can be drawn for a special purpose (§§68–71, pp. 37e–38e).
- **Explanation by examples is a proper explanation.** One gives examples and intends them to be used in a particular way. Even general explanations can be misunderstood (§71, p. 38e).
- **Rules and doubt.** Rules, like signposts, sometimes leave room for doubt and sometimes do not. An explanation need not be final, only enough to prevent actual misunderstanding (§§79–87, pp. 41e–45e).
- **Exactness is goal-relative.** "Inexact" means attaining the goal less perfectly, not "unusable". No single ideal of exactness has been envisaged (§88, p. 46e).
- **Crystalline logic is a requirement, not a discovery.** ("Crystalline" here means perfectly pure and rigid.) The craving for a hidden essence and an ideal order conflicts with real language: "Back to the rough ground!" (§§89–108, pp. 46e–52e).
- **Philosophy as description.** It brings words back from metaphysical to everyday use, gives a surveyable view, and "leaves everything as it is" (§§109–133, pp. 52e–57e).
- **"This is how things are".** It is used as a propositional variable (a placeholder for any statement). Wittgenstein compares our concept of a proposition with those of "game" and number, and says "true/false" belong to it as check belongs to the chess king, not as a test that determines it from outside (§§134–137, pp. 57e–59e).
- **Understanding and use.** A picture before the mind does not fix a use. Use is clear in normal cases, doubtful in abnormal ones (§§138–142, pp. 59e–61e).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §§62–64 — Analysis again (p. 35e)
The unit opens in mid-§62. The "point" of an order is not always clear. Likewise there is not always a clear boundary between the essential and the inessential features of an object's purpose (§62, p. 35e).

Calling a sentence in form (b) the "analysed" form of one in form (a) tempts us to think (b) is more fundamental. Wittgenstein asks whether an aspect of the matter is not lost to the analysed form no less than to the unanalysed one (§63, p. 35e).

In an altered language-game (48), names stand for two-square rectangles, e.g. "U" for half red, half green. People could have names for colour combinations but not for single colours. He asks in what way the symbols of this game need analysis and how far it can be replaced by (48); his answer is that it is just a different language-game, related to (48) (§64, p. 35e).

### §§65–67 — Family resemblance (pp. 35e–37e)
**Objection (an imagined interlocutor):** you talk of many language-games but never say what is essential to language, the "general form of the proposition" (§65, p. 35e).

**Wittgenstein:** this is true. He says these phenomena have no one thing in common in virtue of which we use the same word for all. They have many different kinds of affinity, and we call them all "languages" because of these (§65, p. 35e).

On games, he does not say "they must have something in common". He says "don't think, but look!" (§66, p. 36e). He compares:
- board-games with card-games: some features carry over and others drop out;
- ball-games: winning and losing are present, but not when a child throws a ball at a wall;
- he asks whether all games are "entertaining", comparing chess with noughts and crosses, and whether there is always winning and losing or competition, citing patience;
- skill in chess with skill in tennis, and luck;
- singing and dancing games: entertainment is present, but many other features are gone.

The upshot is a "complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing" (§66, p. 36e).

He calls these similarities **family resemblances**, like the overlapping build, eyes and gait of family members. Games form a family, and so do kinds of number. Perhaps a new thing is called a number because it has a direct affinity with some earlier numbers, and this can be said to give it an indirect affinity with others. The concept is extended "as in spinning a thread": the strength lies in the overlapping of many fibres, not in one fibre running through the whole (§67, p. 36e).

**Objection:** there is something common, the disjunction of all the common properties. **Reply:** this only plays with a word. One could equally say a Something runs through the thread, namely the overlapping of the fibres (§67, p. 37e).

*Intuition note.* We can call things by one word because each is similar to some others, even if no single feature is shared by all of them. The unity is a chain or network, not a hidden core.

### §§68–71 — Boundaries, exactness, blurred concepts (pp. 37e–38e)
**Objection:** the concept is then a "logical sum" of sub-concepts. **Wittgenstein:** need not be so. One can give "number" rigid boundaries, or use it with an open extension, and that is how "game" is used. "Can you say where the boundaries are? No. You can draw some, for there aren't any drawn yet" (§68, p. 37e).

**Objection:** then use is unregulated. **Reply:** it is not everywhere bounded by rules, but neither is tennis, where no rule says how high or hard to throw the ball. Tennis is still a game with rules (§68, p. 37e).

To explain "game" we describe games and add "this and similar things are called 'games'". Not knowing the boundaries is not ignorance, since none have been drawn. A boundary can be drawn for a special purpose; making the concept usable does not take one, except perhaps for that purpose. The comparison is the definition "1 pace = 75 cm" and the measure "one pace". If one says it was inexact before, it was an inexact measure, "though you still owe me a definition of exactness" (§69, p. 37e).

**Objection:** without boundaries you don't know what you mean (§70, p. 37e–38e). **Reply:** saying "The ground was quite covered with plants" does not require a definition of "plant". A drawing plus "it looked roughly like this" explains. Even "exactly like this" does not mean the same blades of grass (§70, p. 38e). A note on this remark: someone says "Show the children a game", and I teach them dice gambling. He says he did not mean that sort. Wittgenstein asks whether he must have had the exclusion of dice before his mind when he gave the order (p. 38e).

A concept can have blurred edges (§71, p. 38e). He asks whether a photograph that is not sharp is a picture of a person at all, whether it is even always an advantage to replace an unsharp picture by a sharp one, and whether the unsharp one is not often just what we need. **Frege's view:** a concept is like a region, and a region without clear boundaries is not a region. Wittgenstein asks whether it is senseless to say "Stay roughly here", pointing at a spot without drawing a boundary. He says this is just how one might explain what a game is: by giving examples and intending them to be taken in a particular way. The learner is not supposed to see a common feature that the teacher could not formulate. Giving examples is not an indirect explanation used for lack of a better one. Any general explanation may also be misunderstood (§71, p. 38e).

*Intuition note.* A vague concept is not a broken concept. It works like "stay roughly here": usable and clear enough for the purpose.

### §§72–77 — Samples, knowing, sharp versus blurred (pp. 38e–41e)
"Seeing what is in common" works in some explanations, e.g. showing multicoloured pictures and saying the colour in all of them is "yellow ochre". He then contrasts further cases: figures of different shapes painted the same colour, and samples of different shades of blue, each with a colour name given for what is common (§72, pp. 38e–39e).

Colour-name explanations by samples resemble a chart, but the comparison can mislead. We are tempted to say understanding means having a sample or picture in the mind. But what would a mental picture of "what is common to all leaf shapes" look like? A schematic leaf or a sample of pure green can serve as a general sample. That a schema is understood as a schema, not as a particular leaf, "resides in the way the samples are applied" (§73, p. 39e). Seeing a sample one way or another may go with using it differently. A cube drawing seen as a flat figure may lead to a different response to "Bring me something like this!" than one seen three-dimensionally (§74, p. 40e).

He asks what it means to know what a game is and not be able to say it, and whether this knowledge is equivalent to an unformulated definition. He also asks whether his concept of a game is not completely expressed in the explanations he could give: describing examples, showing how other games can be built on their analogy, saying he would hardly call this or that a game (§75, p. 40e).

A sharp boundary drawn by someone else is not the one I wanted, since I did not want to draw one. He says his concept is not the same as mine but akin to it, as a picture with sharp patches is to one with blurred patches. The affinity is as undeniable as the difference (§76, p. 40e).

How closely a sharp picture can match a blurred one depends on how little sharpness the blurred one has. If the colours merge without a hint of boundary, "anything — and nothing — is right". Wittgenstein says this is the position in ethics or aesthetics when someone looks for definitions. His advice is to ask how we learned the word (e.g. "good"), from what examples, in what language-games. Then, he says, it will be easier to see that the word must have a family of meanings (§77, pp. 40e–41e).

### §§78–88 — Knowing and saying; names; rules; signposts; exactness (pp. 41e–46e)
Knowing is compared in three cases: how many metres high Mont Blanc is, how the word "game" is used, how a clarinet sounds. Someone surprised that one can know something and not be able to say it is perhaps thinking of a case like the first, certainly not of one like the third (§78, p. 41e).

**Moses (§79, pp. 41e–42e).** "Moses did not exist" may mean various things. **Russell (as Wittgenstein presents him):** the name can be defined by various descriptions, and each choice gives the sentence a different sense. **Wittgenstein's reply:** am I always ready to substitute one such description? Have I decided how much of the Biblical story must turn out false before I give the proposition up? It is as though I have a series of props and lean on another if one is removed. For "N is dead", the meaning of "N" may be: a man I have seen in such places (1), who looked like this (2), who did such things (3), and bore the name (4). Asked what I mean, I would list some of these, different ones on different occasions. He asks whether I would declare "N is dead" false if some point proved false, even one that strikes me as insignificant, and where the boundaries of the insignificant lie. So he says "I use the name 'N' without a fixed meaning". This impairs its use as little as a wobbling table is impaired by standing on four legs, not three. He asks whether he is then using a word whose meaning he does not know and talking nonsense, and answers: say what you please, so long as it does not stop you seeing how things are. He adds that scientific definitions also fluctuate (§79, pp. 41e–42e).

A chair disappears and reappears. Do we have rules for every such case, and do we miss them? Do we then attach no meaning to "chair"? (§80, p. 42e.)

**§81 (p. 42e–43e).** Ramsey said logic is a "normative science". Wittgenstein says that in philosophy we often compare the use of words with games and calculi with fixed rules, but cannot say that someone using language must be playing such a game. Saying that our languages "only approximate" such calculi risks making logic look like a logic of an ideal language, "for a vacuum". "Ideal" is liable to mislead because it suggests languages better and more perfect than everyday language. He says this can appear in the right light only with greater clarity about understanding, meaning something and thinking.

What is "the rule according to which he proceeds"? Is it the hypothesis describing his use, the rule he looks up, or the one he states? What if observation shows none, and his stated explanation is one he would withdraw? He asks how the rule according to which he plays is then to be determined, and what is left for the expression "the rule according to which he proceeds" to say (§82, p. 43e).

He imagines people amusing themselves in a field with a ball, starting several games without finishing them and throwing the ball aimlessly in between. Then someone says they are playing a ball-game the whole time and so are following definite rules at every throw. Wittgenstein asks whether the analogy between language and games throws light here, and whether there is not also the case where we make up the rules as we go along and even alter them (§83, pp. 43e–44e).

What would a game everywhere bounded by rules look like? One could imagine a rule regulating the application of a rule, and so on. But that we can imagine a doubt does not mean we are in doubt. The person who doubts before opening his door whether an abyss lies behind it differs from me, who do not doubt (§84, p. 44e).

**Signpost (§85, p. 44e).** A rule stands like a signpost. Does it leave no doubt which way to go (along the road or the footpath, in the direction of the finger or the opposite)? He asks whether a sequence of signposts or chalk marks has only one way of being interpreted. He concludes that the signpost sometimes leaves room for doubt and sometimes not. Wittgenstein says this is "no longer a philosophical proposition, but an empirical one".

**Chart (§86, pp. 44e–45e).** In a chart version of game (2), B looks up a written sign in a chart. The chart is a rule he follows, and part of the training may well consist in learning to pass the finger along it from left to right. Different reading schemas (arrow patterns) can be added as rules. Can we imagine further rules explaining these? Was the chart incomplete without the arrow schema?

**§87 (p. 45e).** "I take 'Moses' to mean the man, if there was one, who led the Israelites out of Egypt." Similar doubts arise about "Egypt" and "Israelites", and even about "red". **Objection:** if the explanation is never final, I never understand. **Reply:** an explanation may rest on another that has been given, but none needs another unless we require it to avoid a misunderstanding. An explanation serves to remove or prevent a misunderstanding, "but not every misunderstanding that I can imagine". It may easily look as if every doubt merely revealed a gap in the foundations, so that secure understanding is possible only if we first doubt everything that can be doubted and then remove all these doubts. The signpost is in order if, under normal circumstances, it fulfils its purpose.

*Intuition note.* Rules and explanations do not need to close off every imaginable doubt. A signpost is in order if it fulfils its purpose under normal circumstances, and an explanation serves to remove the misunderstandings that would otherwise actually arise.

**Exactness (§88, p. 46e).** "Stay roughly here" may work perfectly. "Inexact" does not mean "unusable". A chalk line has breadth, and a coloured edge is more exact, but the extra exactness may be idle. The words for exactness have different, though related, meanings in different cases: setting a pocket-watch to the exact time, laboratory timing, "exactly one o'clock" for dinner. "Inexact" is a reproach, "exact" praise. What counts depends on the goal, e.g. not giving the sun's distance to the nearest metre, or a joiner a table width to a thousandth of a millimetre. "No single ideal of exactness has been envisaged".

### §§89–108 — Logic as sublime; hidden essence; ideal (pp. 46e–52e)
**Logic as sublime (§89).** Logic seemed to have depth and to lie at the foundation of the sciences, investigating the essence of all things, not caring about facts. In this investigation we want to understand something already in plain view. Augustine on time (he knows what it is if no one asks him, but not when he tries to explain it) is cited. Such a thing "has to be called to mind" (§89, pp. 46e–47e).

The investigation is directed at the "possibilities" of phenomena: we recall the kinds of statements we make about them. Wittgenstein calls the inquiry **grammatical**. It clears away misunderstandings of the use of words caused, among other things, by analogies between forms of expression. Substituting one form for another may be called "analysing" (§90, p. 47e).

He describes how it may come to look as if there were a final analysis, a single completely analysed form of every expression, and as if a state of complete exactness were the real goal of the investigation (§91, pp. 47e–48e). This is expressed in the question about the essence of language, proposition and thought. That question sees the essence as hidden beneath the surface and to be unearthed by analysis, whereas an investigation of nature or function treats it as open to view. The answer is expected once for all and independent of future experience (§92, p. 48e).

For one speaker a proposition is "the most ordinary thing", for another "something very remarkable". The forms of the expressions used about propositions stand in the latter's way. Importance of propositions, plus a misunderstanding of the logic of language, makes us think something extraordinary is achieved by them (§93, p. 48e). This is the "sublimation" of logic: the tendency to assume a pure intermediary between sign and fact, or to purify the sign. Our forms of expression send us in pursuit of chimeras (§94, p. 48e).

"Thinking must be something unique": when we mean that such-and-such is the case, we do not stop short of the fact. He says this paradox, which has the form of a truism, can also be put as: one can think what is not the case (§95, pp. 48e–49e). Thought, language, proposition and world seem to stand in line, each equivalent to the others. The language-game in which these words are used is missing (§96, p. 49e).

Thinking is "surrounded by a nimbus". Its essence, logic, is thought to be the a priori order of possibilities, utterly simple and crystalline, and indeed the "hardest thing there is" (Tractatus 5.5563). Wittgenstein says it is an illusion that our investigation must grasp an incomparable essence of language, a "super-order between super-concepts". If "language", "experience", "world" have a use, it must be as humble as that of "table", "lamp", "door" (§97, p. 49e).

On the one hand every sentence is "in order as it is" and we are not striving after an ideal. On the other hand it seems that where there is sense, there must be perfect order, even in the vaguest sentence (§98, p. 49e). The voice of the second pull: the sense of a sentence may leave things open, but it must be determinate. An indeterminate sense would be no sense, like a boundary that is not sharp being no boundary. One is inclined to think that if I say I have locked a man in a room but left one door open, I have not locked him up at all. Wittgenstein asks: "But is that really true?" (§99, p. 50e).

**Interlocutor:** a game with vague rules is not a game. Perhaps it is not a perfect game. **Wittgenstein:** we would call it a game, but we are dazzled by the ideal and fail to see the actual application of "game" (§100, p. 50e). We think the ideal "must" occur in reality because we think we already see it there (§101, p. 50e). The rules seem hidden in the medium of understanding (§102, p. 50e). The ideal is like glasses on our nose through which we see everything (§103, p. 50e). We take the possibility of comparison, which impresses us, as the perception of a highly general state of affairs. A note cites Faraday: "Water is one individual thing" (§104, p. 50e).

When we believe the ideal must be found in real language, we become dissatisfied with ordinary "sentences" and "words". We rack our brains over the "real sign" (§105, p. 51e). We feel as if we had to "repair a torn spider's web with our fingers" (§106, p. 51e). The more closely we examine actual language, the greater the conflict with our requirement. "The crystalline purity of logic" was not something he had discovered, but a requirement. On slippery ice there is no friction, so the conditions are ideal but we cannot walk. "Back to the rough ground!" (§107, p. 51e).

"Proposition" and "language" lack the formal unity he imagined. They are "a family of structures more or less akin". Does logic lose its rigour? Not by bargaining rigour out of it. The preconception can only be removed by turning the inquiry around on the pivot of our real need (§108, p. 51e). In a note on §108 (b)–(d), philosophy of logic speaks of sentences and words as we do in ordinary life, e.g. "That is a Chinese sentence". We speak of the spatial and temporal phenomenon, not of a non-spatial entity. We talk of words as we talk of chess pieces when stating rules of movement, not physical properties. "What is a word really?" is analogous to "What is a piece in chess?" (p. 52e).

*Intuition note.* The "perfect order" of logic is a demand we bring to language. It is not something discovered in language, and the demand pulls us away from how words are actually used.

### §§109–133 — Philosophy (pp. 52e–57e)
Considerations must not be scientific, theoretical or hypothetical. "All explanation must disappear, and description alone must take its place". The description gets its purpose from philosophical problems. These problems are not empirical, but are solved through insight into the workings of our language and by assembling what we have long known. "Philosophy is a struggle against the bewitchment of our understanding by the resources of our language" (§109, p. 52e).

The view that language or thinking is unique "proves to be a superstition (not a mistake!)", produced by grammatical illusions (§110, p. 52e). Problems from misinterpreting our forms have the character of depth. Wittgenstein asks why we feel a grammatical joke to be deep (§111, pp. 52e–53e). A simile absorbed into our forms produces a false appearance: "But this isn't how it is!" — "Yet this is how it has to be!" (§112, p. 53e). Repeating "This is how it is" gives the feeling that we could grasp the essence (§113, p. 53e). He quotes Tractatus 4.5, "The general form of propositions is: This is how things are". He says one thinks one is tracing nature, but is "merely tracing round the frame through which we look at it" (§114, p. 53e). "A picture held us captive". It lay in our language, which seemed to repeat it to us (§115, p. 53e).

When philosophers use words like "knowledge", "being", "object", "I", "proposition", "name", we should ask whether the word is ever used so in its home language. "What we do is to bring words back from their metaphysical to their everyday use" (§116, p. 53e). He rejects the picture of meaning as an aura the word brings along and retains in every kind of use. If someone says the sentence "This is here" makes sense to him, he should ask in what special circumstances it is actually used; there it does make sense (§117, p. 53e).

The investigation seems to destroy everything great, but it only destroys houses of cards and clears the ground (§118, p. 54e). Philosophy's results are the discovery of plain nonsense and the "bumps" the understanding gets from running against the limits of language (§119, p. 54e).

We must speak ordinary language about language. Explanations already use "language full-blown", so we can only say externalities about it. Your questions were framed in this language, so scruples are misunderstandings. People think of meaning as a thing of the same kind as the word, as the cow one can buy is to the money; Wittgenstein sets against this, on the other hand: money, and what can be done with it (§120, p. 54e). Philosophy speaking of the word "philosophy" is not "second-order", just as orthography dealing with the word "orthography" is not (§121, p. 54e).

A main source of misunderstanding is that we lack an overview of the use of words. A **surveyable representation** produces understanding by "seeing connections", so finding intermediate links matters. Wittgenstein asks whether this is a "Weltanschauung" (§122, pp. 54e–55e). A philosophical problem has the form "I don't know my way about" (§123, p. 55e).

Philosophy must not interfere in any way with the actual use of language, so it can in the end only describe it; for it cannot justify it either. "It leaves everything as it is", including mathematics. A "leading problem of mathematical logic" is for us a problem of mathematics like any other (§124, p. 55e). Its business is not to resolve contradictions by mathematical discovery, but to survey the state of mathematics that troubles us. We lay down rules, and following them things do not turn out as assumed, so we are "entangled in our own rules". This throws light on "meaning something": "That's not the way I meant it". The civic status of a contradiction is the philosophical problem (§125, p. 55e). Philosophy puts everything before us and explains or deduces nothing, since what is hidden does not interest us (§126, p. 55e). The philosopher's work is "marshalling recollections for a particular purpose" (§127, p. 55e). If someone were to advance theses in philosophy, it would never be possible to debate them, because everyone would agree to them (§128, p. 56e). The most important aspects of things are hidden by simplicity and familiarity (§129, p. 56e).

Simple language-games are not first approximations that ignore friction. They are objects of comparison that throw light on our language by similarities and dissimilarities (§130, p. 56e). We can avoid unfairness or vacuity in our assertions only by presenting the model as what it is, an object of comparison or yardstick, not a preconception to which reality must correspond; he adds that we fall into dogmatism easily in philosophy (§131, p. 56e). We want an order for a particular purpose, one of many, not "the" order. This may look like reform, but practical terminological reform is not the issue. The confusions arise when language is "idling", not when it is working (§132, p. 56e). We do not want to refine or complete the system of rules. The clarity wanted is complete clarity, which means the problems should completely disappear. "The real discovery is the one that enables me to break off philosophizing when I want to." A method is shown by examples, and the series can be broken off. Problems, not a single problem, are solved. A note adds that there are different methods, "different therapies" (§133, pp. 56e–57e).

### §§134–137 — "This is how things are" and the concept of a proposition (pp. 57e–59e)
"This is how things are" is itself an English sentence with subject and predicate, and we got it from everyday language, where it stands for some statement. It is used as a propositional schema or variable, as "such-and-such is the case" or the letter "p" could be. No one calls "p" the general form of propositions. To say it agrees or disagrees with reality would be nonsense. This shows that one feature of our concept of a proposition is "sounding like one" (§134, p. 57e).

We do have a concept of a proposition, as we do of "game": asked what one is, we give examples, including an inductive series of propositions. Wittgenstein compares the concept of number (§135, p. 57e).

Giving "This is how things are" as the general form amounts to saying a proposition is whatever can be true or false:

$$\text{'}p\text{' is true} = p, \qquad \text{'}p\text{' is false} = \text{not-}p.$$

To say a proposition is whatever can be true or false amounts to saying we call something a proposition if in our language we apply the calculus of truth functions to it (§136, p. 58e). It looks as if the concept "true" determined what a proposition is, as a cog-wheel engages. Wittgenstein calls this a bad picture, like "The chess king is the piece that one puts in check". It means only that in our chess only the king is put in check. Likewise "only a proposition can be true" says only that we predicate "true"/"false" of what we call propositions. A proposition is determined partly by rules of sentence formation and partly by the use of the sign in the language-game. "True/false" may be a constituent of that game, as check belongs to our concept of the chess king, but it does not "fit" the concept. A game where pawns were checked would be uninteresting, stupid or too complicated (§136, p. 58e).

**§137 (pp. 58e–59e).** "Who or what …?" finds the subject of a sentence much as saying the alphabet up to K finds the next letter L. In that sense "true" and "false" fit propositions. A child could be taught: ask whether you can say "is true" after it, or put "This is how things are:" in front of it.

### §§138–142 — Understanding and use (pp. 59e–61e)
**Opening questions (§138):** can the meaning of a word fit the sense of a sentence, or the meaning of one word fit another? Of course, if the meaning is the use we make of the word, it makes no sense to speak of such fitting; but we grasp the meaning at a stroke, and what we grasp is surely something different from a use extended in time. A note asks whether one must know whether one understands a word, since one may think one does and then find one did not (p. 59e).

When someone says "cube", can the whole use come before my mind? Yes, but meaning is also determined by use. How can what is present in an instant fit a use? If a picture of a cube comes to mind, an imagined objector says that pointing at a triangular prism and calling it a cube does not fit the picture. Wittgenstein replies "But doesn't it fit?" and says it is easy to imagine a method of projection according to which the picture does fit. The picture suggested one use, but another was possible (§139, pp. 59e–60e). Two notes on this remark: (a) the apt word "does not show the existence of a Something", although we choose words as between drawings; (b) a picture of an old man on a steep path might equally show him sliding downhill (p. 60e).

What was my mistake in thinking the picture forced a use? We might say there is psychological, not logical, compulsion. The argument reminded us of other processes we should sometimes be prepared to call "applying the picture of a cube". The "belief that the picture forced a particular application" consisted in only one case occurring to us. The same thing may be in our minds when we hear the word and yet the application differ. Wittgenstein thinks we would deny that it has the same meaning both times (§140, pp. 60e–61e).

If the method of projection comes before my mind, as a schema, different applications of that schema are again imaginable. We accept two kinds of criteria for "the method comes before his mind": the picture he visualizes, and the application he makes of it over time. Whether the picture is imagined or drawn is inessential. A clash is possible in so far as the picture makes us expect a different use, because people in general apply it so. He says: we have here a normal case and abnormal cases (§141, p. 61e). §142 begins: only in normal cases is the use clearly laid out in advance, and "the more abnormal the case, the more doubtful it" (§142, p. 61e; the unit ends mid-sentence).

*Intuition note.* A picture in the mind does not by itself fix how to use a word, because other applications of it can be imagined. Two kinds of criteria are accepted for whether a method comes before someone's mind: the picture he visualizes and the application he makes of it over time.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four objects with features from the set {a, b, c, d, e}:
- X1 = {a, b, c}, X2 = {b, c, d}, X3 = {c, d, e}, X4 = {d, e, a}.

Count how many objects have each feature: a → X1, X4 (2); b → X1, X2 (2); c → X1, X2, X3 (3); d → X2, X3, X4 (3); e → X3, X4 (2). No feature is held by all four, so the intersection is empty.

Yet neighbouring pairs share features: X1 and X2 share {b, c}; X2 and X3 share {c, d}; X3 and X4 share {d, e}; X4 and X1 share {a}. All four form one "family" through overlaps. A fifth object X5 = {e, a, b} shares {e, a} with X4 and {a, b} with X1, so it joins the family without any rule about a core feature. The only unifying "common property" is the disjunction "a or b or c or d or e", which §67 calls playing with a word.

## Concepts
- [[family resemblance]] — a concept's members linked by overlapping, criss-crossing similarities, with no one feature common to all (§§66–67, pp. 36e–37e)
- [[language-game]] — simple language-games are offered as "objects of comparison", not as first approximations for a future regimentation of language; §71 also speaks of the language-game with the word "game" (§71, p. 38e; §130, p. 56e)
- [[meaning as use]] — §138 asks what follows if meaning is the use we make of a word, and sets against it the sense that we grasp meaning at a stroke; §141 accepts the application made over time as one criterion (§138, p. 59e; §141, p. 61e)
- [[rule-following problem]] — a rule, like a signpost, sometimes leaves room for doubt and sometimes not, and that is an empirical rather than a philosophical point (§85, p. 44e)
- [[analysis]] — substituting forms of expression to clear misunderstandings; the idea of a "final analysis" is described as something it may come to look as if there were (§§90–91, p. 47e)
- [[picture theory of meaning]] — the unit cites Tractatus passages on the "crystal" of logic (5.5563) and on the general form of propositions (4.5) and examines the pull they exert; the phrase "picture theory" does not occur in these pages (§97, p. 49e; §114, p. 53e)
- [[blurred concept]] (new) — a concept with no drawn boundary, still usable; sharper boundaries are drawn for a purpose (§§68–71, pp. 37e–38e)
- [[goal-relative exactness]] (new) — "exact"/"inexact" praise or reproach relative to a goal; no single ideal of exactness (§88, p. 46e)
- [[surveyable representation]] (new) — an overview of the use of words that gives understanding by "seeing connections" (§122, p. 54e)
- [[philosophy as description]] (new) — philosophy neither explains nor justifies, and "leaves everything as it is" (§§109, 124–126, pp. 52e–55e)
- [[object of comparison]] (new) — a model offered as a yardstick, not as a preconception about reality (§§130–131, p. 56e)
- [[normal and abnormal cases]] (new) — use is clearly laid out in advance only in normal cases (§§141–142, p. 61e)

## Canonical instances
- [[game-example]] — "game" as the model family concept, with no common feature (§66, p. 36e)
- [[thread-simile]] — strength from overlapping fibres, not one fibre running through (§67, p. 36e)
- [[moses-example]] — a name's meaning as a loose cluster of descriptions ("props") (§79, pp. 41e–42e)
- [[signpost-example]] — a rule leaves room for doubt sometimes, not always (§85, p. 44e)
- [[chess-king-check]] — "true/false" belong to the concept of a proposition as check belongs to the king (§136, p. 58e)
- [[cube-picture-example]] — a mental picture does not force one use (§§139–141, pp. 59e–61e)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] family resemblance (overlapping features, no core) ↔ graph theory: a connected network where no node or edge is shared by all paths — each object is linked to others through shared features.
- [structural-analogy] blurred concepts ↔ fuzzy-set or prototype models of categories: membership comes in degrees and boundaries are chosen for a purpose.
- [metaphor] "exact vs inexact depends on the goal" ↔ measurement error and tolerance in applied science: precision is chosen to fit the use.
- [metaphor] language-games as objects of comparison, not first approximations ↔ models in science used as yardsticks, not as claims that reality matches.

## Questions this raises
- If a concept has no boundary or common essence, what makes a word misapplied? Does the "normal/abnormal case" idea (§§141–142) fully answer this?
- If philosophy only "leaves everything as it is" (§124), how can philosophical clarity change anyone's thinking? What counts as success (§133)?
- How does the cluster view of names in §79 compare with the "one description" view it criticises?
- Does "true/false" fitting propositions only as check fits the king (§136) make "proposition" depend entirely on convention?
