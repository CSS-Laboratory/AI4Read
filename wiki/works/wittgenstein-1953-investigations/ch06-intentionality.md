# Intentionality, expectation, justification, meaning and language (Philosophical Investigations §§427–546)

## L0 — One line
Thought, wish, expectation, order and understanding can seem to reach what is not there through a special mental process, and in these remarks Wittgenstein keeps turning to how the words and signs are used.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
These remarks ask how a thought, wish, expectation or order can reach something that may not exist, and what meaning and understanding are. Wittgenstein sets the puzzling picture beside the way the words are used. Expectation and fulfilment make contact in language, and a sign, the text says, lives in use. Justification by experience comes to an end. Grammar describes the use of signs and does not explain it. "Understanding" covers several related uses, some akin to understanding a musical theme. Along the way the remarks question pictures of meaning as an inner process.

**Prerequisites:** none mathematical. Helpful: the idea that a word's meaning is tied to its use, and the term "language-game" (an activity of using words; named at §486).

## L2 — Key ideas
- A thought does not strike us as mysterious while we think. The question "how was that possible?" comes retrospectively, and then it seems we have captured reality (§428, p. 135e). The agreement of thought and reality consists in how we use words such as *red* (§429, p. 135e).
- A sign by itself seems dead; the text says that in use it lives, then asks whether use is its breath (§432, p. 135e). Adding further signs to an order raises the question "in what signs do we understand?" (§433, p. 136e).
- Expectation and fulfilment make contact in language. The same explanations of the words serve *I expect he is coming* and *He is coming* (§§444–445, p. 138e).
- Wittgenstein resists the idea that using a sentence means imagining something for every word. One operates with words, and a picture may follow (§449, p. 139e).
- If "reasons" are taken to be what a person told himself, then statements about past experience are what we call reasons for expecting the future. The standard of a good reason has no grounds, and justification by experience comes to an end (§§480–485, pp. 143e–144e).
- Grammar only describes the use of signs. Calling a combination of words senseless draws a boundary and withdraws it from circulation (§§496–500, pp. 146e–147e).
- We speak of understanding where a sentence can be replaced by another and where it cannot (like a musical theme). Wittgenstein says these uses make up his concept of understanding (§§531–532, p. 152e).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The text has no headings in this stretch, so the sections below group remarks by number.

### §§427–437: How can a thought reach reality? Signs, orders, and what evades our grasp
- **Mind-reading picture (§427, p. 135e).** Saying *I did not know what was going on in his head* concerns thought processes, not brain processes. The picture should be taken seriously: we would like to see into his head. Yet we only mean what we ordinarily mean by wanting to know what he is thinking. So there is a vivid picture, which expresses something mental, and a use that apparently contradicts it.
- **Retrospective strangeness (§428, p. 135e).** A thought does not strike us as mysterious while we are thinking, but only when we say, as it were retrospectively, *How was that possible?* How could a thought deal with this very object? It seems to us as if we had captured reality with the thought.
- **Agreement of thought and reality (§429, p. 135e).** It consists in this: if I say falsely that something is red, it is red that it isn't. It also consists in this: to explain *red* in *That is not red*, I point at something red.
- **Ruler objection (§430, p. 135e).** A quoted voice says a ruler put against an object does not say how long it is. In itself, the voice is tempted to say, it is dead and achieves nothing a thought can achieve. Wittgenstein compares this with imagining that the outward form is the essential thing about a living human being, making a lump of wood into that form, and being abashed at the lifeless block.
- **Gap objection (§431, p. 135e).** Quoted voices say there is a gap between an order and its execution that has to be closed by the process of understanding. Only in that process, they say, does the order mean that we are to do this; the order itself is nothing but sounds and ink-marks.
- **Life of a sign (§432, p. 135e).** Every sign by itself seems dead. What gives it life? The text answers: "In use it lives." It then asks whether use is its breath.
- **Regress of signs (§§433–434, pp. 135e–136e).** When we give an order, it may look as if the ultimate thing sought had to remain unexpressed. I demonstrate the arm movement I want. This seems unambiguous until we ask how he knows that this is the movement to make, or what to do with any signs I give him. If I add pointing and encouraging gestures, it looks as if the order were beginning to stammer, as if the sign were trying to induce understanding in us. But if we now understand it, in what signs do we do so? The gesture, one would like to say, tries to prefigure but cannot.
- **How does a sentence represent? (§435, p. 136e).** The answer might be: don't you know? you see it when you use one; nothing is concealed. One would like to retort that it all goes by so quickly and one would like to see it more fully laid out.
- **Dead end (§436, p. 136e).** Wittgenstein says it is easy to get into a dead end in philosophizing. There one believes that the difficulty consists in having to describe phenomena that evade our grasp, such as the fleeting present experience, and finds ordinary language too crude.
- **The "must" (§437, p. 136e).** A wish seems already to know what will or would satisfy it, and a proposition or thought to know what makes it true, even when there is nothing there. Wittgenstein asks whence this determining of what is not yet there comes, this despotic demand (the hardness of the logical must).

*Intuition note:* We feel that a thought must somehow already hold its object. According to §428, the puzzlement comes when we look back and ask how it was possible. The remarks then turn to use and to the point that, in asking how a sentence represents, nothing is concealed (§435).

### §§438–453: Wish, expectation, satisfaction; negation; imagining
- **Unsatisfied (§§438–439, pp. 136e–137e).** A quoted remark says a plan, as such, is unsatisfied, like a wish, expectation or conjecture. The speaker explains that an expectation is unsatisfied because it is an expectation of something, and a belief because it is an opinion that something real, outside the process of believing, is the case. Wittgenstein then asks in what sense wishes and the like can be called unsatisfied. What is our prototype of non-satisfaction? A hollow space? Wouldn't that be a metaphor too? Or is it something like hunger, a feeling? In a particular system of expressions we can stipulate that a hollow cylinder is called an unsatisfied cylinder and the solid cylinder that fills it its satisfaction.
- **Wanting an apple (§440, p. 137e).** *I'd like an apple* does not mean that I believe an apple will quell my feeling of non-satisfaction. The utterance expresses not a wish but non-satisfaction.
- **Wishing (§441, p. 137e).** By nature and by training we are predisposed to express wishes in certain circumstances. In this game the question whether I know what I wish before the wish is fulfilled cannot arise at all. That an event stops my wishing does not mean it fulfils the wish; perhaps I would not have been satisfied. The word "wish" is also used in another way (*I don't know myself what I wish for*). To the question whether I know what I long for before I get it, the answer given is: if I have learned to talk, I do.
- **Bang (§442, pp. 137e–138e).** I see someone aiming a gun and say *I expect a bang*. The shot is fired. Did the bang somehow already exist in my expectation, or does the expectation agree with the event in some other respect, so that the noise merely supervened as an accidental property? The text replies that if the noise had not occurred, the expectation would not have been fulfilled; the noise fulfilled it and was not an accompaniment like a second guest. Then, it asks, what was extra? Wasn't I expecting the whole shot? The sentence *The bang was not as loud as I had expected* is met with the question *Then was there a louder bang in your expectation?* The text leaves it as a question.
- **Red imagined vs. seen (§443, p. 138e).** The objection is that the red you imagine is not the red you see, so how can you say it is what you imagined? Wittgenstein points to an analogous case: *Here is a red patch* and *Here there isn't a red patch*. The word *red* occurs in both, so it cannot indicate the presence of something red.
- **Same sense (§444, p. 138e).** One may feel that *he is coming* is used in a different sense in *I expect he is coming* than in *He is coming*. Wittgenstein asks how I could then say my expectation had been fulfilled. The same ostensive explanations of *he* and *is coming* would go for both sentences. His coming looks like a door opening and someone walking in; my expecting looks like pacing and glancing at the clock. The two have not the slightest similarity, so how can the same words describe them? When I say *I expect he'll come in* as I pace, there is a similarity, but of what kind?
- **Result (§445, p. 138e):** "It is in language that an expectation and its fulfilment make contact."
- **Negation (§§446–447, p. 138e–139e).** Wittgenstein says it would be odd to say that a process, or a red patch, looks different when it is there than when it is not, adding that language abstracts from the difference because it speaks of a red patch whether it is there or not. He then notes the feeling that the negation of a proposition has first, in a certain sense, to make that proposition true in order to negate it. His parenthetical remark: the assertion of the negating proposition contains the proposition that is negated, but not the assertion of it.
- **Dreams and pain (§448, p. 139e).** An interlocutor says that to say *I did not dream* I must still know where to look for a dream. Wittgenstein asks whether that means I felt a hint of a dream that marked the place a dream would have occupied. He asks the same about *I have no pain in my arm*, and asks in what sense my painless state contains the possibility of pain. To someone who says that for *pain* to have a meaning pain must be recognized as such when it occurs, one can reply that this is not more necessary than that the absence of pain be recognized.
- **Images (§449, p. 139e).** An interlocutor asks whether I mustn't know what it would be like to be in pain. One cannot shake off the idea that using a sentence consists in imagining something for every word. One fails to bear in mind that one calculates, operates, with words and in due course transforms them into this or that picture. It is as if one believed that a written order for a cow had to be accompanied by a mental image of a cow if it was not to lose its sense.
- **Imagining and mimicking (§§450–451, p. 139e).** Knowing what someone looks like can be being able to imagine it, but also being able to mimic it. Wittgenstein asks whether one need imagine it in order to mimic it, and whether mimicking is not just as good as imagining. For the order *Imagine a red circle here*, he asks whether understanding it means knowing what it is like for it to have been carried out, or being able to imagine that.
- **Seeing an expectation (§§452–453, pp. 139e–140e).** Wittgenstein voices the wish to say that if someone could see an expectation, the mental process, he'd see what was expected. He answers that this is just how it is: whoever sees the expression of an expectation sees what is expected. To say that someone perceives an expectation makes no sense, unless it means that he perceives its manifestations.

*Intuition note:* The text does not look for a ghostly copy of the event inside the expectation. It points out that the same words, with the same explanations, are used for expectation and event, and it says they make contact in language (§§444–445).

### §§454–465: Meaning, pointing, orders, looking for what is absent
- **The arrow (§454, p. 140e).** A quoted voice asks how an arrow points, and says it seems to carry something extraneous within it. The same voice says that not the dead line, only a mental thing, the meaning, can do that. Wittgenstein says this is both true and false. The arrow points only in the application a living creature makes of it, and this pointing is not a hocus-pocus that only the mind can perform.
- **Meaning as going towards (§§455–457, p. 140e).** We are inclined to say that when we mean something there is no dead picture, but it is like going towards someone. The quoted view says that when one means something, it is oneself that means, so one rushes ahead and cannot observe one's rushing ahead. The text assents to this ("Indeed not") and to the comparison with going towards someone (§457).
- **Orders (§§458–459, p. 140e).** *An order orders its own execution* seems to mean that the order knows its execution before it is there. But that was a grammatical proposition: if an order runs *Do such-and-such*, then doing such-and-such is called executing the order. We translate an order at one time into a sentence, at another into a demonstration, at another into action.
- **Justification test (§460, p. 140e).** Wittgenstein asks whether a justification could run: you said *Bring me a yellow flower*, this flower gave me a feeling of satisfaction, so I brought it. He suggests the reply would be that I did not tell you to bring a flower that would give you that feeling.
- **Prophecy (§461, p. 141e).** Wittgenstein asks in what sense an order anticipates its fulfilment: by ordering just that which later is carried out? This would have to run "which later is carried out, or again is not carried out", and that says nothing. An objector says a wish still determines the theme of a fact, whether or not the fact fulfils it. Wittgenstein says we are, as it were, surprised not at someone's knowing the future but at his being able to prophesy at all, right or wrong. A prophecy knows nothing of the future.
- **Looking for him (§462, p. 141e).** I can look for him when he is not there, but not hang him. To *he must be around, if I am looking for him*, the reply is that then he must be around even if I don't find him, and even if he doesn't exist.
- **Looking in mathematics (§463, p. 141e).** To *you couldn't even have known if he was there*, Wittgenstein says the problem really does arise when one looks for something in mathematics, for example how it was possible even to look for the trisection of an angle.
- **Aim (§464, p. 141e):** "to pass from unobvious nonsense to obvious nonsense."
- **Determinacy (§465, p. 141e).** The quoted view is that an expectation is so made that whatever happens either accords with it or not. Asked whether the sense in which an expectation would be satisfied is determined, whatever happens, Wittgenstein replies yes, unless the expression of the expectation is indefinite, for example if it contains a disjunction of different possibilities.

*Intuition note:* §458 treats *an order orders its own execution* as a grammatical proposition: it says what is called executing the order. Looking for someone who is absent (§462) and prophecy (§461) are used against the idea that something must already be there.

### §§466–490: Thinking, belief, reasons, justification
- **Does thinking pay? (§§466–471, pp. 141e–142e).** Wittgenstein asks why people calculate the thickness of boiler walls. It is only a fact of experience that such boilers explode less often, yet he would do anything rather than not calculate, as he would avoid putting his hand in fire after being burnt. Since we are not interested in causes, he says humans do in fact think, and a calculated boiler can still explode (§466). He asks whether man thinks because thinking pays, and compares bringing up children (§467). He then allows that thinking has been found to pay, with fewer explosions since calculations replaced hunches or were checked by a second engineer (§469). Conclusion: sometimes one thinks because it has been found to pay (§470). Often, when we suppress the question "Why?", we become aware of important facts that lead to an answer (§471).
- **Belief and fear (§§472–474, p. 142e).** The character of belief in the uniformity of nature can perhaps be seen most clearly where what is expected is something we fear. Nothing could induce me to put my hand into a flame, though it is only in the past that I have burnt myself. The belief that fire will burn me is of the same kind as the fear that it will burn me. *I shall get burnt if I put my hand in the fire* is certainty, and here we see what certainty amounts to.
- **Object and cause (§476, p. 143e).** A face that inspires fear is the object of fear but is not on that account its cause; one might say it is its target.
- **Reasons (§§477–479, p. 143e).** Wittgenstein asks whether I have reasons, and need reasons, for believing I shall burn myself. When asked, a hundred reasons present themselves, each drowning out the others. *For what reasons do you believe this?* may ask what I am now deriving the belief from, or what reasons I can give with hindsight.
- **What we call a reason (§480, p. 143e).** One could take "reasons" for a belief to mean only what a person said to himself before reaching the belief, the calculation he actually carried out. If someone then asks how previous experience can be a reason for expecting something later, the answer is to ask what general concept we have of reasons for this kind of supposition. This sort of statement about the past is simply what we call a reason for supposing that this will happen. If one is surprised at our playing such a game, Wittgenstein appeals to the effect of past experience (a burnt child fears the fire).
- **The sceptic about information (§481, pp. 143e–144e).** Wittgenstein says he would not understand someone who said information about the past couldn't convince him that something would happen in the future. He asks what such a person would call a reason or convincing. Reasons here are not propositions that logically imply what is believed. Nor is it that belief needs less than knowledge, for this is not a matter of approximating to logical consequence.
- **Good reasons (§§482–484, p. 144e).** We are misled by saying *This is a good reason, for it makes the occurrence probable*. To say this is to say that the reason comes up to a particular standard of good reasons, but the standard has no grounds. A good reason is one that looks like this (§483). One would like to say that it is a good reason only because it makes the occurrence really probable, because it really has an influence on the event, an empirical one (§484).
- **Key result (§485, p. 144e):** "Justification by experience comes to an end. If it did not, it would not be justification."
- **Inference (§486, p. 144e).** Wittgenstein asks whether a chair follows from the sense impressions I get, and whether it follows from propositions describing them, to which he answers no. He also says he makes no inference, and yet sometimes does, for instance from a photograph. That is an inference, but not one belonging to logic. An inference is a transition to an assertion and so also to corresponding behaviour. We draw consequences in deeds as well as words. Asking what justification is called here, he says: "Describe language-games!"
- **Because you tell me to (§§487–490, p. 145e).** Wittgenstein asks whether *I'm leaving the room because you tell me to* describes a connection between my action and his order or makes the connection. He asks whether the answer to *How do you know?* is *I feel it* (§487), and how I judge whether it is so, by circumstantial evidence? (§488). He directs us to ask on what occasion and for what purpose we say this, what actions accompany the words, and in what settings they are used, as with a greeting (§489). How I know that a train of thought led me to an action is a particular picture, for example of a calculation leading to a further experiment (§490).

*Intuition note:* §480 answers the question how past experience can be a reason by asking what we call a reason. §481 adds that reasons here do not logically imply what is believed, and §485 says justification by experience comes to an end.

### §§491–500: Language, grammar, sense and boundary
- **Language and what it does (§491, p. 145e).** Wittgenstein sets aside *without language we could not communicate* and says: without language we cannot influence other people in such-and-such ways, or build roads and machines. Also, without speech and writing human beings could not communicate.
- **Inventing a language (§492, p. 145e).** It can mean inventing a device for a purpose on the basis of the laws of nature, or it has the sense analogous to inventing a game. Wittgenstein says he is saying something about the grammar of the word "language", by connecting it with the grammar of "invent".
- **Cock and hens (§493, pp. 145e–146e).** Wittgenstein asks whether *the cock calls the hens by crowing* is already based on a comparison with our language, and whether we see all this differently if we imagine the crowing moving the hens by physical causation. If it were shown how *Come to me* acts on the person addressed until his leg muscles are innervated, he asks, would that sentence thereby lose the character of a sentence for us?
- **What we call "language" (§§494–495, p. 146e).** It is above all the apparatus of our word-language that we call language, and other things by analogy. I can establish by experience that a human being reacts to one sign as I want and to another not. I can direct a person who has learned only English only by using English. Here Wittgenstein is looking at learning English as adjusting a mechanism to respond to a certain kind of influence.
- **Grammar (§§496–497, p. 146e).** Grammar does not tell us how language must be constructed to fulfil its purpose. It "only describes, and in no way explains, the use of signs." Rules of grammar may be called arbitrary if that means the purpose of grammar is nothing but that of language. Wittgenstein asks what *could* means in *if our language had not this grammar, it could not express these facts*.
- **Nonsense and boundary (§§498–500, pp. 146e–147e).** The combination *Milk me sugar* may have an effect, but if the other person stares, I don't call it an order to stare. Calling a combination senseless excludes it from the sphere of language and bounds the domain of language. A boundary may be drawn for various purposes (a fence, a game, property), so drawing it does not say what it is drawn for (§499). When a sentence is called senseless, it is not its sense that is senseless; the combination is excluded from the language, withdrawn from circulation (§500).

### §§501–518: Meaning something, understanding, nonsense
- **Purpose of language (§§501–502, p. 147e).** A quoted view says the purpose of language is to express thoughts. Then, Wittgenstein asks, what thought does *It's raining* express? The question *What sense?* is compared with *What sentence?*
- **Signs enough (§§503–504, p. 147e).** When I give an order, signs are enough for me. If someone says he sees only the other's signs, Wittgenstein replies that the other too has only his signs.
- **Understanding and acting (§505, p. 147e).** The text gives an unlabelled exchange: *Must I understand an order before I can act on it?* — *Certainly, otherwise you wouldn't know what you had to do!* — *But from knowing to doing is surely a further step!* An absent-minded man turns left at the order *Right turn!*, then says *Oh! right turn* and turns right. Wittgenstein asks what struck him, an interpretation? (§506).
- **Meaning vs. saying (§507, p. 147e).** When we mean words, and don't merely say them, it seems as if something were coupled to them that otherwise would run idle.
- **a b c d (§§508–510, p. 148e).** Put *a b c d* in place of *The weather is fine*. I can't connect it with the sense without more ado, not because of association but because I am not used to using *a* in the place of *the* (compare Fahrenheit temperatures). Wittgenstein asks why *I mean this by these words* is no answer to how words describe what I see (§509). He asks us to try saying *It's cold here* and meaning *It's warm here* (§510).
- **Nonsense (§§511–512, p. 148e).** What does it mean to discover that an utterance doesn't make sense? One wants to say that a sentence that makes sense is one that one can not merely say, but also think (§511). It looks as if the language of imagining doesn't allow nonsense. Wittgenstein asks whether drawings can be nonsensical (for drawings from which bodies are to be modelled, some make sense, some not) and what it is to imagine nonsensical word-combinations (§512).
- **Equations (§513, p. 149e).** *The number of pages in my book is equal to a solution of the equation $x^3+2x-3=0$.* Or: *The number of my friends is $n$, and $n^2+2n+2=0$.* Does this sentence make sense? This cannot be seen immediately. The example shows how something can look like a sentence we understand and yet make no sense; Wittgenstein adds that this throws light on the concepts of understanding and meaning something. (Added step: $x=1$ satisfies the first equation since $1+2-3=0$; the second has no real solution because $n^2+2n+2=(n+1)^2+1>0$.)
- **I am here; the rose (§§514–515, p. 149e).** A philosopher says he understands *I am here* without calling to mind how it is used. If I say *A rose is red in the dark too*, you virtually see the red before you. Two pictures of a rose in the dark: one all black, one painted in detail. Is one right and the other wrong? Don't we talk of a white and a red rose in the dark, and nevertheless say they can't be distinguished there?
- **π (§§516–517, p. 149e).** It seems clear that we understand *Does the sequence 7777 occur in the development of π?* One may say that our understanding reaches just as far as such explanations reach. Wittgenstein asks whether we can be mistaken in thinking we understand a question. Some mathematical proofs (for example the construction of a heptagon) lead us to say we cannot imagine what we believed we could, and so to revise what counts as imaginable.
- **Socrates–Theaetetus (§518, p. 149e).** In the quoted exchange Theaetetus agrees that someone who imagines must imagine something ("Yes, he must") and that it must be something real ("Apparently"). Wittgenstein asks the same about painting, and asks what the object of painting is: the picture of the man, or the man portrayed.

### §§519–527: Propositions as pictures; understanding
- **Order as picture (§519, p. 150e).** One wants to say that an order is a picture of the action carried out on it, and also of the action that is to be carried out.
- **Possibility (§520, p. 150e).** An objector says that a proposition, even as a picture of a possible state of affairs, can at most do what a painting or film does and cannot present what is not the case, so what is called logically possible depends wholly on grammar. *But surely that is arbitrary!* Wittgenstein asks whether it is. It is not every sentence-like formation that we know how to do something with, nor every technique that has a use in our life. When in philosophy we are tempted to count something quite useless as a proposition, that is often because we have not reflected sufficiently on its application.
- **Chemical possibility (§521, p. 150e).** One might perhaps call a combination chemically possible if a formula with the right valencies existed (for example H–O–O–O–H). Such a combination need not exist, but even the formula HO$_2$ cannot have less than no combination corresponding to it in reality.
- **Portrait or genre-picture (§§522–524, p. 150e).** A proposition may be compared to a portrait or to a genre-picture, and both comparisons make sense. A genre-picture tells me something though I don't believe the people in it exist. *A picture tells me itself* is what Wittgenstein would like to say: its telling consists in its own structure, forms and colours; he asks what *A musical theme tells me itself* would mean (§523). That pictures and fictions give us pleasure is to be taken as a remarkable fact, not as a matter of course (§524).
- **Context (§525, p. 151e).** Of *After he had said this, he left her as he did the day before*, Wittgenstein says that if it stood alone he'd say he doesn't know what it is about. Still, he would know how it might be used, and could invent a context for it.
- **Understanding a picture (§526, p. 151e).** Here too there is understanding and not understanding, and the expressions may mean various kinds of thing: not seeing solid objects, not knowing what the objects are for, or not understanding their arrangement.
- **Sentence and musical theme (§527, p. 151e).** Understanding a sentence in language is much more akin to understanding a theme in music than one may think. Asked why this pattern of intensity and tempo, one would like to say *Because I know what it all means*, but Wittgenstein says he would not be able to say what it means. As an explanation he could compare it with something of the same rhythm. There are very different kinds of justification for such comparisons.

### §§528–546: The "soul" of words; hearing-as; feeling and meaning
- **Imaginary languages (§§528–530, pp. 151e–152e).** One might imagine people with something not altogether unlike a language: vocal gestures without vocabulary or grammar. The meaning of the sounds in that case is compared with what it is in music, though Wittgenstein does not say such a language would have to be compared with music. There might also be a language in whose use the soul of the words played no part, where for example we had no objection to replacing one word by an arbitrarily invented one.
- **Understanding (§§531–533, pp. 152e).** We speak of understanding a sentence in the sense in which it can be replaced by another that says the same, and also in the sense in which it cannot (as a musical theme cannot be replaced by another). In the one case, the thought is what is common to different sentences; in the other, it is something expressed only by these words in these positions (understanding a poem). Wittgenstein says he would rather not say "understanding" has two meanings. These kinds of use make up its meaning, make up his concept of understanding, because he wants to apply the word to all this (§532). To see how the second sense is explained, ask how one leads someone to understand a poem or theme (§533).
- **Hearing as (§§534–535, p. 152e).** Hearing a word as having this meaning: how curious that there should be such a thing! Heard this way, the sentence is the beginning of a transition to other sentences, pictures, actions. What happens when we learn to feel the ending of a church mode as an ending?
- **Faces and aspects (§§536–537, pp. 152e–153e).** *I can think of this timid face as courageous too* means an aspect of the face itself, not that I can imagine its owner saving a life. The reinterpretation of a facial expression can be compared with reinterpreting a chord in music, heard as a modulation into this or that key. One can say *I read timidity in this face*; at any rate, the timidity does not seem merely associated with the face but alive in the features. When courage is read into the face, Wittgenstein asks what fits what here.
- **French adjectives (§538, p. 153e).** A related case: Germans are surprised that in French the predicative adjective agrees in gender with the substantive and explain it by saying the French mean *der Mensch ist ein guter*.
- **Kind or malicious smile (§539, p. 153e).** Taking a picture's smile as kind or malicious, I often imagine a spatial and temporal context of kindness or malice. If no special circumstances reverse my interpretation, I conceive the smile as kind, call it kind, and react accordingly.
- **Thinking without language (§§540–542, pp. 153e–154e).** Wittgenstein asks whether it is peculiar that without the institution of language I couldn't think that it will soon stop raining. A man points at the sky and utters unintelligible words, explains them as *Thank heaven it'll soon stop raining*, then comes to himself and says it was nonsense, though it had seemed to him a sentence in a language he knew. Did he understand it as he said it? What did his understanding consist in (§541)? A criterion for the words' having felt familiar is his later saying so; Wittgenstein adds that we should not say the feel of the words in a known language is of a quite particular kind (§542).
- **Feeling and meaning (§§543–546, p. 154e).** A cry or laugh may be called full of meaning, which roughly means that much can be gathered from it (§543). In *Oh, if only he'd come!* the feeling gives the words meaning, but does it give the individual words theirs? One could also say it gave the words truth (§544). Does the feeling give *hope* its meaning? Perhaps it gives the word its ring. If the feeling gives the word its meaning, "meaning" amounts to that which matters. Is hope a feeling? (§545). The unit stops mid-sentence at §546: words can be wrung from us, like...

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A small language-game with four cards: card 1 red, card 2 blue, card 3 red, card 4 green.
- **Order: *Bring a red card.*** The practice fixes what counts as executing it: cards 1 and 3, so 2 of 4 cards. This parallels §458, where doing what the order says is called executing it.
- **Order: *Bring a card that is not red.*** *Red* is explained by pointing at cards 1 and 3, as in §429. Cards 2 and 4 fulfil this order: 2 of 4. Check: 2 + 2 = 4. The word *red* occurs in both orders, though in the second no red card is brought.
- **Expectation.** I say *I expect a red card* before a card is drawn. If card 3 is drawn, the expectation is fulfilled; if card 2, it is not. The same words *the card is red* are used again in the report, as in §444.
- **Indefinite expectation.** *I expect a red or blue card* contains a disjunction (§465). It is fulfilled by cards 1, 2 and 3, so by 3 of 4, and not by card 4. Unlike *a red card*, it leaves open which colour will count.

## Questions this raises
- If expectation and fulfilment make contact in language (§445), what marks a good criterion for *the expectation was fulfilled* in hard cases?
