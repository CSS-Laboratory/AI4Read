# Understanding, reading, following a rule (§§143–242)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein suggests that understanding a rule is not a mental process behind correct use and is not fixed by interpretation; it shows itself in a trained, customary practice of applying the rule, where justifications end in "this is simply what I do".

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What does it mean to understand a series or a rule, and what makes a step correct? Wittgenstein looks at understanding, reading and being guided, and does not find one inner process or experience that is the thing itself. He then argues that no interpretation by itself determines how a rule is applied, and that following a rule is a practice resting on training and custom. Where justifications give out, one acts blindly. This matters because meaning and correctness are tied to use, training, custom and agreement in form of life, not to private inner states.

**Prerequisites:** arithmetic series and simple algebraic formulae; the idea of a language-game (earlier in the book); the contrast between a cause and a reason.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The test of understanding a series is how the pupil goes on. No limit can be stated for how far he must continue correctly before we may say he has mastered it, and the effect of any further explanation depends on his reaction (§§143–146, pp. 62e–64e).
- Understanding is not a mental process, in the sense in which there are processes characteristic of understanding; it is the circumstances that warrant saying one understands. In the case W. describes, *Now I can go on* may be called a signal, judged by what the person goes on to do (§§151–155, §180, pp. 65e–67e, 79e).
- *Reading* is used for a family of cases with different criteria in different circumstances. W. finds no single feature common to all cases, and no experience of reading or of being guided that he is willing to call the essence (§§156–178, pp. 67e–78e).
- The picture that meaning an order already takes all its steps is traced to our ways of talking about meaning, formulae, machines and possibility, which we misunderstand (§§185–197, pp. 80e–86e).
- An interpretation gives no support to what it interprets. W. says there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation and shows itself in following and going against it. Following a rule is a practice, and thinking one follows a rule is not following one (§§198–202, pp. 86e–88e).
- Words like *same* and *regular* are taught by examples, exercises and reactions of agreement or rejection. Reasons give out, and then one acts without reasons (§§208–217, pp. 88e–91e).
- Agreement in judgements, as well as in definitions, is required for communication by language. W. calls it agreement in form of life, not in opinions (§§241–242, p. 94e).

### §§143–149: teaching a series; is understanding a state?
**Teaching the series.** A gives orders; B has to write series of signs by a formation rule. The first series is the natural numbers in the decimal system. Series are written for the pupil and he copies them. At first we guide his hand through 0 to 9; then communication depends on his going on by himself. There are normal and abnormal reactions. If he writes the figures in random order, communication stops. Occasional mistakes in the order differ from that only in frequency. With a systematic mistake, such as copying 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5… as 1, 0, 3, 2, 5, 4…, we are almost tempted to say he has understood us wrongly. There is no sharp line between random and systematic mistakes. Perhaps we can wean him from the systematic mistake, or accept his way and teach the normal one as a variant; here too his ability to learn may come to an end (§143, pp. 62e–63e). W. asks whether the remark that the ability to learn may come to an end reports his own experience, and answers: of course not (even if he has had such experience). He wants to put a picture before the reader; its acceptance consists in the reader's being inclined to regard a given case differently (§144, p. 63e).

**No cut-off point.** The pupil writes 0–9 to our satisfaction only if he is often successful, not if he is right once in a hundred attempts. We then draw his attention to the recurrence of the first series in the units and in the tens. At some point he continues by himself, or he does not: the effect of further explanation depends on his reaction. Once he continues correctly, that is as we do, we say he has mastered the system. But how far must he continue for us rightly to say that? You cannot state a limit (§145, p. 63e).

**Understanding as a source?** *Objector:* to have got the system cannot consist in continuing up to some number, which is only applying one's understanding; understanding itself is a state which is the source of the correct use (§146, p. 63e). *W.* asks whether one is thinking of the derivation of a series from its algebraic formula, or at least of something analogous, and says we are back where we were. A formula can be applied in more than one way, and every mode of application can again be formulated algebraically, which gets us no further. The application is still a criterion of understanding (§146, p. 64e).

**Knowing the application.** *Objector:* I know which series I mean, however far I have developed it, because the series is infinite and my development finite (§147). *W.* asks what this knowledge consists in: is it there always, as with the alphabet and the multiplication table, or is it a state of consciousness or a process such as thinking of something (§148)? If knowing the ABC is called a state of the mind, one is thinking of a state of an apparatus (perhaps the brain) that explains the manifestations. Such a state is called a disposition. But speaking of a state of the mind here is not unobjectionable, since there would have to be two different criteria: the structure of the apparatus, and its effects. Using *conscious* and *unconscious* for the contrast between a state of consciousness and a disposition covers up a grammatical difference (§149, p. 64e).

**Grammar of "understand".** We call dejection, excitement and pain mental states and say they last the whole day or uninterruptedly. We also say *Since yesterday I have understood this word*; but *uninterruptedly*? W. grants that one can speak of an interruption of understanding and asks in what cases; compare *When did your pains get less?* with *When did you stop understanding that word?* Note (b) asks when you can play chess: all the time, or just while making a move? (§149 notes (a), (b), p. 65e).

> *Intuition note.* We are tempted to say that understanding is a state behind the pupil's performance and the source of its correct use. W. replies that the application is still a criterion of understanding and that appealing to a formula only pushes the question back. Here he names no inner thing as the understanding.

### §§150–155: *Now I know how to go on*
*Know* is closely related to *can* and *understand*, to having mastered a technique (§150). There is also the use *Now I know!*, *Now I can do it!*, *Now I understand!* B watches A write 1, 5, 11, 19, 29 and says *Now I can go on!* W. asks what occurred in that moment; various things may have. B may try $a_n = n^2 + n - 1$ after 19, and the next number confirms it. Or, with vague thoughts and tension, he asks for the series of differences, finds 4, 6, 8, 10, and says he can go on. Or he says he knows the series and continues. Or he says nothing and continues, perhaps with a feeling of *That's easy!* (§151, pp. 65e–66e). W. asks whether these processes are understanding. The formula's occurring to B cannot simply be what *he understands* means, since it is conceivable that it occurs and he does not understand; understanding must have more to it than that, and more than any of those concomitant processes (§152, p. 66e). Trying to get hold of a mental process of understanding hidden behind the visible ones does not work: even if something happened in all cases, why should that be the understanding? How can it be hidden, if I said *Now I understand* because I did? If hidden, how do I know what to look for? W. says he is in a muddle (§153). If *Now I understand the system* does not mean the same as *The formula occurs to me*, does it follow that it describes a process behind or beside uttering the formula? If something has to stand behind the utterance, it is particular circumstances that warrant my saying I can go on if the formula occurs to me. W. advises: "Just for once, don't think of understanding as a ‘mental process’ at all!" and asks in what circumstances we say we know how to go on (§154, p. 66e). In the sense in which there are processes (including mental ones) characteristic of understanding, understanding is not a mental process; a pain's increasing and listening to a tune are mental processes (§154, p. 67e). The person may have had a distinctive experience and describe it, but it is the circumstances under which he has it that warrant his saying he understands (§155, p. 67e).

### §§156–171: reading
**Setup.** *Reading* here means rendering written signs aloud, writing from dictation, copying, playing from a score. It does not include understanding what is read (§156, p. 67e). A practised reader takes in some words as wholes, others by syllables or letters, and may attend to the text or function as a mere reading-machine. A beginner spells, guesses from context, may know the passage by heart; the teacher says he is not really reading. We are then inclined to say that reading is a distinctive conscious mental activity. W. says we must admit that, as far as uttering a printed word goes, the same may go on in the mind of the pupil who is pretending as in that of the practised reader. We then want to posit different mechanisms, in the unconscious or in the brain, but W. says these are only hypotheses, models to explain what you observe (§156, pp. 67e–68e).

**Live reading-machines.** Take creatures trained as reading-machines. A pupil who has not taken part in training produces random sounds, sometimes accidentally right; the teacher says he is not reading. Later he reacts correctly to further words and the teacher says *Now he can read!* When did he begin, and which word was first? The question makes no sense unless we stipulate, for example the first of a series of 50 words read correctly. If *reading* meant an experience of transition, the question would make sense; it would also for a machine, once certain parts were connected. But for the live machine *reading* meant reacting to written signs in certain ways, a concept independent of any mental or other mechanism. The change was in his behaviour (§157, pp. 68e–69e).

**Brain connections, "a priori".** *Objector:* better knowledge of the brain would show when the reading connection was set up (§158). *W.* asks what we know about this. If it is a priori, that means it is a form of representation that is very appealing to us (§158, p. 69e).

**Feelings as criteria?** *Tempted voice:* the one real criterion is the conscious act of reading; a man surely knows whether he is reading or pretending. *W.:* A, pretending to read Cyrillic from a memorised sentence, will have none of the feelings characteristic of reading and perhaps various feelings characteristic of cheating (§159, pp. 69e–70e). But take a fluent reader who reads a new text with the feeling of reciting something learnt (perhaps drug-induced). Would we let his feelings count as a criterion of his not really reading (§160)? Under a drug a man utters words for unfamiliar signs with all the outward characteristics and feelings of reading; some would say he was reading, others not. If he reads five signs as A B O V E and, shown them reversed, reads E V O B A, and keeps that interpretation in further tests, we may well be inclined to say he was making up an alphabet ad hoc and reading accordingly (§160, p. 70e). There is a continuous series of cases between reciting from memory and spelling out. Say the numbers 1 to 12, then read them off a watch dial: what did you do to make it reading (§161, p. 70e)?

**Deriving.** *Attempt:* someone is reading if he derives the reproduction from the original (the text, the dictation, the score). A man taught the Cyrillic alphabet and the sound of each letter reads a passage, pronouncing each letter as taught; we will probably say he derives the sound from the written pattern by the rule we gave him. W. asks what we know beyond that we taught him and he read aloud; one reply is that the pupil shows he is using the rule. This is clearer if he has to copy print into handwriting, for then we can give him the rule as a table, and his consulting the table shows he derives (§162, p. 71e). Suppose he always writes b for A, c for B, and so on, and a for Z: surely we would call this too derivation by the table. If he alters the transcription by a simple rule (n for the first A, o for the next, p…), W. asks where the boundary with the random lies, and whether *derive* then has no meaning, since the meaning seems to dissolve when followed through (§163, p. 71e). Case (162) was one case out of the family of cases of deriving, not a special clothing over an essence that must be stripped off (the artichoke image). Likewise *read* is used for a family of cases, and different criteria apply in different circumstances (§164, pp. 71e–72e).

> *Intuition note.* *Reading* is not one inner event. When the criteria are behavioural (reacting to signs in set ways), *When did he first really read?* has no answer unless we stipulate one. When we mean a felt transition, it can.

**The "characteristic experience".** *Tempted voice (§165):* reading is a quite particular process; the spoken words come in a distinctive way, of themselves, and slip in as one reads. *W.* tests this on single letters: *How did the sound come?* We have no idea what to say (§166, p. 73e). With an unfamiliar mark the difference lay in the situation: tension and expectation beforehand. If the mark is used regularly, W. asks whether we can say anything but that after a while the sound comes automatically (§166). *Tempted voice:* reading is one particular process that we recognise. *W.* asks whether the mental process is really the same when one reads a sentence from print and when one writes it from Morse code. On the other side, there is some uniformity in reading print: the look of a printed line is itself characteristic, with familiar letters and words like well-known faces (§167, pp. 73e–74e). The eye passes over print differently from squiggles. But what is essential to reading as such? Not any one feature that occurs in all cases; compare capital letters or right-to-left script (§168, p. 74e).

**Feeling of causation or influence.** Do we feel the look of the words causing our utterance (§169)? Causation is surely established by experiment, though observed regular concurrence is not the only way we establish it. One might rather say I feel that the letters are the reason why I read as I do, a reason I cite if asked. With a letter the sound is automatic, even against my will, and the letter is pronounced with less effort than a squiggle when I try (§169, pp. 74e–75e). We would never have thought we felt the influence of letters had we not compared them with arbitrary marks, and we interpret the difference as influence versus absence of influence. Letting oneself be guided consists only in looking carefully at the letters and perhaps excluding certain other thoughts. We then imagine a feeling that lets us perceive a connecting mechanism, as if we felt levers (§170, p. 75e). One might say the written word intimates the sound, or that letter and sound form an alloy, as famous faces and the sound of their names do (§171). W. asks you to read ordinarily and ask whether you had such experiences, and says: do not say you had them unconsciously (§171, pp. 75e–76e).

### §§172–178: being guided
Cases: led blindfolded by the hand, led by force, guided by a dance partner, led along a footpath while chatting, following a track in a field. They are similar, but what is common to all the experiences (§172, p. 76e)? *Objector:* being guided is a particular experience. *W.:* you are thinking of one particular experience of being guided, for example the conscientious look of a bookkeeper. W. asks whether someone who does something with all the signs of carelessness, but with the expression (and why not the feelings) of great carefulness, is then careful. Asked what you are doing, you list looking at each letter, making a face, writing deliberately, and then want to say it is something more inward, a particular atmosphere that dissipates when you look closely (§173, pp. 76e–77e). W. asks what the experience of doing something deliberately is. A look or gesture occurs to you and you want to say *it just is a particular inner experience*; by saying this you say nothing at all (§174, p. 77e). When copying a doodle, nothing special is noticed at the time. Afterwards no description satisfies, and only then does the idea of an ethereal, intangible influence arise (§175, p. 77e). I want to say I experienced the *because*, but I would not call any phenomenon the experience of the because (§176, pp. 77e–78e). When I reflect on what I experience I look at it through the concept *because*. Drawing under the influence of the original may, under certain circumstances, consist in drawing a parallel line, though that is not in general essential to being guided (§177, p. 78e). A guiding gesture made with nobody to guide did not contain the essence of guiding, yet the word is forced on us (§178, p. 78e).

### §§179–184: back to *Now I can go on*
We would not say B had a right to say *Now I know how to go on* just because the formula occurred to him, unless experience showed a connection between its occurring and his continuing the series (§179, p. 78e). One might then think the sentence means *I have an experience which is empirically known to lead to continuing*, but B does not mean that. The words are correctly used under certain circumstances, for example if he has learnt algebra and used such formulae. That does not mean his statement is only short for a description of all those circumstances. W. asks us to think how we learn these expressions and in what family of language-games. Even if nothing occurred but a sudden *Now I know how to go on*, with relief, and he then worked the series out, we should say in certain circumstances that he did know (§179, pp. 78e–79e). In this last case it would be misleading to call the words a description of a mental state; one could rather call them a signal, and we judge whether it was rightly applied by what he goes on to do (§180, p. 79e). If B says he can go on and then hesitates and cannot, we say different things in different cases (§181, p. 79e).

§182 sets exercises on *to fit*, *to be able*, *to understand* (see Exercises). The criteria for these are much more complicated than might appear, and their role in language is other than we are tempted to think. This role is what we need to understand to resolve philosophical paradoxes, and that is why definitions usually are not enough to resolve them (§182, pp. 79e–80e). *Now I can go on* and *Now the formula has occurred to me* may in the circumstances have the same sense, but in general they do not. We must not suppose a totality of conditions corresponding to each case, so that the person could not but walk if they were all fulfilled (§183, p. 80e). On suddenly knowing a tune, W. asks what it was like: surely it cannot have occurred to me in its entirety at that moment. But may I not have been certain I knew it? So in some sense it was there; but in what sense? If someone says with conviction that he now knows it, then it is somehow present to his mind in its entirety, and this is an explanation of those words (§184, p. 80e).

### §§185–190: beyond 1000; the steps "already taken"
**The case.** The pupil has mastered the natural numbers by the usual criteria and is taught series of the form 0, n, 2n, 3n… at the order *+n*, with exercises and tests up to 1000. Asked to continue *+2* beyond 1000, he writes 1000, 1004, 1008, 1012. Told to look at what he is doing, he does not understand; he says he thought that was how he had to do it, or that he did go on in the same way. Going over the old explanations is no use. We might say he finds it natural, given our explanations, to understand the order as *add 2 up to 1000, 4 up to 2000, 6 up to 3000, and so on*. This is like reacting to a pointing gesture by looking from fingertip to wrist instead of wrist to fingertip (§185, pp. 80e–81e).

**Intuition at every step?** *Objector:* then a new insight (intuition) is needed at every step. *Objector:* the right step is the one in accordance with the order as it was meant. *W.:* did you also mean 1868 after 1866, and so on, infinitely many? *Objector:* I meant the next but one number, and the rest follows. *W.:* what follows is just what is in question. It would be almost more correct to say that a new decision is needed at every point (§186, p. 81e). *I already knew he should write 1002* amounts to something like *if I had been asked, I would have said 1002*, an assumption like *if he had fallen into the water, I would have jumped in* (§187, pp. 81e–82e). Your idea was that meaning the order had already taken all the steps, your mind flying ahead (§188, p. 82e).

**Determination by a formula.** *Objector:* are the steps not determined by the formula? *W.:* the question contains a mistake. We use the expression *the steps are determined by the formula*; how? (1) People are brought by training to use $y = x^2$ so that all work out the same $y$ for the same $x$, or are trained so that all take the same step at the same point on *+3*. We might say that for these people the order *+3* completely determines every step, in contrast with people who do not know what to do, or who react with perfect certainty but each in a different way. (2) We may contrast kinds of formula and kinds of use appropriate to them: formulae which determine $y$ for a given $x$ and those which do not ($y = x^2$ the first kind, $y \neq x^2$ the second, as printed). *The formula determines y* is then a statement about the form of the formula. The question whether $y = x^2$ is a formula which determines $y$ is unclear offhand: it might test a pupil's grasp of *to determine*, or be a mathematical problem (§189, pp. 82e–83e). W. asks what the criterion is for how a formula is meant, and answers: for example, the kind of way we always use it and were taught to use it. In this way meaning something can determine the steps in advance (§190, p. 83e).

> *Intuition note.* *The order already contains all its steps* looks like a claim about a mysterious reach of the mind (§188). W. says that asking whether the formula determines the steps contains a mistake, and he points to how the expression is used: people trained alike take the same step (§189). In that sense, meaning something can determine the steps in advance (§190).

### §§191–197: grasping the whole use at a stroke; machines; possibility
*It is as if we could grasp the whole use at a stroke.* W. asks for a model of this and says there is none; the expression suggests itself as a result of the crossing of different pictures (§191). He calls it a philosophical superlative (§192, p. 83e).

**Machine as symbol.** A machine seems to contain its mode of operation, as if its parts could move only so. We forget that they could bend or melt. When predicting an actual machine we do not forget this. We use a machine or its picture as a symbol, from which one derives the movements, like giving a number as the twenty-fifth in the series 1, 4, 9, 16… Taken as a symbol, the machine's movement is predetermined in a different way from that of an actual machine (§193, pp. 83e–84e). We think a machine contains its possible movements in some mysterious way when we are doing philosophy (§194). The possibility of a movement seems like a shadow of the movement. Odd ideas arise from how we speak: we show a possibility by a picture, and say *it isn't moving yet, but it already has the possibility of moving*. We may doubt whether a physical condition makes a movement possible, but we never discuss whether this is the possibility of this movement or of that. When we do philosophy we are like people who hear how civilised people talk, misinterpret it, and draw odd conclusions (§194, pp. 84e–85e).

**Use "present in some sense".** *Objector:* the use is somehow present, not causally. *W.:* of course, in some sense; the only thing wrong is the expression *in an odd way*. The sentence seems odd only when imagined to belong to a different language-game. (Example: a child amazed that a tailor can sew a dress, thinking it was produced by sewing alone.) (§195, p. 85e). We take the word to signify an odd process (§196). Grasping the whole use at a stroke is just what we sometimes say we do, and nothing in it is strange. It becomes strange if the future must already be present and yet is not. W. asks where the connection is made between *Let's play chess* and all the rules, and answers: in the list of rules, in the teaching, in the everyday practice of playing (§197, p. 86e).

### §§198–202: the paradox and its dissolution
*Objector:* whatever I do can, on some interpretation, be made compatible with the rule. *W.:* no, that is not what one should say. Rather: "every interpretation hangs in the air together with what it interprets, and cannot give it any support." Interpretations by themselves do not determine meaning. What links a signpost to my action is that I have been trained to react to it. *Objector:* that is only a causal connection. *W.:* a person goes by a signpost only in so far as there is an established usage, a custom (§198, p. 86e).

W. asks whether following a rule could be something possible for only one person, once in a lifetime, and calls the question a gloss on the grammar of *to follow a rule*. It is not possible that there should have been only one occasion on which only one person followed a rule; the same goes for making a report or giving an order. Following a rule, reporting, ordering and playing chess are customs (usages, institutions). W. adds: "To understand a language means to have mastered a technique." (§199, p. 87e). Two people in a tribe without games might go through the moves of chess, and we would say they play chess. If a game of chess were translated by rules into yells and stamping, W. asks whether we would still be inclined to say they were playing a game, and with what right (§200).

**§201.** The paradox was: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course can be brought into accord with it. The answer given: if every course can be brought into accord, it can also be brought into conflict, so there would be neither. The mere fact that in this chain of reasoning we place one interpretation behind another shows that there is a misunderstanding. What we thereby show is that there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation and is exhibited, from case to case of application, in what we call following the rule and going against it. One should speak of interpretation only when one expression of a rule is substituted for another (§201, p. 87e).

**§202.** Following a rule is a practice; thinking one is following a rule is not following one. That is why one cannot follow a rule privately: otherwise thinking one was following it would be the same as following it (§202, pp. 87e–88e).

> *Intuition note.* The objector says that whatever one does can be fitted to the rule by some interpretation (§198). W. rejects that way of putting it: an interpretation by itself gives no support to what it interprets, and there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation but shows itself in what we call following it and going against it (§201). He ties this to practice and custom (§§198, 199, 202) and says a rule cannot be followed privately (§202).

### §§203–216: custom, regularity, teaching, reasons running out
Language is a labyrinth of paths (§203). One can invent a game nobody plays; W. asks whether it could be that mankind never played any games, and one game was invented and never played (§204). *Objector:* the remarkable thing about intention is that no custom is needed; two people might begin chess in a world with no games. *W.* asks whether chess is not defined by its rules, and how the rules are present in the mind of someone who intends to play chess (§205, p. 88e).

**Explorer.** Following a rule is analogous to obeying an order; one is trained, and one reacts in a particular way. W. asks who is right if two people react differently. He adds: "Shared human behaviour is the system of reference by means of which we interpret an unknown language." (§206). In an imagined country, people's sounds have no regular connection with their activities, yet gagging them confuses their actions; W. asks whether they have a language, and says there is not enough regularity for us to call it language (§207, pp. 88e–89e).

**Teaching "same".** Does this explain *rule* by *regularity*? W. says *regular*, *uniform* and *same* are taught to someone who lacks the concepts by examples and exercises: showing the same colours, lengths and shapes, continuing an ornamental pattern or a progression, with expressions of agreement, rejection, expectation and encouragement. No word is explained by itself, so there is no logical circle. *And so on* is also explained in this teaching, with a gesture among other things, comparable to pointing. *And so on ad inf.* is not an abbreviation; that we cannot write all the digits of $\pi$ is not a human shortcoming. Teaching meant to cover only the given examples differs from teaching that points beyond them (§208, pp. 88e–89e).

*Objector:* does our understanding not reach beyond all examples? *W.* calls this a curious but natural expression and asks whether he has a deeper understanding himself, or more than he gives in the explanation, and whence the feeling that he has more (§209, pp. 89e–90e). *Objector:* do you not leave it to him to guess your intention? *W.:* every explanation I can give myself I give him. *He guesses what I mean* would amount to various interpretations coming to him and his picking one, in which case he could ask me (§210, p. 90e).

**Reasons give out.** *How does he know how to continue by himself?* W.: how do I know? If that asks for reasons, "my reasons will soon give out. And then I shall act, without reasons." (§211). Ordered by someone I fear to continue a series, I act quickly and the lack of reasons does not trouble me (§212). A doubt was possible in some circumstances, but that is not to say that I did doubt, or even could doubt. If intuition is an inner voice, how do I know how I am to follow it, since if it can guide me right it can also guide me wrong? (§213). If intuition is needed for continuing 1 2 3 4…, it is needed also for 2 2 2 2… (§214, p. 90e).

**Identity.** *Objector:* at least the same is the same; a thing's identity with itself seems an infallible paradigm. *W.:* then are two things the same when they are what one thing is? How am I to apply what the one thing shows me to the case of two? (§215, pp. 90e–91e). *A thing is identical with itself* is a useless sentence tied to a play of the imagination, as if a thing fitted into its own shape (§216, p. 91e).

### §§217–242: bedrock, blindness, inspiration, agreement
**Bedrock.** If *How am I able to follow a rule?* does not ask about causes, it asks for the justification of my acting so. "Once I have exhausted the justifications, I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned." Then I am inclined to say that this is simply what I do. W. adds that we sometimes demand explanations for their form, not their content: an architectural requirement, the explanation a kind of sham corbel that supports nothing (§217, p. 91e). The beginning of a series seems like rails laid invisibly to infinity (§218). *All the steps are really already taken* means I no longer have a choice. W. asks how that would help me, and says the description made sense only if understood symbolically: this is how it strikes me (§219). He then says: "I follow the rule blindly" (§219, p. 92e). The symbolic sentence was meant to highlight a difference between causal and logical dependence (§220), and was a mythological description of the use of a rule (§221).

**Rule and "same".** *The line intimates to me the way I am to go* is only a picture; if I judged what it intimated irresponsibly, I would not say I was following it like a rule (§222). We do not wait on the rule's nod; it always tells us the same, and we do what it tells us (§223). *Accord* and *rule* are cousins (§224); the uses of *rule* and *same* are interwoven, as are those of *proposition* and *true* (§225). Someone continuing 1, 3, 5, 7… by $2x - 1$ asks whether he does the same each time; W. asks whether one who promises daily *Tomorrow I'll come* says the same thing each day (§226, p. 92e). W. asks whether it would make sense to say *if he did something different every time, we wouldn't say he was following a rule*, and answers that it makes no sense (§227). The claim that everything is already contained in the series' one face is not an observation about the segment; it gives expression to the fact that all we do is "read the lips of the rule and act" (§228, pp. 92e–93e). W. says *I believe I faintly perceive a pattern in the segment, which needs only an and so on to reach to infinity* (§229). *The line intimates…* is only a paraphrase of *it is my final court of appeal* (§230). *But surely you can see…!* is the characteristic exclamation of someone compelled by a rule (§231, p. 93e).

**Inspiration versus rule.** If a rule intimates how I am to follow it, as an inner voice, this differs from following a rule. In inspiration I await direction and cannot teach anyone my technique of following the line, unless I teach him some way of listening, some kind of receptivity, but then I cannot expect him to follow the line as I do. These are grammatical remarks, not experiences gained (§232). W. imagines instruction in an arithmetic in which children calculate each in their own way by an inner voice, like composing (§233). He asks whether we could calculate as we do, all agreeing, and still at every step feel guided by the rules as by a spell, astonished perhaps at the agreement (§234). He says there is much to the physiognomy of what we call following a rule (§235). Calculating prodigies who reach the right result but cannot say how: W. asks whether we are to say they do not calculate, and adds *a family of cases* (§236). A man guides one compass-point along a line and varies the opening of the other with no regularity of any kind; we cannot learn his way. Here perhaps we really would say: the original seems to intimate to him how he has to go, but it is not a rule (§237, p. 94e).

**Final remarks.** The rule can only seem to me to produce all its consequences in advance if I draw them as a matter of course, as it is a matter of course for me to call this colour *blue* (§238, p. 94e). W. asks how he is to know which colour to pick out on hearing *red*, and gives the answer *the colour whose image occurs to him*; he then asks how he is to know which colour that is and whether a further criterion is needed. *Red means the colour that occurs to me when I hear the word* would be a definition, not an explanation of what signifying something by a word essentially is (§239, p. 94e). Disputes among mathematicians do not break out over whether a rule has been followed; this belongs to the scaffolding of our language (§240). *Objector:* so human agreement decides what is true and false? *W.:* what is true or false is what human beings say, and they agree in their language. This is agreement "not in opinions, but rather in form of life" (§241). Communication by language requires agreement in judgements as well as in definitions; this seems to abolish logic but does not do so. The text breaks off after the words that it is one thing to describe methods of measurement and another to obtain (§242, p. 94e).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Suppose a pupil is shown: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and told to "go on in the same way".

- Reading A (add 2 each time): $a_n = 2n$. Then $a_6 = 12$, $a_7 = 14$.
- Reading B (add 2 up to 10, then add 4 each time): $a_n = 10 + 4(n-5)$ for $n \ge 5$. Check: $n=5$ gives $10$; $n=6$ gives $14$; $n=7$ gives $18$.
- Both readings fit the first five terms (2, 4, 6, 8, 10), so the examples alone do not choose between them. They split at the sixth term: 12 versus 14.
- Repeating the explanation (*but you must add 2!*) is itself a new string of words, open to readings A and B again. This mirrors §185 and the point of §198 about interpretations.
- In a community trained so that people write 12 and judge 14 a mistake, the order *+2* does "determine every step" in the sense of §189. The pupil who writes 14 has a different reaction, like the pupil of §185.

The toy shows one point of the unit: no formula or interpretation by itself fixes its own continuation. What fixes it is shared training and reaction, until reasons give out (§211, §217).

## Concepts
- [[rule-following problem]] — the paradox that no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course can be brought into accord with it. W. treats it as resting on a misunderstanding, and points to a way of grasping a rule that shows itself in following it and going against it (§§198, 201–202, pp. 86e–88e).
- [[meaning as use]] — W. discusses *derive*, *read* and *determined* by asking how the expressions are used; he reports that we say a word's meaning lies in its use (§§164, 189, 197, pp. 72e, 82e–83e, 86e).
- [[language-game]] — the setting in which *Now I can go on* is used and learnt, within a family of language-games (§179, pp. 78e–79e).
- [[family resemblance]] — *derive* and *read* apply to families of cases with changing criteria; W. uses the word *family* for the cases, not the term *resemblance* (§164, p. 72e).
- [[understanding-not-mental-process]] (new) — in the sense in which there are processes characteristic of understanding, understanding is not a mental process; the circumstances warrant saying one understands (§§154–155, p. 67e).
- [[criterion-vs-symptom]] (new) — the application is a criterion of understanding (§146), and different criteria apply to reading in different circumstances (§164). No symptom contrast and no general definition of *criterion* is given in these pages (pp. 64e, 72e).
- [[interpretation-hangs-in-the-air]] (new) — every interpretation hangs in the air with what it interprets and gives it no support; there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation (§§198, 201, pp. 86e–87e).
- [[bedrock-justification-gives-out]] (new) — when reasons are exhausted one acts without reasons and is inclined to say *this is simply what I do* (§§211, 217, pp. 90e–91e).
- [[agreement-in-form-of-life]] (new) — communication requires agreement in judgements as well as definitions, which is agreement in form of life, not in opinions (§§241–242, p. 94e).
- [[private language argument]] — in this unit only one remark: one cannot follow a rule privately (§202, pp. 87e–88e).

## Canonical instances
- [[series-1-5-11-19-29]] — *Now I can go on* has various accompaniments; understanding must be more than any of them (§§151–152, pp. 65e–66e).
- [[reading-machines-training]] — *when did he first read?* has no answer for the live reading-machine unless we stipulate one, since reading is there a matter of behaviour (§157, pp. 68e–69e).
- [[pupil-plus-two-beyond-1000]] — a pupil continues *+2* as 1000, 1004, 1008…, and going over the old explanations is no use (§185, pp. 80e–81e).
- [[machine-as-symbol]] — a machine's movements seem already contained in it; W. points to our using a machine or its picture as a symbol (§193, pp. 83e–84e).
- [[signpost-and-custom]] — a person goes by a signpost only in so far as there is an established usage or custom (§198, p. 86e).
- [[explorer-unknown-language]] — shared human behaviour is the system of reference for interpreting an unknown language (§§206–207, pp. 88e–89e).
- [[rails-to-infinity]] — a rule pictured as rails laid invisibly to infinity (§218, p. 91e).
- [[compass-man]] — someone who guides a compass point along a line with no regularity we can see: perhaps we would say it is not a rule (§237, p. 94e).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Several rules fit finite examples ↔ philosophy of science: underdetermination of theory by evidence — evidence alone leaves open which continuation is right.
- [structural-analogy] Pupil's divergent generalisation beyond the training range (§185) ↔ machine learning: models fitting training data but extrapolating differently.
- [structural-analogy] Agreement in judgements needed for communication (§242) ↔ social-science coding: inter-coder agreement presupposes shared practice, not only shared definitions.
- [metaphor] Bedrock (§217) ↔ epistemology of justification: reasons running out in practice.
