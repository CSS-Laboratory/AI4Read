# Seeing aspects (PPF xi, first half)

## L0 — One line
Seeing an aspect (seeing a figure that has not changed "as" something) seems to lie between seeing and thinking, and Wittgenstein investigates it as a question about the concept, looking at its use and at the behaviour and abilities that go with it, rather than at an inner picture or a physiological cause.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What happens when a figure such as the duck–rabbit "changes" although nothing in it changes? Wittgenstein says our problem is conceptual, not causal. An exact copy of the figure does not show the change, yet we report it much like a perception. He warns that the idea of an "inner picture" is misleading. He points to exclamations, imitation and fine shades of behaviour as part of how aspect-reports work. For some aspects, seeing them presupposes mastery of a technique. Seeing an aspect is related to imagination and is subject to the will. His interest is the concept and its place among the concepts of experience.

**Prerequisites:** the difference between a causal and a conceptual question; the idea that we should look at how a word is used; the duck–rabbit and the schematic cube as figures.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Two uses of "see": reporting what is in front of me versus noticing a likeness or an aspect. The two "objects" of sight differ in category (§111, p. 203e).
- In a change of aspect the figure does not change, yet I describe the change as if the object had changed. The expression is said to be both a new and an unchanged perception (§§129–130, p. 206e).
- In the puzzle-picture case an exact copy of the impression shows no change (§131, p. 206e). Wittgenstein says the "inner picture" is a misleading model, and that treating the "organization" of the impression as an inner object makes that object chimerical (§§133–134, p. 206e).
- Seeing as is said not to be part of perception, so it is like seeing and again not like seeing (§137, p. 207e). The lighting up of an aspect seems half visual experience, half thought (§140, p. 207e).
- Behaviour such as mimicry, exclamations and "knowing one's way about" with a drawing counts among the criteria for seeing in a given way (§§149, 180, 192, pp. 208e, 213e, 215e).
- For some aspects (the triangle) seeing them presupposes mastery of a technique, and the concept of seeing is then modified (§§222–224, pp. 219e–220e).
- An imagined physiological explanation would add a new criterion for seeing; it could conceal the old problem but not solve it, and the psychological concept hangs out of its reach (§§183, 236, pp. 214e, 221e).
- Aspect-seeing is related to imagination and is subject to the will. Aspect-blindness is introduced as a conceptual investigation (§§254–257, p. 224e).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**§§111–117 (pp. 203e–204e): two uses of "see".**
- Use 1: asked what you see, you say "I see this" and give a description, drawing or copy. Use 2: I say I see a likeness in two faces; let the hearer see the faces as clearly as I do myself. What matters is the categorial difference between the two "objects" of sight (§111, p. 203e).
- One man might draw the two faces accurately, and another notice in the drawing the likeness the first did not see (§112, p. 203e).
- Noticing an aspect: I observe a face, then suddenly notice its likeness to another. I see that it has not changed, and yet I see it differently (§113, p. 203e).
- Its causes interest psychologists. Wittgenstein says we are interested in the concept and its place among the concepts of experience (§§114–115, p. 203e).
- An illustration may appear in several places in a textbook, and each time the text supplies the interpretation (glass cube, upturned open box, wire frame, three boards forming a solid angle). But we can also see it now as one thing, now another: "So we interpret it, and see it as we interpret it" (§116, p. 203e).
- **Objection (voice: "one would like to respond"):** describing immediate visual experience by an interpretation is an indirect description; seeing the figure as a box amounts to having a visual experience empirically found to accompany interpreting it as a box, or looking at a box. **Reply:** if it amounted to this, I ought to know that experience directly, as I can speak of red without calling it the colour of blood (§117, p. 204e).

**§§118–128 (pp. 204e–205e): duck–rabbit and picture-objects.**
- The duck–rabbit (derived from Jastrow) can be seen as a rabbit's head or a duck's. Wittgenstein says he must distinguish continuous seeing of an aspect from its lighting up. The picture might have been shown to me without my ever seeing in it anything but a rabbit (§118, p. 204e).
- A picture-object (for example a picture-face) is something I engage with in some respects as with a real face. I can study its expression, and a child can talk to a picture-man or treat picture-animals like dolls (§119, p. 204e).
- I may have seen the duck–rabbit simply as a picture-rabbit from the first. Asked what I see, I would have answered that it is a picture-rabbit, and explained by pointing to rabbit pictures, perhaps real rabbits. I would not have said I now see it as a picture-rabbit; I would simply have described my perception, as with a red circle. Another person could still say of me that I see the figure as a picture-rabbit (§§120–121, pp. 204e–205e).
- At the sight of a knife and fork, saying that I now see this as a knife and fork would not be understood, nor would saying it is now a fork for me. One does not "take" what one knows to be cutlery for cutlery (§§122–123, p. 205e). If someone says it is now a face for him, one can ask what change he is alluding to (§124, p. 205e).
- I see two pictures with the duck–rabbit surrounded by rabbits in one and ducks in the other, and do not notice they are the same. Does it follow that I see something different? Wittgenstein answers that it gives us a reason for using that expression here (§125, p. 205e).
- Someone's exclamation that he saw it quite differently and would never have recognized it is an exclamation, and there is also a justification for it (§126). Wittgenstein goes on: I would never have thought of superimposing the heads in this way; they suggest a different mode of comparison. The head seen one way has not the slightest similarity to the head seen the other way, although they are congruent (§127, p. 205e).
- Shown a picture-rabbit, I say it is a rabbit, not that it is now a rabbit; I report my perception. Shown the duck–rabbit, I may say it is a duck–rabbit, which is again a report of perception, though I may also react differently. Saying now it is a rabbit is not such a report. Had I answered just that it is a rabbit, the ambiguity would have escaped me (§128, p. 205e).

**§§129–137 (pp. 206e–207e): change of aspect and the "inner picture".**
- Interlocutor: surely the picture has changed altogether. Wittgenstein asks whether what differs is my impression or my attitude, and asks whether I can say. I describe the change like a perception, just as if the object had changed before my eyes (§129, p. 206e).
- Saying that I now see this has the form of a report of a new perception. "The expression of a change of aspect is an expression of a new perception and, at the same time, an expression of an unchanged perception" (§130, p. 206e).
- Puzzle-picture: where there were branches I suddenly see a human figure. Wittgenstein says my visual impression has changed and I now recognize that it has not only shape and colour but also a particular "organization". He asks what the impression was like before and what it is like now. If I represent it by an exact copy, and that is a good representation of it, no change shows up (§131, p. 206e).
- **Interlocutor (voice: "do not say"):** my visual impression isn't the drawing; it is this, which I can't show anyone. **Reply:** it is not the drawing, but neither is it something of the same category that I carry within myself (§132, p. 206e).
- The concept of an inner picture is misleading, since its model is the outer picture, and yet the uses of the two concept-words are no more alike than those of "numeral" and "number" (§133, p. 206e).
- Putting "organization" on a level with colour and shape assumes the visual impression is an inner object. This makes the object chimerical, a strangely vacillating entity (§134, p. 206e).
- If I know the schematic cube has several aspects and want to know what someone sees, I can ask for a model as well as a copy. With a changing aspect the case alters: what seemed a useless specification once there was a copy becomes the only possible expression of the experience. Wittgenstein says this suffices to dispose of the comparison of organization with colour and shape (§§135–136, pp. 206e–207e).
- If I saw the duck–rabbit as a rabbit, I saw such shapes and colours and, in addition, something like this (he points to many pictures of rabbits). This shows the difference between the concepts. "'Seeing as . . .' is not part of perception." And therefore it is like seeing, and again not like seeing (§137, p. 207e).

*Intuition note.* An exact copy of the figure is the same before and after the flip. Wittgenstein says the impression is not the drawing, but also not something of the same category carried inside. He leaves open what differs ("Can I say?"); later remarks point to description, comparison, mimicry and other behaviour.

**§§138–150 (pp. 207e–208e): exclamation, recognition, thought.**
- A report (answering "A rabbit." when asked about an animal) and an exclamation (a rabbit runs past a landscape) both express visual experience. The exclamation does so in a different sense: it is forced from us, and stands to the experience somewhat as a cry to pain (§138, p. 207e).
- Since the exclamation is the description of a perception, one can also call it the expression of thought. Whoever has the visual experience it expresses is also thinking of what he sees. That is why the lighting up of an aspect seems half visual experience, half thought (§§139–140, p. 207e).
- Someone suddenly fails to recognize a familiar object in an unusual position or light. Is it correct to say his visual experience differs from that of someone who recognizes it at once? Wittgenstein asks whether someone couldn't describe an unfamiliar shape as accurately as I can, and whether that isn't the answer; he then adds that generally it won't be so and the description will run quite differently (the animal had long ears versus two long appendages, which he draws) (§§141–142, p. 207e).
- Meeting someone after years, I see him clearly but fail to recognize him; then I see his former face in the altered one. The speaker believes he would portray him differently now if he could paint (§143, p. 208e).
- Is recognition a special sort of seeing, seeing plus thinking, or a fusion? Wittgenstein says the question is why one wants to say this (§144, p. 208e). The very expression that reports what is seen is here a cry of recognition (§145, p. 208e).
- Asked what the criterion of the visual experience is, or should be, he answers: a representation of what is seen. That concept, like the concept of a copy, is very elastic, and so is the concept of what is seen; the two are intimately connected, which is not to say they are alike (§§146–147, p. 208e).
- It is altogether natural to us to represent what we see three-dimensionally; special practice and instruction are needed for two-dimensional representation (§148, p. 208e).
- If someone sees a smile and does not understand it as a smile, does he see it differently? He mimics it differently, for instance (§149, p. 208e). With a face held upside down you may perhaps see that it is smiling, but you cannot imitate the smile or describe its character more exactly, though the picture may be a most exact representation of the face (§150, p. 208e).

**§§151–157 (p. 209e).**
- Figure (a) is the reverse of (b), and (c) of (d). But, Wittgenstein says he would like to say, there is another kind of difference between my impressions of (c) and (d) than between those of (a) and (b): (d) looks neater than (c), and is easy where (c) is hard to copy (§151, p. 209e).
- Duck–rabbit hidden in a tangle of lines: if I later see the aspect change, the duck and rabbit aspects are not seen quite differently from when I recognized them separately. But the change produces a surprise the recognition did not (§152, p. 209e).
- Finding Figure 2 in Figure 1 means seeing Figure 1 in a new way, and noticing the second figure was a new visual experience. He would not necessarily want to say Figure 1 looks quite different now (§§153–154, p. 209e). There is an enormous number of interrelated phenomena and possible concepts (§155).
- Is the copy an incomplete description of the visual experience? No: what further specification is needed depends on circumstances. It may be incomplete if some question still remains (§156, p. 209e).
- A drawing can fall under both "picture-rabbit" and "picture-duck", but an impression is not simultaneously of a picture-duck and a picture-rabbit (§157, p. 209e).

**§§158–161 (p. 210e): what I really see.**
- **Objection:** what I really see must be what the object produces in me. Wittgenstein: then what is produced is a sort of replica, almost a materialization, something spatial that must be describable in purely spatial terms. The concept of friendliness has no place in a description of it, though it may help it (§158, p. 210e).
- Asked what I saw, I may make a sketch, but I will mostly not recollect how my glance wandered (§159, p. 210e).
- The concept of seeing makes a tangled impression, and that is how it is. "Description of what is seen" covers many things, and there is no one genuine, proper case with the rest unclear or to be swept aside (§160, p. 210e).
- Explaining the physical object through "what is really seen" is dangerous. The everyday language-game is to be accepted and false accounts of it characterized as false; the primitive language-game children are instructed in needs no justification (§161, p. 210e).

**§§162–177 (pp. 210e–213e): the triangle and seeing according to an interpretation.**
- A triangle can be seen as a triangular hole, a solid, a geometrical drawing, standing on its base, hanging from its apex, as a mountain, wedge, arrow, an overturned object, a half parallelogram and more (§162, p. 210e). To the instruction that you can regard it now this way, now that way, Wittgenstein asks "What way?", since there is no further qualification (§163, p. 211e).
- The question how it is possible to see an object according to an interpretation presents it as a strange fact, as if something were pressed into a mould it did not fit; but no pressing took place (§164, p. 211e).
- If there seems to be no room for a form, you must find it in another dimension. Likewise there is no room for imaginary numbers in the continuum of real numbers. And this surely means that their application is less like that of real numbers than the calculations suggest; it is necessary to descend to the application, where the concept finds a different place (§165, p. 211e).
- Wittgenstein asks how the account "I can see something as whatever it can be a picture of" would do; what it means is that the aspects in a change of aspects are those the figure could in certain circumstances have permanently in a picture (§166, p. 211e).
- A triangle can stand up in one picture, hang in another, and in a third represent something fallen over, so that I react by saying the glass has fallen over and lies there in fragments, not that it may also represent something fallen over (§167, p. 211e).
- Could I say what a picture must be like to produce this effect? No. Some styles of painting convey nothing to me in this immediate way but do to other people; he thinks custom and upbringing have a hand in this (§168, p. 211e).
- What does it mean that I see the sphere floating in a picture? It is not enough that this is the most suggestive or natural description for me, since it might be merely conventional. Seeing it so, not merely understanding it so, is expressed by, say, "The sphere seems to float", "One sees it floating", or in a special tone of voice "It floats!". This is an expression for taking something to be so, but not being used as such (§169, pp. 211e–212e). Here we are not asking about causes (§170, p. 212e).
- **Interlocutor:** surely I see something different when I see the sphere floating. **Wittgenstein:** this really amounts to: this expression is justified; taken literally it is a repetition. My impression is not that of a real floating sphere either; there are derivative forms of three-dimensional seeing, such as a photograph and the stereoscope (§171, p. 212e). To answer whether the impression is really different, I would like to ask myself whether there is something different in me, but how can I ascertain this? I describe what I see differently (§172, p. 212e).
- Some drawings are always seen flat, others sometimes or always three-dimensionally. One would like to say the visual impression of the latter is three-dimensional (for the schematic cube, a cube); then it is strange that with some drawings our impression should be flat and with others three-dimensional (§§173–174, p. 212e).
- Galloping horse: do I only know this is the movement meant? Is it superstition to think I see the horse galloping? Does my visual impression gallop too (§175, p. 212e)? What does someone tell me by saying "Now I see it as . . ."? What consequences has it, and what can I do with it (§176, p. 212e)?
- Some people associate colours with vowels; for one man a vowel might change colour on repetition. An utterance of "Now I see it as . . ." might have no more significance for us than "a is now red for me". (Linked with physiological observations, even that change might acquire importance for us.) (§177, p. 213e)

**§§178–192 (pp. 213e–215e): knowing one's way about; seeing versus knowing.**
- In talk on aesthetic matters we say "You have to see it like this", "You have to hear these bars as an introduction" (§178, p. 213e).
- A figure meant as a convex step in a topological demonstration: if someone saw it three-dimensionally only for a moment, and then now as concave, now as convex, this might make it difficult for him to follow. If the flat aspect alternates with a three-dimensional one, it is just as if I showed him completely different objects (§179, p. 213e).
- Looking at a drawing in descriptive geometry, I say I know the line appears again here but cannot see it like that. Is it just a lack of facility in operating with the drawing? Such facility is certainly one of our criteria. What convinces us that someone sees the drawing three-dimensionally is a certain kind of knowing one's way about: certain gestures that indicate the three-dimensional relations, fine shades of behaviour (§180, p. 213e).
- An animal in a silhouette picture is transfixed by an arrow: do you see the arrow, or do you merely know that these two bits are supposed to represent part of one? (Compare Köhler's interpenetrating hexagons.) (§180, p. 214e)
- "This isn't seeing" and "This is seeing": it must be possible to give both remarks a conceptual justification. In what way is it seeing (§§181–182, p. 214e)? Against the interlocutor's expectation of a physiological explanation, Wittgenstein says "Our problem is not a causal but a conceptual one" (§183, p. 214e).
- If the picture were shown me for a moment and I had to describe or draw it, I would produce a faulty copy but one showing an animal transfixed by an arrow, or two interpenetrating hexagons; there are certain mistakes I would not make (§184, p. 214e).
- What first jumps to the eye is that there are two hexagons. Do I see them as hexagons the whole time? I would like to reply that I am not thinking of them as hexagons the whole time (§185, p. 214e).
- Someone says he saw it at once as two hexagons, indeed that was all he saw. Wittgenstein thinks he would have given this description at once in answer to what he sees, not as one possibility among several; in this respect it is like the answer "A face" (§186, p. 214e).
- "The impression was that of a rearing animal" is a specific description: was it seeing, or a thought (§187, p. 215e)? Wittgenstein: do not try to analyse the experience within yourself (§188, p. 215e).
- I might first have seen the picture as something else and then said it is two hexagons, so the aspect would have altered; does this prove I saw it as something specific (§189, p. 215e)? Is it a genuine visual experience? The question is in what way it is one (§190, p. 215e).
- It is hard to see that what is at issue is determination of concepts; what forces itself on one is a concept (§191, p. 215e). When should I call it just knowing, not seeing? Perhaps when someone treats the picture as a working drawing and reads it like a blueprint. Fine shades of behaviour matter because they have important consequences (§192, p. 215e).

**§§193–204 (pp. 215e–216e): attitude to pictures.**
- Treating the figure as an animal transfixed by an arrow is my attitude to it, and this is one meaning in calling it a case of seeing (§193). Calling it two hexagons is not the same sense but a similar one (§194, p. 215e).
- Paintings (as opposed to working drawings) play a role in our lives that is by no means uniform; proverbs are sometimes hung on the wall, theorems of mechanics are not (§195, p. 215e). From someone who sees the drawing as such an animal, I expect something different than from someone who merely knows what it is meant to represent (§196, p. 215e).
- Perhaps better: we view a photograph or wall picture as the very object represented in it (§197). We could imagine people who did not, who were repelled by photographs because a colourless or reduced face struck them as inhuman (§198, p. 216e).
- Do we view a portrait as a human being always, if we see it at all? Wittgenstein might go along with this and so determine the concept of viewing a picture; the question is whether yet another, related concept also becomes important, a seeing-as that occurs only while I am concerning myself with the picture as the object represented (§199, p. 216e). He could say a picture is not always alive for me while I see it (§200, p. 216e).
- Duck–rabbit: how can the eye, this dot, look in a direction? "See how it's looking!" is said, but not the whole time one looks at the picture. Does it express a feeling (§201, p. 216e)?
- These examples are not aiming at completeness or a classification of psychological concepts; they are only meant to help the reader cope with conceptual unclarities (§202, p. 216e).
- "Now I see it as a . . ." goes with "I am trying to see it as a . . .". But I cannot try to see a conventional picture of a lion as a lion, any more than an F as that letter, though I may try to see it as a gallows (§203, p. 216e). Do not ask how it works with me; ask what I know about someone else (§204, p. 216e).

**§§205–217 (pp. 217e–218e): aspects of different kinds.**
- Children say a chest is now a house, and it is then interpreted as a house in every detail (§205, p. 217e). Does the child see it as a house? Someone might say he quite forgets it is a chest and for him it actually is a house (there are certain signs of this); Wittgenstein asks whether it would then not also be correct to say he sees it as a house (§206, p. 217e). If someone who knew the game exclaimed with special expression that it is now a house, he would be giving expression to the lighting up of an aspect (§207, p. 217e).
- If I heard someone speak in a certain way about the special expression of the rabbit's face, I would say he is now seeing the picture as a rabbit (§208, p. 217e). A theme played several times in slower tempo until I say it is now at last a march: in this tone of voice the lighting up of an aspect is also expressed (§209, p. 217e). Whistling a theme with the correct expression is an example of fine shades of behaviour (§210, p. 217e).
- The aspects of the triangle are as if an idea came into contact, and for a time remained in contact, with the visual impression (§211). They differ from the concave and convex aspects of the step and from those of the double cross (white on black, black on white; the A aspects) (§§212–213, pp. 217e–218e).
- Wittgenstein's recipe for getting rid of the idea of the private object: assume it constantly changes, but that you do not notice because your memory constantly deceives you (§214, p. 218e).
- A aspects might be reported simply by pointing alternately to a free-standing white and a free-standing black cross, perhaps as a primitive reaction in a child before he can talk. The duck and rabbit aspects could not be described in an analogous way (§215, p. 218e).
- Only someone conversant with the shapes of the two animals can see the duck–rabbit aspects; there is no analogous condition for seeing A aspects (§216). Someone can take the duck–rabbit simply for a picture of a rabbit, but not the bare triangular figure for a picture of an object that has fallen over; to see that aspect demands imagination (§217, p. 218e).

**§§218–224 (pp. 218e–220e): organization, technique, concept modified.**
- A aspects are not essentially three-dimensional and are not connected with the possibility of illusion in the same way as cube or step aspects (§218, pp. 218e–219e).
- Can I see the schematic cube as a paper box, a tin box? What ought I to say if someone assured me he could? I can draw a conceptual boundary here. Yet think of the word "felt" in connection with a picture (§219, p. 219e).
- "Organizational aspects": when the aspect changes, parts of the picture belong together which before did not (§220, p. 219e). Teaching a child to take dots together or let them belong together: these must originally have had another meaning for him than seeing in this way or that. This is a remark about concepts, not teaching methods (§221, p. 219e).
- The words "Now I am seeing this as the apex" cannot so far have significance for a learner who has only just met apex and base. Wittgenstein says he does not mean this as an empirical proposition. Only of someone capable of certain applications of the figure with facility would one say he saw it now this way, now that. "The substratum of this experience is the mastery of a technique" (§222, p. 219e).
- How odd for this to be the logical condition of an experience: you do not say one has toothache only if one is capable of doing something. From this it follows that we cannot be dealing with the same concept of experience; it is a different, related one (§223, p. 219e). Only of someone who can do, has learnt, is master of such-and-such does it make sense to say he has this experience. The concept of seeing is modified here (§224, pp. 219e–220e). We talk, we produce utterances, and only later get a picture of their life (§224, p. 220e).

*Intuition note.* Having toothache does not require any capability. Seeing the triangle's corner as the apex makes sense only of someone who can use apexes and bases. So for such aspects the concept of seeing is modified, as Wittgenstein says. For A aspects he finds no such condition (§216).

**§§225–236 (pp. 220e–221e): expression, sensing, physiology.**
- Wittgenstein asks how I could see that a posture was hesitant before I knew it was a posture and not the anatomy of the creature, and whether that only means I could not then use a concept that does not refer solely to what is visual. Couldn't I nonetheless have a purely visual concept of that hesitant posture, that timid face? Such a concept would be comparable to major and minor, which have emotive value but can be used solely to describe a perceived structure (§§225–226, p. 220e).
- "Sad" applied to a stick-figure face characterizes the grouping of lines in an oval; applied to a human being it has a different but related meaning. A sad expression is not thereby similar to the feeling of sadness (§227, p. 220e).
- I can only see, not hear, red and green, but to the extent to which I can see sadness, I can also hear it. Does he hear the plaint in a plaintive melody? Some would answer "Of course", others "not really"; but it is possible to discern conceptual differences (§§228–230, p. 220e).
- We react to a timid expression differently from someone who does not recognize it as timid. Wittgenstein does not want to say that this reaction is felt in muscles and joints and is the sensing; what we have is a modified concept of sensing (§231, p. 220e).
- Is someone blind to the expression of a face defective in eyesight? This is not simply a question for physiology; "the physiological is a symbol of the logical" (§232, pp. 220e–221e). What someone who senses the solemnity of a melody perceives is nothing that could be conveyed by repeating what was heard (§233, p. 221e).
- A cipher can be seen as a correct or a faulty letter of a foreign alphabet, depending on the fiction around it; there is a close kinship with experiencing the meaning of a word (§234, p. 221e). Seeing the sign in this context is almost as if an echo of a thought; he says one would like to say "The echo of a thought in sight" (§235, p. 221e).
- Imagined physiological explanation: the eyes scan the figure along a path, patterns of eye movement alternate (A aspects), some patterns are physiologically impossible. The interlocutor realizes it is a kind of seeing. Wittgenstein: you have now introduced a new, physiological criterion for seeing. This can conceal the old problem but not solve it. The purpose of the remark was to bring out what happens when a physiological explanation is offered: the psychological concept hangs out of reach of it, and this makes the nature of the problem clearer (§236, p. 221e).

**§§237–246 (pp. 221e–223e): duration of being struck.**
- Wittgenstein says he would like to say that what lights up here lasts only as long as he is occupied with the observed object in a particular way, but he marks this "I'd like to say" and asks whether it is so: how long am I struck by a thing, how long is it new to me (§237, p. 221e).
- There is a physiognomy in the aspect which then fades; it is almost as if a face were there which at first I imitate and then accept without imitating. Isn't this explanation enough, or is it too much (§238, p. 222e)?
- That the likeness struck me for a few minutes can mean the face changed, or that I stopped being struck. Asked how long one was conscious of the likeness, answers look like: I soon stopped thinking about it, or it struck me again from time to time (§§239–240, p. 222e).
- Am I conscious of an object's depth the whole time I see it? Put in the third person, the question shows that knowing what it is to feel pain continuously only confuses. Either answer (continuously; only from time to time) strikes Wittgenstein as resting on a false foundation; it is different if he says the object sometimes seems flat, sometimes three-dimensional (§241, p. 222e).
- Looking at a flower without being conscious of its colour: one can imagine a significant context. "He looked at it without seeing it" is a thing that exists, but what is the criterion? There is a variety of cases (§242, p. 222e). Do not wonder what is going on in the eyes or brain (§243, p. 223e).
- When a likeness strikes me and the striking fades, what can I recall? My own facial expression and what I said aloud or to myself. Is this what being struck is? No: these are the manifestations of being struck, but they are what happens (§244, p. 223e). Being struck is not looking plus thinking; many of our concepts cross here (§245). Thinking and talking in the imagination are different concepts (§246, p. 223e).

**§§247–250 (p. 223e): aspect versus property; seeing versus interpreting.**
- The colour and shape in the visual impression correspond to the object's colour and shape. What I perceive in the lighting up of an aspect is not a property of the object but "an internal relation between it and other objects" (§247, p. 223e).
- Do I really see something different each time, or only interpret differently? Wittgenstein is inclined to say the former, and asks why. To interpret is to think, to do something; seeing is a state (§248, p. 223e).
- We form hypotheses when we interpret, and they may prove false. Seeing this figure as something can be verified as little as (or only in the same sense as) seeing a bright red; so there is a similarity in the use of "see" in the two contexts (§249, p. 223e). "Let the use teach you the meaning" of "state of seeing" (§250, p. 223e).

**§§251–257 (p. 224e): closing remarks.**
- We find certain things about seeing puzzling because we do not find the whole business of seeing puzzling enough (§251). A person looking at a photograph does not feel the lack of a third dimension; a stereoscope looks three-dimensional in a different way again. That we see three-dimensionally with two eyes is anything but a matter of course (§§252–253, p. 224e).
- The concept of an aspect is related to the concept of imagination. Wittgenstein asks whether it doesn't take imagination to hear something as a variation on a theme, and adds that one does perceive something in so hearing it (§254, p. 224e). One can produce a proof in one's imagination (§255).
- Seeing an aspect and imagining are subject to the will: there is the order "Imagine this!" and "Now see the figure like this!", but not "Now see this leaf green!" (§256, p. 224e).
- Could there be human beings lacking the ability to see something as something? We call this "aspect-blindness" and consider what might be meant (a conceptual investigation). The aspect-blind man is supposed not to see A aspects change, but is supposed still to be able to pick out figures containing a black cross and to execute orders like "Bring me something that looks like this!". Whether he is blind to the similarity between two faces Wittgenstein does not want to settle (§257, p. 224e).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four dots at (x,y): (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1). A "copy" lists these coordinates.
- Organization R ("rows belong together"): groups {(0,0),(1,0)} and {(0,1),(1,1)}. Sum of x-values per group: 0+1 = 1 and 0+1 = 1, so (1, 1).
- Organization C ("columns belong together"): groups {(0,0),(0,1)} and {(1,0),(1,1)}. Sum of x-values: 0+0 = 0 and 1+1 = 2, so (0, 2).
The copy is identical, but the same task gives (1, 1) under R and (0, 2) under C. This is our illustration of the idea in §§220–222 that an organization shows in how the figure is used.

## Concepts
- [[aspect-seeing]] (new) — noticing or seeing a figure "as" something though it is unchanged; Wittgenstein treats it as a conceptual, not a causal, question (pp. 203e, 214e)
- [[lighting-up-of-an-aspect]] (new) — the sudden aspect change, expressed as a new perception and an unchanged one, as against continuous seeing (pp. 204e, 206e)
- [[picture-object]] (new) — a figure treated like the thing depicted (picture-face, picture-rabbit) (p. 204e)
- [[inner-picture-private-object]] (new) — the "inner" thing the impression is supposed to be; Wittgenstein finds the model misleading (pp. 206e, 218e)
- [[fine-shades-of-behaviour]] (new) — subtle behaviour, such as gestures, that can serve as criteria for seeing in a given way (pp. 213e, 215e)
- [[conceptual-vs-causal-question]] (new) — the problem is conceptual, not a matter of what produces the experience physiologically (pp. 203e, 214e, 221e)
- [[aspect-blindness]] (new) — a hypothetical inability to see something as something, used for conceptual investigation (p. 224e)
- [[internal-relation]] (new) — what is perceived in the lighting up of an aspect is not a property of the object but a relation to other objects (p. 223e)
- [[meaning-as-use]] — the instruction to let the use teach the meaning of "state of seeing" (p. 223e)
- [[language-game]] — the primitive language-game children are taught needs no justification (p. 210e)

## Questions this raises
- If "seeing as" needs a mastered technique for some aspects, which aspects (A aspects, duck–rabbit) count as seeing, and where does the line go?
- If the aspect-blind man can still pick out crosses and similar things, what exactly does he lack?
- Does the criteria-based view of aspect-reports extend to other private-seeming reports?
- Why does Wittgenstein call interpreting "thinking" and seeing "a state", when elsewhere the concept of seeing is called "modified"?
