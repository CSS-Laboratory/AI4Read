# PPF sections i–x: emotion, meaning-experience, belief, Moore's paradox

## L0 — One line
In these remarks Wittgenstein asks whether psychological words such as hope, meaning something, believing and being afraid get their sense from their use in human practices and contexts, rather than from inner objects, inner pictures or inner observation.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Wittgenstein asks what we are doing when we speak of emotions, experiencing a meaning, mental images, sensations and belief. He sets against the picture of an inner object that we observe or describe the questions: in what context is it said, what follows, what is it used for? He says hope belongs to a complicated form of life (life lived with language), that the experience of hearing or saying a word is not its meaning, that *I'm afraid* may be a cry, and that *I believe p* is used like the assertion *p*, though supposing that I believe p is not used like supposing p (Moore's paradox). Many remarks are questions.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond the idea of a "language-game" (an activity in which words have a use). Other terms are glossed in the bullets below.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Hope is tied to a complicated form of life, the life of those who have mastered a language. Grief is a pattern in the "tapestry of life". Wittgenstein leaves the dog case as a question (§§1–2, p. 184e).
- Wittgenstein questions the idea that "experiencing a meaning" is just another kind of experiencing, like having a mental image but with a different content. He notes that a meaning which occurs to someone and is not forgotten lets him use the word, and that its occurring was the beginning of his knowing it (§§10–14, pp. 185e–186e).
- For an image, what makes it an image *of him* is not pictorial likeness. For the utterance, it is nothing in it or simultaneous with it. If you want to know whom he meant, ask him (§17, p. 187e).
- He asks whether psychology's data are behaviour, in particular utterances that are not about behaviour. He asks whether a "tacit presupposition" (an unspoken assumption) is really present when behaviour reports are treated as expressions of the mental (§§28–33, pp. 188e–189e).
- He denies that the meaning of a word is the experience one has in hearing or uttering it. He argues that a "corona" of uses before the mind cannot explain intentionality. He treats the "if-feeling" (the feeling said to go with the word "if") as something to be seen in the circumstances where it occurs (§§35–51, pp. 190e–192e).
- In the finger example, "knowing" the movement only means being able to describe it. This bears on the supposed "kinaesthetic" (bodily-movement) sensations. In §62 he says he is looking for the grammatical difference (§§56–66, pp. 194e–195e).
- *I'm afraid* can be a cry, an admission or the description of a state, depending on context. Wittgenstein asks whether a cry, though not a description, does the service of a description of the psychological (§§72–85, pp. 196e–198e).
- He contrasts first-person present *I believe* with the past tense and with other persons. In an imagined language with no first-person *I'm inclined to say*, Moore's paradox would not exist (§§86–110, pp. 199e–202e).

## L3 — Walkthrough
Remark numbers are those of this edition. Roman numerals are the author's section numbers. The source does not label speakers. "Interlocutor" and "Objector" are my labels for views Wittgenstein examines. Remarks put as questions are kept as questions. Example sentences are in italics.

### i (§§1–6, p. 184e)
- **§1.** One can imagine an animal angry, fearful, sad, joyful, startled. W asks: hopeful? And why not? A dog believes his master is at the door. Can he also believe his master will come the day after tomorrow? W asks what the dog cannot do here, how he himself does it, and what answer he is supposed to give; no answer is stated. He then asks whether only those who can talk can hope, and answers: only those who have mastered the use of a language. The manifestations of hope are modifications of this complicated form of life. In a parenthesis he says that a concept that points to a feature of human handwriting has no application to beings that do not write.
- **§2.** "Grief" describes a pattern that recurs, with variations, in the tapestry of life. If a man's bodily expression of sorrow and of joy alternated with the ticking of a clock, we would not have the characteristic course of the pattern of sorrow or of joy.
- **§§3–4.** *For a second he felt violent pain* is fine. W asks why *For a second he felt deep grief* sounds odd, and whether it is only because it so seldom happens. *But don't you feel grief now?* may get an affirmative answer, which does not make grief more like sensation. That question was a temporal and personal one, not the logical one.
- **§§5–6.** *I must tell you: I am frightened* (or *it horrifies me*) can be said in a smiling tone. An interlocutor asks whether he does not feel it, and how else he knows it. W: even if it is a report, he does not learn it from his feelings. Suppose the feelings are produced by gestures of horror, the words *it horrifies me* are themselves such a gesture, and hearing and feeling them belongs among the other feelings. W asks why the wordless gesture should be the ground of the verbal one.

*Intuition note.* Hope is linked to the complicated life of those who use language. Grief is described as a pattern in the tapestry of life, and an expression that merely alternated with a clock would not show that pattern. The dog case is left as a question.

### ii (§§7–16, pp. 185e–186e)
- **§7.** *When I heard this word, it meant … to me* refers to a point in time and to a way of using the word. *I was then going to say …* refers to a point in time and to an action. The essential references of an utterance are those that would make us translate an unfamiliar kind of expression into our customary form (p. 185e).
- **§8.** Someone unable to say that *till* can be a verb and a conjunction, or to build sentences with either, could not do schoolroom exercises. But a schoolboy is not asked to take the word one way or another out of any context, or to report how he has taken it.
- **§9.** *The rose is red* is senseless if *is* means "is identical with". W asks whether the sense then disintegrates. A person is taught how to apply a sentence by being told the meaning of each word. If *is* is explained as the sign of identity, he does not learn how to use *The rose is red*. Yet there is something right about "disintegration of the sense": if you want to say *Hail!* expressively, you had better not think of hailstones.
- **§10.** Interlocutor: in both cases one is experiencing something, only something different. W asks what the content of the experience of imagining is (a picture or a description) and what the content of the experience of a meaning is, and says he does not know how to answer. If the interlocutor's remark has any sense, it is that the two concepts stand to each other as red does to blue, and that is wrong (p. 185e).
- **§§11–14.** W asks whether one can keep hold of understanding a meaning as one keeps hold of a mental image. *The whole plan came to my mind at a stroke and stayed there like that for five minutes* sounds odd. One would like to think that what struck me and what stayed in my mind cannot have been the same (§12). *Now I've got it!* was followed by a start and then by presenting the plan. A picture might be what stayed, but the exclamation did not mean "I've got a picture" (§13). If a meaning has occurred to someone and he has not forgotten it, he can use the word in such-and-such a way. Its occurring was the beginning of his knowing it. W asks how this is like experiencing an image (§14, p. 186e).
- **§§15–16.** In *Mr Scot is not a Scot* the first word is a proper name and the second a common name. W asks whether what goes on in my mind at the two must differ (unless I speak "parrot-wise"). Trying to switch the meanings takes effort; I blink as I try to display the right meaning to myself. W asks whether I also display meanings in ordinary use. With the switched meanings the sense disintegrates for me, not for the hearer. Interlocutor: something else takes place in the usual case. W: what takes place is not this displaying of the meaning (p. 186e).

*Intuition note.* §7 treats *it meant … to me* as pointing to a time and a way of using the word. §§10–16 repeatedly ask whether something inner, like an image, has to be there. W's questions do not find such a thing.

### iii (§§17–18, p. 187e)
- **§17.** W asks what makes my mental image of him into an image of him. It is not pictorial likeness. The same question applies to *I see him now vividly before me*: what makes this utterance into one about him? Nothing in it or simultaneous with it ("behind it"). "If you want to know whom he meant, ask him!" One can also visualize or draw a face without knowing whose it is.
- **§18.** Suppose someone draws while imagining, or instead of imagining, even in the air ("motor imagery"). Asked *Whom does that represent?*, his answer would be decisive, as if he had given a verbal description, which can also take the place of the image.

### iv (§§19–26, p. 187e)
- **§19.** *I believe that he is suffering.* W asks whether he also believes that he is not an automaton. Only reluctantly could he use the word *believe* in both contexts. (Believing he suffers but being certain he is not an automaton is called nonsense.)
- **§20.** Suppose I say of a friend *He isn't an automaton*. W asks what information this conveys, and to whom. To a human being meeting him in ordinary circumstances, what information could it give? At the very most, that this man always behaves like a human being and not occasionally like a machine.
- **§21.** *I believe that he is not an automaton*, just like that, so far makes no sense.
- **§22.** "My attitude towards him is an attitude towards a soul." W adds that he is not of the opinion that he has a soul.
- **§23.** Religion teaches that the soul can exist when the body has disintegrated. W asks whether he understands this. He answers that of course he does, since he can imagine various things and pictures have been painted. He asks why such a picture should be only an imperfect rendering and not do the same service as the spoken doctrine, and says the service is what counts.
- **§24.** He asks: if the picture of thoughts in the head can force itself upon us, why not much more that of thoughts in the mind or soul?
- **§25.** "The human body is the best picture of the human soul."
- **§26.** *When you said that, I understood it in my heart*, said while pointing to the heart: one means the gesture, and is not aware of using a mere picture. It is not a picture we choose, not a simile, yet it is a graphic expression.

### v (§§27–34, pp. 188e–189e)
- **§27.** Suppose we watch a point of light on a screen. Important inferences of the most varied kinds could be drawn from its behaviour: path, amplitude and wavelength, velocity, discontinuities, curvature and more. Any of these might be the only feature to interest us. If we were interested in several, each might yield special information, different in kind from the rest. This is how it is with the behaviour of man (p. 188e).
- **§§28–29.** W asks: does psychology treat behaviour, not the mind? What does the psychologist report and observe? Isn't it the behaviour of people, in particular their utterances? But these are not about their behaviour. *I noticed that he was out of humour*: behaviour or state of mind? W's answer: both, "not side by side, however, but about the one via the other".
- **§30.** A doctor asks how he is feeling; the nurse says *He is groaning*. W asks whether there need be any question, for them, whether the groaning is genuine. He asks whether they might not conclude *If he groans, we must give him more analgesic* "without suppressing a middle term". He asks whether what counts is the service to which they put the description of behaviour.
- **§31.** Objector: they make a tacit presupposition. W: then playing our language-game always rests on a tacit presupposition.
- **§32.** I describe a psychological experiment, then say it is a scene in a play; now everything has changed. The objector observes that if the experiment were described in a psychology book, the description would be understood as an expression of something mental precisely because it is presupposed that the subject is not taking us in, has not learnt the replies by heart, and so on. The objector asks: so we are making a presupposition? W asks whether we would ever really say *Naturally I am presupposing that …*, or whether we do not say it only because the other person already knows (p. 189e).
- **§33.** W asks whether a presupposition exists when a doubt exists. Doubt may be entirely lacking. "Doubting has an end."
- **§34.** There is a similarity to how 'physical object' and 'sense impressions' stand to each other: two language-games with complicated mutual relations. Reducing them to a simple formula goes wrong.

*Intuition note.* In §§30–33 W asks, without settling it, whether a hidden extra premise is needed when behaviour reports are used as news about someone's inner life. He links the question to whether doubt exists.

### vi (§§35–51, pp. 190e–192e)
- **§35.** Suppose someone said that every familiar word carries an atmosphere, a "corona" of faintly indicated uses, like shadowy scenes around each figure in a painting. W says: take this assumption very seriously; then it turns out that it cannot explain intentionality. If the possible uses are before our minds, that goes just for us, yet we communicate with others without knowing whether they have these experiences too (p. 190e).
- **§36.** What would we reply to someone who said that understanding, or knowing how to play chess, was an inner process? We would say that when we want to know if he can play chess, we aren't interested in anything inside him. If he retorts that this is just what interests us, we draw his attention to the criteria for his ability and also to the criteria for 'inner states'. Even if someone had an ability only when and only as long as he had a particular feeling, the feeling would not be the ability (p. 190e).
- **§37.** The meaning of a word is not the experience one has in hearing or uttering it, and the sense of a sentence is not a complex of such experiences. "The sentence is composed of the words, and that is enough."
- **§38.** Interlocutor: each word can have a different character in different contexts, yet there is a single character it always has, a face. W: a face in a painting looks at us too.
- **§§39–43.** W asks: are you sure there is a single if-feeling? Have you tried the word in many contexts (§39)? Suppose a man says *if* and *but* feel the same to him. W asks whether we may not believe him. We might think it strange and say he does not play our game. If he used the words as we do, W asks, wouldn't we think he understood them as we do (§40, p. 191e)? One misjudges the psychological interest of the if-feeling if one takes it as the obvious correlate of a meaning. It needs to be seen in the special circumstances in which it occurs (§41). W asks whether a person never has the if-feeling unless he utters *if*, and why one regards it as a matter of course that only this word has this atmosphere (§42). The if-feeling is not a feeling which accompanies the word *if* (§43).
- **§§44–49.** The if-feeling should be comparable to the feeling a musical phrase gives (one says *Here it is as if a conclusion were being drawn*, or makes a gesture) (§44). Can the feeling be separated from the phrase? It is not the phrase itself, since someone can hear the phrase without it (§45). Is it similar to the "expression" with which the phrase is played (§46)? The accompanying movement and feeling would not be recognized in a different context; they are quite empty except while singing this passage (§47). The expression in *I sing it with a quite particular expression* cannot be separated from the passage. It is a different concept, a different game (§48). The experience is this passage played like this (§49, p. 192e).
- **§50.** "An atmosphere that is inseparable from its object … is no atmosphere." Closely associated things seem to fit one another. W asks in what way they seem to fit, and gives as an example that we cannot imagine the man with this name, face and handwriting not to have produced these works but others. He then asks: we cannot imagine it? Do we try?
- **§51.** W says he could easily imagine what a picture *Beethoven writing the Ninth Symphony* would show. For Goethe writing it he could imagine nothing that would not be embarrassing and ridiculous.

### vii (§§52–55, p. 193e)
- **§52.** In the dream language-game people tell certain incidents on waking, are taught *I dreamt*, and later answer *Did you dream anything last night?* with yes, no, or a narrative. W says he assumes he does not dream himself. He asks whether he must make an assumption about whether they are deceived by memory, what sense and what interest this question has, whether we ever ask it when someone tells a dream, and if not, whether it is because we are sure his memory won't have deceived him.
- **§53.** W asks whether this means it is nonsense ever to raise the question whether dreams really take place during sleep or are a memory phenomenon. He answers: it will depend on how the question is used.
- **§54.** W asks whether *It seems that the mind can give a word meaning* isn't like saying *It seems that the carbon atoms in benzene lie at the corners of a hexagon*. But this is no seeming; it is a picture.
- **§55.** The evolution of the higher animals and of man, and the awakening of consciousness at a particular stage: the picture is of a dark world, full of vibrations, until man opens his seeing eye and there is light. Our language describes a picture. How it is to be used is obscure, and it must be explored if we want to understand the sense of our words. But the picture seems to spare us this work, since it already points to a use, and this is how it takes us in.

### viii (§§56–66, pp. 194e–195e)
- **§§56–59.** "My kinaesthetic sensations apprise me of the movements and positions of my limbs." W makes his index finger swing lightly; he hardly feels it or not at all, perhaps a little tension in the tip, yet he can describe the movement exactly (§56). Objector: you must feel it, otherwise you wouldn't know. W: "knowing" it only means being able to describe it. One may tell a sound's direction because it affects one ear more strongly, without feeling this in the ears (§57). The same goes for the idea that some feature of a pain-sensation apprises us of its location, or some feature of a memory-image of its time (§58). Yet a sensation can apprise us of a limb's position, and the character of a pain of its location (§59).
- **§§60–66.** What is the criterion for a sense impression's apprising me of shape and colour (§60)? An interlocutor says a feeling is inexplicable and special. W: it must be possible to teach the use of the words (§61). He is looking now for the grammatical difference (§62). I tell someone *Do this, and then you'll get it* while holding my arm in a position. W asks whether this is a description of a feeling and when he will say the other has understood. The other will have to give a further description afterwards (§63). W asks whether there can, or must, be doubt here if a feeling is meant (§64). *This looks so; this tastes so; this feels so*: *this* and *so* must be differently explained (§65). A 'feeling' has a particular interest for us: its degree of intensity, its location, and how far one can be submerged by another (§66).

### ix (§§67–85, pp. 196e–198e)
- **§§67–71.** If someone observes his own grief, which sense does he use? Observing does not produce what is observed; W calls this a conceptual statement (§67). A touch that hurt yesterday no longer does so today; that is a result of observation (§68). One says someone is observing, roughly, when he puts himself in a favourable position to receive impressions, to describe what they apprise him of (§69). Someone trained to emit a sound at the sight of red, another at yellow, is not yet describing objects by colour; a description is a representation of a distribution in a space (§70). Saying *Red!* on glimpsing a red object is not a description (§71).
- **§§72–76.** W asks whether the words *I'm afraid* are a description of a state of mind (§72). If asked whether it was a cry, a wish to tell how I feel, or an observation on my present state, could I always give a clear answer? Could I never give one (§73, p. 196e)? W lists six variants, e.g. *No, no! I'm afraid!* and *I'm afraid. I am sorry to have to admit it*, each with its own tone of voice and context (§74, p. 197e). Asking what *I'm afraid* really means, or what I aim at, brings no answer, or only an inadequate one. The question is in what sort of context it occurs (§75). No answer comes from repeating the utterance while watching oneself. In a concrete case I can ask why I said it and answer, but not by observing what accompanied the speaking; my answer would supplement or paraphrase the earlier utterance (§76).
- **§§77–81.** W says that to explain fear at a single showing he would act it. Could hope be shown this way? Hardly; and belief (§§77–78)? Describing my state of mind is something I do in a particular context. Is it surprising that I use the same expression in different games (§79)? In a funeral oration *We mourn our …* expresses mourning, not communication; in a prayer at the grave the same words would be a kind of communication (§81, p. 198e).
- **§§82–85.** W asks whether the problem is this: a cry, which cannot be called a description and is more primitive than any description, nevertheless does the service of a description of the psychological (§82). A cry is not a description, but there are intermediate cases, and *I am afraid* may approximate more or less to a cry (§83). We do not invariably say someone is complaining because he says he is in pain; the words may be a cry of complaint or something else (§84). If *I'm afraid* is not always like a cry of complaint but sometimes is, why should it always be a description of a state of mind (§85)?

*Intuition note.* The same words may do different jobs in different contexts (§§74, 79). W proposes asking in what context *I'm afraid* occurs, rather than looking inward to see what it "really" means (§75).

### x (§§86–110, pp. 199e–202e)
- **§§86–89.** W asks how people came to use *I believe …*; did they notice a phenomenon of believing, observe themselves and others, and so discover believing (§86)? Moore's paradox: *I believe that this is the case* is used like the assertion *This is the case*, yet the supposition that I believe it is not used like the supposition that it is the case (§87). So it seems as if the assertion *I believe* were not the assertion of what is supposed in the supposition *I believe* (§88). *I believe it's going to rain* has a similar use to *It's going to rain*, but *I believed then that it was going to rain* is not similar to *It rained then*. Interlocutor: *I believed* must say the very same in the past as *I believe* in the present. W: "Surely $\sqrt{-1}$ must mean just the same for $-1$ as $\sqrt{1}$ means for $1$!" This signifies nothing at all (§89, p. 199e).
- **§§90–93.** Interlocutor: in *I believe …* I describe my state of mind, but indirectly assert the fact believed, as I may describe a photograph to describe what it is a photograph of. W: then I must be able to go on to say the photograph is a good one, e.g. *I believe it's raining, and my belief is reliable, so I rely on it*. In that case my belief would be a kind of sense impression (§90). One can mistrust one's senses but not one's belief (§91). A verb meaning 'to believe falsely' would not have a meaningful first person present indicative (§92). W says it is not a matter of course but a most remarkable thing that *believe*, *wish*, *want* display all the grammatical forms of *cut*, *chew*, *run* (§93).
- **§§94–98.** A report can be given a turn so that it informs the hearer about the speaker, as when a teacher examines a pupil (§94, p. 200e). Suppose *I believe* is introduced as a prefix to reports that give information about the speaker; then no uncertainty need attach to it, and *I believe …, and it isn't so* would be a contradiction (§95). *I believe …* throws light on my state, and inferences about my conduct can be drawn from it; so there is a similarity to manifestations of emotion and mood (§96). If *I believe it is so* throws light on my state, so does *It is so*; the sign *I believe* can at most hint at it (§97). Imagine a language where *I believe it is so* is expressed only by the tone of *It is so*. There they say *He is inclined to say …*, and there is the supposition *Suppose I were inclined etc.*, but no utterance like *I'm inclined to say*. Moore's paradox would not exist in this language, but a verb would lack one form. W adds that one can predict one's own future action in expressing one's intention (§98).
- **§§99–105.** I say *He seems to believe …* of someone else and others say it of me. W asks why I never say it of myself, and whether I do not see and hear myself; one might say that (§99). Interlocutor: one feels conviction within oneself and does not infer it from one's words. W: what is true is that one does not infer one's conviction from one's words, nor from the actions that arise from it (§100, p. 200e). W is tempted to cast around for a variant first-person present indicative (§101). A picture under examination: believing is a persisting state of mind, a disposition, shown to me in others by behaviour and words. About my own case I would then have to attend to myself, listen to myself talking and infer from what I say (§102, p. 201e). My attitude to my own words is wholly different from that of others; I could find the variant conjugation if I could say *I seem to believe* (§103). If I listened to the words issuing from my mouth, I could say someone else was speaking out of it (§104). *Judging from my words, this is what I believe* would make sense in some circumstances. Then someone could say *It is raining and I don't believe it*, if we imagined behaviour suggesting two beings speaking through my mouth (§105).
- **§§106–110.** *Assuming I believe …* presupposes the whole ordinary grammar of *believe*. You would not know what you were assuming, or what would follow, if you were not familiar with its use (§106). *I say it will rain today* simply amounts to *It will rain today*. *He says it will rain* means roughly *He believes it will rain*. *Assuming I say it will rain* does not mean *Assuming it rains* (§107, pp. 201e–202e). "Different concepts touch here and run side by side for a stretch" (§108, p. 202e). The sentence *It's going to rain, but it won't* is misbegotten. W warns against saying that *It's going to rain* really means *I believe it will rain*, asking why not the other way round (§109). "Don't regard a hesitant assertion as an assertion of hesitancy" (§110).

*Intuition note.* W compares the use of *I believe it's going to rain* with the use of *It's going to rain* (§89), and warns against taking a hesitant assertion for an assertion of hesitancy (§110). In his imagined language (§98), with no first-person *I'm inclined to say*, there would be no Moore's paradox.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Test the claim "*I'm afraid* is always a description of a state of mind" (cf. §§72–85). Take five occasions, with the use judged by what happens next:
1. A shout as a snake appears: a cry (nothing to confirm or deny).
2. To a doctor, "I'm afraid of needles, sorry to admit it": an admission.
3. "I'm a bit less afraid than before": a report that invites comparison.
4. A scream before a jump: a cry.
5. "I'm afraid there is no milk": a polite regret, not about fear at all.

Tally: cries 2, admissions 1, reports 1, other 1, total 2 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 5. Only 1 of 5 occasions is a plain description of a mental state. So "always a description" fails, as §85 suggests.

## Concepts
- [[form-of-life]] (new) — the complicated human life of language use of which the manifestations of hope are modifications (p. 184e).
- [[language-game]] — the practice in which an expression has its use, such as reporting, dreams, or *I believe* (p. 188e).
- [[criteria]] (new) — what we point to in order to demonstrate someone's ability, or an 'inner state' (p. 190e).
- [[meaning-experience]] (new) — the supposed "experience of a meaning", which W questions as another kind of experiencing like an image with a different content (p. 185e).
- [[if-feeling]] (new) — the feeling said to go with *if*; W denies that it simply accompanies the word and says it should be seen in the circumstances where it occurs (p. 191e).
- [[expressive-utterance-vs-description]] (new) — *I'm afraid* can be a cry, an admission or a report; W asks whether a cry does the service of a description (pp. 197e–198e).
- [[moores-paradox]] (new) — *I believe p* is used like the assertion *p*, but supposing that I believe p is not used like supposing p (p. 199e).
- [[picture-takes-us-in]] (new) — a picture seems to fix a use and so spares us exploring use (p. 193e).

## Canonical instances
- [[moores-paradox]] — *It is raining and I don't believe it* (§105, p. 201e) and the tense asymmetry (§89, p. 199e).
- [[mr-scot]] — the proper-name/common-name switch (p. 186e).
- [[hail-hailstones]] — "disintegration of sense" (p. 185e).
- [[point-of-light]] — many features, many kinds of information, as with behaviour (p. 188e).
- [[doctor-nurse-groaning]] — behaviour reports used, W asks, "without suppressing a middle term" (p. 188e).
- [[benzene-picture]] — the benzene hexagon is "no seeming; it is a picture", offered as a parallel to the mind giving a word meaning (p. 193e).
- [[dream-language-game]] — telling and asking about dreams (p. 193e).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] The point of light with many measurable features (§27) ↔ measurement: which feature of behavioural data you extract depends on your interest, and different features carry different information.
- [structural-analogy] Cry vs report (§§82–85) ↔ speech acts: one sentence may express, report or complain depending on context.
- [metaphor] Believing falsely having no first-person present (§92) ↔ probability: a stated belief and its own truth are not independent in the speaker's first-person use.
