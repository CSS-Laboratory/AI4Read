# Private language and sensation (§§243–312)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein tries to show that sensation-words do not work by naming private objects that only the speaker can know: the diary case leaves no criterion of correct use, and such a private object drops out of how the word is used.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Wittgenstein asks whether there could be a language whose words name only the speaker's own immediate sensations, so that nobody else could understand it. He tests this with a diary in which the sign "S" marks a sensation (§258), and finds that the diarist would have no criterion, that is, no independent standard, of correct use. If sensation-words are modelled on "object and name", the private object drops out as irrelevant (§293). He suggests that remarks like "Sensations are private" look like facts but are grammatical, that is, about how words are used (§251). Asked if he is a behaviourist in disguise, he calls any fiction he speaks of grammatical; he does not want to deny mental processes (§§305–308).

**Prerequisites:** Ordinary talk of pain, memory and colour; the idea that a word's use is what we look at (the text explains "language-game" only by example). No maths.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A private language is introduced as one whose words are to refer to what only the speaker can know, so that no one else can understand it (§243, p. 95).
- Wittgenstein describes one possible way sensation-words are learned: they are tied to natural expressions of sensation and replace them. The verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it (§244, p. 95). If my words are tied to such expressions, my language is not private (§256, p. 98).
- Giving a name to a sensation presupposes much in the language already. The grammar of the word "pain" (the rules for how it is used) marks the place where a new word is put (§257, p. 98).
- In the diary case he finds no criterion of correctness for the diarist. One would like to say that whatever seems correct is correct, but that only means we cannot talk of "correct" here (§258, p. 99). Checking one imagined memory against another is like buying several copies of the same paper to confirm its story (§265, p. 100).
- In the manometer case and the case of the person who forgets what "pain" meant, a mistaken identification of the sensation makes no difference to the use, and he would like to say the supposed private item is like an idle wheel in a mechanism (§§270–271, p. 101). If the grammar of sensation-words is modelled on "object and name", the private object, like the beetle in the box, drops out as irrelevant (§293, pp. 106–107).
- Remarks like "only I can know whether I am in pain" have the form of empirical propositions, but §251 says what we fend off by "I can't imagine the opposite" is really grammatical. §295 asks whether "I know… only from my own case" is grammatical; the pictures we then see are, he says, illustrations of our grammar (pp. 96, 107).
- Only of a living human being and what behaves like one can we say it has sensations (§281, p. 103). Someone who knows what "pain" means but says he does not know whether this is pain makes a doubt that has no place in the language-game (§288, p. 105).
- Told that an inner process surely takes place in remembering, he says that what he denies is only that the picture of an inner process gives the correct idea of the use of "remember" (§305); asked if he is a behaviourist in disguise, he says any fiction he speaks of is a grammatical fiction (§307) (§§305–308, p. 109).

## L3 — Walkthrough
Remarks are cited as (§N, p. M). Wittgenstein often lets an interlocutor, a voice that objects, speak; I tag voices only where the text shows them. Remarks are grouped by number ranges; the author gives no headings here.

**§§243–245: the question.** A human being can encourage himself, give himself orders, obey, blame and punish himself, and ask himself a question and answer it. So one can imagine people who speak only in monologue. An explorer who listened might succeed in translating their language and so predict their actions (§243, p. 95). But is it conceivable that there be a language in which someone writes down or voices his inner experiences (feelings, moods) for his own use? A voice asks whether we cannot do this in ordinary language; the reply is that this is not what is meant. The words of this language are to refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations, so another person cannot understand it (§243, p. 95).

§244 asks how words refer to sensations. This is the same question as how a human being learns the meaning of sensation-names, for example "pain". One possibility: words are connected with primitive natural expressions of sensation and used in their place. A hurt child cries, and adults teach him exclamations and later sentences, that is, new pain-behaviour. **Interlocutor:** so "pain" really means crying? **Wittgenstein:** on the contrary, the verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it (§244, p. 95). §245 asks how one can even attempt to put language between the expression of pain and the pain (p. 95).

*Intuition note:* The first model of learning a pain-word runs through behaviour that everyone can see. If that is how it works, the word is public from the start.

**§§246–251: in what sense are sensations private?**
- **Interlocutor:** Only I can know whether I am really in pain; another can only surmise. **Wittgenstein:** In one way this is false, in another nonsense. If "know" is used as it is normally used, other people very often know when I am in pain (§246, p. 95).
- **Interlocutor:** Yes, but not with the certainty with which I know it. **Wittgenstein:** It cannot be said of me at all, except perhaps as a joke, that I know I am in pain; what is that supposed to mean, except perhaps that I am in pain? Other people cannot be said to learn of my sensations only from my behaviour, for I cannot be said to learn of them; I have them. This much is true: it makes sense to say that other people doubt whether I am in pain, but not to say it about myself (§246, pp. 95–96).
- "Only you can know if you had that intention" might be said to someone when explaining the word "intention". Then it means: this is how we use it. "Know" here means that an expression of uncertainty is senseless (§247, p. 96).
- "Sensations are private" is compared with "One plays patience by oneself" (§248, p. 96).
- The text asks whether we are over-hasty in assuming a baby's smile is not pretence, and notes that lying is a language-game that must be learned like any other (§249, p. 96). It asks why a dog cannot simulate pain. Even if a dog were taught to howl as if in pain, the right surroundings for real simulation would still be missing (§250, p. 96).
- §251 asks what we mean by "I can't imagine the opposite", for example when someone says my mental images are private or that only I can know whether I feel pain. This does not mean that my powers of imagination fail. The words fend off something whose form gives the illusion of an empirical proposition but which is really a grammatical one (p. 96).
- Example: "Every rod has a length" means something like: we call something "the length of a rod", but nothing "the length of a sphere". Can I imagine every rod having a length? I just imagine a rod, and that is all. This picture has a different role here from the one it has with "This table has the same length as the one over there", where I understand what a picture of the opposite would be. The picture that goes with the grammatical proposition could only show what is called the length of a rod, and what the opposite picture should be is left as a question (§251, p. 97).

*Intuition note:* Sentences like "only I can know my pain" have the form of facts about the mind. Wittgenstein says that what we fend off with "I can't imagine the opposite" is grammatical, and the rod example shows a case where there is no opposite picture to be had.

**§§252–255: identity and method.**
- To "This body has extension" we could answer "Nonsense!" but are inclined to say "Of course!" The text asks why (§252, p. 97).
- "Another person can't have my pains." What counts as a criterion of identity here? Compare what lets us speak of two physical objects as exactly the same, for example two chairs (§253, p. 97).
- In so far as it makes sense to say my pain is the same as his, it is also possible for us both to have the same pain. Siamese twins might feel pain in the same, not merely the corresponding, place (§253, p. 97).
- A person who strikes his chest and says another surely cannot have this pain has not defined a criterion of identity by stressing the word "this". The emphasis merely creates the illusion of a case where we know such a criterion and only need reminding of it (§253, p. 97).
- Replacing "the same" by "identical" is another typical expedient in philosophy. What we are tempted to say is not philosophy but its raw material (§254, pp. 97–98). Wittgenstein writes: "The philosopher treats a question; like an illness" (§255, p. 98).

**§§256–260: the diary of "S".**
- If my words for sensations are tied to my natural expressions, my language is not private; someone else might understand it as well as I do. But suppose I had no natural expression of sensation, only sensations, and simply associated names with them and used them in descriptions (§256, p. 98).
- A quoted view says that without pain-behaviour a child could not be taught the word "toothache". Suppose the child is a genius and invents a name. He could not make himself understood. Does he understand the name without being able to explain it? What would it mean that he "named his pain"? When one says he gave a name to his sensation, one forgets that, in Wittgenstein's words, "Much must be prepared in the language for mere naming to make sense." The grammar of "pain" is what has been prepared; it indicates the place where the new word is put (§257, p. 98).
- The case: I want a diary of the recurrence of a sensation. I associate it with the sign "S" and write it in a calendar on each day I have the sensation. A definition of the sign cannot be formulated. A voice replies that I can still give myself a kind of ostensive definition (one by pointing). How? Not in the ordinary sense; I speak or write the sign and concentrate my attention on the sensation, so as to point to it inwardly (§258, pp. 98–99).
- **Interlocutor:** This is done precisely by concentrating my attention, for in this way I commit to memory the connection between sign and sensation. **Wittgenstein:** "I commit it to memory" can only mean that this process brings it about that I remember the connection correctly in the future, and in the present case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: "whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct." And that only means that here we cannot talk of "correct" (§258, p. 99).
- The balance on which impressions are weighed is not the impression of a balance (§259, p. 99).
- Interlocutor: I believe this is the sensation S again. Reply: perhaps you believe that you believe it. Did the man who made the entry then make a note of nothing at all? A note has a function, and this "S" so far has none (§260, p. 99).

*Intuition note:* In the diary case Wittgenstein finds nothing beyond my present impression to say whether I followed a rule, and he says we cannot talk of "correct" here. In §265 he says justification consists in appealing to an independent authority.

**§§261–269: justification.**
- "Sensation" is a word of our common language, so its use needs a justification everybody understands. Saying "he has Something" does not help, because "has" and "something" also belong to our common language (§261, p. 99). An inarticulate sound is an expression only in a particular language-game, which now has to be described (p. 99).
- One might say: someone who has given himself a private explanation of a word must inwardly resolve to use it in a certain way. How does he resolve that? Does he invent the technique of applying the word or find it ready-made? The text asks this and leaves it open (§262, p. 99).
- **Interlocutor (in quotation marks):** Surely I can (inwardly) resolve to call THIS "pain" in the future. **A second quoted voice:** Is it certain that you have resolved this? Are you sure that concentrating your attention on your feeling was enough? **Wittgenstein (unquoted):** An odd question (§263, p. 100).
- §264 gives the view that once you know what the word signifies, you understand it and know its whole application. No reply appears on these pages (voice unclear) (p. 100).
- Wittgenstein imagines a dictionary-like table that exists only in the imagination and asks whether looking it up only in the imagination can be called a justification. **Interlocutor:** Well, yes, then it is a subjective justification. **Wittgenstein:** But justification consists in appealing to an independent authority (§265, p. 100).
- **Interlocutor:** But I can appeal from one memory to another, for example calling to mind how a timetable page looked. **Wittgenstein:** No; this procedure must actually call forth the correct memory. If the mental image of the timetable could not itself be tested for correctness, how could it confirm the first memory? It is as if someone bought several copies of today's morning paper to assure himself that what it said was true. Looking up a table in the imagination is no more looking up a table than the image of the result of an imagined experiment is the result of an experiment (§265, p. 100).
- §266 notes that the look of a clock can serve to determine the time in more than one way, and adds in brackets "looking at a clock in one's imagination". §267 imagines justifying the dimensions of an imagined bridge by imagined loading tests, and asks whether that would also be called justifying an imagined choice of dimensions (p. 100).
- Wittgenstein asks why my right hand cannot give my left hand money. It can put money into it; it can write a deed of gift and the left hand a receipt. But the further practical consequences would not be those of a gift: one asks what now follows. The same could be asked if a person had given himself a private explanation of a word by saying it and directing his attention at a sensation (§268, p. 101).
- There are criteria in a man's behaviour for his not understanding a word, for his thinking he understands it but not rightly, and for his understanding it correctly. In the second case one might speak of subjective understanding. Sounds that no one else understands but that I appear to understand might be called a "private language" (§269, p. 101).

**§§270–271: a use for "S".**
- Imagine a use for the entry "S" in the diary. I find from experience that whenever I have a particular sensation, a manometer shows my blood pressure rising. This lets me report that my blood pressure is rising without apparatus, a useful result (§270, p. 101).
- It now seems quite indifferent whether I have recognized the sensation correctly. Even if I regularly made a mistake in identifying it, that would make no difference. In his words: "This alone shows that the supposition of this mistake was merely sham." It is like a knob that looks adjustable but is a mere ornament not connected with the mechanism (§270, p. 101).
- What reason is there to call "S" the name of a sensation? Perhaps the way the sign is employed in this language-game (§270, p. 101).
- Take a person who could not remember what "pain" meant and called different things by that name, but used it according to the usual symptoms and presuppositions of pain, that is, as we all do. Here Wittgenstein would like to say: "a wheel that can be turned though nothing else moves with it" is not part of the mechanism (§271, p. 101).

*Intuition note:* In these remarks an item that makes no difference to the use of the word is compared with idle parts of a machine. Wittgenstein says "perhaps" about what does make "S" a sensation-name.

**§§272–280: private colour impressions.**
- §272 says the essential thing about private experience is that nobody knows whether other people have this or something else. It would be possible, though unverifiable, that one part of mankind had one visual impression of red and another part a different one (p. 102). The text gives no speaker tag (voice unclear).
- §273 asks whether "red" signifies something confronting us all, with each person needing another word for his own impression, or whether it signifies something known to all and in addition something known only to each. §274 says that saying "refers to" rather than "signifies" does not help us grasp the word's function, but it is a more psychologically apt expression for an experience in doing philosophy: a sidelong glance at my own colour impression (p. 102).
- When I say spontaneously "How blue the sky is!", the idea never crosses my mind that the impression belongs only to me. If I point, it is at the sky. I do not have the pointing-into-yourself feeling that often goes with "naming sensations" in thinking about the "private language" (§275, p. 102).
- It is as if we detached the colour impression from the object like a membrane, and this ought to arouse suspicion (§276, p. 102).
- When I mean the colour impression that (as I should like to say) belongs to me alone, I turn a different kind of attention on the colour and immerse myself in it (§277, pp. 102–103).
- "I know how the colour green looks to me" makes sense; but what use of the sentence is meant (§278, p. 103)? Compare "But I know how tall I am!" said while laying a hand on one's head (§279, p. 103).
- A quoted view (speaker unmarked) says a picture of an imagined stage set has a double function: it informs others, and for the painter it is also the picture of his image. Wittgenstein asks what right he has to speak of a second representation if these words were correctly used in the first case (§280, p. 103).

**§§281–288: who has sensations.**
- **Interlocutor:** Doesn't that amount to saying there is no pain without pain-behaviour? **Wittgenstein:** It amounts to this: only of a living human being and what resembles (behaves like) one can one say that it has sensations, sees, hears, is conscious or unconscious (§281, p. 103).
- **Interlocutor:** But in a fairy tale a pot can see and hear. **Wittgenstein:** Certainly, but it can also talk. Is it untrue or nonsensical to say a pot talks? It is not as simple as that. We do say of dolls that they are in pain, but this is a secondary use of the concept (§282, pp. 103–104).
- What gives us the idea that things can feel? Is it that I transfer an idea from my own case? Wittgenstein says he does not transfer it to stones or plants. He asks why a pain, even if I imagined turning to stone while in pain, should have a bearer at all. Only of what behaves like a human being can one say that it has pains (§283, p. 104).
- A stone seems unable to feel; a wriggling fly seems able to. Our attitude to the living and the dead differs, and all our reactions are different (§284, p. 104).
- Is it the body that feels pain? How is it decided? Something like this: if someone has a pain in his hand, the hand does not say so, and one does not comfort the hand but the sufferer, looking into his eyes (§286, p. 105). Pity, one may say, is one form of being convinced that someone else is in pain (§287, p. 105).
- **Interlocutor:** I surely cannot be mistaken here; it means nothing to doubt whether I am in pain. **Wittgenstein:** If someone said he did not know whether what he has is a pain, we would think, perhaps, that he does not know what the word "pain" means, and we would explain it, for example by pricking him with a pin. He might understand the explanation rightly, wrongly or not at all, and will show which by his use of the word (§288, p. 105).
- If he said he knows what "pain" means but not whether this, which he has now, is pain, we could only shake our heads. That expression of doubt has no place in the language-game. If expressions of sensation, that is human behaviour, are excluded, doubt looks legitimate, because then I need a criterion of identity for the sensation and error becomes possible (§288, p. 105).

**§§289–295: description, the beetle, and "my own case".**
- To use a word without a justification does not mean to use it wrongfully (§289, p. 106).
- I do not identify my sensation by criteria; I use the same expression. The language-game does not end with this; it begins with it. The word "describe" may trick us: describing my state of mind differs from describing my room (§290, p. 106).
- Descriptions are instruments for particular uses, like an engineer's drawing. Thinking of a description as a word-picture of the facts has something misleading about it (§291, p. 106).
- Do not always think that you read off what you say from the facts, depicting them according to rules; you would still have to apply the rule in the particular case without guidance (§292, p. 106).
- **The beetle.** If I say that I know what "pain" means only from my own case, must I not say the same of other people? Suppose everyone had a box with something in it called a "beetle", and no one could look into anyone else's box. Everyone might have something different, or something constantly changing, or nothing. If the word "beetle" nonetheless had a use, it would not be as the name of a thing. The thing in the box does not belong to the language-game at all; one can "divide through" by it, and "it cancels out, whatever it is" (§293, p. 106). That is to say, if we construe the grammar of the expression of sensation on the model of "object and name", the object drops out of consideration as irrelevant (§293, p. 107).
- If you say he sees a private picture, you have made an assumption about what he has before him, which means you could describe it more closely. If you admit you have no idea what it is, why say he has something at all? Compare saying of someone that he has something but you do not know whether it is money, debts or an empty till (§294, p. 107).
- What kind of proposition is "I know… only from my own case"? An empirical one? No. A grammatical one? Wittgenstein imagines everyone saying it; it might be a kind of exclamation, giving no information but still a picture. When we look into ourselves as we do philosophy we often see such a picture, a pictorial representation of our grammar: not facts but "illustrated turns of speech" (§295, p. 107).

*Intuition note:* The beetle remark allows that the box may hold something different for each person, or nothing. Either way, what is there plays no part in how the word works in the shared game.

**§§296–302: the "Something" and imagined pain.**
- **Interlocutor:** There is a Something that accompanies my cry of pain, and it is important and frightful. **Wittgenstein:** He asks to whom we are telling this and on what occasion (§296, p. 107).
- If water boils in a pot, steam comes out of the pot, and a picture of steam out of a picture of the pot. Wittgenstein asks what if one insisted that something must also be boiling in the picture of the pot (§297, p. 107).
- Wanting to say "This is the important thing" while pointing to the sensation is enough to show how much we are inclined to say something that is not informative (§298, p. 107). Being unable, in philosophical thought, to help saying something does not mean being forced into an assumption or having an immediate insight into a state of affairs (§299, p. 108).
- A quoted view says that not merely the picture of the behaviour but also the picture of the pain belongs to the language-game with "he is in pain". Wittgenstein says it is a misunderstanding to say that the picture of pain enters the language-game. Pain in the imagination is not a picture. Imagined pain certainly enters the game in a sense, only not as a picture (§300, p. 108). What is in the imagination is not a picture, but a picture can correspond to it (§301, p. 108).
- If one has to imagine someone else's pain on the model of one's own, it is none too easy, because I have to imagine pain I do not feel on the model of pain I do feel, not just move pain from one place to another. Pain-behaviour can indicate a painful place, but the sufferer is the person who manifests pain (§302, p. 108).

**§§303–312: belief, inner processes, the fly-bottle.**
- **Interlocutor:** I can only believe that someone else is in pain, but I know it if I am. **Wittgenstein:** One can resolve to say "I believe he is in pain" instead of "He is in pain", but that is all; it just exchanges one way of talking for another. He suggests trying, in a real case, to doubt someone else's fear or pain (§303, p. 108).
- **Interlocutor:** You surely admit there is a difference between pain-behaviour with pain and without it. **Wittgenstein:** Admit it? What greater difference could there be? **Interlocutor:** And yet you keep concluding that the sensation is a Nothing. **Wittgenstein:** Not at all; it is "not a Something, but not a Nothing either". The conclusion was only that a Nothing would render the same service as a Something about which nothing could be said (§304, pp. 108–109). The paradox disappears only if we make a radical break with the idea that language always functions in one way, always serves the same purpose of conveying thoughts (§304, p. 109).
- **Interlocutor:** You surely cannot deny that in remembering an inner process takes place. **Wittgenstein:** What gives the impression that we want to deny anything? What we deny is that the picture of an inner process gives the correct idea of the use of the word "remember". That picture stands in the way of seeing the use as it is (§305, p. 109). That a mental process of remembering has just taken place in me means no more than that I have just remembered. To deny the mental process would be to deny remembering (§306, p. 109).
- **Interlocutor:** Aren't you a behaviourist in disguise, saying everything except human behaviour is a fiction? **Wittgenstein:** If I speak of a fiction, it is a grammatical fiction (§307, p. 109).
- The problem starts with a first step that escapes notice: we talk of processes and states and leave their nature undecided, thinking we will know more later. That commits us to a certain conception of what it is to learn to know a process better, the decisive movement in a conjuring trick. The analogy then falls apart, and we have to deny a not yet understood process in a not yet explored medium. This looks as if we denied mental processes, which we naturally do not want to do (§308, pp. 109–110).
- What is your aim in philosophy? "To show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle" (§309, p. 110).
- I tell someone I am in pain, and he says it is not so bad. Doesn't that prove that he believes in something behind my utterance? Wittgenstein replies: "His attitude is proof of his attitude." He asks us to imagine the words and the reply replaced by instinctive noises and gestures (§310, p. 110).
- Of the question what greater difference there could be, §311 says that in the case of pain I believe I can privately exhibit the difference to myself, whereas the difference between a broken and an unbroken tooth can be shown to anyone. For the private exhibition it is enough to imagine pain, say by screwing up one's face. Wittgenstein asks whether you know that what you exhibit to yourself is pain and not a facial expression, and how you know beforehand what to exhibit. He concludes that this private exhibition is an illusion (p. 110).
- **§312** (first part only; voice unclear, since nothing is in quotation marks): the text asks whether the tooth and pain cases are not similar, since the visual impression corresponds to the sensation of pain, and says that I can exhibit the visual impression to myself as little or as well as the pain. Wittgenstein then asks us to imagine surfaces with patches that cause pain in our skin when touched, so that we would speak of pain-patches as we now speak of red patches. The supplied text ends here (p. 110).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two diarists, A and B, each write "S" on the days they have "the sensation". A privately attends to sensation X, B to sensation Y; neither can see the other's. A manometer shows blood pressure rising on days 2, 3 and 5 of five days. Both write "S" on exactly days 2, 3 and 5.
- Prediction for A: 3 days predicted rising, 3 correct; 2 days predicted not rising, 2 correct. Score = 5/5.
- Prediction for B: the same, 5/5.

Swap X and Y between A and B, and nothing in the diaries or in how "S" is used changes. In this case the inner content "divides through", as in the beetle box (§293). The use of "S" is tied to the public record (days and manometer), not to X or Y (cf. §270).

## Concepts
- [[private language argument]] — Wittgenstein's examination of a language whose words name what only the speaker can know; the diary case leaves the diarist with no criterion of correct use (§§243, 258, pp. 95, 98–99)
- [[language-game]] — an activity in which words are used; §290 says the game with a sensation-word does not end with the same expression but begins with it, and §261 says a sound is an expression only inside a particular game (pp. 99, 106)
- [[meaning as use]] (canon link; not stated in this unit) — perhaps the way "S" is employed is what makes it a sensation-name (§270, p. 101)
- [[rule-following problem]] (canon link; not stated in this unit) — one would still have to apply a rule in the particular case without guidance (§292, p. 106); in the diary case nothing besides my impression is available to check against (§258, p. 99)
- [[grammatical-proposition]] (new) — a remark with the form of an empirical proposition that is really grammatical, as with "I can't imagine the opposite" and the rod example (§251, pp. 96–97); §295 asks whether "I know… only from my own case" is one (p. 107)
- [[criterion-of-identity]] (new) — what settles that something is the same; Wittgenstein asks what counts as one for "my pains", and says stressing "this" does not supply one (§253, p. 97)
- [[beetle-in-a-box]] (new) — a private object that cancels out of the language-game if the word still has a use (§293, p. 106)
- [[logical form vs. grammatical form]] (canon link; not stated in this unit) — §290 suggests the word "describe" may trick us about use, and §246 examines what "know" means in "I know I'm in pain" (pp. 95–96, 106)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] No criterion of correctness for "S" (§258) ↔ measurement/validation: a label with no external ground-truth check cannot be called accurate or inaccurate.
- [structural-analogy] The beetle "divides through" (§293) ↔ identifiability: if swapping a hidden quantity changes no observable use, it is not pinned down by the data.
- [metaphor] The idle wheel (§271) ↔ model parts that do no predictive work.

## Questions this raises
- If "S" in the manometer case gets a use, does it become a sensation-name or just a useful sign? Wittgenstein says only "perhaps" the kind of use decides (§270, p. 101).
- How can he say the sensation is "not a Something, but not a Nothing either" (§304) without lapsing into either view he criticises?
- Which criteria for "living human being, and what resembles it" (§281) mark the limits of ascribing sensations, given the fairy-tale and doll cases (§282)?
- What is the "grammatical fiction" of §307 meant to cover, and how does it differ from an ordinary fiction?
