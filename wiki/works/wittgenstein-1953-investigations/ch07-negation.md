# Negation, meaning, expecting, willing, intending, meaning something (§§547–693)

## L0 — One line
For negating, expecting, intending, willing and "meaning someone", Wittgenstein says we should not look for an inner process that gives words life, but at the surrounding circumstances, the criteria, and the language-game.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Wittgenstein asks what we do when we negate, expect, hope, intend, will, or "mean someone". Philosophers are tempted to find an inner process or feeling that gives words their life. He suggests instead asking what counts as a criterion for such states, and looking at the circumstances and the language-game in which the words are used. He also says that calling meaning something a mental activity is wrong-headed (unless one wants to produce confusion), and that the grammar of "to mean" differs from that of "to think". The remarks carry his interest in use and language-games over to mental words, and they cast doubt on introspected "undertones" as explanations.

**Prerequisites:** the ideas of language-game and of meaning as use (from earlier in the book); no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Wittgenstein suggests, by a rhetorical question, that introspecting while negating does not reveal the essence of negation. He points to the rules for "not" (footnote (b)) and, for a doubled negation, to the technique of applying brackets or emphasis (§§547–549, 557, pp. 155e–157e).
- To say a word has two meanings, look at the rules of its use, e.g. the substitution rule that separates "is" as copula from "is" as equals sign. The "function" of a word comes out in operating the calculus (§§558–559, p. 157e–158e).
- States such as expecting, believing and hoping are to be understood through criteria, circumstances and surroundings, not through a feeling "inside" (§§572–588, pp. 160e–163e).
- The picture of a special "atmosphere" or "undertone" that gives words life arises from comparing different cases; the purpose of the inner-clock picture is to assimilate guessing the time to reading it off (§§592–610, pp. 163e–167e).
- Wittgenstein questions the thought that willing "is merely an experience" that comes when it comes; one might say voluntary movement is marked by the absence of surprise; and he finds an evident kinship but also a fundamental difference between predicting one's own act and predicting the reactions of metals (§§611–632, pp. 168e–171e).
- "I was going to say…" and similar reports are not read off a remembered inner process; he points instead to the background of thoughts, actions and circumstances. The language-game is primary, not an explanation by experiences (§§633–659, pp. 172e–176e).
- Surface grammar and depth grammar can differ; "to mean something" is grammatically unlike "to think", and calling it a mental activity is "wrong-headed", unless one is setting out to produce confusion (§§664, 693, pp. 176e–181e).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §§547–549 (and end of 546): negation as "mental activity" (pp. 155e–156e)
- §546 (cut off at the start of the unit): words can be hard to utter, e.g. renouncing something; "Words are also deeds" (p. 155e).
- §547: Negate something and watch yourself. Perhaps you inwardly shake your head. Is that process more interesting than writing a negation sign? "Do you now know the essence of negation?" The question is rhetorical; the answer it suggests is no (p. 155e).
- §548: One could picture wishing-that-not by crossing out the picture of the event. The word "not" looks like a clumsy expedient. "One supposes that in thought it happens differently" (p. 155e).
- §549: **Interlocutor:** "How can the word 'not' negate?" The sign is our occasion for doing something, perhaps very complicated. But what? "That is not said", as if it only needed hinting at because we already knew (p. 155e).
- Footnote (a), voice: an interlocutor-like claim that three negations giving a negation "must already be contained" in the single negation. Wittgenstein calls this the temptation to invent a myth of "meaning". He adds "there is something right about this": our nature is connected with both (p. 155e).
- Footnote (b): there can be no debate whether these rules are right for "not". Without the rules the word has as yet no meaning; if the rules change, it has another meaning (or none) (p. 155e).

*Intuition note.* We are inclined to suppose that in thought negation happens differently, as some inner process. Wittgenstein asks what such a process would add (§547), and points to the sign as our occasion for doing something (§549) and to the rules for "not" (footnote (b)).

### §§550–556: does "not" or "1" mean the same everywhere? (pp. 156e–157e)
- Negation is a gesture of exclusion, but we use such a gesture in many cases (§550). Whether the negation in "Iron does not melt…" is the same as in "Two times two is not five" is not to be decided by introspection (§551, p. 156e).
- "1" in "This rod is 1 metre long" and "Here is 1 soldier": nothing becomes evident while uttering them. Asked, one would perhaps say "Of course I mean the same: one!". But if the question is framed as measure versus number, one will answer that the meanings differ (§§552–553, p. 156e).
- Imagined people with a "more primitive" logic, in which negation is applied only to certain sentences, perhaps those not yet containing a negation; W. also asks us to think of other ways of expressing negation, such as pitch (§554). Whether their negation means the same as ours is analogous to whether "5" means the same to people whose number series ends at 5 (§555, p. 156e).
- §556: a language with negation words "X" (doubling gives affirmation) and "Y" (doubling gives emphatic negation). Three answers to "same meaning?": (a) different uses, so different meanings, but single-use sentences have the same sense; (b) same function in the language-games, the difference being a capricious custom, so same meaning; (c) different images attach to them ("X" turns the sense through 180°, "Y" is like shaking the head), so they express different ideas (p. 157e).

### §§557–568: meaning, use, and the "point" of a rule (pp. 157e–159e)
- §557: What made my double negation emphatic rather than an affirmation? There is no answer of the form "It consisted in the fact that…". I can pronounce it as emphasis, or insert brackets. **Objection:** brackets may have different roles. **Wittgenstein replies:** no one says they must be taken as brackets, and you explained your own conception in words too. "What the brackets mean lies in the technique of applying them." The real question is when it makes sense to say "I meant…" (p. 157e).
- §558: "is" in "The rose is red" versus "Two times two is four". Grammatical rules alone allow "is" in both. The rule that shows different meanings is that "is" may be replaced by the equals sign in the second but not the first (p. 157e).
- §559: a word's "function" in a sentence is not hidden; it comes out in operating the calculus (p. 158e). §560: "The meaning of a word is what an explanation of its meaning explains": to understand "meaning", look at what is called an explanation of meaning (p. 158e).
- §561: it is remarkable that we say "is" has two meanings yet would not say its meaning is its use as copula and as equals sign. One is inclined to say the union under one word is an "inessential coincidence" (p. 158e).
- §§562–567: how to decide what is essential in a notation? Analogy with games. In draughts a king is two pieces; is that inessential? In chess, drawing lots for colour by hiding kings in hands: is that part of the king's role? W. says he is inclined to distinguish essential from inessential rules in a game, since the game, one would like to say, has not only rules but also a point (§564). §567 then voices the thought that a game is determined by its rules, so a rule prescribing the kings for lots would be an essential part; the objection one might make is that we do not see the point, as with a rule to turn each piece three times (pp. 158e–159e).
- §568: if I understand the character of the game, I might say the lot-drawing is not essential (p. 159e).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein asks whether some reality behind the notation decides what is essential in it, and turns to games. There we are inclined to speak of a game's "point", and he says that if he understands the game's character aright, he might call a feature inessential (§568).

### §§569–571: concepts and psychology (p. 159e)
- Language and its concepts are instruments. One might think the choice of concepts makes no great difference, as with feet and inches versus metres; but even that is not true if a system of measurement demands more time and trouble than we can afford (§569). Concepts express and direct our interest (§570).
- §571: "A misleading parallel": psychology does not treat of mental processes as physics treats of physical ones. The physicist sees, hears and thinks about phenomena and reports them; the psychologist observes the subject's utterances (behaviour).

### §§572–588: expectation, opinion, belief (pp. 160e–163e)
- §572: expectation is grammatically a state, like opinion, hope, knowing. To understand the grammar, ask what counts as a criterion for being in such a state (p. 160e).
- §573: "Having an opinion is a state of what?" The answer "Mr N.N." is correct but not enlightening. Deeper questions: what are the criteria for being of an opinion, when did he reach it, when did he alter it (p. 160e).
- §574: believing is not thinking. Believing, expecting and hoping are less different from each other than from thinking. §575 contrasts sitting on a chair without a thought of collapse with holding fast to a belief despite everything (p. 160e).
- §576: watching a burning fuse, perhaps with no thought, is certainly expecting. §577: "I'm expecting him" can mean "I'd be surprised if he didn't come", or "I'm eagerly awaiting him". A language might use different verbs for these (pp. 160e–161e).
- §578: what does believing Goldbach's conjecture consist in? A feeling of certainty? Wittgenstein says that would not interest us and that he does not know how far the conjecture itself causes the feeling. Instead look at the consequences of the belief, such as searching for a proof, and at what that searching consists in (p. 161e).
- §580: "An 'inner process' stands in need of outward criteria." §581: an expectation is embedded in a situation. §582: whispering "It'll go off in a moment" does not describe a feeling, though it may manifest one (p. 161e).
- §§583–584: **Interlocutor:** you talk as if I weren't really hoping when I thought I was. Wittgenstein: what is happening now has significance in these surroundings. "Hope" refers to a phenomenon of human life. He asks whether one second of ardent hope, or one minute of hoping cut out of its context, would still be hoping; the words one might utter and the institution of money would no longer belong to that language. The coronation example is offered in the same vein (pp. 161e–162e).
- §585: "I hope he'll come" may be a manifestation of hope, not a report. "I can't keep my mind on my work today…" is a description of a state of mind (p. 162e).
- §586: "I've been expecting him all day" is a report of how I spent the day, while "I'm expecting him — I'm longing to see him!" may be called an act of expecting; yet I can utter those same words as the result of self-observation. "It all depends on what led up to these words" (p. 162e).
- §587: "How do you know you believe that?" Introspection answers in some cases, not most. It makes sense to ask "Do I really love her?", and then introspection means calling up memories, imagined situations and feelings (p. 162e).
- §588: "I'm in two minds" may be called a description of a state of mind. "It is my intention to go away tomorrow" tempts one to call intention a feeling of rigidity. But there are many different feelings and attitudes. "Tomorrow I'm going away; it's the end of my holidays" differs from "All right! Then I'll go tomorrow!", which is a decision (pp. 162e–163e).

*Intuition note.* Inner words like "expecting" get their grammar from criteria, history and surroundings. Wittgenstein asks whether a second or a minute of hoping cut out of its setting would still be hoping, and says the surroundings give what is happening its importance.

### §§589–600: meaning "with the heart", atmosphere, one-sided diet (pp. 163e–165e)
- §589: "In my heart I've decided it" should be taken seriously psychologically, as much as the claim that faith is a state of the soul. §590: if someone learned "seriously meaning what one says" by pointing to the heart, ask what shows he has learned it (p. 163e).
- §591: is there an experience of "tending"? Example: drawing and holding a breath before an objection, then exhaling when deciding to drop it. An observer reads it as intention in this situation only. The same experience in another situation might have nothing to do with tending (p. 163e).
- §592: **Interlocutor:** when you say "I intend to go away", it is the mental act of meaning that gives the sentence life; if you merely repeat the sentence after someone else, you utter it without that act. Wittgenstein: when doing philosophy, it may sometimes look like that. Think of really different situations. Was there no undertone when you repeated it? How is the "undertone" separated from the rest of the experience of speaking? (pp. 163e–164e).
- §593: "A main cause of philosophical diseases" is a one-sided diet of examples (p. 164e).
- §594: **Interlocutor:** words uttered significantly have "a dimension of depth". Wittgenstein: something different does take place, but how one expresses it (depth, something in me, atmosphere) "always comes to the same thing". A voice objects: "If we all agree about that, won't it be true?" Wittgenstein, in parentheses: I cannot accept the other person's testimony, because it is not testimony; it only tells me what he is inclined to say (p. 164e).
- §§595–596: familiarity and unfamiliarity. There are feelings of strangeness and of long familiarity, but it does not follow that every well-known object gives a feeling of familiarity. We think the space left by strangeness must be filled by some other atmosphere (p. 164e).
- §597: Germanisms in a German's English; so thinking seems grounded in a thought-schema. §598: in philosophy we hypostatize feelings (treat them as real things) where there are none, to explain our thoughts (p. 165e).
- §599: "In philosophy no inferences are drawn." "But it must be like this!" is not a philosophical proposition. Philosophy states only what everyone concedes to it (p. 165e).
- §600: does everything inconspicuous make an impression of inconspicuousness? (question) (p. 165e).

### §§601–606: recognizing (pp. 165e–166e)
- Recognizing the desk when entering the room: "Certainly!", yet it would be misleading to say any recognizing occurred; I was not surprised (§§601–603, p. 165e).
- §604: it is easy to misconceive recognizing as comparing two impressions, with memory as the agent. §605: more as if the object coincided with the picture, so I see one thing, not two. §606: a spurious expression suggests another face behind it, but a genuine one does not mean two identical faces (p. 166e).

### §§607–610: guessing the time, atmosphere, aroma (pp. 166e–167e)
- §607: How does one guess the time? One asks, pauses, perhaps imagines a clock face, says a time. **Interlocutor:** surely there is a feeling of conviction, an inner clock, a particular state of mind. Wittgenstein: the "conviction" is saying a time without doubt, with calm assurance. Asked about the circumstances, the speaker says he was thinking about his breakfast; what was characteristic was that he said to himself "I wonder what time it is". The speaker says it would never have occurred to him that the sentence had such an aura, if he had not thought of how one might say it differently (quotation, joke, elocution practice). Then "the picture of the special atmosphere forced itself upon me", so long as I do not look at what memory says happened. If I say "I read it off from an inner clock", that is a picture; all that corresponds to it is that I estimated the time. The purpose of the picture is to assimilate this case to the other one (pp. 166e–167e).
- §608: the "intangibility" of the state is important. Is it intangible because we refuse to count what is tangible about our state as part of the specific state we postulate? (a question) (p. 167e). §609: describing an atmosphere is a special application of language for special purposes (p. 167e).
- §610: "Describe the aroma of coffee!" Why can't it be done? Do we lack words? Where do we get the idea that a description must be possible? Have you felt the lack, or tried and failed? (p. 167e).

### §§611–629: willing (pp. 168e–171e)
- §611: one would like to say willing "is merely an experience" that comes when it comes and cannot be brought about. Wittgenstein: what can I bring about, what am I comparing it with? §612: the arm's rising is in the domain where we do it; the heart's thudding is not (p. 168e).
- §613: in the sense in which I can bring anything about, I can bring about wanting (jumping into water to want to swim). Wittgenstein says he was trying to say that I cannot want to want, i.e. that it makes no sense to speak of wanting to want. "Wanting" is not the name of an action. The idea of "immediate non-causal bringing about" rests on a misleading analogy with a mechanism connecting two parts of a machine (p. 168e).
- §614: raising my arm voluntarily uses no means, and my wish is not a means (p. 168e).
- §615: **Interlocutor:** if willing is not a sort of wishing, it must be the action itself. Wittgenstein: if so, it is so in the ordinary sense (speaking, walking, lifting) and also striving, trying, making an effort (p. 168e).
- §616: when I raise my arm I have not wished it to rise; the voluntary action excludes this wish. But "I hope I shall draw the circle faultlessly" does express a wish that the hand move so (p. 168e).
- §617: crossed fingers: we cannot move a finger when it is only pointed at, but can when touched. One is inclined to describe this by saying that we cannot will to move it, because one can find no "point of application" for the will until the finger is touched. Wittgenstein calls this misleading. It is experience that shows the finger is "paralysed" until touched; this could not be known a priori (p. 169e).
- §618: the willing subject is imagined as a motor without inertia. "I will, but my body does not obey me" can be said, not "My will does not obey me" (Augustine). In the sense in which I cannot fail to will, I cannot try to will either (§§618–619, p. 169e).
- §620: doing seems like an extensionless point that is the real agent. §621: when I raise my arm, my arm rises. What is left if I subtract the fact that my arm rises from the fact that I raise it? (p. 169e).
- §622: usually I do not try to raise my arm. §623: striving "at all costs" to get to a house is odd where there is no difficulty (p. 170e).
- §624: someone with eyes shut, under an electric current, says he is moving his arm when it is not moving. We say he has the special feeling of making the movement. Try moving your arm with eyes shut while talking yourself into thinking it is still (p. 170e).
- §625: "How do you know you've raised your arm?" "I feel it." W. asks: so what you recognize is the feeling, and are you certain you recognize it right? Isn't your certainty that you raised your arm the criterion, the measure, of recognizing? (p. 170e).
- §626: feeling hardness "in the tip of the stick"; what goes with it is looking at the tip, not the hand, and describing what one feels as "something hard and round there" (p. 170e).
- §627: a description "I form the decision to pull the bell at 5, and when it strikes 5 my arm makes this movement" wants "And lo and behold!" added. That exclamation does not belong. I do not say "Look, my arm is going up!" (pp. 170e–171e). §628: voluntary movement is marked by the absence of surprise; Wittgenstein does not want "But why isn't one surprised?" asked (p. 171e).

### §§629–632: predicting one's own act (p. 171e)
- §629: people who discuss foreknowledge of the future always overlook the case of predicting one's own voluntary movements (p. 171e).
- §630: two language-games: (a) orders for arm movements, including self-given ones; (b) observing regularities (metals and acids) and predicting. They have an evident kinship and a fundamental difference. Compare the training for each (p. 171e).
- §631: "I'm going to take two powders now, and in half an hour I shall be sick." It explains nothing to say that I am agent in the first case and observer in the second, or that I see the causal connection from inside or outside. Nor is it to the point that the first prediction is no more infallible than the second. It was not based on observing my behaviour; its antecedents were different (p. 171e).
- §632: the expression of intention is not said to be a cause with its fulfilment as effect (physiology might decide that). But we can often predict a man's actions from his expression of decision. This is "an important language-game" (p. 171e).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein questions the picture of willing as a hidden inner push before the arm moves. He treats ordinary doing, trying and deciding as the cases to look at, and remarks that one might say voluntary movement is marked by the absence of surprise.

### §§633–653: "I was going to say…", intention and memory (pp. 172e–175e)
- §633: if, after an interruption, I say what I was going to say, this does not mean I had already thought it, only not said it, unless the certainty of my continuing is taken as a criterion. §634: this is like elaborating a train of thought from brief notes. More than one continuation was possible, but I did not choose between interpretations: "I remembered that I was going to say this" (p. 172e).
- §§635–637: remembered details do not by themselves show the intention, like a snapshot with only scattered details visible while one knows what the whole shows. The details are not irrelevant; the hearer can "fill out the picture", which is part of understanding. I do not read it off from a remembered process, nor interpret the situation (p. 172e).
- §638: why am I inclined to see an interpretation in "For a moment I was going to deceive him"? He asks whether the evidence seems scanty because one takes no account of its background; if, for a moment, I intended to pretend to someone that I was unwell, that required an antecedent context (pp. 172e–173e).
- §639: "Meaning something develops" holds a mistake too (p. 173e). §640: W. asks how a feeling could really link thoughts and says the word "feeling" is very misleading here; one can sometimes say with certainty that a thought is connected with earlier ones without being able to point out the connection (p. 173e).
- §641: even "Now I'm going to deceive him" would not necessarily have been meant seriously, so the most explicit expression of intention is insufficient evidence by itself (p. 173e).
- §§642–644: "At that moment I hated him": it consisted in thoughts, feelings, actions; I could rehearse it by acting the scene. If I am ashamed, I am ashamed of the whole thing; W. asks whether the intention did not lie also in what I did, and says the whole background of the incident justifies the shame (p. 173e).
- §§645–646: try to recall "the inner experience" of intending precisely and it seems to vanish, replaced by thoughts, feelings, movements and connections to earlier situations, as if a microscope were refocused. **Objection:** you have adjusted the microscope wrongly. Wittgenstein: something is right in this, but even if I did remember a sensation, what lets me call it "the intention"? It might be a tickle accompanying every intention (p. 174e).
- §647: the natural expression of an intention: look at a cat stalking a bird (p. 174e).
- §648: remembering my intention may bring nothing before my mind but words, perhaps others that fill out the picture. §649: someone without language cannot have linguistic memories or wishes; these are not mere threadbare representations of real experience (p. 174e).
- §650: we say a dog is afraid his master will beat him, but not that he is afraid he will beat him tomorrow; "Why not?" is left open (p. 174e). §651: remembering that I would have been glad to stay longer: no picture of the desire appears, yet I remember clearly that the feelings were there (p. 174e).
- §652: a reader of "He sized him up with a hostile glance" generally supplies nothing and guesses nothing. If he does guess, it is mainly a context (p. 175e).
- §653: I walked a route using a map I cannot explain as a map (a "private map"). Wittgenstein asks whether I could say I read off my having then meant something, as if from a map though there is no map; that means nothing but that I am now inclined to say I read the intention in certain remembered states of mind (p. 175e).

### §§654–663: the language-game as primary (pp. 175e–176e)
- §654: "Our mistake is to look for an explanation where we ought to regard the facts as 'proto-phenomena'", i.e. to say "this is the language-game that is being played" (p. 175e).
- §§655–656: the point is not to explain a language-game by our experiences but to take account of it. Regard the language-game as primary and feelings as a way of interpreting it. One may ask how people came to make the utterance "reporting a past wish" (p. 175e).
- §§657–658: if reports always took the form "I said to myself…", their purpose might be to acquaint someone with my reactions. "As it were said to himself" does not mean to say something to oneself (p. 176e).
- §659: I tell of an intention not because it too was going on then, but because I want to tell something about myself beyond what happened. I reveal this "not on grounds of self-observation, but by way of a reaction (it might also be called an intuition)" (p. 176e).
- §660: "I was then going to say…" is grammatically related to "I could then have gone on": remembering an intention versus remembering having understood. §661: asking whether remembering having meant him is remembering a process or a state; when did it begin? (p. 176e).
- §§662–663: "I wanted N. to come" may or may not be said to describe my state of mind. A picture that comes to mind is like an illustration to a story: only if one knows the story does one know what it is for (p. 176e).

### §§664–693: meaning someone or something (pp. 176e–181e)
- §664: **surface grammar versus depth grammar.** Surface grammar is what "can be taken in by the ear", the use in sentence structure. Compare the depth grammar of "to mean" with what surface grammar suggests: "No wonder one finds it difficult to know one's way about" (pp. 176e–177e).
- §665: pointing to the cheek in pain and saying "abracadabra", then "I meant toothache". "By 'abracadabra' I mean toothache" is a definition, not a description of what goes on in me (p. 177e).
- §666: pain and piano-tuning both heard; "It'll soon stop". It makes a difference which I mean. In many cases some direction of attention corresponds to it, but what the difference consists in is left open (p. 177e).
- §§667–668: W. asks whether one cannot say that someone simulating pain means the pain, and whether one cannot also lie about what one meant (p. 177e).
- §§669–672: pointing is part of the language-game. Attention to a sensation seems analogous to pointing, since one can point by looking or listening. §669: in certain circumstances even pointing at the object may be inessential to the language-game; §670 asks what pointing is for when one telephones and says "This table is too tall". §671: listening, as it were, searches for an auditory impression, and so cannot point at it, only at the place where it is searching for it (pp. 177e–178e). §673: a mental attitude does not "accompany" words in the sense in which a gesture accompanies them (p. 178e).
- §§674–678: we do not say "I didn't really mean my pain; my mind wasn't on it enough". "I meant this by that word" is used differently from a statement about an affection of the mind (§676). "Did you mean it?" when swearing is like "Were you really angry?" and can be answered by introspection, with degrees (§677). What the meaning (pain or tuning) consists in: no answer comes. Saying "At the time I meant one thing and not the other" only repeats with emphasis what no one has contradicted (pp. 178e–179e).
- §679: "Can you doubt that you meant this?" "No; but neither can I be certain of it, know it." (p. 179e).
- §§680–684: with cursing, how one pictures the victim is irrelevant to the inferences that interest him. Cursing is not said to depend on how the man "means" his victim. Is "I was thinking of the piano-tuning" stating a link or making one? Wittgenstein asks whether he cannot say both (§682). I draw a head and say it is N.: making or reporting a connection? What speaks in favour of saying that my words describe an existing connection is that they refer to things that did not arise only with the words, such as a conditional answer I would then have given (§§683–684, p. 179e).
- §685: "Look for A" does not mean "Look for B", though I may do the same thing in obeying both. To say something different must happen is like saying "Today is my birthday" and "My birthday is on April 26th" must refer to different days because their sense differs (p. 180e). §686: "I meant B" points to a wider context (p. 180e). §§687–688: instead of "I meant him" one can sometimes say "I thought of him" or "we were speaking of him", and W. asks what "speaking of him" consists in; in certain circumstances one can say "As I was speaking, I felt I was saying it to you" (p. 180e).
- §689: how do I speak of N.? **Interlocutor:** there must be a further link, otherwise I would not have meant him. Wittgenstein: such a link exists, "only not as you imagine it: namely, by means of a mental mechanism". One compares "meaning him" with "aiming at him" (p. 180e).
- §690 asks whether, when I mention a person's name openly, I am really thinking specially about him. §691 asks which of the processes taking place while I draw N.'s face from memory could be said to be meaning him, since one would like to say that meaning him is aiming at him; "How does he call him to mind? How does he call him?" (pp. 180e–181e).
- §692: it is correct to say "When I gave you this rule, I meant that in this case you should…", even if I did not think of that case. "To mean it" did not mean to think of it. A criterion for meaning such-and-such is that he has mastered a technique in arithmetic and algebra and taught the expansion of a series in the usual way (p. 181e).
- §693: when teaching a series, I mean him to write a given number at the hundredth place, without necessarily thinking of it. This shows how different the grammar of "to mean" is from that of "to think". "Nothing is more wrong-headed than to call meaning something a mental activity", unless one is setting out to produce confusion (p. 181e). He adds the parallel that one might speak of an "activity of butter" when it rises in price, which is harmless if no problems are produced by it.

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein grants that there is a link between my words and N., but says it does not exist by means of a mental mechanism (§689). He points to the wider context (§686), to what I would then have given as an answer (§684), and, for meaning a rule, to mastery of a technique (§692).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustration of §§692–693. A teacher shows a pupil the series "2, 4, 6, 8, …" (add 2 each time) and the pupil continues it correctly up to the tenth term. The teacher never thought about the hundredth place.

- The n-th term is 2n. So the hundredth term is 2 × 100 = 200.
- Later a pupil writes 202 there. The teacher says "No, I meant 200".
- On Wittgenstein's view (§692) this remark is correct even though the teacher never thought of that place. The criterion is not a past inner act but the teacher's mastery of the technique, shown e.g. by teaching the expansion in the usual way, and by correcting 202 as an error.
- A remembered "inner image of 200" is not required. That is the sense in which meaning is unlike thinking.

## Concepts
- [[meaning as use]] — meaning shown by the rules and technique of applying a sign (brackets, "is", chess king); the meaning of a word is what an explanation of its meaning explains (§§557–560, pp. 157e–158e)
- [[language-game]] — the practice (e.g. reporting a past intention, predicting from a decision) taken as primary, not explained by experiences (§§630, 654–656, pp. 171e, 175e)
- [[surface-depth-grammar]] (new) — surface grammar: use as heard in sentence structure; depth grammar: the deeper pattern of use, e.g. of "to mean" (§664, pp. 176e–177e)
- [[criteria-for-inner-states]] (new) — outward circumstances and behaviour that count as being in a state like expecting or believing; "an inner process stands in need of outward criteria" (§§572, 580, pp. 160e–161e)
- [[atmosphere-of-words]] (new) — the supposed inner "undertone" or aura of significant utterance. W. asks how it is to be separated from the rest of the utterance, and says the picture of it may arise from imagining other ways of saying the sentence (§§592, 594, 607, pp. 163e–167e)
- [[voluntary-action-without-surprise]] (new) — voluntary movement is marked by the absence of surprise; W. questions the thought that willing is merely an experience that "comes when it comes" (§§611–628, pp. 168e–171e)
- [[meaning-not-a-mental-activity]] (new) — "to mean something" is grammatically unlike "to think", and one may mean something without thinking of it (§§692–693, p. 181e)
- [[proto-phenomenon]] (new) — a fact to be accepted ("this is the language-game being played"), not explained by further experiences (§654, p. 175e)

## Canonical instances
- [[double-negation-X-Y]] — two negation words with different doubling behaviour; same or different meaning? (§556, p. 157e)
- [[is-copula-vs-identity]] — "is" in "The rose is red" versus "Two times two is four", separated by a substitution rule (§558, p. 157e)
- [[chess-king-lots]] — drawing lots with chess kings; is it part of the king's role? (§563, p. 158e)
- [[burning-fuse-expectation]] — watching a burning fuse, perhaps with no thoughts or only disjointed ones, is certainly a case of expecting (§576, p. 160e)
- [[guessing-the-time]] — no clock is read; the "feeling of conviction" comes to saying a time without doubt, and the atmosphere picture arises from imagining other ways of saying the sentence (§607, pp. 166e–167e)
- [[crossed-fingers]] — a finger one cannot move until touched; experience, not a priori (§617, p. 169e)
- [[two-powders-prediction]] — predicting one's own action versus predicting metals (§§630–632, p. 171e)
- [[abracadabra-toothache]] — "I meant toothache" as a definition (§665, p. 177e)
- [[pain-and-piano-tuning]] — "It'll soon stop"; what it is to mean one thing or the other (§§666–682, pp. 177e–179e)
- [[microscope-intention]] — the remembered "inner experience" of intending seems to vanish into thoughts, feelings and context when examined; W. also notes an objection and asks what lets one call a remembered sensation "the intention" (§§645–646, p. 174e)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] "Look at how the sign is applied, not what accompanies it" (§557) ↔ analytic-philosophy: the idea of meaning as use / language-game (§§559–560).
- [structural-analogy] The "private map" without a rule of interpretation (§653) ↔ the private language argument: reporting an inner state with no public technique. The text says only that the expression is easy to misunderstand.
- [metaphor] Surface vs depth grammar of "to mean" (§664) ↔ logical vs grammatical form: surface grammar can hide what a sentence really does.

## Questions this raises
- If the "undertone" of a word is inseparable from the utterance (§592), what exactly is Wittgenstein denying: that it exists, or that it explains meaning?
- Why does he say "I meant him" can be both a report of a link and the making of one (§682)? Is "both" an answer or a refusal to choose?
- How far do his criteria for inner states (§572) allow for cases where criteria and what one is conscious of seem to come apart (§§624–625)?
- Does calling voluntary action "absent of surprise" (§628) give an account of agency, or only mark a boundary?
