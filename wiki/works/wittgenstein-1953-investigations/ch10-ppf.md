# Inner and outer, certainty, imponderable evidence, concepts and nature (PPF xi end – xiv)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein asks how meaning, intending, inner speech, knowing one's own mind and certainty should be understood, and looks to use, behaviour, consequences and language-games rather than to hidden inner processes accompanying words and actions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks what it is to experience a word's meaning, to mean or intend, to speak inwardly, to know one's own mind, to be certain, and to remember. Wittgenstein denies that meaning and intending are experiences accompanying words or actions; he looks to behaviour, consequences and the language-game. Inner speech is not hidden behind a veil. The kind of certainty goes with the kind of language-game, and agreement in judgement characterises concepts such as colour statements. Genuineness of feeling is judged partly by subtle, "imponderable" evidence. On memory, he doubts that introspection shows what a memory content is. Psychology's confusion is conceptual, not youthful.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a language-game and of meaning as use (earlier in the *Investigations*); everyday logic of "know", "doubt", "believe". No mathematics beyond multiplication.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Aspect-blindness (not being able to see a picture switch aspect) is said to be akin to lacking a musical ear. The concept matters because it connects seeing an aspect with experiencing a word's meaning (§§258–261, p. 225e).
- The sense that a word is completely filled with its meaning is, Wittgenstein says, figurative speech whose figure forced itself on him. A figurative use cannot conflict with the original use (§265, p. 226e).
- Meaning and intending are not experiences and have no experiential content. Meaning is not a process accompanying a word, since no process could have the consequences of meaning (§§279, 291, pp. 228e–229e).
- Silent inner speech is not hidden behind a veil, though its being hidden from others is part of its concept and "hidden" is called the wrong word. It is wrong to say one knows what one is oneself thinking. A confession matters for the consequences drawn from it, not as a report of a process (§§301–322, pp. 231e–234e).
- "Mathematical certainty" is not a psychological concept; the kind of certainty is the kind of language-game (§332, p. 236e). Our concept of mathematical certainty would not exist if mathematicians generally quarrelled over results. Wittgenstein says only that they do not, not why (§§341–343, p. 237e).
- What has to be accepted, the given, is forms of life, "one might say" (§345, p. 238e). He then argues that without agreement in colour judgements or in calculation, the words and the technique would differ from ours (§§346–347, p. 238e). Agreement among those diagnosed normal characterises the concept of a colour statement (§351, p. 239e).
- There is generally no agreement over whether an expression of feeling is genuine (§352, p. 239e). Judging it draws on "imponderable" (subtle, hard-to-weigh) evidence and on learned experience. This is not a technique: there are rules, but they form no system (§§355, 358–360, pp. 239e–240e).
- Against the belief that certain concepts are absolutely the correct ones, Wittgenstein suggests imagining very general facts of nature different; the formation of other concepts then becomes intelligible (§366, p. 241e).
- Introspection, he says, could show an absence of memory content only case by case, and could not show what "remember" means or where to look (§369, p. 242e).
- Psychology's confusion is conceptual, and its experimental methods and its problems pass one another by. An analogous investigation is possible for mathematics (§§371–372, p. 243).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### xi (end): aspect-blindness and experiencing meaning, §§258–273
**Aspect-blindness (§258, p. 225e).** Someone supposed unable to see the schematic cube *as* a cube could still recognise it as a representation (e.g. a working drawing) of a cube, but for him it would not switch from one aspect to the other. Wittgenstein then asks whether he is supposed to be able to take it as a cube in certain circumstances, as we do. If not, this could hardly be called a sort of blindness. The aspect-blind will have an altogether different attitude to pictures. Such anomalies are easy for us to imagine (§259). Aspect-blindness will be akin to the lack of a "musical ear" (§260).

**Why it matters (§§261–263, p. 225e).** The concept's importance lies in the connection between seeing an aspect and experiencing a word's meaning. Wittgenstein asks what someone would be missing who did not experience a word's meaning. Examples: someone who did not understand the request to say "till" and mean it as a verb, or who did not feel a word turn into mere sound when repeated ten times (§261). In a law court, how someone meant a word can be inferred from facts; it is a question of intention. Wittgenstein asks whether how he *experienced* a word such as "bank" could be significant in a similar way. He leaves this as a question (§262). With a code in which "tower" means bank, a man may understand and act correctly, yet feel the word strange in this use: it has not yet "absorbed" the meaning (§263).

**Reading with feeling (§§264–267, pp. 225e–226e).** **Interlocutor:** surely something goes on in me when I read a poem with feeling. **Wittgenstein replies** by asking which processes, then listing: a different ring, attention to intonation, noticing a wrongly placed intonation (the face shows it), later talking about the reading, sometimes visualising a picture. A word can also be given an intonation that makes it stand out almost as a portrait of the whole thing (§264). An objection (voice unmarked) asks how a word can be completely filled with its meaning if meaning is use. The reply is Wittgenstein's own: what he said was meant figuratively, and the figure forced itself on him rather than being chosen. A figurative use cannot come into conflict with the original use (§265, p. 226e). Why this picture suggests itself could perhaps be explained (compare *mot juste*, §266). If a sentence can strike me as a painting in words, a word uttered in isolation seeming to carry a particular meaning within itself is no more astonishing (§267).

**The city illusion (§§268–270, pp. 226e–227e).** Walking with a friend, I imagined the city lay to our right, with no reason I was aware of; a simple consideration shows it is ahead to the left. I seem to see psychological causes (associations, an earlier canal walk), and might try to discover them psychoanalytically (§268). **Interlocutor:** what a strange experience! Reply: it is not stranger than any other, only of a different kind from the experiences we regard as most fundamental, such as sense impressions (§269). Similar feelings: as if I knew the city was over there; as if the name "Schubert" fitted Schubert's works and face (§270, p. 227e).

**"March" (§§271–273, p. 227e).** You can say "march" to yourself meaning an imperative, or the name of a month. Say "March!" and then "March no further!". Does the same experience accompany the word both times — are you sure? (§271). Wittgenstein asks: if careful attention shows I experience the word now this way, now that way in this game, doesn't it also show that in the stream of speech I often do not experience the word at all? That I then also mean it one way or another, and may explain it later, is not in question (§272). The remaining question is why we speak of "the meaning" and of "meaning it" in this game of experiencing a word. This is a different kind of question. It is characteristic of this language-game that we use the expression for pronouncing a word with a certain meaning, taken over from another language-game. "Call it a dream. It does not change anything" (§273).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein treats the impression that a word carries its meaning within itself as a figure that cannot conflict with the word's use (§265). He also asks whether, in the stream of speech, we often do not experience the word at all while still meaning it (§272).

### Secondary meaning, §§274–278 (pp. 227e–228e)
Given the concepts "fat" and "lean", would you be inclined to say Wednesday is fat and Tuesday lean, or the reverse? Wittgenstein says he is strongly inclined to the former. Do the words then have a different meaning? They have a different use; he wants to use them with their familiar meanings. He says nothing about causes (perhaps childhood associations, but that is a hypothesis); the inclination is there (§274). Asked what he really means, he says he could only explain the meanings in the usual way, not point to Tuesday and Wednesday (§275). One might speak of a "primary" and "secondary" meaning; only someone for whom the word has the former meaning uses it in the latter (§276, p. 227e). Only to someone who has learnt to calculate, on paper or aloud, can one render intelligible, by means of the concept of calculating, what calculating in the head is (§277, p. 228e). The secondary meaning is not "metaphorical": in saying the vowel e is yellow he could not express what he wants to say other than by means of the concept of yellow (§278).

### Meaning and intending are not experiences, §§279–300 (pp. 228e–231e)
- Asking whether you meant *this* bank when you said the word refers to a definite time but not to an experience during that time. Meaning something is as little an experience as intending. They have no experiential content; the images that accompany and illustrate them are not the meaning or intending (§279, p. 228e).
- Intention no more "accompanies" an action than thought accompanies speech. Thought and intention are neither articulated nor non-articulated (§280). Talking and thinking are not concepts of a similar kind, though closely connected (§281). The interest of an experience while speaking differs from that of the intention (§282). Two people saying the same words silently may not thereby express the thought of that man; the words belong to a language and context (§283). If God had looked into our minds, he could not have seen whom we were speaking of (§284, p. 228e).
- A reaction at a certain moment is explained by saying "I thought of…" (§285, p. 229e). Mere explanation of a word does not refer to an occurrence at the moment of speaking (§286). "I mean this" (a later explanation of a word) is quite different from "I thought of… as I said it", which is akin to being reminded of something (§287).
- Asked what importance the report "I have already remembered three times today that I must write to him" has, Wittgenstein answers that it permits certain inferences (§288). The primitive reaction with which such a language-game begins may be a glance, a gesture or a word (§289). "I wanted to convey to you…" expresses the purpose of an action, not a symbolic convention (§290).
- Meaning something is not a process which accompanies a word, because no process could have the consequences of meaning something. Similarly, he thinks, a calculation is not an experiment, since no experiment could have the special consequences of a multiplication (§291, p. 229e). Important characteristic processes accompany talking and are often missing when one talks without thinking, but they are not the thinking (§292).
- Wittgenstein asks, of the exclamation "Now I know!", whether the knowing could be called an accompaniment of the exclamation (§293, p. 230e). The feeling that a word has assimilated its meaning could be alien to some human beings; among us it is manifested in the way we choose and value words (§294). Finding the right word is sometimes like comparing fine differences of smell, but often I only say it isn't right yet (§295). Careful attention could discover only what is going on in me now; I can act it out, imitating gestures, faces and tones of voice (§296). The first judgement ("this word fits") is not the end; the field of a word is decisive (§297).
- For the tip-of-the-tongue remark, what goes on in my mind is not the point; what is of more interest is what went on in my behaviour (§298, p. 231e). **Wittgenstein on James:** James is really trying to say what a remarkable experience it is, but it is not an experience at all. Interpreted as one it looks odd, like an intention interpreted as an accompaniment of action, or −1 interpreted as a cardinal number (§299). The remark is no more the expression of an experience than "Now I know how to go on!" It is used in certain situations, surrounded by behaviour of a special kind and some characteristic experiences, and frequently followed by finding the word (§300).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein's argument at §291 is that meaning has consequences that no accompanying process could have. He does not locate its content in an inner event (§279).

### Inner speech and "hidden" thoughts, §§301–329 (pp. 231e–235e)
**Inner speech.** It is not a half-hidden phenomenon seen through a veil. It is not hidden at all, but the concept may confuse us, since it runs for a long stretch alongside the concept of an outer process without coinciding with it. Whether the laryngeal muscles are innervated may be of interest, but not for this investigation (§301, p. 231e). What was said inwardly can be communicated audibly, and inner speech can accompany outer action (§302). **Interlocutor:** inner speech is an activity I have to learn. **Wittgenstein replies:** what are "doing" and "learning" here? Let the use of words teach you their meaning (§303). You can learn what calculating in the head is only by learning what calculating is, and learn to do it in the head only by learning to calculate (§304, p. 232e). One hears the talk in imagination and does not merely feel its skeleton in the larynx; people could also be imagined calculating silently with laryngeal movements, as on fingers (§305). A hypothesis about what goes on in the body interests us only because it points to a possible use of saying something to myself: inferring the physiological process from the expression (§306).

**Hidden.** That what someone else says to himself is hidden from me is part of the concept of inner speech. "Hidden" is the wrong word, since if it were hidden from me it ought to be apparent to him; but he does not "know" it either, only the doubt that exists for me does not exist for him (§307, pp. 232e–233e). The claim that it is hidden might also just mean that I can mostly not guess it or read it off his larynx (§308).

**Knowing.** The sentence "I know what I want, wish, believe, feel…" is either philosophers' nonsense or, at any rate, not an a priori judgement (§309, p. 232e). "I know" may mean "I do not doubt", but this does not make "I doubt" senseless or doubt logically excluded (§310). One says "I know" where one can also say "I believe" or "I suppose", where one can satisfy oneself; the occasion and purpose of phrases such as "Only you can know what you feel" should be considered (§311). It is possible to imagine a case in which I could satisfy myself that I had two hands; normally I cannot. If I were in doubt about it, I would not need to believe my eyes either (§312, p. 233e). The sentence that the Earth has existed for millions of years makes clearer sense than the sentence that it has existed for five minutes. Of the latter I would ask what observations it refers to and what would count against it, whereas I know what context of ideas and observations the former belongs to (§313). That a rose has no teeth is obviously true yet far from clear, since one has no notion in advance where to look for teeth in a rose; compare pain in someone else's body (§314). I can know what someone else is thinking but not what I am thinking: it is correct to say I know what you are thinking, wrong to say I know what I am thinking. "A whole cloud of philosophy condenses into a drop of grammar" (§315).

**Seclusion.** A quoted view (voice not marked) says a man's thinking goes on in a seclusion beyond any physical seclusion (§316). Wittgenstein asks whether people who always read others' silent soliloquy, say by observing the larynx, would also be inclined to use the picture of complete seclusion. Talking aloud in a language the hearers do not understand also hides my thoughts from them (§317, p. 234e).

**Confession (§§318–322, p. 234e).** Suppose a man always guesses right what I say to myself in my thoughts. What is the criterion for his guessing right? I am truthful and confess he has guessed right. But might I be mistaken, can my memory not deceive me, and might it not always do so when, without lying, I express what I have thought? Now it does appear that "what went on within me" is not the point at all (§318). The criteria for the truth of a confession are not the criteria for a true description of a process. A confession's importance lies in the special consequences that can be drawn from it when its truth is guaranteed by the criteria of truthfulness (§319). Assuming dreams can yield important information about the dreamer, it would be truthful accounts of dreams that yielded it. The question whether memory deceives him could not arise unless we introduced a new criterion for the report's agreeing with the dream (§320). In thought-guessing games the guesser hears something said in a language one does not understand, reads a hidden sentence, or utters the thoughts of someone assembling a jigsaw, who need not be talking to himself (§321). Even if I do not talk to myself, that does not make my thoughts any more hidden than an unperceived physical process (§322).

**Opacity (p. 235e).** Someone says what is internal is hidden from us; the future is hidden too, Wittgenstein remarks, and he asks whether the astronomer thinks this way when calculating an eclipse (§323). Seeing someone writhing in pain with evident cause, I do not think his feelings are hidden from me (§324). Yet a human being can be a complete enigma to another, as in a strange country with strange traditions, even if one has mastered the language, and not because of what the people say to themselves (§325). The remark that I cannot know what is going on in him is above all a picture, the expression of a conviction that does not give reasons (§326). "If a lion could talk, we wouldn't be able to understand it" (§327).

**Intention.** Guessing intentions can be imagined, and guessing what someone will do. To say only he can know what he intends is nonsense. To say only he can know what he will do is wrong, because the prediction contained in my expression of intention need not come true, and someone else may know what will really happen (§328, p. 235e). In many cases someone else cannot predict my actions while I foresee them in my intention; my prediction does not rest on the same foundation as his, and the conclusions to be drawn are quite different (§329).

### Certainty and agreement, §§330–351 (pp. 235e–239e)
I can be as certain of someone else's feelings as of any fact. But a report of depression, the calculation $25\times25=625$ and a statement of my age are not thereby similar instruments. A natural explanation is that the certainty is of a different kind; this seems to point to a psychological difference, but the difference is a logical one (§330, p. 235e). An objection asks whether, if you are certain, you are not shutting your eyes in face of doubt; the reply is that they have been shut (§331, p. 236e). Wittgenstein asks whether he is less certain that a man is in pain than that $2\times2=4$, and whether the first case is therefore one of mathematical certainty. "Mathematical certainty" is not a psychological concept: "The kind of certainty is the kind of language-game" (§332).

**Motives (§§333–337).** That only he knows his motives expresses that we ask him; if he is sincere he will tell us, but I need more than sincerity to guess them. This is where the kinship with knowing lies (§333, p. 236e). We should let ourselves be struck by there being such a language-game as confessing a motive (§334). We do not notice the enormous variety of everyday language-games, because our language's clothing makes them look alike; what is new is always a language-game (§335). Wittgenstein asks what the difference between motive and cause is, and how each is discovered (§336). To ask whether a way of judging motives is reliable, we must already know what judging a motive means, and we do not learn that by finding out what "motive" and "judging" are (§337).

**Length (§338, pp. 236e–237e).** **Interlocutor:** what is judged is independent of the method of judging it. **Wittgenstein:** anyone who thinks so makes a mistake. The meaning of "length" is learnt by learning, among other things, what determining length is. Hence "methodology" has a double meaning: physical or conceptual investigation.

**Certainty is not a feeling (§§339–340, p. 237e).** One would sometimes like to call certainty and conviction tonalities of thought, and they do show in tone of voice. But Wittgenstein advises not to think of them as feelings we have in speaking or thinking: ask how certainty is manifested in action. A quoted view says certainty about another's state of mind is always merely subjective. He answers that the two words point to a difference between language-games.

**Mathematics (§§341–344, pp. 237e–238e).** Disputes over a long addition are rare and short and can be decided "with certainty". Mathematicians in general do not quarrel over results, an important fact. Were it otherwise (one convinced a figure had altered unperceived, or that memory had deceived), our concept of mathematical certainty would not exist (§341). Even then, Wittgenstein notes, one might still say that a calculation always has a definite result, which God knows, and that mathematics is of the highest certainty though we possess only a crude likeness of it (§342). He denies that he is saying the certainty of mathematics is based on the reliability of ink and paper (that would be a vicious circle). He has not said why mathematicians do not quarrel, only that they do not (§343). One could not calculate with paper and ink subject to strange alterations. But that they changed could itself be ascertained only through memory and comparison with other means of calculation; and how are these tested in turn? (§344).

**Forms of life (p. 238e).** "What has to be accepted, the given, is — one might say — forms of life" (§345). Wittgenstein asks whether it makes sense to say people generally agree in colour judgements and what it would be like otherwise (one calls a flower red, another blue). With what right could one then call these people's words our colour-words? How would they learn them, and is their language-game still the one we call the use of colour names? There are evidently differences of degree (§346). The consideration applies to mathematics too: without complete agreement people would not be learning our technique, which would be more or less different from ours, perhaps up to unrecognisability (§347). **Interlocutor:** mathematical truth is independent of whether human beings know it. **Wittgenstein:** the proposition that people believe $2\times2=4$ and the proposition $2\times2=4$ do not have the same sense. He asks what it would be like for everybody to believe $2\times2=5$; he can imagine people with a different calculus or a technique we would not call calculating, and asks whether that would be wrong, comparing a coronation to beings different from us (§348). Mathematics is a body of knowledge but also an activity; false moves can exist only as the exception, since if they became the rule the game would be abrogated (§349). That we all learn the same multiplication table might be a remark about teaching or about the concept of the multiplication table (compare horses in a race generally running as fast as they can, §350). There is such a thing as colour-blindness, and complete general agreement among those diagnosed normal characterises the concept of a colour statement (§351, p. 239e).

*Intuition note.* Wittgenstein asks how certainty is manifested in action (§339), and says the kind of certainty is the kind of language-game (§332). A report of depression, a calculation and an age statement are not similar instruments (§330).

### Genuineness and imponderable evidence, §§352–364 (pp. 239e–240e)
There is generally no agreement over whether an expression of feeling is genuine (§352). I may be sure he is not pretending while a third person is not; Wittgenstein asks whether I can always convince him, and if not, whether there is a mistake in his reasoning or observations (§353). "You don't understand a thing!" is what one says when someone doubts what we recognise as clearly genuine but we cannot prove anything (§354). There are those with better and worse judgement of genuineness, and predictions arising from those with better knowledge of people will in general be more correct. Some can learn this knowledge, not through a course of study but through experience; a teacher gives the right tip from time to time. What one acquires is not a technique but correct judgements; there are rules, but they do not form a system, and only experienced people can apply them rightly, unlike calculating rules (§355). The hardest thing is to express this indefiniteness correctly (§356). **Objection:** genuineness cannot be proved, one has to feel it. **Wittgenstein:** what are the further consequences of such recognition? There are consequences, but of a diffuse kind; experience can inform us of them, but they cannot be formulated in general terms, and the most general remarks yield at best fragments of a system (§357, pp. 239e–240e).

One can be convinced by evidence that someone is not pretending, but there is also "imponderable" evidence (§358, p. 240e). Wittgenstein asks what it accomplishes. If there were imponderable evidence for a substance's chemical structure, it would still have to prove itself as evidence by ponderable consequences. Imponderable evidence might convince someone a picture was genuine, but documentation might also prove that right (§359). It includes subtleties of glance, gesture and tone. I may recognise a genuine loving look and distinguish it from a pretended one (with possible ponderable confirmation) yet be quite incapable of describing the difference, and not because the languages I know lack words for it (§360). Wittgenstein asks how a man learns to get an eye for something, and how this eye can be used (§361). Pretending to be in pain is only a special case of producing expressions of pain without being in pain. If that is possible at all, he asks, why should it always be pretending that is taking place, this very special pattern in the weave of our lives? (§362). A child has much to learn before it can pretend; a dog can be neither hypocrite nor sincere (§363). We might actually say of someone that he believes he is pretending (§364).

### xii: concepts and nature, §§365–367 (p. 241e)
If concept formation can be explained by facts of nature, shouldn't we be interested not in grammar but in its basis in nature? We are also interested in the correspondence between concepts and very general natural facts, which mostly do not strike us because of their generality. But our interest is not thrown back onto these possible causes: we are not doing natural science or natural history, since we can invent fictitious natural history (§365). Wittgenstein is not saying, in the sense of a hypothesis, that different facts would give people different concepts. Rather, if anyone believes certain concepts are absolutely the correct ones and that having different ones would mean failing to realise something we realise, let him imagine very general facts of nature different; then the formation of different concepts will become intelligible to him (§366). He compares a concept with a style of painting and asks whether even our style is arbitrary, whether we can choose one at pleasure, or whether it is just pretty and ugly (§367).

### xiii: remembering, §§368–370 (p. 242e)
When, on the basis of memory, I say that he was here half an hour ago, this is not the description of a present experience; memory experiences are accompaniments of remembering (§368). **Interlocutor:** remembering has no experiential content; surely introspection shows this. **Wittgenstein:** it could only show this from case to case, and it cannot show what "remember" means, and hence where to look for a content. He says he gets the idea of a memory content only through comparing psychological concepts, as in comparing two games (soccer has goals, volleyball does not) (§369). Wittgenstein asks whether it would be conceivable that someone remembers for the first time and says he now knows what remembering feels like. How would he know this feeling is remembering, as opposed to something caused by something past, given that a person learns the concept of the past by remembering? And how would he know it again? One might perhaps speak of a feeling of "Long, long ago", for there is a tone and gesture that go with tales of past times (§370).

### xiv: psychology and mathematics, §§371–372 (p. 243)
The confusion and barrenness of psychology is not to be explained by its being a "young science"; its state is not comparable with physics in its beginnings but rather with certain branches of mathematics (set theory). Psychology has experimental methods and conceptual confusion. The existence of the experimental method makes us think we have the means to get rid of our problems; but "problem and method pass one another by" (§371). An investigation entirely analogous to ours is possible for mathematics. It is just as little a mathematical investigation as ours is a psychological one. It will contain no calculations, so it is not, for example, formal logic, and it might deserve the name of an investigation of the foundations of mathematics (§372).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ten students each compute 25 × 25.
- Method A: 25 × 20 + 25 × 5 = 500 + 125 = 625.
- Method B: (30 − 5)² = 900 − 2·30·5 + 25 = 900 − 300 + 25 = 625.

All ten write 625. If one wrote 615, the others would re-add it and the disagreement would end quickly (compare §§341, 344). Now ten observers judge a recording of someone saying "ouch" while smiling. Suppose six say genuine and four say pretending. No re-adding settles it. Better judges' predictions may prove more correct, but there is no technique with rules (compare §§352–355). Both kinds of judgement can be certain (§330), yet the surrounding practice differs. That difference is what §332 points to when it says the kind of certainty is the kind of language-game.

## Concepts
- [[language-game]] — a context of use; the kind of certainty is the kind of language-game (§332, p. 236e), and "meaning it" in the word-experiencing game takes its expression over from another language-game (§273, p. 227e)
- [[meaning-as-use]] — the objection raised at §265 is that meaning is the use of the word; the reply is that the felt "fullness" of a word is a figure, and a figurative use cannot conflict with the original use (§265, p. 226e)
- [[aspect-blindness]] (new) — inability to see an aspect switch; said to be akin to lacking a musical ear (§§258–260, p. 225e)
- [[secondary-meaning]] (new) — a familiar word used in a new way (Wednesday "fat"); only someone for whom the word has the primary meaning uses it so; not metaphor (§§276–278, pp. 227e–228e)
- [[meaning-intending-not-experience]] (new) — meaning and intending have no experiential content and meaning is not a process accompanying a word (§§279, 291, pp. 228e–229e)
- [[inner-speech-not-hidden]] (new) — silent speech is not behind a veil; hiddenness from others is part of the concept, but "hidden" is the wrong word (§§301, 307, pp. 231e–232e)
- [[kinds-of-certainty]] (new) — the kind of certainty is the kind of language-game; a logical, not psychological, difference (§§330, 332, pp. 235e–236e)
- [[forms-of-life]] (new) — what has to be accepted, the given, "one might say" (§345); the next remarks show that disagreement in colour judgements or calculation would change the concepts and techniques (§§346–347, p. 238e)
- [[imponderable-evidence]] (new) — subtle evidence (glance, gesture, tone) bearing on genuineness; in the chemical-structure example it must prove itself by ponderable consequences, and a loving look may get ponderable confirmation (§§359–360, p. 240e)
- [[concepts-and-nature]] (new) — to someone who thinks certain concepts absolutely correct, Wittgenstein says: imagine very general natural facts different, and other concepts become intelligible (§366, p. 241e)
