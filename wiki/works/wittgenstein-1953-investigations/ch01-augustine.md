# Augustine's picture, language-games, naming and simples (§§1–62)

## L0 — One line
Wittgenstein challenges the picture on which every word is a name of an object, as a picture of all language, and asks us to look at how words are actually used in simple "language-games" (a language-game is a language together with the activities it is woven into).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Augustine's picture says that words name objects and that sentences combine names. Wittgenstein tests this picture against simple uses of language. He describes simple imagined uses of language (builders, shopping slip, colour squares) and points out that words do very different jobs. In his chess example, ostensive definition (explaining a word by pointing) explains the use only if the learner already has a "place" prepared for the word. The philosophical wish for "real names" of indestructible simples is traced to a picture; experience does not show us such simples. He argues that "simple" and "composite" have a clear sense only once a kind of use is fixed. Studying primitive uses is said to disperse the fog around "meaning".

**Prerequisites:** none beyond ordinary reading; the idea of a word's "meaning" as what it stands for; some chess.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Augustine's picture** is that words name objects and sentences combine names. It fits only a narrow area of language, though Wittgenstein admits it describes a real system of communication (pp. 5e–6e).
- **Primitive language-games** (builders, shopping slip) make the use of words surveyable. They let us ask what a word's "meaning" is by looking at what is done with it (pp. 6e–9e).
- **Words are like tools** with diverse functions, and their uniform look misleads us. "Every word signifies something" says nothing until a contrast is specified (pp. 9e–10e).
- **There are countless kinds of sentence and use.** The speaking of language is part of an activity or form of life (a way of living in which speaking occurs), and new uses arise while others die out (pp. 14e–15e).
- **Naming is a preparation (§26 asks "for what?"; §49 calls it a preparation for describing, not yet a move).** Ostensive definition can be variously interpreted, and in the chess case it explains the use only where the learner already has a place for it. How the learner takes it shows in his later use (pp. 16e–19e, 28e).
- **Pointing "at the colour" or "at the shape"** covers many different things, and no single accompanying act is what makes us say what is meant. Circumstances count, and we are tempted to posit a mental activity (pp. 19e–22e).
- **The idea that names must signify simples** (Nothung, and an account in Plato's *Theaetetus*) is examined; one step in the argument uses "meaning" for the bearer of a name, which Wittgenstein calls a solecism. "Simple" has a clear sense only relative to a kind of use (pp. 23e–27e).
- **Samples and paradigms are instruments of language**, not things represented. Of the standard metre one can state neither that it is nor that it is not 1 metre long, and "Red exists" is treated as a remark about the use of the word "red" (pp. 29e, 32e–33e). A paradigm is a sample used as a standard of comparison.
- **Is analysis into parts hidden in the ordinary sentence?** Wittgenstein presses the question. Agreeing in some cases that two orders "come to the same thing" is not general agreement on the use of that expression (pp. 33e–34e).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Preface (pp. 3e–4e)
The book is a collection of remarks, not a smooth treatise, because the author's thoughts "grew feeble" when forced along one track (p. 3e). Its remarks are "sketches of landscapes" made on criss-cross journeys, and the book is "an album" (pp. 3e–4e). The author says he published the new thoughts together with the old ones of the *Tractatus*. He says it suddenly seemed to him that the new ones could be seen in the right light only by contrast with and against the background of his older way of thinking (p. 4e). He credits criticism from Ramsey and Sraffa (p. 4e). He hopes to stimulate readers to thoughts of their own, not spare them thinking (p. 4e). The preface is dated Cambridge, January 1945 (p. 4e).

### §1 Augustine's picture (pp. 5e–6e)
Augustine (*Confessions* I.8) describes learning language. Adults name an object while turning toward it, and the child grasps that the sound signifies the thing. The child gathers this from gestures, expression and tone of voice (p. 5e).
- **Wittgenstein's reading:** this gives "a particular picture of the essence of human language": words name objects, and sentences are combinations of names. From it springs the idea that every word has a meaning, correlated with the word and being the object it stands for (p. 5e).
- Augustine mentions no difference between kinds of word. Wittgenstein believes such a describer thinks mainly of nouns and personal names, and treats other words as something that "will take care of itself" (p. 5e).
- **Counter-example:** a slip "five red apples" is taken to a shopkeeper. He opens the drawer marked "apples", looks up "red" in a colour chart and finds a sample. He recites number-words up to "five" and takes one apple matching the sample for each (pp. 5e–6e).
- **Interlocutor:** how does he know how to look up "red" and what to do with "five"? **Wittgenstein:** I assume he acts as described, and "explanations come to an end somewhere". The question "what is the meaning of 'five'?" was not at issue, only how the word is used (p. 6e).

### §§2–6 Primitive languages and teaching (pp. 6e–8e)
- **§2:** The philosophical notion of meaning belongs to a primitive idea of how language works, or to a language more primitive than ours. Imagine a builder A and an assistant B with the words "block", "pillar", "slab", "beam". A calls a word and B brings the stone he has learnt to bring at that call. Conceive this as a complete primitive language, for which Augustine's description is right (p. 6e).
- **§3:** Augustine does describe a system of communication, but not everything we call language is it. The description holds for a narrowly circumscribed area. Compare "playing a game is moving objects on a surface by rules", which fits only board-games (p. 6e).
- **§4:** Reading a script as mere letter–sound correspondence, ignoring emphasis and punctuation, is like Augustine's over-simple view (pp. 6e–7e).
- **§5:** The general concept of word meaning "surrounds the working of language with a haze". The fog clears if we study primitive uses where purpose and functioning can be surveyed. A child using such forms learns by training, not by explanation (p. 7e).
- **§6:** Imagine the language of §2 as a tribe's whole language. Children are trained to act, to use the words and to react to the words of others. Wittgenstein calls the teacher's pointing and uttering "ostensive teaching of words", not "ostensive explanation", because the child cannot yet ask what the name is (p. 7e).
  - Such teaching may establish an "associative connection". One may think of an image of the object arising in the child's mind. This may be a word's purpose in some uses, but in the language of §2 evoking images is not the purpose (p. 7e).
  - The ostensive teaching helped bring about understanding only together with a particular kind of instruction. With different instruction it would have produced quite different understanding. Compare a brake-lever: only given the rest of the mechanism is it a brake-lever, and separated from its support it is not even a lever (pp. 7e–8e).

*Intuition note:* Augustine's picture is not simply false. It is a correct description of a narrowly circumscribed area of language (§3), and it goes wrong as a description of everything we call language. Pointing at a thing and saying a word teaches a use only against a background of training.

### §§7–10 Language-games (pp. 8e–9e)
- **§7:** In teaching, the learner names objects when the teacher points, or repeats the teacher's words. Such children's activities, and the whole use of words in (2), are "language-games". Wittgenstein also calls "the whole, consisting of language and the activities into which it is woven" a language-game (p. 8e).
- **§8:** Expansion of (2): a series of letters used as number-words, "there" and "this" used with pointing, and colour samples. An order is "d–slab–there": B takes slabs of the sample's colour, one for each letter up to "d", to the indicated place (p. 8e).
- **§9:** Counting is learnt by heart and then through use. Ostensive teaching of the first five or six number-words as group-signifiers is possible. For "there" and "this", pointing occurs in the use of the words too, not only in learning (p. 9e).
- **§10:** Wittgenstein asks (rhetorically) how what the words signify could come out other than in the kind of use they have. One can abbreviate a description of use by saying "this word signifies this object". That is done when it removes a particular misunderstanding, and the kind of "referring" is already known. But making descriptions look alike cannot make the uses more alike, for they are "absolutely unlike" (p. 9e).

### §§11–17 Tools, signifying, samples (pp. 9e–11e)
- **§§11–12:** Words are like tools in a toolbox, and their functions are diverse. What confuses us is their uniform look in speech or print. Compare handles in a locomotive cabin: one is a crank, one a two-position switch, one a brake lever, one a pump (pp. 9e–10e).
- **§13:** "Every word in the language signifies something" says nothing unless we state what distinction is meant. For example, it might distinguish language (8) from nonsense words such as "Tra-la-la" (p. 10e).
- **§14:** Suppose someone says "All tools serve to modify something". To cover a rule, a glue-pot and nails, he must say they modify our knowledge of a length, the temperature of the glue and the solidity of a box. Wittgenstein asks whether anything is gained by this assimilation (p. 10e).
- **§15:** "Signify" applies most straightforwardly when the name is a mark on the object. Naming as attaching a name tag "will often prove useful" to say to ourselves when philosophizing (p. 10e).
- **§16:** Whether the colour samples are part of the language is "as you please", but it is most natural and causes least confusion to count them as tools of the language. The second "the" in "Pronounce the word 'the'" has the role of a sample (pp. 10e–11e).
- **§17:** How we group words into kinds depends on the aim of the classification, as with tools or chess pieces (p. 11e).

*Intuition note:* Saying that every word signifies something says nothing until a contrast is specified (§13), and the uniform look of words confuses us about their different jobs (§11). Wittgenstein points to the use a word has in a given activity.

### §§18–25 Completeness, "Slab!", assertion, variety (pp. 11e–16e)
- **§18:** It is no objection that (2) and (8) consist only of orders. Wittgenstein asks whether our own language is complete, or was before chemical symbolism and the calculus were added, and compares it to an ancient city with new suburbs. He also asks how many houses it takes before a town is a town (p. 11e).
- **§19:** To imagine a language is to imagine a form of life. Is "Slab!" a word or a sentence? It may be called either, perhaps a "degenerate sentence". It is our "elliptical" sentence (pp. 11e–12e).
  - **Objector:** it is a shortened "Bring me a slab", and the speaker means that. **Wittgenstein asks (rhetorically):** why not call the long sentence a lengthening of "Slab!"? Does meaning it consist in saying the unshortened sentence to oneself? Does wanting it consist in thinking a different sentence from the one uttered? (p. 12e)
- **§20:** Is "Bring me a slab" meant as one word or four? Wittgenstein's questions suggest that meaning it as four words does not consist in something that accompanies the utterance. We say we use it in contrast with other sentences because our language contains the possibility of those sentences (pp. 12e–13e).
  - A foreigner might take the sound sequence as one word. We call his conception wrong, but that does not have to lie in anything accompanying his utterance (p. 13e).
  - The sentence is "elliptical" not because it omits something we mean but because it is shortened compared with a paradigm of our grammar (p. 13e). **Objection:** the two have the same sense, so what is it? **Wittgenstein asks:** doesn't their same sense consist in their same use? The question implies yes (p. 13e). In Russian one says "Stone red"; Wittgenstein asks whether the sense they grasp lacks the copula or whether they add it in thought, and leaves the questions open (p. 13e).
- **§21:** The report "Five slabs" and the order "Five slabs!" differ in the part they play in the language-game. Tone may be the same. "Assertion" and "command" can also name grammatical form and intonation, as "Isn't the weather glorious?" is called a question though used as an assertion (p. 13e).
- **§22:** **Frege's opinion** (as Wittgenstein reports it): every assertion contains an assumption, which is what is asserted. It rests on our ability to write any assertion as "It is asserted that such-and-such". But "that such-and-such is the case" is not yet a move in the language-game (p. 14e).
  - One could equally write every assertion as a question plus "Yes!" ("Is it raining? Yes!"). That would not show that every assertion contains a question (p. 14e).
  - An assertion sign is legitimate, for example to distinguish assertion from fiction. The mistake is to think assertion consists of two acts, entertaining and asserting, followed sign by sign like singing from sheet music. "Meaning" the sentence is not like that (p. 14e).
  - The Fregean assertion sign marks the start of a sentence, like a full stop. A boxer picture can be used to tell how to stand, how not to stand, or how someone did stand, so it might be called a "sentence-radical". Frege probably conceived the "assumption" like this (a printed remark, p. 14e).
- **§23:** There are countless kinds of sentence and use, and new language-games arise while others become obsolete. "Language-game" emphasizes that speaking is part of an activity or form of life. Wittgenstein lists examples: ordering, describing, reporting, speculating, forming hypotheses, telling jokes, translating, requesting, thanking, cursing, greeting, praying (pp. 14e–15e). He remarks that it is interesting to compare this variety with what logicians, including the author of the *Tractatus*, have said about language structure (p. 15e).
- **§24:** Someone who forgets this variety may be inclined to ask "What is a question?" The word "description" itself covers many different things. Rewriting a question as "I want to know whether…" does not bring the language-games closer together (pp. 15e–16e). The significance of turning all sentences into "I think…" forms is deferred ("Solipsism") (p. 16e).
- **§25:** It is said animals do not talk because they lack mental abilities. Wittgenstein replies that they simply do not talk, apart from the most primitive forms. Giving orders, asking questions and chatting are as much part of our natural history as walking, eating, drinking and playing (p. 16e).

*Intuition note:* "Statement", "question" and "command" are not settled by grammatical form or tone alone: the difference can lie in the use made of the words (§21). There are countless kinds of use, and they are not fixed once for all (§23).

### §§26–38 Naming and ostensive definition (pp. 16e–23e)
- **§26–27:** Learning language is not just giving names to objects. Naming is a "preparation", but for what? **Interlocutor:** "We name things and then we can talk about them." **Wittgenstein:** this treats what follows as given with naming, whereas we do very various things with sentences, for example exclamations ("Water!", "Away!", "Ow!", "Help!", "No!"). Asking "What is that called?" and inventing names are language-games in their own right (pp. 16e–17e).
- **§28:** Pointing at two nuts and saying "That is called 'two'" is "perfectly exact". But the hearer may take "two" as a name of the group, or the reverse mistake. An ostensive definition can be variously interpreted in any case (p. 17e). A printed remark asks whether one could explain "red" by pointing at something not red, compares this to pointing at an arrogant man to explain "modest", says ambiguity is no argument against such an explanation because any explanation can be misunderstood, and asks whether we should still call it an "explanation" (p. 17e).
- **§29:** The reply "This number is called 'two'" needs the word "number" to be explained first. Whether it is needed depends on circumstances and the person. Explaining by other words forms a chain; Wittgenstein asks what about the last explanation in it, and warns against saying "There isn't a last explanation", which is like saying there is no last house in a road. How the learner takes the explanation shows in how he uses the word (p. 18e).
- **§30:** An ostensive definition explains the use of a word if the role the word is to play is already clear. One must already know or be able to do something before asking what a thing is called (pp. 18e).
- **§31:** "This is the king" explains the piece's use only if the learner already knows the rules except the shape. Or the learner may have learnt games without rules, by watching; the explanation then informs him of the use because "the place for it was already prepared", since he has already mastered a game. It makes sense to ask what something is called only if one already knows how to use the name (pp. 18e–19e).
- **§32:** Wittgenstein thinks we can say that Augustine describes the child as if it came into a foreign country and already had a language, only not this one. That is, it could already think, only not speak, where "think" means roughly "talk to himself" (p. 19e).
- **§33:** **Objection:** one needs only to know or guess what the explainer is pointing at, whether shape, colour or number. **Wittgenstein:** what does pointing at the shape consist in? Pointing at a sheet of paper, then at its shape, colour, number: how was each done? (pp. 19e–20e)
  - Attending to the colour takes different forms in different cases: comparing blues, mixing paint, seeing blue sky, "bring the blue book", "this blue light means…". He asks whether one always does the same thing, and the cases suggest no single act is involved (p. 20e).
  - Compare a chess move: it is not just pushing a piece, nor the accompanying thoughts, but the circumstances we call playing chess (p. 20e).
- **§34:** Suppose someone says he always follows a contour with his eyes when attending to a shape, and gives the explanation "That is called a 'circle'". Wittgenstein asks whether the hearer can't still interpret it differently. That interpretation may consist in how he later uses the word. To "mean" or "interpret" an explanation in a way is not a process accompanying giving and hearing it (p. 21e).
- **§35:** There are "characteristic experiences" of pointing at a shape, but they do not occur in all cases of meaning the shape. Even if one did recur, circumstances before and after would decide whether we say "He pointed at the shape". "To point at the shape" is not used like "to point at this book". Wittgenstein asks whether we know of an experience characteristic of pointing at a piece as a piece in a game; all the same, one can say "I mean this piece is called the 'king'" (p. 21e).
  - A printed remark says one can say "bububu" and mean something only in a language, so the grammar of "to mean" is not like that of "to imagine" (p. 22e).
- **§36:** Because we cannot specify a bodily action that is pointing at the shape, we say a mental activity corresponds. Where our language suggests a body and there is none, "we should like to say, is a spirit" (p. 22e).
- **§37:** The relation between name and thing named is seen in language-games. It may consist in the name calling a picture before the mind, or in the name being written on the thing or uttered when pointing (p. 22e).
- **§38:** In (8) or in "That is called…", it is best not to say "this" or "that" names anything. Yet "this" has been called the real name and other names inexact. This comes from a tendency to "sublimate" the logic of our language (pp. 22e–23e).
  - We call very different things "names", but the use of "this" is not among them. A name is explained by "That is N". Wittgenstein asks whether we also explain "This is called 'this'" (p. 23e).
  - The occult picture of naming arises when a philosopher stares at an object repeating a name or "this". "Philosophical problems arise when language goes on holiday" (p. 23e).

*Intuition note:* Pointing and saying a word does not by itself fix what the word is for. The learner's later use, in a practice he is already partly in, is what shows how he took it. Wittgenstein says we posit a mental activity because we cannot specify a bodily action of pointing at the shape (§36).

### §§39–59 Names and simples (pp. 23e–33e)
- **§39 The argument for simples** (stated as a temptation, not as Wittgenstein's view): a name should really signify a simple. The sword Nothung consists of parts. "Nothung has a sharp blade" has sense even if Nothung is shattered. But if "Nothung" names the object and the object is gone, the word would have no meaning and the sentence would be nonsense. So the sense must be analysed into words naming simples, the "real names" (pp. 23e–24e).
- **§40:** It is a solecism to use "meaning" for the thing that corresponds to a word, which confuses meaning with bearer. When Mr N.N. dies, the bearer dies, not the meaning (p. 24e).
- **§41–42:** In (8) with names for tools, "N" is broken and A calls it. Is the sign meaningless? We have not settled this. It may be called meaningless if it has no use. But one could lay down a convention that B shakes his head, and then "N" has meaning even when its bearer is gone. Even signs never used for a tool could be admitted into the game under such a convention (p. 24e).
- **§43:** For a large class of cases of the employment of the word "meaning", though not for all, this word can be explained thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language. The meaning of a name is sometimes explained by pointing to its bearer (p. 25e).
- **§44–45:** In the Nothung game, names are used in the absence of the bearer. One could imagine a game where names are used only in the bearer's presence and could be replaced by "this" and a pointing gesture. "This" is never without a bearer, but that does not make it a name. A name is explained by a pointing gesture, not used with one (p. 25e).
- **§46:** The idea comes from the Theaetetus. **Socrates** reports that primary elements can only be named and not explained, whereas complexes are interwoven names. Wittgenstein says Russell's "individuals" and his own *Tractatus* "objects" were such primary elements (p. 25e).
- **§47:** What are the simple parts of a chair: wood, molecules, atoms? "It makes no sense at all to speak absolutely of the 'simple parts of a chair'" (pp. 25e–26e).
  - "Composite" has many uses: multi-colouredness, a curve of straight bits, a visual image with trunk and branches. If one stipulates the use, "Is this composite?" gets a clear sense. Without that, the answer is "That depends on what you understand by 'composite'", which rejects the question (pp. 26e–27e).
  - The chessboard is composed of 64 squares, or of two colours and a schema of squares. Asking "Is this object composite?" outside a game is like a boy puzzling over whether "to sleep" is active or passive (p. 26e).
- **§48 Language-game where the Theaetetus account is valid:** the words "R", "G", "W", "B" describe coloured squares on a 3×3 grid numbered 1–9 in rows. "RRBGGGRWW" describes an arrangement of this sort. Here the sentence is a complex of names, and the coloured squares are the elements (p. 27e). Are the squares simple? The text says one would not know what to call simple more naturally in this game, but that in other circumstances a monochrome square might be called composite (pp. 27e–28e). Does the figure consist of four or nine elements? Does the sentence consist of four or nine letters, types or tokens? He asks whether it matters which we say, so long as misunderstandings are avoided in any particular case (p. 28e).
- **§49:** That elements can only be named, not described, may mean that in a limiting case the description of a one-square complex is just the name. Whether "R" is a word or a sentence depends on the situation. Naming is a preparation for describing. Naming "is not yet a move in a language-game", just as putting a piece in place is not a move in chess. This is what Frege meant when he said a word has meaning only in the context of a sentence (p. 28e).
- **§50:** Wittgenstein asks what it means to say that neither being nor non-being can be attributed to elements. One might say: if "being" is the obtaining of connections between elements, it makes no sense to speak of the being of an element. Of the standard metre in Paris one can state neither that it is 1 metre long nor that it is not. This marks its role in measuring, not a remarkable property (pp. 28e–29e).
  - Likewise, if "sepia" means the colour of a preserved sample, it makes no sense to say the sample is or is not of that colour (p. 29e).
  - The sample is "an instrument of the language", a means of representation and not something represented. "What looks as if it had to exist is part of the language", a paradigm, and this is an observation about our language-game (p. 29e).
- **§51:** What does the correspondence of "R" to red squares consist in? Is it that the speaker always says "R" for red? Then what makes a slip a mistake? Or is it that a red square comes before the mind? We must look "from close up" (pp. 29e–30e).
- **§52:** If I think a mouse arises from rags, I should examine the rags. But what in philosophy resists this examination of details we have yet to understand (p. 30e).
- **§53–54:** In (48) we would say a sign names a square of a colour if the users were taught in some way, or if a chart was used in teaching and in deciding disputes. The chart can also be a tool in use, taking over the role that memory and association play elsewhere. A rule of a language-game "may have very different roles": an aid in teaching, a tool of the game, or a regularity an observer reads off, with players' corrections of slips as signs of mistakes (pp. 30e–31e).
- **§55:** **The objector's argument** (voice: the view under examination): names must signify what is indestructible, since the description of the destruction of everything destructible uses words, and what corresponds to those words cannot be destroyed. **Wittgenstein:** "I must not saw off the branch on which I am sitting." The man is what corresponds to his name, yet he is destructible and his name keeps its meaning. A paradigm used in conjunction with a name in a language-game would be an example of something which corresponds to a name and without which it would have no meaning (p. 31e).
- **§56:** **Objector:** a colour kept in mind must be indestructible. **Wittgenstein:** what is the criterion of remembering it right? He asks whether we aren't as much at the mercy of memory as of a sample, and says this shows we do not always treat memory as the highest court of appeal (pp. 31e–32e).
- **§57:** "Something red can be destroyed, but red cannot" (voice: objector). Wittgenstein asks whether we don't say "The red is vanishing". If we forget which colour a name belongs to, the name loses its meaning for us, like losing a paradigm (p. 32e).
- **§58:** **Objector:** "Red exists" cannot be said, as without red it could not be spoken of. **Wittgenstein:** if "X exists" amounts to "'X' has a meaning", it is a sentence about the use of the word "X", not about X. We want to take "Red exists" as "'Red' has a meaning", but the expression looks as if it were about the colour. We quite readily say a colour exists, meaning that something with that colour exists (pp. 32e–33e).
- **§59:** "A name signifies only an element of reality" (voice: the view under examination). Wittgenstein: experience certainly does not show us such elements. We see parts of a composite and a whole which changes while its parts remain, and from these we construct the picture. It is a picture we wanted to use (p. 33e).

*Intuition note:* Wittgenstein says that what looks as if it had to exist is part of the language, a paradigm (§50), and that the idea of simple elements is a picture we wanted to use (§59). Samples and paradigms are used to represent and cannot themselves be said to have or lack the property they set.

### §§60–62 Analysis (pp. 33e–34e)
- **§60:** "My broom is in the corner" could be replaced by a statement giving the positions of stick and brush, a "further analysed" form. The ordinary sentence's sense seems to hide this relation. But the speaker would probably say he did not specially think of stick or brush. The analysed order "Bring me the broomstick and the brush fitted on it" would draw the reply "Why do you put it so oddly?" (pp. 33e–34e).
  - Imagine two games: in (a) composite objects have names, as in (15), and in (b) only parts are named and wholes described. Is an order in (b) an analysed form of one in (a)? Does the former lie hidden in the latter? True, the broom is taken to pieces when stick and brush are separated; but does it follow that the order to bring the broom also consists of corresponding parts? (p. 34e)
- **§61:** **Objector:** surely an order in (a) says the same as one in (b). **Wittgenstein:** I would say they have the same sense, meaning I can say which (b) order has the same sense as an (a) order, or which it contradicts. That is not general agreement on the use of "same sense". In what cases do we say "These are merely two different forms of the same game"? (p. 34e)
- **§62:** Suppose the person who carries out orders in (a) and (b) must look up a table linking names and pictures. Does he do the same in both? "Yes and no." You may say the point of the two orders is the same; the page ends as Wittgenstein begins "I would say…" (p. 34e).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Point:** an ostensive definition leaves several readings open, and only later use shows which one the learner took (compare §§28, 34).

A teacher points to one red square (call it R1) and says "That is called 'zib'." The learner has three live readings:
- **C:** "zib" means the colour red.
- **S:** "zib" means square.
- **O:** "zib" names that very object only.

Now test with four items: red circle, blue square, red square R2 (a second one) and green triangle.
- Under C the learner calls zib: red circle and R2, so 2 items.
- Under S he calls zib: blue square and R2, so 2 items.
- Under O he calls zib: none of these four, so 0 items, since R1 is not among them.

The lesson is that the teacher's pointing was the same in all three cases. The red circle separates C from S, because C picks it and S does not. The blue square separates S from C, because S picks it and C does not. R2 is picked by both C and S, so it does not separate them (it does separate both from O). How the learner "took" the word shows only in his use (§29), against a background in which he already knows what kind of thing a name might be (§31).

## Concepts
- [[augustinian-picture]] (new) — words name objects and sentences are combinations of names; every word has an object as its meaning (p. 5e)
- [[language-game]] — a primitive language, a naming or repeating exercise, or "the whole" of language plus the activities it is woven into (p. 8e)
- [[meaning as use]] — for a large class of cases of the word "meaning", though not all, it can be explained as the word's use in the language (p. 25e)
- [[ostensive-teaching-vs-definition]] (new) — teaching by pointing and uttering, where the child cannot yet ask for the name, versus explanation by definition (p. 7e)
- [[form-of-life]] (new) — the activity-embedded way of living in which speaking occurs; to imagine a language is to imagine one (pp. 11e, 15e)
- [[logical form vs. grammatical form]] — the same grammatical form (a question form, or "You will do this") may do the work of different acts, so function lies in use (p. 13e)
- [[analysis]] — replacing an ordinary sentence by a "further analysed" one; Wittgenstein asks whether the first conceals the second (p. 33e)
- [[simple-vs-composite]] (new) — "simple" and "composite" have sense only relative to a specified use, so absolute talk is rejected (pp. 26e–27e)
- [[sample-as-instrument-of-representation]] (new) — a paradigm that is a means of representation, of which one cannot say that it has or lacks the property it sets (p. 29e)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Wittgenstein's "meaning is use" ↔ pragmatics: what a sentence does (order vs report) depends on its role, not on its form — §21 shows form and function can differ.
- [metaphor] "Naming is a preparation, not yet a move" ↔ game theory: a piece's position matters only within a rule structure that defines moves (compare the chess analogy, p. 28e).
- [structural-analogy] The Theaetetus / Tractatus thesis that names signify simples ↔ logical atomism in philosophy of logic: analysis ends in indestructible elements; here the doctrine is the target (p. 25e).
- [metaphor] Pointing is "variously interpreted" ↔ machine learning: a few labelled examples underdetermine the concept (colour, shape or object). Only later behaviour shows what was learnt.

## Questions this raises
- If explanations come to an end "somewhere", what makes the ending point acceptable to a learner without a prior game, since the child begins without a language?
- If "composite" has sense only inside a game, can any use ever be called "analysis", and what would count as a good analysis?
- What does Wittgenstein mean by "the grammar of 'to mean'"? How does it differ from the grammar of "to imagine"? (p. 22e)
- The text leaves open "what it is in philosophy that resists" close examination of details (p. 30e). What is it?
