# Movie Leaders

## L0 — One line
Among women who go to the movies at least monthly, movie opinion leaders come mostly from the young, unmarried women, and they are more often frequent and choosy goers than non-leaders. Movie leadership is virtually unrelated to gregariousness; the authors think influence flows largely inside movie-going companion groups of age peers, and a movie "expert" is often sought among the young.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks who the movie opinion leaders are among women who go to the movies at least monthly, and how movie influence flows. (Arenas are the book's topic areas: marketing, fashions, public affairs, movies.) Leaders come mostly from the young, unmarried "girls". Within each age group they are also more often frequent goers, and they appear more particular about the picture. Unlike in the other arenas, movie leadership is virtually unrelated to gregariousness. The authors offer three explanations, chiefly that movie-going is itself done in groups. Social status is also unrelated. Reported advice mostly passes between age peers, probably within these groups. When women name a movie "expert", they often choose younger women.

**Prerequisites:** reading percentage tables; the book's operational "opinion leader" (a woman asked for advice) and its life-cycle and gregariousness indices (earlier chapters).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Analysis is limited to women who go at least monthly (about 60% of respondents). Attendance falls sharply with age (p. 296).
- Movie leadership is concentrated among girls (58% vs 23% of wives). Being married or single matters about as much as age (p. 298).
- Within age groups, frequent goers are more often leaders than occasional goers (p. 298).
- Movie leadership is virtually unrelated to gregariousness, unlike the other arenas. The authors give three explanations (pp. 299–301).
- Nearly everyone goes with companions, so the authors suppose the leader's following is at hand. Evidence for in-group influence is only circumstantial (pp. 301–302).
- Leaders go more often with non-family companions and more often choose a particular picture. The authors guess family attendance is more ritualistic (pp. 303–305).
- Reported influence is mostly between age peers. Named movie "experts" are disproportionately young (pp. 306–308).
- Of the three leadership correlates, only life-cycle is relevant. Status is least relevant in this arena (p. 308).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Scope.** About 60% of respondents go to the movies monthly or more. The analysis covers only these "active participants" (p. 296). Table 46 (attendance by age) shows a marked decline. Once a week or more: 68% (under 25), 49% (25–34), 34% (35–44), 19% (45+). Under 25, 88% go monthly or more (68 + 20). At 45+, 37% + 17% = 54% go less than monthly or never (p. 296).

**"Youth Culture": movie leadership and the life-cycle.** Chart XV: well over half of the girls (58%) have recently been asked about movies. The married types show 23%, 27% and 16% (p. 298).
- The girl–wife gap (58 vs 23) is very sharp. The gaps among married types are very small. The authors conclude that being married or single is as important as age (p. 298).
- Girls are freer and have fewer family responsibilities. The authors also say movie-going has a different role in their social affairs (p. 298).
- Leaders are defined as in the other arenas (fn. 2). They told us twice that they were recently asked advice on what picture to see. Or they reported one incident but think they are "more likely" than others to be asked (p. 298).
- Table 47: frequent goers (weekly or more) are more often leaders than occasional goers (1–3 times a month). Under 25: 55% vs 32%. Age 25+: 28% vs 13% (p. 298).

*Intuition note:* Leadership is associated with freedom from family duties and a social role built around movies. The authors say it is not age alone that makes the difference.

The authors expect age homogeneity of influencer and influencee. They also expect some flow from young to old. They postpone this discussion (p. 299).

**Movie leadership and gregariousness: a special case.** In the other arenas leaders are more gregarious. Here there is "virtually no relationship" (p. 299). The authors give three answers.
1. Girls have relatively few organizational ties but relatively many friends, so they tended to fall into the medium-gregariousness category (Ch. IX). Girls fill the leader ranks, so leaders concentrate somewhat in the medium group (pp. 299–300).
2. Women of high gregariousness may have reason to be less interested in movies than women whose social lives are less full (p. 300). The authors say this factor undoubtedly plays a part in explaining why they are not more likely to be leaders.
3. Even the least gregarious women are about as often leaders as the highly gregarious. The authors' answer, which they give as what they think, is that movie-going is itself a gregarious activity that the index cannot measure (p. 301).
   - Almost nine in ten went to their last movie with someone else. This is almost unanimous under 25 and almost so for older women. There is no significant difference between married and unmarried women or between leaders and non-leaders (p. 301).
   - A cited study (Handel, 1950) found 86% of women and 70% of men accompanied. The typical unit is two people (fn. 4, p. 301).
   - Authors' inference: the give-and-take mostly happens inside these groups. The leader's following is at hand. Movie-going is "more properly studied" as a group decision, where leaders "presumably" have the upper hand (p. 301).
   - Marketing is the only other arena with some shared decisions (fn. 5, p. 301).

(Chart XVI values: [unreadable p. 300]. The text layer gives only partial numbers. The chapter states that medium-gregariousness women are somewhat more often leaders, p. 300.)

*Intuition note:* The gregariousness index counts friends and organizations. It does not count the companion a woman sits next to at the cinema. That companion is a ready audience.

**Shared movie-going and the flow of influence.** If this is right, influence should be concentrated in movie-going groups. The authors "can only hint" at this, because the study did not ask directly about joint decisions (p. 302, fn. 6). Most respondents, the authors think, did not link their question about hearing someone talk about it to everyday talk with companions (fn. 6).
- Table 48 compares named companions with named movie leaders (p. 302).

| | Non-family | Family |
|---|---|---|
| Single women: companions (n=66) | 67% | 33% |
| Single women: leaders (n=35) | 76% | 23% |
| Married women: companions (n=316) | 15% | 85% |
| Married women: leaders (n=132) | 40% | 60% |

- Both groups, particularly wives, seek non-family advice in somewhat greater proportion than non-family companions. The authors say this is probably because family is more available and, for wives, more acceptable (p. 303).
- Table 49: leaders go with non-family members more than non-leaders do. Under 25: 53% vs 38%. Age 25+: 31% vs 15%. Unmarried: 70% vs 52%. Married: 26% vs 12% (p. 303).
- Explanation 1: non-family goers may simply recall advice exchanges better. Then, the authors say, Table 49 is of no great importance (p. 303).
- Explanation 2 (authors' guess): family attendance may be somewhat more ritualistic, and so involve less careful choosing. Husband–wife or parent–child units may see whatever is playing on their usual day (pp. 303–304).
- Supporting evidence (Table 50). More leaders say they go when the picture sounds interesting. Frequent goers under 25: 70% vs 56%. Frequent 25+: 76% vs 57%. Occasional under 25: 89% (n=9) vs 58%. Occasional 25+: 71% vs 73% (p. 304).
- A free evening is the reason for 19% of leaders and 27% of non-leaders (p. 304).
- Table 51 (went to see a certain picture). Frequent under 25: 57% vs 54%. Frequent 25+: 63% vs 57%. Occasional under 25: 78% (n=9) vs 26%. Occasional 25+: 81% vs 68%. The authors say the gap is relatively small for young frequent goers and larger in the other categories, consistent throughout (p. 305).
- Authors' reading: leaders' companions may be less particular than the leaders. Non-family groups may be choosier as groups. This calls for further investigation (p. 305).
- The "fundamental point" remains: married women's norm is family companionship and family leadership. Girls' norm is friends (p. 305).

**Age and the flow of influence.** Table 52 (respondent = leader, columns = influencee's age; fn. 8, p. 306).

| Leader's age | Influencee 15–24 | 25–44 | 45+ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15–24 | 30 | 9 | 4 | 43 |
| 25–44 | 11 | 36 | 6 | 53 |
| 45+ | 2 | 8 | 11 | 21 |

Age-peer influence is 70% (30/43), 68% (36/53) and 52% (11/21) of the row totals (p. 306). The authors sum up: girls are most likely to have been influenced by age peers and older women by other adults. The girls were influenced by friends, and older married women primarily by age peers within the family (p. 306).

**The role of the movie expert.** Table 52 suggests a sharp split between "youth culture" and the adult world. The authors raise the possibility that a different kind of transaction typically occurs outside the groups (p. 306). They use a second question, asking whether the respondent knows anybody nearby who usually knows about the movies and can tell what is a good picture to see. About 40% said yes. These respondents were predominantly people who go more often and who were more likely to have gone to see a certain picture rather than just to go (p. 307). The authors suspect specific influentials belong to the group and "experts" do not (p. 307).
- Table 53 (expert's age by respondent's age):
  - Expert 15–24: 24, 27, 12 (total 63).
  - Expert 25–44: 5, 42, 17 (total 64).
  - Expert 45+: 5, 9, 9 (total 23).
  - Respondent totals: 34, 78, 38 (n=150) (p. 307).
- The experts of the youngest group are still mostly people like themselves (24 of 34). The older groups have substituted younger women as experts in far greater proportion than they name such people as specific influentials. 23% of respondents are under 25, but 42% of their named experts are (p. 307).
- The category of children seems to make the major difference. For women 35+, their own children are almost a third of the experts (p. 307).
- Conclusion (hedged): if the "general influential" question is a good indication, it appears that both younger and older women seek movie experts among the young. The final decision may often be modified by primary influentials in the movie-going groups, who are more likely to be in the same age bracket as their groups (p. 308).

*Intuition note:* The two questions seem to pick out two kinds of influence. Asking whom women heard talk about a movie points to people of about the same age, often family for older married women. Asking who knows about movies points to younger women, possibly outside the group (the authors' suspicion).

**Social status.** Of the three correlates, only life-cycle is relevant. Gregariousness is virtually unrelated, and the authors add that so is status. On each status level approximately one person in four is a movie leader. Of the four arenas, status level is least relevant to movie leadership (p. 308).

**Summary.** Movie-going is "a main theme" of American youth culture. Leaders come from the young and carefree. Within every life-cycle bracket the leader is the more frequent goer, more particular, and more "exogamous" in companions (p. 308). The authors think influence flows largely within age-peer movie groups. But for an "expert", people of all ages turn to the girls (p. 308).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* It shows the first of the authors' three explanations only: a composition effect. Suppose gregariousness has no real effect. Girls are leaders 50% of the time and wives 25%. Each gregariousness level has 20 women.
- High: 4 girls, 16 wives. Leaders = 4×0.5 + 16×0.25 = 2 + 4 = 6, so 6/20 = 30%.
- Medium: 12 girls, 8 wives. Leaders = 12×0.5 + 8×0.25 = 6 + 2 = 8, so 8/20 = 40%.
- Low: 4 girls, 16 wives. Leaders = 2 + 4 = 6, so 30%.
Leadership peaks in the medium group although life-cycle alone drives it. This mirrors the chapter's first answer. The authors add two more answers: lower interest among highly gregarious women, and movie-going being itself a group activity.

## Concepts

- movie-going-as-group-decision (new) — movie-going is mostly shared, so decisions are made in companion groups and the leader's followers are at hand (p. 301)

- gregariousness-index (new) — organizational contacts plus friends. Here it misses the gregarious nature of movie-going (p. 301)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Leadership concentrated where a group already exists ↔ complex contagion: influence works through clustered, reinforcing ties, not isolated contacts.
- [metaphor] Younger "experts" consulted by all ages ↔ diffusion: heterophilous contact brings novelty into an age-homophilous group.
