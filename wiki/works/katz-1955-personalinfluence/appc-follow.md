# Appendix C. On Follow-up Interviewing and Analysis

## L0 — One line
To check "who influenced whom" the Decatur team interviewed the people named by respondents, and learned that influential-side and influencee-side follow-ups should not be expected to be mirror images, that some bias must be assumed in the follow-ups, and that a flow table has three interdependent aspects.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How did the Decatur study check respondents' reports of influence by interviewing the named other person? The follow-up questions were not fixed in advance but were developed partly during the field work. Confirmation had two separate steps: did the talk occur, and who influenced whom. Follow-ups of influentials and of influencees were not exactly comparable, though less far apart than might be expected. In the fashion example, many designated influentials were never interviewed, and neighbors and friends were over-represented. The appendix ends with a note on reading a who-to-whom table: direction, homogeneity and range. These matter because the text's flow-of-influence tables rest on these follow-ups, and the authors claim no representativeness.

**Prerequisites:** cross-tabulation (rows, columns, marginal totals, percentages); the idea of a panel survey with an original respondent and a named "designatee".

## L2 — Key ideas
- Follow-up interviews first meant confirming that a reported conversation happened. Pilot data showed a second step: whether influence really flowed, and in which direction (p. 355).
- The follow-up questions were not frozen. Interviewers were told the goals and used ingenuity. Indices of role were developed, such as who used a product first (pp. 356–358).
- Follow-ups with influentials and with influencees should not be expected to be mirror images, because the original respondent knows different things in each case (pp. 360–362).
- An original respondent who reports herself as influential cannot know whether her words were used, so she can over- or underestimate her influence. The text reports only flow tables where the original respondent is the influencee, since these had a higher degree of confirmation and the authors found the influential follow-ups much more useful (p. 362).
- Many designated fashion influentials were not interviewed: 186 of 452 were (Table C1). The authors claim no representativeness (pp. 362–364).
- Word-of-mouth influentials were interviewed more often than "visible example" ones. Neighbors and friends were over-represented and non-household relatives under-represented (pp. 364–366).
- A flow table is read by direction, homogeneity and range. These are interdependent, and the text draws only qualitative inferences from them (pp. 366–368).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 353).** The appendix covers the follow-up aspects of the study. It has four parts: how the follow-up questionnaire evolved (from a field-work overseer's memorandum), why influential follow-ups rather than influencee follow-ups are reported, the "mortality" and "representativeness" of fashion follow-ups, and a note on analysing flow tables (p. 353).

### 1. The Evolution of the Follow-up Questionnaire (pp. 354–359)

**Who was followed up (p. 354).**
- Wave I influentials: persons implicated in a recent decision in marketing (Q12c), fashions (Q20a, b) or public affairs (Q47a).
- Wave I influencees: recent recipients of the respondent's advice in marketing (Q15), fashions (Q28) or public affairs (Q50).
- Wave II influentials: marketing (Sheet B, Q11).
- Wave II influencees: persons to whom the respondent had recently stated her views in public affairs (Q36).
- In each case a specific communication had been named. Fashion influentials include people seen wearing or using the new style (fn. 2).
- "General influentials" in public affairs, and Wave I "specialists" (salespersons, beauty parlor operators, teachers, ministers), are omitted here (fn. 3, pp. 354–355).

**Two steps of confirmation (p. 355).**
- Initially the aim was to confirm that the alleged contact took place.
- Pilot results showed two separate steps. Step One: did the specific communication take place as reported? Step Two: did it exert influence, and what was the relationship and direction of flow between the two? (p. 355)
- The follow-up might also say the two were peers, or that the flow ran the other way (p. 355).
- Direct questions asking a designated person whether she influenced, or was influenced by, a named woman were ruled out. Respondents could hardly be expected to define a phenomenon the researchers were still studying, and tact was needed (p. 355).

**Procedure (pp. 356–357).**
- The questions were deliberately not frozen. Interviewers were told the objectives (p. 356).
- Interviewers first ran the regular panel questionnaire, hoping the follow-up would mention the designator on her own, for example by asking whom she usually talks with about the topic. Introducing the name was awkward and too often aroused suspicion (p. 356 and fn. 4).
- If that failed, the confirmation questionnaire introduced the name casually, with three wordings depending on kinship or earlier mention (p. 356).
- Then three questions (p. 357):
  - Can you recall a recent exchange of views on the topic?
  - Did you suggest the brand or opinion to her (influential follow-ups), or did she suggest it to you (influencee follow-ups)?
  - Who sought advice, or was it volunteered?
- The "Number One point" was to find whether the follow-up is (a) a leader, (b) more or less a follower, or (c) completely equal (a "mutual") to the original respondent. Words like influence, advice, leader and follower were banned for their manipulative connotations (p. 357).

**Problems met (pp. 357–358).**
- Specific conversations were not always recalled. Similar ones were accepted, such as lipstick for nail polish (p. 357).
- Occasionally respondents said their role in that conversation differed from their usual role with the designator. The tendency most difficult to break was for follow-ups to say they just talked about things, with no indication of roles (p. 357).
- The team therefore focused on the product, style or opinion: use, attachment, talk, and which person used it first. Establishing which of them used it first (temporal priority) was seen as important, as a logical means of discerning influence (p. 358).
- For influencee follow-ups the team had little information beyond the general topic, so it began to ask what each uses, who used it first, whether she agrees, and whether she has good ideas. This gave some comparability with the original respondents' questions (p. 358).
- Because this developed during Wave I follow-ups, the specifying information is not uniform across respondents (p. 358).

**Final Wave II questionnaires (pp. 358–359).** The marketing influentials' list has 12 numbered questions; the numbering of the political influencees' list is garbled in the text layer. The final questionnaire was asked of 31 Wave II marketing influentials and 76 Wave II political influencees. It covered:
- (a) general participation in discussion;
- (b) the specific communication: whether it occurred, conditions, what each used or believed, and (marketing only) who used first and who told whom;
- (c) the usual opinion climate: each person's skill, and (politics only) whether such talks were usual (p. 359).

Data on the specific communication exist for all follow-ups. Data on influence (priority of use, skill judgments, whether influencees acted) are less uniform (p. 359).

*Intuition note:* The team did not know in advance how to measure influence. It built measures during the fieldwork and used concrete, everyday facts, such as who used the product first, instead of asking people to label themselves leaders or followers.

### 2. Two Kinds of Follow-up: Influential and Influencee (pp. 359–362)

**Expectation.** One might expect the picture from influencees-then-influentials to mirror the picture from influentials-then-influencees. The authors learned that they had no right to expect a match (pp. 359–360).

**Reasons.**
- *Influentials as follow-ups* (original respondent = influencee). The team knew the change the respondent made (for example coffee A to B) and that she linked it to this person (p. 360).
- *Influencees as follow-ups* (original respondent = influential). The subject matter was vaguer. The original question asked whether the respondent had recently been asked for advice, and the answers to "On what?" were far less specific (p. 360).
- The word "advice" implies a formal, crucial communication ("Mr. Anthony" type) and may hide informal talk. Interviewers were allowed to say "suggestion" (p. 360).
- No record was kept of which wording was used in each interview (p. 361 and fn. 6).
- An influential cannot tell whether her advice was taken. Some follow-ups acted contrary to advice, sometimes deliberately (p. 361).
- Advice-seeking can be a gambit to give advice and be influential (p. 361).
- Advice can be taken without the adviser knowing, as in copying fashions or casual talk (p. 361).
- An influencee-respondent can report advice she took unknown to the influential. An influential-respondent cannot know whether her words were used. So the original respondent as influential can err by overestimating or by underestimating her influentiality (p. 362).

**Decisions (p. 362).**
- The planned tables comparing the two kinds of follow-up are not presented. The authors add that, despite the differences, comparability is not as low as might be expected (p. 362).
- The text reports only flow tables where the original respondent is the influencee, since these had a higher degree of confirmation and the authors judge the follow-up interviews with the influentials so designated much more useful (p. 362).
- Where opinion leaders are defined by self-designation, the self-designating questions are the only option (p. 362).
- Fn. 8: in the text's Tables 6 and 7, influential follow-ups confirmed that the conversation occurred more often than influencee follow-ups did. In marketing and fashions they also acknowledged their role more often. This did not hold in public affairs, where many insisted on mutual exchange. The authors say their argument does not lead to predicting that influential follow-ups should necessarily yield the higher confirmation rate, though perhaps a case could be made for it (fn. 8, p. 362).

*Intuition note:* The same pair of people can be reported from either end. Each end has different blind spots, so the two reports are not simple checks on each other.

### 3. The Follow-up Mortality (pp. 362–363)
The authors repeat that the flow data are not "representative". Some bias is assumed from failures to interview all designatees, and case numbers are small (pp. 362–363).

**Table C1 — fate of 452 designated fashion influentials (p. 363).**

| Fate | N | % |
|---|---|---|
| Interviewed | 186 | 41 |
| Not attempted: respondent did not know person | 71 | 16 |
| Not attempted: incomplete information | 28 | 6 |
| Not attempted: out-of-town residents | 94 | 21 |
| Attempted: moved, died or hospitalized | [text layer: "U"; 7 by subtraction (added step), see Reading notes] | 2 |
| Attempted: temporarily out of town | 13 | 3 |
| Reached but refused | 12 | 3 |

- Of the 186, the text reports only 162, because 24 were men (eleven husbands). Men are few in fashion influence and so were dropped (fn. 9, p. 363).

### 4. Representativeness of the Follow-up Interviews (pp. 363–366)
The check uses the original respondents' testimony about their designatees (p. 363).

**Table C2 — proportion followed up, by type of contact (p. 364).**
- Talked to only: 50% of 188. Visible example only: 33% of 201. Both: 41% of 63.
- 33% of "visible example" designatees were unknown to the respondent (p. 364).
- Word-of-mouth influentials are over-represented. How much this biases results is "indeterminable". If visible examples are of a different sort than the people one talks to, the authors think a definite bias is reasonable to expect, since inter-class communication is quite slight and acting as a visible example is the best way to influence across classes (p. 364).

**Table C3 — by original respondent's traits (p. 365).**
- Under 45: 46%; over 45: 48%.
- High status (A&B): 48%; middle (C): 52%; low (D): 39%. Status is the interviewer's intuitive rating (fn. 12).
- Completed high school: 41%; did not: 51%.
- Conclusion: by and large the differences do not seem very great. Lowest-status and highly educated respondents' leaders were somewhat under-represented. The authors say one can entertain the idea that the follow-ups may not be unrepresentative in age and status (p. 365).
- Table C3 compares original respondents, an indirect check, because data on unreached designatees are incomplete (pp. 364–365).

**Table C4 — by relationship to the influencee (p. 365).**
- Neighbor 50% (26); friend 58% (117); boss/co-worker 39% (36); parent 37% (32); child 35% (40); other blood relatives 41% (56); husband 61% (18); in-laws 30% (27).
- Non-household relatives were harder to reach, since family ties span greater distances (p. 365).
- Neighbors and friends are over-represented, and non-household relatives of all sorts definitely under-represented. The effect on results is "purely conjectural" (p. 366).
- Among original respondents, differences in the proportion of completed follow-ups were only insignificant by marital status, and not important by communication habits, religion or party (p. 366).

### 5. On the Analysis of a Follow-up "Flow" Table (pp. 366–368)
**Definition.** A flow table cross-tabulates a property of an influential against the same property of her influencee (p. 366).

**Table C5 (hypothetical, 100 pairs; rows = influential, columns = influencee; o, m, y = old, middle, young) (p. 366).**

| | o | m | y | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| o | 15 | 7 | 8 | 30 |
| m | 3 | 40 | 7 | 50 |
| y | 2 | 3 | 15 | 20 |
| Total | 20 | 50 | 30 | 100 |

Three aspects:
1. **Direction.** Compare the marginals. Influentials (30/50/20) are somewhat older than influencees (20/50/30), so advice flows slightly downward (p. 366).
2. **Homogeneity.** The main diagonal gives pairs in the same stratum: $15+40+15=70$ of 100 (added step: the sum), so 70% (p. 366). This depends on the number of categories. With five age classes it would usually be smaller. Comparing two properties for homogeneity therefore needs the same number of classes, and in the text properties were always grouped into three when flow tables were compared (p. 367).
3. **Range.** Of the 30 old influentials, half advise younger people. About equal numbers advise the young (8) and the middle-aged (7). The authors note it could well be that one-step differences are relatively more frequent than the more distant flow from old to young (p. 367).

**Interdependence (p. 367).** Any one aspect can be changed without changing one other, but all three must adjust to each other. To reduce range, one can keep direction or homogeneity unchanged, but not both.

- **Table C6** (range decreased, marginals unchanged) (p. 367):

| | o | m | y | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| o | 15 | 12 | 3 | 30 |
| m | 4 | 34 | 12 | 50 |
| y | 1 | 4 | 15 | 20 |
| Total | 20 | 50 | 30 | 100 |

  Diagonal $15+34+15=64$, so homogeneity fell (added check).
- **Table C7** (same range as C6, homogeneity as in C5) (p. 367):

| | o | m | y | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| o | 15 | 12 | 3 | 30 |
| m | 2 | 40 | 8 | 50 |
| y | 1 | 4 | 15 | 20 |
| Total | 18 | 56 | 26 | 100 |

  Diagonal is 70. Its marginals changed, so direction changed (added check). The figures 2 and 8 are not determined by the problem and were kept as similar as possible to C6 (p. 367).

**Caveat (p. 368).** The tables would be affected by the specific indices chosen. The note aims to show qualitative relations, not quantitative results. Only qualitative inferences were drawn from flow tables in the text (p. 368).

*Intuition note:* A flow table answers three different questions: who is older, how often pairs match, and how far apart the partners are. The author says any one aspect can be changed without changing one other, but the three together must adjust to each other: to shrink range, one can keep direction or homogeneity unchanged, but not both.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Forty influential–influencee pairs, classified old (o), middle (m), young (y). Rows = influential, columns = influencee.

| | o | m | y | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| o | 6 | 3 | 1 | 10 |
| m | 2 | 12 | 6 | 20 |
| y | 1 | 3 | 6 | 10 |
| Total | 9 | 18 | 13 | 40 |

- Row sums: 6+3+1=10; 2+12+6=20; 1+3+6=10. Column sums: 6+2+1=9; 3+12+3=18; 1+6+6=13.
- **Direction:** old influentials are 10/40 = 25% against old influencees 9/40 = 22.5%. Young are 10/40 = 25% against 13/40 = 32.5%. Influencees are slightly younger, so advice flows slightly downward.
- **Homogeneity:** diagonal 6+12+6 = 24, so 24/40 = 60%.
- **Range:** of 10 old influentials, 3+1 = 4 advise younger people, and 3 of those 4 advise the middle group (one step).
