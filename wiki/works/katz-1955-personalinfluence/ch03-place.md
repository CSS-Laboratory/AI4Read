# The Place of Impact Analysis in the Study of Influence

## L0 — One line
The authors limit their study to recent, specific, face-to-face decisions and choose the "incident approach", which compares the impact of personal contacts with that of mass media within single decisions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This short opening chapter of Section Two asks how to compare the frequency and effectiveness of personal influence ("opinion leaders") with those of mass media. It first narrows the scope: recent changes and specific choices, not lifelong attitudes, and face-to-face influence, not the whole influence chain. It then separates at least three major approaches to studying influence: group, person and incident. The group approach describes opinion-forming groups. The person approach asks who influences others. The incident approach, which this section uses, takes the personal contact itself as the unit and compares it with mass media. The chapter ends with a roadmap of the following chapters.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an "opinion leader" from Part One (a person who influences others in a specific decision); no math.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The study looks only at specific, recent decisions and changes, such as a recent switch of political opinion, fashion habit or food brand. Long-held habits and tastes are taken as given (p. 163).
- "Opinion leader" means the specific, face-to-face influential. This holds even if that person was himself influenced by a more powerful agent further up the chain (p. 163).
- The authors ask readers not to confuse the "modesty" of their enterprise with blindness to broader problems. They say those problems will "forever be out of reach" if researchers lose patience with very specific investigations like this one (p. 163).
- At least three major approaches are distinguished: group, person and incident (p. 164).
- The group approach describes opinion-forming groups and how they work. Mead's study of three tribes is the example. It is not central here (pp. 164–165).
- The person approach asks what kind of people are likely to influence others on various subjects (p. 165).
- The incident approach takes the personal contact itself as the focus and compares it with other influences, especially mass media. It uses many reports of concrete minor decisions, assessed by standardized interview (pp. 165–166).
- The chapter ends with a map of Chapters IV–VI and a final chapter on when people are more or less effective than the media (p. 166).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Aim of the section (p. 162).** The authors want to contribute to a picture of how people make up their minds. Their interest is the role of personal influence in specific decision-making episodes. This section sets up a scheme to contrast the frequency and effectiveness of influence from "opinion leaders" with influence from mass media (p. 162).

**Restriction 1: short-range changes (p. 163).** The authors say there is no doubt that what parents tell us in early childhood has an everlasting influence on adult beliefs, prejudices, habits and fears (pp. 162–163). The authors take such general attitudes for granted. They study only minor variations on this theme over relatively short periods (p. 163). Their examples:
- Politics: a long-held Republican opinion is not studied, but a recent change is.
- Fashion and food: recent changes of fashion habits or of food brands bought are studied.
- Movies: regular moviegoing or a general preference for Westerns is not treated as data. A recent switch from Westerns to mystery films, or a choice between two Westerns, is (p. 163).

**Restriction 2: face-to-face influence (p. 163).** The previous section's term "opinion leader" for the "specific influential" already implied the restriction to specific or recent choices. The authors now add that their study is concerned with face-to-face influences.
- Example (a supposed case): in a town, a powerful group of liquor dealers hires people to agitate against a dry referendum. If the interviewee tells of a friend who convinced him that prohibition laws never work, this friend is the opinion leader.
- The authors know the friend may himself have been influenced by a much more powerful agent. Their study still concerns face-to-face influences (p. 163).
- They hope more links in the general influence chains will be studied as time goes on, and note that some beginnings of a more general approach have already been indicated. They say the modesty of the enterprise should not be mistaken for blindness to broader problems. Those problems will "forever be out of reach" if we lose patience with very specific investigations like this one (p. 163).

*Intuition note.* The study cuts the long chain of influence at its last link. It looks only at who or what shaped a recent, concrete choice, not at where that person's views came from.

**Three approaches (p. 164).** Even within this frame, at least three major approaches have to be distinguished: group, person and incident (p. 164).

*Group approach (pp. 164–165).*
- Example, Margaret Mead (1937), as reported by the authors. Three tribes make up their minds in three ways (p. 164):
  - Arapesh: everyone may voice an opinion, which grows out of his mood at the moment.
  - Yatmul: they react by specific roles. Sometimes all follow the head of the family. Sometimes the young do the opposite of the elders, following the traditional hostility between generations.
  - Balinese: they do not meet issues with ad hoc opinions. They look for precedents and wise men who say what law and tradition require. There may be disagreement on what is expected in a given situation, but they agree that the requirements of "the order" should be carried out.
- Similar studies could be made in urban society, which is made up of circles and cliques (p. 164):
  - From previous studies and evidence given earlier, the husband is usually "telling the wife", in public affairs at least.
  - Other possible structures: a clique around a local art connoisseur, or a more democratic clique among saloon patrons, where opinions are more likely formed by mutual interaction.
- At the largest scale this approach would be comparative study of governments. At the smallest it would describe types of opinion-forming groups: how many there are, who is in them, how they influence members, how their collective will forms, and how they fit the larger social process (pp. 164–165).
- It is not central in this study, though Part One considered it (p. 165).

*Person approach (p. 165).* It asks who the people are who influence others face to face. It will not describe group types, count their frequencies or assess their effects on members. It will try to show what kind of people are likely to influence others on various subject matters. It is exemplified in the following section (p. 165).

*Incident approach (pp. 165–166).*
- Its focus is neither group nor person but the personal contact itself. The question is how important personal contact is compared with other influences, especially radio and newspapers (p. 165).
- Historical versions of the problem the authors name:
  - The Reformation: the preacher against the pamphlet.
  - Nineteenth-century France: salons against rising newspapers.
  - Modern adult education: radio against discussion groups and study circles (pp. 165–166).
- The authors do not study historical episodes. They collect many reports on concrete situations where people made minor decisions. Then, by a standardized interview, they evaluate the impact of personal contacts compared with that of mass media. This technique is called impact analysis (p. 166).

*Intuition note.* The three approaches answer different questions. The group approach asks what kinds of opinion-forming groups exist. The person approach asks who influences others. The incident approach asks how much a given contact mattered in one decision compared with the media.

**Roadmap (p. 166).**
- Chapter IV: the philosophy of interviewing and the questionnaire. Then how to assess, in each case, the role of a personal contact or a medium. This leads to the impact ratings that summarize all the information.
- Chapter V: the distribution of impact ratings, with a first interpretation. The authors expect that readers will then have many doubts or, at least, many questions about the numerical results.
- Chapter VI: returns to general problems of impact studies and clarifies points left unsettled in Chapter IV.
- Final chapter of the section: examines the conditions under which "people" are more or less effective for each other than the mass media are (p. 166).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
The unit's main idea is that the incident, one concrete decision, is the unit of analysis. Suppose we collect 4 reports of recent minor decisions:

| Incident | Decision | Influences named |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Switch from Westerns to mysteries | a friend |
| 2 | Choice between two Westerns | a newspaper ad |
| 3 | Change of food brand | a neighbour and a radio ad |
| 4 | Change of hat style | a neighbour |

Counting incidents that name a personal contact gives 3 of 4 (incidents 1, 3, 4). Counting incidents that name a medium gives 2 of 4 (incidents 2, 3). This is only a rough count of frequency. The authors' impact ratings, introduced in Chapter IV, are meant to judge how much each influence mattered in each incident. This unit does not define them.

## Questions this raises
- How can one judge the "impact" of a contact or medium from an interview? The authors defer this to Chapters IV and VI.
- Does restricting the study to the last link hide the influence of powerful agents behind the opinion leader?
- Are the decisions that respondents report representative of everyday decisions?
- Which conditions make people more or less effective than the media? This is the topic of the final chapter of the section.
