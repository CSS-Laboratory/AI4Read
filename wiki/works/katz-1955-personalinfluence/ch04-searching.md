# Searching for and Assessing the Impact of Various Influences

## L0 — One line
To compare personal influence with mass media, the authors ask women to recall a recent decision, name every influence, pick the main one, and then compute how often an exposure was judged decisive.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how to measure the relative roles of personal contacts and mass media in everyday decisions: food or product brand, movie choice, and fashion. The answer is a three-step interview. Specific influence questions aim to elicit every source that played a role. A "most important" assessment question follows. A check list then records mere exposure. Each mention gets an impact rating. Ratings are pooled by influence type, and an effectiveness index is computed: among those exposed, the share who called the source most important. This matters because being exposed to a medium is not the same as being influenced by it, and the method makes that gap measurable.

**Prerequisites:** proportions and conditional proportions; the idea of a survey interview; the difference between informing and persuading.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The study compares influences on three kinds of everyday decisions, using a representative sample of women who recently made such a decision (p. 167).
- A general "why" question is usually a good opener if there is much time, but its replies are usually not very valuable for the final analysis. Directed follow-up questions steer attention to sources (pp. 167–168).
- Question wording should not favour one channel. Asking several questions is often advisable, partly because respondents often take questions literally (pp. 168–169).
- The aims are statistical and relative, not absolute. Each act is still assessed individually (p. 169).
- The three cases of Mrs. A, Mrs. C and Mrs. D separate decisive, merely informational and contributory roles (pp. 170–171).
- Respondents themselves name the "most important" influence. The authors admit this is simple, partly chosen for expediency, and could be done more carefully (pp. 171–172).
- Impact ratings (weights given to each mentioned influence) separate contributory influences (some role, none decisive) from effective ones (judged decisive). A check list identifies "exposed" respondents: those who say yes to a check-list item although they had not mentioned that medium as influential (pp. 172–173).
- The effectiveness index separates mere exposure from effectiveness given exposure (p. 174).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: the problem and the sample (p. 167).** The authors examine the roles of differing influences on three kinds of everyday decisions. The sample is representative and consists of women. They changed household product or food brand, chose a movie, or changed hairdo, clothes or cosmetics. Interviews were built to give influences "a maximum chance" of being mentioned and compared.

**The Art of Asking Why (pp. 167–169).**
- Four question types are distinguished: general, specific influence, exposure and assessment (pp. 167, 169).
- *General question* ("Why did you make this change?"): the respondent picks her own focus. If much time is available it is usually a good start, but the replies are usually not very valuable for the final analysis (p. 167).
- The problem is that she may not realise the interest is in influences. She may praise a coffee brand at length and never say she learned of it from a magazine column. She may report the experience as it is structured for her. The interviewer should listen first, then use a follow-up question, for example asking how she knew about the advantages of the brand before she ever used it (p. 168).
- The study had many topics to cover, so it used directed questions from the start. Examples ask how she chose the picture, how she found out about the new brand, and "Who or what suggested this change of fashion to you?" They stress the process of decision and invite her to name a source (p. 168).
- Wording should not favour a channel. Asking how she learned might favour formal media over personal influence; asking how she heard would obviously favour radio over print (p. 168).
- Asking several questions is often advisable. It makes the respondent review the episodes. Respondents also often take questions quite literally: an advertisement may have "helped" them, yet they would not say they took "advice" from it (pp. 168–169).
- These are *specific influence questions*. They aim to bring out, through retrospection, every case where a personal contact, advertisement or story played "however minor a role" (p. 169).

*Intuition note:* People often answer the question they hear, not the one you meant. To bring out all the influences, it is often advisable to ask in several ways and to avoid words that favour one channel.

**The Assessment of Impact (pp. 169–172).**
- Two features of the results are announced. They are statistical: they pertain to groups of decisions, though each act must be assessed individually. They are also relative: the aim is comparison of personal contact and media, with no absolute findings. The authors say this restriction is not inherently necessary (Chapter 5) but makes the discussion easier (p. 169 and n. 2).
- **Mrs. A** hears Mrs. B vividly describe a picture at a tea and leaves early to see it. If she is honest and the interviewer skilful, Mrs. B's personal influence was decisive. The authors add that "further clarification" of what such statements mean is needed (pp. 169–170).
- **Mrs. C** has spare hours downtown and finds four movies in a newspaper. She picks the one with an actor she had long wanted to see. No person is mentioned. The newspaper only informed her that the picture was nearby. So interviewer and respondent would probably agree that the newspaper had no impact, especially if further questioning shows that several other means were at hand to find out what was playing. It served only an informational role, not an influential one, in a context where other sources were equally accessible (p. 170).
- **Mrs. D** is on a lonely ranch with no newspapers. A transient brings a local weekly. From it she learns that a school friend is in a one-night show and drives 30 miles. It can be argued that the paper was at least a "contributory" influence (pp. 170–171).
- For the study the authors chose the simplest procedure. They asked respondents to name the main influence: "Summing up now, what do you think was the most important thing in causing you to pick this particular picture?" The same form was used for brand and fashion. The question came at the end of a somewhat more detailed discussion of the decision, so answers "could not be altogether perfunctory" (p. 171).
- Reasons given for this choice (pp. 171–172):
  1. In earlier studies, media and personal advice were fairly easily assessed. They usually come up in distinct episodes and do not involve very complicated psychological processes. The authors would not use this method for something like professional jealousy in choosing a dissertation topic.
  2. Only relative impact is wanted, so the authors hoped it would be fairly easy for respondents to assess at least the relative role of each medium and of personal advice in such minor decisions. Whether this assumption is true is of rather general interest and should be the object of special, perhaps experimental, studies.
  3. Expediency: the field force was trained locally and the task could not be too hard or costly.
- The authors say there is no doubt that, theoretically, it could have been done in a much more careful way. The investigator could also have made the assessment. Cases like bad company in a delinquent's history, or job loss and marital relations, call for much more detailed analysis (p. 172).

*Intuition note:* "Was this source involved?" and "Did it change the outcome?" are different questions. Mrs. C's paper was involved, but it probably had no impact, especially if other sources of the listing were at hand. The study settles for a simple judgment by the respondent, and the authors acknowledge that this could have been done more carefully.

**The Impact Ratings (pp. 172–174).**
- Among respondents who report being affected by media or personal influence, two groups arise. One names influences but gives none decisive weight. The other assesses one influence as the decisive factor. This yields a **contributory** versus an **effective** influence. These weights are called *impact ratings* (p. 172).
- Footnote 5: the earlier term "discerning" is replaced by "assessment", for a clearer link to MacIver's *Social Causation* (1942) (p. 172, n. 5).
- *Exposure questions* come last. No medium was named during the influence and assessment parts. Afterwards respondents get a check list, for example "Did you hear someone talk about it?", whether she read about it in a newspaper, or whether she passed the theatre and saw that it was on (p. 173).
- **Exposed** women are those who say yes to a check-list item although they had not earlier mentioned that medium as influential, "let alone decisive" (p. 173).
- Each influence mentioned gets an impact rating. For one factor, such as newspapers, one can take the distribution of ratings over the whole group. One can report the share calling it "most important", or how many were merely exposed without giving it saliency (pp. 173–174).
- **Assessment** concerns individual cases. **Evaluation** is the study of the impact ratings for one kind of influence across a group. Both concern one influence type at a time (p. 174).
- **Effectiveness index** (in words): first single out people exposed to a source. Then compute the proportion among them who considered it most important (p. 174).

$$\text{effectiveness index} = \frac{\text{number exposed who rated the source most important}}{\text{number exposed to the source}}$$

(added step: the formula only restates the author's verbal definition.)

- This separates mere exposure to a medium from its effectiveness in case of exposure (p. 174).
- The authors will use "factor", "influence", "exposure" and "source" loosely. Where precision matters, the indices will make the meaning clear (p. 174).

*Intuition note:* Reaching people is not the same as deciding anything for them. The index asks, of those a source reached, what share rated it most important.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Twenty women chose a movie. Assumption: "exposed" to a source means everyone who mentioned it at all or answered yes on the check list.

| Source | Rated most important | Contributory only | Check list only | Exposed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Personal contact | 8 | 4 | 2 | 8+4+2 = 14 |
| Newspaper | 3 | 3 | 9 | 3+3+9 = 15 |

- Personal contact: index = 8/14 ≈ 0.57.
- Newspaper: index = 3/15 = 0.20.
- The newspaper reached about as many women (15 vs 14) but was decisive far less often. This is the gap between exposure and effectiveness.

## Concepts
- [[specific-influence-question]] (new) — directed question about how one chose or learned; aims to elicit every source, however minor (p. 169)
- [[assessment-question]] (new) — "summing up" question asking the respondent to name the most important influence (p. 171)
- [[exposure-question]] (new) — check-list question naming a medium; identifies mere exposure (p. 173)
- [[impact-rating]] (new) — weight assigned to an influence each time it is mentioned (contributory or effective); the distribution of ratings can also report how many were merely exposed (pp. 172–174)
- [[contributory-vs-effective-influence]] (new) — contributory played some role; effective was judged the decisive one (p. 172)
- [[assessment-vs-evaluation]] (new) — assessment is per case; evaluation pools ratings for one influence across a group (p. 174)
- [[effectiveness-index]] (new) — share of those exposed to a source who rated it most important (p. 174)
- [[informational-vs-influential-role]] (new) — a source that only told her the picture was playing nearby did not create the desire (p. 170)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] effectiveness index ↔ marketing: conversion rate among those reached — a conditional proportion, not reach alone.
- [structural-analogy] Mrs. C's newspaper, with other sources equally accessible ↔ causal inference: counterfactual reasoning about whether the outcome would have occurred anyway.
- [structural-analogy] respondent self-assessment of the "main" influence ↔ survey methodology: validity of retrospective self-report attribution.

## Questions this raises
- Can respondents judge the "most important" influence accurately for minor decisions? The authors say this needs special, perhaps experimental, study (p. 172).
- How should "contributory" influences be weighed beyond the simple main-influence rule? (pp. 171, 172)
- What does an impact rating mean precisely? The authors defer this to the actual data (p. 174).
- What changes if the study is not restricted to relative comparisons (n. 2, p. 169)?
