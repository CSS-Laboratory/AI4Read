# The Part Played by People

## L0 — One line
Mass-communications research, like research in the factory, army and city, began with a model of isolated individuals and then found that close personal ties (primary-group relations) were an important intervening variable (a factor between stimulus and response); the authors suggest that work on the opinion leader (a person with unusually great influence on others' votes) led mass-communications research to this discovery.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
If mass media seemed to have only small effects on 1940 vote decisions, what does influence votes? Voters who changed their minds named other people. This led to "opinion leaders" (people with great influence on others' votes) and a "two-step flow" from media to leaders to less active people. The authors then suggest that opinion leadership is part of ordinary personal relations, not a trait of a special group. They review similar "rediscoveries" of the primary group (close personal ties) in the Hawthorne studies, *The American Soldier* and the Yankee City series. Each began with a too-simple model, hit unexplained results, got clues from the subjects, and then saw that interpersonal ties mattered.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an intervening variable (a factor between cause and outcome); survey versus observation; a basic sense of what a "primary group" is (family, friends, co-workers).

## L2 — Key ideas
- In the 1940 campaign study, radio and print seemed to have only negligible effects on vote decisions. The authors asked what does determine votes (p. 31).
- Changers named personal influence, which seemed far ahead of other sources. This suggested opinion leaders, who seemed to be spread across all occupational and social levels (p. 32).
- Leaders reported more media influence than non-leaders, which suggested a "two-step flow" from media to leaders to the less active (p. 32).
- The authors' second idea, put as a suggestion: opinion leadership is not a fixed trait but part of everyday relations, and a leader can best be thought of as a member playing a key communications role (p. 33).
- Three other fields (factory, army, city) show the same pattern of "rediscovery" of the primary group (pp. 34–39).
- The pattern has four elements: a too-simple model, unexplained residue, clues from the subjects, and then recognition of relevance (pp. 39–42).
- The authors speculate that the model was overlooked because "mass" imagery pictured individuals under remote institutional control (p. 40).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 31)
The chapter opens by saying that, in the 1940 study, it became clear that certain people in every stratum of a community serve relay roles in the mass communication of election information and influence (p. 31). In the 1940 campaign study, radio and print seemed to have only negligible effects on vote decisions. They had especially minute effects on *changes* in vote decisions (p. 31). The authors did not stop at this negative finding. They asked: if the mass media are not major determinants of a vote, what is? (p. 31)

Footnote 2 contrasts two approaches (p. 31). Communications research starts with a communication and tracks its influence. A decision-making study starts with a decision and looks for all influences on it. *The People's Choice* "seems" to be the first academically legitimated report combining the two traditions (p. 31).

### The Opinion Leader Idea and the Two-Step Flow (pp. 32–34)
- The authors looked at people who changed their vote intention. Asked what contributed, they answered: other people (p. 32).
- Personal influence seemed far ahead of other sources. People tend to vote, "it seems", as their associates do: wives with husbands, club members with clubs, workers with fellow workers (p. 32).
- The data implied (though not fully adequately for this purpose) that some people had a disproportionate influence on others' vote intentions. These "opinion leaders" were "not at all identical" with those traditionally thought of as wielders of influence. They seemed to be distributed in all occupational groups and at every social and economic level (p. 32).
- Next question: who influences the influentials? Leaders reported more than non-leaders that the mass media influenced them. This gave the suggestion of a two-step flow: ideas often seem to flow from radio and print to opinion leaders, and from them to the less active sections of the population (p. 32).
- The present study tests and extends these ideas. The studies reviewed "tend to bear out" the opinion leader idea. They also "make quite explicit" that the traditional image of mass persuasion must make room for "people" as intervening factors between media stimuli and opinions, decisions and actions (pp. 32–33).

**Rediscovery in two steps (p. 33).** The authors say, with some hedging ("perhaps"), that mass communications research has joined fields "rediscovering" the primary group. (Footnote 3: researchers belatedly recognized informal relations in settings once seen as formal and atomistic. It was mainly the "latent functions" of primary groups that were rediscovered.) The two steps:
1. Opinion leadership was discovered.
2. The wide distribution of leaders, and their relations with family, friends and co-workers, led to a second idea. Opinion leaders are not a group set apart. Opinion leadership is not a trait some people have and others lack. It is part of the give-and-take of everyday personal relations (p. 33).

The authors put it as a suggestion: all interpersonal relations are potential networks of communication, and an opinion leader can best be thought of as a group member playing a key communications role. Tying leaders to the specific others they reach completes the "rediscovery" (p. 33). Footnote 4 notes that Freidson (1953A), in a thoroughly independent appraisal of the concept of "mass", discusses the abandonment of the image of a "totally atomized audience" along some of the same lines (p. 33).

If their model is "anywhere near the truth", this has parallels in social science over three decades. Communications research is "predicated" on the non-existence or irrelevance of interpersonal relations, so it is not strange that it came late (p. 34).

*Intuition note:* The authors suggest, with hedges, that ideas often seem to flow from the media to opinion leaders and from them to others. They also suggest that a "leader" can best be thought of as a role within a group, not as a special kind of person.

### The Rediscovery of the Primary Group: Case Histories of an Intervening Variable (pp. 34–35)
The digression covers three cases: industrial sociology, *The American Soldier*, and Warner's Yankee City series (p. 34). The authors think that an examination of the cases will show researchers were forced by empirical findings to amend their images of their fields (p. 34). They see a pattern across four cases, including their own: research began with a too-simple model, the model did not quite explain events, clues pointing to the primary group turned up, and then relevance was "rediscovered" (p. 35).

### (A) The Hawthorne studies (pp. 35–36)
The account relies on Homans's review of Roethlisberger and Dickson (footnote 5, p. 35).
- The study began by testing the effect of working conditions on productivity (illumination, wage plans, rest periods, hours) (p. 35).
- However conditions were varied, "better" or "worse", the selected test group's productivity increased. The response was not related to the experimental variables (p. 35).
- The girls said "it was fun". They seemed to mean they were a cheerful, intimate group responding to the attention from management and researchers (pp. 35–36).
- Investigators then watched workers in a more natural setting and found an informal group. It influenced production, but here a group norm *depressed* production. Workers held each other to their own idea of an average day's output, within management's broadly acceptable limits. This resisted individualistic piece-rate incentives (p. 36).
- Point: only the discovery that the "model" was wrong could have revealed that primary relationships were relevant to productivity (p. 36).

### (B) *The American Soldier* (pp. 36–37)
- Combat motivation was associated with attachment to an informal group. Protecting friends or conforming to group expectations was often given as the most important reason for entering battle (p. 37).
- These motives were consistently reported as more important than hatred of the enemy, political and ideological goals, or the coercion of discipline and formal orders. Finding this seems to have been "something of a surprise" (p. 37).
- Method: the data came from questionnaires to an atomized cross-section of individual soldiers. This is like the data in communications studies, and unlike the largely observational Hawthorne data. In both, the primary group "had to be imputed" (p. 37).
- Footnote 8 notes the shared theoretical problem: how the group functions relative to the formal goals of an organization (pp. 37–38).

### (C) The Yankee City series (pp. 38–39)
- Warner says the discovery of the clique "came rather late" in the field investigation. He says cliques were eventually found "next in importance to the family in placing groups socially" (p. 38).
- The clue came from the people studied. They used phrases such as "our crowd" and "the X crowd" and evaluated people by their cliques. These turned out to be highly important in assigning social position (p. 38).
- Early research assumed income, neighborhood and family mattered. It did not occur to the researchers that cliques might confer status, or that "mobility" might mean moving from one clique to another (p. 39).

### The Common Elements in the Pattern (pp. 39–42)
1. **A model of expected relevance (pp. 39–40).** One can reconstruct the researchers' assumptions about what mattered. Their image had no place for the primary group. The authors speculate on why. In "mass production", "mass communication" and "mass society", the mass is the newly independent individual who is also subject to remote institutional controls. The individual researchers seem to have had in mind: a worker attuned to individual economic incentive, an anonymous urban dweller, a radio listener alone in his room. These images are "not altogether wrong" but too wrong to ground research (p. 40). Footnote 11 says the soldier was conceived as a social atom responding to formal, impersonal controls (p. 40).
2. **A halt (pp. 40–41).** The model left too much unexplained. In the factory, physical and economic variables were clearly not the whole story. In the army, discipline and war ideals could not fully account for combat motivation. In the city, mobility seemed to be more than mere economic advancement. In mass communications, direct exposure could not fully account for the observed differences in changing vote intentions. Researchers became receptive to other factors (pp. 40–41).
3. **The subjects gave the clue (p. 41).** Researchers asked the subjects to explain. The test-room girls spoke of esprit de corps. City people kept linking status and cliques. Soldiers cited group support, and rarely hatred, patriotism or fear of discipline. Vote changers cited personal influence much more than media (p. 41).
4. **Rediscovery (pp. 41–42).** What was discovered was not that primary groups exist, but that they are *relevant*: to mass production, combat morale, class status and mobility, and communications behavior (pp. 41–42).

*Intuition note:* The shared lesson concerns method. A model that treats people as isolated units can fit a lot of data and still miss the relationships that explain the leftover variation.

**Footnote 12 (p. 42).** The authors deliberately leave out two traditions that used small groups long ago: diffusion of new farm practices in rural sociology, and applied anthropology. They say these bear directly on their concerns and deserve a separate study (p. 42).

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text; invented numbers.* A town has 60 voters: 10 are leaders and 50 are others. Suppose 9 of the 10 leaders follow the campaign in the media, and 10 of the 50 others do. Each leader talks politics with 5 distinct others, so 10 × 5 = 50 others are in some leader's circle.

- A direct-exposure model counts 9 + 10 = 19 of 60 as reached by the media.
- The two-step view adds the others reached through exposed leaders: 9 × 5 = 45 of the 50 others (up to 45, because some may also be directly exposed).
- The direct count leaves 41 people with no media link. This residue is the kind that "left too much unexplained" (p. 40). The relays show a path to most of them.

## Concepts
- [[two-step-flow]] — ideas often seem to flow from media to opinion leaders, then to less active people (p. 32)
- [[opinion-leader]] — person with disproportionate influence on others' vote intentions, seemingly spread across all levels. Later suggested to be a role in relations, not a trait (pp. 32–33)
- [[limited-effects-paradigm]] — here only the finding that media seemed to have negligible effects on vote decisions (p. 31)
- [[primary-group-rediscovery]] (new) — researchers' belated recognition that informal interpersonal ties matter in settings first pictured as atomistic (pp. 33–34)
- [[intervening-variable]] (new) — a factor between stimulus and response; here, interpersonal relations (pp. 33, 39)
- [[atomized-audience-image]] (new) — the picture of the mass as isolated individuals under remote control, which hid interpersonal ties (pp. 40, 33)
- [[decision-making-study]] (new) — a study that begins with a decision and traces all its influences (p. 31)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Two-step flow ↔ network science: information reaching many nodes through a few relay nodes — the text gives only a verbal model.
- [structural-analogy] Omitted-variable residue prompting a new model ↔ statistics: unexplained variance after a mis-specified model (the text says only "too much unexplained").
- [metaphor] "Rediscovery of the primary group" ↔ sociology of science: a field relearning what earlier work had known.

## Questions this raises
- If opinion leadership is a role in relationships, how would one measure it without treating leaders as a trait group?
- Is the four-step pattern shown beyond four hand-picked cases? The authors reconstruct it after the fact.
- How do the two omitted traditions (rural diffusion, applied anthropology) relate to the opinion leader idea?
- Does the ideal of the "atomized individual" survive in any setting, given that the authors call it "not altogether wrong"?
