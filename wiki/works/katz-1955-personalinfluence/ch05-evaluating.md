# Evaluating the Impact of Various Influences

## L0 — One line
In marketing and movie choice, personal contacts had more impact than mass media (fashion is the one exception), and the authors offer as the probably most important reason that people can influence both through what they say and through who they are to us.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter compares personal contacts with mass media (and salespersons) as influences on three kinds of decision: switching brands, choosing a movie, and making a fashion change. It classifies each reported exposure as effective, contributory or ineffective, and builds an index of effectiveness that holds coverage constant. Personal contacts come out strongest in marketing and movies. In fashion they are most frequent, but no source is highly effective. The authors then distinguish four ways of inducing action. Formal media mainly work by "indirect attraction" (by what they tell), while persons can also work by "control" (for the sake of the source). This is offered as a probable reason for the finding.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and ratios; the idea that survey respondents report their own influences (the earlier chapters' interview method).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Each reported exposure is coded into three nested levels: effective, contributory, ineffective. The levels depend on what the respondent says about the source's role (p. 177).
- The index of effectiveness is effective exposure ÷ total exposure. It holds coverage constant, since a medium may look strong merely because it reaches many people (p. 178).
- In marketing, personal contacts have both the largest total exposure and the largest share of effective exposure. Radio comes next in effectiveness (pp. 177–178).
- For movies, newspapers are consulted almost universally but weighted little. Personal contact is again more effective (p. 180).
- In fashion, private non-commercial influences beat salespersons and magazines on both measures. All sources have low effectiveness, perhaps because of trial and error and ego-involvement (p. 182).
- A rough H/L classification (exposure ≥ ⅓, index ≥ .20) summarizes all results. Personal contact is HH in two areas, and fashion is the only exception (pp. 183–184).
- The authors distinguish four types of direction-giving: force, immediate attraction, indirect attraction, control. "Influence" so far means types 3 and 4 (pp. 184–185).
- Persons have two avenues of influence and formal media mainly one. This is the "probably most important reason" for the finding, with admitted exceptions (pp. 185–186).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction (p. 175).** Personal contacts are the main interest. Mass media are included as comparison. For small consumer goods the comparison media are radio and newspaper advertising; magazines and salespersons are also considered. For movies they are magazines and newspapers. For fashion, outside personal contacts only magazines play any role among the formal media. Personal contacts become much more diversified there, so they are split into three types: salespersons, verbal advice from friends, and seeing another person's hairdo or dress.

**Marketing: foods and household goods (pp. 175–179).**
- Base: 386 people who reported a recent brand or product shift. Types of shift: small food items 49%, soaps and cleansing agents 37%, household goods 11%, miscellaneous 8% (p. 176, note).
- The shift was elicited by two questions. The first asked about a newly bought brand "during the last month or so", excluding things bought because nothing else was available. If the answer was no, a prompt about specific products followed (pp. 176–177, note).
- Each bar of Chart I has the same base (386 = 100%). Bar totals add to more than 100% because respondents named several sources (p. 176, note).
- **Effective exposure:** the respondent names the source, gives it a specific role (e.g. it taught or directed them), and calls it the most important factor. **Contributory exposure:** specific role, but not the most important. **Ineffective exposure:** the source is merely mentioned, with no role acknowledged. The remainder up to 100% is no exposure at all (p. 177).
- Personal contacts, example reading: total 37.6% = 14.5% effective + 13.4% contributory + 9.7% ineffective. Radio total is 30.3%, of which 7.5% is effective (p. 176, note).
- Results: personal contacts have the greatest concentration of effective exposure and the largest total exposure (p. 177). Radio is next in importance. Its total exposure does not exceed newspapers', but it is more often reported as effective or contributory (pp. 177–178). Magazines follow newspapers in total exposure but are no more effective (p. 178). Salespersons have less total exposure than the mass media but effectiveness almost as high as radio's (p. 178).
- **Index of effectiveness** = effective exposure ÷ total exposure, printed under each bar (p. 178). (added step: for personal contacts, 14.5/37.6 ≈ .39; for radio, 7.5/30.3 ≈ .25.)
- Conclusion: informal personal advice has more impact than advertising in two respects. Exposure to it is reported more often, and among the exposed, "most important" is more often attributed to people (p. 178). An earlier voting study had given only the impression, without quantitative measurement, that other people influence us more than formal propaganda. Here a limited first step toward such measurement is taken, and personal influence emerges with the strength expected (pp. 178–179).

*Intuition note:* A source may look important only because it reaches many people. Dividing effective cases by all exposed cases asks instead how often a source mattered when it was met at all.

**Selection of a motion picture (p. 180).** The base is 584 movie-goers (Chart II, p. 179). Newspapers are consulted by almost everyone, but no special weight is given to what they say. Personal contact has considerably greater effectiveness than the other media. Total exposure to other people's opinions is about as high as in Chart I. The authors say the chart "undoubtedly" underrates the frequency of discussion before a movie. The magazine result needs qualification in later chapters (p. 180). A footnote cites Handel (1950). Distributors of bad movies aim for rapid saturation, which minimizes word-of-mouth and maximizes advertising. For good movies, distribution is slow, which capitalizes on word-of-mouth (p. 180, n. 3).

**Fashion changes (pp. 180–182).** The base is 502 changes (Chart III). The changes were hairdo 82%, make-up or cosmetic 38%, clothing 29% (p. 181, note; the percentages add to more than 100).
- Salespersons appear more often here for two reasons. The beauty operator is a professional disseminator of grooming opinions. The perfume-counter or dress-shop clerk seems to play a greater role than the grocery clerk (pp. 181–182).
- Non-commercial influence is of two kinds, saying and doing (verbal and visual personal), with surprisingly similar roles (p. 182).
- By all indices, private non-commercial sources have more impact than commercial ones. Salespersons are intermediate between private influences and magazines on the index (p. 182).
- The low effectiveness of every relevant medium seems to be peculiar to the fashion field. The authors say it is "quite possible" that this reflects a trial-and-error situation, and that fashion changes probably involve more ego-involvement for women. Deciding might then be harder, with more information sought but more often neglected than acted on, which should lead one to expect longer decision times. They have no data on decision time (p. 182).

**Summary and interpretation (pp. 182–186).**
- *Classification.* Because the figures show only general trends, exposure is called high (H) if it is one-third or more of all changers in an area. The index is H if it is .20 or larger (p. 183).

| | High exposure | Low exposure |
|---|---|---|
| **High effectiveness** | Marketing: personal contact; Movies: personal contact | Marketing: radio |
| **Low effectiveness** | Fashion: all four factors; Movies: newspapers | Marketing: newspapers, magazines, salespersons; Movies: magazines (to be qualified later) |

(p. 183)

- Personal contact is above average on both counts in marketing and movies. In fashion it is high in exposure and the most frequent source, but its index is "not pronounced". This is the only exception to the conclusion that personal contact has more impact than the other sources studied (pp. 183–184).
- *Four ways of inducing action* (pp. 184–185):
  1. **Force** (football kicked; child put to bed). It does not appear in these areas.
  2. **Immediate attraction** (moth and candle; the hungry person going to the icebox). It applies to the study's material at one point, where a woman sees another's hairdo and copies it. Otherwise it plays no major role.
  3. **Indirect or represented attraction.** A communication makes an object desirable, by word, print or picture. It covers many of the cases studied. The conditioned dog is a borderline case between types 2 and 3.
  4. **Control.** A command is carried out largely irrespective of its content, and the source is what matters. Examples are hypnosis, obedience under terror, compliance with a beloved person, confidence in an adviser, and a child's obedience to parents. In type 3 we act because of what a source communicates, in type 4 for the source itself.
- "Influence" so far has meant types 3 and 4. Examples are a magazine describing a dress appealingly (3) and a boss's advice to see a film, followed because we would be embarrassed otherwise (4) (p. 185).
- *Interpretation (commonsense basis).* Formal media influence mainly by representation, by what they tell. People can influence both ways, so their influence goes beyond the content of what they say (p. 185). This is "probably the most important reason" for the greater impact of personal contact (p. 185).
- *Exceptions.* Someone might trust a favourite magazine, or follow a television announcer they have a crush on. Opinion leaders may accept media influence more often than non-leaders (the authors say they will suggest this much later). So media influence may occasionally be type 4. "By and large", persons have two avenues of influence and formal media one (p. 186).

*Intuition note:* A friend's advice can work because it is the friend, not because of the argument. A broadcast ad has only its message to offer. The authors treat this as a likely explanation, not as something their data prove.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
100 people changed a brand. Source A: 20 report exposure, 8 effective. Source B: 80 report exposure, 15 effective.
- Index A = 8/20 = .40. Index B = 15/80 = .1875.
- B has more effective cases in absolute terms (15 > 8), but A is effective more often when met.
- Exposure: A = 20% (< ⅓, so L), B = 80% (H). Index: A = .40 (≥ .20, so H), B = .1875 (< .20, so L).
- Classification: A = low exposure/high effectiveness, B = high exposure/low effectiveness. Coverage and effectiveness are thus separate dimensions.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Exposure vs. effectiveness index ↔ communication-theory: reach versus influence per contact — separates coverage from impact.
- [metaphor] Control (acting for the source's sake) ↔ communication-theory: trust in the messenger rather than the message.

## Questions this raises
- How reliable is a respondent's own claim that a source was "most important"? Does coding by self-report favour persons over advertisements?
- If opinion leaders use media more than non-leaders, how does media content reach followers?
- Do fashion decisions take longer than food or movie decisions, as the authors guess?
- Do the .20 and one-third cut-offs, which the authors call arbitrary, change the classification if moved?
