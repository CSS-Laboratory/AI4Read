# A Summary of Influences and Influentials

## L0 — One line
Opinion leaders (people others turn to for everyday advice) are concentrated mainly by life-cycle position (such as unmarried "girls" or large-family wives) and gregariousness (extent of friendships and social contacts), less by social status except in public affairs, and it seems each topic area has largely its own leaders.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter sums up two questions from the study: where opinion leaders are concentrated, and how influence flows between leaders and followers. A new question is added: do the same women lead in several areas? The authors find that life-cycle and gregariousness matter more than status, except in public affairs. Interest alone does not make a leader, and influence seems to pass mostly between people who are alike. Overlap of leadership across marketing, fashion and public affairs is about what chance predicts, apart from a tiny three-area group. This matters because the authors say the picture of influence as only flowing down from high-status people must be revised to include horizontal, peer-based influence.

**Prerequisites:** an opinion leader is someone others turn to for advice in an area; gregariousness = extent of friendships and social contacts; life-cycle type = e.g. "girls", "matrons"; proportions and percentages; probability of two independent events (multiply); idea of "controlling" a variable by comparing equal groups.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Rather than describing one "typical" leader, the study tries to locate concentrations of leaders. Claims take the form: among girls there is a greater concentration of leaders than among other life-cycle types (p. 322–323).
- An "index of importance" (Table 59; a 0-to-1 score read comparatively) shows life-cycle as the strongest factor in marketing, fashion and movie-going. Gregariousness is strongest in public affairs, and status is the weakest factor overall (p. 324).
- The authors conclude that leadership is also *horizontal*, found at every status level. Outside public affairs, higher status does not automatically bring more leadership; informal influence there probably comes from everyday contact with peers, not from wealth or high position (p. 325).
- Interest does not explain away the objective factors. It appears that interest produces leadership mainly when one's associates are also interested, so shared interest appears to act as a channel (p. 326–327).
- Flow data are only suggestive. Influence tends to stay within status levels (same-status pairs: almost 7 in 10 in marketing, 6 in 10 in fashion, 46% in public affairs), while age and status directions differ by arena (p. 328–331).
- Each arena has a distinct leader profile; only public affairs shows a more marked downward status flow (fashion: slight) (p. 330–332).
- Multiple-area leadership looks infrequent: 41% lead in at least one area, and almost two-thirds of these lead in only one. Pair overlaps show no significant difference from chance, so the general-leader hypothesis gets little support (p. 333–334).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 321–322).** Apart from the previous chapter on media, the book studied person-to-person influence. Two questions recur.
1. *Social location:* which groups hold disproportionate numbers of leaders, by life-cycle, status and gregariousness? The authors also asked whether subjective interest is enough to determine leadership (p. 321).
2. *Flow:* how are advice-seekers related to leaders? Two ways of looking at flow were proposed, each with stated inadequacies: an "export" index (fashion) and a tentative analysis of influential–influencee pairs by age, status and family membership. The study was also extended in public affairs to a three-link chain of influence. A new question is added: does a leader in one arena also lead in another (p. 322)?

**The social location of opinion leader concentrations (p. 322–325).**
- The claim is about concentration. The careful statement is that, among "girls" (unmarried women under an age the text layer gives as 85), there is a greater concentration of leaders than in any other life-cycle type. The authors note that "girls are fashion leaders" could mean that most girls are leaders, that most fashion leaders are girls, or that girls lead for all other women. Each may be empirically true, but given their data the careful statement is the second, put as a concentration claim (p. 322–323).
- Different arenas call different mixes of the three factors into play. Fashion leadership relates strongly to life-cycle and gregariousness and less to status. Public affairs relates mainly to status and gregariousness, much less to life-cycle (p. 323).
- Measure: the "index of importance", devised by F. Mosteller (maths in Appendix D). A value of 0 means the factor is irrelevant. A value of 1 means it fully accounts for leadership. Values should be read comparatively, not absolutely (p. 323–324).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Life-cycle | .203 | .267 | .089 | .326 |
| Gregariousness | .176 | .126 | .184 | .080 |
| Social status | .055 | .113 | .161 | .040 |

*Table 59 (p. 324).*

- Life-cycle is the most important factor in three areas. For movie-going the authors call it the only relevant factor (Table 59 still lists small values for the other two). In public affairs it is least important but not irrelevant (p. 324).
- Gregariousness is most important in public affairs, quite important in marketing, and much less than life-cycle in fashion. The fashion value is partly low because girls are only moderately gregarious. It is nearly inapplicable to movies, since movie-going is itself an odd gregarious activity (p. 324 and n. 2).
- Status is the least important factor overall. It is negligible in marketing and movies, somewhat more in fashion, and a major key only in public affairs (p. 324).
- **Popular imagery** (the view being corrected) equates opinion leadership with high status and sees influence as only vertical, flowing downward. The authors say this is not the whole story and add *horizontal* opinion leadership, on every rung of the ladder (p. 325).
- Starting hypothesis (p. 324–325): any group that takes part in a given area can be expected to produce leaders who influence others in that area. The picture so far: leaders in varying densities in each life-cycle type, in almost equal densities on every status level, and generally among the more gregarious people (p. 325).
- Outside public affairs, higher status does not automatically bring more leadership. The leader's "power" in marketing, fashion and movies probably comes from casual everyday contact with peers (p. 325).

*Intuition note.* Leaders are not mainly rich people at the top. In most everyday topics, leaders appear at every status level, most densely where the life stage or social life fits the topic.

**Subjective interest as a leadership determinant (p. 325–327).**
- Hypothesis: a person sufficiently interested or active in an area will be turned to for advice, so interest might be enough. Leaders were more interested than non-leaders in fashion and public affairs (information measured interest there). Concentrations of leaders and of interest coincided: girls were most interested in fashion, and upper-status people were better informed on public affairs (p. 325–326).
- Test: "control" interest by comparing groups with equal interest. Girls with high fashion interest still produced a considerably higher proportion of leaders than equally interested women of other life-cycle types. Objective position still matters at high interest (p. 326).
- Leadership and interest are still strongly related, but not because interest directly bestows leadership. It appears that greater interest results in leadership primarily when one associates with others who are also interested (p. 326).
- Example (fashion): the authors suggested that interested matrons were less often leaders than interested girls because matrons meet fewer people seeking advice. This implies matrons associate with matrons and influence runs among life-cycle peers. The flow data seem to corroborate it (p. 327).
- Conclusion: leadership means being interested when others are interested too. Influence seems to run from interested people to people of equal, or perhaps very slightly lower, interest. "Shared interest, in short, appears to be a channel through which communications flow" (p. 327).
- Footnote 5 warns against assuming that "cosmopolitan", highly interested or very gregarious leaders influence people much less so. Researchers should first locate the groups where influence occurs, then see how leaders differ from their close associates (p. 327 n. 5).

*Intuition note.* Being keen is not enough; it appears that someone nearby must also be interested. Interest seems to work through the surrounding group.

**The flow of influence: a review (p. 327–330).**
- *Export index (fashion).* Assumptions: women contact life-cycle and status peers most, and each group has first call on its own leaders. Compute the ratio of fashion leadership to fashion interest for the whole sample, the "average" ratio. Compare each group's ratio with it. Leadership exceeding the group's interest level is read as an "excess" exported to less self-sufficient groups. The formula is: leadership = internal demand (salience of the group's interest) + external demand (the group's attractiveness as a source of influence) (p. 328).
- Findings: among life-cycle types, girls export most, while large-family wives have too few leaders even for their own interest. Among status groups there is almost no exporting. The middle group exceeds the others, if any does; neither high nor low status has more leaders than interest demands (p. 328).
- *Pairs ("survey sociometry").* The findings are treated as suggestive rather than conclusive (p. 328–329).
- *Age, within the family.* Intra-family influence involves more age disparity than extra-family influence, which is more often between age peers (p. 329).
  - Fashions: somewhat more young to old.
  - Public affairs: the reverse, more sharply, and men influence women.
  - Marketing in general: kin who are age peers, more or less, but not members of the same household.
  - Marketing, products certified to particular household members (coffee, cereal): husbands and children exert a substantial share.
  - Movies: if the authors' interpretation is correct, between age peers (largely husband and wife, they expect), since the young look outside the family. When an "expert" is sought, everybody turns to the girls.
- *Age, outside the family.* Marketing has the most crossing of age lines and fashion quite a bit. Marketing shows a flow from older to younger. Fashion shows no clear pattern, though young-to-old is implicit in the export data and in-family. Public affairs and movies show much greater age homogeneity. Public affairs flows old to young. Movies show weak upward evidence for specific decisions but clear upward flow of "expert" advice (p. 329–330).
- *Status (extra-family only).* Same-status pairs: almost 7 in 10 in marketing, 6 in 10 in fashion, 46% in public affairs. Crossing status lines: no direction in marketing; a slight downward flow in fashion, mainly middle to lower; a more marked downward flow in public affairs. Public-affairs "experts" were more often of higher status, and each expert group named new experts of still higher status (p. 330).

**Opinion leader profiles (p. 330–332).**
- *Marketing:* married women with comparatively large families, high gregariousness, spread evenly over status levels. Influence mostly stays within status levels. Older people influence juniors more than the reverse (among adults). Family influence is prominent only for very specific products (p. 331).
- *Fashion:* young, highly gregarious women. Status gives a head start but is not major. There is modest downward status flow and very slight evidence of upward age flow. Women influence women much like themselves. Intra-family fashion influence seems more usual than in marketing. Male influentials are virtually absent from both arenas (p. 331).
- *Public affairs:* high-status women are much more likely to lead. The evidence implies influence travels from upper to lower status, with an equally strong indication that it goes from older to younger, even though younger women lead slightly more often. Gregariousness plays a role. Most marked in this sphere is the dominant role of husbands and male parents (p. 331–332).
- *Movie-going:* leaders concentrate among girls. Influence tends to move from young "experts" to older women. Status plays no role, and the gregariousness index is inapplicable (p. 332).

**Multiple-area leadership (p. 332–334).**
- **General-leader hypothesis (assumptions in exaggerated form):** (1) certain traits are generally required for leadership; (2) in any group, those who embody them most emerge as leaders. If so, leaders in one area should be more likely than others to lead elsewhere. Authors: "We are not at all convinced of this, but let us see." Movie-going is excluded because of its special subsample (p. 332 and n. 7).
- Table 60 (percent of 704 women): three areas 3.1; two areas: fashion+marketing 5.1, fashion+public affairs 2.4, marketing+public affairs 2.8 (total 10.3); one area: fashion 12.0, marketing 12.4, public affairs 3.0 (total 27.4); non-leaders 59.2 (p. 333).
- So 41% lead in at least one area. Of these, almost two-thirds lead in one area, one-fourth in two, and under one-tenth in all three. Overlap looks infrequent, but independence cannot be inferred without a norm (p. 333).
- *Chance norm.* If two leaderships are independent, the share leading in both is the product of the separate shares. For example, 10% × 20% = 2% (p. 333 n. 8).

| | Actual | Chance |
|---|---|---|
| Three areas | 3.1% | .6% |
| Fashion & marketing | 5.1 | 5.3 |
| Fashion & public affairs | 2.4 | 2.6 |
| Marketing & public affairs | 2.8 | 2.7 |

*Table 61 (p. 334).*

- If actual overlap is significantly above chance, a generalized leadership factor seems at work; if not, the evidence does not support the hypothesis (p. 334).
- Result: no significant difference for any pair. Being a leader in one area has no bearing on the likelihood of leading in another. Only the three-area group is about 5 times its chance level (p. 334).
- Caution: these women are so few that no real conclusion can be drawn. A generalized factor of *exaggeration*, or spurious self-designation as a leader, may be at work; the data cannot decide. Overall, the generalized-leader hypothesis receives little support: "Each arena, it seems, has a corps of leaders of its own" (p. 334).

*Intuition note.* If one set of traits made a general leader, leaders in one area would also be over-represented among leaders in another. For the pairs they are not, beyond chance, so it seems each area has leaders of its own; only the tiny three-area group differs.

## Toy example (ours)
Suppose 200 women. 40 lead in topic A (20%) and 50 lead in topic B (25%).
- Chance overlap: 0.20 × 0.25 = 0.05, i.e. 5% of 200 = 10 women.
- If 11 women actually lead in both: 11/200 = 5.5%, close to chance, so no sign of a general leader.
- If 30 women actually led in both: 15%, three times chance, which would suggest a shared factor.

## Concepts
- [[opinion-leader]] — a person who disproportionately shapes others' views; here located by concentrations of leaders in groups, rather than by describing one "typical" leader (p. 321–322)
- [[horizontal-opinion-leadership]] (new) — leadership arising on every rung of the status ladder, not only flowing down from high status (p. 325)
- [[index-of-importance]] (new) — Mosteller's measure (0 to 1, read comparatively) of how much a factor matters for leadership in an area (p. 323–324)
- [[export-index]] (new) — compares a group's leadership-to-interest ratio with the sample average to infer leadership "exported" to other groups (p. 328)
- [[interest-control-and-shared-interest]] (new) — greater interest appears to lead to leadership mainly when associates are interested too; interest does not explain away objective position (p. 326–327)
- [[general-leader-hypothesis]] (new) — the idea that one set of traits makes someone a leader in any area; tested against chance overlap (p. 332–334)
- [[homophily-heterophily]] — the author does not use this term, but finds that people tend to be influenced by others much like themselves (fashion, p. 327; "at least in the everyday kinds" of influence, n. 5) (canon link; not stated in this unit) (p. 327)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] chance overlap = product of marginal proportions ↔ statistics: expected joint frequency under independence — the same calculation.
- [structural-analogy] comparing equally interested groups ↔ causal inference: stratifying on a possible confounder.
- [structural-analogy] influence among similar people ↔ network science: homophily in ties.
- [metaphor] "export" of leadership between groups ↔ economics: net exports from supply minus domestic demand.

## Questions this raises
- Is the "export" index, built on assumed peer contact, a valid way to infer flows between groups?
- Does the 3.1% three-area group reflect real general leaders or exaggeration, and how could a study tell?
- If influence runs between equals, how do new ideas cross group boundaries?
- Would the status findings hold for other places or periods?
