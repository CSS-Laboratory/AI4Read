# The Role and Sequence of Various Influences

## L0 — One line
Any "impact study" contains an accounting scheme that should be made explicit, and applying a simple two-step scheme to Decatur women's movie decisions suggests that most media have a characteristic place in the sequence of influences, with the first exposure most often rated most influential.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The authors defend retrospective "impact" analysis against the objection that people cannot say why they act. They argue it is sometimes adequate, and that it needs an explicit accounting scheme: a chosen list of factors and causal steps. Their own scheme is crude: interest is created, then the decision is clinched. Asking Decatur women the order of their exposures, they find that personal contacts, magazines and books come early, newspapers mostly come second and theatre marquees (the signs outside cinemas) last. The first exposure is most often reported as most influential. Personal contacts are more likely to be rated top either first or last. The authors call these leads, not firm results, and say better interviewing is needed.

**Prerequisites:** reading percentage tables; the idea of causal "factors" and of retrospective survey reports; what the previous chapters' "impact ratings" are (respondents' own reports of what influenced them).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Impact analysis (retrospective reports of influences) is defended as sometimes adequate, not always; experiments or repeated surveys are often more to the point, but similar work on social matters is very expensive (pp. 187–188).
- The general question "why did she act?" does not really make sense as it stands. It must be turned into specific questions about chosen factors, which is the accounting scheme (p. 189).
- Every impact study contains an accounting scheme, and it must be made explicit (p. 190).
- The Decatur scheme is two-step: interest created, then clinching (p. 191).
- Most movie-goers (64%) were exposed to several sources (p. 191).
- Media differ in typical sequence position: books and magazines early, newspapers mostly second, marquees late. Personal contacts are spread over all positions (pp. 193–195).
- The first exposure is most often rated most influential. Personal contacts are more likely to be rated highest at first or last, and newspapers are effective only in first position (pp. 195–197).
- The authors treat the findings as leads and call for case studies, repeated interviews and finer schemes (p. 197).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: defence of impact studies (pp. 187–188)
- Impact studies serve evaluation. Experiments or repeated surveys are often more to the point (p. 187).
- **Objection (some students):** "people don't know why they act the way they do." **Authors reply:** the objection is based on a misunderstanding and, in part too, on self-deception (p. 187).
  - There are many areas where people do know why they act. The researcher's task is to single out legitimate from hopeless uses. The claim is not that impact analysis can always be used, but that it is sometimes adequate for evaluating the role of certain factors (p. 187).
  - Self-deception: believing the best method is "just around the corner." Medicine can test a headache drug easily. In social relations a similar design is logically possible but very expensive and often hopelessly involved (p. 187).
  - A man cannot say what cured his headache. But he can describe how influences acted on him and how his plans were carried out or changed (p. 188).
- How useful such knowledge is should be decided only after systematic collection and analysis of retrospective reports (p. 188).
- Authors usually either reject "reasons" dogmatically or analyse them naively. This chapter and the next discuss the assumptions and decisions in even a simple impact study (p. 188).
- The authors treat the section's results as a basis for general leads, not as quantitative results for detailed inference. The field work failed to avoid many pitfalls (p. 188).

### The five main elements (pp. 188–189)
1. Developing an accounting scheme.
2. Distinguishing classes of cases and selecting appropriate ones.
3. Devising interviewing procedures.
4. Assessing impact for each factor in the scheme.
5. Statistical analysis and interpretation.

This chapter covers Step 1; the next covers the other steps (p. 189).

### The accounting scheme (pp. 189–191)
- Studies of "causes" of crime or "reasons" for voting trace a final act back to earlier points. This has a necessary arbitrariness: a woman's whole preceding life contributes to an act such as buying a coffee brand (p. 189).
- So the general question "why" does not make sense. It must become specific questions on whether a given set of factors played an important role. The factors depend partly on the study's purpose and partly on the area (p. 189).
- **Migration example (pp. 189–190):**
  - At least: disadvantages of the old place and attractions of the new.
  - If evaluating a Chamber of Commerce's propaganda: also how people learned about the new place.
  - With a psychological emphasis: split the "push" into negative features of the old place and a "trigger event."
- **Definition:** the *accounting scheme* is the set of factors included in the inquiry. It is a "primitive model" against which all cases are compared (p. 190).
  - Two-element version: push and pull.
  - Four-element version: push, trigger event, pull, and a channel carrying the pull's attraction (p. 190).
- For cheap purchases, a scheme with at least three elements would serve questions about interest, information and final action: (1) influences that start an action, (2) sources consulted during indecision, (3) clinching factors (p. 190).
- Every impact study has an inherent accounting scheme, which must be made explicit. This is a preliminary decision on which causal factors and steps will be investigated (p. 190).
- **Decatur scheme (p. 191):** a decision to go to a movie or buy a food brand has at least two steps: interest or desire is created, then the matter is "clinched" and the act performed.
  - Some questions distinguished "learning about something" from "making up one's mind."
  - All interviews asked "changers" (marketing, fashions, movies, public affairs) in what order things brought the item to their attention (p. 191).
  - The earlier tables lump all influences together (fn. 1, p. 191).

*Intuition note:* You cannot explain "everything that led to an act," so you choose a few steps and ask about them. That choice is the accounting scheme, and it fixes in advance which factors and steps the study investigates.

### The number and order of influences (pp. 191–195)
**Table 8 (movies, n = 584) (p. 191):** one source only 36%; more than one 64%, made up of two sources 32%, three 20%, four or more 12%.

- Those with several exposures were asked the order. 14% could not say, and those who could probably found it hard (p. 192).
- Fn. 2 (p. 192): recall failure is 18% (2 exposures, n = 318), 8% (3 exposures, n = 146), 16% (4 or more, n = 57). The authors offer a tentative explanation, calling it "somewhat tenuous" but easily testable. Two opposing tendencies: the number of exposures recalled may be partly a function of general recall ability (so more exposures recalled, order better remembered), but order may become harder to remember as exposures increase.
- The first question is descriptive: what are the sequences, and do media have characteristic positions? Impact ratings are not used yet. Because only exposure is counted, bases are large enough to add books, previews and marquees (p. 192).
- **Expectations (p. 193):**
  - Newspapers play a "fact-feeding" role (listing pictures, showing where a film runs), so they should come somewhat later.
  - Personal contacts arouse a favorable predisposition, so they should precede newspapers.
  - Magazines should often come first, since pieces appear well before the film arrives in a city.
  - Books should also come early.
- These expectations are "largely borne out" (p. 193).

**Table 9: position of each influence (p. 193)** (column totals in brackets: number of mentions alongside at least one other source)

| Position | Personal contact (190) | Marquee (61) | Previews (114) | Newspapers (295) | Magazines (139) | Books (36) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 44% | 12% | 48% | 22% | 52% | 64% |
| Second | 37 | 29 | 29 | 51 | 26 | 3 |
| Third or later | 19 | 59 | 23 | 27 | 22 | 33 |

- Caveat (fn. 3, p. 193): Table 9 ignores that sources differ in how many other sources they come with. If newspapers usually occur in three-source combinations, their apparently later position could be spurious.
- **Authors' reading (pp. 193–195):**
  - *Books and personal contacts* both come early, books especially. Personal influences are spread over positions: they can "initiate," "inform" and "clinch." Books are rarely second (p. 194).
  - *Book conjecture:* some people read or reread a book because they know it is on screen, and go only if they like it. The authors note that book sales increase when a book is shown on the screen; this is usually seen as an effect of attendance, but Table 9 "seems to indicate" that more reading also precedes attendance (p. 194).
  - *Magazines* lie between books and personal contacts, and seem more similar to people than to books in their influence role. The data cannot separate serialised stories from film or actor coverage (p. 194).
  - *Previews* resemble personal influences. About one in four come late and obviously serve to reinforce earlier learning; this is probably the case mostly with frequent movie customers (p. 194).
  - *Newspapers* were expected to be terminal, but are most often second. The authors infer that debating continues to the end: the paper is a guide to what is on, and other sources are used for the final decision. In many cases the paper may also have shown nothing desirable, so advice was sought elsewhere (p. 194).
  - *Marquees* rise from first to third place. They perhaps clinch accumulated influences, or many people are "marquee shoppers" who postpone choosing (pp. 194–195).
- A similar analysis of fashion gave no basically new ideas and is not reported. Marketing was not analysed: decisions take too short a time (p. 195).

*Intuition note:* Different channels do different jobs. Some plant the idea early (books, magazines), some give details (newspapers), some perhaps close the choice (marquees), and personal contacts can do all three. The authors infer these jobs from position and do not observe them directly.

### Position and impact (pp. 195–197)
- Three possibilities: the clinching (last) influence feels most effective; the first does; or there is no relation. Chart IV shows a clear result: the first exposure is most often reported most influential. There are many individual exceptions but the trend is strong, and it also holds for fashion (p. 195).
- Chart IV covers only exposed people (fn. 4, p. 195). Example: of movie-goers naming personal contact as the initial influence, 53% were "effectively exposed," 7% "contributory," 40% "ineffectively exposed" (note under Chart IV, p. 196).
- **Most likely explanation (pp. 195–197):** a person decides, prompted by some influence, to see a movie or change her hairdo. She is then more alert to other exposures, such as ads and talk, and uses them for confirmation or detail. So ineffective exposures should be claimed mostly in positions after the most effective one.
- **Alternative path (p. 197):** some women shop around and decide only after hesitation. They appear in the third bar of each block: a third-or-later exposure called most influential.
- **Sources differ (p. 197):**
  - Personal contacts are "almost bimodal": top rating most likely in first or last position, less in the middle.
  - Newspapers are effective only in first position, and less so the later they come.
- **Authors' caution (p. 197):** this only highlights the problem.
  - Only detailed case studies or, still better, repeated interviews with the same people while they are considering a decision will give further insights.
  - Chart IV has only position and an average impact rating for the whole decision; the roles of sources are inferred by speculation.
  - To handle more information a more detailed accounting scheme will be needed; the authors add that the role information could have been obtained directly by more systematic interviewing.
  - Even this elementary procedure has perhaps shed some light on the diversified functions of personal contacts.

*Intuition note:* People often say the first thing that got them interested mattered most, and later exposures often only confirm. But some decisions are made late, and personal contacts can matter at the start or at the end.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Five women's movie decisions; sources in order of exposure:
- W1: magazine, friend
- W2: friend, newspaper
- W3: friend, newspaper, marquee
- W4: magazine, newspaper
- W5: book, friend, marquee

A Table 9-style count uses each mention once.
- Friend: 4 mentions. W2 and W3 first, W1 and W5 second. So first 2/4 = 50%, second 2/4 = 50%, third or later 0%.
- Newspaper: 3 mentions, all second. So second 3/3 = 100%.
- Marquee: 2 mentions, both third. So third 100%.

Because every woman here has at least two sources, all are "multiple exposed." The percentages are over mentions, not women. With real data they also depend on how many sources each woman had (cf. fn. 3, p. 193).
