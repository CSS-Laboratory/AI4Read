# Appendix A. Choice of the City

## L0 — One line
The authors chose Decatur, Illinois as the study site by scoring 18 mid-sized Middle Western cities against their own group average and eliminating the unrepresentative ones.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Which city should host a study needing about 800 interviews? Budget fixed the size at around 60,000 people, and the Middle West was chosen as the region with the fewest sectional peculiarities. Eighteen cities were scored on many social indices, each expressed relative to the group average (= 100). Cluster averages and a grand average gave a "representativeness" score. A separate index measured occupational diversity. Cities were then eliminated for being too high or too low on key traits, for being state capitals, one-industry towns, or marketing towns, and for being "Middletown". Three towns remained, and Decatur was chosen. The appendix shows how a site was chosen for "typicality".

**Prerequisites:** percentages and averages; index numbers (value ÷ group mean × 100); absolute deviation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Study size came from the budget: at least 1 interview per 20 homes, with 800 interviews, implied a city of about 60,000 (p. 335).
- Candidate pool: 28 cities of 50,000–80,000 in seven Middle Western states, cut to 18 by dropping suburban or near-suburban towns (pp. 335–336).
- Each town was scored on many indices in five clusters, each index set so the 18-town average is 100 (pp. 336–337).
- Cluster means and a grand mean were unweighted: every index within a cluster, and every cluster, counted equally (p. 337).
- Occupational diversity needed a different measure: summed absolute deviations, because simple averaging hides offsetting highs and lows (p. 338).
- Selection worked by elimination, using the indices plus a town-type classification from another research office (p. 338).
- Final choice among Decatur, Terre Haute and Springfield (Ohio) fell on Decatur (p. 339). The appendix gives no explicit reason beyond the indices shown.
- The sample inside Decatur was an ordinary probability sample of women aged 16+, alternating older and younger women where a household had more than two (p. 339).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: size and region (p. 335).** The number of feasible interviews set the city size. The authors wanted at least one interview per twenty homes. With a sample of 800, this pointed to a city of about 60,000 (p. 335). They chose the Middle West because it is "least characterized by sectional pecularities" (p. 335; spelling as printed). The task was then to pick the most "typical" town, or few towns, among Middle Western cities of about 60,000 (p. 335).

**1. Procedure used in selecting cities (pp. 335–338).**
1. Cities of 50,000–80,000 in Ohio, Michigan, Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin, Iowa and Kansas were listed. A town qualified if it met the range within its city limits *or* its metropolitan area. This gave 28 cities (p. 335).
2. Suburban or near-suburban towns, meaning those dominated by a large city, were eliminated (p. 336).
3. The remaining 18 cities were studied on "some thirty-six social indices" (p. 336). The indices fall in five clusters (p. 336):
   - Composition of the population: % female, % over 21, % native-born white.
   - Economic status: standard of living, buying income, retail sales, occupation.
   - Commercial structure: manufacturing, retail and service establishments per thousand population.
   - Communications behavior: magazine circulation per thousand for eleven named magazines and their average, radio homes, education.
   - General quality: Thorndike's G-score ("goodness", from 37 items), stability (% owner-occupied dwellings), political participation (1942 registration).
   Sources are census data (1940, 1939), *Sales Management* (1943), *Urban Markets and Retail Sales* (1938), Thorndike's *Your City* (1939), and private sources for magazines (pp. 336–337).
4. For each index, the 18-town average was set to 100 and each town was ranked on that basis. This shows the size and direction of a town's deviation. Indices are scored on the "upper" level, so 110 for education means above-average schooling and 90 means below (p. 337).
5. Each cluster was averaged, with no weighting, to check representativeness in each broad area. A town may be below average in economic status but above in communication behavior (p. 337).
6. A grand average was computed from the cluster averages, again with equal weight for each cluster. It is the "overall score of representativeness" (p. 337).
7. Occupational diversity was handled separately, because it was "not completely parallel" and so was not summed with the rest (p. 337).
   - Each town's percentage of the labor force was computed in eight groups: extractive and construction; manufacturing; transportation, communication and utilities; wholesale trade; retail trade; business, personal and domestic service; professional services; government service (p. 337).
   - Each group's 18-town average was set to 100, and each town got an index per group (pp. 337–338).
   - Simple averaging of these indices would mislead. A town high in one group and low in another might average 100 without being diversified (p. 338).
   - So the total deviations were added regardless of direction. A score of 76 and a score of 124 count as the same deviation (p. 338).
   - The summed deviations were re-indexed so the 18-town average deviation is 100. This index reads in the *opposite* way to the others: lower means more diversified, higher means more concentrated (p. 338).
8. The authors also collected short town descriptions and a classification from another research office's statistical analysis of American towns: institutional, marketing, industrial or balanced (p. 338).

*Intuition note.* Setting the group average to 100 tells you how far each town is from "ordinary" among these 18 towns. Because 100 always means average, deviations on different indices can be read on one 100-based scale. The occupational measure needs absolute deviations because, with signed deviations, a town that is high on some groups and low on others would look average.

**2. Evaluation of the results (pp. 338–339).** The authors say the best method is to eliminate towns rather than select them (p. 338). Eliminations:
1. Four Iowa towns (Cedar Rapids, Waterloo, Sioux City, Davenport). Their economic and educational ratings are good, but magazine reading is unsatisfactorily low. Cedar Rapids and Sioux City are also marketing towns for the surrounding rural area (p. 338).
2. Four state capitals (Topeka, Lansing, Madison, Springfield, Ill.). The first three rank too high in education, "goodness" and magazine reading. Because they contain the state bureaucracy, all four rank as institutional towns; in addition, Madison and Lansing hold state institutions of higher learning. Springfield, Ill. is the least undesirable but has a somewhat larger population than wanted (pp. 338–339).
3. "Low towns" (Hamilton, Bay City, Kenosha, Racine). They rank too low in economic status, education and magazine reading (p. 339).
4. Kalamazoo. It ranks too high on most traits, especially "goodness" and magazine reading (p. 339).
5. Pontiac. It is near average on many traits but too industrialized, a one-industry (automobile) town, and tends to be dominated by Detroit, 25 miles away (p. 339).
6. Muncie. It is near average but a one-industry town, and "it was decided that we ought not to select 'Middletown'" (p. 339).

*Count check (added step):* $4+4+4+1+1+1 = 15$ eliminated, and $18-15=3$ remain, as the text says (p. 339).

Three towns remained: Decatur (Ill.), Terre Haute (Ind.) and Springfield (Ohio). Their table gives seven numeric columns and a town type (p. 339):

**3. Selection of sample (p. 339).** Inside Decatur, the sample was drawn by usual probability methods. In each household, resident women (not domestic help) aged 16 and over were interviewed. Where more than two women lived in a household, interviews alternated between older and younger women (p. 339).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three towns A, B, C; two occupational groups; percentages of the labor force.

| | Manufacturing % | Retail % |
|---|---|---|
| A | 30 | 10 |
| B | 20 | 20 |
| C | 10 | 30 |

- Group averages: manufacturing $(30+20+10)/3=20$; retail $(10+20+30)/3=20$.
- Indices (town ÷ average × 100): A = 150 and 50; B = 100 and 100; C = 50 and 150.
- Simple average of each town's indices: A $(150+50)/2=100$; B $100$; C $(50+150)/2=100$. All equal, so averaging cannot tell the towns apart.
- Total absolute deviation from 100: A $50+50=100$; B $0+0=0$; C $50+50=100$.
- Average deviation: $(100+0+100)/3=66.67$. Re-indexed (÷ 66.67 × 100): A = 150; B = 0; C = 150.
- Reading (lower = more diversified, per p. 338): B is the most diversified; A and C are above the average deviation, so more concentrated.

## Concepts
- [[representativeness-score]] (new) — grand mean of five unweighted cluster means of indices, each indexed to the 18-town average = 100 (p. 337)
- [[group-mean-indexing]] (new) — rescaling each indicator so the candidate-group average is 100, showing size and direction of deviation (p. 337)
- [[occupational-diversity-index]] (new) — sum of absolute deviations across occupation groups, re-indexed to average = 100; lower = more diversified (p. 338)
- [[selection-by-elimination]] (new) — choosing a typical site by removing cities that are too high, too low, or structurally special (p. 338)
- [[town-type-classification]] (new) — institutional, marketing, industrial or balanced, taken from another research office's analysis (p. 338)

## Canonical instances
- [[decatur-illinois]] — the city chosen as site of the study (p. 339)
- [[muncie-middletown]] — Muncie, near average, excluded as a one-industry town and, furthermore, because the authors decided not to select "Middletown" (p. 339)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Indexing each variable to the group mean = 100 ↔ standardization (z-scores): both make different scales comparable around a centre, but here there is no division by spread, so the math differs.
- [structural-analogy] Summing absolute deviations from the mean ↔ dispersion measures (mean absolute deviation): the same idea of ignoring direction to measure spread.
- [metaphor] Choosing a "typical" city by elimination ↔ case selection in comparative research: typicality is argued by excluding extremes and special cases, not by a statistical sampling rule.

## Questions this raises
- Does a city that is average on these indices behave typically for opinion leadership and personal influence? The appendix only shows typicality on social indicators.
- How sensitive is the outcome to equal weights for clusters and indices? The authors say no weighting was used, and the text reports no test of other weights.
- Why was Decatur preferred over Terre Haute and Springfield, Ohio? The page gives the indices but no explicit reasoning.
- What follows from interviewing only women aged 16 and over in a single city? (The appendix describes the sampling but not its consequences.)
