# An Essay in Convergence (Chapter III, opening of Section Two)

## L0 — One line
The authors guess that two features of small primary groups, shared norms ("anchorage") and communication networks (relay and reinforcement), are the keys to understanding how interpersonal ties intervene in mass persuasion, and they plan to look for corroborating evidence in small group research.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Section One argued that research had studied short-run media effects and had gradually added intervening factors, above all interpersonal relations. This section, opened by this chapter, asks which elements of those relations matter for communication effectiveness. The authors guess at two. First, interpersonal ties seem to anchor opinions, attitudes and values, and members seem reluctant to change them alone. Second, interpersonal ties imply communication networks that may relay media content and may reinforce or counteract media messages. They look for corroborating evidence in small group research, which grew from sociologists' and psychologists' converging interests. They then outline Chapters IV–VII. They hope this helps join mass communication research and small group research, which have been almost completely unrelated.

**Prerequisites:** Section One's ideas: the opinion leader tradition and the two-step flow hypothesis; "primary group" as a small, intimate, face-to-face group.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The earlier chapters make two claims. Research has studied short-run media effects. Its history is a successive taking-account of factors that intervene between media and audience (p. 43).
- This section tries to isolate the "active ingredients" of informal primary groups for mass communication. The goal is a more complex but more realistic model of persuasion campaigns (pp. 43–44).
- Hypothesis 1, anchorage: interpersonal relations seem to anchor opinions, attitudes, habits and values. Members are reluctant to give them up unilaterally (p. 44).
- Hypothesis 2, networks: interpersonal ties imply communication networks with two functions. The relay function: more exposed people may pass media content on to the less exposed. The reinforcement function lets person-to-person influence coincide with media messages (pp. 44–45).
- Positive reinforcement is likely, on "substantial reason to suspect", to make communication particularly effective (p. 45).
- The authors look to small group research for corroborating evidence. Sociologists found that institutional effects are mediated by interpersonal ties. Psychologists found that perception, judgment and performance are influenced by other people (pp. 45–46).
- The plan: Chapters IV–V on group norms. Chapters VI–VII on influence within the group and the link to the media ("communication to the group") (pp. 46–47).
- The payoff is a possible convergence of mass communication research (global, "atomized society") with small group research (therapeutic, psychologistic) (p. 47).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Recap and purpose (pp. 43–44).**
- The previous chapters made two assertions. Communications research to date has studied short-run mass media effects. Its intellectual history is best seen as a successive taking account of factors that intervene between mass media and audience, and so modify media effects (p. 43).
- Section One centred on one such intervening variable, interpersonal relations (p. 43).
- Section Two scrutinizes that notion. It asks which elements of social ties bear most on communications effectiveness, i.e. the "active ingredients" of informal primary groups (pp. 43–44).
- The purpose is to guide research planning on transmission of mass persuasion. In particular it aims to build a concern with interpersonal relations into research design. The result may be a more complex, more realistic "model" for studying persuasion campaigns (p. 44).

**Two characteristics of interpersonal relations (pp. 44–45).** The authors start from the earlier illustrations and the opinion leader tradition. They speculate that two characteristics are each "a major key" (p. 44).

1. *Anchorage.* Interpersonal relationships seem to be anchorage points for individual opinions, attitudes, habits and values. Interacting individuals seem to generate and maintain common ideas and behaviour patterns together and continuously. They are reluctant to surrender or modify these unilaterally (p. 44).
   - Conditional claim: if many or most of the ostensibly individual opinions that campaigns try to change are anchored in small groups, then this aspect of group relations deserves attention (p. 44).
   - The authors often call these shared opinions and attitudes "group norms" (p. 45).

2. *Networks of interpersonal communication.* These matter in several interlocking ways (p. 44).
   - *Relay function.* The two-step flow hypothesis suggests that interpersonal networks link to media networks. Some relatively more exposed people pass on what they see, hear or read to less exposed contacts. Primary groups may thus serve as channels for media transmission (pp. 44–45).
   - *Reinforcement function.* Person-to-person influence may coincide with media messages and so counteract or reinforce them. When reinforcement is positive, the communication is likely to be particularly effective; the authors say there is "substantial reason to suspect" this (p. 45). Footnote 1: Lazarsfeld (1942) calls this "supplementation" (p. 45).

The authors describe both characteristics as a "guess": the keys to the intervening role of interpersonal relations (p. 45).

*Intuition note.* A group works like an anchor: it holds members' views in place. It also works like a pipe: information flows through it, and it can add to or cancel the media message. Both points are hypotheses here, not findings.

**Turning to small group research (pp. 45–46).**
- The authors want corroborating evidence from social science. They find "some modest theory plus a body of experimental findings" in small group research (p. 45).
- They say the division of labour seems to pay off here. Sociologists were discovering that the informal group intervenes almost everywhere. Experimental social psychologists were studying the effect of "other people" on individual behaviour (p. 45).
- Sociologists found that effects of the institutional order of mass society are mediated by interpersonal ties. Psychologists found that perception, judgment and test performance are markedly influenced by interpersonal relations. Small group research grew from the combination (p. 46).
- Footnote 2 proposes, with hedges, that there appears to have been a belated "discovery" of the primary group even in social psychology, which seems to have happened in two ways (pp. 45–46):
  - Some experimenters first created "social" situations (presence or absence of anonymous "others"). They then recognized that the small group per se is a natural unit of social structure.
  - Lewin and associates first used small groups as small-scale models of large structures. If that impression is right, their later interest in small groups per se seems also to be a reorientation. The authors flag this as something "one senses", a "hunch". They suggest comparing Newcomb and Hartley (1947) with Swanson, Newcomb and Hartley (1952), and cite Lewin (1951), p. 164.
- Footnote 3 cites Shils (1951), who credits Mayo ("human relations" in industry), Moreno (sociometry, mapping attraction and repulsion) and Lewin ("group dynamics") with founding three traditions (p. 46).
- Scope: the authors will propose a framework for organizing findings in a way meaningful for mass media research. They will not report the whole field. They add that this does not mean they will report only studies with immediate implications for campaigns. They will include studies that seem to have some relevance or may provide some lead (p. 46).

**Plan of the section (pp. 46–47).**
- Chapter IV: evidence that small groups anchor opinions. It then covers existence, origin, and the reasons and dynamics of enforcement of group-shared norms (pp. 46–47).
- Chapter V: how such norms bear on effective communication, including influence attempts from outside the group (p. 47).
- Chapter VI: processes of interpersonal transmission. What accounts for the substantial influence that people in face-to-face contact seem to have. Which people are influential for which others, and under what circumstances (p. 47).
- Chapter VII: how intragroup networks link to the outside world, and whether there is evidence of a link between small group networks and the mass media. The authors call this second step "communication to the group" (p. 47).

**Closing (p. 47).** If the exploration succeeds, it will help understanding of mass communication. It may also help convergence of two areas "almost completely unrelated to date" in substance and method. Mass communications is characterized as having a global reach and an image of an atomized society. Small group research is characterized as having therapeutic and psychologistic concerns. The authors say these now "seem to have something to say to each other" (p. 47).

*Intuition note.* The chapter is a research programme, not a result. It names two group features, says where evidence will be sought, and says why joining two fields could help.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two groups of 4 people each hear a campaign message "vote X".
- **Relay.** In group A, 1 person is exposed and tells the other 3. Reached = 1 + 3 = 4 of 4 = 100%. Direct exposure alone reaches 1/4 = 25%. In group B, 2 people are exposed and nobody passes it on. Reached = 2/4 = 50%.
- **Reinforcement (simple tally).** A person receives the media message (+1 toward X). Case 1: 2 friends also favour X, so the total is +1 + 2 = +3, positive reinforcement. Case 2: 2 friends oppose X, so the total is +1 − 2 = −1, counteraction.
- This only shows what "relay" and "reinforcement" mean. The book offers no such counting model.

## Concepts
- [[intervening-variable]] (new) — a factor between media and audience that modifies media effects; here, interpersonal relations (p. 43)
- [[group-anchorage]] (new) — hypothesis: interacting individuals seem to generate and maintain shared opinions, attitudes, habits and values, which they are reluctant to change unilaterally (p. 44)
- [[group-norms]] (new) — the authors' label for opinions and attitudes shared person-to-person within small groups (p. 45)
- [[two-step-flow]] — more exposed people pass media content to less exposed contacts; here the basis of the relay function (pp. 44–45)
- [[relay-function]] (new) — primary groups may serve as channels for mass media transmission (p. 45)
- [[reinforcement-function]] (new) — personal influence coinciding with media messages, countering or reinforcing them; Lazarsfeld (1942): "supplementation" (p. 45)
- [[opinion-leader]] — named, with earlier illustrations, as the starting point for speculating about the two characteristics (p. 44)
- [[small-group-research]] (new) — field combining sociological and social-psychological work on interpersonal effects (p. 46)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Relay function ↔ communication-theory: diffusion through opinion leaders and social ties — both route information through people rather than directly from the source.
- [structural-analogy] Positive reinforcement from personal contacts ↔ complex contagion: adoption helped by repeated signals from several contacts. The text only claims effectiveness is "likely"; it does not give the adoption mechanism.
- [metaphor] Anchorage ↔ inertia or equilibrium in physical systems — shared views resist unilateral change.

## Questions this raises
- What evidence will Chapter IV give that groups enforce shared norms, and how do norms originate?
- How can the relay and reinforcement functions be measured in a real campaign?
- Does "likely to be particularly effective" hold when reinforcement is negative, or only when it is positive?
- Which people become influential for which others (Chapter VI)?
