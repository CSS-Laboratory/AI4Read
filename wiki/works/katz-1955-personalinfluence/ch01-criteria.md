# Criteria of Influence

## L0 — One line
Which "opinion leaders" you find depends on how you ask, so Katz and Lazarsfeld compare three ways of naming influentials and choose recent, specific influence episodes, mostly located by self-report, as their criterion.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The Decatur study wants to locate everyday opinion leaders: informal, face-to-face guides of opinion. Using public affairs as the example, the authors ask about three kinds of "other people": generally trusted experts, people who influenced a specific opinion change, and everyday conversation partners. These give different pictures. About half of general influentials are non-family; everyday contacts are mostly family, especially husbands. So contact is not the same as influence. The authors prefer the specific-influence criterion. They locate leaders mainly by asking each woman about her own recent advice-giving, and they checked these claims with the people she said she advised (the next chapter evaluates this).

**Prerequisites:** percentages and simple cross-tabulation; basic survey sampling and interviewing; the idea of validating self-reports.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The authors' "opinion leaders" are informal, face-to-face, everyday influentials who guide opinion rather than lead action directly (p. 138). This sets the scope of the whole study.
- Three questions about other people are compared: general trust/competence, specific influence on an opinion change, and everyday talk (p. 139). The relationship profiles of the people they name differ.
- About half of the women named no general influential. Naming one goes with more knowledge, more education and youth (p. 140). Many women, the authors say, are simply not touched by personal influence in this sphere.
- In Table 1, the share of non-family persons rises from everyday contacts (15%) to specific influentials (34%) to general influentials (51%) (p. 144). Among the 136 women who named all three types, the authors read the questions as seeming to rank along two dimensions: frequency of contact and appraisal of competence.
- Husbands account for 53% of everyday contacts, 32% of specific influentials and 18% of general influentials. The authors conclude that contact is not the same as influence (p. 145).
- The authors choose the specific-influence criterion as the middle way. They add, as a speculation, that actual influence may often derive from a compromise between high esteem and easy access (p. 146).
- Leaders are located mainly by self-report: recent advice-asking, twice, or once plus a self-rating as more likely than friends to be asked (pp. 147–148). The reliability of this index is left to the next chapter's follow-up (p. 148).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: what kind of leader (pp. 137–139)
- The central problem is to locate key points in the transmission of personal influence in a middle-sized American city (p. 137).
- Popular imagery has many leader types: official or unofficial, small-group or national, specialized or generalized (pp. 137–138).
- The authors' Opinion Leaders serve informal rather than formal groups, and face-to-face rather than larger ones. They guide opinion and its changes rather than lead directly in action (p. 138).
- The authors say opinion leadership, if it may be called leadership at all, is leadership at its simplest: casual, sometimes unwitting, within friends, family and neighbours (p. 138).
- Sample: about 800 women in Decatur, Illinois. Four arenas: marketing, fashions, public affairs, movie-going. Respondents were asked about themselves and about people who influence them or whom they influence (p. 138).
- For public affairs, three kinds of other people were asked about: (1) trustworthy, knowledgeable persons; (2) persons who influenced a specific opinion change; (3) persons with whom the respondent most often talks over radio and press news. Each woman also gave a self-rating of influentiality and reports of recent occasions on which she claimed to have influenced others (p. 139).
- The chapter treats the three as a "rough scale" whose dimensions are "somewhat unclear." Examples come only from public affairs (p. 139).

### The Generally Influential (pp. 140–141)
- Question (paraphrased): does the respondent know anyone around here who keeps up with the news and whom she can trust to tell her what is really going on? Only persons met face to face were allowed, not prominent public figures (p. 140).
- About half could not or would not name anyone. They had little interest in and knowledge of public affairs. One third of those lowest in knowledge, but almost two thirds of those highest, named someone (p. 140).
- By age and education: 40% of older women who did not finish high school, against 64% of younger high-school graduates (p. 140).
- About half of those named are family. The rest are neighbours, friends or work associates (p. 140).
- Among married women, husbands are the largest group, but there are almost as many neighbours as husbands. Among unmarried women, parents are the largest group. Among once-married women, non-family ties are named more often than family ties (p. 141). The authors say the unmarried women's reliance on parents reflects, at least in part, parents being the most accessible adults, and the once-married women's lower family rate largely reflects the unavailability of family relations (p. 141).
- Two thirds of the general influentials named are men, whatever the respondent's family status. Only a third of these men are outside the kinship circle. A named woman is more likely friend or neighbour than kin (p. 141).
- Explanations offered: face-to-face ties are differentiated along sex lines by family status. In addition, "there may be" a reluctance to rank male friends above male kin (p. 141).

> **Intuition note:** Naming a general expert is in part self-selective: it goes with interest and knowledge, so many women are outside this kind of influence. Among those who do name someone, the choice seems also to reflect who is accessible.

### The Specific Influentials (pp. 141–143)
- Design: nine opinion questions in June and the same nine in August. In August, interviewers carried the June answers. Each time a respondent gave a new opinion, she was asked why. Special attention went to whether she had talked to someone who played a role (pp. 141–142).
- 619 opinion changes were found. Women sometimes changed on several issues, but more than three changes per person was rare (p. 142).
- 58% of the changes (not changers) were apparently made without any remembered personal contact and very often depended on mass media. About 40%, some 260 cases, involved a recalled conversation the respondent said played a role (p. 142).
- Footnote 2: this method depends on recall. Influence that operated without the respondent's awareness is ignored here (p. 142).
- Among married women, 64% of influence relations are intra-family, with husbands most important. Single women lean on parents. Among non-family ties, friends are most important. Male influentials are mostly family and female ones mostly non-family, which again suggests accessibility (pp. 142–143).

### Everyday Contacts (p. 143)
- Question: when you hear something on the radio or read it in the paper, are you inclined to talk it over with someone before making up your mind? If so, whom? (p. 143)
- About half said they were not inclined to. Among the rest, talk is largely within the family: husbands for married women, parents for single women (p. 143).
- Footnote 3: the question is really two questions. A "yes" is fairly unambiguous. A "no" may mean low interest, or confidence in one's own judgement. "Yes" answers typify women who are interested but at least slightly uneasy about deciding alone (p. 143).

### A Comparison and a Tentative Result (pp. 143–145)
- A strict comparison is possible only for the 136 women who named all three types (p. 144).

| Relationship | Everyday contacts | Specific influentials | General influentials |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-family | 15% | 34% | 51% |
| Family | 84 | 64 | 48 |
| – Parent | 21 | 17 | 18 |
| – Husband | 53 | 32 | 18 |
| – Other relatives | 10 | 15 | 12 |
| No answer | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| Total | (136) | (136) | (136) |

*Table 1 (p. 144).*

- Authors' interpretation: the three questions "seem to" rank along two dimensions. By *frequency* of contact: everyday most, general least. By *appraisal* of competence and trustworthiness: general most, everyday least (p. 144).
- Husbands: 18% as general influentials, 32% as specific influentials, 53% as everyday contacts. Husbands matter most for daily talk, less for specific influence, and least as trusted advisers. Hence "Contact… is not synonymous with influence" (pp. 144–145).
- Reading offered (authors' conjecture, "reasonable to surmise" / "safe to assume"): general influentials are special in the network and influence from them is apparently readily accepted, but they probably tend to be at some distance. Everyday consultants are close, but talk with them is probably not regarded as influence and may be talk between equals (p. 145).

> **Intuition note:** The three questions seem to tap different aspects. One leans toward esteem, one toward closeness, and one sits in between. A husband can be the most frequent conversation partner without being named as often as a specific or general influence.

### Choosing a criterion (pp. 145–146)
- Perhaps the easiest to use are general estimations, but they give no information on how frequent the influence is, encourage naming prestigious people known only slightly, and rely more on memory (pp. 145–146).
- Insisting on frequency of contact may load the findings with habitual encounters that matter less for opinion change (p. 146).
- The authors say their choice tends toward the middle way: recent, recollectable influence incidents and the specific influentials in them (p. 146).
- Speculation, which they call simply speculative: actual influence may often derive from a compromise between higher estimation and easier accessibility. Esteemed persons are often not accessible. Accessible persons may influence because they are on hand "when the ripe moment for change occurs" (p. 146).

### Self-Detected Leaders (pp. 146–148)
- Two ways to find specific influentials. (a) Through the testimony of those they influenced: respondents describe or name them. (b) Through the respondents themselves, who say whether they recently influenced others and give names, dates and subjects (pp. 146–147). Method (b) is administratively desirable and was adopted for most analysis (p. 147).
- Questions: in both interviews, "Have you recently been asked your advice about…?" for each of the four arenas, with follow-up details. In the second interview, also "Compared with other women belonging to your circle of friends, are you more or less likely… to be asked your advice?" (p. 147).
- Index. A woman is an opinion leader in an area if (1) she reported advice-asking in both June and August, or (2) she reported it once and also said she is "more likely" than her friends to be asked. Others are not leaders (pp. 147–148).
- The method rests on recent, specific, documented incidents plus self-appraisal (p. 148).
- Reliability is the open question: did the advice happen, and are the names and topics real? The authors went to the named influencees to check. The next chapter evaluates this follow-up (p. 148).

> **Intuition note:** Asking people about their own recent advice-giving is cheap and finds leaders inside the sample. Its value depends on whether the people they name confirm the stories.

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Apply the index of pp. 147–148 to four women, for one arena.

| Woman | Asked for advice in June? | In August? | "More likely than friends"? | Leader? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | yes | yes | no | yes (rule 1: twice) |
| B | yes | no | yes | yes (rule 2: once + "more likely") |
| C | no | yes | no | no (once, no "more likely") |
| D | no | no | yes | no (never asked) |

Now take 10 hypothetical general influentials and 10 everyday contacts named by one woman. Say 5 of the 10 influentials and 2 of the 10 contacts are non-family. The non-family shares are 50% and 20%. The pattern points the same way as Table 1: esteemed advisers are more often outside the family, and daily talk partners are more often kin.

- What of the roughly 58% of opinion changes with no remembered personal contact, which often depended on media (p. 142)?
