# Introduction (Personal Influence)

## L0 — One line
The authors argue that the person who reads and talks about media content must be studied as both a communicator and a relay point in mass communication, and they explain how the Decatur field study and the small-group review fit that aim.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Mass communication research asks "Who says what to whom and with what effect?" The authors say the person who reads and talks about content is not simply like a newspaper; he needs to be studied as a communicator and a relay point. The book has two parts: a review of social-psychology small-group studies, and the main findings of a 1945 survey of women in Decatur, Illinois. The survey looked for people who influence others' decisions, and compared their influence with that of the media. Earlier work suggested a two-step flow and "horizontal" opinion leadership. The authors ask whether these findings can be corroborated. They also say the individual must be studied inside his primary group.

**Prerequisites:** the Lasswell formula; the idea of surveys and samples; basic sociometry (asking group members whom they choose).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The organizing formula of who says what to whom with what effect gained a larger meaning: the individual who reads and talks about content cannot be taken simply as a simile for newspapers or magazines (p. 1).
- The study design merged two ideas: sociometric choice and reports of actual decisions. A person counts as influential by the number of people whose decisions he affected in some concrete way (p. 2).
- The 1940 Ohio voting study suggested that media effects were small compared with personal influences, and suggested "horizontal" opinion leadership and a two-step flow. The authors then raise the question whether these findings could be corroborated in a study centred on personal influence (pp. 3–4).
- The Decatur design sampled women and traced influences in four areas: marketing, fashion, movies, local public affairs. It also identified likely advisers by an inverse procedure (p. 4).
- Five problems guide the study. The crucial one is "impact analysis": comparing personal and media influences in particular decisions (pp. 4–6).
- Horizontal leadership is established in many areas, but like does not always influence like. Vertical influence also matters, and the findings "open up" the topic rather than settle it (p. 6).
- Small-group research shows that opinions are often formed and reinforced in primary groups. The authors integrate it with survey research on media and personal influence (pp. 7–9).
- The Decatur study gave supporting data for the idea that persons are another medium, but that idea proved "too narrow": the individual must be studied in the setting of the primary group. The link to broad social analyses is left for future work (pp. 11–12).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Purpose and place in the series (pp. 1–2)
- Mass communication research already has a structure, often expressed as "Who says what to whom and with what effect?" The series covers content analysis, audience research, the communications industry and effects (p. 1).
- When Lasswell stated the formula, a radio station or newspaper could be likened to a person communicating messages. The authors say renewed interest in person-to-person communication has changed this. The person who reads something and talks about it with others is not just a simile for such social entities. He must be studied as a communicator and as a relay point in the network of mass communications (p. 1).
- Because the investigation of personal influence is quite new, it has not yet been possible to develop a unified approach. The book therefore has two parts: (I) organized social-psychology studies, and (II) main findings of the Decatur, Illinois investigation (p. 1).

*Intuition note:* Earlier thinking compared a medium to one speaker. The authors say real readers are not endpoints. They pass content on.

### History of the field study (pp. 2–4)
- **Origin.** Just before the last war, executives of a national news magazine knew that bankers, lawyers and industrialists read it. They asked whether these readers were actually influential, by example or advice (p. 2).
- **Proposed design (by one of the authors).** Ask a sample in a moderate-sized community who had influenced them in several areas. Then ask the most-named people which magazines they read (p. 2).
- **Two merged ideas.** (1) Sociometry: community members report their interrelations. The sociometrist asks whom people would like to talk or work with, and so finds isolated and sought-after individuals. (2) The choice would not be abstract. It would be the report of a choice already made, from actual decisions and the influences experienced in them. Whether someone counted as influential was to be determined by the number of people whose decisions he had affected in some concrete manner (p. 2).
- **Trial study (footnote 1, directed by Merton).** Individuals named as influential in public affairs were classed as "local" or "cosmopolitan". The "cosmopolitan" leaders were, in disproportionately large numbers, subscribers to national news magazines (p. 2).
- **Parallel finding: "molecular leaders."** These are people influential in their immediate environment but not necessarily prominent in the whole community. Their importance was discovered almost accidentally in the 1940 presidential campaign study in an Ohio community (p. 3).
  - That study found the effect of mass media small compared with personal influences. Voters ended up conforming closely to the political climate of their social environment. Advice from people met in daily life strongly influenced their decisions (p. 3).
- **Macfadden interest.** The research director of Macfadden Publications saw implications for publications aimed at wage earners. The earlier assumption was that opinions formed in the elite and percolated down stratum by stratum (p. 3).
  - The 1940 study "seemed to indicate" parallel *horizontal opinion leadership*: each stratum generates its own opinion leaders. It also "seemed to indicate" a *two-step flow*. In each stratum these leaders were more likely to expose themselves to media tuned to their education and interest; this seemed to suggest that media influences reach opinion leaders, who in turn pass them on (pp. 3–4).
  - The obvious question was whether this could be corroborated in a study centered on personal influence (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* The idea is a shift from an elite-to-masses picture to a picture of media reaching local leaders, who then reach people near them. The authors report it as something one study seemed to indicate, and ask whether it can be corroborated.

### The Decatur design (p. 4)
- **Sample.** A cross-section of women, from whom influential persons were then identified (p. 4).
- **Four areas.** Daily household marketing; fashion (dresses, cosmetics, beauty treatments); movie attendance; opinions on local public affairs under discussion at the time (p. 4).
- **Procedure.** Each respondent was asked whether she had recently changed or decided something in these areas. If so, she was asked many questions about which media and which individuals influenced her. By an inverse procedure the study also identified people who, by several criteria, seemed more likely to be sought out for advice by others near them (p. 4).
- **Locale.** Census data were collected for cities of about 60,000. A small number were most typical on average characteristics. With further considerations, Decatur was the "obvious choice" (selection reported in an appendix) (p. 4).

### The five problems (pp. 4–6)
1. **Concept.** How personal influence, as implicitly defined by the procedure, relates to prestige, persuasion and similar concepts. Merton's study analysed many concepts; Decatur gave comparative data for some (pp. 4–5).
2. **Sociometry in a city.** Mutual-choice methods had been largely confined to small groups such as school classes, where chooser and chosen belong to the same group. In a city of 60,000 only a small part can be questioned. Those named as influential were most likely to fall outside the sample, so they had to be located in separate follow-up interviews. Influentials inside the sample were found by self-designation. The different procedures had to be checked against each other (p. 5).
3. **Who the opinion leaders are.** Their social and demographic traits, interest in the area, and place in the mass-communication network. Also how they relate to those they influence: older or younger, richer or poorer, family, friends, neighbours or co-workers (p. 5).
4. **Impact analysis (the "crucial issue").** How influences from other people compare with media influences in decisions. Can external factors such as advertising or advice be traced into a particular respondent's decision under proper interviewing conditions? This book offers the first detailed analysis of the procedures (p. 5).
5. **Chains of influence.** The authors hoped to interview advisers in more than two steps. Budget limits allowed only token data, which point to a promising area for research (p. 6).

### What the findings imply (p. 6)
- Horizontal opinion leadership is "clearly established" in many crucial areas of daily life. This does not mean that like always influences like (p. 6).
- Vertical leadership plays a considerable role in some areas and and is almost nowhere entirely absent (p. 6). Examples:
  - Movies: young people influence older people.
  - Small consumer goods: the older housewife influences the younger.
  - Presidential vote, where major social allegiances are involved: decided within a stratum, rather independently of choices by people much higher or lower.
  - Local affairs, where party tradition doesn't matter and specific information is needed: the wage earner is more likely to take advice from a better-educated white-collar person.
- Horizontal leadership is most clear-cut for small consumer purchases, where influence rarely crosses strata (p. 6).
- The authors say these findings do not settle the question but open up a complex topic (p. 6).
- **Dignified vs undignified topics.** Many social scientists treat dating as dignified and advertising effects on purchases as undignified. The authors hope their results will show that consumer-action analysis goes beyond commercial implications into general human behaviour (pp. 6–7).

### History and the turn to small groups (pp. 7–9)
- Preparation began in fall 1944 and field work in spring 1945. Analysis was slow because of postwar university workloads and staff changes (p. 7).
- Small-group study had almost become an independent discipline. For the authors, a person seen in isolation or as an element of a statistical aggregate leaves something out. Media influence is "refracted" by the personal environment, and whether one person influences another depends also on how both are embedded in circles of friends, relatives and co-workers. They did not start with a theory of "group dynamics" (pp. 7–8).
- They also noted that small-group authors paid little attention to the mass-media literature, or to how experiments relate to field surveys and to interview-based tracing of influence. The authors therefore saw a systematic integration as a "natural and necessary complement" (p. 8).
- One of the authors spent the better part of two years on Part I. It opens with a historical review showing that the discovery of the small group came simultaneously in several areas of research. Findings are organized as influence within the group and factors impinging from outside (p. 8).
- **Within the group:**
  - (a) Seemingly private opinions are often generated and/or reinforced in small intimate groups. Opinions are more stable if shared, and under pressure of a campaign people are more likely to change opinions jointly than individually (p. 8).
  - (b) Families, friendships and work-groups are communication networks through which influences flow in patterned ways. The leader is strategic: he is more aware of what members think, mediates between them, and represents something like the "typical" group-mind (pp. 8–9).
- **To the group:** how groups keep in touch with their environments. Small-group research contributes only a little here, since much of it is laboratory work that deliberately removes the natural environment (p. 9).
  - Other fields can help. Face-to-face contact probably matters more in underdeveloped areas, where modern media are arriving. There is also anthropological material, and domestic cases such as agricultural agents and fellow farmers who interpret radio and government pamphlets for farmers (p. 9).

### What was left out (pp. 9–11)
- **A contemplated third section.** Influence is co-determined by the broad institutional setting. It would have covered pressure groups, advertising, public relations, and agencies such as the beauty parlor, disc jockey and department store. These affect styles of life derivatively although they are meant to sell goods (pp. 9–10).
- **Riesman's view.** Some theorists, such as David Riesman, are inclined to feel that modern industrial society's intellectual climate is permeated by the "advertising spirit" (p. 10).
- Interviews with beauty parlor operators, advice columnists, lawyers and others were too sketchy for a coherent presentation and were omitted (p. 10).
- **Other omissions.** Panel-technique analysis (respondents interviewed twice) was dropped because much more extensive panel data are now available. Detailed reading and listening answers were judged likely to be out of date (p. 10).
- **Deliberate inclusion.** Statistically weak data on two-partner conversations (adviser and advisee) were kept. The number of cases is small, but the information was new and, to the authors' knowledge, no later study has provided it. A technical appendix discusses shortcomings and promises (p. 11).

### Summary of the book's place and a limit of the design (pp. 11–12)
- The study aimed to extend traditional communications research by considering the role of people. The guiding idea was that persons, especially opinion leaders, can be seen as another mass communication medium like magazines, newspapers and radio. One could study their "coverage," effect and, in a way, "content" (p. 11).
  - Content means conversations. Later 1948 campaign studies collected statistical data on them (footnote 8, p. 11).
- Decatur gave supporting data for this view, but the view "turned out to be too narrow." The individual, as generator or object of influence, must be studied within his primary group (pp. 11–12).
- Small-group studies could be reorganized and reinterpreted along these broader lines; the book presents this secondary analysis. Relating both parts to broad social analyses is left to future work (p. 12).

*Intuition note:* The authors first treated opinion leaders as "another medium." They end by saying that the individual, as generator or object of influence, must be studied within the primary group in which he lives.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the design on pp. 2 and 5: count how many respondents' decisions a person affected, then check what that person reads.

Eight respondents each name the person (if any) who influenced a recent decision:
- R1→P, R2→P, R3→P, R6→P; R4→Q, R7→Q; R5 and R8 name nobody (media only).

Counts: P = 4, Q = 2, nobody = 2. So 6 of 8 report personal influence (4 + 2 = 6; 6/8 = 75%).

By the rule on p. 2, P is the more influential: 4 > 2. If P and Q lie outside the sample, they would need follow-up interviews (p. 5). Suppose P reads magazine M and Q does not. This would be a two-step pattern (media → P → four others) for these respondents. It shows the logic only, not a result.

## Concepts
- [[lasswell-formula]] — the organizing formula of who says what to whom with what effect; the authors say it gained a larger meaning once the receiver is seen as a communicator (p. 1)
- [[opinion-leader]] — a person influential for others in their immediate environment, identified in Decatur by self-designation and by follow-up on those named (pp. 3, 5)
- [[two-step-flow]] — media influence reaches opinion leaders, who pass it to others; reported as something the 1940 study "seemed" to show (pp. 3–4)
- [[horizontal-opinion-leadership]] (new) — each social stratum produces its own opinion leaders, in contrast to elite-to-mass percolation (p. 3)
- [[molecular-leaders]] (new) — early name for people influential in their immediate environments but not necessarily prominent community-wide (p. 3)
- [[impact-analysis]] (new) — tracing external factors (advertising, advice) into a respondent's particular decision through interviewing (p. 5)
- [[sociometric-choice]] (new) — members of a group report whom they choose; here, choices already made in actual decisions (p. 2)
- [[personal-influence]] (new) — influence defined by the number of people whose decisions someone affected concretely (p. 2)
- [[primary-group-setting]] (new) — families, friends and co-workers within which individuals must be studied (pp. 8, 12)

## Canonical instances
- [[decatur-study]] — 1945 field study of women in Decatur, Illinois, on four decision areas (pp. 4, 7)
- [[1940-ohio-voting-study]] — the Ohio community election study whose findings indicated small media effects relative to personal influence (p. 3)
- [[merton-local-cosmopolitan-study]] — trial study; "cosmopolitan" leaders were disproportionately national news magazine subscribers (p. 2)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Horizontal opinion leadership (like influences like within a stratum) ↔ homophily/heterophily: similarity of communicating persons affects who influences whom — the authors do not use the term here.
- [structural-analogy] Persons as relay points between media and others ↔ network analysis: nodes that pass on information — the text only says "network of mass communications."
- [metaphor] Influence "refracted" by the personal environment ↔ optics: the authors use this image (p. 7) for media effects altered by group membership.

## Questions this raises
- Which conditions decide whether influence is horizontal or vertical (the examples on p. 6 differ in need for information and party tradition)?
- How reliable is impact analysis, where respondents themselves report what influenced them?
- If a respondent's name is gained by self-designation and by being named by others, do both methods find the same leaders (p. 5)?
- How would influence chains longer than two steps look (p. 6)?
