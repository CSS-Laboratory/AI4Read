# On Describing the Flow of Influence

## L0 — One line
To describe who leads and who follows in informal networks that lack labelled roles, the authors build three indices (life-cycle position, social-economic status, gregariousness, i.e. extent of social contacts) and first test how these relate to one another.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Factories and hospitals have labelled roles, so influence there could probably be described by role. Informal neighbourhood and friendship groups mostly do not. The authors therefore sort the women in their sample on three dimensions: life-cycle position, status, and gregariousness (extent of social contacts). They then ask whether opinion leaders concentrate in particular positions and whether influence flows vertically (down the status ladder) or horizontally (among equals). Before using the indices on leadership, the chapter checks how they relate. Life-cycle and status are nearly independent. Status and gregariousness are clearly related. One life-cycle–gregariousness relationship only becomes clear once status is controlled (held fixed). The chapter sets up the profiles of leaders in the following chapters.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and medians; reading cross-tabulations; the idea of "controlling" one variable while comparing another.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Informal community networks mostly lack convenient role labels (family members aside), so the flow of influence needs other descriptive frameworks (p. 220).
- Three dimensions are chosen: life-cycle, social-economic status, and gregariousness (pp. 220–221).
- A horizontal-flow hypothesis is set alongside the traditional vertical-flow one; two kinds of evidence will be examined (p. 222).
- Gregariousness is expected to matter because leaders need access to people. Free time depends on life-cycle and status, so comparisons must be made within groups (p. 223).
- The status index is deliberately crude and the gregariousness index simple. Life-cycle gives four types, status gives three levels, and gregariousness gives three levels (pp. 224–228).
- Status and life-cycle are nearly unrelated (p. 229).
- Higher status goes with higher gregariousness (p. 229). Among life-cycle types only matrons (married women over 45) clearly stand out as highly gregarious (p. 230).
- Controlling status reveals a life-cycle pattern: low gregariousness is far more common among lower-status girls, and upper-status women drop in gregariousness on marrying (pp. 231–232).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pp. 219–220).** Earlier sections dealt with kinds of influence and influentials in four arenas: marketing, fashions, public affairs and movie-going (p. 219). They also compared personal influence with mass-media influence (p. 219). Section Three concentrates on the transmitters, the "opinion leaders". Mostly these are "self-detected": women who said they were recently asked for advice on a specific matter (pp. 219–220). In formal settings, such as a factory or hospital, labelled statuses might map the flow. Examples are union officers, skilled workers for unskilled workers, or specialists to general practitioners (p. 220). The authors' setting is a "community" of small informal units: families, friendship groups, neighbours. Apart from family members, these cannot be easily differentiated (p. 220). The question is whether influentials look different from the people they influence (p. 220).

**Three relevant dimensions (pp. 220–223).**
- *(1) Life-cycle:* age, marriage, and number and age of children. The authors expect these phases to relate to interests and to rates of opinion leadership on different subjects (p. 221). They will check whether leaders cluster in some phase and whether there is a patterned direction of flow (p. 221). Examples are older to younger women, or larger-family to small-family wives (p. 221).
- *(2) Status:* high status has traditionally been seen as the almost exclusive determinant of influence. This is the vertical-flow idea: influence moves down by "power" or by emulation (p. 221). Even if influence goes down, it need not reach each rung personally. The authors call it at least as plausible that, if influence does proceed downward, it is transmitted across status lines only at a few "status ambassadors" while most acts occur among equals (p. 222). Footnote 2 adds that it is possible influence stays within levels, and probable that it also flows upward, as with jazz (p. 222). The authors make room for a horizontal hypothesis beside the vertical one (p. 222). They name two kinds of evidence:
  - *Distribution of leaders by status.* An even spread suggests mainly horizontal flow. A disproportionate concentration in one group suggests influence may flow from that group to the others (p. 222).
  - *Influential–influencee pairs.* These exist for part of the sample and are "suggestive" only. If the influential is typically of higher status, the authors will conclude that flow is predominantly vertical. If influence moves more often between equals, they will conclude that horizontal "more nearly fits the facts" (pp. 222–223).
- *(3) Gregariousness:* the extent of contact with others. Opinion leaders "must", in the authors' view, have access to people (p. 223). Free time partly determines gregariousness and is a function of things such as life-cycle (unmarried girl vs. mother of three young children) and on status (rich woman with household help vs. poor woman). So only women of similar life-cycle or status position will be compared (p. 223).

**Building the indices (pp. 223–228).**

*Life-cycle types (pp. 223–225).*
- "Girls": single women, kept to those under 35. Older single women are usually outside the marriage market and would make the type less homogeneous. The sample has 101 single women, 80% of them under 35 (p. 224).
- Wives are split at age 45. Among young wives (under 45), 80% of children are under 15. Among older wives, 91% of children are over 15 (p. 224). The older wives are "matrons" (p. 224).
- Wives under 45 are split by family size. Two or more children is a large family. The cut-off is used because the average number of children for younger wives lies between one and two (p. 224). Childless wives could not be separated because they are few: 55 of 350 (pp. 224–225).

Table 13 (p. 225), N = 693:

| Type | Share |
|---|---|
| Girls | 12% |
| Small family wives | 26% |
| Large family wives | 25% |
| Matrons | 37% |

The authors think these positions relate to interests and to the likelihood of leadership (p. 225). The family also has a status position that may relate to a woman's chances at leadership (p. 225).

*Status (p. 226).* The authors want only a crude measure: high, middle, low. They combine two factors, rent and education. Each is cut where about half the sample falls on each side. The cuts are a high-school diploma and $40 monthly rent (home owners estimated their rent; data from 1945, footnote 4). Both factors are treated as equally important:
- both high = high status;
- both low = low status;
- one high and one low = middle (p. 226).

The printed text gives 35% low, "87"% middle and 28% high. By arithmetic the middle figure is 37% (see Reading notes). Each group is about one third of the sample (p. 226). Footnote 3 says that, ideally, perhaps, social status should be considered apart from economic status, but the authors could not treat them separately (p. 226).

*Gregariousness (pp. 227–228).* The authors postulate that leaders are more likely to have a broader range of contacts. They call this "almost by definition", since leaders imply followers, and the postulate is to be tested (p. 227). The index uses two questions (p. 227):
1. How many friends, not neighbours, does she talk with fairly often?
2. Which organizations, clubs or discussion groups does she belong to?

Each is dichotomised. The median is seven friends, giving 43% "low-friend" and 57% "high-friend". Membership in one or more clubs defines "high" organizational activity: 41% high, 59% low (p. 227).

Table 14 (p. 228), friendships × organizations:

| | High friends | Low friends |
|---|---|---|
| High organizations | 27% | 14% |
| Low organizations | 31% | 28% |

High on both gives "high" gregariousness (27%) and low on both gives "low" (28%). The two mixed cells (14% + 31% = 45%) are pooled as "medium" (p. 228).

**Life-cycle and status as determinants of gregariousness (pp. 228–232).**

*Status × life-cycle (pp. 228–229).* The authors expect no relationship, since income and education cannot appreciably alter life-cycle phases. They find a slight exception at low status: fewer single women (8% vs. 13% and 14% at the other two levels) and slightly more matrons (41% vs. 34% and 37%). This probably reflects younger women getting more education, and education is part of the status index. The difference is "minute" (pp. 228–229).

*Status × gregariousness (p. 229).* Table 15 shows the relationship is clear-cut: the higher the status, the greater the social activity.

| Gregariousness | High status | Middle | Low status |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | 39% | 28% | 15% |
| Medium | 44 | 47 | 46 |
| Low | 17 | 25 | 39 |
| N | (196) | (253) | (243) |

The medium group is about the same everywhere; the variation lies in the extremes (p. 229). The table illustrates different opportunities and styles of life by status. If high status and high gregariousness are later both related to leadership, this link would supply one tie-up between status and leadership (p. 229).

*Intuition note.* Status hardly changes how many women are single or mothers. It does change how socially active they are. So if high-status women turn out to be leaders more often, part of that could pass through their greater social activity.

*Life-cycle × gregariousness (p. 230).* The authors presumed that family responsibilities tie women to the home. They therefore predicted this order from most to least gregarious: girls, matrons, small-family wives, large-family wives (pp. 229–230). Table 16 shows the expectations are "not quite fulfilled".

| Gregariousness | Girls | Small fam. | Large fam. | Matrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High | 21% | 19% | 23% | 34% |
| Medium | 56 | 48 | 48 | 40 |
| Low | 23 | 33 | 29 | 26 |
| N | (80) | (176) | (170) | (257) |

Only matrons are "significantly distinguished" by high gregariousness (34% vs. about 20% in each other group). Girls are the type most likely to score high on number of friendships, but they are not very often particularly active club members. This may reflect husband-seeking girls preferring informal contacts over formal, generally female, organizations. That is why girls cluster in the medium level (p. 230). Overall, variation across life-cycle types is not great (p. 230).

*Controlling status (pp. 231–232).* Chart V shows the life-cycle × gregariousness distribution separately for high and low status. Its bars are not legible in the text layer, and no page image was provided. Only the author's statements are used here (p. 231).
- Among upper-status girls, 6% have low gregariousness. Among lower-status girls, over 40% do. That is four in ten with fewer than the average number of friends and no organization. The authors presume that early obligations restrict her activity; perhaps she helps in the household or has to go to work (p. 231).
- Lower-status small-family wives score low twice as often as upper-status ones. The difference shrinks among large-family wives and matrons (pp. 231–232).
- Within the high-status level, small-family wives are four times as likely as girls to be low. The authors read this as a sharp break in social life when a well-off young woman marries. The low-status woman is ungregarious as a girl and stays so as a small-family wife. Later her gregariousness rises faster than her higher-status peer's (p. 232).

The four summary findings (p. 232):
1. On both status levels, high gregariousness increases with life-cycle progression.
2. Girls on both levels are least likely to score high and most likely to score medium.
3. Lower-status women of each type are more likely to score low than upper-status women. This is strikingly so among girls and quite marked among small-family wives.
4. Upper-status women join the low-gregariousness ranks in the transition from girlhood to marriage. The rate then stays constant from small-family wives onward. Lower-status women show no such swell.

*Intuition note.* Overall life-cycle figures did not show this relationship clearly. Splitting by status shows two different life paths, so "controlling" a variable can reveal a relationship that pooling does not show clearly.

**Closing (pp. 232–233).** The three factors probably matter for leadership through their power, alone and in combination, to determine a woman's interest in certain life-areas and to increase or reduce her opportunities to translate such interests into actual influentiality. The next chapters use them to profile leaders in the four arenas and to describe flow from leaders to followers (pp. 232–233).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Six women. Education: HS diploma or more = high. Rent: $40 or more = high. Friends: median split. Clubs: any membership = high.

| Woman | Friends | Clubs | Edu | Rent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 9 | 2 | high | high |
| B | 8 | 0 | high | low |
| C | 3 | 1 | low | low |
| D | 2 | 0 | low | low |
| E | 7 | 0 | low | high |
| F | 4 | 0 | high | high |

- **Friends median:** sorted 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9 gives (4 + 7)/2 = 5.5. "High-friend" means more than 5.5: A, B, E.
- **Gregariousness:**
  - A: high friends and clubs → High.
  - B: high friends, no club → Medium.
  - E: high friends, no club → Medium.
  - C: low friends, club → Medium.
  - D: low friends, no club → Low.
  - F: low friends, no club → Low.
  - Result: 1 high, 3 medium, 2 low.
- **Status:**
  - A: both high → High.
  - F: both high → High.
  - C: both low → Low.
  - D: both low → Low.
  - B: one high, one low → Middle.
  - E: one high, one low → Middle.
- **Reading by status:** both high-status women (A, F) have high or low gregariousness, none medium. Both low-status women (C, D) have medium or low. This mimics the chapter's logic of cross-tabulating the indices before using them. Six cases are far too few to show a real relationship.

## Concepts

- [[social-economic-status-index]] (new) — a crude high/middle/low index combining rent and education (p. 226)

## Questions this raises
- Does leadership concentrate in particular life-cycle positions, and does it differ by arena?
- Will leaders be found mainly among higher-status women, or spread evenly across levels, and how do the influential–influencee pairs compare?
- Does gregariousness predict leadership within a given life-cycle and status position?
- Would separating social status from economic status, as footnote 3 suggests, change the picture?
