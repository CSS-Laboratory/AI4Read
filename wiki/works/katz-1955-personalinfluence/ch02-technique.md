# A Technique of Confirmation

## L0 — One line
To check women's self-reports of giving or taking advice, the authors interviewed the named partners; the partners confirmed most reports, contradicted few, and confirmed least on public affairs.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Most data on face-to-face influence come from respondents talking about people who were not interviewed. Could these claims be trusted? The authors interviewed 634 of the people named and compared the two accounts. About two-thirds confirmed the conversation, about one in ten denied it, and the rest could not recall it. Of those who confirmed the conversation, most also acknowledged the alleged advice role. Confirmation was weakest for public affairs. The authors judge the results good enough to use the "self-detecting" question (asking people whether they gave advice) to find opinion leaders (specific people who gave others advice) in this exploratory study. They say the technique is only a beginning.

**Prerequisites:** reading percentage tables; the idea of validating self-report data against a second source.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The problem: influence data are respondents' claims about third parties, which may be inflated, so they need independent confirmation (p. 149).
- The method: interview the named partner ("designatee") and compare accounts. Both the conversation and the direction of advice must be checked (pp. 149–151).
- Contact confirmation: about two-thirds confirmed the conversation, 9–10% denied it, and the rest could not recall it. Influentials (partners who gave advice) and influencees (partners who took advice) differed little (pp. 153–154).
- Role acknowledgment: among confirmed cases, 77% of designatees accepted the alleged role and 8% reversed it. Influencees reversed it more often than influentials (pp. 156–157).
- Strict versus lenient standard for influencees: 54% were fully confirmed, but only 25% were explicitly contradicted (p. 158).
- Public affairs gave the weakest confirmation, marketing and fashions more (pp. 159–160).
- Conclusion: the technique is only a beginning, but the authors believe the results allow them, in this exploratory study, to use it to identify opinion leaders (pp. 157, 160).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: why confirm (p. 149)
Most information comes from respondents describing people who were not interviewed. Some respondents might, deliberately or not, claim more advice-giving than occurred. They might also cite conversations with prestigious people that never happened. The authors therefore needed confirmation (p. 149).

### The Follow-up Interview (pp. 149–152)
**Terms.** A *designation* is a reference by a sampled woman (the *designator*) to a person (the *designatee*) with whom she exchanged advice. A designatee who gave advice is an *influential*. One who took advice is an *influencee* (p. 149). Whether a designation can be accepted depends on whether designator and designatee agree about what took place. The authors want to see if the designatee agrees with its essential details (pp. 149–150).

**Two contexts (pp. 150–151).**
- *Influentials* were found while probing why a respondent changed opinion or behaviour. She was asked whether she had heard someone "talk about" the topic (e.g. a new lipstick shade). The designatee must confirm that the conversation happened and that she gave advice.
- *Influencees* came from asking whether the respondent had recently been asked for advice. The designatee must confirm the contact, its content and its direction.

**Fieldwork.** Requests for names were placed in ordinary contexts in the questionnaire, with no special emphasis. Few refusals occurred (p. 151). There were 1,549 designations, but only 634 designatees were reached and interviewed (p. 151). Distance, absence, refusals and other problems blocked the rest (p. 151). The authors call these "follow-up interviews" because they follow the links of influence networks from starting points in the sample (p. 152).

**Procedure.**
- Interviewers of influentials knew the respondent's specific change and her relationship to the designatee. They also knew the respondent's claim that the talk affected her (p. 152).
- Interviewers of influencees knew less: that the designatee allegedly came to the respondent for advice, and the general subject of the conversation (p. 152).
- Only the best interviewers did this work. They never questioned point-blank, and the designator's name was to come up conversationally (p. 152).
- The follow-up first gave a modified version of the basic questionnaire. Then it introduced the designator's name casually. If the two women knew each other, it moved to the topic, who initiated, and who asked advice of whom (p. 152).

### Confirmation of Alleged Contacts (pp. 153–154)
All but three of the 634 knew the designator. The authors suppose these three were their own mistakes, such as wrong addresses (p. 153). Designatees were asked whether they recalled talking with the designator about the topic. In about two-thirds of cases both contact and conversation were confirmed (p. 153). A "complete denial" meant either naming a different topic or saying they never talked about such things. The text says only 9% of the non-confirmed third were complete denials (p. 153); Table 2 gives 9–10% denied out of all cases (p. 154). In about 24% the designatee could not recall. A characteristic of these answers was a general insistence that the talk might well have occurred. The authors suggest the conversation may have mattered more to the designator, so it merged into everyday talk for the designatee (p. 153).

Table 2 (p. 154):

| | Influential (n = 337) | Influencee (n = 297) |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmed | 69% | 64% |
| Denied | 9 | 10 |
| Could not recall | 22 | 26 |

There is no significant difference between the two groups. Influentials show a slight tendency to confirm more (p. 154). The authors say there are no set standards and no comparable studies. They note that direct confirmation occurred in about two of three cases, while complete refutation occurred in only about one of ten (p. 154). The "can't recall" zone is largest for public affairs (35%), intermediate for fashion (22%) and lowest for marketing (18%). Denials stay fairly constant across areas (p. 154).

*Intuition note.* A "can't recall" is neither a yes nor a no. The authors call this group a "twilight zone" and point out that complete refutation was found in only about one case in ten.

### Acknowledgment of Alleged Roles (pp. 154–157)
Interviewers had latitude to depart from the questionnaire. Answers to single questions therefore could not be coded, so each whole interview was classified (p. 155). Only cases where both parties agreed on the conversation and topic were classified (p. 155).
- **I Role Acknowledged:** the designatee's role matches the alleged one.
- **II Role Reversed:** the designatee says her role was the exact opposite.
- **III Mutual Role:** neither took the initiative, and the two were peers.
- **IV Not Ascertainable:** a residual category for interviews too meagre or confused.

Table 3 (confirmed cases, n = 442): Acknowledged 77%, Reversed 8%, Mutual 14%, Not ascertainable 1% (p. 156). The authors think 77% is "quite high": agreement in eight of ten cases, clear disagreement in under one of ten (p. 156).

Table 4 (confirmed cases, p. 156):

| | Influentials (n = 232) | Influencees (n = 144) |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledged | 86% | 82% |
| Reversed | 3 | 11 |
| Mutual | 10 | 6 |
| Not ascertainable | 1 | 1 |

Acknowledgment differs little between the two groups. Reversal (3% vs 11%) and mutual role (10% vs 6%) differ more, and the authors call these differences statistically significant (p. 157). They suggest that designators cannot always tell whether a partner seriously seeks advice or is "making conversation" (p. 157). Examples from influencees (p. 157):
- One asked about a lipstick brand out of curiosity, with no intention of using it.
- One public-affairs "influencee" used advice-seeking as a screen. She said she starts by asking what people think and then, "after they get interested I deliver my message". She called this the "boob technique".

The authors say they are "only at the beginning" of a technique. The high rate of clear confirmation leads them to believe they have "at least opened up the problem" (p. 157).

*Intuition note.* The same conversation can be read as advice-giving by one woman and as chat by the other. The influencee's own view of her role is a check on what the designator says.

### The Extent of Confirmation (pp. 157–160)
Reliability depends on whether designated influencees admit both the talk and the advice-taking (p. 157). Table 5 (all interviewed influencees, n = 221, p. 158):
- Role acknowledged 54%, reversed 7%, mutual 4%, not ascertainable under 1%. Total contacts confirmed 65%.
- Contact denied 14%, can't recall 21%. Total contacts not confirmed 35%.

Strict standard: 54% seem to be confirmed, meaning everything alleged is explicitly substantiated (p. 158). Contradiction standard: denials (14%) + reversed (7%) + mutual (4%) = 25%, one-fourth of cases. The technique looks better on this second standard than on the first. The authors say it depends on how strict the standard should be (pp. 158–159).

**By arena (p. 159).** Confirmed conversations and role acknowledgments fall from marketing to fashions to politics. Marketing and fashions differ little. Both differ clearly from public affairs.

Table 6, influencees (n = 69, 120, 32), marketing / fashions / public affairs:
- Role acknowledged: 57 / 56 / 38%.
- Total contacts confirmed: 70 / 67 / 50%.
- Contact denied: 13 / 12 / 25%.

Table 7, influentials (n = 107, 162, 68):
- Role acknowledged: 71 / 61 / 37%.
- Total contacts confirmed: 76 / 69 / 59%.
- Contact denied: 7 / 9 / 7%.
- Mutual role: 3 / 5 / 19%.

The authors say public affairs designations are apparently the least valid (p. 159). Their possible explanations (p. 160):
1. Prosaic, routine relationships may give more valid answers.
2. In public affairs men are important sources. They may be unwilling to admit influence from a woman, or may not count such talk as "discussion". Footnote 3 reports that in a presidential-campaign voting study, wives often mentioned discussions with husbands but husbands rarely returned the compliment (Lazarsfeld, Berelson and Gaudet 1948, p. 141) (p. 160).
3. Politics is touchier to ask about. The authors say this is "probably more important" than either of the first two.
4. A marketing change may follow a single, easily remembered contact. A political change may not.

The authors say these variations need much more study. They conclude that the confirmation allows them, in this exploratory study, to use the self-detecting question to isolate "opinion leaders" for description and analysis (p. 160).

### Conclusion (pp. 160–161)
Specific influence incidents are called "decisions", and specific influentials are "opinion leaders". Their job is personal contact or personal influence (pp. 160–161). The next section treats decisions and the role of personal influence in them. The final section describes what opinion leaders look like and how they differ from those they lead (p. 161).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Twenty designations of influencees are followed up. Outcomes: 11 role acknowledged, 2 reversed, 1 mutual, 2 contact denied, 4 can't recall (11 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 4 = 20).
- Contacts confirmed: 11 + 2 + 1 = 14, so 14/20 = 70%.
- Strict standard (everything substantiated): 11/20 = 55%.
- Contradiction standard: 2 + 1 + 2 = 5, so 5/20 = 25%.

The same data look weak (55%) or fairly strong (only 25% contradicted), depending on the standard. This is the point of Table 5.

## Concepts
- [[designation-technique]] (new) — checking a respondent's claim of giving or taking advice by interviewing the named partner (pp. 149–150)
- [[designator-designatee]] (new) — the sampled woman who names a partner, and the partner named (p. 149)
- [[influential-influencee]] (new) — a designatee who gave advice vs. one who took it (p. 149)

- [[self-detecting-question]] (new) — asking people whether they gave advice, to find opinion leaders (p. 160)

## Questions this raises
- Does "can't recall" hide real influence that matters more to the influencee than the interview showed?
- Do the confirmation rates differ because of topic, because of gender (men as sources), or because of both?
- Would a different design (e.g. asking all members of a group) detect influence that this design misses?
- Are the influence incidents recalled in an interview a biased subset of all influence?
