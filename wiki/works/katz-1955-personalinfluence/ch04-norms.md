# Norms and Small Groups: The Shared Character of Opinions and Attitudes

## L0 — One line
Drawing on small-group research, the authors argue that people's seemingly private opinions are often maintained, sometimes generated and sometimes merely enforced together with the small groups around them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether research on small groups can help explain the role of people in the flow of mass media influence. The authors say the evidence is not yet very abundant but is persuasive, and they admit that proving group influence is hard. They give five reasons why opinions and attitudes are often anchored in primary groups: conformity brings benefits; groups supply a "social reality"; interaction creates shared norms; people with like values seek each other out; and groups demand uniformity from their members. They close with warnings about their own oversimplifications. The chapter points ahead to Chapter V on interpersonal networks.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an attitude survey; sociometric questions (asking people to name their closest friends); the difference between influence and self-selection into a group.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The working hypothesis: primary groups actively influence and support most of an individual's opinions, attitudes and actions. The authors call the evidence not yet abundant but persuasive (p. 48).
- Proving group influence is hard. One must rule out that people in similar situations respond independently to the same outside stimuli (p. 49).
- Instrumental reason: acceptance, status and leadership go to those who share the group's opinions. Members who deviate too far lose status and may even lose membership. This need not be rationally calculated (pp. 50–53).
- "Social reality": the Westgate authors hypothesise that, where facts cannot usually settle a question, an opinion rests on the belief that others share it. Sherif's experiment shows private standards converging into a group norm (pp. 54–55).
- Norms arise from interaction. In the Westgate housing project, proximity was closely related to friendship and group formation, and courts differed in their shared attitudes (pp. 57–59).
- Shared values may also attract people before interaction ("value homophily"). The Bard College data point that way but do not settle the issue (pp. 59–61).
- Groups demand conformity to protect shared views, boundaries and collective action. The authors stress "automatic" controls, not coercion (p. 62, fn. 25).
- Caveats: "group" is often shorthand for significant "others", and the authors admit several oversimplifications. Reference-group theory and small-group theory are not yet integrated (p. 64). Their closing claim: individual opinion is "not strictly an individual affair" (p. 65).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: focus and hypothesis (pp. 48–49)
- **Primary group** (Cooley's sense): small, durable, informal, face-to-face, with a manifold (unspecialized) purpose. It includes families, friends and informal work teams. More formal groupings such as clubs and organizations are included where members are likely to form **sociometric connections**, meaning mutual attraction as personalities. The authors use these terms interchangeably: primary groups, small groups, intimate social ties, interpersonal relations, and sometimes just others, without trying to be precise too early (p. 48).
- **Hypothesis:** such groups actively influence and support most of an individual's opinions, attitudes and actions (p. 48).
- Support cited:
  - Family members tend, except under certain conditions, to share attitudes on politics and religion. The same holds for most friendships.
  - Reference-group studies suggest people often have particular groups "in mind" when reporting opinions (pp. 48–49).
- **Proof problem:** showing that interacting people influence each other means disproving that people in similar situations respond independently to the same stimuli. Example: Northern students in Southern universities grow more prejudiced each year, though never quite as prejudiced as the Southern students (Sims & Patrick, fn. 4). The authors of that study cannot assert that the Northerners adopt their classmates' attitudes, because they would also have to show (and their data do not permit it) that whatever caused prejudice in the Southerners is not also acting directly on the Northerners (p. 49).
- The authors still feel surer than usual, because case studies give reasons to expect the hypothesis to hold (pp. 49–50).

### The Instrumental Function: The Benefits of Conformity (pp. 50–53)
- **Bennington (Newcomb):** attitudes were recorded over four years.
  - Students who were positively oriented toward the college community, and who wanted acceptance or leadership, tended to take on the prevailing liberal attitudes. They did so despite strongly conservative family backgrounds.
  - A major factor in non-acceptance of the campus climate was strong positive identification with the family.
  - Newcomb: where certain attitudes are approved, individuals acquire them to the degree that the membership group serves as a positive point of reference (p. 50).
  - Authors' gloss: the more attractive the group and the more acceptance is desired, the more the person is motivated, aware or not, to accept its outlook (pp. 50–51).
- **The American Soldier:**
  - 45% of green (no combat experience) soldiers in all-green divisions showed "readiness for combat". Only 28% did among green replacements in veteran divisions. Veterans themselves: 15%.
  - Stouffer et al. say the difference may derive from the two social contexts. They suggest the new men were strongly influenced by the veterans' attitudes, and argue that the new men sought acceptance and adjusted their opinions (p. 51).
- **Conformity is exacted from long-time members too, not only newcomers.** Members who deviate too far lose status or may even lose membership (p. 51).
  - Westgate (Festinger, Schachter, Back): those who conformed least to neighbours' opinions on the item studied tended to be "underchosen" when naming three best friends (p. 51).
  - Club experiment (Schachter): extreme deviants, employed by the experimenter, were named most often as persons to drop (p. 52).
- **Leadership requires conformity (Merei):**
  - Children who showed leadership qualities were separated from the others. The rest formed twelve groups of 3–6, homogeneous in age and sex.
  - Within 3–6 meetings of 35–40 minutes, each group developed "traditions": seating, division of objects, game sequence, jargon.
  - In every case, when an old leader tried to assert authority against a new tradition, the group did not respond. Some never regained power. Others did, but only after fully identifying with and joining the new tradition (p. 52).
- **Conclusion:** these studies seem to indicate that, to attain or keep intimacy, or to "get somewhere" in or via a group, one must identify with the group's opinions. This need not be rationally calculated and may be unwitting. The consequences are the same (p. 53).
- Footnote 11 (Merton & Kitt): privates whose attitudes resembled those of non-commissioned officers more than other privates' did were more likely to be promoted later. Merton and Kitt, generalising, say a private bucking for promotion may be regarded as behaving differently from, for example, an assimilating immigrant or a Bennington student only in a narrow and theoretically superficial sense (fn. 11, p. 53).

*Intuition note:* In these studies, going along with a group's views brings acceptance, friendship and leadership, and going against them costs standing. So the authors expect opinion to be substantially affected by the company one keeps or aspires to keep, even if no one calculates it.

### Providing a Social Reality (pp. 53–56)
- Here the group is a provider of meanings for situations that do not explain themselves, not a source of benefits (p. 53).
- **Lewinian definition:** what is "reality" for an individual is to a high degree determined by what is socially accepted as reality. Their example: to South Sea Islanders the world may be flat, to Europeans round (Lewin & Grabbe, p. 54).
- This offers a supplementary explanation of the soldier data. The "reality" of combat may have differed for those in daily touch with veterans (p. 54).
- **Westgate hypothesis (quoted):**
  - The social reality behind an opinion is the degree to which the individual perceives the opinion as shared by others.
  - An opinion not reinforced by others becomes generally unstable.
  - There are usually no compelling facts that can settle which social opinion is right, unlike checking a claim about a dead-end street against physical reality (p. 54).
- Authors: stereotypes develop this way. Much of the world is inaccessible to direct observation, so people rely on each other to make sense of things (pp. 54–55).
- **Sherif's autokinetic experiment:**
  - A stationary pinpoint of light in a dark room seems to move. Each subject first judged alone how many inches it "moved", and developed a personal norm (a modal number of inches).
  - Then subjects judged in groups of two or three. They started from their old standards, but met others' dissenting judgments and gave way somewhat until a group standard emerged.
  - Reversed order (group first, then alone): individuals took the group standard away with them (p. 55).
- **Objection (a critic of laboratory work, as the authors imagine it):**
  - The situation was unstructured and so unreal, since nobody could know the light did not move.
  - It carried no emotional stake.
  - Subjects were forced to decide.
  - **Authors' reply:** suppose these objections limit generalisation, which the authors say they probably do. They then ask whether real-life situations resemble the experiment and answer yes. Presidential voting is one, for very large numbers of people: social pressures force a decision they would not otherwise make, between candidates about whom they may know nothing and may care not at all. For such people, informal groups may be expected to play a large part. For almost any complex issue, one should not overestimate the objective verifiability of a social situation (p. 56).

*Intuition note:* Where facts cannot settle a question, people tend to rely on what others around them believe. Sherif's lab shows this in miniature. The authors argue that voting is a real case of the same type, for very many people who may know nothing and may care not at all about the candidates.

### Interaction: The Process of Convergence (pp. 56–59)
- Sherif's study shows two ideas.
  1. People turn to others in unclear situations (the "social reality" function).
  2. People interacting about a shared problem develop a collective approach to it and create an opinion or decision that they then hold in common (p. 57).
- Authors' framing: benefits and social reality begin to answer *why* opinions are anchored in groups. Norm creation through interaction begins to answer *how* (p. 57).
- Newcomb (fn. 16) says norms represent shared ways of perceiving things, or more exactly shared frames of reference for perceiving them (p. 57).
- **Westgate in detail:**
  - A new housing community for married veteran students. Residents were in courts of several buildings. Apartments were assigned at random, and residents seem not to have known each other before.
  - The population was homogeneous: young couples of about the same age and status, husbands all WWII veterans and students (fn. 18).
  - In this homogeneous population, friendship ties were related directly to physical and functional proximity (the daily route passes someone's door). The largest share of friendships formed within the same court or building. The authors of the study say ecological factors determine group composition too (p. 58).
  - A sociometric question asked each resident to name the three people they saw most of socially. An attitude questionnaire on a new Tenants' Council followed.
  - In the majority of cases an individual's attitude matched the prevailing attitude of his court. Attitude varied from court to court, so the authors infer that shared opinion arose from mutual contact (p. 58).
  - Courts with greater cohesion (sociometric choices confined to fellow court members) showed greater uniformity of attitudes (p. 58).
  - Non-conformists often had friends and social life centred outside their court, or outside the community altogether (p. 59).
- **Conclusion:** randomly placed residents quickly formed friendship groups, and these adopted shared ways of thinking. This is real-life evidence that ostensibly private opinions are often generated and maintained in interaction with small groups (p. 59).

*Intuition note:* Residents did not choose their neighbours: apartments were assigned at random and they seem not to have known each other. Attitudes to the Tenants' Council usually matched the court and varied between courts, so the Westgate authors conclude that shared opinion arose out of contact.

### The Attraction of Shared Values (pp. 59–61)
- **Value homophily** (Merton, forthcoming at the time): mutual attraction based on shared values. It is hard to study because one must show that values come before interaction, not after (pp. 59–60).
- Footnote 20 contrasts "status homophily" (similar class, religion, nationality), which the authors do not treat here (p. 59).
- **Precker's Bard College study** (which the authors say does not quite solve the problem, p. 60):
  - It was a by-product of another assignment. 242 students and 42 faculty offered about 1,300 criteria for evaluating a student's educational achievement. Three judges condensed them into 39 categories, which everyone then ranked.
  - Each student also named the three students he would most like to keep in touch with after graduation and the faculty member he would most like as adviser.
  - A rank-order correlation compared each chooser's rankings with those of the persons chosen.
  - Result: students tended to choose associates whose values resembled their own. Similarity was greatest where friendship choices were mutual rather than unilateral (pp. 60–61).
- **Interpretation (two readings):**
  - (a) Interaction leads to friendship, which leads to shared values and a wish to continue the friendship. Precker's own data would support this (fn. 22: seniors' rankings matched their actual advisers' more than freshmen's did).
  - (b) Reading (a) cannot explain why those whose choices were not reciprocated chose similar people. So a seeking-out process based on value homophily does seem at work, and in all probability was at work in the initial formation of the mutual friendships (p. 61).
- The conditions under which shared values operate, compared with propinquity or interdependence, would seem, the authors say, to warrant intensive research. They add that this is not an either-or situation (p. 61, fn. 23).

### Interdependent Individuals Demand Conformity of Each Other (pp. 62–63)
- This is the obverse of the instrumental and social-reality reasons: groups require conformity (p. 62).
- Three reasons:
  1. People are discomforted when associates depart from a traditional way of "seeing" (example: disbelief in witches in a Puritan witch-hunting community).
  2. Groups preserve identity, and uniform behaviour makes boundaries clear.
  3. Groups have goals, and goals often cannot be reached without consensus. Uniformity may be a prerequisite for group action. Festinger: pressures toward uniformity may arise because uniformity is desirable or necessary for the group to move toward some goal (Festinger calls this "group locomotion", fn. 24, p. 62).
- Footnote 25: this presupposes that the individual wants to keep the association. The authors are concerned with "automatic" controls, not coercion or special enforcement. People stay within norms to keep the benefits of conformity and to protect their mental security from "seeing" what others do not (pp. 62–63).
- **Summary of the chapter's reasons** (p. 63): benefits of conformity; groups as providers of standards and meanings; norm formation through interaction; value-based attraction preceding interaction; group insistence on uniformity. The authors say they are convinced, primarily from small-group research, that studying the small groups to which an individual is attached is a major key to understanding individual opinions and actions (p. 63).

### Some Hasty Qualifications (pp. 64–65)
- The authors admit oversimplifying (p. 64). They sometimes talk as if:
  - people belong to only one group;
  - only membership groups matter (though reference groups are a primary research focus);
  - as if they had established beyond doubt that standards are taken only from small groups of personally known others and never from people not personally known or from mass media;
  - no other structure or control exists besides informal groups, interpersonal influence and ostracism;
  - all standards originate in the small groups where they are "anchored" (maintained and enforced).
- "Group" is shorthand for the significant "others". Which others are significant is left open. The authors prefer good questions to precise definitions or "answers" at this stage (pp. 64–65).
- Footnote 27: reference-group and small-group research are not yet conceptually integrated. It is an open question whether people relate only to personally known others or also to impersonal status categories. In the second case, one must ask how contact with anonymous others is established. The authors say opinion-leader research can make a major contribution here (p. 64).
- **"Conformity" here** means agreement among intimately interacting individuals. It is a different level from the political debate on orthodoxy. The only implication offered: a non-conformist at the level of society is likely close to a like-minded non-conformist with whom he conforms (p. 65).
- **Closing claim:** the point is not just that primary groups develop norms for their own interaction, which is well established. It is that even seemingly personal opinions and attitudes may be by-products of interpersonal relations. The evidence strongly suggests opinions are often maintained, sometimes generated, sometimes merely enforced, together with others. The individual expression of opinion is "not strictly an individual affair" (p. 65).

*Intuition note:* The claim is graded, not absolute. Groups sometimes create an opinion, sometimes only keep it alive, and sometimes only police it. The authors warn that "group" is often just shorthand for the significant others with whom a person associates.

## Toy example
(Our own illustration of the convergence idea; not Sherif's data.) Two people judge how far a light moves. Private norms: A = 2 inches, B = 8 inches. Assume that each round both people move one quarter of the gap toward the other's last estimate.
- Round 0: gap = 8 − 2 = 6.
- Round 1: A = 2 + 6/4 = 3.5. B = 8 − 6/4 = 6.5. Gap = 3.
- Round 2: A = 3.5 + 3/4 = 4.25. B = 6.5 − 3/4 = 5.75. Gap = 1.5.
- The gap halves each round, and the midpoint stays at 5 because both move equally. The estimates approach 5, and after many rounds both would state about 5.
- If each then judged alone, they would plausibly repeat this shared value, as in Sherif's second condition (p. 55).

## Concepts
- [[primary-group]] (new) — small, durable, informal, face-to-face group with manifold purpose, after Cooley (p. 48)
- [[sociometric-connection]] (new) — mutual attraction between persons, measured by asking whom they choose (pp. 48, 58)
- [[reference-group]] (new) — a group an individual has "in mind" when forming opinions. The authors say its theory is not yet integrated with small-group theory (pp. 49, 64)
- [[group-norm]] (new) — a shared standard or frame of reference that emerges from interaction (pp. 55, 57)
- [[instrumental-function-of-conformity]] (new) — acceptance, status and leadership rewards for sharing group opinions; need not be calculated (pp. 50–53)
- [[social-reality]] (new) — what counts as real for an individual depends on what others are perceived to share (pp. 53–54)
- [[homophily]] — here "value homophily": mutual attraction based on shared values. "Status homophily" is noted but not treated (p. 59)
- [[propinquity]] (new) — physical and functional closeness, which the Westgate study relates directly to friendship formation (p. 58)
- [[group-locomotion]] (new) — Festinger's term for group movement toward a goal, which may require uniform opinion (p. 62)
- [[opinion-leader]] — mentioned only as a source of research on contact with anonymous others (p. 64)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Sherif's convergence of estimates through exposure ↔ opinion-dynamics models: repeated averaging with others' views produces consensus.
- [structural-analogy] Value homophily versus interaction as explanation (Bard) ↔ network analysis: separating selection of ties from influence through ties.
- [metaphor] "Social reality" (belief rests on perceived sharing) ↔ spiral of silence: perceived opinion distribution shapes expression. Unclear whether the chapter licenses this.

## Questions this raises
- How can one separate selection (like-minded people choose each other) from influence (interaction makes people alike) in observational data?
- Which "others" count as significant, and do impersonal categories or mass media work like reference groups?
- Under what conditions does value homophily, as opposed to propinquity or interdependence, drive friendship formation?
- How do these group mechanisms connect to the flow of mass-media influence, the book's main concern?
