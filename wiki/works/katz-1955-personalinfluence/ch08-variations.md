# Variations in Impact

## L0 — One line
The effectiveness of a source of influence varies with the respondent's state of mind (in these small data, dissatisfied or discriminating people seem more open) and, it seems, with how specific the source is on the topic.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Earlier impact ratings only described the Decatur sample. This chapter asks how impact varies with conditions, to reach more general results. Dissatisfied women report more effective exposure (exposure that helped the decision) to every source in fashion; discriminating movie-goers do so for personal contacts and magazines, not newspapers. Personal contact seems to gain most, but the data are "very scanty". More specific sources (experts, specific advice, movie magazines) seem to do better. Education and age gave no consistent findings. A fourth kind of qualification, by decision stage, is proposed but not tested; the authors argue an explicit accounting scheme (a list of factors and stages) makes such analysis possible. The data are small; findings are tentative.

**Prerequisites:** the "index of effectiveness" and effective/contributory/ineffective exposure (Ch. V–VI, only recalled here); reading percentages in simple cross-tables.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Impact graphs describe one situation, not general laws; generalising needs respondent attitudes, or comparison of media with each other (p. 208).
- Women who were dissatisfied before a change show a higher effectiveness index for every source in fashion (Table 10) (p. 210).
- Dissatisfied women reported more exposure in marketing (personal contacts, radio) but not in fashion; the authors want future studies to corroborate this because the figures are small (p. 211).
- Discriminating movie-goers show more exposure and mostly higher effectiveness, except for newspapers (Table 11) (p. 212).
- The authors suggest that "people on the lookout" are more persuadable, and that personal contact seems an especially effective source for them, though their data are "very scanty" (p. 213).
- Among personal contacts, family (especially children, for small food purchases), experts and more specific advice have more impact; among magazines, effectiveness seems to parallel movie content (Table 12) (pp. 213–215).
- Education, age and personality gave no consistent findings; a fourth qualification, by decision stage, is proposed but not tested (pp. 216–217).
- Without an accounting scheme even this limited analysis would be impossible (p. 218).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening
The impact graphs only described an actual situation. With more radio advertisements on the air, radio impact would have been higher, so no generalisations follow (p. 208). Two routes to broader results: add respondents' attitudes and characteristics, or study media in relation to each other. The chapter mostly takes the first route (p. 208).

### The Role of Dissatisfaction
- Women who reported a marketing change were asked (fashion changers got a corresponding question): "When you made the change, were you dissatisfied with the product you used before, or did you just want to make a change?" This gives *satisfied* and *dissatisfied* changers. Dissatisfaction means the attitude *before* the change (p. 209).
- The authors do not claim that dissatisfaction causes change. Many women might be dissatisfied without changing, and fairly satisfied women might still change (p. 209).
- Exposure to a mood can itself be effective or ineffective. The authors cite Rossi (1955), who separates mere dissatisfaction with noise from dissatisfaction that makes a tenant move. Here they ignore that split and count dissatisfaction irrespective of effect (p. 209).
- Proportions: about half of fashion changers were dissatisfied. In marketing, under one-third changed from a brand they were satisfied with, and over two-thirds were dissatisfied (p. 210, n. 1).

**Table 10** (index of effectiveness, fashion changes; satisfied / dissatisfied) (p. 210):

| Source | Satisfied | Dissatisfied |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal personal contact | .09 | .20 |
| Visual personal contact | .13 | .18 |
| Salesperson | .08 | .13 |
| Magazine | .04 | .08 |

- In Table 10 every source is more effective among dissatisfied women. The authors say the same holds in the other areas (p. 210).
- The authors say this is not obvious. Dissatisfied people might be more cautious and less readily influenced, which would reverse the table. The findings are "net results" of many possible patterns (p. 210).
- Amount of exposure (p. 211): in marketing, dissatisfied women reported more exposure to personal contacts and radio advertising. The authors say dissatisfaction "does seem to have had two consequences": such women were more aware of sources and more susceptible. In fashion, total exposure did not differ; only effectiveness did.
- The authors offer a speculation: perhaps women in fashion may always be on the alert, while for consumer goods dissatisfaction may sharpen awareness. They advise waiting for corroboration, since the figures are small. Table 10's differences at least all run the same way (p. 211).

*Intuition note:* Being unhappy with what you use seems to make you more likely to act on advice you meet. Whether you meet more advice depended on the area.

### Another Situational Difference
- Movie question: "Did you go to see a particular picture or just to go to the movies?" It separates *discriminating* from *non-discriminating* movie-goers. It refers to the visit studied, but discriminating people tend to stay so at other times (pp. 211–212, n. 3).

**Table 11** (discriminating / non-discriminating) (p. 212):

| Source | Total exposure | Index of effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Personal contacts | 50% / 37% | .36 / .27 |
| Newspapers | 86% / 75% | .06 / .07 |
| Magazines | 31% / 17% | .14 / .10 |

- Discriminating people report more exposure to all three sources. Effectiveness is higher for personal contacts and magazines, but not for newspapers. Both groups stay equally uninfluenced by newspaper facts (p. 212).
- **Combined formulation (author's tentative view):** dissatisfied and discriminating people are probably special cases of a general attitude: they are on the lookout for a solution, with a specific mental set. Such people are more often subject to persuasion by personal contacts or formal media (p. 213).
- The data are "very scanty". Personal contact seems especially effective here: its difference between the two groups is greater than that of any formal medium in Tables 10 and 11 (p. 213).

*Intuition note:* A person with a goal in mind scans the environment and is easier to sway. Talking to people appears to help most in that state.

### Differentials in the Impact of Personal Influence
Personal contacts differ by kind. Earlier example: a married woman talks more with her husband on public affairs but respects his knowledge less, so his influence is a compromise (p. 213). Three further findings from the impact ratings (pp. 213–214):
1. Family members, especially children, have more impact on small food purchases than outsiders.
2. Experts in an area influence respondents there more than the average adviser.
3. The more specific a suggestion, the more likely it is followed.

Footnote 5 (p. 214): husbands' and children's contacts are excluded from marketing tables. The authors separate *requests* (the wife acts as purchasing agent) from *influence* (suggestions, advice, testimonials). Requests were reported more often than influences. The exclusion therefore understates personal influence in marketing. Chapter X is said to cover coffees and cereals.

### The Role of Specificity
- Each medium can be subdivided by specificity. Magazines named as sources for a film were classed as movie magazines (41%), general (32%), women's (14%); 13% were unclassified (p. 214).

**Table 12** (movie / general / women's) (p. 215):

| | Movie | General | Women's |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effective | 16% | 12% | 9% |
| Contributory | 7 | 17 | 13 |
| Ineffective | 77 | 71 | 78 |
| Index of effectiveness | .20 | .17 | .11 |
| Total (=100%) | (68) | (52) | (23) |

- Effectiveness seems to parallel content emphasis on movies (p. 215).
- Content mentioned: 55% reviews or articles, 25% advertisements, 20% not recalled. Reviews and articles: 15% effective, 16% contributory. Advertisements: 12% effective, 2% contributory (p. 215).

### Some Concluding Questions
- Possible further questions: agreement with environment vs. minority position; varied vs. uniform past experience; personality traits; education; age (pp. 215–216).
- **Negative result:** from the present material no consistent findings on education or age. The one exception is a limited comparison of leaders' and non-leaders' exposure, with a pointer to Chapter XIV (p. 216).
- Three types of qualification: (1) dispositions arising during the action (dissatisfaction, discrimination); (2) specifying the source; (3) type of person, which gave no consistent data (p. 216).
- **A fourth qualification (not exemplified in the study): decision stages.** Drug-use illustration: reasons for initiation vs. reasons for continuation. Social reasons in one stage need not recur in the other. Those who join easily may have "weak resistance" and keep the habit after social pressure ends. Pushes and pulls in moving work the same way. The sign of the relationship between stages is empirical and can be guessed neither in advance nor from a few cases (p. 217).
- This suggests media may act differently at different stages. The authors "have reason to suspect" advertising works better at maintaining decisions than at initiating them. Later Bureau studies (Ennis; Katz and Menzel) are said to lend credence (pp. 217–218, n. 8).
- Closing point: a richer accounting scheme permits such analysis. With none, even this "primitive story" could not be told (p. 218).

## Toy example
*Our own, not from the text.* Twenty women: 10 satisfied, 10 dissatisfied (the dissatisfied ten are shown under two alternative scenarios). Define "effective share" = effective exposures ÷ exposures, a simple stand-in for the index.
- Satisfied: 5 of 10 exposed to a friend's advice; 1 effective → share = 1/5 = .20.
- Dissatisfied, marketing-like: 8 of 10 exposed; 3 effective → share = 3/8 = .375. Both exposure (.8 vs .5) and effectiveness are higher.
- Dissatisfied, fashion-like: 5 of 10 exposed; 2 effective → share = 2/5 = .40. Exposure is equal to the satisfied group's (.5), but effectiveness is higher.
This shows the chapter's point: amount of exposure and effectiveness of exposure are separate quantities that can move independently.

## Concepts
- [[impact-rating]] (new) — the reported effect of a source on a specific decision, here broken down by condition (p. 208)
- [[index-of-effectiveness]] (new) — measure of how much of a source's exposure was effective; defined in Ch. V p. 178 (p. 210, n. 2)
- [[effective-vs-ineffective-exposure]] (new) — exposure that did vs. did not contribute to the decision; also applied to moods (p. 209)
- [[satisfied-vs-dissatisfied-changer]] (new) — classification by attitude *before* a change (p. 209)
- [[discriminating-movie-goer]] (new) — someone who usually goes to see a particular picture (p. 212, n. 3)
- [[people-on-the-lookout]] (new) — the authors' label for people seeking a solution or specific interest; more persuadable (p. 213)
- [[source-specificity]] (new) — greater concentration of a source on the topic seems to go with greater impact in these data (pp. 214–215)
- [[request-vs-influence]] (new) — a request makes the wife a purchasing agent; influence is advice or testimonial (p. 214, n. 5)
- [[accounting-scheme]] (new) — explicit scheme of factors and stages that makes statistical analysis of decisions possible (pp. 217–218)
- [[initiation-vs-continuation]] (new) — decision phases whose reasons may relate positively, negatively or not at all (p. 217)

## Questions this raises
- Does dissatisfaction raise effectiveness, or do effective sources raise reported dissatisfaction afterwards? The question was asked retrospectively.
- Why does exposure rise with dissatisfaction in marketing but not in fashion?
- Do the proposed stage differences (initiation vs. maintenance) hold for advertising?
- Why were no consistent effects of education or age found: true absence, or too few decision areas?
