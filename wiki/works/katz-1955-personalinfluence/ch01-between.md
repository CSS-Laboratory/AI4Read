# Between Media and Mass

## L0 — One line
Early mass-media research seems to have pictured powerful media acting directly on an atomized audience; four "intervening variables" qualified that picture, and the authors argue that interpersonal relations is a fifth one that research had underestimated.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The authors ask what picture of mass communication early research assumed. Both optimists and pessimists about the media imagined isolated individuals receiving messages that produce direct, immediate effects. Research since then has been mostly about short-run "campaigns" to change opinions. Studies of exposure, medium, content and predispositions each qualified the simple picture. The authors then argue that people's social ties, and the norms of their groups, have been greatly underestimated as a factor in how they respond to the media. They cite five case studies, which they call very few, as pointing to the possible relevance of interpersonal relations. They hope this will help bring together research on mass communication and research on small groups and social relations.

**Prerequisites:** what a survey-based "audience study" is; the idea of a variable that modifies the link between a cause and an effect.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Opposite early hopes and fears about the media (a revived "town meeting" vs. instruments of evil) shared one image: an atomistic mass (isolated individuals) receiving a Message as a direct stimulus (p. 16).
- The authors argue that all communications research, whatever its sub-field, is about effect. Sponsors have focused almost exclusively on short-run "campaign" effects on opinions and attitudes (pp. 18–19).
- Audience research, content analysis and similar branches are intermediate steps for imputing effects (inferring them indirectly from more accessible factors). They also gradually "pull apart" the simple model (pp. 19–20).
- Four intervening variables (factors that come between media and audience and modify the expected effect) are examined: exposure, medium, content and predispositions. Each can facilitate or block the flow of communication (pp. 21–24).
- A key exposure finding: the groups most hoped to be reached are often least likely to be in the audience (p. 22).
- As variables accumulate, research becomes more precise and the media look less potent for persuasion. The authors warn against generalizing this to longer-run effects (p. 24, fn. 16).
- Their proposal: the individual's response to a campaign cannot be explained without his social environment and interpersonal relations (p. 25).
- Five illustrations suggest effects of peer integration, group norms, group listening, village-level communication, and nearness to a "social mechanism" (pp. 26–30).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: two opposite inclinations (pp. 15–16)
- Early speculation about the media went in two directions. Some commentators thought the media would recreate the informed opinion of the "town meeting", giving citizens near first-hand access to matters they must decide (p. 15). Park (1925) is cited as attributing this motive to journalists, and Cooley (1909) is quoted as enthusiastic (fn. 1, pp. 15–16).
- Others saw "agents of evil" aiming to destroy democratic society, able to "rubber-stamp ideas" onto defenseless minds. Examples given: the belief in the 1920s that newspaper propaganda "got us into the war", and in the 1930s the Roosevelt campaign as "proof" of a "golden voice" (p. 16).
- **Authors' claim:** these views are not far apart. Both pictured (a) an atomistic mass of readers, listeners and movie-goers ready to receive the Message, and (b) each Message as a direct, powerful stimulus eliciting immediate response. The media were seen as a "simple kind of nervous system" in a society with amorphous organization and few interpersonal relations (p. 16).

*Intuition note:* Optimists and pessimists disagreed about whether the effect was good or bad, not about how it worked. Both assumed that nothing stands between the message and the isolated person.

### The origin of the "model" (p. 17)
- Mass media research seems to have had this "model" in mind when it began in the 1920s, shortly after radio was introduced (p. 17).
- Sources: the popular image of media potency; late-19th-century European sociology, which stressed breakdown of interpersonal relations and impersonal social control; and random sampling and attitude testing, which treat "representative" individuals lifted out of their associations. These techniques link the beginnings of communications research to applied psychology (p. 17).
- Footnote 4 (Shils 1951): early American sociology showed comparatively more interest in the primary group, but this was counterbalanced by an emphasis on its disintegration in cities (p. 17).

### Mass Media Research: The Study of "Campaigns" (pp. 17–19)
- Research is conventionally divided into audience research (how many of what kinds of people attend; the earliest and most prolific), content analysis (language, logic and layout of messages), and effect analysis (p. 17–18).
- **Authors' claim:** this division misleads, because fundamentally all of it studies effect. The one underlying question is "what can the media 'do'?" Research clients (advertisers, radio executives, propagandists, educators) want the effect of their message on the public. The authors say we may assume that commissioning audience or content studies means these aspects are somehow connected with effects (p. 18).
- Effect is not one concept. Fn. 5 gives Lazarsfeld's (1948) sixteen types: 4 kinds of stimulus × 4 kinds of response, with responses ordered in time (immediate, short term, long term, institutional change) (p. 18).
- The authors say it is safe to say sponsors have selected, by and large, one kind of effect for almost exclusive attention: the effectiveness of attempts to change opinions and attitudes in the very short run, i.e., "campaigns" (votes, soap, prejudice reduction) (pp. 18–19).
- Working definition: mass media research has aimed at understanding how, and under what conditions, "campaigns" (rather specific, short-run efforts) succeed in influencing opinions and attitudes (p. 19).
- Fn. 6: other, mostly long-term effects deserve study. Some of these long-range effects "promise to reveal the potency of the mass media much more than do 'campaign' effects" (p. 19). Campaign studies give the impression that media are quite ineffectual in persuasion on social and political matters (fn. 6, p. 19).

### Intervening Variables and the Study of Effect (pp. 19–21)
- Audience research can be seen as an intermediate step: counting the audience and learning its likes is a first step towards specifying the potential effect of a medium or message (p. 20).
- The intellectual history of the field may be seen as the successive addition of readily accessible intermediate factors (audience, content) used to impute effects (p. 20).
- These factors also reveal the complexity of the process: the sequence of events and factors that "intervene" between stimulus and response. Each new factor helps pull apart the scheme of "omnipotent media … atomized masses … and nothing in-between" (p. 20).
- The four factors are exposure, medium, content and predispositions, studied respectively through audience research, media comparison studies, content analysis and attitude study (p. 20). They are set up in order to introduce a fifth, the most recent: interpersonal relations (pp. 20–21).
- Footnote 7: "intervening variable" here is not the survey-analysis sense of a "test" factor used to interpret a correlation (p. 21).

### Four Intervening Variables (pp. 21–25)
The four variables help the flow of communication under some conditions and block it under others; this is the sense of "intervening" (p. 21).

**Exposure** (pp. 21–22)
- Audience research shows people are not exposed to specific messages as much, as easily or as randomly as supposed. Causes: technological, political, economic and, especially in the US, voluntary (not tuning in) (p. 21).
- Perhaps the most important generalization for effective persuasion: the groups most hopefully regarded as targets are often least likely to be in the audience. Examples: educational programs rarely reach the uneducated; goodwill programs rarely reach the prejudiced (p. 22).

**Medium** (p. 22)
- The question is what difference it makes to the effect of Message X if sent through Medium A, B or C. Cantril and Allport's (1935) book called attention to a whole set of "media comparison" experiments. Findings imply persuasion is modified by the channel (p. 22).

**Content** (pp. 22–23)
- Most content research tries to explain or predict differences in effect from differences in content, usually by imputing psychological processes (p. 22).
- Results mentioned: effective techniques (repetition, appeal to authority, band-wagon); the greater sway of "facts" than "opinions"; "don't argue"; one side vs. both sides; documentary vs. commentator; damage from a script at cross-purposes with itself (p. 23).
- These techniques concentrate on the "stimulus", judging it by imputed psychological variables or by actual responses to controlled variations (p. 23).

**Predispositions** (pp. 23–24)
- Prior attitudes can modify, or sometimes completely distort, a message's meaning. Example: a strongly prejudiced person may resist a tolerance message so that it is seen as a defense of prejudice, or as irrelevant (p. 23).
- Attitudes toward the media and the sources matter too: some people trust radio more than newspapers, and the reverse; "prestige suggestion" studies bear on this (p. 24).

**Summary (pp. 24–25).** Exposure and predisposition come from the receiving end; medium and content from the transmission end (p. 24). Each new factor shows how many things must be attuned for a message to be effective, so the initial image of direct media influence is increasingly qualified (pp. 24–25).
- Fn. 16: greater precision has come with increased skepticism about media potency. Outside marketing, influence attempts have fallen far short of communicators' expectations, notoriously in civic and political persuasion. But one should not generalize from such direct, short-run effects to the potency that longer-run, indirect effects might reveal (p. 24).

*Intuition note:* Each variable is a filter or amplifier between message and person. The more factors are found, the more qualified the picture of direct media influence becomes. The authors treat the resulting scepticism as limited to short-run campaign effects.

### Interpersonal Relations: The Discovery of "People" (pp. 25–30)
- **Thesis:** it appears that studies have greatly underestimated how far a person's social attachments, and the opinions and activities he shares with others, influence his response to the media. The authors suggest that an individual's response to a campaign cannot be accounted for without reference to his social environment and the character of his interpersonal relations (p. 25).
- It "promises" to be a key link in the chain and to bring together macroscopic mass communication research and microscopic research on social relations (p. 25).
- Plan: cite case studies; show how research hit on the idea; note parallel "rediscoveries" in other social sciences (pp. 25–26). Fn. 17: this study and some others cited do not deal with campaign effects (the Riley study concerns "uses" or "gratifications"), but the authors say each has manifest implications for the effects they are concerned with (p. 26).

**Five illustrations**
1. **Riley and Riley (1951), peer integration** (pp. 26–27). Children weakly tied to peers said they liked radio action and violence programs somewhat more than well-integrated ones. Among children who liked them equally, "non-members" enjoyed the "creepy", hard-to-forget excitement; "members" more often said the stories gave ideas for group games. The authors suggest members judge media by contribution to social life, non-members turn to them for fantasy and escape. The differing effects could not be accounted for without the interpersonal contexts (pp. 26–27).
2. **Suchman (1941), group norms** (pp. 27–28). Groups may be equally integrated but hold different norms (p. 27). Among those whose taste for serious music was created by radio, almost half said "friends" gave the initial impetus; among those whose interest developed apart from radio, 26% did (p. 27). This suggests social pressure motivates exposure and receptivity. A communication seems effective for a person when it helps him rise in his friends' esteem. Suchman concludes radio's importance lies in following up forces unconnected with it rather than creating interests (pp. 27–28).
3. **Planned listening groups** (p. 28). The authors say there is good reason to suspect, though they add there is really no empirical evidence, that some of the most effective radio broadcasts involve planned listening groups rather than isolated individuals. Father Coughlin's success appears built on it. In Soviet Russia, communication depends heavily on in-person presentations to organized groups, with mass communication superimposed (Inkeles). Shils and Janowitz (1948) found that individuals reject a communication that tries to separate them from their group (p. 28).
4. **One-radio village and no-newspaper town** (pp. 28–29). With the start of international communications research, the authors note such phenomena as the one-radio village and the no-newspaper town. Studies of news and influence flow there point out some communication roles played by people, which may be relevant even in a mass-media society like the US (pp. 28–29).
5. **Wiebe (1951, 1952), "social mechanisms"** (p. 29). Why are the media better at selling goods than "good citizenship"? Successful advertising gives a last push to existing motivations and directs people to a "social mechanism" (usually a store). It must be physically and psychologically "nearby" and suited to the demand. A radio documentary urging listeners to form neighborhood councils against delinquency asked them to build their own mechanisms, with no blueprint. Authors' reading: good intentions do not become action when the person is not "near" an appropriate mechanism, where "near" (they assume) often must mean related to other people (p. 29). Fn. 24: this is the authors' inference, not stated explicitly by Wiebe (p. 29).

**Five consequences for transmission** (pp. 29–30): frequency of association with peers; association with those who share a norm; membership of a group that supplements and reinforces the message; belonging to a group that has "hooked up" its own communication system with the media; being "near" enough to a social outlet for action (pp. 29–30).

The authors stress these are very few studies. Several findings are afterthoughts or speculations, and later ones were partly motivated by an initial "discovery" not yet described (p. 30).

*Intuition note:* The examples suggest that the same message can serve different purposes, or reach different people, depending on a person's ties to others. The authors call such examples very few and speak only of possible relevance.

## Toy example (ours, invented numbers)
A tolerance campaign targets 400 prejudiced people among 1,000.
- Naive model: every target gets the message and changes, so 400 of 400 = 100%.
- Exposure: suppose only 10% of the prejudiced tune in: 400 × 0.10 = 40.
- Predisposition: suppose 75% of these misread it as defending prejudice or as irrelevant: 40 × 0.75 = 30 misread; 40 − 30 = 10 receive it as intended.
- Result: 10 of 400 = 2.5%. Two intervening variables cut the effect sharply, which is the pattern the text describes. A group-norm step could change this further, but the text gives no numbers.

## Concepts
- [[magic-bullet-hypodermic-model]] — Here not named: the early "model" of atomistic mass plus direct, immediate stimulus response, held by both optimists and pessimists (p. 16).
- [[limited-effects-paradigm]] — Partly related: growing scepticism about media persuasion as variables accumulate, limited by the authors to short-run campaign effects (p. 24, fn. 16).
- [[selective-exposure]] — Related: exposure is often voluntary; targets are often least likely to be in the audience (pp. 21–22). The term itself is not used here.
- [[intervening-variable-media]] (new) — A factor (exposure, medium, content, predispositions, interpersonal relations) that facilitates or blocks the media-to-individual flow. Not the survey "test factor" (p. 21, fn. 7).
- [[campaign-effects]] (new) — Specific, short-run efforts to influence opinions and attitudes; the main focus of mass media research (p. 19).
- [[atomized-mass]] (new) — The early image of isolated individuals with few interpersonal relations (p. 16).
- [[social-mechanism-wiebe]] (new) — A nearby, appropriate outlet (e.g., a store) through which motivated action is expressed (p. 29).
- [[group-norms-and-media-use]] (new) — The opinions and values of one's friends and family shape exposure and receptivity (p. 27).
- [[uses-and-gratifications]] — Mentioned only: the Riley study concerns "uses" or "gratifications" (fn. 17, p. 26).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Atomized mass with no ties ↔ network analysis: a population with no edges between nodes; the authors argue ties matter.
- [structural-analogy] Intervening variables as filters ↔ mediation/moderation in statistics: related idea, but the authors say their usage differs from the survey-analysis "test factor" (fn. 7).
- [metaphor] "Nervous system" image of media ↔ biology: the early view of media as a direct wire to every individual (p. 16).

## Questions this raises
- Which indirect, long-run effects might show greater media potency than campaign studies (fn. 6)?
- How could one measure "nearness" to a social mechanism, or the strength of a group's norm, in a survey?
- Are the five illustrations (several of them afterthoughts) enough to support the claim that interpersonal relations are a key intervening variable?
