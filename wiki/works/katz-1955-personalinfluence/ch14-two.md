# The Two-Step Flow of Communication (Katz & Lazarsfeld, ch. XIV)

## L0 — One line
In the Decatur data, opinion leaders in all four realms (marketing, fashion, public affairs, movie-going) are more exposed to the mass media than non-leaders, and often in a way matched to their realm, but only fashion leaders also clearly credit the media more in their own decisions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The "two-step flow" hypothesis (from *The People's Choice*) said ideas flow from media to opinion leaders and then to the less active. It was tested only on election-campaign leaders. This chapter asks whether it holds in marketing, fashion, public affairs and movie-going. Leaders in every realm tend to be more exposed to media generally. Their extra exposure is often to content tied to their realm. The authors then ask whether leaders are also more affected by the media in their own decisions. Only fashion leaders clearly are. Public affairs leaders are not; the authors tentatively suspect longer chains of personal influence there. The authors also call for studying the different "uses" of media.

**Prerequisites:** reading percentage tables; the idea of an opinion leader (a person others consult); what a control variable such as education does in a table.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The two-step flow hypothesis, as the authors state it, claims only that leaders are more *exposed* to media than followers. Its original evidence came from one kind of leader (election campaign) (pp. 309, 316–317).
- Leaders in all four arenas read more magazines than non-leaders at both education levels. For books, radio and movies the pattern holds mostly, with exceptions (pp. 310–312).
- Exposure is selective by realm. Fashion and public affairs leaders are more "cosmopolitan" in news reading, while movie leaders are not and marketing leaders are only slightly so. Fashion leaders are more likely than non-leaders to read fashion magazines, public affairs leaders news weeklies, and movie leaders movie magazines (pp. 314–316).
- Greater exposure does not automatically mean greater effect on the leader's own decisions. The authors say there is no need to expect it (p. 317).
- Fashion leaders credit the media more and other people less than non-leaders do. Marketing and movie data are inconclusive. Public affairs leaders, against expectation, lean if anything on personal influence (p. 318).
- The authors tentatively suspect that the chain of personal influence is longer in public affairs, so the media effect may sit further back in the chain (p. 319).
- Movie leaders, by their own reports, "get more out" of movies. This points to studying the "uses" of media, not just exposure and effect (pp. 319–320).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 309)
- The study could only map a cross section of influential–influencee pairs, not whole networks. Even a full map would not be enough, because influence also comes from the mass media (p. 309).
- **Hypothesis (from *The People's Choice*):** "ideas often flow from radio and print to the opinion leaders and from them to the less active sections of the population" (p. 309). Its evidence came only from leaders during an election campaign. So it is unknown whether it applies in other realms (p. 309).
- The chapter compares leaders' and non-leaders' media behaviour. The authors say that, "in general", they will find the hypothesis "substantiated" in each arena studied. They also add a refinement about the "relay" role at the end (pp. 309–310).
- Footnote 1: influence is not the same as information. Other work covers media versus personal sources in spreading news events (p. 309, n. 1).

### Opinion leadership and exposure to the mass media (pp. 310–312)
- **Expectation:** the knowledge needed for leadership differs by realm. So leaders need not beat non-leaders in every single medium. But the media categories are very broad, so leaders may exceed non-leaders in exposure in general. The authors find both statements true in varying cases (p. 310).
- **Table 54 (magazines, % reading 5 or more).** Columns are Marketing, Fashion, Public Affairs, Movie leaders, then Non-leaders. (Leader groups overlap; only the non-leader group is exclusive, n. 2.) "High education" starts at high school graduation (n. 3) (p. 311).
  - Low education: 41, 58, 60, 58 vs 30. Ns: 91, 79, 30, 64, 270.
  - High education: 65, 69, 63, 71 vs 53. Ns: 75, 80, 50, 58, 146.
  - Every leader type exceeds non-leaders at both education levels (pp. 310–311).
- **Table 55 (books, % reading 1 or more per month).** Same column order (p. 311).
  - Low education: 25, 47, 38, 38 vs 20. Ns: 81, 76, 29, 61, 270.
  - High education: 39, 42, 57, 51 vs 34. Ns: 74, 79, 49, 55, 146.
  - All leaders exceed non-leaders. Marketing leaders exceed them only very little (25 vs 20; 39 vs 34). The authors say this fits intuition: marketing leadership does not "require" more book reading (pp. 311–312).
- **Radio:** leaders tend to exceed non-leaders in hours listened, but the differences are small and inconsistent. Movie leaders at both levels seem particularly attentive, as do low-education marketing and fashion leaders. High-education marketing and fashion leaders and political leaders at both levels do not exceed non-leaders (p. 312).
- **Movie-going:** political leaders are the only group that does not exceed non-leaders (p. 312). Footnote 5 says "These tables are not shown here"; the study was completed before television was generally introduced (n. 4).
- **Conclusion:** "it can safely be stated" that leaders in every realm tend to be more highly exposed than non-leaders. The question of content comes next (p. 312).

*Intuition note.* Leaders do not read more of everything. But across broad media categories they usually have more contact with the media than followers do. The authors say exposure is all that the two-step hypothesis itself claims (p. 317).

### Opinion leadership and the content of mass communications (pp. 312–316)
- **Local vs cosmopolitan.** Merton's earlier study separated public affairs leaders into "cosmopolitans" (outside news, outside media) and "locals" (local news, local media) (n. 6) (p. 313). Here the authors cannot split leaders within public affairs. They compare the four leader types on this dimension instead (p. 313).
- **Index** (from June and August interview questions, n. 7) (pp. 313–314):
  - Cosmopolitan: reads out-of-town newspapers *and* news or news analysis in national magazines.
  - Local: neither, but reads local papers.
  - Intermediate: one of the two.
- **Table 56 (% cosmopolitan, leaders vs non-leaders *in that area*).** The leader and non-leader Ns are given below (p. 314).

| | Marketing L / non | Fashion L / non | Public affairs L / non | Movie L / non |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low ed. | 27 / 20 | 39 / 17 | 50 / 20 | 25 / 24 |
| High ed. | 48 / 43 | 53 / 41 | 55 / 41 | 45 / 47 |

  - Low-ed Ns: 88/324, 79/330, 30/381, 64/159. High-ed Ns: 77/219, 81/218, 51/247, 58/148.
  - Movie leaders do not exceed non-leaders. Marketing leaders exceed them only slightly. Fashion and public affairs leaders exceed non-leaders far more, and the differences are smaller but of the same kind at high education (p. 314).
- **Authors' explanation** (they say it "seems apparent"): movie and marketing leaders have little "need" for out-of-town news to exert their influence. The metropolitan press and national magazines give small-city fashion and political leaders a channel to "big city" fashions and world news. This information "bolsters" their influentiality (pp. 314–315).
- **Specific magazines** (p. 315):
  - Fashion magazines: low education, 9% of fashion leaders vs 2% of non-leaders; high education, 30% vs 15%.
  - News weeklies: 22% of public affairs leaders vs 14% of non-leaders. In no other realm is leadership associated with news-magazine reading at all.
  - Footnote 8 gives data from the "general influence" question, % reading a news magazine. Women: original sample 34% (N = 718), expert group I 41% (N = 122). Men: expert group I 57% (N = 201), expert group II 69% (N = 162). Column heads are partly garbled (see Reading notes).
- **Table 57 (movie magazines).** Low education: 56% of movie leaders vs 34% of non-movie leaders read them (Ns 66, 157). High education: 50% vs 42% (Ns 58, 151) (p. 316).
  - Movie leaders are more likely to buy them themselves, not borrow them. Low education: 74% vs 52%. High education: 52% vs 48% (n. 9).
  - Other leader types do not particularly exceed their non-leaders in movie-magazine reading (p. 316).
- **Conclusion:** the specialised interests tied to leadership in one area "go hand in hand" with some media content and not others (p. 316).

*Intuition note.* Leaders' media diet is tailored to their realm. The same extra reading helps a fashion leader keep up with city styles and a public affairs leader with national news. The authors suggest that it is the right content, not just more media, that "bolsters" their influentiality (pp. 314–315).

### Opinion leadership and mass media effect (pp. 316–319)
- The findings so far make the two-step idea "gain credence". Exposure presumably becomes a (witting or unwitting) part of the influence leaders pass on (p. 316).
- **New question:** the hypothesis claims only greater exposure. Are leaders also relatively more *affected* by the media in their own decisions? (pp. 316–317)
- **Expectations (authors' reasoning):**
  - There is "no need to expect" this will be true. In marketing, leaders probably decide mainly through personal contacts (perhaps other marketing leaders) and use media only as a supplement (p. 317).
  - In fashions and public affairs there is more reason to expect media effect. The relevant "environment" there is less immediate and depends more on media for transmission (p. 317).
- **Table 58** (fashion; recent changers only; % of all *influences* named, answers to "Who or what suggested this change to you?"; n. 10) (p. 318):

| Source | Low ed. leaders / non | High ed. leaders / non |
|---|---|---|
| Heard or saw somebody | 40 / 56 | 37 / 47 |
| Mass media | 42 / 31 | 42 / 33 |
| Other | 18 / 13 | 21 / 20 |
| Total influences | 164 / 308 | 135 / 250 |

  - Fashion leaders credit the media more and other people less than non-leaders do. The differences are "not very large" but consistent. They persist as markedly when level of interest is controlled (n. 11) (p. 318).
- **Other realms** (p. 318):
  - Marketing and movie data are inconclusive. The channels affect leaders about as they affect non-leaders.
  - Against expectation, public affairs leaders do not behave like fashion leaders. If anything they report personal influence as the more significant part of their recent opinion changes.
  - Only fashion leaders significantly exceed non-leaders in crediting the media.
- **Possible reasons for the public affairs result** (speculative; the authors say "may" and "might") (p. 319):
  - The sample may contain mostly "local" leaders; separate "cosmopolitans" might show more media impact.
  - Media effect might show up further back: the "opinion leaders of the opinion leaders", or several steps back.
  - Compared with fashions, the authors are "led to suspect" that the chain of personal influence is longer in public affairs, and that "inside dope" and specific influence episodes are more person-to-person.
- They say the different media–personal mixes "seem to corroborate" much that was merely speculative and point to new research (p. 319).

*Intuition note.* Being exposed to the media and being swayed by them are different things. A leader may just pass on media content while deciding through personal ties. Here, only for fashion does the data show a leader more swayed by media than followers.

### The movies as a source of "uses" (pp. 319–320)
- The decision data for movie-goers concern only *which movie to see*. They do not cover what people get *from* movies (p. 319).
- Movie leaders say movies help them in daily life, and "get more out" of movies than non-leaders (p. 319).
  - Questions covered ideas on clothes or hair from movies, whether movies help with everyday problems, and whether movies make one more or less contented. On the first, leaders, both young and old and with high and low education, answered affirmatively more than matched non-leaders. On the second, leaders again answered "yes" more often than non-leaders. "The same pattern" appears on the third (p. 320).
- **Final call:** study not only exposure and effect on opinions, but also the different "uses" leaders in each realm make of media, compared with non-leaders (p. 320, n. 12).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Twelve women; 4 fashion leaders (L) and 8 non-leaders (N).

**Exposure.** Suppose 3 of 4 leaders read a fashion magazine: 3/4 = 75%. Suppose 2 of 8 non-leaders do: 2/8 = 25%. Difference = 75 − 25 = 50 points. This is the exposure pattern of Table 57's kind.

**Effect on own decisions** (Table 58's method). The base is *influences named*, not people.
- Leaders name 12 influences: 5 media, 5 people, 2 other. That gives 5/12 = 41.7% media, 41.7% people, 2/12 = 16.7% other. Total 100%.
- Non-leaders name 20 influences: 6 media, 11 people, 3 other. That gives 30% media, 55% people, 15% other. Total 100%.
- Leaders credit media 41.7 − 30 = 11.7 points more. This matches the fashion pattern. A leader could be more exposed (the first part) and still show no such media share. That was the public affairs result.

## Concepts
- [[two-step-flow]] — ideas go from media to opinion leaders and on to the less active; here tested beyond elections (p. 309)
- [[opinion-leader]] — person influential for others within a realm (marketing, fashion, public affairs, movies) (p. 309)
- [[cosmopolitan-local-orientation]] (new) — Merton's contrast; operationalised by reading out-of-town papers and national-magazine news (pp. 313–314)
- [[flow-of-influence-vs-information]] (new) — authors distinguish spread of influence from spread of news information (p. 309, n. 1)
- [[exposure-vs-effect-of-leaders]] (new) — the hypothesis claims only exposure. Greater effect on leaders' own decisions is a separate question (pp. 316–317)
- [[uses-and-gratifications]] — here only a "uses" approach: what leaders and non-leaders get from media (not gratification theory as such) (p. 320)

## Canonical instances
- [[decatur-personal-influence-study]] (new) — source of all data; four leader types, two education levels (pp. 311–318)
- [[fashion-leaders-media-effect]] (new) — Table 58: fashion leaders cite media more, people less (p. 318)
- [[public-affairs-leaders-personal-influence]] (new) — public affairs leaders do not show greater media effect; possible longer chain (p. 319)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] leader as relay between media and followers ↔ network science: intermediary nodes in multi-step diffusion — the authors' "opinion leaders of the opinion leaders" suggests longer chains (p. 319).
- [structural-analogy] cosmopolitan vs local orientation ↔ diffusion research: outward-looking channels bring in new information (see pp. 314–315).
- [metaphor] leaders' "use" of media ↔ uses and gratifications: media choice by function (p. 320).

## Questions this raises
- Are leaders more exposed because they lead, or do they become leaders because they are more exposed? The chapter shows association, not direction.
- If media effect in public affairs lies further back in the chain, how many steps are needed, and how could one trace them?
- How large are the leader–non-leader differences relative to sampling error? Several are small (e.g. 50% vs 42% in Table 57, high education).
- Would results differ for "cosmopolitan" public affairs leaders, if they could be separated?
