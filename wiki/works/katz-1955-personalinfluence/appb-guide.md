# Appendix B. A Guide to the Questionnaire

## L0 — One line
The appendix sorts the two-wave Decatur panel questionnaire into eight functional groups, so the reader can relate the questions to parts of the book; it shows the questions behind its key variables (decisions, influences, opinion leadership, status, gregariousness, life-cycle position).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This appendix does not reprint the Decatur questionnaire in full. It sorts the questions of the two-wave panel (June and August 1945; each interview round is a "wave") of women in Decatur, Illinois, into eight groups. It is meant to let the reader relate each group to the book's text. It shows how decisions were detected, how influences on them were traced, how opinion leaders were identified by self-designation (a woman reports that she herself is asked for advice), and which questions measured social status, gregariousness (sociability) and life-cycle position. It matters because it shows how the book's central variables were measured. It also includes some questions the book did not use.

**Prerequisites:** survey-research basics (panel versus cross-section, index construction); the book's idea of "opinion leader" and "influential."

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The basic questionnaire had two waves (June and August 1945) with the same respondents, minus panel "mortality." Follow-up questionnaires for influentials and influencees are treated in Appendix C (p. 340).
- Eight groups of questions "exhaust" the basic panel schedules. Some parts of the questionnaire were not used in the book. Tabulations for many of these unreported items, and the complete questionnaire text, are on file at the Bureau of Applied Social Research (p. 340).
- Decisions were detected in two ways. Wave I relied on retrospective report. Wave II compared Wave I and Wave II answers (brands on the shelf, opinions) (pp. 341–342).
- A "reason for change" sequence (Impact Analysis) then traced who and what influenced each change. It was reproduced in full for small consumer items (pp. 342–344).
- Actual advisers in a specific episode often differ considerably from the people a respondent regards as generally competent ("potential influentials" or "experts"). Separate questions covered each (p. 345).
- Opinion leaders were identified by self-designation on two questions with nearly identical wording across areas. These questions, including a general self-rating (Q15), were combined into an index per area (pp. 346–347).
- Status, gregariousness and life-cycle position were the main indicators. Indexes for gregariousness and status each used two questions chosen from a larger set (pp. 347–348).
- Many attitude, personal-contact and mood questions are listed. Two groups of questions served other purposes (pp. 348–352).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: structure of the questionnaire (pp. 340–341)
- The questionnaire had Wave I (June 1945) and Wave II (August 1945). The respondents were the same minus the "mortality" common to panel studies. There were also special follow-up questionnaires for the influentials and influencees named by panel respondents (p. 340).
- The guide classifies questions instead of reproducing them. The classification includes parts not used in the book (p. 340). Tabulations for many unreported items, and the complete questionnaire text, are on file at the Bureau of Applied Social Research (p. 340, fn. 1).
- Notation: roman numerals I and II give the wave. "I,8,9" means Wave I questions 8 and 9 (p. 340, fn. 2; the footnote breaks off after "8 an", and p. 341 uses "I, 8, 9" for Q8 and Q9).
- The eight groups cover the whole basic panel questionnaire for the cross-sectional sample of Decatur women (pp. 340–341):
  1. factual decision questions;
  2. role of influences in decisions;
  3. potential influentials or experts;
  4. reading and listening habits;
  5. own opinion-leadership position;
  6. position on social status, gregariousness and life cycle;
  7. attitudes on specific matters;
  8. extraneous questions.

### 1. Factual decision questions (pp. 341–342)
- **Wave I** relied on the respondent's retrospective report of a recent change or decision (p. 341).
  - Consumer items: Q8 asks whether, in the last month or so, she bought a new product or brand that she does not usually buy (not one forced on her because it was the only one available). Q9 asks on which item she most recently tried a new brand (I,8,9) (p. 341).
  - Fashion: Q16 asks whether she recently changed hairdo, clothing, cosmetics, or anything "more fashionable." Q17 asks what sort of change (p. 341).
  - Movies: she names the last movie she saw (p. 341).
  - Public affairs: poll-type opinion questions came first (listed under group 7). Then Q44 asks whether she recently changed her opinion on an important issue. Q45 asks, if not, whether she feels more strongly now. Q46 asks on what topic (p. 341–342).
  - Any example given led into the "reason for change" sequence (p. 342).
- **Wave II** added a second technique because people cannot easily recall offhand the changes of opinion they undergo on matters that are often not important to them (p. 342).
  - The Wave I opinion questions were repeated. The interviewer knew the earlier answers, and a different answer counted as a change and was followed up (p. 342).
  - In Wave I each respondent named the brand of breakfast cereal, soap flakes and coffee on her shelves. In Wave II the same questions were repeated. A different brand started the "reason for change" sequence (p. 342).
- The book always states which data group is used. It gives no discussion of differences between reporting techniques. Some of the authors' observations will be used in a general report on panel techniques then in preparation (p. 342).

*Intuition note:* A change can be reported from memory (Wave I) or detected by comparing two interviews (Wave II). The second method was added because people cannot easily recall changes on matters that are often unimportant to them (p. 342); it does not rely on her remembering the change.

### 2. The Impact Analysis (pp. 342–344)
Discussed in the text, Part Two, Section II. The sequence for small consumer items (II, Sheet B; I,13,14) runs as follows (p. 342). Question numbering in the source is partly garbled (see Reading notes).
- **If a substitution was made:** why did you stop using the old brand? **If a brand was added:** how did you happen to start using the new one? (pp. 342–343)
- Then, after either: did you buy it yourself? Did you ask especially for it? If not, the (partly lost) text ends "Did you tell them to get that brand?" A "No" there ends the panel questions (p. 343).
- Was it the only one available? A "Yes" ends the panel questions (p. 343).
- Had you used it before? If yes, why was it not on your shelf in June? (p. 343)
- Before the change, were you satisfied or dissatisfied with the other brands? Were other family members? Who was dissatisfied? What attracted you to the new brand? (p. 343)
- Did other family members say anything good about it? (p. 343)
- How did you find out about it? The channels asked about, in order (pp. 343–344):
  - Someone talking about it. If she knew them personally or knew who they were, further questions asked how she knew them, whether they used it before her, whether they usually have good ideas on what to buy, and the occupation of the breadwinner (p. 343).
  - Radio; newspaper; a storekeeper (only this brand or others too); someone else in the store (same acquaintance questions); magazine; movie (p. 343–344).
- She then ranked the channels she had mentioned by which brought it to her attention first, second and so on. A final question asked what she thought was most important in causing the change (p. 344).
- Other areas were "very much the same," with additions (p. 344):
  - **Fashion** (I,21B): have you considered such a change before? If yes, why did you not change then but only now? Pretests showed that a change might be preceded by considerable hesitation.
  - **Movies** (I,24.2, 27; II,3): the question about regular movie-going on a particular weekday, whether she went just to go to a movie or to see a certain picture or both, and whether she went mainly because someone she went with wanted to or because she wanted to. If someone else, who (relation)? These distinguish types of discussions.

*Intuition note:* Each change is traced backward. The sequence stops in some cases, for example when only one brand was available. The remaining cases are followed through who talked to her and which media she met.

### 3. Sources of potential influence (p. 345)
- In most areas the questions asked whether she knew anybody "around here" who knows about the area. The examples are a person who knows about movies and can tell what is a good picture (II,13.2), and a person who keeps up with the news and can be trusted to say what is really going on (II,35) (p. 345).
- Each "Yes" was followed by how she knows the person, their age and sex, the breadwinner's occupation, and name and address (p. 345).
- The text says there is often considerable difference between the people from whom advice was actually obtained in a specific episode and the people a respondent feels are generally competent. The latter are "general influentials," "potential influentials" or "experts" (Part Two, Sections I and III) (p. 345).

### 4. Communication behavior (pp. 345–346)
- Both waves had many listening and reading questions. A selection of the findings is reported in Part Two, Section III. Many questions were of the conventional audience-survey type and are not reproduced (p. 345).
- Questions singled out: regular listening to discussion of public issues on the air (54); regular reading of biographies of prominent people in magazines (62); magazine articles discussing news events in more detail (65); more or less news listening and newspaper reading since V-E Day (67, 68); short documentary "fact films" seen in the last three months, and where (II,5); movie magazines, and where she gets them (II,6); movie content in other magazines (II,7) (pp. 345–346).

### 5. Self-designated opinion leadership (pp. 346–347)
- For this part of the report, an opinion leader was spotted by self-designation on two questions. The wording was practically identical across areas. The movie version is the example (p. 346).
  - Wave I (32): have you recently been asked your advice about what pictures to see? If yes: which picture, by whom (name, address), and how she knows them (p. 346).
  - Wave II (13.1): the same question, then how she knows them, their age and sex (p. 346).
- Each interview collected different information on the advice-seekers. These people were the starting point for the follow-up interviews with advisees (Appendix C) (pp. 346–347).
- Q15 (Wave II) adds a general self-rating in each of four areas. Compared with other women in her circle of friends, is she more or less likely to be asked for advice on (a) clothes or hairdos, (b) brands and products, (c) movies, (d) what to think about social or political opinions? (p. 347)
- These questions were combined into an index of opinion leadership in each arena (see fn. 1, p. 234, of the book). In a number of cases the ratings were checked with the person who presumably asked for advice (Part Two, Chapter 2) (p. 347).

*Intuition note:* Leadership is measured from the leader's own report of being asked. Some cases were cross-checked with the person who presumably asked.

### 6. Correlates of opinion leadership (pp. 347–348)
- **Life-cycle position:** determined from age, marital status and number of children still living with her (p. 347).
- **Gregariousness:** derived from questions I,62; II,21,57,59,61 (p. 347).
  - The questions were on organizations belonging to, counts of friendly contacts (household, relatives, neighbors, ex-neighbors, co-workers, other friends), liking to meet new people, afternoon social affairs, and visiting by telephone (pp. 347–348).
  - After a "latent structure" analysis, two of these questions were chosen to form the index (see Appendix D and Part Two, Chapter 9). This guide does not say which two (p. 347, fn. 4).
- **Socio-economic status:** questions I,64,65; II,75,76,77. They cover the interviewer's appraisal of economic level, telephone, education, women employed in the household, rent and family income. Two were selected for the index (see Appendix D and Part Two, Chapter 9). This guide does not say which two (p. 348).

### 7. Other attitude questions (pp. 348–351)
- **Fashion** (II,37,40,41,43): importance of being in style; whether women dress more for men or other women; whether she is better dressed than other Decatur women; whether she sees herself as attractive (p. 348).
- **Movies** (p. 349):
  - General motivation for attending (II,3,4): who wanted to go, and when she is most likely to go (tired or depressed, free evening, a picture of special interest, asked by someone).
  - Perceived impact (II,10,11,12): ideas on clothes or hair from movies, help with everyday problems, and whether movies make her more or less contented.
- **Public issues:** only questions repeated in both waves are reproduced (I,33 through 43) (p. 349). They cover:
  - German men helping rebuild European cities;
  - Truman on international problems, and on labor versus business;
  - continuing the war with Japan versus accepting a reasonable peace offer;
  - fairness of veterans' release;
  - Jewish influence in the U.S.;
  - worry about the postwar situation;
  - who can best straighten out the U.S. after the war;
  - opportunity to rise, in the U.S. and in Decatur;
  - U.S.–Russia relations and whose fault if they fail (pp. 349–350).
- **Personal contacts** (II,14, 16–21), because of their central role (p. 350):
  - whether she talks things over before making up her mind, and with whom (14);
  - what happens in disagreements with a woman friend (16–17) or her husband if married (18–19): she wins, each holds own view, she gives in without conviction, or she comes around;
  - whether she met her best friend as a neighbor (20);
  - the number of friendly contacts by type (21) (pp. 350–351).
- **Psychological ("mood") questions** (II,55,56,58,60): worry compared with others; being blue or depressed; whether she would live life differently; whether her family has done better, the same or worse over 10–15 years (p. 351). Appendix D discusses their relation to mass media behavior (p. 351, fn. 6).

### 8. Extraneous questions (pp. 351–352)
- Two groups served participants' own interests. One concerned plans for postwar purchases of durable consumer goods, because postwar economy was much discussed. The other came from the field-work director's interest in occupational mobility. It has about twelve items on the occupational history of her father, herself or her husband, and her occupational plans for her children (pp. 351–352).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Five respondents report the cereal brand on their shelf in both waves (the rule is on p. 342).

| Resp. | Wave I | Wave II | Change? |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | A | A | no |
| R2 | A | B | yes |
| R3 | B | C | yes |
| R4 | C | C | no |
| R5 | A | B | yes |

- Changes: R2, R3, R5 → 3 of 5 = 60%. Only these three get the "reason for change" sequence (p. 342).
- Suppose R2 says the new brand was the only one available. By the rule on p. 343 her sequence ends. Suppose R3 and R5 pass the earlier filters (p. 343) and say it was not the only one available; they then continue to the "how did you find out" questions.
- Result: 3 changes detected, 2 respondents traced through the full sequence. No memory of changing was needed.

## Concepts
- [[panel-design]] (new) — the same women interviewed in two waves, June and August 1945; some are lost by "mortality" (p. 340).
- [[opinion-leader]] — here a woman identified by her own report of being asked for advice, on two questions per area (p. 346).
- [[self-designation-index]] (new) — an opinion-leadership index per area built by combining the questions on being asked for advice (p. 347).
- [[impact-analysis]] (new) — the "reason for change" question sequence tracing influences on a recorded change (pp. 342–344).
- [[potential-influentials]] (new) — people whom a respondent regards as generally competent. They often differ considerably from actual advisers in a specific episode (p. 345).
- [[gregariousness]] (new) — a sociability indicator. Two questions from a larger set formed the index after a latent structure analysis (p. 347, fn. 4).
- [[life-cycle-position]] (new) — defined from age, marital status and number of children at home (p. 347).

## Canonical instances
- [[decatur-study-questionnaire]] (new) — the two-wave panel of Decatur women, grouped into eight question types (pp. 340–341).
- [[brand-change-impact-sequence]] (new) — the consumer-item sequence ending in a ranking of channels and a "most important cause" question (pp. 342–344).
- [[movie-advice-questions]] (new) — questions on being asked advice about pictures, which started the advisee follow-up (p. 346).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Wave II comparison of Wave I and Wave II answers ↔ survey methodology: panel change measurement versus recall. Both detect change without relying on memory.
- [structural-analogy] self-designated opinion leadership ↔ social network research: ego-reported measures of influence, possibly checked against alters.
- [metaphor] "mortality" in panel studies ↔ survival analysis: respondents lost between waves.

## Questions this raises
- How much do the two ways of detecting change (retrospective report, wave comparison) differ in what they find? The guide gives no discussion of this; it says only that some observations will be used in a general report on panel techniques then in preparation (p. 342).
- Which two questions formed the gregariousness index and which two the status index? Appendix D is cited for this (pp. 347–348).
- How well did the advisees' answers agree with the self-designated leaders' reports? The guide only says the ratings were checked in some cases (p. 347).
- What do the unreported tabulations at the Bureau show (p. 340)?
