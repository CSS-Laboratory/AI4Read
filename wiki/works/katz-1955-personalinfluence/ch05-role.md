# The Role of the Group in Influencing Change: Implications for Mass Media Research

## L0 — One line
Because opinions are anchored in group ties, groups can press members to change toward group norms, and groups resist or help outside influence depending on how that influence fits those norms and ties.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
If opinions are kept up with others, changing them cannot be a purely individual matter. The authors ask what small-group research says about change, and what it implies for mass media campaigns. Studies show that groups work as a *medium* of change (pressure from within, toward conformity). They also show groups as a *target*: outside influence meets strong resistance when it attacks norms shared with valued others. The authors judge that influence succeeds more readily when members see that others will go along, though this is not experimentally established. The "group decision" experiments are suggestive but flawed, and they leave open why groups accept change. The authors offer tentative ideas and add a speculation about opinion leaders.

**Prerequisites:** the "benefits" (instrumental) and "social reality" functions of conformity from earlier chapters; basic experimental design (control group, confounded factors).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Change in opinion is social, not only individual, because opinions are created and maintained with others (p. 66).
- Group as medium: seeking acceptance in a new group, or valuing a group, tends to push people toward the group norm (Bales, Asch, Duncker, Berenda, Back) (pp. 67–72).
- Prestige suggestion is expected to work only when the source is prestigious *for the subject*. Some evidence suggests peers and congenial groups may have more influence than adult authority (pp. 68–70).
- Group as target: an attempt to change a norm shared with valued others tends to fail (Kelley–Volkart) (pp. 73–74).
- Lewin-type group-decision experiments show discussion-plus-decision beating lecture or private instruction, but they have design flaws (pp. 74–77).
- The authors judge perceived support of others to seem the most important element; Bennett's work supports this (pp. 77–78).
- The studies give almost no indication of why resistant groups accept influence. Three tentative factors: link to deeper shared values, "objectification", and catharsis (pp. 78–80).
- Interpersonal relations can resist or facilitate mass influence, and the authors ask whether opinion leaders in every kind of group may be more responsive to outside suggestions and more influential in changing group standards (p. 81).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 66)
- If opinions and attitudes are created and maintained in association with others, a change in them cannot be a purely individual matter. There must be social repercussions (p. 66).
- Mass-media effectiveness, the starting point of the authors' interest in small groups, focuses on change. The authors therefore turn to small-group evidence on how opinions change, given that the individual is not as free to change alone as was once thought (p. 66).
- Earlier ideas (benefits of conformity; seeing things as others do) imply that changers will probably change toward a group norm (pp. 66–67).

### The Group as a Medium of Change (pp. 67–72)
Following Cartwright (fn. 10, p. 73), "medium" studies are those where the pressure for change arises *within* the group and aims at particular members. The subject is the individual, not the group.

**Moving to a new group.** Seeking acceptance in a new circle may lead a person to bring opinions into line with it, and may bring group support for him. This guides work in re-education, rehabilitation, leadership training and prejudice reduction (p. 67).
- *Bales on Alcoholics Anonymous (1945):* the newcomer first gets recognition instead of condemnation by admitting hidden thoughts and acts. Then he feels able to resist drink. A second, perhaps more important step makes him advice-giver, teacher and protagonist of the norms for newer members. At this point, according to Bales, he begins to feel that the craving itself has left him (p. 67).
- The group is thus both a support for the goal and the medium for an actual change in an emotional drive (p. 67). English studies of transitional therapeutic "communities" are mentioned as similar (p. 67).
- Seeking and gaining acceptance seem to lead to alignment with the new group. This corresponds to the *instrumental* function ("benefits") of conformity, as in Newcomb's Bennington study and the "green" replacements item in *The American Soldier* (p. 68).

**Prestige suggestion.** The typical experiment tries to change opinions by attributing contrary opinions to presumably prestigious figures or groups (p. 68). This corresponds somewhat to the instrumental function, but most of all to the "social reality" aspect of conformity (p. 68).
- To understand the effect or lack of effect of a suggestion, one must know how prestigious the source is *for each subject* (p. 68).
- *Asch (early study):* subjects ranking professions by importance changed much more when told the opinions of a group they thought congenial than of one they thought antagonistic (pp. 68–69).
- "Suggestion is not simply a bandwagon device." Only when a figure or group is particularly prestigious for the subject is influence expected (p. 69).
- A review cited in fn. 3 concludes that effectiveness of suggestion seems to depend on (a) clarity of the situation and (b) congeniality of the prestige source (p. 68).
- Gestalt psychologists' interviews of subjects apparently led to the idea that the peer group may be prestigious, and to the discovery that apparent changes of attitude are often not changes at all (fn. 5, p. 69).
- *Duncker (1938), children's food choices:* when an adult chose first, 4 of 17 children followed. When other children chose first, the same children made 17 identical choices in 20. Among children known to be close friends, agreement was complete (p. 69).
- The authors read this as follows: children saw adults as authorities, but authority alone was not enough. They followed peers, it seems, because they identified with them (p. 69).
- *Berenda (1950)*, using Asch's design, finds classmates more influential than teachers (pp. 69–70).
- The authors take this as some indication that "authority" figures may have less influence over opinions than congenial groups have (p. 70).

**Asch (1952): group pressure against the evidence of the senses.**
- Procedure: each subject sat with seven confederates. All matched a line to one of three comparison lines, and the naive subject answered last, after seven intentionally wrong, obvious answers (p. 70).
- Result: one-third of subjects yielded in half or more of their trials. Controls made virtually no errors (p. 70).
- Asch's own comment: the subject faced, possibly for the first time, a unanimous group contradicting his senses (p. 70).
- Fn. 8 (Asch reports apparently three kinds of yielders): (1) those whose perception is actually changed and who feel no doubt; (2) those whose judgment is distorted, the largest category, who assume they are wrong; (3) those who know the majority is wrong but follow out of a need not to look different or inferior. One quarter never yielded (p. 70).
- Fn. 7: pressure appears here even though the situation is verifiable. Ambiguity, thought a condition for social pressure (Sherif), is absent (p. 70).
- *Anticipated objection:* no real-life situation matches such an experiment. **Authors' reply:** they have reason to believe that media "monopolization" in totalitarian states is one major key to their communications effectiveness. With no counterpropaganda or open resistance, even propaganda based on a manifest "lie" has a very good chance (p. 71).
- Asch's variation: the subject faces a majority "of all but one" (a second naive subject, or a collaborator who answers correctly). Errors then drop dramatically (p. 71).
- Fn. 9: unopposed propaganda gives reason to expect comparatively greater effects. The authors note in passing ("One is tempted to note") that the "Emperor's New Clothes" tale is the best known experiment in prestige suggestion (p. 71).

**Cohesiveness (Back, 1952).**
- If motivated membership is tied to conformity pressure, pressure should be felt most by those most attached. Cohesiveness measures the strength of the ties holding members together (p. 71).
- Two-member groups of high or low cohesiveness were created by instructions making partners more or less attractive to each other. Each wrote an interpretation of photographs. Each partner's set differed slightly, so they met different interpretations when conferring (pp. 71–72).
- Low-cohesive pairs tended to react passively to the difference. High-cohesive pairs tended to set about reconciling it (p. 72).
- Asked "Did you think that your partner tried to influence you?", less than half of low-cohesive members said yes, against more than two-thirds of high-cohesive members (p. 72).
- Influencing was more successful in high cohesiveness (p. 72).
- *Section conclusion:* in so far as relations are positively motivated, they are continuously active in preserving norms, disciplining deviates and pressing toward conformity (p. 72).

> **Intuition note.** In these studies, the people around you do more than signal what others think. They tend to push you actively toward the group view. The push is felt more by people who are more attached to the group, and people seeking acceptance in a group tend to bring their views into line with it.

### The Group as a Target of Change (pp. 73–81)
Question: do interpersonal relations also modify the effect of *outside* mass-media campaigns on individuals? (p. 73). In "target" studies, pressure originates outside the group but tries to reach individuals by influencing their groups. Such attempts presuppose the group's strength as a medium (fn. 10, p. 73).

**Resistance (Kelley & Volkart, 1952).**
- Expectation: an attempt to change an opinion will not succeed if the person shares the opinion with attached others who do not go along with the change (p. 73).
- Boy Scouts were sorted by questionnaire into those who valued troop membership highly and those who did not. A guest speaker implicitly attacked camping and woodcraft. A "before/after" questionnaire measured change (pp. 73–74).
- Result, as the authors report it: members can be influenced to deviate from group values to the extent that membership matters less to them. Those who value membership highly resist outside attempts to change opinions shared with the group (p. 74).
- The authors write that an influence attempt assailing opinions shared with highly valued others "will surely be resisted". They count this as concrete evidence of how interpersonal relations modify media effects (p. 74).

> **Intuition note.** The more a person values the group, the less an outside message that attacks the group's view moves them. The group acts as a shield.

**Facilitation: Lewin's "group decision" experiments (pp. 74–76).** Underlying hypothesis: a person responds more readily to influence when he perceives that others support the change (p. 74). The studies try to show that group discussion followed by "group decision" is effective (p. 74).
- *Maternity hospital:* mothers received child-care instruction privately, or in groups of six guided to a "decision" (hands raised). The time for the group discussion was not more than that for one mother, and the subject matter was the same. After two weeks and a month, group participants adhered much more closely to the program (pp. 74–75).
- *Wartime meat campaign:* three club-women groups heard a lecture. Three discussed the problem, including obstacles for "housewives like ourselves", and raised hands. Later, 3% of lecture women and 32% of discussion women had served the meats. Lecturer and leader differed, and content was not matched (p. 75).
- *Milk consumption:* six neighbourhood housewife groups split between lecture and discussion. The same person led both, and content was comparable, "we are told". At two and four weeks, group decision was considerably more effective (pp. 75–76).
- *Foremen:* 29 foremen in a 400-man factory were randomly split into lecture, discussion and control groups. Ratings showed higher marks for higher-grade jobs, which management felt was a halo effect. The lecture group was told how to rate equitably. The discussion group reached the same "decision" itself. Time and subject ("how to rate the man, not the job") were equal. At the next rating period, only the discussion group substantially revised its habits (p. 76).
- Conclusion: apparently something about interacting with others about a proposed change, versus isolation, produces marked behaviour change. Which element is not evident (pp. 76–77).

**Problem (1): design flaws and unisolated elements (p. 77).**
- In some studies the content seems to have differed, in others we are not told whether time was controlled, and in one the discussion group alone was forewarned of a follow-up. These are simple matters, mostly handled in newer studies (p. 77).
- More important: discussion, decision and perception of others' opinions were not isolated. Discussion or involvement alone might explain the effect, or the decision alone, or perceived willingness of others (p. 77).
- **Authors' opinion:** the last factor seems most important (p. 77). Fn. 16 asks why a hands-raised "decision" was not tested after a lecture (p. 77).
- *Bennett (1952), dissertation:* decision and perceived unanimity of the decision were significantly related to carrying out the action. Discussion, other things equal, was not more effective than lecture. Public identification of individual decisions did not contribute appreciably (pp. 77–78).
- The authors assume the lecture and private subjects may have been as motivated, but acting meant a unilateral departure, as far as they knew, from accepted ways. "All this is far from being experimentally established" (p. 78).

**Problem (2): why was the reception cordial? (pp. 78–79).** An influence that seeks to change a shared way of behaving is likely to meet very strong resistance (the Boy Scouts), so why did these groups accept it? The authors ask:
- how the influence enters, and whether someone changes first and then influences the others from within;
- when members begin to see the standard shifting;
- what changes which groups accept, and whether any influence can pass when people sit with peers;
- whether a durable, intimate group differs from one assembled and dispersed (pp. 78–79; fn. 17 notes that the maternity-hospital groups were brought together for the occasion, while in the other experiments members tended to know each other more or less well, p. 79).

No comprehensive answers exist. Reviewing studies and tentative summaries (Lewin, Cartwright, Festinger; fn. 18) leads to three generalizations (p. 79):
- **(a) Link to deeper shared values.** The influence may, at least in part, appeal to deeper values that are also part of group norms. Success may depend on "wiring up" the new norm to a shared basic value. The group then changes the *frame of reference* rather than substituting norms. Example: housewives may have come to see unpopular meats as belonging under the norm of patriotism (pp. 79–80).
- **(b) "Objectification".** Fact-finding and talk of what "housewives like ourselves" would do may be clues to locating the wedge by which a new influence enters a group. The authors say this goes only "a little distance" toward answering "how" (p. 80).
- **(c) Catharsis.** Discussion may "talk out" emotional insulation around an attitude. This makes the norm more vulnerable to a counter-proposal (p. 80). The text numbers this "(3)".
- Fn. 19 gives a guess: the only difference between groups and individuals here is perceived social support of significant others and the enforcement interacting individuals require of each other, particularly when important group values are concerned. It cites Cartwright: the more relevant a change is to the basis of the group's attraction, the greater the group's influence (p. 80).
- *Ringer's suggestion (fn. 21):* some groups were assembled for the occasion. Success may then reflect a new group norm being set for the first time, with only private standards changed. The authors extend this: even the experiments with real-life groups may not have changed an already anchored norm, since those groups may have had no felt standard about food. They add that if an influence attempt really hit a norm of a real-life group tied to a salient group concern, resistance might be particularly strong (pp. 80–81).

> **Intuition note.** Groups do change, but the studies do not show how. The authors' tentative guesses are that the change is framed as fitting what the group already values, that fact-finding talk gives the influence a way in, and that talking things through lowers emotional barriers. Ringer and the authors add that some groups may have had no prior norm.

**Closing (p. 81).** The authors' opinion-leader interest leads them to ask whether it might not be that opinion leaders in every kind of group tend to be more responsive to extra-group suggestions and more influential in changing group standards (p. 81). Interpersonal relations "intervene" in two ways. They induce resistance to influences counter to ideas shared with esteemed others. They may facilitate change when shared norms harmonise with the outside influence, or when members are willing to incorporate the change into group norms (p. 81).

## Toy example (ours, invented numbers; not from the text)
Aim: show why the group-decision studies cannot isolate "perceived others' support".
- Real-style design: 10 people discuss and decide by hands; 10 hear a lecture. After a month, 8 of the discussion people and 1 of the lecture people act. Difference: 8/10 − 1/10 = 0.7. But discussion, decision and perceived unanimity all changed together, so the 0.7 cannot be assigned to any one factor.
- Hypothetical isolating design: two more lecture groups of 10, both with a hands-raised decision. In group A all 10 hands are shown. In group B only 3 hands are shown. Suppose 6 and 3 act. Then A − B = 6/10 − 3/10 = 0.3, with the lecture format held fixed and only the perceived support varying.
- Compare with the plain lecture (1/10): the decision alone with weak support adds 3/10 − 1/10 = 0.2. This is the kind of separation the authors say is missing (p. 77).

## Concepts
- [[group-as-medium-of-change]] (new) — pressure for change arises inside the group and targets members (fn. 10, p. 73; pp. 67–72)
- [[group-as-target-of-change]] (new) — outside pressure that tries to reach individuals by first influencing their groups (fn. 10, p. 73)
- [[prestige-suggestion]] (new) — changing opinion by attributing contrary views to prestigious sources; an effect is expected only if the source is prestigious for the subject (pp. 68–69)
- [[group-cohesiveness]] (new) — strength of the ties holding members together (p. 71)
- [[group-decision-method]] (new) — Lewin-style discussion ending in a show-of-hands "decision" to comply (pp. 74–75)
- [[perceived-unanimity]] (new) — a person's perception that others will go along with the change; the authors' key candidate factor (pp. 77–78)
- [[opinion-leader]] — here only a speculation that leaders are more responsive to outside suggestions and more influential in group change (p. 81)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Group as target of change ↔ communication-theory: two-step flow, where interpersonal relations mediate media effects (pp. 73, 81) — same mediating role of personal ties.
- [structural-analogy] Authors' unopposed-propaganda argument (p. 71) ↔ limited effects paradigm: effects are larger when counter-influence is absent, smaller when groups intervene.
- [metaphor] Wiring a new norm to a shared value (p. 79) ↔ framing: both change the frame in which behaviour is seen. Framing is not stated in this unit (canon link).

## Questions this raises
- Which factor is causal: decision, discussion or perceived unanimity? What design would separate them (p. 77)?
- Do real durable groups resist more than ad hoc groups, as Ringer's idea suggests (pp. 80–81)?
- Do opinion leaders change first and then pull the group (pp. 78, 81)?
- How far does the Asch-style yielding transfer to mass-media settings beyond monopolised ones (p. 71)?
