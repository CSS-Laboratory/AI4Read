# Fashion Leaders

## L0 — One line
Fashion opinion leadership depends most on life-cycle position, and, the authors suggest, also on social context (who can seek and recognise advice), not on personal interest alone.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Who are the fashion opinion leaders, and why? Katz and Lazarsfeld find that life-cycle position matters most: leadership falls from girls to matrons, and this is not just a matter of interest, since equally interested older women lead less. They suggest an explanation by context: a leader needs followers who see her as a fit adviser. Gregariousness and, unevenly, social status also matter; high-status, highly gregarious women lead less than expected. An "export" index infers which groups supply advice to others. Follow-up data show age peers as the main source of influence outside the family, but not the expected young-to-old flow there.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and cross-tabulation (holding a third variable constant); ratios and simple indexes; the earlier chapters' marketing-leader study, life-cycle types, gregariousness index and follow-up interviews (used here, not defined).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Fashion leadership falls with each life-cycle step: girls 48%, small-family wives 31%, large-family wives 18%, matrons 14% (p. 248).
- Fashion interest is also highest among girls, but interest does not explain away life-cycle: at equal high interest, girls lead far more than matrons (pp. 250–252).
- The authors think the clue is that advice-giving needs two people; a highly interested woman must also have contact with people who seek advice and accept her as an adviser (pp. 253, 261).
- An "export" index (leader rate relative to interest rate, scaled so the sample average is 1) infers which groups supply advice to outsiders: girls 1.33 (p. 256).
- Outside the family, follow-up pairs show age peers as the main source of influence (about six in ten); within the family fewer than half are the same age. The expected young-to-old flow shows up only as a slight trend within the family, not outside it (pp. 257–258).
- Gregariousness raises leadership, and matters more the later the life-cycle position; for matrons and large-family wives high gregariousness is a necessary condition for a better-than-average chance (pp. 259–263).
- Status matters modestly: leadership is equal at high and middle status and lower at low status. The authors suggest high-status women talk about fashion less (pp. 265–266).
- Status and gregariousness act independently except at high status plus high gregariousness, where leadership drops (p. 269).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 247)
About two-thirds of the sample women said they had recently made a fashion change, and most said personal influence entered the decision (p. 247). The task is to locate the opinion leaders (leaders of change) here. Questions: where do they concentrate by life-cycle, status and gregariousness (p. 247)?

**Definition of leader (fn. 1, p. 247).** A woman is a leader if (a) on both interviews she said she had recently been asked for advice on clothes, cosmetics, etc.; or (b) on only one interview she said so, but considered herself "more likely" than others to be consulted (p. 247).

### The Girls: Life-Cycle Position and Fashion Leadership (pp. 247–249)
**Expectation (authors).** America stresses youth, so leadership should be more typical of girls. Girls are single, many "on the market" for dates and marriage, and have fewer competing interests. Marriage (a goal achieved), motherhood (a competing interest) and matronhood (withdrawal from youth-oriented competitiveness) should each lower leadership (p. 248).

**Finding (Chart IX, p. 248).** Share who are fashion leaders:

| Girls (n=77) | Small-family wives (174) | Large-family wives (169) | Matrons (252) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 48% | 31% | 18% | 14% |

The authors read this as the cumulative effect of age, marriage and motherhood (p. 249).

In marketing, interest was assumed near-universal among married women. For fashion, universality of interest cannot be assumed (p. 249).

### Does Interest Determine Leadership? (pp. 249–252)
**Interest index (pp. 249–250).** Three questions: (1) is being in style very, moderately or not important? (2) any recent change toward something more fashionable? (3) number of new dresses bought or made in the past twelve months. The questions are phrased so that income matters little: a cheap perfume or a short skirt counts as much as an expensive change (p. 249).

Table 19 (n=711, p. 250): very important 37%, moderately 55%, not 8%; recent change yes 56%, no 44%; dresses: 7 or more 29%, 5–6 27%, 3–4 25%, 1–2 13%, none 6%.

**Scoring (fn. 2, p. 250).** "High" = "very important"; "yes" to change; 5+ dresses. Count of high answers: 3 → 18%, 2 → 33%, 1 → 30%, 0 → 19%. "High interest" = 2 or 3 high answers (p. 250).

**Interest by life-cycle (Table 20, p. 250).** High interest: girls 80% (77), small-family wives 59% (173), large-family wives 56% (169), matrons 34% (252). Interest is high among under-45s (a majority) and lower among those 45+ (p. 251).

**Interest and leadership (Table 21, p. 251).** Leaders by interest score: 3 → 49% (129); 2 → 28% (235); 1 → 13% (212); 0 → 4% (135). That is about twelve times as many leaders at the top as at the bottom (p. 251).

**Test (p. 251–252).** *Conjecture:* leadership is determined only by interest, so equally interested women of different life-cycle types should lead equally often (pp. 251–252).

Table 22 (p. 252), % leaders, with interest controlled (high = 2–3 high replies, fn. 3):

| Interest | Girls | Small-family wives | Large-family wives | Matrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High | 55% (62) | 46% (102) | 20% (95) | 27% (84) |
| Low | 20% (15) | 10% (71) | 16% (75) | 7% (168) |

The authors say Table 22 "does not bear out our conjecture at all": life-cycle differences remain at equal high interest (p. 252). Fn. 4 (p. 252): at low interest the results are irregular and the differences small. The authors hypothesise that life-cycle position cannot appreciably affect leadership chances for a woman who is not personally interested. Its effect would be largest at high interest and "almost, but not quite" gone at low interest.

> **Intuition note.** Being interested is not enough. A highly interested matron leads far less often than an equally interested girl. The authors infer that a woman's surroundings, not only her taste, also matter for whether she becomes a leader.

### The Flow of Influence: Two Approaches (pp. 252–258)
**Hypothesis (authors).** Advice-giving involves two people. A highly interested matron may be "ready" to lead but lacks followers. Other matrons are rarely interested, and younger women have different fashion problems (p. 253).

Among girls the "demand" for advice is probably lively, so a high-interest girl is likely to be consulted by another girl (p. 253). Even if advice crosses life-cycle lines, the authors think it is more likely that older women go to the young, for two reasons:
- The fashion market is geared to youth, so girls are the avant garde (p. 253).
- A woman who does not know whom to ask may well know girls are most often interested (8 in 10 girls, versus one-half of wives and one-third of matrons, Table 20). The maxim is to "look where you know there's a concentration of what you're looking for" (p. 253).

**Wives (p. 254).** Small- and large-family wives have almost equal interest. Yet small-family wives have almost as many leaders as highly interested girls, while large-family wives have as few as the uninterested matrons. The authors have no evidence but guess that small-family wives face faster-changing trends and have fewer competing everyday problems. Many marketing leaders among large-family wives is "some evidence" for this. They say this needs testing (p. 254).

**Assumption (p. 254).** The authors assume that fashion influence tends to take place most of all among women of similar life-cycle position; only the follow-up data permit a test of this. The export approach also presumes the life-cycle categories are real groups (p. 254).

#### (1) The "export" index (pp. 254–257)
**Idea.** The rate of opinion leadership in a group is the resultant of two forces: "saliency" (interest in the topic in the group) and "attractiveness" (how far other groups use the group as an adviser). So the leader share has two components: exchange within the group and interaction with other groups (pp. 254–255). Since leadership rate and saliency are measured, attractiveness ought to be deducible (p. 255).

**Computation (p. 255–256).** Fashion interest measures saliency. Whole sample (672 with sufficient information): 155 leaders, 345 highly interested, so
$$\text{average ratio}=\frac{155}{345}=.45 .$$
Slightly less than one leader per two highly interested women (p. 256). The index sets .45 = 1; for each group, (leaders ÷ highly interested) is expressed relative to .45 (p. 256). In symbols (our notation): $\text{index}_g=\dfrac{L_g/I_g}{0.45}$.

**Reading rules (p. 256).** Index near 1: the group's leader supply about equals internal demand. Index above 1: the group supplies itself and is approached by others. Index below 1: the group lacks leaders for itself and presumably turns to others.

Table 23 (p. 256): girls 1.33; small-family wives 1.18; large-family wives .71; matrons .93.

*(added step, check)* From Chart IX and Table 20, girls give $(0.48/0.80)/0.45 = 0.60/0.45 \approx 1.33$, matching the printed value.

Conclusion (p. 256–257): small-family wives and matrons have about enough leaders for their own interest; large-family wives fewer than their interest requires (fn. 5: they may be a less self-contained "group" in fashion); girls have an "overabundance" and so meet internal demand and "export" advice (p. 257).

> **Intuition note.** If a group has more leaders than its interest level would need, the surplus is assumed to serve outsiders. This is an inference from two rates; the index itself does not observe anyone giving advice across groups (the follow-up pairs are a separate check).

#### (2) The follow-up story (pp. 257–259)
**Expectations (p. 257).** (1) Fashion influence is mostly among life-cycle peers; (2) when it crosses ages, young women are more often influentials. The follow-up data concern age, not life-cycle, and are only "suggestive" (p. 257). 33 follow-ups were with family-named leaders; 125 with non-family influentials (p. 257).

Table 24 (within family, p. 257), rows = leader's age, columns = influencee's age 15–24 / 25–44 / 45+ / total:
- 15–24: 4, 4, 6 / 14
- 25–44: 5, 9, – / 14
- 45+: 2, 1, 2 / 5
- Totals: 11, 14, 8 / 33

Fewer than half the pairs are the same age. When ages differ, influence flows somewhat more from young to old; 6 of 8 women over 45 are influenced by women aged 15–24 (pp. 257–258).

Table 25 (outside the family, p. 258):
- 15–24: 27, 12, 2 / 41
- 25–44: 13, 32, 9 / 54
- 45+: 2, 14, 14 / 30
- Totals: 42, 58, 25 / 125

About six in ten pairs are age peers. Among pairs crossing age lines, influence went "no more" from young to old than from old to young. So age peers dominate, but the young-to-old expectation is not confirmed. The authors say it is not clear why this does not support Table 23, and they are left with the "export" suggestion without direct confirmation (p. 258).

**Men (p. 258).** Of many hundreds of women crediting a fashion decision partly to someone's advice, only 13 named men, 11 of them husbands. Men are "spectators rather than direct participants"; their influence is indirect and passive (pp. 258–259).

### Gregariousness and Fashion Leadership (pp. 259–263)
Chart X (p. 259): leaders among high / medium / low gregariousness = 29% (186) / 23% (323) / 15% (194), so twice as many among the high as among the low.

**Reasoning (authors; pp. 259–260).** A woman with many contacts has more opportunity to lead. Also, gregariousness may indicate sensitivity to the impression she makes, which she expresses by being in style. So gregarious women may be more accessible and also under pressure to follow fashion. If so, interest should be higher among them.

Table 26 (p. 260), % with high interest: high gregariousness 56% (178); medium 54% (323); low 43% (174). The relationship is "by no means as strong as we had anticipated"; interest is stimulated, "but not very much". So gregarious women are somewhat more likely to be asked even without much interest. Fn. 6: among low-interest women, 17% of the highly gregarious but only 5% of the ungregarious are leaders (p. 260).

**Conclusion (pp. 260–261).** Personal interest does not by itself create leadership. A leader needs contact with people who (1) seek leadership and (2) recognise her qualities as appropriate. Highly interested girls lead, equally interested matrons do not; highly gregarious women lead, ungregarious ones do not, while interest is almost equal across gregariousness levels (pp. 260–261).

Table 27 (p. 261), export index (average = 1; fn. 7: sample ratio .42 here, versus .45 above due to slightly different case counts): high 1.24; medium 1.02; low .81. Only the highly gregarious have an "excess" and so, on the authors' interpretation, "export" advice (p. 261).

**Joint effect: hypothesis (pp. 261–262).** Gregariousness should matter most for women whose life-cycle position restricts fashion participation (time, energy, money, "social location"): large-family wives and matrons. It should matter least for girls, whose urge toward fashion does not depend completely on it. Small-family wives are in between (p. 262). Test: split each life-cycle type by gregariousness (twelve groups) (p. 262).

Table 28 (p. 263): "x" marks groups with a better-than-average chance (fn. 8: leader-to-non-leader ratio above the sample's):
- High gregariousness: girls, small wives, large wives, matrons.
- Medium: girls, small wives.
- Low: girls only.

All girls, whatever their gregariousness; small-family wives only if at least moderately gregarious; large-family wives and matrons only if highly gregarious. For older or family-anchored women, high gregariousness is "a necessary condition" for a good chance. It seems to act as an equivalent of youth and freedom from family duties (p. 263).

> **Intuition note.** The authors expect that the less the life cycle itself pushes a woman toward fashion, the more a high degree of gregariousness matters for her chance of leading. It seems to act as an equivalent of being young and unburdened.

### Social Status and Fashion Leadership (pp. 263–268)
**Authors' reasoning (pp. 263–264).** The popular image is the glamorous woman showing expensive fashions. The authors study face-to-face influence, where advisers are personally known and similar in status. Advice-seekers turn to people with similar problems. This predicts leaders on all strata and little crossing of status lines (p. 264). Against this: women may consult those slightly above them, and fashion interest or the value of being in style may rise with status. Then leader proportions should rise with status. Equal interest and similarity-seeking would give equal proportions (p. 264).

Chart XI (p. 265): leaders: high status 26% (196); middle 26% (250); low 16% (244). The low level is 10 points below the other two. This is hard to interpret: why no difference between middle and high (p. 265)?

Table 29 (p. 265), high interest: high status 61% (191); middle 53% (253); low 42% (237). Interest rises with status, so the puzzle is why high-status women, given their greater interest, are not more often leaders (p. 266).

**Interpretation (authors, no data to test; p. 266).** High-status women "simply talk about it less". Reasons offered: (1) fashion is one item on a fuller agenda (public affairs, charity, clubs); (2) perhaps deliberate unconcern, since asking for advice may not be good gamesmanship nor offering it good taste. As before, it is a contextual factor, here avoidance of fashion as a topic rather than low interest (p. 266).

Table 30 (p. 267), export index: high status 1.00; middle 1.14; low .88. High-status leaders just satisfy their own interest; the low level has a slight predominance of interest over leadership; only the middle level has an excess, a slight one. The main conclusion is that leadership within each stratum is relatively self-contained (p. 267).

Table 31 (outside the family, p. 267), rows = leader's status, columns = influencee's status Low / Middle / High / total:
- Low: 5, 7, 3 / 15
- Middle: 17, 47, 11 / 75
- High: 2, 13, 17 / 32
- Totals: 24, 67, 31 / 122

Status is the interviewers' A–B–C–D rating (fn. 9). 57% of transactions are between equals. Only low-status women went outside their class appreciably, and crossing was almost always to adjacent levels. The author says the middle level has more influentials (75) than influencees (67) and is the only one to do so, in line with Table 30 (p. 268).

### The Joint Role of Status Level and Gregariousness (pp. 268–269)
In marketing, the upper-status excess of leaders was a mere reflection of gregariousness. Does the same hold here (p. 268)?

Table 32 (p. 268), % leaders (cell sizes not shown):

| Gregariousness | High status | Middle status | Low status |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | 22% | 36% | 24% |
| Medium | 31% | 24% | 17% |
| Low | 21% | 17% | 11% |

Highest-status women have the highest interest, yet no more leadership than the middle level. The table shows the drop is among the most gregarious high-status women (22%), unlike the other levels, where the most gregarious have the best chances. The authors take this to support their suggestion that these women do not talk much about fashion (p. 269). Apart from this group, leader proportion rises both with status and with gregariousness. Each contributes "modestly, and independently" (p. 269).

### The Flow of Fashion Influence: A Summary (pp. 269–270)
- As in marketing, leadership depends most of all on life-cycle type, but in fashion the girls, not large-family wives, are key influentials (p. 269).
- In marketing only gregariousness joined life-cycle. In fashion status counts too, unevenly: high status plus high gregariousness gives a drop in leadership. The lowest status yielded fewer leaders, and apart from that exception it would appear that status still has some part even with gregariousness controlled (p. 270).
- Leader and follower are fairly similar in age and status. Young-to-old flow is supported by the export index and family follow-ups, but not by extra-household pairs (p. 270).
- Across status lines influence came somewhat more from middle-status women; across gregariousness levels, somewhat more from the most gregarious (p. 270).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two invented groups, 100 women each.
- Group A: 60 highly interested, 36 leaders.
- Group B: 40 highly interested, 12 leaders.
- Whole sample: 200 women, 100 highly interested, 48 leaders, so average ratio = 48/100 = 0.48 (this plays the role of the book's .45).
- Group A ratio = 36/60 = 0.60; index = 0.60/0.48 = 1.25 (above 1: surplus leaders, read as "exporting").
- Group B ratio = 12/40 = 0.30; index = 0.30/0.48 = 0.625 (below 1: too few leaders for its interest, read as turning to others).
- Check: A's expected leaders at the average ratio = 60 × 0.48 = 28.8, so 36 is 7.2 above. B's = 40 × 0.48 = 19.2, so 12 is 7.2 below. In this toy example the surplus and deficit balance exactly.

## Concepts

- [[homophily-heterophily]] — the authors expect and find similar age and status between influential and influencee (word not used here) (pp. 258, 264, 267)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Controlling for interest (Table 22) ↔ statistics: stratifying a cross-tab to compare conditional proportions given a third variable.
- [structural-analogy] Leaders and followers similar in age and status ↔ communication-theory canon, homophily/heterophily: influence mostly flows among similar people.
- [structural-analogy] "Export" index ↔ economics: a group's share of an activity relative to the overall share, read as net supply to outsiders.
- [metaphor] Predisposition "not activated" without followers ↔ diffusion research: adoption or influence needs the right contacts, not only a ready individual.
