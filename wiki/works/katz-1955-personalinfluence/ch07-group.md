# The Group and the World Outside: Implications for Mass Media Research

## L0 — One line
Outside messages may have to pass "gatekeepers" to reach a group, and may then be passed on or blocked by influentials and group norms, so mass media research must study people inside their groups, not as isolated respondents.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do influences from outside a small group get in, and what follows for mass media research? Katz and Lazarsfeld argue that gatekeepers link a group to its relevant environment. Situational, structural and cultural factors are as important in choosing them as in choosing leaders inside the group. Gatekeeping is often, but not necessarily, joined with influence inside the group. Studies of a camp, a factory, a street gang, Soviet agitation, a Greek village, Israeli immigrants and Lebanese villages illustrate this. Mass media influence may be blocked by a gatekeeper, an influential or group norms. So respondents must be studied in their group context. The chapter ends with a research agenda.

**Prerequisites:** the opinion leader and two-step flow ideas; group norms; a rough sense of small-group sociology (primary groups, informal leaders).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The two-step relay is supported by some evidence, but the authors call it modest in quantity and scope (p. 117). The chapter asks how outside influence gets into a group at all.
- A gatekeeper (Lewin's term) controls a strategic part of a channel and decides whether what flows through it enters the group (p. 119). The authors treat the gatekeeper as closely related to the opinion leader (p. 118, fn. 3).
- Situational, structural and cultural factors are as important in selecting gatekeepers as in determining the leaders inside the group (p. 118).
- Gatekeeper, originator, transmitter and influential are separable roles. Gatekeeping often goes with influence but need not (pp. 119, 123).
- Organizations and rulers (a camp administration, a settlement house, Soviet agitators) are most effective when they work through informal leaders and group ties (pp. 120–123).
- In traditional communities, new gatekeepers can arise when old leaders cannot link the group to a newly relevant context. In these cases gatekeeping came first and influence may follow (pp. 126–129).
- Mass media influence can fail at three points: gatekeeper, influential, group norms. Success may require using all three (pp. 130–131).
- Mass media research must study respondents within their groups, not as a random sample of disconnected individuals. The chapter lists six research questions (pp. 131–133).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening and "Communicating to the Group" (pp. 116–119)
- Earlier chapters examined communication *within* the group. This chapter asks what that means for mass media effectiveness (p. 116). The authors have so far assumed that media influences "somehow" enter interpersonal networks. Their implications hold *provided* that media do hook up with these networks (p. 116).
- Evidence for the two-step relay exists in several spheres, but it is "quite modest" and covers few areas. Part Two of the book returns to it (p. 117).
- The task here is to find links between a group's internal communication and the "outside" (p. 117).
- **Situational factor.** Defining a situation includes naming its relevant environment. A minority group may nominate "ambassadors" to the majority. In other situations its own culture or the "old country" matters more, and this produces different relay roles (p. 117).
  - Myrdal (accommodating leadership) and Lewin (self-hatred) discuss the leadership that relates minorities to the majority where the minority aspires to acceptance by the majority (p. 117).
  - Merton found that one kind of influential introduces "local" news and another introduces "cosmopolitan" news (p. 117).
- **Structural factor.** Some people sit at a strategic point relative to an environment. Examples: the boss's secretary for office workers; a minority member with majority contacts; the first family with a television set (p. 118).
- **Cultural factor.** Some people relay by cultural role. Examples: adolescents bring pop culture to the family; the family's division of Sunday-paper sections; the sports-section reader (father, perhaps) may become opinion leader during the World Series (p. 118).
- Situational, structural and cultural elements are as important in choosing who links a group to its environment as they are in choosing leaders inside it (p. 118).
- **Gatekeeper** (Lewin, 1952): someone who links interpersonal networks to something outside. Gatekeeping means controlling a strategic portion of a channel (goods, news or people) so as to decide whether what flows enters the group (pp. 118–119).
  - Lewin's example was wartime food-habit campaigns. For food bought at the grocery, the mother (sometimes the maid) is the gatekeeper. For food grown in the garden, father is. So campaigns may do best when aimed at one of these gatekeepers (p. 119).
  - Footnote caveat: even if father is gatekeeper, that does not mean his decision is made independently of the family, nor that the family will eat what he grows. By parallel, an editor may print an item without making readers read or believe it (p. 119, fn. 4).
- Earlier roles (originator, transmitter, influential) apply here too. A gatekeeper may be only an originator who introduces an idea. He may or may not transmit it within the group, and may or may not be influential. Whether gatekeeping leads to influentiality is an important question that runs through the following pages (p. 119).

*Intuition note.* A message reaching the edge of a group is not yet inside it. Someone may stand at the door, and the door-keeper need not be someone the group listens to.

### The Group in an Organizational Context (pp. 120–124)
Plan: a factory, a Japanese relocation center and Soviet Russia first, then international communications research (p. 120).
- **Theme.** Communications sent down an organization are most effective when funneled through the informal leaders of the informal groups that emerge at every level. Shils (1951) cites several studies that conclude the group's normally chosen leaders must mediate if top-down communications are to be effective at all (p. 120).
- **Leighton (1945), relocation center.**
  - The residents' Council was not truly representative. As mistrust grew, it became less adequate as a medium of information and influence (p. 120).
  - Leighton notes a tendency for administrators to have an "almost superstitious credence" in the grapevine and to assume that telling one evacuee meant telling all (pp. 120–121).
  - Only in a real crisis did informal primary groups send their leaders forward. A new Council was then elected, and two-way communication quickly improved (p. 121).
  - Lesson: administrations deal with informally organized groups and must take account of the leadership patterns that emerge (p. 121).
  - Two leadership patterns existed. Young American-reared residents stressed the elected individual who takes responsibility. Most older people held that elders and family heads should lead. The authors read this as a cultural difference between nomination and certification (p. 121).
- **Whyte (1945), Cornerville.** The settlement house did not bring in the Nortons or any other gang. Its staff were unlike the residents in values and behavior, and it aimed at reaching individuals rather than the gang as a whole via its leadership (pp. 121–122). (The text layer is garbled at "whole"; see Reading notes.)
- **Inkeles (1952), Soviet Union.** In the 1930s the Party wanted agitation on a large scale, not left to a small corps of specialists (p. 122).
  - The successful agitator knows how each person in his shop lives, and he builds a relation of "social solidarity" with his work group, like a family (p. 122).
  - Authors' caution: they are developing a theory of communication, not of agitation. Within certain contexts, communication includes passing on a set line in a system that strictly controls all channels, eliminates idea-competition and knows the value of repetition, surveillance and informal opinion leadership (pp. 122–123).
  - Footnote 8 cites Lazarsfeld and Merton (1949): monopolization of the media plus supplementation by face-to-face influencing help explain the effectiveness of totalitarian propaganda (p. 123).
- **Groups reach out themselves.** Informal groups also take the initiative in connecting to contexts they consider important, through group "ambassadors" (p. 123).
  - Horsfall and Arensberg (1949): a 28-man shoe-factory production room with four teams of seven. Each team's context was the other three teams. Men named as leaders were distinguished by a high degree of inter-team contact (p. 123).
  - In *Street Corner Society*, part of the gang leader's prestige came from extragroup contacts with politicians, racketeers and other gangs (p. 123).
- **Which comes first?** Gatekeeping is often coupled with in-group influence, though the two "need not necessarily go together" (p. 123). Does group leadership lead to gatekeeping, or does the ability to link the group to its context lead to influence? Sometimes one comes first, sometimes the other (pp. 123–124).

*Intuition note.* Authorities who ignore informal group leaders risk being ineffective. Groups also pick leaders partly for their outside contacts. Gatekeeping and influence overlap often, but each can exist without the other.

### The Traditional Community: Examples from International Communications Research (pp. 124–130)
The new question: through which channels does world news enter a village, and how do villagers' attitudes form? (p. 124).
- **Kalos, a Greek village (Stycos, 1952).** The population is almost totally illiterate (p. 124).
  - The teacher, one of two men who can read, gets a newspaper and relays the parts he thinks worth relaying to "opinion carriers." They seek news either for its own sake or to gain prestige as relayers themselves (pp. 124–125).
  - He is the community's only living link to the outside, and he makes an annual city trip, so in the authors' terms he is gatekeeper. They also describe his role as both gatekeeper and influential (p. 125).
  - Contrast: the tavern keeper who supervises the town's one radio is an "information controller." Such people, usually illiterate and of low status, are not better informed or more influential. They are only strategically placed for the physical flow of radio (p. 125).
- **Israel (Eisenstadt, 1952).** Immigrants from many cultures were studied for integration and absorption (p. 125).
  - Eastern, highly traditional groups have "traditionally defined situations" that serve as focal points of communication: the Sabbath synagogue gathering, arbitration court, traditional school. The major communication links lie in these settings (p. 125).
  - Spontaneous, informal contacts there tend to be between peers and seem very much less effective than the formal occasions for the flow of influence. European groups show more informal transmission, different opinion leaders for different topics, and wider participation by the rank and file (pp. 125–126).
  - Eisenstadt's suggestion: when leaders can no longer relate their group to a larger social system so that members understand and accept their place in it, new leaders who can do so are likely to arise (p. 126).
  - Requirements for traditional leaders to keep influence: helping members gain status in the new system, mediating the new country's values and demands, explaining its institutions (p. 126). When they cannot or will not, new gatekeepers and influentials tend to encroach on their role (p. 126).
  - Footnote 12 lists five reasons given by those who left their old leaders. They include disillusion with the leaders' ability to secure rights and to interpret the new system, doubts about their prestige in it, and the feeling that attachment to them blocks full status (pp. 126–127).
- **Lebanon (McPhee and Meyersohn, 1951).**
  - The section is a hypothetical reconstruction based on a small number of cases. The authors caution it is tentative (p. 127). The sample was weighted toward radio listeners and newspaper readers, hence few illiterate peasants (p. 127, fn. 14).
  - The young are the new opinion leaders who introduce world news. Youth is not enough. They are young people with some education who stay in contact with their families and illiterate neighbors, and who are mobile (the "trip to the city") (p. 127).
  - Contact with formal media seems to follow mainly from city trips (pp. 127–128).
  - The study's hypothesis: as the outside world enters the village, it tends to "by-pass the traditional leaders" (p. 128).
  - The authors say this seems to be one step earlier than the stage at which Eisenstadt reports traditional leaders losing effectiveness, or perhaps the beginnings of a possibly temporary specialization. Elders keep "local" and traditional matters. The young "cosmopolitans" relate the village to the world (p. 128).
  - The young also *interpret* the news, which is "a danger sign" for traditional leaders even in local affairs (p. 128). A villager's quoted remark says readers "understand more than us" (p. 128).
  - If elders receive the reports of those who "come and go" and keep interpretation and influence for themselves, their leadership will not be lost (pp. 128–129).
- **General proposition (authors).** If leaders can adjust to new demands and meet followers' needs for relatedness to newly relevant parts of the environment, they may well retain influence. If not, new leaders will arise (p. 129).
  - On "which comes first": in these last two studies gatekeeping comes first and *may* be followed by influentiality (p. 129).
  - On who the new gatekeepers are: in the Lebanese case, at least, they are people whose social location permits them to [garbled in the text layer: "iurge tie new gate"]. The last words are garbled (p. 129; see Reading notes).
- **Footnote 16 (dilemma).** Leadership changes with situations. The authors are convinced by Homans (1950) that new activities are continuously generated in the normal course of group life. So leadership should be unstable, yet stable groups have stable leaders. The authors' hypothesis: leaders tend to control the choice of new activities. Only when the leader cannot direct the change is his role threatened (p. 129). Their examples are a gang moving from baseball to social parties, and a Near Eastern village facing the world (p. 129).
- **Footnote 17 (isolates).** Some evidence suggests that people marginal to a community may be predisposed to carry outside ideas in. Whether the group accepts them is another matter (pp. 129–130).
- **Section close.** The authors stress social location, certification and nomination as keys to relaying, reinforcing and blocking messages from outside (p. 130).

*Intuition note.* When a village faces a new context, old leaders who adapt to it may well keep their influence. If they cannot, new leaders will arise. In the Lebanese case, mobile young people connected to the outside gain the gate first, and influence may follow.

### A Summary of Implications (pp. 130–133)
- Two ingredients of interpersonal relations bear on media effectiveness (p. 130):
  1. **Group norms.** Seemingly private opinions are often maintained together with a few valued others. So the success of an attempt at opinion change depends in some measure on the resistance or support the individual meets in his group (p. 130).
  2. **Person-to-person transmission.** Patterned channels exist within the group and between the group and the outside. Those in key roles have a major share in deciding whether a message circulates and is favorably received (p. 130).
- **Blocking points.** (1) The gatekeeper may not relay it; (2) an influential may not endorse it; (3) it may seem to conflict with norms shared with valued others (pp. 130–131).
- **Conditions for success.** A change may have to be tied to a valued group norm or endorsed as a new norm. It may need the support of influentials and transmitters, and contact with a gatekeeper to reach the group at all (p. 131).
- **Conclusion.** Interpersonal relations "intervene" in mass communication. Research on short-run media effects must take account of relatedness-to-others (p. 131). It can no longer rest on "a random sample of disconnected individuals." Respondents must be studied within the groups they belong to or have "in mind" (p. 131).
- **Further research (p. 131 onward).**
  1. The actual distribution of sociometric connections: extent of isolation and who the isolates are, who seeks friends beyond the neighborhood or job, whether club-joining varies with class, whether ties are homogeneous in age and status, and the role of the family of procreation (pp. 131–132).
  2. Which groups (work, family, leisure) breed which norms (p. 132).
  3. Which kinds of opinion leaders emerge for which subject areas (p. 132).
  4. Which opinions are truly individualistic and not anchored in interpersonal relations. For example, is cigarette brand choice individual, or is there greater-than-chance coincidence of brand among interacting people? (p. 132).
  5. Do not assume norms vary from small group to small group. Distinguish small groups generating idiosyncratic norms from those transmitting norms that vary only with larger categories such as class (p. 132).
  6. The degree to which people share opinions may itself be a cultural variable. Riesman's suggested shift from "inner-directed" to "other-directed" implies this (pp. 132–133). Footnote 19 qualifies this: even inner-directed goals come from cultural norms, but the means prescribed are individualistic striving (p. 133).
- **Moral.** Knowledge of an individual's interpersonal environment is basic to understanding his exposure and reactions to media. Future research on short-run effects should build on the study of everyday influence processes and on points of contact between these and the media (p. 133).
- **Footnote 20.** Media research has mostly studied short-run campaign effects. Other effects, such as gratifications and uses, effects on participation, and transmission of cultural values, are little studied and may be more potent. The authors say their prescription "may hold good, too" for these (p. 133).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A village of 100 people is the target of a health campaign. Suppose three conditions must all hold for the message to take hold. We assume independence for simplicity; the book gives no such numbers.
- The teacher (gatekeeper) relays it: 0.5.
- The respected elder (influential) endorses it: 0.6.
- It does not clash with village norms: 0.8.

The chance of success is $0.5 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.24$. Expected adopters: $100 \times 0.24 = 24$.

A study that surveys 100 unconnected individuals would see only the final tally. It could not tell whether failure came from the gate (the 0.5), the endorsement (the 0.6) or the norm (the 0.8). Studying people within their group lets the researcher locate the blockage. This illustrates the chapter's three blocking points (p. 131).

## Concepts
- [[gatekeeper]] (new) — person who controls a strategic part of a channel and decides whether outside content enters the group; Lewin's term (pp. 118–119).
- [[opinion-leader]] — informal influencer in a group; the authors call the gatekeeper idea "very closely related" (p. 118, fn. 3).
- [[two-step-flow]] — relay of media content via interpersonal channels; evidence called modest in quantity (pp. 116–117).
- [[local-cosmopolitan-leaders]] (new) — Merton's contrast between leaders of home-town news and of outside news (p. 117).
- [[originator-transmitter-influential]] (new) — separable roles; a gatekeeper may hold only the first (p. 119).
- [[certification-vs-nomination]] (new) — leadership awarded to an elder by certification versus nomination from among a variety of candidates (p. 121).
- [[leadership-succession-in-social-change]] (new) — when old leaders cannot relate the group to a new context, new gatekeepers arise and may gain influence (pp. 126, 129).
- [[group-norms-as-anchorage]] (new) — private opinions maintained with a few valued others, so change depends on group resistance or support (p. 130).
- [[selective-exposure]] — not discussed under this name here; the chapter says media research must study people in their group contexts and that the interpersonal environment is basic to understanding their "exposure and reactions" (pp. 131, 133).

## Questions this raises
- If gatekeeping can come first and influence may then follow (p. 129), is there evidence from modern settings, or only these case studies?
- How can a researcher measure "groups they have in mind" (p. 131) in a survey?
- What decides whether gatekeepers pass on content faithfully or reinterpret it, as the Lebanese youth do (p. 128)?
- Is the footnote 16 claim, that leaders control the selection of new activities, testable?
