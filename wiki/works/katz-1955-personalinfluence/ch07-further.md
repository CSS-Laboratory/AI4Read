# Some Further Technical Problems (Katz & Lazarsfeld, ch. VII)

## L0 — One line
In an impact study (a study asking people what influenced a recent choice), cases often need pre-sorting, interviews have several pitfalls, and single-case impact ratings are uncertain, so the authors rely on the hope that errors cancel statistically.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter looks at problems in an impact study, a study that asks people what influenced a recent decision. It covers sorting cases before analysis (pre-classification), pitfalls in interviewing, and the problem of rating each influence. Cases without a free choice may need separate handling or may be dropped, depending on the topic. Four kinds of interview difficulty are named, and the logical and psychological orders of questions conflict. Single impact ratings are uncertain; the authors hope errors cancel in large samples and describe a possible check. Impact means a source together with its content, perhaps coverage times effectiveness. The chapter shows the study's limits: Chart II (ch. V) probably understates personal contact in the inclusive sense.

**Prerequisites:** the "accounting scheme" and "assessment" ideas from chs. IV–VI; basic sampling and survey-interview logic; the idea of a panel (same people re-interviewed).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Cases often need to be pre-classified, because people who made no free decision (habitual movie-goers, forced brand changes) call for a different accounting scheme or may be left out of the study (pp. 198–200).
- Whether to discard a non-voluntary group depends on the topic. In the authors' hypothetical study of moving house, they would probably keep forced movers for the "pulls" toward the new home (p. 200).
- Four interview difficulties: poor questionnaire "fit", question wording, respondent misunderstanding, and intrinsic limits such as forgetting the source (pp. 201–204).
- The "companion" finding means Chart II probably understates personal influence in the inclusive sense. For movies, a better accounting scheme would have separated "inner" from "outer" influence (p. 202).
- The logical question order (talked? minor? major?) conflicts with the psychological order (major first). The seeming contradictions this produces are handled by an editing rule (pp. 204–205).
- Impact ratings are uncertain case by case, but the authors hope that errors cancel statistically. They describe an exposed vs. unexposed experiment as a possible check (pp. 205–206).
- "Impact" of a source means the source together with its content; the authors tentatively offer coverage times effectiveness. The study examines channels as they are, not at their best (p. 207).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The Pre-Classification of Cases (pp. 198–200)
- This chapter follows the first step of an impact study (the accounting scheme); the second step is described as closely related to it (p. 198).
- Example: a woman who goes to the same cinema every Tuesday night whatever is showing. It makes little sense to ask why she chose that film. Such patrons might be asked how the habit developed, though this would not be easy. If there were many of them, they might have to be treated as a special group. Pre-classification would direct the authors to different accounting schemes, interviewing techniques and analyses for the two groups. It might also lead them not to study some parts of a sample at all (p. 198).
- For voluntary-change decisions, first consider "supply": what is available to change to, how far change is required, and how far options are used (pp. 198–199).
  - Movies: the supply changes once or twice a week. Apart from the special cases above, the authors can be "pretty sure" each attendance involves a new decision (p. 199).
  - Cigarettes: a recent switcher, a long-time loyalist and a random picker are three very different types. They cannot be interviewed in the same way or put in one statistical class. If the supply does not itself guarantee that most people have made a recent decision, different types must be anticipated and distinguished in the design (p. 199).
- Fashion: the study asked whether the woman had recently changed her hairdo, clothing, cosmetics or anything else toward something more fashionable. 502 women (over half of the June sample) said yes (p. 199).
  - Only the first change mentioned was followed up, so 502 changes equal 502 respondents (fn. 1, p. 199).
- Movies: every reported selection was studied, except those of the several addicted movie-goers. Fashion: only recalled changes (p. 199).
- Marketing: only changes were studied. Some were forced by the brand being unavailable, the purchase was made by someone else, or the respondent was not the regular food buyer. Only changes reflecting a free choice among several brands can be studied by the assessment technique. Where no choice was open to her, or she did not make the choice herself, no influence in the authors' sense could have been at work (pp. 199–200).
- Footnote 2: the study also collected "objective" panel changes. Each woman was asked in June, and again in August, what coffee and cereal she had on her pantry shelf. This gave 439 brand changes, 142 coffee and 297 cereal. 29 per cent were later classed as non-voluntary. The assessment data for these changes are not reported in the text. The authors say they are in general comparable to the recall data (p. 200).
- The definition was refined during the study. The authors began by wanting to study marketing changes loosely and ended with voluntary, personal decisions only. The rest were discarded (p. 200).
- Contrast, residential mobility (hypothetical): the authors would distinguish free-will movers from forced movers and analyse them separately, but would probably not discard the forced group. The "pushes" away from the old home would be of no psychological interest for forced movers. The "pulls" toward the new home are as worth studying as for voluntary movers (p. 200).
- Main point: pre-classification is often an essential part of a systematic impact study, because different groups need different accounting schemes (p. 200).

*Intuition note:* Before asking what influenced a choice, check that a choice was made. Which cases to drop can depend on what you want to learn (marketing versus moving house).

### Pitfalls in Interviewing (pp. 201–205)
- Chapter IV is not repeated. Follow-up questions were used where an influence was likely but not mentioned. Interviewers tried to bring out the respondent's tacit assumptions. Hesitant respondents were reassured that being influenced is no disgrace. Respondents could start the story where it seemed salient to them (p. 201).
- The authors have no doubt that a considerable amount of information was still missed. They distinguish four difficulties (p. 201):
1. **Fit of the questionnaire.** The whole structure may not fit the action studied. Pretests aim to prevent this, but the trouble is often spotted only in the final analysis (p. 201).
   - Quite often respondents said they had not talked with anyone about the movie, yet it later emerged that they had discussed the choice with the companion they went with. Many people seem to treat the companion as part of their own act, not as "another person" (p. 202).
   - The authors conclude that Chart II (ch. V) probably gives a blurred picture. Undoubtedly more respondents than the chart shows were influenced by personal contacts, if the term is used inclusively (p. 202).
   - A better accounting scheme would have avoided this. At least for movies it should have distinguished "inner" influences (the primary group that attended together) from "outer" ones (all other people) (p. 202).
2. **Wording of specific questions.** The final question asked what was the most important thing in causing the choice. The authors had assumed that the earlier questions would set respondents' minds to read this as "which of these influences". Many "broke out" and named an attribute, such as a film actor or a coffee flavour, instead of a channel. There is probably no generally current term that covers channel, source and influence. Considerable pretesting would still be needed to find the wording (pp. 202–203).
3. **Respondent misunderstandings.** Quite a number thought they were asked why they went to the cinema at all that day, not why they chose that picture. Well-trained interviewers would catch this, accept the answer and then re-ask the intended question. Later questions on how they learned of the picture recovered some of the lost information. Even so, the statistics suffer from insufficient interviewer training (p. 203).
4. **Limits of impact studies.** In about one case in fifteen respondents said they could not remember where they got a piece of information they knew was important (p. 203).
   - The three topics here are probably relatively easy. In a hypothetical question about a change of opinion on a local police chief, a topic of heated discussion, sorting out the major factors would take considerable skill (p. 204).
   - Changes that come about slowly, in almost imperceptible steps, are a case where one should not even think of this kind of impact study. What is a minor statistical difficulty for the authors' topics would be a major argument for a different research design on other topics (p. 204).
- **Question order.**
  - Logical order: first ask whether the person talked with anyone at all, then whether it contributed in some minor way, then whether the contact was a major factor (p. 204).
  - Psychologically the order would very likely be all wrong. Starting with explicit cues could affect the respondent's judgment of other factors, and influences not provided for might go unreported. So the questions have to be asked essentially in reverse: most important factor, other contributions, then whether she talked with anyone (p. 204).
  - Side effect: after naming Mrs. X as a strong influence, a respondent may hear the later "anyone?" question as "anyone *else*?". Likewise, a person may cite a radio advertisement as main influence yet answer no when asked whether she heard about the product on the radio (pp. 204–205).
  - These seeming statistical contradictions must be taken care of by editing. Rule: if a factor was mentioned as influential, assume she was also exposed to it, whether or not she said so (p. 205).
- The authors do not claim to have exhausted the interviewing problems (p. 205).

*Intuition note:* Some respondents answer the question they think is meant, not the printed one. A survey can therefore look inconsistent when people skip what they have already said.

### The Problem of Assessment (pp. 205–207)
- Impact ratings assign weights to factors according to their causal role in a decision. The underlying question: would she have acted had this influence not been exercised? The answer depends on whether other influences would have taken its place and how determined she was. The authors never have enough information to make such judgments with very great confidence (p. 205).
- They certainly make many mistakes in single cases, sometimes overrating and sometimes underrating a factor. They hope the mistakes cancel out in larger numbers. A statement such as "people are influenced more by other people than by any other source" is assumed to be statistically correct even if many individual assessments are unjustified. Charts I, II and III in ch. V are typical impact statistics (pp. 205–206).
- How far the cancellation assumption is justified is, the authors say, too complex to discuss here. Confidence is justified to different degrees depending on the material (p. 206).
- Verification: in an individual case the authors probably can never prove an assessment, only make it more or less plausible. Statistical results seem to permit a kind of verification (p. 206).
  - Proposed experiment: expose one group to advertisements for a product and keep another unexposed. Suppose the exposed group buys more, say by 10 per cent. Interview the exposed people who bought. If 10 per cent tell the authors they bought because of the advertisement, the authors may conclude the assessment procedure is correct. The text does not say 10 per cent of whom (p. 206).
  - Near-verifications in the literature: the more crowded people actually are, the more likely they are to give crowding as a reason for moving (Rossi 1955). The farther people live from a department store, the more likely they are to give distance as a reason for not buying there. Burt compared case-by-case assessments of poverty in juvenile delinquency with crime-rate differences between poor and well-to-do groups. The reasonable agreement gave him confidence in his procedure (pp. 206–207).
  - Such comparisons are still very rare, and it was not possible to introduce such costly verification in this study (p. 207).
- Does it make sense to speak of the impact of a *source*? The authors grant it is undoubtedly the argument transmitted by a source that influences. Their language is somewhat elliptic: "impact of a source" means source and content combined (p. 207).
  - One medium may have more impact than another because it has greater coverage, or because it affects those it reaches more strongly. Perhaps the matter can be put in a formula: "impact equals coverage times effectiveness" (p. 207).
- The study examines channels and content as found in a specific situation, as is characteristic of any evaluation study. It does not ask what media could do at their best. Two earlier publications dealt with such properties (reading versus listening; personal influence), with different purposes and techniques (p. 207).

*Intuition note:* A single "why did you do it?" answer is uncertain evidence. The hope is that over- and under-rating roughly balance across many respondents. The proposed experiment would compare the stated reasons with a known difference in behaviour.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Check the proposed verification experiment with invented numbers.
- Exposed group: 200 people, 50 buy (25%). Unexposed group: 200 people, 30 buy (15%).
- Difference: 25% − 15% = 10 percentage points, which is 0.10 × 200 = 20 "extra" buyers among the exposed.
- The author's wording ("10 per cent tell us…") is ambiguous. We read it as 10% of the exposed group, i.e. 20 people. The 50 exposed buyers are then interviewed. If the assessment procedure is right, about 20 of the 50 (40% of buyers) should say they bought because of the ad.
- If 8 said so, the procedure would underrate the ad's impact. If 35 said so, it would overrate it.
- Coverage × effectiveness: medium A reaches 80% and moves 10% of those reached, so 0.80 × 0.10 = 8%. Medium B reaches 20% and moves 40% of those reached, so 0.20 × 0.40 = 8%. Both have equal impact for different reasons.

## Concepts
- [[pre-classification-of-cases]] (new) — sorting respondents by type of decision before choosing accounting scheme, interview and analysis (p. 198)
- [[impact-study]] (new) — study that asks respondents to reconstruct what influenced a recent decision; the assessment technique needs a free choice (pp. 198, 200)
- [[assessment-of-impact]] (new) — rating the causal weight of each influence by asking whether the act would have happened without it (p. 205)
- [[inner-vs-outer-personal-influence]] (new) — influence from the companion group at the event vs. from all other people (p. 202)
- [[logical-vs-psychological-question-order]] (new) — the order that is logical for analysis differs from the order that avoids cueing the respondent (p. 204)
- [[statistical-cancellation-of-errors]] (new) — hope that over- and under-rating in single cases cancel in aggregate; its justification varies by material (pp. 205–206)
- [[impact-equals-coverage-times-effectiveness]] (new) — tentative formula: a source's impact combines how many it reaches and how strongly it affects them (p. 207)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Cancellation of single-case rating errors ↔ measurement theory: random errors average out in aggregates, but only if they are unbiased. The authors say only that how far this is justified is too complex to discuss here.
- [structural-analogy] Exposed vs. unexposed advertising experiment as validation ↔ experimental validation of self-reports against behaviour.
- [structural-analogy] Coverage × effectiveness ↔ decomposing aggregate effect into reach and per-person effect (as in expected value = probability × size).
- [structural-analogy] Pre-classification ↔ eligibility criteria and subgroup definition in sampling design.

## Questions this raises
- Would the inner/outer distinction change the headline finding that personal influence outweighs the media? The text says only that Chart II probably understates personal contact in the inclusive sense (p. 202).
- How could the assumption that errors cancel be tested if errors are systematic, for example if all respondents over-credit people?
- Does the editing rule (assume exposure if a factor is mentioned as influential) bias the exposure figures (p. 205)?
- How far do this chapter's cautions apply to slow opinion change, which the authors say may call for a different design (p. 204)?
