# Marketing Leaders

## L0 — One line
In everyday household marketing, opinion leaders are found at all status levels, are most concentrated among large family wives, and become more common with gregariousness; limited follow-up data suggest advice mostly passes between women of like status, with no clear flow from higher to lower status.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Where are the opinion leaders for everyday buying advice, and whom do they influence? Katz and Lazarsfeld compare women by social status, life-cycle position and gregariousness (number of friends and memberships). Leaders appear in about equal shares on all status levels. They are most concentrated among large family wives (married, with a large growing family) and rise with gregariousness. Limited follow-up interviews with named advisers suggest advisers and advisees usually share a status level, and that influence flows slightly more from old to young. Within the household there is little influencing, and requests are limited except for some products. This challenges a "vertical" (prestige-driven) picture of influence, though the authors stress that the follow-up data are only suggestive.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and simple cross-tabulations; the idea of controlling for a third variable; the book's earlier definitions of opinion leader, life-cycle types and the status index.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Marketing is a near-universal routine. About half the women recently changed a product or brand, and one third of those changes involved personal influence (p. 234).
- A "vertical" hypothesis predicts more leaders among higher-status women. Chart VI shows almost equal shares across the three status levels, so each level seems to have its own leaders (pp. 235–236).
- In the (small) follow-up data, two-thirds of adviser–advisee pairs share a status level. Cross-status advice shows no clear direction (pp. 237–238).
- Large family wives are almost twice as likely as any other life-cycle type to be marketing leaders. The authors take this as strong evidence that, at least for marketing leadership, current "experiencing" seems more important than long "experience" (p. 240).
- Follow-up data on age suggest, only circumstantially, that the 25–44 "wives" are advisers for women of other ages too (so large family wives may serve other life-cycle types), and that the flow is somewhat more from old to young (pp. 241–242).
- Gregariousness raises the chance of leadership within each life-cycle type. Once it is controlled, the status effect almost disappears (pp. 242–244).
- Marketing influence is mostly extra-family. Inside the household there is little influencing, and even requests are limited except for coffee and cereal, where husbands and children are active (pp. 244–245).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the arena and the plan (pp. 234–235)
- For most women, marketing for household goods is "virtually a continuous routine". Yet about half of the sample reported a recent switch to something new, and one third of these switches involved personal influence (p. 234).
- Test: do life-cycle, social status and gregariousness locate concentrations of marketing leaders? (p. 234)
- **Definition (fn. 1):** a marketing leader is a woman who (a) said on both interviews that she was recently asked advice on buying food or household products, or (b) said so on one interview only *and* considers herself "more likely" than others to be consulted (p. 234).

### Marketing leadership and social status: a horizontal flow pattern (pp. 235–239)
- **Hypothesis under test:** if everyday influence is vertical, higher-status women should be leaders more often. The authors see little reason to expect higher-status women to be more skilled; the vertical hypothesis would rest on their greater prestige as advice-givers (p. 235).
- **Chart VI:** 27% of 189 high-status women are marketing leaders, against 21% of 241 low-status women. The middle bar (n = 243) is not legible in the text layer: [unreadable p. 235]. The authors conclude that high status raises a woman's chances "very little", so each level seems to have its own leaders (p. 236).
- Two readings: (a) advice flows regardless of status, or (b) women turn to leaders of their own status. The authors consider (b) the more reasonable expectation (p. 236). Their reasons:
  - Status peers share budget problems.
  - Stores cater somewhat more to one status, so women meet status peers while shopping (p. 236).
- **Follow-up evidence:** 168 names were elicited in connection with 386 changes. About 130 were outside the household, and 76 were interviewed. A separate "panel" group gave 102 names for 297 changes, and 31 were interviewed (76 + 31 = 107). The 76 are analysed here (fn. 2, pp. 236–237; fn. 3, p. 237).
- The authors warn that the material is "suggestive" only. Interviews were incomplete, and the pairs include unconfirmed exchanges (p. 237, fn. 3).
- Only pairs outside the same family are used. Status here is a four-category interview rating (A, B = high; C = middle; D = low), not the earlier index (fn. 4, p. 237).

**Table 17** (counts of pairs, p. 237):

| Leader's status \ Influencee's status | Low | Middle | High | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 4 | 7 | 2 | 13 |
| Middle | 6 | 21 | 3 | 30 |
| High | – | 4 | 19 | 23 |
| Total | 10 | 32 | 24 | 66 |

- The diagonal is $4 + 21 + 19 = 44$, which is two-thirds of 66 (fn. 5, p. 238).
- Off the diagonal, 10 advisees went to higher-status advisers and 12 to lower-status ones, so no direction is discernible (fn. 6, p. 238). The status gap tends to be only one step. The text says only 2 of 22 crossings run between the extremes (p. 238).
- **Conclusion:** leaders are found in roughly equal proportions on each level, and women mostly seek leaders of their own level. Only perhaps among the lowest-status women (just 10 cases) does there seem to be any substantial share of advice-seeking from status unequals (p. 239).

*Intuition note:* If prestige drove advice, leaders would pile up at the top and advice would flow downward. Here leaders are spread evenly and advice mostly stays at the same level. The caveat is the small follow-up sample.

### Life-cycle position: "the large family wives" (pp. 239–242)
- **Expectations:** "girls" should have few leaders. Among married types, there are two rival speculations (p. 239):
  - Current responsibility predicts high leadership for large family wives and low for matrons.
  - Years of experience predict that matrons lead.
- **Chart VII** (percent leaders): girls 20% (n = 79), small family wives 23% (174), large family wives 38% (169), matrons 16% (252) (p. 240).
- Large family wives are almost twice as likely as any other type to be leaders. The matron is no more likely than the girl or small family wife (p. 240).
- The authors call this strong evidence that, at least in marketing leadership, "experiencing" seems more important than "experience". They say current interest and participation are *presumably* at a peak in this group (p. 240).
- **Further question:** do large family wives advise mainly women like themselves, or women of all types? The authors cannot answer directly, since neither the advisers' characterizations nor the follow-up interviews give more than the ages of both partners. Circumstantially, the age data make the second alternative look more plausible (p. 241).

**Table 18** (non-family pairs, by age, p. 241):

| Leader's age \ Advisee's age | 15–24 | 25–44 | 45+ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15–24 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 10 |
| 25–44 | 10 | 17 | 9 | 36 |
| 45+ | 2 | 9 | 18 | 29 |
| Total | 15 | 29 | 31 | 75 |

- About 50% sought age peers. Of 12 girls who consulted an older woman, 10 went to the 25–44 group. Of 13 matrons who consulted a younger woman, 9 did the same (pp. 241–242).
- Women aged 25–44 who went outside their group consulted older women somewhat more than younger ones (p. 242).
- (added step) 12 of 15 youngest women consulted seniors (80%), against 13 of 31 oldest women consulting juniors (about 42%). This matches the text: more of the youngest consulted seniors than the oldest consulted juniors (p. 242).
- **Conclusion:** the flow is somewhat more old-to-young, and the "wives" are the most prominent influentials. The authors call their information "very inadequate", with no life-cycle data and no representative sample, so no conclusive statements are possible (p. 242). They add that, taken together, the findings lend weight to the fruitfulness of the follow-up approach (p. 242).

*Intuition note:* Leadership goes with being in the thick of household buying now, not with age alone. The age tables hint that the 25–44 wives are consulted by women of other ages too, but the authors call this only circumstantial.

### Gregariousness and marketing leadership (pp. 242–244)
- Highly gregarious women (many friends, several organizations) are two and one-half times as likely to be leaders as the least gregarious (p. 242). **Chart VIII:** low gregariousness 13% (n = 194), middle 24%, high about 33% (n = 185). The high bar is garbled in the text layer. The 33% is inferred from the "two and one-half times" statement (p. 243).
- Rationale: a leader needs contact with potential followers (pp. 242–243).
- The relationship holds within each life-cycle type (p. 243).
- Being a large family wife is the single biggest head start. Even the least gregarious large family wives have a better than average chance, meaning a leader ratio above the sample's overall ratio (fn. 8, p. 243). Within every life-cycle position, more gregarious women still do better (pp. 243–244).
- **Status revisited:** among women equally gregarious, low-status women are just as likely to be leaders as high-status women. The small status differences in Chart VI *may be attributed* to the greater opportunity for gregariousness on the high status levels (p. 244).
- A highly gregarious low-status woman is four times as likely to be a leader as a non-gregarious high-status woman (p. 244).

*Intuition note:* Status looked weakly related to leadership, but the authors attribute the small gap to gregariousness. Hold gregariousness fixed and the gap nearly vanishes.

### Marketing influence within the family (pp. 244–245)
- The authors separate **requests** from **influences**. A request ("Let's try Coffee X") makes the requester the decision-maker, and the wife acts as purchasing agent unless she dissents. In influence ("I enjoyed that coffee Mrs. Jones served"), the wife alone decides, although she may weigh advice (pp. 244–245).
- Much intra-family communication is requests, so household communication was excluded from the analysis so far (p. 245).
- Influence is "very largely an extra-family affair". Non-resident relatives rank second only to friends and neighbors, so female kin form a relevant network (p. 245).
- Inside the household there is little influencing and few requests, except for coffee and cereal, where husbands and children are particularly active. The interpretation is that these are the "culturally certified domains" of husband and child (p. 245).
- Fn. 11: the panel changes (coffee and cereal only, objectively detected) show a greater role for husbands and children than the recall changes (wider range of products, volunteered), which suggests the text's interpretation. The footnote adds that part of the difference may also come from the two questionnaire situations, for example recall respondents may have been more selective (pp. 245–246).

### Summary (p. 246)
- Life-cycle position shapes involvement, hence large family wives are the most frequent source of leaders. A wider range of contacts, and hence a better balance of family and outside demands, raises the chance of becoming a leader (p. 246).
- Status does not determine where leaders concentrate, but it marks boundaries for influence flow. For age, the authors had the impression of something of a downward flow of influence from old to young. Friends and neighbors are named most often, then adult female kin (p. 246).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show how a status gap can vanish once gregariousness is held fixed.
- High-status group: 100 women, 60 gregarious and 40 not. Assume leader rates of 30% (gregarious) and 10% (not). Leaders: $0.30 \times 60 = 18$ and $0.10 \times 40 = 4$, so 22 of 100 = 22%.
- Low-status group: 100 women, 20 gregarious and 80 not, with the same rates. Leaders: $0.30 \times 20 = 6$ and $0.10 \times 80 = 8$, so 14 of 100 = 14%.
- Overall, high status looks 8 points better (22% vs 14%). Within each gregariousness level the rates are identical, so the gap comes only from the high-status group having more gregarious women.

## Concepts
- [[opinion-leader]] — in marketing, a woman recently asked for buying advice (twice, or once plus self-rated as more likely to be consulted) (p. 234)
- [[marketing-leader]] (new) — the opinion leader in the food and household goods arena, defined by the fn. 1 criteria (p. 234)
- [[life-cycle-position]] (new) — girls, small family wives, large family wives, matrons; a ladder on which leaders concentrate (p. 239)
- [[gregariousness]] (new) — number of friends and organizational memberships; raises leadership chances within every life-cycle type (pp. 242–243)
- [[vertical-vs-horizontal-influence]] (new) — vertical: prestige pulls advice upward; the authors find horizontal, same-status flow (pp. 235–238)
- [[follow-up-interview-method]] (new) — interviewing the named advisers to describe who influences whom; here only suggestive (pp. 236–237)
- [[requests-vs-influence]] (new) — a request leaves the decision with the requester; influence leaves it with the wife (pp. 244–245)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Same-status adviser–advisee pairing ↔ communication-theory: homophily — similar people exchange advice, though the unit gives no tie-level measure.
- [structural-analogy] Controlling gregariousness removes the status effect ↔ causal inference: confounding/mediation by a third variable — same logic of stratified comparison, with no formal model here.
- [metaphor] "Experiencing" over "experience" ↔ communication-theory: knowledge as use-based rather than accumulated.

## Questions this raises
- How would the status-boundary result change with a representative follow-up sample and with confirmed exchanges only?
- Does gregariousness cause leadership, or do leaders become more gregarious by being asked for advice?
- How far do these findings depend on the narrow arena (household goods) and on women of one community?
- Could the coffee/cereal difference between panel and recall data be separated from the product-domain explanation?
