# Interpersonal Networks: Communicating within the Group

## L0 — One line
Katz and Lazarsfeld review small-group research to examine how patterns of communication, and the key roles in passing on information and influence, are associated (the authors think) with group structure, climate and situation. They say they care less about "leaders" than about key communication roles, which they link to nomination, social location and cultural certification.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how informal person-to-person communication works inside small groups, as groundwork for explaining how mass-media influence reaches people. It reviews small-group studies in two ways: patterns of transmission (shaped by group structure, climate and situation) and "strategic points", meaning key communication roles. The authors distinguish three routes to a leader or influential role: nomination by the group in its situation, a strategic location in its communication network, and certification by its culture. The authors conclude that before studying influence flow, one must specify the topic and the situation and map the network. Within those networks one can then identify initiators, transmitters and influentials.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a primary (face-to-face) group; basic sociometry (asking members whom they choose); percentages; the opinion-leader idea from the book's introduction.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Interpersonal networks matter for mass media in two ways: a relay function (personal transmitters carry messages to the unexposed) and a reinforcement function (media influence works better when matched by personal communication) (pp. 82–83).
- Communication patterns follow group structure: friendship ties, cohesiveness, status and group size each shape who talks to whom and with what effect (pp. 84–90).
- Group "climate" changes communication. Democratic groups seemed to build norms independent of the leader, and higher pressure toward uniformity raised both pressure to communicate and readiness to change (pp. 91–94).
- Shared interest is the basis of communication networks, and content tends to flow among like-minded people, as media exposure does. Different topics flow through different networks, and situation shifts change patterns (pp. 94–97).
- Leadership appears to depend heavily on the situation rather than on fixed traits. Nominated leaders are shaped by the situation, conform most closely to group norms, know group opinion, know they lead, and are best liked (pp. 100–105).
- Opinion leadership has separable roles (originating, sanctioning, diffusing). Strategic location, especially centrality, tends to bring a key communication role (pp. 108–113).
- Culture can certify who has a right to influence, and in which sphere (pp. 114–115).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction and plan (pp. 82–84)
- The previous chapter treated the group-anchorage of opinions. This chapter treats the second "ingredient": person-to-person communication, which the authors call another name for interaction (p. 82).
- Illustrations from earlier chapters suggest two functions (pp. 82–83):
  1. **Relay function:** some individuals seem to serve as personal transmitters. Without them, media messages might not reach otherwise unexposed people. This is the major part of the opinion-leader idea.
  2. **Reinforcement function:** when a media influence attempt coincides with interpersonal communication, it seems to have a much greater chance of success.
- Questions to be asked: what uniformities exist in interpersonal communication; who wields influence in which situations; whether those who bring communications into groups have special characteristics; where in a group to look for early acceptance of change (p. 83).
- Plan: most of this chapter deals with communication *within* a group. The next chapter deals with communication *to* the group from outside. The text writes "Chapter VI" for that next chapter, though this is Chapter VI. Material on outside-to-group communication is sparse because small-group research is "sheltered" (p. 83).
- Two approaches (pp. 83–84):
  - **Patterns and networks** at the level of the whole group.
  - A **diagnostic** approach that singles out **strategic points**, i.e. key roles crucial for the flow of information and influence (a role, in our gloss, is a part a person plays in interaction).
- Variations in both are associated, "we think", with three factors: (a) group structures, (b) group "cultures" or climates, (c) group situations (p. 84).

### The Patterns of Transmission (pp. 84–97)

**(A) Structural connections (pp. 84–91).** Differences in mutual attraction, interdependence, status, propinquity (physical nearness) and group size make for differences in the rate and often the content of communication (p. 84).

*Friendship ties as channels (pp. 85–86)*
- Festinger et al. studied rumors in two communities. In "Regent Hill", a rumor reached 62% of those with close friends, 42% of those with only acquaintances and 83% of those who claimed no friends in the neighborhood (p. 85).
- In Westgate (a student housing development), sociometric friendship choices accounted for the direction of six of fourteen instances in which a planted rumor was passed on (p. 85).
- Moreno found a rumor followed closely the path he predicted from his mapped "psycho-social networks" (p. 85).
- Festinger: friendship implies that an active channel of communication exists (p. 85).
- Authors' comparison: with known sociometric ties, a rumor can be watched almost as a doctor watches a fluoroscopic liquid in the body (p. 85).
- Jennings studied how knowledge of girls running for election spread among 400 girls at a training school. Her mechanism for "publicity" is the immediate friendship group. Where such groups overlap extensively there is much shared knowledge. Where they do not, shared knowledge is restricted (pp. 85–86).
- The authors link this to Festinger's finding that rumors do not usually travel outside self-contained cliques (p. 86).

*Cohesiveness (p. 86)*
- Cohesiveness is, "one might say", the cement that holds sociometric structure together (p. 86).
- Back's experiment: more attached people exert more influence and are more effective. In highly cohesive groups, attempts to change a partner's opinion were more numerous and more successful (p. 86).
- Westgate: opinion was most uniform in the most cohesive courts. The authors read this as implying more and more effective communication there. Cohesiveness was judged from intra-court friendship choices (p. 86).

*Formal hierarchy: factory rumor study (pp. 86–87)*
- Back et al. studied rumor in a factory with five hierarchy levels and 55 members. Strategically placed "cooperating observers" recorded planted rumors over four months (pp. 86–87).
- Result: 17 recorded acts of communication, of which 11 went upward in status, four to peers and two downward (p. 87).
- Footnote check: the observers missed 22% of communications, so the method yielded 78%. Back et al. suspect the observers, who were barred from passing on what they heard, might ordinarily have been important transmitters (p. 87, fn. 7).
- The authors speculate on why the flow was upward (work patterns, mobility aspirations or cross-status friendships). They ask whether non-organizational topics (elections, World Series scores) would flow the same way. They give no answers (p. 87).

*Status in small groups (pp. 88–89)*
- Is communication directed upward in small groups? "The answer seems to be yes—but the yes needs considerable elaboration" (p. 88).
- Homans: (a) "any single person interacts most of all with his equals", (b) the higher a person's status, the more people seek to communicate with him, (c) high-ranking members tend to address more group members than low-ranking ones (p. 88).
- Bales (discussion and conference groups; generalization to friends, neighbors and spouses is unclear): ranking members by how often they speak gives the same order as how often others speak to them (p. 89).
  - The most frequent speakers tend to address the whole group, while others tend to address them as individuals.
  - Popularity follows the same rank order.
  - Hence person-to-person messages move upward toward the popular, while one-to-several communication tends to flow down (p. 89).
- Content: high-ranking people tend to offer information and opinion. Low-ranking people request information and opinion and express agreement or disagreement. Infrequent speakers "react", while frequent speakers "initiate" and make more influence attempts (p. 89).
- Conclusion: status and rank channel communication in small informal groups, as in formal organizations (p. 89).

*Group size (pp. 89–90)*
- Bales: as groups get larger (the text layer reads "8 to 8 people"), more communication goes to the most frequent communicator, and relatively less goes between all members. That person directs more of his remarks to the group as a whole. Communication "tends to 'centralize'" around a leader (pp. 89–90).
- Hare (Boy Scouts discussing camping equipment): five-man groups reached significantly more consensus than twelve-man groups. Dissatisfaction was greater in the larger groups, apparently from lower participation imposed by a time limit. Hare suggests lowered participation results in lowered consensus (p. 90).
- Footnote 14: leaders in large groups had less influence than leaders in small groups. The authors say this does not necessarily mean that gaining centrality loses influence. Their "suspicion" (intuition, not a finding) is that where there is strong pressure toward uniformity and/or considerable interdependence, the central large-group leader is also more influential. Where pressure is weak, his influence is reduced. In Hare's experiment there was a vote but no call for unanimity or interdependence (p. 90).
- Centrality will return as a major concept in the section on strategic points (p. 91).

*Intuition note.* Ties, status and size act like pipes of different widths: communication goes where links are strong, upward toward the popular, and toward one centre as groups grow. The authors say a leader's gain in centrality does not necessarily mean a loss of influence; their explanation (pressure toward uniformity and interdependence) is only a suspicion.

**(B) Comparative climates (pp. 91–94).**
- *Lewin, Lippitt, White "social climates"* (p. 91): four matched clubs of 11-year-olds each went through "democratic", "authoritarian" and "laissez-faire" climates in varying sequences. The climates were set by the carefully rehearsed behavior of the adult leader. Leader personality was controlled by giving each leader more than one group and more than one climate. The study was not framed as communication research, but its results bear on it (pp. 91–92).
- Results (p. 92):
  - Total conversation was more restricted in the authoritarian atmosphere.
  - Authoritarian groups made more attempts to attract the leader's attention, but the interaction was less confiding and less personal.
  - Democratic and laissez-faire groups made more requests for attention and approval from fellow members.
  - Laissez-faire groups far exceeded others in requesting information from the leader. Democratic groups were freest in suggesting group policy to the leader.
- **Authors' interpretation:** the democratic group probably went much further in establishing group-enforced norms independent of the leader. Its communication did not centre exclusively on the leader (p. 92).
- Test episode: the leader was called away. In authoritarian groups, "working time" dropped to a minimum. In the democratic atmosphere the leader's absence or presence made practically no difference (p. 92). Authoritarian groups depended on the leader. The democratic group seemed to pass on shared "traditions" that became group property (pp. 92–93).
- *Festinger and Thibaut (1952)* (p. 93): three climates of pressure toward uniformity.
  - High: told the interest was how a group comes to a unanimous decision.
  - Medium: group rated by how many members reached the experts' "correct" solution.
  - None: told only that a problem-solving group was being studied.
  - Result, which the authors say clearly supports the hypothesis: as pressure toward uniformity increases, both pressure to communicate and readiness to change increase, so there is increasing change toward uniformity of opinion (p. 93).
- Conclusion: climate, like structure, must be counted in analyzing channels of information and influence (p. 94).

*Intuition note.* How a group is run changes how its members talk. In the study, in the leader-centred (authoritarian) groups working time fell to a minimum when the leader left, while the democratic group, which seemed to have shared traditions of its own, was practically unaffected.

**(C) Situations: communications content and interpersonal relations (pp. 94–97).**
- People belong to several groups, so the same group can generate different networks for different interests. Because most small-group studies are laboratory studies, few answers exist, and leads come from "natural" groups (p. 94).
- The "cardinal principle": shared interest in a subject is the basis for interpersonal communication networks. Allport and Postman: "A rumor public exists wherever there is a community of interest" (p. 94).
- Examples (pp. 94–95):
  - The nursery-school rumor reached 62% of community members with children of nursery age and 28% of those without.
  - The Tenants' Council rumor reached those associated with the organization. It was passed on only by active participants or members of their families.
  - In the factory study, a rumor about a small group spread quickly within it and not beyond the few vitally concerned.
- Amendments from Festinger et al. (the authors say the proof is "not quite conclusive") (p. 95):
  - Information favorable to the Council was communicated much more actively within courts that favored it.
  - There was little or no communication on Council matters between courts with conflicting attitudes.
  - **Authors' generalization (the findings "seem to point" to it):** there will be more communication on a matter among those who share a concern in it when (a) their view is homogeneous and (b) the content favors their shared view (p. 95).
- Objections in footnote 20: unfavorable communications were not shown to spread more in unfavorable courts. Hostile rumors spread most to Council activists, who were mostly but not all favorable. Courts with different standards also tended to be different friendship cliques, so friendship channels could explain the limited flow between them (pp. 95–96).
- Link to media habits: people tend not to expose themselves to communications that conflict with their predispositions. Media act as "reinforcers" rather than "converters". The authors now find the same for interpersonal communication (p. 96).
- *Multiple networks.* Different sociometric questions (work with whom? leisure with whom?) get different names. Reference group studies hold that people use different groups for different matters (p. 96).
  - A later section of the book shows that marketing advice flows through different networks than fashion advice, and neither seems to overlap with political influencing (p. 97).
  - First step in describing communication flow in a society: locate individuals in their networks and decide which networks matter for which communications (p. 97).
- *Changing situations.* A group in a hostile environment will rapidly solidify its chain of command and often shift its leadership and communication patterns. Studies of soldiers in training versus combat, and of leadership in disasters, illustrate this (p. 97).
- Footnote 24: pressure toward uniformity may vary with situation for the same group, not only between groups (p. 97).

*Intuition note.* Who talks to whom depends on what the topic is. Content also tends to flow among like-minded people, which parallels selective exposure to media.

### Strategic Points of Transmission (pp. 98–115)

**Introduction: the diagnostic approach (pp. 98–100).**
- The diagnostic approach locates origin, relay points and terminal points of intra-group communication. These are mostly roles people play (p. 98).
- A group is never simply a collection of individuals but a structure of interlocking roles and dependencies. In more or less stable groups, roles become part of group norms. Examples: peacemaker, jokester, initiator, gossip (p. 98).
- Nearly always, one or more persons are recognized as leaders or influentials. "Leader" may be better replaced by "influential", "initiator" or "most popular", but the authors keep "leader" and mean many things by it. Sociometric maps consistently show individuals who are centers of attraction (pp. 98–99).
- The authors "suspect" the small group leader is the key to the interpersonal transmission of influence. Recent studies of leadership, the authors say, stress that leadership is not so much a trait as a response of people reacting together to a situation (p. 99).
- Three kinds of leader and their three "diagnostic keys" (pp. 99–100):

| Key | Leader type | Parallels | Source of influence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nomination | nominated leader | situations | chosen by the group as fitting its needs |
| Location | located leader | structures | strategic position in the communication network |
| Certification | culturally certified leader | climates | a position the culture endows with the "right" to influence (e.g. father of the family) |

- They begin with nomination, after a digression on leadership traits (p. 100).

**(A) Nomination: the situational aspect (pp. 100–108).**

*Traits do not settle it (pp. 100–101)*
- Wartime research prompted a sharp retreat from the trait approach. Sanford: almost no two studies find the same traits. Eleven studies show emotional stability as a leader trait, but five find leaders less stable than followers (p. 100).
- Sanford concludes with "reasonable certainty": (a) there are either no general leadership traits, or none describable in familiar terms; (b) in a specific situation leaders do have distinguishing traits, but these vary from situation to situation (p. 100).
- Carter and Nixon: 100 high school boys in small groups did clerical, intellectual and mechanical tasks. Intellectual leaders also tended to lead the clerical task. Neither intellectual nor clerical leaders rose to leadership in the mechanical task (pp. 100–101).
- Major conclusion: there appear to be no "born" leaders or infallible traits. The situation is one of the major determinants of who will lead (p. 101).

*Leader and group norms (pp. 101–103)*
- The authors say a number of studies suggest that perhaps the most important leader–group relation is the leader's subservience to group norms. In Merei's study, a former leader reintroduced into a group did not regain power unless he adopted the new "traditions" formed in his absence (p. 101). Shared interests and values are therefore prerequisites for leadership and influentiality in general (pp. 101–102).
- Chowdhry and Newcomb studied four natural groups: a religious group, a political group, a medical fraternity and a medical sorority (p. 102).
  - Members named leaders on four criteria: who would be best president, best convention delegate, most influential on opinion, most wanted as a friend. The four were highly intercorrelated.
  - Groups were split into leaders (more than 20% of choices), nonleaders (fewer than 20%) and isolates (no choices).
  - Members answered statements that were relevant, irrelevant or partly relevant to the group's interest, and said where they thought the group stood.
  - Leaders judged group opinion much more accurately than the others, but only on matters relevant to the group's interest.
  - Chowdhry and Newcomb say it seems reasonable to conclude that leaders are chosen, at least in part, for recognized "sensitivity" to other members. Such sensitivity may or may not be general, and if it could become general, leaders are "not motivated" to develop it equally in all directions (p. 102).
  - Footnote 32: tentative, because an earlier study by Hites and Campbell differs somewhat (p. 102).
- The authors read this as fitting the norm-conformity finding: sensitivity to relevant opinion either precedes or follows conformity to norms, or at any rate is correlated with it. They note in passing a possible lead toward a method for studying group opinion without interviewing every member (p. 103).

*Do leaders know they lead? (pp. 103–105)* Lippitt, Polansky, Redl and Rosen studied "social power" in boys' summer camps (p. 103).
- Boys ranked bunkmates and themselves on fighting ability, sports, campcraft and a general question on who is best at getting the others to do what he wants (p. 103).
  - Members of each bunk agreed highly on who had most "power".
  - Self-estimates tended to match the rank others gave. "The leader knows that he's a leader" (p. 103).
  - Nominated leaders were best liked and ranked highest on fighting ability and campcraft, i.e. best at what the group values in that situation (p. 103).
- The authors' four-part summary: nominated leaders (1) conform most to group norms, (2) best know prevailing opinion on salient matters, (3) are aware of their nomination, (4) are best liked (pp. 103–104).
- How leaders influence, by two measures (p. 104):
  - **Behavioral contagion:** the influencer is imitated, though he makes no attempt to transmit influence.
  - **Direct influence:** an influence attempt is manifest (ordering, suggesting, requesting).
- Findings (p. 104):
  - Contagion is more likely from members with high attributed power. A boy follows most closely those he himself names as most influential.
  - High-power boys' manifest attempts were more successful.
  - The average member initiates deferential, approval-seeking behavior toward high-power boys.
  - The high-power recipient makes more frequent and more directive influence attempts, and is more successful. He does not always use the power, "but often enough to make it important".
- Four points the authors draw: agreement on who has influence; high-ranked members perceive their position accurately; they exercise the license to influence; they succeed most in direct attempts and are imitated without attempts (p. 105).
- The authors call this study almost a small-scale model of their own opinion-leadership design (ask who is influential, get self-estimates, trace actual influence, characterize leaders). Footnote 34 notes important differences, such as a city's size and multiple affiliations (p. 105).
- Lippitt et al.: where group living approaches twenty-four-hour living, attributed power tends to be undifferentiated by situation and activity. A boy's initial pre-eminence in one thing is generalized to the range of group activities (p. 105). The authors infer that such generalized influentiality is more characteristic of such groups than of groups with more formal controls and varied commitments (p. 105).

*Doc versus Taylor (pp. 105–108)*
- Two small-group classics, summarized by Homans: the Nortons street-corner gang (Whyte) with "Doc", and the Bank Wiring Room at Hawthorne (Roethlisberger and Dickson) with "Taylor". The Nortons are relatively autonomous and the group governs almost the total lives of its members. The Bank Wiring Room is a closely supervised factory group in which formal factory rules have by far the largest share in regulating behavior (pp. 105–106).
- **Doc:** judges and acts while others are undecided. When he decides, the group has decided. Others may originate action for him, but group action starts with him. Members wait for him. He molds opinion on politics and neighborhood affairs (p. 106).
- **Taylor:** hints only (he had not clearly emerged as a leader when the study ended; fn. 37). He is well informed, argues often and usually wins, and gives advice. Homans' cited paragraph reports him warning Steinhardt about a job transfer, telling Capek which horses to bet on, and telling boisterous men to pipe down (p. 107).
- **Authors' guess:** the Bank Wiremen are more typical of informal relations in modern society than the Nortons. Such groups are not organized around one group or goal and do not control members' major life activities. So an opinion leader's influence may lie in "little" things and occasional "big" ones. Much more than Doc, Taylor fits their image of the opinion leader as transmitter of information and influence in modern society; they add that Doc may have too much power (pp. 107–108).

*Intuition note.* In these studies, the group's situation is one of the major determinants of who is called on to lead, and nominated leaders conform to group norms, know group opinion and are liked. For ordinary modern groups, the authors judge that Taylor, a well-informed adviser on everyday matters, fits their image of the opinion leader much better than Doc, who may have too much power.

**(B) Social location: the structural aspect (pp. 108–114).**
- The authors are interested in key communication roles, not leadership per se. Different people may perform different tasks in the process (p. 108).
- Doc's orders are often discussed and funneled to the gang through two lieutenants. Homans therefore suggests defining a leader by his key position in the channels of interaction rather than as the man who originates interaction for many at once. The authors say "opinion leadership" may refer to three roles: **origin** of an idea, **sanctioning** of it, **diffusion** of it. An individual qualifies as a key communicator if he fulfils any one, or all, of these roles (p. 109).
  - Doc often, not always, originates. He endorses or rejects others' proposals. Sometimes he transmits to the assembled group, and sometimes he passes ideas down a chain (p. 109).
  - Homans' description: an idea goes to Doc directly or through Angelo or Danny. Doc might not take it up. If he does, he consults his lieutenants, passes it down, and the gang acts (p. 109).
  - Other roles are conceivable (not necessarily present in this group): a member who originates ideas and persuades Doc, or one who diffuses approved opinions. Footnote 41 mentions a sociometric role called the power behind the throne (pp. 109–110).
- In the groups discussed, the nominated leader probably combines the roles, though Taylor seems to do more personal transmitting than Doc (p. 110). A located leader may more often be transmitter or originator than influential. Unless he is also nominated, approval may depend on someone else. The study used does not test this (p. 110).
- *Bavelas and Leavitt* (pp. 110–112). Five-member groups were seated so that the number of "neighbors" a member could contact varied.
  - Patterns: **chain** (ends have one neighbor, the three between have two), **circle** (everyone has two), **wheel** (four members each linked only to one man in the centre).
  - Members sat in separate coloured cubicles and communicated through slots along allowed routes. The task was to collect pieces of information from each man, collate them and redistribute the solution to all (pp. 110–111).
  - **Wheel:** after the first several trials a standard communication sequence developed and held for all fifteen trials. It produced the single fastest solution. All who named any leader agreed on the middle man. Wheel members perceived the group structure most accurately, but expressed the most dissatisfaction with their jobs (p. 111).
  - **Circle:** no consistent organization and no perception of how the group was organized. It made the most errors. Only half thought they had a leader, and they disagreed on who. Its members were most satisfied (p. 111).
- Leavitt's **centrality**: the degree of access a member has to all others. Where centrality was distributed evenly, members' influence was about equal. Where the structure gave high centrality to one member, he was most likely to take control, and others recognized their dependence on him. The central person was most satisfied, most often named leader, and had the crucial role in solving the problem (pp. 111–112).
- Implications (pp. 112–113):
  - Seek individuals with greater access to all elements of a task. Whether their influence extends beyond that matter is "quite another question". In every group examined, the leader was better informed on relevant matters, and "the shadow of the 'expert'" looms.
  - Contact and mobility may determine communication roles. In the OWI wartime rumor study (via Allport and Postman), socially active people were more rumor-prone: 60% of the "socially active" were rumor agents against 30% of the secluded. Working women heard and spread more stories than housewives.
  - The route of a gregarious person probably matters more than frequency. Those who move between a group and part of its environment (the minstrel, the traveling salesman) probably play key roles. Footnote 44 gives examples from pre-industrial rural areas: bus drivers, a teacher spending summers in a city, and young people with city contact challenging the old as opinion leaders.
- Summary: strategic location seems to lead to a key communication role. Centrality is the most significant location so far, especially when members are interdependent on a problem. Sometimes peripherality may matter more, since marginal people may be better placed toward an outside environment (p. 113). Footnote 45: transmission need not be accompanied by influentiality (p. 113).

*Intuition note.* In the experiments, the member with access to everyone was most likely to take control and was named leader. The wheel produced the single fastest solution but its members expressed most dissatisfaction; the circle made the most errors but its members were most satisfied. Mobile or marginal people can matter too, because they connect the group to the outside.

**(C) Certification: cultural aspects (pp. 114–115).**
- Cultures ascribe influentiality to certain roles in certain groups; the authors look at relatively formal groups where culture, rather than situational patterns of activity, supplies the behavioral prescriptions. Location and certification both may give a head-start toward nomination (p. 114).
- Strodtbeck: married couples from three cultural groups each privately named the best all-round family and the criteria, then met to agree on one. Cultural differences accounted for who "won", i.e. changed the other's announced opinion (p. 114):
  - Navaho (women independent; husband lives with wife's family): wives won many more decisions.
  - Mormons (husband supreme): husbands won more.
  - Texas Presbyterians (egalitarian norm): husbands and wives won equally often.
- Strodtbeck says the balance of winning was predictable from the social and cultural organization of the samples (pp. 114–115).
- Authors' claim: culture also prescribes the spheres in which influentiality is exercised. In our culture the domestic matter studied showed equal influence, while in politics political studies show the husband has the upper hand, "probably" because the culture awards it to him. Market research shows children, husbands and wives influencing different purchases (p. 115).
- **Closing procedure** (p. 115): specify the subject matter of the influencing; map the situations and networks; identify initiators, transmitters and influentials; and relate these roles to nomination, location and certification. Footnote 47 admits having underemphasized the best-known primary group of all (the word is garbled in the text layer; probably "family") (p. 115).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrate Leavitt's idea that unequal "access" produces a leader. We use a crude proxy of our own: direct links ÷ 4, the maximum possible among five people. Leavitt's real measure, access to all others, is not given in this unit.

- **Wheel** (centre C; spokes S1–S4 linked only to C): C has 4 links, so 4/4 = 1.00. Each spoke has 1 link, so 1/4 = 0.25. Access is very unequal, so we expect C to take control.
- **Chain** (A–B–C–D–E): ends A and E have 1 link, so 0.25. B, C and D have 2 links, so 2/4 = 0.50. The inequality is modest.
- **Circle** (A–B–C–D–E–A): everyone has 2 links, so 2/4 = 0.50. Access is equal, so we expect no clear leader and equal influence.

The wheel's centre is the only member with the full 1.00. The text reports that all who named any leader in the wheel agreed on the middle man, while in the circle only half thought they had a leader and they disagreed on who it was (p. 111).

## Concepts
- [[opinion-leader]] — here a bundle of roles (origin, sanction, diffusion of ideas), not one trait; the authors use "multidimensional" terms (pp. 82, 109, 113 fn. 45)
- [[relay-function]] (new) — personal transmitters carry media messages to otherwise unexposed people (p. 82)
- [[reinforcement-function]] (new) — media influence has a greater chance when it coincides with interpersonal communication (pp. 82–83)
- [[sociometry]] (new) — mapping group connections by members' choices; used to trace flow like a fluoroscopy (p. 85)
- [[group-cohesiveness]] (new) — attachment among members; more attachment means more and more successful influence attempts (p. 86)
- [[group-climate]] (new) — a group's "culture", e.g. democratic, authoritarian, laissez-faire; shapes communication patterns (pp. 91–92)
- [[pressure-toward-uniformity]] (new) — demand for opinion agreement; raises pressure to communicate and readiness to change (p. 93)
- [[selective-exposure]] — people tend to avoid communications conflicting with their predispositions; the authors find interpersonal flow similar (p. 96)
- [[strategic-points]] (new) — key communication roles at the origin, relay and terminal points of intra-group flow (p. 98)
- [[nominated-located-certified-leader]] (new) — three routes to a leader role: group choice in a situation, network position, cultural right (pp. 99–100)
- [[centrality]] (new) — Leavitt: degree of a member's access to all others; the major determinant of leadership where the structure gave one member high centrality (pp. 111–112)
- [[attributed-power]] (new) — influence members credit to someone; predicts imitation and success of influence attempts (pp. 103–104)
