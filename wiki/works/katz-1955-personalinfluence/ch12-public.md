# Public Affairs Leaders

## L0 — One line
Among the women surveyed, public affairs opinion leadership is rare and is related to social status and, more strongly, to gregariousness; influence flows mostly from men, and tends to flow from older to younger people and, it seems, from higher- to lower-status people.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Who are women's opinion leaders in public affairs, and how does influence reach other women? Katz and Lazarsfeld find that such leaders are rarer than in the other three areas studied. Leadership rises with social status. Information and interest alone do not explain this, so status works as an "enabling" factor. Gregariousness (social contact) matters even more. Life-cycle position (girls, small-family wives, large-family wives, matrons) matters little, except at low status. Most influence comes from men, mainly husbands and fathers, and tends to pass from older to younger people. People named as general "experts" tend to be higher in status than those who named them. Leadership here depends on social position and not only on interest.

**Prerequisites:** percentage tables; the idea of "holding a variable constant" (comparing groups equal on one variable); the idea of a follow-up sample reached via nominations.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A public affairs leader is a woman recently asked for advice on a current social or political issue. This is the rarest kind of leadership of the four areas studied (pp. 271, 273–274).
- Leadership rises with status: 19% / 12% / 6% for high / middle / low (p. 273). Status still matters when information is held constant, so it acts as an "enabling" factor (p. 275).
- Specific influence (advice in a recent incident) comes mainly from men, especially from husbands and fathers. Female leaders are mostly non-family friends and neighbours (p. 276).
- A three-jump follow-up of nominated "experts" shows that experts are better informed, more self-confident as influentials, higher in occupation, and women in the expert groups name women more often than women in the original sample do (pp. 280–284).
- Respondents mostly name experts from their own occupational group. Moving up the expert chain, designations tilt toward higher strata (p. 286).
- Gregariousness relates to leadership more strongly than status does. Gregarious women have five times as many leaders as non-gregarious ones, and for non-gregarious women status makes no difference (pp. 288–289).
- Life-cycle differences are weak: leadership declines slightly with age. This is opposite to the pattern in political participation (pp. 290–291).
- Cross-age influence flows mainly from older to younger people, both inside and outside the family (pp. 292–293).
- The decline with life-cycle is steeper at low status (Table 45), which the authors had suspected (pp. 293–294).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 271–272)
- In public affairs, more than in marketing or fashion, women are free to take part or not. Women can get through life without opinions on public affairs more easily than men can (p. 271).
- Women express more ignorance, talk less about these matters and claim less interest than men (p. 271).
- **Definition.** The study treats information and opinion, not direct political activity. A *public affairs leader* is a woman who recently was asked her advice on a social or political issue in the news (p. 271).
- Footnote 2: respondents were asked about domestic and international issues of the time. A woman counts as a leader if (a) she said on both interviews that her advice had been asked, or (b) she said so on one interview only but considered herself "more likely" than others to be consulted (p. 271).
- The authors say this type of leader is relatively rare (p. 272).

### Social Status and Public Affairs Leadership (pp. 272–275)
- **Three factors expected to link status and leadership** (p. 272–273):
  - *Education.* Better-educated people use more current-affairs media. Education is part of the status index (p. 272).
  - *Social climate.* Education needs social reinforcement, i.e. contact with politically active people (politician, lawyer, teacher). Such women feel pressure for at least minimal participation (p. 272).
  - *Leisure.* High-status women have more leisure and are more gregarious (Table 16, p. 230). Their contacts may expose them to the community's intellectual currents (p. 273).
- **Chart XII (p. 273).** Public affairs leaders by status level (n in brackets):

| Status | High (197) | Middle (250) | Low (244) |
|---|---|---|---|
| % leaders | 19% | 12% | 6% |

- Public affairs has the least opinion leadership of the four areas. High-status women are three times as likely as low-status women to be leaders. In marketing and fashion, status matters very little (pp. 273–274).
- **Information index** (fn. 5, p. 274). Two items: the recent British elections and a proposed Central Highway plan. Both correct = "high", one correct = "middle", both wrong = "low". The authors assume high scorers are also the most interested (p. 274).
- **Table 33 (p. 274).** Percent with high information: high status 72% (197), middle 52% (253), low 28% (246).
- **Test (p. 275).** Perhaps status matters only because it brings more informed people. If so, equally informed women should have roughly the same chances at any status level.
- **Table 34 (p. 275).** Percent leaders, information level × status (n in brackets):

| Information | High status | Middle | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | 21% (141) | 15% (127) | 10% (69) |
| Medium | 16% (45) | 11% (79) | 8% (77) |
| Low | – (11) | 2% (40) | 1% (97) |

- A highly informed high-status woman is twice as likely to be a leader as an equally informed low-status woman. The pattern persists "in varying degree" at other information levels (p. 275).
- **Conclusion.** Interest or knowledge cannot by itself generate opinion leadership. An objective "enabling" factor is needed. Here it is social status, which affords social climate, leisure and contextual "demand" for leadership (p. 275).
- *Intuition note:* Knowing and caring are not enough to become someone others ask. Your position must put you where people expect you to know, and give you the time to talk.

### Sex, Social Status and the Flow of Influence (pp. 275–279)
- **Data source.** Follow-up of specific influentials was thin here, and confirmation by designatees was lowest in this arena (Chapter II). The authors therefore report the influencee's own description of her leader (pp. 275–276).
  - Advantage: fairly complete coverage. Drawbacks: reliance on the influencee's testimony, and missing occupation data for the leader's breadwinner. Occupation is used as a status index (p. 276).
- **Who influences** (p. 276). Almost two-thirds of the persons named were men. Two-thirds of those men were immediate family (husbands, parents; fn. 9). About 80% of the named women were non-family. Personal influence figured in about 40% of opinion changes (fn. 8).
- **Table 35 (p. 277).** Relationship of leader to influencee (%):

| Leader is | Married, male leader (116) | Married, female (62) | Single, male (24) | Single, female (16) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Friend, neighbour, co-worker | 16 | 75 | 25 | 87 |
| Parent | 5 | 8 | 59 | 12 |
| Husband | 66 | – | – | – |
| Other relative | 15 | 27 | 16 | – |

  - Married women's male leaders are mostly husbands. Single women's are mostly fathers. Female leaders are mainly friends and neighbours (pp. 276–277). The married-female column sums to 110 (see Reading notes).
- **Outside the family** (mostly female leaders). Table 36 has only 37 cases, so conclusions are tentative (pp. 277–278). Counts of influencee's breadwinner's occupation (columns) by leader's (rows):

| Leader's breadwinner | Wage earner | White collar | Business/prof. | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wage earner | 9 | 2 | 4 | 15 |
| White collar | 3 | 1 | 1 | 5 |
| Business/professional | 5 | 5 | 7 | 17 |
| Total | 17 | 8 | 12 | 37 |

  - Same-status pairs: 46%. For marketing the figure is 66%, for fashion 57%. So there seems to be more cross-status exchange in public affairs (p. 278).
  - Advice-seeking seems directed upward. 5 of the 7 white-collar people who leave their own orbit consult business and professional people. Wage earners also ascend, and professionals often cross status lines too (p. 278).
  - **Authors' speculation, if more cases confirmed it:** in this arena upper-status people become accessible to lower-status people (electioneering, welfare campaigns, civic concerns). Lower-class advisees may gain "borrowed prestige"; upper-class advisers do "a civic good deed" (p. 278).
  - The "real locus" of public affairs influence for these women is inside the household (p. 277).
- **Inside the family** (pp. 278–279). Almost eight of ten incidents are between people of the same status. This is expected because the breadwinner's occupation defines both. Where status differs, influence tends to go from higher to lower (p. 279). The "family" category includes relatives who did not share the respondent's household (p. 279).

### The Experts in Public Affairs (pp. 279–283)
- **General vs specific influentials.** *Specific* influentials are linked to a particular advice incident. *General* influentials ("experts") are named in answer to: "Do you know anyone around here who keeps up with the news and whom you can trust to let you know what is really going on?" (fn. 6, p. 276; p. 279).
- **Procedure: three jumps** (pp. 279–280). Experts named by the original sample were interviewed. They were asked to name their own experts, who were interviewed in turn. This was repeated for a third group.

| Step | Names obtained | Interviewed |
|---|---|---|
| Jump 1 (original sample → experts) | 368 (about half the women named someone) | 322 |
| Jump 2 | 216 (two-thirds of group I named someone) | 214 |
| Jump 3 | 164 (four-fifths of group II named someone) | 107 |

  - About four of five persons named were interviewed. The authors report no appreciable difference between those interviewed and (from what nominators said) those not interviewed (p. 280, fn. 10). The text-layer figures of fn. 10 are garbled.
  - The text prints "3868" for the first count; the context ("initial 368", Table 37) gives 368.
- **Naming an expert goes with being influential** (pp. 280–281).
  - **Objection (implied):** someone who relies on an expert is passive. **Authors reply:** not so (p. 281).
  - **Table 37 (p. 281).** Women who name an expert vs those who do not:

| | Names expert (368) | Does not (347) |
|---|---|---|
| Considers self more likely than peers to be asked for advice | 27% | 17% |
| Recently asked for advice | 22% | 10% |

- **Table 38 (p. 282).** Along the three jumps:

| | Original (718) | Group I (322) | Group II (214) | Group III (107) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Self-appraised more likely to be asked | 22% | 62% | 67% | 69% |
| Recently asked advice | 16% | 46% | 57% | 57% |

  - The further out, the more groups say they know generally influential people and the more they confirm their own influentiality. The authors call this a confirmation of the follow-up technique (pp. 281–282).
- **Information.** Experts score higher on the information index than the original sample. Each group scores higher than the group that named it. Differences are larger for women than for men (p. 282).
  - Fn. 11: an index of ten "formulated opinions" shows the same pattern. Among women the differences persist with education held constant. Among men, differences between expert groups become negligible with education held constant. Men are more likely than women to hold opinions (p. 282).
- **Table 39 (p. 283).** Women's choice of expert's sex:

| Expert is | Original (368) | Group I (75) | Group II (38) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Male | 67% | 52% | 48% |
| Female | 33% | 48% | 52% |

  - Men in expert groups name men almost only (about 95%, fn. 12). So the flow is "clearly" from men to women overall. At the upper end of the hierarchy, women name women more often (p. 283).
  - Fn. 13: March (1954), a League of Women Voters study, found that more politically active women rely less on their husbands' advice. The authors' data "seem to support" this (p. 283).
- *Intuition note:* The people who name experts tend to be somewhat influential themselves. Walking up the chain of nominations leads to ever more informed and self-aware influentials.

### The Experts and the Flow of Influence (pp. 283–287)
- On three criteria (income, interviewers' rating, occupation), designated experts rank higher than those who named them. Each expert group ranks higher than the one before (p. 283).
- **Table 40 (p. 284).** Occupation, in % (n in brackets). Fn. 14: where a person is not the breadwinner, the breadwinner's occupation is recorded.

| | Original (793) | Group I (219) | Group II (189) | Group III (99) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Business/professional | 9 | 24 | 33 | 33 |
| High white collar | 21 | 27 | 39 | 47 |
| Low white collar | 19 | 14 | 10 | 10 |
| Wage worker | 51 | 35 | 18 | 10 |

  - Wage earners are 51% of the population but are under-represented among experts, and decline at each jump. This supports the earlier finding that leaders concentrate in the upper strata (p. 284).
- **Table 41 (p. 285).** Who designates whom: % of experts' occupations named, by respondent occupation (original sample / expert group 1 / expert group 2):

| Expert's occupation | B&P respondents (31 / 53 / 25) | White-collar (103 / 82 / 56) | Wage earner (105 / 59 / 18) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business/professional | 71 / 49 / 60 | 23 / 33 / 32 | 10 / 19 / 22 |
| White collar | 23 / 44 / 28 | 57 / 59 / 64 | 31 / 46 / 44 |
| Wage earner | 6 / 7 / 12 | 20 / 9 / 4 | 59 / 35 / 33 |

  - The largest share usually goes to the respondent's own group: seven of nine columns. The exceptions are wage earners in groups I and II, who name white-collar experts most (p. 284). Even they, when leaving their class, name the middle and not the top group (pp. 284–285).
  - The average status of the designated expert declines "sharply and without exception" as the designator's status declines (p. 285).
  - **Terminal experts** (named by group II). Business/professional ones spread their choices more widely. The authors suggest ("probably") these are top leaders with their "ear to the ground". Wage-earner terminal designatees spread most evenly of all, probably barbers or gas station attendants in contact with higher classes (p. 285).
  - **Middle class.** Moving up the expert chain, white-collar respondents name their own peers more and more (57 → 59 → 64%). The authors call this "parochialism" and leave interpretation to specialists (pp. 285–286).
  - Fn. 15 (small numbers): low white collar is less solidary than high white collar. Business/professional and wage-earner groups both choose high white collar more than low. The high white-collar group is the best educated (p. 286).
- **Summary of general influence** (p. 286). People choose experts mostly within their own group. Up the chain there is a tendency toward *vertical influence*: higher strata are increasingly designated. Two qualifications:
  1. The topic is broad public issues, not elections. Other studies suggest upper-stratum general experts do not seem to influence the actual vote of lower occupational groups.
  2. These are people with a general *reputation* for expertise. They need not be the most influential in everyday face-to-face contact.
- **Comparison with specific influence** (pp. 286–287).
  - Parallel: in specific influence, extra-family influence seems to take a somewhat downward direction, and the authors say that in general this is the case for the experts as well.
  - Differences: specific influentials are mostly inside the family; general ones are mostly extra-family. In specific influence, extra-family leaders were mostly female. For general influence, men were named much more often, inside or outside the family.
  - The authors attribute this to the questions asked: a recent advice incident vs merely knowing a presumably approachable, informed, trustworthy person (p. 287).
  - Fn. 16: the main discrepancy is the white-collar group, specific influence being least solidary. The authors think the discrepancy lies with the high white-collar experts (p. 286–287).

### Gregariousness and Public Affairs Leadership (pp. 287–289)
- **Chart XIII (p. 288).** Leadership rises with gregariousness (n: 189 high, 324 medium, 195 low). Gregarious women have proportionately five times as many leaders as non-gregarious women. The relation is stronger here than in any other area studied (p. 288). Chart percentages are not given in the text I could read.
- Gregarious women are also better informed. With information held constant, gregarious women are still more likely to be leaders than equally or better informed women of lower gregariousness (p. 288). Footnote 18 percentages (high / medium / low gregariousness) at information levels High, Middle, Low (p. 289):

| Information | High greg. | Medium greg. | Low greg. |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | 25% (118) | 14% (155) | 7% (73) |
| Middle | 18% (52) | 12% (97) | 3% (59) |
| Low | – (19) | 1% (72) | 2% (63) |

- In *marketing*, gregariousness explained away the status–leadership link. Equally gregarious women had roughly similar chances at every status level (p. 289).
- **Table 42 (p. 289).** % leaders, gregariousness × status:

| Gregariousness | High status | Middle | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | 25 | 17 | 13 |
| Middle | 17 | 12 | 5 |
| Low | 3 | 6 | 3 |

  - Controlling gregariousness does not fully remove the status differences. Status and gregariousness are independently related to leadership, but gregariousness is the more important (p. 289).
  - A highly gregarious low-status woman has a better-than-average chance (13% vs 12% for the whole sample, fn. 20). For a non-gregarious woman status makes no difference (p. 289).
  - Fn. 21: in *fashions*, the two factors were equally strong. Low-gregarious women there still differed by status. In public affairs they have "no chance at all" (p. 289; the authors' loose wording, since Table 42 shows 3–6% leaders at low gregariousness).
- *Intuition note:* In public affairs, being in frequent contact with many people matters more than status. High status helps further, but a talkative low-status woman can beat the average.

### Life-Cycle Position and Public Affairs Leadership (pp. 290–291)
- **Three views** (p. 290):
  - **Common assumption:** household and child-rearing compete with public affairs, so women with the most domestic duties would be least involved.
  - **Another view:** women's role is changing, especially among the young, e.g. through coeducation. So young women would be most active.
  - **Counter-evidence:** many studies find younger people, men and women, less interested, less informed and less participating. So girls would be less active leaders and older women more.
- **Chart XIV (p. 291).** % leaders: girls 17% (78), small-family wives 13% (174), large-family wives 10% (169), matrons 8% (252). Life-cycle is only weakly related, and least related in this area of the four. Leadership declines slightly and steadily with age (pp. 290–291).
- This contrasts with studies in which political interest and participation rise with age. It suggests that opinion leadership in public affairs may be something qualitatively different from political participation (p. 291).
- **Three possible explanations (authors' "may be")** (p. 291): (1) these leaders are a-political, seeing "news" and "good/bad" community issues instead of partisanship; (2) girls talk more because they move among men on a more equal level and must keep up competence; (3) younger women are not really more concerned, but others think so because of their more recent education and so seek them out.

### Age and the Flow of Influence (pp. 292–293)
- Cross-age influence goes mainly downward: from older influentials to younger influencees, inside and outside the family (p. 292).
- **Table 43 (outside the family, p. 292).** Counts, rows = leader's age, columns = influencee's age (15–24 / 25–44 / 45+):

| Leader's age | 15–24 | 25–44 | 45+ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15–24 | 14 | 2 | 1 | 17 |
| 25–44 | 6 | 19 | 6 | 31 |
| 45+ | 1 | 12 | 20 | 33 |
| Total | 21 | 33 | 27 | 81 |

  - Two-thirds sought a contemporary (the diagonal sums to 53 of 81; added step). Of cross-age cases, the flow is more often old → young (p. 292). Counting cells (added step): older leader → younger influencee = 6 + 1 + 12 = 19; younger leader → older influencee = 2 + 1 + 6 = 9.
- **Table 44 (within the family, p. 292).** Counts:

| Leader's age | 15–24 | 25–44 | 45+ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15–24 | 3 | 6 | 2 | 11 |
| 25–44 | 15 | 43 | 9 | 67 |
| 45+ | 15 | 14 | 30 | 59 |
| Total | 33 | 63 | 41 | 137 |

  - Inside the family the downward flow is even sharper. Young women consult mainly elders; older women exchange more between peers (p. 293).
  - Not surprising, since family leaders are mostly husbands and fathers (p. 293).

### The Joint Role of Status Level and Life-Cycle (pp. 293–294)
- **Hypothesis.** Life-cycle may relate more sharply to leadership at lower status (p. 293).
- **Reasoning.**
  - High-status women face fewer restrictions from family duties (more help and conveniences).
  - They also receive supplementary stimuli throughout life (contact with people "in the know", prestige for knowing public affairs).
  - Low-status women lack these, and participation is marginal for them. They would most likely lose interest with time (pp. 293–294).
  - Hence the authors "suspect" a steeper decline among low-status women (p. 294).
- **Table 45 (p. 294).** % leaders:

| Status | Girls | Small-family wives | Large-family wives | Matrons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High | 24 | 15 | 21 | 17 |
| Middle | 12 | 19 | 5 | 8 |
| Low | 15 | 5 | 6 | 3 |

  - Results are "much as we expected". At high status life-cycle matters little (girls 24% vs large-family wives 21%). At middle status only girls and small-family wives are at or above the 12% sample average. At low status only the girls are (fn. 22, p. 294).
- *Intuition note:* High-status women seem to keep their involvement across life stages. For low-status women, youth seems to be the main window.

### The Flow of Influence in Public Affairs: A Summary (pp. 294–295)
- The typical public affairs leader differs from marketing and fashion leaders. Life-cycle matters little except at low status. Status matters much more than it did in marketing and fashion (pp. 294–295).
- Higher-status women seem to move in a climate that promotes participation. Influence seems to move more often from higher to lower status (p. 295).
- Much of the explanation for more leaders among higher-status women is that they are relatively freer from household responsibility (p. 295).
- Gregariousness is the stronger of the two factors (status, gregariousness) and "seems to be the major key" to leadership in public affairs (p. 295).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
*Does status matter beyond information?* Suppose 200 high-status and 200 low-status women. In each status group, "high information" women are the potential leaders.

**World A: information explains everything.** Leader rate is 20% for high-information women and 2% for low-information women, at any status.
- High status: 150 high-info, 50 low-info. Leaders = 150 × 0.20 + 50 × 0.02 = 30 + 1 = 31, so 31/200 = 15.5%.
- Low status: 50 high-info, 150 low-info. Leaders = 50 × 0.20 + 150 × 0.02 = 10 + 3 = 13, so 13/200 = 6.5%.
- The overall gap (15.5 vs 6.5) disappears within each information level (20 vs 20; 2 vs 2).

**World B: the pattern of Table 34.** High-info women are leaders at 20% in high status and 10% in low status.
- Same 150 / 50 mix for high status: 150 × 0.20 = 30 leaders.
- For low-status high-info women, 50 × 0.10 = 5 leaders.
- The gap remains at equal information (20% vs 10%). So status is an independent "enabling" factor.

This mirrors the authors' logic: control the rival explanation, then see whether the gap remains (p. 275).

## Concepts
- [[opinion-leader]] — here a woman recently asked advice on a current social or political issue, a rarer type than in other areas (pp. 271, 273)
- [[public-affairs-leader]] (new) — the operational definition of leader in this area, with two criteria across interviews (p. 271, fn. 2)
- [[enabling-factor]] (new) — an objective factor (here social status) needed to turn interest and knowledge into actual leadership (p. 275)
- [[gregariousness]] (new) — amount of social contact; here more strongly related to public affairs leadership than status is (pp. 288–289)
- [[specific-vs-general-influentials]] (new) — leaders tied to a recent advice incident vs "experts" named in answer to a general question (pp. 276, 279)
- [[snowball-follow-up]] (new) — chain of three jumps: nominated experts are interviewed and asked to nominate their own experts (pp. 279–280)
- [[vertical-influence]] (new) — tendency for higher strata to be designated as experts at each step up the chain (p. 286)
- [[life-cycle-position]] (new) — girls, small-family wives, large-family wives, matrons; weakly related to leadership (pp. 290–291)
- [[homophily-heterophily]] — much advice-seeking stays within one's own status or age group, with notable cross-status or old-to-young flow (pp. 278, 286, 292)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Three-jump follow-up ↔ social network analysis: snowball (chain-referral) sampling — respondents nominate further respondents.
- [structural-analogy] "Holding information constant" (Table 34) ↔ statistics: stratified comparison to test a confounder.
- [structural-analogy] Experts naming experts of ever higher status ↔ network science: nomination chains drifting toward high-status or high-degree nodes.
- [structural-analogy] Homophily within own occupational group, with a drift upward ↔ homophily/heterophily in diffusion research.
- [metaphor] "Enabling factor" ↔ preconditions or opportunity structure in social action.

## Questions this raises
- Is the gregariousness effect partly circular, since asking advice requires contact with others? (This unit does not discuss the issue.)
- How far do findings from reported advice-giving generalise to behaviours such as voting? (The authors note that other studies suggest upper-stratum general experts do not seem to influence the actual vote, p. 286.)
- Are cross-status flows in Table 36 real, given only 37 cases and influencee testimony?
- Why would leadership decline with age while political participation rises (p. 291)? Which of the three explanations fits?
