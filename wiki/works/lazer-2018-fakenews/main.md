# The science of fake news

## L0 — One line
The authors argue that fake news is poorly measured and poorly understood, so a multidisciplinary research program, with platform cooperation, is needed to define it, measure it and test remedies.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The paper asks how big a problem fake news is and what can be done. The authors define fake news as fabricated content that mimics news in form, not in process or intent. They say prevalence and impact are barely measured. Evidence that fact checking or education will solve the problem is "at best" limited. Platform-level interventions are raised as a possibility, but the authors say platform claims about their efforts are unverifiable without third parties. The authors compare the situation to mid-20th-century fears of propaganda: even if fake news does not alter most people's beliefs, it may reinforce false beliefs and shape the agenda. They call for interdisciplinary research and platform–academic collaboration.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of social media ranking algorithms; basic media-effects vocabulary (attitudes, priming, framing, agenda setting).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Fake news is fabricated content that mimics news in form but not in organizational process or intent; the authors would measure it by publisher rather than by single stories. It is a subcategory of misinformation (pp. 2–3).
- The authors believe that declining trust in news and growing partisan sorting have created a context where fake news can attract a mass audience (pp. 2–3).
- Prevalence and impact are largely unmeasured. Exposure is not impact, and shares or likes can be manipulated (p. 3).
- Fact checking and education are untested or have mixed evidence. Selective exposure (preferring confirming information), confirmation bias (finding belief-consistent information more persuasive) and familiarity effects (familiar claims are more likely accepted as true) may blunt fact checking (pp. 4–5).
- Platforms are the main conduits. Engagement-maximizing algorithms and bots (automated accounts) may amplify fake news, and the authors call for ongoing audits by researchers (pp. 5–6).
- The authors say platform claims about addressing misinformation that third parties cannot verify should be dismissed as self-serving. Regulation and liability both raise difficult issues (p. 7).
- By analogy with propaganda research, fake news may matter even without changing attitudes (pp. 7–8).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction.** The authors call for a multidisciplinary effort to study misinformation in the new news ecosystem, because older safeguards "have proven inadequate" (p. 1). Two groups of questions follow. (1) Definition: how prevalent is fake news, how influential, and in what form? (2) Intervention: can individuals be empowered, can platforms reduce its flow, can it be detected algorithmically, and how can industry and academics collaborate? The essay offers "preliminary assessments" (p. 2).

**Definition.** Fake news is fabricated information that mimics news media output in form but not in organizational process or intent. Fake news outlets lack editorial norms for accuracy (p. 2). It is a subcategory of misinformation, defined as incorrect or misleading information about the world. It is especially harmful because it undermines the credibility of standard outlets. The authors keep the term despite its use as a political weapon, for two reasons: it is a distinct scientific construct, and its salience draws attention (p. 2).

**Background.** Journalistic norms of objectivity and balance arose as a backlash against World War I propaganda and 1920s corporate public relations. They were sustained by the oligopolies of publishing and broadcasting. The Internet removed many constraints on dissemination (p. 2). Figures given:
- US newspaper circulation fell 30% from 1990 to 2012.
- Evening network news ratings fell 56% from 1980 to 2010.
- Trust in media (great deal or fair amount) in 1997 was 64% of Democrats and 41% of Republicans. By 2016 these had fallen 13 points (to 51%) and 27 points (to 14%) (p. 2).

Geographic partisan polarization has risen over 40 years. Homogeneous networks reduce tolerance for other views, amplify polarization, raise acceptance of ideologically compatible news and increase closure to new information (pp. 2–3). The authors "believe" these trends let fake news attract a mass audience (p. 3).

**Prevalence and impact.** Few scientific answers exist (p. 3). The authors advocate measuring publishers rather than individual stories, since intent and process define fake news (p. 3). One study estimated that the average American encountered one to three fake stories in the month before the 2016 election. This is "likely" conservative, because only 156 stories were tracked (p. 3). Counting accounts that shared a story is not counting people who read or were affected, because likes and shares can be manipulated. A story shared by millions of automated accounts and few humans differs from the reverse (p. 3). Human attention to fake news is hard to measure outside the lab. Exposure does not equal impact. Evaluation of the medium-to-long-run impact of exposure on political behavior is "essentially nonexistent" in the literature, and other possible pathways (cynicism, apathy, extremism) are little evaluated (p. 3). The authors ask for shared research panels like the proprietary commercial ones (pp. 3–4).

*Intuition note:* A big share count can mean many bots rather than many persuaded people. Real impact needs data on actual people with known attributes.

**Interventions.** There are two categories: empowering individuals (fact checking, education) and structural changes in platform policy (p. 4).

*Fact checking* means directly identifying factually incorrect content, for example Politifact, Snopes or platform warnings (p. 4). The evidence for its efficacy is "at best mixed" (p. 4). Reasons given:
- People use media more for gratification than for truth seeking.
- Selective exposure and confirmation bias may block acceptance of a fact check, so those most likely to be deceived are least likely to believe the correction.
- Fluency (ease of recall) and familiarity bias: people tend to remember content but forget its context and accept familiar information as true. So providing any information may raise acceptance on re-encounter (p. 4).
- The evidence on repeating the claim is contradictory. The familiarity effect is confirmed, but recent experiments suggest retractions work better when they explicitly repeat the misinformation (pp. 4–5).

*Education.* The authors found no research on the causal impact of general critical-information training on judging source credibility. An emphasis on fake news might lower the perceived credibility of real outlets (p. 5).

*Summary:* Individual-level evidence is "at best quite limited". People typically check credibility only if information violates their preconceptions or they are incentivized. They also align beliefs with their community's values, so polarization can foster self-reinforcing "alternative realities". Preference-based ranking may amplify perceived homogeneity. This points to the possibility of platform interventions that raise the quality and diversity of information (p. 5).

*Intuition note:* Corrections can fail because the people who need them distrust them, and repeating a false claim may make it feel familiar. This is why the authors want conditions tested, not assumed.

**Platform-based detection and intervention.** Platforms are the main enablers and conduits of fake news. Fake sites are cheap to build and easy to monetize through ads (p. 5). About 44% of Americans get news from social media often or sometimes, mostly Facebook. Social media matter far more than mainstream media as conduits for fake news sites. The authors state that Russia "successfully" manipulated all major platforms in 2016, citing Senate testimony (p. 5). Platform business models monetize attention using models that predict engagement. For example, most Facebook users do not see all content produced by their friends, but just the content Facebook predicts they are likely to engage with (p. 6). It is "quite plausible" that this increases selective exposure, and there is some evidence of this for Facebook and Google. But the research gives rapidly fading snapshots. The Google of 2010 cannot be recreated, but the Google of 2017 can be captured. More generally, the authors say researchers need to conduct a rigorous ongoing audit of how major platforms filter information (p. 6).

Platforms are highly vulnerable to manipulation. Social bots (automated accounts impersonating humans) and extreme partisans can amplify fake news and game engagement-prediction algorithms. Bots were responsible for a significant portion of political content in 2016, and some of the same bots later attempted to influence the 2017 French election. A Facebook white paper reports widespread manipulation efforts (p. 6). Possible interventions:
- signals of source quality
- source quality in rankings
- less personalization of political content
- excluding bots from trending measures
- curbing automated spread by bots and cyborgs (humans aided by automated posting) (p. 6)

Platforms have surely tried these. Facebook claims malicious manipulation was under 0.1% of civic content shared, but such claims are unverifiable. The authors say researchers, politicians and the public "should dismiss as self-serving" any platform claims about addressing misinformation that third parties cannot verify (p. 7). They think collaboration barriers are surmountable and that platforms have an ethical responsibility to collaborate (p. 7).

Governance options (p. 7):
- Direct government regulation risks perceived partiality in content-based distinctions.
- Tort suits (for example defamation) could pressure platforms, but a 1996 Communications Decency Act provision gives near-comprehensive immunity.
- Changing that regime raises its own issues.

Structural interventions raise concerns about human agency. The authors note that Internet oligopolies already shape experience globally, so the question is how that power should be exercised (p. 7).

**Conclusion.** Current worry mirrors mid-20th-century fear of Nazi and Communist propaganda. Post-WWII scholars "found little evidence" of such a threat (p. 7). Later scholars found that even if media typically cannot change what we think (our attitudes), they are far more successful at changing what we think about, via priming, framing and agenda setting (p. 8). By analogy, fake news may not alter most beliefs yet may reinforce false beliefs, raise their salience or shape the agenda (p. 8). The authors call for research across psychology, computer science, political science, economics, law and communication, and for redesigning the news ecosystem as 20th-century journalism norms once did (p. 8).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Story A is shared 1,000,000 times by bot accounts and 50 times by humans. Story B is shared 10,000 times, all by humans.
- Raw shares: A = 1,000,000 + 50 = 1,000,050; B = 10,000. A looks about 100 times bigger (1,000,050 ÷ 10,000 ≈ 100).
- Human shares: A = 50; B = 10,000. B has 10,000 ÷ 50 = 200 times more.

The ranking flips once bots are removed. Even human shares do not show who read or was affected, which is the paper's point that exposure is not impact (p. 3).
