# The A_p distribution and rule of succession

## L0 — One line
Introducing the proposition "A_p: the probability of A is p" and keeping its density lets a reasoner store, in one function, what it needs from past experience about A, including how stable that state is, and yields Laplace's rule of succession as a defensible, well-defined inference.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Jaynes asks how a reasoner can store conclusions instead of raw facts, and how two probabilities of 1/2 (a checked coin; life on Mars) can differ in stability. His answer: introduce A_p, "the probability of A is p", and keep the density (A_p|E). Its first moment is P(A|E); its width largely determines stability. A uniform density plus binomial data gives Laplace's rule of succession, (n+1)/(N+2), generalised to K hypotheses as (n₁+1)/(N+K). He defends the rule against classic objections, arguing they ignore extra prior information or change the hypotheses. He compares it with frequentist confidence intervals and cites de Finetti's theorem: every exchangeable sequence has an A_p representation.

**Prerequisites:** sum and product rules; Bayes' theorem; binomial distribution; Beta integral; first and second moments; Laplace transform and residues (for the K-hypothesis proof).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Two propositions with the same P(A) can differ in stability. The density (A_p|E) carries this; P(A|E) is only its first moment, eq. (18.7) (pp. 553–555).
- For "completely resolvable" prior information, the robot needs only (A_p|E) to absorb new evidence F, via the odds formula (18.16). It can store and update this one function instead of searching its memory, though a wholly new proposition still needs a full archive search (pp. 556–558).
- Stability is governed essentially by the width of (A_p|E). This is how a carefully examined penny and Mars can both sit at 1/2 (pp. 558–559).
- "Probability of a probability" is explained as two levels of reasoning, an outer robot and an inner robot. Jaynes calls this a tentative, preliminary exploration (wild speculation, if you prefer) (pp. 559–561).
- A flat (A_p|X) with binomial data gives P(N_n|X) = 1/(N+1) and Laplace's rule (n+1)/(N+2). The K-hypothesis version is (n₁+1)/(N+K) (pp. 562–563, 568–571).
- Jaynes argues that the classic objections (sunrise, hydrogen, elephants on Mars, feathered animals) apply the rule where extra prior information exists or the hypothesis space differs. He says the rule answers a definite problem with two hypotheses, a constant mechanism and no other information (pp. 564–568).
- The Laplace variance of the predicted count is larger than the frequentist plug-in variance V₀. Jaynes argues that the frequentist's total uncertainty is larger still once the uncertainty in p is added, because consistent use of Bayes' theorem handles the finiteness of the preliminary data automatically (pp. 582–585).
- For two hypotheses, Carnap's λ-methods follow from the prior ∝ [p(1−p)]^r (λ = 2 is Laplace's rule). Our intuitive notion of weight of evidence is bound up with the sharpness of the A_p density (pp. 572–575).
- De Finetti: any exchangeable sequence is generated by some g(p). This puts A_p on solid mathematical ground (pp. 586–588).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening and 18.1 Memory storage for old robots (pp. 553–555)
**Problem.** Each new problem makes the robot scan all its memory X. Human brains instead store summaries of past conclusions. Can the robot be given such a mechanism? (p. 553)

**Penny vs Mars.** Jaynes assigns 1/2 to heads on the first toss of a carefully checked penny and 1/2 to there having once been life on Mars, but his internal state differs. After five tails with the penny he would still say 1/2. He is ready to change his Mars probability completely on hearing one more fact (pp. 553–554). Footnote 1 uses Mars to counter the claim that theories can only be disproved: one fossil proves life, while digging up every square foot would not disprove it (p. 554).

**Two-number theory.** One might add a second number for stability. Jaynes says he once proposed this (early 1950s). He now thinks the present theory already contains it (p. 554).

**Definition.** A new, non-Aristotelian proposition is defined by

$$P(A\mid A_pE)\equiv p, \tag{18.1}$$

$$A_p\equiv \text{regardless of anything else you may have been told, the probability of }A\text{ is }p. \tag{18.2}$$

Bayes' theorem lets A_p move to the left of the bar. Jaynes writes (A_p|E) with bare brackets, calls it simply the density for A_p given E, and does not claim it is a "real probability". It only has to obey the rules of probability theory (p. 554).

If X says only that A can be true and can be false, then, as for the "completely ignorant population" of Chapter 12 (transformation-group argument),

$$(A_p\mid X)=1,\quad 0\le p\le 1. \tag{18.3}$$

Bayes' theorem gives

$$(A_p\mid EX)=(A_p\mid X)\frac{P(E\mid A_pX)}{P(E\mid X)}=\frac{P(E\mid A_p)}{P(E\mid X)}. \tag{18.4}$$

The A_p are mutually exclusive and exhaustive (indeed each flatly and dogmatically contradicts every other). The rules are structural, so they apply regardless of the proposition's meaning (p. 555):

$$P(A\mid E)=\int_0^1 dp\,(AA_p\mid E) \tag{18.5}$$

$$=\int_0^1 dp\,P(A\mid A_pE)(A_p\mid E)\tag{18.6}$$

$$=\int_0^1 dp\;p\,(A_p\mid E).\tag{18.7}$$

So P(A|E) is the first moment of the A_p density. Jaynes infers that the density should hold more about the robot's state of mind than P(A|E), and conjectures that such propositions solve both problems raised above and give a powerful analytical tool (p. 555).

*Intuition note.* Think of (A_p|E) as a whole curve, and of P(A|E) as only the curve's balance point. Two very different curves can balance at the same place, so the single number loses information.

### 18.2 Relevance (pp. 555–557)
Let E = E_aE_b with E_a relevant to A and E_b not relevant once E_a is given:

$$P(A\mid E)=P(A\mid E_aE_b)=P(A\mid E_a). \tag{18.8}$$

Bayes' theorem gives "weak irrelevance": given E_a, A is also irrelevant to E_b (p. 556):

$$P(E_b\mid AE_a)=P(E_b\mid E_a)\frac{P(A\mid E_bE_a)}{P(A\mid E_a)}=P(E_b\mid E_a). \tag{18.9}$$

(18.8) says only that the first moments of (A_p|E_a) and (A_p|E_aE_b) agree, so it does not make E_b irrelevant to A_p. "Strong irrelevance" means (18.8) holds for a given E_b whatever E_a is. Then

$$P(A\mid E)=\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid E_aE_b)=\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid E_a). \tag{18.10}$$

If this holds for all (A_p|E_a), the integrands are equal, and A_p is irrelevant to E_b (p. 556):

$$(A_p\mid E_aE_b)=(A_p\mid E_a),\tag{18.11}$$

$$P(E_b\mid A_pE_a)=P(E_b\mid E_a)\ \text{ for all }E_a. \tag{18.12}$$

For new evidence F:

$$P(A\mid EF)=\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid EF)=\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid E)\frac{P(F\mid A_pE)}{P(F\mid E)}.\tag{18.13}$$

In this ratio any part of E irrelevant to A_p cancels, by Bayes' theorem and (18.12):

$$\frac{P(F\mid A_pE_aE_b)}{P(F\mid E_aE_b)}=\frac{P(F\mid A_pE_a)\left[\frac{P(E_b\mid FA_pE_a)}{P(E_b\mid A_pE_a)}\right]}{P(F\mid E_a)\left[\frac{P(E_b\mid FE_a)}{P(E_b\mid E_a)}\right]}=\frac{P(F\mid A_pE_a)}{P(F\mid E_a)}.\tag{18.14}$$

Repeat until the remaining part E_aa contains nothing irrelevant to A_p. Then E_aa is only a statement about A, and A_p cancels it out of the numerator, so (F|A_pE_aa) = (F|A_p). Hence (p. 556)

$$P(A\mid EF)=\frac{1}{P(F\mid E_{aa})}\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid E)\,P(F\mid A_p).\tag{18.15}$$

**Caveat.** Jaynes has not proved that E can always be split into a fully relevant and a fully irrelevant part. The results hold when the prior information is "completely resolvable", which is not an empty case (pp. 556–557).

### 18.3 A surprising consequence (pp. 557–559)
P(F|E_aa) is only a normaliser, and it drops out in the odds:

$$O(A\mid EF)=\frac{P(A\mid EF)}{P(\bar A\mid EF)}=\frac{\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid E)P(F\mid A_p)}{\int_0^1dp\,(A_p\mid E)P(F\mid A_p)(1-p)}.\tag{18.16}$$

E enters only through (A_p|E). So the robot can store one density per proposition. After F it computes (A_p|EF) and erases the old one (p. 557). It cannot discard all past experience, because a new proposition may require a full archive search. Humans also recall past states of mind, not the original arguments (pp. 557–558).

**Stability.** If (A_p|E) is sharply peaked, F can change P(A) appreciably only if P(F|A_p) is even more sharply peaked elsewhere. If (A_p|E) is broad, a small slope in P(F|A_p) can change P(A) a lot. So stability is governed essentially by the width. There appears to be no single number that fully describes it, but when the density is well peaked its variance is a good measure. More prior information means a narrower density and a harder-to-change state of mind (p. 558).

**Penny vs Mars.** Fig. 18.2(a): the penny density is sharply peaked at 0.5. Fig. 18.2(b): Mars is broad, high near 0 and 1, low in the middle, "qualitatively". Both have first moment 1/2 (pp. 558–559).

**Penfield.** A newspaper report on Penfield's findings says the brain stores a very detailed record, but Penfield is quoted as saying that most of what a man can recall are generalizations and summaries (p. 559). Jaynes takes this as the hint for what the A_p density means.

*Intuition note.* P(A|E) says where your belief sits. The width of (A_p|E) says how much new data it takes to move it. The robot keeps both in one stored curve.

### 18.4 Outer and inner robots (pp. 559–561)
This section is explicitly tentative: a preliminary exploration, or wild speculation if you prefer (p. 561).
- The brain has conscious and subconscious functions, and the subconscious sends inspirations to the conscious mind (p. 559).
- By analogy, the **outer robot** reasons about the external world with ordinary probabilities. The **inner robot** reasons about the outer robot's activity, and the A_p density belongs to it. Both levels use Aristotelian propositions in their own context, so the same rules apply. "Probability of a probability" misses the point because the two probabilities are at different levels (p. 560).
- The outer robot should be able to call the inner one at will. Jaynes asks about "inner inner" robots and nested or parallel hierarchies. He compares them to computer levels (BIOS, command shell, programmers) and to brain organisation (p. 560).
- Russell's theory of types may be related, but Jaynes says it seems safest to scrap the early-20th-century "logistic" work and rebuild with information in mind and Kronecker's constructibility criterion (pp. 560–561).
- Lindley's epigraph: evolution should favour Bayesian brains. The outer brain may be corrupted by indoctrination while the inner brain keeps "Bayesian purity", so the joke may be literally true (p. 561).

### 18.5 An application (pp. 561–563)
**Setup.** X: for each trial two hypotheses, A true or A false. The causal mechanism is the same at every trial. Two consequences: P(A) on trial n does not depend on n, and past results stay equally relevant for all time. There is no other prior information. Notation: N_n = A true n times in N past trials; M_m = A true m times in M future trials (p. 561). The prior is (18.17), (A_p|X) = 1, with the same A_p distribution for all trials (p. 562). The binomials are

$$P(N_n\mid A_p)=\binom Nn p^n(1-p)^{N-n},\qquad P(M_m\mid A_p)=\binom Mm p^m(1-p)^{M-m}.\tag{18.18}$$

**Jaynes's claim about textbooks.** He says A_p is how almost all present textbooks introduce probability: an "absolute" or "physical" probability that exists but is never known exactly. Cramér is cited as taking it as an axiom. Whenever p appears as a given number, an A_p hides on the right of the bar. His two differences: he keeps the right-hand side of all probabilities explicit, and, thanks to Cox's theorems, he uses Bayes' theorem on A_p freely (p. 562).

**Prior predictive.**

$$P(N_n\mid X)=\int_0^1dp\,P(N_n\mid A_p)(A_p\mid X)=\binom Nn\int_0^1dp\,p^n(1-p)^{N-n}.\tag{18.19}$$

With the complete Beta function

$$\int_0^1dx\,x^r(1-x)^s=\frac{r!\,s!}{(r+s+1)!},\tag{18.20}$$

$$P(N_n\mid X)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1{N+1}&0\le n\le N\\[2pt]0&N<n,\end{cases}\tag{18.21}$$

the uniform distribution of maximum entropy (p. 563). Bayes' theorem gives

$$(A_p\mid N_n)=(A_p\mid X)\frac{P(N_n\mid A_p)}{P(N_n\mid X)}=(N+1)P(N_n\mid A_p).\tag{18.22}$$

Then

$$P(M_m\mid N_n)=\int_0^1dp\,(M_mA_p\mid N_n)=\int_0^1dp\,P(M_m\mid A_pN_n)(A_p\mid N_n).\tag{18.23}$$

Since P(M_m|A_pN_n) = P(M_m|A_p) by the definition of A_p, the integrand is known. Another Eulerian integral gives (added step: the integral is $\int_0^1 p^{n+m}(1-p)^{N+M-n-m}dp$)

$$P(M_m\mid N_n)=\frac{\binom{n+m}{n}\binom{N+M-n-m}{N-n}}{\binom{N+M+1}{M}}.\tag{18.24}$$

This is not the hypergeometric distribution of sampling theory. For M = m = 1:

$$P(A\mid N_n)=\frac{n+1}{N+2},\tag{18.25}$$

Laplace's rule of succession (p. 563).

*Intuition note.* A flat density over p implies that, before the data, every count n from 0 to N is equally likely (18.21). After the data, the rule can loosely be read as adding one imaginary success and one imaginary failure (cf. p. 575).

### 18.6 Laplace's rule of succession (pp. 563–566)
Jaynes says the rule is the most misunderstood and misapplied in the theory. In his design it is a constructive rule alongside indifference and maximum entropy, and a main link between probability and frequency (pp. 563–564).

**Sunrise.** Laplace applied it to 5000 years of sunrises, giving odds of 5000 × 365.2426 + 1 = 1 826 214 : 1 (p. 564; (added step) this is the n = N case, odds N+1 : 1). Jaynes finds nothing absurd. His claim is that critics (1) quote Laplace out of context and (2) apply the rule where additional prior information exists. Laplace's next sentence warns that the number is far greater for someone who sees the principle regulating days and seasons (p. 564). The rule uses only the information that the event occurred n times in N trials, so the sunrise was an unfortunate example. Footnote 3 notes the 5000-year figure came from biblical chronology (pp. 564–565).

**Objections (critics, as quoted by Jaynes, p. 565):**
1. After one solidification of hydrogen, the rule gives probability 2/3 of repeating. This is said not to reflect any scientist's belief.
2. A 10-year-old boy gets 11/12 of living another year and his 70-year-old grandfather 71/72. This is said to violate common sense.
3. With N = n = 0, any unverified conjecture gets 1/2. Then there is 1/2 for exactly 137 elephants on Mars, 1/2 for 138, and 1/2 for none. This is said to be self-contradictory.

**Jaynes's replies.** In (1) and (2), highly relevant prior information was ignored (p. 565). For (3), the derivation assumed only two propositions, A true or false. Admitting more possibilities already changes the prior information. With 137 ways for A to be false and one way for it to be true, and nothing else, the prior is 1/138, not 1/2 (p. 565). Footnote 4 compares this to the parameter-space size effect in the Chapter 15 marginalization paradox.

**Moral.** Probability theory needs a definite question. State the hypothesis space first. Not knowing what the possible propositions are is mathematically equivalent to not knowing what problem one wants to solve (p. 566).

### 18.7 Jeffreys' objection (p. 566)
**Jeffreys:** if one has seen 1 in 1000 of England's feathered animals, Laplace gives about 1/1000 for "all have beaks". This is said to match nobody's belief.

Jaynes agrees but adds two points. (i) The same rule gives about 1/1000 for each of "all but one", "all but two", and so on. With N feathered animals, r seen (all beaked), P₀ = (r+1)/(N+1) ≈ 1/1000 and

$$P_n=P_0\frac{(N-r)!\,(N-n)!}{N!\,(N-n-r)!},\tag{18.26}$$

$$\sum_{n=n_0}^{N}P_n=\frac{(N-r)!\,(N-n_0+1)!}{(N+1)!\,(N-n_0-r)!}\approx\exp\{-rn_0/N\}.\tag{18.27}$$

For N = 10⁶ and r = 1000, it is an even bet that at least 1000 ln 2 = 693 lack beaks. Jaynes thinks that, if that observation were the only relevant information, this would be the proper and reasonable inference (p. 566). (ii) We have extra information the rule ignores: hereditary stability of form, and the fact that a beakless feathered animal would be a famous curiosity (p. 566).

### 18.8 Bass or carp? (p. 567)
A reliable guide says a lake holds only bass and carp. Ten catches give ten carp. With bass fraction f the posterior is $P(f<f_0\mid DX)=1-(1-f_0)^{11}$, i.e. density $11(1-f)^{10}$.
- P(f < 0.15) = 1 − 0.85¹¹ = 0.833 (odds 5:1). Probability 2/3 that f < 9.5%, odds 10:1 that f < 19.6%.
- Median: $f_{1/2}=1-(1/2)^{1/11}=0.061$ (18.28). Interquartile range (2.6%, 11.8%). Posterior mean 1/12 = 8.3%.
- If the 11th catch is a bass (N = 11, n = 1): density $132f(1-f)^{10}$, mean 2/13 = 15.4%, median 13.6%, interquartile range (8.3%, 20.9%).

Jaynes finds these numbers match common sense. This is the kind of problem to which the rule applies realistically. When it conflicts with intuition, Jaynes suggests that intuition is using extra prior information (p. 567).

### 18.9 So where does this leave the rule? (p. 568)
The 200-year hang-up was what prior information a uniform prior describes. Laplace's own "probability of a probability" language was unclear. Jaynes says that only Jeffreys and Fisher seem to have thought it through enough to see that the unclear prior was the source of difficulty, while others after Venn merely produced conflicting examples and rejected the rule (p. 568). Probability theory solves only idealisations. For hydrogen, the prior information is too complicated to convert into a prior, though theory is in principle competent (p. 568). The rule is the solution of a definite problem: two hypotheses, a constant causal mechanism, and no other information (p. 568).

### 18.10 Generalization to K hypotheses (pp. 568–571)
K hypotheses {A₁,…,A_K}, a constant mechanism, N trials with A_i true n_i times (Σn_i = N), M future trials with m_i (Σm_i = M). The prior is

$$(A_{p_1}\cdots A_{p_K}\mid X)=C\,\delta(p_1+\cdots+p_K-1),\quad p_i\ge0.\tag{18.29}$$

**Normalisation.** Set

$$\int_0^\infty dp_1\cdots dp_K\,(A_{p_1}\cdots A_{p_K}\mid X)=1=C\,I(1),\tag{18.30}$$

$$I(r)\equiv\int_0^\infty dp_1\cdots dp_K\,\delta(p_1+\cdots+p_K-r).\tag{18.31}$$

The Laplace-transform trick avoids messy integration limits:

$$\int_0^\infty dr\,e^{-\alpha r}I(r)=\int_0^\infty dp_1\cdots dp_K\,e^{-\alpha(p_1+\cdots+p_K)}=\alpha^{-K},\tag{18.32}$$

$$I(r)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{-i\infty}^{+i\infty}d\alpha\frac{e^{\alpha r}}{\alpha^K}=\frac1{(K-1)!}\frac{d^{K-1}}{d\alpha^{K-1}}e^{\alpha r}\Big|_{\alpha=0}=\frac{r^{K-1}}{(K-1)!}.\tag{18.33}$$

The contour passes to the right of the origin and is closed by a left half-plane semicircle whose integral is zero. Hence

$$C=\frac1{I(1)}=(K-1)!.\tag{18.34}$$

**Prior on counts.**

$$P(n_1\cdots n_K\mid X)=\frac{N!}{n_1!\cdots n_K!}\int_0^\infty\!\!dp_1\cdots\!\int_0^\infty\!\!dp_K\,p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_K^{n_K}(A_{p_1}\cdots A_{p_K}\mid X)=\frac{N!\,(K-1)!}{n_1!\cdots n_K!}J(1),\tag{18.35}$$

$$J(r)\equiv\int_0^\infty dp_1\cdots dp_K\,p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_K^{n_K}\delta(p_1+\cdots+p_K-r).\tag{18.36}$$

$$\int_0^\infty dr\,e^{-\alpha r}J(r)=\prod_{i=1}^K\frac{n_i!}{\alpha^{n_i+1}},\tag{18.37}$$

$$J(r)=\frac{n_1!\cdots n_K!}{2\pi i}\int_{-i\infty}^{+i\infty}d\alpha\frac{e^{\alpha r}}{\alpha^{N+K}}=\frac{n_1!\cdots n_K!}{(N+K-1)!}r^{N+K-1},\tag{18.38}$$

$$P(n_1\cdots n_K\mid X)=\frac{N!\,(K-1)!}{(N+K-1)!},\quad n_i\ge0,\ \sum n_i=N.\tag{18.39}$$

**Posterior and predictive.**

$$(A_{p_1}\cdots A_{p_K}\mid n_1\cdots n_K)=\frac{(N+K-1)!}{n_1!\cdots n_K!}\,p_1^{n_1}\cdots p_K^{n_K}\,\delta(p_1+\cdots+p_K-1),\tag{18.40}$$

$$P(m_1\cdots m_K\mid n_1\cdots n_K)=\frac{M!}{m_1!\cdots m_K!}\frac{(N+K-1)!}{n_1!\cdots n_K!}\int_0^\infty dp_1\cdots dp_K\,p_1^{n_1+m_1}\cdots p_K^{n_K+m_K}\,\delta(p_1+\cdots+p_K-1).\tag{18.41}$$

The integral is J(1) with n_i → n_i + m_i, so

$$P(m_1\cdots m_K\mid n_1\cdots n_K)=\frac{M!}{m_1!\cdots m_K!}\frac{(N+K-1)!}{n_1!\cdots n_K!}\frac{(n_1+m_1)!\cdots(n_K+m_K)!}{(N+M+K-1)!},\tag{18.42}$$

$$P(m_1\cdots m_K\mid n_1\cdots n_K)=\frac{\binom{n_1+m_1}{n_1}\cdots\binom{n_K+m_K}{n_K}}{\binom{N+M+K-1}{M}}.\tag{18.43}$$

With M = m₁ = 1:

$$P(A_1\mid n_1NK)=\frac{n_1+1}{N+K}.\tag{18.44}$$

For N = n₁ = 0 this reduces to the principle of indifference. If K is a power of 2 it matches Carnap's c*(h,e) (p. 571).

**Small and large N.** Using the rule with very small N is "foolish, not wrong": the values are soft because the A_p density is still broad. When N ≫ K, the answer depends practically only on the observed data, not on K. Jaynes says common sense agrees that N ≫ K is the right criterion (p. 571). He also argues that anyone who equates probability with observed frequency in many trials is doing what the rule says, and finds it incredible that one can reject the rule while advocating a frequency definition. (18.44) supplies a needed refinement: small correction terms when N is not large compared with K (p. 571).

*Intuition note.* In (18.44) the "+1" in the numerator and the "+K" in the denominator can be read as one imaginary observation of each hypothesis. When N is much larger than K these imaginary counts are swamped by the real data.

### 18.11 Confirmation and weight of evidence (pp. 571–573)
For prior evidence E and new evidence F:

$$P(A\mid EF)=\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid EF)=\frac{\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid F)(A_p\mid E)}{\int_0^1dp\,(A_p\mid F)(A_p\mid E)}.\tag{18.45}$$

- **Compatible:** F is compatible with E, as far as A is concerned, if P(A|EF) = P(A|E) (18.46). The A_p density may still sharpen or broaden greatly (p. 572).
- **Confirms:** F confirms the old assignment if it is compatible and the A_p density also narrows (p. 572).
- For F = N_n, (18.22) gives

$$(A_p\mid N_n)=\frac{(N+1)!}{n!(N-n)!}p^n(1-p)^{N-n}\approx(\text{const})\exp\left\{-\frac{(p-f)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\},\tag{18.47}$$

$$\sigma^2=\frac{f(1-f)}{n},\qquad f=n/N,\tag{18.48}$$

via a Taylor expansion of the log about its peak, valid for n ≫ 1 and N−n ≫ 1. If the observed f is close to the prior P(A|E), N_n leaves the first moment alone and sharpens the density, which counts as confirmation. The same data confirm on the frequency view too (p. 572).

**Weight of evidence.**

$$(A_p\mid EF)=(\text{const})\,(A_p\mid E)(A_p\mid F).\tag{18.49}$$

If (A_p|F) is much sharper than (A_p|E), F carries much greater weight, in Jaynes's intuitive sense, and it hardly matters whether E is taken into account. Significant evidence is not necessarily evidence that changes P(A) much; it changes the density for A_p much (pp. 572–573).

**18.11.1 Is indifference based on knowledge or ignorance?** The principle of indifference needs (1) mutually exclusive, exhaustive possibilities and (2) no information favouring any. Condition (2) can hold through ignorance or through positive knowledge. Mr A examines a coin with all of the National Bureau of Standards' facilities and finds it honest. Mr B only knows a shady character is tossing it. Both start at 1/2. After a first head, Mr B shifts toward heads and, after many tosses, tends to the observed frequency. Mr A stays at 1/2, because the symmetry evidence outweighs a few tosses. The difference lies in the A_p density, not in P(A). A perfect-knowledge δ-function density could never change, but that limit is never reached in practice (pp. 573–574).

### 18.12 Carnap's inductive methods (pp. 574–575)
**Carnap's ad hoc principle:** the final P(A|N_nX) is a weighted average of the prior P(A|X) and the observed frequency f = n/N, with weight N on f and arbitrary weight λ on the prior; this defines c_λ(h,e). In the A_p theory these arise as special cases for different priors, and λ becomes the weight of prior evidence. For two hypotheses, take the prior ∝ [p(1−p)]^r with 2r = λ − 2 (p. 574):

$$P(A\mid N_nX)=\frac{2n+\lambda}{2N+2\lambda}=\frac{(n+r)+1}{(N+2r)+2}.\tag{18.50}$$

A larger λ means a more sharply peaked prior. Mr A would use λ of order thousands, Mr B perhaps 5 or 6. λ = 2 is Laplace's rule, "much too broad" for coins. Jaynes adds that, as Chapter 10 showed, this analysis does not always fit real coin tossing (p. 574).

The second form of (18.50) is a prior of (λ − 2) trials, half of them successes. The "+2" can be read as: knowing A is possible both ways is like knowing it was true at least once and false at least once. Jaynes says this is "hardly a derivation" (pp. 574–575).

**Pre-prior.** Interpret X as meaning that A was observed true once and false once, and grant that the flat density describes this state of knowledge. Applying Bayes' theorem backwards gives the earlier state X₀:

$$(A_p\mid X_0)\,dp=(\text{const})\frac{dp}{p(1-p)}.\tag{18.51}$$

This is the Chapter 12 "complete ignorance" measure, suggested by Haldane. Its discrete version is Eq. (6.49) of Chapter 6. Use the flat prior if one knows it is possible for A to be true or false; use (18.51) if even that is uncertain (p. 575). With this pre-prior, for n ≠ 0, N:

$$(A_p\mid N_nX_0)=\frac{(N-1)!}{(n-1)!(N-n-1)!}p^{n-1}(1-p)^{N-n-1},\tag{18.52}$$

$$P(A\mid N_nX_0)=\int_0^1dp\,p\,(A_p\mid N_n)=\frac nN,\tag{18.53}$$

the observed frequency, equal to the maximum-likelihood estimate. For 0 < n < N,

$$P(M_m\mid N_nX_0)=\frac{\binom{m+n-1}{m}\binom{M-m+N-n-1}{M-m}}{\binom{N+M-1}{M}}.\tag{18.54}$$

These results correspond to having observed one less success and one less failure (p. 575).

### 18.13 Probability and frequency in exchangeable sequences (p. 576)
Two connections: (a) observed frequency → probability assignment; (b) probability → predicted frequency. Chapters 11–12 (maximum entropy, transformation groups) gave (b) in some situations. The rule of succession gives (a) for a wide class: if A has been observed over very many trials, and the only knowledge is that result plus a constant mechanism, then P(A) at the next trial is practically the observed frequency. Jaynes says a frequentist does exactly this. He adds that defining probability as frequency is equivalent to saying the rule is the only way to convert data into probabilities (p. 576).

### 18.14 Prediction of frequencies (pp. 576–578)
Take M → ∞, m/M → f in (18.24), and define A_f as the proposition that the frequency of A in an indefinitely large number of trials is f (18.55). Then

$$P(A_f\mid N_n)=\frac{(N+1)!}{n!(N-n)!}f^n(1-f)^{N-n}\tag{18.56}$$

(the page prints N! in the denominator; see Reading notes). This is the same as (A_p|N_n) with f = p. The most probable f is n/N, but the mean is usually a better estimate in the small-sample case:

$$\bar f=\int_0^1df\,f\,P(A_f\mid N_n)=\frac{n+1}{N+2},\tag{18.57}$$

equal to Laplace's P(A|N_n). So the probability Laplace assigns to A at one trial equals the frequency estimate that minimises expected squared error (p. 577).

**Numbers.** For one success in two trials, $\bar f$ = 1/2, yet it is an even bet that f lies outside 0.326 < f < 0.674. With no data, an even bet that f lies outside 0.25 < f < 0.75 (p. 577). The variance is

$$\operatorname{var}P(A_f\mid N_n)=\overline{f^2}-\bar f^2=\frac{\bar f(1-\bar f)}{N+3},\tag{18.58}$$

so the expected error falls like 1/√N. Jaynes says conclusions about reliability are, for practical purposes, the same as the confidence-interval method's (p. 577).

**K hypotheses.** Taking M → ∞ in (18.43):

$$P(f_1\cdots f_k\mid n_1\cdots n_k)=\frac{(N+K)!}{n_1!\cdots n_k!}\,(f_1^{n_1}\cdots f_k^{n_k})\,\delta(f_1+\cdots+f_k-1),\tag{18.59}$$

with the prefactor as printed (see Reading notes). Integrating out f₂,…,f_k by Laplace transforms:

$$P(f_1\mid n_1\cdots n_k)=\frac{(N+K-1)!}{n_1!\,(N-n_1+K-2)!}f_1^{n_1}(1-f_1)^{N-n_1+K-2},\tag{18.60}$$

$$\hat f_1=\frac{n_1}{N+K-2},\qquad \bar f_1=\frac{n_1+1}{N+K},\tag{18.61–18.62}$$

and the mean is the rule (18.44) (p. 578).

**Equivalence of A_p and A_f.** For M → ∞, m/M → f:

$$P(M_m\mid A_p)=\delta(f-p).\tag{18.63}$$

Letting N → ∞ in (18.22), by Bayes' theorem,

$$(A_p\mid A_f)=\delta(f-p).\tag{18.64}$$

For any proposition B, using (18.1) to drop A_f when conditioning on A_p:

$$P(B\mid A_f)=\int_0^1dp\,(BA_p\mid A_f)=\int_0^1dp\,P(B\mid A_pA_f)(A_p\mid A_f)=\int_0^1dp\,P(B\mid A_p)\delta(p-f).\tag{18.65}$$

So for numerically equal f and p, A_p and A_f are equivalent for plausible reasoning (p. 578). In the limit N → ∞ with n/N → f, (18.24) reduces to the binomial (18.18) and (18.43) to the multinomial

$$P(m_1\cdots m_k\mid f_1\cdots f_k)=\frac{m!}{m_1!\cdots m_k!}f_1^{m_1}\cdots f_k^{m_k}.\tag{18.66}$$

**Jaynes's conclusion.** This equivalence explains why probability and frequency are easily confused. When the information is observed frequencies in a large sample plus a constant mechanism, Laplace's theory becomes mathematically equivalent to the frequency theory, which suffices for classical problems like life insurance. Harm arises in physics and engineering, where essential evidence is not frequency-based, or the target quantity has nothing to do with frequencies (p. 578).

### 18.15 One-dimensional neutron multiplication (pp. 579–585)
**Model** (Bellman, Kalaba, Wing 1957; Wing 1962). Neutrons move only along ±x at constant speed. Each fission yields exactly two neutrons, one going each way, and progeny act the same way. A trigger neutron is fired from the left into thickness x. The aim is to estimate the numbers emerging. Since the data do not fix them, the goal is the probability that exactly n are transmitted or reflected. Jaynes wants a detailed comparison of the two theories' rationale (p. 579). He notes that some frequentists say they would give up their theory for one with better understanding and a more efficient formalism (p. 579).

**18.15.1 Frequentist solution** (as Jaynes reports it, pp. 579–581):
- Experimenters measure p = aΔ, the fission frequency in a thin film of thickness Δ: N neutrons fired, fission in n cases. Confidence limits for p (Cramér), with λ the (100 − k)% normal-deviate value:

$$\frac N{N+\lambda^2}\left[\frac{2n+\lambda^2}{2N}\pm\lambda\sqrt{\frac{n(N-n)}{N^3}+\frac{\lambda^2}{4N^2}}\right]\tag{18.67}$$

 will include the true p about k% of the time. For λ = √2:

$$\frac{n+1}{N+2}\pm\frac N{N+2}\sqrt{\frac{2n(N-n)}{N^3}+\frac1{N^2}}\ \approx\ \frac{n+1}{N+2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{2n(N-n)}{N^3}},\tag{18.68}$$

 covering p in about 84% of similar cases (note the +1 and +2). In general

$$\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}dx\,e^{-x^2/2}=\frac k{100}.\tag{18.69}$$

 (18.67) is an approximation for large n and N−n (p. 580).
- p is a definite but imperfectly known physical constant. Fission probability per unit length is assumed the same for each neutron, independent of history. So fission in successive slabs is independent, and "fission in slab 2 but not slab 1" has probability p(1−p) (p. 580).
- The reflection and transmission frequencies p_n(x), q_n(x) are computed (Wing gives numerical results). They are predictions only of limiting frequencies over many repeated experiments. Each experiment's results (r_i, t_i) do not affect them. Agreement is tested by significance tests such as chi-squared. If the observed frequencies depart widely, something is wrong with the assumptions (pp. 580–581).

**18.15.2 Laplace solution** (pp. 581–584): plausible reasoning about a finite number of experiments, with no concern for limiting frequencies. The only prior knowledge is that a neutron either does or does not fission, so the rule applies:

$$p\equiv P(F_{N+1}\mid N_n)=\frac{n+1}{N+2}.\tag{18.70}$$

Here F_m is the proposition that the m-th neutron undergoes fission (18.71). The "accuracy" of this p does not arise, since it is exact by definition. A larger N makes it more stable, not more accurate. It describes our knowledge, not a property of the material (p. 581). Fire a neutron into thickness x = MΔ. Let Fⁿ be fission in the n-th slab and fⁿ no fission there:

$$p\equiv P(F^1\mid N_n)=\frac{n+1}{N+2}.\tag{18.72}$$

The probability of fission in slab 2 but not slab 1 is not p(1−p):

$$P(F^2f^1\mid N_n)=P(F^2\mid f^1N_n)P(f^1\mid N_n)=\frac{n+1}{N+3}\left[1-\frac{n+1}{N+2}\right]=\frac{(n+1)(N-n+1)}{(N+2)(N+3)}.\tag{18.73}$$

The text prints the left side as P(F²F¹|N_n). Note that Jaynes says F¹ (the evidence that a neutron passed through one slab without fission) must be taken into account as fully as N_n, since ignoring one but not the other would be inconsistent (p. 582). The printed text writes the "no fission in slab 1" proposition as F¹ in the product; I write f¹. (Added step: the middle line above is corrected. The text layer shows (n+1)/(N+2) as the first factor, but the last line requires P(F²|f¹N_n) = (n+1)/(N+3), by (18.25) applied to N+1 trials with n successes.) The probability of exactly m first-generation progeny in MΔ is (18.24) again:

$$P(M_m\mid N_n)=\binom Mm\frac{(n+m)!\,(N+1)!\,(N+M-n-m)!}{n!\,(N-n)!\,(N+M+1)!},\tag{18.74}$$

versus the frequentist

$$P(M_m\mid A_p)=\binom Mm p^m(1-p)^{M-m}.\tag{18.75}$$

As N → ∞ with n/N → p, (A_p'|N_n) → δ(p' − n/N) and the two agree (18.76). The difference is negligible when N ≫ M (p. 582).

**Mean and variance.** The mean is

$$\bar m=M\frac{n+1}{N+2}.\tag{18.77}$$

Second moment via (18.23), using $\overline{m^2}=Mp+M(M-1)p^2$ for the binomial, averaged over (A_p|N_n):

$$\overline{m^2}=\frac{(N+1)!}{n!(N-n)!}\int_0^1dp\,[Mp+M(M-1)p^2]p^n(1-p)^{N-n}=M\frac{n+1}{N+2}+M(M-1)\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{(N+2)(N+3)}.\tag{18.78}$$

The variance is

$$V\equiv\overline{m^2}-\bar m^2=M\left[\frac{N+M+2}{N+3}\right]\left[\frac{n+1}{N+2}\right]\left[1-\frac{n+1}{N+2}\right].\tag{18.79}$$

The text layer prints the last bracket as "n −", but the derivation gives "1 −"; see Reading notes. (Added step: with $\bar p=(n+1)/(N+2)$, (18.58) gives $\overline{p^2}=\bar p^2+\bar p(1-\bar p)/(N+3)$. Then $V=M\bar p+M(M-1)\overline{p^2}-M^2\bar p^2=M\bar p(1-\bar p)[1+(M-1)/(N+3)]$.) The frequency theory gives

$$m_0=Mp,\tag{18.80}$$

$$V_0=Mp(1-p).\tag{18.81}$$

If the frequentist uses the centre of the interval (18.68), p = (n+1)/(N+2), the means agree and

$$V-V_0=\frac{M-1}{N+3}\,Mp(1-p).\tag{18.82}$$

**Why Laplace is more precise.** The frequentist's V₀ is not the whole uncertainty. By (18.68), p itself is uncertain by about ±√(2p(1−p)/N) (the text cites (18.81) at this point; see Reading notes), so the mean is uncertain by about

$$\pm\sqrt{\frac{2p(1-p)}{N}}.\tag{18.83}$$

If the two uncertainties are independent, the total mean-square uncertainty is V₀ plus

$$M^2\frac{2p(1-p)}{N},\tag{18.84}$$

which more than wipes out (18.82). The factor 2 changes with the confidence level but not much (pp. 583–584).

Jaynes's reading: in the Laplace theory both effects come from one calculation. The mechanism is that evidence like F¹ is continually included, so the finiteness of N_n is handled automatically and no arbitrary confidence level is needed. The smaller net uncertainty means that the two frequentist sources of uncertainty are not independent. They have a small negative correlation that partly compensates (p. 584).

**Interlocutor's objection:** in practice one group measures N_n and a second group uses it, so confidence intervals are needed for communication. **Reply (Jaynes):** the second calculation needs only the A_p distribution (A_p|N_n) (cf. (18.16), (18.23)). The exact way to communicate what you learned is to give your final A_p distribution, not confidence intervals (pp. 584–585).

**Independence.** The Laplace theory needed no "statistical assumption" of independence between slabs. (18.73) shows slab events are not independent given finite data (p. 585).
- *Frequency theory:* only causal independence is recognised. The coin tosses are "independent repetitions", and then Mr A and Mr B cannot be distinguished (p. 585).
- *Laplace's theory:* independence means P(A|BC) = P(A|C), i.e. logical independence (p. 585).
- Limit theorems require independence in their derivations, so, Jaynes says, if there is causal independence without logical independence, these limit theorems will not hold. Jaynes says frequency-school authors apply them where they are incorrect, a point he says Keynes (1921) stressed (p. 585).

Jaynes concludes that, at least for this kind of problem, the Laplace theory gives the better understanding and more efficient formalism his colleague asked for (p. 585).

*Intuition note.* The frequentist plugs in an estimated p and then adds a separate confidence-interval correction. Laplace's calculation averages over p inside one calculation. Each observed neutron passing a slab is also evidence about p, so slab outcomes are correlated for you even though they are physically independent.

### 18.16 The de Finetti theorem (pp. 586–588)
Define the indicator

$$x_n\equiv\begin{cases}1&A\text{ true on trial }n\\0&A\text{ false on trial }n.\end{cases}\tag{18.85}$$

The most general knowledge about N trials is P(x₁…x_N|N), arbitrary at 2^N points. Question: what is a necessary and sufficient condition for it to come from an A_p distribution? A necessary condition: the probability that A is true in n specified trials and false in the rest depends only on n and N, not on which trials. Such a function defines an **exchangeable sequence**. De Finetti (1937) states the converse: any exchangeable function can be generated by an A_p distribution, i.e. there is g(p) ≥ 0 with ∫₀¹ g(p)dp = 1 such that

$$P(n\mid N)=\int_0^1dp\,p^n(1-p)^{N-n}g(p).\tag{18.86}$$

**Proof sketch.** Expand

$$p^n(1-p)^{N-n}=p^n\sum_{m=0}^{N-n}\binom{N-n}{m}(-p)^m=\sum_{k=0}^N\alpha_k(N,n)p^k,\tag{18.87}$$

with the binomial coefficient as written in the corrected form (see Reading notes). If (18.86) holds,

$$P(n\mid N)=\sum_{k=0}^N\alpha_k(N,n)\beta_k,\tag{18.88}$$

$$\beta_k=\int_0^1dp\,p^kg(p),\tag{18.89}$$

where β_k is the k-th moment of g (the page prints "p^n" and "nth moment"; see Reading notes). Specifying β₀…β_N is equivalent to specifying all P(n|N), n = 0…N. In particular β_N is the probability that x₁ = … = x_N = 1 regardless of later trials. So the question is a Hausdorff moment problem (Widder 1941). The main theorem is: a g(p) ≥ 0 with (18.89) exists if and only if there is a number B such that

$$\sum_{n=0}^N\binom Nn P(n\mid N)\le B,\qquad N=0,1,\ldots.\tag{18.90}$$

Since P(n|N) are probabilities, equality holds with B = 1, which completes the proof (p. 587).

**Second view (heuristic, Jaynes says "might be made into a proof").** Expand

$$g(p)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n\phi_n(p),\tag{18.91}$$

with orthonormal polynomials

$$\phi_n(p)=\frac{\sqrt{2n+1}}{n!}\frac{d^n}{dp^n}[p(1-p)]^n=(-1)^n\sqrt{2n+1}\,P_n(2p-1),\tag{18.92}$$

$$\int_0^1dp\,\phi_m(p)\phi_n(p)=\delta_{mn}.\tag{18.93}$$

Substituting (18.91) into (18.86) leaves only finitely many terms, since φ_k is orthogonal to polynomials of degree below k. For given N, specifying P(n|N) is equivalent to specifying a₀…a_N. As N → ∞, g becomes determined "almost everywhere", like a Fourier series. The difficulty is establishing g ≥ 0 from (18.91) (pp. 587–588).

### 18.17 Comments (p. 588)
The theorem shows the probability–frequency link holds for all exchangeable sequences, and generalises to more than two outcomes. Jaynes interprets Laplace's famous remark, that all possible values of an unknown probability between 0 and 1 may be supposed equally likely, as the flat (A_p|X). He notes that most writers this century rejected it and that A_p calculations have been largely avoided since Laplace. The theorem puts these methods on much more solid ground: whenever the probability of a sequence depends only on the number of successes, all distributions come from one g(p). The technique is also powerful, for instance for deriving (18.24). Jaynes calls its validity as a mathematical tool for exchangeable sequences a proven fact that no philosophical objection can touch (p. 588).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A = "next toss of an unknown coin is heads". X: both outcomes possible, so flat (A_p|X) = 1. Data N = 3 tosses, n = 2 heads.

1. Posterior (18.22): (N+1)·C(3,2)·p²(1−p) = 4·3·p²(1−p) = 12p²(1−p). Check: ∫12p²(1−p)dp = 12(1/3 − 1/4) = 1.
2. Mean: ∫12p³(1−p)dp = 12(1/4 − 1/5) = 12/20 = 0.6 = (n+1)/(N+2) = 3/5, matching (18.25).
3. Memory storage: a 4th toss shows heads (F), likelihood p. New density ∝ p·12p²(1−p) = 12p³(1−p). Since ∫p³(1−p)dp = 1/20, the normalised density is 20p³(1−p), with mean 20(1/5 − 1/6) = 20/30 = 2/3. This equals (3+1)/(4+2) = 4/6. Only the stored density was used, not the raw tosses.
4. Two future heads (18.24), with M = m = 2, n = 2, N = 3: C(4,2)·C(3+2−2−2, 1)/C(6,2) = 6·1/15 = 0.4. Sequentially: 3/5 · 2/3 = 6/15 = 0.4. A plug-in p = 0.6 with independence would give 0.6² = 0.36. The gap is the effect behind (18.73).
5. Stability: the variance of the density is f̄(1−f̄)/(N+3) = 0.24/6 = 0.04 (sd 0.2). Direct check: ⟨p²⟩ = 12∫p⁴(1−p)dp = 12(1/5 − 1/6) = 0.4, and 0.4 − 0.36 = 0.04. With N = 300, n = 200 instead: mean 201/302 ≈ 0.666, variance ≈ 0.666·0.334/303 ≈ 0.00073 (sd ≈ 0.027). The mean is similar (0.666 vs 0.6) but the density is far narrower, so it is far more stable.

## Concepts
- [[a-p-distribution]] (new) — the density (A_p|E) over the proposition "the probability of A is p"; its first moment is P(A|E), its width is stability (p. 554)
- [[laplace-rule-of-succession]] (new) — P(A|N_n) = (n+1)/(N+2) from a flat A_p density; for K hypotheses (n₁+1)/(N+K) (p. 563, p. 571)
- [[bayes-theorem]] — used to move A_p to the left of the bar and to update (A_p|E) (p. 555)
- [[prior-distribution]] — here the flat (A_p|X)=1, or the pre-prior ∝ 1/[p(1−p)] (p. 555, p. 575)
- [[posterior-distribution]] — (A_p|N_n), e.g. (18.22), (18.47) (pp. 563, 572)
- [[posterior-predictive-distribution]] — P(M_m|N_n), eq. (18.24), the A_p density averaged over future trials (p. 563)
- [[sequential-updating]] — the robot replaces (A_p|E) by (A_p|EF) and erases the old one (p. 557)
- [[exchangeability]] — probability of a sequence depends only on counts; by de Finetti every such sequence has a g(p) representation (p. 586)
- [[principle-of-indifference-maximum-entropy]] — contained in (18.44) when N = 0. P(N_n|X) is uniform (maximum entropy) (p. 563, p. 571)
- [[frequentist-vs-bayesian-inference]] — A_p ≡ A_f equivalence; confidence intervals replaced by communicating the A_p density (p. 578, p. 585)
- [[weight-of-evidence]] (new) — the intuitive notion is bound up with the sharpness of the A_p density; evidence with a much sharper density dominates (pp. 572–573)
- [[hypothesis-space]] (new) — the explicit list of propositions to be considered. Changing it changes the answer (p. 565, p. 566)
- [[carnap-lambda-method]] (new) — c_λ: Laplace's rule with prior ∝ [p(1−p)]^r, 2r = λ−2, λ = weight of prior evidence (p. 574)
- [[logical-independence]] (new) — P(A|BC) = P(A|C), as opposed to causal independence; needed for limit theorems (p. 585)
- [[inner-outer-robot]] (new) — outer robot reasons about the world, inner robot about the outer robot's activity; A_p belongs to the inner (p. 560)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] A_p with flat density and binomial data ↔ bayesian: Beta–Bernoulli conjugate updating — posterior Beta(n+1, N−n+1), predictive (n+1)/(N+2)
- [same-math] Carnap λ-method, prior ∝ [p(1−p)]^r ↔ bayesian: Beta prior with pseudo-counts — (n+r+1)/(N+2r+2)
- [same-math] K-hypothesis rule (n₁+1)/(N+K) ↔ information-theory: add-one smoothing of symbol counts — uniform prior on the simplex gives the same formula
- [same-math] de Finetti theorem (p. 586) ↔ bayesian: exchangeability implies i.i.d. given a latent parameter
- [structural-analogy] Predictive variance V = V₀ + extra term (18.82) ↔ bayesian: hierarchical model / total variance — parameter uncertainty adds to sampling variance
- [structural-analogy] Weight of evidence as sharpness of the A_p density ↔ information-theory: Fisher information as curvature of log-likelihood — narrower density means more information
- [metaphor] Outer vs inner robot ↔ bayesian: hierarchical modelling of beliefs about beliefs — levels of reasoning about reasoning

## Questions this raises
- When is the prior information "completely resolvable" (p. 556–557), and what changes if it is not?
- If the general A_p density is stored rather than a flat one, how should the density be chosen for realistic priors such as coins (large λ)?
- Jaynes says the frequentist's two uncertainties are negatively correlated (p. 584). Can the size of that correlation be computed explicitly?
- What breaks in the de Finetti representation (p. 586) for sequences that are not exchangeable, such as ones with trends?
