# Surprise Attack and Disarmament

## L0 — One line
Measures against surprise attack should aim to make each side's retaliatory forces safe, so that neither gains by striking first. That means stable mutual deterrence, not simply fewer weapons.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Schelling asks what "disarmament" should mean when the main danger is surprise attack. His answer: the danger is that a first strike might destroy the other side's retaliatory force, which gives each side an incentive to strike first. Measures should therefore protect retaliatory forces and avoid weapons built to strike first. Literal disarmament might even destabilize. Inspection alone is weak against fast attacks. In a crisis, positive evidence and prearranged verification are needed, so there is a good argument for overbuilding an inspection system. Cooperation may or may not secure stability. Even if both sides' forces were invulnerable, threats of limited retaliation might still be credible.

**Prerequisites:** deterrence (threatening punishment to prevent an act); retaliatory force (weapons kept to strike back after being hit); positive evidence (showing what one is actually doing); expected value with a 50% success rate; the idea of mutual suspicion; no formal math beyond arithmetic.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The surprise-attack problem is the possible vulnerability of each side's retaliatory force. Deterrence comes from the ability to retaliate after being hit, not from the ability to destroy (p. 268–269).
- What makes a balance of terror deter is its stability, not the mere equality of the two sides; it is stable only when neither side can destroy the other's strike-back ability by striking first (pp. 268–269).
- Forces, defenses and arms limits should be judged by what survives a first strike. Counting weapons is misleading (p. 271–273).
- A simplified missile-duel model suggests a limit on missile numbers is more stabilizing the higher it is. Literal disarmament does not guarantee stability (p. 273–275).
- Survivable submarines, even very destructive retaliatory weapons, and mutual "hostages" fit the same logic. Exposed, vulnerable weapons are among the most obvious candidates for limits (p. 275–280).
- Inspection with nothing else may have very poor prospects against fast, mobile forces. It may conflict with the need for concealment and may reveal too much. The value of warning depends on what can be done with it (p. 281–286).
- In a crisis of mutual misapprehension, each side wants to convey the truth. That calls for positive evidence and prior planning, and there is a good argument for a stand-by, overbuilt inspection system (p. 286–293).
- Stability may come without cooperation or fail despite it. Invulnerable forces would weaken threats of massive retaliation but might strengthen threats of limited retaliation (p. 293–296).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The chapter has few headings. Labels A–J are mine; the author's own headings are quoted where used.

### A. Why surprise attack is a different problem (pp. 266–271)
- Disarmament schemes usually assume that fewer, less potent, especially "offensive" weapons promote peace. Since the 1955 open-skies proposal, surprise-attack safeguards have been treated as a less comprehensive scheme and a possible first step (p. 266).
- The innovation: arms are not provocative if clearly held in reserve. It can be even more important not to keep secret that we are *not* planning an attack. We want to assure the enemy, through his own eyes, that we prepare no attack (p. 267).
- **Groves (quoted):** "Our reluctance to strike first is a military disadvantage to us; but it is also, paradoxically, a factor in preventing a world conflict today" (p. 267).
- A main incentive for total war may be the fear of being a poor second. "Self-defense" becomes compounded: he strikes to stop us striking to stop him. So there are secrets we prefer not to keep and capabilities we might prefer not to have (p. 267).
- It is even better if the other side does not have those capabilities either, so the surprise-attack problem may be suitable for negotiation (p. 268).
- The aim of such a scheme is to reduce or remove the advantage of striking first, on the assumption that this reduces the incentive to strike at all (p. 268).
- **Rhetorical question:** if each side can obliterate the other, why does who strikes first matter? The text's answer is that a first strike might destroy the power to retaliate and so not be deterred by it. Deterrence rests on the capacity to retaliate after being attacked, not on the existing capacity to destroy (p. 268).
- Mutual deterrence is constituted not by the "balance" itself (its sheer equality or symmetry) but by its stability. The balance is stable only when neither side, striking first, can destroy the other's ability to strike back (pp. 268–269).
- Old West "equalizer": either man could kill the other, but not both would die. The advantage of shooting first aggravates the incentive to shoot. If both were sure to live long enough to shoot back, there would be no advantage in jumping the gun (p. 269).
- So the special significance of surprise attack is the vulnerability of retaliatory forces. If those forces were invulnerable on both sides, there would be no strong temptation to strike first and less need to react to false alarms (p. 269).
- The most immediate objective is the safety of weapons, not people. The weapons most destructive of people are what such a scheme seeks to preserve. A weapon that can only hurt people and cannot damage the other side's striking force gives no incentive to strike first. Weapons aimed at "military" targets can exploit a first strike (pp. 269–270).
- **Open question:** are safeguards for SAC (Strategic Air Command) a first step toward dismantling it, or a compromise accepting mutual deterrence as the best available? The author raises the question here without answering it (pp. 270–271).

*Intuition note:* Having a huge arsenal does not make you safe if the other side could destroy it in one blow. What protects you is a force that survives and can strike back. Then neither side gains by shooting first.

### B. Judging forces, defenses and limits by vulnerability (pp. 271–273)
- Strength is not measured by counting bombers, missiles and ships. "Who is ahead" will usually be whoever strikes first. If we must assume the other side strikes first, 200 safe bombers may be worth as much as 2000 with a 10 per cent chance of survival (p. 271).
- Chicago cannot be hidden or buried. For the deterrent force, concealment, dispersal, hard shelter and airborne alert are meaningful defenses (p. 271).
- An active defense that saves a large fraction of the strike force may be enough to guarantee prohibitive retaliation. A defense that forces the enemy to triple his attack may be poor for a city but valuable for the retaliatory force (p. 272).
- **Accuracy and zonal limits:** an aiming error of two or three miles is nothing against a metropolitan area. But against a hard-sheltered weapon, several missiles may be needed. So zonal limits on ICBM placement, by reducing accuracy, might reduce vulnerability. This holds less for unsheltered weapons (pp. 272–273).

### C. The missile-duel illustration (pp. 273–275)
- Assumptions: we need an expected 100 missiles left after his first strike to retaliate adequately. One of his missiles has a 50-50 chance of knocking out one of ours (p. 273).
- The author's figures (p. 273):
  - With 200 missiles he must knock out just over half. He needs to fire just over 200.
  - With 400 he must knock out three-quarters, and, at a 50 per cent discount rate, he would need to fire more than twice 400, that is, more than 800.
  - With 800 he must knock out seven-eighths, and he needs "more than 2400".
- Conclusion: the larger the starting number on the defending side, the larger the multiple the attacker needs to push the residual below the safe number (p. 273). See Reading notes: the printed 800 and 2400 do not follow from the stated 50% rate.
- A limit on missile numbers therefore looks more stabilizing the larger the number permitted. Two reasons (p. 274):
  1. The absolute number left over for retaliation is greater.
  2. The absolute and proportionate increase an attacker needs rises, so cheating (hidden extra missiles, or breaking out of the agreement) is more than proportionately harder.
- If the numbers strain both budgets, the economic limit might itself impose stability (p. 274).
- So an arms race does not necessarily lead to a more and more unstable situation: for roughly equal numbers on both sides, the chance of wiping out the other's missiles falls as the numbers rise (p. 274).
- At low numbers a ratio of 2 or 3 to 1 may give dominance. At high numbers it may take 10 to 1. Neither side need panic if it falls a little behind (pp. 274–275).
- **Caveat:** the author calls this greatly simplified model much too specialized to be a strong argument for arms races rather than disarmament. It does demonstrate that, within the logic of stable deterrence, more vs. fewer weapons must be analyzed case by case, and that it is not a foregone conclusion that disarmament in the literal sense leads to stability (p. 275).

*Intuition note:* If each side has few missiles, a lead of 2 or 3 to 1 may let one side strike first and leave the other with too few to strike back. With many missiles it may take a lead of about 10 to 1, so neither side has much hope of drawing far enough ahead to strike with impunity.

### D. Missile submarines (pp. 275–277)
- If the submarine proves for many years a fairly invulnerable site for anti-population missiles, we should perhaps see it as reassuring rather than terrifying. If the best we can hope for is a stable mutual deterrence, a polaris-type missile on a mobile submarine might be the kind of weapon we want on both sides. If it proved both undetectable and highly reliable, it would not need to strike first in order to strike at all (pp. 275–276).
- We should perhaps not even wish that only we had invulnerable submarines. If we have no intention or political capacity for a first strike, it would usually be helpful if the enemy were confidently assured of this. If he must worry about his force's exposure, we must worry too (p. 276).
- We must search for submarine detection because the Russians may find a technique we do not know, and to learn how to make our own boats less detectable. Yet we may simultaneously hope the problem is insoluble. Schelling compares this to a partner you cannot trust: you search hard for a loophole while hoping none exists (pp. 276–277).

### E. Punitive potency and hostages (pp. 277–278)
- If we must assure the enemy that we can punish after being struck, we should welcome technology that raises the anti-population potency of surviving retaliatory weapons. The same logic that protects survival also favors potency (p. 277).
- **Brodie (quoted by Schelling):** deterrence may even call for "super-dirty bombs". They should appear as horrendous as possible before the event (p. 277).
- A stable balance of terror is a modern version of the exchange of hostages: each side holds the other's population within reach as a tacit total exchange of hostages. In a lawless world with no damage suits for breach, hostages may be the only device by which mutually distrustful partners can strike a bargain (pp. 277–278).
- **Not simply a rationalization of arms races:** literal, indiscriminate disarmament, even of the most horrifying weapons, could produce instability and might have to be completely successful not to be disastrous (p. 278).

### F. Strike-first vs. strike-back weapons and five areas for negotiation (pp. 278–281)
- One extreme is the "pure" strike-back weapon: hard to destroy, with no gain from striking first. The other is a weapon too vulnerable to survive, or one specialized for destroying the enemy's forces before launch (pp. 278–279).
- Strike-first weapons give an incentive to jump the gun on ambiguous warning. They are also a tacit declaration that one expects to strike first, which invites the enemy to strike earlier (p. 279).
- Perhaps most weapons fall between the extremes if reasonable precautions are taken for their protection. So no clean first-strike/second-strike distinction can be drawn. Eliminating every weapon with any effect on retaliatory forces might leave too few to promise retaliation (pp. 279–280).
- Five areas where negotiation might usefully concentrate (pp. 280–281):
  1. Exposed, vulnerable weapons. This views deterrence as something to be enhanced, not dismantled.
  2. Restrictions on deployments that affect counter-force rather than counter-population potency. These will not be sought without candid recognition that the aim is to protect each side's strike-back capability.
  3. Cooperative or accommodated behavior that reduces war by misapprehension, perhaps without formal agreements (for example, inspection of polar air traffic).
  4. Arrangements for crises that threaten to explode into unintended war.
  5. Measures that make surprise less likely and so a first strike less attractive (open skies).

### G. Inspection, warning and "getting ready" (pp. 281–286)
- Since 1955 inspection has become harder. Hydrogen weapons need fewer aircraft, missiles cut the available time, and mobile systems such as submarines are hard to watch. Photo inspection is "obsolete". The task is intensive surveillance that can report within hours, later minutes, without intolerable false alarms. There is no practical assurance this can be done (pp. 281–282).
- Pure inspection may have very poor prospects. Combining inspection with deployment limits, for example assembling forces where they are easier to watch, may accomplish something. This raises problems (p. 282).
- **Problem 1, inspection vs. concealment:** when missiles become accurate enough, protection by cement may become nearly impossible or very costly, and mobility and concealment may then have to be the source of security. If the enemy can hit anything he can locate and kill anything he can hit, he has to be made unable to locate it. To the extent that he can keep our retaliatory weapons under continuous surveillance, he has continuous information on their location (pp. 282–283).
- **Problem 2, too much information:** the implications of a temporary disability (the B-36 hurricane case; an epidemic among strategic personnel) are very different depending on whether the enemy knows only in general that such things can happen or knows definitely when it occurs. Better that neither side knows too much about the other's occasional disabilities (p. 283).
- **Problem 3, what to do with warning:** an anticipatory strike on ambiguous warning risks war from a false alarm. A true alarm precludes last-minute deterrence. If "getting ready" meant only "be more alert", we should have been alert already. But some things can be done in the face of imminent attack that cannot be done continuously, such as evacuating, keeping aircraft airborne, or grounding commercial flights (pp. 283–285).
- **Fatigue:** an enemy might wait until our super-alert runs out. The response is twofold (p. 285):
  - Design a super-alert with endurance, compromising peak effectiveness.
  - One may have to engage in a kind of crash disarmament negotiation during the alert. This might mean a much more ambitious scheme than was politically feasible earlier, with a quick deadline (pp. 285–286).
- Warning by itself may not be enough. It gives an opportunity that has to be exploited with skill, and preparations (for example, the content of an ultimatum and verification procedures) may have to be made well ahead of time (p. 286).

### H. Two criteria for inspection; misapprehension cases (pp. 286–292)
- Two criteria for judging an inspection system (pp. 286–287):
  - How well it gets at the truth despite efforts to conceal it. This is like discovering the guilty, and rests on absence of evidence.
  - How well it helps one reveal the truth convincingly when it is in one's interest. This is like letting the innocent establish innocence, and rests on positive evidence.
- The first fits deliberate surprise attack, where the aggressor disguises the truth. The second fits inadvertent war (false alarm, mechanical accident, catalytic third party, mutual panic by feedback), where both sides want the truth conveyed believably and in time (p. 287).
- **"Misapprehension of attack":** merely telling the truth is not enough in most cases. One must authenticate facts about the disposition of forces, and show one's position could not be exploited to double-cross an enemy who restrains his own forces (pp. 287–288).
- **"Misapprehension during limited war":** aircraft flown in limited war could be misread as a strategic strike. One may need to show that complementary actions, which would accompany an all-out counter-force strike, are not being taken (pp. 288–289).
- **"Reciprocal misapprehension":** Gromyko's scenario (quoted by Schelling) has meteors or electronic interference on radar screens. In such a case, if Soviet aircraft proceeded toward the U.S., both air fleets would draw the natural conclusion that a real attack was under way, and Gromyko says mankind would be drawn into the whirlpool of atomic war (p. 289).
- A balanced withdrawal by mutual consent, properly phased and authenticated, might reverse this. Requirements, with only hours or no time (pp. 289–290):
  1. A "solution": a dynamically stable withdrawal to a less menacing alert status that gives neither side a dangerous advantage and is within physical capabilities.
  2. Observable, verifiable, provable compliance. This is a contract-enforcement problem. Each side wants a cheat-proof monitoring system it can submit to.
- This shows the need for prior arrangements, thinking ahead about the proposal, and designing one's flight plan so true information can be given (p. 290). Designing radar to catch a sneak attacker differs from designing it so both sides can voluntarily "parade" before it (p. 291). (The page text is garbled there; see Reading notes.)
- **"Longer-term surveillance":** routine inspection is generally viewed as depending mainly on negative evidence (absence of evidence), with evasion made difficult by the need to stay hidden over a long period. A crisis agreement must rely on positive evidence. The motive to provide it may be enhanced by the emergency (pp. 291–292).

### I. "Overbuilding the system" (pp. 292–293)
- There is a good argument for flexible stand-by communication and inspection arrangements, and for overbuilding an inspection system relative to its agreed use. Reliability should not be judged only by participants' motives in normal operation (p. 292).
- Example: inspectors for a nuclear-test suspension should be evaluated on mobility, location, communications, training, equipment, trustworthiness and numbers. The test should include whether they could serve in a crisis threatening inadvertent war (pp. 292–293).

### J. Does stability depend on cooperation? Third-area deterrence (pp. 293–296)
- It is not at all clear that military arrangements with the Russians will greatly affect stability. Nature may let each side secure its forces unilaterally, or may let both find ways to destroy forces faster than ways to protect them. There is "only a hope—no presumption" that cooperation can arrest instability. Still, cooperation, formal or informal, tacit or explicit, may make a significant difference (p. 293).
- **Question posed:** would we want a perfectly stable balance if we could have one? (pp. 293–294).
- **Objection ("it can be argued"; no named speaker):** deterring anything short of a major assault depends partly on Russian belief that we might be goaded into attack. With invulnerable forces, "a pair of invulnerable SAC's is a pair of neutralized SAC's", a luxury we cannot afford given a large "third area" (p. 294).
- **Schelling's reply** (pp. 295–296):
  - Strategic forces would still have a punitive role. If the threat of knocking out cities was thought potent mainly because of the sheer pain, loss and humiliation involved, rather than because of its effect on the enemy's local military posture, its main ingredient would still be present even if the other SAC were invulnerable (pp. 294–295).
  - Unlimited "massive" retaliation loses credibility. But limited or graduated reprisals might gain credibility, because fear of an all-out strike in return would be less and limited retaliation would be less likely to be mistaken for the start of all-out war (p. 295).
  - Limited reprisal can still escalate, and may be no easier to limit than local war. The argument needs only that it be safe and attractive enough, compared with all-out war, to be a credible threat (p. 296).
  - So one cannot deprecate a world of invulnerable SAC's simply by reference to third-area deterrence. One must show the massive threat is more potent than the limited one (p. 296).
- Only an extreme optimist can think we may ever have a clear choice of accepting or rejecting a scheme that would guarantee total, continuous invulnerability for both sides. The issue still bears on what we may let ourselves hope for (p. 296).

*Intuition note:* When neither side can wipe out the other's retaliatory force, an all-out threat becomes hard to believe. Small punishing steps may become believable, because they carry less risk of triggering total war.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Both sides hold N missiles. Each attacking missile destroys one target missile with probability 0.5. A side needs R = 10 survivors to retaliate. Expected kills = 0.5 × shots, so shots needed ≈ 2 × kills needed (added step). The attacker must kill more than N − 10.

| N | kills needed | shots needed (2 × kills) | shots ÷ N |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | > 10 | > 20 | 1.0 |
| 40 | > 30 | > 60 | 1.5 |
| 80 | > 70 | > 140 | 1.75 |

- With 20 each, an attacker firing about 21 or more missiles expects about 10.5 kills, leaving fewer than 10 (added step: 21 × 0.5 = 10.5; 20 − 10.5 = 9.5). A lead of 1.5 to 1 (30 shots, 15 kills, 5 left) is more than enough.
- With 80 each, the attacker needs more than 140 missiles, a lead of 1.75 to 1 or more. In this model it never needs more than 2 to 1.
- Absolute numbers needed grow with N. This is the author's qualitative point: the larger the starting numbers, the harder a disarming first strike. The ratio grows as well, though the author's printed ratios differ (see Reading notes).

## Concepts
- [[first-strike-advantage]] (new) — the gain from striking before the enemy can strike; reducing it is the aim of anti-surprise-attack schemes (p. 268).
- [[stable-deterrence]] (new) — a balance in which neither side, striking first, can destroy the other's ability to retaliate (p. 269).
- [[retaliatory-force-vulnerability]] (new) — the risk that a first strike disables the forces meant to retaliate. It is the core of the surprise-attack problem (p. 269).
- [[strike-first-weapon]] (new) — a weapon so vulnerable it could not survive to strike second, or so specialized against the enemy's retaliatory forces that it would lose most of its usefulness if held until the other side had started (p. 279).
- [[credible commitment]] — here the "exchange of hostages": a tacit total exchange of hostages by mutual capacity to destroy populations (pp. 277–278).
- [[inadvertent-war]] (new) — war from false alarm, accident, third-party mischief or mutual panic (p. 287).
- [[positive-vs-negative-evidence]] (new) — proof by showing what one is doing, versus inference from absence of contrary evidence (pp. 286–287, 291).
- [[third-area-deterrence]] (new) — deterring aggression in a region outside the two main powers by a threat more credible than mutual suicide (p. 294).
- [[limited-retaliation]] (new) — graduated reprisals intended to work by civilian pain and the threat of more (p. 295).
- [[dominant / dominated strategy]] — not used here.
- [[bounded rationality]] — not used here.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Fear of being a "poor second" ↔ decision-game-theory: payoffs where each side's best reply to expected attack is to attack — mutual suspicion can drive an outcome neither wants.
- [structural-analogy] Hostages in a stable balance of terror ↔ [[credible commitment]]: a bargain made binding by exposing oneself to loss.
- [structural-analogy] Positive evidence in a crisis ↔ [[signaling]]: a party who wants to be believed shows verifiable facts rather than only asserting them.
- [metaphor] Weapons too exposed to survive ↔ any system whose "first move" advantage destabilizes (a gunfight, a bank run).

## Questions this raises
- If both sides' forces were perfectly invulnerable, which threats would remain credible? Schelling says limited retaliation might; is that enough?
- When is more weapons genuinely more stable? The missile model is simplified; what other factors (accuracy, reliability, numbers) matter?
- How could a crisis ultimatum or withdrawal plan be agreed beforehand when mistrust is highest?
- Can a deliberately overbuilt inspection system serve both ordinary monitoring and crisis verification without leaking too much?
