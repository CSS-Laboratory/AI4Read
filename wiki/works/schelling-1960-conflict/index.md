# Schelling, T. C. (1960). *The Strategy of Conflict*. Harvard University Press. (Paperback reissue with a new preface by the author, Harvard University Press, 1981, 328 pp.)

## In one paragraph
Schelling argues that most conflicts are bargaining situations in which interests partly oppose and partly coincide. They are not pure contests. In such games, outcomes depend on mutual expectations. A player can gain by visibly and irreversibly limiting his own options, which makes threats and promises credible. Where players cannot talk, they can still coordinate on "focal points": outcomes that stand out as prominent, unique or simple. Even a threat that only leaves something to chance can work, because it creates a shared risk. Formal game theory built for zero-sum conflict misses these things. So Schelling calls for a reoriented theory with an empirical part. He applies it to deterrence, limited war, surprise attack and disarmament.

## The path through the work
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch01-retarded]] — On Schelling's view, most conflict situations are essentially bargaining situations mixing opposed and common interests, and a theory built on the productive (not realistic) assumption of rationality could help explain threats, deterrence and commitment, including why limiting one's own options may sometimes pay.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch02-essay]] — In pure bargaining, where logic leaves the outcome open, a party can gain by visibly and irreversibly giving up some freedom of choice, so that the other side must adapt.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch03-bargaining]] — When people cannot communicate, they can still coordinate on an outcome that stands out as prominent, unique or simple, and Schelling suggests the same pull on expectations may also help explain explicit bargaining and where to look for limits on war.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch04-toward]] — Schelling proposes to study mixed-motive (bargaining) games by treating pure zero-sum conflict and pure coordination as the two limiting cases, and describing the outcome as a convergence of mutual expectations that may depend on labels, clues and moves, not only on the payoff matrix.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p1]] — A threat works only if some commitment (real or apparent) stands behind it and changes the other player's expectations; promises likewise need enforcement or some substitute for it. So the possible "moves", and the communication and enforcement they need, deserve study, not only abstract payoff comparisons.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p2]] — In mixed-motive games (where players' interests partly conflict and partly coincide), a player can gain by visibly and irreversibly limiting his own options or payoffs, because this can change what the other player expects him to do.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch06-game]] — In mixed-motive games, the principles of good play cannot be derived by purely analytical means from a priori considerations, so game theory needs experiments on how players find shared understandings.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch07-randomization]] — In games mixing conflict and common interest, randomization is not mainly about hiding one's strategy; its role is chiefly to make indivisible things divisible, which lets a threat or promise be "scaled down" when its full size would be too costly.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch08-threat]] — A threat can be credible without a firm commitment to carry it out, if it creates a shared risk of disaster whose final outcome neither side fully controls.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch09-reciprocal]] — If two rational players both know the basic chance that each "irrationally" attacks, fear of the other's fear cannot raise attack gradually: the chance of attack either stays at its basic level or becomes certain; gradual mutual aggravation needs players whose attack probability itself responds to the other's, such as an imperfect warning system.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch10-surprise]] — Measures against surprise attack should aim to make each side's retaliatory forces safe, so that neither gains by striking first. That means stable mutual deterrence, not simply fewer weapons.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/appa-nuclear]] — Limits in limited war rest on tacit, mutually recognised expectations (tradition), not on physical or legal facts, and the nuclear/non-nuclear line is such a limit, which a first use may destroy.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/appb-abandonment]] — A bargaining game whose move structure is perfectly symmetric may collapse into a tacit game, and symmetric solutions cannot be derived from rationality alone; symmetry is only one of several ways players can coordinate expectations.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p1]] — Part of what makes a Luce–Raiffa "solution in the strict sense" appealing to rational players is that it gives them a tacit signal for coordinating their choices, and other clues that do this may do the same job.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p2]] — In tacit non-zero-sum games, the "solution in the strict sense" works partly as a signal that players can use to coordinate, and the Notes spell out how commitments, threats, labels and shared pattern-perception shape such games.
- [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p3]] — These notes extend Schelling's main text on randomization, mutual fear of surprise attack, bargaining and tacit games; in the later notes Schelling argues that tacit coordination can rely on clues and asymmetries, which strict symmetry arguments overlook.

## Big ideas across units
- **Less freedom can be power.** Giving up options, information or even payoffs can be a strategic advantage, because it changes what the other side expects. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch01-retarded]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch02-essay]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p1]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p2]] (the "paradox of strategic advantage"), [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch07-randomization]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p2]].
- **Focal points and tacit coordination.** Without communication, players converge on prominent, unique or simple outcomes. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch03-bargaining]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch04-toward]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch06-game]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appa-nuclear]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appb-abandonment]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p1]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p2]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p3]].
- **Reorienting game theory toward mixed-motive games.** Zero-sum conflict and pure coordination are only limiting cases, and labels, moves and expectations matter. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch01-retarded]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch04-toward]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p2]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch06-game]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appb-abandonment]].
- **The anatomy of threats and promises.** Credibility depends on commitment, enforcement, communication and evidence. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch02-essay]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p1]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch05-enforcement-p2]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch07-randomization]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch08-threat]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p2]].
- **Chance as a tool.** Randomization scales threats and commitments down, and a shared risk can make a "may" threat work (brinkmanship). Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch07-randomization]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch08-threat]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p3]].
- **Mutual fear and the stability of deterrence.** Fear of surprise attack and the vulnerability of retaliatory forces explain instability, and the focus is on what survives a first strike. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch09-reciprocal]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch10-surprise]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p2]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p3]].
- **Limits on war depend on shared expectations.** Tacit limits must be qualitatively distinguishable, and tradition gives them their force. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch03-bargaining]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch08-threat]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appa-nuclear]].
- **Strategy theory has an empirical part.** Coordination depends on how minds meet, so experiments and observation are needed, and symmetry is no purely logical solution. Developed in [[schelling-1960-conflict/ch06-game]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appb-abandonment]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p1]], [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p2]] and [[schelling-1960-conflict/appc-re-p3]].

## How to read it
- **Essential first.** The catalog's focus points to ch02 (bargaining and commitment), ch03 (focal points and limited war), ch04 (interdependent decision) and ch08 (the threat that leaves something to chance). ch01 gives the framing and needs little background, so read it first.
- **Dependencies.**
  - ch04 builds on the ch03 tacit-coordination experiments.
  - ch05 (both parts) uses the commitment idea from the earlier chapters. Part 2 builds on part 1 and adds the enlarged matrix and dominance reasoning.
  - ch06 draws on the coordination-without-communication idea and minimax.
  - ch07 needs expected value and 2×2 matrices.
  - ch08 builds on credible threats and commitment, and ch07 supports it (small-probability threats).
  - ch09 and ch10 are applications to surprise attack. ch09 uses probability and some calculus. ch10 uses deterrence and retaliatory force.
- **Appendices.** Appendix A builds on ch03. Appendix B needs Nash equilibrium and the ch03 focal points. Appendix C part 1 rests on the ch03 coordination game.
- **Can be skipped on a first pass.** The model details in ch07 and ch09 (inequalities, slopes) can be skimmed. The two Appendix C note units (part 2 and part 3) are reference material. Part 3 ends with the table of contents. The sections on surprise attack and disarmament (ch09, ch10) are outside the catalog's focus.
