# The Retarded Science of International Strategy

## L0 — One line
On Schelling's view, most conflict situations are essentially bargaining situations mixing opposed and common interests, and a theory built on the productive (not realistic) assumption of rationality could help explain threats, deterrence and commitment, including why limiting one's own options may sometimes pay.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Schelling asks why deterrence and related conflict behaviour have so little systematic theory, and what such a theory would be. He defines the "strategy of conflict" as the study of rational, goal-seeking behaviour where interests partly conflict and partly coincide. Most conflicts are therefore bargaining situations, not pure contests. He suggests the theory is scarce partly because the military has no academic counterpart and game theory has mostly stayed too abstract; neighbouring fields (criminal law, child discipline, the underworld) seem to lack an explicit theory too. He defends rationality as a productive assumption, not as one evidently close to reality. It yields paradoxes: giving up options, information or rationality can be a strategic advantage.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a payoff or value system; the zero-sum vs. non-zero-sum distinction; no formal mathematics is needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- "Strategy of conflict" studies conscious, rational, "winning" behaviour. Winning means gaining by one's own values, not beating the adversary (pp. 10–12).
- Most interesting international conflicts are variable-sum (total gain not fixed), so Schelling treats them as essentially bargaining situations mixing conflict and mutual dependence (pp. 12–13).
- Deterrence theory grew slowly and informally. Credibility, commitment and the adversary's alternatives were learned piece by piece (pp. 14–15).
- Schelling suggests these reasons for the lack of theory: the military has no academic counterpart, military skills differ from skills of non-use, and game theory has mostly stayed too abstract (pp. 16–18).
- Deterrence also appears in criminal law, child discipline, relations between friends, driving and the underworld. These may offer insight, but none seems to have an explicit theory ready to use (pp. 18–22).
- The theory raises questions on credibility, trust, enforcement, communication, third parties and reputation, and may be called a theory of interdependent decision (pp. 22–25).
- Rationality is a bundle of attributes, not one scale. "Irrationality" can be formalised, and rationality is not always an advantage (pp. 25–29).
- Paradoxes follow: burning bridges, the secret ballot, a negotiator without authority, the dirty bomb, and ancient hostage devices (pp. 29–31).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The chapter has no numbered sections; headings below are ours, in the author's order.)

**Defining the field (pp. 10–12).**
- Theories of conflict divide in two ways (p. 10).
  - Some treat conflict as a pathology to be explained and cured. Others take it for granted and study the behaviour involved.
  - Among the latter, some study participants in all their complexity. Others focus on the more rational, conscious, artful kind of behaviour, crudely speaking as in a contest (p. 10).
- Schelling calls this last field the strategy of conflict. At least three reasons to study it: we are participants, it gives a benchmark for actual behaviour, and it shows how to influence others' behaviour (p. 10).
- The cost: assuming rationality means calculation from "an explicit and internally consistent value system". This limits applicability, and results may be a good approximation or a caricature (p. 11).
- The benefit is not closeness to truth but productivity. It lets us identify with hypothetical participants and demand consistency of them. Whether the theory fits actual behaviour is left to later judgment (p. 11).

*Intuition note:* Rationality is a working tool, not a claim about people. It is chosen because it lets theory get started, and fit to reality is left to later judgment.

**Mixed motives and bargaining (pp. 12–13).**
- The theory does not deny common interests. Pure conflict is a special case, found in a war of complete extermination and otherwise not even in war (p. 12).
- "Winning" is therefore gaining relative to one's own value system. This can happen through bargaining, accommodation and avoiding mutual damage (p. 12).
- Strategy concerns exploiting potential force, not applying force efficiently. It covers partners who distrust each other, and outcomes that are worse (or better) for both than others (p. 12).
- In game-theory terms, most interesting international conflicts are "variable-sum", not "constant-sum": more for one need not mean less for the other (pp. 12–13).
- So conflict situations are essentially bargaining situations, where one side's success depends on the other's choices (p. 13).
  - Bargaining may be explicit (a concession) or tacit (occupying or evacuating territory).
  - It may seek gains from the status quo, or involve threats of mutual damage (strike, boycott, price war, extortion) (p. 13).
- Example: a "successful" strike is not one that ruins the employer financially; it may even be one that never takes place. Something similar can be true of war (p. 13).

*Intuition note:* If both sides can lose together, both have a common interest in avoiding that outcome. That is why it helps to study conflict and common interest together.

**Deterrence as a case of slow theory (pp. 13–15).**
- Over about twelve years, deterrence ideas were refined (p. 14).
  - A threat must be credible, and credibility may depend on the threatener's costs and risks.
  - Credibility can be raised by commitment: a "trip wire", or national honour (the Formosa Resolution).
  - Readiness for limited war may weaken the threat of massive retaliation. Placing retaliation in the hands of the most resolute may strengthen it.
  - Madmen and small children often cannot be deterred by threats. A cornered enemy needs some tolerable recourse.
  - A threat of all-out retaliation leaves the enemy only extreme choices. A threat of massive destruction may deter only if there is also an implicit promise of non-destruction if he complies.
- Too great a first-strike capacity may induce the enemy to strike first. Arms control to improve mutual deterrence is now being considered (pp. 14–15).
- Schelling is impressed by how slow the process was, how vague the concepts remain and how inelegant the theory is. He notes there is no scientific literature comparable to that on inflation, Asiatic flu, school reading or smog (p. 15).
- Writers on deterrence have mostly solved immediate problems, not built a methodology or even a terminology (p. 15).

**Why the theory is retarded (pp. 16–18).**
- The military has no identifiable academic counterpart, unlike economics, medicine or education (p. 16).
  - Service academies teach rather than research. War colleges lack permanent faculty and a research orientation.
  - Universities give military strategy to a few historians and political scientists, on a small scale (p. 16).
- Defence-studies programmes, foundations, RAND and the Institute for Defense Analysis help. For Schelling they are also evidence of the need (p. 17).
- Could the services theorize? Schelling distinguishes applying force from threatening it. A theory of deterrence is "a theory of the skillful nonuse of military forces", which needs broader skills. The services may have them, but not automatically, and their duties take full time (pp. 17–18).
- Game theory studies games of strategy, where my best action depends on what I expect others to do. A deterrent threat fits this definition (p. 18).
  - But its promise for international strategy is unfulfilled. It has helped clarify concepts but, on the whole, has been pitched at a level of abstraction where it has made little contact with problems like deterrence (p. 18).

**Analogies from other fields (pp. 18–22).**
- Criminal law might be expected to have a rigorous deterrence theory (penalties, the criminal's values, detection probability, mistakes, third-party exploitation). He says the literature on law and criminology does not reveal an appreciable body of explicit theory, though he cannot confidently assert that none exists in the underworld (pp. 18–19, 21–22).
- Child discipline shows vividly (p. 19):
  - the need for the child to be rational and to hear the threat;
  - the threatener's determination, and the child's conviction that the threat will be carried out;
  - that punishment may hurt the threatener as much as, or more than, the child.
- This is analogous to a wealthy nation making demands on a poor nation in return for aid (p. 19).
- Deterrence applies between friends as well as enemies. Example: the threat to withdraw to a "peripheral strategy" if France did not ratify the European Defense Community Treaty (p. 20).
  - The concept needs both conflict and common interest. It is inapplicable to pure antagonism and to pure common interest. Between these extremes, deterring an ally and deterring an enemy differ "only by degrees" (p. 20).
- Drivers share an interest in avoiding collision but conflict over who yields. Their threat is conveyed by actions, and the pledge is made "by losing the power to do otherwise" (p. 20).
- Gangs and nations both lack enforceable law and have the threat of violence "continually on call". Racketeers and delinquent gangs also engage in limited war, disarmament, retaliation and alliances. They cannot appeal to a higher authority to enforce contracts (pp. 20–21).
- Simpler analogies can make a principle visible that is hidden by detail or predisposition in the international case (p. 21).

*Intuition note:* Similar problems of threat, promise and credibility appear in nurseries, traffic and gangs. Often a principle is easier to see in a simpler case.

**What a theory would contain (pp. 22–24).**
- It would first define the essentials: deterrence influences another's choices by influencing his expectations of our behaviour (p. 22).
- Questions it should answer (pp. 22–23):
  - Which payoff configurations make a threat credible? How should the mixture of conflict and common interest be measured?
  - What communication and authentication are needed? What rationality is required of the deterred party?
  - Must a threat be matched by a promise to withhold damage if the other complies, or does that depend on the payoffs?
  - Can one threaten that one will "probably" carry out a threat? What devices create commitment to something one would otherwise shrink from?
  - Do deterrent, disciplinary and extortionate threats differ logically? What does a third participant change? Can "reputation", "face" and "trust" be rationalised through a legal system, changed values or relations to other players?
- The area mixes game theory, organization theory, communication theory, theory of evidence, theory of choice and theory of collective decision (p. 24).

**Two points and a name (pp. 24–25).**
- The theory is not about the efficient use of violence. It is about conditioning one's behaviour on others' behaviour, through threats and promises (p. 24).
- It is nondiscriminatory between conflict and common interest. It degenerates when there is no common interest, or no conflict. Between those extremes it could be called the theory of "precarious partnership", "incomplete antagonism" or "interdependent decision" (pp. 24–25).
- Chapter 9 is said to point out that some central aspects of the surprise-attack problem are structurally identical with the problem of mutually suspicious partners (p. 25).
- Viewing threats and arms races as cool-headed is a modelling choice, not a claim that they are. He does not assert that they are entirely cool-headed; if behaviour were actually cool-headed, valid theory would probably be easier to create. Treating results as a benchmark, not a fully adequate theory, should protect against the worst results of a biased theory (p. 25).

**Rationality as a bundle (pp. 25–28).**
- Decision-makers do not lie on one scale from rational to irrational. Rationality is "a collection of attributes", and departures go in many directions (p. 26).
  - Examples: inconsistent values, faulty calculation, faulty communication, random influences, and collective decisions by people with different values.
  - The value, communication and information systems, the collective decision process and an error parameter can formalise "irrationality" (p. 26).
- Hitler, the French Parliament, a bomber commander, the Pearl Harbor radar operators, Khrushchev and the American electorate may be "irrational" in different ways. Even a neurotic may sometimes be treated as two rational entities with a haphazard voting process (pp. 26–27).
- Two observations soften the restrictiveness (p. 27).
  1. **Schelling reports at second hand:** inmates of mental hospitals often seem to cultivate value systems or inabilities (careless attitude to injury, not hearing) that resist threats or help coercion. Theory can show the strategic logic behind such tactics. He says it may not be an exaggeration that sophistication sometimes suppresses sound intuitions (p. 27).
  2. It is not a universal advantage to be manifestly rational. Rationality's attributes are not inalienable: they include a hearing aid, the mails, the legal system and one's agents. One can suspend one's rationality, for example by drugging one's brain, isolating oneself, having assets impounded, or breaking one's signing hand, to evade extortion (p. 28).

**Paradoxical results (pp. 28–31).**
- Theory of strategy can contradict common sense: it is not invariably an advantage, facing a threat, to be rational, well informed or in control (pp. 28–29).
- Examples (pp. 29–30):
  - Burning bridges behind oneself to show one cannot retreat.
  - An old English law forbidding tribute to coastal pirates. It does not necessarily appear cruel or anomalous in this light.
  - The mandatory secret ballot. The voter cannot prove how he voted, so he cannot be intimidated.
  - A negotiator's power often rests on an evident inability to make concessions. This makes the commonly voiced principle that negotiators should be given full flexibility and authority less self-evident than its proponents suggest.
  - A visible escape route may make a threat less credible.
- Giving up options, or making responses automatic, can be an advantage. This is hard to accept, and formal abstraction and analogies in other contexts help (p. 30).
- **Brodie's point:** for deterrence, unlike for a war one expects to fight, a super-dirty bomb may have some utility. Schelling says Chapter 10 treats the "balance of terror" as a modern exchange of hostages (p. 30).
- Modern students tend to equate peace with trust and good faith. Where these are absent, we may learn from the underworld and ancient despotisms how to make agreements work: hostages, shared wine, public meetings, exchanged spies. Schelling thinks it likely that a well-developed theory could throw light on how well such devices work and discover modern equivalents (pp. 30–31).

*Intuition note:* Binding yourself can make a threat or refusal believable. That is why less freedom or less rationality can sometimes be an advantage, and why a formal theory can help show when.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two drivers, A and B, approach a narrow bridge. Each chooses Go or Swerve. Payoffs are (A, B):
- Swerve/Swerve: (2, 2). Sum 4.
- A Go, B Swerve: (4, 0). Sum 4.
- A Swerve, B Go: (0, 4). Sum 4.
- Go/Go (crash): (−10, −10). Sum −20.

Sums differ, so the game is variable-sum. The crash is bad for both (common interest), but each wants to be the one who goes (conflict).
Now A visibly locks the steering wheel straight, losing the power to swerve (p. 20: threat made "by losing the power to do otherwise").
- If B Goes, B gets −10. If B Swerves, B gets 0.
- A rational B, who sees the lock, therefore Swerves, and the outcome is (4, 0).
A has less freedom but gets 4, rather than the 2 from both swerving. The gain depends on B seeing the lock and being rational enough to respond to it.

## Concepts
- [[strategy-of-conflict]] (new) — study of rational, goal-directed behaviour in conflict where interests partly conflict (pp. 10, 24).
- [[variable-sum-game]] (new) — game in which total gains are not fixed, so joint gains or joint losses are possible. Contrast constant-sum (pp. 12–13).
- [[deterrence]] (new) — influencing another's choices by shaping his expectation of how we will respond (p. 22).
- [[credible commitment]] — making a threat believable by committing to carry it out, e.g. a trip wire or honour at stake (p. 14).
- [[strategic (normal-form) game]] — here a "game of strategy": best action depends on expected actions of others (p. 18).
- [[interdependent-decision-theory]] (new) — Schelling's suggested name for the field. It is neutral on the degree of conflict (p. 25).
- [[rationality-as-attributes]] (new) — rationality is a collection of attributes (values, calculation, communication). Failures can run in many directions (p. 26).
- [[strategic-value-of-limited-options]] (new) — deliberately giving up options, information or control can be an advantage (pp. 28–30).

## Canonical instances
- [[strike-bargaining]] — a successful strike need not destroy the employer or even happen (p. 13).
- [[trip-wire-and-honor]] — commitment through a trip wire or the Formosa Resolution (p. 14).
- [[child-discipline-threat]] — threats whose execution hurts the threatener (p. 19).
- [[driver-collision-threat]] — a threat made by losing the power to do otherwise (p. 20).
- [[secret-ballot]] — denying a voter any means of proof removes his vulnerability to intimidation (p. 29).
- [[burning-bridges]] — removing the option to retreat (p. 29).
- [[hostage-exchange]] — ancient device to make agreements work without trust (p. 31).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] variable-sum conflict ↔ game theory: non-constant-sum games, where mutual gains and losses coexist — Schelling names the term himself (pp. 12–13).
- [structural-analogy] deterrent threat ↔ criminal law and parenting: same credibility problems in different settings (pp. 18–19).
- [metaphor] rationality as a bundle of attributes ↔ bounded rationality: departures from rationality have many dimensions, not one scale.
- [structural-analogy] secret ballot ↔ mechanism design: removing verifiability changes what threats can achieve.

## Questions this raises
- If a threat's credibility comes from reduced options, how does the other side verify that the commitment is real?
- Can the analysis separate deterrent, disciplinary and extortionate threats logically (p. 23)?
- How can the mixture of conflict and common interest be measured (p. 22)?
- When does the benchmark of rational behaviour become a good approximation, and when a caricature (p. 11)?
