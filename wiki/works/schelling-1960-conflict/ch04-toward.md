# Toward a Theory of Interdependent Decision

## L0 — One line
Schelling proposes to study mixed-motive (bargaining) games by treating pure zero-sum conflict and pure coordination as the two limiting cases, and describing the outcome as a convergence of mutual expectations that may depend on labels, clues and moves, not only on the payoff matrix.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Traditional game theory carried methods built for pure conflict (zero-sum games: one side's gain is the other's loss) into games that mix conflict and mutual dependence. Schelling instead treats the zero-sum game as only a limiting case. He argues that the opposite limit, pure coordination, is also a real game of strategy. In it, players succeed by finding shared clues, so the "labels" of strategies matter, even for a normative theory (about how players should act). Bargaining outcomes can be described as stabilised convergent expectations. Random minimax play destroys communication, whereas in mixed-motive games moves can carry information and commitment that cheap talk lacks. Mathematical focal points are only one kind of focal point. Players are often uncertain about each other's values.

**Prerequisites:** payoff matrices; zero-sum games and minimax (a strategy that guards against the worst case); the Chapter 3 tacit-coordination experiments (focal points: clues that make players' guesses converge). Also used above: cheap talk = costless speech; mixed-motive game = conflict plus mutual dependence; normal form = the payoff-matrix description of a game.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Zero-sum theory ignores suggestion, threat and promise, because such a relation, unless perfectly innocuous, disadvantages one player, who can destroy it by a minimax strategy (p. 105). Schelling suggests this preoccupation may explain why game theory has said little about mixed-motive games.
- Pure coordination (identical preferences) is the opposite limiting case. It is still a game of strategy, since each player's best choice depends on what he expects the other to do, which depends on the other's expectations of him (p. 108).
- A reclassification: fixed-sum, fixed-proportions and mixed (bargaining or mixed-motive) games, by the correlation of the players' preferences (pp. 109–111).
- Tacit coordination may help to explain mob formation, polarised neighbourhoods, and (probably) the stability of institutions, roles and tradition. It is reasoning about what others guess I guess, not about the "average man" (pp. 111–115).
- Labels and symbolic detail matter, even normatively. The normal form is therefore not logically equivalent to the extensive form once expectations must be concerted (pp. 116–119).
- Even with free speech, mixed-motive play may remain a process of mutual accommodation through moves. Moves can reveal and commit; speech often cannot (pp. 122–127).
- Focal points rest on qualitative distinctiveness, so small concessions are often less likely than large ones. Mathematical solutions are only one species of focal point (pp. 133–137).
- Value systems are often unknowable or uncommunicable, so moves have evidence content and symmetry has limited use (pp. 138–141).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the aim (pp. 104–105)
- Game theory has given insight on pure conflict (zero-sum games). It has given less on nonzero-sum situations: wars, strikes, negotiations, deterrence, traffic jams and so on (p. 104).
- Such games have mutual dependence in their logical structure. They need tacit or explicit accommodation, and signalling of intentions. Initiative, knowledge or freedom of choice need not be an advantage (p. 104).
- Traditional theory applied the methods that worked for pure conflict to these games. Schelling takes the zero-sum game as a limiting case, and extends the theory along two lines. Line 1 (this chapter): the perceptual and suggestive element in forming consistent expectations. Line 2 (next chapter): basic "moves" such as threat and enforcement (p. 105).
- **Why zero-sum theory neglects this.** Suggestions, threats and promises imply a relation between players. Unless perfectly innocuous, this relation must disadvantage one player, and he can destroy it by a minimax strategy, based if necessary on a randomizing mechanism. So pursuit-and-evasion behaviour should not be expected to show what favours mutual accommodation (p. 105). Schelling says the underdevelopment of game theory on these two lines may reflect its preoccupation with the zero-sum game.

### The other extreme: pure collaboration (pp. 105–109)
- The opposite limit is the "pure-collaboration" game. Players have identical preferences and rank all outcomes identically, and this is evident to them (pp. 105–106).
- It is not trivial. Where players cannot agree on a plan beforehand, they must find patterns that make each other predictable. They use clichés, conventions and impromptu codes, and communicate by hint. Examples: two vehicles avoiding collision, dancers to unfamiliar music, separated guerrillas, an audience deciding when to stop applauding (p. 106).
- Chess is the standard zero-sum example; charades may typify pure coordination. Pursuit is to zero-sum what rendezvous is to coordination (p. 106).
- **Moore–Berkowitz experiment.** Two teams of three each; members are separated and linked by one shared telephone line, with no prearranged codes. Between teams the game is pure conflict; within a team it is pure coordination (p. 107). Drop the other team and you get a three-person coordination game. Here the nonzero-sum game overlaps organization and communication theory (p. 107).
- Chapter 3 showed that coordination is possible with no communication. It also showed that there are tacit bargaining situations in which the need to concert may overwhelm the conflict of interest; in those, pure coordination isolates the essential feature of the nonzero-sum game (p. 107).
- The two limits are symmetric. Zero-sum is the mixed game with all scope for cooperation removed; coordination is the mixed game with the conflict removed. In one the premium is on secrecy, in the other on revelation (p. 108).
- **Coordination is a game of strategy in the strict sense.** Each player's best choice depends on the action he expects of the other, which depends on the other's expectations of him. This separates it from games of chance or skill (p. 108).
- **Holmes and Moriarty, three payoffs** (pp. 108–109):
  1. Holmes wins if they get off at different stations, Moriarty if at the same station: zero-sum, preferences perfectly inversely correlated.
  2. Both are rewarded for getting off at the same station, whichever: pure coordination, preferences perfectly positively correlated.
  3. Both are rewarded for matching, but Holmes gains more at one particular station and Moriarty at another; both lose unless they match: the usual nonzero-sum or "imperfect-correlation" game, the mix epitomizing bargaining.
- In all three, each must guess what the other guesses he will guess, "in the familiar spiral of reciprocal expectations" (p. 109).

*Intuition note.* In pure coordination the aim is not to out-think the other player but to find the same clue he will find. The endless spiral of each side expecting the other to expect his own expectation is what makes it a strategic game.

### A reclassification of games (pp. 109–111)
- The zero-sum / nonzero-sum split lacks symmetry and does not name the limit opposite zero-sum (p. 109).
- Draw each outcome as a point whose coordinates are the two players' values. Pure conflict gives points on a negatively inclined line; pure common interest gives a positively inclined line. A mixed game has at least one negatively sloped pair and one positively sloped pair of points (pp. 109–110).
- Names: fixed-sum and fixed-proportions games for the pure cases; perfect negative or positive correlation of preferences; "imperfect-correlation" for the mixed case (p. 110).
- Schelling will call the mixed game a **bargaining game** or **mixed-motive game**. "Mixed-motive" refers to the ambivalent relation to the other player, not to unclear own preferences. "Nonzero-sum" covers the mixed game plus pure common interest; the perfect sharing of interests is the **coordination game** (pp. 110–111).

### Games of coordination (pp. 111–119)
- In a number of instances, pure coordination is a significant phenomenon in itself, not only a clue to mixed games (p. 111).
- **Mobs.** Mob members need to know when to act in unison. Overt leadership solves this but can be removed by authorities. "Incidents" then substitute for leadership as a coordinating signal. A city with no "obvious" central place may be one where mobs find it hard to gather spontaneously. Bandwagon behaviour may depend on mutually perceived signals (pp. 111–112).
- **Neighbourhood polarization.** Schelling says excessively polarised behaviour may be the unhappy result of dependence on tacit coordination. When whites and Negroes (his period wording) see that an area will "inevitably" become occupied exclusively by Negroes, the inevitability is a feature of convergent expectation: what is most directly perceived as inevitable is not the final result but the expectation of it, which in turn makes the result inevitable. No stable focal point exists except at the extremes. Nobody can expect a tacit process to stop at 10, 30 or 60 per cent, because no percentage commands agreement. It is easy to "agree" tacitly to move but impossible to agree to stay. Quota systems can be viewed as efforts to substitute an explicit game with communication and enforcement for the tacit game (pp. 112–113).
- **Institutions and tradition.** Tradition singles out the set of rules everyone expects everyone to be conscious of; it wins by default. Etiquette rules, clothing fads and sociological "roles" can partly be read as solutions to coordination games. On this reading, one is trapped in a particular role, or by another's role, because it is the only role that in the circumstances can be identified by tacit consent (p. 113).
- **Esprit de corps.** The character of army units or colleges, despite turnover, seems largely a matter of convergent expectations. Schelling says he is told that legal identities of depleted units are preserved for this reason. He suggests that tax compliance in some countries may reflect the same phenomenon (p. 114).
- **The intellectual process.** Coordination is not guessing what the "average man" will do. It is guessing what the other will guess one will guess, ad infinitum, so the reasoning detaches from the objective situation except for clues it offers. Examples: newspaper personal-column "meetings", stock or real estate everyone expects everyone to buy, diamonds, and perhaps the monetary role of gold (pp. 114–115).
- **"Name a positive number."** Asked to simply pick a number, most pick 3, 7, 13, 100 or 1. Asked to match the others, the preponderant choice is 1. Reason: there is no unique favoured number, but the positive numbers have a unique first one (p. 115).
- **Formulation (Fig. 8).** One player picks a row, the other a column. If each choice of one player has a single matching choice that wins for both, columns can be arranged so the winning cells lie on the diagonal with positive payoffs, and the other cells hold zeros (p. 116).
- **Labels matter.** Schelling rules out a possible axiom, suggested by analogy with other game theories, that the "labeling" (Luce and Raiffa's term) of rows, columns and players should make no difference. Symbolic labels let players beat chance, and make these games interesting (pp. 116–117). Even the plain matrix prejudices choice by drawing attention to "first", "middle" and "last". With an infinite series of rows, the game is formally "name a positive number", but the different labeling gives less clustering at 3, 7, 13. If strategies carry unordered individual names, the names must coordinate (p. 117).
- **Coordination versus conflict reasoning.** In coordination one seeks contact through introspection and shared clues. The two intellectual processes are of wholly different sorts, at least if one admits the minimax solution, randomized if necessary. In minimax play, especially randomized, one's whole aim is to avoid any meeting of minds, even an inadvertent one (p. 117).
- **Card and train examples.** One player names one card from an ordinary deck of 52 and the other must guess which. If I want you to guess correctly and you know I will try to help your guess, a random device can only guarantee that tacit cooperation is impossible. Holmes can destroy the station labels by coin flip. In the common-interest version they must use the labels, and how may depend more on imagination than on logic, more on "poetry or humor" than on mathematics. Traditional theory assigns no "value" to this game; the matter is empirical, not a priori (p. 118).
- **Normative, not just predictive.** The claim is that players should use labels, not just that they do. A normative theory must give strategies at least as good as what people achieve unaided, and must not expunge details that benefit players jointly (pp. 118–119).
- **Consequences.** Once rational players concert expectations, the normal (abstract) form is not logically equivalent to the extensive (particular) form. "Non-cooperative" is a poor name for tacit coordination, which is cooperative in its own way. Appendix C is said to reinterpret some solution concepts via coordination (p. 119).

*Intuition note.* A payoff matrix with the labels stripped off hides what lets players coordinate. A theory that treats labels as irrelevant leaves out what, on Schelling's account, lets players do better than chance.

### Suggestion and mutual perception in the mixed-motive game (pp. 119–141)

**Cincinnati–San Francisco map game (pp. 120–121).** Two players with identical maps each draw a dividing line. If lines differ, neither gets anything; if identical, both are rewarded by the value of their own piece. Valuation is deliberately vague, so no symmetric split is available (p. 120).
- Each must do what the other expects; neither can "outsmart" the other without outsmarting himself (p. 120).
- Chapter 3 suggests players are not helpless. Some variants are not hard, and some are "won" by the same outcome as if interests were identical. Each must be prepared to accept the clue even if it discriminates against him. A referee's arbitrary line, identical on both maps, might serve as a "mediator", even if biased (p. 121).
- Coordination virtually ceases to be a "game" if players can concert with certainty, without difficulty and without cost. So how pervasive is it in mixed games with free speech? (p. 121)

**Why coordination is pervasive (pp. 121–123).**
1. Tacit bargaining offers an analytical model (perhaps an analogy, perhaps an identification) of the rational process of reaching agreement in pure bargaining, where many outcomes beat no agreement. Coordination of expectations is the role of "mutual perception" (pp. 121–122).
2. Many real games are partly tacit: traffic, neighbours, illicit or diplomatic bargaining, and large-number cases such as racial borders. There, actions are part of the bargaining (p. 122).
- Also, if moves are available, it pays to get on with the maneuver while negotiating, and some maneuvers become visible only after a time lag; so there is no reason to suppose an instantaneous moratorium on maneuver, and the game progresses while talk goes on (p. 122).
- Moves differ from speech. They typically have tactical significance and leave the game irreversibly changed. One may say a gun is loaded without proof until shooting. Moves can reveal information, commit when speech often cannot, and proceed at a unilateral speed (pp. 122–123).
- Hence bargaining typically involves "a dynamic process of mutual accommodation", not pure communication ending in a crystallized agreement (p. 123).

**Illustrative tacit chip game (pp. 123–126).**
- Each player has 100 chips and a US map. Each move, a player places five chips on states. Chips of both players on the same state cancel one-for-one, leaving the difference. After chips run out, players may shift up to five per move; play ends when both tell the referee they will stop (pp. 123–124).
- Payoff: one dollar per surviving chip, plus dollar values per state "possessed" (states with chips, plus unoccupied states enclosed in the home-base area). State values are vaguely like "economic worth" but not the same for the two players, and each knows little of the other's values (p. 124).
- The game is mixed-motive. Joint damage results from poor accommodation: each fight costs each at least a dollar, and chips lost mean fewer left to claim states (p. 125).
- Players still "bargain": they propose, accept, reject, retaliate, convey threats and promises, all through behaviour. If one player wants a state badly, it is better for both to know that early. A player conceding a region must make it conspicuously available and mark its limits by his pattern of play (pp. 125–126).
- Patterns are not richly provided by the mathematical structure; presumably they come from natural boundaries, political groupings, state characteristics, Gestalt psychology and clichés or traditions developed in play (p. 126).

**Explicit communication (pp. 126–127).** Let players talk freely. Talk may help with complex trades and avoid inadvertent clashes. It need not stop costly competition, since being first is valuable, and nobody can prove what he says except by how he plays (lies go unpunished, and there is no written evidence). So the dependence on conveying and perceiving intentions, predictable patterns and acquiescence in limits is much the same as before, so Schelling says introducing speech may not greatly alter the game's character (pp. 126–127).

**Minimax is self-effacing (pp. 127–128).** With a minimax solution a zero-sum game becomes unilateral. One need not know whether there is an opponent. A randomized strategy is "anti-communicative" and strips the game down to its payoff structure. In chess it does not matter what the pieces look like or what the game is called (p. 127).

**Modified chess (p. 128).** Reward pieces left at the end and squares occupied, so both want to limit gross captures. Make each player uncertain of the other's valuations, and use a clock so nobody can stall to talk. Then it may matter whether the game is called "war" or "gold rush", what the pieces look like, and what story is told. Bargaining must proceed by words or moves, and the "incidental details" can help players find regulating patterns (p. 128).

**Tradition-making and retaliation patterns (pp. 129–130).** Schelling calls the fundamental psychic and intellectual process one of participating in creating traditions, and the materials for them are not coincident with the mathematical content (p. 129).
- Outcomes are determined by reciprocal expectations. Players must jointly find "rules of the game" or suffer (p. 129).
- Conveying a pattern of retaliation for acts considered "out of bounds" depends on contextual material and on the other's capacity to recognise the "formula (Gestalt)". Precedent, casuistry, mathematics and aesthetics supply the "menu". Even with full verbal communication, patterns of action may be more telling than words (pp. 129–130).
- The point is again normative. Rational players ought to take the game's suggestive details into account. A rational player who sees the details discriminate against him may rationally accept this, since the other expects him to submit to their discipline (p. 130).

*Intuition note.* In a mixed-motive game, how a game is described or dressed may make a difference to where players settle. Players need some shared way to know where to stop, and the details of the game can help supply it, even when they favour one side.

**A hypothetical experiment (pp. 130–133).** Not carried out, and not offered as evidence (p. 133).
- Invent a machine, like a lie detector, that measures a person's recognition, attention or excitement as he scans possible outcomes (p. 130).
- Two players bargain over shares of a gain, with enough topical content for argument. Each can see his own meter and the other's, and each knows both can see (p. 131). A scanning device moves over the outcomes, regularly or randomly (p. 131).
- Variant 1: no explicit bargaining. Do the two reactions converge on one outcome? A prior solo scan could serve as a control (pp. 131–132). Convergence would show (a) players react to the content of the situation and (b) their reactions interact, because each sees the other's and knows his own reveals his expectations. Schelling conjectures that, like Lot's wife, players often cannot keep attention from outcomes, and that trying to ignore a focal point may increase its power (p. 132).
- Variant 2: explicit bargaining during metering. A player might cite the meter, e.g. that the other "obviously" cannot hold out for $60 when blood pressure shows his mind is on $40 (p. 132).
- Three hypotheses: (1) people have identifiable reactions that differ across alternatives; (2) when visible to both, reactions interact in a bargaining-like way; (3) this phenomenon is related to ordinary bargaining (pp. 132–133).
- Purpose: an operational picture of "convergence of expectations", and the suggestion that convergence may depend on the dynamics of the process, not only the a priori data of the game (p. 133).

**Dynamic characteristics of focal-point solutions (pp. 133–136).**
- A focal point rests on a qualitative distinction from neighbours. So small concessions are often less likely than large ones, and the focal point persuades more as an exact outcome than as an approximation. Example: after persistently demanding 50 per cent, a compromise at 47 is unlikely, since a small concession may signal collapse (p. 133).
- A focal point often gets its character from the fact that small concessions are impossible and encroachments would multiply: "If not here, where?" Likewise, a compromise of just one small drink is notoriously unstable. More people quit cigarettes entirely than reach a stable small daily quota (p. 134).
- Sometimes the focal point is itself unstable and serves as a sign of where to look for the outcome. Examples: test votes and test issues. A small piece of the game comes to symbolise the whole and sets expectations beyond its substance (pp. 134–135).
- Examples listed: recognition of Communist China, loyalty oaths, a key strike settlement, an interrupter at a party, a convention vote. Sometimes a test just reveals how other issues would go; often it is unrepresentative but taken as a clue (p. 135).
- Such tests can ratify an unorganized coalition's existence by showing everyone expects everyone to act together. Even a two-person dare may be psychologically identical to resolving a bargaining game (p. 135).
- Experimental psychology may contribute here (p. 136).

**Empirical relevance of mathematical foci (pp. 136–138).**
- Do not assume participants perceive what the analyst perceives. Features that matter only for sophisticated mathematical solutions may not focus expectations. Perhaps they do only if the players see each other as mathematicians. This may be the empirical reading of the solutions of Braithwaite, Nash, Harsanyi "and others" (p. 136).
- Mathematical properties are one species of focusing influence among aesthetic, historical, legal, moral and cultural ones. Often a mathematical uniqueness or symmetry has a nonmathematical appeal too (pp. 136–137).
- Example: a husband and wife separated in a department store go to the Lost and Found. Two mathematicians, each aware both are, might seek a geometrically unique point (p. 137).
- If agreement is fundamentally psychic, convergence of expectations, there is no presumption that mathematics is its main source (p. 137). Appendix B pursues this (p. 137).
- Outcomes are best described empirically as stabilised convergent expectations. Whoever accepts a bargain, if he has his wits about him, must expect that he could do no better and must recognise that the other reciprocates the feeling (p. 138).

**Communicating subjective information (pp. 138–141).**
- In mixed-motive games, unlike known zero-sum games, there is likely uncertainty about value systems, so moves have information content (p. 138). Schelling says full foreknowledge of the other's preferences cannot be set up as the general case: certain elements are inherently unknowable for some participants, except under special conditions. In the Russian example the reason we cannot know is not solely that they might be unwilling to tell us. Questions posed: how can the Russians make us believe how much they dislike all-out war, or the Chinese, if really determined to take Formosa at the cost of an all-out war, persuade us they could not be deterred? (pp. 138–139)
- Information can sometimes be conveyed: cards turned face up, or a shared belief in a punishing higher power ("cross my heart…"). These are special cases (p. 139).
- **Von Neumann–Morgenstern house example** (Schelling's numbers): seller A will sell for any price above 10; buyers B and C will pay up to 15 and 25. In their solution, C might pay B a share of his saving if B's staying out lets C buy for less than 15. The most B might receive is $15 - 10 = 5$ (pp. 139–140).
- Schelling's point: B could not convincingly communicate his reservation price in the ordinary world. The solution assumes C can discern, or B reveal, a subjective truth that speculators D and E cannot counterfeit (p. 140).
- Symmetry may work in special cases, but in too many cases the opponent is a very different kind of person: the father of a kidnapped boy will not be very successful guessing his own bottom price as kidnapper; a British or French officer may not find it easy to guess what penalty would deter a Mau Mau or Algerian terrorist; a boy finds it hard to guess how much he would like himself as the girl; a customer finds it hard to know how much he would dislike a scene as the waiter (p. 140).
- So talk is no substitute for moves. Moves alter the game through manifest costs, risks or reduced choice, and have evidence content unlike speech. Enforceable threats and promises count as moves (pp. 140–141).
- An efficient outcome requires dividing gains by comparative advantage: each concedes what he wants less than the other, relative to what he gets. Each thus needs to communicate his value system with some truth, although each can also gain by deceiving. Maneuvers may be ambiguous or deceptive but still have an evidential quality that speech lacks (p. 141).
- Uncertainty about values also reduces the usefulness of mathematical symmetry, which "cannot be perceived if one has access to only half the relevant magnitudes". To the extent symmetry helps, it would tend to be a more qualitative symmetry that depends on visible context rather than underlying values (p. 141).

*Intuition note.* You usually cannot read the other side's values, and talk can be cheap. A move that incurs costs or risks has evidence content of a different character from speech, even though maneuvers can be ambiguous or deceptive. That is part of why Schelling says talk is no substitute for moves.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two players each name one of three stations, X, Y or Z. Both get 1 if they name the same station and 0 otherwise.
- **Both randomize uniformly.** Each of the 9 cells has probability $\tfrac13\times\tfrac13=\tfrac19$. A match occurs in 3 cells (XX, YY, ZZ). So $P(\text{match}) = 3\times\tfrac19=\tfrac13$.
- **Both use the label.** If the stations are listed X, Y, Z and each thinks "the first one is the obvious one", both name X. $P(\text{match}) = 1$.
- Labels lift success from $\tfrac13$ to 1. The random device that is optimal when you want to avoid being guessed guarantees the lower figure in a common-interest game. The label-based rule can be written down in the matrix, but it lives in the names and ordering, not in the payoffs. This is Schelling's point that labels are what let players beat chance (pp. 116–118).

## Concepts
- [[focal-point]] — a clue or qualitatively distinct point that makes players' expectations converge on one outcome; here also dynamic (small concessions are often less likely than large ones) (pp. 111, 133–134)
- [[convention]] — Schelling's "tradition": an expected set of rules that wins by default because everyone expects everyone to be conscious of it (p. 113)
- [[strategic-normal-form-game]] — here, used to stress that the abstract matrix loses labels; the normal form is not equivalent to the extensive form once expectations are concerted (p. 119)
- [[zero-sum-minimax]] — treated as a limiting case; randomized minimax is "anti-communicative" and self-effacing (pp. 105, 127)
- [[mixed-strategy]] — randomizing as a tool that destroys communication and labels; in a common-interest game a random device can only guarantee that tacit cooperation is impossible (pp. 117–118)
- [[signaling]] — moves can convey information and commitment that cheap talk often lacks (pp. 123, 140–141)
- [[credible-commitment]] — only hinted at: moves can commit when speech often cannot; threats are left to the next chapter (pp. 105, 123)
- [[mutual-vs-common-knowledge]] — the endless spiral of reciprocal expectations and the mutual awareness of both meters (pp. 109, 115, 131)
- [[pure-coordination-game]] (new) — a game where players have identical preferences over outcomes, and success needs tacit concerting (pp. 105–106)
- [[mixed-motive-game]] (new) — Schelling's name for bargaining games: mutual dependence plus conflict, imperfectly correlated preferences (p. 110)
- [[convergent-expectations]] (new) — the outcome of bargaining seen as stabilised, reciprocal expectations (pp. 112, 138)
- [[labeling]] (new) — symbolic or connotative features of strategies that transcend the mathematical structure and enable coordination (pp. 116–117)
- [[tacit-bargaining]] (new) — bargaining by actions and patterns rather than explicit communication (pp. 122, 125)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Convergent expectations in coordination ↔ Nash equilibrium / convention: an outcome that each expects of the other and that sustains itself.
- [structural-analogy] Normal form versus extensive form not equivalent under tacit coordination ↔ game theory: representation choices change what players can use as clues.
- [metaphor] Mob formation and neighbourhood tipping ↔ threshold or tipping models of collective behaviour: expectations of others' action drive the outcome.
- [structural-analogy] Moves as evidence versus cheap talk ↔ signaling: costly actions convey private information.
- [structural-analogy] Hypothetical meter experiment ↔ experimental economics: an operational test of whether expectations converge.

## Questions this raises
- If labels and clues decide coordination, how can one predict which clue will be salient for a given pair of players?
- How should a normative theory include label-dependent play without becoming unfalsifiable or purely empirical?
- If a focal point works because concession is "collapse", how can a compromise ever be reached once one side stakes everything on it?
- How can a player make a true statement about his own values credible when talk is cheap, and which moves are costly enough to count?
