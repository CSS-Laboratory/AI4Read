# The Threat That Leaves Something to Chance

## L0 — One line
A threat can be credible without a firm commitment to carry it out, if it creates a shared risk of disaster whose final outcome neither side fully controls.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Normally a threat must say "I will", because "I may" shows the threatener still chooses and may back down. Schelling asks how a "may" threat can still work. His answer: it works when the final decision is partly outside the threatener's control. Inadvertent war, limited war, reprisals and Berlin-style actions can all create such risks. The threatener raises a recognizable probability of war for both sides, and, ideally, only the other side's compliance can lower it. Schelling calls this brinkmanship. He says it rests on the view that real government decision-making is imperfect. The idea matters even if we do not consciously use it: it may be used against us, and ignoring it may make us misjudge our own tactics.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a credible threat or commitment; simple probability, including repeated independent trials.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A "may" threat normally fails: it admits the threatener keeps the decision, and a failed bluff confirms the opponent's guess that he would abstain (p. 217).
- A "may" threat can work if the decision is partly outside the threatener's control: chance, accident, third parties, or imperfect decision machinery (p. 218).
- A crisis probably raises the risk of inadvertent war. That rise can work as a deterrent threat even if nobody intends it, and the threat is not discredited when no war occurs (pp. 219–221).
- On Schelling's alternative reading, limited war has two costs: direct damage, and exposure of both sides to a higher risk of general war. This also helps explain why one might threaten limited rather than all-out war (pp. 222–223).
- A "trip wire" is better seen as a graduated series of chance-linked wires than as a switch that certainly works or certainly fails (p. 224).
- Compellent threats (do X, or else) need commitment to act first. A small, irreversible, per-period probability of mutual disaster may serve as such a commitment, if compliance is possible soon enough to keep the cumulative risk tolerable. When the threat starts fulfilling itself over time, Schelling calls the small-probability threat a superior and necessary alternative to a large certain threat (pp. 228–230).
- Brinkmanship is the deliberate creation of a recognizable risk of war that one does not completely control. Leaving the enemy guessing may make sense as long as we are not trying to keep him guessing about our own motivation (pp. 233–235).
- Schelling argues the basis is realistic: government decisions are imperfect, so there are no "firm" plans for every contingency (pp. 235–237).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: threats and the problem with "may" (pp. 217–218)
Strategic threats are typically costly to both sides if carried out. Their purpose is deterrence ex ante (beforehand), not revenge ex post (afterwards). Making a credible threat involves proving that one would have to act, or creating incentives or penalties that would evidently make one want to (p. 217). The US troops in Europe as a "trip wire" were meant to show that escape from the commitment was physically impossible (p. 217).

As a rule one must say "I will act", not "I may act". "May" admits the threatener has kept the power of decision and invites the opponent to guess (p. 217). If the opponent ignores the threat and the threatener abstains, this confirms the opponent's belief that the threatener abstains when he has a clear choice (p. 217).

Some "may" threats can nevertheless work, but through a more complicated process, and they may arise inadvertently (p. 218). The key is that the final decision is not altogether under the threatener's control (p. 218; the book prints "threateneds"). The threat has an element of "I may or may not, and even I can't be altogether sure" (p. 218). The uncertainty must come from outside the threatener's control: chance, accident, third-party influence, imperfect decision machinery, or processes we do not fully understand (p. 218).

> **Intuition note:** A "may" threat is weak because the threatener visibly keeps the choice and may back down. If even he cannot be sure what will happen, the opponent cannot easily dismiss the threat as a bluff.

### The threat of inadvertent war (pp. 218–221)
Inadvertent war could come from accident, false alarm, panic, or misreading of intentions. Its probability probably rises in a crisis, when forces are on alert (p. 219). Schelling asks whether this mechanism is itself a deterrent threat. The implied answer is yes: if the Russians see that aggression raises tension and readiness, they may see the risk of war as depending on their own behavior (p. 219).

As far as this mechanism is concerned, what rises is the risk that war occurs whether intended or not, not the risk that the US decides on war (p. 219). If a mechanism like this exists, this threat could be most credible. It does not depend on our preferring all-out war or being committed to it, and the final decision is left to "chance" (p. 220).

Three features:
- It may exist whether we realize it or not (p. 220).
- It may be a byproduct of precautions we have strong reasons to take (p. 220).
- It is not discredited if no war follows. A person who survives one play of Russian roulette should not conclude it is safe (p. 221).

> **Intuition note:** The threat is a risk that goes up when the other side misbehaves, not a promise. Surviving one round tells you little about the next.

### Limited war as a generator of risk (pp. 221–224)
The usual view of limited war is a sequence of deliberate decisions. If we can resist on a small scale, the enemy must give up or take a step up the scale of violence; at some point there is a discontinuous jump to general war (p. 221). Schelling's alternative is that limited war, and its enlargement, almost certainly raise the danger of all-out war. So the threat of limited war has two parts: direct costs (casualties, territory, face) and exposure of both sides to a heightened risk of general war (p. 222). The odds are a matter of judgment (p. 222).

Why threaten limited rather than all-out war (pp. 222–223)?
1. It threatens a risk, not certainty. It is a lesser threat, suited to some contingencies.
2. It offers an intermediate stage. If the enemy misjudges us, both pay only a risk of general war, which he can reduce by withdrawing or settling.
3. If the enemy is irrational, out of control, or acting through satellites (puppet states beyond his immediate control), there is some prudence in threatening risk. If we threaten all-out war, wrongly thinking it is not too late to stop him, we must carry it out or have the threat discredited. With a one-in-twenty risk we have "nineteen-to-one odds" of escaping general war.

Caveat: scaling the risk down for us also scales it down for him, which may degrade the threat (p. 223). New weapons should be judged also by the extra risk they pose (pp. 223–224).

The trip wire is then a graduated series of wires, each tied to a chance mechanism, with the daily probability of detonation rising as the enemy moves from wire to wire. What matters is that detonation is at least partly outside our control, and the Russians know it (p. 224). Quemoy may fit the same reading: the Chinese or Russians may have been deterred by a numerically small but appreciable risk of major war (p. 224).

> **Intuition note:** On this reading, limited war works as a threat partly because it makes the big war a little more likely for both sides. The enemy can lower that risk by withdrawing or settling.

### Risky behavior in limited war (pp. 224–226)
For the side in danger of losing a limited war, the aim may be not to keep it limited at all costs but to keep the risk of all-out war "within moderate limits above zero" (p. 225). The risk cannot be raised just by announcing it. One must act so that, even if both sides remain careful, everyone is less sure the war can be controlled (p. 225). Actions that do this include breaches of limits, menacing postures, headstrong allies, new weapons, and larger troop commitments (p. 225). Ideally the shared risk is created by irreversible moves, so that only the enemy's withdrawal can calm things. Otherwise it may turn out to be a contest of nerves (p. 226).

### Reprisal and harassment (pp. 226–228)
Between massive retaliation (answering aggression with all-out war) and limited war lies graduated reprisal (less-than-massive retaliation), which has been little analysed (p. 226). Soviet harassment (blockades, radar jamming, sabotage, a war of nerves) may not be effectively countered by like measures; deliberately creating a small but appreciable shared risk of general war might be considered. If not, at least the harassment may need to be read as an attempt to intimidate in the same way (pp. 226–227).

A limited nuclear reprisal may also have two cost parts: direct damage and a created risk of all-out war. The victim may not choose all-out war, but he is likely to do something with consequences that may escalate. Schelling adds that the odds may still be against escalation (p. 227). Schelling is not necessarily proposing these actions, only saying how to interpret them (pp. 227–228).

### Risky behavior and "compellent" threats (pp. 228–232)
Deterring (keeping him from starting something) differs from compelling (making him do, or cease doing, something) in who must act first. To deter an advance, burning bridges behind me may suffice. To compel a retreat I must be committed to advance (p. 228). Blocking your car in the road is a passive deterrent, since you must decide to collide. If you threaten to collide unless I move, the decision is still yours (p. 228).

Compelling therefore often means administering the punishment until the other complies, because committing to an action often means starting it (p. 228). Starting certain mutual disaster irreversibly is no good. Irreversibly starting a moderate risk of mutual disaster may work, provided compliance is feasible quickly enough to keep the cumulative risk tolerable (p. 229). A 1 per cent risk of disaster per week is "somewhat similar" to a steady weekly damage rate of 1 per cent of disaster, with the words "somewhat" and "equivalent" read flexibly (p. 229).

"Rocking the boat" is an example. A threat to tip the boat and drown us both is not believed. Actually rocking it so that it may tip is more impressive. I must really put the boat in jeopardy (p. 229).

Schelling's ideal device is a black box with a roulette wheel and a detonator. It spins daily, with a probability known to the Russians that it provokes total war. It runs until demands are met, and the threatener cannot stop it. The decision is left to the box (pp. 229–230).

A key result: even if the enemy complies, the box might fire before he reacts. So it is better that the daily probability be less than certain. In ordinary deterrence, too big a threat may be superfluous but not self-defeating. When the threat fulfils itself over time from the moment of commitment, "too big a threat can defeat its purpose". Here the small-probability threat is "a superior and necessary alternative" (p. 230).

Example (pp. 230–231): a European country with a modest nuclear force tells the Russians to leave Hungary. A threat of terrible damage is ignored as suicidal. Instead it sends one missile a day, with a random device that may explode it over Russia. The country is thereby incurring a painful risk of all-out war that neither side wants. It says "we may cause an all-out war to occur", not that it will deliberately start a war. The sooner the Russians leave, the sooner the risk ends (p. 231).

The same analysis may apply to an armored column sent to Berlin, or to troops claiming a corridor, which may be read as actions that create a shared risk (pp. 231–232). Alternatively, limited war may contain a deterrent threat against enlargement (p. 232). Even so, an important reason why the threat of even small-scale war might be effective is that it adds a small but appreciable probability of enormous war: small enough that the Russians believe the West could bring itself to create it, large enough to make it unprofitable for them to let it occur (p. 232). So limited war may be potent even without expecting to win it (p. 232).

> **Intuition note:** To compel, one often has to start acting. A small repeated chance of shared disaster lets one start acting without committing to something suicidal. A small chance each day also gives the other side a reason to hurry.

### Brinkmanship (pp. 232–235)
The "brink" is not a sharp cliff edge but a curved, irregular slope. One may slip in spite of one's best efforts, and neither the person standing there nor onlookers know exactly how great the risk is (p. 233). Brinkmanship is the deliberate creation of a recognizable risk of war that one does not completely control. It means letting the situation get somewhat out of hand because that may be intolerable to the other party (p. 233).

Schelling revisits the advice to leave the enemy guessing about our response (p. 233). If the response remains ours to decide, the Russians may guess we would abstain. Being unwilling to commit may then confirm that guess (p. 234). It is different if we are so involved that, while we probably have a way out, we may not. Saying we may or may not retaliate, depending on how it suits us, may look like a bluff to the enemy. Uncertainty from events manifestly somewhat beyond our comprehension and control creates genuine risk for him (pp. 234–235). In sum, it may make sense to keep the enemy guessing as long as we are not trying to keep him guessing about our own motivation (p. 234).

### The imperfect process of decision (pp. 235–238)
The threat rests on the view that some of the most momentous decisions of government are taken by a process that is not entirely predictable or fully under control, so a nation can get into major war somewhat inadvertently (p. 235). Schelling gives three reasons this is not an unusually cynical view:
- Decisions rely on incomplete evidence and ambiguous warning, so an action on a false alarm cannot be ruled out (p. 235).
- Both sides may be committed to irreconcilable positions, or may misjudge each other's commitments (pp. 235–236).
- Even an orderly government is an imperfect decision system. Several people take part with differing values and judgments. Crisis decisions depend on who is present, and some are delegated. There may be necessary contradictions, such as constitutional issues that cannot be settled in advance, and secrecy limits preparation (p. 236).

Hence no government has a "firm" plan for every contingency (pp. 236–237). Schelling says a nation may get into a situation where it looks as though it can extricate itself, but there is some appreciable risk that it may not (p. 237). Governments are not expected to advertise these weaknesses; the point goes without saying (p. 237). The idea still matters even if we do not consciously use it: it may be used against us, and we may misjudge our own tactics, such as limited-war forces in Europe, if we ignore the risk-of-total-war ingredient. The usual view of a trip wire as either working or not mistakes "two simple extremes for a more complicated range of probabilities" (pp. 237–238).

## Toy example (ours, not Schelling's)
Suppose the "black box" fires with probability $p = 0.02$ per day, independently each day, until the enemy complies.
- Chance of no war after $n$ days: $0.98^n$.
- $n=1$: war risk $= 1 - 0.98 = 0.02$.
- $n=5$: $0.98^5 = 0.9039$, so cumulative risk $= 1 - 0.9039 = 0.096$.
- $n=10$: $0.98^{10} = 0.9039^2 = 0.8171$, so cumulative risk $= 0.183$.

The enemy's compliance on day 5 costs both sides a 9.6% chance of war. Waiting until day 10 nearly doubles that to 18.3%. Each extra day of delay therefore adds to the cumulative risk. If $p = 1$, war would occur on day 1 whatever he did, so the threat would defeat its own purpose (compare p. 230).

## Concepts
- [[threat-that-leaves-something-to-chance]] (new) — a threat that leaves it open whether one will act, and even the threatener cannot be sure, where the decision is partly outside the threatener's control (p. 218)
- [[credible-commitment]] — making a threat believable by showing one would have to act or evidently wants to; here the believability comes instead from partial loss of control (pp. 217–218)
- [[shared-risk]] (new) — a recognizable probability of mutual disaster imposed on both sides, which the other side can reduce by complying or withdrawing, and ideally only he can (pp. 222–223, 226)
- [[compellent-threat]] (new) — a threat to make the adversary act or stop, not to keep him from starting; it typically requires the threatener to be committed to act, often by starting the punishment (p. 228)
- [[brinkmanship]] (new) — deliberately creating a recognizable risk of war that one does not completely control (p. 233)
- [[imperfect-decision-process]] (new) — government decisions that cannot be exactly foretold, because of chance, error, divided authority, or limits on preparation (pp. 235–237)

## Canonical instances
- [[trip-wire]] — the US troops in Europe, read as a graduated series of chance-linked wires rather than a switch (pp. 217, 224)
- [[russian-roulette]] — surviving one play does not show the risk was not real (p. 221)
- [[rocking-the-boat]] — a small real chance of tipping is more credible than a bare threat to drown both (p. 229)
- [[black-box-roulette-wheel]] — a device that fires with a daily known probability and cannot be stopped (pp. 229–230)
- [[missile-a-day-over-ussr]] — a European country's daily random-detonation missile aimed at Russia (pp. 230–231)
- [[brink-as-curved-slope]] — brinkmanship as standing on a slope, not at a cliff edge (p. 233)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Chance-linked threat ↔ decision-game-theory/subgame-perfect equilibrium: both address non-credible threats, here by removing the threatener's choice at the critical point.
- [structural-analogy] Daily detonation probability ↔ probability/survival analysis: a repeated per-period risk gives a cumulative risk (our toy example; the text states the idea only informally).
- [metaphor] Brink as a slope ↔ dynamical systems: escalation as a path where the risk of slipping rises toward the chasm.

## Questions this raises
- How can a side raise the risk by an amount it actually knows, when Schelling says the odds are a matter of judgment (p. 222)?
- If both sides use shared-risk tactics, when does it become a "contest of nerves" (p. 226), and who yields?
- How could the enemy tell a real risk from a staged one, given that he cannot see a government's internal decision process (p. 237)?
