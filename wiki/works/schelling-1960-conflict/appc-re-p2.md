# Appendix C (part 2): end of the solution-concept appendix, and the Notes

## L0 — One line
In tacit non-zero-sum games, the "solution in the strict sense" works partly as a signal that players can use to coordinate, and the Notes spell out how commitments, threats, labels and shared pattern-perception shape such games.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The appendix ends by asking how many players matter and what the "solution" of a tacit non-zero-sum game means. Schelling's answer is that coordination-game theory suggests it is to be understood partly, and in some cases largely, through its signalling qualities. Which signals people can appreciate is partly an empirical question, not only a matter of deduction. The rest of the unit is the book's Notes. They give the questionnaire numbers behind the coordination experiments, examples of commitments and threats, comments on Luce and Raiffa and on Nash, and suggestions for experiments. They matter because they show the author's reasoning and evidence in compact form.

**Prerequisites:** payoff matrices; Nash equilibrium and minimax (basic); the idea of bargaining and threats; the book's coordination-game questionnaires. Terms: tacit (players choose without communicating), non-zero-sum (one side's gain is not simply the other's loss), coalition (a subgroup of players acting together), Gestalt (psychology of how people perceive forms).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- With more than two players, ambiguity about the "solution" may grow, and coordination theory may be most relevant of all; coalition formation is a possible meeting ground with sociology (p. 345).
- The "solution in the strict sense" of a tacit nonzero-sum game is, coordination-game theory suggests, to be understood partly, and sometimes largely, by its signalling qualities. It is only one of many possible determinants of a "rational solution" (pp. 345–346).
- What signals can be appreciated is partly an empirical question (p. 346); the notes repeatedly suggest experiments on perception and hinting (pp. 371–373).
- Questionnaire results show high rates of successful tacit coordination on salient answers (heads, the first item, round numbers, a river or bridge) (pp. 353–354).
- Commitment can come from surrendering options or shifting incentives. Many everyday devices (time locks, secret ballots, pooled strike funds) work this way (pp. 349–353, 368).
- Schelling separates "threat" from "warning", and suggests that the Luce–Raiffa view that only one player has a plausible threat may partly come from confining their discussion to 2×2 matrices (pp. 364–365).
- Labels, tradition and Gestalt-style "good form" can make an outcome focal. Pure conflict and pure common-interest games are only limiting cases (pp. 356, 359, 361–362).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Number of players (p. 345)
- The page opens with the end of a parenthesis: if both objective values and utility values were known and not proportionate to each other, the "signals" might lose some force and the problem of confusion or ambiguousness would be aggravated (p. 345).
- The appendix so far dealt with two-person games, apart from a third player in a non-tacit role (p. 345).
- The problem can be extended to any number of players. Rewards could depend on unanimous choice, on majority or plurality choice, or on successful coalitions, somewhat analogous to the questionnaire procedure of Chapter 3 (p. 345).
- Ambiguousness may then become more serious, and coordination may become even more relevant to the rationale of a "solution" (p. 345).
- Schelling thinks coordination theory is "probably" most relevant of all in games with more than two persons, those about forming coalitions. He says the study of signals and communication channels in coalition formation "appears to be a fruitful meeting ground" for game theory and sociology (p. 345).

### Conclusion (pp. 345–346)
- Coordination-game theory suggests that the "solution in the strict sense" of a tacit nonzero-sum game is to be understood partly, and in some cases largely, by its signalling qualities (p. 345).
- Other sources of signals may be present even in the purely mathematical formulation of the game. So the solution's own qualities are but one of many potential determinants of a "rational solution" (pp. 345–346).
- It is partly an empirical question, not solely a matter of a priori deduction, what signals can be appreciated (p. 346).
- *Intuition note:* A solution can work like a landmark, because players may pick it just because it stands out. Deduction alone may not settle which signals people can appreciate, so observation is also needed.

### The Notes {1}–{96} (pp. 348–373)
(p. 347 is a publisher's review request, not Schelling's text.) The chapter to which each note belongs is not shown in these pages. Notes are cited by number.

**Framing the field (pp. 348–349)**
- {1} "Strategy" comes from game theory. It covers games where each player's best course depends on what the others do. It is not the military usage (p. 348).
- {2}–{3} Theoretical work stimulated by military problems may not be openly published. Editors of international-affairs journals face a non-theoretical audience. Brodie and Greene are cited on the weak intellectual tradition in military strategy (p. 348).
- {4} Jessie Bernard expects the needed mathematics to emerge soon. **Schelling's view:** the deficiencies are not in the mathematics. The theory has suffered from social scientists treating it as solely a branch of mathematics (p. 348).
- {5} "Common interest" does not require similar value systems; the parties may just be "in the same boat". "Potential common interest" might be more descriptive (p. 349).
- {7} "Threat" has no necessarily hostile sense. Between friends, a threat of disagreement or reduced cooperation is a sanction supporting demands, as "no sale" enforces an offer (p. 349).

**Commitment devices (pp. 349–353)**
- {11} Schelling's "perhaps" ideal solution to bilateral monopoly: one party irrevocably sells a royalty contract to a third party for a lump sum. The royalties are set so that joint costs exceed joint revenue at every output except the originally joint-profit-maximising one. The other party sees the contract, appreciates the situation and accepts his true minimum profit. The hitch: the royalty buyer must not be available to the loser for renegotiation. Schelling imagines institutions that specialise in royalty purchases and have a reputation for never renegotiating; these might fill this role (pp. 349–350).
- {12} Similar logic: the driver first visibly and fully insured may win the right of way. Pooled strike funds reduce each union's visible incentive to avoid a strike. In both, interest is transferred to a third party and one's visible incentives shift (p. 350).
- {15} If limits on acceptable outcomes make the least favourable acceptable outcome substantially better than stalemate, one's aim may be to forestall the other's misguided commitment. A conservative initial position is then indicated if truth is more demonstrable than a false position. An enforceable penalty on falsehood can help, as a tax return does for income claims. Agents paid for reaching agreement, not for its terms, make even pure bilateral monopoly somewhat like this (pp. 350–351).
- {16} A deterrent threat need not promise more damage to the threatened than to the threatener. There is no such thing as a threat "too large" for deterrence, since a successful threat is not carried out. It is "too large" only if its size undermines credibility (p. 351).
- {17} Mutual defence treaties between strong and weak nations may be seen as a device for surrendering an embarrassing freedom of choice (p. 351).
- {19}–{20} Ways to preclude being threatened: numbered tickets that cannot be erased, bank time locks, the mandatory secret ballot, and a rule that grades cannot be changed. Another is to start an invasion with a small force that attaches "face" and prevents withdrawal. The racketeer cannot sell protection if he cannot find his customer at home, and the kidnapper cannot expect ransom if he cannot communicate with friends or relatives. Rotating or randomly pairing watchmen limits bribes and protects them from threats (pp. 351–352).
- {21} There is no simple, universal way for persons or nations to assume commitments. In most of the world the topic is mainly empirical and institutional: who can commit, how, and with what assurance of appreciation by the other side (p. 352).
- {22} The 1950 Economic Cooperation Administration plan to reward or penalise Marshall Plan countries lacked credible base figures, so it suffered from implausibility (p. 352).
- {23}–{28} Frequent amortisation and frequent exams may reflect an analogous principle. A tactic apparently induced de Gaulle's forces to leave a province in northern Italy in 1945. A threat may seem to be a promise if the pledge behind it is only one's reputation with the adversary, but the second party cannot unilaterally release the threatener from it. Hostages were once exchanged. Inability to make an enforceable promise may protect one from extortion. Keeping avenues open for minor issues may preserve the chance to negotiate the "ultimate issue" (pp. 352–353).
- *Intuition note:* In many of these examples, a party gains by limiting itself in a visible way. The other side may then see that its own options are narrowed.

**Coordination questionnaires (pp. 353–355)**
- {29} Sample results: problem 1, 36 chose "heads" and 6 "tails". Problem 2, the first three numbers got 37 of 41 votes. Problem 3, 24 of 41 chose the upper left corner. Problem 4, an absolute majority met at Grand Central Station (information booth), almost all at 12 noon. Problem 7, 12 of 41 chose $1,000,000, and only 3 entries were not powers of 10. Problem 8, 36 of 41 split fifty-fifty. Problem 9, 20 of 22 chose Robinson. In a tie variant, 16 of 18 chose Jones. In a map like Fig. 1, 7 of 8 met at the bridge (p. 353).
- {30} On a map with one house and many crossroads, the eleven who chose the house all met. The four who chose crossroads picked different ones and did not meet (pp. 353–354).
- {32} A second set of problems, with players in roles such as A, B, C or X, Y: problem 1, 16 of 22 A's and 15 of 22 B's chose heads. Had each tried to win $3, all would have scored zero. Problem 2, 9 of 12 A's, 10 of 12 B's and 14 of 16 C's coordinated on ABC. Problem 4, 36 of 40 chose $50. Problem 6, 14 of 22 X's and 14 of 23 Y's drew the boundary along the river. The other 15 produced 14 different lines, with 55 failures and 1 success among 8 × 7 pairs. Problem 7, 5 of 6 (income $150) and 7 of 10 (income $100) agreed on a 15–10 tax division. Problem 8, all 8 losers and 7 finders agreed on a $5 reward (p. 354).
- {31}, {33} Impotence or "weakness" can be a source of strength in bargaining (p. 354).
- {34}–{36} For UNRRA contributions, the winning formula was a straight 1 per cent of gross national product, "the simplest conceivable formula and the roundest conceivable number". Middle Eastern oil royalties converged quickly on 50–50. One can also confuse the issue by multiplying definitions and adding "noise" to drown a strong signal, as happened in the income-tax variant (pp. 354–355).
- *Intuition note:* In many of these data, a large share of people converge on the simplest or most salient answer. Those who did not use the river scattered: the 15 of them produced 14 different lines.

**The theory of the game (pp. 355–359)**
- {39}–{40} Marschak's team theory and communication-net experiments are cited. Kaysen stresses the interdependence of actions, and Luce–Raiffa describe the problem as one of uncertainty arising from ignorance of what others will do. Schelling notes that Luce and Raiffa dispose of coincident preferences as trivial and treat such players as a single individual (pp. 355–356).
- {41} The pure-conflict and pure-common-interest games are limiting cases. If random devices are allowed, with axes measured in utility units, their outcome points must lie on a downward-sloping line (conflict) or an upward-sloping line (common interest), so, for strictly pure games, the outcome points themselves must lie on a straight line. Pure games cannot admit side payments: a bribe point or a threatened-damage point lies off the line and creates a mixed game. Two simultaneous pure-conflict games provide room for negotiation unless the slopes are identical (p. 356).
- {42} Nonzero-sum games can be classed under partnership theory as properly as under conflict. "Theory of interdependent decision" might be a neutral term (pp. 356–357).
- {44}–{45} "Tipping" and the snicker that ignites laughter show explosively convergent expectations. A radio frequency for interstellar contact is a coordination problem: Cocconi and Morrison propose the 1420-megacycle hydrogen line, and small prime numbers of pulses as a signal. Schelling suggests experiments in which a cooperating "pattern-maker" generates signals for a pattern-seeking subject, with a third party who intercepts them (pp. 357–358).
- {46} Keynes's beauty-contest passage deals with the same problem, but his conception of the "solution" differs. For tacit play with multiple equilibria, the usual link between parametric behaviour and large numbers does not hold; tacit coordination stays nonparametric however many players (pp. 358–359).
- {47}–{48} Luce and Raiffa explicitly rule out labelling of players in cooperative games, and Nash in effect does so through his symmetry assumption; dealing only with normal form implicitly precludes labelling of strategies. Schelling calls this typical of demonstrations in his experiments that the postulate of independence of irrelevant alternatives cannot be credited in the tacit game (and should not be expected to hold in explicit bargaining), since unchosen outcomes can matter for coordination (p. 359).
- {49} Randomisation can help: with a 55 per cent majority among 100 voters, two of six candidates congenial to it, and the top three becoming the board, uncoordinated polling may concentrate too many votes on one majority choice, "leaving the minority two winning candidates with 22 votes apiece". If each majority member flips a coin to vote for one of his party's men, the likelihood of one's getting as few as 22 votes is only one chance in six. In the card-table game with a $2 premium for the player on the other's right, there is no equilibrium in pure strategies. Each decides his seat as in the pure common-interest game, then flips a coin to sit there or opposite. This is an equilibrium pair of mixed strategies worth an expected $2 apiece (pp. 359–360).
- {50} The price of coordinating information leads into Marschak's team theory. A prisoners'-dilemma variant has accomplices who must concert alibis. The payoff matrix is not in the text I was given (p. 360).
- {51} In an experimental nonzero-sum game, "winning" must involve one's absolute score, not the score relative to one's partner. So unless real rewards are given, the game has to be organised as a round robin among more than two players, which is why there are no two-person nonzero-sum parlour games (p. 361).

**Focal outcomes and perception (pp. 361–363)**
- {53} Picking exactly the fruit on one's own side of the line conveys a clearer "proposal" than haphazard picking (p. 361).
- {54} Schelling calls the atomic/non-atomic difference "purely conventional": "constructed of the pure fabric of expectations", resting on a ten-year tradition and on beliefs about others' beliefs. He adds that it is probably one that can be deliberately reinforced or blurred over time, as most traditions can, and says this point is developed at length in Appendix A (pp. 361–362).
- {55} Gestalt experimental evidence (Koffka) gives "some support and clarification" to the idea of "intrinsic magnetism or focusing quality": perception tends toward regular, symmetrical, simple forms. If individual perception follows such constraints, mutual perception in converging expectations must depend on similar restraints at least as rigorous. And since the nonzero-sum game requires some ultimate joint "organization of form", a normative theory of strategy (not just a descriptive psychology) must take them into account (p. 362).
- {56} A football observation quoted by Koffka, which Schelling says "may be hard to believe but is certainly to the point", holds that the goalkeeper is a prominent point that attracts the eyes of the opposing kickers (pp. 362–363).
- {57} When each player values the other's welfare and tries to guess the other's wish, there is no way to test the truth. Overtly recognising one's value system can itself change values (p. 363).

**Threats, promises, commitments (pp. 363–371)**
- {61} In the real-estate example, buyer B might raise the limit on what he can extract from C if he can find some means to bind himself to pay 20 and keep or destroy the house unless he gets a large fraction of 20 − P. In effect B changes his "true" top price (pp. 363–364).
- {63} Schelling notes that "threat" is often also used for merely pointing out that one would have an incentive to act; he suggests calling this a "warning" rather than a "threat". The two are analytically similar in the problem of conveying credible information. If commitment precedes conveying the threat, the commitment changes the true incentives and the later message is a warning (p. 364).
- {64} Schelling discusses Luce–Raiffa's view that only one player has a "plausible" threat (Kaplan also holds that weighing threat positions involves interpersonal comparison of utilities). A threat is "essentially a credible declaration of a conditional choice for second move". It is profitable only if it yields more than either first move or second move alone, and if one can make the other move first, either actually or by promise. In a 2×2 matrix this order cannot hold for both players, which may partly explain why Luce–Raiffa find only one plausible threat. Nash's "threat", assumed by Luce–Raiffa, differs from this: the threatener demands not a particular outcome but some outcome in the efficient range, and shifts the zero point for no agreement, because the implicit legal structure apparently honours no irrevocable commitments (pp. 364–365).
- {67} If Column cannot force Row to move first mechanically, he may do so legally by threatening to choose I unless Row promises ii. In Fig. 4 this needs penalties of at least 1 on a promise. For a promise extracted by a threat, it is an advantage to the threatener to be able to invoke a penalty, and a disadvantage to the victim to be able to invoke a penalty on his own breach, that is, to be able to comply (p. 366).
- {69}–{71} A story in which an eavesdropper should have insisted on sharing guilt. A grant of immunity removes a witness's protection against self-incrimination. Hostages seem to be as pertinent to threats as to promises: American divisions in Europe can probably be viewed as hostages, and if not, their wives and children can (pp. 366–367).
- {74}–{76} Dulles and Acheson are cited for a defence that "passes the decision" to the enemy. A rail-strike sit-in on the tracks has an engineer's countertactic: he climbs down from the moving train and rejoins it. The strikers' counter is to lock themselves to the tracks and throw away the key, if they inform him before he gives up control (p. 367).
- {80} Goffman: hinted communication is deniable communication, and "ratification" of overhearing may determine obligations to respond (p. 368).
- {81}–{82} Ellsberg independently reached the same formulation of a threat or commitment: a selective reduction of some of one's own payoffs in the matrix. A more general "reaction function" is illustrated: if Row can attach adequate penalties to his own selection of any cells other than the starred ones, he secures the most favourable cell among those that leave Column no lower than his minimax value. The matrix is not in the text layer given (pp. 368–369).
- {84} Iterated discarding of dominated rows and columns can leave a different number of identical-payoff cells depending on the order. The payoffs are the same either way. Communication costs or risks of error can make a richer problem with different conclusions (pp. 369–370).
- {85} Casting a game into supergame matrix form is generally not feasible. A 3×3 game with successive commitment opportunities has more than a googol of columns (p. 370).
- {86} A promise to abstain from a choice that would damage the other player may not be welcomed by him, since it may assure the promiser that the other will make that choice, so that the promiser can make a prior choice to his disadvantage. In the example, assuming Row has first move, if he can promise 2 to Column in outcome i,II, he gets 8 and Column gets 3. Without the promise, the outcome is ii,I with payoffs of 1 (Row) and 10 (Column). So Row gains 7 at Column's expense, and Column prefers that Row be unable to commit (pp. 370–371).
- *Intuition note:* A threat is a credible declaration of a conditional choice for second move. It pays only if it yields more than a plain first move (an unconditional commitment) or a plain second move, and if one can make the other move first. A promise can hurt its recipient by letting the promiser plan around it.

**Experiments and perception (pp. 371–373)**
- {89} Von Neumann and Morgenstern put the danger of having one's strategy found out into "an absolutely central position" (p. 371).
- {90} Flood's 100-play tacit game forces players to communicate through their own plays. Whether a proposal is understood evidently depends on a shared sense of pattern, much like the Gestalt tasks. How well hints work is an empirical question, probably amenable to experimental study (pp. 371–372).
- {91}–{92} Schelling does not suggest that limited war can be simulated in the laboratory. Such experiments would be "basic research". Norms can form in practice, for example the 1957 disarmament notion of pie-shaped inspection zones with apex at the North Pole (p. 372).
- {93} Hostility may belong to the "communication structure" if it limits perception of the other's meaning. Deutsch reports that players who had played uncooperatively against a cooperative partner, when their expectation was not confirmed, tended to interpret the other's cooperative choice as indifference or misunderstanding, reinforcing negative sentiment (pp. 372–373).
- {95}–{96} Kissinger on slowing the tempo of limited war. Bavelas's five-player squares experiment, in preliminary runs, revealed very great binding forces against restructuring after a deceptive "success", and that a solution is improbable under much communication restriction (p. 373).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two players must each pick one of three boxes without talking. Two boxes are white (W1, W2) and one is black (B). They win $1 each only if they pick the same box.
- Pure random picking: chance both choose the same box = 3 × (1/3 × 1/3) = 3/9 = 1/3, so expected win = $0.33 each.
- If both treat the unique black box as the obvious pick: chance = 1 × 1 = 1, so expected win = $1 each.
- Nothing in the payoff matrix distinguishes the boxes. The difference comes only from the signal "black is unique", the kind of signalling quality that the appendix says may help determine a "rational solution" (pp. 345–346).

## Concepts
- [[focal-point]] — an outcome made salient by uniqueness, simplicity or convention, so that players converge on it; Schelling speaks of "focusing quality" (p. 362) and of "signaling qualities" (p. 345)
- [[credible-commitment]] — limiting one's own options or payoffs so that a stated course of action is believable (pp. 349–353, 368)
- [[threat-vs-warning]] (new) — a threat is "essentially a credible declaration of a conditional choice for second move", the incentive being created by the act of commitment. A warning only points out that one would have an incentive to act anyway (p. 364)
- [[pure-conflict-and-common-interest-games]] (new) — limiting cases of nonzero-sum games; with chance devices their outcome points lie on straight lines of negative or positive slope (p. 356)
- [[mixed-strategy]] — randomising over choices; here it reduces conflict, as in the card-table game (pp. 359–360)
- [[iterated-elimination-of-dominated-strategies]] — discarding dominated rows and columns; Schelling notes the order can affect which cells remain (pp. 369–370)
- [[mutual-vs-common-knowledge]] — unspoken mutual expectations underlie tacit coordination; the text speaks of "convergent expectations" and the beauty-contest "degrees" (pp. 357–359)
- [[independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives]] (new) — Schelling says unchosen outcomes can matter to coordination, so the postulate cannot be credited in the tacit game (p. 359)

## Questions this raises
- If most of what people can appreciate is empirical, how would one design an experiment to rank signals by strength?
- With many players, does the number of salient focal outcomes grow or shrink, and does coalition formation help or hurt?
- Under what payoff conditions does a second-move threat beat both a first-move commitment and a plain second move?
- Does the order-dependence of iterated discarding matter when the final payoffs are the same?
