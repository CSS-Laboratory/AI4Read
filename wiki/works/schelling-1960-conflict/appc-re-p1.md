# Appendix C (part 1): Why a "strict-sense solution" works: it is a signal

## L0 — One line
Part of what makes a Luce–Raiffa "solution in the strict sense" appealing to rational players is that it gives them a tacit signal for coordinating their choices, and other clues that do this may do the same job.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Luce and Raiffa define a "solution in the strict sense" for non-cooperative games, and say its second condition "prohibits confusion" when there are several equilibria. Schelling asks what this means for rational players. He notes that this confusion is the problem at the heart of the coordination game of Chapter 3. He suggests that an important property of such a solution is a signaling power, a means of tacit (wordless) communication. Where such a signal is missing, players may use other cues: payoff differences, the pattern of other cells, labels, or conventions. These cues may work even in games the definition excludes, and when interests partly conflict. A mediator who can send messages might also control or influence such games. This matters because it ties solution concepts to the coordination reasoning of Chapter 3.

**Prerequisites:** payoff matrices; equilibrium pair (each strategy is a best reply to the other); equivalent equilibria (equal payoffs for each player) and interchangeable equilibria (all pairs formed from the corresponding strategies are also equilibria); "tacit" = without communication; the coordination game of Schelling's Chapter 3.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Luce–Raiffa's "strict sense" condition (equivalent and interchangeable equilibria) is meant to prevent confusion. Schelling treats confusion as the core problem of coordination games (p. 334).
- In Fig. 27, which lacks a strict-sense solution, the players are not in conflict. They are only confused, and with no clue they may expect 1.5 each (p. 335).
- A small payoff difference such as 9 vs 10 is worth little as money. It is worth a lot as a signal for picking one equilibrium over another (p. 336).
- The clue need not be in the payoffs. Underlining, arrows, labels or a footnote can serve, and a completely clueless game would presumably need scrambled labels and perfectly symmetric payoffs (p. 337).
- Schelling's thesis: a strict-sense solution has a "signaling power", a means of tacit communication. This is not its only property, but it may be an important part of the rationale for choosing it (pp. 337–338).
- Uniqueness itself can be the clue. In Fig. 30, if no better means of coordinating can be found, the least-liked equilibrium (iii, III) may be the "solution" because it is unique (p. 338).
- In Fig. 33 the off-diagonal cells seem to "point toward" (ii, II). They guide coordination and are not payoffs anyone expects to receive (pp. 340–341).
- With conflicting interests, coordination needs a discriminatory clue. A symmetric, "fair" clue gives back confusion (pp. 342–343).
- These games might be substantially subject to the control or influence of a mediator who can send messages (pp. 343–344).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: the aim (p. 333).** Schelling says the pure common-interest (coordination) game may clarify the reasoning behind solution concepts for non-cooperative games. By "reasoning" he means the reasoning attributed to the rational players the concepts are meant to appeal to (p. 333).

**Definitions (pp. 333–334).**
- Figs. 25 and 26 are said to have a solution in the strict sense. In Fig. 26 either the second or the third strategy for each player forms the solution (p. 333).
- Luce–Raiffa definition: a non-cooperative game has a strict-sense solution if (1) there is an equilibrium pair among the jointly admissible strategy pairs, and (2) all jointly admissible equilibrium pairs are both interchangeable and equivalent (p. 333).
- *Equilibrium pair*: each strategy is the best (or as good as any) that can be coupled with the other's (p. 334).
- *Jointly admissible pair*: not jointly dominated, i.e. there is no other cell with payoffs that are both better (p. 334).
- *Equivalent* pairs: they give each player equal payoffs. *Interchangeable* pairs: all pairs formed from the corresponding strategies are also equilibrium points (p. 334).
- In Fig. 26 the pairs (ii, II), (iii, III), (ii, III), (iii, II) are equivalent, interchangeable, jointly admissible equilibria (p. 334).
- Schelling adds in a parenthesis that such pairs are equivalent and interchangeable only if all pairs formed from the corresponding strategies are equivalent (p. 334).
- Luce–Raiffa add that condition 2 "prohibits confusion" in the case of non-unique jointly admissible equilibrium pairs (italics are Schelling's). He takes this as his starting point (p. 334).

**Confusion without conflict (pp. 335–336).**
- Fig. 27 has no strict-sense solution. Its second and third strategies are not interchangeable and equivalent. The players' interests do not differ, so there is "simply cause for confusion". Failure to coordinate gives zero. With no clue they may be supposed to have a 50–50 chance of getting 3 each, an expected value of 1.5 (p. 335).
- Why is (ii, II) indicated in Fig. 25, not (i, I)? Partly because its payoff is better. Fig. 28 has the same preference ordering but different strengths of preference: here the point is to get 9 or 10 rather than zero. The two equilibrium pairs are "nearly equivalent but not interchangeable". The players' main interest is to avoid confusion (pp. 335–336).
- In a very abstract matrix there is little to guide the players except the numbers. Of the rules "pick the lesser pair" or "pick the greater", the second is probably more plausible. The extra dollar is worth little as money but much as a signaling device. The 9/10 difference is what makes coordination possible (p. 336).
- Fig. 29: if the players find no rule, their expected value is presumably 5 each (p. 336).

*Intuition note:* When two equilibria are almost equally good, the players mainly want to pick the *same* one. A tiny difference between them can decide which, even though nobody cares about that difference for its own sake.

**Parenthesis on clueless games (p. 337).**
- Fig. 29 as printed may cause no difficulty, as Chapter 3's empirical results imply. A printed matrix allows left–right, upper–lower, first–last–middle distinctions (p. 337).
- For a "clueless" game the strategies must occur with labels such that rational players cannot order them unambiguously. A completely foolproof clueless game would presumably have to have scrambled labels and a perfectly symmetrical set of payoffs. A game with infinitely many strategies apparently has no "pure" form, because a generating formula is likely to offer some ordering (p. 337).
- The situation may not be very different if (ii, II) is underlined, printed in bold face, marked by arrows, or given a footnote suggesting it. If the payoffs give no signal, players can look elsewhere, and strategy labels or connotations may supply a basis for ordering (p. 337).

**The thesis (pp. 337–338).**
- A strict-sense solution has signaling power, a means of tacit communication for tacit cooperation when failure to coordinate is serious. This is not the only significant property, but may be an important part of the rationale (pp. 337–338).
- Alternatively, one could model communication with costs and ask whether it is worth the cost. The clues are then "free communication", and what free communication a rational player can find is an empirical question (p. 338).
- By analogy, esthetic or syntactic constraints on a language reduce garbles in a message. Esthetic, dramaturgical, casuistic or geometric constraints can reduce ambiguity in tacit concerted choice (p. 338).

*Intuition note:* The solution concept is read as a communication device. The players cannot talk, so some clue, in the payoffs or outside them, may have to do the work of a message.

**Uniqueness as a clue (pp. 338–340).**
- Fig. 30: assume the strategies cannot be ordered (no square matrix, no labels, or labels scrambled separately for each player). If no better means exists, the "solution" may be (iii, III) with 9 each. It is the least desired equilibrium but it is unique, while the others offer confusion (p. 338).
- On the payoff structure alone, it is hard to see that this solution is much less, if at all less, compelling than the one in Fig. 31. Fig. 31 meets the Luce–Raiffa definition, while Fig. 30's solution contradicts it (p. 339).

**Figs. 32–33 (pp. 340–341).**
- Neither game has a strict-sense solution, yet there seems to be an argument for (ii, II) in Fig. 33 (p. 340).
- *Insurance argument:* the row chooser gets nothing if he wrongly picks the upper row and 5 if he wrongly picks the lower row ("wrong" = failing to meet the partner for 10). He may pick the lower row because he then gets at least 5 if he misses 10, and his chance of 10 is no worse. Schelling says this may be all "rationality" requires, but it might be more perceptive to reason as follows (p. 340).
- *The perceptive reasoning (a hypothetical player's thought, p. 340):* comparing only (i, I) and (ii, II), we cannot concert. The only other place to look is the cells (ii, I) and (i, II). They seem to "point toward" (ii, II) and give a reason or an excuse for treating it as more prominent. With no competing rule, use this one.
- Schelling's comment: the players do not choose second strategies because 5 beats 0, and they have no serious expectation of getting 5. They use the configuration of fives and zeros as a clue. Each recognizes that the other recognizes that it is useful to note where the fives are, but only as a step in coordinating intentions (p. 341).
- This works in principle like printed arrows pointing to the lower-right corner: arrows with no logical role or authority other than the "power of suggestion" and hence the ability to coordinate expectations (p. 341).

*Intuition note:* Numbers in cells nobody expects to reach can still matter. They act as pointers that both players can see and know the other sees.

**Conflicting interest (pp. 341–343).**
- Figs. 34 and 35 each have two jointly admissible equilibrium points but no strict-sense solution, because the pairs are neither equivalent nor interchangeable (p. 341).
- Fig. 34 appears "insoluble" in abstract form (no labels): at best a random chance of either efficient outcome (pp. 341–342).
- Fig. 35 may not be. Each player prefers his second-best equilibrium to failing to coordinate, so they share an interest in finding a clue. Schelling asks why not take the clue in the other cells, which seems to point toward (ii, II). This is not best for one player, but "beggars cannot be choosers" (p. 342).
- Fairness cannot help. If every clue is equally plausible reversed (feathers instead of arrowheads), the result is confusion. Only a discriminatory clue can point to a concerted choice (pp. 342–343).
- *Desert intersection example:* two drivers on perpendicular roads, with no legal system, dislike, distrust, and no moral obligation. The one approaching on the other's left may still slow down, and the other may anticipate it. The priority convention has no legal or moral force but is expedient. The disadvantaged driver may yield, recognizing that he should be grateful for an arbiter and that he is "trapped" by the other's acceptance of the signal and the other's expectation that both will comply (p. 343).
- By the Chapter 3 reasoning, Fig. 34 may be soluble when presented in a particular matrix form, or with winning pairs labeled "heads" and "tails" and the like (p. 343).

**Manipulation by a third party (pp. 343–344).**
- All these coordination games, with conflicting or coinciding preferences, might be substantially subject to a mediator's control or influence. This holds if the mediator can send messages to the two tacit players (pp. 343–344).
- A benevolent mediator makes the pure common-interest game trivially easy. In Fig. 34 a mediator has "an arbitrary power of justice". In Fig. 36 a mediator with a payoff of his own (shown in parentheses) is in a strong third-player position. His instructions are only suggestive, not authoritative (p. 344).

**Interpretation of the payoffs (p. 344).** The reasoning does not depend on whether payoffs are objectively measurable (money, goods) or game-theoretic "utilities". The sentence continues on the next page, which is outside this part (p. 344).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two players choose A or B at the same time and cannot talk. Payoffs are (row, column).
- Game X: (A,A) = (10,10), (B,B) = (9,9), and (A,B) = (B,A) = (0,0). Both diagonals are equilibria, but they are not equivalent.
- If a player thinks the other picks A, A is best (10 > 0). If the other picks B, B is best (9 > 0). So a player who has no clue picks each with probability ½, and the chance of a match is ½·½ + ½·½ = ½. Expected payoff = ½·(½·10 + ½·9) + ½·0 = 4.75.
- But "10 is bigger than 9" is a clue that both can see. If each expects the other to use it, both pick A and get 10. The extra 1 is worth little as money, but it lets them coordinate. This mirrors Fig. 28 (p. 336).
- Change the payoffs so (A,A) = (9,9) and (B,B) = (9,9). Now no payoff clue exists. Only labels or position could help, as in the "clueless" game (p. 337).

## Concepts
- [[focal-point]] — a clue that makes one equilibrium stand out for tacit coordination; here "clue", "signal" or "power of suggestion" (p. 337)
- [[nash-equilibrium]] — "equilibrium pair": each strategy is a best reply to the other (p. 334)
- [[best-response]] — the player's best strategy (or as good as any other) that can be coupled with the other's (p. 334)
- [[strict-sense-solution]] (new) — Luce–Raiffa: an equilibrium exists among jointly admissible pairs, and all such pairs are equivalent and interchangeable (p. 333)
- [[jointly-admissible-pair]] (new) — a strategy pair not jointly dominated by another pair's payoffs (p. 334)
- [[equivalent-interchangeable-equilibria]] (new) — equal payoffs for each player; all mixed-up pairs are also equilibria (p. 334)
- [[tacit-communication]] (new) — signals from payoffs, labels or conventions, without explicit messages (pp. 337–338)
- [[convention]] — a priority rule without legal or moral force that still coordinates, e.g. a right-of-way rule (p. 343)
- [[mediator]] (new) — a third party who sends non-binding suggestions to the players (p. 344)

## Canonical instances
- [[coordination-game-with-unequal-payoffs]] — Figs. 25/28: a small payoff difference works as a coordinating signal (p. 336)
- [[pointing-cells]] — Fig. 33: off-diagonal fives and zeros "point toward" (ii, II) (pp. 340–341)
- [[desert-intersection]] — an unregulated crossing, where a yielding convention coordinates despite distrust (p. 343)
- [[heads-tails-labels]] — labeled strategies make Fig. 34 potentially soluble (p. 343)
- [[arrows-footnote-signal]] — underlining, bold print or a footnote as a coordinating clue (p. 337)

## Questions this raises
- If any salient clue can coordinate, how does one decide which clue players will find salient without testing real people?
- Is a strict-sense solution only helpful, or is it also needed? Figs. 30 and 33 suggest not needed (pp. 339–341).
- In the conflict games, when does a "discriminatory" clue stay stable? What stops the disadvantaged player from rejecting it (p. 343)?
- Could a mediator who gets a payoff of his own abuse his power in Fig. 36 (p. 344)?
