# Bargaining, Communication, and Limited War

## L0 — One line
When people cannot communicate, they can still coordinate on an outcome that stands out as prominent, unique or simple, and Schelling suggests the same pull on expectations may also help explain explicit bargaining and where to look for limits on war.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Limited war needs agreed limits, yet adversaries often cannot or will not negotiate. Schelling asks how agreement can arise without communication (tacit bargaining). His answer: players coordinate on a focal point, an outcome that stands out as prominent, unique or simple, because each expects the other to see it too. The outcome may be arbitrary or unfair, and the disadvantaged party may still accept it. He suggests the same mechanism may also work in explicit bargaining, where expectations must still converge. His probable conclusion is that tacit limits on war need terms that are qualitatively distinguishable, not matters of degree ("no gas", a river). The analysis helps show where to look for terms, not how likely agreement is.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a game with payoffs; expected value; no further maths is needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Tacit coordination means converging on one option through infinitely reflexive expectations (each expects the other to expect, and so on). Success is not certain, but it is helped by finding a clue both read the same way (pp. 71–72, 75, 91).
- A prime characteristic of most clues is prominence, and uniqueness is also essential. Imagination may matter as much as logic (pp. 75–76).
- With conflicting interests, the party who loses by the focal point often accepts it, because only options that can coordinate expectations are "available" (p. 78).
- Being unable to communicate, or able only to send, can be an advantage (pp. 77–78).
- The tax, finder's-reward and letter-R problems show that small cues such as income figures, a mediator's offer or a first proposal can shift the focal point. In one experiment, clutter apparently drowned a refined cue (pp. 83–86).
- For explicit bargaining, Schelling proposes that a final outcome must be a point where neither side expects the other to retreat, and that focal points may give a reason to dig in. He suggests bargaining skill may shift to shaping the setting (pp. 89–92).
- For limited war, tacit limits probably must be qualitatively distinguishable ("no gas", rivers, water, the atomic/non-atomic line). The world of limits is "lumpy" (pp. 97–100).
- Prior arrangements (open channels, mediators) may help. Even unilateral signals may coordinate (pp. 100–103).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 70–71)
- Limited war and stable maneuvers need limits, and limits need agreement or mutual recognition. Agreement is hard because negotiation is inhibited, communication is difficult, one side may see an advantage in avoiding agreement on limits, and a show of willingness may look like eagerness (p. 70).
- **Tacit bargaining** means bargaining where communication is incomplete or impossible. Examples are jurisdictional maneuvers, a traffic jam and a silent neighbor (p. 70).
- The chapter starts with pure common interest, then conflict, and suggests the principles also help explain explicit bargaining (pp. 70–71).

### Tacit coordination (common interests) (pp. 71–76)
- **Department store.** A couple who lose each other will probably meet. Each thinks of an obvious place and expects the other to expect it, ad infinitum. They must "coordinate predictions" and identify one course on which expectations converge (p. 71).
- **Parachutist map (Fig. 7).** Two people land unseen and want to meet. Can the map suggest one spot both trust the other will read the same way? (p. 72).
- **Nine puzzles** (heads/tails, numbers, sixteen squares, New York meeting place and hour, a positive number, an amount of money, a $100 split into piles A and B, a second ballot) were tried on an unscientific sample. Many people can coordinate. Any solution is arbitrary in that it is "correct" if enough people think so (pp. 73–74). The large plurality for the number 1 in problem 6 seems to rest on logic (p. 76). Scores are in a footnote not given in these pages.
- **Conclusion:** most situations give a focal point (the author's term; a "key" that becomes the key by being mutually recognized). Finding it may depend on analogy, precedent, symmetry, geometry or who the parties are. Success is not guaranteed but is far likelier than random chance (p. 75).
- **Prominence and uniqueness.** Prominence depends on time, place and persons: ordinary people lost on a circular area may naturally go to the center, but only one versed in mathematics would "naturally" expect to meet at the center of gravity of an irregularly shaped area (p. 75). A map with many houses and one crossroads sends people to the crossroads, and the reverse sends them to the house. Uniqueness may matter partly because it avoids ambiguity: with three equal houses the chance of meeting is one in three (pp. 75–76).
- Poets may do better than logicians, since the game is more like "puns and anagrams" than chess. Logic helps, but usually not until imagination has selected a clue (p. 76).

*Intuition note:* In pure coordination you are not guessing what the other will do. You are guessing what both of you will see as the obvious choice. Anything that makes one option unique and conspicuous helps.

### Tacit bargaining (divergent interests) (pp. 76–86)
- Suppose the parachutists dislike walking. With no communication, if one spot is the obvious place, the winner is simply whoever is closer to it. The one farthest from it cannot withhold acquiescence or counter-propose, so the map's "offer", if it provides one, is the only one on the table. The conflict is "ignored" as a by-product of the need to coordinate (pp. 76–77).
- Both may lose compared with what talking could have achieved. One may gain while the other loses more than the first gains (p. 77).
- **Inability to communicate can pay.** When A knew where B was but B did not know A's location, most B-respondents sat tight and nearly all A-respondents walked to B. Being able to send but not receive may be better still: no counteroffer can be heard (pp. 77–78).
- In the conflicting-interest games the party disfavored by the focal point often accepts it because the other expects him to. Choices that cannot coordinate expectations are not really "available" without communication. Neither rival gains by outsmarting the other (p. 78).
- **Eight problems** (pp. 78–82):
  1. Heads/tails: both heads pays A $3 and B $2; both tails pays A $2 and B $3; a mismatch pays nothing. Random choice gives a 50-50 chance of matching and $1.25 expected value apiece (p. 79).
  2. Three players each write an order of A, B, C. Prizes of $3, $2 and $1 go by position, only if all three orders match (p. 79).
  3. One sheet carries an "X", the other is blank. Exactly one X pays the holder of the X $3 and the holder of the blank $2 (p. 79).
  4. Split $100 by written claims. If the claims sum to at most $100 each gets his claim, otherwise nothing (p. 80).
  5. Choose one of K, G, W, L, R with different prize schedules. One party proposes "R" (in the alternate version, the other party does), then communication is cut off (p. 80).
  6. Two commanders draw lines on a map. Overlap means a clash, and a gap means an unstable situation and a clash. Only identical or nearly touching lines avoid one (p. 81).
  7. A has income $100 and B $150. They write shares of a $25 tax. If the shares total less than $25, each pays the full $25 and the collectors keep the surplus (p. 81).
  8. A loses $16 and B finds it. They write claims totaling at most $16, or the house confiscates the money. A mediator suggests a 2-1 split, "rounded off to $11 and $5", to both, and says he told the other the same (p. 82).
- **Results and clues** (pp. 82–86). Participants solve these in a substantial proportion of cases, far better than chance, even when disadvantaged (pp. 82–83). The clues are diverse:
  - Heads apparently beats tails by some conventional priority, and the original X apparently beats the blank because the status quo is more obvious than change (p. 83).
  - R wins because nothing contradicts the first offer. Rivers beat roads on the map because roads are many and the river is unique (p. 83).
  - Tax problem: it is logically the same as problem 4, with $25 instead of $100. Yet the income figures shift the focal point to a 10-15 split rather than 12.5-12.5 (pp. 83–84). The author argues proportionality wins because no particular graduated scale is obvious enough to go without saying. When extra data on family size and spending were added, the preponderant reply was a 50-50 split. The refined signal was drowned by "noise" (p. 84).
  - Finder's problem: it is logically the same as problem 4, but finder and loser lack a compelling equality. The mediator's suggestion is the only other visible signal, and the rounding to $11 and $5 was universally accepted (p. 84).
- Outcomes are determined by something fairly arbitrary and are not particularly "fair". Even 50-50 relies on mathematical purity, and if it is "fair", it is so only because we lack data to judge unfairness (pp. 84–85).
- Problem 5: the strongest argument for R is the rhetorical question "If not R, what then?" The implied answer is that no other letter is obvious enough. Schelling asks whether the first offerer would have any reason to change his choice if the prize schedules were reversed, and says that if all prizes were equal, players would still rally to R (pp. 85–86). A wall sign "choose R" would work the same, like a store sign pointing to the information booth (pp. 85–86).
- If your rival knows your schedule and you do not know his, your own best letter is the indicated choice. It puts on him the responsibility of choosing in your favor, which was the preponderant result. This parallels the case where only one parachutist knows the other's location (p. 86).

*Intuition note:* In these games the outcome is decided by something fairly arbitrary that both can see, not by fairness or cleverness. Trying to outsmart the other does not pay, because each loses unless he does what the other expects.

### Explicit bargaining (pp. 86–96)
- Coordination does not obviously apply when speech is possible. Yet the author finds evidence that something like it operates there (p. 87).
- **Evidence** (pp. 87–89):
  - Outcomes land on round numbers, and "split the difference" often closes a deal.
  - Long negotiations over complicated formulas often converge on equal shares, shares proportional to a common magnitude, or shares from a logically irrelevant earlier deal.
  - Precedent such as a strike or debt settlement sets a "pattern", and the bargaining loses heart.
  - Mediators and fact-finding reports can precipitate agreement through a specific suggestion, not through the facts.
  - Status quo ante, natural boundaries and latitude lines act as focal points.
- **Conclusion** (pp. 88–90): the focus may give bargaining power to a side instead of just reflecting it. It often seems that a cynic could have predicted the outcome from the "obvious" focus, without much regard to the merits, the arguments or the pressures (p. 89). The result may be not "fair" or "in balance" but "just plain 'conspicuous'" (p. 89). Skill shifts to formulating the problem: terms of reference ("dues" or "taxes"), which data are prepared, which precedents are recalled and which issues share an agenda. Much skill is applied before formal talks begin (pp. 89–90).
- **Proposed mechanism** (pp. 90–92):
  - Typically a range of outcomes exists where each party would rather concede than fail. Any outcome is one that either party could have improved by insisting, yet he may have no basis for insisting, since the other knows or suspects he would rather concede. In the proposition Schelling offers, the final outcome must be a point from which neither expects the other to retreat, and the main ingredient of that expectation is what each thinks the other expects of him, and so on: infinitely reflexive expectations (pp. 90–91).
  - Convergence may be brought by the intrinsic magnetism of outcomes with prominence, uniqueness, simplicity, precedent or a qualitative rationale. Along a continuum there is no reason to dig in, while a focal point can defend itself with "If not here, where?" (p. 91).
  - A concession needs a recognizable stopping place. Retreating from 60 to 50 per cent lets one get his heels in. Retreating to 49 per cent signals sliding. Troops retreating to the river expect to be expected to stand there, so there is stability at the river and "perhaps nowhere else" (pp. 91–92).
- **Role of the tacit analogy** (pp. 92–94): without it the proposition would be vague and mystical. The tacit games show that coordination of expectations is a real possibility, and in some contexts a remarkably reliable one. They show it can be perceived by both parties without communication, so the signal should still be perceptible, though weaker, when talking is allowed. The two lines of evidence reinforce each other (pp. 92–93). The $100 split may seem 50-50 for many reasons, such as fairness, balance of power or self-evident inevitability, and the tacit case gives evidence for the last reading (p. 93).
- Even "fairness" and public opinion may work partly by filling the vacuum of indeterminacy and coordinating expectations. The text calls the mediator "in problem 7" a close analogy (p. 94). Even if moral force really constrains, one should ask where the public's own opinion comes from (p. 95).
- If this line of reasoning is valid, tacit and explicit bargaining are not thoroughly separate: gradations of communication all show some dependence on the need to coordinate expectations. Focal points need not be the same when speech is allowed. Much "explicit" bargaining also involves maneuver and indirect signals (p. 95).
- **Wider examples** (offered with "perhaps" and "may") (p. 96): the band wagon at conventions, constitutional legitimacy, an old gang leader, "rallying points", price leadership and non-price competition, and spontaneous revolt, which needs a signal everyone trusts everyone else reads.

### Tacit negotiation and limited war (pp. 96–100)
- The analysis gives no new sense of probability. Korea and gas not being used in World War II persuade more than the argument. It helps show where to look for terms (p. 97).
- **Two conclusions** (which the author calls "probably" the important ones) (p. 97): (1) tacit or haphazardly negotiated agreements need terms qualitatively distinguishable from alternatives, not a matter of degree. (2) When communication is incomplete, participants must be ready to allow the situation itself to exercise substantial constraint over the outcome. A solution that discriminates or imposes "unnecessary" nuisance on both may be the only coordinating one.
- **Gas** (pp. 97–98): "some gas" raises questions of how much, where and when, while "no gas" is simple. Other limits were conceivable and perhaps more impartial. Failure to coordinate on the first try might spoil acquiescence in any limits.
- **Korea** (p. 98): water and a river boundary must have helped. The thirty-eighth parallel seems to have been a powerful focus. The "waist" was a strong alternative because advancing or retreating to it would not signal a determination to go further.
- **Formosan Straits** (pp. 98–99): not only because water favors defenders. An island is an integral unit and water a conspicuous boundary. Sacrificing any part of the island, or keeping any mainland, would be unstable.
- **Weapons and Indochina** (p. 99): the atomic/non-atomic distinction limited weapons, while limits on size or targets would have been harder. Aid to the French was limited to material, not people. One can signal "no ground forces", but not easily "some forces, up to a limit".
- **Retaliation** (pp. 99–100): local aggression defines a place. Retaliating away from the provoking locality may be a "declaration of independence" that hinders stable expectations.
- **Summary** (p. 100): limiting war is a "lumpy, discrete world", better at qualitative than quantitative differences, and it forces acceptance of some dictation from the elements. The author suggests the same holds for restrained competition in every field.

*Intuition note:* A limit seems to be stable at a clear line, and perhaps nowhere else, because a slightly shifted line gives no reason to stop there. That is why "none" and natural boundaries serve better than "a bit".

### Prior arrangements (pp. 100–103)
- Tacit bargaining may fail, or yield an unfavorable result. So prepare beforehand (pp. 100–101).
- Keep communication channels open: who sends and receives, on whose authority, with what backups. A twelve-hour confusion in a nuclear crisis might spoil chances of limits (p. 101). Consider mediators or referees, who need a precedent, tradition or sign of welcome (p. 101).
- An adversary may refuse any preparation. One side may have a tactical interest in keeping war unrestrained, since willingness to retaliate depends on how likely it is that retaliation could stay limited. If one side's refusal to engage deters the other's resolve to act, it might risk forgoing limits to reduce that threat. The parachutist analogy: one may stay silent so the other must "ride quietly" (pp. 101–102).
- **Unilateral signals** can coordinate. If the partner heard it, the proposal of R is the only extant proposal and, being unchallenged, may coordinate by default. Even a denial might simply prove awareness, as long as no rival claim created ambiguity. A remark like "I'd head for the highest hill" may later coordinate the parachutists, though the hearer wanted to object. When both desperately need a signal, even a poor, discriminatory one may command recognition (pp. 102–103).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two people must each pick one of 4 doors in a corridor, without talking. Both win $10 only if they pick the same door. Three doors are identical grey and one is red.
- Random uniform picks: chance of a match = 4 × (1/4 × 1/4) = 1/4. Expected win = 0.25 × $10 = $2.50.
- Picking among the grey doors only: chance = 3 × (1/3 × 1/3) = 1/3 (if both do this). Expected win ≈ $3.33. Each grey door gives only a one-in-three chance, as with Schelling's three houses (p. 76).
- Both pick red: chance = 1. Expected win = $10.
- Add conflict: matching on red pays A $10 and B $8; matching on a grey door pays A $8 and B $10. B is disadvantaged by red, but if B expects A to pick red, any other door pays B $0 while red pays $8. Red is the unique, conspicuous option, so it can coordinate despite being unfair.

## Concepts
- [[focal-point]] — an outcome that stands out as prominent or unique, so expectations converge on it (pp. 75, 91)
- [[mutual-vs-common-knowledge]] — expectations about expectations without end ("what the other expects him to expect to be expected to do"); the author gives no formal definition (pp. 71, 75, 91)
- [[tacit-bargaining]] (new) — bargaining where communication is incomplete or impossible (p. 70)
- [[qualitative-distinguishability]] (new) — a term or limit that differs in kind, not just degree, from its alternatives (p. 97)
- [[convention]] — a "conventional priority", weak for heads before tails and much stronger for the order A, B, C (p. 83)
- [[credible-commitment]] — only loosely related: one needs a "recognizable limit to his own retreat" so a concession is not read as capitulation (p. 91)
- [[bargaining-power-via-focus]] (new) — the focus for agreement may not just reflect the balance of bargaining powers but may provide bargaining power to one side (pp. 88–89)

## Canonical instances
- [[department-store-meeting]] — a couple coordinate their search without agreement (p. 71)
- [[parachutists-map]] — tacit coordination with a unique feature such as a river or lone house (pp. 72, 76, 83)
- [[tax-split-problem]] — income figures shift the focal point to proportionality, 10-15 (pp. 83–84)
- [[finder-reward-mediator]] — a mediator's rounded 2-1 split coordinates claims (pp. 82, 84)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Focal point ↔ decision-game-theory: coordination game with several equilibria, where salience selects one. Schelling gives no formal treatment here.
- [structural-analogy] "If not here, where?" ↔ bargaining theory: the point at which concession stops. This is the text's informal mechanism only.
- [metaphor] Lumpy, qualitative limits ↔ information theory: coarse but robust signals survive noise (cf. cluttered tax problem, p. 84).

## Questions this raises
- How do different cultures or histories change which cues are prominent?
- Does the unilateral first announcement help or hurt the announcer if the other side has a rival cue?
- When is it better to cultivate ambiguity than to commit to a focal limit?
- How would one test the claim that focal points work in explicit bargaining, not just in tacit?
