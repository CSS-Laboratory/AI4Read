# Enforcement, Communication, and Strategic Moves (part 2/2)

## L0 — One line
In mixed-motive games (where players' interests partly conflict and partly coincide), a player can gain by visibly and irreversibly limiting his own options or payoffs, because this can change what the other player expects him to do.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Schelling asks which "moves" besides ordinary choices can change a game's outcome. He treats delegation, mediation, control of communication and the conveying of evidence. He then argues that a threat, promise or commitment can usually be described as a visible, irreversible reduction of the player's own payoffs. Such a move can be written into an enlarged normal-form matrix and, if the game is determinate, solved by eliminating dominated strategies, which Schelling identifies with backward induction. Having fewer or worse options can help a player. Schelling defines a "strategic move" as one that shapes the other player's expectations of one's own behaviour. In zero-sum games, being found out is a danger; in mixed-motive games, being predictable can help.

**Prerequisites:** 2×2 payoff matrices; dominated strategies; sequential versus simultaneous moves; the idea of a credible threat. Terms: a mixed-motive game has partly shared, partly opposed interests; a zero-sum game is pure conflict; a normal-form game lists each player's complete strategies in one matrix; a strategy is dominated if another is at least as good everywhere and better somewhere; backward induction solves the last move first, then earlier ones.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A player can hand part of his decision or interest to an agent with different incentives or firmer resolve, and so make a threat or refusal credible (pp. 167–169).
- A mediator is probably best seen as part of the communication arrangements (or as a third player who controls them), and can also create a focal point by suggestion. He can suppress information, which matters because no player can persuasively commit himself to forget (pp. 170–172).
- Threats and extortion need a communication channel. Destroying or avoiding the channel can win coordination games or protect a player from threats (pp. 172–174).
- Communicating a commitment requires communicating evidence, not just words. The secret ballot works because the voter cannot prove how he voted (pp. 174–176).
- A commitment, threat or promise can usually be represented as subtracting a penalty from one's own payoffs in the cells that break the plan (p. 178).
- In the worked case the enlarged matrix (12 × 8) is solved by successively removing dominated strategies, a process Schelling identifies with backward induction; he says this seems to be the general form of solution when the game has a determinate solution. The matrix only represents the tactics: studying games already in normal form teaches nothing about them (pp. 179–185).
- Paradox of strategic advantage: worsening one's own outcomes, with none improved, may be dramatically advantageous (p. 186).
- A strategic move constrains the other's choice by constraining one's own behaviour. Not being found out is central in zero-sum games, whereas in mixed-motive games the advantage lies in a mode of behaviour the other party takes for granted (pp. 187–189).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**End of the preceding section (extortion).** Potential for destruction is large. Schelling's "hasty calculations" suggest a high-school graduate could destroy about a hundred times the $3,000–$4,000 he can produce in a year (p. 167). Extortion usually depends on the extortionist identifying himself and communicating openly. Schelling says the importance of self-identification is attested by the doctrine that an accused may confront his accuser, by secret Grand Jury testimony, and by keeping eyewitnesses' identities secret until the criminal is caught (p. 167).

**Delegation (pp. 167–169).** A player may delegate interest or decision to an agent who becomes another player (p. 167).
- Examples of delegation: insurance, which shares interests and gives the insurer different incentives (pp. 167–168). Several signatures on a check (p. 168). A collection agency, which gives one-way communication so the firm cannot hear debtors' pleas or threats (p. 168). Giving South Korean troops the means to release prisoners unilaterally, which removes an embarrassing power of decision (p. 168).
- The mutual-defence agreement with Nationalist China is "probably" partly a shift of the response decision to someone whose resolution is less doubtful. The proposal to give European governments nuclear weapons was argued on the same grounds (p. 168).
- Thugs, sadists or a commander of known motivation make credible a response the original decision-maker might shrink from (pp. 168–169).
- Fig. 14: Row, with second move, loses. If he irreversibly surrenders his move to a third party whose payoffs lead to the upper-left cell, Row wins and gets 5 as a by-product. If the third party's rewards come out of Row's payoffs, he still nets 3 in the upper-left cell, against 1 in the lower right (p. 169).

*Intuition note:* Handing the decision to someone who will not back down can make the threat believable. Even a rational player may gain from choosing "irrational" agents.

**Mediation (pp. 170–172).** A mediator is "probably best viewed" as part of the communication arrangements, or as a third player with his own payoffs who controls communication (p. 170).
- He can invent context and make suggestions. Where no focal point exists he can create one by a dramatic suggestion (p. 170).
- Examples: a bystander directing traffic, the square-dance caller, the white line on the road (which "very likely" can err substantially toward one side before the disadvantaged side denies its authority), and daylight-saving time, where a majority needs legislative control of the clock (pp. 170–171).
- A mediator lets rational players set aside some faculties: he can declare whether two offers are compatible without revealing them. No player can persuasively commit himself to forget (p. 171). Examples: a statistical agency denying data to the tax agency, and a bureau that destroys individual data (pp. 171–172). Mediators also seem a common tactic for hiding a buyer's identity, so that an owner does not know who wants to buy (p. 172).
- A mediator becomes an arbitrator if the players irrevocably surrender authority. This must be made enforceable by the players deliberately incurring jeopardy (giving the referee power to punish, or surrendering something complementary to their own values), and they must be able to trust him or extract an enforceable promise from him (p. 172). A third person can "hold the stakes", adding to the means of enforcing promises (p. 172).

*Intuition note:* A mediator can do what the players cannot do alone: coordinate by suggestion, or forget on their behalf.

**Communication and its destruction (pp. 172–177).**
- Threats are useless if they cannot be received, for example when the child is crying too loudly or the witness is in custody (pp. 172–173).
- In coordination games, cutting communication can win: the wife who announces where she will go and hangs up wins the dinner argument (p. 173).
- Mobs can intimidate local police who share their community. Outsiders such as federal troops at Little Rock "may have enjoyed some immunity" (pp. 173–174). Foreign-language troops and officers isolated from enlisted men may gain in the same way (p. 174).
- It matters whether the threatener knows his threat cannot be received. Otherwise he may be forced to carry it out, to the disadvantage of both. So the troops should act impassively and anonymously, as if wearing masks (p. 174).
- **Conveyance of evidence.** To communicate a threat one must communicate the commitment, which needs evidence, not words alone (pp. 174–175). Examples: a signed check versus a phone call, a gun shown versus claimed. Eisenhower's "open-skies" proposal and Szilard's suggestion of immunity for foreign spies illustrate the difficulty (p. 175).
- **Secret ballot.** Mandatory secrecy removes the voter's power to prove his vote. He cannot sell it or be blackmailed, since any punishment would be unrelated to how he actually voted, so the threat "goes idle" (p. 176).
- **Motorist and policeman.** If the motorist evidently sees the policeman's signal and ignores it, the policeman is obliged to ticket him. If he avoids looking, the policeman may think him merely stupid. Conversely, if it is evident that the driver knew the instructions and disobeyed, it is to the policeman's advantage not to have seen him, otherwise he is obliged to ticket him. Children avoid a parent's warning glance, and adults avoid asking for permission they suspect would be denied (pp. 176–177).
- **Bear by the tail.** An efficient outcome needs the bear to be able to make an enforceable promise and show credible evidence of commitment. If the bear has limited rationality and cannot solve games, the partner must formulate the proposal and communicate it to him (p. 177).

*Intuition note:* A threat or commitment has to reach the other side, with evidence, to do its work. A threat that cannot be received is useless, and denying someone the means to prove compliance can disarm blackmail.

**Incorporation of moves in a game matrix (pp. 177–185).**
- Core claim: Schelling says a commitment, promise or threat can usually be characterised as selectively reducing some of one's own payoffs, visibly and irreversibly. Penalising failure to follow a chosen response is mathematically equivalent to subtracting the penalty from one's own payoffs in all cells that do not correspond to the strategy (p. 178).
- Fig. 15: Row commits to ii by subtracting 5 from his payoffs in row i, making ii dominant (matrix B). Committing to i with penalty 5 gives matrix C (p. 178).
- Setup: Column chooses first, Row second, and Row can commit visibly in advance. Row has 3 commitment choices (1, 2 or 0 for none), each combined with 4 response strategies, so 12 strategies. Column has 8, since he picks I or II for each of three contingencies (pp. 179–180). Fig. 16 is this 12 × 8 tacit game (p. 180).
- Notation: Column's strategy such as 0-I, 1-II, 2-I; Row's strategy such as "1; I-i, II-i" (pp. 181–182). Players could send fully instructed agents to play it (p. 182).
- Solution of the original game (author's payoffs). (A) If Row commits to i, Column picks I and Row gets 2. (B) If Row commits to ii, Column picks II and Row gets 5. (C) If Row is uncommitted, Column picks I, Row gets 2. So Row commits to ii, giving payoff $[5\ 2]$ (pp. 182–183).
- This is a solution in the "complete weak sense" (p. 183). It is reached by deleting dominated strategies, where row $a$ is dominated by row $b$ if every Row payoff in $b$ is at least as good and at least one is better (p. 183).
  - Row 3 dominates rows 1, 2, 4 and all others except row 10; rows 3 and 10 remain (p. 183).
  - With only those rows, column 2 dominates columns 1, 3, 5, 7 (p. 184).
  - Then row 10 dominates row 3, leaving row 10 and four columns, all with the same payoffs (p. 184).
- Schelling says the discarding of rows and columns (each domination test using only the strategies not yet discarded) seems to be the general form of solution when the sequential game has a determinate solution, and can be identified with backward induction: compute the last move for every prior history, then the best next-to-last move, and so on back to the first move (p. 184).
- Caveat: tactics such as threats, communication, enforcement and timing disappear once a game is in normal form. So nothing about them can be learned from games already in that form (p. 185).
- The determinacy of such games does not remove their strategic character. The threatened player keeps his freedom of choice. The threat works by constraining his expectations through manipulation of one's own incentives (p. 185).

*Intuition note:* A commitment is just a self-imposed cost that makes the unwanted action unattractive. Once it is written into the matrix, ordinary dominance reasoning can be identified with thinking step by step from the end, in a game with a determinate solution.

**The paradox of strategic advantage (pp. 185–187).**
- If a player's payoffs already show the reduction he would impose deliberately, he wins without making the move (p. 186).
- A worsening of some or even all of a player's outcomes, with none improved, "may be" distinctly advantageous (p. 186). Examples: a sufficiently severe and certain penalty on paying blackmail can protect the potential victim, burning bridges may dishearten an enemy, and a lady might defy a search party by haughtily placing the sought object in her bosom (p. 186).
- Unofficial Korean War report: the Treasury blocked some non-Communist assets, reportedly to immunize their owners against extortion of relatives in China. Owners might likewise seek temporary identification as Communist sympathizers so their funds would be blocked (pp. 186–187).
- Article 26 of the Japanese peace treaty gives the US claims if Japan concedes more to others. Dulles said in 1956 that he had "reminded the Japanese" of it; the evident intention was to strengthen their resistance, and Schelling suggests that by also "reminding" the Russians he helped give Japan the claim "If I did it for you, I'd have to do it for everyone else." Schelling adds, paradoxically, that the US could not give Japan this bargaining gimmick unless it were patently motivated to use its claim if the tactic failed (p. 187).

**"Strategic moves" (pp. 187–189).**
- Definition: a strategic move influences the other's choice favourably by affecting his expectations of one's own behaviour. One constrains the partner by constraining oneself, leaving him a simple maximization problem whose solution is best for oneself (pp. 187–188).
- Zero-sum game: "not being found out" is central. It can be an advantage to have an opponent firmly believe in a mode of play, but only if that belief is in error (p. 188).
- Mixed-motive game: the advantage lies in being able to adopt a mode of behaviour that the other party will take for granted. One wants to convey the truth about one's behaviour, if one has actually succeeded in constraining it along lines that win when anticipated (p. 188).
- Genuine ignorance can be an advantage if recognized by the opponent. This has no zero-sum counterpart (p. 188).
- In a zero-sum game between rational players with full information, moving first can never be an advantage. In a mixed game it certainly can (p. 189).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Column chooses first, Row second. Payoffs (Row, Column): i,I = (3,4); i,II = (1,3); ii,I = (2,0); ii,II = (4,2).

*No commitment.* If Column picks I, Row compares 3 (row i) with 2 (row ii) and plays i, giving (3,4). If Column picks II, Row compares 1 with 4 and plays ii, giving (4,2). Column compares 4 with 2 and picks I. Row gets 3.

*Commit to ii* (subtract 5 from Row's payoffs in row i): i,I becomes −2, i,II becomes −4. Now ii is dominant for Row (2 > −2, 4 > −4). Column picks I and gets 0, or II and gets 2, so he picks II. The outcome is (4,2), and Row gets 4.

*Commit to i* (subtract 5 from row ii): i,I = 3, ii,I = −3, i,II = 1, ii,II = −1. Row plays i; Column picks I and gets 4. Row gets 3.

The best first move is commit-to-ii: 4 > 3. Strategy count: 3 commitment options × 4 responses = 12 for Row; $2^3 = 8$ for Column.

## Concepts
- [[credible-commitment]] — a visible, irreversible reduction of one's own payoffs (or options) that makes a threat or promise believable (pp. 177–178)
- [[strategic-move]] (new) — an action that favourably influences the other's choice by shaping expectations of one's own behaviour (pp. 187–188)
- [[delegation]] (new) — handing interest or decision to an agent with different incentives or firmer resolve (p. 167)
- [[mediator-arbitrator]] (new) — a third party who controls communication, suggests focal points, or holds irrevocable authority (pp. 170, 172)
- [[focal-point]] — a salient point of agreement that a mediator can create by a dramatic suggestion (p. 170)
- [[strategic-advantage-paradox]] (new) — worsening one's own outcomes can help in mixed-motive games (p. 186)
- [[strategic-normal-form-game]] — enlarged "supergame" in which moves become strategies of response; the tactics vanish from view (pp. 179–185)
- [[dominant-dominated-strategy]] — a row is dominated if another is at least as good everywhere and better somewhere (p. 183)
- [[iterated-elimination-dominated]] — the deletion sequence that solves Fig. 16 (pp. 183–184)
- [[backward-induction]] — computing last moves first, then earlier ones. Schelling says deletion is identifiable with it (p. 184)
- [[zero-sum-minimax]] — pure-conflict contrast case: not being found out matters, and between rational players with full information moving first can never be an advantage (pp. 188–189)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Elimination of dominated rows and columns in Fig. 16 ↔ backward induction: Schelling identifies the two procedures (p. 184).
- [structural-analogy] Mediator who compares offers without revealing them ↔ mechanism design: a third party changes what information the players can act on.
- [structural-analogy] Secret ballot ↔ incentive compatibility: removing verifiability removes the threatener's ability to condition punishment on behaviour.
- [metaphor] Burning bridges and blocked assets ↔ everyday precommitment (e.g. giving up an option to resist temptation): restricting one's own freedom can help (loose).

## Questions this raises
- When does the other player's belief in a commitment actually fail, so that the threat must be carried out to both sides' loss (p. 174)?
- Schelling says the normal form hides these tactics. What structure must a model keep to retain timing, communication and enforcement (p. 185)?
- Can a mediator who suppresses information be trusted, and what makes his promise enforceable (pp. 171–172)?
- How can real ignorance be told apart from a feigned one when ignorance is an advantage (p. 188)?
