# Endnotes 97–165 and the table of contents (end matter of *The Strategy of Conflict*)

## L0 — One line
These notes extend Schelling's main text on randomization, mutual fear of surprise attack, bargaining and tacit games; in the later notes Schelling argues that tacit coordination can rely on clues and asymmetries, which strict symmetry arguments overlook.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This unit is not an argument but a set of endnotes. They add detail to chapters on threats, surprise attack and bargaining. Three themes stand out. First, mixed strategies in zero-sum games can also be read as adding continuity to a discrete game, and randomization in threats is not always just a way to set the threat's size. Second, a surprise-attack model gives stability conditions: attack stays unattractive if the expected gain from surprise is below 1 and the chance of "irrational" attack is small enough. Third, symmetric theories of bargaining (Nash, Harsanyi, Luce–Raiffa) are criticised: Schelling argues that players may be able to coordinate without talking by using discriminatory clues (an empirical question). The unit ends with the book's table of contents.

**Prerequisites:** payoff matrices; dominant strategy; mixed strategy; expected value; Nash equilibrium (a profile where nobody gains by switching alone); zero-sum game (one player's gain is the other's loss); saddle point (a pair of pure strategies that are optimal against each other in a zero-sum game); tacit game (a game without overt communication); security level (the payoff a player can guarantee himself); discriminatory clue (a clue that favours one player over the other); tacit supergame (defined in L3, n. 147).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Mixed strategies in zero-sum games can be read as adding continuity to a discrete game, giving it a saddle point. Both readings are useful; keeping the opponent from guessing may better catch the spirit of the problem as a player sees it (n. 98, p. 374).
- In particular cases, proving a threat may require showing evidence that also helps the other side defend, so secrecy and credibility can conflict (n. 99, pp. 374–375).
- In the reciprocal-fear model, if $Qh<1$ and allowing for "irrational" attack with probability $P$, the game is stable if $P_c < 1 - Q_c h$ and $P_r < 1 - Q_r h$ (here $Q$ is the chance of achieving surprise and $h$ the value of winning; n. 114, p. 378). In a special case it may help both sides if the "victim" cannot achieve surprise even in self-defense (n. 114, p. 377).
- The padlock analogy: a partner too poor to buy a lock should be given one by the other partner, or no business can be done (n. 114, p. 378).
- In a last-minute bargaining game where an offer takes a minute and, once started, is visible to the other player, a player who moves second must accept the first player's offer, so "second move" loses; the game then becomes a "tacit supergame" of waiting-and-demanding strategies (n. 147, pp. 383–384).
- Bargaining solutions that need a clear zero point cannot necessarily be applied to matrix games. Security levels as a zero point are arbitrary or rest on a refutable hypothesis (n. 144, p. 382).
- Luce and Raiffa reject the two pure-strategy equilibria of a symmetric matrix by a symmetry argument. Schelling says that whether players can correlate strategies without talking is an empirical question; the Chapter 3 experiments at least indicate that in particular cases they can, using a discriminatory clue (n. 154, pp. 386–387).
- "Hint theory" is an inherently empirical part of game theory. Schelling proposes testing the motive for a choice by asking what a player would pay to play (nn. 158, 160, pp. 388–389).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Notes 97–99: mixed strategies, secrecy (pp. 374–375)
- Tail of the preceding note (before n. 97): Osgood is cited for experimental work on the "biased perception of what is equable" (p. 374).
- n. 97: Von Neumann says of the minimax theorem (every finite zero-sum game has a solution if mixed strategies are allowed) that he saw "no theory of games on these bases without that theorem" (p. 374).
- n. 98: Schelling offers another reading of mixed strategies in zero-sum games. They add continuity to a discrete game that has no pure-strategy saddle point, which turns it into a game that has one. Their role is then not very different from their role in nonzero-sum games (p. 374).
  - A coin can be flipped to keep the opponent from guessing with confidence, or to "average" two strategies in an expected-value sense. Both readings are useful.
  - The first may better catch the spirit of the problem as a player sees it, and it reminds us that even with randomization the aim is to prevent the opponent's anticipation of the actual choice. The machinery of choice, the procedures for recording and communicating it, and any advance preparations must stay inaccessible to the opponent's intelligence system (p. 374).
- n. 99: in particular cases the threatener may face a dilemma between secrecy and revelation. Evidence of commitment or capability may help the other side combat the threat. Demonstrating a weapon, or stationing troops in an ambiguous area, are examples (pp. 374–375).

### Notes 100–113: payoff scaling, randomization, risky action (pp. 375–376)
- n. 100: Payoffs can be scaled so that each player's preferred payoff is $+1$ and his next preferred is $0$, since only comparisons of differences matter. The expression $1/(1+X)$ is then a ratio. Its numerator is the difference between Column's upper-right and upper-left payoffs. Its denominator is the sum of that difference and the difference between his lower-right and upper-left payoffs. One parameter therefore captures three valuations. A problem involving the lower-left cell would need a second parameter (p. 375).
- n. 101: Randomization may be part of the threat's arrangement, or enter the decision process whether the threatener wants it or not. Reading it as just a way to manipulate the threat's size is applicable only in some cases (p. 375).
- n. 103: A condition (its referent is in the main text; the note points to the earlier footnote on the scaling of payoffs) holds as long as Column's lower-right payoff falls short of his upper-left payoff as much as his upper-right payoff exceeds it (p. 375).
- nn. 104–105 cite Snyder and Kaplan on deterrence and limited retaliation (p. 375).
- n. 106: Starting risky action, if it is not irreversible, does not necessarily "win". The war of nerves can continue. It does replace a situation in which the opponent won by default if neither side acted (pp. 375–376).
- n. 107: The tactic may be the less risky, the more automatic the mechanism is; the enemy then has less incentive to test my intentions in a war of nerves and prolong the period of risk (p. 376).
- n. 108 (author's opinion): the 1958 US troops to Lebanon were successful precisely because of the risk, which the Communists could lessen or aggravate by their response (p. 376).
- n. 110: The problem in the main text is the nonzero-sum counterpart of the zero-sum "dueling game". It asks whether to shoot, not when (p. 376).
- n. 111: If the non-cooperative game has a "solution in the strict sense" (Luce–Raiffa), it is assumed to prevail. The condition here is somewhat stronger, because the solution is jointly preferred to all alternative outcomes, not just to other equilibria (p. 376).
- n. 112: The players are in effect choosing between one pure strategy and one mixed strategy. The mixture is fixed by an autonomous parameter (p. 376).
- n. 113: Prisoner's dilemma is a payoff configuration giving both players dominant incentives, absent enforceable agreement, to choose strategies that leave both worse off than the opposite choices (p. 376).

### Note 114: reciprocal fear of surprise attack (pp. 376–378)
Symbols: $P_r, P_c$ are the probabilities that R or C attacks "irrationally". $h$ is the value of winning a war. $Q$ is the chance of achieving surprise. They are used here as in the main text, which is not in these pages.
- **Case $P_r = 0.2$, $P_c = 0.6$.** R's modified matrix has entries 0, .2 (upper row) and −.4, .12 (lower row). R still has a dominant strategy of attack, but he is worse off than if neither side had elected to attack. It is C's knowledge of R's dominant strategy that makes both get zero (p. 377).
  - C's "irrationality" gives R a motive to attack in "self-defense". A small "impurity" in that motive is R's chance of surprising C (p. 377).
  - If R could not surprise C, his upper-right payoff would be 0 instead of 0.5. The matrix becomes 0, 0 / −.4, .08. This worsens both right-column payoffs, the upper more than the lower. R's motive to attack disappears, C knows it, and the outcome is joint no-attack (p. 377).
  - It may therefore help both players if the "victim" cannot achieve surprise even in self-defense. The condition is $1 - h < P_c < 1/(1+h)$ (p. 377).
- **General case.** The value of winning, $h$, may exceed 1. If it does, and if attack is always a winning strategy, both gain zero when they might have gained more by abstaining (the source sentence is incomplete as printed). If surprise succeeds only with probability $Q$, the expected value of unilateral attack is $Qh$ (pp. 377–378).
  - If $Qh<1$, joint no-attack is again strictly preferred. Allowing for irrational attack, the game is stable if
$$P_c < 1 - Q_c h \quad\text{and}\quad P_r < 1 - Q_r h .$$
  - If $P_c, Q_c$ meet the first condition, R benefits as well as C if the second holds. If $P_r$ cannot be manipulated, R should want $Q_r < (1-P_r)/h$. Only then can he and C gain more than zero (p. 378).
  - If R can, at his own expense, improve the enemy's alert system or visibly blunt his own surprise capacity, to hold $Q_r$ below the limit, he should do so (p. 378).
- *Intuition note:* A side's chance of achieving surprise adds to its own motive to attack, even a motive of "self-defense" (p. 377). If each side's expected gain from surprise is small enough, and the chance of "irrational" attack is small enough, joint no-attack is stable (p. 378).
- **Padlock analogy.** Two distrustful partners keep separate padlocks on the vault. If one cannot afford a padlock, the other should pay for it. Only then can they do business (p. 378).

### Notes 115–124: formulas, asymmetries, warning systems (pp. 378–379)
- n. 115: A general nonsymmetrical formula, written with R's payoffs $R_{11}, R_{12}, R_{21}, R_{22}$, is given as a display that is not in the text layer. Its numerator is the "cost" of erroneously attacking and its denominator the "cost" of erroneously failing to attack. The criterion is the same as if $P$ and $1-P$ were sure probabilities (p. 378).
- n. 116: With communication and a check on each other's understanding, the players could agree informally not to shoot, leaving no incentive to cheat, if both parameters are evident to both (p. 378).
- n. 117: The model allows for one side shooting when he shouldn't ("nervousness") but not for failing to shoot when he ought to (e.g., wet ammunition). Such a possibility would apparently be stabilizing (pp. 378–379).
- n. 118: The usual rationale for a mixed strategy has no relation to this case (p. 379).
- n. 119: It is not necessarily so that response goes one way. If more danger of being attacked goes with less vulnerability of the enemy to surprise, the response may go the opposite way (p. 379).
- n. 120: Assumptions: a false-alarm attack is like a premeditated one, with the same chance of surprise. The time dimension of $B$ (false-alarm probability per unit time) is ignored, and the time horizon is fixed (p. 379).
- n. 121: Definition of an equilibrium point: a pair of strategies, each optimal against the other (p. 379).
- n. 122 (economists' analogy): two producers allocate scarce resources between two goods. "Security against false alarm" has external economies, and "security against surprise" has external diseconomies (p. 379).
- n. 123: If the $A$'s, $B$'s and $R$'s are equal, $V_r$ and $V_c$ equal $(1-P)^2$, which is maximal at $B=0$. If the $B$'s and $R$'s are equal but the $A$'s are not,
$$\frac{dV_r}{dB} = -2(1-B)(1-A_c)(1-A_r) + (A_c - A_r)\frac{h}{f'},$$
which can be positive with $A_c > A_r$ and $f'$ small. Then the player with smaller $A$ prefers some common warning system to none. It still involves lower $B$ and $R$ than parametric behaviour would give. Symbols $A, B, R, V, f'$ are used as in the main text, which is not in these pages (p. 379).

### Notes 137–153: bargaining and tacit games (pp. 381–386)
- n. 138: Luce and Raiffa define cooperative two-person games by three stipulations. Preplay messages are transmitted undistorted. Agreements are binding and enforceable. Players' evaluations are not disturbed by negotiation (p. 381).
- n. 140: The model is "abstract, artificial, and unrealistic". It tests whether it is fruitful to treat perfect symmetry in move structure as the general case and asymmetry as a special case (p. 381).
- nn. 141–142: Allowing instantaneous offer changes does not alter the argument if both can do it equally. A mechanical assumption is that one can stop and start an offer again. If an offer started 1.5 minutes before the deadline is necessarily the last, the case is more complicated (pp. 381–382).
- n. 143: Nash's 1953 model is explicitly tacit in its final stage, and its relation to the cooperative game was heuristic. Schelling argues the relation is likely to be mechanical rather than intellectual if the move structure is strictly symmetrical. With strict symmetry it is difficult, perhaps impossible, to define the corresponding non-tacit game (p. 382).
- n. 144: Solutions that need a clear zero point (Nash, Harsanyi) cannot necessarily be applied to a cooperative game based on a matrix. A matrix (unless perhaps all payoffs are zero except on the diagonal) has no zero point defined by the rules, so there is no "normal form" with a zero point unless a fully adequate theory solves the tacit game. Security levels (maximin values) as zero point are arbitrary, or they assume players alone could do no better than that. That hypothesis is weak and empirically refutable, since players often correlate strategies without communicating (p. 382).
- n. 145: With a divisible object like money, the house removes the "overlap" in offers. If one demands 65% and the other 55%, the second has been accorded 35% and the first 45%. These amounts form the "agreement" (p. 382).
- n. 146: One might conclude that a truly cooperative game needs a legal definition of agreement that makes the final tacit game perverse. The players must then define "agreement" for their own prior agreement. With a definition like the earlier one, they only get a benign cooperative game one minute shorter, equivalent to a tacit game two minutes shorter (p. 383).
- n. 147: Suppose an offer takes one minute and cannot be stopped once started. An offer begun in the next-to-last minute is then final (p. 383).
  - Suppose a locked visible board shows one player's offer within seconds. If the two offers in the final minute are not simultaneous, the player who moves second makes his final offer knowing the other's and must accept it, so "second move" loses if the first mover knows the other is waiting (p. 383).
  - The game becomes: wait until 23:58, then play a one-minute game. Each player has three strategies: (1) assume the other waits and demand 99%; (2) assume simultaneous offers and demand the tacit-game solution; (3) wait. If both wait, play continues (p. 383).
  - With finitely many waits, the "tacit supergame" consists of strategies like wait-once-then-demand-99%. Its "solution in the strict sense" (if we wish to accept it) is all strategies, of any wait length, that end in demands matching the tacit-game solution (pp. 383–384).
- *Intuition note:* A bargaining deadline looks symmetric, but if an offer, once started, becomes visible to the other player, moving second can mean losing (p. 383). The game then reduces to a choice among strategies about how long to wait and what to demand (p. 383).
- n. 148: Schelling notes an objection: the expected value of the game may still be divided symmetrically, so the analyst may still view it as symmetrical in average outcomes. He replies that an analyst who does so commits himself to a minimum of insight into the game and the way it will be played (p. 384).
- nn. 149–150: Harsanyi says that everybody will expect two duopolists with the same cost functions and market conditions to reach equal profits. His symmetry axiom says the two parties' optimal-strategy functions have the same mathematical form, apart from interchanging the variables. The idea is that a rational bargainer won't expect a rational opponent to grant larger concessions than he would make himself under similar conditions (p. 384).
- n. 151: Viewed in this way, reaching "rational expectations" in a full-communication bargaining game is virtually the same intellectual process as coordinating in a tacit game, although the actual solutions might differ with different contexts. Both depend on an agreement reached by tacit consent (pp. 384–385).
  - There is a qualification. Tacit bargaining often requires a further degree of coordination, even among equivalent divisions of the gains. Boundary lines and troop dispatch differ: claims may overlap although the terrain values claimed are consistent. There is no a priori assurance that the tacit solution lies in the equivalent set of the explicit game (p. 385).
- n. 152: The working hypothesis for rational players seems to be that some rule must be used if success is to exceed coincidence, so the best rule found is a rational rule. This premise would support, for example, Nash's view of an unsmoothed tacit game as the limit of a smoothed one, although that view is not logically necessary. It equally supports any other procedure that produces a candidate (p. 385).
- n. 153: In this view, Nash's maximum-utility-product theory and Braithwaite's theory respond to there being too many types of uniqueness or symmetry. The two solutions conflict, which suggests mathematicians may lack a common aesthetic to coordinate on one outcome (p. 385). Braithwaite's one-person arbitration framing and Luce and Raiffa's arbitration reformulation of Nash seem to emphasise that intellectual coordination is at the heart of the theory; a legalistic solution requires some rationalization of a unique outcome, and "pure casuistry is helpful if the alternative is vacuum" (p. 386).

### Notes 154–155: Luce–Raiffa symmetric matrix; Nash solution (pp. 386–388)
- n. 154: **Luce and Raiffa's view:** in a symmetric matrix with pure-strategy equilibria in the upper-left and lower-right corners, any rationalization for one player has a similar one for the other, "so it seems inevitable that we both lose". They also find the maximin and minimax pairs unsatisfactory. **Schelling replies:** the question is whether players can correlate strategies without communicating. This is an empirical question; the Chapter 3 experiments give an affirmative answer, or at least indicate that in particular cases the answer may be yes (p. 386).
  - The hardest part is recognizing they have to concert on a nonsymmetrical pair. They must find a clue jointly. Only a discriminatory clue can point to a concerted choice. Schelling says we have to suppose that a rational player can discipline himself to accept the lesser share if the clue points that way (p. 386).
  - Luce and Raiffa say that it is difficult to see how to exploit the game's symmetries. Schelling says the real key is that the game may, in context, possess asymmetries, and the object is to exploit them (p. 387).
- n. 155: Nash's solution for bargaining under full information maximises the product of the two players' utilities. On a utility plot of the region of outcomes, the solution is a unique point on the upper-right boundary (p. 387).
  - It is unique because, if there were two, they could be joined by a line of probability mixtures whose points yield higher products. The region is presumed convex because of the possibility of probability mixtures, and a convex region has a single maximum-product point (p. 387).
  - The solution is invariant to the exchange rate between the players' utility scales. When any exchange rate gives a symmetrical region, the upper-right midpoint is the solution. Nash showed that any solution meeting his conditions leads to the maximum product (p. 387).
  - For present purposes Schelling takes the symmetry requirement as the generic characteristic. The other axioms refine this crude symmetry to a unique solution (p. 387).
  - The theory uses the arbitrariness of the utility exchange rate as a principle, so it depends on the inherent incommensurability of utilities. If utilities were in principle commensurable, it would have little virtue (p. 388).
  - The notion of arbitration may be compatible with utility comparison. Giving up the match between utility and choice theory might lead either to measuring utility psychologically or physiologically, or to fixing a legal convention for comparison (p. 388).
- *Intuition note:* Nash's rule gives the same answer for any fixed weights attached to the players' utilities (p. 387). Schelling says that if utilities could in principle be compared, there would be little virtue in a theory built on this invariance; the theory seems to depend on utilities being inherently incommensurable (p. 388).

### Notes 156–165: tacit games, hint theory (pp. 388–390)
- n. 156: "Noncooperative" may suggest that cooperation is absent when communication is absent. Reciprocated cooperation is an essential, even dominant, element in many tacit nonzero-sum games (p. 388).
- n. 157: The Luce–Raiffa solution concept is akin to, but distinct from, Nash's of 1951 (p. 388).
- n. 158: The skill needed in these games is something like that for solving riddles. In a riddle, the rule is that the clue must be neither too hard nor too easy to find. One cannot deduce a priori whether a rational partner can take a hint, so "hint theory" is inherently empirical (pp. 388–389).
- n. 160: To tell the "insurance" motive from the "coordination-clue" motive for choosing ii or II, vary the 5's in Fig. 33 over 0–9 (keeping the 10's and zeros) and ask a player what he would pay to play (p. 389).
  - If his bids are fairly insensitive to the changed payoff while it is positive, he still attaches a high value to the game with some positive payoff, and his bid is something like the random-strategy expected value for the all-zero game (Fig. 32), Schelling says we can conclude that those payoffs are mainly signals to him (p. 389).
  - Example bids: $9.50 (about 90% expectation of II) for Fig. 33, $8.65 for 1 in place of 5 (85%), $9.95 for 9 (95%), and $5 for Fig. 32. These suggest a coordinating-clue function (p. 389).
  - (added step) Assuming each bid is the expected payoff of his chosen strategy against probability $p$ (10 on a match, the replaced payoff otherwise; the matrices are not in these pages, but all four bids fit), the bid equals $10p + (\text{replaced payoff})(1-p)$. For example, $10(0.85) + 1(0.15) = 8.65$.
  - Bids implying probabilities invariant to those payoffs, especially at the arithmetic mean, would indicate insurance. Strategies must not be presented in a square matrix or with labels like "i" and "ii"; if they are labeled, the labels must come from a random process separate from the one used for the other player (p. 389).
- n. 162: Fig. 35 also has an equilibrium with Column mixing 80:20 and Row 40:60. It gives each 3.6 and is jointly dominated by the upper-left and lower-right cells (p. 390).
- n. 163: Mutually perceived signals seem to lie behind Luce–Raiffa's "psychological dominance" (p. 390).
- n. 164: Two cars approaching an asymmetrical narrow place were included in the Chapter 3 questionnaire. The results bore out the general principle but were omitted for brevity (p. 390).
- nn. 159, 161, 165 are cross-references or invitations to collect evidence (pp. 389–390).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take $h = 1.5$ and $P_r = 0.3$.
- $Q_r < (1-P_r)/h = 0.7/1.5 \approx 0.467$.
- Try $Q_r = 0.4$: $Q_r h = 0.6 < 1$ and $1 - Q_r h = 0.4 > 0.3 = P_r$. R's stability condition holds (C's condition is assumed to hold too).
- Try $Q_r = 0.5$: $Q_r h = 0.75$ and $1 - 0.75 = 0.25 < 0.3$. The condition fails.
Blunting R's surprise ability from 0.5 to 0.4 restores stability, which is the point of the padlock analogy.
