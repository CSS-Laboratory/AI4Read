# Enforcement, Communication, and Strategic Moves (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
A threat works only if some commitment (real or apparent) stands behind it and changes the other player's expectations; promises likewise need enforcement or some substitute for it. So the possible "moves", and the communication and enforcement they need, deserve study, not only abstract payoff comparisons.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Schelling asks which moves nonzero-sum games (games where gains and losses need not cancel out) contain beyond choosing a strategy: commitment, threat, promise, hostage-taking, giving up the initiative, self-identification. He argues that a threat needs some commitment, real or apparent, behind it. It does not depend on the threatener suffering less than the victim. Whether a move is available and effective depends on the communication system and on the ability to enforce a commitment; identifying each other is a related information problem. He suggests game theory may have defined away these ingredients by being too abstract. He hopes that analysing such moves may give insight into real problems such as deterrence, disarmament and holdups.

**Prerequisites:** payoff matrices (2×2 games); first move versus simultaneous move; the idea of a commitment from Chapter 3.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Game theory has said little about deterrence, trip wires and similar ideas. The author suggests it may have been too abstract, ignoring communication and enforcement and assuming symmetry (p. 142).
- A commitment is a "strategic move". It converts indeterminate bargaining into a two-move game, but the outcome still depends on the other player (pp. 145–146).
- A commitment fixes one's action. A threat fixes a reaction, i.e. a commitment to a strategy for second move (p. 147).
- The "hurt-more" view of threats (Luce and Raiffa) is rejected as a general criterion. What matters is whether the other player believes in the commitment, and that is true for any payoffs with the same preference order (pp. 148–150, 154).
- Commitment can be loosened. A finite penalty works only if it is large enough (in the author's Fig. 10 example, greater than 2). Allowing error probabilities can make a symmetric case favour one player's threat (pp. 151–154).
- A promise may be better defined as a commitment the second party can enforce or release. It is probably best to treat threat and promise as two aspects of one tactic of conditional self-commitment (pp. 156–158).
- Enforcement depends on at least two things: authority and detection. Trust, coordination-type agreements, hostages and leaving populations undefended are other routes to enforcible agreement that the author discusses (pp. 155, 158–161).
- One may give up the initiative so the other must choose. Identification can be costly: revealing yourself can expose you to a threat (pp. 162–166).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 142–145)
- Deterrence, trip wires, face-saving exits and taxi drivers' wide berth are "evidently" game theory. Yet formal theory has clarified them little (p. 142).
- **Author's suggestion:** nonzero-sum theory *may* have missed its best field by abstracting from communication and enforcement, and by treating symmetry as the general case (p. 142).
- Questions posed: what model fits massive retaliation, what makes a threat efficacious, why dogs, children and martyrs are immune to threats (p. 143).
- Game-type analogies for cold-war stalemate: poison arrows across a canyon, a shepherd and a cornered wolf, a pursuer with a grenade, neighbours with dynamite in each other's basements (p. 143).
- The chapter aims to suggest typical moves (threat, promise, destruction of communication, delegation of decision) and structural elements (communication and enforcement provisions) worth exploring in game theory (p. 145).
- **Model: twenty men and a gunman with six bullets** (pp. 143–144). They can overwhelm him if they accept losing six and can decide which six. They can win without loss if they can commit to a threat and also promise no capital punishment. He can deter their threat if he can visibly commit himself to shoot in disregard of any later threat, or if he can show that he could not believe their promise. Each case turns on communication (a foreign language), agreement among themselves, who goes first, and a way to let him escape or take a hostage (pp. 143–144).

> *Intuition note:* In this model, much of the outcome turns on who can speak to whom, who can bind himself, and who must go first, and not only on the number of bullets.

### An illustrative move: commitment (pp. 145–146)
- A buyer's single "final" offer, with a heavy penalty for amending it, leaves the seller one decision. Bargaining becomes a two-move game and is "determinate" (p. 145).
- Availability depends on the communication structure and on the ability to enforce the commitment against oneself. The move structure may be asymmetric: the winner is the one who can assume the commitment or, if both can, the one who can do it first (p. 145).
- Still a strategic game, not a foot race. The commitment does not win automatically. It constrains the other's choice "by affecting his expectations" (p. 146).
- Commitment is equivalent to first move. Without legal means, an irreversible maneuver works: a "prior" engagement, deliberately catching cold. The same tactic can be used against oneself: Luce and Raiffa's diet example (announcing the intention or accepting a wager), or, by maneuver, a vacation deep in the wilds without cigarettes (p. 146).

### Threats (pp. 147–155)
- **Definition:** a threat asserts that one will do, in a contingency governed by the other's behaviour, what one would manifestly prefer not to do. Like a commitment it surrenders choice. It works only if it carries some appearance of obligation (p. 147).
- Commitment fixes a course of action. A threat fixes a course of reaction (p. 147).
- A threat can be effective only if the other player has the first move or can be forced to move first. A player who must move first or simultaneously can still force the legal equivalent of first move on the other by attaching his threat to a demand that the other promise in advance, if the game has communication and enforcement structures that make promises feasible and that the victim cannot destroy in advance. Example: the holdup man needs a hostage while he waits for payment (pp. 147–148).
- **Luce and Raiffa's view:** a threat is plausible only if it "will hurt you more than it hurts me". Where both threaten, the outcome depends on "bargaining personalities" (p. 148).
- **Schelling's reply (Fig. 9):** Column has first move. Row threatens ii unless Column chooses II, committing to "ii,I or i,II". Column either believes it, and prefers 1 to 0 and chooses II, or does not believe it, and ignores it. This holds for any numbers with the same preference order, including the right-hand matrix (pp. 148–150).
- The issue is whether the game has "moves" that irreversibly alter the payoffs, the order of choices or the information. If it has none, "personalities" may decide. Otherwise a threat needs commitment, real or fake (p. 150).
- **Commitment interpreted broadly:** (1) removing the option of non-fulfilment, e.g. driving too fast to stop; (2) shifting the decision to a party with an ex post motive, e.g. sadists, or an insurance company; (3) worsening one's own payoff if one fails to comply, e.g. a plate-glass window or public cowardice. Goffman's street salesmen trap customers by considerateness (pp. 150–151).
- **Loosening 1: finite penalty.**
  - Fig. 10: Row's commitment to i works only if the penalty subtracted from his ii payoffs exceeds 2. Otherwise Column can see that Row's response to II will still be ii (p. 152).
  - Fig. 11: without threats the solution is iii,II. Column's persuasive penalty must be at least 4. Row's must be at least 3. With a single penalty size: below 3, unused, outcome iii,II; above 4, either can win, and the first to threaten wins; between 3 and 4, only Row can threaten and he wins (pp. 152–153).
  - "Hurt-more" here compares Row's cost of fulfilling his own threat with Column's cost of fulfilling his. It does not compare who is hurt more by what Row threatens. In the matrix shown, Row's successful threat would hurt him more to fulfil than it would hurt Column, while Column's unsuccessful potential threat would hurt him less to fulfil than it would hurt Row (p. 153).
- **Loosening 2: error.** With error probabilities $P_R$ and $P_C$, the possible loss from carrying out a threat can differ. Then, even with equal $P$'s and equal penalties, one player may find it advantageous to threaten and the other not (pp. 153–154). The author says this modification goes somewhat in the direction of the hurt-more criterion (p. 154).
- **Conclusion:** game theory adds more insight by emphasizing that a threat does not depend on the victim suffering more, rather than by exaggerating the possible truth in the hurt-more intuition. Threats of war, damage suits, prosecution, extortion and deterrence generally "cannot be understood" while insisting on the utility-comparison criterion. Relevant asymmetries are in communication, enforcibility, speed of commitment, expected rationality, and sometimes relative damage (pp. 154–155).

> *Intuition note:* A threat constrains the other player only if he sees at least some appearance that you are obliged to carry it out. Schelling's point is that a threat need not hurt the other more in order to work.

### Promises (pp. 155–162)
- Enforcement depends on at least two things: an authority to punish and an ability to detect noncompliance. Disarmament and inspection show how hard this is. Agreements that cannot be defined operationally must be left out; non-discrimination, for example, works only if defined in objective terms capable of objective supervision (p. 155).
- **Unilateral promise (Fig. 12):** in the left matrix with simultaneous moves, only a pair of promises works. In the right matrix, Row's promise "brings its own reward", since Column can safely choose II. With moves in turn on the left, the second mover must have the power to promise, and the promise must be conditional (p. 156). A witness to a crime may promise silence, and a nation about to have an absolutely potent surprise-attack weapon may have reason to foreswear it unilaterally (p. 156).
- **Threat plus promise (Fig. 13):** Row threatens ii against I and promises i if II. The promise gives Column 4 instead of 0 and costs Row 1. Without it Column would win 5. The threat alone cannot force II (II,ii gives Column zero instead of 1). With the promise Column gets 4 instead of 5, and Row gets 5 instead of 2 (p. 157). Spies must also be promised immunity (p. 157).
- **Definition problems:** a promise might be a commitment the second party can enforce or release. Timing matters. If Column can renounce the promise in advance, the threat is deterred. The definition breaks down when an irrevocable act replaces a legal commitment (p. 158).
- With more than two choices threat and promise mix. They are probably "different aspects of the same tactic" of selective, conditional self-commitment (p. 158).
- **Enforcement schemes.** Agreements fail without an outside authority or with undetectable noncompliance. Terms should give no incentive to cheat or should make cheating visible (pp. 158–159).
  - Trust often arises from a continuing relation, where the value of the tradition outweighs one gain from cheating. A single instance can be divided into increments (p. 159).
  - Agreements that depend on coordination or complementarity lend themselves to enforcible agreement. Once made, the agreement is the only focal point and nobody prefers to deviate (p. 159). One may add elements that create jeopardy for non-coordination, e.g. tearing a treasure map in half (p. 160).
  - Hostages, wine from one glass and public meeting places work similarly. A principal drawback of exchanging hostages, assuming rational behaviour, is the inherent unknowability of each other's value system (p. 160).
  - The kindergarten-in-Russia example would probably guarantee the Russians against American surprise attack, though we cannot be quite sure they would be sure of this. A reciprocal scheme may be less of a deterrent to the Russian government, since it can hardly persuade us of its concern for Russian children. A unilateral promise can still beat none (pp. 160–161).
  - Giving up defences makes hostages of one's own population. A stable "balance of terror" is, if it exists and is stable, equivalent to a total exchange of all conceivable hostages. "Stable" means neither side can destroy the other's retaliatory power by surprise (p. 161).
- **Denial of enforcement.** Making contracts unenforcible (gambling, prohibition liquor) discourages activities. It can give power to whoever can enforce contracts, e.g. the bigger gangs, which could guarantee their liquor's quality (p. 162).

### Relinquishing the initiative (pp. 162–164)
- Since finding a means to commit is hard, one can instead maneuver into a position with no effective choice, so the other party decides alone (p. 162).
- **Dulles passage (Dulles's voice):** he says it may become feasible for defenders to confront an aggressor with the choice between failing and himself initiating nuclear war. Schelling presents this as the kind of tactic Dulles was looking for (pp. 162–163). The "tables may be turned" (p. 163).
- The difference between the 1950s and 1960s deterrence types is who makes the final decision. The author says the US cannot find, or bring itself to trust, a persuasive commitment to the threat of massive retaliation against certain types of aggression (p. 163).
- Other cases: exploding n−1 bombs in the atmosphere-tolerance speculation (pp. 163–164); Canyon's burning ring of gasoline (in his comic strip), which leaves the enemy the "last clear chance" (p. 164); sit-downs on railway tracks; the "suicide sit-down fleet" (p. 164).

### Identification (pp. 164–166, part ends mid-section)
- Players may be unable to identify potential partners (bank employee and robber, boy and girl, kidnaper, Southern anti-segregation minority) because declaring one's interest is penalised (pp. 164–165).
- Identification, like communication, is not necessarily reciprocal; self-identification may sometimes be reversible and sometimes not (p. 165).
- *Measure for Measure:* Angelo has no reason to torture the brother until the sister arrives. Once she is a recipient for the threat, threatening has value for him. Her identifying herself cost her (pp. 165–166).
- Motorists' club cards identify a person whose promise to keep quiet is enforcible. Because it is shown only after arrest, it is "contingent identification" at the motorist's option (p. 166). Sutherland: most police are "more or less fair" to thieves simply because it pays them to be (p. 166).
- The final paragraph (on an economic fact ignored in the economics of production) begins at the bottom of p. 166. This part ends there.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Payoffs are (Row, Column). Column moves first, then Row.

| Column move | Row reply | Payoffs |
|---|---|---|
| I | i | (4, 2) |
| I | ii | (0, 0) |
| II | i | (6, 1) |
| II | ii | (2, 0) |

- **No threat:** after I, Row picks i, since 4 > 0, so Column gets 2. After II, Row picks i, since 6 > 2, so Column gets 1. Column picks I, giving (4, 2).
- **Row commits to "ii after I, i after II".** Column compares I → (0, 0), so Column gets 0, and II → (6, 1), so Column gets 1. Column picks II, giving (6, 1). Row gets 6 instead of 4.
- Carrying out the threat costs Row 4 (from 4 to 0) and Column only 2 (from 2 to 0). So hurt-more is not needed.
- **Finite penalty p** on Row's choosing i after I: Row's payoff there is 4 − p. The commitment is believed only if 4 − p < 0, i.e. p > 4. With p = 3, Row still prefers i (1 > 0), so Column ignores the threat.

## Concepts
- [[credible-commitment]] — irreversible limit on one's own options (real or apparent) that shapes the other's expectations (pp. 145–146, 150)
- [[strategic-move]] (new) — an action that induces the other to choose in one's favour by affecting his expectations (p. 146)
- [[threat]] (new) — a commitment to a reaction, costly to carry out, conditional on the other's behaviour (p. 147)
- [[promise]] (new) — a commitment that, on the author's tentative better definition, the second party can enforce or release; one aspect of conditional self-commitment (pp. 156–158)
- [[hurt-more-criterion]] (new) — Luce and Raiffa's claim that threats need the victim to suffer more; Schelling rejects it (pp. 148, 154)
- [[enforcement]] (new) — authority to punish plus the ability to detect noncompliance (p. 155)
- [[hostage]] (new) — a person or exposure placed in the other's power as a pledge, unilateral or mutual; leaving one's population undefended is equivalent (pp. 160–161)
- [[focal-point]] — the only possible point for tacit collaboration after a coordination agreement (p. 159)
- [[shadow-of-the-future]] — trust from a continuing relation where cheating loses the "tradition of trust" (p. 159)
- [[relinquishing-initiative]] (new) — placing oneself where one has no effective choice so the other must decide (p. 162)
- [[identification-problem]] (new) — the difficulty of recognising a compatible partner or of being recognised only when safe (pp. 164–166)

## Questions this raises
- If "commitment" can be fake, how can a victim tell a real commitment from a bluff?
- Does the finite-penalty analysis still work when both players can commit at the same time, or when moves are hard to observe?
- When is trust built by "increments" strong enough to replace outside enforcement?
- Why would a stable balance of terror be equivalent to exchanging all hostages, and what if it is not stable?
