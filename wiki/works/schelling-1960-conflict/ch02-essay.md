# An Essay on Bargaining

## L0 — One line
In pure bargaining, where logic leaves the outcome open, a party can gain by visibly and irreversibly giving up some freedom of choice, so that the other side must adapt.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
When any outcome in a range beats no agreement, each side's best move depends on what it expects the other to accept, and logic alone gives "no resting place". Schelling asks which tactics settle the outcome. One important class of tactics is commitment: a voluntary, irreversible, visible loss of options. He applies it to bargaining positions, threats and promises. A threat to do something that is not in the threatener's own interest needs commitment. Promises need it wherever an agreement leaves an incentive to cheat. Commitment depends on institutions, agendas, reputation and communication. Six small two-person games (Figs. 1–6) show these effects. So being weaker, or having fewer options, can be an advantage.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a payoff table or game; "reservation price" (the worst deal a party would still accept); rough familiarity with first-move advantage.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The chapter treats the distributional side of bargaining (who gets more), not the efficiency side. In pure bargaining each side is guided by expectations of the other's expectations, so the range of acceptable deals has no logical resting place (pp. 32–33).
- The core tactic is a voluntary, irreversible sacrifice of freedom of choice. The paradox is that the power to constrain an adversary may depend on the power to bind oneself (p. 33).
- A commitment works only if the other side can see it and understand it. Some are less effective than they seem: a contractual commitment usually just shifts the negotiation to another party (pp. 35–37).
- Institutional features affect who can commit and how easily: agents, publicity, linked negotiations, agenda, compensation, principles and precedents. Linking or separating issues can change who wins (pp. 41–48).
- A threat that the threatener would not want to carry out needs a commitment. Credibility rises if the threat is precise, tied to visible acts, and divisible into small steps (pp. 49–57).
- A promise is a commitment to the other party. Where fulfilment is hard to observe or enforce, the bargain may have to be restructured, or built up in small steps (pp. 58–61).
- Six two-by-two games show first commitment, conditional commitment, promise, combined promise-and-threat, and extortion by linked games. A point moved "unfavorably" for a player can improve his outcome (pp. 61–69).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 32–33)
- The subject covers explicit bargaining and tacit bargaining, where adversaries interpret each other's behaviour. Examples range from wage talks to taking the right of way from a taxi (p. 32).
- Schelling excludes the "efficiency" aspect (finding mutually profitable adjustments). He studies the "distributional" aspect, where a better bargain for one means less for the other (p. 32).
- Pure bargaining: each party is guided mainly by his expectations of what the other will accept. Expectations compound: "I must concede because he won't. He won't because he thinks I will…" (p. 33).
- Any point in the range better for both than no agreement may be insisted on, since one can always recede. But if both know the limits of the range, any outcome is a point from which at least one side would retreat, and the other knows it. "There is no resting place" (p. 33).
- If logic does not give the outcome, tactics may. An important class of tactics rests on a voluntary but irreversible sacrifice of freedom of choice. Schelling says weakness is often strength and freedom may be freedom to capitulate (p. 33).

*Intuition note:* If you are sure the other side will give in, you need not give in. If both think this, nothing fixes the outcome. A visible commitment can end the circle by removing your own option to retreat.

### Bargaining power: the power to bind oneself (pp. 33–41)
- Skill, intelligence, resources, strength or ability to withstand losses are not universal advantages in bargaining; they often have a contrary value (p. 34).
  - A man who says he will stab himself on the porch unless given $10 is more likely to get it if his eyes are bloodshot (p. 34).
  - A government that cannot control its resources may receive aid that would be denied otherwise.
  - Price leadership in oligopoly may be an unprofitable role that small firms evade and the large firm assumes (p. 34).
- **A cited definition:** bargaining power as "the ability to set the best price for yourself and fool the other man". Schelling replies that there are two kinds of fooling: deceiving about facts, and a purely tactical kind. Suppose each knows everything about the other, and the buyer says he is firmly resolved not to budge above sixteen (though he would pay twenty). Schelling asks whether a seller who capitulates was fooled or convinced of the truth, and concludes that the notions of bluffing and fooling do not adequately convey what has occurred (pp. 34–35).
- Truth is easier to prove than falsehood. Schelling's answer for using this advantage is to make the claim true. Example: a buyer wishes to persuade the seller that he would not pay more than $16,000 for a house that is really worth $20,000 to him (p. 35).
  - Moving his business would make it true but would be unprofitable (p. 35).
  - An irrevocable, certified bet with a third party (forfeit $5,000 if he pays more than $16,000) changes his true incentive. He can then present the truth, and the seller can take it or leave it (p. 36).
  - If the buyer can commit unambiguously, he can squeeze the range of indeterminacy down to the point most favourable to him. Whether he can may depend on who he is, who the seller is, where they live, and legal and institutional arrangements (p. 36).
- Other devices: the "cross my heart" culture (but the seller may beat the buyer to it with "$19,000"); an agent with authority limited to $16,000 whom the board cannot reconvene; staking personal prestige or reputation so firmly that paying more would mean intolerable loss and would necessarily be known; if the seller appreciates this, a loud declaration alone may then provide the commitment (pp. 36–37).
- A contractual commitment is usually a contingent "transfer cost", not a "real cost". The seller can approach the third party, offer a modest sum for release, and threaten to sell at $16,000 otherwise. If all interested parties can be brought in, the range of indeterminacy remains; to the extent that reaching the third party is costly, the commitment is truly irrevocable (p. 37).
- **A model with free absolute commitments** (pp. 38–39):
  - If each knows the other's reservation price, the first firm offer wins.
  - If they bargain by letter, both may sign before either letter arrives. Then both are bound to incompatible positions (stalemate).
  - Being known to be unavailable for messages may favour a party. Ignorance of the other's ceremonies may help if the other appreciates it.
  - If only part of the population belongs to the binding cult and everyone knows who does, the members have the advantage (p. 39).
  - If reservation prices are unknown, discovering them merges with creating commitments, which change the "true" reservation prices in effect (p. 39).
- **Examples** (pp. 39–41):
  - Union officials have often stirred up members' excitement before a wage negotiation. A plausible purpose suggested by the analysis, Schelling says, is to make clear that the negotiators could not accept less than $2 even if they wished.
  - A US executive negotiating under legislative constraint has a firm position visible to its partners, if it is evident that Congress will not be reconvened in time.
  - National representatives often seem to create a bargaining position by public statements that arouse a binding public opinion.
- Common features: the commitment must be communicated persuasively. It is hard to establish and its strength is unclear. Both sides may use it. It is not equally available to all (democracy vs. totalitarian government). It risks stalemate (p. 41).

*Intuition note:* A claim that you will not budge is easier to believe when it is true. A commitment makes it true by giving you a cost the other side can see. Bluffing and fooling are involved, but they do not adequately convey what happens.

### Institutional and structural characteristics (pp. 41–48)
- **Bargaining agent** (pp. 41–42): an agent given visible instructions that are difficult or impossible to change may be able to commit. An "agent" may also be a principal with a different incentive structure, as with an insurance company, which is more obliged to carry out threats of suit to protect its reputation.
- **Secrecy vs publicity** (pp. 42–43): staking reputation requires publicity of the offer and the outcome. A party without a "public" may try to exclude the other's public. If both fear stalemate from simultaneous use of this tactic, they may try to enforce an agreement on secrecy.
- **Intersecting negotiations** (p. 43): the party with many other negotiations (the union) can credibly stake its reputation, and the one with none cannot. Defences include enlarging the subjects or replacing the wage by fringe benefits, which gives the committed party an "out" and weakens its commitment.
- **Continuous negotiations** (pp. 43–44): the other party is also the "third party" to whom reputation is pledged. The threatener points to the long-run value of fulfilled threats, not to the gain in this instance.
- **Restrictive agenda** (p. 44): a latent extortionate threat can be exploited only if it can be attached to an ordinary negotiation; putting it on the same agenda makes it effective. In tariff talks on cheese and automobiles, confining the negotiators to that agenda keeps out threats about other tariffs.
- **Possibility of compensation** (pp. 45–46):
  - Agreement may require some means of redistributing gains. If compensation is impossible, a less optimal joint outcome may be chosen so that the initial split matches the agreed division (p. 45).
  - *Example:* two one-man projects, each cost 3, each worth 2 to A and 4 to B, with compensation institutionally impossible. While the projects are kept separate, B is forced to pay the whole cost of each, because A has no incentive to carry out either alone. Linked, B offers to do one if A does the other and threatens to abandon both. A then gets 4 for a cost of 3 and takes it. B's cost is halved (6 to 3, added arithmetic) (pp. 45–46).
  - Economics over-represents divisible, compensable objects. A drainage ditch costs $1,000 and is worth $800 to each of two neighbours. Schelling notes we usually assume they will get together to carry it out. A scoutmaster job (10 hours; each values the troop at 8 hours) is a one-man job and a deal is far from certain. Narrow roads and parliamentary deadlocks without logrolling are other cases (p. 46).
- **Mechanics of negotiation** (pp. 46–47): penalties for false information or insincere offers, time limits, auction forms, status quo, renegotiation, observability of compliance, and order of items are questions Schelling lists as deserving mention, without working out their implications. He notes that parliamentary rules, such as an all-or-nothing veto or the order of votes, substantially alter incentives.
- **Principles and precedents** (p. 48): to be convincing, commitments usually have to be qualitative rather than quantitative and rest on some rationale. A scale is too continuous to give good resting places except at round numbers like $2.00. Putting a principle itself in jeopardy may create something of a commitment.
- **Casuistry** (pp. 48–49): conceding can mark a prior commitment as a fraud, so one needs an "excuse". One can also undo an opponent's commitment by showing it is ambiguous or miscalculated. But when the opponent has resolved on a moderate concession, one may help him with a rationalization that denies oneself too great a reward.

### The threat (pp. 49–57)
- A threat to fight if attacked, or to cut price if the rival does, only communicates one's own incentives. If it deters, it benefits both (p. 49).
- The more interesting threat is of an act the threatener would have no incentive to perform, either before or after the event (pp. 49–50).
  - If he thinks the threat may succeed, he has an incentive to bind himself, since the threat and not its fulfilment gains the end.
  - The more certain fulfilment is, the less likely it is to occur.
  - The threat is ineffectual unless he can show he would, ex post, have an incentive to carry it out (p. 50).
- Ways to commit: bluffing about costs, pretending an erroneous belief or a strong revenge motive, staking reputation (even with the threatened person himself), legal commitment through a third party, or an agent whose pay depends on carrying it out (pp. 50–51).
- **Last clear chance:** a commitment leaves the last clear chance to decide with the other party, who fully appreciates it. If one driver speeds up so he cannot stop and the other realises it, the latter must yield. A legislative rider leaves the President the last chance. Smithies's "coercive deficiency" is exhausting a budget early (p. 51).
- A related tactic is maneuvering into a status quo from which one can be dislodged only by an overt act that precipitates mutual damage: visible explosives, a token force that cannot escape, Lippmann's plate-glass window (p. 52).
- **Defences of the threatened** (pp. 52–53):
  - Do the act before the threat.
  - Be already committed.
  - Share the risk with others.
  - Change or misrepresent one's incentives.
  - Appear unable to understand the threat, or too obstinate to heed it. Genuine ignorance or obstinacy may be best, but if the threatener commits anyway, both lose.
  - Be unavailable for messages.
  - The time to show disbelief is before the threat is committed, not just before it is fulfilled (p. 53).
- Commitments are unclear, may build up gradually, and communication is unreliable. This raises the chance of simultaneous commitment, and recognising it deters taking commitments (p. 53).
- If a threat fails to deter, both parties want to undo the commitment, but how to do so divides their interests. If the commitment of reputation was real, undoing means disconnecting the threat from one's reputation (pp. 53–54).
- **Making a threat credible** (pp. 54–57):
  - Leave loopholes visible to the threatened and credibility falls. Leave little room for judgment, because otherwise both parties' interests coincide in avoiding fulfilment (pp. 54–55).
  - Attach it to overt, visible acts, perhaps ancillary ones (carrying weapons, not their use). Punishment must be clearly discernible (p. 55).
  - Reputation needs continuity. Decomposing a threat into consecutive smaller threats lets the first few be carried out as a "lesson". The foreign-aid example is a small cut for each small misuse (pp. 55–56).
  - The threatener may not be able to divide the act into steps; for this to work, both the act deterred and the punishment must be divisible. The principle suggests the unwisdom of defining transgression by a critical amount (p. 56).
  - A punitive act that grows over time is similar (cutting off the food supply, or shutting off utilities to tenants). The threatener gets the overt act out of the way while it is minor, and if the sufferer alone can tell how near to catastrophe he is, the last clear chance is his in a real sense (p. 57).
  - The threatened person can also act piecemeal, or hasten an initial stage that commits him (p. 57).

*Intuition note:* A threat that would hurt the threatener too is believed only if the other side thinks he would really carry it out, for example because he has bound himself. Making it precise, tied to visible acts and divisible into small steps makes it more believable, since the first steps can be carried out as a lesson at little cost.

### The promise (pp. 58–61)
- The right to be sued is the power to accept a commitment. Earlier commitments had to be to a third party, with the adversary unable to release one. A promise is a commitment to the second party, needed whenever an agreement leaves any incentive to cheat (p. 58).
- Credible promises can be hard to make. Examples: the kidnapper and his prisoner; a no-strike pledge; a contractor with no assets (pp. 58–59).
- Bargaining may then concern the "incentive" system. Examples: fair-trade laws, exchanging shares, redesigning products, destroying an island's usefulness. In effect, a "third-party commitment" has to be assumed when an effective second-party commitment cannot be devised (p. 59).
- If fulfilment is not observable, the bargain may be tied to something observable, even if not the intended object (pay a salesman by sales, police by crime statistics), or to arbitrary limits (pp. 59–60).
- **Decomposition** also applies to promises (pp. 60–61):
  - Agreements are often enforced by the value of future opportunities, which would be lost if trust is destroyed.
  - Small preparatory bargains cultivate mutual expectations.
  - *Example:* each of two parties pledges $1 million to the Red Cross if the other does. Each is tempted to cheat if the other goes first. If the sum is split into consecutive small contributions, no one risks more than one small contribution at a time (p. 61).
  - A history of successful bargaining also protects against the inference of overeagerness when proposing a bargain (p. 61).

### An illustrative game (pp. 61–69)
- Setup: North chooses A or α; East chooses B or β. Each of the four outcomes gives each a gain or loss. Outcomes are plotted with North's gain vertical and East's horizontal (pp. 61–62). No compensation and no threats outside the game are allowed, and the eight values are assumed obvious to both (p. 62).
- **Fig. 1 (ordinary bargaining)** (p. 63):
  - Aβ and αB are the alternative agreements; AB and αβ, with zero for both, are "no sale".
  - Whoever can first commit wins. If North commits to A, East's choice is between Aβ and AB and he takes Aβ. If East commits first to B, North gets αB.
  - First commitment is like first move. If both commit by mistake (A and B), stalemate at AB.
- **Fig. 2 (deterrent threat)** (p. 64):
  - AB is the status quo. North plans to shift to α, giving αB. East threatens β, giving αβ.
  - If North moves first, East can only lose by moving to β. If East commits to the threat, North chooses between αβ and AB, and chooses AB.
  - It is not enough for East to commit his choice (as in Fig. 1). He must commit to a conditional choice: B or β depending on whether North chooses A or α.
  - With moves in turn, North wins αB whoever moves first.
- **Fig. 3 (promise)** (p. 65):
  - αB is a "minimax": either can achieve it alone, and neither can threaten anything worse.
  - Both prefer Aβ to αB, but the other has an incentive to cheat. If North chooses A, East can take AB. If East chooses β first, North can choose αβ.
  - At least one party must be able to commit himself to abstention, and then the other can move first. If moves are simultaneous, both need enforceable promises.
- **Fig. 4** (αB moved leftward) (p. 66):
  - Without communication, North wins at αβ.
  - If East can communicate a conditional commitment, he can force North to choose A and an outcome of Aβ; it is both threat and promise: threaten αB if North chooses α, and promise "not AB" if North chooses A.
  - The threat alone fails because North prefers αB to AB. East must do the opposite of what he would otherwise do for either choice.
- **Figs. 5 and 6 (extortion by linked games)** (pp. 67–69):
  - Separately, Fig. 5 has a minimax at αB with no threats possible. Fig. 6 has an identity of interest, with outcome AB.
  - If the two games are decided together by the same parties and either can commit to a threat, he may improve his position: East could threaten β in game 6 unless North chooses A in game 5 (or North could threaten α in 6 unless East chooses β in 5), assuming the intervals in game 6 are large enough.
  - If the threat is effectively committed and communicated, it succeeds and is not carried out. The threatener gets AB in 6 and his preferred choice in 5, at no cost.
  - Game 6 supplies the earlier-ruled-out threat of an act outside the game. Gratuitous extortionate threats are often impracticable. Joining two negotiations on one agenda can succeed where they would not.
- **North's defences** (pp. 68–69), if he cannot commit to a counter-threat:
  - Prevent communication.
  - Keep the games off the same agenda.
  - Give each game to a different agent paid only for his own game.
  - Force game 6 first.
  - Commit his choice in game 5 before any threat.
  - But if he can commit in game 5 and game 6 is played first, East can threaten him into prior commitment to A. Then North's ability to commit is a disadvantage (p. 69).
- Closing principle: dropping AB in Fig. 2 below αβ makes North immune to East's threat, which then is not made, and he wins at αB. Moving a point "unfavorably" for a player may improve his outcome; this is an abstract example of the principle that, in bargaining, weakness may be strength (p. 69).

*Intuition note:* In a game, a worse fallback can protect you, because it removes what the other side could threaten you with. The order of moves and who can commit can change who wins, even with the same payoffs.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Use the Fig. 1 structure with made-up payoffs written (North, East): Aβ = (4, 2), αB = (2, 4), AB = (0, 0), αβ = (0, 0). They must agree before choosing.
1. If North commits to A, East chooses between Aβ (East gets 2) and AB (East gets 0). He takes Aβ. Result: (4, 2).
2. If East commits to B first, North chooses between αB (North gets 2) and AB (0). He takes αB. Result: (2, 4).
3. If both commit (A and B), the result is AB = (0, 0), the stalemate Schelling describes.
4. So the first to commit gets his preferred agreement. Each prefers any agreement to none (2 > 0 and 4 > 0), so the other side concedes.

## Concepts
- [[credible-commitment]] — voluntary, irreversible, visible loss of options that changes the other side's expectations (p. 33).
- [[pure-bargaining]] (new) — bargaining where each party is guided mainly by expectations of what the other will accept (p. 33).
- [[range-of-indeterminacy]] (new) — outcomes better for both than no agreement, among which logic alone does not choose (pp. 33, 36).
- [[distributional-vs-efficiency-bargaining]] (new) — whether gains are shared out, versus whether mutual gains can be found (p. 32).
- [[threat-vs-promise]] (new) — a threat is a commitment to an act against one's later interest; a promise is a commitment to the second party to forgo cheating (pp. 50, 58).
- [[conditional-commitment]] (new) — commitment to a response that depends on the other's move, not just a fixed choice (pp. 64, 66).
- [[last-clear-chance]] (new) — commitment leaves the final decision with the other party, who must then yield (p. 51).
- [[decomposition-of-threats-and-promises]] (new) — splitting a threat or promise into small steps to make it credible or reduce risk (pp. 55–56, 60–61).
- [[issue-linkage]] (new) — joining negotiations on one agenda, enabling compensation or an extortionate threat (pp. 44–46, 67–68).
- [[zero-sum-game-minimax]] — here "minimax" is an outcome either party can secure alone, with no worse threat available (p. 65).
- [[strategic-game]] — the two-by-two North/East game with choices and payoffs depending on both (p. 61).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Commitment and first-mover advantage ↔ extensive-form games and backward induction: committing first changes the later player's best response.
- [structural-analogy] Threat that is not in the threatener's later interest ↔ subgame-perfect equilibrium: a non-credible threat is one he would not carry out when the time comes.
- [structural-analogy] Decomposing a pledge into small steps and the "value of future opportunities" ↔ repeated game and shadow of the future: later gains from trust outweigh the momentary gain from cheating.
- [structural-analogy] Promises in Fig. 3 ↔ prisoner's-dilemma-like incentives to cheat (the text does not use that name here).
- [metaphor] Plate-glass window and burned bridges ↔ costly signalling: visible costs make claims believable.

## Questions this raises
- If both sides can commit, how do they avoid simultaneous commitment and stalemate? Does recognising that risk deter commitments, as Schelling suggests (p. 53)?
- How can one tell a real commitment from a bluff when the other's costs and values are not fully known (pp. 41, 53)?
- When is linking issues good for both sides, as with compensation, and when does it merely enable extortion (pp. 45–46, 67–69)?
- Which commitments or threats can be made precise enough to be believed without becoming unreasonably rigid?
