# For the Abandonment of Symmetry in Game Theory

## L0 — One line
A bargaining game whose move structure is perfectly symmetric may collapse into a tacit game, and symmetric solutions cannot be derived from rationality alone; symmetry is only one of several ways players can coordinate expectations.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Nash, Harsanyi and others analyse bargaining with perfectly symmetric moves and justify symmetric solutions by saying that denying symmetry would tend to contradict rationality. Schelling asks whether that holds. Part 1: if moves are symmetric, the game must end in a tacit game at a "warning bell". The cooperative game may then degenerate into an ordinary tacit game, yet seemingly trivial rule details can change what that tacit game is like. A richer analysis needs non-symmetric moves such as commitments and threats. Part 2: a lopsided shared expectation, such as 80:20, cannot be shown irrational. Symmetry is one means of coordinating expectations among others. Its appeal is therefore introspective, not purely mathematical. This ties bargaining solutions to tacit coordination.

**Prerequisites:** normal-form games and Nash equilibrium; utility as payoff; the idea of a tacit game (choices made without communication); focal points as in Chapter 3.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A bargaining game is not defined by its payoff matrix. The operations of agreeing and of ending the game must be specified (pp. 310–311).
- With symmetric moves there is a last moment to change an offer. The last offers are made blind and cannot be answered, so the game turns tacit at a "warning bell" (pp. 313–314).
- If the tacit game has a predictable efficient solution, the cooperative game's solution must equal it (pp. 314–315). But the tacit game may lack such a solution, and details of the cooperative game can change it (pp. 315–317).
- Perfect move symmetry is a limiting case; the game seems a little like a foot race among equally fast runners. The interesting moves (commitment, threats, destroying communication) are potentially asymmetric (pp. 318–321).
- A shared expectation of an asymmetric outcome (80:20) cannot be shown irrational. Symmetry cannot be deduced from rationality, and defining rationality as symmetric begs the question (pp. 324–326).
- Symmetry is one coordinating device among others. Its claim on the game theorist is introspective or empirical, not mathematical (pp. 327–329).
- A normative theory of strategy that depends on intellectual coordination therefore has an inherently empirical component: how people can coordinate (p. 329).
- The Nash point may appeal because it is focal in pure coordination games. That seems to give something like a theoretical basis for the Nash axioms; the resulting focal point is limited to the universe of mathematics (pp. 330–332).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Part 1 — The moveless game (pp. 310–321)

**Aim (p. 310).** Part 1 argues that the pure "moveless" bargaining game (Nash, Harsanyi, Luce and Raiffa) may not exist, or is different in character from what is supposed. Its starting point is the operational meaning of "agreement". Part 2 argues that symmetry cannot rest on "rational expectations". Its starting point is the operational identification of irrational expectations.

**Setting.** A cooperative game, i.e. a bargaining game, needs more than a payoff matrix. Usually it is sketched by "binding agreements" and free communication, for instance two players dividing $100 once they agree (p. 310). Its move structure is symmetric even if payoffs are not: identical privileges to communicate, refuse and agree (pp. 310–311). Harsanyi adds a postulate of symmetric rules of behaviour (p. 311). Schelling will examine "agreement" and "non-agreement" under perfect symmetry.

**Termination (pp. 311–312).** To avoid adding discount rates to the payoffs, time preferences must not enter. If they are anything but a continuously uniform discount rate, the game changes with time, and a player could change it by not agreeing. So the game is assumed to end soon enough. Simplest rule: a bell rings at a time fixed in advance, called "midnight". Ending after a number of refused offers would turn communication into "real moves" (p. 312).

**Agreement (pp. 312–313).** Each player keeps a current official offer, recorded on a blackboard, in a sealed envelope or on a keyboard. At midnight the referee checks compatibility. If compatible, gains are divided accordingly. If the claims jointly exceed what is available, both get nothing. What happens if claims fall short is deferred (p. 313). Schelling thinks other definitions of agreement will generally share the property below, if moves are perfectly symmetric (p. 313).

**Key property (p. 313).** Making or changing an offer takes some minimum time. So there is a last moment at which a player can begin to record his final offer. Both know it, and by symmetry it is the same for both. Hence the last offer is made without knowing the other's final offer, and the other cannot respond to it (pp. 313–314). If either could see the other's final offer in time to act, it would not be final (p. 314).

**Result: the warning bell (p. 314).** The symmetric cooperative game necessarily gives way, at a definite penultimate moment, to a tacit (non-cooperative) game, and each player knows it. Players must agree overtly by the "warning bell" or play the tacit variant. If the tacit game has a clearly recognized and efficient solution, each player has a pure minimax behaviour strategy: abstain from agreement until the warning bell. Neither can gain more from a rational opponent by verbal bargaining (p. 314). So the cooperative solution must equal the tacit solution, if the latter is predictable and efficient (pp. 314–315). It then seems the cooperative game as a distinct game does not exist (p. 315).

*Intuition note:* If the last move is always blind, earlier talk matters through its effect on that blind final stage, which Schelling calls a "dominating influence" (p. 316). He still holds that the cooperative game need not vanish, because preplay communication can change how players coordinate in the tacit stage (p. 317).

**Why that conclusion is unwarranted (p. 315).** First, a tacit game may lack a confidently predicted efficient solution. Second, details of the cooperative game, and non-binding preplay communication, may change the tacit game.
- *Partial-agreement variant (pp. 315–316).* Players divide whatever portion they have agreed on at the bell, and disputed items revert to the house; e.g. 80 of 100 items agreed, 20 revert. If we had already concluded that explicit bargaining reaches an efficient solution, this would look inconsequential: players list claims and delete items. In the tacit game the incentives become perverse. Neither has reason to demand less than the whole, because a residual dispute costs no more than he would lose by cutting his claim. The single equilibrium yields zero for both (p. 316).
- *100 objects (pp. 316–317).* The objects are fungible in value, but agreement must say which items go to whom. If the rules require full and exhaustive agreement, then in the tacit game players must sort the objects into two piles identically. If one player has demanded specific items worth 80% and the other refused, the first gains an advantage. His is the only extant proposal, and the chance of concerting on another division may be so small that they are forced to accept it despite its bias. So preplay communication has tactical significance (p. 317).
- Insisting on symmetric behaviour seems to preclude this tactic. Each player opening his mouth to drown out the other would find the other's mouth open too (p. 317).

**Is the moveless game worth studying? (pp. 317–321).** The fruitful alternative to symmetry is non-symmetry, which admits symmetry and asymmetry as possibilities, not asymmetry as a foregone conclusion (p. 318).
- *Foot race (p. 318).* $100 goes to whoever reaches the end first. This is a game of skill with a predictable winner, and ties need only an auxiliary rule. If everybody runs exactly equally fast and knows it, every race ties, so why run? The moveless bargaining game seems a little like this: every player knows that all tactics are foredoomed to neutralization by the opponent's symmetric potentialities (pp. 318–319).
- *Moves to add (p. 319).* Commitments, threats, promises; tampering with communication; penalties on promises, commitments and threats; conveying true information, self-identification; contextual detail. These were discussed in Chapters 2–5.
- *Turnstile (pp. 319–320).* A player may leave but not return, and his current offer stays on the books. Whoever records a favourable offer known to the other and leaves has the winning tactic. This is still a game of strategy: he wins only by constraining the other's choice (p. 320).
- Symmetry can be reintroduced by a coin flip deciding who is nearer. The outcome is still asymmetric, since each runs to leave a standing offer. Ties become possible with two turnstiles, but as an interesting possibility, not a foregone conclusion (p. 320).
- A player who makes an offer and destroys communication may win the tacit game, because his is the only extant offer to converge on. The case of identical capacities to destroy communication, where both may do so at once, seems to be a special case, not the general one (pp. 320–321).

**Part 1 conclusion (p. 321).** The perfectly move-symmetric cooperative game is a limiting case that may degenerate into an ordinary tacit game, not a fruitful general case. The moves are what is interesting, and their potential asymmetry makes them most so.

### Part 2 — Symmetry and rational expectations (pp. 321–332)

**The target (pp. 321–322).** Nash's and Harsanyi's theories postulate symmetry. It is expedient because it often yields a mathematical solution. It has been justified on the ground that contradicting symmetry would tend to contradict the rationality of the players, and this is what Schelling attacks. His thesis: symmetry is consistent with rationality, but asymmetry cannot be shown inconsistent with it. Building symmetry into the definition of rationality begs the question (p. 322).

**Harsanyi's formulation (p. 322).** Harsanyi says the bargaining problem has an obvious solution in completely symmetric situations, where it is natural to assume the parties tend to share the net gain equally; later he calls the symmetry axiom the "fundamental postulate". His wording: a rational bargainer "will not expect a rational opponent to grant him larger concessions than he would make himself". Schelling reads two postulates in it. (1) A bargainer will not concede more than he would expect to get in the other's position. (2) The only basis for his idea of what he would concede there is his perception of symmetry.

**Shared expectation (p. 323).** One player's readiness to concede expresses what he expects the other to expect, and so on without end. To avoid this regress, Schelling says both sense a shared expectation of one outcome, which each takes as virtually inevitable. Both accept the game's "power" to dictate its own solution, through their ability to perceive it. Schelling rephrases the explicit and implicit parts of Harsanyi's hypothesis as two postulates:
- the *rational-solution postulate*: in any bargaining game with perfect information about utilities, there is an outcome such that a rational player on either side can recognize that any rational player would recognize it as the indicated solution;
- the *symmetry postulate*: that outcome is fixed by mathematical symmetry.

**Can asymmetry be shown irrational? (pp. 324–326).** Take $100 to divide, with dollars proportional to utilities. The players readily agree on $80 for A and $20 for B. We must not define rationality so that symmetry is built in (p. 324). By their own account (which we suppose we can check), B expected A to demand $80, A knew that B expected this, and so on. Both were correct and internally consistent, and the rational-solution postulate is borne out (p. 325). If we feel we would not have perceived the same outcome, one of four hypotheses is false: (1) the rational-solution postulate, (2) A's and B's rationality, (3) our own rationality, (4) the identity of the game we play introspectively with theirs. "On the evidence", we cannot declare (2) false (p. 325). If B had insisted on $50, or A had been content to demand $50, claiming to be rational and confident of a shared expectation of that outcome, both players would have been in "error", and we could not tell which was irrational or whether both were. At best this gives a necessary condition for the joint irrationality of both players; there is no sufficient condition and no necessary condition applicable to a single player (p. 326). Asking how they arrived at their expectations does not help. Any consistent story, such as a sign on the blackboard or a bulletin about other players, suffices (p. 326).

*Intuition note:* In the 80:20 case Schelling finds no error: each player's beliefs fit the other's. Unless symmetry is built into the definition of rationality, the evidence does not show the players irrational (pp. 324–326).

**Objection and reply (pp. 326–328).**
- **Objection:** the 80:20 case could not occur. Picking the same point among 9,999 divisions of $100 (to the nearest penny) would be at odds of 9,999 to 1 against, unless some device makes one point stand out (pp. 326–327).
- **Reply:** they can concert, as shown in Chapter 3, by any clue, suggestion or rule of elimination leading to an unambiguous choice or a high probability of concerted choice. One such rule is mathematical symmetry (p. 327). In a game with only mathematical structure there may be nothing to work on but a continuum of numbers, which can be sorted into symmetric and asymmetric divisions. If all numbers but one are asymmetric, symmetry is a sufficient rule for concerting (pp. 327–328). It may be possible to build such a "sanitary" game (p. 328).
- Symmetry may focus expectations because it is one means of concerting them; how potent it is may depend on the alternatives. The Chapter 3 experiments seem to show that other means exist, some perhaps outweighing symmetry. These often depend on topical content, such as labelling (p. 328).

**Consequence (pp. 328–330).** Schelling has no basis for saying how often symmetry dominates. But once it has competitors, there is no a priori reason to suppose it does 99% or 1% of the job. Its appeal becomes introspective, not mathematical, and further argument rests on the game theorist's personal appeal or on observation (p. 329). A normative theory of strategy that depends on coordination therefore has an inherently empirical component, depending on skill and context. Equating symmetry with rationality assumes that rational players cannot concert on anything else. Whether they can is an empirical question (pp. 329–330).

**Introspective game (pp. 330–332).** Payoffs are the points on or within a boundary in the upper-right quadrant. Suppose we are in a frame of mind that accepts the Nash point as the rational outcome of explicit bargaining (p. 330). Schelling conjectures or proposes that, in that frame of mind, we would pick the Nash point in each of three variants ("probably" for the first):
1. a tacit game, where each picks a value on his own axis and gets it if the point is on or within the boundary;
2. a tacit game that pays only if the point is exactly on the boundary;
3. a pure coordination game: perfect partners, the same prizes for any boundary point, nothing off the boundary (pp. 330–331).

The reason is that we need a rationalization leading to a unique point, and the bargaining analogy supplies one. Unless there is a sharp corner, a simple mid-point (straight line or circular arc), a suggestive shape, or an impurity such as a printer's dot (some of these coincide with the Nash point anyway), we may be led to search for a "unique" definition of symmetry. Nash-type symmetry is "as plausible as any", less simple than the 45° line but less ambiguous (p. 331). If the Nash point appeals to us powerfully in bargaining, it must be because we are confident it appeals equally to our partner, whom we believe to be aware that our views coincide. So it must appear as a unique point the partner finds "obviously obvious" (p. 331).

**Reversal and conclusion (p. 332).** Schelling does not argue for the Nash point. The theorist's appeal may run the reverse way. Schelling suggests it may be the Nash point's focal quality in the pure coordination game that makes it controlling in the tacit boundary-line variant. That in turn may make it a guide in the tacit bounded-area variant, and that discourages any explicit bargainer from hoping expectations focus elsewhere. By postulating a need to coordinate expectations, we seem to get a theoretical basis for something like the Nash axioms. Such a theory needs the premise that a solution exists. Observed tacit coordination is evidence that rational expectations can sometimes focus on a unique, perhaps efficient, outcome, and this leads one to suppose the same may be possible in a game with nothing but mathematical properties. Nash's theory vindicates this supposition, completely if it dominates all competing mathematical solutions in "mathematical esthetics". But the focal point is limited to the universe of mathematics, which should not be equated with the universe of game theory (p. 332).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two items, 1 and 2, each worth $50. Each player writes a claim set $S$ or $T$, a subset of $\{1,2\}$, with no communication.

**Rule Y (partial agreement, as on p. 316).** Items claimed by both go to the house. A player gets the items only he claimed: $u_A(S,T)=50\cdot|S\setminus T|$.
- Against $T=\{\}$: $S=\{1\}$ gives $50\cdot1=50$; $S=\{1,2\}$ gives $50\cdot2=100$.
- Against $T=\{2\}$: $S=\{1\}$ gives $50\cdot|\{1\}|=50$; $S=\{1,2\}$ gives $50\cdot|\{1\}|=50$.
- In general $|\{1,2\}\setminus T|\ge|S\setminus T|$, so claiming everything is never worse and is sometimes better.
- If both claim everything: $(0,0)$.

**Rule X (exhaustive agreement needed).** Players get their claims only if the two sets split $\{1,2\}$ exactly; otherwise both get 0.
- $(\{1\},\{2\})$ gives $(50,50)$; $(\{2\},\{1\})$ gives $(50,50)$.
- $(\{1,2\},\{\})$ gives $(100,0)$; $(\{\},\{1,2\})$ gives $(0,100)$.
- Other pairs give $(0,0)$.
- So four exhaustive outcomes exist, and the players must coordinate on one. Whichever proposal is "extant" gains force.

Both rules look like "agree, or lose" in explicit bargaining, but the tacit games differ sharply. That is why the cooperative game's details matter once the tacit stage is seen to dominate.

## Questions this raises
- If other focal devices can outweigh symmetry, how can one measure their relative strength empirically?
- When moves are non-symmetric (turnstile, destroying communication), how does one analyse who gets to use them first?
- Does the warning-bell collapse depend on there being a definite last moment? What if termination is random, as with dice?
- If the Nash point appeals mainly through focal quality, what would "complete vindication" in mathematical esthetics require?
