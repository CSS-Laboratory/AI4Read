# Nuclear Weapons and Limited War

## L0 — One line
Limits in limited war rest on tacit, mutually recognised expectations (tradition), not on physical or legal facts, and the nuclear/non-nuclear line is such a limit, which a first use may destroy.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can nuclear weapons be treated as just another weapon in limited war, now that their physical effects are no longer special? Schelling says this argument ignores how limits arise: through tacit (unspoken), mutually recognised expectations, not through physical or legal facts. Such limits have to be qualitative and discrete, and he argues also simple and "obvious", to focus expectations. The nuclear/non-nuclear line has this quality, as a tradition of nonuse. So a first use may destroy the limit, will set precedents for later wars, and opens a second, tacit contest over nuclear "roles" alongside the original objectives. Policy should follow from whether we want to keep or erode the distinction.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a coordination problem (both sides gain if they choose the same thing); no maths needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The "just-another-weapon" argument looks only at weapon effects, not at the process of limiting war (pp. 297–298).
- Nationality, borders, and air/ground/naval distinctions have little physical basis, yet shape limits. Nationality and borders are "legalistic" (honoured by expectation, with no legal force), not legal (pp. 298–301).
- A border is useful as a limit mainly because there is usually no other plausible line. It works as a stopping place, not as a legal duty (pp. 299–300).
- Tradition is the essence of a limit: its authority lies in the expectations, not in what they attach to (pp. 301–302).
- Because nuclear effects vary rather continuously, there seems to be no natural line inside nuclear use. Legalistic limits have to be qualitative and discrete (pp. 302–303).
- A prize-for-agreement exercise gives "simplicity" and "uniqueness" an objective meaning for tacit bargaining (pp. 303–305).
- Four conclusions: the distinction is real and can be strengthened or eroded; first use may remove the inhibition; precedents perhaps matter at least as much as the local objective; there are at least two simultaneous contests (pp. 305–308).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**The problem (pp. 297–298).** Small-yield weapons, nuclear depth charges and rockets mean their technical characteristics give little basis, if any, for treating nuclear weapons as peculiarly different in limited war (p. 297). Political revulsion against them is a fact (p. 297). Schelling asks about another basis: our relations with the enemy in the limiting process. A distinction may be "psychic, perceptual, legalistic, or symbolic" without being physical (p. 297). **Opposing argument ("just another weapon"):** if there is no compelling weapon-effects basis for a distinction, there is no basis at all that is pertinent to the limiting process (p. 298). **Schelling replies** by analogy. Russians and Chinese differ no more than nuclear and other weapons do, and terrain just north and south of a border differs little. Yet nationality and boundaries matter in limiting war (p. 298).

**"Legal" versus "legalistic" (pp. 298–299).** **Objector:** these distinctions are legal and so real. **Schelling:** no authority forces the Russians to treat a border penetration as a qualitative change, or the Chinese to retaliate at the Yalu (pp. 298–299). The inhibition is the risk of enemy response. An important determinant of that response is what the enemy thinks he has tacitly accepted if he does not respond, or responds only incrementally (p. 299).

**Borders as stopping places (pp. 299–300).** A border is pertinent mainly because there is usually no other plausible line (p. 299). Unless an "obvious" limit lies inside it, so that it is clear to the Russians where we would stop, obvious to us that there is a limit to how far they would let us advance, and the Russians know that we know it, no other stopping place can be tacitly acknowledged by both sides (p. 299). If the USSR accepted penetration without dramatic retaliation, it would admit its territory is "fair game" for a gradually expanding war (p. 299). So the border is useful, not legally mandatory, and useful to both sides, in default of any plainly recognizable alternative, because both have an interest in finding some limit. Its uniqueness makes it a plausible limit: it is one of the few lines, perhaps the only one, that both sides could tacitly recognise as the "obvious" geographical limit (p. 300).

**Other distinctions (pp. 300–301).** The US gave Indochina equipment but no manpower, and gave Greece equipment, leadership and advice but no combat troops. It gave direct naval support in the Formosa Straits (p. 300). **An economist** can argue, as persuasively as those who call small-yield weapons just another form of artillery, that equipment and manpower are fungible and that air intervention is not "really" different from ground intervention. The controversy about redefining service functions suggests that air-ground and naval-ground distinctions rest on nothing but tradition. **Schelling:** that is the point, because in limiting war "tradition matters" (pp. 300–301).

**Tradition as the essence of limits (pp. 301–302).** Limited war concerns precedent, convention and unwritten law. Their aggregate sanction is the need for mutual forbearance. Their sanction in each case is the risk that breaching a rule collapses it, leads to a worse limit or none, and weakens the other rules (p. 301). Bows and arrows are not banned because a tradition exists for using them. For atomic weapons there is a tradition of nonuse, an expectation that they may not be used despite declared readiness and tactical advantage (p. 301). A limit's authority comes mainly from perceived mutual acknowledgement, a "tacit bargain". It grows with each side's lack of confidence in alternative limits. The authority "is in the expectations themselves", not in the thing they attach to (p. 302).
*Intuition note:* A limit holds because each side expects the other to respect it, not because it is natural or legal. Breaking the expectation may collapse the limit, and it gives evidence that the authority of the remaining limits cannot be taken for granted.

**Why limits must be discrete (pp. 302–303).** Nuclear effects, forms, conveyance and targets vary rather continuously. So there seems to be no "natural" break between limited uses, and a line could technically be drawn anywhere (pp. 302–303). That is precisely why it is hard to find a rationale for any particular line: no degree, size or distance is so much more plausible than the others that it gives both sides a focal point (p. 303). Legalistic limits must be qualitative and discrete. This is not only about spotting violations or controlling one's own commanders. A stable limit needs evident symbolic character, so that breaching it is an overt, dramatic act (p. 303). Limits are found by tacit maneuver ("jockeyed for"), so each needs a quality that sets it apart from the continuum. A parallel of latitude, the date line or the north pole may serve when nothing else is obvious (p. 303).

**The prize exercise (pp. 303–305).** Each of us writes, separately and without prior arrangement, a proposed limit on nuclear weapons, along any dimension (size, use, user, frequency, clean/dirty, offensive/defensive, tactical/strategic, cities, warning). The extremes "no limits" and "no atomic weapons" are allowed. We win a prize only if all specifications are identical (pp. 303–304). Schelling **predicts** that simple, discrete, qualitative, "obvious" limits will attract partners, and that others will find few or none. Other virtues of a limit are irrelevant here (p. 304). The exercise does not prove which limits are stable. It does give simplicity, uniqueness and discreteness an objective meaning relevant to tacit negotiation (p. 305).
*Intuition note:* When you cannot talk, you pick what stands out. A limit with no standout feature is one nobody can be sure the other side has picked.

**Conclusion 1 (pp. 305–306).** The nuclear/non-nuclear distinction is relevant to limiting war. We can strengthen it by talking and acting consistently with it. We can erode it, but "not readily destroy" it, by acting as if we do not believe in it (p. 305). The right policy depends on whether we see it as a shared asset that minimises violence, or as a nuisance (p. 305). The issue is an understanding between us and the Russians, not only the views of neutrals or allies (p. 306). We should not want them to read our first use as a challenge to all limits (p. 306). If we want freedom to use nuclears, we should work to erode the distinction. Schelling's examples are "nuclear dynamite" in earth-moving and training friendly troops to survive explosions (p. 306). If we want to keep it, test-suspension talks "will probably contribute" (pp. 306–307).

**Conclusion 2 (p. 307).** The main inhibition may disappear with first use. We probably cannot use nuclears once and then rely on the distinction in the next war. Sanctuary concepts such as the role of naval vessels may also be by-products of the ban and need re-examining (p. 307).

**Conclusion 3 (pp. 307–308).** At first use we should perhaps be at least as concerned with precedents and our "nuclear role" as with the original objectives. In a Quemoy example, we probably ought to be much less concerned about the outcome on Quemoy than about the character of the nuclear exchange, the precedents it sets and the roles each side assumes (pp. 307–308). Schelling's analogy is the teacher facing a boy with a switchblade (p. 308).

**Conclusion 4 (p. 308).** At least on the first occasion of nuclear use, the enemy too will be engaged in at least two limited-war activities at once: the struggle over the original objectives and the tacit negotiation over nuclear weapons themselves. In considering whether the Chinese or Russians would use nuclears in return, we should perhaps not worry mainly about what they think this would do for the invasion of Quemoy. Much more important to them, it seems, would be their "response": they would want to avoid a submissive role and demand "parity" if not dominance (p. 308). Unless we are ready for a decisive showdown, we must be as willing to "negotiate" (by our actions) over nuclear dominance, precedents and rules, as for any other limited objective (p. 308).

## Toy example (ours)
Two officers must each name a limit on nuclear yield, from 11 options: "no use" plus 10 yield caps. They cannot communicate, and they win only if they match.
- With no salient option, each picks uniformly. P(match) = 11 × (1/11)² = 1/11 ≈ 0.091.
- If "no use" stands out and each picks it with probability 0.8, and the other ten options get 0.02 each: P(match) = 0.8² + 10 × 0.02² = 0.64 + 0.004 = 0.644.
A single standout option raises the chance of agreement from about 9% to about 64%. This is Schelling's point about discrete limits.

## Concepts
- [[focal point]] — a limit with uniqueness and qualitative character on which both sides' expectations converge (p. 303)
- [[convention]] — a tradition or precedent whose authority lies in mutual expectations, here the nonuse of atomic weapons (p. 301)
- [[mutual vs. common knowledge]] — an "obvious" limit must be clear to the Russians, obvious to us, and known by the Russians to be known by us (p. 299)
- tacit bargaining (new) — finding limits by tacit maneuver and negotiation ("jockeying"), without explicit communication (p. 303)
- legalistic vs. legal limit (new) — a limit honoured because of expectations, not because of any binding authority (p. 298)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] pure coordination game ↔ decision-game-theory: choosing identical limits for a prize has the same payoff structure as matching games.
- [structural-analogy] precedent-setting at first use ↔ repeated game: today's act changes expectations about future play.
- [metaphor] switchblade and teacher ↔ reputation: an act is judged for the role it implies, not only for its local stakes.

## Questions this raises
- How can we tell when a distinction is strong enough to remain a focal point, and when it has been eroded?
- If both sides prefer a limit, why might each still try to "jockey" for a more favourable one?
- Does a tradition of nonuse remain stable if one side declares readiness to use nuclears?
