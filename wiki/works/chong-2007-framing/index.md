# Chong, D., & Druckman, J. N. (2007). Framing theory. *Annual Review of Political Science*, 10, 103–126. doi:10.1146/annurev.polisci.10.072805.103054

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[chong-2007-framing/main]].

## In one line
In the authors' model, framing is a change in the weight people give to considerations (frames can also introduce new ones), and its effects depend on the strength of the frame, competition between frames, and individual characteristics, and so they are not simply good or bad for democracy.

## The gist
Small changes in how an issue is described can produce large opinion changes. This worries scholars who think opinions are already shallow. The authors review what a frame is, how to find frames in communication, and how frames work in the mind. They use an expectancy-value model of attitudes, with evaluations and weights. They discuss moderators (conditions that change effects), such as values and knowledge, competing frames, and the links to priming and persuasion. They list research gaps: strong frames, frame production, and learning over time. They argue that framing is intrinsic to opinion formation. Both too much and too little susceptibility to frames can be liabilities for democracy. The ideal is informed but open-minded citizens.

**Prerequisites:** weighted sums; survey and experiment basics (treatment vs control group); the idea of an "attitude" as a summary of beliefs.
