# Framing Theory (Chong & Druckman 2007)

## L0 — One line
In the authors' model, framing is a change in the weight people give to considerations (frames can also introduce new ones), and its effects depend on the strength of the frame, competition between frames, and individual characteristics, and so they are not simply good or bad for democracy.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Small changes in how an issue is described can produce large opinion changes. This worries scholars who think opinions are already shallow. The authors review what a frame is, how to find frames in communication, and how frames work in the mind. They use an expectancy-value model of attitudes, with evaluations and weights. They discuss moderators (conditions that change effects), such as values and knowledge, competing frames, and the links to priming and persuasion. They list research gaps: strong frames, frame production, and learning over time. They argue that framing is intrinsic to opinion formation. Both too much and too little susceptibility to frames can be liabilities for democracy. The ideal is informed but open-minded citizens.

**Prerequisites:** weighted sums; survey and experiment basics (treatment vs control group); the idea of an "attitude" as a summary of beliefs.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An attitude is modelled as $\sum v_i w_i$, with evaluations $v_i$ and salience weights $w_i$. Framing is a change in the weights; persuasion is a change in the evaluations (pp. 105, 115).
- A "frame in thought" is the set of considerations a person uses. A "frame in communication" is what a speaker emphasizes. Frames in communication can be found by an inductive content-analysis procedure (pp. 105–108).
- Framing can work at three levels: making a consideration available in memory, making it accessible, and making it seem applicable or strong. Availability and accessibility are required for an effect; applicability judgments matter when people evaluate consciously (pp. 110–111).
- Strong frames are those that audiences find compelling. Strength is not the same as truth or moral quality (pp. 111, 116).
- Predispositions (values) reduce framing effects, and knowledge has mixed effects. In the authors' own experiment on competing frames, preferences depended on prior values and on the relative strength of the competing frames (pp. 111–114).
- Emphasis frames and equivalency frames differ. The authors argue that, if their model is correct, framing and priming share common processes, and that framing and persuasion are analytically distinct but overlap in practice (pp. 114–116).
- Open questions: what makes a frame strong, how frames are produced, and whether citizens learn to resist biased frames. Most framing studies use a single session, so the learning question needs longer-term designs (pp. 116–119).
- Normatively, framing effects are intrinsic to opinion formation. Both excessive instability and excessive stability of opinion can be liabilities (pp. 120–121).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 103–104)
- Hirschman (1989) is cited: firm, full, articulate opinions are admired and having opinions may be essential to well-being (p. 103).
- Public opinion research finds that high-quality opinions (stable, consistent, informed, tied to abstract principles) are rare (p. 103). Mass-public findings from the 1950s–60s: poor knowledge, superficial views, unstable answers, fragile support for democratic values when applied to specific cases. Some answers looked like coin flips (pp. 103–104).
- **Framing effects** are cases where (often small) changes in presentation produce (sometimes large) opinion changes (p. 104). Examples:
  - 85% favored a hate-group rally with a preface stressing the importance of free speech, but only 45% with a preface citing the risk of violence (Sniderman & Theriault 2004; respondents were self-identified egalitarians) (p. 104).
  - About 20% say too little is spent on "welfare", but about 65% say too little is spent on "assistance to the poor" (Rasinski 1989) (p. 104).
- **Some scholars' reading:** such swings indicate that opinions are too superficial to be indicative of preferences. If opinions can be arbitrarily manipulated by framing, there can be no legitimate representation of public interests (Riker, Zaller, Entman, Bartels) (p. 104).
- The review covers psychological mechanisms, mediators and moderators, social processes that may reduce framing effects, conceptual issues, future research, and normative assessment. It focuses on policy opinion (p. 104).

### What is framing? (pp. 104–109)
**Premise.** An issue can be seen from many perspectives and can implicate several values. Framing is the process by which people form a particular conceptualization of an issue or reorient their thinking about it (p. 104).

**Expectancy-value model** (Ajzen & Fishbein; Nelson et al.). An attitude is the weighted sum of evaluative beliefs about an object (p. 105):
$$A=\sum_i v_i\, w_i,\qquad \sum_i w_i = 1 .$$
Here $v_i$ is the evaluation of the object on attribute $i$ and $w_i$ is the salience weight of that attribute (p. 105).
- Housing example: the project helps the economy ($i=1$, $v_1>0$) but harms the environment ($i=2$, $v_2<0$). The attitude depends on the relative size of $v_1$ and $v_2$, discounted by weights $w_1, w_2$ (p. 105).
- If only one dimension matters, $w_i=1$ for it. $i$ can be read as a dimension, consideration, value or belief (p. 105).
- **Caveat:** the model is an idealization. People may have only vague notions and may be unable to rank or aggregate their considerations. A survey answer may then reflect only the beliefs accessible at that moment. The model is still a useful abstraction (p. 105).
- **Frame in thought** = the set of dimensions affecting a person's evaluation (e.g., free speech alone, or free speech + public safety + community reputation) (pp. 105–106).
- **Frame in communication** = the speaker highlights certain features or values of a policy (e.g., calling a rally a free-speech issue). No straightforward guidelines for identifying it exist, so the authors propose an inductive approach (p. 106).

*Intuition note:* An attitude is like a score made of several parts, each with a rating and an importance weight. Framing turns the importance knobs; it need not change any ratings.

**Frames in communication: identification steps** (the authors say the most compelling studies tend to take these steps; pp. 106–108).
1. Identify an issue or event. A frame can be defined only relative to a specific issue, event or actor. The same issue at different times may have different frames (p. 106).
2. Isolate a specific attitude. Different attitudes (e.g., welfare reform vs. causal attributions for being on welfare) may involve different frames (pp. 106–107). Examples: costs, humanitarianism and individualism (Feldman & Zaller); episodic vs thematic frames (Iyengar) (pp. 106–107).
3. Inductively identify an initial set of frames to build a coding scheme. Sources are prior literature; elite materials such as court briefs, editorials and interest-group publications (Gamson & Modigliani), which give "culturally available frames"; and open-ended survey questions (p. 107). In the authors' social security study (1997–2000) they found seven initial frames: magnitude of the problem, beneficiaries and victims, old-age security, individual choice, projected outcomes, partisan strategy, egalitarian solution (p. 107).
4. Select sources (usually mass media, via keyword searches; the article is usually the unit of analysis). Coders judge the presence or absence of each predefined frame (p. 107).
- **Machine coding** needs a dictionary of words marking each frame. Kellstedt (2003) tracked individualism and egalitarianism in over 4000 *Newsweek* and 2500 *New York Times* articles; mentions of fairness and of equal protection marked egalitarianism. Shah et al. analysed nearly 20,000 articles on the Clinton–Lewinsky scandal (p. 108).
- **Manual coding** with prototypes is more flexible. The authors' coders found discussion of uncertain forecasts and added a forecasting frame. Costs: possibly lower reliability and smaller samples. Intercoder reliability checks are imperative (p. 108).
- Framing is a process that evolves over time. New issues lack agreement on how to construe them. Older issues have a defined structure and routine considerations (p. 108).
- Reframing can turn a "traditional" issue into a "new" one. Example: campus hate-speech regulation advocates compared racial harassment on campus with sexual harassment at work, shifting the value dimension from the First Amendment to civil rights (pp. 108–109).
- **Footnote 2 (p. 107):** de Vreese et al. distinguish issue-specific from generic frames. The authors prefer to tie a frame to an issue and an evaluation. A feature such as conflict that is not tied to an issue and evaluation should be called something else (Entman: "script") (p. 107).

**Effects of frames in communication on individuals** (pp. 109–110).
- Politicians, media and citizens borrow frames from one another. The literature has mostly studied how elite frames affect citizens' frames and attitudes, usually called a **framing effect** (p. 109).
- Three standards for effect size (the authors say the "best" one may depend on the study's assumptions and purpose) (p. 109):
  1. Variance in preferences produced by alternative frames.
  2. Variation in the correlation between framed preferences and relevant personal values. This assumes a value dimension represents the "true" preference, so low correlation means a large effect (Sniderman & Theriault).
  3. Comparison of treatment groups with a control group that gets only basic descriptive information (Druckman 2001a).
- Effects have been shown in experiments, surveys and case studies (p. 109). Examples: Kinder & Sanders show that an "undeserved advantage" frame raises the salience of racial resentment in whites' evaluations of affirmative action, while a "reverse discrimination" frame raises concern for group interests (pp. 109–110). Sniderman & Theriault show that attitudes to spending for the poor depend on how consequences are represented, without measuring changes in considerations (p. 110).
- All such studies assume the same model: framing changes attitudes by ostensibly altering the considerations used in evaluation (p. 110).

### How framing effects work (pp. 110–114)

**Mediational processes** (pp. 110–111).
- **Available:** the consideration must be stored in memory. Someone who does not understand free speech cannot be affected by a free-speech frame (p. 110).
- **Accessible:** its activation potential must exceed a threshold so that it is retrieved from long-term memory. Regular or recent exposure to a frame raises accessibility, through a passive, unconscious process (p. 110).
- **Applicable:** people sometimes use accessible considerations without deliberation (Higgins 1996). Other times they consciously judge applicability, so accessibility is not sufficient for influence. Perceived applicability rises with perceived strength or relevance of the frame (p. 110).
- Applicability evaluation occurs if one of two conditions holds: (1) people are sufficiently motivated, or (2) they are exposed to opposing considerations, which makes all individuals more motivated to evaluate consciously (pp. 110–111).
- Summary: framing can work at all three levels, by making new beliefs available, making available beliefs accessible, or making beliefs applicable or "strong" (p. 111).
- Nothing makes a strong frame inherently superior except its appeal to audiences. Strong frames can be built on exaggerations and lies, often rest on symbols, endorsements, and links to partisanship or ideology, and may work through heuristics. The strength, fairness and relevance of arguments must be judged separately (p. 111). Constitutional frames invoking Supreme Court rulings have disproportionate influence on civil-liberties attitudes (Chong 1993). Frames have also rallied people around racism and xenophobia (p. 111).
- Framing effects depend on a mix of frame strength, repetition, competitive environment and individual motivations. Under some conditions the aggregate impact of several frames differs from the sum of their separate effects (p. 111).

*Intuition note:* For a frame to work, the consideration must be understood (available) and must come to mind (accessible); when people judge consciously, it must also be judged relevant (applicable). Frames can work at any of these levels without being true or morally better.

**Moderators** (pp. 111–112).
- Predispositions such as values are perhaps the clearest limit. Strong predispositions generally reduce framing effects by increasing resistance to disconfirming information (Brewer 2001, gay rights) (p. 111). Even people with firm values are susceptible on new issues without a settled interpretation. Elite frames aim to appeal to the audience's partisan and ideological leanings: Bush's argument that social security is unfair to minorities connects a conservative policy to a liberal value, and may create cross-pressure for a liberal Democrat (p. 112).
- Knowledge: studies conflict, some finding stronger effects on the less knowledgeable, others the opposite. Druckman & Nelson (2003) argue that the conflict stems partly from failing to control for prior attitudes, since knowledgeable people tend to have entrenched priors. After controlling for priors, knowledge enhances framing effects because the frame's considerations are more likely available or comprehensible (p. 112).
- Other moderators: credible sources and frames invoking longstanding cultural values make shifts more likely. Prior access to deliberation (Price & Na 2000) or later conversation with people holding varied opinions (Druckman & Nelson 2003) mutes a frame's effect (p. 112).

**The effects of competition** (pp. 112–114).
- **Sniderman & Theriault's claim:** people often face competing frames, not one, and then choose the alternative consistent with their values: exposure to opposing sides increases consistency between policy decisions and principles (p. 112). They tested issues that are important, longstanding and competitively contested. Egalitarians were more likely to support spending, and growth advocates more likely to oppose it, when they got a dual message than when they got a one-sided message contrary to their values. The most knowledgeable were most value-consistent (pp. 112–113).
- **Authors' reply:** "we suspect" these results apply only when preferences are anchored by a core value dimension (e.g., freedom vs. order; individualism vs. egalitarianism). On new issues the public is uncertain, and each side may claim to fit the voters' core values (p. 113). In the Bush example, if egalitarians accepted Bush's egalitarian frame and ignored Democrats' counter-claim, they would be acting in accord with their values, yet the authors ask whether that shows immunity to framing; they say it would seem more accurate to conclude that the Bush strategy worked to perfection by appropriating a liberal value (p. 113).
- Little is known about frame dynamics in competition. Competing frames might cancel and reinforce values, push people in conflicting directions, or prompt careful evaluation (p. 113).
- **Authors' experiment:** participants evaluated a local urban growth boundary proposal, with arguments of varying strength on both sides. Preferences were stabilized by the relative priority placed on environment vs. economic growth. Participants also gauged the frames' relative strengths and were influenced more by the stronger frame (p. 113).
- The authors suggest that the strength of the opposition frame determines how far one is pulled from one's values, even when a value-congruent frame is present. If frames canceled, people given a congruent frame would agree with it equally whether the competing frame was strong or weak (p. 113).
- Either side can create alternative "home positions" for voters. A campaign generally cannot anchor all its supporters with a reassuring value-consistent frame. Some voters (extreme ideologues and partisans especially) may not be movable, but most voters may be partially susceptible to competing messages (pp. 113–114).

*Intuition note:* Values give people an anchor, but a strong opposing frame can still pull them away from it. The pull depends on how strong that frame is compared with the one that fits the person's values.

### Conceptual clarifications (pp. 114–116)
- **Emphasis (issue) vs equivalency (valence) frames** (Druckman). Equivalency effects occur when logically equivalent phrases change preferences (Tversky & Kahneman), e.g., "90% employment" vs "10% unemployment", "97% fat free" vs "3% fat". Emphasis frames highlight qualitatively different but possibly relevant considerations (free speech vs public safety). Unless more free speech logically entails less public safety, the two types cannot be equated. The perceived tradeoff may be "socio-logic" (p. 114). The psychological model should apply to both. With equivalency frames, a motivated person would presumably recognize the equivalence and be indifferent (p. 114).
- **Framing vs priming.** Iyengar & Kinder define priming as changes in the standards people use to make political evaluations, because news emphasizes some matters over others (e.g., defense news makes defense performance weigh more in presidential approval) (pp. 114–115). Psychologists define priming as a procedure that increases accessibility of a construct (Sherman et al.). Iyengar & Kinder and many others assume media emphasis passively increases accessibility, but Miller & Krosnick (2000) present evidence that it does not work through accessibility (p. 115).
- **Authors' view:** the model generalizes to priming if each $v_i$ is a separate issue dimension or image. Attention to an issue should raise its weight via accessibility and applicability. If this is correct, framing and priming effects share common processes and the terms can be used interchangeably (p. 115).
- **Framing vs persuasion** (Nelson & Oxley): framing = change in weight $w_i$; persuasion = change in evaluation $v_i$. Example: a communication makes economic considerations more important relative to environmental ones (framing); it changes beliefs about the project's economic consequences (persuasion) (p. 115). Little attention has gone to whether their moderators and mediators are similar (p. 115). Footnote 3 mentions agenda setting as a related concept (p. 115).
- The distinction is analytically useful, but many attitudes are fragmentary. Frames in communication therefore often introduce new considerations as well as highlighting existing ones (pp. 115–116). Example: a re-election incumbent must both convey a good economic record and convince voters that the economy should be the basis of judgment. The authors call such messages both persuasive and framing messages at once (p. 116).

### Directions for research (pp. 116–119)
- **Mediators and moderators:** beyond attitudes, frames affect belief content, open-ended responses, narratives, emotion and perceptions of public opinion. Relationships between these effects and their moderators remain unclear (p. 116).
- **What makes a strong frame?** Strong frames emerge from public discussion as the best rationales for contending positions. A typical strategy links a proposal to a widely available positive value, e.g., conservationists link land purchases to "clean drinking water" (p. 116). Value-based frames do not work on every issue: the environmental frame is strong in local growth debates but secondary in presidential elections (p. 116). Persuasion work says little about conditions of strength (p. 117). Gerber & Green found it made no difference whether turnout appeals centered on civic duty, neighborhood solidarity or closeness of the election. Civil-liberties studies find constitutional principles and attitudes toward groups are the primary frames (p. 117). Result: an inventory of specific findings but no general theory predicting which frame will be most applicable (p. 117).
- **Production of frames:** a model of elite framing strategies is needed. Entman's (2004) cascade model (at least on foreign policy): administration frames shape other elites, media and public. Public reaction feeds back to media and elites, who influence the administration's revised view. This determines whether one frame dominates or competing frames are at parity (p. 117). Influence is not exclusively one-way: frames are chosen with the audience in mind (p. 117).
- **Strategies of framing:** the social-movement literature is the most advanced, treating framing as a tactic of political entrepreneurs to coordinate individuals (Snow & Benford). It is largely separate from public-opinion work, though there are recent steps toward integration (pp. 117–118; fn. 4 notes work on candidate strategies, media choices and types of frames, p. 118).
- **Do citizens learn over time?**
  - Most studies use one session with effects tested soon after exposure. Future studies should use longer durations and streams of competing information, to examine learning, forgetting and possible inoculation (p. 118).
  - **Authors' normative expectations** ("we think", "we expect"): opinion should be malleable early, when people know little about the consequences. Susceptibility should fall after exposure to both sides. Competition makes accessible a more representative sample of considerations (pp. 118–119).
  - The authors say these conjectures remain untested (p. 119). Balanced debate can diminish framing effects only if citizens can learn over a campaign. An opinion that cannot be separated from the context where it was elicited ("doorstep opinion") is not a real opinion (p. 119).
  - Prior studies suggest only limited inoculation, except among the most knowledgeable. In civil-liberties rally experiments, one-sided frames still influence most people (more so the less knowledgeable), suggesting that alternative considerations do not come spontaneously to mind (p. 119).
  - Competition may only raise short-term accessibility. Lasting resistance needs long-term learning, e.g., independently generating counterarguments. Experimentally induced framing effects endure only briefly (Druckman & Nelson 2003). This may make them seem innocuous, but it may also mean that people are continually susceptible to the latest message (p. 119).

### Normative assessments of framing effects (pp. 120–121)
- Framing can be seen negatively (manipulation) or neutrally (learning shared beliefs, as in coordination around a social norm). In social-movement work, individuals overcome collective action problems by developing shared frames (p. 120).
- In public-opinion research it usually has a negative connotation: it suggests arbitrary preference distributions and elite manipulation. Researchers are reassured when deliberation or competition reduces framing effects (p. 120).
- **Authors' counterpoint:** framing effects are intrinsic to opinion formation, which involves selective acceptance and rejection of competing frames. They are a liability only if people never develop a basis for discriminating among frames and remain constantly vulnerable to changing representations of issues. Resistance is also problematic if it blocks recognizing good arguments. If debate can only remind people of existing values, persuasion is impossible and "Deliberation is pointless" (p. 120, quote).
- Strong attitudes help people recognize value-consistent sides but also promote motivated reasoning (Lodge & Taber 2000: evaluating information to support preconceptions and devalue contrary evidence). Strong-attitude holders are least susceptible to new information and most likely to counterargue (p. 120).
- Stability alone is "a misguided criterion" for quality, because stable attitudes can reflect sophisticated reasoning or dogmatism. Both excessive instability and excessive stability can be liabilities. The authors say it is "not apparent" which extreme is less desirable (pp. 120–121).
- Openness to contrary evidence should be fostered. Following Hirschman, the ideal is in the middle: informed opinions, tolerance of alternatives, and amenability to compelling arguments (p. 121).

### Summary and conclusion (pp. 121–122)
- Polling is now commonly used to test alternative ways of presenting proposals. To the extent people lack independent ideas, they are more easily manipulated (p. 121).
- The review suggests firm, full, articulate opinions are possible. Deliberation, discussion and exposure to alternative arguments can raise opinion quality by reducing ambivalence and uncertainty. Better-informed people are less swayed by others' framing (p. 121).
- Having opinions is "only half the problem": people must also be flexible. Becoming engaged can produce knee-jerk ideology as well as reflection (p. 121).
- The 1950s response to apathy (Berelson et al. 1954): the actual electorate may underpin a stable democracy better than an ideal highly engaged one, which might lead to constant factional dispute (p. 122). The authors ask how to get a more participatory democracy without an intransigent public. They cite a dependable finding that greater participants support democratic values such as free expression, whatever their ideology (p. 122).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An attitude toward a new housing project with two attributes: economy ($i=1$) and environment ($i=2$). Say $v_1=+0.8$ (good for economy) and $v_2=-0.6$ (bad for environment).
- Baseline weights $w=(0.5,0.5)$: $A = 0.5(0.8)+0.5(-0.6)=0.40-0.30=+0.10$ (mildly favorable).
- **Framing** (weights change, evaluations fixed). An economic frame gives $w=(0.8,0.2)$: $A=0.64-0.12=+0.52$. An environmental frame gives $w=(0.2,0.8)$: $A=0.16-0.48=-0.32$. The same beliefs give a supportive or an opposed attitude.
- **Persuasion** (evaluation changes, weights fixed). New information lowers $v_1$ to $+0.2$ at $w=(0.5,0.5)$: $A=0.10-0.30=-0.20$.
- **Competition** (rough illustration). Someone who gives the environment high priority starts near $w=(0.2,0.8)$ ($A=-0.32$). A strong economic frame might move the weights to $(0.6,0.4)$: $A=0.48-0.24=+0.24$. A weak one might move them only to $(0.3,0.7)$: $A=0.24-0.42=-0.18$. The stronger the opposing frame, the further the person moves from the value-anchored position.

## Concepts
- [[framing-effect]] — change in opinion caused by changing the emphasis or presentation of an issue; defined here via changed weights $w_i$ (pp. 104–105, 115)
- [[expectancy-value-model]] (new) — attitude $=\sum v_i w_i$, evaluations weighted by salience, $\sum w_i=1$ (p. 105)
- [[frame-in-thought]] (new) — the set of considerations an individual actually uses to evaluate an issue (pp. 105–106)
- [[frame-in-communication]] (new) — the key considerations emphasized in a speech act; tied to an issue and an evaluation (pp. 106, 107 fn. 2)
- [[emphasis-vs-equivalency-framing]] (new) — emphasis frames highlight different considerations; equivalency frames re-describe logically identical information (p. 114)
- [[availability-accessibility-applicability]] (new) — three levels at which framing can work: a consideration must be stored (available) and retrievable (accessible); perceived relevance (applicability) matters when people evaluate consciously (pp. 110–111)
- [[strong-frame]] (new) — a frame audiences find compelling or applicable; not necessarily truthful or morally better (pp. 111, 116)
- [[priming-agenda-setting]] — changes in the standards used to evaluate leaders after media emphasis; authors argue it shares processes with framing (pp. 114–115)
- [[motivated-reasoning]] — evaluating information to support preconceptions and devalue contrary evidence (Lodge & Taber); linked to strong attitudes (p. 120)
- [[heuristics-cues]] — strong frames may work through symbols, endorsements and partisan links rather than substance (p. 111)
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — "knowledge" as moderator; effects mixed unless prior attitudes are controlled (p. 112)
- [[text-as-data-validation]] — frame content analysis with dictionaries or human coders; intercoder reliability needed for manual coding (pp. 107–108)
- [[non-attitudes]] — survey answers that look random because no real opinion exists, as in the opening literature review (pp. 103–104)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — in the social-movement literature, shared frames help people overcome it (p. 120)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Weights $w_i$ over considerations ↔ spatial model of voting: voter salience on policy dimensions (p. 105 cites Enelow & Hinich) — both let importance vary across dimensions.
- [structural-analogy] Framing vs persuasion (weights vs evaluations) ↔ Receive–Accept–Sample model: considerations received, accepted and sampled — both treat opinion as drawn from available considerations.
- [metaphor] Strong frames work via cues and symbols ↔ heuristics/cues: shortcuts that substitute for substantive information.
- [structural-analogy] Framing as coordination around shared beliefs (p. 120) ↔ collective action problem: shared frames help groups act jointly.
- [structural-analogy] Motivated reasoning limiting framing effects ↔ identity-protective cognition: strong commitments increase resistance to uncongenial information.

## Questions this raises
- How can the "strength" of a frame be measured independently of its observed effect, so that the argument is not circular?
- Do effects survive across weeks and repeated exposure to competing campaign messages, as in real campaigns rather than single-session experiments?
- When is greater resistance to framing a sign of reasoning and when a sign of dogmatism, given that the authors say stability alone is misleading?
- How would frame production be modelled as strategic interaction among elites, media and public, beyond the cascade model?
