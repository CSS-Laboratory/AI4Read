# Tumbling Down into a Democratical Republick: "Pure Democracy" and the Pitfalls of Popular Control

## L0 — One line
Achen and Bartels argue that American reforms built on the "folk theory" (the idea that more direct popular control is the only cure) have often failed to empower ordinary citizens and have sometimes harmed them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what happened when Americans answered each failure of government with "more democracy." The authors sketch the rise of popular sovereignty in American culture. They then examine two reforms it inspired: the direct primary (with later "opening" of nominations) and the initiative and referendum. Their claim is that these reforms did not simply move power from professional politicians to ordinary people. Elite and organized-interest influence persisted. Voters with little time and information sometimes chose against their own interests (fluoridation, Proposition 13, fire services). The authors call the folk theory a flawed blueprint and end by noting an alternative view, taken up in later chapters: voters judging officials after the fact.

**Prerequisites:** the "folk theory of democracy" from chapter 2; basic US institutions (primaries, ballot measures, legislatures); percentages and simple before/after comparisons.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The Founders wanted a balance of popular influence and experienced leadership. Over two centuries, American political culture moved toward the folk theory, in which "more democracy" is the only trusted cure (pp. 52–53, 57–58, 66–67, 86–87).
- Direct primaries were sold as taking power from politicians and giving it to the people. Observers soon saw that organized groups adapted, and later reforms (McGovern-Fraser) produced unforeseen, less predictable nominations (pp. 61–64).
- Reform debates were never purely about principle. Candidates and factions backed whichever nominating method suited them, and the folk theory gave them a rhetorical edge (pp. 66–67).
- Initiative and referendum often won only pluralities, because many voters skipped these items ("roll-off"). Voters turned out much more for issues they cared about, like liquor (pp. 70–73).
- Cain argues that initiatives empower "election entrepreneurs." The authors say Matsusaka's finding that initiatives follow majority preferences is unsurprising in one sense, since any adopted measure must be majority-preferred, and that the range of policies sellable to a majority may be broad enough to leave room for special interests (pp. 73–76).
- Term-limit and open-primary reforms had unintended effects or missed their stated aims. In California's 2012 top-two primary voters lacked the "fine-grained knowledge" needed, and the Proposition 103 evidence suggests even relatively well-informed voters responded to extraneous factors (pp. 77–81).
- Case studies (Proposition 13, Illinois fire districts, New York assessors) show direct control can be costly and can favor the affluent. The authors do not say it is always undesirable (pp. 81–85).
- The folk theory treats representatives as instructed delegates. Many political scientists have turned to an alternative closer to the trustee model, in which elections let voters hold officials accountable afterwards; the authors set it out in chapter 4 (pp. 88–89).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 52–55)
**The Founders and the present.** The Declaration of Independence says governments derive their just powers from "the consent of the governed." The Founders also believed direct popular control would be dangerous (p. 52). Madison argued in Federalist 10 that representation would "refine and enlarge" public views, so that the public voice, spoken by representatives, may be "more consonant to the public good" than if spoken by the people themselves (p. 52). Today, the authors say, most Americans see democracy as rule by the people, as unambiguously good, and as curable only by more democracy: the folk theory is "triumphant" (p. 53).

**Reforms must look "more democratic."** The authors say this view reflects, and probably also feeds, periodic frustration with government. Reforms must be defended as more democratic than what they replace. Cain (2015) is quoted: American reform has tried to fix representative democracy with more chances for citizens to observe, participate and control (p. 53). Few respectable alternative theories exist, and self-interested politicians use the rhetoric too. Power has repeatedly been taken from parties and politicians and "bestowed" on voters, and the history is told as a happy ending (p. 53). Footnote 3: expanding popular control and extending the franchise are logically distinct. In the authors' view any defensible definition of democracy entails universal adult suffrage (p. 53).

**Two opening cases (voter confusion).** In the 1950s–60s many towns decided on fluoridating water. The evidence that fluoride reduced tooth decay was compelling (p. 54). Cities where administrators or councils decided adopted it overwhelmingly, but referendums voted it down 60% of the time. Cambridge, Massachusetts, voted three times: defeat, a narrow pass, then a decisive defeat (p. 54). Voters saved a few pennies in taxes and paid in dental bills. The authors' "simple answer" is that voters were confused: crackpots, rogue doctors and extreme right-wing groups opposed fluoridation, and many voters, including many college graduates, could not tell gurus from experts (p. 54). Childhood immunization is a parallel case. In states with easy exemptions, measles and pertussis outbreaks followed (p. 54).

**Plan.** The chapter treats the two most significant attempts to align practice with the folk theory: democratizing party nominations, and initiative and referendum at state and local level. In both, reformers believed cutting the influence of professional politicians would raise ordinary citizens' influence (pp. 54–55). New England town meetings are set aside because the evidence is thinner and scholarly opinion mixed (fn. 5, p. 55).

> **Intuition note.** The chapter's test is simple. If "more direct democracy" is a good cure, it should have raised ordinary people's influence and produced decisions that serve them. The authors look for evidence on both.

### The Triumph of Popular Sovereignty in American Political Culture (pp. 55–60)
**Antidemocratic roots.** At America's founding, respectable thought was antidemocratic. Shaftesbury said a monarchy needs an army or the nobility "or else the Monarch cannot long support itself from tumbling down into a Democratical Republick" (p. 55). Morgan adds that no one wanted England to tumble down there. Eighteenth-century theorists often meant a "virtual" popular consent with no practical limit on kings. Algernon Sidney was hanged in 1683 for notes on a right to depose rulers. Rousseau argued democracy was possible only in small states (p. 55).

**Colonial reality and the Founders.** Colonial popular sovereignty was "muscular." Rhode Island had bitterly fought elections, ballot-box fraud and disorderly assemblies (Middlekauff, p. 56). During the Stamp Act agitation, Crown representatives had homes burned and lives threatened, and other officials resigned (p. 56). Rhode Island was cited in the Federalist Papers as an example of unstable democracy (p. 56). Footnote 7 adds that Rhode Island was the last state to ratify the Constitution, after several referendums and elections. The Constitution reflected this heritage: Beard (1913) argued the Founders wanted to protect prosperous landowners (p. 56). It limited popular rule through separation of powers, federalism and indirect election of the president and Senate, balancing popular forces and experienced leadership (pp. 56–57). Franklin's "a republic, Madam, if you can keep it" suggests even a limited republic might be fatally unstable (p. 57). Morgan: the sovereignty of the people "has been filled with surprises for those who invoked it" (p. 57).

**Jackson to Tocqueville.** After the Revolution, the quasi-aristocratic Federalists were gone by the early 19th century. Jefferson became president in 1801, and "Democratic Republicans" dominated the next quarter century (p. 57). Elkins and McKitrick: norms suited to a capitalist, democratic, equalitarian culture were fully in place by about 1830 (p. 57). Jackson's inauguration seemed a new era. In the National Bank fight, everyone believed sovereignty belonged to the people (Schlesinger), and Tocqueville wrote that the people reign "as God reigns over the universe" (p. 58). States loosened property requirements for voting. Democrats had incentives to enfranchise immigrants and the poor, and Whigs often followed to avoid blame for obstruction (p. 58). The franchise extensions were a significant, if limited, step. Women and African-Americans were still excluded (p. 58).

**Civil War to Populists.** The Civil War brought a further set of democratizing reforms: African-Americans were constitutionally enfranchised and increasingly allowed to vote, though the South stepped back after Reconstruction ended in the 1870s (p. 58). Industrial expansion created "robber barons" who alienated farmers, miners and workers and controlled state legislatures such as Standard Oil's in California and Anaconda's in Montana (pp. 58–59). Economic contractions enhanced third parties. Farmers, miners and cotton farmers formed Populism, which ran a third-party presidential campaign in 1892. It was antimonopoly and, seeing itself in bondage to big-city machines, also anti-party and pro-direct democracy (p. 59). Bryan's 1896 fusion campaign of Democrats and Populists took 80% in Montana but lost nationally. The authors say Populists' "political naiveté" about party organization and coalition building proved costly (p. 59).

**Progressives.** Progressives saw boss control, big-city Catholics and crooked machines as the chief obstacle to progress. Their third party faded, but the progressive wings of both major parties, under Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson, enacted very limited versions of trust-busting, labor rights, the income tax and direct primaries (pp. 59–60).

**Section conclusion.** The Populists' and Progressives' democratic theory was the folk theory, and it gave reform a script with "fateful consequences" (p. 60). The authors grant that two dilemmas remain thorny: what is the role of parties in a democracy, and how should they be reformed when they stray (p. 60).

> **Intuition note.** The authors present the folk theory as a historical result, not a timeless truth. Early distrust of popular rule gave way to near-total faith in it as each reform wave tried to fix real abuses.

### "A Simple Faith in Democracy": Party Reform (pp. 60–68)
**Why nominations matter.** Elections can express "the will of the people" only within a fixed choice among candidates, usually those put forward by party organizations. Who chooses them? (p. 60) The Founders did not foresee parties. **Schattschneider's view:** voters' sovereignty is freedom to choose, like a consumer's in a market; "Democracy is not to be found in the parties but between the parties" (p. 60). The authors say this view has fared poorly in practice (pp. 60–61).

**Steps toward "rank-and-file" control.** Key (1964) says each major step in the nominating process was a further bow to the doctrine that the rank and file should prevail. Ranney notes earlier reforms replaced mass meetings with legislative caucuses, then "mixed" caucuses (p. 61). Jackson's second-term nomination by a national convention removed nominations from congressional caucuses. Lowell: the convention was adopted because the caucus seemed undemocratic (p. 61). About 80 years later the direct primary arrived, "the most radical of all the party reforms" (Ranney, p. 61). In 1900 La Follette promised a citizen's right to choose "by direct vote, without the intervention … of any political agency" and said no machine would stand between voter and official (p. 61). Key (1942): advocates had "a simple faith in democracy" that selected candidates would serve the people as a whole (p. 61).

**Early skeptics and early experience.** **Ford (1909):** politics is carried on by politicians, so reforms can only change which politicians gain advantage (p. 62). By 1910 an Iowa journalist said politicians controlled the machinery more than under the caucus. Key (1942) found that a small, cohesive group leads in organizing candidate support under any nominating process, and the party organization adapted (p. 62). The authors add that the controlling group has often been unelected, intense minorities or well-heeled private interests, and that simple citizens "were no match" (p. 62). Machines herded voters to the polls, and low turnout mocked reformers' image of a public-spirited citizen (Reynolds, pp. 62–63). Enthusiasm burned out by World War I, and three states briefly returned to conventions in the 1920s. The primary stayed because politicians who rose through it had no incentive to go back (p. 63). The authors add "simple institutional inertia" as another possible reason (p. 63).

**1968 and McGovern-Fraser.** The 1968 Democratic contest was turbulent, yet delegates, mostly chosen before the events, nominated Humphrey, who lost (p. 63). Reformers demanded more democratization, and the McGovern-Fraser Commission said popular control was necessary for the party's survival (p. 63). The share of delegates chosen in primaries rose from 40% (1968) to about 60% (1972) to over 70% (1976). Republicans followed, with 70% by 1976 (p. 63, fn. 8, p. 64). The authors say this seems not to have been intended or foreseen. Commissioner Ranney recalled that most commissioners preferred a reformed convention and expected more open caucuses to fade the demand for primaries, but "quite the opposite happened" (p. 64). Key (1964) had reported "second thoughts" favoring a legitimate party role in nominations. The authors say those thoughts proved fleeting (p. 64).

**Results.** The reforms made nominations more open, with less elite control and more unpredictability (p. 64). Examples:
- 1972: insiders favored Muskie, whose candidacy collapsed in New Hampshire, and McGovern won (p. 64).
- 1976: Jimmy Carter was not even listed among 31 potential Democratic candidates in a December 1974 Gallup poll (p. 64).
- 1976: Reagan challenged Ford strongly despite Ford's four-to-one advantage in insider endorsements (p. 64).
- 1980 and 1984: endorsement leaders (Carter, Reagan, Mondale) still had to survive substantial primary challenges (pp. 64–65).

**Disputed recovery.** **Cohen et al. (2008)** argued the disruptions were temporary: "by the 1980s, both parties were back on an even keel" (p. 65). **Authors' doubt:** elites failed to coordinate in three of the four most recent contests (fn. 9, p. 65). Footnote 9 gives the figures:
- 2004: only 5% of Democratic governors endorsed before the primaries.
- 2008 Republican endorsements: McCain 35%, Giuliani 32%, Romney 27%.
- 2008 Democratic endorsements: Clinton 44%, Edwards 43%, Obama 9%.

The authors concede Romney's early 2012 endorsements fit Cohen et al.'s emphasis that insiders winnow the field, but then popular sovereignty is limited if primary voters merely "ratify" (p. 65).

**Can primary voters choose well?** Lau (2013): in nominating contests voters do "much worse than voters in general election campaigns, often barely doing better than chance" in selecting the candidate who best represents their own values and priorities. In January 2012, early in a three-way Democratic race, barely 30% picked the best-matching candidate. With two candidates left (Clinton, Obama) it was 56%, "better than chance—but not by much" (pp. 65–66; see Reading notes on the date).

**Presidential quality.** The authors "know of no authoritative answer" on whether candidate quality changed. They use the average of four rankings (Schlesinger 1996, *Wall Street Journal* 2005, C-SPAN 2009, Siena 2010) (p. 66, fn. 11). On a 40-point scale, pre-reform presidents since World War II (Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, Johnson, Nixon) average 25.6 and post-reform ones (Carter, Reagan, G. H. W. Bush, Clinton) 19.7 (fn. 12, p. 66). Adding Ford would lower the post-reform average (fn. 12, p. 66). The authors conclude that by "this rough test, at least" the open system "has entailed real costs" and caveat the limits of such ratings (p. 66).

**Procedure and advantage.** Debates about procedure were entangled with advantage (p. 66):
- 1824: attitudes to the caucus correlated highly with candidate preference (Ranney, p. 66).
- 1832: conventions arrived because dominant factions thought they would ease things for them (p. 67).
- La Follette was inspired when Wisconsin bosses twice passed him over (p. 67).
- McGovern praised his commission's rules, then within a year wanted some repealed. An aide said "We were running for president then" (p. 67).
- Progressive-era primary enthusiasm rested partly on expectations of advantage for "the respectable element," well-established Protestants appalled by Catholic immigrants (p. 67).

Even so, the authors say these were not "raw power politics," because invocations of the folk theory gave reformers an advantage. American history has moved toward larger direct popular control (p. 67). **Other countries.** Spies and Kaiser note many Western European parties adopted more inclusive nominations with "unintended and undesired consequences." Comparisons find inclusive parties slightly less representative of voters than centralized ones (pp. 67–68).

> **Intuition note.** The primary was meant to take nominations from "bosses." The authors' account says organized groups adapted, later reforms had unplanned effects, and the authors find no sign of improved presidential quality (they know of no authoritative answer, p. 66) or of more representative parties in Europe (pp. 67–68).

### "The Absolute Sovereignty of the People": The Initiative and Referendum (pp. 68–73)
**Motives.** The movement aimed to circumvent legislatures as primaries circumvented bosses. Senator Bourne (1912): restore "the absolute sovereignty of the people" (p. 68). Lowell saw it as a result of distrust of legislatures, partly from real defects and partly from "popular exaggeration" (p. 68). Ford: legislatures were "despicable" (p. 68). Gilded Age legislatures were malapportioned, machine-influenced and corporate-controlled (p. 68). Some proponents called representative government a failure and wanted "pure democracy" (p. 68). Others saw direct democracy as an emergency expedient to be dropped later. Ford said Oregon leaders expected to keep it "in reserve" (p. 69). The authors note Oregon voters considered 107 statewide measures between 2000 and 2012 (p. 69).

**Competence and "political fiction."** Lowell suggested limiting referendums to general principles and facts the public knows, but offered no reliable way to tell such matters apart (p. 69). He argued turnout on a question is a measure of public interest. He said a majority of votes cast "may sometimes be a political fiction rather than a fact." Examples: constitutional amendments in Massachusetts and Michigan approved by fewer than 3% of those who went to the polls (p. 69). The authors say this is hard to reconcile with an initiative justified as restoring absolute sovereignty (p. 69).

**Table 3.1 (votes on direct-democracy measures, 1904–1912; p. 71).** "Roll-off" = percentage of voters who went to the polls but did not vote on the measure (p. 70). Yes and no are percentages of total turnout (p. 70).
- 17 measures in the table. Yes beat No in 16. The exception: Oregon 1910 (Yes 30.8, No 36.9) (p. 70).
- Averages: Yes 45.8%, No 18.2%, roll-off 35.9% (p. 71).
- A third or more skipped many measures. Colorado 1912 roll-offs were 64.6%, 68.5% and 63.4% (p. 71).
- Nevada 1912: the general initiative had Yes 49.7, No 5.1, roll-off 45.2, and recall had 48.1/5.9/46.0. Pluralities were eight- and ten-to-one, but neither reached an absolute majority of those at the polls (pp. 70–71).
- The authors say most measures adopted actually failed to win a majority of those who showed up (p. 70).
- Minnesota required an absolute majority at the polls. Despite large pluralities at two attempts, roll-off doomed it, and the effort lay dormant for over half a century (p. 70).

**Table 3.2 (15 liquor measures, 1904–1912; p. 72).** Averages: Yes 36.9%, No 46.3%, roll-off 16.8% (p. 72). On most, over 85% voted Yes or No, and average roll-off was less than half that of table 3.1 (p. 73). Examples: Maine 1911 repeal of prohibition failed narrowly (42.6 vs 43.1; roll-off 14.2). In Colorado 1912, more than twice as many voted on prohibition as on recall or nonpartisan ballots (pp. 72–73). **Authors' conclusion:** voters could be moved to vote on issues important in daily life, but "direct democracy" procedures did not rise to that level. Progressive-era citizens resembled the disengaged citizens of chapter 2 (p. 73).

> **Intuition note.** When many people skip a ballot item, "the vote" can reflect a smaller, more engaged group. Whether a majority of voters or a majority of those at the polls counts then matters for what we call popular will.

### "The People Themselves"? (pp. 73–79)
**Did power shift to ordinary citizens?** More than 20 states have experience with direct democracy (p. 73; fn. 14 reports 27 states with initiative or referendum, 21 with both). **Skeptic Hollingsworth (1912):** the initiative transfers lawmaking to "non-official lawmakers," self-appointed and irresponsible like machine bosses (p. 73). Oregon's W. S. U'Ren organized the People's Power League to back measures. Asked whether Oregonians always vote as he wants, he said, "They always have thus far" (p. 73). Later observers:
- Key and Crouch (1939): California initiators were mostly pressure organizations that had failed in the legislature (p. 74).
- Broder (2000): wealthy individuals and special interests learned to subvert the process, and many measures were designed by a handful of people (p. 74).
- Cain (2015): it empowers "election entrepreneurs" who formulate policy, organize ballot access and supply cues. "Average citizens will sporadically give input … organized interests are a constant presence" (p. 74).

**Matsusaka's counter-evidence.** Matsusaka (2004) challenges the idea that special interests use the initiative to thwart voters. Comparing fiscal policy in states with and without the initiative over three decades, he finds three differences (pp. 74–75):
1. The initiative reduces total government spending by about 4%.
2. It shifts spending from state to local governments.
3. It shifts revenue from taxes to user fees and charges, leaving "fewer possibilities for redistribution of wealth."

He argues each shift fits ordinary Americans' preferences (p. 75).

**Authors' reply.** The findings are in one sense unsurprising. Any measure adopted must be preferred by a majority of voters to the status quo (p. 75). But the range of policies sellable to a majority may be very broad, leaving room for wealthy interests via agenda-setting and framing. The tax reductions Matsusaka finds are "particularly likely" to benefit the prosperous disproportionately (p. 75). Such selective appeals are especially likely in narrow, complex, low-salience areas where preferences are unclear (p. 75; fn. 15 lists California and Oregon 2012 measures).

**Houston, 1997.** Opponents of the city council's rewording called it a heavy-handed way of obscuring principles, while supporters hailed the plain up-or-down wording (New York Times, quoted p. 76). **Authors' question:** if the opponents' original wording would have passed, would that result have been more or less legitimate? The doctrine of direct democracy "provides no sensible way to answer such questions" (p. 76).

**Majority will versus good policy.** Matsusaka (2004) himself said his evidence does not show whether the initiative is a good form of government, only that it promotes the majority's will (p. 76). **Lupia and Matsusaka (2004)** claim every study to date points to "higher-quality (or no worse) decisions." They concede it is hard to define a good policy objectively. Their evidence ranges from the modest (initiatives do not lead to prima facie irrational policy) to "heroically indirect," the authors say, citing Swiss research finding happier people in cantons with more direct democracy after controlling for income and demographics (p. 76).

**Term limits.** In the early 1990s voters in 20 of the 24 initiative states approved term limits. Nebraska approved them in 1992, 1994, 1996 and 2000 (p. 77). Courts struck some down (fn. 17). **Polsby (1990):** limits shift power to unelected officials, bureaucrats and influence peddlers. Surveys (Carey, Niemi and Powell 1998) found legislative leaders lost influence while governors, staffs and bureaucrats gained (p. 77). **Kousser (2005)** documents a "dismantling of state legislative professionalism": less deference to committees, a diminished role in budgets, and less innovative policies. Term limits did not fill legislatures with citizen members. Experienced politicians were mostly replaced by less experienced professional politicians moving up from local government (pp. 77–78).

**Open primaries.** Reformers next asked why nominees should be chosen by party members rather than the whole public (p. 78). California's 1996 blanket primary (later struck down by the Supreme Court): Bullock and Clinton (2011) found officials moved away from their party's extremists only in "the most moderate districts—of which there are increasingly fewer" (p. 78). McGhee et al. (2014): primary openness has "little, if any, effect" on extremity (p. 78). California's 2010 "top two" system advances the two leading vote-getters regardless of party. Proponent Keisling called it a "direct, simple fix" for hyper-partisanship (p. 78, fn. 18). In 2012 it seemed no more effective (*Los Angeles Times*: few centrists advanced) (p. 79). **Ahler, Citrin and Lenz (2013):** voters struggled to distinguish same-party candidates and challengers' positions, so even willing, moderate voters could not choose more centrist candidates (p. 79). **Authors' caution:** this does not prove the policy is bad, and more experience is needed. But backers' hopes were torpedoed by voters' lack of "fine-grained knowledge." Cain: populist reform fails when it overestimates citizen capacity and ignores intermediaries (p. 79).

### Citizen Competence and the Price of Popular Control (pp. 79–85)
**The "Man of Good Will."** Hofstadter (1955): the Progressives assumed a citizen who could inform himself in ample detail. Without that assumption the initiative, referendum and recall movement is "unintelligible" (p. 79). Chapter 2 found little evidence of such mastery (p. 79). Some political scientists are nonetheless confident about heuristics. **Lupia and Matsusaka (2004):** voters are competent to make the decisions direct democracy poses (p. 80). The authors ask whether this holds when real complexities are involved (p. 80).

**Lupia (1994).** This is the primary evidence for the optimistic view. An exit poll of 339 Los Angeles voters covered five 1988 auto insurance initiatives. He measured detailed knowledge and knowledge of the insurance industry's position on each. Low-knowledge voters who knew the industry's position voted like high-knowledge ones (p. 80). **Kuklinski and Quirk (2000):** "being relatively well informed falls far short of being well informed" (p. 80). The authors add that the relatively well-informed voters overwhelmingly backed a 20% auto-insurance rate rollback, a "superficially appealing but shortsighted (and, as it turned out, unworkable)" attempt to repeal supply and demand (p. 80; fn. 21 reports Proposition 103 mostly provided work for lawyers and actuaries, and rates stayed frozen for years). Footnote 20 adds (p. 80):
- The knowledge scale had 20 binary questions, and the median score was 12, only slightly better than chance.
- Almost 30% scored below 10 (worse than random guessing).
- The scale's effects on votes were modest and imprecisely estimated.

Even well-informed citizens responded to extraneous factors such as dislike of insurers and wishful thinking (p. 81).

**Bowler and Donovan (1998).** They conclude voters "make choices purposefully, using available information" in ways that make sense in terms of their values and interests (p. 81). The authors call this a very low standard of competence (one would not choose a pilot who proposed it). Broder cites Donovan and Bowler for the view that tax-limitation measures like Proposition 13 often reduce popular services below what the public wants (p. 81).

**Proposition 13.** Lupia and Matsusaka ignored this measure and Sears and Citrin's book-length study of it (p. 81). Sears and Citrin (1985) found (pp. 81–82):
- Little evidence that voters reasoned carefully from preferences to votes.
- Public employees' self-interest was not primarily why they opposed the tax revolt.
- Parents' preferences about school spending did not affect their Proposition 13 votes more than non-parents' did.
- Voters seemed to respond to symbols "in an affectively consistent but not necessarily logical" way.

They also found a persistent inconsistency: voters wanted "smaller government" and the same or more spending on specific services (p. 82). Four possible explanations were tested: belief in eliminating waste, sophistication, unpopular services such as welfare, and survey wish-fulfilment. None explained it away. It prevailed even among those who believed in small waste, the most sophisticated, and both liberals and conservatives (p. 82).

**Tessin (2009): fire protection in Illinois.** (Unpublished dissertation, summarized with permission, fn. 22, p. 82.)
- *Design.* Some counties required voter approval of property-tax levy increases above inflation: five Chicago suburban counties in 1991, Cook County in 1994, and 34 downstate counties over 13 years (p. 83). Tessin used geographic and timing variation across independent fire districts and excluded Chicago for lack of a comparable area (p. 83). Fire quality is relatively uncontroversial and expert measures exist, and affected and unaffected downstate districts were similar (p. 83).
- *Revenue.* With controls, revenue grew 3.9% a year with the requirement versus 6.2% without. The typical household's property tax bill rose $1.07 versus $1.50, which is 43 cents less per year (p. 83).
- *Quality.* A retired fire chief said constrained districts cut training and deferred equipment maintenance. Average response time across all Illinois districts was 11.5 minutes, versus a widely accepted 4–5 minute standard. Districts with the requirement were almost one minute (about 7%) slower (p. 83). Tessin called this "a serious decline in public safety" (p. 84). The gap was about 45 seconds in the first four years and about 80 seconds after, presumably cumulative (p. 84).
- *Rational trade-off?* Tessin considered whether voters rationally traded lower taxes for slower response. He noted most citizens want to maintain or improve fire services even when told about trade-offs, and the tax savings may have been smaller than the savings on homeowners insurance with better fire protection. He concluded voters with more power over inputs "produced worse outcomes" than under the counterfactual with more official independence (p. 84).

**Oakland Hills, 1991.** The firestorm destroyed over 3,000 homes, killed 25 and seriously injured over 150 (Simon and Dooling) (p. 84). According to FEMA, drought and winds made firefighting difficult, but Proposition 13 (1978) exacerbated it by eliminating firefighting companies, cutting staffing and cutting funds to remove dead trees (p. 84). Detailed voting data for the area are unavailable, but Piedmont, at the fire's edge, voted 73% for Proposition 13, so victims had probably voted heavily for it (p. 84).

**Work-arounds.** Tessin found cases with no visible decline. Voter-approval rules for sewer and prison bonds had no apparent ill effects (p. 84). Officials quietly used workarounds such as user fees and unguaranteed bonds, which cost more through higher interest and legal fees. Voters thus "pay a price for a largely symbolic form of popular control" (Tessin, p. 85).

**Sances (2015): New York property assessors.** Hundreds of New York towns switched from electing to appointing assessors over 25 years, under state pressure (p. 85, fn. 23). The comparison was within the same towns before and after, allowing for persistent town differences and yearly statewide fluctuations (p. 85). Direct election of assessors reduced the probability of reassessment in a given year by 9 percentage points, raising the average interval from about five to nine years. It increased the advantage in effective tax rates of the most expensive homes over the least expensive by 26 percentage points (p. 85). The authors say greater "electoral accountability" here "mostly seems to have involved" catering to an affluent (and probably more attentive) minority (p. 85).

**Lesson.** The authors do not say direct popular control is always undesirable. They say it is often costly, sometimes "frighteningly costly," and a culture that equates "good government" with "more democracy" cannot weigh costs against benefits (p. 85).

> **Intuition note.** On complex issues, the studies the authors cite find voters responding to symbols and extraneous factors and sometimes choosing against their own stated aims (pp. 80–84). Tessin found that officials' work-arounds could limit the damage, but not at zero cost (p. 85).

### Conclusion (pp. 86–89)
**Folk theory as blueprint.** The authors call it "hopelessly flawed." Primaries and referendums with no party or legislative admixture produced "a mishmash" of responsiveness to popular impulses, behind-the-scenes elite influence and self-defeating choices (p. 86). They say this is not a claim that American politics is sufficiently democratized: they suspect government would work better if the poor were better organized and more secure, turnout higher and more equal, and campaigns publicly financed. In Cain's terms these are cases where "populist" and "pluralist" impulses coincide (p. 86).

**Founders and Beard.** The Founders believed in both leadership and popular influence, with balance correcting the flaws of each. They opposed both kingship and majoritarianism (p. 86). The authors say Beard's reminder of the Founders' wealth made it psychologically easy to dismiss their republican principles as elitist. This allows us to avoid the hard task of reconciling those ideas with our simplicities (p. 86).

**One cure.** The authors compare this to prescribing powerful antibiotics for every illness: sometimes cure, sometimes harmless waste, sometimes harm (p. 87). **Merriam and Overacker (1928):** they say most objections to primaries apply equally to elections, universal suffrage and the whole plan of democracy, and they describe "disbelievers in popular government" as asserting that the mass should "abdicate in favor of the few" (p. 87). The authors call this shrill and morally certain, and note it conflicts with the same book's earlier qualms about voters (fn. 24, p. 87). "More democracy" was once a good idea and appealing ideal against elite domination. It cured many evils (despotism, corrupt aristocracies, suffrage restrictions). But with no recipe for reconciling popular control with competent leadership, the cure was applied in every crisis (pp. 87–88).

**Representation models.** The "populist" view treats representatives as unfortunate substitutes for direct democracy, necessitated by the cost of frequent plebiscites, and as instructed delegates who mechanically vote district preferences as measured in polls (p. 88). Technology is lowering that cost: the Capitol Bells smartphone app lets constituents "vote" on House bills in real time (p. 88). The authors see little reason to think this would improve legislating (p. 88). **The alternative:** Burke and Madison favored the trustee model, in which officials use their own judgment about the best interests of district and country. A YouGov survey (May 2012) found 55.6% agreed that "the will of the people on most issues is pretty clear, and politicians should just follow it" (23.5% strongly), and 19.5% disagreed (3.1% strongly) (p. 88, fn. 26).

**Political science's benchmark.** Following Miller and Stokes (1963), scholars have mostly studied representation by measuring how faithfully officials follow constituents, often implicitly accepting the instructed-delegate benchmark. Trustee ideas have declined partly because "judgment" and "best interests" are harder to measure (p. 89). Chapter 2 found these studies produce mixed results: officials' positions are usually only mildly correlated with constituents', and whether the gaps fit a trustee model is "simply unknown" (p. 89). Many scholars therefore favor a conception closer to the trustee model, in which elections let voters hold officials accountable after the fact. The authors set it out in chapter 4 and assess it in chapters 5–7 (p. 89).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Question.** Why can "the people approved it" be ambiguous when many voters skip a ballot item?

Suppose 2,000 people go to the polls. On ballot measure A, 900 vote Yes, 150 vote No, and 950 leave it blank.
- Roll-off = 950 / 2,000 = 47.5%.
- Yes as a share of turnout = 900 / 2,000 = 45.0%. No = 150 / 2,000 = 7.5%. Check: 45.0 + 7.5 + 47.5 = 100.
- Yes-to-No ratio = 900 / 150 = 6 to 1.
- Yes as a share of those who voted on A = 900 / 1,050 ≈ 85.7%.
- Under a plurality rule (more Yes than No), A passes.
- Under the Minnesota-style rule (absolute majority of those at the polls, more than 1,000 Yes), A fails, because 900 < 1,000.

The same tally gives "6 to 1 landslide" or "defeated," depending on the rule. The text's check: Nevada 1912's general initiative had Yes 49.7, No 5.1. That is about 9.7 to 1 (49.7 / 5.1), but 49.7 < 50, so no absolute majority of turnout (p. 71). A second check: guessing on 20 binary questions gives an expected score of 20 × 0.5 = 10. That is why a median of 12 is "only slightly better than chance" (fn. 20, p. 80).

## Concepts
- [[folk-theory-of-democracy]] — idealized view that informed citizens rule directly and "more democracy" is the cure for political ills; the guide for Populist and Progressive reform (pp. 53, 60)
- [[institutions]] — here, political rules such as nominating procedures, initiatives and referendums, judged by whether they actually shift power (pp. 54–55)
- [[principal-agent-problem]] — officials' relation to voters is not modeled as this in the chapter; it contrasts the instructed-delegate and trustee conceptions of representation (pp. 88–89)
- [[heuristics-cues]] — shortcuts such as knowing the industry's position; the authors judge the optimistic evidence insufficient (pp. 80–81)
- [[framing-effect]] — outcomes may depend on question wording (Houston affirmative action) (pp. 75–76)
- [[agenda-control]] — wealthy interests can shape which policies reach a majority, "through agenda-setting and framing" (p. 75)
- [[retrospective-voting]] — in the chapter, the alternative view that elections hold officials accountable after the fact (p. 89)
- [[direct-primary]] (new) — nomination by direct vote of rank-and-file voters instead of caucus or convention (p. 61)
- [[initiative-and-referendum]] (new) — procedures letting voters make or unmake laws directly (p. 68)
- [[roll-off]] (new) — share of voters at the polls who do not vote on a given measure (p. 70)
- [[election-entrepreneur]] (new) — Cain's term for actors who formulate, organize and sell ballot measures (p. 74)
- [[instructed-delegate-vs-trustee]] (new) — representatives vote constituents' measured preferences versus using own judgment for their interests (p. 88)
- [[populist-vs-pluralist-reform]] (new) — Cain's contrast between reform to increase popular control and reform that empowers intermediaries or groups; the authors say they sometimes coincide (p. 86)

## Canonical instances
- [[fluoridation-referendums]] — cities voted it down 60% of the time when referendums were held (p. 54)
- [[mcgovern-fraser-reforms]] — primaries rose from 40% to over 70% of delegates, an outcome the authors say was unforeseen (pp. 63–64)
- [[lau-correct-voting]] — primary voters barely beat chance in picking the best-matching candidate (pp. 65–66)
- [[oregon-direct-democracy-uren]] — organized group backs measures; "They always have thus far" (p. 73)
- [[houston-affirmative-action-referendum]] — wording could change the result (pp. 75–76)
- [[term-limit-initiatives]] — shifted influence to governors, staff and bureaucrats; weakened legislatures (pp. 77–78)
- [[california-top-two-primary]] — ineffective at electing centrists in 2012 (pp. 78–79)
- [[lupia-1994-auto-insurance]] — cue-taking evidence and the authors' critique, including Proposition 103 (p. 80)
- [[proposition-13-sears-citrin]] — "something for nothing" and symbolic voting (pp. 81–82)
- [[tessin-illinois-fire-districts]] — referendum requirement tied to lower revenue growth and slower response times (pp. 82–84)
- [[oakland-hills-firestorm]] — tied by the authors to Proposition 13 cuts (p. 84)
- [[sances-new-york-assessors]] — elected assessors reassess less often and favor expensive homes (p. 85)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Instructed delegate vs trustee ↔ principal–agent / delegation: the representative as agent either tracks the principal's stated wishes or uses discretion, with monitoring and measurement as the trade-off.
- [structural-analogy] Roll-off and rules requiring a majority of turnout ↔ social choice: what counts as the "majority" depends on the aggregation rule and the participating population.
- [structural-analogy] Election entrepreneurs and organized interests ↔ collective action problem: concentrated, constant actors can organize while dispersed citizens give only sporadic input.
- [structural-analogy] Sances's before/after town comparison and Tessin's geographic/temporal variation ↔ causal inference (difference-in-differences style designs): each compares units with and without a rule while allowing for stable differences.
- [metaphor] "Antibiotics for every illness" (p. 87) ↔ policy dose-response: an all-purpose remedy applied without checking the diagnosis.

## Questions this raises
- If voters cannot judge policy details, why should they be better at judging officials after the fact (the chapter 4 alternative)?
- Do the harms found (Illinois fire, Proposition 13) generalize, or are they specific to tax-and-service trade-offs?
- How could one design cues or intermediaries (parties, endorsements) so that open nominations do not simply hand influence to organized minorities?
- Can the "legitimacy" of a popular vote be judged without first deciding which wording or turnout rule counts?
