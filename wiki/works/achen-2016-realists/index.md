# Achen, C. H., & Bartels, L. M. (2016). *Democracy for Realists: Why Elections Do Not Produce Responsive Government*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. doi:10.1515/9781400882731

## In one paragraph
Achen and Bartels test the usual picture of democracy, in which informed voters choose policies and governments follow. They argue that this "folk theory" fails. Voters know little, their opinions are unstable, and their policy views often follow their party. The fallback idea, retrospective voting, also fails as a form of accountability. Voters punish incumbents for events no government controls, such as shark attacks and droughts. They also react mostly to income growth in the months just before the election. In its place the authors put a group and identity theory. People choose parties that fit their social identities, and policy agreement is mostly a by-product. Elections still bring real but limited benefits. The authors say a realist theory of democracy must give a central place to group power and inequality.

## The path through the work
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch01-democratic]] — The authors argue that the "folk theory" of democracy (informed voters choose policy and governments follow) is a comforting fiction that the evidence contradicts, and they announce a group- and identity-based view in its place.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch02-elusive]] — The folk theory says elections make government follow majority preferences, but this fails on logic (multidimensional spatial models and Arrow's theorem) and on evidence (voters' weak, unstable, party-driven opinions, and weak links between opinion, votes and policy).
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch03-tumbling]] — Achen and Bartels argue that American reforms built on the "folk theory" (the idea that more direct popular control is the only cure) have often failed to empower ordinary citizens and have sometimes harmed them.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch04-rational]] — Retrospective voting (rewarding or punishing incumbents, the current office-holders, for results) can in theory select good leaders and discipline them, but its effectiveness depends on how precisely voters can discern leaders' contribution to their well-being, and even relatively small amounts of randomness can significantly degrade it.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch05-blind]] — Voters punish incumbents for bad times that no government could control (sharks, droughts, floods), so retrospective voting is natural but not clearly a reliable form of accountability.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch06-musical]] — US presidential voters respond almost only to income growth in the last six months before the election, which gives little sign of competence-based selection, gives politicians a reason to manipulate election-year income, and makes the most important single factor in who wins essentially arbitrary from the standpoint of accountability.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch07-chicken]] — Achen and Bartels argue that the New Deal realignment (a lasting shift in which party most voters support), and other Depression-era electoral shifts, came in significant part from voters' short-sighted reactions to election-year income rather than from a verdict on ideology or policy.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch08-very]] — Achen and Bartels argue that democratic theory should rest on the older realist, group-based view of political psychology, in which social identities come before policy preferences, and not on the rationalist "folk theory" of the individual voter.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch09-partisan]] — Achen and Bartels argue that most voters pick and change parties because of who they are (group loyalties and social identities), not because of policy or ideology, and that policy views often follow party.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch10-it]] — Party identification often shapes what citizens think the parties stand for, what they believe are facts, and even their unrelated issue views; this consistency feels like thinking, and it is often strongest among the well-informed.
- [[achen-2016-realists/ch11-groups]] — Voters mostly follow group and party identities rather than policy judgments or sound retrospection; elections still bring real but limited benefits, and the authors argue that a realist theory of democracy will have to give a central place to group power and inequality.
- [[achen-2016-realists/app-retrospective]] — Two simple formal models show that a voter who judges incumbents by past well-being learns less, or disciplines less, the more random luck clouds that well-being.

## Big ideas across units
- **The folk theory fails as an account of democracy.** It is announced in ch01, tested by logic and survey evidence in ch02, and traced through "more democracy" reforms in ch03. It reappears in ch08 and ch10, and ch11 gives the final verdict. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch08, ch10, ch11.
- **Retrospective voting is the fallback, but it offers weak accountability.** The theory of selection and sanctioning is set out in ch04, tested on disasters in ch05, on time horizons in ch06 and on the Depression in ch07. The formal models are in the appendix, and ch11 summarizes the failure. Units: ch04, ch05, ch06, ch07, app-retrospective, ch11.
- **Retrospection is blind and myopic.** Voters punish events no government controls (ch05). They weight only the last months of a term (ch06). The "skipping tally" carries election-year income into lasting party loyalties (ch07). Units: ch05, ch06, ch07, ch11.
- **Randomness limits what voters can learn or enforce.** Luck in well-being weakens both selection and sanctioning (ch04, appendix). Short, volatile windows tell voters little about competence (ch06). Units: ch04, ch06, app-retrospective.
- **Citizens' opinions are weak, unstable and follow party.** Issue voting is often persuasion or projection, and heuristics and aggregation do not save the day (ch02). Opinions follow party in the abortion and southern cases (ch09). Partisans misperceive parties and facts (ch10). Units: ch02, ch09, ch10, ch11.
- **Group identity comes before policy.** The realist group tradition (ch08) is tested in historical episodes (ch09). Party identification shapes beliefs (ch10). It becomes the book's positive theory of partisanship (ch11). Units: ch01, ch08, ch09, ch10, ch11.
- **Rationalization feels like thinking.** Partisan consistency is often strongest among the well-informed. Units: ch09, ch10, ch11.
- **Elections look like musical chairs, and realignments may be accidents of timing.** The key factor in who wins is arbitrary for accountability (ch06). The New Deal realignment is read as short-sighted income voting (ch07). Elections are described as "musical chairs" (ch11). Units: ch06, ch07, ch11.
- **Democracy still has limited benefits, and power and inequality matter.** Ch01 sets the question of democracy's vague prestige. Ch11 names the real benefits and calls for less inequality and less money in politics. Units: ch01, ch11.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch01 (the framing and the plan), ch02 (the case against the folk theory), ch05 (blind retrospection), and ch06 (myopic retrospection). The catalog focus is chs. 1–2 and 5–6, with ch05 as the highlight. Ch11 gives the conclusion.
- **Dependencies:**
  - Ch02 builds on ch01's folk theory, and ch03 builds on ch02.
  - Ch04 needs the spatial model from ch02. Ch05 and ch06 both need ch04's retrospective-voting ideas, and ch07 builds on ch06.
  - Ch08 builds on the folk theory and retrospective voting from earlier chapters, and ch09 builds on ch08's group theory. Ch10 develops party identification.
  - Ch11 draws on all the earlier claims.
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch03 (American reform case studies), ch07 (the detailed Depression and Alberta evidence), and the appendix (formal models behind ch04, needing basic calculus and probability). Read ch04 for its verbal argument, and use the appendix only if you want the formal version.
- **Alternative route:** to see the positive theory, go from ch01 to ch08, ch09, ch10 and ch11.
