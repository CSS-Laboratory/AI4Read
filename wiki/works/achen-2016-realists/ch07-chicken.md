# A Chicken in Every Pot: Ideology and Retrospection in the Great Depression

## L0 — One line
Achen and Bartels argue that the New Deal realignment (a lasting shift in which party most voters support), and other Depression-era electoral shifts, came in significant part from voters' short-sighted reactions to election-year income rather than from a verdict on ideology or policy.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Conventional accounts treat the New Deal realignment as voters ratifying Roosevelt's policies and ideology. The chapter asks whether Depression voters really judged policy. Using state- and district-level income and vote data from the 1930s, comparisons with other democracies, and the case of Alberta, the authors argue that voters rewarded or punished incumbents for election-year income changes, largely ignoring earlier gains and specific policies. These myopic judgments were then carried forward into lasting party loyalties, a "skipping tally" counting only election years. The authors conclude that realignments may look ideological while resting on the accident of election timing. This challenges the folk theory of democracy (the "romantic image of the people" the chapter doubts).

**Prerequisites:** reading regression tables (coefficient, standard error, adjusted R²); percentage change as a difference of logs; the idea of "retrospective voting" from chapter 6; party identification as a lasting loyalty.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Challenge to the standard story.** The conventional view (Key, Sundquist, Hershey, Skocpol) sees 1936 as a policy-based "ratification" of the New Deal. The authors say voters behaved as in ordinary times: punish incumbents when things get worse, reward them when things get better (pp. 178–181).
- **State-level evidence for 1936.** Across 37 non-southern states, each extra point of 1936 income growth raised Roosevelt's vote by about 0.36 points. Income growth in 1933–35 had little or no effect, so voters were myopic (pp. 185–187).
- **Little sign of ideology.** Poor, farm, immigrant and black populations did not shift toward Roosevelt in the way an ideological story predicts. Demographic controls changed little (p. 188).
- **Counterfactual.** With the 1938 economy, the authors' rough simulation puts Roosevelt at 46.2% outside the South and 258 of 531 electoral votes. They say this would have ended the New Deal realignment prematurely (pp. 189–191).
- **Congress and the "skipping tally."** Election-year income had sizable estimated effects on House votes in 1936, 1938 and 1940, but none discernible in 1934. In the cumulative model the estimated effects carried forward undiminished (persistence estimate 1.02, meaning earlier effects carry over in full; its standard error leaves room for some discounting), while off-year income had rather little effect. The authors conclude that only election years mattered (pp. 193, 198–199).
- **Comparative test.** Across eight democracies, incumbents of widely varying ideology were driven out with impressive consistency, and new incumbents who presided over recoveries were rewarded with long runs in office. The ideological reading of US voters "does not turn out to travel well" (pp. 200–205).
- **Extreme case: Alberta.** Albertans embraced the untried Social Credit Party, whose platform critics called unconstitutional and unworkable, and destroyed the incumbent, welfare-minded United Farmers of Alberta (UFA). In neighbouring Saskatchewan the Liberals won in 1934, while the socialist Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF) flourished and governed from 1944 (pp. 205–211).
- **Implication.** Realignments can look like ideological judgments, but the evidence for that reading is weak (p. 212).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 177–178)
**Conventional account.** The authors first state the standard story. Real income per capita fell 28% from 1929 to 1932. Roosevelt won 57% in 1932, and the Democrats won 313 of 435 House seats. Real incomes then rose by one-third in his first term and unemployment fell by one-third. In 1936 he won more than 60% and 46 of 48 states, and the Democrats dominated Congress for six decades (pp. 177–178). **Key's view:** 1936 "ratified a sharp turn in public policy" (p. 178).

**Authors' aim.** To challenge this reading with electoral and economic data. Voters in the Depression "behaved in much the same myopic retrospective way" as in chapter 6. The apparent triumph of folk democratic theory was, they say, "an accident" of election dates and economic swings. Policies were "mostly irrelevant" (p. 178).

### The Roosevelt Elections (pp. 178–181)
- **1932.** **Key (1947):** the campaign gave no clear policy alternatives apart from prohibition. All classes moved to the Democrats, wanting change without insisting on what the "new deal" was (pp. 178–179). Footnote 2 notes that one contemporary account credited the return of beer with restoring confidence in Roosevelt (p. 179).
- **1936.** Roosevelt ran well to the left of 1932. Gallup found 45% of respondents, and 83% of Republicans, agreeing that his policies "may lead to dictatorship". He still won a landslide (p. 179). **Key (1958)** read this as "a popular ratification of the broad features of new public policy"; **Sundquist** spoke of voters "attracted by the Democratic program" (pp. 179–180).
- **Why the conventional view might expect non-myopic voters.** **Skocpol** and **Hershey** suggest that big policy debates and extreme distress may have focused voters on policy and remedies (p. 180). **Conventional ideological reading:** voters punished Hoover's orthodoxy in 1930 and 1932, rewarded early New Deal progressivism, and "tapped the ideological brakes" in 1938. **Sundquist** explained the 1938 Republican gains as independents rebelling against "Democratic excesses" (pp. 180–181).

### Myopic Retrospection in the Roosevelt Realignment (pp. 181–191)
**Authors' alternative.** Voters responded to the economic collapse "in much the same way that they respond to ordinary economic vicissitudes" (p. 181).

**Figure 7.1 (income and incumbent vote, 1928–1940).** The pattern matches the retrospective story:
- Republicans lost in 1930 and were repudiated in 1932.
- Roosevelt took office at almost exactly the low point of the Depression.
- Democrats gained in 1934 and 1936 during three years of recovery.
- In 1938 the economy turned sharply worse and Democrats lost ground, ending well behind 1932.
- In 1939–40 the recovery resumed and Democrats regained some ground. They still held fewer seats than in 1933 and fewer than Republicans in 1929 (pp. 181–182).
- Footnote 4 gives US unemployment: 3.2% (1929), 23.6% (1932), 24.9% (1933), 14.3% (1937), 19.0% (1938), 14.6% (1940) (p. 181).

The authors call this correlation "suggestive" but "far from definitive" (pp. 182–183).

**Data and design.** The Depression gave the first standardized state-level economic data, and state conditions varied widely.
- In 1936, real personal income per capita rose 18% or more in Colorado, Delaware and Nevada.
- It fell 8% in Iowa, 14% in North Dakota and 23% in South Dakota (p. 183).

Choices made:
- **Focus on 1936.** It is the key case for the ideological reading (p. 183).
- **Sample.** Southern (Confederate) states are excluded. The authors give the Solid South's suppressed black vote, limited two-party competition and low white turnout as reasons (p. 183).
- **1932 not studied.** No clean partisan baseline exists. 1928 was distorted by Al Smith's Catholicism, 1924 by La Follette's candidacy, and the economic data begin only in 1929 (fn. 6, p. 183).
- **Contemporaries saw the link.** Farley and Hopkins were accused of buying the election with relief funds. Transfers jumped in 1936 but not in 1937–40 (pp. 183–184, fn. 8). The authors could not isolate relief; 1936 transfers had "no discernible effect" beyond their contribution to income (fn. 7, p. 184).
- **Variables.** Income change is the difference of logged income from the preceding year, times 100 (fn. 9, p. 184). They use real personal income rather than GDP, since chapter 6 suggested voters respond more to income (p. 184). Prices are deflated with the urban CPI. The choice of index matters little, because any alternative would add roughly a constant (fn. 10, p. 184).
- **Control for partisanship.** They use the 1932 vote, not an average of earlier elections. The 1936–1932 turnout-weighted correlation of Democratic margins is .65, versus .13 to −.02 for 1936 with 1928, 1924 and 1920 (fn. 11, p. 184).

**Figure 7.2.** Across 37 non-southern states there is a strong, fairly consistent tendency for Roosevelt to gain where income grew in 1936 and lose where it fell. Excluding thinly populated farm states would make the line steeper (p. 185, fn. 12). The authors call this "quite consistent" with their myopic, nonideological interpretation.

*Column 1.* The 1932 vote is a regressor, rather than modelling the change, to allow for erosion in states that were heavily Democratic in 1932. Turnout change is included because Roosevelt is said to have appealed to new voters (p. 187).
- Each extra point of 1936 state income growth raised Roosevelt's share by "more than one-third" of a point. This implies differences of up to 16 points across states (p. 187). *(added step: $0.36\times$ a range of about 44 income points $\approx 16$.)*
- The model's average error is under 3 points. Roosevelt's fortunes depended heavily on local economic conditions, over and above any nationwide recovery effect (p. 187).

*Column 2.* Adding 1933–35 income changes gives estimates near zero, while the 1936 effect stays strong. This matches chapter 6's evidence of voter myopia (p. 187). In 1934–35 real income per capita rose 17%, recouping over half of the earlier loss, yet "he got little or no credit at the polls" (p. 187). The authors hedge on responsibility: "Whether he was truly responsible or not."

*Turnout.* The turnout coefficients imply Roosevelt's share was 10–15 points higher among new voters than among old voters, who gave him almost 60% (p. 188). This is consistent with **Campbell et al. (1960)**. Fn. 14 says new voters probably added about 5 points to the "normal" Democratic presidential vote across the decade, and that he also gained from converted Republicans (p. 188, fn. 14).

*Columns 3–4: demographics.* If poor people, farmers, immigrants and African-Americans had responded especially to the New Deal, states with more of them should have moved toward Roosevelt. "For the most part … the reverse seems to be true." Effects are small and imprecise; where there is a pattern, Roosevelt gained less among these groups (p. 188). The authors add that many in these groups were already Democrats, and that "unusually clear evidence of the Democrats' new ideology favoring them" seems to have made no difference (p. 188). Controls leave the economic result substantially unchanged. The election-year effect stays substantial though less precise, and earlier-year effects stay much smaller (pp. 188–189). The result is consistent across specifications, estimation methods and samples; dropping outliers improves fit but the pattern is "just the same—only myopic economic considerations mattered" (p. 189). Outliers are noted in fn. 17: Montana gained despite falling income (the authors suggest wheat prices and silver policy), while Oklahoma, Missouri and Maine lost ground despite growth (p. 189).

*Intuition note.* Voters seem to judge the incumbent on how their own state's income moved in the election year. Earlier growth, even large, left no visible mark, so the recovery that began in 1933 earned little credit.

**Historical simulation (Table 7.2).** The question: what if Roosevelt had faced a 1938-type economy in 1936? Real income fell over 6% in 1938 (p. 189).

| | 1936 actual | 1938 simulated |
|---|---|---|
| National election-year real income change (%) | +11.2 | −6.4 |
| Roosevelt vote share, non-South (%) | 58.8 | 46.2 |
| States carried (incl. South) | 46/48 | 29/48 |
| Electoral votes (incl. South) | 523/531 | 258/531 |

*Method.* Table 7.1 gives only state-level effects. The national economy does not vary across states, so its effect cannot be measured directly. Rough calculations from aggregate vote shifts, and from modern data, suggest that the national effect is probably similar to the state effect, so the total economic effect is about twice the table's coefficient (p. 189–190).
- Footnote 18's evidence: Hoover's two-party share fell 18 points when real income fell 17% in 1932. Roosevelt's rose 3.4 points when income grew 11% in 1936. A 28-point difference in income growth went with a 21-point difference in vote gain, about three-fourths of a point per income point, roughly double the state-level estimate (p. 190, fn. 18).
- Postwar data also suggest a total effect about twice the state effect. The per-point shift seems larger postwar, "perhaps" because small shifts draw more attention now (fn. 18).
- The authors therefore assume the state-level 1936 coefficient applies to both state and national changes. Concretely, each state's hypothetical share is the actual share minus [(state 1936 − state 1938 growth) + (national 1936 − national 1938 growth)] × the average election-year coefficient from Table 7.1 (fn. 19, p. 190).

*Result.* Roosevelt would have got about 46% outside the South and "probably" lost 17 of his 46 states, including New York, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Ohio and Michigan. Even with the Solid South he would have fallen short of an Electoral College majority (pp. 190–191). Footnote 20 says the pivotal state would be New Jersey, projected at 47.4% (p. 191). The authors call such a calculation "far from exact" (p. 189).

*Intuition note.* The realignment's survival depended on a lucky election-year economy. A different economic year would probably have cut it short.

**Section conclusion.** In their interpretation voters "rendered no meaningful verdict on the substance of New Deal policies" (p. 191). **Krugman (2014)**, citing Gallup, notes that voters after 1936 favoured a balanced budget by over 2-to-1 (p. 191). Voters focused on concrete conditions in the election year. Earlier income growth was "water under the bridge". The realignment depended on a positive answer to "What have you done for us lately?" (p. 191).

### Congressional Elections and Short-Term Retrospections (pp. 191–196)
**Problem.** The legacy of the New Deal was durable Democratic House majorities (Democrats held the House for 58 of the next 62 years). Many scholars see this as ideological or programmatic. **Campbell et al.** credit New Deal welfare legislation and Roosevelt's personality. **Green, Palmquist and Schickler** call it an exceptional period of "unusually clear ideological divide" (p. 192).

**Authors' claim.** The realignment resulted "in significant part" from accumulating myopic assessments in successive election years. A short-run effect does not by itself imply long-run effects. Voters might be focused only on the present, so the long-term link must be shown, and it does not take the form some theories (Fiorina 1981; Achen 1992) predict (p. 192).

*Caveats.*
- Economic data exist only at the state level and are used as proxies for districts. This "no doubt induces non-negligible measurement error", so the results are "suggestive rather than definitive". The similarity to the presidential results gives some reassurance (fn. 21, p. 193).
- Excluded: multimember and at-large seats, some California and Pennsylvania cases with cross-filed candidates, and Minnesota and Wisconsin (third parties dominant) (fn. 23, p. 194). Standard errors are clustered by state to allow for correlated factors such as gubernatorial coattails (fn. 24, p. 194).
- 1940 is a stopping point because war increasingly distracted voters from domestic policy (fn. 22, p. 193).

*Findings.*
- No discernible election-year effect in 1934. The effect is sizable in the other three elections, and the average across the four (.31) is comparable to the presidential estimate (p. 194).
- Off-year income had little impact, an average effect only half as large (p. 194).
- Turnout and incumbency were positive in 1934 and 1936 and then ebbed (p. 194).
- In 1938 the election-year effect is of roughly average size and the previous-vote coefficient is "exactly 1.0", implying a stable party balance (p. 194).

**Figure 7.3 (1938).** Axes are the same as figure 7.2 for comparability, though 1938 had less variation. Only five sparsely populated western states gained income. Most states lost 5–10%, and Michigan and Delaware lost over 15% (p. 195). The summary line is "noticeably less steep" than in 1936, yet the relationship accounts for differences of up to 6 points in expected Democratic vote (pp. 195–196).

*Outliers have no ideological logic.*
- Democrats gained slightly or held steady in Massachusetts, Maryland, Maine, Kentucky and Arizona. These states would "hardly be expected" to lead support for the "second New Deal".
- North Dakota and Montana slipped most; both were hit by a wheat-price bust. Wheat fell from $0.35 per bushel (1932) to $1.14 (1936) and back to $0.45 (1938) (p. 196, fn. 25).

*Demographics.* They had "unimpressive and inconsistent" effects. Richer states became more Democratic in 1936 but less in 1940, and states with many black residents seem to have become more Democratic in 1938 but less Democratic in 1934. The authors call this "puzzling" for ideological accounts, with the caveat that these data are also state-level only (p. 196, fn. 26). Controls change little: the average election-year effect fell from .326 to .291, and the average prior-year effect from .153 to .085 (fn. 27, pp. 196–197).

### Partisan Realignment and the Cumulation of Short-Term Retrospections (pp. 197–199)
**Question.** Are economic retrospections absorbed into durable party loyalties, or are they short-term blips? **Fiorina (1981)** called partisanship a "running tally" of retrospective judgments (p. 197). Here 80% or more of the Democratic share carries over from one election to the next (p. 197).

**Design.**
- The 1932 congressional vote is the fixed baseline for partisanship over the decade.
- Turnout and incumbency changes are measured against that baseline.
- Election-year and prior-year income changes enter as before. Income changes from earlier cycles (from 1933) may persist through partisan loyalty.
- A **single persistence parameter** gives the proportional effect of earlier economic retrospections in each later cycle, since persistent effects are expected to be proportional to the original ones (p. 197).
- Estimation is by nonlinear seemingly unrelated regression (fn. 29, p. 198). The sample is 255 districts (about 10% fewer than Table 7.3), weighted by average turnout (fn. 28, p. 197).

*Results.*
- Election-year income growth had a significant impact. Prior-year growth had "rather little effect" (p. 198).
- The persistence estimate implies myopic retrospection "carried forward undiminished" (p. 198).
- The standard error allows some discounting, but even at the low end of the plausible range persistence is substantial (fn. 30, p. 198).
- The constrained model fits less well than the separate models, increasingly so later. The authors attribute this to its ignoring noneconomic partisan change after 1932 (fn. 29, p. 198).

**Interpretation.** This "odd durable emphasis on economic experience in even-numbered years" is hard to square with rational updating. Conventional accounts of rational partisan updating imply that voters rely on "a simple average of past benefits" (**Achen 1992**) or smoothly weight past experience in proportion to its current relevance (**Gerber and Green 1998**) (p. 198). It follows naturally from myopic updating at elections. Good times in 1936 affected 1938 because the 1936 support carried over; good times in 1935 mattered much less because 1935 was not an election year (p. 198).

**Conclusion of section.** Myopic retrospections dominated in the short run and in the long run through durable partisan attachments. The result is "not quite the 'running tally' … but more like a *skipping* tally. Only election years mattered" (p. 199).

*Intuition note.* Think of a loyalty score that is updated only at elections. Whatever happens in that year is added to it, and it then barely decays. Good years between elections are mostly not written into the score (the estimated off-year effect is small, not zero).

### The U.S. Experience in Comparative Perspective (pp. 200–205)
**Claim.** Incumbent governments of widely varying ideology were driven out when the world economy fell into depression. In almost every case, the new incumbents then enjoyed stable majorities for a decade or more (p. 200). Such long-term shifts are hard to attribute to ideological judgments, given the diversity of the policies embraced and the irrelevance of Depression-era policies to later problems. Instead they resemble a "moving average" of reactions (**Berelson, Lazarsfeld and McPhee 1954**, quoted p. 200).

**Figure 7.4** (real GDP per capita, eight countries, 1929 = 100; Maddison data, with acknowledged measurement error): Britain and Sweden had mild declines then steady growth. Germany and France had steeper declines, then rapid growth in Germany and stagnation in France. Australia had a sharp 1930–31 drop then steady increase. Ireland had little fluctuation. The US and Canada had the deepest and longest depressions, with output down 30% and one-third respectively and no full rebound until 1940 (p. 200). Footnote 31 gives unemployment: US 3.2% to 24.9% (1929–33), Canada 4.2% to 26.5% (p. 200).

**The US case looks ideological:** a conservative government was replaced by a liberal one, and real income per capita rose 46% from 1932 to 1940 (p. 201). The authors say the story is "a good deal less simple" abroad. Cases (pp. 202–204):
- **Canada.** Liberals were punished in 1930 and the Conservatives took over. Real income kept falling and unemployment kept rising through 1933. The Conservatives were punished more severely in 1935, though conditions had improved (unemployment still near 20%). Liberals won nearly three-fourths of the seats and were returned in 1940, 1945, 1949 and 1953 before falling in 1957.
- **Australia.** The economy fell in 1930–31 under Labor. Labor's vote fell from 49% (46 seats) in 1929 to 27% (14 seats) in 1931. The United Australia Party won with 40%, the economy improved, and the UAP held power through three subsequent elections.
- **Britain.** An orthodox Labour minority government was followed by the 1931 multiparty National Government, which won the 1931 election, with 470 of 615 seats held by the Conservative Party (the remnant of Labour won 52). The Conservatives won again in 1935 after two years of growth and stayed in power until 1945.
- **Ireland.** Fianna Fáil came to power in 1932 after poor harvests and won a majority in 1933 after a rebound. It governed 15 years and dominated Irish politics until 2011.
- **Sweden.** A Conservative government was replaced by a Liberal one in 1930. Unemployment rose from 12% to 22% over two years. The Social Democrats won in 1932 and, with a brief 1936 interlude, held power until 1976.
- **France.** Under the Third Republic's coalition politics, accountability was "greatly complicated". The 1932 defeat of the incumbents produced unstable governments, then the 1936 Popular Front (57%) was hampered by internal friction. Output regained its 1929 level only in 1939, mostly due to armaments.
- **Germany.** Unemployment doubled from 15% to 30% between 1930 and 1932, and Nazi support rose from 18% (September 1930) to 37% (July 1932). Hitler became chancellor in January 1933, and parties were banned by July 1933. Unemployment then fell and GDP rose rapidly, in large part through a military buildup.

**Lessons the authors draw (pp. 204–205).**
- Where conservatives held office they were often replaced by liberals or socialists (US, Sweden). Where leftist governments held office they were often replaced by conservative ones (Britain, Australia).
- In Ireland, where the party system was built around noneconomic issues, the "outs" cannot sensibly be classified left–right.
- Where two major parties both had to stand for reelection in the worst years (Canada, Sweden), voters rejected both in turn.
- Where coalition politics diluted responsibility (France, Germany), voters turned to unstable coalitions, fringe parties or the streets.
- "There is no consistent ideological logic" outside the US. Voters usually like the incumbent's ideology when incomes rise, but become ripe for defection "to anyone who promised to bring home the poultry" when incomes erode (p. 205).

### "A Promise of Salvation": Social Credit Sweeps Alberta (pp. 205–211)
**Why this case.** Other cases might show only sensible ideological inconsistency. The authors argue that voters under distress may embrace even proposals that are not respectable, a case of "blind retrospection" (p. 205). They add nothing to existing scholarship (Irving 1959) and use it as illustration (p. 205). Footnote 33 notes the Townsend Plan in California as a similar economic nostrum (p. 205).

**Background.** The agrarian populist United Farmers of Alberta (UFA) had governed since 1921 and was effectively an agrarian socialist governing party by 1935, linked to the national Co-operative Commonwealth Federation (CCF). The Liberals were the main mainstream opposition with about 25% in each of the previous two elections (p. 206).

**Social Credit.**
- The movement was led by William Aberhart, a preacher, educator and radio broadcaster, using the Calgary Prophetic Bible Institute. He was seen by followers as a "Man of God". It became a party only months before the 1935 election (p. 206).
- Methods: study groups, parades, open-air meetings, and radio broadcasts featuring a "Man from Mars" confused by "poverty in the midst of plenty" (p. 207).
- Platform: $25 monthly Basic Dividends per citizen, Just Prices, and suspension of foreclosures pending interest-free provincial loans. Critics said the dividends were unconstitutional and inflationary (p. 207). Fn. 34 quotes an opponent pamphlet: dividends would need $120,000,000 a year against about $15,000,000 in provincial taxes (fn. 34, p. 208).
- Interviews showed followers did not understand the technical mechanisms and left them to Aberhart and "the experts" (**Irving**, quoted p. 207). One editor excused such acceptance: "desperate diseases require desperate remedies" (p. 207).
- Social Credit's appeal was partly that its explanation of the Depression accentuated hostility to the existing system. Party attachments were weak: Liberals and Conservatives together got under 40% in 1930 (p. 208, fn. 35). The UFA was "old in the sense that it had been tried" (**Morton**, quoted p. 208). The UFA's defence was that it had steered Alberta through the Depression better than any other government, and that its powers were constrained. Voters "were clearly not impressed" (p. 208). A young man overheard after a UFA rally said Social Credit was "no darn good" but he would vote for it anyway (pp. 208–209).

**Outcome.** Social Credit won 56 of 63 seats and the UFA none. Voters who lined up for $25 the next morning were disappointed (p. 209). Social Credit won nine consecutive elections and governed more than 35 years. The UFA never again won 5% of seats, "the proponent of all the social welfare programs" voters supposedly needed (pp. 209–210; figure 7.5, p. 209).

**Saskatchewan contrast.** The Conservatives collapsed in 1934 and the Liberals won, also in 1938. Meanwhile the CCF, the UFA's agrarian-socialist ally, flourished and governed from 1944 to 1964, as North America's first avowedly socialist government. **Lipset (1968)** noted there is no adequate explanation of such different reactions in two similar provinces (p. 210). **Bennett and Krueger (1968)** said farmers there voted for parties that furnished solutions "regardless of ideological doctrine" (pp. 210–211). The authors add that those parties were "associated with solutions" only in that they were in office when conditions improved (p. 211).

### Conclusion (pp. 211–212)
- **Irving (1959)** said Alberta's experience corrects "the abstract concepts of the classical philosophers of democracy". The authors agree that these cases "cast profound doubts" on the romantic image of "the people" in the folk theory of democracy (p. 211).
- Voters are busy, and politics is complex even for full-time participants. Not knowing the best policies, voters ask whether events "have gone well or badly lately" and vote that myopic judgment (p. 211).
- The 1945 loss of Churchill is offered as an example. The authors suggest the voters' minds were on the housing shortage and that they "voted for a change" (p. 211). (The Churchill quotation is a rhetorical imagined reply, not a source claim.)
- When the election-year economy is dreadful, voters are likely to feel more strongly about their retrospections. This forms strong partisan aversions to incumbents and, if times improve, strong attachments to their successors. These preferences endure and are passed on to children, imprinting the party system (pp. 211–212).
- Voters then adopt more or less coherent ideological explanations, and the lucky party's policies are enacted. Political scientists and historians may read the result as a momentous ideological judgment, but "the evidence for such interpretations is weak" (p. 212). "Political insiders debate policy alternatives, but the electorate votes on a very different basis" (p. 212).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**A. Simulating a different economy (the method of Table 7.2).** Use a state coefficient of 0.36 (Table 7.1, column 1) and assume (as the authors do) that it also applies to the national change. Suppose State X actually had +12% election-year income growth and a 1936 vote share of 55%.
- Hypothetical state growth under the "bad" economy: −8%. State difference: $12-(-8)=20$.
- National difference (from Table 7.2): $11.2-(-6.4)=17.6$.
- Vote change: $-(0.36\times 20 + 0.36\times 17.6) = -(7.2+6.336) = -13.536$.
- Hypothetical share: $55-13.536\approx 41.5\%$.

**B. A "skipping tally" versus a "running tally."** Use invented weights: 0.15 for election-year income change, 0.04 for off-year change, with full persistence. Income changes: 1934 (election) +6, 1935 (off) +10, 1936 (election) +12, 1937 (off) +4, 1938 (election) −6.
- Skipping tally: $0.15(6)+0.04(10)+0.15(12)+0.04(4)+0.15(-6)=0.9+0.4+1.8+0.16-0.9=2.36$.
- Running tally (every year weighted 0.15): $0.15(6+10+12+4-6)=0.15\times 26=3.9$.
- The skipping tally gives off-year growth little weight, so the same economic history yields a smaller accumulated shift.

## Concepts
- [[retrospective-voting]] — voting on how conditions have gone, not on policy promises; here applied to election-year income (p. 181)
- [[myopic-retrospection]] (new) — retrospective judgment that counts only recent, election-year conditions (pp. 178, 187)
- [[folk-theory-of-democracy]] — the romantic image of "the people" that the authors say these cases cast doubt on (p. 211)
- [[realignment]] (new) — durable shift in the balance of party support, here the New Deal shift (p. 177)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Skipping tally ↔ political science: Fiorina's running-tally party identification — both are accumulations of retrospection, but with different weights by year.
- [metaphor] Election-year-only updating ↔ behavioral science: recency-biased or "peak-end" memory (canon link; not stated in this unit).
- [metaphor] "A chicken in every pot" ↔ public choice: the incumbent's reward for delivering private goods at election time.

## Questions this raises
- If voters only reward election-year income, how can incumbents strategically time economic gains, and is that what Farley and Hopkins were accused of?
- The authors assume the national economic effect equals the state-level effect. How sensitive is the 1938 simulation to that assumption?
- Why did similar provinces (Alberta, Saskatchewan) go in such different directions? The authors offer no deeper explanation beyond office-holding during improvement.
- Does a persistence estimate of 1.02 (SE 0.11) really rule out discounting of past retrospections?
