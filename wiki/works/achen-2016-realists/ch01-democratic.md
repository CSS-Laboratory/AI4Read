# Democratic Ideals and Realities

## L0 — One line
The authors argue that the "folk theory" of democracy (informed voters choose policy and governments follow) is a comforting fiction that the evidence contradicts, and they announce a group- and identity-based view in its place.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether the usual picture of democracy, in which citizens' preferences become government policy, survives modern evidence. The authors say it does not. They separate a populist ideal from a retrospective-voting model. They argue that both fail empirically, and they preview a third, group-based view in which social identities and partisan loyalties drive political behavior. They compare popular sovereignty to the divine right of kings: a fiction that lends legitimacy and stability. The chapter then maps chapters 2–11. It matters because it separates democracy's near-universal prestige from its vague meaning, and both from the claim that voters actually steer policy.

**Prerequisites:** basic idea of elections and parties; what a survey is; no mathematics needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The "folk theory" says voters hold preferences, choose leaders or policies accordingly, and so the majority rules. The authors say evidence has undercut it (pp. 1–2).
- They test two models: the populist ideal (representative or direct) and Schumpeter's leader-selection model, whose main modern form is retrospective voting. They say both fail on inspection (pp. 2–4).
- Almost everyone endorses "democracy," but people mean very different things by it. Dahl's demanding criteria are admittedly unmet by any state, and the authors think none could meet them (pp. 4–7).
- A "critical tradition" (Bryce, Lowell, Schumpeter, Lippmann, Niebuhr) saw voter limits early. Survey research (Columbia, Michigan, Converse) later gave the same picture, which was harder to dismiss (pp. 9–12).
- The book's plan: no policy-based control in chapters 2–3, blind and myopic retrospection in chapters 4–7, group and identity theory in chapters 8–10, and implications in chapter 11 (pp. 13–18).
- The authors say group and partisan loyalties, not policy preferences or ideology, are fundamental in democratic politics (p. 18).
- Popular sovereignty is likened to the divine right of kings: a fiction in which failures can always be attributed to the people being misadvised or the people's will being deflected (pp. 18–20).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 1–2)
**The folk theory.** In the conventional view, ordinary people have preferences about government action. They choose leaders who will act on them, or enact them directly in referendums. Either way the majority's wish becomes policy. Legitimacy comes from consent ("of the people, by the people, and for the people"). The authors call this a "folk theory": accessible, appealing ideas assuring people that their government is ethically defensible and has their interests at heart (p. 1).

**The authors' counter-claim.** Scientific evidence, they say, shows that most citizens pay little attention to politics. At election time they are swayed by "the nature of the times," especially the economy, and by loyalties typically acquired in childhood (p. 1). Those loyalties, not facts or policy, are the main drivers of behavior. From the viewpoint of current democratic theory, election outcomes are "largely random events." They are well determined by powerful forces, but not the ones theory says should decide them (p. 2).

**Stance.** The aim is not to turn liberals into conservatives. It is to show both liberals and conservatives that their mental framework can "now be maintained only by willful denial" of credible evidence (p. 2).

*Intuition note:* On p. 2 the authors do not call elections undetermined: they say powerful forces determine them, but not the forces that democratic theory says should (mood, economy and loyalty rather than voters' policy judgments).

### Two Contemporary Approaches to Democracy (pp. 2–4)
1. **Populist ideal.** Ordinary citizens determine policy (Dahl 1998, 37–38). It rests on Enlightenment ideas about human nature and 19th-century British liberalism, and it underlies Fourth-of-July rhetoric (pp. 2–3). It has two versions:
   - Representative: the public "decide[s] issues through the election of individuals who are to assemble in order to carry out its will." This is Schumpeter's wording, and he was an unsympathetic critic of the account (p. 3).
   - Direct: initiative and referendum.
   
   The authors say the assumptions behind both are "highly unrealistic" (p. 3).
2. **Leader-selection model.** Schumpeter defined the democratic method as the arrangement in which individuals gain power by "a competitive struggle for the people's vote." For him, democracy means only that people can accept or refuse their rulers, not that they rule (p. 3). He said little about how voters should choose. The most influential later account is the **retrospective theory of voting**, in which voters appraise "past events, past performance, and past actions" (Key 1966, 61; p. 3). Outcomes then depend on approval of incumbents' performance, not on ideas. It appeals to skeptics because it demands less of voters. The authors' metaphor: citizens drive "by looking in the rearview mirror." They say this works about as well in government as on a highway, and that this model also "crumbles upon empirical inspection" (p. 4).
3. **Preview of the alternative.** Even the most informed voters typically choose on the basis of "who they are," that is, their social identities. These shape how they think and where they belong in the party system. If so, elections do not produce popular control of policy (p. 4).

Footnote 2 says the two models do not exhaust the meanings of democracy. Deliberative, participatory and face-to-face traditions are not the focus, because they seem to the authors less relevant to national-scale politics than the models considered here (p. 2). Footnote 3 reports that Pateman denied any "classical theorists" exist, though some family resemblance can be extracted from Bentham and James Mill (p. 3).

### But Isn't Democracy Doing Just Fine? (pp. 4–9)
**Survey evidence of prestige.** The World Values Survey (6th wave, 2010–2014) asked how important it is to live in a democracy (1–10). Majorities in many countries said 10. The US averages 8.4, "unremarkable" (p. 4). A second question asked how democratically the country is governed. In every country, perceived reality falls short of the ideal (figure 1.1, pp. 5–6). Perceptions are still "surprisingly robust" in Rwanda, Malaysia and Kazakhstan, and Chinese respondents are "virtually indistinguishable" from Americans on both questions (p. 6).

**Agreement is mostly verbal.** The authors say the agreement does not extend much beyond the use of the word "democracy." Western, communist, fascist and dictatorial governments mean very different things by it, as the *Yes, Prime Minister* exchange about "People's Democratic Republic of East Yemen" satirizes (p. 6). Scholarly criteria also vary, and may run to half a dozen or more items (p. 6).

**Dahl's criteria.** Dahl (1971) stressed continued responsiveness to citizens' preferences. Later (1998) he listed five criteria: effective participation, voting equality, enlightened understanding, control of the agenda, inclusion of adults. He wrote that "each is necessary" for political equality (p. 6).
- Authors' view: no government meets them all, and "no possible government could." Dahl himself conceded none has done so and none is likely to (p. 7).
- **Dahl's defense:** the criteria remain useful standards and guides for reform (p. 7).
- **Authors' reply:** that defense works only if the ideals are not too unrealistic. They cite Wallas's analogy of the medical treatise on an "ideal man" who needs no food, and Lippmann's "fat man" trying to be a ballet dancer (p. 7). Naive ideals poorly guide policy and distract from attainable incremental improvements (p. 7). Footnote 6 cautions that Wallas is sometimes unreliable (p. 7).

**Citizens' own ambivalence.** In the US, 46% said it is "absolutely important" to live in a democracy, but only 7% said the country is governed in a "completely democratic" manner (p. 8). A 2011 Pew survey found 72% rating democratic government a "very important" factor in national success (fn. 8). On the American National Election Studies item, 69% in 2008 said government is "run by a few big interests" and 29% said "for the benefit of all." Across 16 surveys from 1974 to 2008 the average was 65% to 29% (fn. 9) (p. 8). Authors' summary: people are "dreamily idealistic and grimly pessimistic" (p. 8).

**Whitman.** In 1847 Whitman exalted democracy's "great future." In 1871 (*Democratic Vistas*) he admitted "appalling dangers of universal suffrage" yet kept "unshaken faith" in the masses (pp. 8–9). The authors say thinkers and scholars alike combine enthusiasm for democracy with a realistic view of practice. They usually ignore the contradiction or blame corrupt leaders or faulty institutions (p. 9).

*Intuition note:* Near-universal praise for "democracy" shows the label is popular, not that people share a theory. The authors treat the gap between ideal and reality as a warning sign about the ideal.

### The Critical Tradition (pp. 9–12)
**Why citizens are inattentive.** People are busy with work, family and troubles. Sorting out the right foreign policy toward Asia is not a priority, and "nor should they" engage in much deliberation without neglecting more important obligations (p. 9).

**Early critics.**
- *Bryce (1894):* the beliefs of nineteen in twenty people reduce to prejudices, loyalties to a leader or party, and catchwords. The authors think he was right about widespread inattention. A century of political science has "done remarkably little" to change the picture (p. 10).
- *Lowell (1913):* he rejected theories that assume the multitude is omniscient, and reforms that presuppose a radical change in human nature (p. 10).
- *Schumpeter (1942):* citizens drop "to a lower level of mental performance" in politics (p. 10).
- *Lippmann:* political beliefs are stereotypes and simplifications, because the real environment is too big, complex and fleeting for direct acquaintance. We must reconstruct it on a simpler model (pp. 10–11). The authors call him the deepest critic of the folk theory's psychological foundations (p. 11).
- *Niebuhr:* judgment is also warped by self-interest and the will to power. Idealistic justifications of democracy can cover for those who profit from its distortions (p. 11).

These writers were dismissed as elitist and cynical, and some shared the racial and religious prejudices of their time. By the 1950s–60s they were little read (p. 11).

**Survey research.** New tools made the findings harder to dismiss. Columbia (Lazarsfeld et al.) and Michigan (Campbell et al.) painted a bleak picture of "habitual, socially determined" behavior (p. 11). Converse (1964) used random national samples and found that citizens' "belief systems" are generally thin, disorganized and ideologically incoherent. Few scholars disputed that central point (pp. 11–12). The authors say chapter 2 will show his argument is "if anything" better supported fifty years later. Even well-informed citizens' ideological thinking often mechanically reflects what favorite group and party leaders told them (p. 12).

**Scholars' ambivalence.** Many scholars end their books hoping that institutional reform, civic education, better media, mobilization of the poor, or moral exhortation will close the gap. In "sober moments" most admit these prescriptions have repeatedly failed (p. 12). The retreat to Churchill's line ("the worst form of government except all those others") is, the authors say, "no defense at all of the folk theory" (p. 12). Footnote 10 says arguments that democracy promotes freedom, development and wealth face hard causal questions. Even if the effects exist, the authors say this line of argument generally does not speak to how democracy matters or how it should be organized (p. 12).

### The Plan of This Book (pp. 12–18)
**Method.** The book first documents the collapse of conventional thinking, then points toward firmer foundations (pp. 12–13). Unlike a treatise on "ideal boys," it tests ideals rather than merely affirming them (p. 13). Dahl (1956) is cited: one needs empirical facts before deciding what rules to follow (p. 13). Evidence is mainly American, from Wilson's reelection (1916) to Obama's (2012). The cases are the New Deal realignment, the South after the 1960s, Watergate, and baby boomers' views on abortion (p. 13). The authors believe the findings are likely relevant elsewhere, and "as best we can tell" history and culture do not change the central conclusions (p. 13).

**Chapter previews** (all statements are the authors' announced claims):
- *Ch. 2, popular sovereignty.* Kinder: if citizens reasoned ideologically, prospects for democratic control would improve. The spatial model (Downs; Enelow and Hinich) shows how (p. 13). Converse found most Americans "thoroughly innocent of ideology," largely sustained since (p. 14). Citizens of other advanced democracies seem similar. The authors conclude the populist ideal of electoral democracy is "largely irrelevant in practice," leaving officials "mostly free" to follow their own notions or party and interest-group pressures (p. 14).
- *Ch. 3, direct democracy.* Reforms rest on the Progressive adage "the cure for the ills of democracy is more democracy" (p. 14). Examples: the 1970s Democratic nominating reforms and the resulting spread of primaries. These made nominations "more democratic" in crude populist terms but diluted political professionals' influence (pp. 14–15). Initiatives and referendums have mostly empowered "millionaires and interest groups" (Broder), and Illinois voters cut fire district budgets, degrading fire protection (Tessin) (p. 15). Footnote 12 notes Cohen et al.'s argument that professionals have since regained much control (p. 15).
- *Ch. 4, retrospective voting:* its logic and implications (p. 15).
- *Ch. 5, responsibility.* Voters punish incumbents for droughts, floods and shark attacks, which suggests their judgments of credit and blame are highly circumscribed; "in that case" retrospection "will be blind" and accountability "greatly attenuated" (p. 15).
- *Ch. 6, economic voting.* Voters reward or punish real income growth but are myopic. They focus almost entirely on the months just before the election and nearly discount the whole term (p. 16).
- *Ch. 7, Great Depression.* Voters behaved much as at other times, with short memories and little regard for ideology or policy (p. 16).
- *Conclusion from 4–7.* Outcomes are "mostly just erratic reflections of the current balance of partisan loyalties." In a competitive two-party system, the choice is "essentially a coin toss." The retrospective model "will not bear the normative weight" its proponents want (p. 16).
- *Ch. 8, group theory of democracy.* Citizens are first of all members of social groups, whose identities shape loyalties and behavior (pp. 16–17).
- *Ch. 9, three cases.* (a) Religious identity in responses to Kennedy in 1960. (b) The South's realignment, usually read as "issue evolution" (Carmines and Stimson), which the authors read mainly as identity: white southerners, even with moderate racial views, felt the Democratic Party no longer belonged to "people like them." (c) Abortion: partisan and gender identities conflicted. Many women moved to the party matching their view, while men more often changed their view to fit their party (p. 17).
- *Ch. 10, bias in perception.* Perceptions of party stands and policy views are colored by party preference. The deficit fell by more than half during Clinton's first term, yet most Republicans in a 1996 survey believed it had risen. Moderately informed Republicans were less accurate than the least informed. Authors' reading: a modicum of information tells them what they should want to be true, but not what is true. Democrats behaved much the same on other issues (pp. 17–18).
- *Overall conclusion.* Group and partisan loyalties are fundamental. A realistic theory should be built on the critics of Enlightenment, British liberalism and Progressivism, who saw that "human life is group life" (p. 18).
- *Ch. 11.* Implications for what "more" and better democracy would mean. The book is mainly empirical, not prescriptive (p. 18).

### The Challenge: Taking on the Divine Right of the People (pp. 18–20)
**Difficulty.** Democracy is the justifying ideology of our era, so it is hard to see its contradictions. Stimson calls it the "civil religion of America." Dahl: rejecting the democratic creed is in effect refusing to be an American (p. 18).

**The analogy.** The divine right of kings (with the "mandate of heaven" in China) gave political stability but strained against the gap between ideal and real kings (pp. 18–19). The "King's Two Bodies" doctrine excused failures. In Morgan's account of 17th-century England, the king's natural body was misled by schemers, while his political body always wanted what was best (p. 19).

**Authors' claim.** Popular sovereignty plays much the same role today. It is "a quasi-religious commitment" (Stimson), a fiction that gives legitimacy and stability. It feels natural in the Enlightenment mind-set, and practical successes support it (p. 19). It is sturdier because it is hard to pin down (Maine's oracle analogy). Unjust or unsuccessful policies can always be attributed to a mistranslation or temporary deflection of the people's will, with "special interests" playing the schemers' role. The authors say contemporary thought has its own "two bodies" doctrine: when majorities go seriously astray, it is not the people that "advised themselves" but the people misadvised by others. They then quote Rousseau: "The people are never corrupted, but sometimes deceived" (pp. 19–20).

**Dunn (1999).** Being ruled is necessary and discomfiting. Accountability softens this, but suggesting we can make rulers act as we wish is "probably a somewhat malign illusion." A political science doing justice to democracy must recognize and explain these sentiments, not dismiss them as irrational (p. 20).

**Aim.** The authors say the history of democratic thought is marked by "an addiction to romantic theories" (their phrase, not Dunn's). Their first aim is to document the gap between ideals and realities, then to start toward the more sober understanding Dunn advocates (p. 20).

*Intuition note:* The authors do not say democracy is bad. They say popular sovereignty is a fiction that gives legitimacy and stability, and that because failures can always be attributed to a deflection of the people's will, conventional thought has avoided grappling with the evidence (pp. 19–20).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the claim (p. 16) that when partisan loyalties are balanced, a small swing can decide a close election while policy plays no part.

- 1,000 voters, two parties. Party A is the incumbent.
- 480 are loyal to A, 480 are loyal to B, and 40 are swing voters who back the incumbent if income growth in the months before the election was good, and otherwise back B.
- Good late growth: A gets 480 + 40 = 520, B gets 480 + 0 = 480. A wins by 40.
- Poor late growth: A gets 480 + 0 = 480, B gets 480 + 40 = 520. B wins by 40.
- Check: in each case 520 + 480 = 1,000.
- The winner is set by a short-term economic swing among 4% of voters, not by anyone's policy preference. If the economy's timing is roughly a coin toss, so is the result.

## Concepts
- [[folk-theory-of-democracy]] — idealized view that informed voters choose policy and governments respond; legitimacy from consent (pp. 1, 3)
- [[populist-ideal-of-democracy]] (new) — citizens determine policy, via elected delegates or direct votes (pp. 2–3)
- [[leader-selection-model-of-democracy]] (new) — Schumpeter's view: democracy is competition for votes, and people only accept or refuse rulers (p. 3)
- [[retrospective-voting]] — voters appraise past performance of incumbents instead of weighing policy proposals (p. 3)
- [[belief-system-constraint]] — Converse's finding: citizens' belief systems are thin, disorganized and ideologically incoherent (pp. 11–12)
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — Downs's account of ideological reasoning helping control of elites; challenged by Converse (pp. 13–14)
- [[group-theory-of-democracy]] (new) — citizens as members of social groups whose identities drive political loyalty (pp. 16–17)
- [[partisan-perceptual-bias]] — party preference colors perceptions of facts and policy stands (p. 17)
- [[polyarchy]] (new) — Dahl's less freighted term for qualifying democracy (p. 6)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [metaphor] Folk theory's "misadvised people" ↔ political theology: the King's Two Bodies excuse for royal failure — the authors draw this analogy themselves (p. 19)
- [structural-analogy] Partisan loyalty colors factual beliefs ↔ political-science: motivated reasoning / identity-protective cognition — the deficit case shows belief shaped by partisan desire (p. 18)
- [structural-analogy] Retrospective voting is "driving by the rearview mirror" ↔ principal–agent problem: voters monitor only past outcomes of their agents (p. 4)

## Questions this raises
- If voters are driven by identities and economic mood, what gives democracy value, if not popular control of policy?
- How can the claim that results are "largely random" be reconciled with the claim that elections are "well determined by powerful forces"?
- Do the authors' evidence and conclusions hold outside the US, as they believe (p. 13)?
- What would a "more realistic" institutional design look like (chapter 11 is promised on this, p. 18)?
