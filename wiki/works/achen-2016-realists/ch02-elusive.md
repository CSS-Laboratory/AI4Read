# The Elusive Mandate: Elections and the Mirage of Popular Control

## L0 — One line
The folk theory says elections make government follow majority preferences, but this fails on logic (multidimensional spatial models and Arrow's theorem) and on evidence (voters' weak, unstable, party-driven opinions, and weak links between opinion, votes and policy).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Achen and Bartels ask whether electoral competition can deliver popular control of policy, as the folk theory assumes. They answer no, by two routes. Logically, once policy has more than one dimension, no stable "will of the majority" need exist. Empirically, survey research finds that most citizens lack coherent, stable policy views. They know little, and they often adopt their preferred party's positions rather than choose parties by issues. Election results and representatives' behavior are only loosely tied to citizens' preferences. The authors argue that deliberation, heuristics and "the miracle of aggregation" do not rescue the populist ideal, and they see little evidence that changing electoral institutions would. This sets up the next chapter on direct democracy.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a median; correlation coefficients (0 = none, 1 = perfect); majority voting between two options; the basic folk-theory idea from chapter 1 (elections make government follow what a majority prefers); single-peaked preferences (one favourite point; options further away on either side rank lower); Arrow's theorem (any collective decision process meeting certain reasonable-sounding conditions faces difficulties like those of majority rule).

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Two challenges to the folk theory.** A logical one from collective-choice theory (Black, Arrow, Downs) and an empirical one from survey research (Columbia, Michigan). (p. 23)
- **Downs's one-dimensional spatial model is comforting.** With two parties and single-peaked preferences, both converge on the median voter's ideal point, which looks like responsive government whoever wins. (pp. 24–25)
- **In several dimensions the comfort disappears.** A majority-preferred policy generally need not exist. The "chaos theorems" (results for several dimensions) show that the sufficient conditions for one are fragile: once ideal points are not symmetric, any policy is very likely beatable by another in a majority vote. Agenda rules and deliberation can produce an answer, but one that is, from a populist standpoint, arbitrary. (pp. 26–29)
- **The model's starting point is shaky.** Expressed preferences shift with wording and framing. Converse found little ideological constraint and unstable issue views, while party identification was stable. Later work found little change. (pp. 30–36)
- **Political ignorance is large and persistent.** Heuristics and "the miracle of aggregation" do not rescue voters. Uninformed votes differ from informed ones, and correlated errors defeat aggregation. (pp. 36–41)
- **"Issue voting" is often an illusion.** Issue proximity (closeness between a voter's and a candidate's positions) can reflect persuasion (voters adopt their favourite's positions) or projection (voters assume their favourite shares theirs) rather than policy-based choice. Lenz's panel evidence (repeated interviews with the same people) shows candidate choice shaping issue positions. (pp. 41–45)
- **Elections only loosely constrain policy.** Extremism costs little, and policy shifts mainly with which party wins. House members from similar districts vote very differently by party. Direct assessments find little consistent difference between proportional and majoritarian systems. (pp. 45–49)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 21–23)
**Epigraph and framing.** Dahl (1956) warned that traditional theory expects more of elections than they can give: revealing the majority's "will" is something elections "rarely do, except in an almost trivial fashion." (p. 21)

Dahl's "Populistic Democracy" (political equality, popular sovereignty, majority rule) corresponds closely to the authors' "folk theory." Its sovereignty criterion is that the alternative enforced as policy is the one most preferred by the community's members (Dahl 1956, 37). Two mechanisms are meant to deliver this: electoral competition (this chapter) and direct democracy through initiatives and referenda (next chapter). (p. 21)

**Historical enthusiasm.** Bryce (1894), Beard (1912) and Dewey (1927) expressed faith in popular rule, and even the skeptic Mencken (1916) defined democracy in colorfully populist terms. By mid-century the populist ideal was entrenched. Schumpeter (1942) attacked the "classical doctrine" as unrealistic. Dahl stressed "Madisonian" and "polyarchal" alternatives. Riker (1953) still wrote that responsible government needs elections in which choosing men is deciding policy, though later (1982) he called populism a "totalitarian sleight-of-hand." (p. 22)

**Two challenges, same decades.**
- *Logical:* Black (1948; 1958), Arrow (1951) and Downs (1957) gave the ideal a clearer form but also exposed difficulties in popular sovereignty itself. (p. 23)
- *Empirical:* survey research, such as the Columbia studies of the 1940 and 1948 elections, found citizens' motivations, knowledge and reasoning poorly matched to the ideal. Berelson et al.: "By such standards the voter falls short." (p. 23)

The chapter takes the logical challenge first, then the empirical one. (p. 23)

### The "Spatial Model" of Voting and Elections (pp. 23–30)
**Setup (Downs 1957).** The spatial model gives the folk theory mathematical form. It is in line with the "liberal view" that democracy aggregates individual preferences (Miller 1992). (pp. 23–24)
- Policies lie on **one left–right dimension**.
- Each voter has an **ideal point**, the policy she prefers to all others.
- Each party has a **platform**, the policy it will enact if elected.
- Voters vote for the closest party. Parties choose platforms to maximize their chance of winning office. (p. 24)
- Footnote 3: in multiparty systems voters might vote "strategically" for a farther party. Downs worried about the calculation burden, and Kedar (2005) argued it need not be demanding. This does not arise with two parties. (p. 24)

**Median voter theorem.** With two parties, both adopt identical platforms at the median of voters' ideal points. (p. 24)
- Footnote 4 gives the argument. A party at the median beats any platform to its left, because it wins the median voter and everyone to her right. It beats any platform to its right likewise. (p. 24)
- Downs barely noted this and moved to an "improved" model with abstention by extremists, which yields distinct platforms. There the predictions are sensitive to the distribution of ideal points and to auxiliary assumptions. (pp. 24–25, fn. 4)
- It is a special case of collective choice with **single-peaked preferences** (Black 1948). A voter's preferences are single-peaked if every platform to the left of her ideal point beats any platform further left, and the same on the right. Preferences that are a monotonic function of distance satisfy this. (p. 25, fn. 5)

**Why it seemed good news.** The median position has a smaller average distance from all voters' ideal points than any other feasible policy. So competition appears to satisfy voters as well as possible in a utilitarian sense, whichever party wins. (p. 25) The model also seemed to explain muted mid-century politics and the landslide defeats of Goldwater (1964) and McGovern (1972). By the 1980s, "the center of voter opinion exerts a powerful force" (Enelow and Hinich 1984). (p. 25)

> *Intuition note.* If everyone prefers the nearer party, standing at the middle wins. Competition then pushes policy to the point that is closest to voters on average. This is the "comforting" story the rest of the section undermines.

**Elaborations, then multidimensionality.** Later work added probabilistic voting, valence factors (charisma, incumbency), policy-motivated parties, platform constraints and uncertainty about platforms. The most important elaboration for the authors replaced one dimension with a multidimensional policy space (Davis and Hinich 1966; 1967). One dimension conflicted with evidence that 1950s citizens held virtually uncorrelated views on economic, social and foreign policy (Stokes 1963). Davis, Hinich and Ordeshook (1970) accepted that models need "more than one dimension of conflict and taste." (pp. 25–26)

Stokes called unidimensionality the "most evident—and perhaps least fundamental" unrealism, and, from a purely technical standpoint, he was right: the assumption was dropped within a few years. But the authors say this created conceptual difficulties for a model serving as both empirical theory and normative populist theory. (p. 26)

**Loss of a majority-preferred policy.** In one dimension the median voter's ideal point is a unique, normatively attractive solution. Davis, Hinich and Ordeshook (1970) noted that positions preferred by a majority to every alternative "in general, do not exist for a multi-dimensional world." Even if preferences are single-peaked on every issue dimension, there may be no platform with a logical claim to be "the will of the majority." (p. 26)
- Davis, Hinich and Ordeshook described conditions that guarantee a single dominant position for any number of dimensions: symmetry and unimodality of the preference density (Plott 1967). The authors of the 1970 paper stressed "the eminent restrictiveness" of these conditions. (p. 26)
- Subsequent "chaos theorems" (Kramer 1973; McKelvey 1976; Schofield 1983) show the sufficient conditions are "exceedingly fragile." Once ideal points deviate from multidimensional symmetry, it is very likely that any feasible policy can be beaten by another in a straight majority vote. (p. 26)
- Footnote 7: Barry (1970) assumed parties would come together where they split the vote equally. The authors say no such convergent strategy is an equilibrium, because at least one party could gain by deviating. (pp. 26–27)

**Arrow and Plott.** This problem is a manifestation of the "paradox of voting" (Condorcet, Dodgson). Arrow's (1951) theorem shows that any collective decision process satisfying certain reasonable-sounding conditions, not only majority rule, must face similar difficulties. The authors read it as showing that a reliable procedure turning coherent individual preferences into a coherent collective choice is logically unattainable. Plott (1976) called it a "gigantic cavern." The authors add that Arrow's theorem and the multidimensional model are specific formulations within the folk-theory framework, "not the sum total" of what has been said about society's wants. Still, the incoherence of the populist ideal in that framework prompted a major reassessment. (p. 27)

**Institutions as a partial answer.** Shepsle (1979), Riker (1980) and Calvert (1995) studied how institutions shape choices without "equilibria of tastes." Germaneness rules, committee specialization and conference procedures may simplify choices enough to give stable equilibria, where preferences alone would leave outcomes open to agenda manipulation and logrolling. (p. 27)

**Why that does not restore popular will.** Johnston et al. (1992): the electorate expresses collective opinion only through a limited menu of alternatives, and parties and leaders shape the menu. Schattschneider: a sovereign with a vocabulary of "Yes" and "No." The authors say agenda-setting research is fruitful but leaves Plott's "gaping hole." If outcomes depend on how alternatives are shaped and on institutions created by collective choices, in what sense do they reflect popular will? Institutional choice is subject to Arrow's theorem too, and the authors say the spatial modeling tradition has so far produced no satisfactory solution. (p. 28)

**Deliberation as an escape?** Some theorists suggest deliberation might reduce multidimensional choices to one dimension or a sequence of one-dimensional ones (Miller 1992; Knight and Johnson 2011). (pp. 28–29)
- Footnote 8, Mackie (2003): voting one dimension at a time gives "the intersection of medians." The authors reply that this depends on an arbitrary choice of axes, and that strategic voters would logroll across dimensions, which brings Arrow back. (p. 28)
- List et al. (2013) found, in deliberative polls, that participants were somewhat more likely to express single-peaked preferences after deliberation. The authors say whether this reassures depends on deliberation quality and on how often such reorganization happens in ordinary politics. (p. 29)
- Knight and Johnson's example: parties might agree that the interstate highway system is about national defense rather than interstate commerce. The authors ask on what basis they would agree. They add that the agreement would be "patently mistaken," since the system is clearly both. (p. 29)
- Any agreement splitting a multidimensional issue into one-dimensional pieces shapes the outcome in a way that is, from a populist standpoint, "fundamentally arbitrary." So deliberation is no easy escape. (p. 29)

**Verdict.** Collective-choice theory was a "mixed blessing." The one-dimensional model has "great curb appeal" (Zaller 2012): the rationally ignorant median voter gets what he wants with little effort. The multidimensional model has "no curb appeal at all": even a perfectly rational, highly informed median voter does not get what she wants. (pp. 29–30)

> *Intuition note.* In one dimension "the middle" is well defined. With several issues, majorities can keep overturning any proposal, so "what the majority wants" may not exist. Rules and discussions can produce an answer, but the answer then depends on the rules or framing chosen.

### Public Opinion and Political Ideology (pp. 30–36)
**The model needs informed voters with real preferences.** Zaller's phrase combines the spatial model with Downs's argument on information costs. Because one vote plays an "infinitesimal role," returns to information are so low that many rational voters buy none (Downs 1957). But the spatial model still needs voters to know their own preferences and the parties' platforms. The authors ask what happens if voters do not know what they want. Then the model "loses its starting point." (p. 30)

**Wording and framing effects (p. 30–31).**
- 63–65% of Americans in the mid-1980s said the federal government spent too little on "assistance to the poor." Only 20–25% said it spent too little on "welfare" (Rasinski 1989). The authors "can suggest no sensible way" to say which reflects the majority's will. (pp. 30–31)
- In the mid-1970s almost half would "not allow" a communist to give a speech, but only about one-fourth would "forbid" it. (p. 31)
- Before the 1991 Gulf War, almost two-thirds were willing to "use military force," fewer than half to "engage in combat," and fewer than 30% to "go to war." (p. 31)
- Mathematically equivalent framings of quantitative choices also give different results (Tversky and Kahneman 1981). This casts doubt on the assumption that citizens have definite preferences to be elicited and aggregated. (p. 31)

**Columbia and Michigan portraits.** Berelson et al.: much "don't know," "sometimes reflecting genuine indecision, more often meaning 'don't care.'" Voters misperceived candidates' positions and exaggerated support for their favorites. Votes were "relatively invulnerable to direct argumentation." (p. 31) Campbell et al. (*The American Voter*): "general impoverishment of political thought." Election shifts mostly came from defections by relatively unsophisticated voters with little grasp of issues. (p. 32)

**Converse (1964), three kinds of evidence.** He concluded that many citizens "do not have meaningful beliefs" even on long-contested issues. (p. 32)
1. *Use of ideological concepts.* About 3% of voters were "ideologues" and 12% "near-ideologues." Most thought in terms of group interests, the "nature of the times," or with "no shred of policy significance." (p. 32)
2. *Constraint*, meaning correlations among related policy views. Domestic policy views averaged .23, foreign policy views were similarly modest, and there was virtually no correlation across the two domains. Policy views and party preference averaged .07. Congressional candidates showed much higher correlations. (pp. 32–33)
3. *Stability over time.* Across three interviews at two-year intervals (1956–1960), issue-view correlations from one interview to the next ranged from a bit under .50 down to a bit under .30, so views were "extremely labile." Party identification correlations exceeded .70. Converse inferred that parties are more central to citizens' belief systems than the policy ends they pursue. (pp. 33–34)

Converse saw constraint as mostly social learning, not logic. Critics asked whether people might have idiosyncratic personal belief systems. The instability evidence answers that. (p. 33)

Footnote 11 qualifies the instability. Some of it reflects measurement error. People may sample different considerations each time (Zaller 1992). Better knowledge-based sophistication measures show the best-informed fifth with stability .60 versus .25 for the least informed. But the authors insist the best-informed are not "sophisticated independent thinkers," only more consistent. (p. 33, fn. 11)

**Reception and cross-national evidence.** Most critics agreed with Converse's conclusion even if no single argument was fully persuasive. Kinder and Kalmoe: genuine ideological identification is rare, found mainly among the deeply engaged, and Americans identify with party more resolutely than with ideology. (p. 34)

Other countries are similar (p. 34–35):
- Ideological self-placement is often driven by partisanship (Inglehart and Klingemann 1976).
- Most French voters did not understand "left" and "right" (Converse and Pierce 1986).
- In a 1968 Italian survey, 50% of identifiers with the right-wing Monarchist party took left-wing policy positions.
- Half of German voters could not place "Die Linke" on a left–right scale (Blais, personal communication).
- Footnote 12: Latin American legislators' policy preferences typically explain under 10% of the variance in their left–right self-placements.

**Attempts to evade the finding, and their outcome.**
- *The "Changing American Voter" (1970s).* Further scrutiny attributed most apparent change to changed questions. With the old questions, 1970s voters looked like 1950s voters. (p. 35)
- *"Core values."* These are somewhat more stable than policy preferences but "significantly colored" by party identification and short-term vote intentions. (p. 35)
- *Polarization.* Elite polarization since the 1980s led rank-and-file partisans to adopt consistent labels. Yet the average correlation between pairs of policy preferences rose only from .16 (1972) to .20 (2012). Footnote 14: at that rate it would take about 300 years to match partisan elites' structure. (p. 36)
- A replication of *The American Voter* with 2000 and 2004 data found no change in most respects and "glacial" improvement in the rest (Lewis-Beck et al. 2008). (p. 36)

The authors conclude that the folk theory is of little use in understanding actual democratic politics. (p. 36)

> *Intuition note.* If people do not hold consistent, lasting policy views, "voters choosing the closest party" has little to work with. Party loyalty is the steadier thing, and ideology often follows it.

### Political Ignorance, Heuristics, and "The Miracle of Aggregation" (pp. 36–41)
**Knowledge levels (Delli Carpini and Keeter 1996).** In 1952 only 44% could name a branch of government. In 1972 only 22% knew something about Watergate. In 1985 only 59% knew their governor's party. In 1986 only 49% knew which nation had used nuclear weapons. Their summary: large numbers are "woefully underinformed." (pp. 36–37)

Little has changed. New media raised the variance of information but not the average (Baum and Kernell 1999; Prior 2007). Rising education raised knowledge little overall. Delli Carpini and Keeter: public knowledge is "little different today than it was fifty years ago." (p. 37)

**Rebuttals the authors reject.**
- Surveys underestimate knowledge because of low motivation (Prior and Lupia 2008; Bullock et al. 2013). The authors reply that insufficient motivation is endemic to mass politics, not an artifact of surveys. Paying people to learn is impractical and would be very expensive. (pp. 37–38)
- "Visual political knowledge" (recognizing faces) as another road to competence (Prior 2014). The authors reply that putting photographs on ballots would raise problems of voter bias. (p. 38)
- Spatial models with "uninformed" voters (McKelvey and Ordeshook 1985; 1986). Their "uninformed" voters know the distribution of ideal points, the levels of informed support (from polls), and the left–right order of candidates. The authors say real voters mostly do not know even what "left" and "right" mean, so these assumptions will rarely hold. (p. 38)

**Heuristics.** In the early 1990s books (Popkin 1991; Sniderman, Brody and Tetlock 1991; Page and Shapiro 1992) argued voters can use shortcuts or "heuristics": cues from trusted people, stereotypes, personal experience. (p. 38)
- Cue-taking from better-informed people is real. The authors tell the Trillin story, in which a couple divide up countries to think about, to point to a limitation: the arrangement works for two well-informed, compatible people, but most citizens have no "Cyprus watcher in the house." (pp. 38–39)
- Psychologists study heuristics mainly for the biases they produce. Political scientists mostly treat them as a boon, probably, the authors suspect with Kuklinski and Quirk, because a competent citizenry is normatively attractive. (p. 39)
- Where tasks are defined clearly enough for benchmarks, they are simplified until real political difficulty vanishes (Lupia and McCubbins 1998). Otherwise behavior is called "rational" because it looks reasonable. The authors' example is Popkin on Ford and the unhusked tamale. They accept it as an "obvious and easy test" but say they "have no idea" whether it is an accurate basis for inferring Ford's sensitivity to Mexican-Americans. (p. 39)

**Do the uninformed mimic the informed?** Statistical analyses find substantial divergences between uninformed and informed preferences (Delli Carpini and Keeter; Althaus). After intensive education and discussion, people often change their minds. (p. 40) Bartels (1996): actual vote choices fell about halfway between fully informed voting and coin-flipping. Footnote 16: "fully informed" is a misnomer, since the imputation uses variation across a five-point information scale. So effects of low knowledge were "almost certainly underestimated." (p. 40) Lau and Redlawsk, on a looser standard that takes each voter's own partisanship, positions and evaluations as given: about 70% voted for the best-matching candidate. They ask, "Is 70 percent correct enough?" (p. 40)

**The "miracle of aggregation."** Converse's name for the Condorcet idea. If jurors judge independently, a majority is quite likely to be correct even if each is only modestly better than chance. Applied to elections, the electorate may be much wiser than any voter. (pp. 40–41)

The authors' objection is that real voters' errors are unlikely to be statistically independent, as the logic requires. When millions misconstrue the same fact or respond to the same ad, errors do not cancel. Censorship or distortion by incumbents, or even unintentional errors by neutral statistical agencies or news media about the economy (Hetherington 1996), may distort collective judgment, and incumbents' distortion yields errors that are not random. (p. 41)
- Footnote 17: formal theorists question the argument's logical basis, which hinges on sincere rather than strategic voting.
- Footnote 18: correlated voter errors can lead to "random" outcomes, meaning outcomes unpredictable from rational considerations. (p. 41)
- Bartels (1996), six presidential elections: the average gap between actual and hypothetical "fully informed" outcomes was three percentage points, enough to swing a close contest. Studies in Sweden, Canada, Denmark and elsewhere find similar effects. So lack of knowledge matters for entire electorates, policies and parties. (p. 41)

> *Intuition note.* Averaging helps when errors are independent and point in random directions. If many people are misled the same way, the errors do not cancel and the average is misled too.

### The Illusion of "Issue Voting" (pp. 41–45)
**Setting.** Spatial theory made "issue proximity" the main determinant of votes. The American National Election Studies (ANES) began using seven-point issue scales, on which respondents place themselves and candidates. Analyses then related votes to issue proximity. (pp. 41–42)

**Brody and Page's three interpretations** of the positive correlation between proximity and vote (1972). (p. 42)
1. *Policy-oriented evaluation:* voters compare candidates' positions with their own and choose. This is the folk-theory reading.
2. *Persuasion:* voters change their own positions to match the candidate or party they favor.
3. *Projection:* voters convince themselves their favorite shares their positions, whether or not it does.

In cases 2 and 3, proximity is a consequence of candidate preference, not a cause. Brody and Page warned that any procedure not controlling for both is "undependable." (p. 42)

**Attempts to separate them.** They underlined fragile and untestable statistical assumptions. Markus and Converse (1979) and Page and Jones (1979) estimated complex simultaneous-equation models on the same ANES data in the same APSR issue and reached very different conclusions. The authors infer that results depended as much on analysts' preconceptions as on voter behavior. (pp. 42–43)

Many scholars then turned to single-equation models in which issues affect votes but not vice versa. The authors say this relies on folk-theory assumptions rather than evidence. Ansolabehere, Rodden and Snyder (2008) collapsed issue questions into two broad positions, treated the rest as measurement error, imputed or dropped missing positions, and attributed the whole issue–vote relationship to issue voting. They unsurprisingly found "strong evidence of issue voting." (p. 43)
- Footnote 19: using sample-average perceptions of candidates reduces projection bias but not persuasion bias, and is theoretically hard to justify. (p. 43)

**Lenz's panel evidence (2009; 2012).** Interviewing the same people repeatedly, Lenz found that persuasion plays a large role and policy-oriented evaluation little: "candidate choices determine issue positions, not vice versa." (p. 44)
- *Example, 2000 campaign.* Bush proposed allowing individuals to invest Social Security funds in the stock market. In a typical battleground market the candidates together ran about 200 ads on it in the final week. The link between views on privatization and Bush–Gore preference (controlling party ID) more than doubled over the campaign. Lenz found this was almost entirely illusory. Supporters learned their candidate's position and adopted it, so vote intentions did not change. (p. 44)
- Similar vote-driven issue shifts appear for public works (1976), defense spending (1980), European integration in Britain and nuclear power in the Netherlands, with little evidence of issue-driven vote changes. Zaller: partisans "do not seem to care" about the positions they take. Lenz called this "disappointing" and noted it leaves politicians "considerable freedom." (pp. 44–45)

> *Intuition note.* A correlation between "agreeing with a candidate" and "voting for the candidate" does not say which causes which. The panel data suggest the vote often comes first and the agreement follows.

### Elections and Public Policy (pp. 45–49)
**Does issue voting discipline the parties?** Goldwater and McGovern's losses had less to do with issue positions than with election-year economic conditions. Extremism probably cost a few percentage points (Bartels and Zaller 2001; Cohen and Zaller 2012). Zaller: the penalty for extremism, "if real, is not large." (p. 45)

**Macro Polity (Erikson, MacKuen and Stimson 2002).** Policy outcomes shifted substantially when partisan control changed. The public's "policy mood" also affected policy, but far less. Moving from the most conservative to the most liberal mood moved White House policy activity only about one-third as much as replacing a typical Republican president with a typical Democrat. Effects of partisan control on Congress were even larger. The implication is that citizens affect policy, if at all, almost entirely by replacing one partisan team with another. (pp. 45–46)

Policy mood might still drive who wins the presidency, but the apparent mood effect evaporates once election-year economic conditions are controlled (Cohen and Zaller 2012). Forecasters generally ignore policy mood, issues and ideology. (p. 46)

**Congress, Figure 2.1.** The figure plots House members' DW-NOMINATE conservatism, 112th Congress (2011–2013), against district conservatism. (pp. 46–47)
- Data: a 12-item district opinion scale from CCES surveys (2010 and 2012). It runs from 0 (most liberal) to 100 (most conservative), but district averages run only 24.5 to 59.0. (pp. 46–47, fns. 21–23)
- The figure shows Republicans clustered high and Democrats clustered low, with two nearly separate bands. (p. 47)
- The most liberal districts elected Democrats in 2010, and the most conservative almost all elected Republicans. In the broad middle, outcomes were unreliable reflections of preferences. Moderately liberal districts (second quartile) elected Republicans 46% of the time, and moderately conservative ones (third quartile) elected Democrats 25% of the time. (p. 48) Footnote 24: Senate outcomes are even more weakly related. (p. 48)
- Within each party, a modest positive relationship links district preference to members' votes. The gap between the two lines, the expected difference between a Republican and a Democrat representing identical districts, dwarfs these slopes. (p. 48)
- The authors say it does not matter for their purposes whether the gap comes from convictions, party pressure or other factors. The key point is that members' voting is not strongly constrained by constituents' views, so elections do not force winners to reflect the median voter. (pp. 48–49) Footnote 25: the measures are incommensurate, so they cannot say which party's members are more extreme than their constituents. (p. 48)

**Not a fluke.** Similar Republican–Democrat differences for similar constituencies appear in the Senate (late 1980s–early 1990s) and House (1999–2000). A historical analysis back to the 1870s (using presidential returns as proxies) finds them fairly common across 140 years (Bartels, Clinton and Geer, forthcoming). Footnote 27: polarization since 1994 matches 1874–1920, while the mid-1930s to mid-1970s was consistently low. (p. 49)

**Institutions.** Powell (2000) argued that proportional systems get governments closer to the median citizen. The authors say this rests on untested assumptions. More direct assessments find little consistent difference between proportional and majoritarian systems (Kang and Powell 2010; Bartels 2015). "Perhaps some other institutional arrangement would work better; we do not know." But there is little evidence that changing electoral institutions would ensure popular control through electoral competition. (p. 49)

### Conclusion (pp. 49–51)
- The folk theory has shaped both political science and popular thinking. "Issue voting" is a norm of good citizenship, and voting-advice websites embody it. (p. 50, fn. 28)
- Arrow's theoretical insight and Converse's empirical work punched holes in the populist notion. Attempts to circumvent them have failed, leaving the "strong challenge to democratic hopes" (Kinder 2003) intact. (p. 50)
- These findings have had little practical effect. Schumpeter (1942) noted that action continued on the theory while it was being "blown to pieces," and the authors say seven more decades have not changed that. (p. 50)
- Frustration with elections has led to reforms aiming to bypass professional politicians. Perhaps the most radical were Progressive Era "direct democracy" reforms (initiatives and referendums), which the next chapter examines. (pp. 50–51)

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**A. Median voter in one dimension.** Five voters have ideal points 1, 2, 4, 8, 9 on a 0–10 line. The median is 4.
- Party A at 4 versus party B at 6. The midpoint is 5, so voters at 1, 2, 4 prefer A (3 votes) and voters at 8, 9 prefer B (2 votes). A wins.
- Party A at 4 versus B at 2. The midpoint is 3, so voters at 1, 2 prefer B (2 votes) and voters at 4, 8, 9 prefer A (3 votes). A wins.
- Total distance from a platform at 4: 3+2+0+4+5 = 14 (average 2.8). At 3: 2+1+1+5+6 = 15. At 5: 4+3+1+3+4 = 15. The median has the smallest sum, matching the text's utilitarian point (p. 25).

**B. Why "the majority will" can vanish.** This is a rankings-level version of the cycle behind the "paradox of voting" (p. 27). Three voters rank options X, Y, Z: voter 1: X>Y>Z; voter 2: Y>Z>X; voter 3: Z>X>Y.
- X vs Y: voters 1 and 3 prefer X, so X wins 2–1.
- Y vs Z: voters 1 and 2 prefer Y, so Y wins 2–1.
- Z vs X: voters 2 and 3 prefer Z, so Z wins 2–1.
- Every option loses to another, so no option can claim to be "the majority's choice."

**C. Persuasion looks like issue voting.** Ten voters never change their votes: 5 back party P and 5 back party Q. Before the campaign, 3 of 5 P-supporters favor policy S (60%) and 2 of 5 Q-supporters do (40%), a 20-point gap. After the parties' positions on S become known, all 5 P-supporters adopt favoring S (100%) and 0 of 5 Q-supporters do (0%), a 100-point gap. The issue–vote association has risen 20 to 100 points, though no vote changed. This mimics what Lenz found for Social Security privatization (p. 44).

## Concepts
- [[folk theory of democracy]] — idealized view: informed voters choose policies, and elections make government follow majority preferences. Dahl's "Populistic Democracy" (p. 21)
- [[spatial model of voting]] — voters and parties placed in policy space. Voters choose the closest party, and parties choose platforms to win (p. 24)
- [[median voter theorem]] — with two parties, one dimension and single-peaked preferences, both converge on the median ideal point (p. 24)
- [[single-peaked preferences]] — everything left of a voter's ideal point ranks above anything further left, and likewise on the right (p. 25, fn. 5)
- [[Condorcet winner]] — a position preferred by a majority to every alternative. In general it does not exist in several dimensions (p. 26)
- [[cycling / intransitivity]] — the problem behind "chaos theorems": any policy can be beaten by another in a majority vote (p. 26)
- [[paradox of voting]] — here used for the majority-decision paradox explored by Condorcet and Dodgson (p. 27)
- [[Arrow's impossibility theorem]] — any process meeting certain reasonable conditions faces similar difficulties as majority rule (p. 27)
- [[agenda control / agenda setter]] — institutions and those shaping the menu of alternatives determine outcomes when tastes yield no equilibrium (p. 28)
- [[rational ignorance]] — Downs: a single vote matters so little that many rational voters buy no political information (p. 30)
- [[framing effect]] — expressed preferences shift with wording or framing, e.g. "welfare" vs "assistance to the poor" (p. 31)
- [[belief-system constraint]] — correlations among related policy views. Converse found them modest outside elites (p. 32)
- [[heuristics / cues]] — shortcuts such as trusted individuals, stereotypes or experience, said to let uninformed voters choose well. The authors are skeptical (p. 38)
- [[Condorcet jury theorem]] — "miracle of aggregation": majority accuracy despite weak individuals, if errors are independent (p. 40)
- [[issue voting]] (new) — voting by comparing own policy positions with candidates'. Distinguished from persuasion and projection (p. 42)
- [[persuasion vs projection]] (new) — Brody–Page alternatives: voters adopt the favorite's positions, or believe the favorite shares theirs (p. 42)
- [[policy mood]] (new) — Erikson et al.'s measure of the public's ideological tenor, with small effect on policy (p. 45)
- [[chaos theorems]] (new) — results showing majority-dominant policies in multiple dimensions are fragile (p. 26)

## Canonical instances
- [[goldwater-mcgovern-landslides]] — seemed to confirm spatial theory but are explained more by economic conditions (pp. 25, 45)
- [[welfare-vs-assistance-to-the-poor]] — wording changes support by about 40 percentage points (p. 30)
- [[forbid-vs-not-allow]] — framing effects with a tenuous substantive difference (p. 31)
- [[converse-1964-belief-systems]] — constraint, ideologue counts and stability evidence (pp. 32–34)
- [[delli-carpini-keeter-knowledge-data]] — persistent low factual knowledge across 50 years (p. 37)
- [[ford-tamale-popkin]] — an example of a "shortcut" the authors regard as unvalidated (p. 39)
- [[bartels-1996-informed-vote]] — votes about halfway between informed and coin-flipping, with a 3-point average outcome gap (pp. 40–41)
- [[lenz-social-security-2000]] — apparent issue effect produced by persuasion (p. 44)
- [[house-112th-figure-2-1]] — party gap in roll-call voting among similar districts (pp. 46–49)
- [[interstate-highway-dimensionality]] — Knight and Johnson's example of deliberative simplification, which the authors call mistaken (p. 29)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Median voter's minimal average distance (p. 25) ↔ statistics: the median minimizes mean absolute deviation (not stated in this unit).
- [structural-analogy] Correlated voter errors defeating the "miracle of aggregation" (p. 41) ↔ statistics/ML: the benefit of averaging independent estimates shrinks when errors are correlated.
- [structural-analogy] Persuasion and projection as reverse causation (p. 42) ↔ causal inference: endogeneity, and panel data to untangle it.
- [structural-analogy] Dependence of outcomes on the agenda or axis decomposition (pp. 28–29) ↔ social choice: agenda control when majorities cycle.

## Questions this raises
- If majority will may not exist in multiple dimensions, what standard of "responsiveness" can be used to judge governments?
- If partisan identity drives issue positions, what produces the party positions that voters adopt?
- The authors report that policy shifts mainly with party control and only modestly with public mood. What would it take to link citizens' preferences more reliably to outcomes?
- Direct democracy bypasses politicians. Will it solve the problems of preference coherence and information (the next chapter's topic)?
