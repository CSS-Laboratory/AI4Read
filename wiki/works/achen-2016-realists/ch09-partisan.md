# Partisan Hearts and Spleens: Social Identities and Political Change

## L0 — One line
Achen and Bartels argue that most voters pick and change parties because of who they are (group loyalties and social identities), not because of policy or ideology, and that policy views often follow party.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Do voters choose parties for their policies, as the folk theory says (voters pick parties for policy)? The authors say no for rank-and-file voters. Because identity and issues are tangled, they pick historical episodes that isolate identity. These are Boston ethnic groups in the New Deal era, Catholics and Protestants in 1960, white southerners' long move to the Republicans, and abortion attitudes. In these cases, group attachments fit the pattern better than policy, the authors argue. Party identification often changes first, and policy views follow. Recent experiments on identity threat fit this picture. If elections have policy content, the authors say it comes mainly from party–group coalitions, not from voters' policy convictions. The evidence is suggestive, not conclusive.

**Prerequisites:** correlation and simple regression (intercept, slope, standard error); party identification as a survey measure; the "group theory" of chapter 8 (voters judge parties as collections of social groups).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- For ordinary voters, group loyalties matter a great deal and policy details little; professionals (leaders, journalists) mostly do behave as the policy story says, which misleads us (p. 233).
- Partisanship is often inherited like religion, so people can support a party for reasons their great-grandparents held. This helps explain why partisan leanings are often only modestly correlated with policy preferences (pp. 233–234).
- Identity is hard to measure and entangled with ideology. So the authors study unusual episodes, expecting that big shifts in a party's group alliances should shift the loyalties of the groups affected (p. 235).
- Boston in 1920–40: five ethnic groups realigned at different times and in different ways. This is hard to square with a single verdict on Roosevelt's policies (pp. 236–240).
- In 1960, religious identity moved both Catholic and Protestant votes. A small panel result suggests that weak Catholic identity (measured in 1956) made it more likely that Republican Catholics went against their church and voted for their party in 1960 (pp. 242–245).
- White southerners' move to the Republicans was slow in party identification and only weakly tied to racial-policy views. Warm feelings toward "southerners" predicted both early Democratic loyalty and later exit (pp. 250–258).
- Abortion panel data fit "party shapes opinion", and the authors reason that women's gender identity helped them resist persuasion more than men (pp. 261–264).
- In experiments, identity threats (demographic change, a "microaggression") shifted party leaning. Parties are coalitions of groups, and policy content, if elections have any, comes primarily from party–group relationships (pp. 265–266).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the group-centric view of partisanship (pp. 232–235)
**Epigraph and aim.** Berelson, Lazarsfeld and McPhee compare political preferences to cultural tastes. Both come from ethnic, class and family traditions, and both are matters of sentiment and disposition more than "reasoned preferences" (p. 232). The authors take group attachments to be the most important factor in voters' judgments. Group stances are much easier to learn than foreign affairs, and parties are the most salient groups (p. 232).

**Partisanship is real and durable.** Most American voters identify with a party, and this shapes their votes. The turbulence of the 1960s–70s was read by some as "dealignment", but it proved temporary and partisan voting rebounded (p. 233). Behavioral partisanship (consistent voting for one party or bloc) also matters in France, Britain and other established democracies. In new democracies, partisan attachments are often taken as a sign of consolidation (p. 233).

**Whose view: "party government" proponents.** They hold that parties "stand for something", voters know it, and voters choose accordingly (p. 233). The authors reply that leaders, issue-group officials and journalists do behave this way because politics is their profession, and they dominate discourse. So we slip into thinking ideology drives partisanship generally. Among the rank and file, group loyalties matter a great deal and policy details not very much. Hence party policies are often at odds with what their voters favor (p. 233).

**Inheritance.** Children tend to adopt their parents' partisanship, and it often persists even after class mobility (pp. 233–234). Partisanship, like religion, tends to be "inherited, durable, and not about ideology or theology" (p. 234). V. O. Key found U.S. counties whose mid-20th-century voting traced back to the Civil War (p. 234). Stability is not universal. Groups drift, and rare "party realignments" can shift the balance quickly. Per group theory, significant change usually stems from shifts in the political resonance of group identities (p. 234).

**Objection (Abramowitz and Saunders 2006):** party identification correlates more strongly with ideological self-description than with group memberships, so partisanship is rooted in ideology (p. 234). **Authors' reply, two points (p. 234):**
1. It oversimplifies identity by adding up "effects" of demographic indicators (married, female, union member). For some people the category is an identity, for others not. Many strong identities (Cuban-American, transgender, college professor) are missing from surveys.
2. It assumes correlation of ideology and party ID means ideology causes party. Careful efforts to separate the two directions (Miller 2000; Goren 2005) *suggest* ideology is more often an effect of partisanship than its cause.

**Method.** Most scholars seem to agree partisanship is a form of, and in significant part a product of, social identity (pp. 234–235). Still, the authors know of no reliable way to gauge the overall impact of identities. *Voting* (1954) studied union norms, social interaction and cross-cutting attachments. *The American Voter* (1960) measured identification with prominent groups. Neither gives an overall gauge (p. 235). So the chapter uses episodes with unusual opportunities. The key implication tested is that major shifts in group alliances within parties should produce realignments among the group members affected (p. 235). Cases: the New Deal, the post-Jim Crow Solid South, and abortion (p. 235). The authors illustrate with the name "Reagan", unknown before his presidency and in the top 100 girls' names by 2012. They suspect most of these children grew up in Republican households (p. 235, n. 1).

> **Intuition note.** The authors do not say policy never matters. They say that for the rank and file, policy is often an effect of group loyalty. The chapter looks for episodes that isolate the impact of identity (p. 235).

### Ethnic politics and partisan change in the New Deal era (pp. 236–240)
Source: Gerald Gamm's study of ethnically homogeneous Boston precincts, 1920–1940, with one chapter per group (p. 236). Gamm concluded that each cluster responded in its own way, producing "many realignments" at different times with different stimuli and mechanisms (p. 236). The authors say this is hard to explain if the New Deal realignment was fundamentally a verdict on Roosevelt's policies, but makes sense as reflecting group circumstances (p. 236). Polls were rare then, so some cases can be read either way; others test the two readings more clearly (p. 236).

- **Yankees (ambiguous case).** Prosperous Beacon Hill and Back Bay precincts stayed Republican. The Democratic presidential share peaked at 29% in 1928 and Roosevelt never exceeded 22% in three elections. Turnout rose only from 47% (1932) to 54% (1940). Economic insulation, ideology or distaste for alignment with lower-status Democratic groups are all possible. More data would be needed (pp. 236–237).
- **Jews.** They shifted toward the Democrats between 1928 and 1936, and disproportionately in upper-middle-class precincts, not working-class ones. This is against a class-based response to New Deal policies (p. 237). Gamm: from 1936, Jews "regardless of their income" voted with unusual ethnic solidarity (p. 237). **Folk-theory suggestion** (Hitler and Roosevelt's opposition to him): the authors say this gets the timing wrong for origins. Hitler's party got 6% in 1924, and Fuchs says there seemed to be no crucial issues dividing the parties in that "pre-Hitlerian age" (pp. 237–238). By 1928 a large majority of Jews had left the Republicans. What had changed was the Democrats' visible acceptance of Catholics and Jews (p. 238). In New York, Jewish Republican loyalty began to collapse when Al Smith ran for governor in 1924. Defections were smaller in cities with Irish machines, such as Boston (p. 237).
- **African-Americans (less clear).** The Democratic presidential share in black precincts rose from 32% (1928) to 70% (1940), fairly steadily, so no obvious tie to specific policies. Turnout surged in 1936 (26% to 46%). Causes could be New Deal support, signals of welcome, or purely local factors. The lack of a consistent statistical link across non-southern states (ch. 7) may suggest local factors (p. 238).
- **Italians.** Smith won 94% in Italian precincts in 1928. Turnout grew from 13% (1924) to 24% (1928), 27% (1932), 40% (1936) and 46% (1940). Roosevelt's 1936 share was 85%, below Smith's, and fell to 57% in 1940. The authors conclude the mobilization cannot plausibly be read as a response to recovery or New Deal policy (p. 239).
- **Irish.** They were overwhelmingly Democratic regardless of class. The Democratic presidential share fell steadily from 1928 to 1940, and Roosevelt ran even with or behind Democratic congressional candidates. Gamm: the Irish responded to Smith's personal appeal, and "perhaps" had no permanent attachment to Roosevelt (pp. 239–240). Four Irish precinct groups from "poor" to "lower middle class" moved together in direction and degree. The small wealthy–poor gap did not widen in the late 1930s when class appeals were supposedly at a peak (p. 240).

**Conclusion of section.** Gamm's explanations range from pure identity (Smith as a symbol) to intergroup tension (anti-Irish-machine feeling among Jews) to group-specific policy and personnel concerns. His analysis "cannot demonstrate conclusively" why the groups differed. Yet it seems clear that responses differed and were significantly shaped by ethnic attachments (p. 240).

> **Intuition note.** If one policy verdict drove the New Deal vote, similar voters should move together. Instead, groups moved at different times and, in the Irish case, regardless of class. That pattern fits group-based explanations better.

### Religious identity in the 1960 election (pp. 240–246)
**Background.** Catholic immigrants found a home in urban Democratic organizations, and Smith's 1928 candidacy reinforced this. Kennedy was the first Catholic since Smith nominated by a major party. In West Virginia his support fell as voters learned he was Catholic ("no one in West Virginia knew you were a Catholic in December"). A late TV blitz stressing church–state separation helped him win (p. 241). In the general election he met "the old echo of fear" among "gut-Democrats", and addressed it in his Houston speech to Protestant ministers: "I do not speak for my church on public matters" (p. 241). Footage was replayed for seven weeks, and its effect was unmeasured. Nixon avoided cultivating anti-Catholic votes, but "neither could erase the intrusion of religious feeling" (p. 242).

**Converse's 1966 analysis (p. 242).** Nearly half of Protestants spontaneously raised Kennedy's Catholicism in pre-election interviews. Estimated Democratic presidential vote relative to "normal":
- White Protestants who seldom or never attended church: 7% below normal.
- Churchgoing white Protestants: 21% below normal.
- Churchgoing Catholics (the substantial majority): 15% above normal.
- Catholics who seldom or never attended: 10% above normal.

Kennedy's declaration of independence from prelates did not erase his religion's significance for Catholics. The authors find it "farfetched" to attribute these defections from traditional patterns to anything but group loyalties (pp. 242–243).

**Figure 9.1 (p. 243).** Catholics were about 10 points more Democratic than non-Catholics in both party ID and presidential vote in the 1950s. In 1960, 83% of Catholic ANES voters reported voting for Kennedy versus 39% of non-Catholics. Catholics calling themselves strong or weak Democrats rose from 53% (1952–58) to 60% (1960), a much more modest spike (p. 243).

**Cross-pressure and identity strength (p. 244).** Converse quotes a Republican Catholic woman torn between religion and party. In 1956 she had said she did not feel much closer to Catholics than to other people and had "not much interest at all" in how Catholics were getting along, and her reported church attendance varied over three interviews. The 1956 interest question predicted 1960 voting over and above church attendance:

| 1956 interest in how Catholics are getting along (Republican Catholics) | N | Voted Nixon 1960 | Voted Kennedy 1960 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Not much interest at all | 15 | 10 | 2 |
| A good deal of interest | 18 | 4 | 12 |

The numbers are small, but the authors read the strong correlation as showing identity strength (measured four years earlier) shaped who defected (p. 244).

**More results (p. 245).** Stokes: party-identification bias was effective among the mildly religious. Party loyalty could markedly affect the strongly religious only if the party faith were itself strongly held. Converse: white Protestants "high" in Protestant identification were 22% less Democratic than expected, versus 10% for "low". So identity "cut both ways". Converse and colleagues (1961) estimated that "the Catholic issue" cost Kennedy 2.2% of the national vote, concentrated in the South, which he mostly carried anyway. Outside the South, they estimated a net gain of 1.6% of the two-party vote. Given his narrow margins in several key states (e.g., Illinois by 0.2%, New Jersey 0.8%), the authors think it "seems likely" the Catholic issue was crucial to his victory (p. 245 and n. 18).

**Temporary effect (pp. 245–246).** By 1964 the Catholic party-ID edge was back to its 1950s level, and the vote edge eroded after 1968. In 2004 Catholics were no more likely than non-Catholics to identify as Democrats, and Kerry's vote edge among Catholics was only 2 points. Today the group–party tie has moved to evangelicals. Experimental work shows that labeling a hypothetical candidate Catholic has no partisan effect, while labeling one evangelical raises support among Republicans and lowers it among Democrats (Campbell, Green and Layman 2011). Other work finds that associating positions with evangelical leaders lowers their popularity among Democrats, and that evangelicals in Congress are seen as more conservative than mainline Protestants with indistinguishable records (p. 246).

> **Intuition note.** The Kennedy bump appeared and faded as the social meaning of a Catholic candidacy faded. The authors stress that "the Catholic issue" was not a political issue in the usual ideological sense (p. 245), so they read the deviations as group loyalty.

### Race, social identity, and realignment in the South (pp. 246–258)
**Setting.** The authors apply group theory to the realignment of white southerners and, later, of moral conservatives and liberals (p. 246). They say social identities mattered as much or more than policy differences in triggering and reinforcing realignment (p. 246). Key (1949) said race overshadowed all other factors in southern politics (p. 247). The Solid South was a one-party white regime. The "second reconstruction" (protests, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965) ended legal segregation and enfranchised millions of African-Americans (p. 247). Southern blacks were already mostly Democratic and became overwhelmingly so after 1964. Their average Democratic identification rose from 56% (1952–60) to 82% (1964–72) (p. 247, n. 19).

**Pattern (Figs. 9.2–9.3, pp. 248–249).** Democratic margin means % Democratic minus % Republican. White southerners' presidential vote shifted rapidly after 1964, helped by Wallace's 1968 candidacy and Nixon's "southern strategy". Party identification moved much more slowly, and Republicans did not outnumber Democrats until the 1990s. Green, Palmquist and Schickler: disruption showed at the voting booth at once but altered self-conceptions much more gradually (pp. 248–249). Generational replacement explains part, not all. The middle cohort (born 1921–45) averaged a 7-point lower Democratic margin than the older cohort, and the younger cohort (born after 1945) another 13 points lower. But Jim Crow–era cohorts started at about +60 margins in the early 1950s and had lost half or more by the 1970s (p. 249). Beck (1977) estimated that a roughly 7-point drop came from replacement and an additional 12 points from within-cohort change (n. 20).

**Competing explanation: "issue evolution" (Carmines and Stimson).** This is the conventional view that elite shifts on civil rights drove realignment (p. 250). The authors accept that elite cues mattered, but as signals of where one's group belonged. Beck and Campbell also found group signals mattered while racial policy mattered less (p. 250 and n. 21). Two points support them:
1. Change was under way before Democratic elites took a clear civil rights stand.
2. Party ID was only weakly related to specific policy views (p. 250).

**Evidence from policy questions.**
- *School integration (Fig. 9.4, pp. 251–252).* In the early 1960s most southern whites opposed government-enforced integration and were overwhelmingly Democratic. By 2000, the remaining opponents (about 40%) were solidly Republican, but conversion was gradual, and through the 1980s opponents still leaned Democratic. Beck found a "surprisingly high amount" of misperception of party stands, and no more than one in ten had brought party loyalty into line with racial attitudes. Beck called this "surely not the stuff of issue-based partisanship!" (pp. 251–252). More puzzling, white southerners who did *not* oppose integration were also leaving. By the early 1990s the Democratic advantage had vanished even among moderates (p. 252). The authors note the two groups in Fig. 9.4 are not fixed sets of people, but the similar trend lines are inconsistent with opinions about integration being a significant driver (n. 22).
- *Their interpretation (p. 252).* Conversion to the GOP was not primarily about racial policy but about white southern identity. Green et al.: the one-party system hinged on the link between Democrats and white supremacy, and mobilized southern blacks broke that link. Policy and identity later became mutually reinforcing, as party social imagery shifted once a critical mass changed. The authors say racial attitudes "no doubt had some causal impact", but racial and regional identity was the more important factor (pp. 252–253).
- *Government aid to blacks (Fig. 9.5, pp. 253–254).* Asked from 1970, the question yields a gap in party ID between supporters and opponents of less than ten points before 1984. It widened in the Reagan era and doubled by 2012. **Issue-evolution reading (as the authors present it):** whites were slowly catching on to party stands. **Authors' reading:** the gap appeared so late and gradually that it is more plausibly an effect than a cause of partisan change. Many gravitated to the GOP and then adopted consistent policy views. Even those not opposing aid kept moving toward the GOP, so the Democratic edge had vanished among them by 2004. This is explained by identity as more and more fellow white southerners became Republicans (p. 254). Footnote 23: self-described conservatives went from an 18-point Democratic margin (1972) to a 57-point Republican margin (2008). Moderates and liberals fell from +33 (1970s) to +16 (2000s).

**Feeling-thermometer analysis (Table 9.1, pp. 255–257).** ANES respondents rated "southerners", "whites" and "blacks" from 0 (cold) to 100 (warm). The authors recode ratings to −1 to +1, so 0 is neutral (n. 26). These ratings are theoretically distinct from identity, but plausibly proxy for it (p. 255). The outcome is the Democratic party-ID margin (−100 to +100) among white southerners. The model is OLS with linear trends over 1964–2008 (nn. 25–26). In my notation (added step), for respondent $i$ in year $t$:
$$M_{it} = a_t + b_t\,S_{it} + \varepsilon_{it},$$
where $S_{it}$ is the recoded rating and $a_t$ (intercept) and $b_t$ (slope) are reported for 1964, 2008 and their change (standard errors in parentheses).

Authors' readings:
- In 1964 the Democratic advantage was almost 25 points for neutral feelings toward southerners, and almost 55 for very warm feelings. (Added check: 24.6 + 29.3 = 53.9.) The authors say this supports a role for southern identity in the Solid South (p. 255).
- The decline was far from uniform. Those most attached in 1964 were most likely to have left by 2008, and by 2008 southern identity pushed toward Republican ID (pp. 255–256). The text says the cumulative shift was about 20 points for neutral feelings and twice that for very warm feelings (p. 256). On p. 257 it says the estimated change in the effect implies a shift of almost 40 points for the very warm, more than twice that of the neutral. (These figures do not match the table's neutral-intercept change of −39.7; see Reading notes.)
- Feelings toward "whites" were positively related to Democratic ID in 1964, but less strongly than feelings toward southerners. Feelings toward "blacks" showed no apparent partisan resonance. The authors read this "non-finding" as consistent with 1960s Democratic loyalty resting on positive social identities rather than racial animus (p. 257).
- By 2008, attitudes toward whites and blacks were each modestly, uncertainly positively related to Democratic ID, while the "southerners" effect was about twice as large and pointed toward the GOP (p. 257).

**Section conclusion (pp. 257–258).** Racial issues were clearly important in precipitating the realignment, as issue evolution says. But for most white southerners policy preferences were "probably" secondary. Loyalty eroded before the parties clearly split on civil rights, continued among both proponents and opponents of federal efforts, and policy divisions between the parties' white supporters emerged only decades later. Feelings toward southerners mattered both for 1960s loyalty and for its erosion (pp. 257–258).

> **Intuition note.** The authors point to white southerners who did not take the racial-conservative position yet also left the Democrats, and to policy gaps that opened late. They read this as pointing to group belonging, with policy views following.

### Issues and partisan change: the case of abortion (pp. 258–264)
**Background.** *Roe v. Wade* (1973) barred states from forbidding first-trimester abortion, and allowed regulation in the second trimester and a ban in the final three months (p. 258). It set off a backlash among traditionalists, then a countermobilization. Both parties were initially divided, but leaders and activists polarized in the late 1970s. The 1980 Republican platform clearly opposed abortion, and later platforms strengthened the language (p. 258 and n. 27). By the late 1990s ordinary partisans diverged too. In the Youth-Parent Socialization Panel Study (high school seniors of 1965; 935 people), the correlation between abortion attitude and party ID was .07 in 1982 and .22 in 1997. Among the best informed it was .04 and .36 (p. 259). Abortion attitudes are unusually stable: the 1982–1997 correlation is .59 (party ID: .63), and only 9 and 12 respondents lacked opinions in 1982 and 1997 (p. 259, n. 29).

**The inference problem.** The authors say it seems obvious that abortion attitudes shape party ID and vote. But most tests confound issue voting and partisan persuasion (p. 259). Layman and Carsey (2002) are a prominent exception. They studied short-term responses and cognitive bases, and used complex multivariable models assuming the same linear, additive effects for everyone. The authors prefer simpler, more flexible analysis because causal processes may differ between groups (pp. 259–260).

**Two design choices (p. 260).**
1. Only non-Catholics are analyzed. Catholics' relation to abortion is unique and complex, with left and right wings, and pro-choice Catholics disproportionately left the denomination. (Evangelicals showed much weaker selection effects and are grouped with other non-Catholics, n. 32.)
2. Men and women are analyzed separately. Women hold two potentially competing identities on this issue, gender and party. Abortion affects men less deeply. Women's abortion opinions are more reliable. In the 1992–94–96 ANES panel, the estimated reliability among the best-informed 30% was .84 for non-Catholic men and .97 for women, using the Wiley-Wiley measurement-error model (pp. 260–261). Gender differences are smaller at lower information levels (n. 33).

**Predictions (p. 261, then pp. 262–263).**
- Women should move between *parties* more than men, since the issue is bound up in their identity.
- Men should move their *abortion views* toward their party more than women should.
- Both patterns should be stronger among the well informed.
- Among 1982 pro-life respondents, Democrats should become pro-choice most, Republicans least, and Independents in between. This is because society was trending pro-choice, Republicans had party reinforcement, and pro-life Democrats faced pressure from both society and party.
- Democratic men should liberalize more than Democratic women.

**Figure 9.6 (p. 262).** Share of 1982 non-Catholic Republicans who were still Republican in 1997, by 1982 abortion opinion (98 women, 87 men; a rule of thumb is that differences of about 7 points or more are statistically significant). Read from the figure:

| 1982 abortion opinion | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Never/rarely | ≈ 83% | ≈ 81% |
| Sometimes | ≈ 86% | ≈ 67% |
| Always | ≈ 76% | ≈ 52% |

Pro-choice Republican women left substantially. More than one-third of those with pro-choice views in 1982 ("sometimes" or "always") had left by 1997. Among men the shift was not substantial (p. 261). The authors read this as a key aspect of party polarization.

**Figure 9.7 (pp. 263–264).** Share of 1982 pro-life respondents (never or rarely) who were pro-choice (sometimes or always) in 1997, by 1982 party ID. Read from the figure:

| 1982 party ID | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|
| Democrats | ≈ 57% | ≈ 43% |
| Independents | ≈ 43% | ≈ 26% |
| Republicans | ≈ 27% | ≈ 24% |

About half of pro-life Democrats became pro-choice, twice as many as among pro-life Republicans, and more than half of Democratic men did (p. 263). The Democratic cells are small (14 men, 14 women), so those percentages are "suggestive rather than definitive" (n. 36). Footnote 35 notes that some pro-choice Republican women moved to pro-life, which fits "partisanship causes issue positions" or may be response error averaging out.

**Conclusion (p. 264).** The patterns are those one would expect if parties help shape opinion. The authors reason that gender identity helped women resist, so they were relatively more likely to change party than view. Men more often changed their view. Most people changed neither, but the changes that occurred were substantial. Social identity spurred partisan change, and prior partisan identity shaped views even on a salient issue (p. 264).

> **Intuition note.** If abortion views were fixed and simply drove party choice, 1982 pro-life people should have stayed pro-life whatever their party. Instead, pro-life Democrats were far more likely than pro-life Republicans to become pro-choice (Fig. 9.7, p. 263). The authors also expect women, for whom the issue is more tied to identity, to change party rather than view more often than men (pp. 261–264).

### Conclusion (pp. 264–266)
The authors state that in these cases, "and presumably many others as well", partisan choices were powerfully shaped by group loyalties. Even on race and abortion, most people choose parties based on who they are rather than what they think (p. 264).

**Experimental support.**
- *Craig and Richeson (2014), study 1:* white political Independents were asked whether they had heard California had become majority-minority, versus a control question. The first group leaned Republican significantly more. The effect was twice as strong in the West as nationally, with an 11-point rise in Republican leaning and a 15-point fall in Democratic leaning (p. 265).
- *Study 2:* white subjects reading about a minority-majority projection for 2042 expressed more conservative views than those reading about geographic mobility. This held even on seemingly unrelated issues (defense spending, health care reform). The authors' interpretation is that threats to white numerical dominance heightened white identity and associated Republican attachments and conservative views (p. 265).
- *Kuo, Malhotra and Mo (2014):* a staged "microaggression" (a research assistant questioning Asian-American students' U.S. citizenship) raised Democratic partisanship by 13 points (3 points among white students). It also made these students see Republicans as closed-minded and ignorant, though Republicans were never mentioned (pp. 265–266).

**Parties as coalitions (p. 266).** For most people, partisanship reflects where "people like me" belong, and they often let their party tell them what to think. Self-described liberals mostly end up Democratic and conservatives Republican, but the folk-theory reading of that link is "quite misleading". If elections have policy content, it comes primarily from the relationships between parties and social groups (Bawn et al. 2012), not from voters. Parties are "coalitions with loose edges and indeterminate centers". Some groups are included for historical or idiosyncratic reasons (beer distributors and evangelicals now share a party). When coalitions shift, politicians adjust positions (Karol 2009), and intellectuals and policy communities define what each party stands for (Noel 2013). Party elites in office then pursue a predictable "ideological" agenda, but not because rank-and-file partisans hold coherent policy positions (p. 266). The chapter closes by asking how much of the rest of political worldviews is built on partisan foundations (p. 266).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* The logic of Figs. 9.4–9.5: if policy views drove party change, people with different views should move differently. Take 100 hypothetical white southerners, using margin = % Democratic − % Republican.

Year 1:
- Group A (policy conservatives, 60 people): 42 D, 18 R. So 42/60 = 70% D, 18/60 = 30% R, margin = 70 − 30 = **+40**.
- Group B (policy moderates, 40 people): 28 D, 12 R. So 28/40 = 70%, 12/40 = 30%, margin = **+40**.

Year 2:
- A: 24 D, 36 R. So 40% D, 60% R, margin = **−20**.
- B: 16 D, 24 R. So 40% D, 60% R, margin = **−20**.

Both groups moved 40 − (−20) = 60 points. The partisan gap between the two groups was 0 before and after. A policy-driven story predicts the conservatives move more than the moderates, and here they did not. This is the pattern the authors read as evidence that group belonging (a shifting regional-identity balance), not policy, moved party ID. One toy dataset cannot prove this; it only shows what the test looks for.

## Concepts
- [[group-theory-of-politics]] (new) — voters judge parties as collections of social groups and align with "people like me" (pp. 232, 266)
- [[partisan-identification-as-social-identity]] (new) — party loyalty is both a social identity and largely a product of other identities, often inherited like religion (pp. 234–235)
- [[folk-theory-of-democracy]] — the view that informed voters choose policies and parties respond. The authors say its reading of the ideology–party link is misleading (pp. 237, 266)
- [[party-realignment]] (new) — rare, quick shifts in the balance of group loyalties, usually from changed political resonance of group identities (p. 234)
- [[issue-evolution]] (new) — the view (Carmines and Stimson) that realignment follows elite shifts on an issue. The authors accept a role for elite cues but as group signals (p. 250)
- [[partisan-persuasion]] (new) — party loyalty shaping issue positions. The authors say most tests confound it with issue voting (p. 259)
- [[cross-pressures]] (new) — conflicting group attachments, such as a Republican Catholic facing a Catholic Democratic candidate (p. 244)
- [[feeling-thermometer]] (new) — 0–100 rating of warmth toward a group, used here as a proxy for identity (p. 255)
- [[generational-replacement]] (new) — change from younger cohorts entering the electorate with different loyalties. It explains part, not all, of southern change (p. 249)
- [[heuristics-cues]] — elite cues as signals of where one's group belongs (p. 250)

## Canonical instances
- [[gamm-boston-new-deal]] — five Boston ethnic groups realigned at different times and ways, 1920–40 (pp. 236–240)
- [[kennedy-1960-religious-voting]] — Catholic and Protestant votes deviate from "normal" for religious identity (pp. 241–245)
- [[catholic-republicans-1956-1960-panel]] — weak Catholic identity predicts voting for the Catholic candidate despite party (p. 244)
- [[southern-white-realignment]] — slow shift in party ID, weak policy link, and the thermometer analysis (pp. 246–258)
- [[abortion-panel-1982-1997]] — party shapes abortion views, and gender identity resists (pp. 258–264)
- [[craig-richeson-demographic-threat]] — demographic-shift information moves party leaning (p. 265)
- [[kuo-malhotra-mo-microaggression]] — a staged microaggression raises Democratic ID among Asian-Americans (pp. 265–266)
- [[key-counties-civil-war]] — counties' voting persisting from the Civil War (p. 234)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Disentangling ideology→party from party→ideology ↔ causal inference: reverse causation and confounding in observational data (pp. 234, 259).
- [structural-analogy] Party label shaping issue positions ↔ heuristics/cues and motivated reasoning in political psychology — the same direction of influence (party first, opinion later); the authors attribute it to group loyalty (pp. 262–264, 266).
- [metaphor] Parties as "coalitions of groups with loose edges" ↔ social capital/network membership as basis for collective action — membership shapes behavior more than stated goals (p. 266).

## Questions this raises
- How can identity and policy be separated cleanly in modern data, where groups and policy views are tightly aligned? The authors say no method gauges overall impact (p. 235).
- If policy content comes from party–group coalitions, what makes elites choose a particular bundle of policies for a coalition (p. 266)?
- The abortion test uses small cells and treats gender identity as the moderator. Would the same pattern hold for other issues where identity is less tied to the issue (pp. 260–264, n. 36)?
- Are the experimental identity-threat effects lasting, or do they fade like the Kennedy 1960 bump (pp. 245, 265)?
