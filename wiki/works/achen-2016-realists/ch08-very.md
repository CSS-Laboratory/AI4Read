# The Very Basis of Reasons: Groups, Social Identities, and Political Psychology

## L0 — One line
Achen and Bartels argue that democratic theory should rest on the older realist, group-based view of political psychology, in which social identities come before policy preferences, and not on the rationalist "folk theory" of the individual voter.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what can replace folk-democratic and retrospective theories, which expect too much of ordinary citizens. Achen and Bartels argue for the older realist, group-theoretic tradition: people think through the groups they belong to, and group ties come before policy preferences. They trace this view from Madison and nineteenth-century sociology through Bentley, then its decline after the 1960s, and its return as identity theory. They say identity is still imperfectly integrated into political science, often not measured, and that a realistic political psychology is needed as the base of democratic theory.

**Prerequisites:** The "folk theory of democracy" and "retrospective voting" from earlier chapters; the idea of rational choice; what a survey or lab experiment is. No mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The authors set up the chapter as a search for an alternative after two earlier foundations, populist/folk theory and retrospective voting, each of which the authors say asks too much of ordinary people (p. 214).
- The realist tradition runs from Madison (Federalist 10) through nineteenth-century sociologists. It holds that humans live in groups and that thought is shaped by group cultures. The authors call rational-choice liberalism "a scientific error" for thinking about democracy (pp. 214–216).
- Bentley (1908) made groups the basis of politics and of attitudes ("the very basis of reasons"). He said little about how groups shape opinions (pp. 217–218).
- Experiments (Asch, Sherif, Tajfel) and community studies show that group pressure and group attachment are strong and easily created. The causal mechanisms are still disputed (pp. 219–221).
- Group theory was the starting point of key 1950s–60s works in political science, including the Columbia voting studies and *The American Voter*. Pluralism, an offshoot, defended the equilibrium of group pressures as tolerably democratic and, in the authors' view, at its weakest showed 1950s complacency. Pluralism's reputation fell after the turmoil of the 1960s and group theory declined with it (pp. 222–225).
- Folk theory returned via participatory theory and Downsian rational choice. Psychology's "cognitive turn" and lab methods also favoured isolated individuals over real groups (pp. 225–228).
- Identity theory (emotional attachment to a group as part of the self) brought groups back. It explains why people adopt their group's opinions (pp. 228–229).
- The authors call for measuring identity, studying elites' role in making cleavages, testing identity effects outside the lab, and making identity theory more cognitive. Chapters 9–10 begin this work (pp. 229–231).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: where the argument stands (pp. 213–214)
- Earlier parts of the book covered "populist" theories: the preferences of ordinary people are the foundation of good government. The authors also call this the "liberal," "individualist" or "intellectualist" family. It relies on careful thought by citizens (p. 213).
- The authors judge that real citizens rarely match the attentive, judicious individuals of the folk theory (pp. 213–214).
- The second class, retrospective voting, looks for ways for citizens with modest information to control leaders (Schumpeterian). The authors say it asks too much of people and yields "capricious judgments" at elections (p. 214).
- The chapter outlines a political psychology that the authors think is more accurate. Most of it, they say, is not new. The Founders and social scientists a century ago understood it better than we do now (p. 214).
- Why it was lost, in their account: folk-democratic thinking and myopic retrospection also afflict political science. Popular research tools fit bad ideas better than good ones (p. 214).

### The Realist Political Tradition in the West (pp. 214–222)
**Madison.** Realist thinking appears in the Hebrew Bible, Aristotle and Thucydides, and in the Federalist Papers (p. 214). Madison (Federalist 10) says people divide into parties and are more disposed to vex each other than to cooperate. Even "frivolous and fanciful distinctions" can kindle conflict (pp. 214–215).
- The authors read this as foreshadowing group theory: people readily form groups, build "us" and "them," and emotion rather than reason directs group activity. They also read it as anticipating the 20th-century finding that group attachments are easily generated and deeply felt (p. 215).

**Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century social science.**
- Core principle: humans everywhere live in groups, and thought is deeply conditioned by culture, including subcultures (p. 215).
- Marx: classes generate subcultures that shape beliefs, values and willingness to act (p. 215).
- Others extended this to national, racial, ethnic, religious and professional groups. Tarde stressed imitation. Le Bon stressed the irrational psychology of groups. Simmel stressed the inevitability of group conflict and the exacerbating or moderating role of overlapping group memberships. Ross and Small brought the tradition to the US. Gumplowicz pioneered most of the central ideas (pp. 215–216).
- For these scholars "mental life was group life." Religion, nationality, gender roles, partisan loyalties and so on are taught by family, culture and subcultures, and socialization continues in adulthood. "Thinking for yourself" is usually a switch between two preformulated sets of ideas (p. 216).
- The authors adopt this realist tradition. They argue liberalism is "too shallow" for the weight put on it, and that rational-choice liberalism is "a scientific error" for thinking about democracy (p. 216).
- Qualification: liberal rational choice works well for choices such as more or less pay for similar work, or cheap versus expensive food of the same quality (p. 216). Schumpeter observed that once one leaves economics for politics, the choices take on a completely different character. The authors add that most people have little or no direct experience of politics (p. 216).
- Imported economic ideas, such as the spatial model of elections, perform poorly empirically (chapter 2). The same logic applies to deliberative democracy, which the authors call an idealistic version of liberalism (pp. 216–217).
- Footnote 3 (authors' view): they have no dispute with rational choice itself, which is nearly tautological. The issue is the citizen's starting point. Citizens begin from group affiliations, not from preferences over policies. Group theory can be given a rational-choice microfoundation (Truman, Hechter, Hardin). "Self-interested individualistic rational choice is the wrong 'how'" (p. 217).

**Neglect of government, then Bentley.**
- Early sociologists spoke to politics only in part (states from conquest; conflict once states exist). Policy-making got little attention (p. 217).
- Nineteenth-century American political scientists also ignored interest groups. Burgess, Wilson and Bryce are given as examples (p. 217).
- Bentley (*The Process of Government*, 1908) stressed all sorts of groups: organized interest groups, self-conscious but unorganized groups, potential groups, and governmental actors. He saw most government decisions as shaped by the power and intensity of rival groups. Intense minorities can win when the majority is apathetic or unorganized (p. 218).
- The authors judge that little of theoretical import in later group theories of policy-making is missing from Bentley. In their estimation it is the most important book of 20th-century political science (p. 218).
- For the authors' point here, Bentley treated groups as the "mainsprings" of attitudes and interests, "the very basis of reasons." He said little about how groups shape opinions, taking earlier sociology as having established it (p. 218).

**Filling in the evidence.** Later public-opinion scholars (for example Graves 1928) followed Bentley. The authors say these early works were humanistic and lacked inferential rigor, leaving scope for later empirical work (pp. 218–219).
- Community studies: the Lynds' "Middletown" and Warner's "Yankee City." Participant observation confirmed that people took views from their groups, often because others made it hard not to. A Middletown attorney recalled that he had believed what professors told him but now thought they were wrong (p. 219).
- Early socialization: Hyman, Greenstein, Hess and Torney, Langton, and Jennings and Niemi established strong effects of family and school on adult allegiances. Effects were not immutable but not easily changed. Most adults felt the same ties to nation, religion, race, ethnicity and party as their parents (pp. 219–220).
- **Asch (1951):** unacquainted male undergraduates judged which of three lines matched a reference line. Alone, more than 99% were correct. In groups whose other members were confederates giving wrong answers at some trials, and speaking first, a large majority conformed on at least some trials and some conformed always. Debriefings ranged from "knew it was wrong but went along" to "my eyes must be deceiving me," and a few said they had not noticed the evidence of their own eyes (p. 220). Sherif's experiments are cited alongside (p. 220).
- **Tajfel's "minimal group" paradigm:** people assigned arbitrarily to meaningless groups quickly favoured their own group. They knew nothing about the others involved, did not profit from the bias, and no prior conflict existed. The authors conclude that the capacity for joining groups and disliking other groups is close to the surface and easily mobilized (p. 220). Blau (1964) offered a proto-formal theory of approval, status, power and group exchange (p. 220).
- Everyday cases: college football rivalries and out-group stereotyping (Hastorf and Cantril), usually harmless (p. 221). Footnote 6 gives an anecdote from an Ann Arbor high-school game (p. 221).
- With larger stakes, stereotyping can make the "other" seem evil or subhuman, and campaigns can mobilize group tensions against a minority. Allport found good-"us" versus bad-"them" boundaries widespread. Later work finds that many, "perhaps most," individuals organize political thinking around social groups and their role in parties (p. 221).
- **Conclusion of this section.** The evidence validates the realist view of how people think. Mechanisms remain in dispute: are group attachments dark irrationalities or a misguided pursuit of self-interest? Do group ideas come mainly from people's own needs or from elite cues? The authors say the central realist claim is "beyond serious dispute" (pp. 221–222).

*Intuition note.* The authors present group attachment as natural ("People are naturally group-oriented," p. 215) and close to the surface, easily mobilized (p. 220). Even a meaningless group label produced favouritism in Tajfel's experiments, and in Asch's experiments a group of confederates giving wrong answers led some subjects to go along against their own eyes.

### The High Point of Group Theory Influence and Its Decline (pp. 222–226)
- In the 1950s–60s, the key works on American politics (Truman), empirical political theory (Easton) and comparative politics (Almond and Powell) all started from groups making demands on government. Individuals played little role. Elections appeared mainly as a "feedback loop" (p. 222).
- The Columbia studies showed that group memberships (Protestant versus Catholic, union versus not, white versus minority) strongly shaped vote choice. People adapted their ideas to their candidate's, or less-informed people assumed (sometimes wrongly) that he agreed with them (p. 222).
- Critics: memberships might proxy for attitudes, and the locales might be unrepresentative. Later researchers used more attitude batteries and national samples. This gained representativeness but lost community context (p. 222). Footnote 7 names Huckfeldt and colleagues as an exception (p. 222). The authors say national surveys of unrelated individuals suited folk-theory thinking (p. 222).
- *The American Voter* reached conclusions similar to Columbia's in most respects: typical people lacked clear, stable issue attitudes and thought about politics in terms of groups, not ideology. It added that party identification was fundamental to vote choice and shaped issue positions, so group memberships largely drove policy views and not vice versa (p. 223).
- **Pluralism**, as the authors describe it, conceded the roles of groups and the inequalities among them, but defended the resulting equilibrium as tolerably democratic (p. 223).
  - Dahl (New Haven): almost everyone has some resources, no single resource works everywhere, so no group dominated all aspects of city life (p. 223).
  - Banfield (Chicago): where no meaningful public interest criterion exists, competition among interested parties supplies one (p. 223).
  - Authors' assessment: this was a heretical departure from earlier group theorists, who saw politics as inevitably conflictual. Pluralists, writing after the Depression and the Second World War, stressed stability. They were "realists in their view of people, and meliorists in their view of American democracy," had "gotten well past the folk theory," but at their weakest showed "1950s complacency" (pp. 223–224).
- The 1960s: civil-rights protest met violent resistance, assassinations, urban unrest, Vietnam War protests and police suppression. The authors say this would not have surprised Bentley, who included "battle and riot" in group pressure (p. 224). Footnote 8 says the 1960s demonstrations and riots fit Bentley's arguments, though not always those of the pluralists who claimed his name (p. 224).
- The pluralists' reputation was shaken. Critics saw the establishment as out of touch. Theories of popular mobilization and moral resistance gained attention, and the demands of ordinary people were seen as morally compelling (pp. 224–225). Truman complained that critics blamed pluralism for the system's failures (p. 225).
- Authors' account of the outcome: group theory, which could have made sense of the 1960s, was forgotten or misremembered as early pluralism. Pluralism and group theory declined together (p. 225).
- What replaced them, in the authors' view, was the folk theory returning. The science against it kept accumulating but was ignored (chapter 2). Groups were "moved offstage" (p. 225).
  - Participatory theory (Pateman) made direct citizen participation the ideal. Footnote 9: it descends from the same liberal tradition (p. 225).
  - Downs's economic approach took preferences as given, rooted ideology in self-interest, and had no social groups (p. 225).
- The authors judge that this body of work was advanced in method and antiquated in ideas. It had limited impact on the discipline, and its assumptions ignored well-established findings (p. 226).
- They treat its two big results as substantial achievements that any serious theory must address. Downs's party convergence to the center led to empirical oversimplification, and Arrow's theorem led to conceptual tangles. In their view this tradition is "not foundational" (p. 226).

*Intuition note.* The authors say group theory was dropped for the wrong reason: it was blamed together with pluralism after the 1960s. They also say the group theories they describe as forgotten would have made sense of the 1960s (p. 225).

### Developments in Political Psychology (pp. 226–228)
- Psychology had a "cognitive turn." Simple stimulus-response models of attitudes were inadequate, so researchers stressed mental processing. Social cognition focused on individual thinking (p. 226).
- Experiments could not randomize gender, religion or class, so groups in experiments were usually ad hoc lab groups. This raised doubts about external validity and relevance. Tajfel is cited on social psychologists' unease about "the social vacuum" of experiments (p. 227). Survey experiments raise similar worries (p. 227).
- The preferred tools worked best for artificially isolated individuals, so psychology studied individuals. Footnote 10: group affiliation can be made more or less salient, but not altered (p. 227).
- For political science, a macro social science, borrowing from an increasingly micro psychology became harder. Political groups were addressed only indirectly, and using the results required "complex chains of auxiliary reasoning" (p. 227).
- Some political scientists mastered cognitive psychology and became skeptical of human political cognition (Lodge and Taber). A few studied national political culture (McClosky and Zaller) or party elites' effects on partisans (Zaller 1992). Most used an eclectic "psychologism" as a weak alternative to rational choice (p. 227).
- Conclusion: liberal individualism fit the available tools, so when pressed about democracy, scholars fell back on the folk theory with "occasional nods" to retrospective voting (pp. 227–228).

### Identity Theory in Politics and the Road Forward (pp. 228–231)
- New realities revived scholarship: religious conservatives' electoral role from the 1980s, nationalism after September 11, 2001, and growing Latino and Asian populations (p. 228). The concept of **identity** became central (p. 228).
- Definition used here: identity distinguishes groups an individual belongs to but does not treat as central from groups that are integral to the self. Some Catholics have a purely nominal tie; others take a disparaging remark about Catholics as an attack on themselves. The latter have a Catholic identity. Cantril (1941) is quoted on feeling insulted when one's flag is torn down (p. 228).
- Identities are "not primarily about adherence to a group ideology or creed" but emotional attachments that "transcend thinking." Illustration (Heaney): an atheist visitor in Northern Ireland is asked whether he is a "Protestant atheist or a Catholic atheist" (pp. 228–229).
- Identity theory explains why people adopt their group's opinions, which group theory had stressed but not explained. Example: the temperance movement, hard to see as self-interest, becomes understandable as a "symbolic crusade" to reinforce Protestant identity under threat from Catholic immigration (Gusfield). Self-sacrifice also becomes understandable because "in some sense" people act in their own interest (p. 229). Turner's self-categorization theory links identity to cognitive psychology (p. 229).
- Race: Dawson found that African-Americans' political behavior is "powerfully influenced" by perceptions of group interest ("shared fate"), despite disagreements on policy. Hutchings and Jefferson found racial identity a key predictor of Democratic identification among African-Americans, beyond policy views (p. 229).
- Other identity work: Cramer's study of rural consciousness among working-class whites in upstate Wisconsin. The authors call the new scholarship "a genuine intellectual advance" with deeper evidence than earlier work (p. 229).
- **Shortcomings** (authors' view, p. 230):
  - Much research mentions identity but does not measure it.
  - Group membership is substituted for identity, as if members were interchangeable. Degrees of identity then cannot explain within-group differences.
  - Without careful measurement, identity effects cannot be separated from correlated attitudes. Does attitude motivate identity, or identity produce attitudes? The inference problem of the Columbia studies remains.
- A realist theory of democracy must rest on a realistic political psychology, and "nothing of that kind exists" now (p. 230). They urge setting Bentham aside and bringing Madison back in (p. 230).
- **Agenda** (p. 230):
  1. Bring identity theory into the macro political world and understand how elites structure cleavages. Identities are malleable and constructed, but politicians do not make cleavages "just as they please."
  2. Show identity effects in the real political world, not only the lab. For example, trace a surprise candidate's support to identity groups and not only to preferences or ideology.
  3. Make identity theory more cognitive. An implicit stimulus-response account of identity acquisition "will not do." Formalize how people acquire identities and use them to adopt ideas.
- The authors admit they can make only a start in the remaining chapters (p. 230).
- **Preview:** Chapter 9 argues that social identities have trumped policy reasoning in the politics of religion, race and abortion. Issue positions are "generally derivative" (p. 231). Chapter 10 builds a framework in which partisan identity shapes perceptions of candidates, issues and simple facts. It allows for differing exposure to information and differing partisan attachment. It is said to account, with considerable accuracy, for differences between Democrats and Republicans across the range of political expertise (p. 231). The concluding chapter draws implications for normative theories and policy-making (p. 231).

*Intuition note.* In the authors' account, identity is an emotional attachment to a group and is not primarily about adhering to its creed (p. 228). If so, issue positions may follow identity, and substituting group membership for measured identity means degrees of identity cannot explain differences within the group (p. 230).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ten voters sit at issue positions 1–10 (1 = far left, 10 = far right). Blue's platform is at 3 and Red's at 8. Identities: Blue identifiers are voters at 1, 2, 3, 4, 6. Red identifiers are voters at 5, 7, 8, 9, 10.

**Issue-distance model.** Each voter picks the nearer platform.
- Voter 5: |5−3| = 2 and |5−8| = 3, so Blue.
- Voter 6: |6−3| = 3 and |6−8| = 2, so Red.
- Voters 1–4 go Blue and 7–10 go Red. Result: 5 Blue, 5 Red.

**Identity model.** Each votes for the identity party. Result: 5 Blue, 5 Red, with voter 5 voting Red and voter 6 voting Blue.

Both models give the same totals, but the issue model gets voters 5 and 6 wrong. If the observed votes follow identity, the issue model predicts 8 of 10 individuals correctly (80%) and the identity model 10 of 10.

**A test of the two models.** Blue moves its platform to 9.
- Issue model: voters 1–8 are nearer Red (for example, voter 8: |8−8| = 0 versus |8−9| = 1). Voters 9 and 10 are nearer Blue (voter 10: 1 versus 2). Result: 2 Blue, 8 Red.
- Identity model: nothing changes, so 5 Blue, 5 Red. Under the chapter's account, Blue identifiers would adopt their party's new position.

Which prediction holds is an empirical question. The toy only shows why the two views come apart.

## Concepts
- [[folk-theory-of-democracy]] — idealized view of informed citizens choosing policies. Here it is the "populist/liberal" foundation the authors reject (pp. 213–214).
- [[retrospective-voting]] — judging leaders by past results. The authors say it asks too much of people and yields capricious judgments (p. 214).
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — imported economic model of voters picking the nearest party. The authors say it performs poorly empirically (pp. 216–217).
- [[arrows-impossibility-theorem]] — mentioned as a major result of the Downsian tradition that the authors see as leading to conceptual tangles (p. 226).
- [[group-theory-of-politics]] (new) — view that groups are the basis of political attitudes and interests, and that government decisions result from group pressure (pp. 215, 218).
- [[realist-tradition]] (new) — Madison and 19th-century sociologists' view of people as group-bound and often emotional. The authors set it against Enlightenment rationalism (pp. 214–216).
- [[social-identity]] (new) — a group tie that is part of the self-concept and emotional, not mainly a creed (p. 228).
- [[minimal-group-paradigm]] (new) — Tajfel's finding that arbitrary, meaningless groups quickly produce in-group favouritism (p. 220).
- [[conformity-experiment]] (new) — lab studies (Asch, Sherif) showing group judgments can override individuals' own (p. 220).
- [[pluralism]] (new) — offshoot of group theory defending the equilibrium of competing groups as tolerably democratic (p. 223).
- [[out-group-stereotyping]] (new) — drawing boundaries between a good "us" and a bad "them" (p. 221).
- [[cognitive-turn]] (new) — psychology's shift to individual mental processing, which pulled attention away from groups (p. 226).
- [[symbolic-crusade]] (new) — a movement understood as defending group identity, such as temperance as Protestant identity under threat (p. 229).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Minimal-group favouritism ↔ political-science: partisan identity that persists without policy agreement — both show attachment preceding reasons.
- [metaphor] "Switching from one preformulated set of ideas to another" ↔ cognitive science: an implicit stimulus-response model of belief acquisition, which the authors say will not do (p. 230).
- [structural-analogy] The authors' call to separate identity from correlated attitudes ↔ causal inference: confounding and reverse causation in observational data.

## Questions this raises
- How can political scientists measure the strength of an identity (not just membership) so that its effect is separated from correlated attitudes (p. 230)?
- What are the limits of elite malleability: how far can politicians construct cleavages, and what stops them (p. 230)?
- If identities come first, how does the authors' framework in chapter 10 produce partisan perceptions of simple facts (p. 231)?
- Are group attachments irrational, or a misguided pursuit of self-interest, and do group ideas come from within or from elite cues (p. 221)?
