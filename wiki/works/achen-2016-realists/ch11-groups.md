# Groups and Power: Toward a Realist Theory of Democracy

## L0 — One line
Voters mostly follow group and party identities rather than policy judgments or sound retrospection; elections still bring real but limited benefits, and the authors argue that a realist theory of democracy will have to give a central place to group power and inequality.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The concluding chapter asks what is left of democratic theory once the evidence of the book is accepted. The authors review why the folk theory (voters choose policies) and the retrospective theory (voters reward or punish performance) both fail. They argue instead that voters mostly choose parties to match their social identities, so election results are close to random changes of elite coalition. Elections still bring real benefits: accepted rulers, turnover, tolerated opposition, and avoidance of flagrant abuses. Policy is made by political elites and organized groups, so group power matters. The authors call for more social and economic equality and less money in politics, while admitting they offer no finished theory.

**Prerequisites:** The earlier chapters' main claims (folk theory, retrospective voting, party identification); basic US and Weimar political history; no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The folk theory of democracy fails as an empirical account: voters know little, follow party and group loyalties, and often "mirror" parties. Three proposed rescues also fall short: cues (information shortcuts for voters), party government (parties as carriers of voters' ideology) and deliberative models (citizens reasoning together) (pp. 299–302).
- Retrospective voting (judging incumbents by past performance) also fails to give reliable accountability. Voters punish incumbents for events beyond their control and weight election-year income growth too heavily (pp. 303–306).
- The authors' alternative: people choose parties to align with social-group coalitions. Policy agreement is mostly a by-product. Stated reasons are often after-the-fact rationalizations, and this holds for the educated too (pp. 307–311).
- In the authors' account, elections work roughly like "musical chairs". When the party balance is close, outcomes turn on swing voters (the less-informed "pure" Independents), and officials' views match constituents' views only loosely. Representation is more about identity congruence (politicians matching voters' identities) than issue congruence (matching their policy views) (pp. 311–313).
- Hitler's rise is read as an identity-group story: Protestant nationalist voters shifted to the Nazis, while Socialist, Communist and Catholic blocs held. Retrospection mattered but was channelled by identity groups (pp. 314–316).
- Democracy still has real benefits: authoritative agreement on who rules, turnover, tolerated opposition, civic development, and punishment of flagrant norm violations. These are not the benefits the folk theory promises (pp. 316–319).
- Policy-making is done by political elites of several kinds, including organized groups, so power is central. How groups gain clout, whether leaders are faithful, and how party systems aggregate interests are open research questions (pp. 319–324).
- "More democracy" would require less economic and social inequality and less influence of money. The folk theory, by the authors' account, props up elite rule (pp. 325–328).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening and plan of the chapter (pp. 297–299)
- **Epigraph (Schattschneider, 1960):** the "crisis" is in democratic theory, not in democracy. People define democracy first and are then confused by what they see (p. 297).
- **The folk theory's dominance.** Proponents thought human dignity required self-government: people choose their leaders at the polls and hold them accountable. The argument assumed good citizens would monitor government thoughtfully. The folk theory became dominant in American popular and scholarly thought, and in virtually every established democracy, even parliamentary ones (pp. 297–298).
- **The authors' claim.** Consensus on the folk theory has "impeded thought". Critical traditions exist, but critics often "back off" and affirm the conventional faith (p. 298). The defences are "in tatters" empirically. Yet many thinkers keep the folk theory as a normative benchmark (p. 298).
- **Limits of the chapter.** The authors do not supply a finished new theory. They (i) summarize the evidence against existing theories, (ii) clear ground for a realist theory and highlight concepts likely to loom large in it, (iii) show their framework fits Hitler's 1932 victory, and (iv) draw practical implications (pp. 298–299).

### Democratic citizens and the populist ideal (pp. 299–303)
- **Citizen capacities.** The folk theory needs people to form policy preferences, locate candidates, set aside bias, and pick a candidate matching their views (p. 299).
- **Chapter 2 findings.** Most people follow little news beyond headlines, do not know policy details or party positions, and often vote for parties whose long-standing positions differ from their own. They identify with ethnic, racial, occupational, religious or other groups and often with a party. Even attentive citizens mostly adopt party positions: "mirrors of the parties, not masters" (p. 299).
- **Consequence.** The folk theory fails, and so do its scholarly versions, including the spatial theory of voting. The authors compare it to ether theory: it rests on 19th-century foundations and the evidence "has passed it by". They add that the internal contradictions of the framework, illuminated by Arrow's theorem, cut off "all obvious escape routes" (pp. 299–300).
- **Cues.** Some scholars rely on cues (Popkin, Lupia, Lupia–McCubbins). Per chapter 2, many voters ignore cues or find them hard to read, and cues are less informative than proponents suppose. Cue-taking does not prevent voting wrong. Aggregating many choices dilutes errors but does not eliminate them (p. 300).
- **Party government.** If partisanship reflected considered ideology, it would reflect thoughtful citizenship. The authors say partisanship often has little ideological content. Issue preferences rarely cohere, and citizens often choose parties for non-ideological reasons: a past economic recovery, group solidarity, childhood teaching of "people like us". Issue congruence is "largely a byproduct" of such connections (pp. 300–301). Voters' stated views often match their party because parties teach them what to say (Lenz 2012, fn. 3, p. 301).
- **Deliberative and participatory theory.** The authors say these models assume rationality and mutual consideration, so they rest on the same unrealistic view of human nature. In practice deliberation is very likely to be "undemocratic": many people dislike political debate and disagreement, fall silent, and let better-educated people dominate (Mansbridge's New England town meetings) (p. 301). The authors call this a telling sign that deliberative theorists are trying to "whip the public" into doing things it does not want to do (pp. 301–302).
- **Canadian citizens' assemblies (British Columbia, Ontario).** Each assembly spent a year on education and deliberation and recommended a new voting rule nearly unanimously. Voters rejected it in each referendum (p. 302).
  - BC: 58% in the first vote (60% needed), then 39% in a second referendum four years later.
  - Ontario: under 40% (fn. 4, p. 302).
  - The authors do not know which causes were to blame, but call it an "expensive, dramatic failure" if the aim was to shape the electorate's judgment (p. 302).
- **Practical stakes.** Reforms based on the folk theory (more referendums, weaker party officials in presidential nominations) strengthened narrow interest groups and produced self-defeating choices (chapter 3 examples: car insurance, children's dental health, homes burned down) (p. 302). Plebiscitary reform often damages citizens' real interests. Effective democracy needs a balance of popular preference and elite expertise. The aim is "more effective popular influence", not maximum popular influence (p. 303).

> *Intuition note.* If voters mostly follow parties and groups, theories that assume voters weigh policies do not describe elections well. Reforms built on those theories may hand power to organized interests instead of to ordinary people.

### Democratic citizens and the retrospective theory (pp. 303–306)
- **The theory.** Retrospective voting needs only that voters know whether times were good or bad under the incumbent. Reward good times and punish bad (p. 303).
- **Two requirements (chapter 4).** For the mechanism to work effectively, voters must (1) judge the government's own role in good or bad outcomes, and (2) judge sensibly whether times were good or bad. Both are hard. Examples: people know crime mainly through local news. Their views of environmental threats are "virtually uncorrelated" with experts'. Even on the economy, they may fixate on current conditions (pp. 303–304).
- **Failure of attribution (chapter 5).**
  - Voters on the Jersey Shore punished President Wilson for the 1916 shark attacks, cutting his vote share there by "as much as 10 percentage points". He could not have prevented them or remedied them (p. 304).
  - Across the 20th century, presidents' parties lost votes in times and places too wet or too dry. If voters were judging responses, as many incumbents should have been rewarded as punished. Instead, punishment was much more common (p. 304).
- **Failure of assessment (chapter 6).** Voters respond strongly to income growth in the months before an election. They respond little to growth before the election year. Examples: Carter (1980) was punished for an election-year recession despite earlier growth. Reagan (1984) won in a landslide on an election-year boom despite weak earlier growth (p. 305).
  - Implications: myopic voters are unlikely to select competent managers, and the authors find no evidence that voters' judgments predict later economic performance. They also give incumbents little reason to pursue sustained growth, and the authors find an "electoral cycle", with real income growth peaking in election years and falling afterwards (p. 305).
- **Crisis (chapter 7).** The authors conclude that voters in the Great Depression did not transcend myopic retrospection. FDR's 1936 support rested on election-year income growth. Their analysis suggests that if the 1938 recession had occurred two years earlier, FDR would not have been reelected (pp. 305–306).
- **Depression elections elsewhere.** The American notion of ideological realignment "does not travel well". Conservative incumbents were generally replaced by more liberal parties (United States), but leftist governments were often replaced by conservatives (Britain, Australia). Where all mainstream parties were discredited, people turned to extremist parties or protest. Alberta replaced its agrarian socialist government with the Social Credit Party; the economy revived and Social Credit dominated for decades (p. 306).
- **Verdict.** The authors' concern is not that retrospection is "thin" but that "it simply does not work". It "will not bear the normative weight" its proponents place on it. Democratic theory is "in a shambles": the conventional defences romanticize, mathematize or bowdlerize human nature, and none portrays human beings realistically (p. 306).

> *Intuition note.* Retrospective voting sounds undemanding, but for it to work well voters must separate government's effect from luck and must weigh a whole term. The evidence says they do neither well, so reward and punishment are partly random.

### Democratic citizens and social groups (pp. 307–309)
- **Factions and identity.** Madison held that humans are not angels and are prone to faction. Group attachments (kinship, ethnic, racial, religious, occupational, national) are powerful. A person who defines themselves by such a category has an "identity" (p. 307).
- **Core claim.** The most important factions are parties. Voters choose parties "first and foremost" to align with a coalition of social groups, not mainly by calculating policy distance. Footnote 5: this differs from the older idea that parties are coalitions of interest groups, since social groups may lack organized interest groups (p. 307).
- **Evidence from chapter 9 (author's summary).**
  - Ethnic groups in Boston responded differently to the New Deal. Group politics explained this better than policy calculations (p. 307).
  - Catholics and Protestants responded to JFK differently in 1960 than to other Democratic candidates, not because his platform differed but because his Catholic identity activated loyalties and antipathies (p. 307).
  - Southern white partisan realignment: those who felt most warmly toward "southerners" as a group showed a drop of almost 40 points in attachment to the Democratic Party over four decades. Those with neutral feelings dropped less than half as much. Democratic support fell at fairly similar rates among supporters and opponents of racial integration. Views on government aid to blacks sharply distinguished Democrats from Republicans only from the 1980s (pp. 307–308).
  - Abortion (conflicting identities): almost half of pro-choice Republican women in 1982 had left the party by 1997, with a much smaller effect among men. The authors attribute this to abortion's greater role in women's gender identity. Conversely, more than half of pro-life men who were Democrats in 1982 became pro-choice by 1997, versus about one-fourth of pro-life Republican men. This shows partisan influence on views (p. 308).
- **Implications.** People rank competing group loyalties, so no group belongs wholly to one party. The authors say this is an implication of their account, not a disconfirmation. Parties make sense "in social identity terms, not as ideological frameworks" (pp. 308–309).
- **Candidates and accountability.** Candidates are judged partly on whether they can "speak our language" (Fenno). Talk is not action, and accountability to groups is hard. It may be easier when politicians belong to the group: Broockman found African-American legislators more likely to help African-American citizens outside their districts. Carnes found that members of Congress with blue-collar work experience took more liberal economic positions even after controlling for party and district. Burden found personal characteristics sometimes cause deviation from party orthodoxy (p. 309). More often the party cannot or does not do much for the group, and usually this seems not to matter (Egan 2013). So group politics does not automatically solve the accountability problem (p. 309).

> *Intuition note.* On this view, a voter asks "which party is for people like me?", not "which party has my policies?". That makes party loyalty stable even when policies and group members' views do not line up.

### Voter rationality and rationalization (pp. 309–311)
- **Interview talk.** Interviews make citizens sound ideological and rational. Some fieldworkers conclude the other evidence misleads. The authors reply (chapter 10) that this apparent rationality is "a byproduct rather than the foundation of group politics" (pp. 309–310).
- **Rationalization.** People adopt the views of their parties and groups. The highly engaged may build ideologies justifying their loyalties, and sometimes construct convenient "facts". Better-informed citizens have more elaborate worldviews because their rationalizations are "better rehearsed". In the budget-deficit case, more knowledgeable citizens showed more partisan bias (p. 310).
- **Intellectuals.** The task of ideal citizenship "is too hard for everyone" (p. 310). Educated people have gone astray as often as others. The authors' examples:
  - antebellum southern professors defending slavery;
  - German professors and students and German nationalism and Nazism;
  - professors backing Bismarck against Catholics;
  - American intellectual prejudice;
  - intellectual support for communism in France, China and elsewhere;
  - American political scientists advising on Vietnam, or favouring Ho Chi Minh (pp. 310–311).
- **Point.** Le Bon and Lippmann are cited: expertise in one field gives no special competence on social questions. "When we say that voters routinely err, we mean all voters", including the authors and readers (p. 311).

### A realist view of elections (pp. 311–316)
- **How elections work.** Democrats, Republicans and Independents make up roughly equal shares of the US population. Campaigns largely remind voters of partisan identities. Group communications help people see how their groups connect to candidates. The great majority vote consistently for their party (p. 311). Lieber (1839): parties teach us what to think more often than we teach them (p. 311).
- **Defections and Independents.** Defections often reflect salient religious, racial or gender identities rather than issue preferences. Many Independents "lean" and act like partisans. Because the parties are of about equal size, these effects "approximately cancel" (p. 312).
- **Swing voters.** "Pure" Independents are typically less informed, unclear about which groups belong to which party, and swayed by incumbent familiarity, a challenger's charisma, or "time for a change". When the party balance is close, as a "law of the pendulum" suggests it usually is in two-party systems, outcomes turn on these voters. Converse is cited: the least informed members hold the critical balance (p. 312).
- **Conclusion.** From the standpoint of representativeness and accountability, outcomes are "essentially random choices among the available parties—musical chairs". "Throw the bums out" elections typically do not produce real policy mandates, even landslides. They put a different elite coalition in charge (p. 312). Footnote 6 lists exceptions: unclear responsibility in coalitions, or non-credible alternatives, as in Japan for most of the post-war period (p. 312). This peaceful change beats revolution but is not deliberate policy change (p. 312).
- **Representation.** Officials' views are only roughly like their voters'. The relationship is "correlation, not causation", and Miller–Stokes correlations are modest (p. 313). Footnote 8: the authors now think those relationships are mainly descriptive, not causal (p. 313).
- **Identity congruence test.** Voters primarily look for politicians matching their identities. If so, politicians' and electorates' identities should correlate strongly. In the 2014–2015 House, 98% of black-majority districts elected black representatives, and only 5% of white-majority districts elected African-Americans (p. 313). By contrast (chapter 2), moderately liberal districts elected Republicans 46% of the time and moderately conservative districts elected Democrats 25% (p. 313).

> *Intuition note.* If the policy match between voters and politicians is weak while the identity match is nearly total, voters may be choosing "one of us" rather than "my policies". The authors read the district pattern this way.

- **Hitler (pp. 314–316).**
  - Folk-theory question: how could Germans have favoured those policies? The authors cite most scholars: voters first decided whether they liked Hitler, then adopted his views. Allen (1984): people were drawn to anti-Semitism because they were drawn to Nazism. Anti-Semitism mattered to a minority and was not a top Nazi electoral priority in the last Weimar elections (Gellately) (p. 314).
  - Retrospection: many observers say hard times and discredited mainstream parties explain the vote, which had a "catchall" quality early on. A woman quoted by Owings recalled jobs and bread arriving after Hitler's rise; the authors call it a clear example of blind retrospection (p. 314).
  - But simple retrospection implies "uniform swing" against incumbents, which fits Hitler's vote poorly. Socialist and Communist areas resisted, and Catholics (especially rural) were nearly immune. These two groups had almost the same vote share in 1932 as in 1924. What collapsed were the old Protestant nationalist parties, the third identity grouping. The right's total share was almost unchanged from 1924, but the Nazis were now the largest, averaging just over a third of the total vote (p. 315).
  - Interpretation: Hitler spoke the language of German nationalism in post-war humiliation. Protestant nationalists recognized him as "one of their own", and desperation led them to leave parties of the same kind. Other groups saw him as anti-socialist and non-Catholic. Economic retrospection mattered but only for a fraction of voters, "channeled by Weimar's political identity groups" (p. 315). Footnote 12: the authors judge social-class analysis to be of "secondary causal relevance" (p. 315).
  - General claim: popular movements need a crisis, blind retrospection and a message fitting a group's identity (chapter 5). The authors say the desire for a strong leader who names domestic enemies is "latent in every democratic electorate" until widespread suffering lets it spread (p. 316).

### What is good about democracy? (pp. 316–319)
- **Answer to "would you rather live in a dictatorship?"** No. Actual democratic processes have "quite real practical virtues unrelated to" the folk theory's ideals. The authors accept Churchill's pragmatic argument. The open task is to identify which features are essential (p. 316).
- **Five benefits of elections** (judiciary and free speech set aside; focus on elections) (p. 317):
  1. *Authoritative agreement on who rules.* Example: the 2000 US presidential election, resolved by a few hundred votes and a five-to-four Court decision, was accepted as legitimate. Key (1961b): elections have "more than a trace of the lottery", which is acceptable if all abide by the result (p. 317).
  2. *Turnover.* Winning parties are eventually defeated, perhaps randomly, and voters increasingly reject incumbents the longer they hold office. This prevents entrenchment, and losers expect a later turn, so they accept the result (p. 317).
  3. *Tolerated opposition.* Competition gives rulers some incentive to tolerate opposition (p. 317). "Loyal opposition" arrived late in theory (p. 318). Many citizens still dislike conflict and compromise, want "a real leader, not a politician", and do not see that skilled compromisers are "the soul" of democracy (p. 318).
  4. *Character development.* Following Mill, engagement may build civic competence. The authors say empirical evidence is scant but suggestive (p. 318).
  5. *Avoiding flagrant norm violations.* Key: public opinion sets "vague limits of permissiveness". Examples: no president will strangle a kitten on camera. Chicago's Mayor Bilandic was replaced after the 1979 snow. Accountability works when the causal chain is clear, the outcome unambiguous and the evaluation widely shared (Arnold). Dictatorships have more corruption because outrage has no organized outlet (pp. 318–319).
- **Limits.** These benefits rarely touch day-to-day national government. Most important causal chains are "complex and untraceable". Dewey's cobbler-and-wearer analogy fits dentists but not presidents, since "thousands of cobblers" are at work (p. 319). The benefits are real and modest, and they do not show that everyday policy-making serves people's interests (p. 319). Footnote 13: Mueller calls elections "useful, but not essential"; Cleary found more electoral competition did not make Mexican municipal governments more responsive (p. 317).

> *Intuition note.* The authors defend democracy for the real but limited benefits above: peaceful, accepted changes of rulers and punishment of obvious abuse. They do not defend it as a machine that turns public opinion into policy.

### Power and identity groups (pp. 319–324)
- **Policy is made by elites and groups.** Pressures from parties, interest groups and the wealthy often trump broad left-leaning majorities (minimum wage, social spending). Pressures from parties, groups and educated elites trump broad right-leaning majorities (silent prayer, parental notification) (p. 320). Policy-making is "a job for specialists". Ordinary citizens are rarely involved. Jacobs and Page found foreign policy-makers responsive to elite and expert opinion, not ordinary citizens (p. 320).
- **Grossmann's study.** It drew on 268 policy histories covering 790 notable enactments in 14 domestic domains. Presidents, agency heads, influential members of Congress and advocacy groups made policy. He wrote that policy-making is "rarely responsive to the public" (p. 320).
- **Power.** The recurring concepts are power and influence. Size helps, but wealth, prestige and media access often bring greater power. Schattschneider: the pluralist chorus "sings with a strong upper-class accent" (p. 321). Power was once central to political science but is neglected in democratic theory. In the folk and retrospective theories every citizen is equal by assumption (p. 321).
- **The authors' normative standard (equal power).** Interest groups and parties must carry voters' interests, with power proportional to their presence in the electorate. The rich and well-organized "cannot have extra power". Spelling this out shows how far we are from democracy and how hard institutional design will be (pp. 321–322).
- **Open empirical questions (authors' list):**
  - How large are biases from resource differences and from unorganized "potential interest groups"? Mostly "rough extrapolation and guesswork" (p. 322).
  - Does the K Street roster reflect society's interests? Unlikely. Grossmann: a few groups are heavily represented and most have minimal representation (p. 322).
  - How do interest groups gain clout, and how well do they sway followers' opinions and votes? Few modern studies exist (p. 322).
  - Are group leaders faithful agents? The monitoring problems of chapters 4–7 recur. Lipset, Trow and Coleman found a rare exception to oligarchy in one union. The authors know of no comparable contemporary studies (pp. 322–323).
  - How do party systems shape group influence? In proportional systems, small parties for isolated groups may get outsized power (ultra-conservative religious parties in Israel). In majoritarian systems, aggregation may shear off the more self-interested or "wicked" demands, or submerge group interests (p. 323).
- **Leadership examples.** Competent leadership means forging effective coalitions. FDR did, overcoming earlier Populist and Progressive failures. The antebellum second party system joined northern "plain Republicans" with southern planters, which papered over slavery (pp. 323–324). Parties' governing ideas are understudied. Croly's ideas helped shape the American welfare state (Noel) (p. 324).
- **Zaller's contrast.** The Downs-style claim: "The rationally ignorant median voter gets what he wants without much effort." The group-politics view: organized policy demanders routinely get what they want at the expense of the unorganized, but ordinary voters, if numerous, cohesive, attentive and pivotal, "also get some of what they want" (p. 324). The authors call this realistic, though not a basis for Fourth of July rhetoric (p. 325).
- **Normative caution.** If voters' demands reflect "affective tribal loyalties", satisfying them is not always desirable (new democracies; 20th-century Germany). Current normative thinking is "hopelessly entangled" with the folk theory's empirical assumptions (p. 325).

### What would it mean to have "more democracy"? (pp. 325–328)
- **The folk-theory slogan.** "The cure for the ills of democracy is more democracy" fails: universal enfranchisement does not automatically yield egalitarian preference reflection (p. 325).
- **Proposed focus: equality.** Effective democracy needs more economic and social equality. Corporations beat workers, big media beat bloggers, affluent views count more than the poor's, and majority groups win more often than minorities. Quantitative studies (Bartels, Gilens, Page) show the imbalances are "very large" (pp. 325–326). Group theory explains why a more egalitarian society would give a more egalitarian politics. Under-represented groups would gain power, and both corporate interests and college professors would count for less (p. 326).
- **Obstacles.** Americans lack "the ideas, the will, and the political organization". Protest groups bound to the folk theory resist leadership and coalition-building, so they fade (p. 326).
- **Money.** A smaller aim is to reduce direct effects of wealth. The United States does less than most established democracies to limit money's effects. Congress's regulation often protects incumbents. Conservative Supreme Court majorities treat spending as protected free speech and have gutted limits. The authors say most big contributors are Republicans, but both parties favour the affluent, insiders and intense minorities (pp. 326–327).
- **Consistency point.** Calls for campaign-finance reform by folk-theory believers contradict their faith: under populist logic, universal suffrage should already suffice. If spending sways elections or donations sway policy, voters are not principled issue voters and elections do not bind policy-makers to the median voter (p. 327).
- **Closing.** Government is "an all-too-human enterprise". Misleading theories are not costless, and the authors state that the folk theory "props up elite rule" (p. 327). "America is a democracy, but it is not very democratic" (p. 327). Abandoning the folk theory is a prerequisite for clarity and real change, but they have no ready answers to the questions their approach raises (p. 328).

## Toy example (ours; not from the text)
A town of 100 voters, two parties, and an economy that is good in year 1 and bad in year 2. All numbers are invented to show the chapter's mechanism.

- 40 Democratic identifiers: 95% vote Democratic, so 38 D and 2 R.
- 40 Republican identifiers: 95% vote Republican, so 38 R and 2 D.
- Partisan totals: D = 38 + 2 = 40; R = 2 + 38 = 40. They cancel.
- 20 pure Independents decide the result.
  - Year 1 (incumbent is D, good economy): 14 vote D, 6 vote R. Totals: D = 40 + 14 = 54, R = 40 + 6 = 46. D wins.
  - Year 2 (bad economy): 5 vote D, 15 vote R. Totals: D = 40 + 5 = 45, R = 40 + 15 = 55. R wins.
- The 80 partisans' views did not change, and the winner flipped because 9 of the 20 swing voters switched (D votes from 14 to 5). The margin moved from D +8 to R +10, an 18-vote swing. Under the authors' account, nothing in this swing reveals a policy mandate. It resembles "musical chairs" (p. 312).

## Concepts
- [[folk-theory-of-democracy]] — idealized view that informed citizens choose policies and government follows; the authors treat it as empirically refuted but still used as a normative benchmark (pp. 297–298).
- [[retrospective-voting]] — voters judge incumbents by past performance; the authors say it fails because voters misattribute responsibility and weigh the term poorly (p. 303).
- [[blind-retrospection]] (new) — punishing or rewarding incumbents for events beyond their control (e.g., shark attacks, droughts) (p. 304).
- [[myopic-retrospection]] (new) — heavy weight on election-year income growth, little on earlier growth; produces randomness in rewards and an "electoral cycle" (p. 305).
- [[heuristics-cues]] — shortcuts for low-information voters; the authors say they work less well in practice and do not prevent mistakes (p. 300).
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — Downsian policy-distance model; the authors count it among the failed scholarly versions of the folk theory (p. 299).
- [[arrow-impossibility-theorem]] — cited as illuminating the folk theory's internal contradictions (pp. 299–300).
- [[identity-group-theory-of-partisanship]] (new) — voters choose parties to align with social-group coalitions; policy agreement is a by-product (p. 307).
- [[identity-congruence]] (new) — representation as match of politicians' and constituents' identities, tested by race and district (p. 313).
- [[post-hoc-rationalization]] (new) — reasons offered for beliefs are often after-the-fact justifications of loyalties, more elaborate among the engaged (p. 310).
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — in this chapter, more engaged citizens show more partisan bias, and sophistication does not guarantee better judgment (p. 310).
- [[swing-voters]] (new) — "pure" Independents, less informed, who decide close elections (p. 312).
- [[uniform-swing]] (new) — a similar shift of all districts against incumbents, expected from simple retrospection (p. 315).
- [[group-politics-view-of-democracy]] (new) — Zaller's phrase: organized policy demanders usually win, but numerous, cohesive, pivotal ordinary voters get some of what they want (p. 324).
- [[rational-ignorance]] and [[median-voter-theorem]] — appear only in Zaller's summary of the Downsian optimistic view (p. 324).
- [[political-power-and-inequality]] (new) — policy depends on group power (size, wealth, prestige, media access); the authors' normative standard is power proportional to presence in the electorate (p. 321).

## Questions this raises
- If election results are close to random, what can voters still influence (e.g., limits set by public norms)?

- Does the chapter's attention to Protestant nationalists in Weimar generalize to other democratic breakdowns?
