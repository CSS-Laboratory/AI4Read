# Perception of Risk (Slovic)

## L0 — One line
Laypeople's idea of "risk" is richer than the experts' apparent "expected annual deaths", so risk communication efforts are destined to fail unless they are two-way.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people fear some technologies much more than the statistics suggest? Slovic reviews psychometric studies, in which people rate hazards on many scales. Lay risk judgments are closely related to qualitative features, most importantly "dread" (and how well a risk is understood). Experts' risk judgments correlate highly with annual fatality estimates, and they appear to equate risk with expected annual deaths. Accidents also act as signals: a small accident in a poorly understood system may have immense social consequences if it is seen as a harbinger of worse mishaps. So one-hazard statistics and risk comparisons may fail to reassure the public. The paper argues that lay views contain "wisdom as well as error" and that risk communication must be two-way. The paper is a brief review of results from this research.

**Prerequisites:** correlation; the idea of factor analysis (condensing many correlated ratings into a few factors); basic cost-benefit thinking.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Policy question "How safe is safe enough?" drives the research. Psychometric studies measure risk opinions quantitatively (pp. 1, 4).
- Starr's revealed-preference method inferred acceptable risk from history; questionnaire-based expressed-preference studies came later (pp. 5–6).
- Lay "risk" is sensitive to factors such as catastrophic potential. Experts' risk judgments correlate highly with technical estimates of annual fatalities (p. 6).
- Many risk characteristics condense into factors: understood/unknown and dread. Lay risk perception tracks the dread factor (pp. 8–9).
- Events have ripple effects; Three Mile Island shows costs far beyond direct harm (p. 10).
- The impact of an event depends partly on what it signals, which relates to the hazard's place in the factor space (p. 11).
- One-number risk comparisons may not be very satisfactory, and people may "read between the lines" (pp. 12–13).
- Some risk debates may not be about risk at all (p. 14).
- Public attitudes contain wisdom as well as error; communication must be two-way (p. 14).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction (p. 1).** "How safe is safe enough?" became a major 1980s policy issue in industrialized societies. People increasingly see themselves as victims, not beneficiaries, of technology. Numerous observers argue that the public's apparent pursuit of a "zero-risk society" threatens political and economic stability (p. 1).
**Wildavsky's view (quoted):** the richest, best-protected civilization is becoming "the most frightened". He asks why numerous small dams, whose risks he says far exceed those of nuclear reactors, are not feared (p. 1).

**Research agenda (pp. 2–4).** Researchers study opinions about hazards, aiming to learn what people mean by "risky" and what drives those perceptions. The aim is to help policy makers communicate, educate and predict reactions (p. 2). Eight question groups are listed:
1. determinants of perceived risk;
2. accuracy of lay perceptions;
3. steps toward enlightened behaviour;
4. judgment in expert assessments;
5. perception of benefits;
6. what determines acceptability;
7. what makes a risk analysis acceptable;
8. how to reduce polarized conflict (pp. 2–4).

**The psychometric paradigm (pp. 4–5).** A taxonomy of hazards could explain extreme aversion, indifference, and gaps with expert opinion (p. 4). The paradigm uses psychophysical scaling and multivariate analysis to build quantitative "cognitive maps" of risk attitudes (p. 4). People judge current and desired riskiness and desired regulation. These are related to five other judgments:
1. characteristics such as voluntariness, dread, knowledge, controllability;
2. benefits;
3. deaths in an average year;
4. deaths in a disastrous year;
5. seriousness of each death relative to other causes (p. 5).

**Revealed and expressed preferences (pp. 5–7).** Starr (1969) assumed society has reached an "essentially optimum" risk-benefit balance by trial and error. So historical data reveal acceptable tradeoffs (p. 5). His conclusions:
1. acceptable risk is roughly proportional to the cube of benefits;
2. the public accepts risks from voluntary activities (e.g., skiing) roughly 1000 times greater than from involuntary ones (e.g., food preservatives) with the same benefits (pp. 5–6).

Doubts about his assumptions led Fischhoff et al. (1978) to analyse questionnaire data, giving *expressed* preferences (p. 6). Findings:
- Perceived risk is quantifiable and predictable. "Risk" means different things to different people (p. 6).
- Experts' risk judgments correlate highly with technical estimates of annual fatalities (p. 6).
- Laypeople can estimate annual fatalities, but their "risk" judgments also respond to catastrophic potential and threat to future generations. So they tend to differ from their own fatality estimates (pp. 6–7).
- People tend to view current risk levels as unacceptably high for most activities. Across hazards there seems to be little systematic relation between perceived risks and benefits (p. 7).
- Higher risk is tolerated for activities seen as highly beneficial, as Starr claimed. But Starr saw voluntariness as the key mediator. Expressed-preference studies suggest familiarity, control, catastrophic potential, equity and knowledge also seem to matter (p. 7).

Table 1 (30 activities, four groups, rank 1 = riskiest): nuclear power is ranked 1 by League of Women Voters and by college students, 8 by active club members, and 20 by experts. Motor vehicles are ranked 2, 5, 3 and 1 (p. 19).

*Intuition note:* The same hazard can be ranked very differently by different groups. Experts' risk judgments correlate highly with fatality estimates (p. 6), and in Table 1 the experts' ranking differs from the lay rankings for some hazards.

**Factor-analytic representations (pp. 8–9).** Many characteristics correlate: "voluntary" hazards tend to be "controllable" and "well-known". Factor analysis condenses them into a few higher-order factors (p. 8). The factor space in Figure 1 (81 hazards, 18 characteristics; p. 20) has been replicated across lay and expert groups. Its factors reflect how well a risk is understood and how much dread it evokes. A third factor, number of people exposed, appeared in several studies (p. 8). Making hazards more specific (e.g., splitting nuclear power) had little effect on the structure (p. 8). The higher a hazard's score on "Dread Risk", the higher its perceived risk, the greater the wish to cut risk, and the stronger the wish for strict regulation (pp. 8–9). Experts' risk perceptions are not closely related to these factors. Experts appear to see risk as synonymous with expected annual mortality. So some conflicts may result from different definitions (p. 9).

*Intuition note:* Laypeople and experts may be using two different meanings of "risk". Some conflicts over "risk" may then result from the different definitions (p. 9).

**Accidents as signals (pp. 9–12).** Risk analyses typically count direct harm. Indirect costs to the responsible company or agency can far exceed direct costs, sometimes spreading to a whole industry or beyond. The author's image: a stone dropped in a pond, with ripples (pp. 9–10). Three Mile Island (1979): no one died and few if any latent cancer deaths are expected, yet no other accident has had such costly societal impacts. One source estimated costs at 500 billion dollars. The author says traditional analyses tend to neglect such higher-order impacts and so greatly underestimate costs of certain mishaps (p. 10). Seriousness and higher-order impacts are determined in part by what an event signals (p. 11). Signal potential appears systematically related to hazard characteristics and position in the factor space (Figure 3; caption p. 20).
- A deadly accident in a familiar, well-understood system (e.g., a train wreck) may cause relatively little social disturbance (p. 11).
- A small accident in an unfamiliar or poorly understood system (nuclear reactor, recombinant DNA lab) may have immense consequences if seen as a harbinger of worse mishaps (p. 11).

**New Yorker editorial on Bhopal (quoted):** what grips us is "not so much the numbers as the spectacle of suddenly vanishing competence" (p. 11). Implication: effort beyond what cost-benefit analysis indicates might be warranted to prevent "high-signal accidents". Hazards in the upper-right quadrant of Figure 1 seem particularly likely to make large ripples (pp. 11–12).

**Placing risks in perspective (pp. 12–14).** A frequently advocated approach is one-number comparisons: risk per hour, annual death probability, or reduction in life expectancy. These have no logically necessary implications for acceptability. Research suggests they may not even improve intuitions well, because perceptions depend on the many qualities in Figure 1 (p. 12). Example comparison: to many people, a statement that living near a nuclear plant is "equivalent to" riding an extra three miles by car gives inadequate consideration to differences in the nature of the risks (pp. 12–13). So "riskiness" means more than expected fatalities. Fischhoff, Watson and Hope (1984) show that widening the definition of risk can greatly change the assessed risk of energy technologies (p. 13). People may "read between the lines" and generalize. **Editors reacting to an EPA statement on the pesticide EDB:** worry about "body burden" and hope there are no "cousins of EDB" waiting to "ambush us" (p. 13). So messages about one hazard may fail to calm fears. If people trust the risk manager, these broader concerns will probably not surface (p. 14). Sociological and anthropological work suggests some debates may not be about risk. Risk may be a rationale or surrogate for other concerns. Then risk communication is "simply irrelevant", and hidden agendas should be surfaced if possible (p. 14).

**Conclusion (p. 14).** Laypeople sometimes lack information, but their conceptualization of risk is much richer than experts' and includes legitimate concerns typically omitted from expert assessments. Communication efforts are destined to fail unless they are two-way (p. 14). Each side, expert and public, has something valid to contribute and must respect the insights and intelligence of the other (p. 14).

*Intuition note:* Statistics alone are not enough: the author says each side, expert and public, has something valid to contribute (p. 14).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three hazards with invented numbers. Deaths per year: X = 1000, Y = 10, Z = 100. Dread on a 1–10 scale: X = 2, Y = 9, Z = 5.
- Expert-style ranking by deaths: X (1000) > Z (100) > Y (10).
- A simple lay-style score, invented for illustration (Slovic gives no such formula): $\log_{10}(\text{deaths}) + \text{dread}$.
  - X: $3 + 2 = 5$.
  - Y: $1 + 9 = 10$.
  - Z: $2 + 5 = 7$.
- The lay-style ranking is Y > Z > X, the reverse of the expert ranking.

Neither side has misread the numbers; they weigh different features. This echoes the paper's point about different definitions of risk (p. 9).

## Concepts
- [[psychometric-paradigm-dread-unknown]] — questionnaire ratings of hazards on many scales, analysed into factors such as dread and understood/unknown (pp. 4, 8)
- [[revealed-vs-expressed-preferences]] (new) — acceptable risk inferred from historical data (Starr) versus from questionnaire judgments (pp. 5–6)
- [[dread-risk-factor]] (new) — factor on which high-scoring hazards get higher perceived risk and a stronger wish for regulation (pp. 8–9)

- [[signal-potential]] (new) — how far an event is judged to warn of further mishaps; related to its social impact (pp. 11, 20)
- [[ripple-effect]] (new) — higher-order costs spreading outward from direct victims to firms, agencies and industries (pp. 9–10); the canon's social amplification of risk is a related concept (canon link; term not used in this unit)
- [[two-way-risk-communication]] (new) — expert and public each contribute; one-way efforts are "destined to fail" (p. 14)
