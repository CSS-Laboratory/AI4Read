# Chapter One: The Basics

## L0 — One line
A system is elements plus interconnections plus a purpose, and much of its behavior over time can be understood from stocks changed by flows, together with balancing (goal-seeking) and reinforcing (runaway) feedback loops.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What makes a collection of things a system, and how do systems behave over time? Meadows says a system has elements, interconnections and a purpose. Purpose and interconnections often matter more than elements. Much behavior over time can be understood through stocks (accumulations) that flows fill or drain. Stocks usually change slowly, so they act as delays and buffers. When a stock's level affects its own flows, a feedback loop exists. Balancing loops seek a goal and resist change. Reinforcing loops amplify change and give exponential growth (growth that goes faster and faster) or runaway collapse. Seeing these loops shifts the question from who is to blame to what the system is.

**Prerequisites:** reading simple line graphs; percentages and compound interest; the idea of a rate (amount per unit time).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A system has three kinds of parts: elements, interconnections, and a function or purpose. Sand scattered on a road by happenstance is not a system, because it has no particular interconnections or function (pp. 11–12).
- Purpose is deduced from behavior, not from stated goals. It is often the most crucial determinant of behavior. Interconnections are also critically important, and elements are often (not always) least important. Asking which of the three matters most is called unsystemic (pp. 14, 16–17).
- A stock is an accumulation, the memory of past flows. Stocks change over time through the actions of flows (p. 18).
- The bathtub rules: inflows above outflows raise the stock, outflows above inflows lower it, and equal flows hold it in dynamic equilibrium (its level stays constant while water keeps flowing through). A stock can be raised by cutting its outflow as well as by raising its inflow (pp. 21–22).
- Stocks usually change slowly. They can act as delays, buffers and sources of momentum (they keep going in the same direction for a while). They let inflows and outflows be decoupled (independent of each other and temporarily out of balance). Time lags can cause problems but can also be sources of stability and give room to maneuver (pp. 23–24).
- A feedback loop is a closed chain of causal connections from a stock, through decisions or rules that depend on its level, back to a flow into or out of that stock (p. 27).
- A balancing loop (B) is goal-seeking: it opposes imposed change and approaches its goal fast at first, then slowly. Having a feedback mechanism does not guarantee that it works well (pp. 28–30).
- A reinforcing loop (R) enhances imposed change. It gives exponential growth or runaway collapse, and 70 divided by the growth rate in percent approximates the doubling time (pp. 31–33).
- In real systems loops rarely come singly: a single stock is likely to have several reinforcing and balancing loops of differing strengths pulling it in several directions (p. 34).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### More Than the Sum of Its Parts (pp. 11–12)
**Definition.** A system is an interconnected set of elements that is coherently organized in a way that achieves something (p. 11). It therefore must consist of three kinds of things: elements, interconnections, and a function or purpose (p. 11).

Examples from the text:
- Digestive system: teeth, enzymes, stomach and intestines are linked by food flow and chemical signals. Its function is to break food into nutrients and move them to the bloodstream (p. 11).
- Football team: its interconnections include the rules, the strategy, the communications and the laws of physics. Its purpose is to win games, or have fun, or get exercise, or make money, or all of these (p. 11).
- Systems nest inside other systems (tree, forest, earth, solar system, galaxy) (pp. 11–12).

Not a system: a conglomeration with no particular interconnections or function, such as sand scattered on a road by happenstance. You can add or remove sand and still have sand. Adding or removing football players or digestive organs quickly destroys the system (p. 12). A dead creature loses its "system-ness". Some people say an old neighborhood where people know each other is a social system, and a new apartment block of strangers is not until relationships arise (p. 12).

A system shows integrity and mechanisms that maintain it. It can change, adapt, seek goals and mend injuries, and it is often self-repairing over at least some range of disruptions. It may be self-organizing and resilient, and many systems are evolutionary: new systems can arise out of one (p. 12). The author also lists a check for a system: can you identify parts, do they affect each other, and do they together produce an effect different from the effect of each part on its own? Perhaps also: does the behavior over time persist in a variety of circumstances? (p. 13, box).

*Intuition note.* A system is defined by how its parts are tied together and what they do together, not by what they are made of. Remove the ties and you have a heap.

### Look Beyond the Players to the Rules of the Game (pp. 12–17)
**Elements** are the easiest to notice. They can be physical (roots, trunk, students) or intangible (school pride, academic prowess). Listing sub-elements has almost no end, and you can lose sight of the system (pp. 12–13).

**Interconnections** are the relationships holding elements together. In a tree they are flows and chemical reactions. For example, if the roots experience dry soil, the loss of water pressure signals the leaves to close their pores (p. 13). In a university they include admission standards, degree requirements, grades, budgets, gossip and, most important, the communication of knowledge (p. 14). Some interconnections are physical flows. Many are flows of information that go to decision points (p. 14). Information about the existence of a problem may be necessary but not sufficient to trigger action. Information about resources, incentives and consequences is necessary too (p. 14).

**Function or purpose** is the hardest to see. It need not be stated, and the best way to deduce it is to watch how the system behaves (p. 14). A frog that catches flies while turning left, right or backward has the purpose of catching flies. A government that proclaims environmental protection but spends little on it does not have that purpose. The author concludes that purposes are deduced from behavior, not from rhetoric or stated goals (p. 14).

Language: the word function is generally used for nonhuman systems and purpose for human ones, but the split is not absolute (p. 15). Examples: a thermostat-furnace system keeps a building at a given temperature. One purpose of a national economy, judging from behavior, is to keep growing. An important function of almost every system is its own perpetuation (p. 15).

System purposes need not be human purposes. They are not necessarily those intended by any single actor, because subunit purposes may add up to an overall behavior no one wants (p. 15). Example: the combined purposes of desperate people, farmers, dealers and bankers, pushers and police, governments, wealthy people living near poor people, and self-protecting nonaddicts. Together they make a system from which it is extremely difficult to eradicate drug addiction and crime (p. 15).

Purposes nest too. In a university the student may want grades, the professor tenure and the administrator a balanced budget. Any sub-purpose could conflict with the overall purpose (the student could cheat, the professor ignore students, the administrator fire professors). The author calls keeping sub-purposes and overall purposes in harmony an essential function of successful systems (pp. 15–16).

**Which part matters most?** The author imagines changing each part in turn (pp. 16–17):
- *Elements:* usually the least effect. If you change all the players, it is still recognizably a football team, though it may play much better or worse, since particular elements can be important. A tree, your body and a university keep their identities through constant replacement of cells or people. A system generally goes on being itself, changing only slowly if at all, even with complete substitution of elements, as long as its interconnections and purposes remain intact (p. 16).
- *Interconnections:* changing them may alter the system greatly. Football rules changed to basketball rules give a whole new ball game. A tree that did the reverse of its gas exchange (taking in oxygen and emitting carbon dioxide instead) would no longer be a tree; the text says it would be an animal. A university where students graded professors, or where arguments were won by force, would need a different name (p. 16).
- *Purpose:* a change can be drastic, for example from winning to losing, even if every element and interconnection stays the same (pp. 16–17).

Conclusion: asking which of the three is most important is an unsystemic question. All are essential and all interact. But the least obvious part, function or purpose, is often the most crucial determinant of behavior. Interconnections are also critically important. Elements are often (not always) least important, unless changing one also changes relationships or purpose (p. 17).

Changing one national leader (the text names Brezhnev to Gorbachev, Carter to Reagan) may or may not turn a nation in a new direction. Because land, factories and people are long-lived, slowly changing physical elements, there is a limit to the rate at which any leader can turn the nation (p. 17).

*Intuition note.* You can swap every player and keep the game. Change the rules or the goal and it becomes a different game, even with the same players.

### Bathtubs 101—Understanding System Behavior over Time (pp. 17–25)
**Stock.** A stock is the foundation of any system: the elements you can see, feel, count or measure at a given time. It is a store, quantity or accumulation of material or information built up over time (pp. 17–18). It can be physical (water, trees, money) or not (self-confidence, good will) (p. 18).

**Flow.** Stocks change over time through the actions of a flow: filling and draining, births and deaths, deposits and withdrawals, and so on (p. 18). A stock is "the present memory of the history of changing flows within the system" (p. 18).

**Diagram conventions (Fig. 1, p. 18).** Stocks are boxes. Flows are arrow-headed pipes into or out of stocks. The small T on a flow is a faucet that can be turned up, down, on or off. Clouds are sources and sinks being ignored in the discussion (p. 18).

Examples (pp. 18–19):
- Fig. 2: a mineral deposit with only a mining outflow. The author ignores the inflow because it is minute for any period shorter than eons. All system diagrams and descriptions are simplified versions of the real world (p. 18).
- Fig. 3: a reservoir with inflows of rain and river water, and outflows of evaporation and discharge (pp. 18–19).
- Fig. 4: wood in living trees (inflow: tree growth; outflows: natural deaths and logging). Logging feeds a lumber inventory, which has an outflow of lumber sales (p. 19).
- Fig. 5: the bathtub, one stock with one inflow and one outflow (p. 19).

If you understand the dynamics of stocks and flows (their behavior over time), you understand a good deal about the behavior of complex systems (p. 19).

**Bathtub behavior (pp. 19–22).**
- Plug pulled, faucets off: the level falls until the tub is empty. Fig. 6 shows a straight line from 50 gallons at minute 0 to 0 gallons at minute 10 (p. 20).
- Start again with a full tub and open the drain. When it is about half empty, turn on the inflow so that inflow equals outflow. The level then stays constant at whatever level it had reached. This is **dynamic equilibrium**: the level does not change although water continuously flows through (p. 21). In Fig. 7 the outflow is 5 gallons/minute, the inflow rises to 5, and the stock levels off at about 25 gallons (p. 21).
- Turn the inflow up while the outflow stays constant and the level slowly rises. Turn the inflow back down to match the outflow and the rise stops. Turn it down some more and the level falls slowly (pp. 21–22).

**Reading graphs of behavior over time (p. 20, box).** Systems thinkers look at trends over time rather than at individual events. They ask whether the system approaches a goal or a limit and how quickly. The shape of the line and the points where it changes shape or direction are important; the precise numbers on the axes are often less important (p. 20).

**Three principles from the bathtub (p. 22).** The model is very simple: one stock, one inflow, one outflow. The author assumes evaporation from the tub is insignificant over the minutes of interest, and notes that all models are simplifications of the real world.
- As long as the sum of all inflows exceeds the sum of all outflows, the level of the stock will rise.
- As long as the sum of all outflows exceeds the sum of all inflows, the level of the stock will fall.
- If the sum of all outflows equals the sum of all inflows, the stock level will not change. It is held in dynamic equilibrium at whatever level it happened to be when the two sets of flows became equal.

*Intuition note.* The level of a stock builds up from what came in minus what went out. So what decides whether it rises, falls or holds is the balance between total inflows and total outflows.

**Inflows versus outflows (p. 22).** The human mind seems to focus more easily on stocks than on flows, and, among flows, on inflows more easily than on outflows. So we sometimes miss that a stock can be raised by decreasing the outflow rate. Examples in the text: burning less oil can achieve the same result as discovering new oil deposits. A breakthrough in energy efficiency is equivalent, in its effect on the stock of available oil, to a new oil field, though different people profit. A company can build up its workforce by more hiring or by reducing quitting and firing, and these strategies may have very different costs. A nation's wealth can be boosted by investment, or, often more cheaply, by decreasing the rate at which factories and machines wear out or are discarded (p. 22).

**Stocks change slowly (pp. 23–24).** Flows can be adjusted abruptly, but it is much more difficult to change a stock quickly, because "flows take time to flow" (p. 23). The author calls this a vital point and a key to understanding why systems behave as they do. Stocks usually change slowly and can act as delays, lags, buffers, ballast and sources of momentum. Examples (p. 23):
- populations, forests, reservoirs and mines change slowly;
- an economy cannot build up a large stock of factories and highways overnight, even if a lot of money is available, and once it has many oil-burning furnaces and engines it cannot change quickly to ones that burn another fuel, even if the oil price suddenly changes;
- ozone-damaging pollutants took decades to accumulate and will take decades to remove;
- groundwater contaminants can be washed out only at the rate of groundwater turnover, which may take decades or even centuries.

Changes in stocks set the pace of system dynamics (p. 23). Time lags from slowly changing stocks can cause problems but also can be sources of stability. Examples: soil built over centuries rarely erodes all at once, and groundwater can be pumped faster than it recharges for a long time before damage. Lags allow room to maneuver, experiment and revise policies that aren't working (p. 23). Knowing the rates of change means you don't expect things faster than they can happen and don't give up too soon. You can also use a system's momentum, much as a judo expert uses an opponent's (pp. 23–24).

**Decoupling (p. 24).** Stocks allow inflows and outflows to be independent of each other and temporarily out of balance. Examples: gasoline in storage tanks lets the refinery rate differ from the rate cars burn it, and a forest is not harvested at the exact rate trees grow. Human-made stock-maintaining mechanisms include reservoirs, banks, and product inventories along the distributor–wholesaler–retailer chain (p. 24).

**Decisions regulate stocks (pp. 24–25).** Most individual and institutional decisions are designed to regulate stock levels. Examples: cut prices when inventories get too high, go shopping when kitchen food runs low, and take corrective actions when reservoir levels are too high or too low (p. 24). People monitor stocks and act to raise or lower them or keep them within acceptable ranges (pp. 24–25). So systems thinkers see the world as a collection of stocks plus mechanisms that regulate the stock levels by manipulating flows. That means seeing the world as a collection of "feedback processes" (p. 25).

*Intuition note.* Stocks are quantities you can measure and that change slowly, and decisions act on them through flows, which can be adjusted quickly. That arrangement leads directly to feedback, as the author says (p. 24).

### How the System Runs Itself—Feedback (pp. 25–27)
When a stock grows by leaps and bounds, declines swiftly, or is held within a range no matter what else is going on, the author says it is likely that a control mechanism is at work. A behavior that persists over a long time is the first hint of a feedback loop (p. 25).

**Definition.** A feedback loop is formed when changes in a stock affect the flows into or out of that same stock (p. 25). The boxed form is: a closed chain of causal connections from a stock, through decisions, rules, physical laws or actions that depend on the stock's level, and back through a flow to change the stock (p. 27).

Examples:
- *Savings account:* the stock determines the interest inflow through the bank's percent-per-year rule, so the inflow is not a fixed amount (p. 25).
- *Checking account:* as cash falls you may work more hours (raising inflow). If the account grows very large you may work less. This keeps cash in an acceptable range. You may also be able to adjust the outflow, so you can imagine an outflow-adjusting loop for spending (pp. 25–26).

Feedback loops can cause stocks to maintain their level within a range, or to grow or decline. Whoever or whatever monitors the stock starts a corrective process, adjusting inflow or outflow or both. "The stock level feeds back through a chain of signals and actions to control itself" (p. 26).

**Fig. 8 (p. 26).** The diagram shows the stock, the flow that changes it, and the information link (thin curved arrow) that directs the action. The caption stresses that action or change always proceeds through adjusting flows (p. 26).

**Not all systems have feedback loops.** Some are relatively simple open-ended chains of stocks and flows. Outside factors may affect them, but the stock levels do not affect the flows (p. 27).

### Stabilizing Loops—Balancing Feedback (pp. 27–30)
One common kind of loop stabilizes the stock level. The level may not remain completely fixed but stays within an acceptable range (p. 27).

**Coffee drinker (Fig. 9, pp. 27–28).** The drinker holds in mind a desired stock level (energy for work). The gap, the discrepancy, between actual and desired energy drives the decision about daily coffee intake (pp. 27–28). In the diagram, coffee intake acts on the flow that mobilizes energy from a second stock, stored energy in the body. That flow feeds energy available for work, which is drained by energy expenditure (Fig. 9, p. 27).
- Labels are direction-free ("coffee intake", not "more coffee"). The loop can work in both directions, correcting an excess (too much energy, so less coffee) as well as a shortage (p. 28).
- The author drew the extra stock to indicate that there is more to the system than one simple loop. Caffeine is a short-term stimulant and does not refill the personal fuel tank. When it wears off, the body is left more energy-deficient than before. That drop could reactivate the loop (the author points to the addiction discussion later) or trigger slower, healthier responses such as eating, walking or sleeping (p. 28).

**Definition.** A **balancing feedback loop** (marked B) is stabilizing, goal-seeking or regulating. Each tries to keep a stock at a given value or within a range. It opposes whatever direction of change is imposed: if pushed up too far it tries to pull the stock down, and if shoved too far down it tries to bring it up (p. 28).

**Cooling coffee (Figs. 10–11, pp. 28–29).** This loop works through physical law, not a decision. The rate of cooling depends on the difference between coffee and room temperature: the greater the difference, the faster the cooling. Iced coffee warms up by the same loop. The system's function is to bring the discrepancy to zero, whatever its direction (pp. 28–29). In Fig. 11, with room temperature 18 °C, the curves start from just below boiling down to just above freezing (as the text says). All approach 18 °C, homing on the goal. The change is faster at first and slower as the discrepancy shrinks (pp. 29–30).

The same pattern of gradual approach to a goal can be seen in radioactive decay, a missile finding its target, asset depreciation, reservoir levels, blood sugar and stopping a car at a stoplight (p. 30). Balancing loops are both sources of stability and sources of resistance to change (p. 30, margin).

**Caveat (p. 30).** The presence of a feedback mechanism does not necessarily mean that it works well. It may not be strong enough to bring the stock to the desired level. Information can arrive too late or at the wrong place, or be unclear, incomplete or hard to interpret. The triggered action may be too weak, delayed, resource-constrained or ineffective. The goal may never be reached by the actual stock. In the simple coffee case, though, the drink eventually reaches room temperature (p. 30).

*Intuition note.* A balancing loop measures the gap to a goal and pushes the other way. The push shrinks as the gap shrinks, so the approach is fast first and slow later.

### Runaway Loops—Reinforcing Feedback (pp. 30–34)
A **reinforcing feedback loop** (marked R) is amplifying, reinforcing, self-multiplying, snowballing: a vicious or virtuous circle that can cause healthy growth or runaway destruction (pp. 30–31). It generates more input to a stock the more is already there, and less input the less is there. It enhances whatever direction of change is imposed on it (p. 31).

Examples (p. 31): two children pushing each other back harder and harder; the wage–price spiral; rabbit parents and baby rabbits; soil erosion (less plant growth, fewer roots, more erosion); piano practice (more pleasure, more playing, more practice).

Reinforcing loops are found wherever a system element can reproduce itself or grow as a constant fraction of itself, such as populations and economies (p. 31). Fig. 12 shows the interest-bearing account: more money gives more interest, which is added to the money and earns still more interest (pp. 31–32).

**Fig. 13 (p. 32).** $100 in the bank for twelve years, with no deposits or withdrawals, at five interest rates:

| Rate per year | Value after 12 years |
|---|---|
| 2% | $126.82 |
| 4% | $160.10 |
| 6% | $201.22 |
| 8% | $251.82 |
| 10% | $313.84 |

This is not simple linear growth. Growth at lower rates may look linear in the first few years, but in fact it "goes faster and faster". This is called exponential growth, and it is good or bad news depending on what grows (money, HIV/AIDS, pests, an economy, an arms race) (p. 32). Reinforcing loops lead to exponential growth or runaway collapse over time (p. 32, margin).

**Fig. 14 (pp. 32–33).** More capital (machines and factories) gives more output. A fraction of output is invested, which adds to capital. The author calls this reinforcing loop "the central engine of growth in an economy" (p. 32).

**Doubling-time hint (p. 33, box).** For an exponentially growing stock, the doubling time is approximately 70 divided by the growth rate in percent. At 7% a year, $100 doubles in 10 years (70 ÷ 7 = 10). At 5% it takes 14 years (p. 33).

*Intuition note.* In a reinforcing loop the stock feeds its own growth, so a bigger stock gets a bigger push. That is why the curve bends upward instead of rising in a straight line.

**Seeing loops everywhere (pp. 33–34).** The author sometimes challenges students to think of a human decision made with no information about the level of the stock it influences. The most common suggestions are falling in love and committing suicide. The author leaves it to the reader whether these actually involve no feedback (p. 33). If you see feedback loops everywhere, you are in danger of becoming a systems thinker: instead of seeing only how A causes B, you begin to wonder how B may also influence A (p. 33). Examples: when the news says the Federal Reserve has done something to control the economy, you also see that the economy must have done something to affect the Federal Reserve. When someone says population growth causes poverty, you ask how poverty may cause population growth (pp. 33–34). The box asks: if A causes B, is it possible that B also causes A? (p. 34). You then think about a dynamic rather than a static world, stop looking for who is to blame and start asking "What's the system?". The concept of feedback opens up the idea that a system can cause its own behavior (p. 34).

**Many loops together (p. 34).** In real systems feedback loops rarely come singly. They are linked in often fantastically complex patterns. A single stock is likely to have several reinforcing and balancing loops of differing strengths pulling it in several directions. A single flow may be adjusted by the contents of three or five or twenty stocks. The loops tug against each other, trying to make stocks grow, die off or come into balance. As a result, complex systems do much more than stay steady, explode exponentially or approach goals smoothly (p. 34).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Two small loops, each with its own stock.

**Balancing loop.** A water tank with goal 20 liters. Each minute, an inflow equals 25% of the gap between goal and level. Start at 0 L.
- Minute 0: gap = 20 − 0 = 20; inflow = 0.25 × 20 = 5; level = 0 + 5 = 5.
- Minute 1: gap = 20 − 5 = 15; inflow = 0.25 × 15 = 3.75; level = 5 + 3.75 = 8.75.
- Minute 2: gap = 20 − 8.75 = 11.25; inflow = 0.25 × 11.25 = 2.8125; level = 8.75 + 2.8125 = 11.5625.

The inflow shrinks (5, 3.75, 2.81) as the gap shrinks: fast at first, then slower. If you started at 30 L, the gap would be −10 and the loop would drain (−2.5 in the first minute), so it works in both directions.

**Reinforcing loop.** A bank account of $100 at 7% a year, no deposits: year 1 interest = 0.07 × 100 = 7, balance 107. Year 2 interest = 0.07 × 107 = 7.49, balance 114.49. The yearly addition grows (7, then 7.49) because the stock is bigger. The 70-rule predicts doubling in 70 ÷ 7 = 10 years. Exact compounding gives $100 × 1.07¹⁰ ≈ $196.7, so the rule is close but approximate.

## Concepts
- [[system]] (new) — interconnected elements, coherently organized, that achieve something; needs elements, interconnections and a function or purpose (p. 11)
- [[system-purpose]] (new) — what the system does, deduced from its behavior rather than from stated goals (p. 14)
- [[stock]] (new) — accumulation of material or information; the memory of past flows (pp. 17–18)
- [[flow]] (new) — a rate (filling, draining, births, deaths) that changes a stock (p. 18)
- [[dynamic-equilibrium]] (new) — stock level constant although flows continue, because total inflow equals total outflow (pp. 21–22)
- [[stock-and-flow-diagram]] (new) — boxes for stocks, pipes with faucets for flows, thin arrows for information links (pp. 18, 26)
- [[delay]] — here used loosely: slowly changing stocks act as delays, lags and buffers. This is wider than the canon's sense of a lag between an action and its effect (p. 23)
- [[feedback]] — a closed chain of causation from a stock, through level-dependent decisions or rules, back to a flow into or out of that stock (pp. 25, 27)
- [[open-loop-vs-closed-loop]] — Meadows contrasts systems with feedback loops with open-ended chains of stocks and flows whose stock levels do not affect the flows (p. 27)
- [[negative-balancing-feedback]] — balancing loop (B): goal-seeking, opposes imposed change (p. 28)
- [[reference-set-point]] — the desired level or goal, e.g. desired energy level, room temperature (pp. 27, 29)
- [[error-signal]] — the discrepancy between actual and desired stock level, which drives the correction (pp. 27–28)
- [[positive-reinforcing-feedback]] — reinforcing loop (R): amplifies imposed change, giving exponential growth or collapse (p. 31)
- [[exponential-growth]] (new) — growth as a constant fraction of the stock (p. 31), so growth goes faster and faster (p. 32)
- [[doubling-time]] (new) — approximately 70 ÷ percentage growth rate (p. 33)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Balancing loop with desired level and discrepancy ↔ control theory: negative feedback with reference and error signal — same loop structure, in plain-language diagrams rather than equations.
- [structural-analogy] Reinforcing loop ↔ control theory: positive feedback — the stock's size sets its own growth rate.
- [structural-analogy] Coffee cooling in proportion to the gap from room temperature ↔ first-order linear dynamics approaching an equilibrium. This is only an analogy here: the text gives a verbal rule (greater difference, faster cooling) and plotted curves, not an equation.
- [structural-analogy] Stock as the memory of the history of changing flows ↔ control theory: state variable that integrates its inputs.
- [metaphor] Using a system's momentum the way a judo expert uses an opponent's ↔ control design that exploits slow dynamics.
