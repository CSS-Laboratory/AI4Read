# A note from the author; a note from the editor

## L0 — One line
Meadows aims to give readers a basic ability to understand and deal with complex systems while warning that the book is biased and incomplete, and her editor hopes it will help readers who are frustrated by how hard lasting change is and who want to shape a better future.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Meadows says the book distils thirty years of systems modeling (building models of systems) and teaching by dozens of people. She thanks that intellectual community, says she also drew on thinkers from other fields, and warns that the book is biased and incomplete. Her main aim is a basic ability to understand and deal with complex systems. The editor, Diana Wright, and her colleagues published the manuscript after Meadows died in 2001. Wright thinks it can help people frustrated at making lasting change. Pirsig's epigraph says that if the rationality behind a factory is left standing, it will simply produce another factory. Likewise, if the patterns of thought behind a government are left intact, they will repeat themselves.

**Prerequisites:** none. No math appears in this unit. Only general knowledge of recent history helps for the editor's list of early-1990s events.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The book is distilled from thirty years of work by dozens of people, mostly linked to the MIT System Dynamics group, with Jay Forrester foremost (p. ix).
- Meadows also drew on thinkers from many fields who, as far as she knows, never simulated a system on a computer. She says systems thinking transcends disciplines and cultures (p. ix).
- She uses the language and symbols of the school in which she was taught, system dynamics. She presents only the core of systems theory, not the leading edge (p. ix).
- She calls the book biased and incomplete. Her main purpose is a basic ability to understand and deal with complex systems, even if one's formal systems training begins and ends with this book (pp. ix–x).
- The editor says the draft dated from 1993, was not published then, and circulated informally. Meadows died in 2001 before completing it (p. xi).
- The editor believes systems, big or small, can behave in similar ways. She says that understanding this is perhaps our best hope for lasting change (p. xi).
- The editor kept many examples from Meadows's 1993 draft because, she says, their teachings are as relevant now as then. She says that once you see events as parts of trends, and trends as symptoms of underlying system structure, you will be able to consider new ways to manage and live (pp. xii–xiii).
- Pirsig's epigraph claims that if a factory is torn down, or a government destroyed by a revolution, while the rationality or patterns of thought that produced it are left in place, they will produce the same thing again (p. xv).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A note from the author (signed Donella Meadows, 1993)
**Sources of the book (p. ix).**
- The book is distilled from the wisdom of thirty years of systems modeling and teaching by dozens of creative people. Most were originally based at, or influenced by, the MIT System Dynamics group (p. ix).
- Jay Forrester, the group's founder, is foremost among them (p. ix).
- Meadows names other teachers, some of them students who became her teachers: Ed Roberts, Jack Pugh, Dennis Meadows, Hartmut Bossel, Barry Richmond, Peter Senge, John Sterman and Peter Allen (p. ix).
- She expresses admiration and gratitude to the members of this large intellectual community (p. ix).
- She also drew on thinkers in other disciplines who, as far as she knows, never used a computer to simulate a system. She calls them natural systems thinkers: Bateson, Boulding, Daly, Einstein, Hardin, Havel, Mumford, Myrdal, Schumacher, some modern corporate executives, and anonymous sources of ancient wisdom (p. ix).
- Her claim: systems thinking transcends disciplines and cultures and, "when it is done right, it overarches history as well" (p. ix).

**Limits and bias (pp. ix–x).**
- Systems analysts have human personalities and have formed many "fractious schools" (p. ix).
- She uses the language and symbols of system dynamics, the school in which she was taught (p. ix).
- She presents only the core of systems theory, not the leading edge, and does not deal with the most abstract theories. She is interested in analysis only when she can see how it helps solve real problems (p. ix).
- She believes the abstract end of the theory will some day help solve real problems. If it does, another book will have to be written (p. ix).
- Her warning: this book, like all books, is biased and incomplete. There is much more to systems thinking, for readers to discover if interested (pp. ix–x).
- Two purposes: to make readers interested, and, the main one, to give a basic ability to understand and deal with complex systems, even if formal systems training begins and ends here (p. x).

*Intuition note:* The author says there is much more to systems thinking than the book presents, and she warns up front that it is biased and incomplete. She tells readers that she used one school's language and symbols and presented only the core.

### A note from the editor (signed Diana Wright, 2008)
**History of the manuscript (p. xi).**
- Meadows (Dana) completed a draft in 1993. It was not published then but circulated informally for years (p. xi).
- She died unexpectedly in 2001, before completing the book (p. xi).
- The editor says Meadows's writings continued to be useful to a wide range of readers, and that Meadows was writing the book to bring the concept to a wider audience; that is why she and colleagues at the Sustainability Institute decided to publish it posthumously (p. xi).

**Context and claims (editor's voice) (p. xi).**
- *The Limits to Growth* (1972): Meadows was lead author of this best-selling, widely translated book (p. xi).
- **Editor:** its cautions are now recognized as the most accurate warnings of how unsustainable patterns could, if unchecked, wreak havoc worldwide (p. xi).
- The book observed that continual growth in population and consumption could severely damage the ecosystems and social systems that support life. It also observed that a drive for limitless economic growth could eventually disrupt many local, regional and global systems (p. xi).
- **Editor:** these findings are again front-page news (peak oil, climate change, 6.6 billion people) (p. xi).
- **Editor:** it is widely accepted that systems thinking is a critical tool for today's environmental, political, social and economic challenges (p. xi).
- **Editor:** systems, big or small, can behave in similar ways, and understanding those ways is "perhaps our best hope" for lasting change (p. xi).

**Why another book (pp. xi–xii).**
- Wright asks whether another book will help, and answers "I think so" (pp. xi–xii).
- Readers who may benefit: people who work in or own a company, policy makers who see others "push back", managers whose fixes are followed by new problems, advocates whose years of progress are undone in a few swift reactions, and frustrated citizens (p. xii).
- Wright sees a need for an approachable, inspiring book about systems and us: why we find them baffling and how we can better manage and redesign them (p. xii).

**About the text (pp. xii–xiii).**
- When writing, Meadows had just completed *Beyond the Limits*, the twenty-year update of *Limits to Growth* (p. xii).
- She was a Pew Scholar in Conservation and the Environment, served on a National Geographic Society committee, and taught at Dartmouth. She understood events of the day to be the outward behavior of often complex systems (p. xii).
- The manuscript was edited and restructured, but many examples come from the 1993 draft. They may seem dated, but Wright kept them because their teachings are as relevant now as then (p. xii).
- She lists early-1990s events: dissolution of the Soviet Union, NAFTA, Iraq's invasion of Kuwait, Mandela freed and apartheid laws repealed, Walesa and Havel elected, the first IPCC report, and the Rio conference (pp. xii–xiii).
- In one week of reading the International Herald Tribune while travelling, Meadows found many examples of systems needing better management or complete redesign. The editor says Meadows found them in the newspaper because they are all around us every day (p. xiii).
- **Editor:** once you see events as parts of trends, and trends as symptoms of underlying system structure, you will be able to consider new ways to manage and live (p. xiii).
- Wright hopes to increase readers' ability to understand and talk about the systems around them and to act for positive change (p. xiii). She calls it "a simple book for and about a complex world" (p. xiii).

*Intuition note:* The editor's idea is a three-step reading of the news: an event, a trend of events, and a system behind the trend. Her claim is conditional: if readers learn to see this way, they will be able to consider new ways to act.

### Epigraph (p. xv)
**Pirsig:** If a factory is torn down but the rationality that produced it is left standing, that rationality will simply produce another factory. If a revolution destroys a government but the patterns of thought that produced it are left intact, those patterns will repeat themselves. He adds: "There's so much talk about the system. And so little understanding." (p. xv)

*Intuition note:* Pirsig says that removing a visible product does not change whatever produced it. If that producer stays in place, he says the product will come back. This is Pirsig's claim, not a result proved in this unit.

## Toy example
(Our own, not from the text.) A made-up rule: "each year, if total capacity is below demand 100, build one 20-unit factory." The producing rule stands for Pirsig's "rationality".
- Start: 5 factories × 20 = 100. Capacity equals demand, so nothing is built.
- Tear down two factories: capacity = 100 − 2×20 = 60.
- Year 1: 60 < 100, so build one: 60 + 20 = 80.
- Year 2: 80 < 100, so build one: 80 + 20 = 100.
- Year 3: 100 is not below 100, so nothing is built. The original state is back.
- If we instead remove the rule ("build nothing"), capacity stays at 60.

This simple deterministic case shows an outcome returning while its producing rule stays. Pirsig's claim is about rationality and patterns of thought, which is much broader.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Pirsig's rationality that rebuilds the factory ↔ control-theory: a controller whose fixed rule restores the target after a disturbance — the rule, not the disturbance, decides the outcome.
- [structural-analogy] Meadows understood events to be "the outward behavior of often complex systems" (p. xii) ↔ control-theory: a measured output reflects the plant's internal state and dynamics.

## Questions this raises

- What does the editor's phrase "systems in need of … complete redesign" imply for how to intervene?
