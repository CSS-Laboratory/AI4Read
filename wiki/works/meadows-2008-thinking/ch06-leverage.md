# Leverage Points—Places to Intervene in a System

## L0 — One line
Meadows offers a tentative ranked list of twelve places to intervene in a system, from weak (numbers) to strong (goals, paradigms), and, following Forrester, warns that people often push leverage points in the wrong direction.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Where can a small change in a system produce a large change in behavior? Meadows offers a tentative list of twelve places to intervene. It runs from weakest (numbers such as taxes and standards) to strongest (staying unattached to any paradigm). The middle items concern delays, feedback loops, information flows, rules and self-organization. The top items concern system goals and shared beliefs. She says leverage points are often counterintuitive, and people often push them the wrong way. Higher points are harder to reach, and the system resists change there more. She calls the list a work in progress, an invitation to think more broadly about system change, not a recipe.

**Prerequisites:** stocks and flows (the "bathtub"), balancing and reinforcing feedback loops, and the idea of a delay in a loop (Chapter One and Chapter Four are cited in the text). Terms used below: *gain* = strength of a loop; *buffer* = a big stabilizing stock; *self-organization* = a system's power to change its own structure.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A leverage point is a place where a small change can lead to a large shift in behavior. Forrester's view: people inside a system often sense where it is, but more often than not push in the wrong direction (pp. 145–146).
- Parameters (numbers) are last on the list. Most attention goes to them, but they rarely change system behavior. They become leverage points when they move into a range that triggers an item higher on the list (pp. 148–149).
- Physical items (buffers, stock-and-flow structure, delays) are low on the list because they are slow, costly or fixed. It is usually easier to slow the rate of change than to shorten a delay (pp. 149–152).
- Feedback structure comes next. Reducing the gain of a reinforcing loop is usually more powerful than strengthening balancing loops, and adding a missing information flow can be cheap and strong (pp. 153–157).
- Rules, self-organization and goals are higher. A goal can bend everything below it on the list (pp. 158–162).
- Paradigms, the shared deep beliefs behind a system, are second from the top. They can change in a mind instantly, but societies resist change hardest here (pp. 162–164).
- The top point is to transcend paradigms, treating none as "true". The list is tentative and "slithery", and the higher the point, the more the system resists (pp. 164–165).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: what leverage points are (pp. 145–147)
- **Leverage point** = a place where a small change could lead to a large shift in behavior. Forrester says the average manager can define the problem, identify the structure causing it, and guess where leverage lies. Yet people deep inside a system more often than not push the change the wrong way (p. 145).
- Example: Forrester's World model (for the Club of Rome) gave growth as the leverage point. Meadows says growth has uncounted costs (poverty, hunger, environmental destruction), so the need is for much slower or different growth. World leaders push the other way (p. 146).
- Example: his 1969 urban-dynamics study found that subsidized low-income housing is a leverage point. The less there is, the better off the city, even its low-income people. He was derided then; many such projects have since been torn down (p. 146).
- Meadows has no quick formula for finding leverage points. She proposed the list in a moment of frustration at a meeting on global-trade regimes. She offers it "with much humility" as a work in progress (pp. 146–147).
- Simple stocks and flows plumbed together (checking account → bank → federal reserve system) become too complex to figure out easily. This is why leverage points are often not intuitive (p. 147).
- The list is ordered here from 12 (weakest) to 1 (strongest).

### 12. Numbers — constants and parameters (subsidies, taxes, standards) (pp. 147–149)
- Parameters set the size of flows ("faucets" and "drains" of the bathtub). Some are locked in physically; many can be varied, so they are popular intervention points (p. 147).
- Examples: the national debt (taxes shrink the "money hole", spending deepens it), air-quality standards, allowed forest cuts, minimum wage. Changing the people at the faucets changes little if the system, its information, goals and rules stay the same (pp. 147–148).
- Meadows says numbers are "dead last" in power. Her phrase is "arranging the deck chairs on the Titanic". She says probably 90, 95 or 99 percent of attention goes to parameters (p. 148).
- They matter in the short term and to people standing in the flow. But they rarely kick-start a stagnant economy, stabilize a wild one, or slow a runaway one. Examples: campaign-contribution caps, interest-rate changes, Los Angeles air standards, police spending (p. 148–149).
- **Caveat:** parameters become leverage points when they move into ranges that trigger an item higher on the list. Interest rates or birth rates, for example, control the gains of reinforcing loops. System goals are parameters that can make big differences. Such critical numbers are less common than people think, because most systems stay far from critical ranges (p. 149).
- Story (a friend's, via the Internet): a landlord could not find a "fair" rent. A money adviser told her that a large gray area exists where both sides get a fair deal, so she should stop worrying (p. 149).

*Intuition note:* Changing a number is like turning a tap a bit more. It matters, especially in the short term, to the person under the tap, but it rarely changes how the plumbing, the information or the goals work. A number becomes a leverage point when it moves into a range that triggers something higher on the list, such as the gain of a reinforcing loop.

### 11. Buffers — the sizes of stabilizing stocks relative to their flows (pp. 149–150)
- A stock that is big relative to its flows is more stable than a small one (lake vs. river; rivers flood catastrophically more often). A big stabilizing stock is called a buffer (pp. 149–150).
- Examples: money in the bank, store inventory, breeding populations, calcium buffers in soils (eastern U.S. soils are more sensitive to acid rain than western ones) (p. 150).
- Increasing buffer capacity can often stabilize a system. A buffer that is too big makes the system inflexible and slow, and big buffers of some kinds cost a lot to build or keep (hence just-in-time inventories) (p. 150).
- Buffers are usually physical and hard to change, so they are not high on the list (p. 150).

### 10. Stock-and-flow structures — physical systems and their nodes of intersection (pp. 150–151)
- The physical layout can have an enormous effect. Example: the Hungarian road system sent all cross-country traffic through central Budapest, which determined a lot about air pollution and commuting delays that are not easily fixed by pollution control devices, traffic lights or speed limits (p. 150).
- Fixing a poorly laid-out system means rebuilding it if you can. Example: Lovins and Rocky Mountain Institute saved energy by straightening bent pipes and enlarging small ones (pp. 150–151).
- Rebuilding is often the slowest and most expensive change. Some structures cannot be changed: the baby-boom bulge moving through schools, jobs and retirement; CFC lifetimes; aquifer washout; a ten-to-twenty-year car-fleet turnover (p. 151).
- Conclusion: physical structure is rarely a leverage point. The leverage is in good design at the start, then in knowing the limits and bottlenecks and not straining capacity (p. 151).

### 9. Delays — lengths of time relative to rates of system change (pp. 151–152)
- Delays in feedback loops are critical determinants of behavior and common causes of oscillation. Delayed information about a stock, or a delayed response, makes you overshoot and undershoot your goal (p. 151).
- Example: power plants take several years to build and will likely last thirty years, so it is impossible to build exactly the right number to supply rapidly changing demand. Almost every electricity industry swings between over- and undercapacity. Meadows says a massive central-planning system (the Soviet Union, General Motors) "necessarily functions poorly" because it cannot respond to short-term change with long-term delays (p. 151).
- Other delays: pollutant to groundwater, child to parent, new technology to widespread use, price adjustment to imbalance (p. 152).
- A delay matters relative to the rate of change of the stock the loop controls. Too short: overreaction, "chasing your tail", amplified oscillations. Too long: damped, sustained or exploding oscillations, depending on how much too long. Overlong delays in a system with a threshold (a point past which damage is irreversible) cause overshoot and collapse (p. 152).
- She would rank delay length high except that delays are rarely changeable ("Things take as long as they take"). It is usually easier to slow the rate of change so that delays cause less trouble. That is why growth rates rank higher than delay times (p. 152).
- Hence in the World model, slowing growth is a bigger leverage point than faster technology or freer prices. The world's physical capital stock can change only so fast, and prices and ideas do not change instantly either, not through a whole global culture (p. 152).
- If a delay can be changed, the effect can be big, so check the direction. Her example: pushing to cut information and money-transfer delays in financial markets "is just asking for wild gyrations" (p. 152).

*Intuition note:* A delay is the lag between seeing a problem and fixing it. If the lag is long compared with how fast things change, corrections arrive too late and cause swings. Since lags are hard to shorten, slowing the change is often the easier fix.

### 8. Balancing feedback loops — strength of feedbacks relative to the impacts they correct (pp. 153–155)
- Here the list moves from the physical part of the system to the information and control parts, where more leverage can be found (p. 153).
- A balancing loop needs a goal (thermostat setting), a monitoring and signaling device (thermostat), and a response mechanism (furnace, air conditioner) (p. 153).
- Complex systems have many loops, some inactive most of the time (emergency cooling, sweating, shivering). Their presence is critical long term. Stripping "emergency" mechanisms because they look costly narrows the range of conditions the system can survive. Examples: encroaching on habitats and on our own rest time (p. 153).
- Loop strength depends on all its parameters and links: accuracy and speed of monitoring, speed and power of response, directness and size of corrective flows (p. 153).
- Markets are balancing loops. Price is the central signal. The clearer, more timely and truthful the price, the better markets work. Companies and governments distort it with subsidies, taxes and other confusion. The real leverage is stopping that: antitrust law, truth-in-advertising law, internalizing costs (pollution fees), removing perverse subsidies (pp. 153–154).
- Democracy is also a balancing loop (informed voters respond to what representatives do). Heavy spending to bias that information flow weakens it, and market and democracy both erode (p. 154).
- A loop must be strong enough relative to the impact it corrects. Examples: a thermostat with all windows open; fishing controls overtaken by sonar and drift nets; big industry needing big government; a global economy needing global regulation (p. 154).
- Examples of strengthening balancing loops: preventive medicine, integrated pest management, the Freedom of Information Act, environmental monitoring, whistleblower protection, impact fees and pollution taxes (pp. 154–155).

### 7. Reinforcing feedback loops — the strength of the gain of driving loops (pp. 155–156)
- A reinforcing loop is self-reinforcing: the more it works, the more power it gains. Examples: flu infection, births, interest on savings, soil erosion, neutron chain reaction (p. 155).
- Reinforcing loops are sources of growth, explosion, erosion and collapse. Unchecked, one ultimately destroys its system, which is why there are few of them. Usually a balancing loop kicks in (epidemic runs out of people; death rate rises; soil erodes to bedrock) (p. 155).
- The first outcome in each pair is what happens if the loop runs its course. The second is what happens if someone reduces its self-multiplying power. Reducing the gain of a reinforcing loop (slowing growth) is usually a more powerful leverage point than strengthening balancing loops, and far preferable to letting the loop run (pp. 155–156).
- World model: slowing population and economic growth gives the balancing loops (technology, markets), which have limits and delays, time to work. Meadows compares it to slowing the car rather than calling for better brakes (p. 156).
- "Success to the successful" loops: winners get resources to win bigger next time (interest, tax advice, inheritances, education). Antipoverty programs are weak balancing loops against strong reinforcing ones. Progressive income tax, inheritance tax and universal public education aim to weaken the reinforcing loops. If the wealthy weaken them, government itself shifts toward reinforcing success to the successful (p. 156).

*Intuition note:* Meadows says that lowering the gain of a runaway loop is usually a more powerful move than strengthening the balancing loops behind it. Slowing down gives those balancing loops, which have limits and delays, time to work. Her picture: slow the car rather than call for better brakes.

### 6. Information flows — who does and does not have access to information (pp. 156–157)
- Example (from Chapter Four): Dutch houses with the electric meter in the visible front hall used 30 percent less electricity than houses with the meter in the basement, with no other differences (p. 156).
- This is not a parameter change or a change in an existing loop. It is a new loop delivering feedback where there was none. Missing information flows are among the most common causes of malfunction. Adding information is often much easier and cheaper than rebuilding infrastructure (p. 157).
- Fisheries tragedy of the commons: little feedback from fish population to the decision to invest in vessels. The price of fish does not supply it. Scarcity raises price and makes catching the last fish more profitable, a perverse reinforcing loop. Population information is what is needed (p. 157).
- The feedback must arrive in the right place and in compelling form. Telling aquifer users the water level is dropping could start a race to the bottom. More effective: a water price that rises steeply once pumping exceeds recharge (p. 157).
- Her "suppose" examples: taxpayers specify how their taxes are spent; water intakes placed downstream of one's own wastewater outflow; nuclear-plant decision-makers storing the waste on their lawn; war-declaring politicians serving at the front (p. 157).
- Humans tend to avoid accountability for their decisions, hence the missing loops. This leverage point is popular with the masses, unpopular with the powers that be, and effective if it can be made to happen (p. 157).

### 5. Rules — incentives, punishments, constraints (pp. 158–159)
- Rules define a system's scope, boundaries and degrees of freedom. Gorbachev opened information flows (glasnost) and changed economic rules (perestroika), and the Soviet Union changed greatly (p. 158).
- Constitutions are the strongest social rules. Physical laws are absolute. Laws, punishments, incentives and informal agreements are progressively weaker (p. 158).
- Exercise in imagination: a college where students grade teachers, there are no degrees, or classes are graded as groups. Imagining new rules shows their power (p. 158).
- Power over rules is real power. The Supreme Court, which interprets the "rules for writing the rules", has more power than Congress. For the deepest malfunctions, look at the rules and who controls them (p. 158).
- Her judgment of the new world trade system: rules designed, run and benefiting corporations; most meetings closed even to the press; almost no feedback from other sectors; nations forced into a "race to the bottom". Meadows reads this as a recipe for "success to the successful" loops and huge centralized systems that will destroy themselves (pp. 158–159).

### 4. Self-organization — the power to add, change or evolve system structure (pp. 159–161)
- Self-organization (evolution in biology, technical advance or social revolution in economies) means changing any aspect of the system lower on this list: new structures, new loops, new rules (p. 159).
- It is the strongest form of system resilience, because a system that can evolve can survive almost any change by changing itself. Examples: the immune system and the brain (p. 159).
- It seems miraculous (economists model technology as magic). Meadows says the creator of such a system, if there is one, "just has to write marvelously clever rules" governing how, where, and what the system can add onto or subtract from itself under what conditions (p. 159). Simple rules can produce complex patterns, as computer models show. DNA has four "letters" in three-letter "words", constant for about three billion years (p. 159–160).
- Self-organization needs raw material (a highly variable stock of information) and a means of experimenting and selecting. Biology: DNA, mutation, a changing environment. Technology: accumulated science, human creativity, market or funding selection (p. 160).
- Hence biologists worship biodiversity. Extinction is "a systems crime", like destroying journals or scientists. Cultures are a stock of behavior for social evolution. Insisting on one culture shuts down learning and cuts resilience (p. 160).
- A system that cannot self-evolve, scorns experimentation and wipes out innovation's raw material is, she says, doomed over the long term. The intervention is encouraging variability and diversity. This means "losing control", so it is unpopular (pp. 160–161).

### 3. Goals — the purpose or function of the system (pp. 161–162)
- Goal is superior to self-organization. If the goal is to bring the world under one central system (empires, the Church, Wal-Mart, Disney), everything lower on the list is twisted to serve it (p. 161).
- So Meadows will not argue whether genetic engineering is "good" or "bad". It depends on who wields it with what goal (p. 161).
- Single balancing loops have their own goals (thermostat setting), which are leverage points for parts of systems. There are larger, less obvious whole-system goals (p. 161).
- Example: "to make profits", most corporations would say, but she calls that just a rule, a necessary condition to stay in the game. She names the real goal as growth and control over the environment, to be shielded from uncertainty, and cites Galbraith. She notes this is also the goal of a cancer and of every living population, and is bad only when higher-level balancing loops do not limit it (p. 161–162).
- Keeping the market competitive must trump each firm's goal of eliminating competitors, as in ecosystems (p. 162).
- Exception to "changing the players is low-level": at the top, a single player can have the power to change the system's goal. She has seen this only very occasionally. Example: Reagan's repeated goal of getting government "off our backs". Meadows grants that larger changes and corporate power let him do it, but says public discourse changed, showing the leverage of repeatedly stating a new goal (p. 162).

### 2. Paradigms — the mind-set out of which the system arises (pp. 162–164)
- Forrester's saying: however a tax law is written, actual payments will push up against society's shared idea of "fair" (p. 162).
- A paradigm is the deepest set of unstated shared beliefs about how the world works. Examples: money measures real worth; growth is good; nature is a resource stock; one can "own" land (p. 163).
- Paradigms are the sources of systems: goals, information flows, feedbacks and stocks all come from them. Meadows cites Emerson (ideas shape material things). Changing paradigms (Copernicus, Einstein, Adam Smith) transforms systems (p. 163).
- One could say paradigms are harder to change than anything else, but Meadows points out that nothing in the process of paradigm change is physical, expensive or even slow. In one person it can take a millisecond. Whole societies resist most here (pp. 163–164).
- How, following Kuhn: keep pointing at anomalies of the old paradigm; speak and act from the new one with assurance; put people with the new paradigm in visible, powerful places; work with change agents and the open-minded middle, not reactionaries. Modelers say building a model takes you outside the system to see it whole, as it did for Meadows (p. 164).

### 1. Transcending paradigms (pp. 164–165)
- The highest point is to stay unattached in the arena of paradigms: to see that no paradigm is "true", that this realization is itself a paradigm, and to let go into not-knowing. She links this to what Buddhists call enlightenment (p. 164).
- Most people flee this idea. Meadows says that those who entertain it find it a basis for radical empowerment: if no paradigm is right, you can choose one that helps your purpose, or listen to the universe (p. 164).

### Closing caveats (p. 165)
- The list is tentative and its order "slithery". Every item has exceptions that can move it up or down. The higher the leverage point, the more the system resists changing it (p. 165).
- Magical leverage points are not easily accessible. Mastery takes hard work, whether rigorous system analysis or rigorous casting off of paradigms. She concludes that, it seems, mastery has less to do with pushing leverage points than about "letting go and dancing with the system" (p. 165).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* A thermostat loop. Each hour, room temperature moves a fraction $k$ of the way to the goal $G$: $T_{next} = T + k\,(G - T)$. Start at $T = 10$.

- **Parameter change** ($G = 20$). With $k = 0.5$: $10 + 0.5\cdot10 = 15$; $15 + 0.5\cdot5 = 17.5$; $17.5 + 0.5\cdot2.5 = 18.75$. With $k = 0.6$: $10 + 0.6\cdot10 = 16$; $16 + 0.6\cdot4 = 18.4$; $18.4 + 0.6\cdot1.6 = 19.36$. A different $k$ changes the speed. Both runs head toward 20.
- **Goal change** ($k = 0.5$, $G = 22$): $10 + 0.5\cdot12 = 16$; $16 + 0.5\cdot6 = 19$; $19 + 0.5\cdot3 = 20.5$. The heading is now toward 22.

Tuning the parameter changes how fast the loop gets there. Changing the goal changes where it goes. This illustrates the chapter's ordering, with parameters low and the goal higher (pp. 148, 161).

## Concepts
- [[leverage-point]] (new) — a place where a small change can produce a large shift in system behavior (p. 145)
- [[reference (set point)]] — here called the "goal" of a balancing loop (thermostat setting) or of the whole system. Meadows ranks the whole-system goal above self-organization (pp. 153, 161)
- [[negative (balancing) feedback]] — loop that keeps a stock near its goal. Needs goal, monitor and response (p. 153)
- [[positive (reinforcing) feedback]] — self-reinforcing loop, a source of growth, erosion and collapse (p. 155)
- [[gain]] — strength of a loop. Meadows says reducing the gain of a reinforcing loop is usually a more powerful leverage point than strengthening balancing loops (pp. 155–156)
- [[delay (dead time)]] — lag in a feedback loop, critical relative to the rate of change of the controlled stock (p. 152)
- [[buffer]] (new) — a large stabilizing stock relative to its flows (p. 150)
- [[self-organization]] (new) — the power to add, change or evolve system structure, loops and rules (p. 159)
- [[paradigm]] (new) — a society's deepest unstated shared beliefs, the source of its systems (pp. 162–163)
- [[information-flow-structure]] (new) — who has access to which information. Adding a missing flow means a new loop (pp. 156–157)
- [[homeostasis]] — loosely related: dormant "emergency" balancing loops keep the system within survivable bounds (p. 153)

## Questions this raises
- Why does Meadows rank items by leverage while saying the order is "slithery"? What evidence or criteria would let us test the order?
- Is slowing a reinforcing loop always better than strengthening balancing loops, or only "usually" (as p. 155–156 says)? In which cases would it not be?
- How could one tell, in a real system, whether a parameter has entered a critical range that triggers a higher-level effect (p. 149)?
- If higher leverage points meet more resistance (p. 165), how should a change agent trade off leverage against feasibility?
