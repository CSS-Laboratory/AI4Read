# System Traps… and Opportunities

## L0 — One line
Many common, damaging behaviours (policy resistance, commons ruin, eroding standards, arms races, winner-take-all, addiction, rule beating, wrong goals) come from recurring feedback-loop structures, so they can be escaped by recognizing the structure early or changing it, not by blaming people.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do some systems produce persistent trouble whoever runs them? Meadows names eight "archetypes", recurring feedback structures that generate problematic behaviour. Each is a trap, because blaming, firing or pushing the same lever harder does not fix it. Each is also an opportunity, because the structure can be changed. The usual remedies are to reformulate goals, strengthen, weaken or add feedback loops, align actors' goals, or avoid entering the trap. Each trap comes with a short "the way out" box. This matters because it lets you diagnose a problem in terms of loops and goals instead of in terms of culprits.

**Prerequisites:** stocks and flows; balancing vs reinforcing feedback loops; goal vs actual state (discrepancy drives action); the idea of bounded rationality (each actor sees only part of the system).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An **archetype** is a system structure that produces a common pattern of problematic behaviour. Such problems are consequences of structure, not of particular actors, so standard responses like blaming or tinkering do not fix them (pp. 111–112).
- **Policy resistance:** actors with different goals pull one stock in different directions, and each counters the others' gains. The way out is to let go and align goals under an overarching goal (pp. 113–116).
- **Tragedy of the commons:** feedback from a shared, erodable resource to its users is missing or too delayed. The remedies are exhortation, privatization and regulation; the last two restore the feedback link (pp. 116–121).
- **Drift to low performance** (eroding goals) and **escalation** are both reinforcing loops. In the first, the goal follows perceived performance downward. In the second, each actor's goal is set by the other's state (pp. 122–126).
- **Success to the successful:** winners receive the means to win again, so, if the loop is left unchecked, the winners eventually take all. The remedies are diversification, antitrust limits, levelling the playing field and rewards that do not bias the next round (pp. 127–130).
- **Shifting the burden (addiction):** an intervention that hides symptoms can let the system's own corrective capacity atrophy. The best exit is to avoid the trap, or to strengthen the system's own capability (pp. 131–135).
- **Rule beating** and **seeking the wrong goal** are near opposites. In the first the system evades the rule; in the second it obeys a badly chosen indicator, e.g. confusing effort with result (pp. 136–141).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 111–112)
- Delays, nonlinearities and fuzzy boundaries appear in almost any system. They are generally not properties to be changed. Being less surprised is mainly a matter of learning to expect, appreciate and use complexity (p. 111).
- Some systems are "perverse": their structure produces truly problematic behaviour. Meadows calls common structures of this kind **archetypes**, e.g. addiction, drift to low performance, escalation (p. 111).
- Damage from archetypes is often blamed on actors or events, though it is a consequence of structure. Blaming, disciplining, firing, twisting policy levers harder, hoping for better driving events and tinkering at the margins will not fix structural problems (p. 112). Hence the name **traps**.
- Traps can be escaped by recognizing them in advance, or by altering the structure: reformulating goals, weakening, strengthening or altering feedback loops, adding new loops. Hence "opportunities" (p. 112).

### Policy resistance — fixes that fail (pp. 112–116)
- A balancing loop's main symptom is that little changes despite outside pushes. That is good for body temperature at 37 °C, but some persistent patterns are undesirable (p. 112). Examples named: farm programs and overproduction, wars on drugs, health-care costs, job-creation programs (pp. 112–113).
- **Mechanism.** Each actor monitors some variable and compares it with his, her or its own goal. If there is a discrepancy, the actor acts, usually more emphatically for a larger gap (p. 113). Resistance arises when subsystem goals differ and are inconsistent (p. 113).
- **Drug example.** One stock (drug supply on the streets) is pulled by several actors. Addicts want it high, enforcement low, pushers in the middle, citizens want safety from addict crime (p. 113). If enforcement cuts imports, the others double their efforts: prices rise, addicts commit more crime, profits buy planes and boats to evade patrols. The result is a standoff the stock barely changes from, which nobody wants (p. 113).
- The structure can work as a **ratchet**: more effort from one actor brings more effort from all, and backing off takes a lot of mutual trust (pp. 113–114).
- **Romania.** In 1967 abortion was banned for women under 45 (p. 114).
  - The birth rate tripled, then slowly fell back nearly to its earlier level, mainly through dangerous illegal abortions that tripled maternal mortality.
  - Many unwanted children ended up in orphanages. Families were too poor to raise the many children the government wanted (p. 114).
  - Ceausescu tried to overpower the resistance. After his government was overturned he was executed, and the first law the new government repealed was the ban (p. 114).
- **Ways out.**
  - *Overpower:* it can work if one keeps wielding enough power, at the cost of resentment and possible explosion if power lapses (p. 114).
  - *Let go:* give up ineffective policies. Much of the action you were correcting was a response to your own action, so if you calm down, those pulling against you will calm down too, Meadows says. Prohibition's end in 1933 is the example (p. 114).
  - *Look at the feedbacks:* Hungary found cramped housing a reason for small families and rewarded larger families with more living space. This was only partially successful, but far better than Romania's policy (p. 115).
  - *Align goals:* the most effective way, usually by providing an overarching goal that lets actors break out of their bounded rationality (p. 115). Examples: wartime mobilization, disaster recovery. Sweden in the 1930s found agreement on the quality of child care, not on family size. Its policy included free contraceptives and abortion, sex education, free obstetrical care and more investment in education and health (p. 115).
  - Harmonizing goals is not always possible but is worth looking for. It requires letting go of narrow goals and considering the long-term welfare of the whole system (pp. 115–116).
- **Box (p. 116).** Trap: when actors pull a stock toward different goals, any effective new policy pulls it farther from others' goals and produces more resistance. Way out: let go, bring in all actors, find satisfying ways to meet all goals, or larger goals everyone pulls toward.
- *Intuition note:* Several thermostats with different settings all fight over one room. Each works harder when the room drifts from its own setting. Stopping the fight, or agreeing on one setting, frees all the wasted effort.

### The tragedy of the commons (pp. 116–121)
- The trap arises with escalation or simple growth in a commonly shared, erodable environment (p. 116).
- **Hardin's argument** (1968; the pasture example, pp. 116–117). A herdsman asks what adding one more animal is worth to him. He gets all the proceeds, so the utility is nearly +1. The overgrazing cost is shared, so for him it is only a fraction of −1. **Hardin's conclusion:** each rational herdsman adds another animal, and each is locked into a system that compels him to increase his herd without limit in a limited world (p. 117). Meadows: "Bounded rationality in a nutshell!" (p. 117).
- **Structural ingredients** (p. 117).
  - A shared resource that is limited and *erodable*: beyond some threshold, the less there is, the less it regenerates or the more likely it is destroyed. Less grass leads to less soil, which leads to worse grass: another reinforcing loop running downhill.
  - Users who have reason to increase, at a rate not influenced by the condition of the commons.
  - Hence the tragedy arises from missing (or too long delayed) feedback from the resource to the growth of its users (p. 117). The more resource is used, the less per user. Without feedback, overharvesting continues until the erosion loop kicks in and all users are ruined (p. 118).
- Examples: national park access, fossil fuels and climate, children whose costs are borne by society (Hardin's motivating case) (p. 118). The same holds for common *sinks* (shared pollution dumps): a polluter gains a lot and bears a fraction of the cost. In these cases, feedback on the rate of use of the common resource (source or sink) is weak (p. 118).
- **Three ways out** (p. 119).
  - *Educate and exhort.* It tries to keep use low by moral pressure. Some "primitive" cultures managed commons this way for generations, but Hardin does not think it dependable. Meadows adds that resources protected only by tradition or an honor system may attract those who do not respect it or have no honor (p. 119).
  - *Privatize.* It links resource condition to users by putting gains and losses on the same decision maker. The owner may still abuse it, but that now takes ignorance or irrationality (p. 119). It works more reliably than exhortation, if society will let some individuals learn the hard way. Many resources (atmosphere, fish of the sea) cannot be privatized (p. 120).
  - *Regulate* ("mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon", Hardin). Regulation is an indirect feedback link through regulators. For it to work, regulators need expertise to monitor the commons, effective deterrence, and the good of the whole community at heart (p. 119). To be effective, it must be enforced by policing and penalties (p. 119).
- Everyday regulated commons (pp. 120–121): traffic lights (take turns), parking meters (charge and limit time), bank safes and police, broadcast permits, and garbage fees by amount (which restore the missing feedback). Most people comply most of the time when systems are mutually agreed and understood, but all need police power for occasional noncooperators (p. 121).
- **Box (p. 121).** Way out: educate and exhort, and restore or strengthen the missing feedback link, by privatizing or regulating access.
- *Intuition note:* Each user takes the whole benefit but pays only a small share of the damage, so no one feels the signal that would make them stop. The structural fixes work by restoring that signal: privatizing puts gains and losses on one decider, and regulation links resource condition to users indirectly, through regulators.

### Drift to low performance (pp. 121–123)
- Some systems keep getting worse. Examples: falling market share, eroding hospital service, dirtier rivers, regained fat after diets, the author's jogging program (p. 122).
- **Mechanism.** The actor has a goal and compares it with the state; this alone is an ordinary balancing loop (p. 122). Two additions turn it into a trap (p. 122):
  1. *Perceived ≠ actual state.* The actor tends to believe bad news more than good. The best results are dismissed as aberrations and the worst are remembered, so things seem worse than they are.
  2. *The goal is influenced by the perceived state.* When perceived performance slips, the goal is allowed to slip ("that's about all you can expect").
- The balancing loop is overwhelmed by a reinforcing loop heading downhill. Lower perceived state gives a lower desired state, which gives a smaller discrepancy and less corrective action, which gives a lower state (p. 122).
- Other names: "eroding goals", "boiled frog syndrome". Meadows says she does not know whether the frog story is true (pp. 122–123). The drift is gradual; a quick plunge would trigger agitated correction (p. 123).
- **Two antidotes** (p. 123): keep standards absolute regardless of performance; or make goals sensitive to the *best* past performances instead of the worst. The same structure then becomes a reinforcing loop upward.
- **Box (p. 123).** Trap: standards influenced by past performance, especially with negative bias in perception. Way out: absolute standards, or standards raised by the best actual performance.
- *Intuition note:* If you lower the target whenever you miss it, you are never far from the target, so you never push back. Anchor the target and the correction survives.

### Escalation (pp. 124–126)
- The kids' fight from earlier in the book is an example: "you hit me, I hit back a little harder" (p. 124). Decision rule: "I'll raise you one" (p. 124).
- **Structure.** A reinforcing loop of competing actors. One actor's goal is not absolute (like a thermostat setting) but is related to another actor's state. Each takes its desired state from the other's perceived state and ups it (p. 124). Meadows says escalation is not necessarily bad if the goal is desirable (efficient computers, a cure for AIDS), but is insidious for hostility, weaponry or noise (p. 124).
- Examples: US–Soviet arms race, where each side exaggerated the other's armaments. Meadows says it is more systemic to say that each side was escalating itself, rather than blaming the other (pp. 124–125). Also negative campaigning, price wars (possibly ending in a competitor's bankruptcy), advertising, cocktail-party loudness. Escalation in a good direction, such as hospital diagnostic machines or morality, can also be a problem because it is hard to stop (p. 125).
- Being a reinforcing loop, escalation builds exponentially and can reach extremes faster than expected. If nothing breaks the loop, it usually ends with one or both competitors breaking down (p. 125).
- **Ways out.**
  - Unilateral disarmament: reduce your own state to induce reductions in the competitor's. It is almost unthinkable within the system's logic, but it can work if done with determination and if one can survive the competitor's short-term advantage (pp. 125–126).
  - Negotiate a disarmament: a structural change that creates new balancing loops (parental pressure, advertising regulation, peace-keeping troops) (p. 126).
- **Box (p. 126).** The best way out is to avoid getting in; otherwise refuse to compete or negotiate balancing loops.
- *Intuition note:* Each side's target is a bit more than the other has. Every move sets a higher target for the other, so the growth feeds itself.

### Success to the successful — competitive exclusion (pp. 126–130)
- **Trap.** The winners of a competition receive, as part of the reward, the means to compete more effectively later. This reinforcing loop divides the system into winners who keep winning and losers who keep losing (p. 127).
- Examples: Monopoly (hotel owners extract rent and buy more hotels, p. 127); the neighbourhood Christmas-lights contest, where the $100 prize was spent on more lights, and the contest was suspended after three wins in a row (p. 127). If the winning is in a limited environment, what the winner gains is taken from the losers (p. 127).
- **Ecology.** In the competitive exclusion principle, two different species cannot live in exactly the same niche competing for exactly the same resources. One will reproduce faster or use the resource more efficiently, win a larger share and multiply more. It will drive the other to extinction, usually by appropriating the resource, not by direct confrontation (p. 127).
- **Marx.** Meadows presents Marx's critique as the same loop for firms: a slight advantage gives more income to invest, so that firm's capital-accumulation loop turns faster. With a finite market and no antitrust law, one firm takes over everything as long as it keeps reinvesting (p. 128). She argues this analysis (market competition systematically eliminates market competition) is not disproved by the Soviet fall. Her examples: US auto companies reduced to three, not one, because of antitrust laws; one newspaper left in most major US cities; fewer, larger farms (p. 128).
- **Greatest damage:** the rich get richer and the poor poorer. Mechanisms listed: poor children get the worst schools; people with low assets cannot borrow from banks and so pay moneylenders or cannot invest; tenant farmers pay part of the crop and cannot buy land, while the landowner buys more with the rents (pp. 128–129). The poor also pay the highest prices because they buy small quantities, receive a small share of government spending, get ideas last and disease and pollution first (p. 129).
- **Ways out** (pp. 129–130):
  - *Diversification:* new products or niches. It is not guaranteed if the dominant firm can crush or buy offshoots. Meadows says it does not work as a strategy for the poor (p. 129).
  - *Feedback loops that stop any competitor taking over entirely*, such as antitrust laws. Very big companies can use their winnings to weaken administration of those laws (p. 129).
  - *Periodically levelling the playing field:* Monopoly restarting, handicaps in sports, the potlatch (p. 129). Also progressive taxes, charity, welfare, unions, universal health care and education, inheritance tax ("starting the game over with each new generation") (p. 130).
  - Such mechanisms may come from morality or from the practical understanding that hopeless losers could destroy the playing field (p. 130).
- **Box (p. 130).** Winners eventually take all while losers are eliminated, if the loop is not held in check. Ways out: diversification, limits on any winner's share, levelling policies, rewards that do not bias the next round.
- *Intuition note:* The prize is also the tool for winning the next round. Unless something resets or caps the advantage, small early differences grow.

### Shifting the burden to the intervenor — addiction (pp. 131–135)
- Addiction can appear in larger systems: industry on subsidy, farmers on fertilizer, Western economies on cheap oil, weapons makers on government contracts (p. 131).
- **Structure.** A stock with in-flows and out-flows is maintained by an actor's balancing loop, comparing the goal with a *perception* of the state (p. 131). Drugs, subsidy and fertilizer change the perception or the state quickly but do nothing for the real situation (e.g. subsidies keep an ineffective firm profitable; fertilizer gives a crop without improving soil). The effects do not last: intoxication wears off, the subsidy is spent, the fertilizer is consumed or washed away (pp. 131–132).
- Examples of dependence: family care of the aged replaced by institutions, highways displacing rail, calculators and arithmetic skill, vaccines and natural immunity, modern medicine and individual health practices (p. 132).
- Shifting a burden can be good, and is often done purposefully. A 100% smallpox vaccine, if it lasts, is preferable to partial natural immunity (Meadows: some systems really do need an intervenor, pp. 132–133).
- **How the trap forms** (p. 133).
  - A corrective process within the system does a poor job. An efficient intervenor steps in and quickly brings the system to the wanted state.
  - The original problem reappears because its root cause was not addressed. More "solution" is applied, disguising the real state.
  - The trap is formed if the intervention, by destruction or simple neglect, undermines the system's original capacity to maintain itself. If that capacity atrophies, more intervention is needed, which weakens it further.
- **Why entered** (p. 133). The intervenor may not foresee growing dependency that strains its own capacity (the US health-care system is cited). The helped party may not think through the long-term loss of control and increased vulnerability.
- Addictive policies are insidious, easy to sell. Examples: pesticides that allow more monocultures and bring larger pest outbreaks; fixing the oil price after the 1970s shocks, and later war for oil or a search for more oil (pp. 133–134).
- **Withdrawal.** It is painful, since it means confronting the real, usually deteriorated state. It can sometimes be gradual, or eased by a nonaddictive policy that restores the degraded system (group support, home insulation and efficient cars, polyculture and crop rotation). Sometimes one must go "cold turkey" (p. 134). It is far preferable to avoid addiction by helping the system help itself, which can be much cheaper than taking over (p. 134).
- Opening questions for such intervening (pp. 134–135): why are the natural correction mechanisms failing? How can obstacles to them be removed? How can mechanisms for their success be made more effective?
- **Box and advice (p. 135).** Beware of symptom-relieving or signal-denying policies; put the focus on long-term restructuring. If you are the intervenor, restore or enhance the system's own ability to solve its problems, then remove yourself. If you are the dependent party, rebuild your own capabilities before removing the intervention, and do it right away, since waiting makes withdrawal harder (p. 135).
- *Intuition note:* A helper that fixes the symptom lets the system's own repair mechanism wither. The more it withers, the more help is needed, so the dependence grows.

### Rule beating (pp. 136–137)
- Rule beating is evasive action that gets around the *intent* of rules, keeping the letter but not the spirit. It is a problem only when it leads to large distortions, behaviour that would make no sense without the rules (p. 136).
- Examples (pp. 136–137): end-of-fiscal-year spending to avoid a smaller next budget; Vermont's Act 250 (approval for lots of ten acres or less) leading to many lots just over ten acres; European feed-grain import limits leading to cassava imports from Asia; landowners hunting or poisoning endangered species so the land can be developed.
- It produces the appearance of compliance (drivers slow near police cars), so the law must be designed with the system's self-organizing evasions in mind (p. 137).
- It is usually a response of lower levels in a hierarchy to overrigid, deleterious, unworkable or ill-defined rules from above (p. 137).
- Two responses (p. 137): stamp it out by stronger rules or enforcement, which usually brings greater distortion (the way into the trap); or treat rule beating as useful feedback and revise, improve, rescind or better explain the rules, foreseeing effects on subsystems and turning their self-organizing capacity in a positive direction (p. 137).

### Seeking the wrong goal (pp. 138–141)
- A goal is the direction-setter of the system, so if it is defined badly or does not reflect real welfare, the system cannot produce a desirable result. Systems tend to produce "exactly and only" what you ask for (p. 138).
- Examples: security measured by military spending produces spending, not necessarily security; education measured by money per student or by test scores produces those numbers, and whether they track good education is a question Meadows says deserves thought (p. 138). In early Indian family planning, a target of IUDs implanted led doctors to implant without patient approval (pp. 138–139). These confuse effort with result (p. 139).
- **GNP** (money value of final goods and services) is perhaps the worst such mistake, in Meadows's view (p. 139).
  - It lumps goods and bads (more accidents raise it). It counts only marketed goods. It ignores distribution: a rich family's second home raises it more than a poor family's basic house.
  - It measures effort and gross production, not efficiency. Light bulbs using one-eighth the electricity and lasting ten times as long lower it.
  - It measures throughput (flows) instead of capital stocks (p. 139). Meadows says it could be argued that the best society maintains its stocks with the *lowest* possible throughput (pp. 139–140).
  - Governments respond to faltering GNP with many wasteful actions, some harmful in the long term, e.g. overharvesting forests (p. 140).
  - A society defining its goal as GNP will produce GNP, not welfare, equity, justice or efficiency, unless these are defined, measured and reported (p. 140).
- **Contrast with rule beating** (p. 140): in rule beating the system evades a rule while appearing to obey; in wrong-goal seeking it obediently follows the rule and gets a result nobody wants. Both can occur with the same rule (p. 141).
- **Box (p. 140).** Way out: specify indicators and goals that reflect the real welfare of the system, and do not confuse effort with result.
- *Intuition note:* A balancing loop is only as good as the number it tries to hold. Hold the wrong number and the loop works perfectly toward the wrong result.

### Interlude: the goal of sailboat design (p. 141)
- Sailboat races began with ordinary boats. Races are more interesting when boats are roughly equal, so rules defined classes by length, sail area and other parameters. Boats were then designed to win within the rules, becoming strange, extremely fast, nearly unseaworthy and needing expert crews. Meadows says they are "so optimized around the present rules that they have lost all resilience", and any rule change would render them useless (p. 141).

## Toy example
*Our own, not from the text.* It shows why the goal-setting rule decides between "corrected" and "drifting".

Performance P starts at 100. Each period an outside force lowers P by 4. The actor then closes half of the gap between goal G and P, so $P_{t+1} = P_t + 0.5\,(G_t - P_t) - 4$.

**A. Absolute goal, G = 100 always.**
- P1 = 100 + 0 − 4 = 96.
- P2 = 96 + 0.5(4) − 4 = 94.
- P3 = 94 + 3 − 4 = 93.
- P4 = 93 + 3.5 − 4 = 92.5.
- The state settles where 0.5(100 − P) = 4, i.e. P = 92.

**B. Eroding goal.** The same rule, but the goal slips halfway toward current performance: $G_{t+1} = 0.5\,G_t + 0.5\,P_{t+1}$.

| t | P | G |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100 | 100 |
| 1 | 96 | 98 |
| 2 | 93 | 95.5 |
| 3 | 90.25 | 92.875 |
| 4 | 87.5625 | — |

Arithmetic: P1 = 100 + 0 − 4 = 96, and G1 = 50 + 48 = 98. P2 = 96 + 0.5(2) − 4 = 93, and G2 = 49 + 46.5 = 95.5. P3 = 93 + 0.5(2.5) − 4 = 90.25, and G3 = 47.75 + 45.125 = 92.875. P4 = 90.25 + 0.5(2.625) − 4 = 87.5625.

In A the loop pushes back against the outside erosion and stabilizes at 92. In B the gap stays small, so little correction happens, and after the first period (a drop of 4), P falls by about 2.7 to 3 each period. This simplified model leaves out the text's negative perception bias; it keeps only the goal slipping with performance (p. 122).

## Concepts

- [[bounded-rationality]] (new) — each actor sees only part of the system and pursues own goals; Meadows applies it to policy resistance and the commons (pp. 113, 117)
- [[policy-resistance]] (new) — actors with inconsistent goals pull one stock apart, and effective policy provokes counter-effort (p. 113)
- [[tragedy-of-the-commons]] (new) — overuse of a shared, erodable resource because feedback to users is missing or delayed (p. 117)
- [[drift-to-low-performance]] (new) — eroding goals: a reinforcing loop in which the goal follows perceived performance down (p. 122)

- [[success-to-the-successful]] (new) — winners get the means to win again, driving competitive exclusion (p. 127)

- [[rule-beating]] (new) — evading the intent of rules while obeying the letter (p. 136)
- [[wrong-goal]] (new) — the system obediently produces what an inaccurate goal indicator specifies (p. 138)
- [[negative-feedback]] — balancing loops hold behaviour in place (here also blocking change in policy resistance) (p. 112)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Drift to low performance ↔ control theory: a controller whose reference is adapted to the measured output has no fixed set point, so error never persists.
- [structural-analogy] Shifting the burden ↔ control theory: a controller that masks an error lets the plant's own regulation weaken, raising the required control effort.
- [metaphor] Seeking the wrong goal ↔ control theory: a loop that tracks a measured proxy perfectly while the true variable of interest is not controlled.
- [structural-analogy] Tragedy of the commons ↔ control theory: missing or delayed feedback from the resource state to users' growth, i.e. an open loop on the key variable.

## Questions this raises
- When do actors in policy resistance truly share a harmonizable goal, and what if no overarching goal exists (p. 116)?
- Can the commons remedies (exhortation, privatization, regulation) be compared, e.g. by how fast each restores feedback (pp. 119–120)?
- How can one tell whether an intervention supports a system's own capacity or erodes it (pp. 134–135)?
- How do we choose indicators that avoid both rule beating and wrong-goal seeking (pp. 140–141)?
