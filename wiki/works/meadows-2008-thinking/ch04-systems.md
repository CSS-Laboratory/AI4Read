# Why Systems Surprise Us

## L0 — One line
Systems surprise us, in part, because our mental models are limited: we watch events, think linearly, draw false boundaries, ignore changing limits and delays, and forget that each actor sees only partial information.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do systems keep surprising us, even after we have studied them? Meadows starts from the view that everything we think we know is a model, and every model falls short of the world. She gives a warning list of six traps: seeing only events, expecting linear responses, drawing boundaries wrongly, ignoring shifting limits, overlooking delays, and forgetting bounded rationality (sensible choices on partial information). Her advice includes looking at history to infer structure, watching for loops that gain and lose dominance, redrawing boundaries for each question, anticipating the next limiting factor, planning ahead for delays, and redesigning information flows and incentives rather than replacing people. Understanding these properties may make surprise less frequent, not impossible.

**Prerequisites:** stocks, flows, and balancing and reinforcing feedback loops (earlier chapters); the idea of a graph of a variable over time; simple proportionality.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Everything we think we know about the world is a model. Models usually match the world well, but they fall far short of representing it fully, which is why we are regularly surprised (pp. 86–87).
- Events are the visible tip; behavior over time is deeper; structure (stocks, flows, loops) is the source of behavior (pp. 88–89). There is no reason to expect stable statistical links between flows: flows respond to stocks, and a fitted equation holds only until the structure changes (p. 90).
- Nonlinear relationships break the expectation that a bigger push gives a proportionally bigger response. They also shift the relative strength of feedback loops (p. 92). The spruce budworm case shows this (pp. 93–94).
- Boundaries, including the "clouds" that mark them in diagrams, are mental constructs. Too narrow a boundary causes surprises; too wide a boundary can obscure the answer with piles of information. The right boundary depends on the question (pp. 95–99).
- Growth runs through layers of limits. The most limiting input changes as the system grows, and limits to growth can be self-imposed; if they are not, they will be system-imposed (pp. 100–103).
- Delays are everywhere. They set how fast and how accurately systems react, and Meadows states that overshoots, oscillations and collapses are always caused by delays. Foresight is essential when feedback delays are long (pp. 103–105).
- Bounded rationality: reasonable local decisions based on partial, delayed information can add up to results nobody wants. Replacing people rarely helps; redesigning information, incentives and goals does (pp. 106–110).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 86–87)
- **Berry epigraph** (p. 86): gaining knowledge reveals ignorance; our knowledge of the world first shows us that the world is greater than our knowledge of it.
- Meadows says the zoo systems still surprise her. Three truths recur (p. 86):
  1. Everything we think we know is a model: words, maps, statistics, equations, programs, and mental models. None is the real world (p. 86).
  2. Models usually have strong congruence (close match) with the world, which is why humans succeed as a species (p. 86).
  3. Models fall far short of the world, so we make mistakes. In our heads we track only a few variables at one time. We often draw illogical conclusions from accurate assumptions, or logical ones from inaccurate assumptions. Most people are surprised by exponential growth, and few can intuit how to damp oscillations (pp. 86–87).
- The book rests on a duality, Meadows says: we know a tremendous amount, but not nearly enough. Understanding can improve but cannot be made perfect (p. 87).
- The chapter is a "warning list" of snags. One cannot navigate well in a feedback-dominated world unless one looks at long-term behavior and structure, not short-term events, and is also aware of false boundaries, bounded rationality, limiting factors, nonlinearities and delays. Resilience, self-organization and hierarchy must be respected, or one is likely to mistreat, misdesign or misread systems (p. 87).
- Even with all this understanding, "you may be surprised less often, but you will still be surprised" (p. 87).

*Intuition note:* A model is a simplified copy of reality. The copy is useful but incomplete, so the gaps show up as surprises. Learning more shrinks the gaps; it does not close them.

### Beguiling Events (pp. 87–90)
- **Boulding's poem** (p. 88): a system is a black box (a system known only by what goes in and out). Input–output pairs may let us predict if the relation is "good and stable"; if not, we must "force the lid".
- Systems fool us by presenting themselves (or we fool ourselves by seeing the world) as a series of events: elections, floods, stock prices. Events are the black box's moment-by-moment outputs (p. 88).
- Events are emotionally gripping, but seeing the world as a series of events gives it almost no predictive or explanatory power. Events are like an iceberg tip: the most visible part, not always the most important (p. 88).
- **Behavior** means performance over time: growth, stagnation, decline, oscillation, randomness, evolution. We are less likely to be surprised if we see how events accumulate into patterns (streaks, trends, rising variance) (p. 88).
- A systems thinker first looks for data, time graphs and history, because long-term behavior gives clues to structure, and structure explains why things happen (p. 89).
- **Structure** = interlocking stocks, flows and feedback loops (the "spaghetti-and-meatball diagrams") (p. 89).
  - Structure determines which behaviors are latent in the system.
  - A goal-seeking balancing loop approaches or holds a dynamic equilibrium. A reinforcing loop generates exponential growth.
  - The two linked are capable of growth, decay or equilibrium. With delays they may produce oscillations. If they work in periodic, rapid bursts they may produce even more surprising behaviors (p. 89).
- Systems thinking moves back and forth between structure (diagrams) and behavior (time graphs). Example: hand releasing a Slinky (event), oscillations (behavior), mechanical coil (structure) (p. 89).
- Much analysis stays at events, for example nightly explanations of the stock market (blaming, say, a fall in the dollar). These are "event-event" analyses with no power to predict tomorrow or to change the system (p. 89).
- Most economic analysis goes one level deeper, to behavior over time (econometric models, statistical links among past trends). These are more useful than event-based ones but have two problems (pp. 89–90):
  1. They typically overemphasize flows and underemphasize stocks (for example GNP, a flow, gets attention rather than the stock of capital). Without seeing how stocks affect flows through feedback, one cannot understand the dynamics (p. 90).
  2. Searching for stable statistical links between flows is, in Meadows's view, a search for something that does not exist: there is no reason to expect a stable link. Flows respond to stocks, not to other flows (p. 90).
- **Thermostat example** (p. 90): with data on heat flows into and out of a room but no knowledge of thermostats, you could fit an equation for how the flows varied together. They vary together because the same stock (room temperature) governs them. The equation holds only until the structure changes (window opened, insulation, furnace tuned, oil forgotten). It would not help to warm the room, fix a sudden temperature drop, or cut the fuel bill. For those you must dig into structure.
- So behavior-based econometric models are "pretty good" at near-term prediction, "quite bad" at longer-term prediction, and "terrible" at telling how to improve performance (p. 90).
- That is one reason systems surprise us: we are fascinated by events, give too little attention to history, and are not skilled at reading structure from history (pp. 90–91).

*Intuition note:* Two flows that move together in the data may only share a common cause, the stock. If the structure changes, the link disappears. Knowing the structure tells you what to change; the pattern alone does not.

### Linear Minds in a Nonlinear World (pp. 91–94)
- **Gleick epigraph** (p. 91): linear equations are solvable and their pieces add up. Nonlinear systems generally cannot be solved or added together, but they allow rich behavior never seen in linear systems.
- A **linear relationship** is a straight line, with constant proportions. Fertilizer example: 10, 20, 30 pounds give +2, +4, +6 bushels (p. 91).
- A **nonlinear relationship** does not give a proportional effect and can only be drawn with curves or wiggles. Fertilizer example: 100 pounds gives +10 bushels, 200 gives no gain, 300 lowers yield, because too much damages the soil (p. 91).
- Learning that a small push gives a small response, we expect twice the push to give twice the response. In a nonlinear system it could give one-sixth, the square, or none (p. 91).
- Examples (p. 92):
  - Traffic: speed drops only slightly over a large range of density, then rapidly, and can reach zero (jam).
  - Soil erosion: little effect on yield until topsoil reaches the root-zone depth, then yields plummet.
  - Advertising: a little tasteful advertising awakens interest; a lot of blatant advertising causes disgust.
- Nonlinearities foil the expectation that more of a cure does proportionally more good, or more harm only a bit more harm. They are also important because they change the relative strengths of feedback loops and can flip a system between modes. They are the chief cause of shifting dominance, for example from exponential growth under a dominant reinforcing loop to decline under a suddenly dominant balancing loop (p. 92).

**Interlude: Spruce budworms, firs and pesticides (pp. 92–94)**
- Tree rings show the budworm has periodically killed spruce and fir in North America for at least 400 years. It became a "serious pest" only when the virgin pine was gone and the lumber industry turned to spruce and fir (pp. 92–93).
- From the 1950s forests were sprayed with DDT, then other insecticides after DDT was banned. A budworm resurgence still came every year. In 1980 New Brunswick spent $12.5 million, and the budworm still killed as many as 20 million hectares (50 million acres) per year (p. 93).
- Holling and Baskerville built a computer model. Before spraying, the budworm was barely detectable in most years because predators (birds, a spider, a parasitic wasp, diseases) controlled it. Every few decades an outbreak lasted 6–10 years, then subsided (p. 93).
- Chain of nonlinearities (pp. 93–94):
  - Balsam fir is the most competitive tree and the budworm's preferred food. Left alone it would crowd out spruce and birch. Each outbreak cuts back fir, opening space for spruce and birch (p. 93).
  - As fir builds up, outbreak probability rises nonlinearly. Budworm reproductive potential rises more than proportionally to its food supply. The final trigger is two or three warm, dry springs. An event-level analysis would blame the outbreak on those springs (p. 93).
  - Predators multiply faster as budworms increase, over a wide range. Beyond some point they cannot multiply faster. A reinforcing relationship becomes a "nonrelationship" and budworms take off (p. 94).
  - The outbreak stops when budworms kill the fir that feeds them. The reinforcing reproduction loop yields dominance to the balancing starvation loop, and the cycle restarts (p. 94).
- The system oscillates over decades but is ecologically stable within limits. Here what is ecologically stable is economically unstable, since eastern Canada's economy depends on logging (p. 94).
- Spraying kills pest and natural enemies, weakening the loop that holds budworms in check, and keeps fir density high. This moves the budworms up their nonlinear reproduction curve to the edge of explosion. Holling calls the result "persistent semi-outbreak conditions". Managers are locked into a policy such that, if it fails, there will be an outbreak of an intensity never seen before (p. 94).

*Intuition note:* The same system can sit on a flat part or a steep part of a curve, and its response to a push differs. Spraying pushed the system toward the steep part. A policy that did not stop the yearly resurgences also moved the system toward a bigger hidden risk.

### Nonexistent Boundaries (pp. 95–99)
- **Hardin epigraph** (p. 95): "side-effects" means roughly effects the speaker had not foreseen or does not want to think about. They are no more "side" than the principal effect.
- **Clouds** in diagrams stand for ignored stocks (sources and sinks) at the edge of a diagram. They rarely mark a real boundary, because systems rarely have real ones. There are only boundaries of word, thought, perception and social agreement: artificial, mental-model boundaries. The greatest complexities arise exactly at boundaries, and disorderly, mixed-up borders are sources of diversity and creativity (p. 95).
- **Car dealer example** (pp. 95–96, Fig. 47): cars come from raw materials, capital, labor, energy, technology and management, and go to consumers' home stocks. Whether clouds will do depends on whether these stocks influence behavior over the time period of interest. If raw materials are guaranteed abundant and consumers keep demanding, clouds do. If there could be a shortage or a glut and the boundary excludes those stocks, we could be surprised (pp. 95–96).
- Figure 47 still has clouds. Further out are chemical plants, smelters and refineries, the earth, employment, wages, profits, pollution, landfills, incinerators and recycling (p. 96).
- Whether the full "cradle to grave" flow matters depends on who wants to know, for what purpose and over how long. As the ecological footprint grows, the long term increasingly turns into the short term. Landfills and mines can fill or be exhausted with surprising suddenness (p. 96).
- Over geological time even mines and dumps are not the end: on Earth there are no ultimate boundaries. "Everything physical comes from somewhere, everything goes somewhere, everything keeps moving" (p. 96).
- Still, a model need not follow every connection to the whole planet. Simplifying requires boundaries, and clouds are a necessary part of models of metaphysical flows such as anger or love. Population with births and deaths from and to clouds (Fig. 48) is usually safe. It would not be if there were significant migration or if cemetery space were the issue (p. 97).
- **Lesson:** there is no single legitimate boundary. We invent boundaries for clarity, and they cause problems when we forget we made them. Where to draw one depends on the purpose and the questions asked (p. 97).
- Too narrow (pp. 97–98):
  - Traffic: highways without thinking of settlement patterns attract housing and become clogged again.
  - Sewage: dumping into a river forces the boundary to include the whole river, possibly soil and groundwater, but probably not the next watershed or the planetary hydrological cycle.
  - National parks: boundaries are crossed by nomadic peoples, wildlife, waters, development, acid rain and climate change.
- Too large: analysts produce multi-page diagrams connecting everything, the game of building ever bigger models. The piles of information may obscure answers. Modeling Earth's climate in full detail may not be necessary for reducing a country's CO₂ emissions (p. 98).
- The right boundary rarely matches an academic discipline or political border. Rivers make handy borders but are the worst for managing water, and air is worse still. People get attached to familiar boundaries, and universities too often show boundary rigidity (p. 98).
- Figure 49 (jail sentences, fuel rods, registered unemployed) shows clouds that should start, not stop, thinking beyond the border: what drives new sentences, where do used fuel rods go, what happens when registration lapses (p. 99).
- Conclusion: boundaries are of our own making and should be reconsidered for each new discussion, problem or purpose (p. 99).

*Intuition note:* A boundary is a decision about what to ignore. It is fine while the ignored part stays quiet, and a trap when it starts to matter.

### Layers of Limits (pp. 100–103)
- We like single causes and single effects and dislike thinking about limits. In fact, many causes routinely come together to produce many effects, and virtually all inputs, and so outputs, are limited. Meadows lists inputs needed by a factory (capital, labor, energy, raw materials, land, water, technology, credit, insurance, customers, management, public services, functioning families, a healthy ecosystem) and by a patch of grain (sunlight, air, water, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, minor nutrients, soil and microbes, weed and pest control, protection from industrial wastes) (pp. 100–101).
- **Liebig's law of the minimum:** it does not matter how much nitrogen is available if phosphorus is short, and more phosphorus does not help if potassium is low. Bread needs yeast however much flour there is (p. 101).
- **Limiting factor:** at any given time, the most important input to a system is the one that is most limiting (p. 101). Meadows calls the concept simple but widely misunderstood. Examples (p. 101):
  - Agronomists ask whether unidentified nutrients exist and how artificial fertilizers affect soil microbes.
  - Rich countries transfer capital or technology to poor ones, though these may not be the most limiting factors.
  - Economic production functions track mainly labor and capital, which were the common limits when economics evolved. As the economy grows relative to the ecosystem, limits shift to clean water, clean air, dump space, energy and materials.
- **Forrester's corporate-growth model** (pp. 101–102): a young company grows fast and must handle limits that shift as it grows. Too-good salespeople create orders beyond factory capacity, so production capacity limits. Managers expand plants; people hired in a hurry are trained too little, so labor skill limits. Training improves quality, orders pour in, and order-fulfillment and record-keeping clogs. "And so forth" (p. 102).
- Layers of limits surround every growing plant, child, epidemic, product, company, city, economy and population. Insight comes from seeing which factor limits and that growth itself depletes or enhances limits. When one factor stops limiting, growth occurs and changes relative scarcity until another factor limits. Shifting attention from abundant factors to the next potential limiting factor gives real understanding of, and control over, growth (p. 102).
- A growing entity and its limited environment form a "coevolving dynamic system" (p. 102).
- This is not a recipe for perpetual growth. Perpetual growth is impossible for a physical entity in a finite environment, so the choice is what limits to live within. A "perfect" product or city attracts orders or people until some limit degrades it (p. 102).
- "There always will be limits to growth." They can be self-imposed; if not, they will be system-imposed (p. 103).

*Intuition note:* At any moment one input is the most limiting, so adding more of the other inputs does no good. Removing the bottleneck lets the system grow until the next input becomes the bottleneck.

### Ubiquitous Delays (pp. 103–105)
- **Havel epigraph** (p. 103): one cannot hurry history, as a child cannot make a plant grow faster by pulling it. Havel believes we must learn to wait as we learn to create.
- We are surprised by how long things take. Forrester advised asking everyone in the system how long a delay is, making a best guess, and then multiplying that by three. Meadows says this also works for writing a book (p. 103).
- Every stock is a delay. Most flows have shipping, perception, processing or maturation delays (pp. 103–104).
- Examples (p. 104): disease infection to diagnosis (days to years); pollution emission to harm; breeding-population build-up causing commodity-price cycles of 4 years (pigs), 7 years (cows), 11 years (cocoa trees); changing family-size norms (at least a generation); designing a car (3–8 years), model life (about 5 years), cars on the road (10–15 years).
- Which delays matter depends on the purpose and on the frequencies one tries to understand. If you worry about oscillations of weeks, you probably need not think about delays of minutes or years, and for decades-long development you can usually ignore week-long oscillations (p. 104).
- Changing the length of a delay may utterly change behavior, so delays are often sensitive leverage points if they can be shortened or lengthened (p. 104).
- Responding to delayed information, or responding with a delay, makes decisions off target: too much or too little. Acting too fast may amplify short-term variation nervously and create unnecessary instability (pp. 104–105).
- Delays determine how fast systems react, how accurately they hit targets and how timely information is. Meadows states: "Overshoots, oscillations, and collapses are always caused by delays" (p. 105).
- Illustrations: Gorbachev could change the Soviet information system overnight but not the physical economy; German absorption caused longer hardship than politicians foresaw; power-plant delays cause overcapacity then brownouts; ocean response delays mean emissions have induced climate change not fully seen for a generation or two (p. 105).
- When feedback loops have long delays, some sort of foresight is essential. To act only when a problem becomes obvious is to miss an opportunity to solve it (p. 105).

*Intuition note:* If the information arrives late, or the action takes effect late, the correction is aimed at a situation that no longer exists. This can lead to overshoot or oscillation (the chapter points back to the zoo systems), and with long delays some foresight is essential.

### Bounded Rationality (pp. 105–110)
- **Adam Smith's view** (epigraph, p. 105): an individual pursuing his own gain is led by an "invisible hand" to promote the public interest, frequently more effectively than when he intends to (Smith's own words). Meadows says it would be so nice if this really worked, since mathematical models of the economy would then be much easier to make, but the world offers many cases of rational short-term action giving unwanted aggregate results (pp. 105–106).
- Her examples (p. 106): tourists ruin Waikiki or Zermatt; farm surpluses make prices plummet; fishermen overfish; corporations' collective investment causes business-cycle downturns; poor people have more babies than they can support.
- Daly calls this the "invisible foot"; Simon calls it **bounded rationality**: people make reasonable decisions on the information they have, which is imperfect, especially about distant parts of the system (p. 106). Fishermen do not know the fish count or other boats' catch. Businessmen do not know others' investment plans, market share or market size. Information and responses are incomplete and delayed, so they systematically invest too little at some times and too much at others (p. 106).
- **Simon's view:** we are not omniscient, rational optimizers but "satisficers", who meet needs well enough before the next decision. Meadows adds: we can take into account only what we know, we rarely see the full range of possibilities, and we find a choice we can live with and change our behavior only when forced (p. 106).
- **Behavioral scientists' findings** (p. 107): we misperceive risk; live in an exaggerated present, giving too little weight to the past; discount the future at rates that make no sense; weight signals wrongly; and reject unwelcome news. So we do not even optimize our own good.
- Smith-derived theory assumes homo economicus acts with perfect optimality on complete information, and that these actions add up to the best outcome for everybody. Meadows says neither assumption stands up long against the evidence (p. 107). The next chapter will describe common structures that can cause bounded rationality to lead to disaster: addiction, policy resistance, arms races, drift to low performance, tragedy of the commons (p. 107).
- **Role switch** (pp. 107–108): if you were put in the position of someone whose behavior you never understood (critic becomes government; laborer becomes management), you would face that position's information, incentives, goals and pressures. You might keep your old view and innovate, but it is "distinctly unlikely". A manager probably sees labor as a cost, a financier probably over-invests in booms and under-invests in busts, a poor person sees the rationality of many children, a mortgaged fisherman overfishes (p. 108).
- Teaching games give students partial information streams. As fishermen they overfish, as ministers they favor industry, as the upper class they look after themselves, as the lower class they become apathetic or rebellious. Meadows also cites Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment, in which players quickly took on the attitudes and behaviors of guards and prisoners (p. 108).
- This understanding does not excuse narrow-minded behavior; it explains why that behavior arises. Behavior is reasonable within what a person in that part of the system can see. Swapping individuals is not likely to make much difference, and blaming individuals rarely helps (p. 108).
- Change starts by stepping outside the limited view and getting an overview. Then information flows, goals and incentives can be restructured so bounded, rational actions add up to results everyone wants. Slightly enlarging bounded rationality with better, more complete, timelier information can change behavior quickly (p. 108).

**Interlude: Electric meters in Dutch houses (p. 109)**
- Near Amsterdam, alike houses had the meter either in the basement or in the front hall. During the 1970s energy crisis, some houses were found to use one-third less electricity than others. Price and families were similar. The low-use houses had the meter in the front hall, where the turning wheel was seen many times a day (p. 109).

- Some systems are structured to function well despite bounded rationality: the right feedback reaches the right place at the right time (the liver; undisturbed ecosystems; traditional cultures). They are self-regulatory (p. 109).
- Since Smith it has been widely believed that the free competitive market is one. In some ways it is. It lets producers and consumers, who have the best local information, make locally rational decisions. But those decisions cannot by themselves correct its tendency to create monopolies and externalities, discriminate against the poor, or overshoot its sustainable carrying capacity (p. 109).
- Paraphrased prayer: serenity to exercise bounded rationality freely in appropriately structured systems, courage to restructure those that are not, wisdom to know the difference (p. 110).
- Closing statement: the bounded rationality of each actor may or may not lead to decisions that further the system's welfare. If it does not, new actors will not improve performance. What matters is redesigning the information, incentives, disincentives, goals, stresses and constraints on specific actors (p. 110).

*Intuition note:* People act sensibly on what they can see. If the structure shows them the wrong things, the result is likely to be poor whoever sits there. Change what they see and what they are rewarded for.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A pest population $B$ (in thousands) grows 30% a year: growth $=0.3B$. Predators remove $\min(0.2B,\,10)$ per year, proportionally up to a cap of 10 (they "can multiply no faster", as on p. 94). Net yearly change $=0.3B-\min(0.2B,10)$.

| $B$ | growth $0.3B$ | removal $\min(0.2B,10)$ | net |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 3 | min(2, 10) = 2 | 1 |
| 50 | 15 | min(10, 10) = 10 | 5 |
| 100 | 30 | min(20, 10) = 10 | 20 |
| 200 | 60 | min(40, 10) = 10 | 50 |

A linear thinker who saw only the small range ($B=10$, net 1, so net $=0.1B$) would predict net 10 at $B=100$ and 20 at $B=200$. The real values are 20 and 50. Past the cap, the reinforcing growth loop dominates and the predator balancing loop has stopped scaling. This illustrates shifting loop dominance (p. 92), though the numbers and the model are invented.

## Concepts
- [[black box]] — a system known only by its inputs and outputs; events are its outputs (p. 88)
- [[feedback]] — loops of stocks and flows that generate behavior; the chapter stresses that structure drives behavior (p. 89)
- [[negative (balancing) feedback]] — goal-seeking loop that approaches or holds equilibrium (p. 89)
- [[positive (reinforcing) feedback]] — loop that generates exponential growth (p. 89)
- [[delay (dead time)]] — lag in a feedback loop; cause of overshoot, oscillation, collapse; every stock is a delay (pp. 103–105)
- [[stability]] — a system can be ecologically stable within limits (the budworm cycle) yet economically unstable (p. 94)
- [[event-behavior-structure]] (new) — events are outputs; behavior is performance over time; structure is stocks, flows, loops that cause behavior (pp. 88–89)
- [[nonlinearity]] (new) — cause does not give proportional effect; changes loop strengths and can flip modes (pp. 91–92)
- [[shifting dominance]] (new) — different loops dominate at different stock levels, often through nonlinearities (p. 92)
- [[system boundary]] (new) — mental, purpose-dependent edge around a system; clouds mark where flows are ignored (pp. 95–97)
- [[limiting factor]] (new) — the input most limiting at a given time; changes as growth occurs (pp. 101–102)
- [[bounded rationality]] (new) — reasonable decisions on partial, delayed information; satisficing instead of optimizing (p. 106)
- [[leverage point]] (new) — a place where policy can change system behavior; the chapter says delay lengths are often sensitive ones (p. 104)

## Canonical instances
- [[spruce-budworm]] — nonlinear predator saturation and fir growth cause outbreaks; spraying creates semi-outbreak lock-in (pp. 92–94)
- [[thermostat-flow-regression]] — fitting heat flows without knowing the stock fails when structure changes (p. 90)
- [[forrester-corporate-growth-model]] — growth shifts the limiting factor from production capacity to labor skill to order-fulfillment and record-keeping (p. 102)
- [[dutch-electric-meters]] — houses where the meter was seen daily used about one third less electricity (p. 109)
- [[liebig-law-of-minimum]] — the scarcest nutrient limits grain growth (p. 101)
- [[commodity-price-cycles]] — gestation/maturation delays give 4-, 7-, 11-year cycles (p. 104)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Delay in feedback loop ↔ control-theory: dead time causing overshoot and oscillation — Meadows says delays always cause overshoot and oscillation; in control theory dead time is a main cause.
- [structural-analogy] Black box / input–output pairs (Boulding) ↔ control-theory: black-box identification — behavior-level models fail when structure changes, like fitted input–output models.
- [structural-analogy] Shifting loop dominance through saturation ↔ control-theory: nonlinear controllers or actuators with saturation, where linear analysis holds only near an operating point.
- [metaphor] Foresight under long delays ↔ control-theory: feedforward or predictive control — acting before the problem is obvious.
- [metaphor] Giving actors better, timelier information ↔ control-theory: improving sensing or measurement in a loop.

## Questions this raises
- How can one tell from time-series data alone which structure produced it, given that different structures may yield similar behavior?
- How should one choose a boundary or a time horizon for a particular question, rather than by habit or discipline?
- When delays are long, what kind of "foresight" works in practice, and how does it avoid the nervous over-correction the chapter warns about?
- Which real systems are structured so that bounded rationality gives good outcomes, and what features make them so?
