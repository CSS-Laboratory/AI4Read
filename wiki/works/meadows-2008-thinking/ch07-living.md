# Living in a World of Systems

## L0 — One line
Systems thinking does not give prediction and control of complex systems; it teaches us to "dance" with them through a set of practices built on feedback, learning, humility and care.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Meadows asks what systems thinking is good for once we accept that self-organizing, nonlinear feedback systems cannot be controlled or exactly predicted. Her answer: they can still be understood in a general way, designed and redesigned, and learned from. She closes the book with a list of "systems wisdoms" under fifteen headings, such as: watch the system's behavior first, make your mental models visible, protect and distribute information, build feedback policies that learn, keep responsibility inside the system, stay humble, and widen your time horizon and circle of caring. She also says systems thinking by itself cannot bridge the gap between understanding and doing; that takes more than analysis.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a feedback loop (a stock, a goal, a correction); the book's earlier terms (nonlinear, self-organizing, delay, system trap, hierarchy, resilience, drift to low performance).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Meadows says it was a "terrible mistake" to assume that systems analysis is the key to prediction and control. Self-organizing, nonlinear feedback systems are not controllable and are understandable only in the most general way. (pp. 166–168)
- Understanding a system is different from changing it. The systems students did not follow their own advice. (pp. 166–167)
- Her alternative to control is "dancing": stay alert, pay attention, respond to feedback. Systems can be designed and redesigned, and surprises can be expected and learned from. (pp. 169–170)
- Several lessons concern information and models: study actual behavior first, expose mental models, distribute information, use language carefully, and do not neglect what cannot be quantified. (pp. 170–177)
- Policies for feedback systems should themselves be feedback policies. Especially under great uncertainty, the best ones also contain meta-feedback loops (loops that alter, correct and expand other loops), which build learning into management. (pp. 177–178)
- Meadows advises aiding the forces and structures that help a system run itself, and designing systems so that feedback about the consequences of decisions reaches the decision makers quickly and directly (intrinsic responsibility). (pp. 178–180)
- Stay a learner: act in small steps, monitor constantly, embrace error. (pp. 180–181)
- Widen time horizons, disciplines and the boundary of caring. Moral and practical rules turn out to be the same rules in an interconnected world. (pp. 182–184)
- Meadows says the goal of morality has been eroded by the *drift to low performance* trap (a goal sinks as expectations follow bad news downward). Systems thinking can only tell us what to do about it; it cannot do it, and by itself it cannot bridge the gap between understanding and implementation. It points beyond analysis to what the human spirit must do. (pp. 184–185)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: what systems thinking turned out to be (pp. 166–170)
**Epigraph (Chesterton):** the world is "nearly reasonable, but not quite"; it looks a little more mathematical and regular than it is. (p. 166)

**The mistake.** People raised in the industrial world, when they get enthusiastic about systems thinking, tend to assume it is the key to prediction and control. That is because the industrial mind-set assumes such a key exists. Meadows says she and her MIT fellow students assumed this too and exaggerated their findings, from hope rather than intent to deceive. She compares it to explorers seeking India who found the Western Hemisphere instead: systems thinking proved worth more than they thought, but not in the way they thought. (p. 166)

**Knowing is not doing.** Understanding how to fix a system is one thing; wading in and fixing it is quite another (pp. 166–167). The group discussed "implementation" as getting managers and mayors to follow their advice. They did not follow their own advice: they lectured on addiction and could not give up coffee, knew about eroding goals and let their jogging programs erode, and warned of escalation and shifting the burden yet created them in their marriages (p. 167). Social systems express cultural thinking patterns and human needs, emotions, strengths and weaknesses. Changing them is not simply a matter of announcing that everyone should change, or of trusting that whoever knows the good will do the good (p. 167).

**Insights that raise questions.** Systems insights did not explain everything; they raised at least as many questions as they answered, especially about the human mind and heart (p. 167). On p. 168 there are four boxes of "systems insights", and on p. 169 four boxes of questions. They match in content and order, so they are listed below as pairs 1–4. The question boxes pose questions and give no answers:
1. **Insight:** a new information feedback loop would make the system behave better, but decision makers resist the information (they ignore it, distrust it, or cannot interpret it). **Questions:** why do people sort and screen information as they do, and why do different people draw different conclusions from the same information? (pp. 168–169)
2. **Insight:** if the loop were oriented around real values (energy services, not energy; sufficiency and security, not GNP; progress, not growth), the system would give a result everyone wants. **Questions:** what are values, where do they come from, and how can a feedback loop be keyed to qualities that cannot be measured? (pp. 168–169)
3. **Insight:** a system can seem perverse on all counts, yet sweeping it away would leave no system, and she says nothing is more frightening than that. Meadows has the former Soviet system in mind, but says it is not the only example. **Questions:** why are periods of minimum structure and maximum freedom so frightening, how do systems create cultures and cultures create systems, and must a failed culture change through breakdown? (pp. 168–169)
4. **Insight:** people put up with harmful behavior because they fear change, distrust that a better system is possible, and feel powerless. **Questions:** why are people so easily convinced of their powerlessness, and why do they listen more to those who say they cannot change things? (pp. 168–169)

Other disciplines ask the same questions. What is unique, Meadows says, is that a tool born of engineering, mathematics, computers and a mechanistic quest for prediction and control leads its users, "inexorably I believe", to the deeply human mysteries. It makes clear, she says, that getting along in a world of complex systems takes more than technocracy (p. 167).

**Main conclusion.** Self-organizing, nonlinear feedback systems are "inherently unpredictable", not controllable, and understandable only in the most general way (p. 167). Perfect foresight is unrealizable. At best, a complex system can be made to do just what you want only for a while (p. 168). Science itself (quantum theory, chaos mathematics) leads to irreducible uncertainty. For any non-trivial objective we cannot optimize, and we do not even know what to optimize (p. 168). She adds that we cannot keep track of everything, and that we cannot find a sustainable relationship to nature, each other or our institutions while playing the role of omniscient conqueror. For those who stake their identity on that role, the uncertainty is hard to take: if you cannot understand, predict and control, what is there to do? (pp. 168–169)

But systems thinking also says, she writes, that there is plenty to do, of a different sort of "doing". The future cannot be predicted but can be envisioned. Systems cannot be controlled but can be designed and redesigned. We can expect surprises and learn from them, and we can listen to the system instead of imposing our will (pp. 169–170). The line to remember: "We can't control systems or figure them out. But we can dance with them!" (p. 170).

*Intuition note:* Meadows does not say systems thinking is useless. She says it changes the goal from controlling a system to working with it. Her images come from whitewater kayaking, gardening, music and skiing, which need close attention and a response to feedback (p. 170).

She describes dancing as requiring being wide awake, paying close attention, participating fully and responding to feedback. Living in a world of systems takes more than calculation: "our full humanity" (rationality, intuition, compassion, vision, morality) (p. 170). The rest of the chapter lists "systems wisdoms" the author has seen colleagues adopt. She says the list is probably incomplete and not unique to systems thinking (p. 170).

### Get the Beat of the System (pp. 170–172)
Before disturbing a system, watch how it behaves; learn its history, ask long-time participants, and use time graphs of real data, since memories are not always reliable about timing (pp. 170–171). Starting with behavior keeps attention on facts over theories. Her examples (p. 171):
- People say rainfall is decreasing, but the data show variability increasing: deeper droughts and bigger floods.
- She was told with great authority that milk prices were rising when they were falling, that real interest rates were falling when they were rising, and that the deficit was a higher fraction of GNP than ever when it was not.
- New Hampshire selectmen believe growth lowers taxes; plotting growth rates against tax rates gives a random scatter, with no discernible relationship.

Looking at behavior over time also prompts dynamic questions (how did we get here? where will we end up if we do not change direction?) and questions about strengths (what is working well?). It discourages defining a problem as the lack of our favorite solution. People tend to leap to solutions in a mode of predicting, controlling or imposing their will, without looking at what the system is doing (pp. 171–172).

### Expose Your Mental Models to the Light of Day (p. 172)
Drawing diagrams and writing equations forces assumptions into view, and models must be complete, consistent and add up. Mental models are "slippery". Diagrams are not required; words, lists or arrows also work. Making models visible helps one admit uncertainty, fix mistakes and stay flexible, which matters in a world of flexible systems. Everything anyone knows is "only a model". Meadows advises collecting many hypotheses, treating all as plausible until evidence rules one out, and inviting others to challenge assumptions. She calls this practising the scientific method, which she says is done too seldom even in science and hardly at all in social science, management, government or daily life (p. 172).

### Honor, Respect, and Distribute Information (p. 173)
Decision makers cannot respond to information they lack, to inaccurate information, or in time to late information. Meadows "would guess" most of what goes wrong in systems goes wrong because of biased, late or missing information. She would add an eleventh commandment: do not distort, delay or withhold information (p. 173).

**Example (Toxic Release Inventory).** A 1986 law required US companies to report each factory's hazardous air emissions, and the Freedom of Information Act made the data public. The first data appeared in July 1988, and newspapers published lists of the top ten local polluters. There were no lawsuits, required cuts, fines or penalties. Within two years reported emissions nationwide fell by 40 percent, and some companies set goals of 90 percent cuts (p. 173).

Those who control public information flow (media, public relations, politicians, advertisers) have more power than most people realize, and often filter it for short-term self-interest (p. 173).

*Intuition note (our reading):* In feedback terms, publishing the data supplied a loop with information it had lacked. The emitters now "saw" their own output through public reaction, and this alone changed behavior without any other rule.

### Use Language with Care and Enrich It with Systems Concepts (pp. 174–175)
Information streams and mental models are mostly language. Honoring information means avoiding "language pollution" and enlarging language to talk about complexity (p. 174).

- **Kofman's view:** language and information systems do not just describe reality; they structure members' perceptions and actions. His line: "we don't talk about what we see; we see only what we can talk about" (p. 174).
- **Meadows' examples (p. 174):** a society that talks of "productivity" but not "resilience" will become productive but not resilient. One that lacks "carrying capacity" will exceed it. One that treats "creating jobs" as only companies' work will not inspire others to create jobs.
- **Berry's view (as quoted):** "tyrannese" is language that refers to nothing in particular, uses abstractions and supports impersonal technological action. He also says it is his impression, and he believes, that for perhaps a hundred and fifty years language has been losing meaning, in parallel with the disintegration of persons and communities (pp. 174–175). Meadows lists "Peacekeeper" missile, "collateral damage", "Final Solution" and "ethnic cleansing" as examples (p. 174).

Two steps: keep language concrete, meaningful and truthful; then enlarge it to match enlarged understanding. Her analogy is that the Eskimos have many words for snow because they have learned to use it. Industrial society is "just beginning" to have words for systems; her spell-checker needed *feedback, throughput, overshoot, self-organization, sustainability* (p. 175).

### Pay Attention to What Is Important, Not Just What Is Quantifiable (pp. 175–177)
If we think what we can measure matters more than what we cannot, quantity becomes more important than quality. If quantity forms the goals of our feedback loops and the center of our attention, language and institutions, then quantity will be the result, she says (p. 176).

**Prejudice example.** Modelers have been ridiculed for including variables like "prejudice" or "self-esteem" and giving them made-up numerical scales. In one model for a firm that wanted to treat minority workers better, prejudice was scored on a scale (the scale was arbitrary: 1–5 or 0–100 would do). Everyone interviewed agreed there was a real link to performance. Workers drew a relationship between the two that was one of the most nonlinear Meadows has seen. Leaving prejudice out would have been "much more unscientific" than including it (p. 176).

Pretending something does not exist because it is hard to quantify leads to faulty models, and goals set on what is easy to measure create the system trap seen earlier. Meadows urges readers to be quality detectors and to say so if something is ugly or demeaning. No one can define or measure justice, democracy, freedom, truth or love, but if no one speaks for them and systems are not designed to produce them, she says they will cease to exist (pp. 176–177).

### Make Feedback Policies for Feedback Systems (pp. 177–178)
A self-adjusting system cannot be governed by a static, unbending policy; policies that change with the state of the system are easier, more effective and usually cheaper (p. 177).

- **Carter's gasoline tax:** a tax proportional to the fraction of US oil that was imported. If imports rose, the tax would rise until demand fell and substitutes appeared; if imports fell to zero, the tax fell to zero. It never passed (p. 177).
- **Carter on Mexican immigration:** he argued that nothing could be done while the opportunity gap existed, so money should go to building the Mexican economy until immigration stopped, rather than to border barriers. It never happened (p. 177).
- **Meta-feedback loops:** especially where there is great uncertainty, the best policies contain not only feedback loops but meta-feedback loops, which alter, correct and expand loops and so design learning into the management process (p. 177).
- **Montreal Protocol (1987):** it set targets for phasing out the most damaging chemicals, but required monitoring and reconvening an international congress to change the schedule if damage differed from expectations. In 1990 the schedule was hurried forward and more chemicals added because damage was "much greater" than foreseen. Meadows calls it a feedback policy structured for learning and adds that she hopes, along with everyone, that it worked in time (pp. 177–178).

*Intuition note:* A fixed rule is like a thermostat locked at one setting. A feedback policy reads the state of the system and adjusts. A meta-feedback policy also revises the adjusting rule when the evidence shows the rule was wrong.

### Go for the Good of the Whole (p. 178)
Hierarchies exist to serve the bottom layers, not the top. Do not maximize parts while ignoring the whole, nor go to great trouble to optimize something that should never be done at all (Boulding). Aim to enhance total properties such as growth, stability, diversity, resilience and sustainability, whether easily measured or not (p. 178).

### Listen to the Wisdom of the System (pp. 178–179)
Support the forces and structures that help the system run itself, many of which lie at the bottom of the hierarchy. **Example (Nathan Gray, Guatemala):** aid agencies came to "create jobs" and walked past a thriving local market full of self-made small businesses. Gray talked to the people there and concluded that what was needed was inside investors, not outside ones: small loans at reasonable interest, plus literacy and accounting classes. These, he judged, would do more long-term good than an imported factory (pp. 178–179).

### Locate Responsibility in the System (pp. 179–180)
For analysis, look at how the system creates its own behavior. Attend to triggering events, but sometimes blaming or trying to control outside influences blinds one to the easier task of increasing responsibility inside the system (p. 179). **Intrinsic responsibility** means the system sends feedback about the consequences of decisions directly, quickly and compellingly to the decision makers; a pilot riding in the front of the plane is an example (p. 179).

**Dartmouth thermostats.** Dartmouth removed thermostats from offices and classrooms and gave temperature decisions to a central computer to save energy. Meadows' observation was greater temperature oscillations. An overheated office needed a phone call across campus, corrections came after hours or days, and they often overcorrected, causing another call. She suggests letting professors control their own thermostats and charging them for the energy used ("privatizing a commons") (p. 179).

Her further examples of what designing for intrinsic responsibility could mean, or of responsibility lost (pp. 179–180):
- Intake pipes placed downstream of one's own outflow.
- Neither insurance companies nor public funds paying medical costs from smoking or from not wearing a helmet or seat belt.
- Congress not exempting itself from its own laws.
- Lost responsibility: rulers who declare war no longer being expected to lead troops into battle, and warfare by pushing a button at a distance.
- Hardin's suggestion: people who want to prevent others' abortions are not practicing intrinsic responsibility unless they are personally willing to bring up the resulting child.

Meadows concludes that our culture looks little for responsibility inside the system that generates an action (p. 180).

*Intuition note (our reading):* In control terms, the Dartmouth change put a long delay and a distant actor into the loop, and the result was overcorrection and oscillation. Moving the sensor and the decision close together shortens the loop.

### Stay Humble—Stay a Learner (pp. 180–181)
Systems thinking has taught Meadows to trust intuition more and "figuring-out rationality" less, and to lean on both while expecting surprises (p. 180). When you do not know, do not bluff and do not freeze; learn by experiment, by "trial and error, error, error" (Fuller). She says "stay the course" is only a good idea if you are sure you are on course. Pretending to be in control is a recipe for mistakes and for not learning from them. What is appropriate is small steps, constant monitoring and willingness to change course (p. 180).

This needs "error-embracing" (Don Michael), which takes courage. **Michael's view (quoted):** acting as if we knew what we were doing decreases credibility, and acknowledging uncertainty could help reverse the growing distrust of authority. A second quoted passage (note 9; the speaker is not named on this page) says error-embracing is the condition for learning, means seeking and sharing information about what went wrong, and brings out our personal and societal vulnerability (p. 181).

### Celebrate Complexity (pp. 181–182)
The universe is "messy": nonlinear, turbulent, dynamic, spending its time in transient behavior rather than neat equilibria, and self-organizing and evolving. Part of the human mind likes straight lines, whole numbers, uniformity and certainty. Another part, formed by complex feedback systems, recognizes that nature designs in fractals, and produces cathedrals, carpets, symphonies and novels (pp. 181–182). Some make a moral code of this. Meadows quotes Leopold's land ethic: a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic community (p. 182).

### Expand Time Horizons (pp. 182–183)
Meadows calls the interest rate (which led to payback periods and discount rates) one of the worst ideas humanity ever had, because these give a rational, quantitative excuse for ignoring the long term. Industrial society's horizon is the next election or the payback period. Families look further, and many Native American cultures considered the seventh generation. She states as her claim that the longer the operant time horizon, the better the chances for survival (p. 182). **Boulding's view (quoted):** he says there is a great deal of historical evidence to suggest that a society that loses its identity with posterity and its positive image of the future also loses its capacity to deal with present problems, and soon falls apart (p. 182).

In a strict systems sense, she says, there is no distinction between long term and short term. Phenomena at different time-scales are nested; present actions have immediate and decades-long effects; fast and slow processes couple and uncouple. Her image is a walker on an obstacle-strewn path who must watch both the next step and the far view (p. 183).

### Defy the Disciplines (p. 183)
Follow a system wherever it leads, across disciplinary lines. Learn from economists, chemists, psychologists and theologians without being limited by them, and discard the distortions of their narrow lenses. Seeing systems whole takes more than being "interdisciplinary" in the usual sense of putting people from different fields together to talk past each other. Interdisciplinary communication works only if there is a real problem to be solved and the participants care more about solving it than about being academically correct; they must go into learning mode and admit ignorance (p. 183).

### Expand the Boundary of Caring (p. 184)
Meadows says there are moral reasons to widen caring, and that if moral arguments are not sufficient, systems thinking supplies practical reasons: the real system is interconnected. Examples: your heart cannot succeed if your lungs fail, nor your company if your workers fail, nor the rich in Los Angeles if the poor fail, nor Europe if Africa fails, nor the global economy if the environment fails (p. 184). She adds that most people already know about the interconnections that make moral and practical rules the same rules; they only have to bring themselves to believe what they know (p. 184).

### Don't Erode the Goal of Goodness (pp. 184–185)
Meadows calls the erosion of the goal of morality by modern industrial culture the most damaging example of the archetype called *drift to low performance*. Bad behavior is magnified by the media as typical, while the far more numerous examples of goodness are "not news". Expectations drop, the gap between desired and actual behavior narrows, fewer actions affirm ideals, and cynicism fills public discourse (p. 184). **Krutch (quoted):** man is complacent about what he has but accepts a very low estimate of what he is; "poor in spirit" (p. 185). The remedy: do not weigh bad news more than good, and keep standards absolute. But systems thinking, she writes, can only tell us to do that; it cannot do it. By itself it cannot bridge the gap between understanding and implementation, but it can lead us to the edge of what analysis can do and then point beyond, to what can and must be done by the human spirit (p. 185).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrate a feedback policy versus a static policy, in the spirit of Carter's tax (p. 177). Toy model: $f$ is the import fraction, and each year imports rise by pressure $p = 0.04$ and fall by $0.01$ per percentage point of tax $t$:
$$f_{n+1} = f_n + 0.04 - 0.01\,t_n.$$

- **Static policy, $t = 2$:** $f_{n+1} = f_n + 0.02$. From $f_0 = 0.30$: 0.32, 0.34, 0.36, and imports keep rising each year (until the fraction reaches 1).
- **Feedback policy, $t = 20 f$:** then $0.01\,t = 0.2 f$, so $f_{n+1} = 0.8 f_n + 0.04$. From 0.30: $0.8 \times 0.30 + 0.04 = 0.28$; $0.8 \times 0.28 + 0.04 = 0.264$; $0.8 \times 0.264 + 0.04 = 0.2512$. It settles where $f = 0.8 f + 0.04$, i.e. $f = 0.2$, with $t = 20 \times 0.2 = 4$.
- **Pressure rises to 0.06:** the feedback rule gives $f_{n+1} = 0.8 f_n + 0.06$. From 0.2: 0.22, then $0.8 \times 0.22 + 0.06 = 0.236$. It heads toward $f = 0.3$ ($t = 6$) with no one changing the rule. A static $t = 4$ (right for the old pressure) would now let $f$ grow by $0.02$ per year.
- **If $f$ reached 0,** the feedback tax would be $20 \times 0 = 0$, as in Carter's design.

## Concepts
- [[feedback]] — information about results used to adjust later decisions; with nonlinearity and self-responsible systems, one of the three ideas her worldview rests on (p. 170)
- [[open loop vs closed loop]] — a static, unbending policy versus a policy that changes with the system's state (p. 177)
- [[negative (balancing) feedback]] — the gasoline tax rises as imports rise and falls as they fall, pushing imports back down (p. 177)
- [[delay (dead time)]] — slow central thermostat corrections led to overcorrection and oscillation (p. 179)
- [[meta-feedback-loop]] (new) — a loop that alters, corrects and expands other loops; builds learning into policy (p. 177)
- [[intrinsic-responsibility]] (new) — the system feeds the consequences of decisions quickly and directly to the decision makers (p. 179)
- [[mental-model]] (new) — a person's assumptions about how a system works; slippery, and "only a model" (p. 172)
- [[drift-to-low-performance]] (new) — a trap where a goal erodes as expectations follow bad news downward (p. 184)
- [[information-delay-bias]] (new) — biased, late or missing information makes feedback loops malfunction (p. 173)

## Canonical instances
- [[toxic-release-inventory]] — publishing emission data alone cut reported emissions 40% in two years (p. 173)
- [[carter-gasoline-tax]] — a feedback policy: the tax tracks the import fraction, and falls to zero if imports do (p. 177)
- [[montreal-protocol]] — a policy structured for learning, with monitoring and a revisable schedule (pp. 177–178)
- [[dartmouth-thermostats]] — removing local control raised oscillation in room temperature (p. 179)
- [[prejudice-performance-model]] — a qualitative variable put on an arbitrary scale gave a strongly nonlinear relationship (p. 176)
- [[guatemala-market]] — an existing local market that outside aid agencies overlooked (pp. 178–179)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Meta-feedback loops (policies that revise their own rules) ↔ control-theory: adaptive control, where the controller's parameters are tuned from observed performance. Both adjust the rule, not only the action.
- [structural-analogy] Static policy versus feedback policy (p. 177) ↔ control-theory: open-loop versus closed-loop control.
- [structural-analogy] Dartmouth central thermostat (p. 179) ↔ control-theory: delay in a loop causing overshoot and oscillation.
- [metaphor] "Dancing with systems" ↔ control-theory: continuous sensing and small corrections instead of a fixed plan.

## Questions this raises
- How can a feedback policy be kept simple enough to explain to the public and still be passed, given that Carter's two feedback proposals failed (p. 177)?
- When does making information public change behavior (as in the emissions case) and when does it not? The text gives one example only.
- How would we test, with data, the claim that most system failures come from biased, late or missing information (p. 173)? Meadows frames it as a guess.
- If we do not even know what to optimize (p. 168), how should one choose which total-system properties to enhance?
