# Two Dogmas of Empiricism

## L0 — One line
Quine argues that the analytic/synthetic distinction and the reductionist idea that single statements have their own experiential content are ill-founded dogmas, and proposes that only science as a whole faces experience.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Quine examines two assumptions of modern empiricism. The first is a sharp line between analytic truths (true by meaning) and synthetic truths (true by fact). The second is reductionism: each meaningful statement is equivalent to one about immediate experience. He argues that each attempt he examines to explain analyticity (through meaning, definition, synonymy, interchangeability or semantical rules) leans on notions no clearer than analyticity itself. Radical reductionism has long left Carnap's philosophy, but a subtler form survives in the belief that single statements can be confirmed on their own. His alternative: statements face experience together, as one system, and any statement can be revised. The supposed boundary between metaphysics and natural science blurs, and pragmatism is carried further.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a logically true sentence; truth-functional connectives ("if and only if"); the difference between a name and what it names; basic idea of a scientific hypothesis and of testing it against observation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Two dogmas: a cleavage between analytic and synthetic truths, and reductionism. Quine argues both are ill founded, and that they are at root identical (pp. 20, 38).
- Meaning is not reference. Once the two are separated, meanings as entities may well be abandoned, and the business of a theory of meaning becomes the synonymy (likeness of meaning) of linguistic forms and the analyticity of statements (pp. 21–23).
- Logical truths are the first class of analytic statements. The major difficulty lies in the second class, which depends on synonymy, a notion no less in need of clarification than analyticity (pp. 23–24).
- Definition, interchangeability (swapping terms without change of truth value, *salva veritate*) and semantical rules (rules of an artificial language) each either presuppose synonymy or analyticity, or fail to capture it, apart from explicitly conventional new notations. Quine calls the interchangeability argument "not flatly circular, but something like it" (pp. 24–34).
- If the verification theory (meaning = method of confirmation) gave an adequate account of statement synonymy, analyticity would be saved. Radical reductionism (every meaningful statement translatable into sense-datum terms, i.e. terms for immediate sensory experiences) is the most naive view of that method. Carnap's *Aufbau* pursued it, and Quine says its treatment of physical objects fell short in principle (pp. 35–38).
- Quine's countersuggestion is that our statements about the external world face experience only together, as a corporate body, not one by one. He suggests it is nonsense to split a single statement's truth into a linguistic and a factual component (pp. 38–39).
- If Quine's view is right, any statement can be held true come what may, given drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system, and no statement is immune to revision (p. 40). Quine says the difference between questions that seem more of fact and those that seem more of conceptual scheme is one of degree (p. 43).
- Physical objects, atoms, forces and classes are posits that simplify our treatment of experience. Under this view, ontology is on a par with science, and Quine's pragmatism extends over the analytic/synthetic boundary (pp. 41–43).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 20)
Quine names two dogmas of modern empiricism.
1. A fundamental cleavage between **analytic** truths (grounded in meanings, independent of fact) and **synthetic** truths (grounded in fact).
2. **Reductionism**: each meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms referring to immediate experience.

He argues both are ill founded. Effects of abandoning them: a blurring of the boundary between speculative metaphysics and natural science, and a shift toward pragmatism (p. 20).

### I. Background for analyticity (pp. 20–24)
- **History.** Kant's cleavage was foreshadowed by Hume (relations of ideas vs. matters of fact) and Leibniz (truths of reason vs. truths of fact; he spoke of truths of reason as true in every possible world). Defining analytic statements as those whose denials are self-contradictory has small explanatory value. Self-contradictoriness needs as much clarification as analyticity, so the two are "two sides of a single dubious coin" (p. 20).
- **Kant's version.** An analytic statement adds nothing to what is conceptually contained in its subject. This covers only subject-predicate statements, and containment is metaphor. Restated by Quine as Kant's intent: a statement is analytic when true by virtue of meanings and independently of fact (p. 21).
- **Meaning is not naming.** 'Evening Star' and 'Morning Star' name Venus, but the identity is established by astronomical observation. If the meanings were alike, the identity would be analytic. Parallel cases: 'Scott' and 'the author of Waverley'; '9' and 'the number of planets' (p. 21).
- **Meaning is not extension.** The extension of a general term is the class of things it is true of. 'Creature with a heart' and 'creature with a kidney' are perhaps alike in extension but unlike in meaning (pp. 21–22).
- **Essence vs. meaning.** Aristotle's essence belonged to things. Meaning is what essence becomes "when it is divorced from the object of reference and wedded to the word". Only linguistic forms have meanings (p. 22).
- **Meanings as entities.** Meanings are supposed to be mental or Platonic ideas (abstract objects existing independently of minds), which are so elusive that there seems little hope of a fruitful science about them. It is unclear when two forms have the same meaning. Once meaning is separated from reference, the business of a theory of meaning is just the synonymy of linguistic forms and the analyticity of statements. Meanings as intermediate entities may be abandoned (pp. 22–23).
- **Two classes of analytic statements** (p. 23):
  - (1) *No unmarried man is married*: a *logical truth*, true under all reinterpretations of its components other than the logical particles.
  - (2) *No bachelor is married*: it becomes a logical truth by putting synonyms for synonyms. This class still needs "synonymy", which is no clearer than analyticity.
- **Carnap's state-descriptions.** A state-description is an exhaustive assignment of truth values to atomic statements. Carnap then explains a statement as analytic when it is true under every state-description. This works only if the atomic statements are mutually independent. Otherwise one state-description would make 'John is a bachelor' true and 'John is married' false, and 'All bachelors are married' would count as synthetic. So the criterion is at best a reconstruction of logical truth, and the major difficulty lies in the synonymy class (p. 24). Quine says Carnap's model is aimed mainly at probability and induction (p. 24).

*Intuition note.* "True by meaning" sounds clear until you ask what a meaning is. Quine drops meanings as objects and is left with two questions: when are two expressions synonymous, and when is a statement analytic?

### II. Definition (pp. 24–27)
- **Lexicographer's definition.** It reports a synonymy already present in usage. It cannot be the ground of synonymy (p. 24). A scientist's or philosopher's "definition" is ordinarily the same kind of report (p. 25).
- **Explication (Carnap).** It aims to improve the definiendum (the expression being defined) by refining its meaning. It still rests on pre-existing synonymies between the favoured contexts of the definiendum and the corresponding contexts of the definiens (the defining expression). Two non-synonymous definientia may suit equally well. Choosing one creates, by fiat, a new synonymy (p. 25).
- **Extreme case.** Explicitly conventional new notations for abbreviation create synonymy by definition, which Quine calls a really transparent case. For the rest, definition rests on synonymy rather than explaining it (p. 26).
- **Formal systems.** Two economies compete: brevity of expression, and minimal basic vocabulary. The usual solution is two languages, one part of the other, correlated by translation rules (the "definitions") (pp. 26–27). Each definition may be expected to be a faithful paraphrase, an explication, or a new notation (p. 27).
- **Conclusion.** Definition, except in the conventional-notation case, hinges on prior synonymy. It does not hold the key to synonymy and analyticity (p. 27).

### III. Interchangeability (pp. 27–31)
- **Proposal.** Synonymy is interchangeability in all contexts without change of truth value (*salva veritate*, Leibniz's phrase). The matching of vagueness is allowed (p. 27).
- **Counterinstances.** 'Bachelor of arts', 'bachelor's buttons' and the quotation *'Bachelor' has less than ten letters*. These can perhaps be set aside by treating such phrases as indivisible words and ignoring occurrences inside words. This reduces synonymy to wordhood, itself a difficulty (pp. 27–28).
- **Cognitive synonymy.** It is not identity of associations or poetic quality. Assuming analyticity, 'bachelor' and 'unmarried man' are cognitively synonymous iff statement (3), *All and only bachelors are unmarried men*, is analytic. Quine wants an account of cognitive synonymy that does not presuppose analyticity (p. 28).
- **The "hocus-pocus" argument.** (4) *Necessarily all and only bachelors are bachelors* is evidently true, even if 'necessarily' is read so narrowly that it applies truly only to analytic statements. If 'bachelor' and 'unmarried man' are interchangeable *salva veritate*, then (5) *Necessarily, all and only bachelors are unmarried men* is also true. That means (3) is analytic (p. 29). This presupposes a language with 'necessarily', true exactly of analytic statements. Such a language is intelligible only if 'analytic' already makes sense. The argument is "not flatly circular, but something like it" (p. 29).
- **Extensional language.** Take a language with predicates, truth functions (connectives like 'not', 'and'), quantification ('all', 'some') and contextually definable singular terms (names definable through the sentences they occur in). It can be adequate to classical mathematics and scientific discourse, except for devices like modal adverbs and contrary-to-fact conditionals. Any two predicates that agree in extension are interchangeable *salva veritate*. So interchangeability there guarantees only that (3) is true, not that it is analytic (pp. 29–30).
- **Conclusion.** In an extensional language, interchangeability is not a sufficient condition of the needed cognitive synonymy. In a language with 'necessarily' it is sufficient, but that language is intelligible only if analyticity is already understood (p. 30).
- **Reversal.** Perhaps explain analyticity first and derive synonymy from it. Singular terms are synonymous when their identity statement is analytic. Statements are synonymous when their biconditional ('p if and only if q') is analytic (p. 31).

### IV. Semantical rules (pp. 31–34)
- **Quine's own case.** He does not know whether 'Everything green is extended' is analytic. His indecision reflects the trouble with 'analytic', not an incomplete grasp of 'green' or 'extended' (p. 31).
- **Target.** It is often hinted that artificial languages with explicit semantical rules make the distinction clear. Quine calls this a confusion. Analyticity is a relation 'S is analytic for L' with variable 'S' and 'L'. The problem is no less stubborn for artificial languages (pp. 31–32).
- **Rule form 1.** A rule lists the analytic statements of $L_0$. It uses 'analytic' without explaining it. It defines only 'analytic-for-$L_0$' (or 'K'), not 'analytic' or 'analytic for' (p. 32).
- **Rule form 2.** A rule of truth says certain statements are among the truths. Then 'analytic' means true according to the semantical rules. But then 'semantical rule' is the unexplained phrase. Not every true statement that says some statements are true counts as one (p. 33).
- **Ordered pairs.** Taking a language to be a pair (language, semantical rules) is no better than taking it to be a pair (language, class of analytic statements). Quine adds that we might just "stop tugging at our bootstraps" altogether (p. 33).
- **Translation rules.** Where the rules translate into ordinary language, analyticity is just inherited from the ordinary language (p. 34).
- **Verdict.** Artificial languages with semantical rules are a *feu follet* (will-o'-the-wisp) for the problem. Their rules are of interest only if analyticity is already understood. A model that takes analyticity as an irreducible character is unlikely to throw light on explicating it (p. 34).
- **Source of the dogma.** Truth depends on language and on extralinguistic fact ('Brutus killed Caesar' would also be false if 'killed' meant 'begat'). It then seems that truth splits into a linguistic and a factual component, and for analytic statements the factual one is null. But Quine says a boundary has simply not been drawn. Quine calls its existence an "unempirical dogma of empiricists, a metaphysical article of faith" (p. 34).

*Intuition note.* Each explanation of analyticity that Quine examines ends up leaning on another notion (synonymy, necessity, semantical rule) that is no clearer than analyticity or needs it to be understood. Quine calls the interchangeability argument "not flatly circular, but something like it" (p. 29), and says the trouble lies in 'analytic' itself, not in the words of an example (p. 31).

### V. The verification theory and reductionism (pp. 34–39)
- **Verification theory.** The meaning of a statement is its method of empirical confirmation or infirmation. An analytic statement is the limiting case that is confirmed no matter what (p. 35). Equivalently, statements are synonymous iff alike in method of confirmation or infirmation. A statement is analytic when synonymous with a logically true statement. If this is adequate, "analyticity is saved after all" (p. 35). Quine asks what these methods are, and how a statement relates to experience (pp. 35–36).
- **Radical reductionism.** Every meaningful statement is translatable into a statement about immediate experience. Its forebears: Locke and Hume (every idea originates in sense experience or is compounded from such ideas), restated via Tooke as a term-by-term doctrine. Quine calls the term-by-term demand unnecessarily and intolerably restrictive. More reasonable, within radical reductionism, is statement-by-statement translation (p. 36). That needed two developments: the emphasis on verification, and Russell's incomplete symbols defined in use (expressions given meaning only within whole sentences) (p. 36).
- **Carnap's Aufbau.** Carnap's starting language included logic up to higher set theory, so it was not a minimal sense-datum language. Its extralogical, sensory part is very parsimonious. He was the first empiricist to take serious steps toward carrying out the reduction (p. 37). For physical objects, spatiotemporal point-instants are quadruples of real numbers. Qualities are assigned so as to give "the laziest world" compatible with experience. Quine thinks this is a good schematization, deliberately oversimplified, of what science does. But his treatment fell short of reduction not merely through sketchiness but in principle: it gives no way to translate 'Quality $q$ is at $x;y;z;t$' into the sense-datum language: 'is at' stays an undefined connective (pp. 37–38). Carnap later abandoned translatability (p. 38).
- **Surviving form of reductionism.** The notion lingers that each statement has a unique range of sensory events that would confirm it, and another that would disconfirm it. Quine's countersuggestion, issuing essentially from Carnap's doctrine of the physical world in the *Aufbau*: our statements about the external world face the "tribunal of sense experience" not individually but "only as a corporate body" (p. 38).
- **The dogmas are linked.** If it is significant to speak of confirmation of any statement, it seems significant to speak of one confirmed come what may, which is analytic (p. 38). Quine says the two dogmas are "at root identical". For an empiricist, the factual component of a statement's truth must boil down to a range of confirming experiences, and the analytic case is the one where only the linguistic component matters (pp. 38–39).
- **Conclusion.** Quine suggests that it is nonsense, and the root of much nonsense, to speak of a linguistic and a factual component in the truth of an *individual* statement. Science as a whole depends on language and experience, but this duality is not significantly traceable into statements one by one. Statement-sized units are still "too finely" drawn: "The unit of empirical significance is the whole of science" (p. 39).

*Intuition note.* A sentence like *there are brick houses on Elm Street* is felt to have a sharper empirical reference than highly theoretical statements (p. 41). Yet Quine holds that a clashing experience can be accommodated by revising other statements instead (p. 40). So not even this sentence faces experience entirely on its own, and Quine thinks it folly to look for a boundary between statements that depend on experience and statements held come what may (p. 40).

### VI. Empiricism without the dogmas (pp. 39–43)
- **The picture.** All beliefs, from geography to atomic physics, mathematics and logic, form a man-made fabric touching experience only at the edges, like a field of force with experience as boundary conditions. A conflict at the periphery forces re-evaluation inside, and logical laws are themselves elements of the field. The field is so undetermined by experience that there is much latitude in what to revise. Particular experiences are linked to particular interior statements only indirectly (pp. 39–40).
- **Consequences, if this view is right.** Speaking of the empirical content of an individual statement is misleading (especially for statements remote from the periphery), and seeking a boundary between synthetic statements and statements held come what may is folly. Any statement can be held true if drastic enough adjustments are made elsewhere. No statement is immune to revision, not even the law of excluded middle (proposed for revision in quantum mechanics). Quine asks what difference in principle there is between that and Kepler/Ptolemy, Einstein/Newton or Darwin/Aristotle (p. 40).
- **"Periphery" without metaphor.** Statements "germane" to particular experiences lie near the edge. This is only a loose association reflecting how likely we would be to revise one statement rather than another. Examples: brick houses on Elm Street, or the absence of centaurs. Our tendency to disturb the system as little as possible focuses revision on these. Highly theoretical statements are relatively "central", meaning merely that little preferential connection with any particular sense data obtrudes (pp. 40–41).
- **Posits.** As an empiricist, Quine takes the conceptual scheme as a tool for predicting future experience. Physical objects are irreducible posits, comparable epistemologically to Homer's gods. He, as a lay physicist, believes in physical objects and not in Homer's gods, and considers it a scientific error to believe otherwise. In point of epistemological footing they differ only in degree and not in kind. Quine judges the myth of physical objects epistemologically superior to most, because it has worked better than other myths for building a manageable structure into the flux of experience (p. 41).
- **Analogy.** Start with the rational numbers. Adding mythical irrational numbers simplifies the algebra, and we can get from one law about rationals to another faster. Likewise experience is analogous to the rationals and physical objects to the irrationals. Objects are no more reducible to experience than irrationals to rationals, but they ease passage between statements about experience (pp. 41–42). Quine thinks there are just two salient differences: the simplification is more overwhelming for physical objects, and positing them is far more archaic, coeval (he expects) with language, since language is social (p. 42).
- **Further posits.** Atomic and subatomic objects, forces and abstract entities (classes) are posits in the same spirit. Quine says these are myths on the same footing as physical objects and gods, neither better nor worse except in how far they expedite dealings with sense experience. Total science is similarly but more extremely underdetermined by experience than rational-number algebra by its own base. The edge of the system must be kept squared with experience; the rest has as its objective the simplicity of laws (p. 42).
- **Ontology and Carnap.** Under this view, ontological questions are on a par with scientific ones. Whether to admit classes is whether to quantify over class-valued variables. Carnap calls this a choice of convenient framework. Quine agrees only on the proviso that the same be conceded for scientific hypotheses generally. Carnap has recognized that he can keep a double standard only by assuming an absolute analytic/synthetic distinction, which Quine rejects (p. 43).
- **Degree, not kind.** Classes seem more a question of conceptual scheme, and centaurs more a question of fact. The difference is one of degree. It turns on our "vaguely pragmatic inclination" to adjust one strand rather than another. Conservatism and simplicity both figure (p. 43).
- **Ending.** Carnap and Lewis are pragmatic about language forms but stop at the analytic/synthetic boundary. Quine espouses a "more thorough pragmatism". Where rational, the considerations guiding us in fitting our scientific heritage to sensory prompting are pragmatic (p. 43).

## Toy example
*(Our own, not from the text.)* Take three statements.
- A: All swans are white.
- B: The bird on the pond is a swan.
- C: The bird on the pond is not white (what we seem to see).

A and B together imply "the bird is white", which contradicts C. So {A, B, C} cannot all be kept. Rejecting one statement at a time:
- Drop A: B and C stay consistent (a non-white swan).
- Drop B: A and C stay consistent (not a swan).
- Drop C: A and B stay consistent (we misperceived; a "hallucination" option, p. 40).

That gives 3 single-statement ways out, so the experience alone does not say which to revise. A is not tested alone, because by itself it says nothing about this bird. Choice among the options goes by conservatism and simplicity (p. 43), for example whichever disturbs the rest of our beliefs least. Quine's further claim is that even logic could be revised (p. 40), which would add a fourth way out. In this mini-system no statement has "its own" experiential content, which is the holism of Sections V–VI.

## Questions this raises
- If no statement is immune to revision, what separates logic or mathematics from ordinary empirical claims, beyond "degree"? Do any pragmatic considerations remain that make revision of logic costlier?
- Quine rejects a sharp analytic/synthetic line but keeps degrees of "germaneness" to experience. Is a graded distinction enough to do the work analyticity did?
- Is the circle in Sections III–IV a refutation, or only a failure so far to find a non-circular account?
- Does the pragmatic rule (conservatism, simplicity) give enough guidance in choosing which strand of the system to revise?
