# More Is Different (P. W. Anderson, 1972)

## L0 — One line
Reductionism (everything obeys the same fundamental laws) does not imply constructionism (rebuilding the universe from those laws), because scale and complexity bring entirely new properties at each level, which need research as fundamental as any other; Anderson suggests that broken symmetry helps to explain this.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Anderson opposes the apparent corollary that, if all matter obeys the same fundamental laws, only the people studying those laws do fundamental science. The reductionist hypothesis does not imply a constructionist one: scale and complexity bring entirely new properties. His main tool is "broken symmetry": a large system can settle in a state with less symmetry than the laws that govern it. Ammonia, sugar, crystals, nuclei and superconductors illustrate this. Mathematically sharp transitions belong to the limit of infinitely many particles. He speculates that life and higher levels continue this hierarchy. Each science is therefore not "just applied" the one below it. The relation of whole to parts is "a one-way street": analysis is possible, synthesis rarely.

**Prerequisites:** basic quantum mechanics ideas (stationary state, superposition, tunnelling), the idea of symmetry, and what a crystal is.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Reductionism does not imply constructionism: knowing the simple laws does not give the ability to rebuild the universe from them (pdf 1).
- At each level of complexity, entirely new laws and concepts are needed; psychology is not applied biology, nor biology applied chemistry (pdf 1).
- A stationary state (one that does not change in time) of a system must have the same symmetry as the laws of motion; this is why ammonia has no net electric dipole moment (an overall charge separation) in a stationary state (pdf 2).
- Small molecules such as ammonia invert, so their stationary states keep the symmetry. In bigger systems (sugar molecules, crystals) inversion no longer happens in practice, so the system stays in a less symmetric state. This is "broken symmetry": the laws keep their symmetry, but the state does not (pdf 2).
- Mathematically sharp symmetry breaking belongs to the $N\to\infty$ limit (the limit of infinitely many particles). A nucleus has a rigorously definable shape only in that limit, so deriving its shape from fundamental laws would be extremely difficult (pdf 3).
- In the $N\to\infty$ limit, broken symmetry leaves behind characteristic behaviour: sound waves, superconducting conduction and the rigidity of crystals. Anderson says this is breaking, not a real violation, of the symmetry of space and time (pdf 3).
- Anderson speculates on a ladder: crystals, then information-bearing regularity (DNA), ordering in time, functional structure (which he places possibly between crystallinity and information strings) and finally hierarchy or specialisation of function. At some point "decreasing symmetry" becomes "increasing complication" (pdf 3–4).
- The system–parts relation is a one-way street. Anderson says synthesis is expected to be "all but impossible", while analysis may be fruitful (the Josephson effect, a macroscopic quantum-interference effect in superconductors). He closes with the Fitzgerald–Hemingway exchange, "the rich are different" (pdf 4).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: reductionism vs. constructionism (pdf 1).**
- Anderson thinks most active scientists accept the reductionist hypothesis: all matter is controlled by the same fundamental laws (pdf 1).
- An apparent corollary is that only those studying the fundamental laws do truly fundamental work. Anderson wants to oppose this view (pdf 1).
- **Weisskopf's view (quoted):** research is "intensive" (seeking fundamental laws) or "extensive" (explaining phenomena with known laws). Anderson says he heard this passage quoted by a leader in materials science, who urged participants at a condensed-matter meeting to accept that there were "few or no" fundamental problems left and that nothing remained but extensive science, which the speaker seemed to equate with device engineering (pdf 1).
- **Anderson's reply:** the main fallacy in this thinking is to forget that the reductionist hypothesis does not by any means imply a constructionist one. The more particle physicists say about the fundamental laws, the less relevant these seem to the rest of science (pdf 1).
- The constructionist hypothesis breaks down under scale and complexity. Large aggregates are not a simple extrapolation of a few particles. At each level new properties appear, and understanding them is research "as fundamental in its nature as any other" (pdf 1).
- Sciences can be arrayed roughly linearly. The elementary entities of science X obey the laws of science Y (e.g. chemistry ← many-body physics ← elementary particle physics; psychology ← physiology; social sciences ← psychology). But X is not "just applied Y" (pdf 1).
- Anderson says his own field, many-body physics, is perhaps closer to its fundamental underpinnings than any other science in which nontrivial complexities occur. The theory of "broken symmetry" may clarify how quantitative change becomes qualitative change (pdf 1).

*Intuition note:* Knowing the rules of the parts does not tell you how to build the whole. Every new level needs its own ideas.

**Two clarifications (pdf 2).** (1) He does not mean that new scales obey different fundamental laws (general relativity for cosmology, quantum mechanics for atoms). All ordinary matter obeys electrodynamics and quantum theory. (2) Particle physicists' "broken symmetry" is, in his view, only an analogy, "whether a deep or a specious one remaining to be understood" (pdf 2).

**The ammonia example (pdf 2).**
- Chemists picture NH₃ as a triangular pyramid with a dipole moment (the electric charge separation of N⁻ and H⁺). A professor's theorem said nothing has a dipole moment. It is correct because it concerns only *stationary states*, which do not change in time (pdf 2).
- By quantum tunnelling the nitrogen leaks through the hydrogen triangle and turns the pyramid inside out ("inversion"), about $3\times10^{10}$ times per second. A truly stationary state is an equal superposition of the pyramid and its inverse, which has no dipole moment (pdf 2).
- **Result:** a stationary state must have the same symmetry as the laws of motion. Reason: in quantum mechanics there is always a way to go from one state to another, unless symmetry forbids it. Only a symmetric sum of all the unsymmetrical states is stationary. The symmetry here is parity (left–right equivalence). Observed parity violations are too weak to matter for ordinary matter (pdf 2).
- Heavier molecules: PH₃ (twice as heavy) inverts at one-tenth of the ammonia frequency. PF₃ is not observed to invert at a measurable rate, though theory says it would in reasonable time (pdf 2).
- Sugar (about 40 atoms): inversion makes no sense. Sugar made by living organisms is spiral in one sense and never inverts, by tunnelling or thermal agitation. Made by a chemical reaction near thermal equilibrium, left and right forms are equal on average. Living matter was needed to produce an actual asymmetry in populations (pdf 2).
- Really large inanimate aggregates can have a net dipole moment or optical rotating power. Many crystals have a dipole moment per unit cell (pyroelectricity), and in some it can be reversed by an electric field (ferroelectricity). The opposite state has the same energy, but the system is so large that no conversion occurs in a finite time compared to the age of the universe (pdf 2).

**Three inferences (pdf 2–3).**
1. Symmetry matters greatly. Symmetry means different viewpoints from which the system looks the same. Anderson says it is "only slightly overstating" to call physics the study of symmetry (pdf 2). He suggests the first demonstration of the power of this idea may have been Newton's question whether space and matter are homogeneous and isotropic (pdf 2).
2. The internal structure of matter need not be symmetric even if the total state is. Anderson challenges the reader to predict ammonia inversion from the fundamental laws without going through the stage of using the unsymmetrical pyramid, even though no "state" ever has that structure (pdf 2). Similarly, a nucleus never has a dipole moment but can be football- or plate-shaped. This was realised only decades ago. It was an inspiration, needed no new fundamental law, and would have been very hard to derive from them (pdf 2–3).
3. A really big system need not have the symmetry of its laws; it usually has less. Example: the crystal is less symmetric than perfect homogeneity of space (pdf 3).

**Why nuclear shape is hard to derive (pdf 3).** For three, four or ten particles, no "plate" or "football" is rigorously defined. Rigour comes only in the $N\to\infty$ limit. From fundamental laws and a computer one would need to solve an infinite-body problem and then apply the result to a finite system, "two impossible things" (pdf 3).

*Intuition note:* A nucleus's shape is a property of the whole, rigorously definable only in the limit of infinitely many particles. A small system such as a nucleus is merely approaching macroscopic behaviour (pdf 3).

**Broken symmetry in macroscopic bodies (pdf 3).**
- The regularity of crystals could be deduced semiempirically in the mid-19th century without complicated reasoning. For superconductivity, the new symmetry may be unexpected and hard to visualise. Thirty years passed between having every fundamental law needed and explaining it (pdf 3).
- In the $N\to\infty$ limit, matter undergoes mathematically sharp, singular phase transitions. Microscopic symmetries and even the microscopic equations of motion are "in a sense violated". Symmetry survives only as characteristic behaviour: long-wavelength vibrations (sound), superconductor conduction, and the rigidity of crystal lattices (pdf 3).
- The system does not truly violate the symmetry of space and time; it breaks it. Its parts find it energetically better to keep fixed relationships. So only the body as a whole responds to external forces. This "rigidity" also describes superconductivity and superfluidity (pdf 3).
- Open questions remain, e.g. glasses and amorphous phases. But this type of broken symmetry is now understood in principle. The whole becomes "not only more than but very different from the sum of its parts" (pdf 3).

**Beyond simple phase transitions (pdf 3).**
- Liquids, gases and glasses are, "in any real sense", more symmetric. The next stage is a regular system that carries information: DNA, a line of type, a film. This "information-bearing crystallinity" seems essential to life. Whether life needs further symmetry breaking is "by no means clear" (pdf 3).
- Another feature, universal or remarkably common in living things, is ordering in time. It has at least two roles: extracting energy through time-periodic machines (oscillators, generators), and handling information (speech; all computers use temporal pulsing). A possible third role, suggested by some theories, is using phase relationships of pulses to control the growth of cells and organisms (pdf 3–4).

**Speculative ladder and one-way street (pdf 4).**
- Anderson says structure in the functional (teleological) sense must also be considered a stage in the hierarchy of broken symmetries, possibly intermediate between crystallinity and information strings. The next stages could be hierarchy or specialisation of function. At some point we stop talking about decreasing symmetry and speak of increasing complication. Each stage has fundamental questions about combining simpler pieces (pdf 4).
- There may well be no useful parallel between simple many-body cases and truly complex cultural or biological ones, except perhaps the following. In general, system–parts is "a one-way street": synthesis is expected to be "all but impossible", whereas analysis may be fruitful (pdf 4).
- Example: without understanding broken symmetry in superconductivity, Josephson would probably not have discovered his effect. It is a macroscopic quantum-interference phenomenon. Anderson says it has extended measurement accuracy and may lead to major technological achievements (pdf 4).
- Biology has a new aspect from reducing genetics to biochemistry and biophysics (pdf 4).
- **A recent article (ref. 7, listed as A. B. Pippard's book):** we should each "cultivate our own valley". Anderson says that is not true: roads between sciences are often the quickest shortcut, but are not visible from one science alone (pdf 4).
- He says the "arrogance" of the particle physicist (the positron discoverer said "the rest is chemistry") may be behind us, but that we have yet to recover from that of some molecular biologists, who seem determined to reduce everything about the human organism to "only" chemistry. Surely, he says, there are more levels between human ethology and DNA than between DNA and quantum electrodynamics, and each level can require a whole new conceptual structure (pdf 4).
- **Closing:** Marx said quantitative differences become qualitative. Anderson prefers the Fitzgerald–Hemingway exchange: "The rich are different from us." / "Yes, they have more money." (pdf 4).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A two-state model of "inversion". Let $|L\rangle$ and $|R\rangle$ be orthonormal states with dipole $-d$ and $+d$. Take $H=-t\,(|L\rangle\langle R|+|R\rangle\langle L|)$, with eigenstates $S=(L+R)/\sqrt2$ (energy $-t$) and $A=(L-R)/\sqrt2$ (energy $+t$).
- Dipole in $S$: $\tfrac12(-d)+\tfrac12(+d)=0$. Same for $A$. So stationary states have no dipole, as in Anderson's theorem.
- Start in $L$: $L=(S+A)/\sqrt2$. With $\varphi=t\tau/\hbar$, $\psi(\tau)=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(e^{i\varphi}S+e^{-i\varphi}A)=\cos\varphi\,|L\rangle+i\sin\varphi\,|R\rangle$.
- Dipole: $-d\cos^2\varphi+d\sin^2\varphi=-d\cos2\varphi$. It oscillates (inversion at $\varphi=\pi/2$) and averages to 0. A bigger tunnelling barrier means smaller $t$, so slower inversion. In the limit of huge systems the flip time becomes effectively infinite and the asymmetric state is stable.

## Questions this raises
- How can one say when a "new level" with its own laws has truly appeared, rather than a hard-to-compute consequence of the lower one?
- Is the later speculation (information, time-order, function) a form of symmetry breaking or a different kind of mechanism? Anderson himself says there may well be no useful parallel between complexity in simple many-body cases and in truly complex cultural or biological ones (pdf 4).
- What does "approaching macroscopic" mean quantitatively for small systems such as nuclei (pdf 3)?
- Do the social sciences have analogues of the $N\to\infty$ limit?
