# Does the autistic child have a "theory of mind"?

## L0 — One line
In a Sally–Anne false-belief test, most of 20 relatively able autistic children failed (they pointed to where the marble really was), while almost all Down's syndrome and normal preschool children passed; the authors take this as a deficit in representing mental states, specific to autism.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The authors ask why autistic children have such severe social difficulties, which mental retardation alone does not explain. Using Leslie's model of metarepresentation (the ability to represent representations), they predict that autistic children lack a "theory of mind": they cannot attribute beliefs to others. They test this with the Sally–Anne puppet task in three groups: autistic (higher mental age), Down's syndrome and normal preschoolers. Sixteen of 20 autistic children failed the Belief Question, while about 85% of each control group passed. All groups passed the control questions about reality and memory. The authors conclude that the deficit is independent of mental retardation and specific to autism.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a false belief (believing something that is not true); mental age vs chronological age; basic percentages and the idea of a control group.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Social impairment in autism is not sufficiently explained by low IQ alone: some autistic children have normal IQ, and Down's children are socially competent for their mental age (p. 38).
- Leslie's model: a capacity for "second-order representations" (representations of representations) underlies both a theory of mind and pretend play. Its absence would predict poor pretend play and poor mentalizing together (p. 38).
- The test is Wimmer and Perner's Sally–Anne task. It needs the child to see that another's belief differs from the child's own knowledge (pp. 39, 41).
- Control questions (Naming, Reality, Memory) were all answered correctly by every child. Failure on the Belief Question therefore cannot be put down to memory or task confusion (pp. 41–43).
- Results: 80% of autistic children (16/20) failed on both trials, versus 85% (normal, 23/27) and 86% (Down's, 12/14) passing on both trials in the control groups (p. 42). The autistic group had the higher mental age (p. 40).
- The failing autistic children pointed to the real location, not a random one. This argues against position preference, negativism or random pointing (p. 43).
- A deficit in mental-state representation is distinct from perceptual perspective-taking, which can be solved with visuo-spatial skills alone (pp. 43–44).
- The authors conclude the deficit is specific and largely independent of general intellectual level. Four autistic children passed, and the authors suggest this subgroup deserves study (pp. 43–44).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Abstract (p. 37)
A new model of metarepresentational development is used to predict a cognitive deficit that could explain a crucial component of the social impairment in autism. A "theory of mind" is one manifestation of a basic metarepresentational capacity. If autistic children lack it, they cannot impute beliefs to others or predict their behaviour. The hypothesis was tested with Wimmer and Perner's puppet play paradigm. Normal and Down's syndrome children served as controls. Although the autistic children's mental age was higher, only they failed to impute beliefs (p. 37).

### 1. Introduction (pp. 37–39)
- **The condition.** Childhood autism is a severe developmental disorder, affecting about 4 in 10,000 children. The diagnostic criteria are behavioural. The main reliably identified symptom is impairment in verbal and nonverbal communication, which is part of a core disorder in understanding and coping with the social environment, regardless of IQ (pp. 37–38). Additional symptoms can occur: mental retardation, islets of ability and "insistence on sameness" (p. 38).
- **Social picture.** Autistic children find even the immediate social environment unpredictable and incomprehensible. They are often said to "treat people and objects alike" (p. 38). Studies describe a range from total withdrawal to repetitive pestering. Competence stays low even in able autistic children (p. 38).
- **Retardation is not enough.** Most autistic children are mentally retarded, and some symptoms may be due to that. But it cannot be a sufficient explanation of the social impairment, for two reasons. First, some autistic children have IQ in the normal range. Second, non-autistic retarded children such as Down's syndrome children are socially competent relative to their mental age (p. 38).
- **Need for a cognitive mechanism.** The authors argue that a mechanism independent of IQ must be considered. They say nobody has yet characterised one even in "quasi-computational" terms (p. 38).
- **Leslie's model.** From Leslie's model of metarepresentational development the authors take a mechanism for conceiving mental states: knowing that others know, want, feel or believe things. This is Premack and Woodruff's "theory of mind". A theory of mind is impossible without the capacity for "second-order representations". In the model this capacity appears in the second year of life and also accounts for the emergence of pretend play. Absence of the capacity would therefore lead to a lack of both theory of mind and pretend play (p. 38).
- **Link to pretend play.** Autistic children are known to show a striking poverty of pretend play. This holds even for high-IQ autistic children, whereas severely retarded Down's children do pretend. The authors say mental age is not helpful for explaining this pattern, but supposing a lack of second-order representations makes sense of the association (p. 39).
- **Logic of the test (p. 39).**
  - Prediction: autistic children will lack a theory of mind.
  - Having a theory of mind would not guarantee competence, because social competence depends on many factors.
  - If autistic children did use a theory of mind, a deficit in second-order representations would be ruled out.
  - If they lacked it, specificity would still need showing: (a) even autistic children with average IQ should lack the ability, and (b) severely retarded non-autistic children (Down's) should possess it.
- **The paradigm.** Premack and Woodruff defined theory of mind as the ability to impute mental states to oneself and others. Inferring what others believe lets one predict what they will do. Wimmer and Perner's paradigm shows an explicit theory of mind is within the capacity of a normal four-year-old. The child must realise that different people can have different beliefs about a situation. It uses the case where the child's own belief differs from someone else's, which is the strongest evidence of the capacity to conceive mental states (Dennett, cited) (p. 39).

*Intuition note.* The authors' logic runs: a missing basic ability (representing representations) should show up in two places, pretend play and belief attribution. A false-belief test checks one of them. Low IQ alone looks insufficient as an explanation, since Down's children, with lower IQ, pass.

### 2. Method (pp. 40–42)
**2.1 Subjects (p. 40).** Twenty autistic children were diagnosed by established criteria (Rutter, 1978). There were also 14 Down's syndrome and 27 clinically normal preschool children. The autistic group was a deliberately high-functioning subgroup, chosen for a stringent test of the specific-deficit hypothesis. Their mean IQ was 82 (derived from non-verbal MA), mostly 70–108, with one subject below 70. The Down's IQs ranged 42–89, mean 64. The normal group's MA was assumed to roughly equal CA, so it was, if anything, lower than that of the handicapped groups (p. 40).

Table 1 (p. 40), in years;months:

| Group | n | CA mean (SD; range) | Nonverbal MA (Leiter) | Verbal MA (BPVT) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Autistic | 20 | 11;11 (3;0; 6;1–16;6) | 9;3 (2;2; 5;4–15;9) | 5;5 (1;6; 2;8–7;5) |
| Down's | 14 | 10;11 (4;1; 6;3–17;0) | 5;11 (0;11; 4;9–8;6) | 2;11 (0;7; 1;8–4;0) |
| Normal | 27 | 4;5 (0;7; 3;5–5;9) | – | – |

The autistic group's mean MA was higher than the Down's group's on the non-verbal scale and on the more conservative verbal scale (p. 40).

**2.2 Procedure (pp. 41–42).** Two dolls, Sally and Anne, were used (Fig. 1, p. 41). The steps were:
1. *Naming Question:* check the child knows which doll is which.
2. Sally puts her marble in her basket and leaves.
3. Anne moves the marble and hides it in her box.
4. Sally returns. *Belief Question:* "Where will Sally look for her marble?"

Pointing to the previous location (the basket) passes, because it shows appreciation of the doll's false belief. Pointing to the current location fails, because the child ignores the doll's belief (p. 41).

These conclusions are warranted if two control questions are answered correctly: "Where is the marble really?" (*Reality*) and "Where was the marble in the beginning?" (*Memory*). They ensure the child knows the real location and remembers the earlier one (pp. 41–42). The authors see no reason to think the three questions differ in psycholinguistic complexity, but they hypothesise that they differ in conceptual complexity (p. 42). The scenario was repeated with a new marble location (experimenter's pocket), so three locations were available: basket, box, pocket (p. 42).

### 3. Results (p. 42)
- All children passed the Naming Question. All children, without exception, answered the Reality and Memory Questions correctly on both trials (p. 42).
- Each child's Belief answers were consistent across trials, except one Down's child who failed trial 1 and passed trial 2 (p. 42).
- Passed both trials: 23/27 normal (85%) and 12/14 Down's (86%). Failed both: 16/20 autistic (80%). The group difference was highly significant: $\chi^2 = 25.9$, df $= 2$, $p < .001$ (p. 42).
- All 16 failing autistic children pointed to where the marble really was, rather than to the other possible locations ($p = .006$, binomial test, one-tailed) (p. 42).
- The four autistic children who passed did so on both trials. Their CA was 10;11–15;10, non-verbal MA 8;10–10;8, verbal MA 2;9–7;0. They were fairly average on all available variables, and other children of equal or greater MA and CA failed (p. 42).

*Intuition note.* The contrast is between failing a question about someone else's belief and passing questions about reality and memory. The same children can know where the marble is and remember where it was, yet still point to the real place when asked about Sally.

### 4. Discussion (pp. 42–44)
- **What the controls allow.** Because every child answered the control questions correctly, all knew (and implicitly believed) that the marble was moved after Sally left (p. 42).
- **Controls.** Down's and normal children pointed to the first location. The authors infer they appreciated that their own knowledge differed from what could be attributed to the doll, and predicted behaviour from the doll's belief (pp. 42–43).
- **Autistic pattern.** The autistic group pointed consistently to the real location. On trial 2 they never pointed to the box (the "wrong" place on trial 1) but to the pocket, where the marble really was. The authors say this rules out position preference and negativism. Autistic children were not "contrary" on Reality or Memory Questions, and earlier work (Clark & Rutter) found no negativism. The authors also say the failure was not due to random pointing, nor to failure to understand and remember the task or narrative, since all children answered the Naming, Memory and Reality Questions perfectly (p. 43).
- **Conclusion (authors).** The autistic children did not appreciate the difference between their own and the doll's knowledge. The results "strongly support" the hypothesis that autistic children as a group fail to employ a theory of mind. The authors wish to explain the failure as an inability to represent mental states, leaving autistic subjects unable to impute beliefs and so disadvantaged in predicting others' behaviour (p. 43).
- **The four who passed.** They may be able to use a theory of mind, yet by definition they show social impairment. From Leslie's model the authors predict that, if these children can form second-order representations, they will also pretend-play, and that their social impairments will show a different pattern from those who fail (p. 43).
- **Not perceptual perspective-taking.** The ability tested may be called conceptual perspective-taking. Perceptual tasks ("line of sight", "three mountains") ask what can be seen from another viewpoint and can be solved with visuo-spatial skills alone, without imputing beliefs (p. 43). Hobson (1984) found autistic children do as well as expected for MA on perceptual tasks with doll protagonists. This suggested to Hobson that the abilities for perceptual viewpoints are unlikely to be those underlying the social disability. The present results are said to confirm this interpretation and to point to a distinction between understanding perceptual situations and attributing higher-order mental states (pp. 43–44).
- **Final claim.** The failure is a specific deficit. It cannot be attributed to the general effects of mental retardation, since the more severely retarded Down's children performed close to ceiling. The authors say they demonstrated a cognitive deficit largely independent of general intellectual level, with potential to explain both the lack of pretend play and social impairment by a circumscribed cognitive failure (p. 44).

*Intuition note.* The authors separate "what can she see?" from "what does she think?". The first can be solved by spatial reasoning. The second requires representing another mind, and this is where autistic children failed.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A Sally–Anne variant: Ben puts a coin in a tin and leaves. Cara moves the coin to a jar. Ben returns. Questions: Reality ("Where is the coin?"), Memory ("Where was the coin at the start?"), Belief ("Where will Ben look?").

| Child | Reality | Memory | Belief | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | jar | tin | tin | passes: predicts from Ben's (false) belief |
| B | jar | tin | jar | fails: answers from own knowledge |
| C | tin | tin | tin | uninterpretable: failed Reality control |

## Concepts
- [[theory of mind / mentalizing / mindreading]] — the ability to impute mental states to oneself and others, hence to predict behaviour (Premack & Woodruff, as adopted here) (p. 39)
- [[unexpected transfer task]] — Sally–Anne: an object is moved while the protagonist is away; "Where will Sally look?" (p. 41)
- [[first-order false belief]] — passing means pointing to where the doll believes the marble is, not where it is (p. 41); the paper does not use this label
- [[second-order representation]] (new) — Leslie's capacity to represent representations, required for a theory of mind and for pretend play; not the same as "second-order false belief" in the canon (p. 38)
- [[mindblindness]] — related to the hypothesis of a specific deficit in attributing mental states in autism; the paper does not use the term, and its claim is a deficit in representing mental states, specific to autism (p. 43)
- [[perspective-taking (level 1 / level 2)]] — the paper contrasts perceptual perspective-taking (what can be seen) with conceptual perspective-taking (what is believed) (p. 43)
- [[control questions (Reality, Memory)]] (new) — questions ensuring a failed Belief answer is not due to ignorance or forgetting (p. 41)
- [[specific deficit]] (new) — impairment largely independent of general mental retardation, to be shown by testing autistic children of average IQ and including a retarded non-autistic control group; here the autistic group had the higher mental age (pp. 39, 40, 44)

## Canonical instances
- [[sally-anne-task]] — the doll scenario with basket, box and pocket, tested on autistic, Down's and normal children (p. 41)
- [[perceptual-perspective-taking-tasks]] — "line of sight" and "three mountains", contrasted as solvable by visuo-spatial skills (p. 43)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Controls with lower mental age but intact performance ↔ research design: using a comparison group to rule out a confound (here, general intellectual level)
- [structural-analogy] Answering from reality instead of from the doll's belief ↔ theory-of-mind canon: reality bias / curse of knowledge (the paper itself does not use these terms)
- [metaphor] Control questions that must be passed before interpreting the key question ↔ machine theory of mind evaluation: sanity checks before interpreting a model's failure on a false-belief item

## Questions this raises
- How can the four autistic children who passed be understood? Do they pretend-play, and is their social impairment of a different pattern, as the authors predict (p. 43)?
- Is failure on this one task enough to infer a missing general capacity, given that the authors admit competence depends on many factors (p. 39)?
- What do the two tests in the paper (mental age and Down's controls) rule out, and what other explanations remain open?
- Does the same deficit show up in other tasks of mental-state attribution besides this puppet paradigm?
