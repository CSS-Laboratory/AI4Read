# Information and the Change in the Paradigm in Economics (Stiglitz, Nobel lecture) — Part 1

## L0 — One line
Stiglitz argues that even small information imperfections undermine the core "laws" of the competitive-equilibrium model (market clearing, the single price, price equal to marginal cost, informationally efficient stock markets), and presents information economics as a fundamental change in the prevailing paradigm.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Stiglitz asks why the standard competitive model, built on perfect information, fits the real world so badly: unemployment, credit rationing, sharecropping, and puzzling cycles. His answer: the model is not robust. Small departures from perfect information change equilibria, so that markets need not clear and markets are almost never Pareto efficient. He traces this through his work on screening, efficiency wages, incentives and adverse selection. He also explains why treating information as just another transactions cost is the wrong fix. The unit stops mid-way through how actions convey information. It matters because it shows why market failure is pervasive and why policy advice that ignores information problems can go wrong.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand and market clearing; Pareto efficiency; expected value; the idea of an equilibrium as a state where no agent wants to change. Terms used above: screening = sorting people by a hidden quality such as ability; efficiency wages = pay above the market-clearing wage; adverse selection = a change in price changes who takes part; adverse incentives (moral hazard) = contract terms change behaviour; credit rationing = lenders do not raise the rate even though loan demand exceeds supply; pooling = types are not told apart.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Stiglitz says information economics undermines Smith's "invisible hand" claim and the limited view of government built on it (p. 473). The hand may be absent, or "palsied."
- Stiglitz found the standard model seriously wrong: it left out or denied unemployment, incentive problems, the role of institutions and the role of wealth distribution. Its standard proofs did not even list perfect information among their assumptions (pp. 473, 476, 483).
- The old hope (Marshall's "nature does not make jumps") was that nearly perfect information gives nearly perfect-information results. He reports that the research showed this to be false (p. 475).
- Four research strands linked to his time in Kenya: education as screening (employers sorting workers by ability), efficiency wages (firms paying above the market-clearing wage), sharecropping and incentives, and wage and price distributions. Efficiency-wage theory shows that unemployment can exist in equilibrium, so markets need not clear (pp. 478–482).
- Cornerstone "laws" (demand = supply, single price, price = marginal cost, efficient markets) were rejected or shown to hold only under restrictive conditions. Actions convey information, and participants know it (p. 485).
- Information is not just a transactions cost (Stigler's view, which Stiglitz calls wrong). He links information problems to thin or missing markets, market power and externalities (pp. 477, 485–486, 489, 493).
- Overcoming information asymmetries is only partial. In his simple screening model there can be multiple equilibria, and the pooling one (types not told apart) Pareto-dominates the one with full revelation. Where the more able cannot fully convey their ability, investment in screening requires imperfect competition in screening. Because gathering information has a cost, markets cannot be fully informationally efficient (pp. 490–492).
- Under adverse selection (price changes who takes part) or adverse incentives (price changes behaviour), price affects "quality," so equilibrium may feature credit rationing (the interest rate does not rise to clear the loan market) or unemployment (pp. 485, 494).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 472–474)
**Aim (p. 472).** Stiglitz places his work (with Akerlof and Spence) within a larger research programme and the history of economic thought. He wants to show that information economics is a fundamental change of paradigm. He also says information problems matter for political economy, treated in the last section (not in this part).

**Smith and policy (pp. 472–473).** Free markets leading to efficient outcomes "as if by an invisible hand" had suggested relying on markets without government intervention. Stiglitz says the ideas he presents undermined Smith's theory and the view of government that rested on it. The hand may be invisible because it is not there, or because, if there, it is palsied (p. 473).

**Personal motivation (p. 473).** Stiglitz grew up in Gary, Indiana, which was marked by poverty, periodic unemployment and racial discrimination. The theories he was taught said all markets clear (so unemployment is a "phantasm"), and that the profit motive prevents economic discrimination. Footnote 1 gives Becker's argument: enough unprejudiced employers would bid wages up to marginal productivity. He says that if the central theorems arguing that the economy was Pareto efficient (that, "in some sense, we were living in the best of all possible worlds") were true, it seemed to him that we should be striving to create a different world. As a graduate student he set out to build models with assumptions and conclusions closer to the world he saw.

In Kenya (1967 visit, longer stay 1969) he noted imperfect information, absent markets and persistent "dysfunctional" institutions such as sharecropping. He says that, if conventional economics were correct, workers who give up 50% or more of income to landlords would have greatly attenuated incentives. Traditional economics said neither institutions nor wealth distribution mattered. Footnote 2 says Cheung's thesis tried to show sharecropping did not matter, but its unreasonable assumptions about information helped convince Stiglitz of the need for an alternative. He also saw massive African urban unemployment that unions and minimum wages could not explain (p. 473).

**Macroeconomics (p. 474).** In the 1970s economists became increasingly critical of Keynesian ideas, partly because of their assumed lack of micro-foundations. Stiglitz says rebuilding macro on *traditional* micro (well-functioning markets) was doomed, because recessions with massive unemployment signal market failure. If people could borrow at safe rates, a six-month job interruption would hardly matter. But the unemployed lack access to capital markets on reasonable terms, and they cannot buy private insurance. A main result of information economics is macro models that explain why the economy amplifies shocks and makes them persistent. They also explain why unemployment and credit rationing may exist even in competitive equilibrium (p. 474).

**Policy and politics (p. 474).** Stiglitz believes that some of the huge policy mistakes in managing the East Asia crisis and the post-communist transition might have been avoided with a better understanding of bankruptcy and corporate governance. He says the Washington-consensus policies were market-fundamentalist and ignored information-theoretic concerns, which explains "at least in part" their failures. He also notes asymmetries between governing and governed (Sweden legislated for transparency two centuries earlier), and says we should limit them and mitigate their consequences.

### The historical setting (pp. 475–478)
**Perfect information as tradition (p. 475).** Stiglitz will not review the models. He highlights their impact and notes that much of the work probed the limits of the prevailing paradigm (whether it could embrace information imperfections, which, he adds, turned out not to work very well). For over a hundred years formal economics modelled perfect information. Economists hoped, following Marshall ("Natura non facit saltum"), that nearly perfect information would give nearly perfect-information results. His result is that even a small information imperfection can profoundly change the equilibrium.

**Smith anticipated adverse selection (p. 475).** Smith wrote that as interest rates rise, the best borrowers drop out. Stiglitz's reading: *if* lenders knew each borrower's risk, this would matter little, as each would pay a risk premium. It is because lenders do not know default probabilities that adverse selection has important consequences.

**Puzzles for the standard theory (p. 476).** He says something was seriously wrong with the competitive equilibrium models: they seemed to say that unemployment did not exist and that efficiency and equity could be neatly separated. He then lists empirical puzzles:
- Micro: why firms seemingly do not minimise tax liabilities; why asset prices seem so volatile; why firms respond to risk unlike the theory predicts.
- Macro: the cyclical behaviour of consumption (Hall's random-walk prediction is not supported), inventories (they move pro-cyclically, not counter-cyclically), real product wages, real consumption wages and interest rates.
- Footnote 16: Lucas (1987) used a perfect-markets model with a representative agent to argue that cyclical fluctuations have small welfare costs.
- Unexplained institutions: why IPOs are typically sold at a discount, and why equities, which diversify risk better than debt, play a limited role in financing new investment.

**Ptolemaic defences (p. 477).** Stiglitz calls these "Ptolemaic attempts" to patch the old model.
- Stigler's view: once the real costs of information are included, standard results still hold; information is just a transactions cost. In the approach of many Chicago economists, information economics was like any other applied field (as agricultural economics is), analysing the demand and supply of information.
- The mathematical version: put "I" (information) into production functions, produced by inputs like labour.
- **Stiglitz:** his analysis showed that this approach, and the conclusions derived from it, were wrong.

**Neoclassical synthesis under attack (p. 477).** The synthesis was Keynesian intervention to keep full employment, after which standard neoclassical propositions would hold again. By the 1970s–80s it was attacked from two sides.
- One side attacked its missing micro-foundations (why would rational actors stay out of equilibrium), which effectively denied the phenomena Keynes tried to explain.
- The other side saw unemployment as interference (minimum wages, union monopoly power) with free markets. **That view's implication:** unemployment would vanish with more flexible markets. Even if wages fell a third in the Great Depression, in this view they should have fallen even more.

**Stiglitz's alternative (Greenwald–Stiglitz) (p. 477).** Why not believe that massive unemployment is just the tip of the iceberg of more pervasive market inefficiencies that are harder to detect? Debt contracts mean falling wages and prices cause bankruptcy and worsen downturns. With more wage and price flexibility, matters might have been even worse. Neither government nor unions constrained wages and prices in many sectors. At the very least, those who argued that the problem was wage and price rigidities had to look for other market imperfections, and any policy remedy (including a call for greater flexibility) had to take those factors into account (pp. 477–478).

*Intuition note:* Some economists saw unemployment as the result of interference with free markets. Stiglitz's alternative view is that if markets function so badly at times, they must be malperforming in subtler ways much of the time. He says the economics of information bolstered this view.

**Where the research began (p. 478).** The model was "not robust": slight departures from perfect information had large consequences. The key period was 1969–71 at the Institute for Development Studies, University of Nairobi (Rockefeller Foundation support).

### Four strands from Kenya (pp. 478–482)
**1. Education as a screening device (pp. 478–479).**
- Kenya asked how much to invest in education. A better education gets a better job, since the credential put one at the head of the job queue.
- Gary Fields built a simple model. Private returns (higher chance of a good job) differ from social returns. Private returns might rise as more people get educated (it becomes even more necessary to hold the credential) even though the social return might decline.
- Education here does something different from the traditional human-capital view (where education just added to human capital and raised productivity) (p. 478). Footnote 23 mentions other work stressing socialisation and credentialisation.
- Weakness of Fields's model: wages were fixed, not competitively determined.
- Stiglitz asked what equilibrium would look like if wages equal mean marginal products *conditional on available information*. That raised the question of the incentives and mechanisms for employers and employees to acquire or transmit information.
- Within a group of applicants facing the same wage, the employer has an incentive to screen, *if he could keep the information private*. He often cannot. If others learn true ability, the wage is bid up and he cannot *appropriate* the return. Stiglitz says this identified one of the key issues in information economics: the difficulty of appropriating the returns (pp. 478–479).
- On the employee side, those who know they are above average want to convince the employer. Those at the bottom want no revelation. So there are incentives for secrecy ("lack of transparency"), and the question is how the two forces balance. The equilibrium is described later (p. 479).

**2. Efficiency wage theory (pp. 479–481).**
- Context: heavy urban unemployment in Kenya. The Harris–Todaro model: high urban wages attract migrants who risk unemployment. What was missing was an explanation of the high wages, well above the minimum wage and, it seemed, not forced by government or unions.
- Leibenstein had postulated that in poor countries higher wages raise productivity, so cutting wages may not pay. Stiglitz's insight: when information and contracting are imperfect, productivity may depend on wages for many reasons. Then firms may pay above the minimum needed to hire labour (**efficiency wages**), and unemployment can exist in equilibrium (p. 479).
- Four explanations (besides nutrition):
  - **(a) Turnover.** Lower wages mean higher turnover and turnover costs borne by the firm. Why the firm had to bear these costs was explained later from information limits (Arnott–Stiglitz) (p. 480).
  - **(b) Adverse selection.** Managers say higher pay attracts better workers. This applies the insurance idea that when premiums rise the best risks stop buying. Firms are not passive wage-takers, because market clearing is no constraint on them. If all pay the market-clearing wage, one firm might gain by offering more. Stiglitz's conclusion: the notion that markets had to clear is simply not true if information is imperfect (p. 480).
  - **(c) Incentives (Shapiro–Stiglitz 1984).** With identical workers at the same wage and full employment, a fired shirker gets another job at once, so a high wage gives no incentive. With unemployment, shirking has a price. They showed that in equilibrium there had to be unemployment: unemployment is the device that disciplines workers (p. 480).
  - **(d) Morale and fairness.** Briefly noted earlier, fully developed by Akerlof and Yellen (1986) (p. 481).
- In practice workers differ, so adverse selection and incentives intertwine (being fired conveys information; there is a stigma) (p. 481).

*Intuition note:* If a worker fired for shirking can find the same pay at once, a high wage does not discipline. Unemployment makes losing a job costly, so firms can rely on it. This is why the model needs unemployment in equilibrium.

**3. Sharecropping and the general theory of incentives (pp. 481–482).**
- Traditional theory had no real incentive problem: with perfect information, pay for the contracted service. With imperfect information, firms must motivate and monitor, rewarding good observed performance and punishing bad.
- Sharecropping (worker surrenders half, sometimes two thirds, of produce) looked like a 50% tax. Alternatives: renting (worker bears all output risk, often lacks capital) or wage labour (landlord must monitor). Sharecropping is a compromise between risk bearing and incentives.
- The information problem: the worker's input is unobservable, only output is, and output is not perfectly correlated with input. The contract is like a rental contract *plus* insurance (landlord "rebates" part of the rent if crops fail). Full insurance (a wage contract) would attenuate all incentives. The adverse effect of insurance on incentives is **moral hazard** (p. 481; fn 32 says the term arises in insurance literature).
- Stiglitz (1974b) analysed the equilibrium sharecropping contract. He saw the incentive problems as isomorphic (formally the same) to those of corporations giving managers incentives, later called the principal-agent problem (fn 33).
- Contracts must be based on observables (processes, observable inputs such as fertiliser). Many adverse-selection results had parallels in "adverse incentives." With Arnott he analysed an equilibrium with partial insurance (p. 482).

**4. Equilibrium wage and price distributions (p. 482).**
- Turnover work implies firms facing higher wages may pay more, and quits depend on the wage distribution. The challenge was an equilibrium model with a wage distribution.
- Efficiency wage levels may vary across firms (higher turnover costs, costly worker inefficiency, harder monitoring). Similar labour may receive different pay, so wage gaps may not be explained solely by ability differences (p. 482).

### From the competitive equilibrium paradigm to the information paradigm (pp. 482–488)
**Internal logic of the old model (pp. 482–483).** Stiglitz's second motivation came from the model itself.
- It made economics almost a branch of engineering, he says (with no aspersions on engineers). Households maximise utility under budget constraints, firms maximise profits, with full information, in competitive markets (p. 482).
- Peculiar implications: no disagreement about what a firm should do; "risky" should mean correlated with the business cycle, not a high chance of failure (fn 36); no unemployment or credit rationing; only a limited subset of information problems; incentives and motivation not addressed (p. 483).
- Research effort went to technical issues. The mathematics needed convexity and continuity assumptions, with which one could prove existence of equilibrium and Pareto efficiency. The standard proofs did not even list information assumptions. The listed *economic* assumptions concerned externalities and public goods (p. 483).
- A "curious disjunction": economists spoke of information efficiency but modelled only one problem, scarcity. The model had no shocks. The full equilibrium was solved at the start and everything after was an unfolding of plans for each contingency. The real question is how well markets handle information problems (p. 483).
- It also said institutions do not matter, wealth distribution does not matter (fn 39: the second welfare theorem, with well-defined property rights), and history (by and large) does not matter (pp. 483–484).

**Questioning each premise (p. 484).**
- *Convexity.* With imperfect information, diminishing-returns (convexity) assumptions are no longer plausible. Radner–Stiglitz (1984) showed a **fundamental non-concavity in the value of information**: under quite general conditions it never pays to buy just a little information. Arnott–Stiglitz (1988a) showed such problems are pervasive in even the simplest moral hazard problems. Stiglitz says they did not repeal diminishing returns but showed its domain to be more limited. Footnote 41 adds that even small fixed costs of information imply markets are not perfectly competitive but better described by monopolistic competition.
- *Existence.* Rothschild–Stiglitz showed that under natural formulations of a competitive market with imperfect information, equilibrium often did not exist, even with an arbitrarily small degree of information imperfection. Later work proposes alternative equilibrium definitions. Stiglitz says "we remain unconvinced," because most violate the natural meaning of competition (each participant so small that he believes he has no effect on others' behaviour) (pp. 484–485). Footnote 44 notes that with a continuum of types (as in Spence's models) a full equilibrium never existed.

**Basic "laws" undermined (p. 485).** The laws were: demand and supply (equilibrium = market clearing), the single price, the competitive price (price = marginal cost), and the efficient markets hypothesis (prices convey all relevant information from informed to uninformed). Stiglitz says each of these cornerstones was rejected, or was shown to hold only under much more restrictive conditions. His four claims:
- When price affects "quality" (by incentive or selection effects), equilibrium may feature demand not equal to supply. Firms will not pay lower wages even when they can, because labour costs would rise. Firms will not charge higher interest rates despite excess credit demand, because the average default rate would rise and expected returns fall.
- The market will show wage and price distributions even with no exogenous noise and with otherwise identical firms and workers.
- In equilibrium, firms will charge prices above marginal cost, or pay wages above reservation wages. The "surplus" provides the incentive to maintain a reputation. Even without reputation rents, information imperfections give market power (imperfect competition), mostly of the Chamberlinian monopolistic kind (fn 47).
- If prices reflected all information, nobody would pay to collect it. Grossman–Stiglitz (1976, 1980) showed that the price system imperfectly aggregated information and that there was an equilibrium amount of "disequilibrium."

*Intuition note:* If prices already told everyone everything, then (whenever gathering information costs anything) nobody would have an incentive to gather it. So prices cannot be perfectly informative, and some "disequilibrium" must persist.

**Actions convey information (pp. 485–486).** The most fundamental difference: *actions (including choices) convey information, market participants know this, and this affects their behaviour.* Examples:
- A guarantee signals confidence in the product, not only risk-absorbing capacity.
- An insured takes a policy with a large deductible not because he is not risk averse, but because the action tells the insurer that he thinks an accident is unlikely.
- A firm may avoid assigning an employee to a highly visible job, because the assignment signals quality and a rival may hire him away (p. 486).

**Missing markets and efficiency (p. 486).** Akerlof (1971) showed markets may be thin or absent. The old paradigm assumed a complete set of markets (including capital and risk markets). Missing markets (e.g. for risk) affect other markets; e.g. employers provide *some* insurance in labour contracts, and the design of these imperfect contracts affects efficiency. Stiglitz says, perhaps most importantly, that under the standard paradigm markets are Pareto efficient except for a limited number of market failures, while under imperfect information "markets are almost never Pareto efficient." Information economics also explained long-unexplained phenomena (e.g. sharecropping).

**Some problems in constructing an alternative paradigm (pp. 486–488).**
- Economists knew information was imperfect. The real reason there were no models was that it was not obvious how to build them: information is perfect in one way but imperfect in infinitely many ways. One of the keys to success was simple models where the relevant information could be fully specified. The danger is that such oversimplified models may allow full information revelation, which never happens in the real world; he says this is why some later work, e.g. Grossman–Stiglitz, used models with infinitely many states (pp. 486–487).
- Footnote 49 example: higher interest rates may induce more risk taking, so expected return to the lender may fall, and the optimal rate may be below the market-clearing rate (credit rationing). In simple models collateral could solve this (Bester), but with both adverse selection and incentive problems it no longer does, since those most willing to give collateral may be wealthy people more willing to take risky projects (Stiglitz–Weiss 1985).
- The hardest problem was modelling equilibrium. Both sides of each market must be modelled as rational in some sense, with beliefs about the consequences of actions that in turn depend on the inferences others draw. Stiglitz wanted competitive behaviour (actors small, believing they are small), though others' inferences about them might change. One must also fix the feasible set of actions for extracting or conveying information (p. 487).
- Early confusion arose partly from unclear assumptions. In the standard adverse selection model, quality depends on price, buyers know the statistical relationship, and equilibrium is where demand equals supply. But this is an equilibrium **if and only if** a seller of a good car cannot convey that fact and the buyer cannot sort good from bad cars. He says that typically there are such ways (p. 487).
- So market clearing, selling at one price to all comers, and zero profits in competitive equilibrium could not be assumed. A new entrant that lowers prices slightly may change the mix of customers so that its profits become negative. One had to rethink all conclusions "from first premises" (p. 487).
- Progress came from highly simplified models of *particular* markets (insurance, education, labour, land tenancy). From these he drew general principles and compared how they operate across markets. Footnote 50: earlier general-equilibrium work (Radner, Hurwicz, Marschak) recognised information problems, but modifying the abstract theory proved, in the end, less fruitful than starting from simple concrete models (p. 488).

### Sources of asymmetries of information (pp. 488–490)
- Information imperfections are pervasive. The research focuses on **asymmetries**: workers know their ability better than the firm; insured people know their health better; a car owner knows the car better than buyers; a borrower knows his risk better than the lender (p. 488).
- Stiglitz calls it the essential feature of a decentralized market economy that different people know different things. The earlier literature had not asked how asymmetries are created or what follows. Imperfections run deeper than simple asymmetry: an individual may know little about his own health, and an insurer may become more informed through an examination (p. 488).
- Some asymmetries are inherent (one knows oneself). Some arise from economic processes (pp. 488–489):
  - **Employer example.** The current employer learns more about an employee than potential rivals. This creates a "used labor" market that does not work well. Rivals bid more tamely, knowing they would lure the worker away only if they bid too much. A rival bid below productivity is matched, so labour mobility is impeded and the first employer gains market power. Recognising this affects the "new labor" market too: a worker who expects to be locked in will be more risk averse in accepting offers, and the initial contract must reflect his diminished bargaining power (p. 489). Footnote 52 adds that asymmetries *reduce* the extent of mobility.
  - **Oil exploration.** A firm finds information relevant to neighbouring tracts, which is an informational externality. Bidding with known asymmetries differs from bidding without. Uninformed bidders presume they win only by overbidding, which exacerbates the **winner's curse** (the bidder with the most positive estimate bids highest and knows that if he wins, others' information is less positive; fn 55). The government as lessor should take this into account, and initial bidders count information rents from later rounds (pp. 489–490).

**Creating asymmetries (p. 490).** Later work asked how markets *create* information problems, partly to exploit market power.
- Managers *entrench* themselves by taking actions that increase information asymmetries (Edlin–Stiglitz 1995), which reduces competition for management. This is part of corporate governance.
- Information imperfections give market power, which firms exploit through "sales" and other ways of differentiating among people with different search costs. Price dispersions are created by the market, not just failures to arbitrage shock-caused differences.

### Overcoming information asymmetries (pp. 490–494)
**Incentives for gathering and disclosing information (pp. 490–491).**
- More able people (lower-risk, better-product firms) get higher pay or lower premiums if they can establish their type. Less able people prefer non-disclosure (p. 490).
- Early result: if the more able can establish their ability (costlessly), the market is fully revealing, even though all below average would prefer no revelation. In the simplest models this is an **unraveling**: the best reveal; the rest are grouped and paid the group mean; the best of that group reveal; and so on until full revelation (p. 491; fn 57: Stiglitz jokingly called this Walras' Law of Sorting).
- If the able cannot credibly convince others, the *other* side has an incentive to gather information, but if competition then bids up the wage the screener cannot appropriate the return. Hence, in markets where the more able cannot (fully) convey their attributes: "if there is to be investment in screening there must be imperfect competition in screening" (p. 491).
- The economy must in effect choose between imperfections of information and imperfections of competition. In the end there will be both. Footnote 58 says there is no reason to think the market balances them optimally. Footnote 59 suggests this helps explain why banking competition is so imperfect (p. 491).
- Interplay of imperfections: sharecropping incentive problems could be solved if workers could borrow to buy land, but capital-market limits, themselves arising from information imperfections, block this (p. 491).
- If information spread instantly and perfectly, nobody would gather it when gathering has any cost. So markets cannot be fully informationally efficient (Grossman–Stiglitz) (p. 491).

**Mechanisms for reducing asymmetries (pp. 491–492).** Simply asking fails: the least able have an incentive to lie, since "talk was cheap." Information must be conveyed credibly some other way (p. 492).

**Screening by examination (p. 492).** Stiglitz (1974a) built a simple competitive equilibrium model with two general principles.
- The gains of the more able are largely at the expense of the less able: proving higher ability raises one's wage and proves that others are of lower ability.
- Private returns to education exceed social returns; he adds that there were important externalities associated with information.
- There can be multiple equilibria: one with full revelation and one without (a **pooling equilibrium**). The pooling equilibrium Pareto-dominated full revelation.
- Two policy results which, he says, have remarkably not been fully absorbed into policy discussions even today: markets do not give appropriate incentives for information disclosure (a role for government in principle), and expenditure on information may be too great. Footnote 61 credits Hirshleifer (1971) independently.

*Intuition note:* Showing you are good also shows others are worse, and your private gain is partly someone else's loss. So people may spend more on proving ability than society gains.

**The simplest adverse selection model (pp. 492–493).**
- Much information comes from inferences from behaviour, not from exams. In insurance, as premiums rise the least likely to have accidents stop buying (Arrow 1965), so willingness to buy conveys information. Akerlof saw it was general: willingness to sell a used car says something about whether it is a lemon (p. 492).
- Greenwald (1979, 1986) applied it to labour and capital markets: an employer's unwillingness to match a competitor's bid conveys information about the employee's ability. Insiders' willingness to sell stock at a given price conveys their view of the price relative to expected return. Akerlof's insight that this makes markets thin or absent helped explain why labour and capital markets often work badly, and provided part of the explanation of why firms raise so little of their funds through equity (Mayer 1990) (p. 493).
- **Stiglitz:** "Stigler was wrong: imperfect information was not just like a transactions costs." Weak equity markets mean risks cannot be divested, so firms act in a risk-averse way. These capital-market imperfections are central to the macro theories described later (not in this part) (p. 493).

**The simplest adverse incentive model (p. 493).** Imperfections also concern what people *do*: a worker can work harder, a borrower take more risk, the insured take more care. If observable, these could be specified in the contract. Insurers may try to monitor, as in the adverse selection model they may examine. But exams and monitoring are costly and leave residual imperfection. Contract terms affect the average riskiness of applicants and also the level of risk taking. Borrowers' risk taking is affected by the interest rate (Stiglitz–Weiss 1981) (p. 493).

**Efficiency wage theory, credit rationing (p. 494).**
- Early adverse selection work had the seller (insurer, employer, lender) rational enough to see that quality depends on price, but not rational enough to exploit the information as fully as he could (p. 494).
- No law requires an insurer to sell to all who apply at its premium, a lender to lend to all who apply at its rate, or an employer to hire all who apply at its wage. Economists simply assumed that firms take prices as given. Under perfect information and competition a firm charging more than others loses all customers. In the new models what matters is not just the number of customers but their "quality" (p. 494).
- Since quality may rise with price, it may pay to offer a higher wage than the market-clearing wage, and (as the page words it) for the lender to lend at an interest rate that exceeds the market-clearing rate; this wording conflicts with the credit-rationing result a few lines later (see Reading notes). This holds whether the dependence comes from adverse selection or adverse incentives (or morale or nutrition effects).
- What matters is *imperfect* information, not asymmetry. The healthy who decline high-premium insurance need not *know* they are healthy; they may just have different preferences (e.g. spending on recreational sports) (p. 494).
- Consequence: equilibrium may feature demand not equal to supply. The equilibrium interest rate is *lower* than the rate at which loan demand equals supply (**credit rationing**; Stiglitz–Weiss 1981, Keeton 1979). The wage is *higher* than the rate at which labour demand equals supply (**unemployment**) (p. 494). Footnote 65: equilibrium models are harder than they seem, since each firm's results depend on others' wage offers.

**Conveying information through actions (p. 494).** The section begins: there is a much richer set of informative actions than traditional adverse selection models consider; an insurer wants healthy applicants and "might realize that by locating…". The unit breaks off here.

## Toy example (our own, not from the text)
Illustrates the mechanism of footnote 49 (p. 487): higher interest rates can lower the lender's expected return, so a lender may not raise the rate. All numbers are made up. Assume each borrower borrows 100 and has limited liability (pays nothing if the project fails; added assumption). Let $s = 1 + r$ be the repayment per unit borrowed.
- **Safe project:** revenue 260 for sure. Borrower's profit $= 260 - 100s$.
- **Risky project:** revenue 400 with probability 0.5, else 0. Borrower's expected profit $= 0.5(400 - 100s) = 200 - 50s$. If the project fails, the lender gets 0.
- The borrower picks safe if $260 - 100s \ge 200 - 50s$, i.e. $60 \ge 50s$, i.e. $s \le 1.2$ (rate ≤ 20%); assume ties go to safe.

| Rate $r$ | Safe profit | Risky exp. profit | Choice | Lender's expected repayment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% ($s=1.1$) | 260−110 = 150 | 200−55 = 145 | safe | 110 |
| 20% ($s=1.2$) | 260−120 = 140 | 200−60 = 140 | safe (tie) | 120 |
| 30% ($s=1.3$) | 260−130 = 130 | 200−65 = 135 | risky | 0.5 × 130 = 65 |

The lender's expected repayment peaks at 20% and then falls (120 → 65). If demand for loans still exceeds supply at 20%, the lender does not raise the rate, so demand ≠ supply in equilibrium: **credit rationing** (as on pp. 485, 494). Here the effect comes from incentives (the choice of risk); the text says adverse selection can work the same way.

## Concepts
- [[asymmetric information]] — different parties know different things; Stiglitz says this is the essential feature of a decentralised economy (p. 488).
- [[adverse selection]] — willingness to trade at a price conveys hidden quality, and price changes alter who trades (pp. 475, 492).
- [[moral hazard]] — insurance weakens incentives to avoid the insured contingency; here the related incentive problems are also called "adverse incentives" (pp. 481–482).
- [[screening]] — an uninformed party (or an exam) sorts types; here also the informed party's costly credential, e.g. education (pp. 478, 492).
- [[separating vs pooling equilibrium]] — full revelation versus no revelation. The pooling one Pareto-dominates full revelation in the model here (p. 492).
- [[market failure / externality]] — private returns to proving ability exceed social returns; information externalities (pp. 489, 492).
- [[Grossman–Stiglitz paradox]] — fully informative prices would remove the incentive to gather information (pp. 485, 491).
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — as Stiglitz states it here: in stock markets, prices convey all relevant information from the informed to the uninformed (p. 485).
- [[incomplete contracts]] — missing risk and capital markets mean contracts give only partial insurance and are imperfect (p. 486). (The term is not used by Stiglitz here; he writes "imperfect and incomplete.")
- [[equilibrium]] — in Stiglitz's use, an equilibrium need not clear markets, and may fail to exist (pp. 484–485).
- [[efficiency wage theory]] (new) — firms pay above the market-clearing wage because productivity depends on the wage (turnover, selection, incentives, morale); this yields equilibrium unemployment (p. 479).
- [[credit rationing]] (new) — in equilibrium, loan demand exceeds supply because a higher rate lowers the lender's expected return (pp. 485, 494).
- [[winner's curse]] (new) — the winning bidder, with the most positive estimate, bids knowing others' information is less positive (p. 489, fn 55).
- [[principal-agent problem]] (new) — designing incentives for a manager or worker whose actions are unobserved (p. 481–482, fn 33).
- [[unraveling of disclosure]] (new) — if the best types can credibly reveal themselves, the best of the remaining group then has an incentive to reveal, and so on down the line (p. 491).
- [[non-concavity of the value of information]] (new) — under quite general conditions, it never pays to buy just a little information (p. 484).
- [[informational externality]] (new) — one party's information collection yields information relevant to others, as with oil tracts (p. 489).

## Canonical instances
- [[sharecropping]] — landlord–tenant contract as compromise between risk bearing and incentives (p. 481).
- [[education as screening]] — private returns exceed social returns (pp. 478, 492).
- [[used labor market]] — current employer's information locks workers in (p. 489).
- [[oil tract bidding]] — informational externality and winner's curse (p. 489).
- [[Harris–Todaro migration model]] — urban unemployment from rural–urban migration; needs an explanation of high urban wages (p. 479).
- [[Rothschild–Stiglitz insurance market]] — equilibrium often did not exist under natural formulations of competition with imperfect information (pp. 484, 488).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] unraveling of disclosure ↔ adverse selection/lemons: both are processes in which one group's exit or disclosure changes the pool that remains.
- [structural-analogy] Grossman–Stiglitz equilibrium amount of disequilibrium ↔ finance: informational efficiency of markets — if gathering information is costly, prices cannot reflect all information fully.
- [structural-analogy] credit rationing from rate-dependent default ↔ debt-driven instability: interest rates changing borrower behaviour and the quality of the borrower pool.
- [metaphor] "Ptolemaic" patches to the old model ↔ philosophy of science: adding epicycles to a failing paradigm.

## Questions this raises

- Why did Stiglitz believe that small, highly simplified models of particular markets were more fruitful than modifying general-equilibrium theory (p. 488)?
