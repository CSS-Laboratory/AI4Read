# Information and the Change in the Paradigm in Economics (part 2/3): self-selection, pervasive market failure, applications

## L0 — One line
Once actions are seen to convey information, markets can sort people by self-selection (their choices reveal their type), competitive equilibrium becomes fragile, and Stiglitz argues that market failures are pervasive, so the old efficiency-versus-distribution split and the price-equals-marginal-cost rule fail.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part of Stiglitz's Nobel lecture asks what happens to economic theory once actions are recognised as carrying information. Answer: people choose actions partly for what they signal, markets can sort types through self-selection (people reveal their type by which option they pick), and the competitive equilibrium may not exist or may not be robust. Stiglitz argues that markets with imperfect information are essentially never constrained Pareto efficient (no one could be made better off even after allowing for missing markets), and that the separation of efficiency from distribution fails. He then lists applications: contracts, corporate finance, governance, macroeconomics, money, growth, research, and policy design for taxation and regulation. It matters because he argues the fixed-information model seems increasingly irrelevant.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand; Pareto efficiency and the two welfare theorems (informally); the idea of an equilibrium of a game; basic insurance and risk aversion; basic corporate finance (debt vs equity).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Actions (schooling, deductibles, guarantees, share retention) may convey information. People therefore choose them partly for their effect on others' beliefs, and some may lie or obfuscate (pp. 495–496).
- Self-selection can let an uninformed party sort types through a menu of choices. In the Rothschild–Stiglitz competitive insurance model, a pooling equilibrium (all types buy the same contract) can never exist and a separating one (types choose different contracts) may not exist (pp. 496–497).
- Signaling (the informed side acts to show its type) and screening (the uninformed side designs the test) differ mainly in who moves first. Many "separating" outcomes exist, but only one is a full equilibrium (pp. 498–499).
- Imperfect information gives richer contracts (sharecropping, relative pay, collateral). It also breaks "price = marginal cost" and can produce price and wage dispersion even among identical firms (pp. 500–503).
- Markets with imperfect information are essentially never constrained Pareto efficient (no one could be made better off even after allowing for missing markets). Efficient solutions cannot in general be decentralised. Equity and efficiency cannot be separated, in part because lump-sum redistribution is impossible with imperfect information (pp. 503–506).
- Non-market institutions are not a safe fix: family insurance can crowd out market insurance and lower welfare (p. 507).
- Information imperfections (credit and equity rationing, risk-averse firms, bankruptcy) underpin Stiglitz's new theories of the firm, macro fluctuations, money, growth and research (pp. 507–515).
- Policy design must treat observability as central, for taxation and for regulation and privatization (pp. 516–517).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The part starts mid-section; the earlier part of the lecture is not in these pages, and the first section headings below are our labels. "I" is Stiglitz; "we" means Stiglitz and co-authors.)

### Actions convey information (pp. 495–496)
- Stiglitz's examples of actions that convey information:
  - Willingness to walk up five floors reveals a strong heart, so a health insurer can attract healthy applicants. Throwing in a health-club membership at a higher premium attracts those who will use it, who are likely healthier (p. 495).
  - A good guarantee can convey that the firm believes its product is reliable. Years of schooling may reflect ability, so part of the wage gain from schooling may be sorting rather than added human capital (p. 495).
  - A high deductible may suggest a lower accident risk. An entrepreneur's willingness to hold shares can convey his belief in the firm. A promotion may convey the firm's assessment of the person (p. 495).
- Footnote 66: sorting versus human capital is hard to separate empirically. Stiglitz did not argue that education fails to raise productivity. Wages jump at graduation, which fits sorting (p. 495).
- Because actions convey information, behavior changes (p. 495).
  - Some actions are designed to obfuscate. Waldman (1984) is cited on firms' promotion decisions (p. 495).
  - Others are designed to convey information credibly. Knowing that selling shares sends a negative signal, an entrepreneur may keep more shares, be less diversified, and act more risk averse (p. 495).
- Some individuals want information conveyed and some do not, for example because it would lower others' opinion of them or interfere with appropriating rents. This is why information imperfections have such profound effects (pp. 495–496).
- Two results follow (p. 496):
  1. People choose partly for how the choice changes others' beliefs about them, such as staying in school longer for the signal rather than for what is taught. Firm and household decision-making must be rethought.
  2. The less able have an incentive to "lie". If walking up five floors signals health, even the unhealthy might do it to fool the insurer. One must therefore look for ways information is conveyed *in equilibrium*.
- The critical insight came in the 1976 paper with Rothschild (p. 496).
  - If types act differently in an observable way, it may be possible to design a set of choices so that types identify themselves by self-selection (p. 496).
  - Knowing one's own type (asymmetric information) is only one basis for self-selection (p. 496).
  - Example: an insurer offers (a) high premium, no deductible, and (b) low premium, high deductible. Those who know they are low risk accept (b), so the choices separate the types (p. 496).

*Intuition note.* A choice is a message. If different types would choose differently, a menu of choices sorts them without anyone saying anything. But if a message pays, those who should not send it have a reason to send it too, so one must look for ways information is conveyed in equilibrium.

### Monopoly and self-selection (pp. 496–497)
- The easiest case is a monopolist (Stiglitz 1977a). He can build a choice set that differentiates types and check whether it is profit-maximizing to separate fully or to partly pool (p. 497).
- This laid the foundation of a general theory of price discrimination (p. 497).
  - Under standard monopoly theory *with perfect information*, firms would price-discriminate perfectly and extract each customer's full surplus. Monopoly would then be non-distortionary (p. 497).
  - Yet most models assumed one price for all and called monopoly distortionary, without explaining why there was no discrimination (p. 497).
  - With limited information, firms can discriminate, but only imperfectly. Salop (1977) and Adams and Yellen (1976) explored how a monopolist learns customers' characteristics. Discrimination is limited by the ability to identify each person's surplus, the maximum they would pay (p. 497).
- Stiglitz's conclusion: the economics of information provided the first coherent theory of monopoly (p. 497).

### Self-selection and competitive equilibrium (pp. 497–498)
- The hard question is full competitive equilibrium: a set of insurance contracts such that no one can offer an alternative set that would be profitable. Each firm controls its own offers but not others', and customers choose from the whole set (p. 497).
- Three striking results from Rothschild–Stiglitz (1976) (p. 497):
  1. Under plausible conditions, with the natural definition of equilibrium, equilibrium might not exist.
     - A *pooling* equilibrium (types not distinguished) never could exist. Given a single contract everyone buys, another firm could offer a contract that "breaks" it.
     - A *separating* equilibrium (types distinguished) might not exist, because the cost of separation is too great. Any putative separating equilibrium can be broken by a profitable pooling contract that both low-risk and high-risk types would buy.
  2. Even small information imperfections can change the standard results. Equilibrium never exists when the two types are very near each other. The competitive equilibrium model is "simply not robust".
  3. The fact that actions convey information affects equilibrium and reinforces the earlier adverse-selection analysis (pp. 497–498).
- Perfect-information models imply full divestment of risk and risk-neutral behavior. The insurance analysis explained why most risk-averse people buy only partial insurance (p. 498).
- The result extends to equity markets (p. 498).
  - Original owners may sell shares to "insure" against a bad outcome. An owner who believes a bad outcome is less likely sells fewer shares.
  - Retaining shares is thus a sorting mechanism, and willingness to keep them is a "signal" of confidence (fn. 68).
- Landlord–tenant and employer–employee relations can be viewed as containing an insurance component. Limits on divesting risk help explain many contractual relationships (p. 498).

*Intuition note.* The insurance model may have no resting point. A pooled contract can always be broken by another contract that a rival firm could offer. A separating set of contracts can in turn be broken by a profitable pooling contract that both low-risk and high-risk types would buy, because the cost of separation is too great.

### Sorting, screening, signaling (pp. 498–499)
- In equilibrium both sides know the informational consequences of their actions (p. 498).
  - If the insurer, employer or employee takes the initiative to sort applicants, self-selection is an alternative to examinations (p. 498).
  - If the insured, employee or borrower takes the initiative to show he is a better risk, he is *signaling* (Spence 1973) (p. 498).
- The differences between signaling models and self-selection (screening) models lie in game-theoretic technicalities, in particular whether the informed or the uninformed side moves first (p. 498).
- Footnote 70: who moves first should be treated as endogenous. In that case screening equilibria seem more robust than signaling equilibria. For instance, a pooling signaling equilibrium sustained by firms' out-of-equilibrium beliefs could be broken by an earlier or later move of firms (p. 498).
- Some of the seeming differences come from not specifying a *full* equilibrium (p. 499).
  - There are many separating sets of contracts but a unique separating equilibrium. Otherwise a firm could offer another set and profit (p. 499).
  - Education example, two types (low and high ability) (p. 499):
    - The low type goes to 12 years. Any education system in which the high type goes sufficiently long, say more than 14 years, might separate.
    - But the low type knows that going 11 years would still mark him as low. The unique equilibrium level for the low type maximises his net income.
    - The unique equilibrium level for the high type is the lowest level such that, if his wage equals his productivity there, the low type still prefers to stay at his own level.
  - Footnote 71, more accurately: the high type's level is the minimum of that level and the level that maximises his own net income (discounted income minus education expenditures) (p. 499).
- Education is a poor setting for studying *market* equilibrium (p. 499).
  - Its structure is largely public choice and differs markedly across countries. The minimum level is set by government, and examinations matter as much as self-selection or signaling (p. 499).
  - The insurance existence problems are irrelevant there because the "competitive" supply side is absent (p. 499).
  - Footnote 72: even without government dominance there would be a coordination problem. A single firm cannot propose a different wage schedule to break an inefficient separating equilibrium, because the employee does not know that he will necessarily stay with the firm for his whole working life (p. 499).
- Where signaling concepts are applied in a robust competitive market, existence problems cannot easily be ignored (p. 499). Footnote 73: with a continuum of types (Spence's model), a screening equilibrium never exists. Types arbitrarily close to each other always exist, and at the bottom (highest-risk) end a profitable contract can always attract the worst types (p. 499).

### Existence of equilibrium in the insurance market (p. 500)
- Insurance markets do exist, though far from complete. They show some instability: rates vacillate, coverage is curtailed, the public demands reform, and calm periods are followed by another "crisis". Most states regulate rate setting, at least partly for prudential reasons, which may help stabilize (p. 500).
- There is evidence for selection processes, but also that the market is far from as rational as theory suggests. Most health policies do not price on the number of children, and there is less experience rating than expected (p. 500).
- Stiglitz's "own suspicion" is that the major limitation of Rothschild–Stiglitz is its assumption of perfect competition. Competition is far more limited, with search costs and uncertainty about getting the insurer to pay. Self-selection is still relevant, but monopoly or monopolistic competition may be more relevant than perfect competition (p. 500).

### Theory of contracts and incentives (pp. 500–501)
- The Rothschild work was related to Stiglitz's earlier sharecropping work. One focused on selection effects, the other on incentives, and both entailed an equilibrium in "contracts" (p. 500).
- Standard-model contracts were extremely simple: pay if you do such-and-such. With perfect information, people simply did not sign contracts they did not intend to fulfil. Insurance contracts paid if and only if specified events occurred (p. 500).
- With imperfect information a far richer set of contracts is used, which started a large literature on contract theory (p. 500).
- Sharecropping contracts (Stiglitz 1974b) involved shares, fixed payments and plot sizes. More generally, optimal payment structures relate payments to *observables*: inputs, processes, outputs (pp. 500–501).
- Markets are *interlinked*: what happens in one affects others, so credit, labor and land markets cannot be decentralised as the standard model hypothesised. The theory served as a basis for rural organization in developing countries (p. 501).
- Further applications (p. 501):
  - Labor contracts (Stiglitz 1975a).
  - Pay on *relative* performance, which may convey more information than absolute performance. A stock that rises when all stocks rise may say very little about the manager. Nalebuff and Stiglitz (1983a, b) analysed these contests. One of the strong arguments for competitive, decentralised structures is that they provide information for better incentive pay.
  - Credit contracts: lenders specify an interest rate and also collateral and equity requirements, which have both incentive and selection effects.
  - The simultaneous presence of selection and incentive effects matters. Without selection effects, it might be possible to raise collateral *and* interest rates and still ensure the borrower takes the safe project.
- Footnote 77: the literature has not fully resolved why real contracts are much simpler than theory predicts (e.g. linear in output) and do not adjust to changed circumstances (p. 501).

### Incentives and reputation in market equilibrium (p. 502)
- Incentives rest on rewards and punishments. In modern economies the most severe punishment is firing, but it costs nothing if the person could get an identical job. Good behavior is driven by earning a surplus over what one could get elsewhere (p. 502).
- So the wage must exceed what the worker could get elsewhere (possibly zero under unemployment). In goods markets, price must exceed marginal cost so a firm feels a loss when it loses a customer through a shoddy product (p. 502).
- Therefore the old presumption that price equals marginal cost in competitive equilibrium "cannot" hold in markets with imperfect information (p. 502).
- Footnote 83: this is not quite accurate if a bond can be posted. But workers may lack the wealth, and "moral hazard" arises if the firm can falsely claim the worker shirked (p. 502).

### Equilibrium wage and price distributions (pp. 502–503)
- In everyday life the same good does not sell at the same price everywhere. The differences are more than quality differences (p. 502).
- Stigler (1961) and most of the search literature took the price or wage distribution as given and did not ask how it arose. Could it be sustained given search costs? (p. 502)
- Efficiency wage theory offers an alternative explanation. It pays firms to pay more than they have to, for example to reduce turnover costs, and it might pay some firms to pay more than others (p. 502).
- Key insight: there could be a wage distribution even if all firms were identical and faced the same search costs (p. 502).
- Diamond's (1971) argument (p. 503):
  - Suppose all firms charge the competitive price and the cost of going to another store is some small $\epsilon$.
  - A firm charging $\epsilon/2$ more loses no customers, so it pays to raise price. The same holds for every firm at each new price.
  - Prices rise until the monopoly price. Even small search costs lead a market with many firms to charge monopoly prices.
- Salop and Stiglitz (1977, 1982, 1987) showed that with small search costs markets are characterised by a price distribution. If everyone charged the same price, some firm would gain by raising its price (to exploit high-search-cost customers it would not lose) or by lowering it (to steal customers) (p. 503).
- The standard wisdom that not everyone needs to be informed for the market to act perfectly competitively is not true in general (p. 503).

*Intuition note.* Perfect competition needs customers to compare prices at no cost. A tiny cost of looking elsewhere gives each seller a little monopoly power over its own customers, and that is enough to move prices far from the competitive level.

### Efficiency of the market equilibrium and the role of the state (pp. 503–507)
- The old view (Stiglitz calls it perhaps the most important single idea in economics) rests on the two fundamental theorems of welfare economics (p. 503).
  - Competitive economies lead as if by an invisible hand to Pareto efficiency.
  - Every Pareto-efficient allocation can be reached through a competitive mechanism with appropriate lump-sum redistribution.
  - Together these justify reliance on free markets and the belief that distribution can be separated from efficiency. If society dislikes the distribution, it should redistribute.
- The economics of information showed neither result is true in general. Economists had already identified some market failures, such as pollution externalities, but the scope was seen as limited (p. 503).
- Early work laid the foundation for the idea that economies with information imperfections would not be Pareto efficient, "even taking into account the costs of obtaining information" (p. 503).
  - Incentives to disclose and acquire information are far from perfect. Imperfect appropriability could cause too little acquisition, yet much of the gain is "rents" (gain at others' expense), which suggests possibly *excessive* spending on information (pp. 503–504).
  - Price discovery in capital markets is often advertised as a strength. But if having information a nano-second earlier changes no real decision (e.g. investment), the gain is largely redistributive, at others' expense (p. 504).
  - Classroom example: $100 bills fall at each student's left foot. Waiting until the lecture ends is not a Nash equilibrium, since it would pay anyone to scoop up his bill quickly. Each who does so is no better off than if all had waited, and there is a great social cost, the interrupted lecture (p. 504, fn. 86).
- Other potential inefficiencies (p. 504):
  - Information can have adverse effects on volatility (fn. 87 refers to the East Asia crisis).
  - Information can destroy markets. Incentives to obtain information create asymmetries that can destroy insurance markets and lower welfare. Welfare might rise if acquiring such information were prohibited. Genetic testing is a current policy concern.
  - Use of some information may have discriminatory intent or effect where direct discrimination would be prohibited (fn. 88).
- Asymmetries are related to absent or imperfect markets, which helps explain why markets for lemons, credit, labor and equity worked imperfectly (p. 504).
- **Stigler's perspective (reported by Stiglitz):** because information is costly, markets might be fully efficient once its cost is taken into account. Stiglitz says Stigler had essentially argued this, but without proof, and that his research showed the assertion, or hope, to be incorrect (p. 504).
- Earlier work showed that if markets are absent or imperfect, equilibrium might be *constrained* Pareto inefficient: taking the absence of the market into account, everyone could be better off (p. 505).
  - Footnote 89: some economists had tried to show the Arrow–Debreu efficiency result was more robust to missing markets (e.g. Diamond 1967). Stiglitz says those results depended on overly simple models, e.g. a single commodity (p. 505).
- Asymmetries of information give rise to market power, and perfect competition is required for efficiency, so it is perhaps not surprising that such markets are far from efficient (p. 505).
- The failures from imperfect information are "far more profound" than poor incentives to acquire information (p. 505).
  - Moral hazard intuition: the premium reflects *average* risk and so average care. Each person ignores the effect of his own care on the premium, but when all take less care the premium rises. Each person's lack of care exerts a negative externality on others (p. 505).
  - Greenwald and Stiglitz (1986): externality-like effects are pervasive whenever information is imperfect or markets incomplete ("that is always"), so markets are "essentially never constrained Pareto efficient". Market failures are pervasive (p. 505). Footnote 90: the 1986 paper focuses on models with adverse selection and incentive problems.

*Intuition note.* In moral hazard, my carelessness raises everyone's premium, but I ignore that effect when I choose how much care to take. This is an externality that nobody can price directly, because care cannot be observed. Stiglitz concludes that such effects are pervasive whenever information is imperfect or markets are incomplete.

- Two further implications (pp. 505–506):
  1. **Non-decentralizability.** Greenwald and Stiglitz showed that decentralised decision-making cannot in general give an efficient allocation. Example: an insurer cannot monitor smoking. Government cannot monitor it better, but it can tax complements to smoking and subsidise substitutes with less adverse effect (p. 505, fn. 92). Braverman (1982) had shown the consequences in interlinked land, labor and credit markets (pp. 505–506).
  2. **Equity cannot be separated from efficiency.**
     - Agency problems, such as a landowner differing from the person working the land, depend on the distribution of wealth, so efficiency depends on distribution (p. 506).
     - The second welfare theorem needs lump-sum redistribution. Mirrlees (1971) pointed out that with imperfect information this is impossible and all redistributive taxation is distortionary. So market interventions that change the before-tax distribution of income could be desirable, as they lessen the burden on redistributive taxation (p. 506).
     - Footnote 97: the second theorem also needs other assumptions, e.g. convexity, which may not hold with imperfect, endogenous information (p. 506).
- Markets are also interlinked over time (p. 506).
  - Standard theory says a badly treated employee just moves to another firm. Informational asymmetries impair mobility and partly lock in the employee, or the borrower with his creditor (p. 506).
  - With perfect information and markets, ex ante competition could correct some of the consequences of reduced ex post competition, but there is little reason to believe it does (p. 506).
- Stiglitz's reading of Arrow–Debreu: it identified the single set of assumptions under which markets are Pareto efficient, including that information (beliefs) is not endogenous. In an information economy, a model assuming fixed information seems increasingly irrelevant (p. 506).

### Dysfunction institutions (pp. 506–507)
- Arguments against government intervention were put forward as the case that imperfect-information markets are inefficient grew clearer (p. 506).
  - **Argument 1: government faces informational imperfections too.** Stiglitz replies that government's incentives and constraints differ from the private sector's, so even with identical informational constraints welfare could be improved (p. 507, fn. 98).
  - **Argument 2: non-market institutions (burial societies, families) will solve market failures.** Stiglitz calls this the *functionalist fallacy*: moving from "an institution arises to fulfil a function" to "in equilibrium it serves that function" (p. 507).
- Arnott and Stiglitz (1991) showed non-market institutions could make matters worse (p. 507).
  - Family insurance can crowd out market insurance. Insurers recognise that the insured take less risk and cut the insurance offered. Family institutions divest risk poorly, so welfare falls.
  - Footnote 99: the result depends on what family members can observe. If they have no more information than the insurer, welfare falls. If they have more, the insurer can free-ride on it and welfare could rise.
- Stiglitz's rhetorical question: if markets could be shown to be efficient only under very special circumstances, why expect an equilibrium of markets plus non-market institutions to be efficient? (p. 507)

### Applications of the new paradigm (pp. 507–515)

**The new theory of the firm and the foundations of modern macro-economics (pp. 507–508).**
- The greatest market failure is extended underutilisation of resources, especially human resources. It is worsened by capital-market imperfections: an unemployed person with good prospects cannot borrow to sustain his living standard (p. 507).
- Traditional Keynesian explanations lacked micro-foundations. One of two resulting schools used the old perfect-market paradigm with representative-agent models and rational expectations (p. 507). The text here describes only this school.
- Stiglitz's criticism: a representative-agent model by construction rules out the information asymmetries at the heart of macro problems. In his words, only if an individual (the representative agent) had a severe case of schizophrenia could such problems arise. A model assuming markets clear gives little insight into unemployment (p. 508).
- Building a macro model that embraces information imperfections in labor, product and capital markets has been his major preoccupation for fifteen years. It is hard because each market is complex. At its heart is a new theory of the firm resting on three pillars: corporate finance, corporate governance, organizational design (p. 508).

**The theory of corporate finance (pp. 508–509).**
- Under perfect information, absent tax distortions, debt versus equity does not matter (Modigliani–Miller theorem). Willingness to hold or sell shares conveys information, so how firms raise capital does matter (p. 508).
- Footnote 101: Stiglitz (1969a) showed the result was more general than the original proof suggested. It had one critical assumption, bankruptcy, which Modigliani and Miller ignored (p. 508).
- Firms rely heavily on debt, and bankruptcy matters. Because of bankruptcy costs and limits in designing managerial incentive schemes, firms act risk averse, and the risk concerned is more than correlation with the business cycle (p. 508).
- Footnote 102 on "equity rationing": firms do not use equity to divest risk as perfect-information theories predict. Issuing equity signals that owners and managers think the market has overvalued the shares, and the market responds by lowering the price. So the cost of equity is extremely high (p. 508).
- With credit rationing (or its potential), the firm's net worth matters, and so does its asset structure, including liquidity (p. 509). Footnote 106: the very distinction between illiquidity and insolvency rests on information asymmetries (p. 509).
- Investment example (p. 509):
  - Traditional neoclassical theory says investment depends on the real interest rate and perceived expected returns. Cash flow and net worth should make no difference.
  - Kuh and Meyer (1957) suggested otherwise. But because theory said these variables could not matter, they were excluded from econometric work for two decades after Hall and Jorgenson (1967).
  - Only when asymmetric-information theory restored theoretical respectability were they included. They were then found to be crucial, especially for small and medium-sized firms.
- Traditional theory has no corporate veil: outsiders see what happens inside the firm, so distributing or retaining profit makes no difference (other than taxes). With imperfect information there is a corporate veil that cannot easily be pierced (p. 509).

**Corporate governance (pp. 509–511).**
- Traditional theory: firms maximise the expected present discounted value of profits (equal to market value). With perfect information this is an engineering problem, so disagreement and governance mattered little (p. 509).
- In reality governance matters. Disagreements arise from differing judgments and objectives. Managers can advance their interests at shareholders' expense, and majority shareholders at minority shareholders' (pp. 509–510).
- Owners (the "principal", in Ross's 1973 language) cannot monitor workers and managers (the agents), and typically do not even know what the agents *should* do (p. 510).
- Berle and Means (1932) had noted the consequences of the separation of ownership and control. Information economics supplied a coherent way to think about them (p. 510). Footnote 111 says Marshall (1897) and Adam Smith (1776) had also noted the problem.
- Governance problems arise from information imperfections *and* the public-good nature of oversight: if one shareholder pays for oversight and improves performance, all shareholders benefit equally (p. 510).
- **Takeover argument (held by some defenders of value maximisation):** takeovers, and the threat of them, ensure competition in the market for managers and so stock-market value maximisation (p. 510).
  - Stiglitz raised theoretical doubts early (1972b) (p. 510).
  - Grossman and Hart (1980): a small shareholder who believed the takeover would raise market value would not sell. Only takeovers expected to be value *decreasing* would succeed (p. 510).
  - Edlin and Stiglitz (1995): existing managers can reduce the takeover threat by increasing information asymmetries (p. 510).
- Proving that a firm does not maximise market value is hard, but Stiglitz gives clear instances (p. 511).
  - Closed-end mutual funds regularly sell at a discount, although dissolving the firm would raise market value.
  - There are many "tax paradoxes", actions that would cut the total tax bill (corporate plus individual).
  - Some dispute remains over whether these reflect irrational investors or non-value-maximising managers.

**Organization design (p. 511).**
- With perfect information organization design matters little, but in practice it is central. Information imperfections limit efficient decentralisation. Having alternative units perform comparable tasks lets a firm glean information for better incentive systems (Nalebuff and Stiglitz 1983a, b) (p. 511).
- Even well-intentioned people with limited information make mistakes. Sah and Stiglitz studied how organizational structures handle mistakes (good projects rejected or bad ones accepted). They suggested that in a variety of circumstances, especially when good projects are scarce, decentralised polyarchical structures have distinct advantages (p. 511).

**Macro-economics (pp. 511–513).**
- The central issue is unemployment. The models he described earlier (not in these pages) explain unemployment *in equilibrium*. Macro also concerns dynamics: why the economy seems to amplify shocks and why their effects persist (p. 511).
- Credit and equity rationing, arising from information asymmetries, are key. They lead to risk-averse firms and to households and firms affected by cash-flow constraints (p. 512).
- **Wage and price rigidities (Keynesian interpretations):** these emphasised rigidities without a convincing explanation (p. 512).
  - Adjustment-cost theories (Mankiw 1985; Akerlof and Yellen 1985) leave open why markets adjust quantities rather than prices, when quantity adjustment seems costlier.
  - The Greenwald–Stiglitz (1989b) theory: at least for commodities with reasonably low inventory costs, the risks arising from informational imperfections are greater for price and wage adjustments than for quantity adjustments. Risk-averse firms make smaller adjustments where the consequences are more uncertain.
- Wages and prices are not perfectly flexible, but neither are they perfectly rigid. They fell considerably in the Great Depression, and earlier periods and other countries had large fluctuations with high flexibility (p. 512).
- Greenwald and Stiglitz argued that other market failures, notably capital-market imperfections and incomplete contracting, explain part of the key macro phenomena (p. 512).
  - Debt contracts are typically not indexed to prices. When prices fall below the expected level (or real interest rates rise above it), wealth is transferred from debtors to creditors. So excessive downward price flexibility, not just rigidity, could cause problems (Fisher 1933 emphasised these phenomena, fn. 117).
  - These redistributive changes had large real effects and could not be insured against because of capital-market imperfections (p. 512).
  - Large shocks can lead to bankruptcy and, especially with liquidation, a loss of organizational and informational capital (pp. 512–513).
  - Even if such changes are forestalled, the firm's access to credit is impaired until resolution, "for good reason". Without "clear owners" those in control lack incentives to maximise value (p. 513).
  - Footnote 116: the theories do not fully explain incomplete contracting. Part may lie in non-verifiability of variables. Asymmetric information helps explain why inefficient contracts persist: a party proposing a change to a standard contract may be inferred to be the one who benefits, and in a near zero-sum world the other side resists (p. 512).
- Even shocks large enough to lead to bankruptcy affect firms' ability and willingness to take risk (p. 513).
  - All production is risky, so shocks affect aggregate supply as well as investment demand. Net worth is restored only over time, so effects persist (p. 513).
  - There are hysteresis effects: an interest-rate rise that depleted net worth has effects even after rates are reduced. If firms are credit rationed, reductions in liquidity can have particularly marked effects (p. 513).
  - The theories help explain the "anomalous" behavior of inventories, which move pro-cyclically because cash constraints give money a high shadow price in recessions (p. 513).
  - In recessions the shadow price of capital is high, firms invest less in acquiring customers and worry less about losing workers, so mark-ups rise. Real product wages could fall even though the marginal productivity of labor is rising (p. 513).
- Summary: the analysis emphasises supply-side effects of shocks, the interrelations between supply and demand effects, and the importance of finance in propagating fluctuations (p. 513).

**Theory of money (pp. 513–514).**
- Traditional view: the interest rate equates demand and supply for money, mainly for transactions, with the rate as opportunity cost (p. 513).
- Stiglitz's view: in modern economies credit, not money, is required for most transactions. Most transactions are exchanges of assets and not directly related to GDP. Most money bears interest, and the gap between, say, a money-market rate and T-bill rates reflects transactions costs, not monetary policy (p. 513).
- What matters is the availability of credit and its terms, tied to banks' and other institutions' certification of creditworthiness. Information is at the heart of monetary economics (pp. 513–514).
- Banks are like other risk-averse firms: their ability and willingness to bear lending risk depends on net worth (fn. 121). Because of equity rationing, shocks to bank net worth cannot be instantly undone, so they can have large adverse macro consequences (p. 514).
- Stiglitz says the theory shows how traditional instruments (reserve requirements) and regulatory instruments (risk-adjusted capital adequacy requirements) can affect credit supply, interest rates charged and bank risk portfolios. Excessive reliance on capital adequacy requirements could be counterproductive (p. 514).
- Policy implications (p. 514):
  - A new basis for a "liquidity trap": in severe downturns monetary policy may be relatively ineffective.
  - It explains some recent policy failures, the Fed's inability to forestall the 1991 recession and the IMF's failures in East Asia in 1997.
  - It shifts emphasis from the Fed Funds rate or money supply to the level of credit and interest rates charged to firms, and explains the spread between those rates and the Federal Funds rate.
  - It predicts scope for monetary policy even under dollarization.
- Credit interlinkages: many firms both receive and give credit (violating Polonius' injunction against being both lender and borrower). The dispersed nature of information explains this. A shock to one firm is transmitted to others, and a large enough shock can cause a cascade of bankruptcies (p. 514).

**Growth and development (pp. 514–515).**
- Capital markets are important for growth. Without them firms rely on retained earnings. "Equity rationing", especially in developing countries, impedes firms' willingness to invest and take risks and slows growth (p. 515).
- Policies that let firms bear more risk (e.g. smaller macro fluctuations) or that enhance their equity base (by suppressing interest rates so profits are larger) enhance growth. IMF-style policies with very high interest rates discourage debt and force reliance on retained earnings (p. 515).
- Market failures are typically more prevalent in less-developed countries and often tied to information problems. This explains failures of policies that assumed well-functioning markets and also points to remedies (p. 515).

**Research (p. 515).**
- One of the most important determinants of the pace of growth is research investment (developed countries) and efforts at closing the knowledge gap (less-developed countries). Knowledge is a form of information, so appropriability, the fixed costs of research (which cause imperfect competition) and information's public-good nature matter (p. 515).
- Stiglitz's papers on industry equilibrium and growth (fn. 128 notes several precursors to what is now called endogenous growth theory) give one conclusion that stands out: economies where research and innovation matter are not well described by the standard competitive model, and market equilibrium without government intervention is not in general efficient (p. 515).

### Policy frameworks (pp. 516–517)
- Since markets with asymmetries are not in general constrained Pareto efficient, government has a potentially important role. The implications go beyond preventing or overcoming asymmetries, since policy must also handle indirect consequences (missing markets, capital-market imperfections, risk-averse and cash-constrained firms). For example, tax-incidence analysis based on perfect competition, perfectly informed consumers and risk-neutral firms is likely to go astray (p. 516).

**Pareto efficient taxation (pp. 516–517).**
- Asymmetries also arise between citizens and government. The problem of citizens controlling government is for the final section of the paper (not in these pages). Here Stiglitz notes the problems government faces in taxation, regulation and production (p. 516). Only taxation and regulation are discussed in these pages.
- Government must raise revenue and may want to redistribute, but it cannot easily identify who is better able to pay. The self-selection mechanisms used for insurance and discriminating monopoly can be used here (p. 516).
  - The government's problem of maximising social "profit" (welfare) subject to information constraints is closely analogous to the monopolist's problem of maximising private profit subject to such constraints (p. 516).
  - This is why Mirrlees' (1971) optimal-taxation paper was a precursor (p. 516).
- The critical question for tax design is *what is observable* (pp. 516–517).
  - Under perfect information, lump-sum taxes and redistribution make sense. If ability is unobservable, government infers from other observables such as income, and people alter their behavior as they recognise this (p. 517).
  - Mirrlees assumed only income observable. Other cases: hours worked (so wages observable), quantity of each good purchased by a particular individual, or only aggregate quantities produced (p. 517).
- For each information structure there is a Pareto-efficient tax structure (no one or group can gain without another losing). The choice among them depends on the social welfare function (attitudes to inequality) (p. 517).
- Two results (p. 517):
  - Optimal commodity taxes in the Ramsey (1927) sense are part of a Pareto-efficient tax system only under highly restricted conditions, e.g. no income tax (Sah and Stiglitz 1992).
  - In a central benchmark case, it is not optimal to tax interest income.
- Footnote 130: Mirrlees' work confounded the two stages of analysis. The zero marginal tax rate at the top is a property of *any* Pareto-efficient structure, but it is not robust, since it depends on his assumption that relative wages across ability types are fixed (p. 517).

**Theory of regulation and privatization (p. 517).**
- Government faces in regulation the same information-asymmetry problem as in taxation. Over the past quarter century a huge literature using self-selection mechanisms has allowed far better regulation than before (p. 517).
- In the 1980s there was a strong movement to privatise state enterprises, even natural monopolies, replacing ownership with regulation. Problems with government ownership were frequent. But imperfect-information theory made clear that even the best-designed regulatory systems work imperfectly, which raised the question of when privatization could be sure to improve welfare (p. 517).
- The text breaks off at the page end, as Stiglitz begins citing Simon (1991) (p. 517).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
*Aim: show how an action can separate types by self-selection (pp. 496, 499).* This version simplifies by assuming schooling adds no productivity.

- Low type: productivity 10, cost of each year of schooling 2. High type: productivity 20, cost 1 per year.
- Employers see only years $e$. They pay 20 if $e \ge e^*$ and 10 otherwise.
- **Low type's choice.** With no schooling the payoff is $10-0=10$. By mimicking at $e^*$ it is $20-2e^*$. Mimicking does not pay if $20-2e^* \le 10$, i.e. $e^*\ge 5$. At $e^*=5$ the low type is exactly indifferent.
- **High type's choice.** Schooling to $e^*$ gives $20-e^*$. This is at least 10 if $e^*\le 10$.
- So every $e^*$ with $5 < e^* < 10$ strictly separates the types (the endpoints are ties). Example $e^*=6$: low mimicking gets $20-12=8<10$, so he stays at 0. High gets $20-6=14>10$, so he schools. This matches the idea that many separating schedules exist (p. 499).
- The *least* costly separating level is $e^*$ just above 5, where the high type gets just under $15$. This parallels the "lowest level … such that the low ability still prefers to remain" rule (p. 499).
- If $e^*=3$, the low type who mimics gets $20-6=14>10$, so he "lies" and separation fails (p. 496).
- The toy has no competing firms offering rival schedules, so it does not show the existence problem of the insurance case (p. 499).

## Concepts
- [[self-selection]] (new) — people reveal type by choosing among options designed so that different types choose differently (p. 496)
- [[screening]] — the uninformed side designs choices or tests to sort types (p. 498)
- [[signaling]] — the informed side takes the initiative to show he is a better risk, employee or borrower (p. 498)
- [[separating vs pooling equilibrium]] — types are distinguished by actions vs types buy the same contract. Here, pooling never exists and separating may not exist in the competitive insurance model (p. 497)
- [[asymmetric information]] — one side knows more. Here it is only one basis of self-selection (p. 496)
- [[adverse selection]] — Stiglitz says the 1976 analysis reinforced earlier results that such markets do not function well (p. 498)
- [[moral hazard]] — care cannot be observed. Each person ignores his effect on the average-risk premium, giving a negative externality (p. 505)
- [[market failure / externality]] — Stiglitz says externality-like effects are pervasive under imperfect information or incomplete markets (p. 505)
- [[constrained Pareto efficiency]] (new) — no one could be made better off even after taking absent or imperfect markets into account (p. 505)
- [[non-decentralizability]] (new) — efficient allocations cannot in general be reached by decentralised decisions with imperfect information (p. 505)
- [[Nash equilibrium]] — in the $100-bill example, waiting is not one because each could gain by deviating (p. 504)
- [[incomplete contracts]] — contracts not indexed or contingent as theory predicts. Stiglitz offers only partial explanations (p. 512, fn. 116)
- [[efficiency wages]] (new) — it pays firms to pay more than they must, e.g. to reduce labor turnover costs (p. 502)
- [[price dispersion under search costs]] (new) — small search costs give monopoly prices (Diamond) or a price distribution (Salop–Stiglitz) (p. 503)
- [[credit and equity rationing]] (new) — firms do not rely on equity to divest risk, and credit availability depends on net worth. Issuing equity sends a bad signal (pp. 508–509, 512)
- [[functionalist fallacy]] (new) — assuming an institution that arises for a function serves it in equilibrium (p. 507)
- [[agency problem / corporate governance]] (new) — owners cannot monitor or know what agents should do. Oversight is a public good (p. 510)
- [[Pareto efficient tax structure]] (new) — tax structure where no group can gain without another losing. It depends on what is observable (p. 517)

## Canonical instances
- [[rothschild-stiglitz-insurance]] — competitive insurance equilibrium with no pooling equilibrium and a possibly missing separating one (p. 497)
- [[spence-education-signaling]] — two ability types, separating schooling levels, continuum case with no screening equilibrium (pp. 499, fn. 73)
- [[diamond-epsilon-search-cost]] — an $\epsilon$ search cost drives all firms to the monopoly price (p. 503)
- [[hundred-dollar-bills-classroom]] — information acquired earlier can be purely redistributive, with a social cost (p. 504)
- [[sharecropping-contracts]] — payments tied to observables, interlinked markets (pp. 500–501)
- [[family-insurance-crowding-out]] — non-market insurance lowers welfare when it crowds out market insurance (p. 507)
- [[smoking-complement-taxes]] — government can tax complements and subsidise substitutes when smoking is unobservable (p. 505)
- [[closed-end-fund-discount]] — a clear case of a firm not maximising market value (p. 511)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Moral-hazard externality (each takes less care, the premium rises) ↔ social dilemma: individually rational choices produce a collectively worse outcome (p. 505).
- [structural-analogy] Government maximising welfare under information constraints ↔ discriminating monopolist maximising profit under information constraints. Stiglitz himself calls them "closely analogous" (p. 516).
- [structural-analogy] Credit interlinkages and cascades of bankruptcies ↔ financial instability and bank-run contagion (p. 514).
- [structural-analogy] Gains from information a nano-second earlier being redistributive ↔ Grossman–Stiglitz paradox on incentives to acquire information (p. 504).
- [metaphor] Debtor-to-creditor transfers when prices fall below expectations ↔ debt deflation (the text cites Fisher 1933 but does not use the term) (p. 512).

## Questions this raises
- If competitive insurance equilibrium often fails to exist, what notion of equilibrium should replace it, and does moving first (screening vs signaling) settle it (pp. 497–498)?
- Stiglitz suspects monopoly or monopolistic competition may be more relevant for insurance than perfect competition (p. 500). What evidence would separate this from the selection story?
- If real contracts are much simpler than theory predicts (p. 501), what limits contract complexity?
- When can privatization be sure to raise welfare, given that even the best regulation works imperfectly (p. 517)?
