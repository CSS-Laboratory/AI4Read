# Stiglitz, J. E. (2002). Information and the Change in the Paradigm in Economics. *American Economic Review*, 92(3), 460–501. https://doi.org/10.1257/00028280260136363 (Nobel Prize lecture delivered 8 December 2001.)

## In one paragraph
Stiglitz argues that the standard competitive model assumes perfect information, and that this assumption is not harmless. Even small information imperfections break its main "laws": markets need not clear, there is no single price, price need not equal marginal cost, and markets are almost never Pareto efficient. Because actions and prices carry information, people screen, signal and self-select. Prices also change the quality of who takes part or how they behave, which can produce credit rationing and unemployment. He applies this to contracts, finance, macroeconomics, growth and policy. He claims that advice based on the perfect-information model, such as the Washington Consensus, IMF crisis management and rapid privatization, often failed. He ends by suggesting that the old paradigm survived partly because it served certain interests.

## The path through the work
- [[stiglitz-2002-paradigm/main-p1]] — Stiglitz argues that even small information imperfections undermine the core "laws" of the competitive-equilibrium model (market clearing, the single price, price equal to marginal cost, informationally efficient stock markets), and presents information economics as a fundamental change in the prevailing paradigm.
- [[stiglitz-2002-paradigm/main-p2]] — Once actions are seen to convey information, markets can sort people by self-selection (their choices reveal their type), competitive equilibrium becomes fragile, and Stiglitz argues that market failures are pervasive, so the old efficiency-versus-distribution split and the price-equals-marginal-cost rule fail.
- [[stiglitz-2002-paradigm/main-p3]] — Stiglitz argues that policies built on the perfect-information competitive model (Washington Consensus, IMF crisis management, rapid privatization) often failed, and that information economics, extended to history, change and politics, helps explain why.

## Big ideas across units
- **The perfect-information model is not robust.** Small departures overturn market clearing, the single price, price = marginal cost and efficient markets. Information is not just a transactions cost. Units: main-p1, main-p2, main-p3.
- **Actions and prices carry information.** This leads to screening, signaling and self-selection. It also means a pooling equilibrium may fail to exist in insurance, and a separating one may not exist. Units: main-p1, main-p2.
- **Price affects "quality."** Adverse selection and adverse incentives lead to credit rationing and equilibrium unemployment (efficiency wages). Units: main-p1, main-p2, main-p3 (credit and bankruptcy in East Asia).
- **Market failure is pervasive, and efficiency and distribution cannot be separated.** Markets are essentially never constrained Pareto efficient, and efficient solutions cannot in general be decentralised. The invisible hand may be absent or "palsied." Units: main-p1, main-p2, main-p3.
- **Information explains institutions and applied fields.** These include richer contracts (sharecropping, collateral), the theory of the firm, corporate governance, macro fluctuations, money, growth and research. Units: main-p1, main-p2, main-p3.
- **Policy built on the wrong model fails.** The examples are taxation and regulation design, privatization, the Washington Consensus, the IMF in East Asia, and Russia. Units: main-p2, main-p3.
- **Beyond the equilibrium picture.** Multiple equilibria, hysteresis and evolutionary processes appear as further deficiencies of the old paradigm. Units: main-p1, main-p3.
- **The politics of information.** Disclosure rules are contested, and secrecy creates rents for officials. The old paradigm may have survived partly because of the interests it served. Units: main-p3.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** main-p1 and main-p2. They contain the core argument. Part 2 picks up exactly where part 1 stops, in the middle of "actions convey information," so read them in order.
- **Applications:** main-p3 depends on both. Its policy discussion continues the privatization passage that begins in main-p2's "Policy frameworks," and it uses ideas such as principal–agent problems, credit and bankruptcy, and asymmetric information. It is essential if your interest is policy or political economy.
- **Skippable on a first pass:** the long list of applications in main-p2 (firm theory, money, growth, research) can be skimmed. The references in main-p3 (pp. 525–540) can be skipped.
