# Information and the Change in the Paradigm in Economics — part 3: policy debates, going beyond information economics, politics of information

## L0 — One line
Stiglitz argues that policies built on the perfect-information competitive model (Washington Consensus, IMF crisis management, rapid privatization) often failed, and that information economics, extended to history, change and politics, helps explain why.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part applies Stiglitz's information paradigm to policy and then looks beyond it. He links the failures of Washington Consensus advice, of IMF handling of the 1997 East Asia crisis, and of rapid privatization in Russia to a competitive model with perfect information. He then lists further weaknesses of the old paradigm: fixed technology and preferences, and his doubt that equilibrium models describe change well. Next he discusses how information shapes politics: transparency rules are contested, and secrecy gives officials rents. He closes by suggesting the old paradigm may have survived partly because it served certain interests. The references begin on p. 525 and run to p. 540.

**Prerequisites:** Pareto efficiency; competitive equilibrium; asymmetric information; principal–agent problem; bankruptcy and leverage (debt relative to assets); the idea of multiple equilibria.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Privatization need not improve welfare. Stiglitz and Sappington showed that the conditions under which it would necessarily be welfare enhancing are extremely restrictive, closely akin to those under which competitive markets are Pareto efficient (p. 518).
- Washington Consensus policies (development advice that prevailed in the international economic institutions from the early 1980s) rest on "market fundamentalism", a simplistic view of competitive markets with perfect information. Stiglitz says it is not surprising that policies based on models so far from reality so often led to failure (p. 518).
- In East Asia, Stiglitz's earlier work stressed that credit and bankruptcy matter for aggregate supply. In his account, high IMF interest rates forced bankruptcies, and hysteresis (effects that persist after their cause is removed) meant lowering rates later did not undo them. He says Malaysia, closer to what his theories suggested, recovered faster than Thailand (pp. 518–519).
- Russia's privatization strategy paid little attention to corporate governance (rules that make managers answer to owners). Stiglitz says it led to asset stripping and wealth destruction, and empirical work he cites finds that countries that privatized rapidly without good governance did not grow faster (p. 519).
- The information paradigm is robust: general principles hold while details vary by case. Changing just one plausible assumption broke the standard theory (p. 520).
- Further deficiencies of the old paradigm: fixed technology and preferences, ignored psychological and social factors, and a possible poor fit between equilibrium models and the dynamics of change. Information economics points to hysteresis, multiple equilibria and evolutionary processes (pp. 520–522).
- Disclosure rules ("information rules of the game") are political. Positions on transparency shifted with the interests involved (p. 522).
- Politics has principal–agent problems with limited exit. Officials have strong incentives to reduce transparency; secrecy is an artificial scarcity of information that creates rents (income from that scarcity). Right-to-know laws are an imperfect remedy (pp. 523–524).
- The old paradigm's survival may partly reflect the interests it served (p. 524).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the privatization discussion (p. 518)
This part begins mid-sentence. The text first notes that there was no compelling theoretical argument for why large private organizations would solve incentive problems better (p. 518).
- With David Sappington (1987b), Stiglitz showed that the conditions under which privatization would *necessarily* be welfare enhancing were extremely restrictive (p. 518).
- These conditions were closely akin to those under which competitive markets would give Pareto efficient outcomes (p. 518). Pareto efficient means no one can be made better off without making someone worse off.

*Intuition note.* Privatization is no automatic cure. Showing it must improve welfare takes conditions closely akin to those needed for markets to be Pareto efficient. If those fail, privatization may not help.

### Key policy debates: applying basic ideas (p. 518)
Stiglitz states that the new information paradigm shaped theoretical approaches to policy. In many concrete issues it also led to policy stances markedly different from those of the old paradigm (p. 518).

**Washington consensus.**
- The development controversies concerned Washington Consensus policies. These were based on "market fundamentalism", which he defines as the simplistic view of competitive markets with perfect information (p. 518).
- He calls this view inappropriate even for developed countries and particularly inappropriate for developing ones. It had prevailed in the international economic institutions since the early 1980s (p. 518).
- He says he has documented the failures of these policies in development, in the transition from Communism, and in crisis management (footnotes 133–134, p. 518).
- His conclusion: it is not surprising that policies based on models departing so far from reality often led to failure (p. 518).

**Bankruptcy, aggregate supply, and the East Asia crisis** (the crisis began in Thailand on July 2, 1997).
- *What the theory said.* Stiglitz's earlier work stressed maintaining the credit supply and the risks of poorly managed bankruptcy. Poorly designed policies could cut credit unnecessarily and raise bankruptcies unnecessarily. Both would hurt aggregate supply and worsen the downturn (p. 518).
- *What the IMF did, in his account.* It raised interest rates to extremely high levels in countries where firms were already highly leveraged. This forced massive bankruptcy, and the economies fell into deep recession and depression. Capital fled instead of being attracted (p. 518).
- He says the policies even failed in their stated purpose, stabilizing the exchange rate (p. 518).
- *Hysteresis* (effects persisting after the cause is removed). When rates were later lowered, bankrupt firms did not become "unbankrupt". Firms with depleted net worth did not see immediate restoration (p. 519).
- *Alternatives.* Debt standstills followed by corporate financial restructurings might not have avoided a downturn, but would have made it shallower and shorter (p. 519).
- *Comparison.* Malaysia's policies conformed much more closely to what Stiglitz's theories would have suggested. It recovered more quickly and had less debt legacy to impair future growth than neighboring Thailand. Thailand conformed more closely to the IMF's recommendation (p. 519).

*Intuition note.* In Stiglitz's account, very high interest rates forced bankruptcies and depleted firms' net worth, and lowering rates later neither undid the bankruptcies nor restored net worth immediately. He points to debt standstills followed by corporate financial restructuring as an alternative that would have made the downturn shallower and shorter.

**Corporate governance, open capital markets, and the transition to a market economy.**
- Stiglitz calls the transition from communism to a market economy one of the most important economic experiments of all time. He treats Russia's failure (so far) and China's successes as informative (p. 519).
- *Expectation versus result.* Central planning, he says, would have needed more information gathering, processing and dissemination than any technology could manage. It also lacked incentives and was full of distortions, so it was seen as highly inefficient. The market was assumed to bring enormous income gains (p. 519).
- Instead, incomes plummeted. This was confirmed by GDP statistics, household surveys and social indicators. Poverty went from 2% to upwards of 50%, depending on the measure (p. 519).
- Among many dimensions of failure, one stands out. The privatization strategy paid little attention to corporate governance (p. 519).
- Empirical work (Stiglitz 2001e) confirms that countries that privatized rapidly but lacked "good" corporate governance did not grow more rapidly (p. 519).
- The Sappington–Stiglitz paper had warned that privatization might not raise social welfare. Instead of creating wealth, it led to asset stripping and wealth destruction (p. 519).

*Intuition note.* Without rules that make managers answer to owners, selling state assets can reward people for stripping them. The incentive problems of information economics apply to ownership changes too.

### Beyond information economics (pp. 519–522)

**Robustness of the paradigm.**
- Stiglitz states that the competitive paradigm, dominant for two centuries, was not robust, did not explain key phenomena, and led to misguided policy prescriptions (p. 519).
- His research over the past thirty years has focused on only one aspect of his dissatisfaction with the paradigm. He thought the best way to attack the paradigm was to stay within the standard framework as much as possible. He therefore varied only the perfect-information assumption, in plausible ways (pp. 519–520).
- **Objection (early critics):** opening the model to imperfect information was opening a Pandora's box, because information can be imperfect in so many ways (p. 520).
- **Stiglitz replies:** there might be only one way information is perfect, but surely it is better to understand the consequences of *different* forms of imperfection that might exist in the real world. If the competitive model was not robust against all these different forms of imperfection that existed in the world, surely it was not a model upon which we could rely (p. 520).
- Over time, the imperfect-information paradigm proved itself highly robust. There were quite general principles, though the detailed workings of models might differ across situations (p. 520).
- Stiglitz says they showed two things. First, the standard theory was not robust, since changing one assumption in totally plausible ways had drastic consequences. Second, an alternative robust paradigm with great explanatory power could be built (p. 520).

*Intuition note.* A good theory should survive small, realistic changes to its assumptions. Stiglitz says the perfect-information model fails this test, while the information paradigm gives stable general lessons even when details differ.

**Other deficiencies.**
- *Fixed technology and preferences.* The standard theory assumed both were fixed. Stiglitz says technological change and R&D are at the heart of capitalism. The new information economics, extended to include changes in knowledge, at last began to address these foundations systematically (p. 520).
- *Fixed preferences and development.* Thinking about development, he became convinced that fixed preferences are inappropriate. He criticizes Washington Consensus strategies partly because they saw development as only raising the capital stock and reducing distortions. In his view development is a far more fundamental transformation of society, including changes in "preferences" and attitudes, acceptance of change, and abandonment of traditional ways of thinking (p. 520).
- *Rational beliefs (footnote 138).* Much recent economic theory assumes beliefs are in some sense rational. As noted earlier in the paper, many aspects of economic behavior seem hard to reconcile with this hypothesis (p. 520).
- *Economics separated from social concerns.* He feels this more strongly after deeper immersion in development problems. He cites civil strife in Africa as a major impediment to development, itself partly a consequence of economic circumstances (p. 520).

**Policy implications (pp. 520–521).**
- Some policies are more conducive than others to a development transformation. He calls many IMF policies counterproductive, including how it interacted with governments and its basing of loans on conditionality (p. 520).
- A fundamental change in development strategy at the World Bank, during the years he was there, embraced this more comprehensive approach (pp. 520–521).
- Policies that ignored social consequences have frequently been disastrous. Example: in Indonesia the IMF policies eliminated food and fuel subsidies for the very poor just as the country was falling into depression, with wages plummeting and unemployment soaring. This predictably led to riots, and the economic consequences are still felt (p. 521).

**Psychology and the efficiency wage (p. 521).**
- Stiglitz says he is returning to a theme from 30 years earlier, his efficiency-wage work in Kenya. There he suggested that psychological factors, such as morale from a sense of receiving a fair wage, could affect effort.
- He presents this as an alternative and, in some cases, more persuasive reason for the efficiency wage theory. It was later developed further by Akerlof and Yellen (1986) (p. 521). Efficiency wage theory says that paying above-market wages can raise worker productivity.
- He finds it curious that economists have almost studiously ignored such factors, even though they are central to daily life and to business school education (p. 521).
- His argument: surely, if markets were efficient, firms would not give such attention to corporate culture and extrinsic rewards unless these matters were of considerable importance. And if they are important within a firm, he says they are equally important within a society (p. 521).

**Dynamics: history, multiple equilibria, evolution (p. 521).**
- Stiglitz states he is convinced the dynamics of change *may not* be well described by the equilibrium models long central to economics (p. 521).
- Information economics alerted us that history matters and that there are important hysteresis effects. Random events, such as the black plague, have irreversible consequences (p. 521).
- Dynamics may be better described by evolutionary processes than by equilibrium processes. Such processes are hard to describe fully, but there is no reason to believe they are "optimal" in any general sense (p. 521; footnote 143 lists the evolutionary-economics literature and says some of it stresses capital constraints).
- The same themes as in the simpler information-theoretic models recur here. In those models, multiple equilibria, some Pareto dominating others, could easily arise. So too in this setting (Stiglitz 1995b) (p. 521).
- Three consequences follow (pp. 521–522):
  1. One cannot simply predict where the economy will be by knowing preferences and technology (and initial endowments). There can be a high level of indeterminacy (p. 521).
  2. As in Darwinian ecological models, the major determinant of one's environment is the behavior of others. Their behavior may in turn depend on their beliefs about others' behavior (Hoff and Stiglitz 2000) (p. 521).
  3. Government intervention can *sometimes* move the economy from one equilibrium to another. Having done that, continued intervention *might* not be required (p. 522).
- To support the second point, Stiglitz quotes Darwin on the Galapagos. Island plants and animals differ greatly although the islands share geology, height and climate. Darwin says the puzzle arises in chief part from the deeply seated error of treating a country's physical conditions as the most important for its inhabitants. The other inhabitants, with which each species competes, are at least as important and generally far more important for success (p. 522; the printed quote is awkwardly worded, see Reading notes).

*Intuition note.* If several stable outcomes exist, knowing the "fundamentals" is not enough to say which one occurs. A temporary push can move the economy to a better outcome, and it may then stay there without further help. Our toy example below shows this with numbers.

### The political economy of information (pp. 522–524)
Stiglitz states that information imperfections and asymmetries are pervasive in every aspect of life and society. He says he will discuss three ways information affects political processes (p. 522). Only the first is labeled "First" in the text. I group the later passages as I read them (see Reading notes).

**1. Disclosure rules are political (p. 522).**
- The distributive consequences of disclosure were noted earlier. So the "information rules of the game", for the economy and for politics, can become subjects of intense political debate (p. 522).
- The US and the IMF argued strongly that lack of transparency was at the root of the 1997 crisis, and that East Asian countries had to become more transparent (p. 522).
- Stiglitz says the IMF and US Treasury's demand for quantitative data on capital flows (outstanding loans) could have been taken as a concession that the competitive paradigm, in which prices convey all relevant information, is inappropriate. He argues the more appropriate view of the debate is that it was *political* (p. 522).
- Stiglitz says the political reading became clear when it was noted that partial disclosures could be of only limited value and could possibly be counterproductive, as capital would be induced to move through channels with less disclosure, such as less-regulated offshore banking centers (p. 522).
- When demands for transparency extended to Western hedge funds and offshore banking centers, advocates "became less enthralled". They began praising the advantages of partial secrecy for strengthening incentives to gather information (p. 522).
- The US and US Treasury then opposed an OECD initiative to fight money laundering through greater transparency of offshore centers. Stiglitz says these institutions served particular political and economic interests. The Treasury changed position when it became clear that terrorists might be using them to finance operations (p. 522).

**2. Asymmetric information and delegation in politics (pp. 522–523).**
- Political processes inevitably involve asymmetries of information: leaders are *supposed* to know more about defense threats, the economy and so on than ordinary citizens do. Responsibility for day-to-day decisions is delegated, as within a firm (pp. 522–523).
- The problem is to give incentives to those entrusted to act for those they serve. This is the standard principal–agent problem (p. 523).
- Democracy, meaning contestability of political processes, checks abuses of delegated power, as it does in economic processes (p. 523).
- Stiglitz draws a parallel with the corporate takeover mechanism. The takeover mechanism is an imperfect check, so too is the electoral process (p. 523).
- Current management has an incentive to *increase* information asymmetries to strengthen its market power and discretion. So do officials in public life (p. 523).
- Disclosure requirements (more transparency) and specific rules of the game (e.g. on corporate governance) affect how well the takeover mechanism works and the quality of corporate governance. The same factors affect political contestability and the quality of public governance (p. 523).
- Where "exit" options are limited, abuses need special concern. If a firm is mismanaged, customers can at least switch. In politics, those who see public services deteriorate cannot leave as easily (p. 523).
- If people were as mean-spirited and selfish as economists have traditionally modeled them, matters would be bleak. Stiglitz recalls his own line that ensuring the public good (public management) is itself a public good (p. 523).
- But he says there is a wealth of evidence that the traditional model of the individual is too narrow. Intrinsic rewards, such as those of public service, can be even more effective than extrinsic ones, such as money. This does not mean compensation is unimportant (p. 523).
- Public-spiritedness, even if mixed with a modicum of self-interest, shows in civil society organizations, where individuals voluntarily work collectively for their perception of the collective interest (p. 523).

**3. Forces for secrecy (pp. 523–524).**
- Those in government have strong incentives to reduce transparency. It exposes mistakes and also corruption. He uses the saying "sunshine is the strongest antiseptic" (p. 523).
- Officials may advance specious arguments for secrecy and then say, in effect, "trust me … if you only knew what I knew" (p. 523). Footnote 144 cites Senator Moynihan (1998), who shows secrecy was abused during the Cold War in ways that led to unnecessarily large military expenditures (p. 523).
- A further rationale: secrecy is an artificially created scarcity of information. Like most such scarcities, it gives rise to rents (p. 523).
- These rents are collected through outright corruption (selling information) in some countries. In others they are part of a "gift exchange". Reporters give puff pieces praising an official who gave them privileged access, in ways designed to enhance the official's influence but distorting news coverage (pp. 523–524).
- Stiglitz says he watched this process work closely and "quite effectively" (p. 524).
- Without unbiased information, the check that citizens can provide is limited. Without good information, political contestability can be undermined (p. 524).
- *Remedies.* A lesson of information economics is that these problems cannot be fully resolved, but laws and institutions can decidedly improve matters (p. 524).
  - Right-to-know laws demanding transparency have been part of governance in Sweden for two hundred years.
  - They have become an important but imperfect check on government abuses in the US over the past quarter century.
  - In the last five years there has been a growing international movement, with some countries, such as Thailand, including them in their new constitution.
  - Regrettably, he says, these principles have yet to be endorsed by the international economic institutions.

*Intuition note.* Without unbiased information, the check that citizens can provide is limited. Officials have strong incentives to reduce transparency, so disclosure requirements can affect how well political contestability works, and disclosure rules are themselves fought over.

### Concluding remarks (p. 524–525)
- Stiglitz says the talk traced the replacement of one paradigm by another. The neoclassical paradigm's deficiencies were of two kinds: predictions that seemed counter to what was observed (some so glaring that refined econometric testing was hardly needed), and phenomena left unexplained. They made it "inevitable" that it would be challenged (p. 524).
- *Why it persisted so long.* Partly, he says, because it did provide insights into many economic phenomena (p. 524).
- Some markets exist where the phenomena discussed are not important, such as wheat or corn. Even there, pervasive government intervention limits the relevance of the competitive paradigm (p. 524).
- Demand and supply forces remain important. In the new paradigm they are only part of the analysis, not the whole of it (p. 524).
- He adds that one cannot ignore the possibility that the paradigm survived partly because belief in it, and its policy prescriptions, served certain interests (p. 524).
- *Personal remarks.* He says he tried to follow the analysis wherever it leads, and knows that ideas can be used, abused or ignored (p. 524). He quotes Shakespeare's line that all the world's a stage, and says that each of us, if only as a voter, is an actor in this drama. What we do is affected by our perceptions of how this complex system works (p. 524).
- He says he entered economics hoping to do something about unemployment, poverty and discrimination. He feels lucky to have hit upon some ideas that he thinks do enhance understanding of these phenomena. As an educator, he says he has had the opportunity to reduce some asymmetries of information and to have had first-rate students who pushed the research agenda forward. As a public servant, in the American government and at the World Bank, he has had the chance to put ideas into practice himself (pp. 524–525).
- He ends by noting that we live in democracies and that academics are protected by academic freedom, and that with freedom comes responsibility: to use that freedom to do what we can to ensure the future world has greater economic prosperity and also more social justice (p. 525).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
*Idea shown: with multiple equilibria, a temporary policy can move an economy from a bad equilibrium to a good one, and continued intervention may then not be needed (p. 522, a conditional claim).*

Setup: 10 firms each choose Invest or Stay out. Staying out pays 0. If k *other* firms invest, a firm that invests gets $k - 4.5$.

1. *Best response.* Invest if $k - 4.5 > 0$, i.e. $k \ge 5$ (since $k$ is an integer). Otherwise stay out.
2. *Equilibrium "none invest".* k = 0 for every firm. Investing would pay $0 - 4.5 = -4.5 < 0$. So staying out is a best response. This is an equilibrium with payoff 0 each.
3. *Equilibrium "all invest".* Each firm sees k = 9. Investing pays $9 - 4.5 = 4.5 > 0$. Deviating to Stay out pays 0. This is an equilibrium with payoff 4.5 each. It Pareto dominates the first (4.5 > 0 for everyone).
4. *Intermediate states are not stable.* Suppose 3 firms invest. An investor sees k = 2 and gets $2 - 4.5 = -2.5$, so leaves. A non-investor would see k = 3 if she joined and get $3 - 4.5 = -1.5$, so stays out. The economy slides to "none invest".
5. *Temporary subsidy.* Pay each investor 5 for one period. Investing pays $k - 4.5 + 5 = k + 0.5$. This is positive for every k ≥ 0, so every firm invests whatever others do. All 10 invest.
6. *Remove the subsidy.* We are back at step 3. Each firm still sees k = 9, gets 4.5 by investing and 0 by deviating, so no one leaves. The economy stays at the good equilibrium with no further spending.
7. *Caution.* If the subsidy had paid only 3 (investing pays $k - 1.5$), a firm invests only when $k \ge 2$. With nobody investing yet (k = 0), nobody would invest, and the push would fail. A push must be large enough for the example to work.

## Concepts
- [[market-fundamentalism]] (new) — Stiglitz's term for the simplistic view of competitive markets with perfect information; basis of Washington Consensus policies (p. 518)
- [[washington-consensus]] (new) — development policies based on market fundamentalism, dominant in the international economic institutions since the early 1980s; criticized by Stiglitz as often failing (p. 518)
- [[hysteresis]] (new) — effects that persist after their cause is removed; e.g. bankrupt firms did not recover when rates fell (p. 519; p. 521)
- [[corporate-governance]] (new) — rules and incentives that make managers answer to owners; its neglect in privatization led to asset stripping (p. 519)
- [[asymmetric information]] — one party knows more than another; Stiglitz says it is pervasive and shapes politics as well as markets (p. 522)
- [[coordination game / multiple equilibria]] — multiple equilibria, some Pareto dominating others, could easily arise; consequences include a high level of indeterminacy and that government intervention can sometimes move the economy between equilibria, after which continued intervention might not be needed (pp. 521–522)
- [[equilibrium]] — Stiglitz doubts equilibrium models describe the dynamics of change well (p. 521)
- [[price as signal]] — in the competitive paradigm prices convey all relevant information; Stiglitz uses this to frame the transparency debate (p. 522)
- [[rational expectations]] — footnote 138 notes that recent theory often assumes rational beliefs, which many behaviors seem hard to reconcile with (p. 520)
- [[principal-agent-problem]] (new) — giving incentives to those entrusted to act for others; applies to politics as well as firms (p. 523)
- [[efficiency-wage-theory]] (new) — the idea that wages affect productivity; here Stiglitz adds a psychological route (morale from a fair wage) (p. 521)
- [[evolutionary-dynamics]] (new) — change described as evolutionary process, not equilibrium; no general reason to expect it to be "optimal" (p. 521)
- [[secrecy-rents]] (new) — secrecy as artificial scarcity of information that yields rents to officials (p. 523)
- [[pareto-efficiency]] (new) — no one can gain without someone losing; used for the privatization and competitive-market conditions (p. 518)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Hysteresis after forced bankruptcy (net worth destroyed, not restored when rates fall) ↔ economics-finance: debt deflation / balance-sheet recession — both involve balance-sheet damage with lasting demand effects.
- [structural-analogy] Multiple equilibria with outcomes depending on beliefs about others' behavior ↔ economics-finance: bank run equilibrium / coordination game — self-fulfilling good or bad outcomes.
- [metaphor] Darwin's "environment is other inhabitants" ↔ economics-finance: agent-based modeling / adaptive markets — success depends on the ecology of other agents' strategies.
- [structural-analogy] Voters as principals, officials as agents; elections as imperfect check ↔ economics-finance: principal-agent problem and the takeover mechanism in corporate governance — Stiglitz draws this parallel himself (p. 523).
- [structural-analogy] Secrecy as artificial scarcity yielding rents ↔ economics-finance: dispersed/tacit knowledge and price as signal — who controls information gets power over outcomes.

## Questions this raises
- How large must a temporary intervention be to move an economy between equilibria, and how could one tell in practice whether a second, better equilibrium exists?
- Stiglitz presents Malaysia vs Thailand as support for his theory. What other differences between the two economies could also explain the gap?
- If partial disclosure can push capital into less transparent channels, what kinds of disclosure rules avoid this?
- If intrinsic rewards can matter more than money for officials, how can institutions be designed to build on public-spiritedness without relying on it alone?
