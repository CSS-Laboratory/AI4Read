# Do 15-Month-Old Infants Understand False Beliefs? (Onishi & Baillargeon, 2005)

## L0 — One line
In a nonverbal looking-time task, 15-month-olds looked longer when an actor searched where she did not believe the toy was, whether her belief was true or false; the authors read this as implicit understanding of belief.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
In the standard verbal false-belief task, most 3-year-olds fail and 4-year-olds typically pass. Some researchers see this as part of the evidence for a shift at about age 4 to a "representational" theory of mind (beliefs as representations that may be wrong). Others blame task demands. The authors ask whether 15-month-olds expect an actor to search according to her belief, true or false. Infants watched a toy hidden, then saw a change that the actor either watched or did not watch. In the test they looked longer when she reached into the box inconsistent with her belief. This held for true and false beliefs alike. Simple rules ("search where the toy is") fail to explain this. The authors prefer a belief-attribution reading and doubt a fundamental change at age 4.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a false belief; the logic of a controlled experiment; the meaning of an interaction in a factorial design (ANOVA).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Background dispute: a late shift to a representational theory of mind at about age 4, versus early competence masked by task demands (paras. 2–3).
- Method: violation of expectation. Infants look longer at events that contradict what they expect. It had earlier been used for goals (para. 4).
- Design: 2 (belief: green/yellow) × 2 (belief status: true/false) × 2 (test reach: green/yellow), 7 infants per cell, N = 56 (para. 9).
- Prediction: looking time depends on the interaction of the actor's belief and her action, in both true- and false-belief conditions (para. 8).
- Result: the interaction was reliable overall, within true-belief and within false-belief conditions, and in each of the four conditions (para. 10).
- Control logic: the four conditions rule out "actual location," "previous search," "last attended" and "repeat last action" rules (para. 12).
- Rival reading: infants only track what the actor saw disappear. The authors prefer their own reading for a theoretical reason and an empirical reason (parsimony) (paras. 13–14).
- Implications are proposed for autism and animal cognition research, not tested here (para. 15).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Abstract and introduction.** Researchers have argued for more than two decades that young children do not understand mental states. Part of that evidence is preschoolers' failure at verbal false-belief tasks (§ "Abstract"). The introduction opens with a child who ate the cookies and sees her brother reach in; the authors say that to make sense of his behaviour she must understand that he falsely believes the box still holds cookies (para. 1).

- **View A (change at ~4 years).** Some researchers suggest that at about 4 years children come to see beliefs as representations that may be inaccurate, rather than as direct reflections of reality (para. 2).
- **Standard task.** A character hides a toy and leaves. A second character moves it. Asked where the first will look, most 4-year-olds say the first location. Most 3-year-olds say the actual location (para. 2).
- **View B (early competence).** A representational theory of mind is present much earlier. Some researchers suggest that difficulties with the standard task stem primarily from excessive linguistic, computational and other task demands (para. 3).
- **Support for B.** Three-year-olds, and some 2-year-olds, succeed when merely probed to look where the character will search ("I wonder where she will look"). The authors say this suggests some implicit understanding; it is part of the support offered for View B (para. 3).
- **This study.** It tests 15-month-olds with an entirely nonverbal task (para. 3).

**Method (violation of expectation).** In an earlier goal study, infants saw an actor grasp one of two toys. The toys' positions were then swapped. Infants looked longer when she reached for the other toy. The authors infer that infants had encoded the toy as her goal and expected her to reach for it (para. 4). This study asks whether infants expect search based on belief about location, whether that belief is true or false (para. 4).

**Procedure.**
- *Familiarization (3 trials).* In trial 1 an actor hid a toy watermelon slice in the green box (the other box was yellow). In trials 2 and 3 she reached into the green box and paused. Looking times for the pretrial and paused portions were computed separately (para. 5).
- *Belief induction (1 trial, four versions).* The four versions were (para. 6):
  - TB-green: the actor watched through an opening as the yellow box moved and returned. The toy stayed in green.
  - TB-yellow: she watched the toy move from green to yellow.
  - FB-green: the toy moved to yellow, but the opening stayed shut. Only the infant saw it. The actor believes "green."
  - FB-yellow: she saw the toy go to yellow, then shut the opening. The toy returned to green unseen by her. She believes "yellow."
- *Attention.* All but five infants looked continuously during the pretrial, which lasted 8 s (24 s for FB-yellow). Maximum look-away was 0.6 s (para. 6).
- *Test (1 trial).* Half the infants in each belief condition saw the actor reach into the green box, half into the yellow box. She then paused (para. 7).

**Predictions.** If infants expect search based on the actor's belief rather than the actual location, they should look longer when the searched location is inconsistent with her belief. The same logic applies to true and false belief. So a belief × action interaction is predicted within both (para. 8).

*Intuition note:* The test looks for a crossover. For an actor who believes "green," the yellow reach should be the surprising one, and vice versa. If infants used the toy's real location instead, the pattern would flip in the false-belief conditions.

**Participants.** There were 56 infants (27 female), mean age 15 months 7 days. Seven infants were assigned to each of 8 groups (2 × 2 × 2). Fourteen more were excluded: inattentive (4), looking more than 3 SD beyond the condition mean (4), fussy (2), parental interference (2), observer error (2) (para. 9).

**Results (ANOVA on test looking time; between-subjects factors: belief location, belief status, action).** Results were as follows (para. 10):
- Belief × action interaction: $F(1,48)=31.24$, $P<0.0001$.
- Within true belief: $F(1,24)=14.49$, $P<0.0008$.
- Within false belief: $F(1,24)=16.69$, $P<0.0004$.
- Planned comparisons: in each of the four belief conditions, infants looked reliably longer at the belief-inconsistent reach ($F>5.34$, $P<0.05$).

The authors conclude that infants expected search according to belief. They suggest that 15-month-olds possess, "at least in a rudimentary and implicit form," a representational theory of mind (para. 11).

*Intuition note:* The effect is not only a true-belief effect. It appears even when the actor's belief clashes with what the infant knows.

**Low-level strategies (para. 12).** The four conditions together show infants did not simply expect search where the toy actually was (FB conditions). They did not expect search where she had previously searched (TB-yellow, FB-yellow), or where she had last attended (TB-green). They also did not just expect her to repeat her last action when confused (FB-yellow).

**Rival, more sophisticated reading (para. 13).** The authors raise it themselves. Perhaps infants expect a searcher to look where she last saw the object disappear. This would require infants to (i) separate their perceptions from the actor's, (ii) track what she saw, and (iii) use her perceptions to predict her. It would extend work such as O'Neill's study of 2.7-year-olds, where children pointed more when the parent was absent. The extension would be that infants handle a mistaken actor, not merely an ignorant one.

**Why the authors prefer belief attribution (para. 14).**
- *Theoretical reason:* like other researchers, they assume children are born with an abstract system for interpreting behaviour through goals, perceptions and beliefs. Development is then mostly learning which states underlie which actions.
- *Empirical reason:* their own recent results (cited as unpublished talks) show infants predicting search when the actor must infer the toy's location from useful or misleading cues. They say belief attribution is more parsimonious than many separate superficial perception–action expectations.
- *Conclusion:* the authors propose that the results suggest infants expect search where the actor believes, rightly or wrongly, the toy is; they say this interpretation calls into question a fundamental preschool shift to a representational theory of mind.

**Implications (para. 15).** Autistic children generally fail standard false-belief tasks. Nonverbal versions, if adapted, could open research on autism's nature and early detection. Nonverbal tasks may also inform work on animal theory of mind. Both are possibilities, not findings.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Label cells as (condition, test reach). Rule B: expect search at the *belief* location. Rule R: expect search at the *actual* location. A cell is "surprising" if the reach differs from the expected location.

| Condition | Belief | Actual | Reach green: B / R | Reach yellow: B / R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| TB-green | green | green | no / no | **yes / yes** |
| TB-yellow | yellow | yellow | **yes / yes** | no / no |
| FB-green | green | yellow | no / **yes** | **yes** / no |
| FB-yellow | yellow | green | **yes** / no | no / **yes** |

Rule B marks 4 of 8 cells as surprising. Rule R also marks 4 of 8. They agree in the 4 true-belief cells and disagree in all 4 false-belief cells. So only the false-belief conditions can separate B from R, and longer looking there at B's cells supports B. The paper reports exactly this pattern (para. 10).

## Questions this raises
- Can a "last-seen" rule be distinguished from belief attribution by design alone, or is the choice partly theoretical (paras. 13–14)?
- Do the unpublished cue-inference results (para. 14) hold up when fully reported?
- Would the pattern remain if the looking-time measure were replaced by action or prediction measures?
- Why do preschoolers fail verbal tasks if early competence exists (paras. 2–3)? The authors point to task demands but do not test this here.
