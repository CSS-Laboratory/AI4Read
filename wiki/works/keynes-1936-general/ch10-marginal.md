# The Marginal Propensity to Consume and the Multiplier

## L0 — One line
Given the marginal propensity to consume, each increase in investment raises income by a multiple (for a given MPC), k = 1/(1 − MPC), so a small change in investment can produce a much larger change in employment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How much total employment and income follow from an increase in investment? Keynes says the answer depends on the community's marginal propensity to consume (MPC: the share of an extra unit of income that is consumed). The investment multiplier is k = 1/(1 − MPC). Extra investment needs extra saving, so, ordinarily, income must rise until saving matches it. Real-world "leakages" (rising interest rates, falling confidence, imports, a falling MPC) can make the effective multiplier smaller. The theory holds at every moment, even though effects take time to spread. A poor community's larger multiplier does not mean larger employment swings; in Keynes's example the wealthier community's employment swings more. Keynes ends with the claim that "wasteful" loan-financed spending can raise wealth when unemployment is involuntary.

**Prerequisites:** consumption, saving and investment as shares of income; the sum of a geometric series; the difference between marginal and average values.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Income in wage-units (Y_w) is used as a working index of real income; the psychological law translates (roughly, with obvious qualifications) into: ΔC_w and ΔY_w have the same sign, with ΔY_w > ΔC_w (§ "I").
- ΔY_w = ΔC_w + ΔI_w gives ΔY_w = kΔI_w with 1 − 1/k = MPC. Kahn's employment multiplier k′ (total employment per unit of primary employment) need not equal k (§§ "I", "II").
- The multiplier follows from saving: investment can rise only if saving rises, and, ordinarily, saving rises only if income rises (§ "II").
- A high MPC means big employment swings but easy full employment; a low MPC means small swings but hard-to-cure low employment. Keynes judges that the actual MPC seems much nearer unity than zero (§ "II").
- Offsets (interest rate, confidence, foreign trade), a falling MPC at higher employment, and relief financed by loans may all lower the effective multiplier. Keynes's rough, non-general figures are a multiplier not much below 5 for a closed system and possibly as low as 2–3 in an open one with loan-financed relief (§ "III").
- The logical theory has no time-lag. An unforeseen expansion raises investment gradually and may move the MPC temporarily away from its normal value (§ "IV").
- A high marginal multiplier (the multiplier at the margin) is compatible with a small absolute effect if the average propensity to consume (consumption as a share of total income) is also high. The numerical example shows larger employment swings in the wealthier community (§ "V").
- In Keynes's example (on its assumptions), public works have a bigger employment effect in a deep slump than near full employment (§ "V").
- Under involuntary unemployment, even "wasteful" loan spending can add to wealth on balance (§ "VI").

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction
- Chapter 8 showed that employment can increase only in step (*pari passu*) with investment, unless the propensity to consume changes (§ "Intro").
- Keynes now adds a definite ratio, the **multiplier**, between income and investment. It also links total employment to *primary employment*, the employment directly in investment (§ "Intro").
- The idea comes from R. F. Kahn's 1931 article. Given the propensity to consume, the change in employment is a function of the net change in investment (§ "Intro").

### I. Marginal propensity to consume and the investment multiplier
- The fluctuations studied come from applying different amounts of labour-units to a given capital equipment (§ "I").
- With decreasing returns at the margin, income in wage-units rises more than in proportion to employment. Employment in turn rises more than in proportion to real income measured in product (§ "I").
- Real income and income in wage-units move together in the short period, so Y_w is a usable index. It changes in greater proportion than real income, and this matters in some contexts (§ "I").
- The psychological law is that consumption moves in the same direction as income, but less. It translates (approximately, subject to obvious qualifications) into: ΔC_w and ΔY_w have the same sign, and ΔY_w > ΔC_w (§ "I").
- **Marginal propensity to consume** = dC_w/dY_w. It shows how the next increment of output splits between consumption and investment (§ "I").
- Since ΔY_w = ΔC_w + ΔI_w, Keynes writes

$$\Delta Y_w = k\,\Delta I_w, \qquad 1 - \frac{1}{k} = \text{MPC}.$$

  Here k is the **investment multiplier**: income rises by k times the increment of investment (§ "I").
- (added step) Rearranged: $k = 1/(1-\text{MPC})$.

*Intuition note:* Each extra unit of income is partly spent and partly saved. Investment is the part not consumed, so the smaller the saved share, the more income must grow to make room for a given investment.

### II. Employment multiplier, saving logic, and size of the MPC
- Kahn's multiplier is the **employment multiplier** k′. If investment ΔI_w creates primary employment ΔN₂, then total employment is ΔN = k′ΔN₂ (§ "II").
- In general k need not equal k′. The aggregate supply functions of the two groups of industries may have different shapes. This is likelier when the marginal propensity to consume is widely different from the average propensity. Keynes then simplifies to k = k′ (§ "II").
- Example: if people consume nine-tenths of an income increment, k = 10. Public works then give total employment ten times the primary employment, assuming no reduction of investment elsewhere (§ "II").
- If consumption stays unchanged despite higher income, the rise in employment is limited to the primary employment (§ "II").
- If people try to consume the whole of any increment, there is no point of stability and prices rise without limit (§ "II").
- Under normal psychology, higher employment goes with lower consumption only if the propensity to consume itself changes, for instance through wartime propaganda (§ "II").
- Keynes's reasoning: an investment increment in wage-units cannot happen unless the public saves more. Ordinarily they do this only if aggregate income rises. Their effort to consume part of the extra income stimulates output until saving matches the extra investment (§ "II").
- Keynes's image: "If saving is the pill and consumption is the jam, the extra jam has to be proportioned to the size of the additional pill." (§ "II")
- Unless the psychological propensities differ from his assumption, employment for investment must stimulate the consumption industries. Total employment then rises by a multiple of primary employment (§ "II").
- **If MPC is near unity:** small investment swings cause wide employment swings, but a small investment increment reaches full employment. Involuntary unemployment would be easily remedied (§ "II").
- **If MPC is near zero:** swings are small, but a large investment increment may be needed for full employment. Employment may settle at a low level and resist all but drastic remedies (§ "II").
- Keynes judges that the actual MPC lies between these extremes, much nearer unity. So we get "the worst of both worlds": big fluctuations, and a required investment increment too large to handle easily (§ "II").
- At full employment, further investment makes money-prices rise without limit whatever the MPC. This is true inflation. Before that point, rising prices go with rising real income (§ "II").

*Intuition note:* The multiplier is a consistency condition, not a chain of events in time. Income has to end up high enough that people's own saving equals the investment being done.

### III. Offsets and leakages
- The formula applies to a *net* increment of investment, with no offset elsewhere and no change in the propensity to consume. Kahn's article was mainly about which offsets matter (§ "III").
- Example: 100,000 extra men on public works with k = 4 does not safely give 400,000 more jobs (§ "III").
- Following Kahn, the important factors are (§ "III"):
  1. Financing the policy, and the extra working cash needed, may raise the interest rate and retard other investment unless the monetary authority acts. Dearer capital goods also lower their marginal efficiency, so an actual fall in the interest rate is needed to offset it (§ "III").
  2. Through "confidence", the programme may raise liquidity-preference or lower the marginal efficiency of capital, retarding other investment (§ "III").
  3. In an open economy, part of the multiplier benefits foreign employment, because some extra consumption reduces the home country's favourable foreign balance. The home country may recover part of this through the multiplier abroad (§ "III").
- Keynes says items 1 and 2 are not fully intelligible until Book IV (§ "III").
- For large changes the MPC itself shifts. It is not constant across employment levels, and probably tends to fall as employment rises (§ "III").
- Two further factors probably reinforce this fall (§ "III"):
  - Diminishing returns raise the entrepreneurs' share of income, and their marginal propensity to consume is probably below the community average.
  - Unemployment is likely to go with negative saving in some quarters (living on savings, or on relief partly financed by loans). Re-employment removes this and lowers the MPC faster.
- Hence the multiplier is likely larger for a small net increment than for a large one. For large changes, use the average multiplier over the range (§ "III").
- Keynes gives rough, non-general figures (§ "III"):
  - Closed system, unemployed's consumption paid by transfers: a community that would probably tend to consume not much less than 80% of an increment, giving a multiplier not much below 5.
  - Foreign trade equal to about 20% of consumption, and the unemployed receiving up to about 50% of normal consumption from loans: the multiplier may fall to 2 or 3.
  - So investment swings produce milder employment swings in Britain in 1931 than in the United States in 1932.
- The general principle explains how investment, a small share of national income, can generate employment and income swings of much larger amplitude (§ "III").

### IV. Time-lags
- The earlier analysis assumed the investment change was foreseen early enough for consumption industries to keep pace (§ "IV").
- If the expansion in capital-goods industries is not foreseen, its full effect on employment takes time. Keynes says this is often confused with the theory of the multiplier. The theory itself "holds good continuously, without time-lag" (§ "IV").
- Two points reconcile them (§ "IV"):
  1. An unforeseen expansion raises aggregate investment gradually, not at once.
  2. It may temporarily move the MPC away from its normal value, after which it returns.
- In every interval, the increment of aggregate demand equals the multiplier (set by the MPC in that interval) times the increment of aggregate investment (§ "IV").
- Extreme case: no consumption-goods output rises at first. Consumer prices rise until balance comes from three sources (§ "IV"):
  - postponed consumption, which temporarily lowers the MPC and so the multiplier;
  - income redistributed toward savers through higher profits;
  - depletion of stocks, so aggregate investment rises by less than the capital-goods increment.
- Later the consumption industries adjust. Deferred consumption lifts the MPC above normal for a time, and rebuilding stocks makes aggregate investment temporarily larger than the capital-goods increment (§ "IV").
- This matters for the trade cycle, but does not affect the multiplier's significance as a guide to the total employment benefit. Unless consumption industries are near capacity, only a brief interval need pass before employment in them keeps pace with the capital-goods industries (§ "IV").

### V. Marginal vs average propensity: Keynes's numerical example
- **Apparent paradox:** a poor community saves little, so its multiplier is larger and it seems more prone to violent fluctuations than a wealthy one. This overlooks the average propensity to consume. A high marginal propensity gives a larger *proportionate* effect from a given percentage change in investment. The *absolute* effect can still be small if the average propensity is also high (§ "V").
- **Assumptions** (§ "V"):
  - Up to 5,000,000 men employed, the community consumes all its income.
  - Of the output of the next 100,000 men it consumes 99%, of the next 100,000 98%, of the third 97%, and so on.
  - 10,000,000 men is full employment.
- With 5,000,000 + n·100,000 men employed, the marginal multiplier is 100/n. The share of national income invested is n(n+1)/[2(50+n)] per cent (§ "V").
- **Poorer case, 5,200,000 employed:** multiplier 50, investment only 0.06% of income. If investment falls by about two-thirds, employment falls to 5,100,000, about 2%. If investment falls to zero, employment falls about 4% (§ "V").
- **Richer case, 9,000,000 employed:** marginal multiplier 2½, investment 9% of income. Keynes says a two-thirds fall in investment takes employment to 6,900,000, "namely, by 19 per cent" (see Reading notes). If investment falls to zero, employment falls 44% (§ "V").
- The same reasoning applies if poverty comes from inferior skill, technique or equipment. So the multiplier is larger in a poor community, but employment swings are much greater in a wealthy one, provided its investment is a much larger share of output (§ "V").
- **Public works in the example:** 100,000 extra men at 5,200,000 employed lift total employment to 6,400,000. At 9,000,000 employed they lift it only to 9,200,000 (§ "V").
- Keynes's conclusions (§ "V"):
  - Public works, even of doubtful utility, may pay for themselves "over and over again" in severe unemployment, if only from lower relief costs. This assumes a smaller share of income is saved when unemployment is greater.
  - They become more doubtful near full employment.
  - If the MPC falls steadily toward full employment, a further given gain in employment gets harder to secure.
- **Statistics:** Keynes thinks a chart of the MPC over the cycle could be compiled from income and investment data, but the statistics are too poor. Kuznets's US figures, "very precarious", suggest a multiplier under 3, probably near 2.5 when years are grouped in pairs. That suggests an MPC not exceeding 60–70%, plausible for the boom but, in Keynes's judgment, "improbably low" for the slump (§ "V").
- One possible explanation: US corporate financial conservatism. Provision for wastage continued even while repairs and replacements fell, which blocked the rise in the MPC. Keynes says he suspects this may have worsened the slump. Another possibility is that the statistics overstate the fall in investment, "alleged" to be over 75% (1932 vs 1929), with net capital formation down over 95% (§ "V").

*Intuition note:* A multiplier tells you the effect per unit of investment change. The total effect also depends on how large investment is relative to income. When investment is almost nothing, even a large percentage fall removes very little.

### VI. "Wasteful" loan expenditure
- When involuntary unemployment exists, the marginal disutility of labour is necessarily less than the utility of the marginal product. For someone long unemployed, some labour may even have positive utility. Hence "wasteful" loan spending may enrich the community on balance (§ "VI").
- Pyramid-building, earthquakes and even wars may increase wealth, if classical-economics education of statesmen stands in the way of anything better (§ "VI").
- Keynes says common sense prefers wholly wasteful loan spending (such as gold-mining) to partly wasteful forms, which get judged on strict business principles (§ "VI").
- His example: the Treasury buries banknotes in bottles in disused coal-mines and lets private enterprise dig them up. He says there need be no more unemployment, and real income and capital wealth would probably become much greater. Building houses would be more sensible (§ "VI").
- Gold-mining is the real analogue. Keynes says it is a highly practical form of investment, if we are precluded from increasing employment by means that also add to useful wealth, because (§ "VI"):
  - owing to its gambling attractions it is done without close regard to the interest rate;
  - more gold does not lower its marginal utility, whereas each extra house lowers prospective rents on further houses.
- Ancient Egypt had pyramids and precious metals, whose fruits do not "stale with abundance". Two pyramids or two masses are twice as good as one, but not two railways from London to York (§ "VI").
- Conclusion: we are too prudent to build houses for posterity on loan, so we lack an easy escape from unemployment. We apply to the State maxims suited to enriching an individual (§ "VI").

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Suppose MPC = 0.75, so k = 1/(1 − 0.75) = 4. The government adds ΔI = 100.
- Income rises by ΔY = 4 × 100 = 400.
- Consumption rises by ΔC = 0.75 × 400 = 300.
- Saving rises by 400 − 300 = 100, equal to the investment. This is the "pill and jam" condition.
- Round by round: 100 + 0.75·100 + 0.75²·100 + … = 100 + 75 + 56.25 + 42.19 + … → 100/(1 − 0.75) = 400.
- With leakage: if 20% of each round's extra consumption goes abroad, the effective spending ratio is 0.75 × 0.8 = 0.6, so k = 1/(1 − 0.6) = 2.5 and ΔY = 250. This shows why Keynes says offsets lower the multiplier (§ "III").

## Questions this raises
- How can the marginal propensity to consume be measured, given Keynes's own doubts about the statistics? (§ "V")
- How do the interest-rate and confidence offsets (§ "III", items 1–2) work? Keynes defers them to Book IV.
- When is "wasteful" spending an acceptable policy, and what changes once involuntary unemployment is gone? (§ "VI")
- If the MPC falls toward full employment, how large must investment be to get from a deep slump to full employment? (§§ "II", "V")
