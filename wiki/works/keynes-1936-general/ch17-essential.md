# The Essential Properties of Interest and Money

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that in the economy we know, money's own-rate of interest tends to be the one that resists falling as output grows, because money has a high liquidity-premium, negligible carrying cost and (near) zero elasticities of production and substitution, so investment can stop short of full employment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why should the money-rate of interest limit employment more than the wheat-rate or house-rate? Keynes defines an own-rate of interest for every durable asset, $q-c+l$ (yield minus carrying cost plus liquidity-premium). In equilibrium the returns on all assets, measured in one standard, are equal. As output rises, own-rates fall for ordinary assets. Money's own-rate may fall reluctantly, because money, as we know it, is hard to produce by labour, has no substitutes, and is held for liquidity at almost no cost. Investment stops when the highest own-rate equals the highest marginal efficiency of capital. Any asset whose own-rate resists falling would cause the same trouble. He also drops the "natural rate" in favour of a "neutral rate".

**Prerequisites:** compound interest and discounting; the marginal efficiency of capital (the discount rate that equates an asset's expected yields to its supply price); the idea of a wage-unit; liquidity-preference (demand to hold cash).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Every durable commodity has its own rate of interest, defined by forward versus spot quantities; these rates need not be equal (§ I).
- An asset's own-rate is $q-c+l$; in equilibrium the money-reduced returns $a_1+q_1$, $a_2-c_2$, $l_3$ are equal (§ II).
- Production of capital stops when no asset's marginal efficiency reaches the highest own-rate. It is the own-rate that declines most slowly, not necessarily the money-rate, that does the blocking. Changing the standard of value to wheat would not cure the trouble (§ II).
- Money as we know it commonly has these properties: near-zero elasticity of production, near-zero elasticity of substitution, and a liquidity-premium well above its carrying cost (§ III).
- Falling wages might lower the money-rate, but several combined reasons make it very probable that the rate often stays too high. The most fundamental reason is liquidity-preference (§ III).
- These characteristics reinforce one another. Money's low carrying cost and low elasticities tend to raise the expectation of sticky money-wages, which in turn enhances money's liquidity-premium (§ IV).
- Liquidity is a matter of degree. Land may once have played money's role; in Keynes's opinion, high liquidity-premiums on land and now on money explain why the world has accumulated so little capital, which differs from Marshall's view (§ V).
- Keynes gives up the "natural rate" idea; if any rate is unique and significant, it is the "neutral rate", the natural rate consistent with full employment. He reads the classical theory as tacitly assuming that the actual rate equals it, or equals the rate that keeps employment at some fixed level (§ VI).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Own-rates of interest for every asset
- **Puzzle.** It seems that the money-rate sets a standard the marginal efficiency of a capital-asset must reach if it is to be newly produced. Why is money special, and what would happen in a non-monetary economy? (§ I)
- **Definition.** The money-rate of interest is the percentage excess of a sum contracted for forward delivery (e.g. a year ahead) over the "spot" (cash) price of that sum (§ I).
- **Own-rates.** Take 100 quarters of wheat for spot delivery. The forward quantity with the same exchange value today defines the wheat-rate. If it is 105 quarters, the wheat-rate is +5% a year; if 95, it is −5%. So there is a wheat-rate, copper-rate, house-rate and so on, each in terms of itself (§ I).
- **Worked example (Keynes's numbers).** Spot price £100 per 100 quarters; one-year future £107 per 100 quarters; money-rate 5%. £100 spot buys £105 forward. £105 forward buys $\tfrac{105}{107}\times 100 \approx 98$ quarters forward. Also £100 spot buys 100 quarters spot. So 100 quarters spot buy 98 quarters forward, and the wheat-rate is −2% a year (§ I).
- Own-rates need not be equal across commodities, and the spot–future relation differs notoriously between them. The same holds for different currencies, e.g. sterling and dollars (§ I). Keynes suggests (as a possibility, "it may be") that the greatest own-rate "rules the roost", because that is the rate the marginal efficiency of a new asset must attain. He adds that there are reasons, which he says he will show, why money's rate is often the greatest: certain forces that reduce other own-rates do not act on money (§ I).
- **Standards.** Any commodity can serve as the standard for measuring marginal efficiency. If no change is expected in the relative value of two standards, an asset's marginal efficiency is the same in either. If wheat is expected to appreciate at $a$% a year in money, an asset with marginal efficiency $x$% in money has $x-a$% in wheat. All assets shift by the same amount, so their ranking is unchanged (§ I).
- A composite commodity that was strictly representative would give a unique rate of interest and marginal efficiency, but Keynes says the obstacles are the same as for setting up a unique standard of value (§ I).
- So far the money-rate has no uniqueness; the question of why it matters remains open (§ I).

*Intuition note.* Each good has its own rate of interest, found by comparing forward and spot quantities of that good. Money's rate is one rate among many until something distinguishes it.

### II. Own-rates, equilibrium, and what limits output
- **Three attributes of assets** (§ II):
  - (i) yield $q$: output from assisting production or giving services, in terms of the asset itself;
  - (ii) carrying cost $c$: wastage or cost from the mere passage of time. It does not matter exactly where the line between $q$ and $c$ is drawn, because Keynes is concerned only with $q-c$ here;
  - (iii) liquidity-premium $l$: the amount people will pay for the potential convenience or security of power of disposal. This leaves nothing to show in output at period's end.
- **Own-rate:**
$$\text{own-rate} = q - c + l .$$
- **Typical patterns** (§ II):
  - Assets in use (a machine, a house): normally $q > c$, with $l$ probably negligible.
  - Surplus stocks: carrying cost with no yield, and $l$ usually negligible once stocks exceed a moderate level (though it can be significant in special circumstances).
  - Money: yield nil, carrying cost negligible, liquidity-premium substantial.
  - Essential difference: for money $l$ much exceeds $c$; for all or most other assets $c$ much exceeds $l$.
- **Illustration.** Houses have yield $q_1$ only; wheat has carrying cost $c_2$ only; money has $l_3$ only. So the house-rate is $q_1$, the wheat-rate is $-c_2$, the money-rate is $l_3$ (§ II).
- **Reduction to one standard.** Let money be the standard (it may be only a money of account). Let $a_1, a_2$ be the expected percentage appreciation of houses and wheat. Then $a_1+q_1$, $a_2-c_2$ and $l_3$ are the house-, wheat- and money-rates of money-interest. Wealth-owners direct demand to whichever is greatest. In equilibrium demand-prices adjust so that
$$a_1+q_1 = a_2-c_2 = l_3 .$$
Changing the standard shifts all terms equally, so this result is unchanged (§ II).
- **Production rule.** Assets whose normal supply-price is below demand-price are newly produced. These are the assets whose marginal efficiency exceeds the rate of interest, both in the same standard. As the stock grows, marginal efficiency falls. When no asset's marginal efficiency reaches the rate of interest, new capital production stops (§ II).
- **Adjustment.** Suppose some asset (e.g. money) has an interest rate that is fixed, or falls more slowly than any other as output rises. This is a hypothesis at this stage. Since $a_1+q_1$, $a_2-c_2$ and $l_3$ must stay equal while $q_1$ and $-c_2$ fall, $a_1$ and $a_2$ must rise. The present money-price of non-money goods falls relative to expected future price. If $q_1$ and $-c_2$ keep falling, production eventually becomes unprofitable unless future production cost is expected to exceed present cost by enough to cover carrying a stock to that date (§ II).
- **Correction.** Keynes says the earlier statement that the money-rate sets the limit was not strictly correct. The correct statement: it is the asset whose rate declines most slowly as the stock of assets increases that knocks out the profitable production of the others (barring the special cost-expectation case) (§ II).
- **Standard of value.** Decreeing wheat or houses the standard would not cure the difficulty (as "some have supposed"), since it recurs if any asset's own-rate is reluctant to fall. For example, gold may keep this role in a country on an inconvertible paper standard (§ II).

*Intuition note.* Imagine every asset's return racing downward as more of it is built. One asset's return refuses to drop, and everything else stops being worth producing once its return falls below that floor. Which asset it is need not be the one we call "money".

### III. Why money-as-we-know-it is that asset
Keynes tacitly assumed that money has special characteristics making its own-rate more reluctant to fall than any other. Keynes thinks, on reflection, that the following peculiarities, which commonly characterise money as we know it, can justify this (§ III).

- **(i) Elasticity of production near zero.**
  - Meaning here: the response of labour applied to producing money to a rise in the labour a unit of money commands. Private enterprise cannot readily produce money.
  - It holds strictly for inconvertible managed currency, and approximately for gold, except where gold-mining is the major industry.
  - For other assets, own-rates fall because output raises their stock. Money's supply is fixed in this sense, which gives a prima facie presumption that its own-rate is reluctant to fall.
  - If money could be grown like a crop, depressions would be avoided or mitigated, because labour would flow into producing it as other prices fell (§ III).
- **(ii) Elasticity of substitution near zero.**
  - Pure rent-factors also have zero production elasticity, so a second condition is needed.
  - Money's utility derives solely from its exchange-value. As its exchange value rises, nothing is substituted for it (except trivially where the money-commodity is used in manufacture or the arts).
  - Money is "a bottomless sink for purchasing power": there is no value of money at which demand is diverted so as to spill over into other things.
  - One qualification: if a rise in money's value creates doubt that the rise will last, $a_1$ and $a_2$ increase. That raises the commodity-rates of money-interest and stimulates output (§ III).
- **(iii) Is the effective money supply really fixed?**
  - A fall in the wage-unit releases cash from other uses to meet the liquidity-motive, and the money stock becomes a larger share of wealth.
  - Keynes says one cannot dispute on purely theoretical grounds that this might allow an adequate fall in the money-rate. Yet he holds that several reasons, taken in combination, make it very probable that, in an economy of the type we are accustomed to, the money-rate will often prove reluctant to decline adequately (§ III):
  - **(a)** A wage fall also affects marginal efficiencies in money terms. If it creates an expectation of a later rise, the result is wholly favourable. If it creates an expectation of further falls, the damage to marginal efficiency may offset the lower interest rate.
  - **(b)** Money-wages tend to be sticky, which tends to limit how readily the wage-unit falls. If they fell easily, it might be worse, since this would often create expectations of further falls. If wages were fixed in wheat, they would probably not stay sticky. Wages fixed in money tend to be sticky because of money's other characteristics, especially those that make it liquid.
  - **(c)** The most fundamental reason: liquidity-preference. In certain circumstances, often met, the rate is insensitive, especially below some figure, even to a substantial rise in the money stock relative to other wealth. Beyond a certain point money's liquidity yield falls far less than other assets' yields would when their quantity rises comparably. Money's low carrying cost is essential here. For other commodities, a larger stock eventually means a loss because of storage, wastage and so on. For money this is not so.
- Hence reformers who want stamped legal-tender currency, which creates an artificial carrying cost, are in Keynes's view on the right track, and the practical value of their proposals deserves consideration (§ III).
- **Combined conclusion.** The money-rate matters because it may be unresponsive to the money-to-wealth ratio, and money has (or may have) zero or negligible elasticities of production and substitution. The three conditions (liquidity-motive, production, substitution) mean, respectively: demand may go predominantly to money; labour cannot then produce more money; and no cheap substitute can do money's job. The only relief, apart from changes in marginal efficiency of capital and with liquidity-preference unchanged, is more money or a rise in money's value (formally the same thing) (§ III).
- **Consequence.** A higher money-rate retards output of everything with elastic production, but cannot stimulate output of money (whose production is, by hypothesis, perfectly inelastic). In an ordinary commodity, inelastic demand for liquid stocks would let small demand changes move its rate "with a rush", and elastic supply would prevent a high spot premium. Left to natural forces, such commodities' rates would tend to fall until full employment gave them money-like supply inelasticity. So without money, or any commodity with money's properties, interest rates would reach equilibrium only at full employment (§ III).
- Keynes's summary: unemployment develops because "people want the moon". The only remedy is to persuade the public that "green cheese" is practically the same and to have a green-cheese factory (a central bank) under public control (§ III).
- Gold's traditionally praised trait, inelastic supply, is precisely the trouble (§ III).
- **General conclusion** (propensity to consume given): no further rise in investment is possible when the greatest own-rate of own-interest equals the greatest marginal efficiency of any asset, both measured in the asset whose own-rate is greatest. This is necessarily satisfied at full employment. It may also be satisfied before full employment if some asset has zero or relatively small elasticities of production and substitution, and its rate declines more slowly, as output grows, than the marginal efficiencies measured in it (§ III).

*Intuition note.* Money, as we know it, is an asset that people can hold cheaply for liquidity, that labour cannot make more of, and that has no substitute. So when people want more money, jobs cannot be created to supply it. Nothing then stops demand for money from soaking up purchasing power.

### IV. Is money's role tied to being the standard for debts and wages?
- Being the standard of value is not sufficient to make a commodity's rate the significant one (§ IV).
- **Contracts and wages in money.** Contracts fixed, and wages usually somewhat stable, in money play a large part in attracting a high liquidity-premium to money. But money's low carrying cost plays quite as large a part, since what counts is $l - c$. For most commodities carrying cost is at least as high as the liquidity-premium of the contract standard. Even if sterling's premium were transferred to wheat, the wheat-rate would probably stay at or below zero. So the fixed standard is probably insufficient alone (§ IV).
- **Stickiness.** The expected stability of output value in money depends on wages being sticky in money terms, not on their being contracted in money. A rival to money would need two things (§ IV):
  - costs expected to stay stable in wage-units at any scale of output;
  - surplus stock that can be carried without cost, i.e. $l > c$.
  
  It is not logically impossible that such a commodity exists, but Keynes says it "does not seem probable".
- **Keynes's conclusion:** the commodity in terms of which wages are expected to be stickiest cannot be one with other than the least production elasticity and least excess of carrying cost over liquidity-premium. In other words, Keynes says the expectation of relative stickiness of wages in money is a corollary of money's excess of liquidity-premium over carrying cost being greater than that of any other asset (§ IV).
- The characteristics reinforce one another cumulatively. Low elasticities and low carrying cost tend to raise the expectation of stable money-wages. That expectation enhances money's liquidity-premium, and prevents an exceptional correlation between the money-rate and other assets' marginal efficiencies that might "rob the money-rate of interest of its sting" (§ IV).
- **Pigou (with others)** has been accustomed to assume a presumption that real wages are more stable than money-wages. Keynes replies that this could hold only if employment were presumed stable, and that wage-goods have high carrying costs. Fixing wages in wage-goods would make money-prices swing violently between zero and infinity in response to small shifts in the propensity to consume and the inducement to invest. Money-wages being more stable than real wages is a condition of inherent stability, so Pigou's assumption is a mistake in logic as well as in fact, if the system is supposed stable (§ IV).

### V. Liquidity as a matter of degree; land
- Liquidity and carrying costs are matters of degree. Money's peculiarity is only that liquidity is high relative to carrying cost (§ V).
- **Non-monetary economy** (Keynes's best definition): one with no asset whose liquidity-premium always exceeds its carrying cost. There capital equipments differ in (a) the variety of consumables they can help produce, (b) the stability of the value of their output, and (c) how quickly their embodied wealth becomes "liquid" through output that can be re-embodied differently. Owners weigh lack of liquidity against estimated yield after risk (§ V).
- Liquidity-premium is partly similar to the risk-premium and partly different. The difference corresponds to that between our best estimates of probabilities and our confidence in them. Earlier chapters did not separate them, but own-rates must allow for both (§ V).
- There is no absolute standard of liquidity, only a scale. The conception of what contributes to liquidity is partly vague and depends on social practices and institutions. The order of preference at any time is definite, and that is all the analysis needs (§ V).
- **Land.** In certain historic environments land may have had a high liquidity-premium. Since its elasticities of production and substitution may be very low, it is "conceivable" that wanting land played money's role in keeping interest too high. There was no forward price of land in terms of land, but high mortgage rates, often above the net yield of cultivation, are analogous. Usury laws targeted these, "rightly so", since such rates may have retarded investment in newly produced assets (§ V).
- **Marshall's view versus Keynes's.** In his own opinion, the world's poverty in accumulated capital is to be explained neither by improvidence nor even by war damage, but by liquidity-premiums formerly on land and now on money. **Marshall** held that accumulation is checked, and interest sustained, by people's preference for present over deferred gratification, their unwillingness to "wait" (§ V).

### VI. Natural rate versus neutral rate
- In the *Treatise on Money* Keynes defined a "natural rate" as the one preserving equality of saving and investment. He thought this developed **Wicksell's** natural rate, which preserved stability of some not clearly specified price level (§ VI).
- **Self-correction.** On that definition there is a different natural rate for each level of employment, and each rate of interest is "natural" for some employment level. A unique natural rate is a mistake, due to not understanding that equilibrium could occur below full employment. He no longer thinks the concept contributes much: it is merely the rate that preserves the status quo, in which he has no predominant interest (§ VI).
- **Neutral rate.** If any rate is unique and significant, it is the natural rate consistent with full employment, given the other parameters (perhaps better called the "optimum rate"). More strictly, it is the equilibrium rate when output and employment are such that the elasticity of employment as a whole is zero (§ VI).
- **Classical theory.** Keynes says the tacit assumption needed to make sense of it is that the actual rate always equals the neutral rate, or the rate that maintains employment at some specified constant level. So interpreted, there is little in its practical conclusions to object to. The volume of output then depends solely on the assumed employment level with current equipment and technique, in a "Ricardian world" (§ VI).

## Toy example (ours)
Take money as the standard. Suppose money's liquidity-premium is fixed at $l_3 = 2\%$. Equilibrium requires $a_1+q_1 = a_2-c_2 = l_3$.

- With $q_1 = 4\%$: $a_1 = 2-4 = -2\%$ (houses expected to lose 2%).
- Wheat with $c_2 = 3\%$: $a_2 = 2+3 = +5\%$.
- More houses are built and $q_1$ falls to 3%: $a_1 = 2-3 = -1\%$.
- Then $q_1 = 2\%$: $a_1 = 0\%$; then $q_1 = 1\%$: $a_1 = +1\%$.

So $a_1$ rises from −2% to +1% as $q_1$ falls, as § II says. The present house price falls relative to its expected future price. Producing houses now pays only if the expected rise covers the cost of carrying.

Standard check (§ I): a machine yields $x = 6\%$ in money. If wheat is expected to appreciate by $a = 2\%$, its marginal efficiency in wheat is $6-2 = 4\%$.

## Concepts
- [[own-rate-of-interest]] (new) — for any durable asset, $q-c+l$, measured in terms of itself (§ II)
- [[liquidity-premium]] (new) — amount people will pay for the convenience or security of disposal power over an asset, apart from yield and carrying cost (§ II)
- [[carrying-cost]] (new) — wastage or cost of holding an asset through time, measured in terms of itself (§ II)
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — discount rate equating the present value of an asset's expected yields to its supply price. It can be measured in any standard (§ I)
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — the public's readiness to hold money for liquidity, which can keep the money-rate from falling when money's quantity rises (§ III(c))
- [[elasticity-of-production-and-substitution]] (new) — how far labour or other factors can be turned to produce or replace the asset when its value rises. Both are (or may be) near zero for money as we know it (§ III)
- [[neutral-rate-of-interest]] (new) — the natural rate consistent with full employment, where employment's elasticity is zero (§ VI)

## Canonical instances
- [[wheat-rate-of-interest example]] — spot £100, future £107, money-rate 5%, giving wheat-rate −2% (§ I)
- [[house-wheat-money own-rates]] — $q_1$, $-c_2$, $l_3$ and equalisation of $a_1+q_1$, $a_2-c_2$, $l_3$ (§ II)
- [[gold-mining and the elasticity of production]] — labour diverts to gold only marginally (§ III(i))
- [[stamped money proposal]] — an artificial carrying cost on currency (§ III(c))
- [[land and mortgage rates]] — high mortgage rates as analogue of a high liquidity-premium on land (§ V)
- [[Pigou's real-wage stability]] — wage-goods have high carrying costs, and wages fixed in them would cause violent oscillation of money-prices (§ IV)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Money as a "bottomless sink" with no substitutes ↔ market failure: demand cannot be redirected, so the market does not clear in goods and labour.
- [structural-analogy] Equal money-reduced returns across assets ↔ no-arbitrage pricing: the same condition of indifference among alternatives, here with a liquidity term.
- [metaphor] The difference between liquidity-premium and risk-premium, which Keynes likens to the difference between estimated probabilities and confidence in them ↔ Knightian uncertainty: confidence in estimates matters as well as the estimates themselves.

## Questions this raises
- If money's own-rate is the binding one, does the choice of what counts as money matter (gold, paper, bank deposits)? Keynes says gold may still play the role under an inconvertible paper standard (§ II).
- Could negative carrying costs (stamped currency) work, and what would they do to wage-stickiness and expectations?
- Is Keynes's account of land in history testable, given there is no forward price of land in terms of land (§ V)?
- How would the "neutral rate" be measured, if it requires knowing when the elasticity of employment is zero (§ VI)?
