# The Propensity to Consume: I. The Objective Factors

## L0 — One line
Aggregate consumption depends mainly on aggregate income and rises by less than income does, so employment can rise only if investment fills the gap between income and consumption (unless the propensity itself changes), and financial provisions for the future can widen that gap.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks what determines the part of aggregate demand that comes from consumption. He defines the "propensity to consume" as consumption as a function of income, both in wage-units (labour-cost units). He lists six objective factors that could shift it and concludes it is fairly stable. Its normal shape is the "fundamental psychological law": consumption rises with income, but by less. Hence, unless the propensity changes, extra employment pays only with extra investment. Depreciation allowances and sinking funds (reserves for replacement) can withdraw spending power long before replacement is due. He gives US and British examples and statistics. He ends with a warning: ever more capital makes it harder to find investment that fills the gap between income and consumption.

**Prerequisites:** aggregate supply and demand functions; wage-unit; derivative as slope; the difference between gross and net investment.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Employment is set where aggregate supply meets aggregate demand. Demand is expected consumption plus investment, and this book treats the consumption part (§ I).
- The propensity to consume is the function relating consumption to income, both in wage-units. This removes pure wage-unit changes from the picture (§ I).
- Of six objective factors, windfall changes in capital-values (unforeseen changes in the money-value of wealth) are among the major short-period ones. Substantial changes in interest rates or fiscal policy may make some difference. The rest are unlikely to matter much in ordinary circumstances, so the function is fairly stable (§ II).
- Basic law: $dC_w/dY_w$ is positive and less than 1. Consequently employment can rise only in step with investment, unless the propensity itself changes (§ III).
- In a slump, consumption falls by less than income. Keynes gives this as the explanation why a new equilibrium can usually be reached within a modest range (§ III).
- Consumption depends on net income (what is left after financial provision for wear and replacement), not on income before that provision. Provisions that exceed actual upkeep drag on employment until replacement is made (§ IV).
- Keynes offers 1929 US and 1935 British sinking funds as examples of this drag, and the Clark and Kuznets figures to show how large the deduction from gross investment is (§ IV).
- Capital is not self-subsistent. Every weakening of the propensity to consume, regarded as a permanent habit, also weakens the demand for capital (§ IV).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Setting the problem
- Aim: find what determines the volume of employment. The preliminary result is that it is fixed where the aggregate supply function meets the aggregate demand function. Supply involves few unfamiliar factors. The neglected part is demand, treated in Books III and IV (§ I).
- Aggregate demand relates a level of employment to its expected "proceeds". These proceeds equal consumption spending plus investment, and the two are governed by largely distinct factors. Consumption is treated here, investment in Book IV (§ I).
- Strictly the relation is $C$ to employment $N$. Keynes instead uses consumption $C_w$ and income $Y_w$, both in wage-units (labour-cost units). He grants that $Y_w$ is not a unique function of $N$, because the distribution of $N$ across employments may matter slightly. He judges uniqueness a good approximation (§ I).
- Definition: the **propensity to consume** is the functional relationship between $Y_w$ and $C_w$, written $C_w = \chi(Y_w)$, or $C = W\chi(Y_w)$ with $W$ the wage-unit. (The function symbol is missing in the extracted text; $\chi$ is our placeholder.) (§ I)
- Spending depends partly on income, partly on objective circumstances, and partly on subjective needs, habits and the way income is divided. Keynes warns that such classification risks false division. The subjective factors (psychology, institutions) are unlikely to change much in the short run. He takes them as given and assumes the propensity changes only through objective factors (§ I).

### II. Six objective factors
1. **Change in the wage-unit.** Consumption depends on real income more than on money income. As a first approximation, consumption at a given employment changes in the same proportion as the wage-unit. A wage-unit change might also alter the distribution of a given real income between entrepreneurs and rentiers, and in some circumstances an allowance may have to be made for the effect of this on consumption. Apart from this, defining the function in wage-units already allows for wage-unit changes (§ II (1)).
2. **Difference between income and net income.** Consumption depends on net income. If the two are not stably related, the part of an income change not reflected in net income is neglected, and a change in net income not reflected in income must be allowed for. Keynes doubts its practical importance save in exceptional circumstances (§ II (2)).
3. **Windfall changes in capital-values** not allowed for in net income. They bear no stable relation to income. The consumption of wealth-owners may be very sensitive to them. This is "amongst the major factors" for short-period changes (§ II (3)).
4. **Rate of time-discounting** (exchange rate of present for future goods), approximated by the rate of interest.
   - **Classical view:** it was convenient to suppose that consumption is, other things equal, negatively sensitive to the interest rate, so that a rise would appreciably reduce it. Keynes notes it has long been recognised that the total effect is complex and uncertain, because some saving motives are satisfied more easily and others weakened by a rise.
   - Over long periods, substantial changes probably tend to modify social habits considerably, though in which direction is hard to say. The usual type of short-period fluctuation is not likely to have much direct influence (few people will alter their way of living because the rate falls from 5 to 4 per cent, income unchanged).
   - The indirect effect via security prices is already covered by factor 3. Keynes's conclusion is that the short-period effect is "secondary and relatively unimportant", except perhaps for unusually large changes.
   - A very low rate may encourage buying annuities for old age, a source of negative saving. Extreme uncertainty about the future perhaps belongs here too (§ II (4)).
5. **Fiscal policy.** Income taxes, taxes on capital-profits and death-duties matter as much as the interest rate. Using fiscal policy to equalise incomes raises the propensity to consume more. Government sinking funds paid out of ordinary taxation are a species of corporate saving and reduce it. Switching between borrowing and sinking funds can cause severe contraction or marked expansion of effective demand (§ II (5)).
6. **Expectations about present vs. future income.** Listed for completeness. It is likely to average out across the community, and uncertainty limits its influence (§ II (6)).

**Conclusion:** the propensity to consume is fairly stable once wage-unit changes are eliminated. Windfalls can shift it, and substantial changes in interest or fiscal policy may make some difference. The other factors are unlikely to matter in ordinary circumstances. Aggregate income in wage-units is, as a rule, the principal variable, which justifies the portmanteau function (§ II).

*Intuition note:* Most other things that could move consumption are either small or likely to average out. Surprise gains or losses in wealth are the main exception, and big changes in interest rates or fiscal policy may add some effect. So we can treat consumption mostly as "a function of income".

### III. The normal shape of the function
- **Fundamental psychological law:** men are disposed, as a rule and on average, to increase consumption as income increases, but by less than the increase in income. Thus $\Delta C_w$ has the sign of $\Delta Y_w$ but is smaller, so $dC_w/dY_w$ is positive and less than unity. Keynes says we may depend on this law with great confidence, both a priori from knowledge of human nature and from the facts of experience (§ III).
- Short periods: habitual living standards claim income first, and adjustment to income changes is imperfect. A rising income often brings more saving and a falling one less (§ III).
- Over higher absolute income levels, primary needs come before accumulation motives, so as a rule a greater proportion is saved. Whether or not that holds, Keynes takes it as fundamental that consumption does not rise by an equal absolute amount. A greater absolute amount is therefore saved, unless a large unusual change occurs in other factors. He says stability of the system "essentially depends" on this rule, to be shown later. So not all extra employment is needed for extra consumption (§ III).
- Falling employment: consumption may even exceed income, through people using reserves and through government deficits or relief financed by borrowing. Consumption falls by less than real income, which is why a new equilibrium can usually be reached within a modest range. Otherwise a fall might go to extreme lengths (§ III).
- Consequence: employment can increase only *pari passu* (in step) with investment, unless the propensity changes. Consumers spend less than the rise in aggregate supply price, so the extra employment is unprofitable unless investment fills the gap (§ III).

*Intuition note:* When income grows, some of it is not spent. Someone else must spend that part, as investment, or the extra jobs do not pay. The same braking works downward in a slump.

### IV. Net income and financial provisions
- Employment depends on expected consumption and investment, but consumption depends on net income (consumption plus net investment). The larger the financial provision made before reckoning net income, the less favourable a given investment is to consumption and employment (§ IV).
- If the whole provision is spent on upkeep, the point is not likely to be overlooked. If it exceeds current upkeep, the excess neither gives rise to current investment nor pays for consumption. New investment must balance it, so less new investment is left to provide income. The same applies to unrepaired wastage in user cost (§ IV).
- **House example:** a landlord writes off part of the rent without spending it on upkeep or counting it as net income. This drags on employment through the house's life, then is made good in a lump at rebuilding (§ IV).
- In a stationary economy the allowances and replacements offset each other. In a non-static economy, especially after a burst of long-lived investment, much new investment may be absorbed by provisions for plant that is not yet due for replacement. Incomes then cannot rise above a level matching low net investment. Sinking funds are apt to withdraw spending power long before replacement is due: they diminish current demand and increase it only in the year the replacement is made. Writing off faster than equipment wears out, as "financial prudence" advises, may be very serious (§ IV).
- **United States, 1929:** the capital expansion of the previous five years had created huge sinking funds and depreciation allowances for plant not needing replacement. Absorbing them required an enormous volume of new investment. Keynes says this factor "alone was probably sufficient to cause a slump". Large corporations' continued prudence obstructed early recovery (§ IV).
- **Great Britain, 1935:** post-war building produced sinking funds far above current repair needs. Local authorities' funds are, "I think", more than half their annual new spending. Keynes doubts that the Ministry of Health realises it may be aggravating unemployment. Building-society mortgage repayments rose from £24m (1925) to £68m (1933), against new advances of £103m, and repayments are probably higher still now. Keynes adds that a wish to clear debt faster than the house deteriorates may lead owners to save more, a factor he thinks perhaps better classed as lowering the propensity to consume directly rather than through net income (§ IV).
- **Colin Clark, Great Britain (£ million):** output statistics show gross, not net, investment. Keynes warns that Clark's gross figure may include part of user cost (§ IV).

  Net formation was steady in 1925–29 (only a 10 per cent rise late on). The deduction stayed high even at the slump's bottom. Keynes says Kuznets's method must surely lead to too low an estimate of the annual increase in depreciation. Net formation in 1932 was 95 per cent below the 1925–29 average (§ IV).

*Intuition note:* Money set aside "for later" is income not spent now. If the later replacement is far off, demand is lost in the meantime. A society that already has a large stock of capital must make a large deduction of this kind before it reaches the net income available for consumption.

- **Why the figures matter.** Keynes calls the preceding figures to some extent a digression, but stresses the size of the deduction that must be made from the income of a society with a large capital stock to reach the net income available for consumption. Overlooking it may lead us to underestimate the drag on the propensity to consume, even where the public is ready to consume a very large proportion of its net income (§ IV).
- **General argument.** Consumption is "the sole end and object of all economic activity". Aggregate demand comes only from present consumption or present provision for future consumption. A community can provide for the future only by current physical output, not by financial expedients. Where financial and physical provision are separated, financial prudence may reduce demand and well-being. The more is provided in advance, the harder it is to find something further to provide, and the greater the dependence on present consumption. Yet larger incomes widen the gap between income and consumption. Failing some novel expedient, Keynes says, the only answer to this riddle is enough unemployment to keep us poor enough that the gap equals the physical provision which pays to produce today (§ IV).
- **Disinvestment argument.** Consumption from earlier-produced objects (disinvestment) reduces current demand. Goods made now for later consumption expand it. All capital-investment is destined, sooner or later, to end in capital-disinvestment. So new investment must keep outrunning disinvestment to fill the gap, increasingly hard as capital grows. It can exceed disinvestment only if future consumption is expected to rise. Keynes cites the Fable of the Bees: the gay of tomorrow are needed to give a reason for the grave of today. He also says a diminished propensity to consume today can be accommodated to the public advantage only if an increased propensity to consume is expected some day, and that each time we secure today's equilibrium by increased investment we aggravate the difficulty of securing tomorrow's (§ IV).
- **Popular objection:** "What will you do when you have built all the houses and roads…?", raised against public investment. Keynes replies that the same difficulty applies to private investment and industrial expansion, which is only less visible (§ IV).
- **Conclusion:** capital is not a self-subsistent entity apart from consumption. Every weakening of the propensity to consume, as a permanent habit, weakens the demand for capital as well as for consumption (§ IV).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take an invented linear propensity in wage-units: $C_w = 20 + 0.6\,Y_w$. Here $dC_w/dY_w = 0.6$, positive and below 1.
- $Y_w = 100$: $C_w = 20 + 60 = 80$. Gap $= 100 - 80 = 20$, so investment of 20 is needed.
- $Y_w = 150$: $C_w = 20 + 90 = 110$. Gap $= 40$. Income rose by 50, consumption by 30 and the gap by 20, so extra employment needs extra investment of 20.
- $Y_w = 30$: $C_w = 20 + 18 = 38 > 30$. Consumption exceeds income by 8, as in a deep slump when reserves are used or relief is borrowed.
- $Y_w = 50$: $C_w = 20 + 30 = 50$, so the gap is 0 and zero investment is consistent with that income.
- Hence if investment were 20, income could settle at 100 and not at 150. The fall from 100 to 50 cuts consumption only from 80 to 50, by 30, which is less than the 50 cut in income.

## Questions this raises
- If $dC_w/dY_w < 1$ as a rule, what fixes the level of investment that fills the gap? (Book IV is said to treat this.)
- How large are sinking-fund effects if depreciation is mismeasured, as Keynes argues for Kuznets?
- Is the "novel expedient" he mentions a policy such as public investment or income redistribution?
- Does the claim that capital's demand depends on consumption hold when future consumption is expected to rise?
