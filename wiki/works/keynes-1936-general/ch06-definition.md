# Chapter 6. The Definition of Income, Saving and Investment

## L0 — One line
Keynes defines income through "user cost" (the value sacrificed by using equipment), and with consistent definitions saving must equal investment, since both equal income minus consumption; saving is a mere residual.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Economists used "income", "saving" and "investment" in conflicting senses. Keynes asks for definitions that fit common usage and are relevant to employment decisions. He defines income as sales minus user cost. Net income also deducts supplementary cost, which is relevant to consumption decisions. Saving is income minus consumption, and investment is output not consumed, so the two are equal by definition. Keynes treats saving as a residual: only decisions to consume and to invest are independent. His appendix applies user cost to supply price, long-period cost and redundant equipment. It matters because income, saving and investment so defined are the concepts relevant to the theory of employment.

**Prerequisites:** basic accounting (sales, costs, depreciation); marginal vs average cost; discounting; the idea of a firm's profit-maximising output.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Income is the entrepreneur's sales minus prime cost (factor cost + user cost). Aggregate income is $A-U$. It is unambiguous and is the quantity that matters for employment decisions (§ I, paras 4–5).
- User cost is the value sacrificed by using equipment rather than leaving it idle. It is normally positive in an economy where equipment is mostly made by other firms, and marginal user cost is hard to imagine as other than positive (§ I, paras 4, 6).
- Net income also deducts supplementary cost $V$, the involuntary but expected loss. It matters for consumption decisions, but its boundary with windfall losses is partly conventional (§ I, paras 10–14).
- Saving $=A_1-U=$ investment. This follows from three conditions that Keynes calls conformable to common sense and traditional usage, whatever the finer definitions (§ II, paras 3–5).
- Saving is a residual. Only decisions to consume and to invest are independent, so Keynes replaces the "propensity to save" with the propensity to consume (§ II, paras 6, 9).
- The appendix defines user cost for a firm as $U=A_1-I$, and states that short-period supply price = marginal factor cost + marginal user cost. Supposing marginal user cost to be zero is, in general, illegitimate (§ App. I, paras 2–4).
- Long-period supply price = prime + supplementary + risk + interest cost. Short-period supply price = marginal prime cost (§ App. I, para. 5).
- User cost is one of the links between present and future. It is the discounted value of the future yield lost by using equipment now, and it depends on how long redundancy is expected to last (§ App. II–III).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Income
**Set-up.** Over a period an entrepreneur sells finished output worth $A$ and buys finished output from other entrepreneurs worth $A_1$. He ends with capital equipment (including unfinished and finished stocks) of value $G$. Part of $A+G-A_1$ is due to equipment inherited from the previous period. Defining income means finding how much to deduct for that part (§ I, paras 1–2). Two principles are possible, one tied to production and one to consumption (§ I, para. 3).

**(i) User cost.** Suppose the entrepreneur had not used the equipment. He would still optimally have spent $B'$ on maintaining and improving it, and it would then be worth $G'$ at period end. So $G'-B'$ is the maximum net value that could have been conserved (§ I, para. 4). The sacrifice made to produce $A$ is the **user cost**:

$$U=(G'-B')-(G-A_1).$$

- **Factor cost** $F$ is what he pays other factors of production for their services. It is their income.
- **Prime cost** $=F+U$.
- The entrepreneur's **income** $=A-F-U$, which is his gross profit, the quantity he tries to maximise.
- The rest of the community's income equals $F$, so **aggregate income $=A-U$** (§ I, para. 4).

Keynes calls this income "completely unambiguous". It is causally significant for employment, because the entrepreneur maximises his expected excess of this quantity over payments to other factors when he decides employment (§ I, para. 5).

*Intuition note.* User cost is the "wear" of using something rather than keeping it. Income is what remains of sales after paying factors and charging for that wear.

**Sign of user cost.** $G-A_1$ may exceed $G'-B'$, so $U$ is negative. This can happen if input has risen but the output is not yet finished and sold. It also happens with positive investment when firms make their own equipment. Where equipment is mostly made by different firms, $U$ can normally be taken as positive. It is "difficult to conceive" of $dU/dA$ being other than positive (§ I, para. 6).

**Aggregates.** For the community, consumption $C=\Sigma(A-A_1)$ and investment $I=\Sigma(A_1-U)$. In a fully integrated system ($A_1=0$), $C=A$ and investment is $-U=G-(G'-B')$ (§ I, para. 7). (The text prints "S" for the summation sign; see Reading notes.)

**Effective demand.** It is the aggregate income (proceeds) entrepreneurs expect from the employment they decide to give. The aggregate demand function relates hypothetical employment levels to expected proceeds. Effective demand is the point on it that becomes effective, because with the supply conditions it matches the employment that maximises expected profit (§ I, para. 8). Marginal proceeds can also be equated to marginal factor cost. That gives the same sort of propositions as were stated by those economists who ignored user cost or set it to zero and equated supply price to marginal factor cost (§ I, para. 9).

**(ii) Supplementary cost and net income.** There may also be an involuntary change in equipment value from market changes, obsolescence, passage of time or catastrophe (§ I, para. 10).

- Some involuntary losses are broadly expected. Examples are loss through time, "normal" obsolescence (Pigou: regular enough to foresee "at least in the large") and insurable risks.
- The excess of this expected depreciation over user cost is the **supplementary cost** $V$. Keynes says it differs from Marshall's definition, though the idea is similar (§ I, para. 10).
- The involuntary and unforeseen loss is the **windfall loss**. It is charged to capital account and ignored in net income (§ I, para. 12).

As producer, the entrepreneur looks at prime cost and gross profit. As consumer, he treats $V$ as if it were part of prime cost. So **aggregate net income $=A-U-V$**. This comes nearest to common usage and is relevant to consumption (§ I, para. 11). Changes in $V$ tend to affect him like changes in gross profit. A windfall loss does enter his decisions, but not on the same scale as an equal $V$ (§ I, para. 13).

*Intuition note.* Gross income guides production decisions. Net income is what the ordinary man feels free to spend, after setting aside for expected, unavoidable losses.

**The line is conventional.** The boundary between $V$ and windfall loss is partly conventional or psychological, and no unique principle fixes it (§ I, para. 14).

- The windfall capital loss is the discounted value of the difference between the former and the revised expectation of the series of $U+V$ (§ I, para. 14).
- A widely approved accounting rule, endorsed by the Inland Revenue, fixes $U+V$ when equipment is acquired and leaves it unaltered. Then $V$ = that figure minus actual $U$, and windfall gain or loss over the equipment's whole life is zero (§ I, para. 14).
- In certain circumstances recalculating at accounting intervals (e.g. annually) is also reasonable, and businessmen differ. Keynes names the initial figure the **basic** supplementary cost and the recalculated one the **current** supplementary cost (§ I, para. 14).
- $V$ cannot be given a closer quantitative definition than as the deductions a typical entrepreneur makes before declaring a dividend or deciding his consumption. In doubt, assign an item to capital account (§ I, para. 15).

**Other authors.** Net income comes very close to Marshall's practice of treating, broadly speaking, as income whatever the Income Tax Commissioners treat as such. It also matches the money value of Pigou's latest "national dividend" (§ I, para. 16).

**Hayek's view:** an owner of capital goods might keep his investment-income constant and not spend until he had offset any decline in it. Keynes doubts such an individual exists but allows it as a possible psychological criterion of net income. Keynes says Hayek's inference that saving and investment are correspondingly vague is right only if he means *net* saving and net investment. Saving and investment as relevant to employment are objectively defined (§ I, para. 17).

It is therefore a mistake to put all the emphasis on net income, which is relevant only to consumption decisions, and to overlook (as has been usual) income proper, which is relevant to production decisions (§ I, para. 18).

**The *Treatise on Money*.** There Keynes defined entrepreneurs' profit as a "normal" or equilibrium profit, so saving exceeded investment by normal profit minus actual profit. He fears this caused considerable confusion, since conclusions valid only in his special sense were frequently adopted as if the terms had their familiar sense. He discards those terms (§ I, para. 19).

### II. Saving and Investment
So far as Keynes knows, everyone agrees that saving is income minus expenditure on consumption. Any doubt must therefore concern income or consumption (§ II, para. 1). Consumption is the value of goods sold to consumers. Any consistently applied line between consumer-purchasers and investor-purchasers serves, and having defined $A_1$ Keynes has implicitly settled it. So consumption $=\Sigma(A-A_1)$ (§ II, paras 1–2). Aggregates are written $A$, $A_1$, $U$.

Income is $A-U$ and consumption is $A-A_1$. Therefore:

$$\text{saving}=(A-U)-(A-A_1)=A_1-U,\qquad \text{net saving}=A_1-U-V.$$

Current investment is the addition to the value of capital equipment from the period's production. Entrepreneurs sold $A$, equipment changed by $-U$ after purchases $A_1$, and $A-A_1$ passed into consumption. The excess of $A-U$ over $A-A_1$ is therefore $A_1-U$, which equals saving. Net investment is $A_1-U-V$ (§ II, paras 3–4).

Saving results from consumers' collective behaviour and investment from entrepreneurs'. Yet they are necessarily equal. Keynes says this does not depend on peculiarities of his definitions. It follows provided three things hold: income equals the value of current output, investment equals the value of the output not consumed, and saving equals income minus consumption (§ II, para. 5).

$$\text{Income}=\text{output value}=\text{consumption}+\text{investment};\quad \text{saving}=\text{income}-\text{consumption};\quad\Rightarrow\ \text{saving}=\text{investment}.$$

The conclusion can be avoided only by denying one of these conditions (§ II, para. 5).

*Intuition note.* All output is sold either to consumers or to other entrepreneurs. What is not consumed is, by the same accounting, what adds to equipment. The two sides are one number seen from two directions.

**Why: bilateral transactions.** Each entrepreneur's investment is his purchases of equipment from others minus his own user cost. In aggregate, saving cannot differ from investment (§ II, paras 6–7). Saving is a "mere residual". Decisions to consume and to invest determine incomes. If investment decisions become effective, they must either curtail consumption or expand income, so saving rises by a corresponding amount (§ II, para. 7).

**A proviso.** Individuals might be so wilful (*tête montée*) that no price equilibrium existed, and output would then have no definite market value. Keynes says experience shows this is not so (§ II, para. 8). A market value for output is a necessary condition for money-income to have a definite value. It is also a sufficient condition for aggregate intended saving to equal aggregate intended investment (§ II, para. 8).

Clarity is perhaps best reached by thinking in terms of decisions to consume or invest, which are within individual power. Income and saving result from those choices, but neither can take an independent value from a separate set of decisions taken irrespective of the decisions to consume and to invest. So the **propensity to consume** will replace the propensity to save (§ II, para. 9).

### Appendix on User Cost
**I. Definition and supply price.** Keynes thinks user cost has an importance for classical value theory that has been overlooked (§ App. I, para. 1). Write the current investment in his own equipment as $I=G-(G'-B')$. Then (§ App. I, para. 2):

$$U=A_1+(G'-B')-G=A_1-I.$$

Part of outgoings to other entrepreneurs is balanced by investment in own equipment. The rest is the sacrifice made by the output sold. If $A_1=0$, $U$ is the current disinvestment from using equipment. The advantage is that at no stage of the analysis does factor cost have to be allocated between goods sold and equipment retained. Employment by a firm can be seen as one consolidated decision (§ App. I, para. 2).

- **Short-period supply price** of a unit of a firm's output = marginal factor cost + marginal user cost (§ App. I, para. 3).
- Modern value theory often equates it to marginal factor cost alone. Keynes says this is legitimate only if marginal user cost is zero, or if supply price is defined net of it, as "proceeds" and "aggregate supply price" are (§ App. I, para. 4).
- Applying that net-of-user-cost definition habitually and tacitly to one firm or industry "deprives our analysis of all reality" (§ App. I, para. 4).
- Even in a fully integrated firm, marginal user cost is not generally negligible (§ App. I, para. 4).

**Long-period supply price.** Long-period cost = expected prime cost (averaged over the equipment's life) + basic supplementary cost. To yield normal profit, the long-period supply price must exceed this by an amount set by the current interest rate on loans of comparable term and risk, reckoned as a percentage of the cost of the equipment. With a standard "pure" interest rate one must also include a third term, the **risk cost**. So long-period supply price = prime + supplementary + risk + interest cost. Short-period supply price = marginal prime cost (§ App. I, para. 5).

Hence in long-period equilibrium the excess of marginal over average prime cost equals supplementary + risk + interest cost (§ App. I, para. 5). The trading account breaks even where marginal prime cost equals average prime plus supplementary cost. This is zero net profit, and with smaller output there is a net loss (§ App. I, para. 6).

**Two extreme cases** of supplementary cost (§ App. I, para. 7):
- (i) If, for physical reasons, the whole of the current depreciation must be made good while using the equipment (e.g. oiling), user cost (apart from outside purchases) would be equal and opposite to supplementary cost. In long-period equilibrium marginal factor cost would then exceed average factor cost by the risk and interest cost.
- (ii) If all loss of value could occur only through use, supplementary cost would be zero.

An entrepreneur uses first the equipment with the lowest user cost plus factor cost per unit of output, not simply the oldest. So total user cost for a given output does not bear a uniform relation to marginal user cost (§ App. I, para. 8).

**II. How user cost is calculated.** User cost is one of the links between present and future. It is the expected sacrifice of future benefit from present use (§ App. II, para. 1). It equals the discounted value of the additional prospective yield obtained later if the equipment is not used now. That is at least the present value of the chance to postpone replacement, and it may be more (§ App. II, para. 2).

- With no surplus stock, marginal user cost is calculable from the shortening of life or efficiency and the current replacement cost (§ App. II, para. 3).
- With redundant equipment, it also depends on the interest rate and current supplementary cost until the redundancy is expected to be absorbed (§ App. II, para. 3).
- **Copper** (zero factor cost): the user cost of a ton of surplus copper is the greatest of the values obtained by taking the estimated future value at some date and subtracting interest cost and current supplementary cost from then to now (§ App. II, para. 4).
- For a redundant ship, factory or machine: its replacement cost discounted at the rate of its interest and current supplementary costs to the expected date of absorption (§ App. II, para. 5).
- If replacement will not be identical, take a proportion of the new equipment's user cost, given by comparative efficiency (§ App. II, para. 6).

**III. Redundancy and slumps.** When equipment is merely redundant, the gap between actual and normal user cost varies with the expected time until absorption (§ App. III, para. 1).

- If equipment is of all ages, marginal user cost will not decline greatly unless redundancy is exceptionally large (§ App. III, para. 1).
- In a general slump it depends on how long entrepreneurs expect the slump to last. A rise in supply price when affairs mend may partly reflect revised expectations (§ App. III, para. 1).
- Scrapping half the redundant plant can raise prices at once. It brings absorption of the redundancy nearer, raising marginal user cost and current supply price. Businessmen seem to hold the idea implicitly (§ App. III, para. 2).
- If supplementary cost is heavy and marginal costs are close to average, surplus equipment is likely to mean a net loss. The return to normal profit is gradual, not sudden (§ App. III, para. 2).

**IV. Other authors.**
- Marshall includes part of user cost in prime cost as "extra wear-and-tear" but gives no method (§ App. IV, para. 1).
- **Pigou** expressly assumes marginal disinvestment is negligible. Keynes says the notion runs through much recent theory (§ App. IV, para. 1).
- A very low marginal user cost belongs to particular situations (heavy idle-plant maintenance, rapid obsolescence, great redundancy), not to the short period as such (§ App. IV, para. 2).
- For raw materials the need to allow for user cost is obvious, but Keynes says copper is only an extreme case of what happens whenever capital equipment is used (§ App. IV, para. 3).
- User and supplementary costs apply to working, liquid and fixed capital alike. The difference is that liquid capital's return is a single term, while fixed capital yields a series of user costs and profits over periods (§ App. IV, para. 4).

*Intuition note.* Using a machine today shortens its useful future. Keynes says the price a firm needs must cover that lost future, which is why he says it is, in general, illegitimate to set marginal wear to zero.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take one firm. It buys $A_1=20$ of goods from other entrepreneurs and sells $A=100$. Left idle, its equipment would need $B'=5$ of upkeep and be worth $G'=100$ at period end. Actually it ends worth $G=105$.

- User cost: $U=(G'-B')-(G-A_1)=(100-5)-(105-20)=95-85=10$.
- Check with the appendix: $I=G-(G'-B')=105-95=10$, so $U=A_1-I=20-10=10$ ✓.
- Suppose the economy has one more firm: a supplier that sells its whole output, worth 20, to the first firm, and has zero user cost. The first firm sells all its output to consumers. Then aggregate sales are $A=100+20=120$, sales between entrepreneurs are $A_1=20$, and aggregate user cost is $U=10$.
- Income $=A-U=120-10=110$. Consumption $=A-A_1=120-20=100$.
- Saving $=110-100=10$. Investment $=A_1-U=20-10=10$. They are equal.
- If expected, unavoidable losses of $V=5$ are deducted: net income $=A-U-V=105$, net saving $=A_1-U-V=5$, net investment $=5$.

## Concepts
- [[user-cost]] (new) — value sacrificed by using equipment now rather than leaving it idle: $U=(G'-B')-(G-A_1)$ (§ I, para. 4)
- [[prime-cost]] (new) — factor cost plus user cost (§ I, para. 4)
- [[supplementary-cost]] (new) — involuntary but expected depreciation beyond user cost; boundary with windfall loss is conventional (§ I, paras 10, 14)
- [[windfall-loss]] (new) — involuntary, unforeseen change in equipment value, charged to capital account (§ I, para. 12)
- [[net-income]] (new) — $A-U-V$, the income relevant to consumption decisions (§ I, para. 11)
- [[income-saving-investment-identity]] (new) — saving equals investment because, given the three conditions stated, both equal income minus consumption (§ II, para. 5)
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — aggregate income entrepreneurs expect from the employment they choose to give (§ I, para. 8)
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — the conception, built on decisions to consume, that takes the place of the "propensity to save" (§ II, para. 9)
- [[marginal reasoning]] — Keynes uses marginal factor cost, marginal user cost and marginal proceeds to fix output and supply price (§ App. I, paras 3–5)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] user cost ↔ economics-finance: opportunity cost / value of waiting — using now forfeits discounted future yield.
- [structural-analogy] saving = investment by definition ↔ accounting identities in national accounts: one quantity measured from two sides.
- [metaphor] marginal user cost depending on expected length of the slump ↔ expectations-driven pricing: current price reflects beliefs about future recovery.

## Questions this raises
- If saving equals investment by definition, how do the plans of savers and investors get coordinated in practice? (Keynes only says that investment decisions, if effective, must curtail consumption or expand income, so saving rises correspondingly: § II, para. 7.)
- How sensitive is user cost to expectations about when redundancy will be absorbed?
- Does the conventional line between supplementary cost and windfall loss affect measured consumption behaviour?
- Is marginal user cost really never negative at the margin? (§ I, para. 6 says only that it is difficult to conceive.)
