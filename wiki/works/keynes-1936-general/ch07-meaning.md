# The Meaning of Saving and Investment Further Considered

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that saving and investment, taken in their plain senses, are necessarily equal for the community as a whole. He traces the rival views to differing definitions of investment or income, to a vague "forced saving" notion, and to treating a depositor's saving as one-sided.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter 6 defined saving and investment so that they are always equal. This chapter asks how that fits other writers' usage, where the two can differ. Keynes finds that the differences come from special definitions of investment (a possible one from Hawtrey) or of income (his own *Treatise on Money*, Robertson). "Forced saving" has no clear meaning without a norm. Keynes thinks the belief that banks can make saving and investment differ comes from ignoring that a depositor's dealing with a bank has two sides. Individuals can choose their saving, but all of them cannot raise total saving by cutting consumption, because incomes change. This matters because aggregate theory differs from theory of the single unit.

**Prerequisites:** income = consumption + saving; the idea of aggregate vs individual; what a bank deposit and bank credit are; basic idea of output and employment.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Keynes says that, as far as he knows, everyone agrees saving is income minus consumption; disagreements about whether saving equals investment come from definitions of investment or of income (§I, para. 2).
- Keynes's investment is the net addition to all capital equipment. He says this matches popular usage, assuming popular income matches his net income and since exchanges of old assets and debts cancel out in the aggregate (§II, paras. 1–2).
- Hawtrey has suggested a possible definition of investment that leaves out changes in unsold stocks. Keynes prefers to stress the total change in effective demand (the demand entrepreneurs expect for output), not only the part that reflects stock changes (§II, para. 3).
- In Keynes's own *Treatise on Money*, the gap between saving and investment came from an income defined with entrepreneurs' "normal profit". He says it was a way of handling changes in profit, and that the new argument is, in essence, a development of it (§III, paras. 1–2).
- Robertson's "excess of saving over investment" means the same as a fall in income in Keynes's sense (§III, para. 3).
- "Forced saving" (saving above some standard) has no meaning until a standard rate of saving is specified. With full-employment saving as the standard, forced excess would be rare and forced deficiency usual (§IV, para. 3).
- Saving cannot happen without an asset being acquired, so aggregate saving equals new investment. Keynes says the idea that bank credit allows investment with no "genuine" saving can only come from isolating one consequence of the credit (§V, paras. 1–2).
- Individual saving is apparently free, but all individuals cannot save any given sums at once, since an attempt to save more by cutting consumption affects incomes and defeats itself (§V, para. 4).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Aim of the chapter
- Chapter 6 defined saving and investment so that they are necessarily equal, as two aspects of one thing. Some writers, including Keynes in the *Treatise on Money*, use definitions on which they can differ. Others assume they can differ without defining them. Keynes classifies the usages (§I, para. 1).
- Keynes says that, as far as he knows, everyone agrees that saving is income minus consumption. The differences lie in the definition of investment or of income (§I, para. 2).

### II. Definitions of investment
- Popular usage commonly means buying an asset, old or new. Keynes counts the sale of an investment as negative investment (disinvestment), so exchanges of old investments cancel (§II, para. 1).
- Creditor and debtor positions also cancel in aggregate, since the rise in one equals the rise in the other. So aggregate investment in the popular sense coincides with his net investment: the net addition to all capital equipment, after allowing for value changes of old equipment that enter net income. This assumes popular income corresponds to his net income (§II, para. 1).
- Investment includes fixed, working and liquid capital. Apart from the gross/net distinction, the significant differences of definition come from excluding one or more of these (§II, para. 2).
- **Hawtrey**, who attaches great importance to liquid capital (undesigned changes in the stock of unsold goods), has suggested a possible definition of investment that excludes such changes. On it, excess saving is an undesigned rise in unsold stock. **Keynes objects:** this stresses only the correction of unforeseen changes. He grants that for consumption goods stock changes play an important part in output decisions, but sees no reason to exclude other influences, and prefers to stress the total change of effective demand. For fixed capital, unused capacity plays the part of unsold stock, and he does not see how Hawtrey's method handles it (§II, para. 3).
- **Austrian school:** Keynes thinks their "capital formation" and "capital consumption" probably do not match his terms. He could not find a clear explanation of them (§II, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Keynes's investment is the net addition to capital equipment. Buying an old asset just moves ownership from one person to another, so it adds nothing to the total.

### III. Definitions of income
- In the *Treatise on Money*, income counted, "in some sense", entrepreneurs' normal profit instead of their realised profit. An excess of saving over investment then meant output at which entrepreneurs earned less than normal profit. A larger excess meant falling actual profits and a motive to cut output (§III, para. 1).
- **Present view (Keynes's own, as he now sees it):** the entrepreneur fixes employment to maximise present and prospective profits. The profit-maximising volume depends on the aggregate demand function, given by expected proceeds from consumption and investment (§III, para. 2).
- In the *Treatise*, changes in the excess of investment over saving (as defined there) were a way of handling changes in profit, though Keynes did not there distinguish clearly between expected and realised results. He argued there that a change in that excess was the motive force governing changes in output. He now thinks the new argument much more accurate and instructive, yet essentially a development of the old. In *Treatise* language: the expectation of a larger excess of investment over saving induces entrepreneurs to raise employment and output. Both arguments aim to show that employment is determined by entrepreneurs' estimates of effective demand, an expected rise of investment relative to saving (as defined in the *Treatise*) being a criterion of a rise in effective demand. Keynes adds that the *Treatise* exposition is very confusing and incomplete in the light of these developments (§III, para. 2).
- **Robertson:** today's income equals yesterday's consumption plus investment, so his saving today equals yesterday's investment plus the excess of yesterday's consumption over today's. So saving can exceed investment by yesterday's income (Keynes's sense) minus today's income. Robertson's "excess of saving" thus equals a fall in income in Keynes's sense. If current expectations were always set by yesterday's results, effective demand would equal yesterday's income. Keynes says Robertson's method might be regarded as an alternative attempt, perhaps a first approximation, to make the same distinction that he makes between effective demand and income (§III, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* On Keynes's account, the gaps between saving and investment in these two writers come from how income is defined (normal versus realised profit in the *Treatise*; yesterday's versus today's income in Robertson). They do not arise on his present definitions, on which the two are equal (§I, para. 1).

### IV. "Forced saving"
- Hayek and Robbins have not said exactly what they mean by "forced saving", but Keynes says it is clear that, in their sense, it results directly from, and is measured by, changes in the quantity of money or bank credit. He is no longer confident that his *Treatise* gap had much affinity with earlier uses of the phrase, and feels sure that the newer uses have no definite relation to it (§IV, para. 1).
- Keynes says a change in output and employment changes income measured in wage-units, and a change in the wage-unit redistributes income between borrowers and lenders and changes aggregate money income. Changes in the quantity of money may, through the rate of interest, change the volume and distribution of income (as he says will be shown later). In each case the amount saved may change, but this is no more "forced" than any other change in saving. Only a stated norm can separate cases. The size of the change in saving from a money change varies a great deal (§IV, para. 2).
- With full-employment saving at long-period equilibrium as the norm, forced saving would be the excess of actual saving over what would be saved at full employment in long-period equilibrium. This would make good sense, but a forced excess would then be very rare and unstable, and a forced deficiency usual (§IV, para. 3).
- Per Hayek's note, the term began with **Bentham**, who meant more money when "all hands" were employed. Real income then cannot rise, so extra investment forces frugality. Keynes says that stretching this clear notion to less than full employment brings difficulties and needs explanation or qualification, which modern writers seem as a rule to overlook. Diminishing returns mean some sacrifice for those already employed, but he thinks trying to link that loss to the investment accompanying more employment is unlikely to be fruitful (§IV, para. 4).

### V. Why saving and investment are thought to differ
- Keynes thinks the belief is explained by an "optical illusion": seeing a depositor's relation to his bank as one-sided. Savings are supposed to vanish into the banks, or banks to enable investment without saving (§V, para. 1).
- **His argument:** no one can save without acquiring an asset (cash, debt or capital goods). An asset one did not have can only be acquired if an equal-value asset is newly produced (then there is new investment) or someone else parts with one. In the second case that person is dis-saving: consuming more than income, not suffering a capital-value loss. If a bank parts with an asset, someone parts with cash. So the saving of the first person plus others equals current new investment (§V, para. 1).
- **Bank credit:** the "no genuine saving" idea isolates one consequence of the credit. Extra credit that allows investment which would not otherwise have occurred raises incomes, normally faster than investment. Except at full employment, real income rises too. The public exercises "a free choice" in dividing its extra income between saving and spending, so the borrowing entrepreneur's intention cannot become effective (except in place of other entrepreneurs' investment that would otherwise have occurred) faster than the public decides to increase its saving. These savings are as genuine as any other. Employment, incomes and prices must move until someone chooses to hold the extra money (§V, para. 2).
- Keynes grants that unforeseen investment may make saving and investment irregular. Credit also creates three tendencies: higher output, a higher marginal product in wage-units, and a higher wage-unit in money. They may redistribute real income. But they come with rising output as such, whatever starts it, and could be avoided "only by avoiding any course of action capable of improving employment" (§V, para. 2).
- The old view that saving always involves investment, though incomplete and misleading, is "formally sounder" than the new view of saving without investment. The error lies in the plausible inference that an individual's saving raises aggregate investment by the same amount. Individual saving raises one's own wealth, but the conclusion that it raises aggregate wealth ignores that it may react on someone else's savings and wealth (§V, para. 3).
- **Reconciliation:** saving, like spending, is two-sided. One's own saving hardly affects one's own income, but one's consumption affects others' incomes. So all individuals cannot simultaneously save any given sums. An attempt to save more by cutting consumption "necessarily defeats itself". Likewise the community cannot save less than investment, because the attempt raises incomes until saving equals investment (§V, para. 4).
- **Analogy with money:** individuals may change their money holdings freely, yet total balances must equal the cash the banking system created. Holdings depend on incomes and on securities prices, which change until holdings equal that cash. Keynes calls this the fundamental proposition of monetary theory (§V, para. 5).
- Both propositions follow from there being no buyer without a seller. An individual whose transactions are small can ignore this. For aggregate demand it is nonsense to do so. This is the vital difference between aggregate theory and theory of the individual unit (§V, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* One person's spending is another person's income. When everyone cuts spending to save more, everyone's incomes are affected, so the attempt defeats itself and the group cannot achieve what each person tries to do alone.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume investment is fixed at $I=20$. Assume people want to save a fraction $s$ of income, and income adjusts to equal spending $C+I$ (our added assumption).
- Start: $s=0.2$. Equilibrium needs $S=I$, so $0.2Y=20$ and $Y=100$. Then $C=80$ and $S=20$.
- Everyone tries to save more: $s=0.25$. At $Y=100$ they would save $25>20$, so spending is $75+20=95<100$ and income falls. Equilibrium needs $0.25Y=20$, so $Y=80$, $C=60$, $S=20$.
- Result: the attempt to save more leaves total saving at 20 (= investment), but income is lower by 20. This is Keynes's point that the attempt "defeats itself" (§V, para. 4).

## Concepts
- [[saving-investment-identity]] (new) — for the community, saving and investment are equal as aspects of one thing (§I, para. 1)
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — entrepreneurs' estimate of demand, based on expected proceeds from consumption and investment; Keynes says employment is determined by these estimates (§III, para. 2)
- [[forced-saving]] (new) — saving above a chosen norm; meaningless without a norm (§IV, para. 3)
- [[two-sided-transaction]] (new) — buying needs selling; ignoring this fails at the aggregate level (§V, para. 6)
- [[fallacy-of-composition]] (new; Keynes does not use this term) — what an individual can do (save more) cannot be done by all together (§V, para. 4)

## Questions this raises
- If saving and investment are equal by definition, which variable adjusts when plans differ? Keynes's answer here is income.
- How would "forced saving" be measured with a norm other than full employment?
- Is Robertson's lagged income only a first approximation, as Keynes suggests it perhaps is?
- What does Keynes mean by "genuine" saving when someone holds the extra money?
