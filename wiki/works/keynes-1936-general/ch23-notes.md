# Notes on Mercantilism, the Usury Laws, Stamped Money and Theories of Under-Consumption

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that the mercantilists, the old usury laws, Gesell and the under-consumption writers each grasped part of a truth that classical theory buried: the interest rate and the volume of investment are not self-adjusting at the optimum, and too much thrift can harm employment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks whether the heretics rejected by classical economists were really nonsense. He finds that each saw a real problem, though he judges their analysis incomplete. Mercantilists, in his account, saw that a trade surplus brings money, lowers interest and stimulates investment. He now reads the medieval usury rules as an effort to keep interest low while the return on capital stayed high. Gesell proposed stamped money, a carrying cost on money, to cut the interest rate. Mandeville, Malthus and Hobson argued that thrift can reduce employment. Keynes reads the mercantilist record as showing that a weak inducement to invest has long been the key problem. The classical school ignored it by building premisses in which it cannot arise.

**Prerequisites:** Keynes's earlier chapters: liquidity-preference (the wish to hold money), marginal efficiency of capital (the expected return on new investment), inducement to invest, propensity to consume, effective demand, wage-unit (the money wage per unit of labour); the gold standard.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Mercantilist concern with a favourable trade balance had, in Keynes's words, an element of scientific truth. Under stated conditions a surplus adds foreign investment and brings precious metals, which lower the domestic interest rate (§ II).
- Keynes names two limits: rising domestic costs, and foreign lending that outruns the surplus. He says a favourable balance is stimulating "provided it is not too large". He also says there are strong general presumptions against trade restrictions unless justified on special grounds (§ II).
- In Keynes's reading, mercantilists were aware that interest depends on liquidity-preference and the quantity of money. They feared hoarding and the "fear of goods". Keynes reads their experience as showing a chronic tendency for saving to exceed the inducement to invest (§ III).
- Keynes argues that the international gold standard ties domestic prosperity to a competitive scramble for markets and metal. He proposes an autonomous interest rate and national investment programmes instead (§ III).
- The mercantilists perceived the problem without solving it. The classical school ignored it by building premisses in which it cannot arise, which created a cleavage between theory and common sense (§ IV).
- Keynes now reads the usury laws and the schoolmen's discussions as aiming to keep the interest rate low while the marginal efficiency of capital stayed high. He revalues them (§ V).
- Gesell saw that the money-rate of interest limits capital growth and proposed stamped money. He lacked a theory of liquidity-preference. Money's liquidity-premium differs only in degree from that of other assets (§ VI).
- Mandeville, Malthus and Hobson–Mummery held that thrift can reduce output. Keynes says Hobson misplaced the evil in over-accumulation of capital, and had no independent theory of the interest rate (§ VII).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Mercantilism versus free trade
- For some two hundred years, theorists and practical men did not doubt that a favourable balance of trade is a peculiar advantage and an unfavourable one a grave danger, particularly if precious metals flow out (§ I).
- For the past hundred years opinion has diverged. The majority of statesmen and practical men in most countries, and nearly half even in Britain, stayed with the old view. Almost all theorists held that anxiety about the balance is groundless except on a very short view, because foreign trade is self-adjusting. They said interference is futile and greatly impoverishes those who practise it, by forfeiting the advantages of the international division of labour (§ I).
- Keynes calls the old view *mercantilism* and the new one *free trade*, with the terms read according to context (§ I).
- Modern economists have held that the mercantilist argument is based "from start to finish" on an intellectual confusion. Marshall had no regard for their central theory and did not mention the elements of truth Keynes will examine. Concessions such as infant industries or terms of trade do not touch the real substance of the case (§ I).
- Keynes quotes himself in 1923, as a faithful pupil of the classical school with no reservations: "If there is one thing that Protection can not do, it is to cure Unemployment" (§ I).

*Intuition note:* Keynes confesses that he once shared the orthodoxy he now criticises. The chapter re-examines that view through the history of rejected ideas.

### II. What is true in mercantilism (Keynes's own terms)
- The advantages claimed are avowedly national, and unlikely to benefit the world as a whole (§ II).
- **The argument.** In a country growing rapidly in wealth, under laissez-faire, further progress is liable to be interrupted by insufficient inducement to invest. Aggregate investment is home plus foreign investment, the latter including accumulation of precious metals. Where investment is determined by the profit motive alone, home investment is governed in the long run by the domestic interest rate. Foreign investment is determined by the size of the favourable trade balance. Where there is no direct investment by public authority, the government's proper concerns are therefore the domestic interest rate and the trade balance (§ II).
- **Conditions.** If the wage-unit is fairly stable (a condition Keynes says is almost always satisfied), if liquidity-preference (averaged over short fluctuations) is fairly stable, and if banking conventions are stable, then the interest rate tends to be governed by the quantity of precious metals, measured in wage-units, available to satisfy the desire for liquidity. When substantial foreign loans and overseas ownership are scarcely practicable, changes in that quantity depend largely on the trade balance (§ II).
- **Conclusion.** Pursuing a favourable balance served both aims. For authorities with no direct control over the domestic rate or other inducements to home investment, it was the only direct means of raising foreign investment. Through the metal inflow it was also their only indirect means of lowering the domestic rate and so raising home investment (§ II).
- **Two limits.**
  1. If the interest rate falls so low that investment pushes employment past critical points where the wage-unit rises, higher domestic costs react unfavourably on the trade balance. The effort then defeats itself.
  2. If the domestic rate falls so far below rates elsewhere that foreign lending is out of proportion to the favourable balance, an outflow of metal may reverse the gains.
  - The risk is greater for a large, internationally important country, because with small mine output an inflow of metal into one country is an outflow from another (§ II).
- **Examples.** Spain in the later 15th and 16th centuries had its foreign trade destroyed by the effect of excess precious metals on the wage-unit. Pre-war twentieth-century Britain had excessive facilities for foreign lending that frequently stood in the way of the fall in the domestic rate needed for full employment. India is described as impoverished by a liquidity-preference so strong a passion that even an enormous, chronic metal inflow has been insufficient to bring the rate down to a level compatible with growth (§ II).
- **Result under stated conditions.** For a society with a stable wage-unit, given propensities to consume and for liquidity, and money rigidly tied to precious metals, it is essential to prosperity that authorities pay close attention to the trade balance. A favourable balance, provided it is not too large, will prove extremely stimulating. An unfavourable one may soon produce persistent depression (§ II).
- **Caveats.** It does not follow that maximum import restriction gives the maximum favourable balance. Earlier mercantilists often opposed restrictions for this reason. Keynes says it is arguable that near-complete free trade best produced a favourable balance in mid-nineteenth-century Britain. Post-war European restrictions often had the opposite effect to that intended (§ II).
- Keynes gives three grounds for a general presumption against trade restrictions, unless they can be justified on special grounds: (a) the advantages of the division of labour are real, though the classical school greatly overstressed them; (b) one country's gain from a favourable balance is liable to involve another's loss, a point the mercantilists were fully alive to. So moderation is needed, and an immoderate policy may lead to senseless competition that injures all; (c) a policy of restrictions is a treacherous instrument, since private interest, incompetence and difficulty may divert it into results opposite to those intended (§ II).
- **Target of Keynes's criticism.** It is the theoretical foundation of laissez-faire he was taught: the notion that the interest rate and the volume of investment are self-adjusting at the optimum level, so that preoccupation with the balance of trade is a waste of time. The faculty of economists proved guilty of presumptuous error in treating as a puerile obsession what had for centuries been a prime object of statecraft (§ II).
- **Bank rate with rigid exchange parity.** Under this faulty theory the City of London devised bank rate coupled with a rigid parity of the foreign exchanges. That wholly ruled out keeping a domestic rate consistent with full employment. Since the balance of payments cannot be neglected, a means of controlling it was evolved that sacrificed the domestic rate to blind forces. Keynes says London bankers have recently learnt much, and he can almost hope bank rate will never again be used to protect the foreign balance where it is likely to cause unemployment at home (§ II).
- Keynes says classical theory, as the theory of the individual firm and of distribution given resources, cannot be impugned. As a contribution to statecraft, concerned with the whole system, the early pioneers may have reached fragments of practical wisdom that Ricardo's abstractions first forgot and then obliterated. He lists their preoccupations: keeping the interest rate down by usury laws, maintaining the stock of money, discouraging rises in the wage-unit, and devaluing in the last resort to restore a deficient money stock (§ II).

*Intuition note:* In Keynes's account, with money tied to metal, a fairly stable wage-unit and authorities lacking direct control of interest, a trade surplus was their only direct way to raise foreign investment and their only indirect way to lower interest. That explains why a preoccupation with surpluses served both aims. The policy has limits, because costs rise and money flows out.

### III. The mercantilists' own reasons (mainly quoted from Heckscher)
Keynes says the early pioneers may have hit on their maxims without having much cognisance of the underlying theory. He examines the reasons they gave, using Heckscher's work (§ III).

**(1) Interest and the quantity of money.**
- Keynes says mercantilists never supposed that the interest rate adjusts itself to the right level. They were emphatic that an unduly high rate was the main obstacle to the growth of wealth. In his view they were even aware that it depends on liquidity-preference and the quantity of money. Several made clear that they wanted more money in order to lower interest (§ III).
- **Heckscher:** for the more perspicacious mercantilists, money was a factor of production like land. Interest was the rent paid for money, and the objective reason for its level was the total quantity of money (§ III).
- Evidence cited:
  - Malynes (1622) said plenty of money lowers the rate of usury. Misselden replied that plenty of money may be the remedy for usury.
  - Child (1668) demanded a legal maximum rate. Petty (1676) explained the fall in the "natural" rate from 10 to 6 per cent by more money.
  - French merchants (1701, 1706) blamed scarcity of coin for high interest (§ III).
- **Locke.** Keynes says he was perhaps the first to express the link abstractly, in his controversy with Petty. He opposed a maximum rate as impracticable, like a maximum rent for land, because the natural value of money in yielding interest depends on the quantity of money in the kingdom in proportion to the whole trade. Money has a value in use (given by the interest rate, like land) and a value in exchange (like a commodity) (§ III).
  - Keynes's reading: Locke was the parent of twin quantity theories. The first makes the interest rate depend on the quantity of money (allowing for velocity) relative to the value of trade. The second makes money's value in exchange depend on money relative to goods (§ III).
  - Keynes says Locke stood with one foot in the mercantilist world and one in the classical. He was confused about the relation between the two proportions and overlooked altogether fluctuations in liquidity-preference. Keynes adds that Locke never, he thinks, reached a genuine synthesis (§ III).
  - Locke held that a lower interest rate has no direct effect on the price-level. It affects prices only as it brings in or carries out money or commodities (§ III).
- Keynes says a 1621 pamphlet quoted by Locke illustrates how easily the mercantilist mind separated the interest rate from the marginal efficiency of capital. It says high interest decays trade because the advantage from interest is greater than the profit from trade, so rich merchants put out their stock to interest and the lesser merchants break (§ III).
- **Hoarding.** Mercantilists did not overlook that hoarding of the metal influx would lose the interest-rate advantage. Mun nevertheless advocated state treasure. Others opposed it. Heckscher reports that Schrötter drew a parallel between monastery treasure and the export surplus of precious metals, and that Davenant blamed Eastern poverty on treasure suffered to stagnate in princes' coffers. Heckscher adds that private hoarding was to be shunned like the pest, and that he does not think a single dissentient voice could be found (§ III).

**(2) The fallacy of cheapness.**
- Malynes (1622): do not undersell others to the commonwealth's hurt. Cheapness comes from scarcity of money, and trade increases when money is plentiful and goods dearer (§ III).
- **Heckscher:** over a century and a half the view was that a country with relatively less money than others must "sell cheap and buy dear". Hales (mid-16th century) preferred to raise our prices as foreigners raise theirs. Petty and Coke wanted more treasure than neighbouring nations (§ III).

**(3) The fear of goods and unemployment.**
- Import bans justified by unemployment appear in Florence (1426) and England (from at least 1455). A French decree of 1466 was not directed against foreign goods but mentioned giving work to the unemployed. Writings of the 1530s, two of them believed to be by Clement Armstrong, blame imports for scarcity of money and for idle handicrafts (§ III).
- **1621 House of Commons.** Sir Edwin Sandys described idle looms and broken contracts, "not (thanks be to God) for want of fruits of the earth, but for want of money" (§ III).
- Heckscher says the mercantilists' policy "killed two birds with one stone": the country was rid of a surplus of goods believed to cause unemployment, and the stock of money rose. Keynes adds the resulting advantage of a fall in the interest rate (§ III).
- **Keynes's inference.** He says it is impossible to study the mercantilists' notions without perceiving a chronic tendency throughout history for the propensity to save to be stronger than the inducement to invest. "The weakness of the inducement to invest has been at all times the key to the economic problem." Today the cause may chiefly lie in large existing accumulations. Formerly, risks and hazards may have played a larger part. The result is the same (§ III).

**(4) Nationalism and war.**
- Mercantilists were open about aiming at national advantage and relative strength. Keynes finds their realism intellectually preferable to the confused thinking of modern advocates of an international fixed gold standard and laissez-faire in lending, who believe these promote peace (§ III).
- **Keynes's argument.** If money contracts and customs are fixed over an appreciable period, and the domestic money stock and interest rate are set by the balance of payments (as in pre-war Britain), there is no orthodox means of countering home unemployment except struggling for an export surplus and metal imports at neighbours' expense. The gold (earlier silver) standard thus made domestic prosperity depend on a competitive pursuit of markets and of precious metals. The struggle might abate when new supplies of gold and silver were abundant. With growing wealth and a diminishing marginal propensity to consume, it has tended to become increasingly internecine (§ III).
- When some countries threw off the obligations that had made an autonomous interest rate impossible, orthodox economists taught that restoring them was a necessary first step to recovery. Keynes says the opposite holds good. An autonomous interest rate and a national investment programme directed to optimum domestic employment are twice blessed: they help ourselves and our neighbours. Their simultaneous pursuit by all countries is capable of restoring economic health internationally (§ III).

*Intuition note:* Keynes says the mercantilists perceived the problem but could not push their analysis to a solution. Under a rigid metal standard, one country's relief is another's loss. That is why he proposes that each country have an autonomous interest rate.

### IV. Why the classical school lost touch with common sense
- The mercantilists perceived the problem without solving it. The classical school ignored it by building premisses in which it cannot arise, which created a cleavage between theory and common sense. Keynes says its extraordinary achievement was to overcome the beliefs of the "natural man" and, at the same time, to be wrong (§ IV).
- **Heckscher:** the notions about money were deep-rooted from the Crusades to the 18th century. With the exception of the laissez-faire period no age has been free from them. The unique intellectual tenacity of laissez-faire overcame the beliefs of the natural man only for a time. The fear of goods is the most natural attitude in a money economy (§ IV).
- Keynes recalls Bonar Law's mingled rage and perplexity at economists for denying the obvious. He compares the sway of the classical school with that of certain religions, since exorcising the obvious takes more power than introducing the recondite (§ IV).

### V. The usury laws
- For centuries enlightened opinion held that the interest rate is not self-adjusting at the socially best level but constantly tends to rise too high, so a wise government curbs it by statute, custom and the moral law. The classical school repudiated this as childish. Keynes says it deserves rehabilitation and honour (§ V).
- In the ancient and medieval worlds, destruction of the inducement to invest by excessive liquidity-preference was the outstanding evil and the prime impediment to growth. Some risks lower the marginal efficiency of capital and others raise liquidity-preference. In a world no one reckoned safe, it was almost inevitable that the rate would rise too high unless curbed by every instrument society had (§ V).
- Keynes was taught that the Church's attitude was absurd and that its distinctions between money-loan returns and returns on investment were merely jesuitical. He now reads them as an honest effort to keep separate the rate of interest and the marginal efficiency of capital. It now seems clear to him that the schoolmen sought a formula allowing the marginal-efficiency schedule to be high while rule, custom and moral law kept interest down (§ V).
- Adam Smith was moderate on usury laws. He knew savings may go into investment or into debts, with no security for the former. He favoured a low rate so savings more likely find outlets in new investment, and defended a moderate application of the usury laws (§ V).
- **Bentham's objection.** Bentham attacked Smith as too severe on "projectors", people who try improvements with the help of wealth. A maximum rate leaves too little margin for legitimate, socially advisable risks. Keynes agrees that Bentham is right to protest against laws that stand in the way of legitimate risk-taking. Keynes asks whether this is what Smith meant, or the voice of 19th-century England speaking to the 18th. Nothing short of the exuberance of the greatest age of the inducement to invest could have made it possible to lose sight of the theoretical possibility of its insufficiency (§ V).

### VI. Silvio Gesell and stamped money
- Keynes calls Gesell a strange, unduly neglected prophet whose work contains flashes of deep insight. Keynes and other academics first treated him as a crank. Keynes saw the merit only after reaching his own conclusions (§ VI).
- **Life and work (as Keynes tells it).**
  - Gesell was a successful German merchant in Buenos Aires, led to monetary study by the crisis of the late 1880s.
  - His standard work is called *The Natural Economic Order* in English.
  - In April 1919 he joined the short-lived Soviet cabinet of Bavaria as Finance Minister and was afterwards tried by court-martial.
  - He died in 1930. The movement's main strength now lies in the United States, where Irving Fisher, alone among academic economists, has recognised its significance (§ VI).
- Keynes describes the book's purpose as an anti-Marxian socialism built on repudiating the classical hypotheses and unfettering competition. He believes the future will learn more from the spirit of Gesell than from that of Marx (§ VI).
- **Gesell's contribution.**
  1. He distinguishes the interest rate from the marginal efficiency of capital, and argues that the interest rate sets a limit to the growth of real capital.
  2. He says interest is a purely monetary phenomenon. Money, as a store of wealth, involves negligible carrying charges, and forms of wealth that do involve them yield a return because of the standard set by money.
  3. He cites the stability of the interest rate across the ages as evidence that it cannot depend on purely physical characters. In Keynes's terms, the interest rate depends on constant psychological characters and has remained stable. The widely fluctuating characters, which primarily determine the schedule of the marginal efficiency of capital, have determined not the interest rate but the rate at which the (more or less) given interest rate allows real capital to grow (§ VI).
- **The defect.** Gesell shows that only the existence of a money rate of interest allows a yield from lending out stocks of commodities. His Robinson Crusoe dialogue is, Keynes says, an excellent economic parable for this point. Having given the reason why the money rate, unlike most commodity rates, cannot be negative, he overlooks the need to explain why it is positive. He fails to explain why it is not governed by the yield on productive capital, as the classical school holds. This is because liquidity-preference had escaped him. Keynes says he has constructed only half a theory of the interest rate (§ VI).
- **The proposal.** Gesell argues that the growth of real capital is held back by the money rate. If the brake were removed, growth would be so rapid that a zero money rate would probably be justified within a comparatively short time, though not forthwith. The prime necessity is therefore to reduce the money rate, and he pointed out that this can be done by making money incur carrying costs like other barren goods. Notes would keep their value only if stamped each month, like an insurance card, with stamps bought at a post office. Keynes says the idea may carry the essence of what is needed, though it is not feasible in the form proposed (§ VI).
  - Keynes's rule: the stamp charge should roughly equal the excess of the money rate of interest (apart from stamps) over the marginal efficiency of capital corresponding to a rate of new investment compatible with full employment.
  - Gesell suggested 1 per mil per week, equal to 5.2 per cent per year. Keynes thinks that would be too high in existing conditions. The correct figure, to be changed from time to time, could only be reached by trial and error (§ VI).
- **Keynes's assessment.** The idea behind stamped money is sound, and means might be found to apply it on a modest scale. But Gesell did not face many difficulties. He was unaware that money is not unique in having a liquidity-premium. It differs only in degree from many other articles, deriving its importance from a greater liquidity-premium. If notes lost their premium, a long series of substitutes would step in: bank-money, debts at call, foreign money, jewellery, precious metals. Keynes also says there have probably been times when the craving to own land kept up the interest rate, though under Gesell's system land nationalisation would remove that (§ VI).

*Intuition note:* Gesell's stamp charge makes holding cash costly, so cash no longer sets a high floor for the interest rate. Keynes accepts that the money rate needs to be reduced but sees difficulties, because people can move into other liquid assets.

### VII. Theories of under-consumption
- The theories above concern the inducement to invest. The other constituent of effective demand is the propensity to consume. Under-consumption explanations, equally unpopular with the classical economists, played a much smaller part in 16th–17th-century thought and have only gathered force in comparatively recent times (§ VII).
- **Mercantilists.** Heckscher quotes a belief in the utility of luxury and the evil of thrift. Thrift was blamed for unemployment because (a) real income was thought to fall by the money that did not enter into exchange, and (b) saving was believed to withdraw money from circulation. Examples include Laffemas (1598), Petty (1662), Fortrey, von Schrötter, Barbon (1690) and Cary (1695) (§ VII).
- **Mandeville.** *The Fable of the Bees* popularised Barbon's opinion. It was convicted as a nuisance by the Middlesex grand jury in 1723, and only Dr Johnson is recorded as speaking well of it. Its poem "The Grumbling Hive" describes a prosperous community that suddenly turns frugal and then suffers falling prices, idle trades and unemployment. The commentary rejects the idea that frugality, good for a private family, has the same effect for a whole nation. It holds that national greatness depends on giving everybody an opportunity to be employed (§ VII).
- Keynes says moralists and economists for two centuries preferred austerity. He cites Gladstonian finance and a state that "could not afford" hospitals and noble buildings (§ VII).
- **Malthus.** The doctrine did not reappear in respectable circles for another century, until the later phase of Malthus. In passages Keynes quotes from his own essay, Malthus argues that:
  - An attempt to accumulate very rapidly, by greatly impairing the usual motives to production, must prematurely check the progress of wealth.
  - Smith's claims about parsimony are true to a great extent, but not to an indefinite extent.
  - There must be some intermediate point of saving, which political economy may not be able to ascertain, where the encouragement to wealth is greatest.
  - Say's dictum that a product consumed is a closed market appears to him the opinion most directly opposed to just theory (§ VII).
- Ricardo was stone-deaf to what Malthus was saying. The last echo is in J. S. Mill's wages-fund theory, which played a vital part in his rejection of Malthus. Mill's successors rejected the wages fund but overlooked that his refutation of Malthus depended on it. They dismissed the problem by not mentioning it (§ VII).
- **Hobson and Mummery.** Under-consumption theories hibernated until their *Physiology of Industry* (1889).
  - Hobson tells how the book led the London Extension Board to refuse him courses in Political Economy, and a lecture invitation to be withdrawn (§ VII).
  - **Their claims.** Their preface attacks the doctrine that saving enriches and spending impoverishes. They say undue saving is possible, impoverishes the community, throws labourers out of work, drives down wages and produces depression (§ VII).
  - **Their argument.** Capital serves production for consumers. Saving raises capital while reducing consumption, so undue saving creates capital beyond what is required, as general over-production (§ VII).
  - They conclude that the quantity produced may be reduced far below the maximum set by natural agents, capital and labour. In the normal state of modern communities "consumption limits production and not production consumption". They also say free-trade arguments assume over-supply is impossible (§ VII).
  - Keynes calls the later argument admittedly incomplete but the first explicit statement that capital comes into existence in response to demand from actual and prospective consumption, not from the propensity to save. To the claim that a fall in profit rates would check saving, they reply that it must work either by inducing more spending or by inducing less production. Falling aggregate income does not induce more spending, and a profit fall due to over-supply does check production, which is the centre of their argument (§ VII).
- **Keynes's criticisms.**
  - Hobson wrongly supposes that excessive saving causes an actual accumulation of capital beyond requirements. Keynes says this is a *secondary* evil that arises only through mistakes of foresight. The *primary* evil is a propensity to save, at full employment, more than the capital required, which prevents full employment except through a mistake of foresight (§ VII).
  - A page or two later Hobson puts one half of the matter, Keynes says, with absolute precision. But he still overlooks the possible role of the interest rate and business confidence, which Keynes says he presumably takes as given (§ VII).
  - Hobson had no independent theory of the interest rate. He therefore put too much emphasis on under-consumption leading to over-investment. Keynes says a relatively weak propensity to consume helps to cause unemployment because it requires, and does not receive, a compensating volume of new investment. That investment is in general prevented by prospective profit falling below the standard set by the interest rate (§ VII).
- **Major Douglas.** His advocacy has largely depended on orthodoxy having no valid reply to much of his destructive criticism. His detailed diagnosis, in particular the A + B theorem, includes much mere mystification. Keynes says he would be nearer the truth if his B-items were limited to financial provisions with no matching current expenditure. Even then provisions may be offset by new investment elsewhere and by increased consumption. Douglas is entitled to claim that he has not been wholly oblivious of the outstanding problem. But he has scarcely established an equal claim to rank with Mandeville, Malthus, Gesell and Hobson (§ VII).

*Intuition note:* Keynes honours these writers because they followed intuition and preferred to see the truth obscurely and imperfectly rather than keep a clear but wrong result built on unsuitable hypotheses. In this chapter he singles out two things the classical theory confused or lacked: the separation of the interest rate from the marginal efficiency of capital (§ V–VI), and a theory of the interest rate (§ VII).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates Keynes's stamp-sizing rule (§ VI) with invented numbers.
- Suppose the money-rate of interest (apart from stamps) is $r = 5\%$ per year. Suppose the marginal efficiency of capital at an investment level compatible with full employment is $m = 2\%$.
- Keynes's rule: stamp charge $\approx r - m = 5\% - 2\% = 3\%$ per year.
- Per week: $3\%/52 \approx 0.0577\%$, which is about $0.58$ per mil per week.
- Check against Gesell's figure: $1$ per mil per week $= 0.1\% \times 52 = 5.2\%$ per year, which matches the 5.2% in the source.
- Effect on a holder: cash earns $0$, and the stamp costs $3\%$. Holding cash therefore costs $3$ per $100$ per year.
- A bond or loan paying $2\%$ then beats cash by $2\% - (-3\%) = 5$ points. Without the stamp it beats it by $2\% - 0\% = 2$ points. The stamp lowers the floor that cash sets under interest rates, which is Gesell's aim. Keynes warns that substitutes such as bank-money or jewellery might escape the charge (§ VI).

## Concepts
- [[mercantilism]] (new) — old doctrine that a favourable trade balance and metal inflow benefit a nation; Keynes finds a truth in it (§ I–II)
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — desire to hold money; with the quantity of money, it governs the interest rate in Keynes's account (§ II–III, VI)
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — Keynes's term for the return schedule of new capital; used here but defined in earlier chapters; must be kept distinct from the interest rate (§ III, V, VI)
- [[usury-laws]] (new) — legal and moral curbs on interest, read here as defence against too-high interest (§ V)
- [[stamped-money]] (new) — Gesell's scheme: notes need periodic stamps to keep value, which charges a carrying cost for money (§ VI)
- [[under-consumption]] (new) — view that too little consumption (too much thrift) limits output and employment (§ VII)
- [[propensity to consume]]/[[inducement to invest]] (new) — two constituents of effective demand, each with a historical group of theories (§ VII)
- [[self-adjusting rate of interest]] (new) — classical belief that interest and investment adjust themselves to the optimum; Keynes rejects it (§ II, V)
- [[fear of goods]] (new) — mercantilist worry that imports cause unemployment and money scarcity (§ III, IV)
- [[gold standard as competitive struggle]] (new) — Keynes's view that a fixed metal standard sets each country's advantage against its neighbours' (§ III)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Stamped money ↔ monetary economics: charging for the holding of cash to push interest rates down toward zero — both aim to remove a floor under interest rates.
- [structural-analogy] Mercantilist surplus-seeking under the gold standard ↔ international trade: beggar-thy-neighbour competition — one country's gain is another's loss.
- [metaphor] Mandeville's "private vices" and "public benefit" ↔ the paradox of thrift: individual saving versus aggregate demand.

## Questions this raises
- Keynes says a favourable balance helps only under stated conditions (stable wage-unit, metal-linked money). How far do these conditions hold under other monetary systems?
- If money differs from other assets only in degree of liquidity-premium, can any charge on cash ever lower the whole structure of interest rates?
- Keynes calls Hobson's "excess capital" a secondary evil caused by mistakes of foresight. What would show that the primary evil is the saving propensity?
- Which of Keynes's historical readings of mercantilist writers come from Heckscher's selection, and how far do they depend on it?
