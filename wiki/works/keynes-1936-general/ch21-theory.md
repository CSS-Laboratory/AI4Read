# The Theory of Prices (Keynes, General Theory, Ch. 21)

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that there is no separate "theory of money": the price level depends on the cost-unit (a weighted average of the rewards of the factors in marginal cost) and on output, so a rise in the money supply spreads over output, wages and prices in proportions that depend on several variable factors (§§ I–VI).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks how changes in the quantity of money affect prices. Keynes states the quantity theory by adding extreme assumptions: constant returns and rigid wages while unemployment lasts, then full employment. In reality an increase in effective demand (total spending that firms expect) generally splits between output and prices. Five complications explain why. Its primary effect on demand runs through the interest rate, liquidity-preference, the marginal efficiency of capital and the multiplier. Keynes writes the price response as a product of elasticities, but doubts the value of such formulas. In the long run, price stability depends on how fast the wage-unit (the money wage rate) rises relative to productive efficiency; the long-run money–income relation depends on liquidity-preference (§ VII).

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand; elasticity as a ratio of proportional changes; the earlier chapters' ideas of effective demand, multiplier, liquidity-preference and wage-unit.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Keynes suggests that the proper division in economics is not value versus money. It is the individual firm or industry with given total resources versus output and employment as a whole (§ I). This matters because it brings the theory of prices back into close contact with value theory.
- Money matters because it links present and future. Any durable asset can carry monetary attributes (§ I).
- The general price level depends partly on the wage-unit (or cost-unit) and partly on the volume of employment (§§ II–III).
- Keynes enunciates the simple quantity theory by adding simplifying assumptions. If supply is perfectly elastic until full employment and perfectly inelastic after, and effective demand changes in proportion to money, then employment moves with money first and prices move with money after (§ III).
- Five complications make prices rise gradually as employment rises (§ III). These are demand not proportional to money, diminishing returns, bottle-necks (goods whose supply stops being elastic while other resources are still unemployed), a wage-unit that rises early, and unequal changes in factor rewards (§ IV).
- Keynes argues for an asymmetry around the point of "true inflation" (all extra demand goes into the cost-unit, none into output): because factors resist cuts in money rewards but not rises, contraction lowers effective demand measured in cost-units, while expansion beyond that point does not, in general, raise it (§ V).
- Keynes writes the price response as $e = e_d(1 - e_e e_o + e_e e_o e_w)$. He attaches little value to it, though perhaps its best use is to show the complexity, and he warns against treating factors as independent (§§ III, VI).
- Long-run price stability depends on wage-unit growth versus efficiency growth. A nineteenth-century balance may not repeat, because acceptable interest rates may be too high for adequate employment (§ VII).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Value and money: where to draw the line
- Economists teach that prices follow supply and demand, with marginal cost and short-period supply elasticity. In the money chapters they switch to the quantity of money, velocity, hoarding and forced saving, with little or no attempt to link these to elasticities (§ I).
- Keynes's attempt to rationalise this: in the simpler discussions it seems that supply elasticity must have become zero and demand proportional to money. The sophisticated versions are "a haze" (§ I).
- **Keynes's view (hedged with "I think", "I suggest"):** the split between value theory and money theory is false. The right split, he suggests, is the theory of the firm or industry (given total resources) versus the theory of output and employment as a whole (§ I).
- Another way to say it: stationary equilibrium versus shifting equilibrium. In shifting equilibrium, changing views about the future influence the present (§ I).
- Money is "a subtle device" linking present and future. Abolishing gold and silver would not remove the problem, since any durable asset can have monetary attributes (§ I).
- The theory of shifting equilibrium needs a monetary economy, but it is still value theory, not a separate "theory of money" (§ I).

*Intuition note:* Money matters once people's plans depend on guesses about the future. Keynes considers a world where views about the future are fixed and reliable as a simplified starting point, and says money's peculiar properties must enter once expectations can be disappointed.

### II. Industry and the whole economy
- For one industry, price depends on factor rewards in marginal cost and on the scale of output. The same holds for industry as a whole: the price level depends on those rewards and on the volume of employment (equipment and technique given) (§ II).
- What is new at the aggregate level is on the demand side. Changes in demand affect both costs and volume (§ II).

### III. The quantity theory as a special case
- Simplification: assume the rewards of the factors entering marginal cost all change in the same proportion as the wage-unit. Then the price level (equipment and technique given) depends partly on the wage-unit and partly on employment. The effect of money on prices is compounded of its effect on the wage-unit and on employment (§ III).
- Add two simplifications: (1) unemployed resources are homogeneous and interchangeable; (2) the factors are content with the same money-wage while any are unemployed. Then returns are constant and the wage-unit rigid while there is unemployment (§ III).
- Result: more money has no effect on prices while unemployment lasts. Employment rises in exact proportion to effective demand. At full employment, the wage-unit and prices rise in exact proportion to effective demand (§ III).
- If effective demand changes in proportion to money, the quantity theory reads: with unemployment, employment changes in proportion to money; at full employment, prices do (§ III).
- Five complications (§ III):
  1. Effective demand does not change in exact proportion to money.
  2. Resources are not homogeneous, so returns diminish.
  3. Resources are not interchangeable, so some supplies become inelastic before others.
  4. The wage-unit tends to rise before full employment.
  5. Factor rewards do not all change in the same proportion.
- An increase in effective demand therefore, generally speaking, splits between employment and prices, and prices rise gradually as employment rises (§ III).
- The five factors are not strictly independent. The output/price split can affect how money relates to effective demand, and so can differences in factor rewards (§ III).
- **Keynes on method:** analysis is "an organised and orderly method of thinking," not a machine for infallible answers. One isolates factors, then goes back and allows for interactions (§ III).
- He criticises "pseudo-mathematical" formalisation, such as section VI, because it assumes strict independence and loses its authority if that is denied. In ordinary discourse one can keep reservations "at the back of our heads". He adds that too much recent "mathematical" economics is "merely concoctions", as imprecise as its initial assumptions (§ III).

*Intuition note:* The textbook quantity theory is a limiting case with two tidy regimes. Real economies sit between them, so one extra unit of spending buys some extra output and some higher prices.

### IV. The five complications in turn
**(1) Money and effective demand.**
- The primary effect runs through the interest rate. If this were the only reaction, the size could be derived from three elements: (a) the liquidity-preference schedule (how far the rate must fall for new money to be held); (b) the marginal-efficiency schedule (how much investment a fall in the rate brings); (c) the multiplier (how much effective demand a rise in investment brings) (§ IV(1)).
- But (a)–(c) depend partly on complications 2–5. Examples: how much new money enters the income and industrial circulations; expectations about future monetary prospects; and how new income is spread across consumer classes (§ IV(1)).
- Even so, with all facts known there are enough simultaneous equations for a determinate result. Only in highly exceptional circumstances does more money go with less effective demand (§ IV(1)).
- The ratio of effective demand to money resembles "income-velocity," except that effective demand is expected, not realised, income, and gross, not net. **Keynes's view:** income-velocity is "merely a name which explains nothing," need not be constant, and has caused confusion (§ IV(1)).

**(2) Diminishing returns.**
- If workers are paid strictly by efficiency, labour costs per unit stay constant. If the wage is uniform within a grade, labour costs rise. Non-homogeneous equipment with higher prime cost per unit raises marginal prime cost further (§ IV(2)).
- So, in general, supply price rises as output rises from given equipment, and prices rise even without any change in the wage-unit (§ IV(2)).

**(3) Bottle-necks.**
- If specialised unemployed resources were perfectly balanced, all would reach full employment together; in general they do not. Some supplies become perfectly inelastic, for the time being, while others still have surplus (§ IV(3)).
- Prices of bottle-necked goods must rise until demand is diverted elsewhere. The general price level probably does not rise much while efficient unemployed resources of every type remain, but a sharp rise is likely in certain commodities once bottle-necks are reached (§ IV(3)).
- Supply elasticity partly depends on time. A moderate rise in demand amid widespread unemployment may raise prices very little and mainly raise employment. A larger, unforeseen rise that hits temporary bottle-necks raises prices more at first than later (§ IV(3)).

**(4) The wage-unit rises early.**
- Each group gains from its own wage rise, other things equal, and employers yield more readily in good business. Part of any rise in effective demand is therefore likely to be absorbed by the wage-unit (§ IV(4)).
- There are "semi-critical points" before full employment where money-wages rise, though not fully in proportion to wage-goods prices. In practice the wage-unit changes discontinuously, at points set by workers' psychology and by employer and union policy (§ IV(4)).
- These points may be seen as "semi-inflation," a very imperfect analogy to absolute inflation, and they are historically important. But they "do not readily lend themselves to theoretical generalisations" (§ IV(4)).

**(5) Unequal factor rewards.**
- Factor rewards differ in rigidity and in supply elasticity. Without this, the price level would be compounded of two factors, the wage-unit and employment (§ IV(5)).
- Perhaps the most important element in marginal cost that moves differently from the wage-unit, and within much wider limits, is marginal user cost. It may rise sharply as employment improves, if (as will probably be the case) rising effective demand rapidly changes expectations about when equipment must be replaced (§ IV(5)).
- Keynes suggests it might be better, perhaps, to take a weighted average of marginal prime-cost rewards, the **cost-unit**; the cost-unit (or, approximately, the wage-unit) can then be regarded as the essential standard of value. Given technique and equipment, the price level depends partly on the cost-unit and partly on output, and rises more than in proportion to the cost-unit as output rises (§ IV(5)).
- Full employment is reached when the marginal return from a representative factor unit has fallen to the minimum at which enough of the factors to produce this output are available (§ IV(5)).

### V. True inflation and the asymmetry
- When more effective demand yields no more output and goes entirely into a proportionate rise in the cost-unit, there is **true inflation**. Before that point the effect of monetary expansion is a matter of degree, and no earlier point marks the start of inflation (§ V).
- **Asymmetry:** a contraction below the critical level reduces effective demand measured in cost-units. An expansion beyond it does not, in general, increase it (§ V).
- Reason: factors, especially workers, resist cuts in money rewards but have no corresponding motive to resist rises. Keynes calls this assumption "obviously well founded" (§ V).
- If money-wages fell without limit whenever employment was below full, the asymmetry would vanish. But there would then be no resting place below full employment until the interest rate could fall no further or wages were zero. Some money-valued factor must be at least sticky for stability (§ V).
- **Keynes's view:** the claim that any rise in money is inflationary (unless "inflationary" merely means rising prices) rests on the classical assumption that lower real rewards always curtail factor supply (§ V).

*Intuition note:* Because wages resist falling but not rising, the economy responds differently to shrinking and growing demand. Keynes adds that some money-valued factor must be at least sticky if a monetary system is to have any stability of values.

### VI. Symbolic form
Using Chapter 20's notation, with $M$ the quantity of money, $V$ income-velocity and $D$ effective demand:
$$MV = D$$
- If $V$ is constant, prices change in proportion to $M$ provided $e_p = \dfrac{D\,dp}{p\,dD}$ equals 1. By Chapter 20 this holds if $e_o = 0$ or $e_w = 1$, where $e_w = \dfrac{D\,dW}{W\,dD}$ and $e_o = \dfrac{D\,dO}{O\,dD}$ (§ VI). (Reading $W$ as the wage-unit and $O$ as output is inferred from context.) Output is then unaltered (§ VI).
- If $V$ is not constant, introduce
$$e_d = \frac{M\,dD}{D\,dM}$$
so that
$$\frac{M\,dp}{p\,dM} = e_p \times e_d,\qquad e_p = 1 - e_e e_o(1-e_w) \tag{§VI}$$
- Writing $e = \dfrac{M\,dp}{p\,dM}$:
$$e = e_d - (1-e_w)\,e_d\,e_e e_o = e_d\,(1 - e_e e_o + e_e e_o e_w)$$
- Keynes says this expression can be regarded as a generalised statement of the quantity theory. $e_e$ is not defined in this chapter; it comes from the Chapter 20 notation (§ VI).
- **Keynes's caution:** he puts little value on such manipulations. They involve as much tacit assumption about independent variables as ordinary talk does. Perhaps the best purpose served by writing them down is to show the "extreme complexity" of the relation (§ VI).
- Meaning of the terms: $e_d$ = liquidity factors; $e_w$ = labour (prime-cost) factors; $e_e$ and $e_o$ = physical factors fixing the rate of decreasing returns (§ VI).
- Special values: $e_d = 1$ if the public holds a constant fraction of income as money; $e_w = 0$ if money-wages are fixed; $e_e e_o = 1$ with constant returns throughout; $e_e e_o = 0$ with full employment of labour or equipment (§ VI).
- $e = 1$ if $e_d = 1$ and $e_w = 1$; or if $e_d = 1$, $e_w = 0$ and $e_e e_o = 0$; or if $e_d = 1$ and $e_o = 0$; there are other special cases (§ VI).
- In general $e \ne 1$. Keynes thinks it, "perhaps", safe to say that, on plausible assumptions and excluding a "flight from the currency" (where $e_d$ and $e_w$ become large), $e$ is as a rule less than unity (§ VI).

*Intuition note:* $e$ is the proportional rise in prices per proportional rise in money, for small changes. It is $e_d$ (how much demand rises per unit of money) times $e_p$ (how much of that rise goes into prices rather than output). The more output responds (large $e_e e_o$) and the less wages respond (small $e_w$), the less of the extra demand reaches prices.

### VII. The long run
- Keynes says the long-run relation is a question for historical generalisation, not pure theory (§ VII).
- **If** liquidity-preference is fairly uniform in the long run, there may be a fairly stable proportion of national income more than which people will not readily keep as idle balances for long periods (provided the interest rate exceeds a psychological minimum). Money in excess of this proportion tends, sooner or later, to push the interest rate toward that minimum. Effective demand then rises, reaches semi-critical points, and the wage-unit rises discontinuously. The opposite happens if surplus money is abnormally low (§ VII).
- These tendencies probably work with less friction upward than downward. If money stays deficient for long, the escape is normally a change of monetary standard or system, not a falling wage-unit that would raise the debt burden. Hence very-long-run prices have "almost always" risen (§ VII).
- Nineteenth century: population growth, invention, new lands, confidence and war, with the propensity to consume, seem to have produced a marginal-efficiency schedule that allowed fair employment at an acceptable interest rate. There is evidence that for almost 150 years the typical rate in the leading financial centres was about 5 per cent and the gilt-edged rate 3–3½ per cent (§ VII).
- The wage-unit or monetary system was adjusted so that money in wage-units met liquidity-preference at rates seldom much below these. Sauerbeck's index: highest quinquennial average 1820–1914 only 50 per cent above the lowest. Employment was well below full but not intolerably so. Keynes describes the price stability as no accident but a balance of forces: employers were strong enough to stop the wage-unit rising much faster than the efficiency of production, and monetary systems were fluid yet conservative enough to supply money in wage-units that let the lowest readily acceptable interest rate prevail (§ VII).
- Today, and presumably for the future, the marginal-efficiency schedule is much lower. The contemporary problem may be that the interest rate allowing reasonable employment is unacceptable to wealth-owners and cannot be readily established merely by manipulating the quantity of money (§ VII).
- Keynes calls the minimum acceptable rate the most stable element, which "may prove" so in future. If tolerable employment requires a rate much below nineteenth-century averages, he thinks it "most doubtful" that this can be achieved merely by manipulating the quantity of money. From the borrower's expected gain one must deduct the cost of bringing borrowers and lenders together, taxes, and the lender's risk allowance. If the net yield is infinitesimal at tolerable employment, "time-honoured methods may prove unavailing" (§ VII).
- Conclusion: the long-run money–income relation depends on liquidity-preferences. Long-run price stability depends on the wage-unit (cost-unit) trend relative to the growth of efficiency (§ VII).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take the formula $e = e_d(1 - e_e e_o + e_e e_o e_w)$ with $e_d = 1$ (constant money-to-income proportion).
- Case A: partial wage response and diminishing returns. Let $e_e e_o = 0.6$, $e_w = 0.5$. Then $e = 1\times(1 - 0.6 + 0.6\times0.5) = 1 - 0.6 + 0.3 = 0.7$. A small 10% rise in money raises prices by about $0.7\times10\% = 7\%$.
- Case B: rigid wages and constant returns. $e_e e_o = 1$, $e_w = 0$. Then $e = 1 - 1 + 0 = 0$. Prices do not move and all the extra demand goes into output, as in section III.
- Case C: full employment. $e_e e_o = 0$. Then $e = 1\times(1 - 0 + 0) = 1$. Prices move in proportion to money.
The three cases show the quantity theory's two regimes as ends of a scale.

## Concepts
- [[quantity-theory-of-money]] (new) — in Keynes's simple statement, employment changes in proportion to money under unemployment and prices at full employment; he reaches it by adding simplifying assumptions (§ III)
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — expected, gross income that sets production moving (§ IV(1))
- [[wage-unit]] (new) — money wage rate used as a standard; the cost-unit generalises it (§§ III, IV(5))
- [[cost-unit]] (new) — weighted average of marginal prime-cost rewards (§ IV(5))
- [[income-velocity]] (new) — ratio of effective demand to money; "merely a name" (§ IV(1))
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — schedule showing by how much the interest rate must fall for new money to be absorbed by willing holders (§ IV(1))
- [[bottle-necks]] (new) — commodities whose supply ceases to be elastic while resources for other goods are still unemployed (§ IV(3))
- [[true-inflation]] (new) — extra demand goes wholly into the cost-unit, none into output (§ V)
- [[sticky-wages-asymmetry]] (new) — resistance to cuts but not rises in money rewards (§ V)
- [[marginal-user-cost]] (new) — element of marginal cost that may change differently from the wage-unit and within wider limits; may rise sharply as employment improves (§ IV(5))
- [[equilibrium]] — Keynes contrasts stationary with shifting equilibrium (§ I)

## Questions this raises
- If the five factors interact, can the single elasticity $e$ be treated as stable, or does it shift with each episode?
- How would Keynes's claim that $e$ is "as a rule less than unity" be tested empirically?
- What policies could lower the net yield wealth-owners demand, if manipulating the quantity of money is not enough (§ VII)?
- Why does Keynes write the formula down while saying he places little value on it?
