# The Psychological and Business Incentives to Liquidity

## L0 — One line
Money demand splits into an income-driven part, $M_1 = L_1(Y)$, and a speculative part, $M_2 = L_2(r)$, that depends on the interest rate and on expectations about it; because the long-term rate rests heavily on conventional expectations, a policy that public opinion distrusts may fail to push it down.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks why people hold cash and how the quantity of money affects the rate of interest. He lists four motives (income, business, precautionary, speculative). He groups them into $M_1$ (mainly income-driven) and $M_2$ (mainly driven by the interest rate and expectations). Uncertainty about the future rate explains $M_2$. A low rate leaves little yield to cover possible capital losses, so $M_2$ may tend to grow almost without limit below some rate. Keynes suggests the long rate is highly conventional and may stay too high for full employment. Authorities may also lose control over it. With $M_2 = 0$ the framework gives something much like the quantity theory, $MV = OP$ (money × velocity = output × price).

**Prerequisites:** bond prices fall when interest rates rise; the idea of a demand function; the national-income identity; simple percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Cash is held for four motives: income, business, precautionary and speculative. The holder need not keep them in separate compartments (§ I).
- The speculative motive (cash held because of uncertainty about the future interest rate) is particularly important in transmitting the effects of a change in the quantity of money. Experience indicates that its demand usually responds continuously to gradual changes in the interest rate; without that, open-market operations (the banking system buying or selling bonds for cash) would be impracticable (§ I).
- News that everyone reads alike changes the interest rate with no trading. Increased dealing has room only where people interpret the news differently or are affected differently (§ I).
- Money demand is written $M = L_1(Y) + L_2(r)$, with $L_1(Y) = Y/V$. $V$ is not constant in general, but can be treated as nearly constant over a short period when its determinants do not change materially (§ II).
- $M_2$ depends on the gap between $r$ and a "safe" rate. The yield available to offset capital loss shrinks sharply as $r$ falls (4% vs 2% example) (§ II.iii).
- Keynes suggests the rate might be better called "highly conventional" than "highly psychological". A level accepted with sufficient conviction as durable will be durable, and such a level may stay too high for full employment (§ II.iii).
- Four limits on the monetary authority's control of the rate structure: its own practice, possible absolute liquidity-preference (almost everyone preferring cash to debt), crises, and lending costs and risk (§ III).
- With $M_2 = 0$ (a static society, or none feeling uncertainty about future rates) Keynes obtains $MV = OP$ (money × velocity = output × price), "much the same" as the traditional quantity theory. He criticises that theory for not separating price changes due to output from those due to the wage-unit (a term this chapter does not define) (§ IV).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. The motives for holding cash
- Liquidity-preference is much the same subject as the "demand for money". It is closely connected with the income-velocity of money (a rise in which may be a symptom of decreased liquidity-preference), but is not the same thing. The individual chooses between liquidity and illiquidity with respect to accumulated savings, not income (§ I).
- Keynes thinks "income-velocity" wrongly suggests that total money demand is proportional to income. He says this holds only for part of cash holdings, and that the term overlooks the rate of interest (§ I).
- The three deposit types of his *Treatise on Money* (income, business, savings) form one pool. They need not be segregated, even in the holder's mind. So aggregate demand can be treated as one decision with several motives (§ I).
- **(i) Income-motive:** cash bridges the gap between receiving income and spending it. Its strength depends chiefly on the amount of income and the usual length of the gap (§ I).
- **(ii) Business-motive:** cash bridges the gap between incurring costs and receiving sale proceeds, including dealers' stocks. It depends chiefly on the value of current output (and so current income) and on the number of hands output passes through (§ I).
- **(iii) Precautionary-motive:** cash for sudden expenditures, for unforeseen bargains, and as an asset fixed in money terms against a money liability (§ I).
- All three weaken if temporary borrowing (overdraft) is cheap and reliable. They also depend on the cost of holding cash (profitable assets given up, interest earned, bank charges avoided). Keynes suggests this may well be a minor factor except where large changes in the cost are in question (§ I).
- **(iv) Speculative-motive:** this needs closer study because it is less understood and because it transmits changes in the quantity of money (§ I).
  - Money for the transactions and precautionary motives mainly follows economic activity and money income. Demand for the former is generally irresponsive to anything but changes in activity and income (§ I).
  - Experience indicates that speculative demand usually responds continuously to gradual changes in the interest rate, as given by bond and debt prices. If not, open-market operations would be impracticable (§ I).
  - In normal circumstances the banking system can buy or sell bonds by moving prices a modest amount. The larger the cash it creates or cancels, the larger the change in the rate (§ I).
  - In the US in 1933–34, purchases were limited to very short-dated securities. The effect may then stay mostly in the short rate, with little reaction in long rates (§ I).
- Two sources of change in $r$ must be separated: a change in the money supply with the liquidity function unchanged, and a change in expectations that shifts the liquidity function itself. Open-market operations can work through both, since they may alter expectations of future policy (§ I).
- Shifts in the liquidity function from news will often be discontinuous, giving discontinuous changes in $r$ (§ I).
- Dealing in bonds increases only insofar as individuals interpret the news differently or are affected differently. If everyone is affected identically, the rate adjusts at once with no transactions (§ I).
- In the simplest case, with everyone similar and similarly placed, no money moves. The rate shifts just enough to offset each person's wish to change his cash (§ I).
- In general, holdings are somewhat redistributed. Even then, Keynes says, the rate shift deserves our main attention and is usually the most prominent part of the reaction. Newspapers say bond-price movement is "out of all proportion to the activity of dealing". Keynes says this is as it should be, because people are more alike than different in reacting to news (§ I).

*Intuition note:* When everyone reads news the same way, price does all the adjusting and nobody needs to trade. Extra trading needs disagreement or differing circumstances; the price move reflects the common reaction.

### II. The liquidity functions
- Cash for transactions and precautionary motives ($M_1$) and for speculation ($M_2$) are not wholly independent. Treating them as largely independent is "a safe first approximation" (§ II).
- $L_1$ mainly depends on income; $L_2$ mainly on the relation between the current rate and the state of expectation (§ II):
$$M = M_1 + M_2 = L_1(Y) + L_2(r).$$
- Three things to investigate: (i) how changes in $M$ relate to $Y$ and $r$; (ii) the shape of $L_1$; (iii) the shape of $L_2$ (§ II).

**(i) How changes in $M$ arise (§ II.i).**
- Suppose $M$ is gold coin and changes come from gold-miners' returns. Then new money first accrues as income. The same holds if the government prints money to meet current spending (§ II.i).
- The higher income will not stay high enough for $M_1$ to absorb all the increase. Some money seeks securities or assets until $r$ falls. The fall raises $M_2$ and also stimulates $Y$, so the new money is absorbed in $M_2$ or in the $M_1$ that matches the higher $Y$ (§ II.i).
- At one remove this equals the case where banks relax credit by buying a bond or debt for new cash. Keynes takes that case as typical (§ II.i).
- A change in $M$ operates by changing $r$. The change in $r$ then alters $M_2$ directly and $M_1$ through $Y$. The split depends on how investment responds to a lower rate and how income responds to investment (§ II.i).
- Since $Y$ partly depends on $r$, $r$ must change enough for the changes in $M_1$ and $M_2$ to add up to the change in $M$ (§ II.i).

**(ii) The shape of $L_1$ (§ II.ii).**
- Keynes defines income-velocity $V$ as the ratio of $Y$ to $M_1$, not to $M$ (§ II.ii):
$$L_1(Y) = \frac{Y}{V} = M_1.$$
- $V$ is not constant in general. It depends on banking and industrial organisation, social habits, the income distribution between classes, and the effective cost of holding idle cash. Over a short period without material change in these, $V$ can be treated as nearly constant (§ II.ii).

**(iii) The shape of $L_2$ (§ II.iii).**
- Chapter 13 (not in this unit) is cited for the claim that uncertainty about the future rate is the "sole intelligible explanation" of the $L_2$ type of liquidity-preference (§ II.iii).
- So a given $M_2$ has no fixed link to the absolute level of $r$. What matters is how far $r$ diverges from a level thought fairly safe, given the probabilities relied on (§ II.iii).
- Even so, Keynes gives two reasons for expecting that, in any given state of expectation, a fall in $r$ will be associated with a rise in $M_2$ (§ II.iii):
  1. If the view of the safe rate is unchanged, a fall in $r$ widens the gap to the safe rate and raises the risk of illiquidity.
  2. A fall in $r$ cuts the current earnings from illiquidity, which act as an insurance premium against capital loss. The cut equals the difference between the squares of the old and new rates.
- **Keynes's example:** at a long-term rate of 4%, it is preferable to give up liquidity unless one fears the rate may rise faster than 4% of itself per year, i.e. by more than 0.16% per year. At 2%, the running yield offsets a rise of only 0.04% per year (§ II.iii).
- This is "perhaps the chief obstacle" to a fall to a very low rate. Unless reasons are believed to exist why future experience will be very different from past experience, a long rate of (say) 2% leaves more to fear than to hope (§ II.iii).

*Intuition note:* A bond's yield is a cushion against price falls. The lower the yield, the thinner the cushion, so more holders may prefer cash (Keynes gives a second reason too: the market rate falls further below the "safe" rate).

- The rate is "a highly psychological phenomenon" (§ II.iii).
  - Keynes says Book V will show it cannot be in equilibrium below the full-employment rate, because true inflation would result and $M_1$ would absorb ever more cash (§ II.iii).
  - Above that level, the long rate depends on current policy and on market expectations of future policy. The short rate is easily controlled, because people believe policy will not change much soon and the possible loss is small compared with the running yield (§ II.iii).
  - The long rate may be "recalcitrant" once it falls to a level that representative opinion considers "unsafe". Under an international gold standard, a rate below those abroad inspires distrust. Pulling the domestic rate up to the highest foreign rate may be much higher than domestic full employment allows (§ II.iii).
- A policy seen as experimental or easily changed may fail to reduce the long rate, because $M_2$ may rise almost without limit when $r$ falls below some figure. The same policy may succeed if it seems reasonable, in the public interest, rooted in conviction and run by an authority unlikely to be replaced (§ II.iii).
- Keynes adds that it might perhaps be more accurate to say "highly conventional" rather than "highly psychological". Any level accepted with enough conviction as likely to be durable will be durable, with fluctuations around the expected norm (§ II.iii).
  - It may fluctuate for decades around a level too high for full employment. This is especially so if opinion holds that the rate is self-adjusting and unrelated to unemployment (§ II.iii).
  - A conventional, stable long rate combined with a fickle marginal efficiency of capital makes it hard to keep effective demand high enough for full employment (§ II.iii).
- Keynes's hope: the convention is not rooted in secure knowledge. So it may yield to a modest, persistent policy, "up to a point". Britain's long rate fell after leaving the gold standard in a series of discontinuous jumps (§ II.iii).

### III. Summary and limits of monetary control
- In any given state of expectation, the public has a potential for holding cash beyond transactions and precautionary needs. It realises itself according to the terms on which the monetary authority will create cash. This is $L_2$. With other things equal, a given quantity of money matches a determinate set of rates for debts of different maturities (§ III).
- The same could be said of any single factor. The analysis is useful and significant only in so far as there is a specially direct or purposive connection between the quantity of money and the rate. Keynes's reason for supposing there is one is that, broadly speaking, the banking system and monetary authority deal in money and debts, not in assets or consumables (§ III).
- If the authority bought and sold debts of all maturities on stated terms (and especially debts of varying risk), the link would be direct. Keynes suggests that a standing offer to buy and sell gilt-edged bonds of all maturities, instead of a single bank rate for short bills, may be the most important practical improvement in monetary technique (§ III).
- In practice the authority's price is sometimes more effective in one direction than the other, and the authority often tends to concentrate on short-term debts. Long-term prices then follow with "belated and imperfect reactions". Keynes sees no reason it must do so (§ III).
- **Limits on control of the rate structure (§ III):**
  1. The authority's own practice of dealing only in certain debts.
  2. Liquidity-preference may become virtually absolute at a low enough rate. Then control is lost. Keynes knows of no example so far, and says there has been little test because authorities seldom deal boldly in long debts. In that event the state could borrow without limit at a nominal rate.
  3. Complete breakdowns of stability have occurred in very abnormal circumstances. Examples are the flight from the currency in Russia and Central Europe after the war, and the liquidation crisis in the US in 1932.
  4. The cost of linking borrower and ultimate lender, and the allowance for risk (especially moral risk), do not necessarily fall in step with the pure rate. The borrower's rate may bottom out. A bank may charge 1½ to 2% even if the pure rate to the lender is nil.

### IV. Relation to the quantity theory
- In a static society, or one with no uncertainty about future rates, $L_2$ (the "propensity to hoard") is always zero in equilibrium. So $M_2 = 0$ and $M = M_1$ (§ IV).
- Then a change in $M$ moves $r$ until income reaches a level where the change in $M_1$ equals the change in $M$. With $M_1 V = Y$ and $Y = OP$ ($O$ = quantity of output, $P$ = price), we get $MV = OP$. This is "much the same" as the traditional quantity theory (§ IV).
- Keynes's criticism: the quantity theory does not separate price changes due to output from those due to the wage-unit. Perhaps this is because it assumes no hoarding and always full employment. Then $O$ is constant, $M_2 = 0$, and with $V$ constant, the wage-unit and price level are directly proportional to $M$ (§ IV).

*Intuition note:* $MV = OP$ follows when no one holds money for speculation ($M_2 = 0$), and Keynes calls the result "much the same" as the traditional quantity theory. He suggests that its neglect of the wage-unit may come from also assuming no hoarding and always full employment.

## Toy example (ours; not from the text)
We check Keynes's rule "a rise of about $r^2$ per year wipes out the running yield" with a perpetual bond (never repaid, pays a fixed coupon forever; price = coupon / $r$). This is our own device.
- $r = 4\%$: coupon 4, price $4/0.04 = 100$. If $r$ rises by 0.16 points to 4.16%, price $= 4/0.0416 \approx 96.15$. Capital loss $\approx 3.85$, close to the coupon 4.
- $r = 2\%$: coupon 2, price 100. If $r$ rises by 0.04 points to 2.04%, price $= 2/0.0204 \approx 98.04$. Loss $\approx 1.96$, close to the coupon 2.
- Why $r^2$: for a perpetual, the proportional loss is about $\Delta r / r$. Setting it equal to the yield $r$ gives $\Delta r = r^2$. Here $0.04^2 = 0.0016 = 0.16\%$ and $0.02^2 = 0.0004 = 0.04\%$. The match is approximate, not exact.
- Lesson: at 2%, a rise of just 0.04 points cancels a year's income. That helps show why, at very low rates, holders may prefer cash and $M_2$ may tend to grow very large.

## Concepts
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — the public's choice between holding cash and illiquid assets, out of accumulated savings (§ I)
- [[transactions-precautionary-motive]] (new) — cash held to bridge income/cost-to-receipt gaps and to meet contingencies; $M_1 = L_1(Y)$ (§ I–II)
- [[speculative-motive]] (new) — cash held because of uncertainty about the future rate of interest; $M_2 = L_2(r)$ (§ I, II.iii)
- [[income-velocity-of-money]] (new) — here $V = Y/M_1$, not $Y/M$ (§ II.ii)
- [[open-market-operations]] (new) — the banking system buying or selling bonds for cash, moving the rate (§ I)
- [[conventional-expectations]] — Keynes suggests the rate may be better called "highly conventional": a level accepted with sufficient conviction as durable will be durable (§ II.iii)
- [[absolute-liquidity-preference]] (new) — the possibility that, once the rate has fallen to a certain level, almost everyone prefers cash to a debt yielding so low a rate; the authority would then have lost effective control (Keynes knows of no example so far) (§ III)
- [[quantity-theory-of-money]] (new) — $MV = OP$, obtained when $M_2 = 0$ (static society or no uncertainty about future rates); Keynes calls it "much the same" as the traditional quantity theory (§ IV)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Conventional long rate that is durable if believed durable ↔ coordination game / multiple equilibria: self-fulfilling expectations select the outcome.
- [structural-analogy] No trades when everyone reads news identically, only a price jump ↔ efficient markets and common information: price absorbs news, volume reflects disagreement.
- [metaphor] "Safe" rate and running yield as insurance premium ↔ risk and insurance: a premium that must cover expected loss.

## Questions this raises
- If the long rate is a convention, what could move it other than persistent policy, and how far can policy go?
- Is $M_1$ really independent of $r$, given Keynes says $Y$ depends partly on $r$?
- Could the speculative demand curve flatten at a positive but low rate, and how would one detect it?
- Does the quantity-theory special case ($M_2 = 0$) hold approximately in any real period?
