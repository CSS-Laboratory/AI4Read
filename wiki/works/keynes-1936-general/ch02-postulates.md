# The Postulates of the Classical Economics

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that classical employment theory rests on one assumption in three forms (real wage = marginal disutility of work, no involuntary unemployment, supply creates its own demand), and that it fits only the special case of full employment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks whether classical economics can explain unemployment in a depression. He sets out two classical postulates about wages and employment. He keeps the first (wage = marginal product of labour). He rejects the second (the real wage equals the marginal disutility of employment). Its hidden premise is that workers can set the real wage by bargaining over the money wage. He defines "involuntary unemployment," which the classical postulates exclude. He then links the second postulate to Say's doctrine that supply creates its own demand. The three assumptions stand or fall together. The classical theory is therefore a theory of full employment only, and applying it to a depression is misleading.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand schedules; marginal product and diminishing returns; nominal (money) vs real wage.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The classical theory is a special case, a "limiting point" of possible equilibria, and its teaching misleads when applied to actual economies (ch. 1).
- Two postulates fix employment: wage = marginal product (labour demand); utility of the wage = marginal disutility of employment (labour supply). Only frictional and voluntary unemployment are allowed (ch. 2, § I).
- Objection 1: workers resist money-wage cuts but do not quit when prices rise. Labour supply is therefore not a function of the real wage alone, and employment becomes indeterminate on the classical method (§ II).
- Objection 2, the fundamental one: it is not obviously true that labour as a whole can set the real wage by money bargains, and there may be no way for it to do so. Keynes says he will try to show that other forces primarily determine it (§ II).
- Money-wage struggles mainly shift the distribution of real wages between groups, not the average level (§ III).
- Involuntary unemployment is defined by a thought experiment: a small price rise would leave both supply and demand for labour above current employment (§ IV).
- Keynes keeps postulate I. With technique and equipment given, more employment in general means a lower real wage (§ V).
- Say's doctrine seems plausible partly because it is hard to tell from true identities about aggregate income; Keynes calls it an "optical illusion" about saving and investment decisions. The equality of demand price and supply price of output behind it is the classical "axiom of parallels" (§ VI).
- The three classical assumptions all stand and fall together (§ VII).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter 1 — The General Theory
- The title stresses "general" to contrast Keynes's arguments with the classical theory he was brought up on. That theory dominates governing and academic thought and has done so for a hundred years (ch. 1).
- Keynes's claim: the classical postulates apply to a special case only. The situation they assume is a limiting point of possible equilibrium positions (ch. 1).
- The special case does not describe the society we live in, so applying the theory to experience is "misleading and disastrous" (ch. 1).

### Chapter 2, § I — The two postulates
- Most value theory takes the volume of employed resources as given. The pure theory of what determines actual employment has had only a bare mention, being thought "simple and obvious" (ch. 2, § I).
- Keynes thinks classical employment theory rests, "practically without discussion," on two postulates (§ I):
  - **I. Wage = marginal product of labour.** The wage equals the value lost if employment fell by one unit (net of other costs avoided). The equality may be disturbed if competition and markets are imperfect.
  - **II. The utility of the wage at a given volume of employment = the marginal disutility of that employment.** The real wage is just enough, in the workers' own estimation, to bring forth the labour actually employed. Combination among workers may disturb the equality. "Disutility" covers every reason to withhold labour at a wage below some minimum.
- Postulate II allows two kinds of unemployment (§ I):
  - *Frictional*: time-lags, miscalculation, or being "between jobs."
  - *Voluntary*: refusal or inability to accept a wage matching marginal productivity, because of law, social practice, collective bargaining, slow response, or obstinacy.
  - Keynes says these two categories are comprehensive. The postulates do not admit a third category, "involuntary" unemployment.
- Postulate I gives the demand schedule for employment and II the supply schedule. Employment is fixed where the utility of the marginal product balances the disutility of marginal employment (§ I).
- Keynes says it follows that there are only four ways to raise employment (§ I):
  - (a) better organisation or foresight, reducing frictional unemployment;
  - (b) lower marginal disutility of labour, reducing voluntary unemployment;
  - (c) higher marginal physical productivity of labour in the wage-goods industries (goods whose price governs the utility of the money wage, Pigou's term);
  - (d) a higher price of non-wage-goods relative to wage-goods, with non-wage-earners shifting spending toward non-wage-goods.
- To the best of Keynes's understanding this is the substance of Pigou's *Theory of Unemployment*, the only detailed classical account (§ I).

*Intuition note:* The classical model is a labour market with a demand curve and a supply curve. Any worker left jobless is there by choice (a wage demand too high) or by delay. Nobody is stuck in a state where they want work at the going wage and cannot get it.

### § II — Why postulate II fails
- **Apparent counterexample:** people usually would work more at the current money wage. **The classical reply:** workers have an open or tacit agreement not to work for less. Such unemployment is then only "apparently involuntary" and counts as voluntary (§ II).
- **First objection** (about worker behaviour; Keynes says it is "not theoretically fundamental") (§ II):
  - Suppose labour would withdraw if money wages fell. It does not follow that the real wage measures marginal disutility. Labour may demand a minimum *money* wage within some range. A price rise that cuts the real wage need not make it withdraw.
  - If labour supply is not a function of the real wage alone, the classical argument "breaks down entirely." Employment becomes "quite indeterminate," because the supply curve shifts bodily with every price movement.
  - Keynes says experience shows beyond doubt that labour stipulating (within limits) for a money wage rather than a real wage is the normal case. Workers usually resist money-wage cuts but do not withdraw labour whenever wage-goods prices rise. He grants it may look illogical but says it behaves this way. He adds that it may be less illogical than it seems (reasons given later in the book, "p. 14").
- **Facts of the depression:** it is not plausible that US unemployment in 1932 came from labour obstinately refusing money-wage cuts or demanding too high a real wage. Labour is not more truculent in a depression, nor less productive. Keynes calls this a prima facie ground for doubting the classical analysis (§ II).
- **Money and real wages:** Keynes expects, and says a statistical enquiry would probably show, that general money wages and real wages almost always move in *opposite* directions. In the short period, falling money wages and rising real wages are each likely to accompany falling employment, for independent reasons. Workers accept cuts when employment falls. Real wages rise as output falls because of the "increasing marginal return" to fixed equipment (§ II).
- If the real wage were an absolute minimum below which no more labour appeared, involuntary unemployment would not exist. But more labour is usually available at the money wage even when prices rise. So the wage-goods equivalent of the money wage does not measure marginal disutility, and postulate II fails (§ II).
- **Second, fundamental objection** (§ II):
  - Postulate II presumes labour can decide its real wage (not the quantity of employment) by bargaining over the money wage. The classical conclusions concern all labour in a closed system. They do not rest on foreign-trade effects or on banking and credit effects (promised for chapter 19).
  - They rest on a belief that, in a closed system, at any rate in the short period and subject only to minor qualifications, a cut in the general money wage brings some reduction in the real wage, not always a proportionate one.
  - Keynes says this is "not obviously true" and clashes with classical price theory, in which prices follow marginal prime cost and money wages largely govern that cost. One would then expect prices to fall in nearly the same proportion as money wages, leaving the real wage nearly unchanged.
  - He suggests the classical school was diverted by the conviction that labour sets its own real wage and, "perhaps," by the quantity theory of money. He also says the belief has been maintained by confusion with a different claim: that labour can decide which real wage corresponds to full employment.
- **Summary** (§ II): objection 1 is that a price rise with unchanged money wages does not, as a rule, cut labour supply below current employment. Keynes says the "strange supposition" that all unemployed workers would withdraw at even a small rise in the cost of living apparently underlies Pigou's theory and is tacitly assumed by the orthodox school. Objection 2 is that there may be no way for labour as a whole to set the real wage by money bargains. Keynes says he will try to show in later chapters that other forces primarily determine it.

*Intuition note:* One worker can take a pay cut and gain a job. That does not show all workers can cut the real wage. If prices follow wages down, the real wage may hardly move.

### § III — What the money-wage struggle is about
- Labour is imperfectly mobile. A group that accepts a money-wage cut relative to others suffers a relative real cut, which justifies resisting it (§ III).
- Resisting every real-wage cut caused by a change in money's purchasing power, which hits all workers alike, would be impracticable. Such cuts are not usually resisted unless extreme (§ III).
- So the money-wage struggle mainly shapes the *distribution* of the aggregate real wage among labour groups, not its average, which depends on other forces (§ III).
- Keynes says workers are "instinctively" more reasonable economists than the classical school. They resist money-wage cuts, which are seldom or never all-round, even when the real equivalent exceeds marginal disutility. They do not resist real-wage cuts that come with more employment, unless the real wage would fall below marginal disutility. Hence they are not the obstacle the classical school claims (§ III).

### § IV — Involuntary unemployment defined
- It is not mere unused capacity to work (an eight-hour day is not unemployment). It is not workers withholding labour for a minimum real reward. Frictional unemployment is excluded (§ IV).
- **Definition:** men are involuntarily unemployed if, after a small rise in the price of wage-goods relative to the money wage, both the aggregate supply of labour willing to work at the current money wage and the aggregate demand for it at that wage would exceed existing employment. An equivalent definition is promised in chapter 3 (§ IV).
- Postulate II, realistically read, corresponds to the absence of involuntary unemployment. Keynes calls this "full" employment, and frictional and voluntary unemployment are consistent with it. The classical theory is best seen as a theory of distribution at full employment (§ IV).
- Classical writers are therefore driven, logically on their assumption, to say apparent unemployment (apart from the admitted exceptions) is at bottom a refusal to accept a wage matching marginal productivity (§ IV).
- Keynes says that if the classical theory applies only to full employment, it is fallacious to apply it to involuntary unemployment, "if there be such a thing (and who will deny it?)" (§ IV).
- Keynes's analogy: classical theorists are Euclidean geometers in a non-Euclidean world. They rebuke "parallel" lines for meeting. The remedy is to drop the axiom of parallels. In economics, drop postulate II and work out a system where involuntary unemployment is possible (§ IV).

### § V — The point of agreement: postulate I
- Keynes keeps postulate I with the same qualifications (§ V).
- With organisation, equipment and technique given, real wages and output (hence employment) are uniquely and inversely correlated. More employment, in general and in the short period, means a lower real wage and higher profits. This is the obverse of short-period decreasing returns (§ V).
- Once postulate II is dropped, falling employment need not come from labour demanding more wage-goods, and accepting lower money wages need not cure unemployment. The full theory waits for chapter 19 and its Appendix (§ V).

### § VI — Supply creates its own demand
- From Say and Ricardo on, the classical economists taught that, in some "significant, but not clearly defined" sense, all production costs must be spent in aggregate on buying the product (§ VI).
- **Mill** (quoted): sellers are inevitably buyers. Doubling productive power would double supply and purchasing power together. **Marshall** (quoted): income saved is spent on labour and goods for future production just as income "spent" is. The corollary is that individual saving leads to investment (§ VI).
- Keynes says the doctrine is no longer stated so crudely but still underlies the classical theory, "which would collapse without it." He gives Pigou's view that money matters only frictionally as an example. He adds that post-war economists seldom hold the view consistently (§ VI).
- Why it seems plausible (§ VI):
  - possibly a false analogy with a Robinson Crusoe economy, where income is the output itself (Keynes says the conclusions "may have been applied" this way at first);
  - confusion with the indubitable proposition that aggregate income equals the value of output;
  - the sum of individuals' net wealth increments equals the community's.
- Keynes calls the result an "optical illusion." Decisions to abstain from consumption are not simply linked to decisions to provide for future consumption (§ VI).
- Equality of the aggregate demand price and supply price of output is the classical "axiom of parallels." Granted it, Keynes says, all follows: the benefits of thrift, the traditional view of interest, the classical theory of unemployment, the quantity theory of money, the unqualified advantages of laissez-faire in foreign trade, and much else he will question (§ VI).

### § VII — Summary
- The three assumptions are: (1) real wage = marginal disutility of existing employment; (2) no involuntary unemployment; (3) aggregate demand price = aggregate supply price at all output levels. They stand and fall together, since any one logically involves the other two (§ VII).

*Intuition note:* If every unit of output creates income that must be spent, demand can never fall short of supply. Then no worker could be stuck without work except by choice. Rejecting one assumption therefore means rejecting the others.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Numbers are invented. **Part A: prices follow wages.** A money wage of 10 and a wage-goods price of 2 give a real wage of $10/2 = 5$. Suppose money wages are the only marginal cost, so prices move in proportion to them. Cut the money wage 20% to 8. The price falls to $2 \times 0.8 = 1.6$. The real wage is $8/1.6 = 5$, unchanged. This is the classical reasoning Keynes says the school left aside (§ II).

**Part B: the definition test.** 100 workers want jobs at money wage 10 and 80 are employed. The price of wage-goods rises 5% to $2.1$, so the real wage becomes $10/2.1 \approx 4.76$. Assume the aggregate labour supply at that money wage stays 100 (workers do not withdraw). Assume firms' demand rises to 85 because the real wage is lower (postulate I). Both 100 and 85 exceed 80, so by Keynes's definition the 20 unemployed are involuntarily unemployed (§ IV).
