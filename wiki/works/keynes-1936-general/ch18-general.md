# The General Theory of Employment Re-stated

## L0 — One line
Keynes summarises his theory as a chain from psychological factors to investment, income and employment, then argues that four plausible behavioural conditions explain why the economy fluctuates without collapsing and sits below full employment, though not by necessity.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks which variables determine national income and employment, and why the real economy is neither explosive nor at full employment. He sorts factors into given factors, independent variables and dependent variables. He then restates the causal chain: investment, then consumption via the multiplier, then employment, with feedback onto liquidity-preference. Finally he lists four conditions of stability, based on experience and plausible human psychology. They explain why fluctuations stay moderate, reverse, and settle around a middling employment level. He insists this outcome is an observed fact about the world, not a law of necessity. The chapter matters because it is his own compact statement of the whole system.

**Prerequisites:** the marginal propensity to consume; the investment multiplier (income increment per investment increment); the marginal efficiency of capital (prospective yield of new capital relative to its supply-price) versus the interest rate; liquidity-preference (demand for money); and the wage-unit (money wage per unit of labour).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Keynes separates given factors (skill, equipment, technique, tastes and others), independent variables (propensity to consume, marginal efficiency of capital, interest rate) and dependent variables (employment, income in wage-units) (§ I).
- The given factors are not assumed constant; their effects are simply set aside here (§ I).
- The division into given and independent is "quite arbitrary" in absolute terms. It is chosen from experience: slow or minor factors go into the given group (§ I).
- Chain of causation: the interest rate and the marginal efficiency of capital fix investment; the multiplier turns investment into income and, as a first approximation, into employment (§ II).
- Employment changes tend to feed back onto liquidity-preference through three channels, so the real course of events is very complex (§ II).
- Stable fluctuation around a subnormal level is an observed fact, not a logical necessity (§ III).
- Four conditions explain it: a multiplier above 1 but not large, investment not hypersensitive, money-wages not hypersensitive to employment, and investment that eventually reverses its own marginal efficiency of capital (§ III).
- The resulting "mean position" is not a law of necessity; it can be changed (§ III, end).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Given factors, independent and dependent variables
- **Given factors** (taken as not changing *in this context*, not assumed constant): available labour (skill, quantity), equipment, technique, degree of competition, consumer tastes and habits, disutility of labour and of supervision, and the social structure that shapes income distribution (§ I, para. 2).
- **Independent variables, first instance:** the propensity to consume, the schedule of the marginal efficiency of capital, and the rate of interest. Each can be analysed further (§ I, para. 3).
- **Dependent variables:** volume of employment and national income, measured in wage-units (§ I, para. 4).
- The given factors influence the independent variables but do not fully determine them. Example: the marginal-efficiency schedule depends partly on existing equipment (given) and partly on long-term expectation, which cannot be inferred from the given factors (§ I, para. 5).
- Some derived items are determined so completely by the given factors that they can be treated as given (§ I, para. 5):
  - There is a unique correlation between employment and national income in wage-units.
  - The shape of the aggregate supply functions is fixed: the employment that goes with each level of effective demand.
  - The supply function of labour (or effort) is also furnished by the given factors, so they tell us, among other things, at what point the employment function for labour as a whole will cease to be elastic.
- The marginal-efficiency schedule depends partly on the given factors and partly on the prospective yield of capital-assets of different kinds. The interest rate depends partly on liquidity-preference and partly on the quantity of money in wage-units (§ I, para. 6).
- So the "ultimate" independent variables can *sometimes* be taken as (§ I, para. 6):
  1. three psychological factors: propensity to consume, attitude to liquidity, expectation of future yield from capital-assets;
  2. the wage-unit, set by bargains between employers and employed;
  3. the quantity of money, set by the central bank.
  
  Keynes adds that these are still not "atomic" elements.
- The given/independent split is arbitrary in principle. It is made from experience. Given factors change slowly or matter little in the short run; independent variables have a dominant influence (§ I, para. 7).
- The aim is to find the factors whose changes *mainly* determine income and employment. The final task might be to pick those that central authority can control (§ I, para. 7).

*Intuition note:* A model must hold some things fixed to say anything. Keynes says the split is made from experience, not as a claim that the fixed things never change. His final task might be to pick the variables that matter most and that central authority can actually control.

### II. Summary of the argument (in reverse order of introduction)
- There will be an inducement to push investment until the supply-price of capital-assets, taken with their prospective yield, makes the marginal efficiency of capital roughly equal to the interest rate. Four things jointly determine the investment rate: supply conditions in the capital-goods industries, confidence about prospective yield, attitude to liquidity, and the quantity of money, preferably in wage-units (§ II, para. 2).
- A rise (fall) in investment will have to carry with it a rise (fall) in consumption, because the public, in general, is only willing to widen (narrow) the gap between income and consumption if income is rising (falling). Consumption therefore, in general, moves in the same direction as income but by less (§ II, para. 3).
- The marginal propensity to consume gives the consumption increment that goes with a saving increment. The investment multiplier is the resulting ratio of income increment to investment increment, in wage-units (§ II, para. 3).
- *As a first approximation*, the employment multiplier equals the investment multiplier. Applying it to the change in investment gives the change in employment (§ II, para. 4).
- Feedback: employment changes tend to shift the liquidity-preference schedule in the same direction, through three channels (§ II, para. 5):
  1. output value rises with employment even at unchanged wage-unit and prices;
  2. the wage-unit tends to rise as employment improves;
  3. prices in wage-units rise with output because of increasing short-period cost.
- The equilibrium position is affected by these and other repercussions. Every factor is liable to change without much warning, and sometimes substantially. Hence the "extreme complexity" of events. The schema still makes problems more manageable and gives practical intuition better material (§ II, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* The chain runs: expectations, interest and liquidity set investment. Investment sets income through the multiplier. Income and employment then push back on the money demand that helped set interest. It is a loop, not a one-way line.

### III. Why the system is stable but subnormal
- Some special characteristics of the economic system can safely be generalised from experience but are not logically necessary. They concern the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency schedule and the interest rate (§ III, para. 1).
- **Observed facts** (§ III, paras. 2–3):
  - Output and employment fluctuate severely, but the system is not violently unstable.
  - It seems capable of staying in chronic subnormal activity for a considerable period, with no marked tendency either to recover or to collapse.
  - The evidence indicates that full or near-full employment is rare and short-lived.
  - Fluctuations may start briskly but seem to wear themselves out before great extremes and eventually to reverse; the theory of business cycles with a regular phase is founded on this.
  - Prices, after a disturbance, seem able to find a level at which they can remain, for the time being, moderately stable.
- Since these facts are not logically necessary, one must suppose that the environment and psychological propensities of the modern world are of a character to produce them. Method: ask which propensities would give stability, then ask whether they plausibly describe contemporary human nature (§ III, para. 3).

**The four conditions** (§ III, para. 4):
- (i) The multiplier is greater than unity but not very large.
- (ii) Moderate changes in the prospective yield of capital or in the interest rate do not cause very great changes in investment.
- (iii) Money-wages move in the same direction as employment but not in great disproportion. This is a condition of price stability more than of employment stability.
- (iv) Investment above (below) its former rate begins, after a period not very long in years, to lower (raise) the marginal efficiency of capital. Keynes says it provides not so much stability as the tendency of a fluctuation to reverse itself in due course.

**Support for each condition:**
- (i) *Highly plausible psychologically.* As real income rises, present needs press less and the margin above the accustomed standard grows. Consumption therefore, at any rate on the average of the community, rises but by less than income. The same is likely for governments, since rising unemployment forces relief paid from borrowing (§ III, cond. (i)).
  - *Experience.* If the law failed, any small investment rise would start a cumulative rise in demand up to full employment, and any fall would drive employment to zero. Keynes grants that there *may* be a narrow range where instability prevails.
  - If the multiplier were enormous, a given investment change would swing consumption hugely (§ III, cond. (i)).
- (ii) *Likely reason:* the increasing cost of producing a much larger output from existing equipment. If large surplus resources exist for capital goods, there may be instability within a range until the surplus is largely used. The condition also limits instability from sharp swings in business psychology or epoch-making inventions, perhaps more upward than downward (§ III, cond. (ii)).
- (iii) *Reason:* the wage struggle is mainly over relative wages. It is likely to intensify as employment rises, because bargaining power improves and workers are readier to take risks. These motives operate within limits: workers neither demand much higher money-wages in good times nor accept very large cuts to avoid all unemployment (§ III, cond. (iii)).
  - *Experience.* If competition among the unemployed always caused very large wage cuts, the price level would be violently unstable.
  - There might then be no stable equilibrium except at full employment. The wage-unit might have to fall without limit until abundant money, in wage-units, lowered the interest rate enough to restore full employment (§ III, cond. (iii)).
- (iv) *Presumption:* capital-assets have various ages and wear out. If investment falls below a certain minimum, it is a matter of time (barring large changes in other factors) before the marginal efficiency of capital recovers and investment rises above it. Symmetrically, higher investment eventually lowers the marginal efficiency of capital and brings a recession, unless other factors compensate (§ III, cond. (iv)).
- Even the degrees of recovery and recession that the other conditions allow are likely, if they persist long enough and other factors do not interfere, to cause a reverse movement, until the same forces reverse direction again (§ III, after cond. (iv)).
- Together the conditions explain why the economy oscillates around a position appreciably below full employment and appreciably above the minimum at which life is endangered (§ III).
- **Caveat.** This mean position follows from "natural" tendencies that persist if no measures are taken to correct them. It is not established by laws of necessity. It is a fact about the world as it is or has been, "not a necessary principle which cannot be changed" (§ III, last para.).

*Intuition note:* Stability here comes from moderate reactions: people spend part of extra income, investors respond with limited force, and workers do not demand or accept wild wage changes. The subnormal resting point is how the system behaves, not something it must do.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
The text says the multiplier is set by the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) but gives no formula, so the geometric-series formula below is our added step. Suppose investment rises by 100 wage-units and MPC = 0.5.
- Round 1: income +100 → consumption +50.
- Round 2: that 50 is income → consumption +25.
- Round 3: +12.5, then +6.25, and so on.
- Total income change = 100 + 50 + 25 + 12.5 + … = 100 × 1/(1 − 0.5) = 200. The multiplier is 2: above 1 but not large, as in condition (i).
- If MPC = 0.9, the total is 100 × 1/(1 − 0.9) = 1000, a multiplier of 10. Small investment changes would then cause huge swings, like the instability Keynes says experience excludes.
- If MPC = 1, every round adds the full 100 and the series never settles. This is the cumulative case Keynes describes.

## Concepts
- [[given-vs-independent-variables]] (new) — given factors are set aside, not assumed constant; independent variables drive the dependent ones; the split is made from experience (§ I)
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — how consumption responds to income; the marginal propensity gives the extra consumption per extra income (§ II)
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — expected return on new capital relative to its supply-price; investment goes on until it is roughly equal to the interest rate (§ II)
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — the demand for money, which with the quantity of money sets the interest rate and tends to rise with employment (§§ I–II)
- [[investment-multiplier]] (new) — ratio of an income increment to the investment increment behind it, set by the marginal propensity to consume (§ II)
- [[wage-unit]] (new) — money-wage per unit of labour; income, employment and money are measured in it (§ I)
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — demand level, in wage-units, that determines the employment given by the aggregate supply function (§ I)
- [[stability-conditions]] (new) — Keynes's four behavioural conditions explaining bounded fluctuation (§ III)
- [[equilibrium]] — the position determined by the factors, which is itself shifted by repercussions; Keynes notes a "resting-place" for wages is possible only at full employment under the counterfactual in condition (iii) (§§ II–III)

## Canonical instances
- [[subnormal-activity-equilibrium]] (new) — the economy rests for long periods below full employment, without recovery or collapse (§ III)
- [[business-cycle-regular-phase]] (new) — fluctuations that wear out and reverse, as in cycle theory (§ III)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Four stability conditions ↔ dynamical systems: bounded oscillation needs gain below some threshold; a multiplier of 1/(1 − MPC) with MPC < 1 is a convergent series, while the unstable case diverges.
- [structural-analogy] Condition (iv), investment reversing its own marginal efficiency of capital ↔ negative feedback in cyclical models.
- [metaphor] "Natural" tendencies persisting absent correction ↔ self-organised regularities in complex systems, which can change if rules change.

## Questions this raises
- How large is the marginal propensity to consume in practice, and does the multiplier "greater than unity but not very large" hold empirically?
- What would happen to stability if condition (ii) failed during large surplus capacity, as Keynes mentions?
- Which of the independent variables can central authority control, and how, given the final task he mentions?
- Why did Keynes treat the employment multiplier as equal to the investment multiplier only "as a first approximation"?
