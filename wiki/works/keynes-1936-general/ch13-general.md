# The General Theory of the Rate of Interest (Keynes, ch. 13)

## L0 — One line
The rate of interest is the reward for parting with liquidity, so it is set where the public's wish to hold cash equals the available quantity of money, not where saving equals investment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks what determines the rate of interest. Older theories tie it to saving and the marginal efficiency of capital. He says they neglect a second decision: in what form to hold the wealth one has not consumed. Interest rewards giving up liquidity. Liquidity-preference (the demand to hold money) comes from transactions, precaution and speculation, and uncertainty about the future rate of interest is a necessary condition for holding money as a store of wealth; it also gives a further, speculative ground where there is an organised debt market. The rate is fixed by liquidity-preference together with the quantity of money, $M = L(r)$. This puts money into the causal chain. He warns that several things can break the link from more money to more employment.

**Prerequisites:** the marginal efficiency of capital (ch. 11); bond prices fall as interest rates rise; the idea of a schedule (a demand curve).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The marginal efficiency of capital is a different thing from the rate of interest, so a separate theory of the rate is needed (§ I, para. 1).
- Time-preference involves two decisions: how much to consume, and in what form to hold the rest. Accepted theories neglect the second (§ II, paras. 1–3).
- Interest is a reward for parting with liquidity, not for saving as such. Hoarded cash saves just as much but earns nothing (§ II, paras. 4–5).
- Money enters the theory through $M = L(r)$: the rate equilibrates desire to hold cash with the quantity of cash (§ II, para. 5).
- Uncertainty about future interest rates is a necessary condition for liquidity-preference as a way of holding wealth (§ II, paras. 6–8).
- Three motives (transactions, precautionary, speculative) divide liquidity-preference. An organised debt market lowers the precautionary demand but allows large speculative swings (§ II, paras. 9–12).
- The liquidity curve normally slopes down, but a large increase in money may move the rate little. The system's stability depends on variety of opinion (§ II, paras. 13–15).
- Many "slips" separate more money from more employment (§ III).
- "Hoarding", if taken as an actual increase in cash-holding, is an incomplete idea. As long as hoarding means actual cash-holding, the public cannot change it, only the rate at which the desire to hoard equals the cash available (§ V).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. The problem
- Ch. 11 showed that the marginal efficiency of capital is a different thing from the ruling rate of interest. The marginal-efficiency schedule "may be said to govern" the terms on which loanable funds are demanded for new investment; the rate of interest governs the terms on which funds are currently supplied (§ I, para. 1).
- Earlier theories (to be treated in ch. 14) broadly speaking make the rate depend on the interaction of that schedule with the propensity to save. Keynes says this fails, because the rate cannot be deduced from these two factors alone (§ I, para. 2).

### II. Liquidity-preference
**Two decisions** (§ II, paras. 1–3):
1. The propensity to consume decides how much income is consumed and how much is reserved as command over future consumption.
2. Then the person decides in what form to hold that reserve: liquid (money) or parted with for some period. Keynes defines the degree of liquidity-preference by a schedule of the resources, valued in money or wage-units, that one wishes to hold as money in different circumstances.

The accepted theories derive the rate from the first decision and neglect the second.

**Interest is the reward for parting with liquidity** (§ II, paras. 4–5):
- A man who hoards savings in cash earns no interest though he saves as much as before. So interest is not a return to saving or waiting as such.
- By definition the rate is the inverse proportion between a sum of money and what one obtains for parting with control over it, in exchange for a debt, for a stated period.
- It measures the unwillingness of money-holders to part with liquid control. It is not the price that balances investment demand against abstinence. It is the price that equilibrates the desire to hold wealth as cash with the available cash.
- At a lower rate, desired cash would exceed supply. At a higher rate, there would be unwanted surplus cash.
- With $r$ the rate, $M$ the quantity of money and $L$ the liquidity-preference function:
$$M = L(r)$$
This is where the quantity of money enters the economic scheme.

*Intuition note:* Money pays no interest, so the interest rate is what you must be paid to give up holding it. If people want more cash than exists, the rate must rise until they are content.

**Why liquidity-preference exists** (§ II, paras. 6–8):
- Money has a transactions use and a store-of-wealth use. For transactions it pays, up to a point, to sacrifice some interest for convenience.
- Why hold wealth in money rather than in a bond that pays interest, with equal default risk? The full answer waits for ch. 15, but there is a necessary condition for any liquidity-preference for money as a way of holding wealth: uncertainty about the future of the rate of interest, i.e. of the complex of rates for varying maturities that will rule at future dates.
- If all future rates were certain, they could be inferred from current rates. With ${}_1d_r$ the present value (in year 1) of £1 deferred $r$ years, and $_nd_r$ the value in year $n$ of £1 deferred $r$ years from then:
$$ {}_nd_r = \frac{{}_1d_{n+r}}{{}_1d_n} $$
- So the rate at which any debt can be turned into cash $n$ years hence is given by two of the current rates. In that certain case, if the current rate is positive for every maturity, buying a debt must always be more advantageous than holding cash.
- If the future is uncertain, ${}_nd_r$ need not equal ${}_1d_{n+r}/{}_1d_n$ when the time comes. If cash may be needed before $n$ years expire, buying a long debt risks a loss compared with cash. The actuarial expectation of gain (if it can be calculated, "which is doubtful") must compensate for that risk.

**Speculative ground** (§ II, paras. 9–10):
- Uncertainty gives a further ground for liquidity-preference, provided there is an organised market for debts. Different people estimate the prospects differently, and someone who differs from the predominant opinion in market quotations may have good reason to keep liquid resources, to profit if he turns out to be right.
- This parallels the marginal efficiency of capital, which is fixed by market valuation under mass psychology, not by the "best" opinion. Someone who expects higher future rates than the market has reason to hold cash. Someone expecting lower rates has reason to borrow short to buy longer debts.
- The market price settles where the sales of the "bears" and the purchases of the "bulls" balance.

**Three motives** (§ II, para. 11):
- Transactions: cash for current personal and business exchanges.
- Precautionary: security as to the future cash value of part of total resources.
- Speculative: profit from knowing better than the market.
- Dilemma: without an organised market, precautionary liquidity-preference would be greatly increased. With one, there is room for wide fluctuations in speculative liquidity-preference.

**Illustration** (§ II, para. 12):
- Assume transactions and precautionary demands absorb cash not very sensitive to the rate (apart from effects via income). The remaining money serves the speculative motive. The rate and bond price must then make the cash that "bears" want exactly equal to that remainder.
- Each increase in money must raise bond prices enough to exceed some bull's expectations, so that he sells and joins the bears.
- If speculative demand is negligible except briefly, more money must lower the rate almost at once. It must fall enough to raise employment and the wage-unit, so that the extra cash is absorbed by the transactions and precautionary motives.

*Intuition note:* If the need for everyday and precautionary cash does not react much to the rate, the rest of the money must be held by people who think bonds are a bad buy. When there is more money, a higher bond price (lower rate) is needed to turn enough bulls into bears.

**Shape of the schedule** (§ II, paras. 13–15):
- As a rule, Keynes supposes a smooth curve with the rate falling as money increases. There are several causes.
- First, *cet. par.*, a lower rate likely absorbs more money for transactions. If the fall raises national income, the convenient transactions balance rises more or less in proportion. The interest cost of holding cash also falls. If higher employment raises wages, a similar result follows, unless liquidity-preference is measured in wage-units.
- Second, a fall in the rate may raise the cash held by those whose views differ from the market.
- Exception: even a large increase in money may have a comparatively small effect on the rate. It may raise uncertainty and strengthen the precautionary motive. Or opinion may be so unanimous that a small change in rates causes a mass movement into cash.
- Keynes remarks that stability and sensitivity to money depend on variety of opinion about what is uncertain. Control by changing the money supply is more precarious in the United States, where everyone tends to hold the same opinion at once, than in England.

### III. Slips between cup and lip
Money has now entered the causal chain (§ III). Each step is only expected *cet. par.* and can fail:
- More money lowers the rate, unless liquidity-preferences rise by more than the money does.
- A lower rate raises investment, unless the marginal-efficiency schedule falls faster than the rate.
- More investment raises employment, though this may fail if the propensity to consume is falling.
- If employment rises, prices rise, partly by the physical supply functions and partly by the wage-unit rising. Higher output and prices then increase the money needed to hold a given rate.

### IV. Relation to the *Treatise on Money*
Speculative liquidity-preference corresponds to the earlier "state of bearishness" but is not the same (§ IV). Bearishness there related the price of assets and debts *together* to the quantity of money. Keynes says this confused effects of a change in the rate of interest with effects of a change in the marginal-efficiency schedule. He hopes he has avoided this confusion here.

### V. Hoarding
- Hoarding is a first approximation to liquidity-preference ("propensity to hoard" would be about the same). As an actual increase in cash-holding it is incomplete and misleading if it suggests a simple choice. The decision balances advantages, so one must know what is in the other scale (§ V).
- As long as 'hoarding' means the actual holding of cash, the public cannot change actual hoarding by its decisions. Hoarding equals the quantity of money (on some definitions, minus the transactions requirement), and the public does not determine that quantity. The propensity to hoard only determines the rate at which the desire to hoard equals the available cash.
- Keynes suggests that overlooking this may be part of the explanation why interest was regarded as the reward of not-spending, "whereas in fact it is the reward of not-hoarding".

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four investors each would hold £10 of speculative cash if the bond rate $r$ is below their own "expected normal rate". Their rates are 3%, 4%, 5% and 6%. Speculative demand $L_s(r)$:
- $r=2.5\%$: all four hold cash, so 40.
- $r=3.5\%$: the 4%, 5%, 6% investors hold, so 30.
- $r=4.5\%$: 5% and 6% investors, so 20.
- $r=5.5\%$: 6% investor only, so 10.
- $r=6.5\%$: none, so 0.

Suppose $M=80$ and transactions plus precautionary needs are fixed at 60. Then 20 is available for speculation, and $L_s=20$ needs $4\%<r<5\%$. Raise $M$ to 90: 30 is available, so $r$ must fall into $3\%<r<4\%$ and one more investor joins the bears. If all four held the same view (say 4%), $L_s$ would jump from 0 to 40 at that one rate, so a small rate change would cause a mass movement into cash, as in § II, para. 15.
