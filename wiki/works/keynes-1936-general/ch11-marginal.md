# The Marginal Efficiency of Capital

## L0 — One line
Keynes defines the marginal efficiency of capital as the discount rate that equates an asset's expected future returns with its replacement cost, so that investment depends on expectations of the future, not merely on current yields.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What decides how much firms invest? Keynes defines the marginal efficiency of capital (MEC). It is the discount rate that makes the present value of the returns expected from an asset over its life equal to its supply price (replacement cost). Investment is pushed to the point where MEC equals the market rate of interest. Because MEC rests on expected yields, changes in expectations (about costs, technology, the value of money, interest rates) shift it, and, Keynes says, this makes MEC fluctuate somewhat violently, which explains the trade cycle. Keynes also separates borrower's risk from lender's risk, and finds older ideas of capital's productivity ambiguous. The chapter links present to future through durable equipment.

**Prerequisites:** present value and discounting; interest rate as a price of time; the idea of marginal decisions.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- MEC is the discount rate equating the present value of an asset's expected yields with its supply (replacement) price (§ I).
- MEC of a type of capital falls as investment in it rises, partly because its prospective yield falls and partly because, as a rule, its supply price rises. Summing over types gives the investment demand-schedule (§ I).
- Investment goes on until MEC equals the market rate of interest. The rate of interest cannot be deduced from yields or MEC (§ I).
- Older notions of the marginal productivity of capital are ambiguous (physical vs value, absolute vs ratio, one period vs whole series). Keynes says his definition is fairly close to what Marshall intended and identical to Fisher's "rate of return over cost" (§ II).
- Expected changes in costs, technique or the value of money move the MEC schedule. Existing asset prices adjust at once, so the effect works through new production (§ III).
- Fluctuations of MEC relative to the interest rate are said to explain the trade cycle (§ III).
- There are two risks: borrower's risk and lender's risk. The latter partly duplicates the former, and both tend to be underestimated in booms (§ IV).
- MEC is the main channel by which expectation of the future influences the present, through the demand price of durable equipment (§ V).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Definition and investment rule
- Buying a capital-asset buys the right to a series of prospective returns $Q_1, Q_2,\dots,Q_n$, net of running expenses, over its life. This series is its **prospective yield** (§ I).
- **Supply price** = not the market price, but the price that would just induce a manufacturer to produce one more unit, i.e. replacement cost (§ I).
- **MEC** of a type of asset = the rate of discount that makes the present value of the expected annuities equal to the supply price (§ I). In symbols, with $\rho$ the MEC *(added step)*: $\sum_{r=1}^{n} Q_r/(1+\rho)^r = \text{supply price}$. The greatest of these can be regarded as the MEC in general (§ I).
- It uses *expected* yield and the *current* supply price, not the historical result of past investments (§ I).
- More investment in a type of capital lowers its MEC. First, prospective yield falls as supply grows. Second, pressure on producers usually raises the supply price. The second factor usually matters more in the short run, the first more over longer periods (§ I).
- Aggregating over types gives the **investment demand-schedule** (the MEC schedule): aggregate investment against the resulting MEC (§ I).
- Rule: investment is pushed until no asset class has an MEC above the market interest rate. At that point MEC in general equals the rate of interest (§ I).
- Equivalent form: let $d_r$ be the present value of £1 deferred $r$ years at the current interest rate. Then $\sum Q_r d_r$ is the **demand price**. Investment continues until it equals the supply price. If it falls short, there is no current investment in that asset (§ I). (The source text shows "S" for $\sum$.)
- The inducement to invest therefore depends on the demand-schedule and on the interest rate. Neither prospective yield nor MEC lets us deduce the interest rate or the asset's present value. The rate must come from elsewhere, and only then can the yield be "capitalised" (§ I).

*Intuition note:* Compare what the asset will earn, discounted, with what it costs to build. If the discounted earnings exceed cost at the market interest rate, building is worthwhile. MEC is the break-even discount rate.

### II. Relation to earlier usage
- Keynes finds three ambiguities in "marginal productivity/efficiency of capital" (§ II):
  1. Physical product vs value. Defining a physical unit of capital raises difficulties that he believes both insoluble and unnecessary; he knows no way to turn the physical claim into an intelligible ratio without bringing in values.
  2. Absolute quantity vs ratio. Treating it like an interest rate suggests a ratio, but the two terms are rarely stated.
  3. The increment from extra capital in the existing situation ($Q_1$) vs the whole expected series $Q_1,Q_2,\dots$. Keynes says neglect of this distinction has been the main cause of confusion and misunderstanding.
- Looking at $Q_1$ alone is legitimate only in a static theory in which all the $Q$'s are equal. The ordinary theory that capital gets its marginal productivity *now* is valid only in a stationary state (§ II).
- Aggregate current return to capital has no direct relation to MEC. The current return at the margin is the marginal user cost, also not closely connected to MEC (§ II).
- **Marshall** (Keynes's condensed summary of a passage in Marshall's Principles): an extra £100 of machinery adds £3 a year to net output. If investors have pushed capital into every occupation that seems likely to reward it, and at equilibrium the machinery still only just pays, Marshall infers that the interest rate is 3% (§ II). Marshall adds that such illustrations cannot become a theory of interest without reasoning in a circle. In his hat-trade example, more machinery is used as the rate falls (20%, 10%, 6%, 4%, then 3%), and the utility of the last machinery worth using is then measured by 3 per cent (§ II). **Keynes's reading:** here Marshall appears to accept that the interest rate fixes how far new investment goes, given the MEC schedule. Keynes says Marshall is less cautious elsewhere (chapter 14) (§ II).
- **Fisher**'s "rate of return over cost" equates present worth of all costs and returns. Fisher says investment needs it to exceed the interest rate. Keynes says it is identical in sense and purpose to MEC (§ II).

### III. Dependence on expectation
- MEC depends on prospective, not merely current, yield. Example: equipment built today must compete with later equipment made at lower labour cost (the wage-unit) or with better technique, and profits fall if all output gets cheaper. If such developments are foreseen even as possible, today's MEC is diminished (§ III).
- Hence expected changes in the value of money affect output. Expecting a fall in the value of money raises the MEC schedule and stimulates investment. Expecting a rise lowers it and depresses investment (§ III).
- **Keynes on Fisher's "Appreciation and Interest"** (real rate = money rate corrected for changes in the value of money): it is unclear whether the change is foreseen. If unforeseen, there is no effect on current affairs. If foreseen, existing goods' prices adjust at once, so it is too late for holders of money to gain or suffer from a change in the interest rate that would offset the prospective change in the value of money. Pigou's device (foreseen by some, not others) does not escape the dilemma (§ III).
- Keynes's diagnosis: expected price changes do not act directly on the interest rate. Prices of *existing* assets always adjust. The effect is on readiness to produce *new* assets, through MEC (§ III).
- If the interest rate rose in step with MEC, there would be no stimulus. The stimulus needs MEC to rise *relative* to the interest rate. Keynes says Fisher's theory could best be rewritten with a real rate of interest defined as the rate that would leave current output unaffected by the change in expectations (§ III).
- An expected *future fall* in interest rates lowers the MEC schedule, because today's equipment must compete with later equipment content with a lower return. The effect is small, since expected future rates are partly reflected in today's rates, but there may be some depressing effect (§ III).
- Keynes says it is chiefly this dependence that makes MEC subject to somewhat violent fluctuations, which he calls the explanation of the trade cycle. He says chapter 22 will show that boom and slump can be described in terms of MEC fluctuations relative to the interest rate (§ III).

*Intuition note:* Old machines will face cheaper rivals later. If you expect that, a new machine is worth less to you now, so you build fewer. The same logic runs in reverse when you expect prices to rise.

### IV. Two kinds of risk
- **Borrower's (entrepreneur's) risk:** doubt about actually earning the hoped yield. It is the only relevant risk if one invests one's own money (§ IV).
- **Lender's risk:** where loans are made against real or personal security. It comes from moral hazard (voluntary default or escape) or from an insufficient margin of security (involuntary default after disappointed expectations). A third possible source is an adverse change in the monetary standard, though this should be mostly reflected in durable-asset prices (§ IV).
- The first is in a sense a real social cost, reducible by averaging and better foresight. The second is a pure addition to the cost of investment, absent if borrower and lender were the same person (§ IV).
- It also duplicates part of the entrepreneur's risk, which is added *twice* to the pure interest rate to give the minimum yield that induces investment. The borrower wants a wider margin between expected yield and interest rate. The lender wants a wider margin over the pure rate, except where the borrower offers exceptional security. The lender cannot enjoy the borrower's hope of a very favourable outcome (§ IV).
- Keynes says the duplication may be important in certain circumstances. In booms, popular estimates of both risks are apt to become unusually and imprudently low (§ IV).

### V. Why it matters
- MEC is of fundamental importance because the future influences the present mainly through it, much more than through the rate of interest (§ V).
- Keynes says the mistake of regarding MEC primarily as current yield, which would be correct only in a static state, has broken the theoretical link between today and tomorrow. The interest rate is virtually a current phenomenon (§ V).
- Static assumptions import unreality into theory. User cost and MEC, Keynes thinks, bring theory back to reality with minimal adaptation (§ V).
- Durable equipment links future to present, so expectation should work through its demand price (§ V).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An asset costs 100 to produce (supply price). Expected yields: $Q_1=55$, $Q_2=60.5$.
- MEC: at $\rho=10\%$: $55/1.1=50$ and $60.5/1.21=50$, sum $=100$. So MEC $=10\%$.
- Interest 8%: $d_1=1/1.08$, $d_2=1/1.1664$. Demand price $=55/1.08+60.5/1.1664\approx50.93+51.87=102.80>100$. Build.
- Interest 12%: $55/1.12+60.5/1.2544\approx49.11+48.23=97.34<100$. Do not build.
- Expectation effect: if cheaper rivals are expected, $Q_2$ drops to 49.5. At 3%: $55/1.03+49.5/1.0609\approx53.40+46.66=100.06$. MEC is now just above 3%, far below 10%, though nothing about today's yield $Q_1$ changed.
