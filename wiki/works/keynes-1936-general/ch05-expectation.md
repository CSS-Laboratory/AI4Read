# Expectation as determining output and employment

## L0 — One line
Employment at any moment is governed by today's expectations of costs and sale-proceeds, taken together with today's capital equipment, not by past results as such.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Production takes time, so producers and investors must act on expectations about what consumers will pay later. Keynes separates short-term expectation (the price for finished output) from long-term expectation (the returns from adding to capital equipment). Generally, a change in expectation works itself out slowly. On the way to the new steady "long-period" employment, employment can overshoot or undershoot, so a change in expectation alone can look like a cycle. Past expectations matter only because they are embodied in today's equipment. Short-term expectations can often be treated as following recent results. Long-term expectations cannot be checked quickly and can be revised suddenly, so they cannot be dropped from the analysis.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a firm's costs and sale-proceeds; the difference between consumption goods and capital equipment; the idea of a steady state versus a transition path.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Production takes time, so the entrepreneur (producer and investor alike) must be guided by expectations of future consumer demand (§ I, para. 1).
- Two kinds of expectation: short-term (price of finished output when the process starts) and long-term (future returns from adding to capital equipment) (§ I, para. 2).
- Firms' daily output follows short-term expectations, which for additions to capital equipment and even sales to distributors depend largely on other parties' long-term (or medium-term) expectations (§ I, para. 3). Realised results matter to employment only insofar as they change later expectations (§ I, para. 3).
- In general a change in expectation produces its full effect only over a considerable period (§ I, para. 4). Each state of expectation nevertheless has a definite corresponding "long-period employment", even if actual employment never reaches it (§ I, para. 5).
- The transition can overshoot or undershoot the new long-period level, so an expectation change alone can produce a cycle-shaped movement (§ I, para. 6).
- Expectation is liable to constant change, with new changes coming before earlier ones have worked out, so the economy is occupied with overlapping activities from many past expectations (§ I, para. 7).
- Today's employment is governed by today's expectations plus today's equipment, which embodies the past (§ II, para. 1).
- Express reference to short-term expectation can often safely be omitted, because its revision is gradual and carried on largely in the light of realised results (§ II, para. 2). Express reference to long-term expectation can seldom be avoided: it cannot be checked at short intervals by results and is liable to sudden revision (§ II, paras. 2–3).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Expectation and the time-taking of production
**Why expectations are needed.** All production ultimately serves a consumer. Time, sometimes much time, passes between the producer's costs and the final purchase. The entrepreneur must therefore form "the best expectations he can" about what consumers will pay later. He has no choice but to follow them if he uses processes that take time (§ I, para. 1).

**Two groups of expectation.** Some individuals or firms specialise in the first type, others in the second (§ I, para. 2).
- *Short-term expectation:* the price a manufacturer expects for "finished" output when he commits to the process that makes it. Output is "finished" for him when it is ready to use or to sell to a second party.
- *Long-term expectation:* what the entrepreneur can hope to earn in future returns if he buys, or perhaps makes, finished output as an addition to his capital equipment.

**What drives a firm's daily output.** A firm's daily output is determined by its short-term expectations. These cover the cost of output at various scales and the sale-proceeds expected (§ I, para. 3).
- For additions to capital equipment, and even for sales to distributors, these short-term expectations largely depend on the long-term (or medium-term) expectations of other parties.
- Employment depends on these expectations.
- Realised results are relevant to employment only insofar as they modify later expectations.
- The original expectations that led the firm to acquire its equipment and stocks are not relevant either. Each decision is made with reference to the existing equipment and stock, but in the light of current expectations (§ I, para. 3).

> *Intuition note:* A firm looks forward, not back. Yesterday's hopes and yesterday's profits matter only through what they change in today's forecast, and through the machines and stocks they left behind.

**Gradual working-out.** A change in expectation, short- or long-term, will in general produce its full effect on employment only over a considerable period. The effect on day 2 differs from day 1, and so on, even with no further change (§ I, para. 4). Reasons given:
- *Short-term, change for the worse:* changes are not, as a rule, violent or rapid enough to stop all processes that now look like mistakes.
- *Short-term, change for the better:* preparation takes time before employment reaches the level it would have reached had expectations been revised sooner.
- *Long-term:* equipment that will not be replaced keeps giving employment until worn out. After a change for the better, employment may at first be higher than after the equipment has been adjusted to the new situation (§ I, para. 4).

**Long-period employment.** Suppose a state of expectation lasts long enough that, broadly speaking, no piece of employment is going on that would not have occurred had the new state always existed. The steady level of employment then reached is called the *long-period employment* corresponding to that state (§ I, para. 5).
- Expectation may change so often that actual employment never reaches this level.
- Even so, every state of expectation has a definite corresponding long-period employment (§ I, para. 5).

**The transition path (uninterrupted by further change).** Take first a change after which long-period employment will be greater than before (§ I, para. 6).
- As a rule only the rate of input is much affected at first: work on the early stages of new processes. Consumption-goods output and work on the later stages of processes already started stay much the same.
- Stocks of partly finished goods may modify this, but the initial rise in employment is likely to be modest.
- Employment then rises gradually. It may rise at some stage above the new long-period level, because building up capital for the new expectation can bring more employment and more current consumption than the long-period position will.
- The result may be a "gradual crescendo" to a peak, then a decline to the new long-period level.
- The same can happen if the new long-period level equals the old. This may happen if the change shifts the direction of consumption and makes some existing processes and equipment obsolete.
- If the new long-period employment is lower, employment during the transition may fall for a time below that new level.
- So a mere change in expectation can produce an oscillation shaped like a cyclical movement. Keynes says he discussed movements of this kind in his *Treatise on Money*, in connection with building up or depleting stocks of working and liquid capital (§ I, para. 6).

> *Intuition note:* Building up capital for a brighter outlook can employ more people, and support more current consumption, than the long-period position will. Employment can therefore rise and fall even though expectations changed only once.

**Real life is messier.** Expectation is liable to constant change. A new expectation is superimposed long before the previous change has worked out. At any time the economy is occupied with overlapping activities due to various past states of expectation (§ I, para. 7).

### II. Relevance for the present purpose
**Past expectations are embodied in equipment.** Employment at any time depends, "in a sense", on the states of expectation over a past period. Past expectations not yet worked out are embodied in today's capital equipment, and they influence today's decisions only insofar as so embodied. Hence today's employment "can be correctly described" as governed by today's expectations in conjunction with today's capital equipment (§ II, para. 1).

**Short-term expectation can often be left implicit.** Reference to current long-term expectations can seldom be avoided. Reference to short-term expectation can often safely be omitted (§ II, para. 2).
- Revision of short-term expectation is gradual and continuous, carried on largely in the light of realised results, so expected and realised results overlap in their influence.
- Output is determined by short-term expectations and not by past results. Yet the most recent results usually play a predominant part in forming those expectations.
- Working out expectations afresh each time would be too complicated and a waste of time, since most circumstances continue substantially unchanged from day to day.
- So it is sensible for producers to assume the most recent results continue, except where there are definite reasons to expect change. Forecasts are more often modified gradually in light of results than in anticipation of prospective changes (§ II, para. 2).

**Why long-term expectation cannot be dropped.** For durable goods, the producer's short-term expectations rest on the investor's current long-term expectations. Keynes gives two reasons why these cannot be replaced by realised results (§ II, para. 3).
- It is of the nature of long-term expectations that they cannot be checked at short intervals in the light of realised results.
- As chapter 12 will show, they are liable to sudden revision.

So current long-term expectation "cannot be even approximately eliminated or replaced by realised results" (§ II, para. 3).

> *Intuition note:* For everyday output, "tomorrow will look like yesterday" is a workable shortcut. For investment in durable assets there is no quick feedback from results, and views can shift suddenly, so the factor of current long-term expectation cannot be dropped from the analysis.

## Toy example
*(Ours, not Keynes's numbers. It shows only the shape he says is possible.)* Employment = work on consumption goods (C) + work on building capital (K).

| Period | K | C | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (old long-period) | 20 | 80 | 100 |
| 1 (expectation improves) | 30 | 80 | 110 |
| 2 | 50 | 85 | 135 |
| 3 | 35 | 92 | 127 |
| 4 (new long-period) | 25 | 95 | 120 |

- In period 1 only input-side work (K) moves; C is unchanged. This matches the text's point that the early stages of new processes react first (§ I, para. 6).
- The peak, 135 in period 2, is above the new long-period level of 120. Capital building has pushed employment above it.
- Employment then falls back to 120, which gives the "crescendo to a peak, then decline" (§ I, para. 6).
- Expectation changed once, yet the path looks like a small cycle.

## Concepts
- [[short-term-expectation]] (new) — a producer's expectation of costs and sale-proceeds of finished output when he starts a process; it drives daily output (§ I, paras. 2–3).
- [[long-term-expectation]] (new) — the entrepreneur's expectation of future returns from adding finished output to capital equipment (§ I, para. 2).
- [[long-period-employment]] (new) — the steady employment reached when a state of expectation has fully worked out (§ I, para. 5).
- [[conventional expectations]] — related idea, but Keynes does not use the term here: producers base expectations on the assumption that recent results continue unless there are definite reasons to expect change (§ II, para. 2).

## Canonical instances
- [[expectation-driven-cycle]] (new) — a single change in expectation can generate a rise to a peak and a decline, shaped like a cycle (§ I, para. 6).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Short-term expectations modified gradually by recent results ↔ economics-finance: adaptive expectations — both revise forecasts partly in the light of recent outcomes. Keynes gives no formula.
- [metaphor] Employment overshooting while capital is built up ↔ capital-stock adjustment dynamics. Both involve a transition path that differs from the steady state.
