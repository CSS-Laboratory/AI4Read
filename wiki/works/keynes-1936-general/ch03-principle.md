# The Principle of Effective Demand

## L0 — One line
Employment is set where expected proceeds meet the aggregate supply price of output (effective demand), and nothing in general guarantees that this point is full employment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks what fixes the volume of employment. His answer: entrepreneurs hire up to the point where the proceeds they expect equal the aggregate supply price (proceeds that just justify employment). Because consumption rises less than income, current investment must fill the gap. So employment depends on the supply function, the propensity to consume (how consumption responds to income) and investment, not on the marginal disutility of labour, except that labour supply sets a maximum. The classical doctrine ("Supply creates its own Demand") instead assumes demand always matches supply, which implies no obstacle to full employment. Keynes rejects this and argues that equilibrium below full employment is possible. He also asks why Ricardo's view won so completely.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand curves; the idea of equilibrium; income, consumption, saving and investment; the idea of a function $y=f(x)$; marginal disutility of labour (the burden of working one more unit).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Entrepreneurs' profit is output value minus factor cost and user cost. Total income is factor cost plus profit, called "proceeds" (para. 1).
- Employment is fixed where the aggregate demand function meets the aggregate supply function. $D$ at that point is *effective demand* (para. 4).
- The classical Say's law amounts to $D=Z$ at every $N$. This makes effective demand indeterminate and amounts to the same thing as no obstacle to full employment (paras. 5–6).
- Consumption rises with income but by less. The gap must be filled by investment, so $\phi(N)-c(N)=D_2$ (paras. 8, 13–14, 17).
- Equilibrium can be stable with $N$ below full employment. Employment determines the real wage, not the other way round (paras. 17–18).
- This explains the paradox of poverty in the midst of plenty. The gap between actual and potential output tends to be wider in richer communities (paras. 19–21).
- Keynes holds that Ricardo's doctrine must have won through its fit with its environment, despite its failure at prediction. He thinks its optimism comes from neglecting deficient demand (paras. 24–26).

## L3 — Walkthrough
Paragraph numbers count from the start of the chapter text. Notation note: the text layer prints both functions as "f(N)". I write $Z=\phi(N)$ for supply and $D=f(N)$ for demand so they can be told apart.

### I. Definitions and effective demand (paras. 1–6)
- **Costs and income.** Employing labour costs an entrepreneur two things:
  - *Factor cost*: payments to factors of production (not other entrepreneurs) for current services.
  - *User cost*: payments to other entrepreneurs, plus the sacrifice of using equipment instead of leaving it idle (para. 1).
- Profit (the entrepreneur's income) is the output's value minus factor cost minus user cost. Factor cost plus profit is the *total income* from the employment, also called the *proceeds*. Profit is what the entrepreneur tries to maximise (para. 1).
- The **aggregate supply price** is the expected proceeds that just make a given employment worth offering (para. 1).
- So employment depends on the proceeds entrepreneurs expect, given technique, resources and factor cost per unit of employment (para. 2).
- Let $Z=\phi(N)$ be the aggregate supply price of output from $N$ men (*aggregate supply function*). Let $D=f(N)$ be the expected proceeds from $N$ men (*aggregate demand function*) (para. 3).
- If $D>Z$ at some $N$, entrepreneurs gain by expanding employment, even by bidding up factor costs, until $Z=D$. Employment is therefore at the intersection of the two functions. The value of $D$ there is the **effective demand** (para. 4). Keynes says this is the substance of his General Theory of Employment; later chapters examine what the two functions depend on (para. 4).
- **Classical doctrine.** "Supply creates its own Demand" must mean $\phi(N)=f(N)$ for all $N$ (para. 5).
  - Then proceeds always adjust to the supply price.
  - Effective demand has no unique value but "an infinite range of values all equally admissible".
  - Employment is indeterminate, except that the marginal disutility of labour sets an upper limit (para. 5).
- If this were true, competition would push employment to the point where output stops responding to higher effective demand. That is full employment. Keynes says an alternative, equivalent criterion to the earlier definition in terms of labour's behaviour is that aggregate employment is inelastic in response to more effective demand (para. 6).
- Say's law ($D=Z$ for all output volumes) is therefore equivalent to the proposition that there is no obstacle to full employment. If it is not the true law, a "vitally important chapter" of theory is still unwritten (para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Employers compare what they expect to take in with what it takes to make hiring worthwhile. Keynes says the classical view involves a special assumption: that these two always match.

### Summary of the theory (paras. 7–22; the text has no marker "II")
Simplification: money-wages and other factor costs are constant per unit of labour. Keynes says this is introduced solely to ease the exposition and will be dropped later, and that the essential character of the argument is the same whether or not money-wages change (para. 7).

**Narrative outline (paras. 8–9).**
- Rising employment raises real income. Consumption rises too, but by less than income (para. 8).
- Entrepreneurs would make a loss if all the extra output served only extra consumption. So, to justify any given employment, current investment must absorb the excess of output over what the community chooses to consume at that employment; otherwise entrepreneurs' receipts fall short (para. 8).
- Given the *propensity to consume*, equilibrium employment (the level at which employers as a whole have no inducement to expand or contract employment) depends on current investment. Investment depends on the *inducement to invest*, which depends on the relation between the marginal efficiency of capital schedule and interest rates on loans of various maturities and risks (para. 8).
- Given the propensity to consume and the rate of new investment, only one employment level is consistent with equilibrium, since any other level makes the aggregate supply price and demand price unequal. It cannot exceed full employment, but "there is no reason in general" to expect it to equal full employment. Full employment is a special case, reached only by accident or design when investment demand exactly fills the gap, i.e. equals the excess of the supply price of full-employment output over what the community will spend on consumption when fully employed. Keynes adds that this relationship corresponds to the classical theory's assumptions and is in a sense an optimum (para. 9).

**The eight propositions (paras. 10–17).**
1. Given technique, resources and costs, income depends on $N$ (para. 10).
2. The relation between income and expected consumption $D_1$ depends on the community's psychological propensity to consume. So consumption depends on the level of income, and hence on $N$, except when the propensity changes (para. 11).
3. Entrepreneurs' choice of $N$ depends on $D=D_1+D_2$. $D_2$ is the amount expected to go to new investment, and $D$ is effective demand (para. 12).
4. With $D_1=c(N)$:
$$D_1+D_2=D=\phi(N),\qquad\text{so}\qquad \phi(N)-c(N)=D_2 .$$
(para. 13; the printed formula and the clause naming the aggregate supply function have lost their Greek letters. I read the lost symbol as $\phi$. Since $D=Z$ at the intersection, $D=\phi(N)$ is the equilibrium condition (added step).)
5. Equilibrium employment therefore depends on (i) the supply function, (ii) the propensity to consume, (iii) investment $D_2$. Keynes calls this the essence of his General Theory of Employment (para. 14).
6. Each $N$ has a marginal productivity of labour in wage-goods industries, which determines the real wage. So (5) holds only if $N$ does not push the real wage below the marginal disutility of labour. Not all changes in $D$ are compatible with constant money-wages, so the assumption must eventually go (para. 15).
7. On the classical theory, if $D=\phi(N)$ for all $N$, employment is in neutral equilibrium at every $N$ below its maximum. Competition may be expected to push it to the maximum, the only point of stable equilibrium on that theory (para. 16).
8. When employment rises, $D_1$ rises by less than $D$. Keynes says the key to our practical problem lies in this psychological law (para. 17).
   - The gap between $Z$ and $D_1$ widens as employment grows.
   - With an unchanged propensity to consume, employment cannot rise unless $D_2$ rises to fill the gap.
   - Except on the classical theory's special assumption that some force always makes $D_2$ rise enough as employment grows, the system may find itself in stable equilibrium with $N$ below full employment, at the intersection of the two functions (para. 17).

*Intuition note:* People spend only part of each extra pound of income. Someone else, for example investors, must spend the rest, or employers lose money and cut jobs. Nothing guarantees that investment fills the gap at full employment, so the equilibrium level of jobs can sit below it.

**Consequences (paras. 18–22).**
- Employment is not determined by the marginal disutility of labour in real-wage terms, except that labour supply at a given real wage sets a maximum. The propensity to consume and new investment determine employment, which is uniquely related to the real wage, not the other way round (para. 18).
- If effective demand is deficient, employment falls short of the labour supply at the existing real wage. The equilibrium real wage then exceeds the marginal disutility of the equilibrium employment (para. 18).
- This explains the paradox of poverty in the midst of plenty. Insufficient effective demand may, and often will, halt employment growth before full employment, even though labour's marginal product still exceeds the marginal disutility of work (para. 19).
- **Richer communities.**
  - The richer the community, the wider tends to be the gap between actual and potential output (para. 20).
  - A poor community consumes most of its output, so modest investment gives full employment. A wealthy one needs much ampler investment opportunities if its richer members' saving is to fit with employing its poorer members (para. 20).
  - If the inducement to invest is weak, output is cut until the community is poor enough that its surplus over consumption matches that weakness (para. 20).
  - Also, the marginal propensity to consume is weaker in a wealthy community. Its larger capital stock makes new investment less attractive unless the interest rate falls fast enough. Why it does not automatically fall is left to Book IV (para. 21).
- Three main gaps in existing knowledge must be filled: the analysis of the propensity to consume, the definition of the marginal efficiency of capital, and the theory of the rate of interest. When they are, Keynes expects the theory of prices to fall into place as subsidiary. Money plays an essential part in the theory of interest (para. 22).

### III. Why the classical view prevailed (paras. 23–26)
- **Ricardian economics** neglects the aggregate demand function. Malthus opposed Ricardo's view that deficient effective demand was impossible. He could not explain clearly how and why it could be deficient, so he gave no alternative construction. Ricardo "conquered England" and the topic vanished. Keynes says the puzzle is not mentioned even once in the works of Marshall, Edgeworth and Pigou. It could live on only furtively, below the surface, with Marx, Gesell and Douglas (para. 23).
- **Why it won.** Keynes calls the victory something of a curiosity and a mystery and says it must have been due to a complex of suitabilities of the doctrine to its environment. He lists features of the doctrine, hedging the first with "I suppose": its conclusions differed from lay expectation; its teaching was austere; it carried a consistent logical structure; it excused social injustice as part of progress; and it justified the free activity of capitalists, so authority favoured it (para. 24).
- **Cost to economists' prestige.** Its failure at prediction has damaged economists' standing, since the ordinary man sees the mismatch with the facts (para. 25).
- **Optimism.** The "Candide" optimism of economists is traced, "I think", to neglect of the drag from insufficient effective demand. The classical theory may describe how we would like the economy to behave, but to assume it does is "to assume our difficulties away" (para. 26).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume $\phi(N)=10N$ (supply price in £, $N$ workers), so income at employment $N$ is $10N$. Assume the propensity to consume is $c(N)=6N$ (60% of income). Let investment be $D_2=200$, and let full employment be $N_f=80$.
- Equilibrium: $\phi(N)-c(N)=D_2$ gives $10N-6N=200$, so $N=50$.
- Check $N=50$: $D=6\cdot50+200=500=Z$.
- $N=40$: $Z=400$, $D=240+200=440>Z$, so employers expand.
- $N=60$: $Z=600$, $D=360+200=560<Z$, so they contract.
- Full employment needs $D_2=4\cdot80=320$. With only $200$, 30 workers stay unemployed in a stable equilibrium.
- Classical Say's law would need $D_2=4N$ to rise automatically with $N$.

## Concepts
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — value of $D$ where aggregate demand meets aggregate supply; fixes employment (para. 4)
- [[aggregate-supply-function]] (new) — expected proceeds that just justify employing $N$ men (paras. 1, 3)
- [[aggregate-demand-function]] (new) — proceeds entrepreneurs expect from employing $N$ men (para. 3)
- [[says-law]] (new) — classical claim $D=Z$ at all $N$; equivalent to no obstacle to full employment (paras. 5–6)
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — psychological characteristic fixing how expected consumption depends on income; Keynes holds that consumption rises with income, but by less (paras. 8, 11, 17)
- [[inducement-to-invest]] (new) — what the amount of current investment depends on; the inducement itself is said to depend on the relation between the marginal efficiency of capital schedule and interest rates (para. 8)
- [[equilibrium]] — employment level with no inducement to expand or contract; may be below full employment (paras. 8, 17)
- [[full-employment]] (new) — employment is inelastic to more effective demand; equivalent to the earlier definition via labour behaviour (para. 6)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Stable equilibrium below full employment ↔ game theory: a stable but inefficient outcome (unlike multiple-equilibrium games, Keynes finds one equilibrium for a given propensity and investment, para. 9).
- [structural-analogy] Saving exceeding investment forcing output down ↔ balance-sheet recession: weak investment demand shrinks output when saving stays high.
- [metaphor] Classical neutral equilibrium at every $N$ ↔ a ball on a flat table: no force pins it down until it hits the boundary.
