# Sundry Observations on the Nature of Capital (Keynes, ch. 16)

## L0 — One line
Saving cancels a present order for consumption without placing any specific future order, so it does not by itself raise investment. Capital earns a yield because it is scarce, and if accumulating capital pushes its expected yield down faster than the interest rate can fall, leaving the economy to itself (laissez-faire) can leave a society with low employment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks why individual saving does not automatically become investment, and what accumulating capital does to a society. Saving withdraws present demand. It adds no expected future demand, so the expected yield of new investment does not rise. Capital's return reflects scarcity, kept up by the interest rate on money, not physical productivity. If capital becomes abundant while the interest rate cannot fall below a floor, an economy left to itself (laissez-faire) may settle at low employment. Keynes guesses that, with the interest rate and investment managed, capital's marginal efficiency (its expected yield relative to cost) could fall to about zero within a generation. The rentier (the person living on interest) would then disappear.

**Prerequisites:** the interest rate as the price of holding money against other assets; investment as spending on new capital goods; the idea of an expected return on an asset.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Saving is a decision not to consume now, not an order for specific later consumption. It is a net fall in consumption demand, and it may also lower the expected yield of existing capital (§I ¶1).
- The "fallacy" is that a greater wish to hold wealth is much the same as a greater wish to hold investments, and so stimulates their production. An owner wants prospective yield, and yield depends on expected demand for a specific article at a specific date (§I ¶3–4).
- A saver can acquire wealth when someone transfers to him an article of wealth, old or new, so saving does not need a new capital asset to be created (§I ¶4).
- The yield of new investment cannot fall below the interest rate, because money and debts are the alternative. The rate depends on desires to hold wealth in liquid versus illiquid form and on their relative supplies, not on the strength of the desire to hold wealth (§I ¶5).
- Capital's excess yield comes from scarcity, not physical productivity. Keynes prefers to treat labour as the sole factor of production, and long processes are not efficient merely because they are long (§II ¶1–3).
- In a society so well equipped with capital that its marginal efficiency is zero, where interest cannot go negative and people would still save at full employment, laissez-faire equilibrium has employment so low that aggregate saving is zero (§III ¶1–2).
- Keynes thinks the practicable floor for the long-term rate may perhaps be as high as 2 or 2½ per cent. He cites Britain and the US after the war as examples of the problem (§III ¶4–5).
- With managed interest and investment, Keynes guesses that in a properly run community with a slowly growing population, capital's marginal efficiency could reach about zero within a generation, so the rentier would disappear while skilled risk-taking remains (§IV ¶2–4).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Why saving does not itself stimulate investment
- **Saving as cancellation.** Saving means, so to speak, "a decision not to have dinner to-day". It does not order any specified consumption at any specified date. So it lowers today's consumption business without raising any future consumption business. It is "a net diminution" of demand, not a substitution of future for present demand (§I ¶1).
- **Spill-over to investment.** Expectations of future consumption rest largely on present consumption. Saving may therefore not only lower consumption-goods prices but may also tend to lower the marginal efficiency of existing capital (expected yield relative to cost). In that event it may reduce present investment demand as well (§I ¶1).
- **If saving included a forward order.** The effect might then differ, since expected yield would improve. Even so, the resources released would not necessarily be re-employed at once. The wanted delay might need a method so "roundabout" (indirect and long) that its efficiency is below the current interest rate. Then the immediate effect on employment would still be adverse (§I ¶2).
- **Actual saving** only cancels a present order. So a lower propensity to consume depressing employment, other things equal, is not paradoxical, since expected consumption is the only reason for employment (§I ¶2).
- **Opposing view, which Keynes calls a fallacy.** A greater desire to hold wealth is much the same as a greater desire to hold investments. It therefore raises demand for investments and stimulates their production, so investment rises as much as consumption falls (§I ¶3).
- **Keynes's reply, first step.** The owner of wealth desires an asset's prospective yield, not the asset. Yield depends wholly on expected future effective demand relative to future supply conditions. If saving does not improve yield, it does not stimulate investment (§I ¶4).
- **Keynes's reply, second step.** A saver need not be supplied with a new asset. Saving is two-sided and forces someone else to transfer some article of wealth, old or new, to the saver. Such transfers need not create new wealth and may even work against it (§I ¶4). New wealth is created only if its prospective yield reaches the standard set by the current interest rate. That yield depends on expected demand for a specific article at a specific date, not on anyone's wish for more wealth (§I ¶4).
- **Second objection (owner wants the best available yield).** On this view more desire for wealth would push down the yield producers must accept. **Keynes replies:** money and debts are always an alternative to real capital. So that yield cannot fall below the standard set by the current interest rate (§I ¶5). The rate depends on the desires to hold wealth in liquid versus illiquid form, together with their relative supplies. It does not depend on the strength of the desire to hold wealth (§I ¶5).
- **Keynes's test question (rhetorical).** With the money supply unchanged, why should a fresh act of saving reduce the sum people wish to hold in liquid form at the existing rate? The question suggests that it should not (§I ¶5). Deeper puzzles are deferred to the next chapter (§I ¶6).

*Intuition note.* A firm builds a machine only if it expects buyers for the output at a given date. Saving gives it no such order, and may even lower its expectations. It means someone stopped buying dinner today.

### II. Capital's yield is scarcity, not productivity
- It is better to say capital yields more over its life than its cost than to call it "productive". The excess exists because capital is scarce, and it is kept scarce by competition from the interest rate on money. If capital grows less scarce, the excess yield falls without capital becoming physically less productive (§II ¶1).
- Keynes sympathises with the pre-classical view. Labour is the sole factor of production, working in a given setting of technique, natural resources, capital equipment and effective demand. This partly explains why Keynes could take the unit of labour as the only physical unit needed, besides money and time (§II ¶2).
- **Roundabout processes.** Some lengthy processes are physically efficient, and so are some short ones. Length itself is not the source of efficiency. Some, probably most, lengthy processes would be physically very inefficient because of spoiling and waste with time. With a given labour force there is a limit to the roundabout labour that can be used to advantage, and, apart from other considerations, machine-making and machine-using labour must be in due proportion (§II ¶3).
- **When length alone pays.** Only if the wish to postpone consumption were so strong that full employment needed investment so large as to give a negative marginal efficiency of capital would a process pay merely for being lengthy. A correct theory must cover both positive and negative interest rates. Keynes thinks only the scarcity theory can do this (§II ¶3).
- **Analogy.** Smelly or risky work is paid more because it is scarce, and, Keynes says, we do not devise a productivity theory of smelly or risky processes as such. If lapse of time becomes an agreeable circumstance, then short processes must be kept scarce (§II ¶4).
- **Optimum roundaboutness.** Production should be organised to be as efficient as possible while delivering at the dates when consumers' demand is expected to be effective. Example: if, after hearing full particulars of the meals possible at different hours, the consumer is expected to choose 8 o'clock for dinner, the cook should make the best dinner for 8 o'clock. The cook should do so even if a different hour would give a better dinner when time counted for nothing (§II ¶5).
- **Role of the interest rate.**
  - At a zero rate there is an optimum interval between input and consumption that minimises labour cost. Shorter is technically less efficient, and longer brings storage cost and deterioration (§II ¶6).
  - A positive rate adds a cost that grows with process length, so the optimum interval shortens. A negative rate (if possible) reverses this (§II ¶6).
  - Even at zero there is a strict limit to the share of prospective consumer demand it pays to provide for in advance. As the rate rises, that share shrinks in step (pari passu) (§II ¶6).

*Intuition note.* Capital earns more than its cost because there is not too much of it, not because machines "create" value. If it became plentiful, its yield would fall even though it had not become less productive in the physical sense.

### III. A society so well equipped with capital that its marginal efficiency is zero
- **Assumptions.** Keynes recalls that capital must be kept scarce enough in the long period that its marginal efficiency at least equals the interest rate over its life. Take a society whose marginal efficiency is zero and would be negative with more investment. Suppose its money will "keep" at negligible storage cost, so that in practice interest cannot be negative. Suppose its people would still save at full employment (§III ¶1).
- **Result under laissez-faire.** Starting from full employment, entrepreneurs make losses if they use all the capital stock. Capital and employment must shrink until the community is so poor that aggregate saving is zero. Equilibrium then has low employment and a miserable standard of life (§III ¶2).
- **Cycles.** More probably there is a cyclical movement around this position, because uncertainty lets marginal efficiency sometimes rise above zero (boom) and then overshoot downward (slump). With correct foresight the equilibrium capital stock is smaller than full employment would need (§III ¶2).
- **The only other equilibrium.** Wealth at zero marginal efficiency would also have to satisfy people's entire desire to provide for the future at full employment, with no interest bonus. Keynes calls it an unlikely coincidence. If satiation does come, it will probably come earlier, while the rate is still falling (§III ¶3).
- **Floors above zero.** Institutional and psychological factors set a floor "much above zero". These include the costs of matching borrowers and lenders and uncertainty about the future rate. In present circumstances it may perhaps be as high as 2 or 2½ per cent on long-term rates. If this proves correct, the awkward possibilities may soon be realised. A higher floor also makes satiation before the floor is reached less likely (§III ¶4).
- **Evidence and corollary.** Keynes treats post-war Britain and the United States as examples. He says these are actual examples of how an accumulation of wealth, so large that its marginal efficiency has fallen faster than the interest rate can fall, can interfere with a reasonable level of employment and the standard of life under mainly laissez-faire conditions (§III ¶5). Of two equal communities with the same technique, the one with less capital may for a time live better. When the poorer one catches up (as, presumably, it eventually will), both "suffer the fate of Midas". This holds only if consumption and investment are mainly left to laissez-faire (§III ¶6).
- **Wasteful mitigations.** If, for whatever reason, the interest rate cannot fall as fast as marginal efficiency would with the rate of accumulation the community would choose to save at full employment, then even diverting the desire to hold wealth towards assets that yield no economic fruit increases economic well-being. Keynes's examples are millionaires' mansions, pyramids, cathedrals and monasteries, which may postpone the day when abundance of capital interferes with abundance of output. "To dig holes in the ground", paid for out of savings, would raise both employment and the real national dividend (output of useful goods and services). A sensible community should not rely on such fortuitous, wasteful remedies once it understands effective demand (§III ¶7).

*Intuition note.* If capital is so plentiful that its return is zero and extra investment would lose money, and interest cannot go below zero, full employment cannot be kept up. Capital and employment shrink until aggregate saving is zero, with some people's saving offset by others' dis-saving.

### IV. A managed path toward abundant capital
- **Assumptions.** The interest rate is made consistent with the rate of investment for full employment. State action balances the growth of capital so that saturation is approached without too heavy a burden on the present generation (§IV ¶1).
- **Keynes's guess.** On these assumptions a properly run community with modern technique and slowly growing population could bring the marginal efficiency of capital to about zero within one generation. It would become quasi-stationary, with change coming only from technique, taste, population and institutions. Capital goods would then be priced in proportion to the labour embodied in them (§IV ¶2).
- **Social consequence.** If this is as easy as Keynes supposes, it may be the most sensible way to remove many objectionable features of capitalism. A person could still save earned income but it would not grow. Keynes compares this to Pope's father, who kept a chest of guineas at Twickenham and drew on it (§IV ¶3).
- **Enterprise remains.** The rentier would go, but skill in judging uncertain yields would still have a place. The argument concerns the pure rate of interest, not gross yields including a reward for risk. Unless the pure rate were negative, skilled investment in doubtful assets would still earn a positive yield. With some measurable unwillingness to bear risk, the aggregate net yield would also be positive. But it is "not unlikely" that eagerness for doubtful investments could push the aggregate net yield negative (§IV ¶4).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town spends 100 per week on dinners, and cooks hire 100 of labour to make them. One household saves 20 and holds it as money, so dinner spending is 80.
- Cooks' orders fall by 20, so labour hired falls from 100 to 80.
- No cook has received an order for a dinner next month. Expected future demand is unchanged, or lower if people extrapolate from current dinners.
- A builder weighing a new kitchen sees no higher expected yield, so investment does not rise. Employment is down by 20 and nothing offsets it.
- The 20 is held as money that already existed, so the saver gains wealth without any new asset being built.

## Concepts
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — expected demand that gives entrepreneurs reason to employ labour. Keynes says expected consumption is the only reason for employment (§I ¶2).
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — expected yield of new investment relative to its cost. Investment is worth doing only if it reaches the interest rate (§I ¶4).
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — the tendency to spend income on consumption. A lower propensity depresses employment, other things equal (§I ¶2).
- [[scarcity-theory-of-capital-yield]] (new) — capital yields more than its cost because it is kept scarce by the interest rate on money, not because it is physically productive (§II ¶1).
- [[equilibrium]] — here, in the capital-saturated society Keynes describes, a position where aggregate saving is zero and employment is low (§III ¶2).
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — the strength of desires to hold wealth in liquid versus illiquid form. Keynes says this, with relative supplies, sets the interest rate (§I ¶5).
- [[paradox-of-thrift]] (new) — saving fails to create matching investment and can lower employment. Keynes calls the opposite belief a "fallacy" (§I ¶3), and says there is "nothing paradoxical" in the depressing effect on employment (§I ¶2).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Saving as withdrawn demand with no future order ↔ economics-finance: coordination failure, where individually sensible acts need not give a good aggregate result — individual choices fail to coordinate.
- [structural-analogy] Interest as a floor on investment yield, because money and debts are the alternative ↔ economics-finance: no-arbitrage — assets must offer at least the return of the riskless alternative.
- [metaphor] Zero-saving equilibrium reached by shrinking employment ↔ systems: adjustment through quantity (output) rather than price (interest).

## Questions this raises
- Keynes says the interest rate depends on liquid versus illiquid preferences and relative supplies (§I ¶5). What lies behind those preferences? He defers "certain deeper perplexities" to the next chapter (§I ¶6).
- How would the zero-saving equilibrium change if the interest rate could go negative, or if the floor were lower than 2–2½ per cent (§III ¶4)?
- How would the State "balance" capital growth without burdening the present generation (§IV ¶1)?
- If doubtful investments can show negative aggregate net yield, who would then bear risk (§IV ¶4)?
