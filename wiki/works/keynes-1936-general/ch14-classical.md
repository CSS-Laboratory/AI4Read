# The Classical Theory of the Rate of Interest

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that the classical theory, in which interest equates saving and investment, fails because, in general, shifting the schedules changes income; saving and investment are results of the system, not its determinants.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks what the classical theory of the rate of interest says and where it goes wrong. He reads it as: the rate of interest equates investment demand and saving, as a price equates demand and supply. He objects that if either schedule shifts, income in general changes, so the schedules, drawn for a given income, cannot fix the rate. Saving and investment are results, not determinants. If spending and investment determine employment, not the rate, then less spending, other things equal, reduces employment. An appendix discusses Marshall, Pigou, Ricardo and von Mises's followers.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand curves; saving = income minus consumption; the idea of a schedule (a curve relating two variables).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Keynes reconstructs the classical view: the rate of interest is the price that equates investment demand with saving (para. 2). Keynes says ordinary men and trained economists have also taken away the idea that saving lowers the rate and so stimulates investment automatically, with no monetary authority needed (para. 4).
- Keynes says the classical school would accept, and he does not dispute, that if income is assumed given, the rate lies where the demand curve for capital (his investment demand schedule) cuts the curve of saving out of that income (para. 6).
- Error: the classical theory lets the two curves shift while holding income fixed. Keynes calls this "a nonsense theory", because a shift of either curve in general changes income (para. 8).
- A diagram shows that the two curves alone give no rate of interest. They give income only if the rate is known from another source; Keynes's example is liquidity-preference (a term not defined in this chapter) together with the quantity of money (para. 9–10).
- Keynes holds that interest is the reward for not-hoarding (not defined here; the text links it to money as a store of value), not for waiting as such. Two familiar points might perhaps have warned the classical school (paras. 11–14).
- Keynes says saving and investment are determinates (results), not determinants (independent variables). The determinants are the propensity to consume (an earlier-chapter term, not defined here), the marginal efficiency of capital schedule (his investment demand schedule), and the rate of interest. Deriving the rate from the marginal efficiency of capital is circular (paras. 15–16).
- Practical stake: if spending and investment determine employment, not the rate, then less spending, other things equal, lowers employment (para. 17).
- Appendix: Keynes comments on Marshall and Pigou. He says Ricardo's doctrine is valid on stated suppositions (notably always-full employment), while Ricardo and his successors overlook that employment need not be full. He says von Mises and his disciples confuse the marginal efficiency of capital with the rate of interest (App. I–III).

## L3 — Walkthrough
Paragraph numbers are mine (see Reading notes).

### Chapter, section I — the classical theory stated (paras. 1–4)
- Keynes finds it hard to state the classical theory precisely or to find an explicit account of it in leading modern classical treatises (para. 1).
- Fairly clear: the tradition treats interest as what brings the demand for investment and the willingness to save into equilibrium. Investment is the demand for investible resources, saving is the supply, and the rate of interest is the price that equates them, as a commodity price equates demand and supply (para. 2).
- Marshall does not state this in these exact words, but Keynes says his theory seems to be this, and it is what Keynes himself taught (para. 3). Evidence Keynes cites (para. 3):
  - Marshall: interest tends to a level where aggregate demand for capital equals the aggregate stock forthcoming.
  - Cassel: investment is the "demand for waiting" and saving the "supply of waiting"; Keynes found no actual words to quote.
  - Carver: interest equilibrates the marginal disutility of waiting with the marginal productivity of capital.
  - Flux: an automatic adjustment between saving and opportunities for employing capital.
  - Taussig: supply and demand curves of saving.
  - Walras: Keynes says he argues expressly that for each rate there are a sum saved and a sum invested, that the rate brings them to equality, and that he is therefore firmly in the classical tradition.
- The ordinary man and the trained economist have taken away the idea that an act of saving automatically lowers the rate. They also hold that the fall automatically stimulates capital output by just the amount of the saving, and that the process is self-regulating, needing no "grandmotherly care" from the monetary authority. Similarly (an even more general belief), each extra act of investment will necessarily raise the rate if it is not offset by a change in readiness to save (para. 4).

*Intuition note.* The old picture treats money for investment like any commodity: saving shifts supply, and the "price" adjusts. Keynes's chapter argues that this account is erroneous: the two curves alone do not fix the rate.

### Points of agreement (paras. 5–6)
- The analysis of the previous chapters, Keynes says, makes plain that this account must be erroneous. He starts from what is agreed (para. 5).
- The classical school proper holds that saving and investment are equal, unlike the neo-classical school. Keynes thinks Marshall surely believed this, although Marshall did not expressly say so. Most classical writers carried the belief much too far, holding that each individual act of saving brings a corresponding act of investment (para. 6).
- Keynes sees no material difference, relevant in this context, between his schedule of the marginal efficiency of capital (MEC; his investment demand schedule) and the demand curve for capital of some classical writers (para. 6).
- On saving, they stress the influence of the rate of interest. Keynes says they presumably would not deny that income matters. He for his part would not deny that the rate may perhaps influence saving out of a given income, though perhaps not as they suppose (para. 6).
- Proposition that the classical school would accept and Keynes does not dispute: if income is assumed given, the current rate must lie where the demand curve for capital cuts the curve of amounts saved out of that income (para. 6).

### The error (paras. 7–8)
- No quarrel if the classical school only inferred that, given the two curves, income and the rate are uniquely correlated. A further true proposition: if the rate is given, income must be the factor that brings saving equal to investment (para. 7).
- But the classical theory neglects changes in income and "involves formal error" (para. 7).
- It assumes that when the demand curve for capital shifts, the new rate is the intersection of the new curve with the saving-out-of-given-income curve. It keeps the income assumption fixed (para. 8).
- Keynes: this is a nonsense theory. Constant income is inconsistent with the two curves shifting independently. If either shifts, then in general income will change, and the given-income schematism breaks down (para. 8).
- The position could be saved only by a complicated assumption: an automatic change in the wage-unit, through its effect on liquidity-preference. The change would have to produce a rate that offsets the shift and leaves output unchanged (para. 8).
  - The writers quoted give no hint of such an assumption.
  - At best it would be plausible only for long-period equilibrium, not a short-period theory. Keynes sees no ground for it even in the long period.
- The classical theory was not alive to income possibly being a function of the rate of investment (para. 8).

### The diagram (paras. 9–10)
- Axes: investment (or saving) $I$ vertically, rate of interest $r$ horizontally. $X_1X_1'$ and $X_2X_2'$ are two positions of the investment demand schedule. $Y_1, Y_2, Y_3,\dots$ are curves of saving out of incomes $Y_1, Y_2, Y_3,\dots$ against $r$ (para. 9).
- Start with $Y_1$ consistent with $X_1X_1'$ and rate $r_1$. If the investment schedule shifts to $X_2X_2'$, income in general shifts too. The diagram lacks the data to say what the new income is, so we do not know which $Y$-curve applies (para. 9).
- If liquidity-preference and the quantity of money say the rate is $r_2$, the position is determinate. The $Y$-curve cutting $X_2X_2'$ vertically above $r_2$, namely $Y_2$, is the right one (para. 9).
- So the $X$- and $Y$-curves tell nothing about the rate. They tell what income will be, if the rate comes from some other source. If liquidity-preference and the quantity of money are unchanged, the rate is unchanged. Then the curve $Y_2'$ that cuts the new schedule vertically below where $Y_1$ cut the old one gives the new income (para. 9).
- Conclusion: these functions furnish no theory of the rate. They can tell us income, given the rate. They can also tell us what the rate must be if income is to be held at a given figure, e.g. full employment (para. 10).

*Intuition note.* The curves are drawn for a given income, but the diagram does not tell us what income is. So they cannot also tell us the rate. The rate has to be known from another source; Keynes's example is liquidity-preference together with the quantity of money (para. 9).

### The source of the mistake (para. 11)
- Keynes: the mistake is to see interest as the reward for waiting as such, instead of for not-hoarding. Returns on riskier loans are rightly seen as the reward for running risk, not waiting.
- There is no sharp line between these and the "pure" rate; all reward the running of the risk of uncertainty.
- Only if money were used solely for transactions and never as a store of value would a different theory become appropriate.

### Two familiar warning points (paras. 12–14)
- Keynes says two familiar points might perhaps have warned the classical school that something was wrong (para. 12).
- First: since Cassel it has been agreed that saving out of a given income need not rise with the rate. Nobody doubts that investment demand falls as the rate rises. If both curves fall, there is no guarantee that a given $Y$-curve cuts a given $X$-curve anywhere. Keynes says this suggests that the two curves alone cannot determine the rate (para. 13).
- Second: it is usual to suppose that more money tends to lower the rate, at least at first and in the short period. No reason is given why money should affect either the investment schedule or saving out of a given income (para. 14).
  - So the classical school had one theory of interest in the volume on value and another in the volume on money. Keynes knows of no attempt to bridge them.
  - Keynes says the neo-classical attempt at a bridge has led to the worst muddles of all (para. 14).
  - The neo-classical inference: investment has two sources of supply, savings proper plus money newly made available, balanced by a levy on the public called "forced saving".
  - This leads to a "natural" or "neutral" rate, one equating investment to savings proper without forced saving.
  - It finally leads to what, assuming those writers are on the right track at the start, is the most obvious solution: that holding the quantity of money constant would remove the supposed evils. At this point Keynes says the argument is in deep water (para. 14).

### Determinants and determinates (paras. 15–16)
- The traditional analysis failed to isolate the independent variables. Saving and investment are the determinates (results), not the determinants (para. 15).
- The determinants are the propensity to consume, the MEC schedule, and the rate of interest. Each can be affected by prospective changes in the others, but their values cannot be inferred from one another (para. 15).
- The tradition knew saving depends on income. It overlooked that income depends on investment, so that when investment changes, income changes by just the degree needed to make the change in saving equal to the change in investment (para. 15).
- Theories that make the rate depend on the MEC fare no better. In equilibrium the rate equals the MEC, because investment is pushed until they are equal. But deriving the rate from this is circular, as Marshall found half-way through such an account. The MEC partly depends on the scale of current investment, and that scale cannot be known until the rate is known (para. 16).
- Conclusion: the MEC schedule tells where investment will be pushed, given the rate, not what the rate is (para. 16).

*Intuition note.* Saving and investment are treated as equal in the classical view that Keynes takes as a starting point (para. 6). The question is what brings them there. Keynes's answer is that income changes by whatever amount is needed, so they are outcomes, not drivers (para. 15).

### Practical significance (para. 17)
- Keynes says the economic principle behind economists' advice has almost invariably, in effect, assumed that, ceteris paribus (other things equal), a decrease in spending will tend to lower the rate and an increase in investment to raise it (para. 17).
- Keynes: if these two quantities determine, not the rate of interest, but aggregate employment, our outlook changes profoundly. Less readiness to spend is then seen as, other things equal, diminishing employment, instead of as a factor that increases investment (para. 17).

### Appendix I — Marshall, Pigou
- Keynes says Marshall, Edgeworth and Pigou offer no consecutive discussion of the rate, only a few obiter dicta (passing remarks) (App. I, para. 1).
- Marshall (*Principles*, quoted): for a small market, extra demand for capital is met by supply from surrounding areas. For a whole large country, we cannot regard supply as altered quickly and to a considerable extent by a change in the rate. The capital stock is the product of labour and waiting, and the extra work and waiting that a higher rate would induce would not quickly amount to much beside the existing stock. An extensive rise in demand is therefore met for a time not so much by more supply as by a rise in the rate, which makes capital withdraw partially from uses where its marginal utility is lowest. Only slowly and gradually does the rise in the rate increase the stock (App. I, para. 2).
- Marshall (quoted): the phrase for the rate of interest applies to old investments of capital only in a very limited sense. The proper statement is that net interest on new (marginal) investments is, say, about 3 per cent. The aggregate net income of all the trade capital, if capitalised at 33 years' purchase (interest at 3 per cent), would then amount to about seven thousand million pounds. Existing capital's value is the discounted value of its estimated future net incomes (App. I, para. 3).
- Pigou, *Economics of Welfare* (quoted): "waiting" is postponing consumption, letting resources take the form of production instruments. Its unit is a year-value-unit. Capital accumulated in a year is not necessarily equal to savings, because some savings are misdirected into wasteful uses (App. I, para. 4).
- Pigou, *Industrial Fluctuations* (quoted): bankers who create more credit make a "forced levy" of real things from the public. This lowers the real rate on long and short loans. The bankers' rate is tied mechanically to the real rate on long loans, but the real rate is not determined wholly outside bankers' control. Keynes thinks this is Pigou's only significant reference to the determination of the rate (App. I, para. 5).
- Keynes's comments:
  - His perplexity over Marshall is due, he thinks, to bringing in "interest", a concept of a monetary economy, in a treatise that ignores money. He says interest has really no business to turn up there, since it belongs to another branch of the subject (App. I, para. 6).
  - Keynes says that, in the *Economics of Welfare*, Pigou leads us to infer that the unit of waiting is the same as the unit of current investment and that the reward of waiting is quasi-rent, and that Pigou practically never mentions interest, which Keynes says is as it should be.
  - Yet these writers presume money and banking. The rate scarcely plays a larger part in Pigou's *Industrial Fluctuations* and *Theory of Unemployment* than in the *Economics of Welfare* (App. I, para. 7).

### Appendix II — Ricardo
- Ricardo (quoted): the interest of money is regulated not by the rate at which the Bank lends but by the rate of profit that can be made by employing capital, and that profit rate is totally independent of the quantity or value of money. A Bank lending one million or a hundred millions would not permanently alter the market rate, only the value of money. A bank charging less than the market rate could lend without limit (App. II, para. 1).
- Keynes: this is clear-cut and a better starting-point than later, hazier writers. It is a long-period doctrine, with the stress on "permanently" (App. II, para. 2).
- Assumption required: the usual classical one that there is always full employment, so that, assuming no change in the supply curve of labour in terms of product, there is only one possible level of employment in long-period equilibrium. With the usual ceteris paribus (no change in psychological propensities and expectations other than those arising from a change in the quantity of money) the Ricardian theory is valid, in that sense, on these suppositions, since only one rate is compatible with full employment in the long period (App. II, para. 3).
- Ricardo and successors overlook that, even in the long period, employment is not necessarily full. Each banking policy then has its own long-period employment level, so several long-period equilibria exist (App. II, para. 3).
- Had Ricardo argued only that a given quantity of money (ten millions or a hundred millions) makes no permanent difference, his conclusion would hold with flexible money-wages (App. II, para. 4).
- But Ricardo expressly means the terms on which the authority changes the quantity of money, i.e. the rate of interest at which it increases or decreases its assets (by discounts or open-market operations). Then policy is not nugatory (without effect) and several policies are compatible with long-period equilibrium (App. II, para. 4).
  - In the extreme case where money-wages fall without limit under involuntary unemployment, there are two long-period positions: full employment, and the employment level at which liquidity-preference becomes absolute, if that is less than full employment.
  - With flexible wages the quantity of money as such is nugatory in the long period. The terms on which it is changed enter as a real determinant.
- Ricardo's closing sentences suggest he overlooked changes in the MEC with the amount invested. This can be read as consistency: if employment and psychological propensities are given, only one rate of accumulation and one MEC are possible (App. II, para. 5).
- Keynes's contrast: Ricardo lives consistently in a hypothetical world remote from experience, while with most successors common sense breaks in, with injury to consistency (App. II, para. 5).

### Appendix III — von Mises, Hayek, Robbins, Hansen
- Keynes reports a "peculiar theory" propounded by von Mises, adopted by Hayek and, Keynes thinks, also by Robbins. It identifies changes in the rate of interest with changes in the relative price levels of consumption-goods and capital-goods (App. III, para. 1).
- Keynes says it is not clear how they reach this. His reconstruction: the MEC is taken, by drastic simplification, as the ratio of the supply price of new consumer goods to that of new producer goods. That ratio is identified with the rate. A lower rate favours investment, "ergo" a lower ratio does (App. III, paras. 1–2).
- This links individual saving to investment: saving lowers consumer-goods prices, perhaps more than producer-goods prices, which is read as a lower rate that stimulates investment (App. III, para. 2).
- Keynes's objection: a lower MEC of particular assets, and so of the schedule in general, has exactly the opposite effect to what the von Mises argument assumes. Investment is stimulated by raising the MEC schedule or by lowering the rate. In Keynes's view, von Mises and his disciples have confused the two and so have got their conclusions exactly the wrong way round (App. III, para. 2).
- Hansen, quoted by Keynes as an example of the confusion: **Hansen's view:** the claim of "some economists" that reduced spending lowers consumer prices and so dampens investment is incorrect. Lower consumer prices relative to producer prices, Hansen says, in effect mean a lower rate of interest, which stimulates investment (App. III, para. 3).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show that with the rate set elsewhere, income adjusts to make saving equal investment.

Assumptions: saving is $S = 0.25\,Y$ and does not depend on $r$. Investment demand is $I = 140 - 10r$ (old schedule) or $I = 160 - 10r$ (new schedule).

- Rate fixed at $r = 4$ by something outside the two curves. Old schedule: $I = 140 - 40 = 100$. Then $0.25Y = 100$, so $Y = 400$.
- New schedule at $r = 4$: $I = 160 - 40 = 120$. Then $0.25Y = 120$, so $Y = 480$. Income rises by 80, and saving rises from 100 to 120, equal to the rise in investment (matches para. 15).
- Classical reading, holding $Y = 400$ so that $S = 100$: set $160 - 10r = 100$, so $r = 6$. The two answers (income 480 at $r = 4$ versus rate 6 at income 400) differ. Keynes's point is that the classical answer holds income fixed illegitimately (para. 8).

## Concepts
- [[classical-saving-investment-theory]] (new) — Keynes's reconstruction: the rate of interest is the price equating investment demand and saving (para. 2). Some writers, e.g. Walras, state it expressly (para. 3).
- [[equilibrium]] — in this theory the rate "comes to rest" where investment equals saving at that rate (para. 2). Keynes accepts that the rate lies at the intersection if income is assumed given (para. 6).
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — Keynes's schedule of investment demand. It partly depends on the scale of current investment (paras. 6, 16).
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — with the quantity of money, it is Keynes's example of what could tell us the rate in his diagram. Not defined in this unit (para. 9).
- [[determinants-vs-determinates]] (new) — determinants are the independent variables (propensity to consume, MEC schedule, rate). Determinates are the results: saving and investment (para. 15).
- [[interest-as-reward-for-not-hoarding]] (new) — Keynes's alternative to "reward for waiting". "Hoarding" is not defined here. The text links it to money as a store of value (para. 11).
- [[forced-saving]] (new) — in the neo-classical account, a levy on the public that balances newly available money (para. 14).
- [[natural-rate-of-interest]] (new) — neo-classical "natural"/"neutral" rate: investment equals savings proper (para. 14).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Rate not fixed by the $X$ and $Y$ curves ↔ econometrics: a system with too few equations for its unknowns is underdetermined.
- [structural-analogy] Von Mises's confusion of the MEC and the rate ↔ microeconomics: a shift of a curve versus a movement along it.
- [metaphor] Determinates versus determinants ↔ modelling: endogenous outcomes versus exogenous drivers.

## Questions this raises
- Para. 9 supposes that liquidity-preference and the quantity of money tell us the rate. How are they supposed to do this?
- Para. 8 says the classical result needs a wage-unit adjustment that offsets any shift. Is this ever plausible even in the long period?
- In Appendix II, how do the results depend on whether money-wages are flexible?
- Does Keynes's reading of the von Mises argument match what von Mises wrote? Keynes himself says it is "not clear" how that conclusion is reached.
