# Notes on the Trade Cycle

## L0 — One line
Keynes suggests that the trade cycle is mainly a cyclical swing in the marginal efficiency of capital (the expected return on new investment), so a higher interest rate is the wrong remedy for the boom, and a lower rate by itself is usually not enough to cure the slump.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks whether his theory of employment can explain the trade cycle. He suggests the cycle is mainly a cyclical swing in the marginal efficiency of capital (MEC), complicated and aggravated by changes in liquidity-preference (demand for money) and the propensity to consume (share of income spent). The typical crisis is a sudden collapse of MEC, not primarily a rise in the interest rate. The slump tends to last roughly three to five years, tied to the life of durable assets and the carrying costs of surplus stocks. He rejects a higher rate as the boom cure and favours abolishing slumps through socially controlled investment and a higher propensity to consume. So he looks beyond monetary tuning to controlling investment.

**Prerequisites:** the General Theory's basic variables (investment, propensity to consume, liquidity-preference, marginal efficiency of capital, rate of interest) and the idea that employment follows output.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Keynes suggests the cycle's regularity is mainly due to how MEC fluctuates; other variables complicate and often aggravate it (intro). The crisis often comes suddenly and violently; the upturn as a rule has no sharp turning point (§ I).
- Keynes suggests the more typical, often predominant, explanation of the crisis is a sudden collapse of MEC, not primarily a rise in the interest rate. Higher liquidity-preference follows and may seriously aggravate it (§ II, paras. 3–4).
- The slump is intractable because the MEC collapse may be too deep for any practicable rate cut to fix, and the return of confidence is hard to control. The slump's length (Keynes's rough figure: three to five years) is tied to the life of durable assets and to the carrying costs of surplus stocks (§ II, paras. 5–8).
- A serious fall in MEC tends to cut stock-market values and so the propensity to consume. Keynes concludes that ordering investment "cannot safely be left in private hands" (§ II, paras. 9–10).
- On Keynes's analysis the boom has "over-investment" only in the sense of investment resting on expectations destined to disappoint, not capital so abundant that more is wasteful. So he says the remedy for the boom is a lower rate of interest, not a higher one (§ III).
- Keynes says under-consumption schools are right as practical guides in existing conditions (or at least those until lately). He thinks the wisest course is to advance on both fronts, investment and consumption (§ IV). He thinks work-sharing premature (§ V).
- Keynes thinks Robertson's high-rate policy arguable, though hard to judge even on its own assumptions, and in his view defeatist. He says the other arguments for a high rate rest on confusion of mind (§ VI).
- Keynes finds agricultural stock changes (Jevons) and inventory changes important for investment fluctuation, and attributes the slow recovery from a slump mainly to reducing redundant stocks (§ VII).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction
- Keynes claims the preceding chapters show what determines the volume of employment; if he is right, the theory must be able to explain the trade cycle (intro, para. 1).
- Any real cycle is complex. Fluctuations in the propensity to consume, in liquidity-preference and in MEC all play a part. Keynes suggests the essential character of the cycle, especially its regularity of sequence and duration, is *mainly* due to the way MEC fluctuates. The cycle is, he thinks, best regarded as occasioned by a cyclical change in MEC, complicated and often aggravated by changes in the other short-period variables (intro, para. 2).
- Developing this would need a book. He says the short notes are sufficient to indicate a line of investigation (intro, para. 2).

### I. What a cyclical movement is
- Cyclical movement: upward forces gather strength and reinforce each other, then lose strength, until they tend to be replaced by opposite forces. These in turn gather force, peak and wane (§ I, para. 1).
- Cyclical also means some recognisable regularity in time-sequence and duration (§ I, para. 1).
- The explanation must also cover the *crisis*. The switch from upward to downward is often sudden and violent. As a rule there is no such sharp turning point from downward to upward (§ I, para. 2).
- Any investment fluctuation not offset by a change in the propensity to consume changes employment. Investment has complex influences, so it is highly improbable that all fluctuations in investment or MEC are cyclical. Agricultural fluctuations are treated in § VII (§ I, para. 3).
- Keynes suggests there are definite reasons why, in a typical nineteenth-century industrial cycle, MEC fluctuations had cyclical characteristics. The reasons are familiar. His aim is to link them to the theory (§ I, para. 3).

### II. The boom's end, the crisis and the slump
- MEC depends on how abundant capital-goods are, their current cost of production, and current expectations of future yield. For durable assets expectations naturally dominate. Their basis is precarious, so they change suddenly and violently (§ II, para. 2).
- The usual explanation of the crisis stresses a rising interest rate from higher demand for money. This may aggravate and occasionally perhaps start a crisis. Keynes suggests the more typical, often predominant, explanation is a sudden collapse in MEC (§ II, para. 3).
- Late in the boom, optimism offsets growing abundance of capital-goods, rising costs and probably a rising interest rate. Organised markets contain buyers ignorant of what they buy and speculators who forecast the next shift of sentiment rather than future yield. So disillusion falls suddenly and with catastrophic force (§ II, para. 4).
- Dismay after the collapse sharply raises liquidity-preference and hence the interest rate. This may seriously aggravate the fall in investment. But "the essence" is the MEC collapse, especially for the capital types behind the earlier heavy investment. Liquidity-preference not tied to trade and speculation does not rise until after the MEC collapse (§ II, para. 4).
- This makes the slump intractable. A falling interest rate will later help recovery and is probably a necessary condition. But the MEC collapse may be so complete that no practicable rate reduction is enough. If a rate cut could work by itself, recovery might come without a long delay and by means more or less directly under the monetary authority's control. That is usually not so, because MEC is determined by the "uncontrollable and disobedient psychology of the business world" (§ II, para. 5).
- Keynes says bankers and businessmen were right to stress confidence. He says economists who trusted a "purely monetary" remedy underestimated it (§ II, para. 5).
- The time element lies in what governs recovery of MEC. Two factors give a duration that is not fortuitous and shows some regularity of habit, "let us say" three to five years (§ II, para. 6).
- **First factor: life of durable assets.** In the boom much new investment showed a not unsatisfactory current yield. Doubts arise about prospective yield, perhaps as current yield falls while the stock of new durable goods grows, or if costs are thought higher than they will be later. Doubt spreads. At the slump's start there is probably much capital with negligible or negative MEC (§ II, para. 7).
- The interval before use, decay and obsolescence create scarcity may be a somewhat stable function of average durability in the epoch. If the epoch shifts, the interval changes. Moving from rising to declining population would lengthen the phase (§ II, para. 7).
- **Second factor: carrying-costs of surplus stocks.** After the crisis, the sudden stop of new investment will probably lead to a pile-up of surplus stocks of unfinished goods. Carrying costs will seldom be below 10 per cent per annum. So the fall in their price needs to be enough to bring about absorption within, say, three to five years at the outside (§ II, para. 8).
- Absorbing stocks is negative investment and deters employment. Cutting working capital as output falls is also disinvestment and is cumulative downwards (§ II, para. 8).
- Keynes's sequence of stock and working-capital effects, which he hedges with 'probably', 'may' and 'likely' (§ II, para. 8):

| Phase | Stocks | Working capital |
|---|---|---|
| Earliest slump | investment in rising stocks (offsets) | disinvestment |
| Next | disinvestment | disinvestment |
| After the bottom | disinvestment (partly offsets) | reinvestment |
| Recovery well on | favourable | favourable |

- Once a fall in durable-goods investment starts the cycle, there is little encouragement to recover it until the cycle has partly run (§ II, para. 8).
- A serious MEC fall also lowers the propensity to consume, because stock-exchange equity values fall. Active holders, especially with borrowed funds, are depressed. They may respond more to changes in the value of their investments than to income. With a "stock-minded" public like the U.S. then, a rising stock market may be almost essential for a satisfactory propensity to consume (§ II, para. 9).
- Recovery, once started, feeds on itself. In the downward phase the MEC schedule may fall so low that no practicable rate cut corrects it. With markets as they are, MEC estimates may swing too widely for interest rates to offset, and stock-market falls may depress consumption when it is most needed (§ II, para. 10).
- Conclusion: under laissez-faire, avoiding wide employment swings "may" prove impossible without a far-reaching change in investment-market psychology, which there is no reason to expect. So "the duty of ordering the current volume of investment cannot safely be left in private hands" (§ II, para. 10).

*Intuition note.* Investment in durable assets depends on guesses about future yield. When those guesses collapse together, cheaper money may not be enough to bring confidence back. Keynes suggests the slump's length is tied to how long wear and obsolescence take to restore scarcity, and how long surplus stocks take to be absorbed.

### III. Over-investment and the interest rate
- **Opponents' view:** over-investment characterises the boom. Avoiding it is the only remedy for the slump. A low rate cannot prevent the slump, but a high rate can avoid the boom. Keynes grants force in the claim that a high rate is more effective against a boom than a low rate against a slump. Inferring the opponents' conclusion from his analysis, however, would be a serious error (§ III, para. 1).
- "Over-investment" is ambiguous (§ III, para. 2):
  - (a) investments destined to disappoint the expectations behind them, or useless under severe unemployment; or
  - (b) every kind of capital-good so abundant that no new investment is expected, even at full employment, to earn more than replacement cost over its life.
- Only (b) is over-investment strictly: more investment would be sheer waste. Even if (b) were normal in booms, the remedy would not be a high rate. That would probably deter some useful investment and might lower consumption. The remedy would be drastic steps, by redistributing incomes or otherwise, to raise the propensity to consume (§ III, para. 2).
- On Keynes's analysis the boom has over-investment only in sense (a). Investment is made in unstable conditions that cannot endure, prompted by expectations destined for disappointment (§ III, para. 3).
- Some assets are probably produced in such excess that part is waste on any criterion. This is "misdirected investment", which can also occur without a boom (§ III, para. 4).
- The essential feature of the boom is different. Investments that would really yield, say, 2 per cent at full employment are made expecting 6 per cent and are valued accordingly. After disillusion an "error of pessimism" expects less than nothing. New investment collapses, and in the resulting unemployment they do yield less than nothing. There is a shortage of houses, yet no one can afford to live in the existing ones (§ III, para. 4).
- So the remedy for the boom is a lower rate of interest, which may let the boom last. The right remedy is "abolishing slumps and thus keeping us permanently in a quasi-boom", not abolishing booms and staying in a semi-slump (§ III, para. 5).
- A boom destined to end in a slump arises when a rate that would be too high for full employment under correct expectations is combined with misguided expectations that stop it deterring. Over-optimism triumphs over a rate that, seen more coolly, would be excessive (§ III, para. 6).
- **U.S. 1928–29** (§ III, para. 7):
  - Keynes doubts that we have any recent experience, except during the war, of a boom so strong that it led to full employment.
  - Employment was satisfactory on normal standards, but he saw no evidence of labour shortage except among a few specialised groups. There were bottle-necks, but output could expand further.
  - There was no strict over-investment in housing, transport, public services or agricultural improvement. "Quite the contrary."
  - Investment over the previous five years had been so large that the prospective yield of additions was, coolly considered, falling rapidly.
  - Correct foresight would have brought MEC to an unprecedentedly low figure. The boom could then have continued soundly only with a very low long-term rate and no misdirected investment.
  - In fact the rate deterred new investment except where speculative excitement ruled. A rate high enough to overcome that excitement would also have checked all reasonable investment. A higher rate "cures the disease by killing the patient".
- Keynes admits that prolonged near-full employment in rich countries, with the existing propensity to consume, could lead to *full investment* (no gross yield above replacement cost from any further durable goods). This might come within about 25 years or less. He says this is no denial of his view, because he asserts that full investment in the strict sense has never yet occurred, even momentarily (§ III, para. 8).
- Even if booms did involve momentary full investment, a higher rate would be absurd. The under-consumption case would then be "wholly established". The remedy would be raising the propensity to consume so that a given employment needs less investment (§ III, para. 9).

*Intuition note.* On Keynes's analysis a typical boom ends because its investments are priced on hopes that were too high, not because capital is so abundant that more is useless. In the 1929 case, a rate high enough to stop the speculative excitement would have stopped sound investment too.

### IV. Under-consumption schools
- These schools (voice: Keynes describing them) trace chronic under-employment to a propensity to consume that is unduly low (§ IV, para. 1).
- With investment unplanned, left to ignorant or speculative private judgment, and a long-term rate that seldom falls below a conventional level, Keynes says that in existing conditions (or at least those until lately) they are undoubtedly right as practical guides. If it is impracticable to raise investment materially, only more consumption can raise employment (§ IV, para. 2).
- Practically Keynes differs only in that they may stress consumption a little too much while investment still brings much social advantage. Theoretically they neglect that there are two ways to expand output. He is impressed by the advantages of increasing capital until it is no longer scarce, but this is "a practical judgment, not a theoretical imperative" (§ IV, para. 3).
- He would advance on both fronts: socially controlled investment aiming at a progressive decline in MEC, plus policies raising the propensity to consume, because full employment is unlikely with the existing propensity (§ IV, para. 4).
- **Keynes's illustration** (round numbers; § IV, para. 4): output is 15 per cent below continuous full employment. Of output, 10 per cent is net investment and 90 per cent consumption. With the existing propensity, net investment must rise 50 per cent for full employment. Output then goes from 100 to 115, consumption from 90 to 100, and net investment from 10 to 15. He suggests we might aim, perhaps, at altering the propensity so that at full employment consumption rises from 90 to 103 and net investment from 10 to 12 (103 + 12 = 115, added check).

### V. Reducing the labour supply
- Another school would redistribute the existing employment without raising output. Keynes thinks this premature, more clearly than raising consumption. He thinks the evidence is strong that most people prefer more income to more leisure. He sees no reason to compel them to take leisure (§ V).

### VI. The high-interest-rate school
- Keynes says the only line of argument along which any justification for checking the early boom with higher rates can be found is Mr D. H. Robertson's (voice: Keynes describing Robertson). He says Robertson assumes, in effect, that full employment is an impracticable ideal. The best we can hope for is much more stable employment, averaging perhaps a little higher (§ VI, para. 1).
- Keynes: if major policy changes are ruled out, it is "arguable" that a bank policy nipping an incipient boom could produce a better average state of expectation. Slump disappointment causes loss and waste, so average useful investment might be higher. He is unsure whether this is correct even on its own assumptions. It may overlook the social benefit of the consumption that accompanies even misdirected investment. Monetary control with the Federal Reserve's weapons of that time might make little difference to a 1929-type boom. He adds that such an outlook seems to him dangerously and unnecessarily defeatist (§ VI, para. 2).
- The "austere view", a high rate against any rise of employment much above the previous decade's average, is usually supported by arguments Keynes says rest on "confusion of mind". His replies (§ VI, paras. 3–4):
  1. **Claim:** in a boom investment outruns saving, and a higher rate restores equilibrium. **Reply:** this implies saving and investment can be unequal, so it is meaningless unless the terms are defined in some special sense.
  2. **Claim:** the extra saving that comes with extra investment is unjust, because prices rise. **Reply:** then any rise in output would be deprecated. Prices rise because short-period supply price usually increases with output (diminishing returns, or rising money cost-unit). With constant supply price there would be no price rise, but saving would still rise. Output creates the saving, and the price rise is a by-product. It occurs equally if the propensity to consume rises instead. No one has a legitimate vested interest in prices that are low only because output is low.
  3. **Claim:** a fall in the rate engineered by more money is the evil. **Reply:** the old rate has no special virtue. The new money is not "forced" on anyone. It is created to meet the liquidity-preference that goes with the lower rate or more transactions, and is held by people who prefer money to lending.
  4. **Claim:** the boom involves "capital consumption" (negative net investment, excess consumption). **Reply:** unless cycles are confused with a flight from the currency, such as the post-war European collapses, the evidence is wholly contrary. Even if true, a lower rate would be the more plausible remedy for under-investment.
- Keynes says he can make no sense of these views, except perhaps by supplying a tacit assumption that output cannot change. A theory assuming constant output is obviously not very serviceable for explaining the cycle (§ VI, para. 4).

### VII. Agricultural and inventory fluctuations
- **Jevons's theory:** earlier studies (notably Jevons) found the cycle's explanation in seasonal agricultural fluctuations. Keynes finds this "extremely plausible" in his own theory's light. Year-to-year changes in agricultural stocks are still among the largest single causes of changes in current investment. In Jevons's time they must have far outweighed all others (§ VII, para. 1).
- **Restated:** a big harvest adds to the carry-over. Farmers treat the proceeds as income. The extra carry-over is not a drain on other groups' income-expenditure, because it is financed from savings. It is therefore an addition to current investment, even if prices fall sharply (§ VII, para. 1).
- A poor harvest draws on the carry-over. Part of consumers' spending creates no current income for farmers, so current investment falls. With other investment constant, the difference may be overwhelming in a mainly agricultural community. Hence upturns are marked by bountiful harvests and downturns by deficient ones. Theories of a physical cycle of harvests are a different matter (§ VII, para. 1).
- Keynes does not mean the recent theories that bad harvests are good for trade (§ VII, para. 2).
- Agriculture matters less now. Its share of output is smaller, and a world market averages out seasons. In old days Keynes finds it difficult to see any cause of investment fluctuation, except war, comparable in size to changes in the agricultural carry-over (§ VII, para. 3).
- Today, changes in stocks of raw materials still matter. Keynes attributes the slow recovery after the turning point *mainly* to the deflationary effect of reducing redundant stocks. Stock accumulation after the boom breaks moderates the collapse but is paid for later by slower recovery. Sometimes stock reduction may have to be virtually complete before recovery is measurable (§ VII, para. 4).
- **The New Deal example** (which Keynes thinks a signal example): when Roosevelt's loan expenditure began, stocks, especially agricultural, were very high. Reducing them to normal took about two years and was a substantial offset to the loan expenditure. Only after that was the way prepared for substantial recovery (§ VII, para. 5).
- Inventory fluctuations cause minor oscillations within the cycle. Manufacturers produce for consumption expected months later and tend to run a little ahead. They then contract below current consumption to absorb the excess. This effect shows clearly in U.S. statistics (§ VII, para. 6).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text. Simplification: a perpetual yearly yield on a project costing 100; Keynes's MEC is defined over an asset's life.)*

A project costs 100 and is expected to return 6 per year. Since 6 ÷ 100 = 6%, its expected return is 6%.

- **Boom:** the interest rate is 4%. Expected 6% > 4%, so the project is built.
- **Truth at full employment:** the yield is only 2%. At a 4% rate, correct expectations would have rejected it (our label: an error of optimism).
- **Slump:** the error of pessimism expects −1%. Cutting the rate from 4% to 1%, or even to 0%, still leaves −1% < rate. No investment results, so a rate cut alone is not enough.
- **Keynes's remedy for the boom:** a rate of 1% against a true yield of 2% makes the project sound (2 > 1), so it can proceed without a crash.

## Concepts
- [[trade-cycle]] (new) — upswings and downswings with cumulative forces and a recognisable regularity of timing and length (§ I)
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — expected return on new capital goods, depending on scarcity, cost and expected future yield (§ II, para. 2)
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — demand to hold money; rises after the MEC collapse and pushes up the rate of interest (§ II, para. 4)
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — how much of income is spent; hurt by falling stock values (§ II, para. 9)
- [[over-investment]] (new) — Keynes distinguishes (a) investment destined to disappoint from (b) strict over-investment, where all further investment wastes resources (§ III, para. 2)
- [[under-consumption]] (new) — the view that under-employment comes from an unduly low propensity to consume (§ IV)
- [[inventory-cycle]] (new) — stock accumulation and run-down as investment and disinvestment (§ II, para. 8; § VII)
- [[beauty contest / higher-order beliefs]] — speculators forecast the next shift in market sentiment instead of true yield (§ II, para. 4)
- [[conventional expectations]] — Keynes mentions a long-term rate that seldom falls below a "conventional level" (§ IV, para. 2)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Boom resting on over-optimism, then a sudden crash ↔ financial instability hypothesis: stability encourages riskier financing.
- [structural-analogy] Speculators forecasting market sentiment ↔ beauty contest / higher-order beliefs: price depends on beliefs about others' beliefs.
- [structural-analogy] Stock-market fall depressing consumption ↔ balance-sheet recession: wealth and balance-sheet damage cut spending.
- [structural-analogy] Cumulative upward or downward forces ↔ coordination game / multiple equilibria: self-reinforcing optimism or pessimism.

## Questions this raises
- If confidence cannot be controlled by monetary policy, what instruments could "order" the volume of investment?
- How would one test empirically whether crises come first from MEC collapses or from interest-rate rises?
- Do the 3–5 year slump length and the inventory mechanism hold in modern economies with different asset lives and growth rates?
- What would count as evidence for strict full investment, as opposed to expectations that merely disappoint?
