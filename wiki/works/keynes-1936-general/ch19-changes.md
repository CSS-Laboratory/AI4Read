# Changes in Money-Wages

## L0 — One line
Keynes argues that a cut in money-wages has no lasting tendency to raise employment except through its effects on the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency of capital or the rate of interest, and he judges a stable money-wage level the most advisable policy on balance.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks whether wage flexibility can make the economy self-adjusting, as classical theory supposes. He argues that classical reasoning, as he reconstructs it, moves an industry-level labour demand curve to the whole economy, which assumes aggregate demand fixed. By his own method, a wage cut has no lasting tendency to raise employment except via consumption or investment, and several repercussions are probably or possibly adverse. In theory a wage cut acts like a money increase, but is harder to carry out, more unjust and heavier on debtors. He therefore prefers stable money-wages. The Appendix argues that Pigou's *Theory of Unemployment* assumes full employment and is one equation short if wages are bargained in money.

**Prerequisites:** effective demand, wage-unit (the money-wage used as the unit of measurement), propensity to consume, marginal efficiency of capital, liquidity preference and multiplier (from earlier chapters); elasticity; simple algebra.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- On Keynes's reconstruction, the classical case for wage cuts moves an industry-level labour demand schedule to the whole economy, which would require aggregate demand to be fixed. He calls this an *ignoratio elenchi*: it answers a different question from the one at issue (§ I, last para.).
- Keynes's test: employment follows effective demand in wage-units. Effective demand cannot rise if the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency of capital and the interest rate are unchanged (§ II, "first question").
- If entrepreneurs expand output expecting a wage cut to pay, they are bound to make a loss equal to the excess of the saving out of those incomes over investment, whatever the wage level, unless the marginal propensity to consume is 1 or investment rises (§ II, "Perhaps it will help").
- Seven repercussions of a wage cut are listed. Keynes judges the redistribution effect probably adverse, and says debt burdens, expected further cuts and unrest may offset favourable effects (§ II, items (1)–(7)).
- Expectations of slowly falling wages weigh on the marginal efficiency of capital; an expected 2% yearly sag is roughly equivalent to a 2% rise in interest payable. As far as the marginal efficiency of capital is concerned, rigid wages are better than sagging ones (§ II, "contingency").
- If the quantity of money is virtually fixed, a wage cut can in theory lower the interest rate by raising money in wage-units, as a money increase does, and so shares its limits. Four practical differences favour monetary policy (§ II, "(i)–(iv)").
- Keynes now judges a stable general money-wage level, on balance, the most advisable policy for a closed system, with some flexibility between industries (§ III, para. 3).
- In the Appendix, Keynes argues that Pigou's analysis seems to need n = x + y (no involuntary unemployment), so it cannot say what determines actual employment and has no direct bearing on involuntary unemployment (App. paras 8–12).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. The classical argument and Keynes's objection
- Keynes says it would have helped to discuss wage changes earlier. Classical theory rests self-adjustment on fluid money-wages and blames rigidity for maladjustment (§ I, para. 1). A wage cut can, in certain circumstances, stimulate output. He says his difference from classical theory is mainly one of analysis (§ I, para. 2).
- **Classical view (as Keynes reconstructs it):** a cut lowers product prices, ceteris paribus (cet. par., "other things equal") raises demand, and employment rises until the agreed wage cut is just offset by the falling marginal efficiency of labour as output rises from given equipment (§ I, para. 3).
  - In crude form, demand is unaffected. Keynes says some economists might argue that demand is unaffected because it depends on MV (quantity of money times income-velocity), or even that profits must rise (§ I, para. 4).
  - The commoner form: some workers lose purchasing power, but other factors' real demand rises as prices fall. Workers' own demand will very likely rise with employment, unless the elasticity of demand for labour in response to money-wage changes is below unity. Hence, in the new equilibrium, more employment, except perhaps in some unusual limiting case with no reality in practice (§ I, para. 4).
- Keynes says this analysis is seldom written down. He suggests the way of thinking is probably reached as follows: industry demand and supply schedules yield a labour demand schedule for the industry. This is carried over, with little change, to industry as a whole. Money versus real wages is held immaterial because prices do not move in exact proportion to money-wages (§ I, para. 6).
- **Keynes's objection:** industry schedules assume fixed schedules elsewhere and fixed aggregate effective demand. Transferring the argument therefore transfers that assumption, which makes it an *ignoratio elenchi* (answering a question other than the one asked). Nobody denies that a wage cut with unchanged aggregate demand raises employment. The real question is whether aggregate demand stays the same in money terms, or falls less than in proportion to the wage cut (§ I, para. 7).
- Without the analogy, classical theory has no method to answer the question. Keynes says Pigou's *Theory of Unemployment* seems to him to get all that can be got out of the classical theory, so that the book becomes a striking demonstration that the theory has nothing to offer on what determines total employment (§ I, para. 7).

*Intuition note:* what holds for one industry's labour market need not hold for all of them at once. When firms across the economy cut wages, many of the buyers of their products are the workers whose pay was cut.

### II. Keynes's own analysis
- **Two questions** (§ II, para. 1): (i) Does a wage cut directly raise employment with the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency of capital schedule and the interest rate unchanged? (ii) Does it affect employment through probable repercussions on these three?
- **Answer to (i): no.** Employment is uniquely correlated with effective demand in wage-units. Effective demand is expected consumption plus expected investment, so it cannot change if the three factors do not (§ II, "first question"). If entrepreneurs raised employment anyway, proceeds would fall short of supply-price (§ II, "first question").
- **The most favourable case for the classical view:** entrepreneurs expect the cut to pay and expand output. They gain only if the marginal propensity to consume equals 1, or if investment rises to fill the gap between the increments of income and consumption. The latter needs the marginal efficiency of capital schedule to rise relative to the interest rate (§ II, "Perhaps it will help").
  - Otherwise employment falls back. If planned incomes would lead to saving above current investment, entrepreneurs are bound to make a loss equal to the difference, whatever the level of money-wages. At best, their own investment in working capital delays the disappointment (§ II, "Perhaps it will help").
- **Conclusion:** a wage cut has no lasting tendency to raise employment except through the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency of capital or the interest rate. There is no other method of analysis (§ II, "Thus the reduction").

*Intuition note:* a wage cut changes costs, but jobs depend on total spending. Spending can rise only through a change in the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency of capital or the interest rate.

**Seven repercussions** (§ II, items (1)–(7)):
1. *Redistribution.* Prices fall somewhat. Real income shifts (a) from wage-earners to other factors whose pay is not cut, and (b) from entrepreneurs to rentiers (people with fixed money incomes). The first shift probably lowers the propensity to consume. The second is more doubtful, but is unfavourable if rentiers are richer and have the least flexible living standards. The net result is a guess, "probably" more adverse than favourable.
2. *Open system.* A cut relative to wages abroad favours investment through the balance of trade, unless offset by tariffs or quotas. Keynes suggests this may explain why belief in wage cuts is stronger in Britain than in the more closed US.
3. *Terms of trade.* In an open system a cut probably worsens the terms of trade. Real incomes fall except for the newly employed, which may raise the propensity to consume.
4. *Expectations of wages.* If the cut is expected to be a reduction relative to future wages, it raises the marginal efficiency of capital and may help consumption. If it raises the expectation, or serious possibility, of further cuts, the opposite holds: investment and consumption are postponed.
5. *Liquidity preference.* A smaller wage bill and lower prices reduce the need for cash, lowering liquidity preference (the demand for money) and the interest rate, cet. par. If wages and prices are expected to rise again, the effect is much weaker on long-term than on short-term loans. Political unrest may raise liquidity preference by more than the cash released.
6. *Optimism and resistance.* A cut helps an individual firm, so a general cut may produce optimism and break a vicious circle of pessimistic estimates. But workers may make the same mistake, causing labour troubles. A simultaneous equal cut in all industries is, as a rule, not possible, so it is in the interest of every group of workers to resist a cut in its own case. Employers' downward revision of wage bargains is resisted much more than a gradual fall in real wages through rising prices.
7. *Debt.* A greater debt burden may partly offset cheerful reactions. A large fall may make indebted entrepreneurs insolvent, severely hurting investment. A lower price level also raises the real burden of the national debt and taxation, which is likely to prove very adverse to business confidence.

Keynes says this is not a complete list but covers the usually most important effects. If we restrict the argument to a closed system and assume nothing is to be hoped from the redistribution of real incomes, hopes of favourable results must rest mainly on (4), a higher marginal efficiency of capital, or (5), a lower interest rate (§ II, after item (7)).

- **Expectations and the marginal efficiency of capital.** The favourable case is when wages are believed to have touched bottom. The worst is when wages sag slowly and each cut weakens confidence. A sudden large cut to a level nobody expects to last would be most favourable, but would need decree. Rigid wages are better than a gradual downward drift, with a further moderate cut expected for, say, each 1% rise in unemployment (§ II, "contingency").
  - For example, an expected 2% sag in wages over the coming year is "roughly equivalent" to a 2% rise in the interest payable for that period. The same holds, *mutatis mutandis*, for booms (§ II, "contingency").
  - Keynes concludes that, given actual practices and institutions, a rigid money-wage policy is more expedient than a flexible one as far as the marginal efficiency of capital is concerned. He then asks whether this conclusion is upset when we turn to the rate of interest. The next paragraphs and difference (iv) take up that question (§ II, "It follows that").
- **Interest rate and the quantity of money.** Those who believe in self-adjustment must rest their argument on the effect of a falling wage and price level on the demand for money, though Keynes is not aware that they have done so. If money quantity depends on the price level, there is nothing to hope for. If it is virtually fixed, its quantity in wage-units can be increased indefinitely by cutting wages. Relative to incomes it can be largely increased, the limit depending on the share of wage-cost in marginal prime cost and the response of other costs (§ II, "It is, therefore, on the effect").
  - A wage cut with fixed money can therefore, in theory, move the interest rate as much as a money increase with fixed wages. It has the same limits. A moderate change may be inadequate for the long-term rate, and an immoderate one may shatter confidence (§ II, "We can, therefore").
  - Hence there is no ground for believing flexible wages can maintain continuous full employment, any more than for believing open-market policy alone can. If labour were always able, and willing, to cut its money demands whenever employment was short, we would in effect have monetary management by the trade unions (§ II, "There is, therefore").

*Intuition note:* a wage cut and a money increase both raise the money supply in wage-units. The wage cut does this by shrinking the unit of measurement, not by adding money.

**Four differences between wage flexibility and monetary flexibility** (§ II, "(i)–(iv)"):
- (i) Uniform cuts are impossible outside a socialised community. Changes are gradual, irregular and unjust, and the weakest bargainers suffer. Most governments already have the power to change the money quantity.
- (ii) With rigid wages, price changes mainly follow diminishing marginal productivity. This keeps the greatest practicable fairness between labour and factors fixed in money, such as rentiers and salaried staff.
- (iii) Cutting the wage-unit raises the real debt burden proportionately. Increasing money has the opposite effect.
- (iv) A sagging interest rate brought by sagging wages gives a "double drag" on the marginal efficiency of capital and a double reason to postpone investment.

### III. Policy conclusions
- If labour offered its services at gradually falling money-wages, this would not usually reduce real wages and might even raise them, through lower output. The main result would be great price instability, perhaps violent enough to make business calculation futile. A flexible wage policy is thus not an adjunct of laissez-faire. It could work only in a highly authoritarian society, which Keynes illustrates with Italy, Germany or Russia but not France, the US or Britain (§ III, para. 1).
- **Australia:** if real wages are fixed by law in a closed system, employment would oscillate violently between the corresponding level and zero, according to whether investment is below or above a critical rate. Prices would race to zero or infinity. Stability could be found, if at all, only in the money quantity being so determined that some money-wage level always holds investment at the critical level; employment would then stay constant while money-wages and prices fluctuate rapidly. Australia escaped through the law's inefficacy and because it is open: wages affected foreign investment, and the terms of trade affected real wages (§ III, para. 2).
- **Keynes's opinion:** stable general money-wages are, on balance, most advisable for a closed system. The same holds for an open one if equilibrium abroad can be secured by fluctuating exchanges. Some flexibility within particular industries helps transfers from declining to expanding ones (§ III, para. 3).
- This gives fairly stable prices, more stable than flexible wages would. Short-run price changes follow employment's effect on marginal prime cost, and long-run ones follow technology and equipment. Large employment swings still bring large price swings, but smaller ones (§ III, paras 4–5).
- Long-run choice: falling prices with stable wages, or rising wages with stable prices. Keynes prefers the latter: easier to keep employment near full, lighter debt, easier shifts to growing industries and psychological encouragement. He adds that no essential point of principle is involved (§ III, para. 6).

### Appendix: Pigou's *Theory of Unemployment*
- **Pigou's setup, as Keynes summarises it.** Employment depends on (i) the real wage workers stipulate and (ii) the Real Demand Function for Labour. The money wage divided by the price of wage-goods is taken to measure the real wage (App. para. 1).
  - Industries making wage-goods (and exports that buy wage-goods abroad) employ $x$ men. The others employ $y$. Wage-goods output is $F(x)$ and the wage rate is $F'(x)$. This tacitly assumes marginal wage-cost equals marginal prime cost (App. para. 3). Also $x+y=f(x)$, and the elasticity of real demand for labour is
$$E_r=\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\times\frac{F'(x)}{F''(x)}.$$
- **Translation.** With Pigou's wage-goods as Keynes's consumption-goods, $F(x)/F'(x)$ is Keynes's $C_w$. With $\Delta x=k'\Delta y$ ($k'$ is the employment multiplier), $f'(x)=1+\frac{1}{k'}$ (added step: $\Delta(x+y)=\Delta x+\Delta y=\Delta x(1+1/k')$). The elasticity thus mixes technical conditions ($F$) with the propensity to consume wage-goods ($f$), but only when marginal labour-cost equals marginal prime cost (App. paras 4–5).
- **Supply side.** Pigou takes labour supply to depend on the real wage and nothing else. Since the real wage is $F'(x)$, a function of $x$, supply at the existing real wage is a function of $x$ alone: $n=c(x)$. The equations $x+y=f(x)$ and $n=c(x)$ have three unknowns. Keynes says it seems clear that Pigou gets round this by taking $n=x+y$, i.e. assuming no involuntary unemployment in the strict sense. Then $f(x)=c(x)$ gives $x=n_1$, $y=c(n_1)-n_1$ and total employment $c(n_1)$ (App. paras 6–8).
- **Implications.**
  - On this assumption, if labour supply shifts, employment in non-wage-goods industries is bound to adjust so as to keep $f=c$. Keynes reads the assumption as meaning that labour can always fix its own real wage, and that the interest rate always adjusts to the marginal efficiency of capital so as to preserve full employment. Without this assumption, he says, Pigou's analysis breaks down (App. paras 9–10).
  - Keynes finds it strange that the book involves no reference to changes in investment due to the interest rate or confidence. He calls its title something of a misnomer: the book discusses how much employment there will be, given the labour supply function, when the conditions for full employment are satisfied. Alternatively it is a non-causative study of which real wage matches each employment level. It has no direct bearing on involuntary unemployment (App. paras 11–12).
- **Money-wage stipulation.** Pigou agrees that, within certain limits, workers often stipulate a money wage. Then supply depends on $F'(x)$ and also on the money price of wage-goods, which depends on aggregate employment, so the system is "one equation short" (App. paras 13–14).
  - British money-wages in 1924–1934 stayed within 6%, while real wages moved by over 20%. A general theory must cover fixed money-wages (App. para. 14).
- **Inconsistencies.** Pigou rejects one argument (his p. 101) on the ground that it is valid only if employment remains unaltered, which is the very point under discussion. Keynes says Pigou makes the same mistake on the next page by assuming that non-wage-earners' money income is unchanged, which is valid only if employment does not remain unaltered, again the very point under discussion (App. para. 15). He rejects the multiplier by assuming no extra labour is available at a lower real wage. Keynes calls it fantastically far removed from the facts to assume this when over 2,000,000 men were willing to work at the existing money-wage (App. paras 16–17). The same assumption would also rule out increased "primary" employment (App. para. 18).
- **Pigou's view.** Keynes quotes Pigou: with perfectly free competition and mobile labour, wage-rates tend to be so related to demand that everybody is employed, and existing unemployment is due wholly to changing demand conditions and frictions. Keynes reads Pigou (his p. 253) as concluding that unemployment is primarily due to a wage policy that fails to adjust, so that in the long run it can be cured by wage adjustments. Keynes replies that the real wage (subject only to a minimum set by the marginal disutility of employment) is not primarily determined by wage adjustments, though these may have repercussions, but by other forces of the system, in particular the relation between the marginal efficiency of capital and the interest rate, which Pigou has failed, if Keynes is right, to include (App. paras 19–20).
- **Cycles.** Pigou's "real demand for labour" depends only on $F(x)$ and $f(x)$. These change slowly, so Keynes expects it to be virtually constant over a trade cycle. Pigou omits the unstable factor, fluctuations in investment (App. paras 21–22). Keynes says he criticises Pigou at length not because Pigou is more open to criticism than other classical economists, but because Pigou's is the only attempt he knows of to write down the classical theory precisely (App. para. 23).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Closed economy; all amounts in wage-units. Consumption $C=0.8Y$, so the marginal propensity to consume is 0.8. Investment $I=10$.
1. Equilibrium: $Y=C+I=0.8Y+10$, so $0.2Y=10$ and $Y=50$.
2. Suppose a wage cut makes entrepreneurs expect to profit from $Y=60$. Incomes would be 60; consumption $0.8\times60=48$; saving $60-48=12$.
3. Investment is only 10, so spending is $48+10=58<60$. Entrepreneurs lose $12-10=2$ wage-units, and output falls back toward 50.
4. If the cut raised investment to 12, then $Y=12/0.2=60$. The expansion lasts, and the wage level never entered.

## Concepts
- [[effective-demand]] (new) — expected consumption plus expected investment; employment is uniquely tied to it in wage-units (§ II, "first question")
- [[wage-unit]] (new) — the money-wage rate used as the measuring unit; the quantity of money measured in wage-units rises when money-wages fall (if the nominal quantity of money is virtually fixed) (§ II, "It is, therefore, on the effect")
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — how consumption responds to income; for an expansion to pay, the gap between the increments of income and consumption must be matched by investment (§ II, "Perhaps it will help")
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — expected return on investment; wage expectations shift it (§ II, item (4))
- [[liquidity-preference]] (new) — demand for money; may be lowered by lower wages and prices, and may be raised by political unrest (§ II, item (5))
- [[involuntary-unemployment]] (new) — unemployment of workers who would work at the existing real wage; on Keynes's reading, Pigou's n = x + y assumes it away (App. para. 8)
- [[fallacy-of-composition]] (new, our label) — treating an industry-level result as an economy-wide one while holding aggregate demand fixed (§ I, para. 7)
- [[conventional-expectations]] — loose fit only; Keynes does not use the term here, but treats expectations about future wages as shifting investment: an expected wage sag is roughly equivalent to a rise in interest (§ II, "contingency")
- [[debt-deflation]] — here: falling wages and prices raise real debt, with insolvency risk. Keynes does not use the term (§ II, item (7) and difference (iii))

## Canonical instances
- [[pigou-theory-of-unemployment]] — the classical theory written down precisely, in Keynes's view; one equation short if workers stipulate money-wages (App. paras 7–14)
- [[entrepreneurs-loss-saving-gap]] — the loss equal to saving minus investment after an unwarranted expansion (§ II, "Perhaps it will help")
- [[australia-real-wage-legislation]] — fixing real wages by law would, in a closed system, bring violent swings in employment and unstable prices (§ III, para. 2)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Wage cut ≈ money increase ↔ monetary economics: both raise real money balances; the mechanism differs.
- [structural-analogy] Expected wage sag ≈ rise in interest ↔ intertemporal choice: expected deflation raises the real cost of borrowing.
- [metaphor] Industry demand curve for labour, applied to the economy ↔ micro–macro aggregation: ignores feedback through aggregate income.

## Questions this raises
- What does the argument imply when the wage cut does not change the quantity of money? How large are the effects on the interest rate and on confidence?
- How would the conclusion change in an open economy with fixed exchange rates?
- Can the claim that flexible wages mean unstable prices be tested with historical data?
- Does Keynes's reading of Pigou fairly state Pigou's own view?
