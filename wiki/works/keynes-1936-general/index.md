# Keynes, J. M. (1936). *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*. Macmillan. Chapter 12, "The State of Long-Term Expectation".

## In one paragraph
Keynes argues that classical economics describes only the special case of full employment. In general, employment is set by effective demand: the spending that entrepreneurs expect. That spending depends on consumption, which rises by less than income, and on investment. Investment depends on the marginal efficiency of capital set against the interest rate. Long-term expectations, which drive investment, rest on fragile convention and animal spirits, not on calculable probability. The interest rate is a reward for giving up liquidity, not for saving, so saving and investment are results of the system, not its determinants. An economy can therefore settle below full employment. Cutting wages is no reliable cure. Chapter 12 is the catalog focus. These digests cover the whole book, chapters 1–23, with chapters 1–2 treated together.

## The path through the work
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch02-postulates]] — Keynes argues that classical employment theory rests on one assumption in three forms (real wage = marginal disutility of work, no involuntary unemployment, supply creates its own demand), and that it fits only the special case of full employment.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch03-principle]] — Employment is set where expected proceeds meet the aggregate supply price of output (effective demand), and nothing in general guarantees that this point is full employment.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch04-choice]] — For analysing the whole economy, Keynes proposes using only two homogeneous units (units whose parts are alike, so they can be added), money-value and labour-hours (weighted by pay), and keeping vague aggregates such as output as a whole, capital equipment as a whole and the general price-level only for rough historical comparison.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch05-expectation]] — Employment at any moment is governed by today's expectations of costs and sale-proceeds, taken together with today's capital equipment, not by past results as such.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch06-definition]] — Keynes defines income through "user cost" (the value sacrificed by using equipment), and with consistent definitions saving must equal investment, since both equal income minus consumption; saving is a mere residual.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch07-meaning]] — Keynes argues that saving and investment, taken in their plain senses, are necessarily equal for the community as a whole. He traces the rival views to differing definitions of investment or income, to a vague "forced saving" notion, and to treating a depositor's saving as one-sided.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch08-propensity]] — Aggregate consumption depends mainly on aggregate income and rises by less than income does, so employment can rise only if investment fills the gap between income and consumption (unless the propensity itself changes), and financial provisions for the future can widen that gap.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch09-propensity]] — Because subjective motives to save change slowly and objective factors such as the interest rate are often of secondary importance in the short run, short-run consumption largely depends on income; and a rise in the interest rate, unless investment demand offsets it, lowers actual saving because it lowers investment and hence income.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch10-marginal]] — Given the marginal propensity to consume, each increase in investment raises income by a multiple (for a given MPC), k = 1/(1 − MPC), so a small change in investment can produce a much larger change in employment.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch11-marginal]] — Keynes defines the marginal efficiency of capital as the discount rate that equates an asset's expected future returns with its replacement cost, so that investment depends on expectations of the future, not merely on current yields.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch12-state]] — Because the basis for forecasting investment yields is very weak, investment rests on a fragile convention, on speculation about other investors, and on animal spirits, which can make it unstable.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch13-general]] — The rate of interest is the reward for parting with liquidity, so it is set where the public's wish to hold cash equals the available quantity of money, not where saving equals investment.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch14-classical]] — Keynes argues that the classical theory, in which interest equates saving and investment, fails because, in general, shifting the schedules changes income; saving and investment are results of the system, not its determinants.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch15-psychological]] — Money demand splits into an income-driven part, $M_1 = L_1(Y)$, and a speculative part, $M_2 = L_2(r)$, that depends on the interest rate and on expectations about it; because the long-term rate rests heavily on conventional expectations, a policy that public opinion distrusts may fail to push it down.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch16-sundry]] — Saving cancels a present order for consumption without placing any specific future order, so it does not by itself raise investment. Capital earns a yield because it is scarce, and if accumulating capital pushes its expected yield down faster than the interest rate can fall, leaving the economy to itself (laissez-faire) can leave a society with low employment.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch17-essential]] — Keynes argues that in the economy we know, money's own-rate of interest tends to be the one that resists falling as output grows, because money has a high liquidity-premium, negligible carrying cost and (near) zero elasticities of production and substitution, so investment can stop short of full employment.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch18-general]] — Keynes summarises his theory as a chain from psychological factors to investment, income and employment, then argues that four plausible behavioural conditions explain why the economy fluctuates without collapsing and sits below full employment, though not by necessity.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch19-changes]] — Keynes argues that a cut in money-wages has no lasting tendency to raise employment except through its effects on the propensity to consume, the marginal efficiency of capital or the rate of interest, and he judges a stable money-wage level the most advisable policy on balance.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch20-employment]] — Keynes recasts the supply curve as an "employment function" in wage-units, so that effective demand splits between output and price, and, apart from two practical qualifications, employment rises only until full employment, after which more spending raises only money values (prices, money-wages, profits) in proportion.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch21-theory]] — Keynes argues that there is no separate "theory of money": the price level depends on the cost-unit (a weighted average of the rewards of the factors in marginal cost) and on output, so a rise in the money supply spreads over output, wages and prices in proportions that depend on several variable factors (§§ I–VI).
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch22-notes]] — Keynes suggests that the trade cycle is mainly a cyclical swing in the marginal efficiency of capital (the expected return on new investment), so a higher interest rate is the wrong remedy for the boom, and a lower rate by itself is usually not enough to cure the slump.
- [[keynes-1936-general/ch23-notes]] — Keynes argues that the mercantilists, the old usury laws, Gesell and the under-consumption writers each grasped part of a truth that classical theory buried: the interest rate and the volume of investment are not self-adjusting at the optimum, and too much thrift can harm employment.

## Big ideas across units
- **Classical theory is a special case.** Full employment is a limiting case, and equilibrium below it is possible. Says-law-type assumptions are rejected, and later chapters revisit this for wages and interest. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch02-postulates]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch03-principle]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch14-classical]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch19-changes]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch23-notes]].
- **Effective demand sets employment.** Employment follows expected proceeds, not the marginal disutility of labour. This is re-stated as a causal chain and later as an employment function. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch03-principle]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch18-general]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch20-employment]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch21-theory]].
- **Consumption, the multiplier and saving as a residual.** Consumption rises by less than income, so investment must fill the gap. A multiplier follows, saving equals investment by definition, and the interest rate affects saving only through income. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch06-definition]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch07-meaning]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch08-propensity]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch09-propensity]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch10-marginal]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch16-sundry]].
- **Aggregate differs from individual.** What works for one saver, firm or industry fails for the whole. Examples are saving more, cutting wages, and measuring output. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch04-choice]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch07-meaning]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch16-sundry]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch19-changes]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch21-theory]].
- **Expectations drive investment but rest on weak ground.** Long-term expectations cannot be checked quickly. They lean on convention, speculation about other investors, and animal spirits. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch05-expectation]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch11-marginal]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch12-state]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch15-psychological]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch22-notes]].
- **The marginal efficiency of capital against the interest rate.** Investment continues until the marginal efficiency of capital equals the interest rate. Swings in the former explain the trade cycle, and fixing the rate by monetary means is not enough. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch11-marginal]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch16-sundry]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch17-essential]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch18-general]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch22-notes]].
- **Interest as the price of liquidity.** The rate is set by liquidity-preference and the quantity of money, not by saving and investment. Money's special properties explain why the rate can stay too high. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch13-general]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch14-classical]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch15-psychological]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch17-essential]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch23-notes]].
- **Wage flexibility is not a cure, and stable wages are preferred.** The wage-unit appears as a measuring device. A wage cut works only through consumption, investment or interest. Price effects depend on elasticities, and stable money-wages are preferred. Units: [[keynes-1936-general/ch02-postulates]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch19-changes]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch20-employment]], [[keynes-1936-general/ch21-theory]].
