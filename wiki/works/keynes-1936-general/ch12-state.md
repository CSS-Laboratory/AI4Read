# The State of Long-Term Expectation

## L0 — One line
Because the basis for forecasting investment yields is very weak, investment rests on a fragile convention, on speculation about other investors, and on animal spirits, which can make it unstable.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What decides the expected yield of investments, which sets how much firms invest? Keynes says the knowledge behind long-term forecasts is usually very slight and often negligible, so investors assume the present will continue unless they have specific reasons to expect change. Organised stock markets make this convention workable for individuals, because they can sell quickly, but they can make it fragile. Most professionals then try to guess market psychology, not long-run yield. Also, much enterprise rests on spontaneous optimism, not calculation. Keynes suggests a transfer tax might help in the United States, and expects the State to take more direct responsibility for investment. He also says the interest rate keeps a great, but not decisive, influence in normal circumstances.

**Prerequisites:** marginal efficiency of capital (the expected return on an extra unit of capital, set against its supply price); the interest rate as the cost of funds; the idea of share prices as present valuations of expected yields.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Expectations rest partly on known facts and partly on uncertain forecasts (§ I). It is reasonable to lean on facts we feel somewhat confident about, so the existing situation enters, in a sense disproportionately, and is projected forward (§ II).
- Confidence matters through the marginal-efficiency schedule. It is not a separate second factor (§ II).
- Long-term knowledge is very precarious. Markets cope through a convention: assume the present continues unless there is specific reason to expect change. It does not mean people believe this (§ III).
- Liquid markets make fixed community investments "liquid" for each individual, which helps investment but exposes it to a fragile convention (§ III).
- Professionals mostly forecast market psychology, as in the beauty-contest image. The reason is the market's structure, not foolishness (§ V, point 4).
- A collapse can come from weaker speculative confidence or weaker credit. Recovery needs both (§ V, point 5).
- Keynes separates speculation from enterprise. He suggests that a government transfer tax might help in the United States, and notes a dilemma: making investment illiquid might also impede it (§ VI).
- Animal spirits drive much activity. Still, the state of expectation is often steady, and some factors somewhat mitigate the effects of ignorance of the future (§§ VII–VIII).
- The interest rate keeps, in normal circumstances, a great but not decisive influence on investment. Keynes is somewhat sceptical of a merely monetary policy and expects the State to take more direct responsibility for investment (§ VIII).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Setting
- Investment depends on the interest rate compared with the marginal-efficiency-of-capital (MEC) schedule. The MEC depends on the supply price of an asset versus its prospective yield (§ I).
- Expectations of yield rest partly on facts that can be taken as known more or less for certain (the existing capital stock, current consumer demand). They rest partly on forecasts: future capital stock, tastes, effective demand (total spending on output), and the money wage-unit (the money wage of one unit of labour) (§ I).
- The psychological state covering the forecasts is "long-term expectation". Short-term expectation is the producer's estimate of what he will get for a product made with existing plant (chapter 5) (§ I).

### II. Weight, confidence, method
- It is reasonable to lean on facts we feel confident about, even if they are less relevant than vague ones. So present facts enter, "in a sense disproportionately". The usual practice is to project the existing situation, modified only for definite reasons (§ II).
- Long-term expectation depends on the best forecast *and* on confidence in it. This is how highly we rate the chance that the best forecast is quite wrong (§ II).
- Practical men watch the "state of confidence" closely. Economists, as a rule, have not analysed it carefully and have discussed it only in general terms. Keynes says it matters because it is a major factor in the MEC schedule, the same thing as the investment demand schedule (§ II).
- Little can be said a priori. This digression rests on observation of markets and business psychology, so it is at a different level of abstraction from most of the book. Keynes also assumes a constant interest rate here. He says rate effects are easily superimposed (§ II).

*Intuition note:* Confidence is not an extra force beside the MEC. It is part of what fixes how attractive investment looks. Confidence is weak when large changes are expected but their precise form is very uncertain (§ II).

### III (the text has no IV; the convention passage comes under III)
- **Precarious knowledge.** Our knowledge of what an investment will yield years ahead is, he says, usually very slight and often negligible. Those who seriously try to estimate are often too few to govern the market (§ III).
- **Former times.** Owner-managers invested from "sanguine temperament", partly as a lottery. Even afterwards nobody knew whether average results beat the interest rate. Keynes judges it probable that, excluding natural resources and monopolies, average results have disappointed hopes (§ III).
- **New factor.** With ownership separated from management and organised markets, investment is sometimes facilitated and sometimes made more unstable. The Stock Exchange revalues daily. This lets the individual, though not the community, revise his commitment, as if a farmer could tap his barometer after breakfast and move his capital out of farming between 10 and 11 in the morning (§ III).
- Keynes says these daily revaluations inevitably have a decisive influence on the rate of current investment. It makes no sense to build new at more than the price of a similar existing enterprise. And it pays to spend a lot on a new project if it can be floated at an immediate profit. So some investment follows the average expectation of Stock Exchange dealers, not the entrepreneur's genuine expectation (§ III).
- **The convention.** In practice we have tacitly agreed, as a rule, to assume (though it does not work out quite so simply) that the existing state of affairs continues indefinitely, except where we have specific reasons to expect change. We do not really believe this, since experience says it is unlikely. Nor can equal probabilities for errors in each direction justify it, for that leads to absurdities (§ III).
- Effectively we treat the market valuation as uniquely correct given present knowledge. Keynes says that, philosophically speaking, it cannot be, since our knowledge does not provide a sufficient basis for a calculated mathematical expectation. Many irrelevant things enter market valuation (§ III).
- **Why it works.** Keynes says the convention is compatible with considerable continuity and stability so long as it can be relied on. Given organised investment markets and a reliable convention, an investor can legitimately tell himself that the only risk he runs is a genuine change in the news over the near future. He can form his own judgment on that, and such a change is unlikely to be very large, so he need not lose sleep over the value ten years ahead. Investment then becomes reasonably "safe" for the individual over short periods, and so over any succession of short periods, if he can fairly rely on no breakdown of the convention and on having the chance to revise his judgment in time. Investments "fixed" for the community become "liquid" for the individual (§ III).
- Keynes is sure that leading investment markets developed on some such basis, but it is not surprising that a convention, in an absolute view of things so arbitrary, has weak points. Its precariousness creates no small part of the problem of securing enough investment (§ III).

*Intuition note:* The convention is a shared shortcut, not a belief about the future. It lets investors tolerate ignorance by looking only at near-term news. It is compatible with considerable stability so long as it can be relied on to be maintained.

### V. Factors that increase precariousness
1. More equity is owned by people who do not manage and lack special knowledge. So "real knowledge" in valuation has seriously declined (§ V).
2. Ephemeral day-to-day profit changes have an excessive influence. Examples: American ice-company shares reportedly sell higher in summer. A bank holiday may raise the market value of the British railways by several million pounds (§ V).
3. A convention built on mass psychology of ignorant individuals can change violently on trivial news. It has no strong roots of conviction. In abnormal times there are waves of optimism and pessimism, unreasoning and yet in a sense legitimate where no solid basis exists (§ V).
4. **Professionals.** One might expect expert competition to correct the ignorant. But professionals mostly foresee changes in the conventional basis of valuation shortly before the public. Keynes says this is an inevitable result of the market's organisation (§ V).
   - His example: it is not sensible to pay 25 for an investment you think worth 30 if you expect the market to value it at 20 in three months (§ V).
   - Keynes calls the fetish of liquidity surely the most anti-social of orthodox finance's maxims. For the community there is no liquidity of investment. The social aim is to defeat "the dark forces of time and ignorance". The private aim is "to beat the gun" and pass the bad, or depreciating, half-crown (a British coin) to the other fellow (§ V).
   - This game needs no gulls and can be played among professionals. He compares it to Snap, Old Maid and Musical Chairs, played even when all know the Old Maid is circulating (§ V).
   - **Beauty contest.** In a newspaper contest, competitors pick the six prettiest faces from a hundred photographs. The prize goes to whoever is closest to the average choice. So each picks the faces he thinks likeliest to catch the others' fancy, not those he finds prettiest. Going beyond even what average opinion genuinely thinks prettiest gives the "third degree": anticipating what average opinion expects average opinion to be. Some, Keynes believes, go to the fourth, fifth and higher degrees (§ V).
   - **Objection (reader):** a skilled long-term investor should profit from the game-players. **Keynes replies:** such people exist and matter greatly, but several factors endanger their predominance (§ V):
     - long-term investing is scarcely practicable, laborious and risky, and there is no clear evidence that the socially advantageous policy is also the most profitable;
     - human nature wants quick results and discounts remote gains steeply;
     - an investor who ignores near-term fluctuations needs greater resources for safety and must not use borrowed money on so large a scale, if at all;
     - the long-term investor, who most promotes the public interest, must be eccentric in the eyes of average opinion and in practice gets most criticism where funds are managed by committees, boards or banks. "Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally" (§ V).
5. **State of credit.** The speculator may not have unlimited money at the market rate. The confidence of lenders (the "state of credit") also matters. A collapse may come from weaker speculative confidence or weaker credit. Recovery needs both. Strengthening credit is necessary but not sufficient (§ V).

*Intuition note:* If a price depends on what others will pay soon, forecasting others beats forecasting value. Skill then goes into guessing opinion. This can happen without fools in the market.

### VI. Speculation, enterprise, remedies
- **Definitions.** Speculation is forecasting the market's psychology. Enterprise is forecasting the prospective yield of assets over their whole life. Speculation does not always predominate, but the risk rises as markets are better organised (§ VI).
- New York: the influence of speculation is "enormous". Americans, he is told, rarely invest "for income" and buy hoping for capital appreciation. Speculators may be harmless on top of a steady stream of enterprise, but the position is serious when enterprise becomes "the bubble on a whirlpool of speculation". In his view, Wall Street's success in directing new investment to the most profitable channels cannot be claimed as one of the outstanding triumphs of laissez-faire (free-market, little state intervention) capitalism, which is not surprising if its best brains have been directed to a different object (§ VI).
- Keynes says it is usually agreed that casinos should be inaccessible and expensive, and perhaps the same is true of stock exchanges. That the sins of the London Stock Exchange are less than Wall Street's may be due less to national character than to the fact that, to the average Englishman, it is inaccessible and very expensive (jobber's turn, the dealer's margin between buying and selling price; brokerage; transfer tax). Introducing a substantial government transfer tax on all transactions might, he says, prove the most serviceable reform available for mitigating the predominance of speculation over enterprise in the United States (§ VI).
- Making purchases permanent "like marriage" might force long-term thinking. But this meets a dilemma. Each investor's belief that his commitment is "liquid", though this cannot be true for all investors collectively, calms his nerves and makes him much more willing to run a risk (§ VI).
- If purchases were illiquid, new investment might be seriously impeded while hoarding (holding savings as idle money) or lending are open alternatives. Keynes adds that, so long as they are, capital assets cannot be made attractive enough (especially to owners who do not manage them) except by markets where they can easily be realised for money (§ VI).
- The "only radical cure" for crises of confidence would give the individual no choice between consuming his income and ordering a specific capital asset. He might then consume more when in doubt. That would avoid the "cumulative" repercussions of spending on neither (§ VI).
- Writers who stress the dangers of hoarding had something similar in mind, but overlooked that the phenomenon can occur without any commensurate change in hoarding of money (§ VI).

### VII. Animal spirits
- Apart from speculation, a large proportion of positive activity depends on spontaneous optimism. Most, probably, of our decisions to act, with consequences that unfold over many days, result from "animal spirits", a spontaneous urge to action, not a weighted average of benefits times probabilities. Enterprise only pretends to itself that it is mainly moved by the statements in its prospectus; it is based on exact calculation of future benefits only a little more than an expedition to the South Pole is (§ VII).
- If spontaneous optimism falters and we depend on mathematical expectation alone, enterprise will fade. Fears of loss may be no more reasonable than the earlier hopes (§ VII).
- Keynes says enterprise based on hopes stretching into the future benefits the community, but individual initiative is adequate only when calculation is supported by animal spirits (§ VII).
- Unfortunately, slumps are exaggerated in degree, and prosperity is excessively dependent on a political and social atmosphere congenial to business. Fear of a Labour Government or a New Deal may depress enterprise without calculation or plot (§ VII).
- **Caveat:** not everything depends on irrational waves. The state of long-term expectation is often steady, and other factors compensate when it is not (§ VII).

### VIII. Mitigating factors and conclusion
- Compound interest and obsolescence mean many investments' prospective yield is "legitimately dominated" by the near future (§ VIII).
- Buildings: risk can frequently be transferred to or shared with occupiers through long-term contracts. Public utilities: yield is largely guaranteed by monopoly privileges and permitted rates. Public authorities: they invest on a presumption of social advantage, but the rate they pay may still decide the scale (§ VIII).
- So the interest rate still has, in normal circumstances, a great but not a decisive influence on investment. Only experience can show how far managing it can sustain the right volume of investment (§ VIII).
- Keynes is "somewhat sceptical" of a merely monetary policy. He expects the State, which can judge capital's marginal efficiency on long views and general social advantage, to take ever greater responsibility for directly organising investment. He gives the reason that it seems likely that market estimates of MEC will fluctuate too much for practicable interest-rate changes to offset (§ VIII).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A share is priced at 40. Your long-term estimate of its worth "for keeps" is 50. You think that in three months the market will price it at 35 with probability 0.6, or at 45 with probability 0.4.
- Expected resale price: 0.6 × 35 + 0.4 × 45 = 21 + 18 = 39.
- Expected gain from buying to resell: 39 − 40 = −1. So a pure resale-minded trader would not buy, though the share looks 10 below your estimate of its worth (50 − 40 = 10).
- If you instead believed 0.4 chance of 35 and 0.6 chance of 45: 14 + 27 = 41, gain +1. Now you buy, because of the guessed crowd move, not the long-term value.
Both cases show the § V point: the decision follows the forecast of market opinion, not the forecast of yield.

## Concepts
- [[conventional expectations]] — assume the present state continues unless specific reasons point to change; not a belief that it will (§ III)
- [[animal spirits]] — spontaneous urge to action that probably drives most positive decisions, rather than weighted-average calculation (§ VII)
- [[beauty contest / higher-order beliefs]] — picking what you expect others to pick; "third degree" is guessing average opinion's expectation of average opinion (§ V)
- [[Knightian uncertainty]] — related idea (canon link; not stated in this unit): Keynes says no basis exists for a calculated mathematical expectation of distant yields (§ III)
- [[state-of-confidence]] (new) — confidence in the best forecast; matters through its effect on the MEC schedule (§ II)
- [[state-of-credit]] (new) — lenders' confidence toward borrowers; its strengthening is necessary but not sufficient for recovery (§ V)
- [[speculation-vs-enterprise]] (new) — forecasting market psychology versus forecasting asset yield over its whole life (§ VI)

- [[marginal efficiency of capital]] (new) — link between an asset's supply price and its prospective yield; defined in the previous chapter (§ I)

## Questions this raises
- If professionals mostly guess opinion, what keeps prices tied to yields at all? Keynes says the state of expectation is often steady, but how does that happen?
- Would a transfer tax cut speculation without cutting useful liquidity? Keynes states the dilemma but does not say how the two effects would balance.
- What exactly do "genuine" long-term expectation and a "genuine change in the news" mean when long-term knowledge is said to be nearly negligible?
- If the State takes over direct organisation of investment, how would it make its long-view calculations of the MEC?
