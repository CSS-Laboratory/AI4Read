# The Propensity to Consume: II. The Subjective Factors

## L0 — One line
Because subjective motives to save change slowly and objective factors such as the interest rate are often of secondary importance in the short run, short-run consumption largely depends on income; and a rise in the interest rate, unless investment demand offsets it, lowers actual saving because it lowers investment and hence income.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks which subjective and social motives govern how much of a given income is spent. He lists the motives of individuals and of institutions, then takes them as given because they change only slowly. With objective factors such as the interest rate often of secondary importance, short-run changes in consumption largely depend on changes in income. A higher interest rate may raise saving out of a given income, but (unless investment demand offsets it) it cuts investment, and saving is governed by investment. Income therefore falls until saving falls too, so actual saving is lower. He then qualifies this: if interest were managed to keep continuous full employment, thrift (the wish to save) would matter again.

**Prerequisites:** saving = income − consumption; investment as spending on capital goods; the interest rate as the cost of borrowing.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Eight subjective motives make individuals refrain from spending: Precaution, Foresight, Calculation, Improvement, Independence, Enterprise, Pride, Avarice (§ I, para. 2–3).
- Governments, institutions and corporations withhold a large amount of income (perhaps one-third to two-thirds of total accumulation in a modern industrial community), mainly for four motives that are largely analogous to, but not identical with, those of individuals (§ I, para. 4).
- Negative saving (consumption above income) also occurs; unemployment relief financed by borrowing is best regarded as negative saving (§ I, para. 5).
- The strength of the motives depends on institutions, habits and the distribution of wealth. Keynes takes the background as given because it changes slowly (§ I, para. 6).
- Because the subjective background changes slowly and objective factors such as the interest rate are often of secondary importance in the short run, short-period changes in consumption largely depend on changes in income, not on changes in the propensity to consume (the amount consumed out of a given income) (§ II, para. 1).
- Moderate interest-rate changes usually have little effect on the propensity to consume. Their influence on the amount actually saved is of paramount importance, but in the opposite direction to that usually supposed (§ II, para. 2).
- Aggregate saving is governed by aggregate investment, so a higher interest rate (unless offset by a change in the investment demand schedule) lowers investment and so lowers income until saving falls by as much as investment. Saving and spending both fall (§ II, para. 2–4).
- Keynes first says virtue and vice play no part in actual saving and spending, then calls this an overstatement. If the interest rate were governed so as to maintain continuous full employment, the rate of capital accumulation would depend on the weakness of the propensity to consume (§ II, para. 5–6).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. The catalogue of subjective factors
**Scope.** This is the second category of factors behind consumption out of a given income. It covers subjective and social incentives, given income in wage-units and given the objective factors already discussed. Keynes says the analysis raises no point of novelty, so it may be sufficient to give a catalogue of the more important motives without long discussion (§ I, para. 1).

**Individual motives for not spending (§ I, para. 2–3).**
1. Reserve against unforeseen contingencies (Precaution).
2. Provision for an expected future gap between income and needs, such as old age, education or dependents (Foresight).
3. Enjoying interest and appreciation, i.e. preferring larger later consumption to smaller immediate consumption (Calculation).
4. Enjoying a gradually increasing expenditure, since people like to expect a rising standard of life (Improvement).
5. A sense of independence and power to act, without a definite plan (Independence).
6. A *masse de manoeuvre* (a pool of funds) for speculative or business projects (Enterprise).
7. Bequeathing a fortune (Pride).
8. Pure miserliness: unreasonable inhibitions against spending as such (Avarice).

A corresponding list of motives to consume could also be drawn up: Enjoyment, Shortsightedness, Generosity, Miscalculation, Ostentation, Extravagance (§ I, para. 3).

**Institutional saving.** A large amount of income, varying perhaps from one-third to two-thirds of total accumulation in a modern industrial community such as Great Britain or the United States, is withheld by central and local government, institutions and business corporations. Their motives are largely analogous to, but not identical with, those of individuals, and are mainly the four following (§ I, para. 4):
- Enterprise: fund investment without debt or new market capital.
- Liquidity: hold resources for emergencies and depressions.
- Improvement: secure a rising income, which also shields management from criticism, because income rising from accumulation is seldom told apart from income rising from efficiency.
- Financial prudence: provide more than user and supplementary cost, so as to repay debt and write off assets ahead of wastage and obsolescence. Its strength depends mainly on the quantity and character of the capital equipment and the rate of technical change.

**Negative saving.** Several motives for saving have a later counterpart in negative saving, for example saving for family needs or old age. Unemployment relief financed by borrowing is best regarded as negative saving (§ I, para. 5).

**Taking the background as given.** The strength of all these motives varies with institutions, habits (race, education, convention, religion, morals), hopes and experience, capital equipment and the distribution of wealth. Keynes will not, except in occasional digressions, treat far-reaching social change or slow secular progress. He takes the main background of motives as given. The distribution of wealth too can be taken as given, so far as it is determined by the more or less permanent social structure, since it is subject only to slow change over a long period (§ I, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Many different reasons for saving exist, and their strength varies with institutions, habits and the distribution of wealth, which change slowly. Keynes therefore takes the background as given.

### II. Consequences for short-period analysis
**Main conclusion.** The subjective background changes slowly, and the short-run influence of the interest rate and other objective factors is often of secondary importance. So short-period changes in consumption largely depend on changes in the rate at which income (in wage-units) is earned, not on changes in the propensity to consume out of a given income (§ II, para. 1).

**A guard against misunderstanding.** This says that moderate interest-rate changes usually have a small effect on the *propensity* to consume. It does not say they have a small effect on the *amounts* saved and consumed. The effect on actual saving is "of paramount importance", but in the opposite direction to that usually supposed (§ II, para. 2).

**The argument (§ II, para. 2–3).**
1. Suppose a higher rate of interest, by offering a larger future income, lowers the propensity to consume.
2. Even so, a rise in the rate of interest reduces the amount actually saved. Aggregate saving is governed by aggregate investment.
3. A rise in the interest rate, unless offset by a change in the investment demand schedule, diminishes investment.
4. So incomes must fall to a level at which saving decreases by as much as investment.
5. Incomes fall by a greater absolute amount than investment. Consumption therefore falls, but this does not widen the margin for saving: saving and spending both decrease.
6. The same line of argument can tell us the size of the fall, other things equal. Incomes must fall (or be redistributed) by just the amount that, with the existing propensity to consume, cuts saving by the same amount as the higher interest rate cuts investment, given the existing marginal efficiency of capital (a term this unit uses without defining). Keynes defers the detail to the next chapter.

**Thrift is penalised (§ II, para. 4).** A higher rate might make people save more if incomes stayed unchanged. But if it retards investment, incomes "must necessarily fall" until the falling capacity to save offsets the stimulus to save. The more determinedly thrifty and obstinately orthodox we are in national and personal finance, the more our incomes must fall when interest rises relatively to the marginal efficiency of capital. "Obstinacy can bring only a penalty and no reward" (§ II, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Aggregate saving is governed by aggregate investment, so the adjustment comes through income, not through the wish to save. A higher interest rate (unless offset) lowers investment, and income falls until saving has fallen by as much.

**First conclusion and Keynes's correction.** Keynes first says actual saving and spending do not depend on the eight motives, and "Virtue and vice play no part". Everything depends on how far the interest rate favours investment, given the marginal efficiency of capital (§ II, para. 5). He then corrects himself: "No, this is an overstatement." If the interest rate were governed so as to maintain continuous full employment, "virtue would resume her sway", and the rate of capital accumulation would depend on the weakness of the propensity to consume (§ II, para. 6). The classical economists' tribute to thrift is due to their concealed assumption that the interest rate is always so governed (§ II, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Keynes first says virtue and vice play no part, then calls that too strong. If the interest rate were governed so as to maintain continuous full employment, the weakness of the propensity to consume would again determine the rate of capital accumulation.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume saving = investment, and saving $S=(1-c)Y$, where $c$ is the share of income consumed.
- Low interest rate: $I=200$, $c=0.8$. Then $0.2Y=200$, so $Y=1000$, $S=200$, $C=800$.
- Higher rate: $I$ falls to 150, with $c$ unchanged. Then $Y=150/0.2=750$, $S=150$, $C=600$. Income falls by 250, more than the fall in investment (50).
- Now suppose thrift also rises, $c=0.75$. Then $0.25Y=150$, so $Y=600$, $S=150$, $C=450$.

Saving is 150 in both high-interest cases and below the original 200, even though people save more out of a given income. This matches the text: saving and spending both fall (§ II, para. 2).

## Concepts
- [[propensity-to-consume]] (new) — the amount consumed out of a given income, shaped by objective and subjective factors (§ I, para. 1)
- [[subjective-motives-to-save]] (new) — eight individual and four institutional motives, treated as slowly changing (§ I, para. 2–6)
- [[negative-saving]] (new) — consumption exceeding income; borrowed unemployment relief is best regarded as negative saving (§ I, para. 5)
- [[marginal-efficiency-of-capital]] (new) — a factor, used but not defined in this unit, that is taken into account with the interest rate in judging how far interest favours investment (§ II, para. 2–4)
- [[saving-governed-by-investment]] (new) — aggregate saving is set by aggregate investment, so income adjusts (§ II, para. 2)
- [[social-dilemma]] — here, a later reading and not Keynes's wording: a more thrifty community sees its income fall when interest rises relative to the marginal efficiency of capital (§ II, para. 4)

## Canonical instances
- [[interest-rise-lowers-saving]] (new) — a higher interest rate cuts investment, income and hence actual saving (§ II, para. 2–3)
- [[thrift-penalty]] (new) — the more thrifty and orthodox the community, the larger the income fall (§ II, para. 4)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Thrift lowers aggregate income ↔ social dilemma: individually sensible saving gives a collectively worse result, though Keynes's mechanism runs through income, not payoffs.
- [metaphor] Classical "concealed assumption" of full employment ↔ equilibrium: results depend on which adjustment variable (interest rate or income) is assumed to move.

## Questions this raises
- How exactly does income have to fall? Keynes defers this to the next chapter (§ II, para. 3).
- What does "governing" the interest rate to hold full employment require in practice (§ II, para. 6)?
- If the motives change slowly, which "far-reaching social changes" would alter the conclusions (§ I, para. 6)?
- Why are the objective factors of secondary importance in the short run (§ II, para. 1)?
