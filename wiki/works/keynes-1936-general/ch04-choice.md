# The Choice of Units

## L0 — One line
For analysing the whole economy, Keynes proposes using only two homogeneous units (units whose parts are alike, so they can be added), money-value and labour-hours (weighted by pay), and keeping vague aggregates such as output as a whole, capital equipment as a whole and the general price-level only for rough historical comparison.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Keynes asks which units of quantity suit a causal theory of the whole economic system. Aggregates such as net national output, capital equipment and the general price-level mix unlike goods or are admittedly vague, so exact measurement is not possible. He therefore keeps them for rough historical description. Causal theory uses money-value and the quantity of employment. Employment is counted in labour-units, with skilled hours weighted by their pay. Supply conditions can then be expressed through an aggregate supply function that relates employment to expected proceeds, with no need to total physical outputs. Keynes proposes these units for dealing with the theory of employment.

**Prerequisites:** supply curve and elasticity; the idea of an index or weighted sum; function notation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- This chapter and the next three are a digression to clear up three perplexities: units of quantity, the role of expectation, and the definition of income. Only the first is treated here (§ I, paras 1–2).
- The national dividend (net output), the netting of new equipment against worn-out equipment, and the general price-level are unsatisfactory as exact units. Output is a non-homogeneous complex, strictly measurable only in special cases. Pigou's deduction for "normal" obsolescence covertly brings in value changes. The price-level is admittedly vague (§ II, paras 2–5).
- These "conundrums" do not enter business decisions or the causal sequence of events. Keynes says it is natural to conclude that they are not only imprecise but unnecessary, and that quantitative analysis must avoid vague expressions (§ II, para 6).
- Such aggregates belong to historical and statistical description, where approximate comparisons can have significance within limits. Using such vague concepts as the basis of quantitative analysis would give only a mock precision (§ II, paras 7–8).
- For all firms together one can speak accurately only of employment applied to a given capital equipment. For description or rough comparison, employment on a given equipment is presumed to be a satisfactory index of output (§ III, para 1).
- The two units are money-value (strictly homogeneous) and employment, measured in labour-units (one hour of ordinary labour counts as 1; special labour is weighted by its pay), so far as relative pay is more or less fixed. This gives $E = N \times W$ (§ III, para 2).
- Skill differences are handled by pay-weighting if pay is proportional to efficiency, or else as one factor in the equipment's diminishing returns (each extra worker adds less output). The homogeneous labour-unit causes no difficulty unless relative pay is highly unstable, and even then Keynes says it can be dealt with (§ III, para 3).
- Supply conditions are expressed by the aggregate supply function $Z_r = f_r(N_r)$, where $Z_r$ is the expected proceeds, net of user cost, that induce employment $N_r$. Employments $N_r$ can be summed, but outputs $O_r$ cannot (§ IV).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. Purpose of the digression
- This chapter and the next three are a digression. They clear up perplexities that are not peculiar to Keynes's main theme. He treats them because he finds existing treatments inadequate for his enquiry (§ I, para 1).
- The three perplexities that impeded him are: (1) units of quantity for the economic system as a whole, (2) the part played by expectation, (3) the definition of income (§ I, para 2).

### II. Why the usual units are unsatisfactory
- **(i) National dividend.** Keynes reports the definition of Marshall and Pigou. It measures the volume of current output (real income), not its money value. It depends, in some sense, on *net* output: additions to resources after allowing for wastage of the opening capital stock (§ II, para 2).
  - Objection (Keynes): output is a non-homogeneous complex. Strictly it can be measured only in special cases, for instance when one output contains all items in the same proportions as another (§ II, para 2).
- **(ii) Net capital addition.** Computing net output requires comparing new equipment with equipment that has perished (§ II, para 3).
  - **Pigou's view:** deduct obsolescence "as may fairly be called 'normal'", meaning depletion regular enough to be foreseen at least in the large (§ II, para 3).
  - Keynes's criticism: the deduction is not in money terms. Pigou therefore assumes a change in physical quantity without any physical change, so he covertly introduces value changes. He also has no satisfactory formula to compare new and old equipment when technique has changed (§ II, paras 3–4).
  - Keynes believes Pigou's concept is the right one for analysis. He holds that its precise definition is impossible until a satisfactory system of units exists. He says the problems of comparing outputs and of setting new equipment against wastage are conundrums that, he can confidently say, have no solution (§ II, para 4).
- **(iii) General price-level.** Its admitted vagueness makes it unsatisfactory for a causal analysis that ought to be exact (§ II, para 5).
- **Keynes's conclusion.** These difficulties are rightly seen as "purely theoretical": they never enter business decisions or the causal sequence of events, which is determinate despite the quantitative indeterminacy of these concepts. Keynes says it is natural, therefore, to conclude that they not only lack precision but are unnecessary. He adds that quantitative analysis must be expressed without quantitatively vague expressions, and hopes to show that one can get on much better without these concepts (§ II, para 6).
- Incommensurable collections of objects cannot themselves supply material for quantitative analysis, but this need not stop approximate statistical comparisons based on broad judgment, which may have significance and validity within certain limits (§ II, para 7). The proper place for net real output and the price-level is historical and statistical description, where perfect precision is neither usual nor necessary (§ II, para 8).
  - Example: saying that net output today is greater but the price-level lower than ten years ago is a proposition like the statement that Queen Victoria was a better queen but not a happier woman than Queen Elizabeth. It has meaning and interest but is unsuitable for the differential calculus (§ II, para 8).
  - Keynes adds that precision will be a mock precision if such partly vague, non-quantitative concepts are used as the basis of quantitative analysis (§ II, para 8).

*Intuition note.* You cannot add loaves, ships and haircuts into one exact number. Keynes accepts such totals as rough pictures of history. He does not want them to carry causal reasoning that claims to be exact.

### III. The two units
- **Decision setting.** An entrepreneur decides the scale on which to work a *given* capital equipment. When expected demand rises (the aggregate demand function shifts up), firms are induced to associate more employment with their equipment (§ III, para 1).
- For one firm or industry with a homogeneous product, speaking of output is legitimate. For all firms together one can speak accurately only of employment applied to a given equipment. The concepts of output as a whole and its price-level are not required here, since no absolute measure of aggregate output is needed that would let us compare it with the output from a different equipment and a different quantity of employment (§ III, para 1).
- For description or rough comparison, one relies on a presumption: employment on a given equipment is a satisfactory index of output. The two are presumed to move together, though not in a fixed numerical proportion (§ III, para 1).
- **The units** (§ III, para 2):
  - Quantities of money-value, which are strictly homogeneous.
  - Quantities of employment, which can be made homogeneous. Take one hour of ordinary labour as the unit and weight special labour by its remuneration, provided relative remuneration is more or less fixed. An hour paid at double rates counts as 2 units.
  - The *labour-unit* is the unit of employment. The *wage-unit* is the money-wage of one labour-unit. With $E$ the wages-and-salaries bill, $W$ the wage-unit and $N$ the quantity of employment:
$$E = N \times W$$
- **Is homogeneity defensible?** Keynes argues as follows (§ III, para 3):
  1. If pay is proportional to efficiency, skill differences are handled by counting workers in proportion to their pay.
  2. If a firm must hire labour less suited to it per wage-unit as output grows, this is one factor among others behind diminishing returns from the equipment. Keynes says the non-homogeneity is subsumed in the equipment.
  3. If no surplus of specialised labour exists and less suitable labour raises cost per unit of output, then returns from the equipment diminish more rapidly than if there were a surplus.
  4. In the limiting case of labour so specialised that units cannot substitute for each other, the elasticity of supply of output from that equipment falls suddenly to zero once all the specialised labour is employed.
  5. The assumption therefore involves no difficulty unless relative pay of different labour-units is highly unstable. Even then, Keynes says it can be dealt with by supposing a rapid liability to change in labour supply and in the shape of the aggregate supply function.
- **Which unit for which purpose** (Keynes's belief that much unnecessary perplexity can then be avoided). Limit the whole-system analysis to money and labour. Use units of particular outputs and equipments only for analysing individual firms or industries in isolation. Use vague aggregates only for historical comparison that is avowedly imprecise and approximate within limits that may be fairly wide (§ III, para 4).
- Changes in current output are measured by hours of labour paid for on the existing equipment, whether the product serves consumers or becomes new capital. Skilled hours are weighted by pay. To predict how entrepreneurs with a given equipment respond to a shift in aggregate demand, one need not compare output, living standards or price levels across dates or countries (§ III, para 5).

*Intuition note.* Because capital equipment is taken as given, the thing firms actually choose is how many hours to employ. Counting hours, with skilled hours weighted by pay, gives a clean measure without totalling physical goods.

### IV. Supply conditions in the two units
- The usual supply curve and elasticity of supply can be expressed through the aggregate supply function, with no reference to quantities of output. This holds for a firm, an industry or the whole of industry. For firm $r$ (§ IV, formulas):
$$Z_r = f_r(N_r)$$
  Here $Z_r$ is the proceeds, net of user cost, whose expectation induces employment $N_r$.
- If employment $N_r$ yields output $O_r = y_r(N_r)$, the ordinary supply curve follows, with $U_r(N_r)$ the expected user cost at that employment (§ IV, formulas):
$$p = \frac{Z_r + U_r(N_r)}{O_r} = \frac{f_r(N_r) + U_r(N_r)}{y_r(N_r)}$$
- For each homogeneous commodity, where $O_r = y_r(N_r)$ has a definite meaning, $Z_r = f_r(N_r)$ can be evaluated in the ordinary way. The $N_r$ can then be aggregated, but the $O_r$ cannot, since $\sum O_r$ "is not a numerical quantity" (§ IV, last para).
- If in a given environment aggregate employment is distributed between industries in a unique way, so that $N_r$ is a function of $N$, further simplifications are possible (§ IV, last para).

*Intuition note.* Each industry's supply price can still be written in the usual way. Only employment adds up across industries, because outputs of different goods have no common unit. Keynes's aggregation therefore runs through $N$.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two firms. Firm A makes 50 loaves. Firm B makes 30 metres of cloth. "80 units of output" would be meaningless.

Labour: Firm A uses 60 hours of ordinary labour at £1/hour. Firm B uses 20 hours of ordinary labour and 10 hours of skilled labour paid £2/hour.
- Labour-units: A = 60. B = 20 + 10×2 = 40. Total $N = 100$.
- Wage-unit $W$ = £1. Wages bill = 60×1 + 20×1 + 10×2 = £100, which equals $N \times W = 100 \times 1$.

Supply price for A: say expected $Z_A = 90$ and $U_A = 10$. Then $p = (90 + 10)/50 = £2$ per loaf. The $N$'s add (60 + 40 = 100). The outputs (50 loaves, 30 metres) do not.

## Concepts
- [[labour-unit]] (new) — one hour of ordinary labour; special labour is weighted by its pay, so double pay counts as 2 (§ III, para 2).
- [[wage-unit]] (new) — money-wage of one labour-unit; $E = N \times W$ (§ III, para 2).
- [[aggregate-supply-function]] (new) — $Z_r = f_r(N_r)$: expected proceeds net of user cost that induce employment $N_r$ (§ IV).
- [[user-cost]] (new) — expected cost $U_r(N_r)$ that is added to $Z_r$ to get the supply price; the text does not define it further (§ IV).
- [[non-homogeneous-output-aggregate]] (new) — output of the whole economy is a mixed complex, not exactly measurable, so unfit for exact causal analysis (§ II, para 2).
- [[national-dividend]] (new) — Marshall–Pigou term for the volume of current output or real income (not money-income), which depends in some sense on net output (§ II, para 2).
- [[expectation]] — not developed in this chapter. It appears only in the expected proceeds that induce employment and in the expectation of increased demand (§ III, para 1; § IV).

## Canonical instances
- [[pigou-normal-depreciation]] (new) — Pigou's deduction for obsolescence that may fairly be called normal, which Keynes uses to show that changes in value are covertly introduced into a physical measure (§ II, para 3).
- [[victoria-elizabeth-comparison]] (new) — a meaningful but non-calculable comparison, used as an analogy for the statement that net output is greater but the price-level lower than ten years ago (§ II, para 8).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Aggregating employment but not outputs ↔ statistics/index numbers: summing only commensurable quantities, or weighting by a common value measure.
- [structural-analogy] Weighting skilled hours by pay ↔ quality-adjusted or efficiency-weighted labour input: a weighted sum with prices as weights.
- [metaphor] "Mock precision" ↔ measurement in the social sciences: spurious exactness from indices built on incommensurable parts.

## Questions this raises
- When relative pay is unstable, how well do pay weights track true differences in labour efficiency?
- Is employment a good enough index of output when technique changes? Keynes says only that the two move together, not proportionally.
- Can statistical aggregates that Keynes assigns to "description" ever safely feed into causal models?
- How does the aggregate supply function later combine with expectations of demand? (The chapter introduces only the supply side.)
