# Schelling, T. C. (1971). *Dynamic models of segregation*. Journal of Mathematical Sociology, 1(2), 143–186. https://doi.org/10.1080/0022250X.1971.9989794

## In one paragraph
Schelling asks how strong segregation can arise when nobody organizes it. He uses simple models in which individuals move if too few of their neighbors are like them. First come "tabletop" experiments with counters on a line and on a checkerboard. Then he builds a model of one shared, bounded neighborhood, where each person leaves if the other group's share passes a personal limit. Even mild demands, such as wanting about half of one's neighbors to be alike, produce neighborhoods far more homogeneous than anyone required. Outcomes depend on group sizes, vacancies and how tolerance is distributed, and the same results can come from integrationist or separatist preferences. So we cannot read individual motives off aggregate patterns. Tipping is often hard to pin to one point. Collective results need explicit analysis of how individual decisions combine.

## The path through the work
- [[schelling-1971-segregation/main-p1]] — Individuals who only mildly prefer not to be a local minority, and who move when dissatisfied, can produce strong collective segregation that nobody demanded (pp. 143, 150–151, 158).
- [[schelling-1971-segregation/main-p2]] — Individual tolerance limits, applied to a shared area, can give one or several self-reinforcing equilibria, and which one is reached depends on group sizes, tolerance schedules and starting conditions, not on average tolerance alone.

## Big ideas across units
- **Micromotives vs. macrobehavior:** aggregate outcomes need not match what individuals intend or want. Part 1 uses analogies and the counter experiments. Part 2 says collective results need explicit analysis of how decisions combine. (main-p1, main-p2)
- **Mild preferences, strong segregation:** a modest demand for like neighbors gives striking separation. Part 1 shows this on the line and checkerboard. Part 2 adds that a pure wish for three like neighbors segregates about as strongly as a demand for majority status. (main-p1, main-p2)
- **Motives cannot be inferred from patterns:** segregation can emerge without a segregationist plan. Part 2 shows that integrationist and separatist preferences both fit the model. (main-p1, main-p2)
- **Equilibrium and stability:** movement continues until no one wants to move. Part 2 asks which equilibria are stable: mixed ones, or only all-white or all-black ones. (main-p1, main-p2)
- **Group size and composition matter:** minorities form absolutely larger clusters and live in denser, more fully occupied territory (about 83% vs about 64%). Stable mixtures depend on the schedules and their relative sizes. (main-p1, main-p2)
- **Thresholds, nonlinearity and tipping:** the outcome rises rapidly as the demand goes from about one-third to one-half. In the bounded-neighborhood model a tipping point is often unclear and need not sit at the typical tolerance value. (main-p1, main-p2)

## How to read it
- **Essential:** part 1 (main-p1). It gives the core emergence result and the checkerboard rules that part 2 builds on.
- **Part 2 depends on part 1.** It assumes the star/zero checkerboard rules, and it adds curves, cumulative distributions and stable equilibria.
- **Part 2 is the one to read for tipping,** the bounded-neighborhood model and stability. On a first pass, a reader mainly after the emergence message could skim its detailed curve cases (Fig. 18 and the discussion of limits and quotas). The material does not mark any units as skippable.
- The page citations in part 2 (pp. 13–36) do not match the paper's stated range of 143–186. They appear to follow a different page numbering.
