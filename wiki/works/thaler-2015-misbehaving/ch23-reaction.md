# The Reaction to Overreaction

## L0 — One line
To save the no-free-lunch part of market efficiency, defenders had to explain the higher returns of Loser and value stocks as compensation for risk; Thaler argues that beta does not support this, and Fama and French conceded they had no theory for why their size and value "factors" are risks.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Once Loser stocks were shown to beat the market, the efficient market hypothesis (EMH) could survive only if they were riskier. Fama's "joint test" point says every such test also tests a risk model. The standard model, the CAPM, measures risk by beta. By beta, Thaler and De Bondt's Losers were less risky than the Winners. Fama and French then declared the CAPM dead but kept market efficiency, adding size and value factors, for which they conceded they had no theory of risk. They later announced a five-factor model that adds profitability and investment. Thaler thinks value stocks are not riskier. Fama says risk versus overreaction cannot be settled.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of average returns and risk; correlation; diversification (holding many assets).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Tests of "you cannot beat the market" are joint tests of efficiency and a risk-return model. A high return may therefore be read as mispricing or as a reward for risk (p. 178).
- Under the CAPM, only a stock's correlation with the market (beta) is rewarded with extra return in a rational world (p. 179).
- In De Bondt–Thaler's tests the Winners had higher beta (1.37) than the Losers (1.03). Risk adjustment made the anomaly bigger (p. 180).
- Value stocks, by various measures, outperformed growth stocks and were also less risky by beta (p. 180).
- Fama and French called the CAPM dead (1996) but kept efficiency, adding size and value factors (p. 180–181).
- Those factors were chosen because they work empirically, not because a theory says they are risks (p. 181).
- Thaler is convinced by Lakonishok–Shleifer–Vishny that value stocks are not riskier. Fama and French are not, and even Fama concedes the question cannot be settled (p. 181).
- Thaler's closing view: in a rational world only beta would matter, and beta is dead (p. 181–182).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The joint hypothesis (p. 178–179)
- With the facts confirmed, the only way to save the no-free-lunch part of the EMH (it is impossible to beat the market) was a technicality. Beating the market by taking more risk does not violate the EMH. The difficulty is how to measure risk (p. 178).
- **Fama's point (Thaler endorses it):** every test of no-free-lunch is a "joint test" of market efficiency and some model of risk and return (p. 178).
- Example: if new firms outperform old firms, one could say they are riskier and the extra return is compensation (p. 178).
- The same argument applies to Graham, Basu, Dreman and others who found that value stocks were good investments. The question becomes whether the findings show mispricing (against the EMH) or risk (p. 178).
- Footnote: by "mispricing" Thaler means a price that will predictably move so that a "free lunch" is possible. De Bondt and he had no conclusive evidence that Loser prices diverged from intrinsic value, only that Losers earned higher returns (p. 182, note *).

*Intuition note:* A higher return can be read as mispricing or as pay for risk, and which reading fits depends on how risk is measured. Thaler says the difficulty lies in knowing how to measure risk (p. 178).

### Bankruptcy risk and the CAPM (p. 179)
- Bankruptcy risk was already handled. A delisted stock was "sold" at the price obtainable on another exchange, or recorded as a total loss (p. 179).
- At the time, the proper risk measure was the CAPM (Lintner and Sharpe). In it, the only rewarded risk is how far a stock's return is correlated with the rest of the market (p. 179).
- If stocks move independently, their movements tend to cancel in a portfolio. If they are positively correlated, diversification helps less (p. 179).
- That correlation is **beta**. Beta 1.0 means moves are proportional to the market. Beta 2.0 means a 10% market move gives about a 20% move in the stock. Zero beta means no correlation (p. 179).
- Footnote: this beta is unrelated to the beta in the beta–delta present-bias models (p. 182, note †).

### Test against beta (p. 179–181)
- The EMH could be reconciled if Losers had high betas and Winners low betas (p. 179).
- Thaler and De Bondt had checked and found the opposite. With three-year formation and three-year test periods, the average beta was 1.37 for Winners and 1.03 for Losers (p. 180).
- So adjusting for risk "made our anomalous findings even more anomalous" (p. 180).
- The same holds for other value measures, such as low price/earnings or low price to book value. Value stocks outperformed growth stocks and were less risky by beta (p. 180).

*Intuition note:* The risk story would have worked if Losers had high betas. They had lower betas than Winners, so by the CAPM's own measure Losers looked less risky, and the puzzle got worse.

### Fama and French (p. 180–181)
- The value effect was "officially true" only after Fama and French published similar findings. They were partly provoked by Thaler's findings and by Banz's small-firm effect (p. 180).
- From 1992 they documented that value and small stocks earned more than the CAPM predicts. In 1996 they declared the CAPM dead ("The CAPM Is Wanted, Dead or Alive") (p. 180).
- They kept market efficiency and proposed the **three-factor model**: beta plus two factors, size and value. Value stocks' returns are correlated with each other, and so are small-cap stocks' (p. 180–181).
- They conceded they had no theory for why size and value should be risk factors. The CAPM was meant as a normative theory based on rational investors. The new factors were used because research showed they matter (p. 181).

### The continuing debate (p. 181–182)
- **Thaler's view:** there is no evidence that small or value portfolios are observably riskier than large growth portfolios. Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (1994, "Contrarian Investment, Extrapolation, and Risk") settled the question for him. It also convinced its authors, who founded the successful value-investing firm LSV Asset Management (p. 181).
- It did not convince Fama and French. Even Fama concedes it is impossible to say whether value returns are due to risk or overreaction (p. 181).
- Fama and French announced a five-factor model. The new factors are profitability (predicts high returns) and aggressiveness of investment (predicts low returns). Profitability is a trait Graham sought. Thaler finds it hard to tell a plausible story in which profitable firms are riskier than loss-making ones (p. 181).
- Many practitioners would add a sixth factor, momentum: firms that did well over the last 6–12 months tend to keep doing well over the next 6–12 months (p. 181).
- **Conclusion (Thaler's belief):** in a rational world only beta would matter, and beta is dead. The other factors "would all be SIFs" in a world of Econs (p. 181–182). The text does not define SIF in these pages.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume a simple risk rule: required excess return = beta × market excess return (our assumption). The market's excess return is 6%.
- Losers: beta 1.0, so required = 1.0 × 6% = 6%. Observed excess return 10%. Unexplained = 10 − 6 = +4%.
- Winners: beta 1.4, so required = 1.4 × 6% = 8.4%. Observed 7%. Unexplained = 7 − 8.4 = −1.4%.
- Raw gap Losers − Winners = 10 − 7 = 3%. Gap after risk adjustment = 4 − (−1.4) = 5.4%.

Adjusting for beta widens the anomaly, as in the chapter. A "defender" must then offer another risk measure. That is the joint-test problem.

## Concepts
- [[joint-hypothesis-problem]] — a test of "no free lunch" also tests a risk-return model, so a rejection is ambiguous (p. 178)
- [[efficient-market-hypothesis]] — here, the no-free-lunch part: beating the market is impossible without extra risk (p. 178)
- [[capm-and-beta]] — only correlation with the market (beta) is rewarded; beta 2.0 means double the market's moves (p. 179)
- [[diversification-and-covariance]] — independent stocks' movements cancel in a portfolio, correlated ones do not (p. 179)
- [[mispricing-predictable-drift]] (new) — Thaler's usage: a price that predictably moves enough to give a free lunch (p. 182, note *)
- [[value-and-size-premium]] (new) — value and small stocks earn more than the CAPM predicts (p. 180–181)
- [[multifactor-model]] (new) — beta plus extra factors (size, value, profitability, investment, and for many practitioners momentum); Fama and French conceded no theory for why size and value are risk factors (p. 181)

## Canonical instances
- [[winner-loser-portfolios]] — Losers had beta 1.03, Winners 1.37, so risk adjustment worsens the anomaly (p. 180)
- [[fama-french-three-factor-model]] — CAPM plus size and value factors, with no theory behind them (p. 180–181)
- [[lsv-contrarian-investment-1994]] — paper that Thaler says shows value stocks are not riskier (p. 181)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Joint hypothesis ↔ statistics: testing a claim jointly with a model assumption, so rejection may blame the model.
- [structural-analogy] Adding factors to match returns ↔ regression: adding regressors raises fit, but without theory it may only describe the data.
- [metaphor] Diversification cancelling independent moves ↔ portfolio theory: covariance, not individual volatility, drives portfolio risk.

## Questions this raises
- What would count as independent evidence that a factor is a risk, rather than a label for an anomaly?
- If risk and overreaction cannot be told apart by returns alone, what other data could separate them?
- Why would momentum (a sixth factor) be a risk rather than a mispricing?
