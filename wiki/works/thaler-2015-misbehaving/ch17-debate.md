# The Debate Begins

## L0 — One line
At the 1985 Chicago conference, behavioral economics got its first major public hearing in a debate with rationalist economists, and even a leading rationalist (Miller) conceded that a behavioral model best describes how firms pay dividends.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler describes the October 1985 Chicago conference, held on rationalist home ground. Tversky and Kahneman presented violations of economic principles, such as the Asian disease problem (same options, different wording, different choices). Arrow argued that optimization is neither necessary nor sufficient for good economic theory. Thaler, as discussant, used humor and ended with two false statements both sides should reject. Miller's paper explained why firms should not pay dividends under the tax code, yet admitted they do, and that a "behavioral" model best fits their payouts. Miller still defended rational market models. Shiller replied that he tends to see behavioral extensions as, in a sense, enhancing the efficient-market models, and that a revolution need not abandon the assumption of rational expectations. Both sides were confident they had won; the finance debate was only beginning.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of tax on income vs. capital gains; what a share buyback is; the "Econ" (fully rational agent) idea.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The conference put behavioralists (Simon, Tversky, Kahneman, Arrow) against Chicago rationalists (Lucas, Miller, Fama) on the rationalists' home ground (p. 118).
- The Asian disease problem shows that logically identical options are chosen differently depending on wording (p. 119).
- Arrow: rationality (optimization) is neither necessary nor sufficient for good theory; it needs auxiliary assumptions, and theorists assume of agents what they themselves struggle to do (pp. 120–121).
- Thaler's "Conference Handbook" listed the standard objections with retorts, hoping to pre-empt them, and ended with two statements he called false: that rational models are useless, and that all behavior is rational (p. 122).
- The Miller–Modigliani irrelevance theorem (under certain assumptions, payout form does not matter) and the life-cycle hypothesis (household consumption depends only on total wealth) both treat money as fungible, i.e. interchangeable. Thaler says this is wrong for the life-cycle hypothesis and questionable in corporate finance (p. 123).
- Under 1985 U.S. taxes, firms should not pay dividends, yet most did; Shefrin and Statman explained this by self-control and mental accounting (mentally labeling some money as spendable "income") (pp. 123–124).
- Miller conceded that a "behavioral" model (Lintner's) best describes dividend policy, yet said his paper aimed to show that rational market models are "alive and well", or at least in no worse shape than comparable models (pp. 124–125).
- Shiller: he tends to view behavioral extensions of efficient-market models (the standard rational model of financial markets) as, in a sense, enhancing them; he could teach them with more relish if he could describe them as extreme special cases of more realistic models (p. 126).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Setting (p. 118)
- Organizers: Robin Hogarth (psychologist) and Mel Reder (economist), at the University of Chicago, October 1985. The aim was to sort out whether psychology and behavioral economics deserved serious attention (p. 118).
- Behavioral team: Simon, Tversky, Kahneman, with Arrow. Younger behavioralists (Shiller, Zeckhauser, Thaler) were discussants. Rationalist captains: Lucas and Miller. Fama and Rosen moderated but, Thaler says, sided with the rationalists (p. 118).

### Tversky and Kahneman's paper (p. 119)
- **Asian disease problem.** 600 people are sick. Group 1 chooses between A: 200 saved for sure, and B: a one-third chance all 600 are saved, a two-thirds chance all die. Most choose A.
- Group 2 chooses between C: 400 die for sure, and D: a one-third chance nobody dies, a two-thirds chance all die. A majority choose D (p. 119).
- A equals C and B equals D, so preferring A to B but D to C is illogical. A similar problem posed to physicians gave the same results (p. 119).
- Kahneman also presented the Ultimatum and Dictator Game work. Thaler reports that economists called fairness a silly concept and brushed aside survey data. The games with real money were somewhat more troubling, but the stakes were small, so the usual excuses were available (p. 119).

> **Intuition note:** The two versions describe the same outcomes, one as lives saved and one as deaths. If the wording alone flips the choice, the choice is not being driven by the outcomes alone, which is why Thaler calls it not logical.

### Arrow's talk (pp. 119–121)
- Thaler's summary: rationality (meaning optimization) is neither necessary nor sufficient to do good economic theory (p. 120).
- **Not necessary.** Arrow rejected the view that without rationality "there can be no theory". A rigorous habit theory is possible: after a price change, the consumer picks the affordable bundle closest to her previous one. (Thaler adds that even a theory of maximizing the letter K in brand names could be formal.) So, Thaler concludes, the rationality assumption should not be defended on the ground that there are no alternatives (p. 120).
- **Not sufficient.** To get useful predictions, theorists add auxiliary assumptions, such as identical utility functions. Thaler adds that this assumption is demonstrably false and leads to predictions inconsistent with the facts (p. 120).
- **Inconsistency.** Theorists work for months on a hard problem, then blithely assume their agents behave as if they can solve the same problem: "scientific analysis imputes scientific behavior to its subjects" (p. 120). Arrow ended by accepting Simon's insight that rationality is bounded (p. 121).

> **Intuition note:** In Thaler's summary of Arrow, optimization is not the only possible basis for formal theory, and on its own it delivers few useful predictions.

### Thaler as discussant (pp. 121–122)
- Thaler discussed papers by Simon, Kahneman–Tversky, and Einhorn–Hogarth. He mostly agreed with them, so he chose humor (p. 121).
- He built on Stigler's "Conference Handbook", which numbers tiresome comments, based on a prison joke where inmates number the jokes (p. 121).
- Thaler's "Psychology and Economics Conference Handbook" listed the usual objections with retorts, for example: (1) with high enough stakes people will get it right; (2) people will learn in the real world; (3) errors cancel in aggregate (p. 122).
- He closed with two false statements: "Rational models are useless" and "All behavior is rational." Both sides tend to misstate the other's views, so agreeing these are false saves time (p. 122).

### Day two: Modigliani, Miller and dividends (pp. 122–125)
- News came that Modigliani had won the Nobel Prize, primarily for the life-cycle hypothesis. Miller joked that he and Modigliani had shown that moving a ten-dollar bill between pockets leaves wealth unchanged, adding that they had proved it rigorously (pp. 122–123).
- The joke refers to the **irrelevance theorem**: under certain assumptions, it does not matter whether a firm pays a dividend, repurchases shares, or reduces debt. It fits the life-cycle hypothesis too, where consumption depends on wealth, not on how it is held. Both assume money is fungible. Thaler says this was wrong for the life-cycle hypothesis and equally questionable in corporate finance (p. 123).
- **The puzzle.** The theorem assumes no taxes. In the U.S. of that time, income (including dividends) was taxed at rates up to 50% or more, while capital gains were taxed at 25%, and only when realized. A firm can turn a dividend into a capital gain by repurchasing shares. So firms should not have paid dividends, but most large firms did (p. 123). Tax-exempt holders would be indifferent (p. 124).
- **Shefrin and Statman's answer:** self-control plus mental accounting. Some shareholders, such as retirees, like inflows labeled "income" that they can spend without guilt. A rational retiree could sell some shares instead and pay less tax. The rule of spending the income and leaving the principal alone was strong among retirees who had lived through the Great Depression (p. 124).
- **Miller's response.** In his talk, he said behavioral explanations might fit his Aunt Minnie and a few others, and no further (p. 124). His written paper was milder: mostly a clear tutorial on why firms should not pay dividends (p. 124).
- Miller agreed that the model that best describes dividend decisions is Lintner's, which he called "behavioral". Firms raise dividends only when confident they will not need to cut them later. Lintner built it by interviewing chief financial officers. Miller noted no one had derived it as the solution to a maximization problem in thirty years (p. 124).
- Miller's stated purpose was to show that rationality-based equilibrium models are "alive and well—or at least in no worse shape" than comparable models (p. 125). He also said stories behind individual holdings (family quarrels, legacies, divorce settlements) are left out of models because they may be too interesting and distracting, not because they are uninteresting (p. 125). Thaler says he could not tell which side Miller was arguing (p. 125).

> **Intuition note:** In Thaler's reading, Miller's paper gave the behavioral side support: a theory that predicts "no dividends" fails, and a model Miller called behavioral fits. Miller's defense was that models should abstract from individual stories to capture the pervasive market forces.

### The Kleidon–Shiller session and aftermath (pp. 125–126)
- The afternoon session, chaired by Fama, paired Miller with Kleidon, who attacked a Shiller paper (discussed in chapter 24). Shiller was discussant with Roll and Ross. Shefrin and Statman could only heckle. Thaler calls the deck stacked (p. 125).
- Kleidon and Miller both referred to Kuhn's model, where paradigms change only after enough anomalies are accepted. Shiller replied that a revolution need not abandon rational expectations "in favor of mass psychology" (p. 126).
- Shiller: he tends to view behavioral extensions of efficient-market models as leading, in a sense, to their enhancement. He could teach them with more relish if he could describe them as extreme special cases before moving to the more realistic models (p. 126).
- Both sides were confident they had won. The finance debate continued for the next thirty years (p. 126).
- Footnote (printed on p. 126, attached to the Great Depression remark on p. 124): foundations and endowments long left the principal alone and spent only the "income", which tended to push them toward high-dividend holdings. They later moved to spending a set percentage (say 5%) of a three-year moving average of value, which let them invest in assets like venture capital (p. 126).

## Toy example (ours)
An investor holds shares, and the firm has $100 to return to her. Use the chapter's rates: dividends taxed at 50%, capital gains at 25% (p. 123).
- **Dividend:** $100 × (1 − 0.50) = $50 after tax.
- **Buyback:** her shares rise in value by $100. When she realizes that $100 gain by selling, it is taxed at 25%, so $100 × (1 − 0.25) = $75. If she delays selling, the tax is delayed too (p. 123).
- **Tax-exempt investor:** $100 either way, so she is indifferent.

A taxpaying Econ prefers the buyback ($75 > $50), so dividends are a puzzle. A retiree who spends only "income" avoids selling shares and prefers the dividend (p. 124).

## Concepts
- [[mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion]] — mental accounting: labeling money (e.g., "income") makes spending feel different; Thaler also mentions loss aversion in connection with dividend cuts (p. 124)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — Simon's insight, accepted by Arrow, that rationality is limited (p. 121)
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — Thaler calls it the standard rational model of financial markets and says the strongest statement Miller could muster was that it was "not quite dead" (p. 125)
- [[rational expectations]] — named in Shiller's reply as what a revolution need not abandon (p. 126)
- [[framing effect]] (new) — logically equivalent options chosen differently depending on wording (p. 119)
- [[irrelevance theorem]] (new) — Miller–Modigliani: under certain assumptions, payout form does not matter (p. 123)
- [[fungibility of money]] (new) — assumption that money is interchangeable regardless of where it is held or how it is paid out (p. 123)
- [[dividend puzzle]] (new) — firms pay dividends although the tax code makes them costly to taxpaying shareholders (pp. 123–124)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Arrow's habit-based consumer ↔ bounded rationality / heuristics: choosing the closest affordable bundle replaces full re-optimization.
- [structural-analogy] Kuhn's anomaly-accumulation model ↔ philosophy of science: Kleidon and Miller invoked it to suggest the revolution was not yet upon us, and Shiller replied in its terms.
- [metaphor] Shiller's "special cases" ↔ nested models: efficient-market models as a limiting case of richer behavioral models.

## Questions this raises
- If a "behavioral" model fits dividends best, what would an optimizing derivation of Lintner's model need to assume?
- Does Arrow's argument that rationality is not sufficient apply equally to behavioral models, which also need auxiliary assumptions?
- Would changing tax rates change dividend payouts, and how would that test the Shefrin–Statman story?
- Can Shiller's "special case" view be tested against Miller's "alive and well" view?
