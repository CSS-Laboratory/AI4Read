# The Gauntlet

## L0 — One line
Thaler lists the standard one-line objections economists used to dismiss behavioral findings ("as if", incentives, learning, markets) and sketches his first replies.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Before starting at Cornell in 1978, Thaler expected to meet repeated put-downs of his research, which he called the Gauntlet. Each had a first reply. "As if": theory must describe ordinary people, not experts. "Incentives": higher stakes did not remove preference reversals (preferring A to B and also B to A). "Learning": practice is rare for big decisions, so errors may be more likely at high stakes. "Markets": no logic shows that markets make people rational. Thaler says a snappy reply to "as if" was not enough; he needed hard empirical evidence, and later studies of real markets can now be cited. The chapter ends with his first publication and his two next topics.

**Prerequisites:** marginal cost = marginal revenue (ch. 4, as recalled on p. 13); the idea of a rational optimizing agent; the distinction between descriptive and normative.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Thaler faced a standard set of objections, the Gauntlet. Thaler says economists would resist change if for no other reason than that they had invested years building their own corner of the field (p. 12).
- "As if": Friedman held that a theory should be judged by its predictions, not by its assumptions' realism. For many economists this settled the matter for decades (pp. 13–15).
- Thaler's reply to "as if": economics is meant to be a theory of everyone, and typical people do not act like experts. Choosing a dominated option (one worse in every way) cannot be "as if" correct (p. 15).
- Incentives: the claim that higher stakes fix errors was "unsupported by any evidence". In Grether and Plott's experiment, real money made preference reversals more frequent and more severe (pp. 16–17).
- Learning needs frequent practice and immediate feedback, so big, rare decisions give little chance to learn. The learning and incentives objections therefore partly contradict each other (pp. 18–19).
- Markets: no logical route leads from competition to rational individuals, and most errors are not fatal (p. 20). A better version is that prices stay rational even if people do not; Thaler calls it plausible but wrong (p. 21).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 12).** Thaler accepted the Cornell job, starting August 1978. He had two tasks: produce research showing what the new approach could teach, and answer one-line put-downs. One well-known economist candidly asked what he should do, since his skill was solving optimization problems. Thaler reads his point this way: if optimization models were poor descriptions of behavior, that economist's toolkit would be obsolete. Thaler named the list of objections "the Gauntlet".

**As if (pp. 13–16).**
- After World War II, Arrow, Hicks and Samuelson made theory more mathematical. Two central ideas stayed: agents optimize and markets reach a stable equilibrium (p. 13).
- Marginal analysis: a profit-maximizing firm sets price and output where marginal cost equals marginal revenue. It hires workers until the last worker's cost equals the revenue that worker adds (p. 13).
- Lester (Princeton, late 1940s) asked manufacturers how they decide hiring and output. None reported anything like equating at the margin. They did not seem to think about the effect of price changes, and did not appear to think wage changes would affect hiring or output much. They tried to sell as much as possible and adjusted the workforce to demand (p. 13). Lester wrote that his paper "raises grave doubts" about conventional marginal theory (p. 14).
- **Machlup's reply:** economists are not interested in what people say they do. Actions would approximate the theory's predictions without explicit calculation, like a driver passing a truck. Thaler notes that Machlup criticised Lester's data but offered none of his own (p. 14).
- **Friedman's view:** a theory should be judged by the accuracy of its predictions, not the realism of its assumptions. His example is the expert billiard player who plays "as if" he knew the formulas. Friedman says his confidence in this hypothesis does not rest on a belief that players do the calculations, but on the belief that, unless they were in some way capable of reaching essentially the same result, they would not be expert players (p. 14).
- The AER ended the debate and economists stopped worrying about realism. Thaler says "as if" is still used to dismiss unwelcome results (pp. 14–15).
- **Thaler's reply:** Kahneman and Tversky showed that people do not act "as if" rational. Choosing a dominated option, one worse in every way, cannot be "as if" correct. Rosett's wine buying could not be called rational either (p. 15).
- His first behavioral paper was titled "Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice", in homage to Friedman. Its argument: economics should be a theory of everyone, not only experts. Typical bar players aim at the ball nearest a pocket and often miss (p. 15).
- A snappy retort was not enough. He would need hard empirical evidence (p. 15).
- Survey evidence is dismissed as "mere", yet polls used by skilled statisticians predict elections well. The jobs numbers and the unemployment rate also come from surveys (pp. 15–16). In 1980 survey questions would not overcome the "as if" grunt; proper data on real-life choices were needed (p. 16).

*Intuition note:* An expert might act "as if" he knew the formulas, like the billiard player. But Thaler says theories of ordinary shoppers and savers had better not assume they behave as if they were experts.

**Incentives (pp. 16–17).**
- The objection: with higher stakes people would think harder and get it right. Kahneman and Tversky's experiments often had nothing at stake, and lab stakes were typically a few dollars. Thaler calls the objection unsupported and notes that nothing in economic theory limits it to large stakes (p. 16).
- Lichtenstein and Slovic found preference reversals. Economic theory needs well-defined preferences, meaning people consistently know what they like. Without them there is nothing to optimize (p. 17).
- The gambles were the p bet (97% chance to win $10) and the $ bet (37% chance to win $30). Most subjects preferred the p bet when asked to choose. Asked for the lowest selling price, a majority of these subjects demanded more for the $ bet. This implies p is preferred to $ and $ to p (p. 17).
- Grether and Plott hypothesised that incentives drove this, so they ran the experiment for real money. The frequency and severity of reversals increased (p. 17).
- This did not end the objection, but it was one paper against the claim that money solves the problems (p. 17).
- Footnote: they kept this hypothesis even though Lichtenstein and Slovic had replicated the result with real money in a Las Vegas casino. They also considered whether psychologists' reputation for deception explained the results (p. 22).

*Intuition note:* More money does not automatically produce more consistent choices. In this one experiment it produced less.

**Learning (pp. 18–19).**
- The objection: one-shot lab tasks differ from the real world, where people practise (p. 18).
- Thaler's reply: learning needs frequent practice and immediate feedback. Life is not *Groundhog Day*, where one day repeats and variables can be changed one at a time. Thaler says many of life's problems do not offer these opportunities (p. 18).
- Hence the learning and incentives arguments are "to some extent, contradictory" (p. 18).
- **Binmore's version (Thaler's account):** Binmore said he would consult Thaler's research if running a supermarket, where it might possibly matter, but that it would be of little relevance at a car dealership, since at high stakes people would get stuff right (p. 18).
- **Thaler's reply, the "Binmore continuum":** goods are ordered by purchase frequency, from cafeteria lunch (daily) and milk and bread (twice a week) to sweaters, cars, homes, careers and spouses (not more than two or three per lifetime for most of us). Retirement saving is done once. Thaler says Binmore had it backward: because learning takes practice, we are more likely to get things right at small stakes. Critics must choose an argument: if learning is crucial, quality is likely to fall as stakes rise (p. 19).

*Intuition note:* Big decisions are rare, so there is little practice. That is why they may go wrong more often than small ones.

**Markets: the invisible handwave (pp. 19–21).**
- At a conference organized by Michael Jensen of the Rochester business school, Amos Tversky had Jensen describe the foolish decisions of his wife and students. Amos then asked why Jensen assumes the agents in his models are geniuses (pp. 19–20).
- **Jensen's reply:** "you just don't understand." He gave a speech that Rochester people attributed to Friedman, which began "Suppose there were people doing silly things … and those people had to interact in competitive markets, then …". Thaler says he has not been able to find such an argument in Friedman's writings (p. 20).
- Thaler calls it the "invisible handwave", the vague claim that markets discipline misbehaviour. He sees no logical route to the conclusion that markets make people rational. Sunk-cost errors, loss aversion and overconfidence in business are generally not fatal. The market "cannot make you rational" (p. 20).
- Experts: the high-stakes version says people hire experts. But true experts without conflicts of interest are hard to find. Thaler calls it illogical to think that someone not sophisticated enough to choose a good portfolio will somehow be sophisticated about finding a financial adviser (p. 20).
- Competition: this argument has some merit but is "vastly overrated". GM was badly run for decades yet was long the largest car company, and a bailout helped it survive 2009. Thaler concludes that competitive forces apparently are slow-acting (p. 21).
- Thaler also offers a more coherent version: market prices may be rational even if individuals are not. He calls it plausible, perhaps compelling, but wrong. The reasons come in Section VI (p. 21).
- Now one can cite studies of real people interacting at high stakes in markets, including financial markets (p. 21).

**Arriving at Cornell (pp. 21–22).**
- Thaler finished two papers in California: one on the List and "An Economic Theory of Self-Control". "Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice" was rejected by six or seven major journals. In hindsight he says he was not surprised: the paper had plenty of ideas but little hard evidence (pp. 21–22).
- It appeared in the inaugural issue of the *Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization* (p. 22).
- For tenure he would need top-journal papers. His two next topics were mental accounting (household spending and saving psychology) and self-control, i.e. choosing between now and later (p. 22).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrating the Binmore continuum, with invented purchase counts over a 40-year buying life:
- Cafeteria lunch, once per working day: about 5 days × 50 weeks × 40 years = 10,000 purchases.
- Sweaters, about 2 per year: 2 × 40 = 80 purchases.
- Cars, one every 8 years: 40 ÷ 8 = 5 purchases.
- Home, assumed 3 purchases.
- Retirement saving, once (the text says "exactly once", p. 19).

Practice falls from 10,000 to 1, so the chance to learn by feedback falls steeply as the stakes rise. This is the point of the continuum.

## Concepts
- [[as-if-defense]] (new) — theory is defended by its predictions even if people do not really optimize (p. 13)
- [[well-defined-preferences]] (new) — people consistently know what they like; stable preferences are what theory optimizes (p. 17)
- [[preference-reversal]] (new) — a subject says A is preferred to B and also B to A; in the gamble study, subjects chose the p bet but demanded more to sell the $ bet (p. 17)
- [[incentives-objection]] (new) — claim that higher stakes remove errors (p. 16)
- [[learning-objection]] (new) — claim that errors disappear with practice in real life; learning needs frequent practice and immediate feedback (p. 18)

- [[equilibrium]] — one of economics' two central concepts, with optimization (p. 13)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Friedman's "as if" ↔ economics-finance: the efficient market hypothesis, where realistic assumptions matter less than predictive success — both defend a model by its outputs.
- [metaphor] The Gauntlet ↔ the joint-hypothesis problem: rejecting a model can always be blamed on an auxiliary assumption.
