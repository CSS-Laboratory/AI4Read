# Going Public

## L0 — One line
Thaler tells how "libertarian paternalism" became *Nudge*: policy can reduce people's predictable errors without removing their freedom to choose, and wording and design matter.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How should government policy respond if people make systematic mistakes, if at all? Thaler and Sunstein answer with "libertarian paternalism": help people reach their own goals without restricting their choices. Because people are Humans, not Econs (who already do the right thing), they make predictable errors. Small features of the environment (a fly in a urinal, bumpy lane markers) can reduce those errors. Thinking of policy as "choice architecture", like product design, gave them an organizing principle. In organ donation, research led them to prefer asking people at license renewal ("prompted choice") over presumed consent (you are a donor unless you opt out). A naming episode ("mandated" versus "prompted" choice) shows that words matter.

**Prerequisites:** default options (what happens if you do nothing); the idea of a rational "Econ" versus a real person; basic opt-in/opt-out logic.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Several groups were asking at the time how systematic errors should affect policy, if at all. Examples: "asymmetric paternalism", "cautious/optimal paternalism" (p. 277).
- "Paternalism" here means helping people reach their own goals. "Libertarian" means doing so without restricting choices. The authors say the term is not an oxymoron (p. 278).
- Nudging is not a claim to solve everything. Some bans and mandates are inevitable. The authors' stated objective is to influence choices so that choosers are better off "as judged by themselves" (p. 279).
- Thaler defines a nudge as a small environmental feature that draws attention and changes behavior; it works on Humans, not Econs (p. 280).
- Rereading Don Norman's design book gave Thaler the missing organizing principle; the authors then adopted the term "choice architecture" (p. 280).
- Organ donation: defaults are powerful, but a deeper look led the authors to conclude that presumed consent was not the best policy. Thaler favors asking at license renewal (pp. 281–282).
- The label "mandated choice" drew an objection from a state official. "Prompted choice" is more accurate and less charged (p. 282).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Origins of the idea (pp. 277–278)
- Thaler tells Cass Sunstein about his term "libertarian paternalism". It is more constructive than Sunstein's "anti-anti-paternalism" (p. 277).
- Related work: Camerer, Loewenstein, Rabin, O'Donoghue and Issacaroff's "Asymmetric Paternalism". Their definition: a regulation is asymmetrically paternalistic if it "creates large benefits for those who make errors, while imposing little or no harm on those who are fully rational" (p. 277).
- Rabin and O'Donoghue had earlier coined "cautious paternalism", later "optimal paternalism". The shared question was how systematic mistakes should affect government policy, if at all (p. 277).
- Peter Diamond, AEA president-elect, organized behavioral sessions for the January 2003 meeting, including one on paternalism. Thaler and Sunstein wrote a five-page paper there. Sunstein expanded it into a law review article of over forty pages (p. 277).
- Thaler then asked whether there was a book in it. Sunstein loved the idea (p. 278).
- **Premise:** in a complicated world people cannot be expected to have the expertise to make anything close to optimal decisions in all the domains in which they are forced to choose, yet we all enjoy choosing for ourselves, even if we sometimes make mistakes. Are there ways to make it easier for people to make what they will deem to be good decisions, before and after the fact, without explicitly forcing anyone to do anything? (p. 278)
- **Definitions:** paternalism means trying to help people achieve their own goals. Giving accurate subway directions counts. "Libertarian" means helping without restricting choices (p. 278). A footnote notes a law professor's reply, "Libertarian Paternalism Is an Oxymoron" (Mitchell, 2005) (p. 282).
- "Nudge" was suggested by an editor who then declined the book. A prestigious but sleepy university press took it; its skill set, they later learned, did not include marketing. Success had to come by word of mouth, and trade paperbacks came later (p. 278).

*Intuition note:* The word "paternalism" usually means telling people what to do. Here it means trying to help people reach their own goals. The word "libertarian" then adds that this is done without restricting choices.

### Scope and premise (pp. 279–280)
- Nudging does not solve every problem. Some bans and mandates are inevitable. Examples: compulsory schooling, bans on assault, driving-side rules. Even ardent libertarians agree you may not shoot a neighbor (p. 279).
- The goal was limited: see how far helping without ordering can go (p. 279).
- **Premise:** people are Humans, not Econs, so they make predictable errors. If errors can be anticipated, policies can reduce them (p. 279).
- **Example:** bumpy center-line strips wake a drowsy driver. Reflective bumps are better, because they also help navigation in the dark (p. 279).
- Thaler complains that critics think the authors know what is best for everyone. The book states the goal as to "influence choices in a way that will make choosers better off, as judged by themselves" (p. 279). Thaler's view: most people want a comfortable retirement, but the choice is theirs. The aim is to reduce what people would themselves call errors (p. 279).

*Intuition note:* The standard of success is the chooser's own judgment, not the planner's. The aim is to reduce what people would themselves call errors.

### The fly and choice architecture (p. 280)
- Schiphol airport urinals carry an etched housefly. Airport management reported "spillage" fell by about 80%. Thaler knows of no careful empirical analysis. Variations appear elsewhere (p. 280).
- Thaler's definition: a nudge is a small environmental feature that attracts attention and influences behavior. It is effective for Humans, not Econs, who already do the right thing. Nudges are "supposedly irrelevant factors" that make us better off (p. 280).
- The authors could recognize good nudges but lacked an organizing principle. Rereading Don Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things* gave one. Thaler's iPhone needed no manual (p. 280).
- They adopted "choice architecture": user-centered choice environments. It gave a checklist of principles, many borrowed from the design literature. Good policy design resembles consumer-product design (p. 280).

*Intuition note:* Thaler's point is that policy can be designed like a consumer product: easy for real people to use. Choice architecture is the name for that design approach, aimed at reducing errors.

### Choosing topics: a dead end and a changed view (pp. 280–282)
- Some topics led nowhere. A Hurricane Katrina chapter was cut. Its one interesting idea was John Tierney's: offer those who stay a permanent marker to write their Social Security number on their body (pp. 280–281).
- **Organ donation.** Johnson and Goldstein had shown the power of defaults. Most countries adopt some version of opt-in, where donors must take a positive step. Some European countries, such as Spain, use opt-out "presumed consent". Where the default is donor, almost no one opts out. Under opt-in, often less than half opt in (p. 281).
- The first thought was to switch to presumed consent. Digging deeper changed this view (p. 281):
  - Most presumed-consent countries do not apply it strictly. Staff still ask the family (p. 281).
  - The question often comes at a time of severe emotional stress. The family may have no idea what the donor's wishes were, since most people do nothing (p. 281).
  - Not filling out an opt-out form is "not a strong indication" of belief (p. 281).
- They preferred the Illinois variant, also used in other U.S. states. At license renewal people are asked and the answer is recorded at once (pp. 281–282). Alaska and Montana have reached donation rates above 80% (p. 282). Most states pair this with "first person consent" laws, so that the family need not decide (p. 282, footnote).

### The naming episode (p. 282)
- The literature called the policy "mandated choice", and the book used the term (p. 282).
- After Thaler's *New York Times* column, a *USA Today* writer called Illinois's secretary of state, Jesse White. He denied that any such policy existed. His objection was to "mandated": no one is required to answer, and silence is recorded as a no (p. 282).
- Thaler's reading: White knew voters dislike mandates. Thaler now says "prompted choice", which is more accurate and less charged: "When dealing with Humans, words matter" (p. 282).
- Footnote: Thaler suggests the Obama health law's unpopular "mandate" could have been replaced. He would favor automatic enrollment with opt-out, plus a bar on buying a policy for a period such as three years after opting out (p. 282).

## Toy example (ours; numbers assumed)
Assume 1,000 license holders. After reflection, 700 would want to be donors and 300 would not.
- **Opt-in:** suppose only 50% of those who want to donate (350) register. Errors: 700 − 350 = 350 people who want to donate but are not registered.
- **Opt-out:** suppose 5% of the 300 non-donors (15) fail to opt out. Errors: 15 people are registered against their wishes. The 700 donors are all registered.
- Errors fall from 350 to 15, and everyone remains free to choose. This only shows how defaults change error counts. The chapter's own caution is that a silent default is a weak sign of wishes, which is why it prefers asking.

## Questions this raises
- How can we check that a nudge helps people "as judged by themselves", when their own judgments may be shaped by the nudge?
- If the Schiphol fly lacks careful evidence, what evidence should a nudge need before it is adopted?
- Could a default or prompt designed with good intentions be abused by someone with other goals?
- Which topics will need mandates rather than nudges?
