# The Endowment Effect

## L0 — One line
Thaler found evidence that people value things already in their endowment (what they own) more than things that are only available to them, a pattern he tied to people feeling opportunity costs less than cash costs.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
While estimating the value of a life for his thesis, Thaler asked people two questions: what they would pay to remove a small death risk, and what they would demand to accept the same risk. Theory says the answers should be nearly equal. Typical answers were far apart, about $2,000 versus $500,000. He then found the same pattern elsewhere: a wine collector who neither sells nor buys, credit-card "discount" versus "surcharge" wording, and free tickets that some people use and others sell. He named it the "endowment effect". Thaler links several of these cases to opportunity costs: giving up a sale does not hurt as much as paying cash. At this point Thaler "had no idea what to do with it".

**Prerequisites:** opportunity cost (value of the best alternative given up); the idea of an "Econ", a fully rational agent; basic risk as a probability.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Schelling's "identified life" versus "statistical life": we act strongly for named victims and weakly for abstract ones, though Econs would not (pdf 24–25).
- Thaler's standard thesis method: in a world of Econs riskier jobs would have to pay more (otherwise no one would do them), so wages and mortality data imply a value per life. The current estimate is roughly $7 million (pdf 26).
- Willingness to pay (WTP) for a risk cut and willingness to accept (WTA) a risk rise should be nearly equal. Typical respondents gave A and B answers that were not even close to equal (pdf 27).
- Thaler says logical consistency, not only theory, demands that the two answers be equal (pdf 27). A wealth effect (a difference in how well off you are in the two scenarios) can technically make them differ, but he says it cannot explain gaps of this size (marker on pdf 28, footnote text on pdf 30).
- Thaler's wine-cellar example shows the same pattern. Forgone sale revenue (an opportunity cost) hurts less than out-of-pocket payment (pdf 28).
- Discount versus surcharge are identical to an Econ but not to consumers. This is later called "framing" (pdf 29).
- Name: "endowment effect" — Thaler's finding suggested people valued things already owned more than things available but not yet owned. The ticket story shows it in practice (pdf 29–30).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: thesis and Schelling (pdf 24–25).**
- Thaler was a modest graduate student at Rochester. His advisor Sherwin Rosen said "We did not expect much of him" (pdf 24). His thesis, "The Value of a Life", used a completely standard approach (pdf 24).
- Schelling's essay contrasts a sick girl who needs an operation (an "identified life") with a barely perceptible rise in preventable deaths from hospital cuts (a "statistical life"). Money flows to the first, not the second (pdf 24–25).
- Typical public decisions are abstract. Example: a wider highway median costs $42 million and prevents 1.4 fatal accidents per year for thirty years (pdf 25).
- In a world of Econs, society would not pay more to save one identified life than twenty statistical lives (pdf 25).
- The right question, per Schelling, is how much highway users would pay to make each trip a bit safer. No one had a way to answer it (pdf 25).
- Zeckhauser's Russian roulette: a 1,000-chamber gun with 4 bullets, one pull. How much would Aidan pay to remove one bullet? This is elegant but gives no data (pdf 25).

**Wage-risk method (pdf 25–26).**
- Thaler's idea: Econs would require riskier jobs to pay more, compensating for the risk. Matching occupational mortality rates to wages then implies the price of risk (pdf 26).
- His father Alan, an actuary, supplied a Society of Actuaries book of mortality data. Rosen became advisor, and they co-wrote "The Value of Saving a Life" (pdf 26).
- Updated versions are still used in government cost-benefit analyses; the current estimate is roughly $7 million per life saved (pdf 26).

*Intuition note:* Wage differences reveal choices that trade money for risk. So, if workers behave as Econs would, the price of risk can be estimated from occupational mortality and wage data without asking anyone to play Russian roulette.

**The two questions (pdf 26–28).**
- Thaler also asked hypothetical questions. WTP: how much to cut your death risk by 1 in 1,000? WTA: how much to take on the same added risk? A fifty-year-old US resident faces about 4 in 1,000 per year (pdf 26–27).
- Version A: you may have been exposed to a rare fatal disease with a 1-in-1,000 chance. What is the most you would pay for an antidote that removes the risk? Version B: what is the least you would demand to expose yourself to the same 1-in-1,000 risk, with no antidote? (pdf 27).
- Theory predicts nearly equal answers. Moving the risk from .005 to .004 should be worth about the same as moving from .004 to .005 (pdf 27).
- Result: answers were "not even close" to equal. Typical: at most $2,000 in A, at least $500,000 in B. Many said they would not take part at any price in B (pdf 27).
- Logic: the A answer implies the rise .004→.005 costs at most $2,000. The B answer implies it costs at least $500,000. Both cannot hold (pdf 27).
- Footnote: a wealth effect can make the answers differ technically. Thaler says it cannot explain gaps of this size, and telling A-respondents they had been given, say, $50,000 did not remove the gap (footnote ‡: marker on pdf 28, text on pdf 30).
- Thaler's report: Rosen told him to stop wasting his time and get back to the thesis; to an economist the findings were "somewhere between puzzling and preposterous". Thaler says the logic is inescapable, though many people resist it even when it is explained. Thaler was hooked and found similar cases everywhere (pdf 28).

*Intuition note:* The same risk change is priced very differently depending on whether you are buying safety or selling safety. That is logically inconsistent, and the gap is far too big for a wealth effect.

**Examples from everyday life (pdf 28–29).**
- Rosett's wine: bought for $10, now worth over $100, and Woody would buy at that price. He sometimes drinks a bottle but would not pay $100 for one, and he did not sell (pdf 28).
- Thaler's argument: if he is willing to drink a bottle he could sell for $100, drinking it has to be worth more than $100, so why wouldn't he be willing to buy one? Rosett knew this was irrational (pdf 28).
- Opportunity cost is what you give up. Whether he drinks his own bottle or buys one, it is what Woody would pay. But giving up a sale hurts less than paying cash, because opportunity costs are "vague and abstract" next to cash (pdf 28).
- Credit cards: some merchants wanted to charge credit-card users more. The industry preferred the higher price to be "regular", with a cash "discount", over a cash regular price with a card "surcharge" (pdf 28–29).
- For an Econ, $1.03 versus $1 is the same either way. A surcharge is out-of-pocket; a missed discount is a "mere" opportunity cost. Kahneman and Tversky later called this "framing" (pdf 29).

**Naming and the ticket story (pdf 29–30).**
- Definition: the stuff you own is your endowment. Thaler says he had stumbled on a finding that suggested people valued things already in their endowment more highly than things that were available but not yet owned (pdf 29).
- Tickets often sell below market price. Holders must decide whether to attend or sell; Thaler says "few people other than economists" think about this decision correctly (pdf 29).
- Karlan example: Dean and a divinity-school friend each got two free tickets to a bulls–Wizards playoff game. The friend attended. Dean sold his for several hundred dollars each. Each thought the other's behaviour was "nuts" (pdf 30).
- Thaler's closing line: it was real, but he did not know what to do with it (pdf 30).

*Intuition note:* Dean counted the cash he could get from selling as a cost of attending, while his friend counted the tickets as free; each thought the other's choice was "nuts". Thaler uses the story to show people who do not think about the decision as an economist would (pdf 29–30).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Mia values a game at $150. The market price of a ticket is $200.
- Econ, ticket bought for $200: net value 150 − 200 = −50, so she does not attend.
- Econ, ticket received free: attending forgoes a $200 sale, so net 150 − 200 = −50, so she sells. The decision is the same.
- Endowment-effect thinker with a free ticket: she counts the forgone sale as cost 0, so net 150 − 0 = +150 and she attends. Her stated "pay at most" price (at most $150) and "accept at least" price (above $200) differ, which is the WTP/WTA gap.

## Questions this raises
- If WTA exceeds WTP, which number should a government use for the value of a life?
- Does the endowment effect disappear with experience or market practice, as with ticket resellers?
- Is the effect about ownership itself, or about the pain of a loss versus the pleasure of a gain?
- How large can the gap be before the wealth-effect explanation fails?
