# Buckets and Budgets

## L0 — One line
Many households and organizations treat money as sorted into labelled "buckets", so spending can depend on which bucket the money is in, which can violate the economic principle that money is fungible.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Standard economics says money has no labels (it is "fungible"). Thaler argues that people and organizations often use budgets and mental accounts in practice. Evidence: students' weekly entertainment budgets presumably shaped theater-going, and when gas prices halved, shoppers upgraded to premium gasoline far more than a fungible-money model predicts. Wealth also sits in accounts of different "sacredness": cash is easy to spend, long-term savings are hard. Home equity moved from a sacred account to a leaky one, which helps explain why the housing crash hit spending harder than the tech crash. Budgets are "not entirely silly", since a serious budget plan can make it easier to live within one's means, but they can produce bad decisions.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of utility maximization and "Econs" (fully rational agents); the difference between income, savings and debt; what a mortgage and a 401(k) are.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Economics treats money as fungible (no labels), but households and firms use envelopes, jars and category budgets (p. 40–41).
- Budgets have sensible uses, such as cost control and easier planning, but they can block sensible reallocation (pp. 40–41, 45).
- Heath and Soll: a $50 basketball ticket reduced willingness to buy a theater ticket; a $50 parking ticket did not (p. 41).
- Hastings and Shapiro: after gas prices fell, premium-grade purchases rose fourteen times more than a fungible-money world predicts; milk and orange juice showed no upgrade (pp. 41–42).
- Wealth sits in a hierarchy of mental accounts: cash, checking, savings, then long-term accounts, which are the most sacred. This can lead to saving at low interest while borrowing at high interest (pp. 42–43).
- The "sacred" status of home equity eroded after the 1986 tax change, falling interest rates and mortgage brokers (pp. 43–44).
- Home equity became a driver of consumption, which helps explain why the housing bust hurt more than the tech bust (p. 44).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: envelopes and budgets (pp. 40–41).** Thaler's family interviews showed that many households, especially tight-budget ones, used explicit rules. Cash households used envelopes or mason jars for rent, food and utilities, often a method learned from parents (p. 40). Organizations also have departmental budgets with category limits (p. 40).
- **Fungibility** = money has no label restricting its use. Logic: leftover utilities money spends equally well at a shoe store (p. 40).
- Budgets exist for sensible reasons: a boss does not want to approve every expense, and budgets are a crude cost control that leaves discretion (p. 40).
- Yet an urgent need may go unmet while another budget sits idle. If interests change, labels should be ignored, "But we don't. Labels are SIFs." (pp. 40–41). (SIF is Thaler's shorthand; it is not defined in these pages.)

*Intuition note:* A budget is a crude rule that helps keep costs under control while leaving people discretion, and helps a household live within its means. The cost is that a dollar in the "wrong" bucket is treated as less usable than the same dollar in the "right" one.

**Individuals and the play ticket (p. 41).** Heath and Soll found most MBA students had weekly food and entertainment budgets and monthly clothing budgets. Thaler suspects these relaxed once graduates earned more (p. 41). Two groups were asked whether they would buy a weekend play ticket. One group had spent $50 on a basketball game (same budget); the other had received a $50 parking ticket (different budget). The basketball group was significantly less likely to go, "presumably" because the entertainment budget was already spent (p. 41).

**Gasoline: Hastings and Shapiro (pp. 41–42).** Thaler calls this the most rigorous demonstration of mental budgeting to date.
- *Setting:* gasoline price fell about 50% in 2008, from about $4 to just under $2. The authors had purchase data from a grocery chain that also sold gasoline (p. 41).
- *Econ prediction:* a household spending $80/week on regular at $4 now spends $40. It takes more trips and treats the $40 as extra take-home pay. Some might go to higher grades, but only a minuscule amount; on average, a $1,000/year income rise raises the propensity to buy non-regular gasoline by only 0.1% (pp. 41–42).
- *Human prediction:* with a gas budget (perhaps a mason jar), the household may think "I might as well buy the good stuff" (p. 42).
- *Result:* the shift to higher grades was fourteen times greater than expected if money were fungible (p. 42).
- *Control:* no tendency to upgrade milk or orange juice, which Thaler says further supports the mental-accounting interpretation. He adds that this is not surprising, since families were trying to cut back at the start of the financial crisis. The one exception was upscale gasoline (p. 42).

*Intuition note:* On the mental-accounting reading, the saving on gasoline stayed in the "gas" bucket and was spent there. If it had been treated as general income, it would have been spread across many goods, not concentrated on premium fuel.

**Wealth as mental accounts (pp. 42–43).** Cash is at the bottom of the hierarchy and is the easiest to spend. Checking is slightly harder to reach. Money labelled "savings" is drawn down reluctantly (p. 42).
- This can produce saving at near-zero interest while carrying credit-card debt at over 20% a year. The obvious fix is to repay the card with the savings, but people may be anticipating that the strategy would backfire if they never repaid the money "borrowed" from savings (pp. 42–43).
- The most sacred accounts are long-term ones: retirement and children's education (p. 43).
- 401(k) loans are typically small and repaid within a few years. The bigger leak is job changes: workers offered their balance in cash often take it, especially small balances, despite tax and a 10% surcharge. Thaler says rollover should be made as easy as possible, preferably the default (p. 43).

**Home equity (pp. 43–45).**
- Historically sacrosanct: parents' generation paid mortgages off quickly, and as late as the early 1980s people over sixty had little or no mortgage debt (p. 43).
- *Tax change:* before 1986 all interest was deductible. After 1986 only home mortgage interest was, which encouraged home equity lines of credit. Borrowing on a home to buy a car made sense (often lower, deductible interest), but it eroded the norm (p. 43).
- *Norm destroyed by two further factors:* falling interest rates (double digits to essentially zero) and mortgage brokers, who made refinancing easy and offered extra cash during the housing bubble (pp. 43–44).
- *Data:* households headed by someone aged 75 or older with any mortgage debt rose from 5.8% (1989) to 21.2% (2010). The median amount owed rose from $35,000 to $82,000 (2010 dollars) (p. 44).
- *Consumption:* homeowners spent paper gains in equity "as readily as they would a lottery windfall" (p. 44). Citing Mian and Sufi (*House of Debt*), by 2000 home equity was a strong driver of consumption, especially durables. Car sales jumped where prices boomed and crashed when equity vanished or homes were "underwater" (mortgage exceeding home value) (p. 44).
- *Tech vs housing:* Thaler says this helps explain why the 2000–01 tech bust caused a milder recession. Most non-wealthy households hold stocks only in retirement accounts, which are "still relatively sticky places to keep their money, especially for those with non-trivial account balances" (p. 44).
- Whether the pay-off norm returns is open. If interest rates rise, refinancing becomes less enticing and the norm may resume. Otherwise home equity may stay a "leaky bucket" (pp. 44–45).

*Intuition note:* How freely money is spent depends on its bucket, and buckets can change. Once home equity became spendable, house prices fed directly into consumption.

**Conclusion (p. 45).** Non-fungible budgets are "not entirely silly": a household making a serious plan lives within its means more easily, and the same holds for businesses. But budgets can lead to bad decision-making, for example deciding that the Great Recession is a good time to upgrade the gasoline you buy (p. 45).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A household budgets $80/week for gas. At $4/gal it buys 20 gal regular ($4 × 20 = $80). Price falls to $2: regular for 20 gal costs $2 × 20 = $40, freeing $40.
- *Econ:* treats the $40 as general income. If premium costs $0.30/gal more (invented), upgrading costs 20 × $0.30 = $6. Econ would upgrade only if that is its best use of $6; Thaler says Econs would spend only a minuscule amount on higher grades.
- *Human with a gas jar:* the $80 jar stays labelled "gas". Premium at $2.30 × 20 = $46 fits inside it, so the household upgrades. $46 is $34 below the old $80 bill, so the rest of the jar still feels like "gas money" (used for more driving).
The same $40 saving produces different behavior depending on its label.

## Concepts
- [[fungibility]] (new) — money has no label limiting what it can be spent on; Thaler says the existence of budgets can violate this (p. 40)
- [[mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion]] — here only mental accounting: money is sorted into budgets or accounts with different spendability (p. 42)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — budgets and envelopes as crude rules for cost control and for living within one's means (pp. 40, 45)

## Questions this raises
- How large is the budget effect outside gasoline, and can other price-change settings separate budgeting from a simple taste for quality?
- Would making rollover the default substantially reduce 401(k) leakage?
- If interest rates rise, will the pay-off norm return (as Thaler speculates), or have mortgage brokers and tax rules changed it permanently?
- When do budgets help (self-control, delegation) and when do they harm, and can a rule be designed to keep the benefits?
