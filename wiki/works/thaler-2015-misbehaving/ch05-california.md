# California Dreamin'

## L0 — One line
In 1977–78 at Stanford, Thaler learned from Kahneman and Tversky that asking people hypothetical questions can yield useful evidence, was not ready to call himself primarily an experimental economist, decided to go "all in" on behavioral work, and took a job at Cornell.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler (a memoir voice) tells how he got to Stanford for the year Kahneman and Tversky were there. He watched them finish "Prospect Theory" and discussed methods with Kahneman. He learned that asking people hypothetical questions can reveal behavior. Economists distrust this, but it was the practical way to study losses. He also met the two founders of experimental economics, Vernon Smith and Charlie Plott. Their aims and methods differed from his, so he decided to study "behavior" with mixed methods. He then chose a job market talk on self-control, which led to an offer from Cornell. The chapter shows how a new field's method and Thaler's career took shape.

**Prerequisites:** The idea of an "Econ" (a fully rational agent); expected utility as the standard theory; what a controlled experiment is.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Victor Fuchs, director of the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) office at Stanford, put Thaler on his grant, first for the fall and then until the next summer; Thaler does not know whether Fuchs was intrigued or took pity on him (pp. 5–6).
- Thaler's driving puzzle: speeding saves a lot of time early in a trip but little near the end, which seems to say "slow down gradually"; the footnote answer is to drive the same speed throughout (pp. 6, 11).
- "Prospect Theory" was crystal clear because of Kahneman and Tversky's endless editing and Amos's goal of "getting it right" (p. 7).
- Their method was to test hypothetical choices on themselves, then on students. Economists put little stock in it, yet experiments where subjects could really lose substantial money were nearly impossible to get approved (pp. 7–8).
- The defense of the method: if stated choices are reasonably accurate and are inconsistent with expected utility theory, that creates at least a presumption of doubt about the theory (p. 8).
- Thaler does not think any economist would have come up with prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky's theory that people react differently to losses than to gains), because an unwillingness to rely on hypothetical questions would have kept them from learning the nuances of behavior (p. 8).
- Smith's induced-value experiments (subjects trade tokens with private values set by the experimenter) tested economic principles such as supply and demand, in line with Thaler's remark that Smith tried to show how well theory works; Plott tested rule changes; Thaler wanted to stay open-minded about methods (p. 10).
- Wanting a place that would tolerate "a little heresy", he presented a self-control paper on the job market; he got offers from Cornell and Duke and chose Cornell (p. 11).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Getting to Stanford (pp. 5–6)
- Thaler planned to spend summer 1977 at Stanford with Sherwin Rosen on the value of a life. He then learned that Kahneman and Tversky would be there for the academic year, so he wanted to stay for the fall (p. 5).
- His first behavioral paper was then titled "Consumer Choice: A Theory of Economists' Behavior". The title hinted that only economists behave like Econs. Tversky replied briefly and in a friendly way (p. 5).
- Victor Fuchs, director of the NBER office, offered to put him on his grant for the fall. Later he extended the offer to cover Thaler's salary until the following summer (pp. 5–6).

### The driving puzzle (p. 6)
- On the drive west Thaler asks how fast to drive 300 miles. Going 70 mph instead of 60 saves 43 minutes, and that might justify the risk of a ticket (p. 6).
- With 30 miles left, the same speedup saves only 4.3 minutes, which seems not worth it. Should he slow down gradually? Thaler rejects this, because he will drive again tomorrow, and asks whether a uniform policy is better (p. 6).
- Answer (footnote): drive the same speed throughout. The chance of a ticket is proportional to driving time, other things equal (p. 11).
- Along the way he visited Fischhoff, Slovic, Lichtenstein and Bar-Hillel, who later became his "psychology tutors" (p. 6).

### Meeting Kahneman and Tversky (pp. 6–7)
- Fuchs arranged the first lunch. Afterwards Thaler could walk up the hill to the Center and visit Kahneman. Tversky's office was too far away (p. 6).
- He sometimes saw them finishing "Prospect Theory", with Kahneman at the keyboard. They argued over almost every word, mixing Hebrew and English. Sometimes the switch to English seemed related to technical terms such as "loss aversion", for which they had not invented Hebrew equivalents (pp. 6–7).
- Thaler says academic writing is often dull, and some treat dullness as a badge of seriousness. In footnote he names Stigler and Schelling as exceptions. The prospect theory paper is "hardly an easy read" but is crystal clear, thanks to their endless editing and Amos's goal of "getting it right" (pp. 7, 11).

### Hypothetical questions (pp. 7–8)
- Thaler and Kahneman took walks and taught each other how their professions think (p. 7).
- Kahneman and Tversky's earlier work used simple scenarios. Example: after gaining $400, choose between a sure loss of $200 and a 50% chance to lose $400 (50% to lose nothing). Most choose to gamble (p. 7).
- They tried questions on themselves first. If they agreed, they provisionally assumed others would agree. Then they checked with subjects, typically students (p. 7).
- **Economists' view (as Thaler reports it):** economists care more about what people do than about what they say they would do (p. 7).
- Kahneman and Tversky had little choice. A key prediction of prospect theory is that people react differently to losses and gains. Getting permission to run experiments where subjects could lose substantial money is nearly impossible, and the review committees might not approve them (p. 8).
- **Their defense (published):** by default, hypothetical choice is the simplest procedure for investigating many theoretical questions. The method relies on the assumptions that people often know how they would behave in actual choices and that subjects have no special reason to disguise their true preferences (p. 8). Thaler's reading: if subjects were reasonably accurate in predicting their real choices and those choices were inconsistent with expected utility theory, that should at least create a presumption of doubt about whether the theory describes behavior well (p. 8).
- The defense apparently satisfied the journal editor but stayed a "bugaboo" among economists. Prospect theory gained acceptance by explaining behavior in high-stakes settings with observable choices. Thaler does not think any economist would have developed it, because the reluctance to use hypotheticals would have blocked these insights (p. 8).
- **Intuition note:** The argument is conditional. The method relies on the assumption that people often know how they would act and do not disguise their preferences. If stated choices are reasonably accurate and clearly conflict with the theory, that creates at least a presumption of doubt (p. 8).
- Thaler found this liberating, though his earlier belief that readers would share his intuitions was naive (p. 8).
- Kahneman and Tversky turned his clock radio and television example into a jacket and calculator question. In one version the jacket costs $125 and the calculator $15; in the other, $15 and $125. The calculator is on sale at another branch, 20 minutes away, for $10 or $120 respectively. Subjects were more willing to drive to save on the cheaper item, as Thaler had conjectured (pp. 8–9). Seven years later, Thaler and Kahneman relied almost exclusively on hypothetical questions in the fairness project (ch. 14) (p. 9).

### The slow hunch (p. 9)
- At NBER Thaler faced a paradox: he had a big idea, but research moves in small steps, and he did not know which. He had to publish to stay employed (p. 9).
- He cites Steven Johnson's "slow hunch": a vague impression that something important is nearby. It may lead nowhere (p. 9).

### Experimental economics: Smith and Plott (pp. 9–10)
- Thaler contacted the founders of experimental economics, Plott (Caltech) and Smith (Arizona), who test economic ideas in the laboratory instead of using historical data (p. 9).
- Smith's agenda differed from Thaler's. Thaler said that Smith tried to show how well theory works, whereas Kahneman did the opposite. Footnote: this referred to Smith's early work, and later Smith produced asset-pricing bubbles (pp. 10, 11).
- Smith used the **induced value methodology**: subjects trade tokens, each with a private value set by the experimenter (e.g., $8 for me, $4 for you). This let him test supply and demand (p. 10).
- Thaler's worry: real buyers set their own valuations, which may depend on retail price, recent spending or a tax refund. He tested this much later with coffee mugs (ch. 16) (p. 10).
- Plott used a wind tunnel analogy. He cared more about testing what happens when market rules change than about showing that basic principles work (p. 10).
- **Intuition note:** Smith and Plott used experiments to test theory or rules. Thaler wanted to observe behavior in whatever setting fit, including experiments, questions and, if he could work out how, people in their natural habitats (p. 10).

### Going "all in" (pp. 10–11)
- Thaler was not ready to call himself exclusively, or even primarily, an experimental economist. He would run experiments, ask questions, and also study people "in their natural habitats" if he could (p. 10).
- During the year he decided to go "all in". Rochester's senior faculty were wedded to traditional methods (p. 11).
- On the job market he presented his self-control paper rather than safe "value of a life" work, to find a place tolerating "a little heresy". He received offers from Cornell and Duke and chose Cornell, 90 miles from Rochester (p. 11).
- Footnote: he got the Rochester business school job after being a student in its economics department because he had been teaching there as a graduate student and, when his first job fell through, the dean, Bill Meckling, offered him a one-year stopgap position (p. 11).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: see how a hypothetical-question tally is read.
- Take the Kahneman–Tversky question: sure loss of $200 versus a 50% chance of losing $400 (50% of losing nothing).
- Expected value of the gamble: $0.5 \times (-400) + 0.5 \times 0 = -200$. Expected value of the sure loss: $-200$. They are equal.
- Suppose we ask 20 students and 14 choose the gamble. That is $14/20 = 70\%$. The numbers are invented.
- A risk-neutral agent would be indifferent. A majority preferring the gamble is a pattern worth noting, although 20 students is a small sample. Under the defense on p. 8, it counts as evidence about real behavior to the extent that students' stated choices match real ones.

## Concepts
- [[hypothetical-questions-method]] (new) — asking subjects what they would choose and treating answers as evidence about behavior (p. 7)
- [[prospect-theory]] (new) — Kahneman and Tversky's theory; a key prediction is that people react differently to losses than to gains (p. 8)
- [[loss-aversion]] — named in passing as a technical term Kahneman and Tversky used; not defined in this chapter (p. 7)
- [[induced-value-methodology]] (new) — Smith's method of giving subjects private token values set by the experimenter (p. 10)
- [[experimental-economics]] (new) — testing economic ideas in the laboratory; Smith tests economic principles, Plott tests rule changes (pp. 9–10)
- [[slow-hunch]] (new) — a vague sense that something important is near, with no way to know whether it leads anywhere (p. 9)
- [[econs]] (new) — term used in passing and not defined in this chapter; Thaler's early paper title implicitly suggested that only economists behave like Econs (p. 5)

## Questions this raises
- How much do stated choices differ from real choices when stakes are small or hypothetical?
- If induced values remove preferences from the experiment, what do token markets tell us about real markets?
- Why does the same saving feel different when it is a larger or smaller share of the price?
- Does a "slow hunch" need a method, or does it need patience?
