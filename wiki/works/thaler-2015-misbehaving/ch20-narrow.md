# Narrow Framing on the Upper East Side

## L0 — One line
People who judge risky choices one at a time, instead of as a portfolio, tend to take too little risk, and this may help explain managers' timidity, the equity premium puzzle and cab drivers' hours.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
When do people get into trouble by treating events one at a time rather than as a portfolio? The idea is called "narrow framing" (p. 143). Managers turn down good bets because each is judged alone and failure is punished. Investors who check returns often tend to hold fewer stocks. Thaler and Benartzi call this "myopic loss aversion" (loss aversion plus frequent checking) and propose it to explain the equity premium puzzle (stocks beat bonds by more than theory predicts). Experiments, including a natural experiment in Israel, support the idea. Cab drivers seem to frame narrowly, working fewer hours on good days. Practical fixes: pool decisions, reward choices that were sound when made, and look at returns less often.

**Prerequisites:** expected value; probability of a coin-flip sequence; loss aversion; the idea of risk aversion; a basic stock/bond return comparison.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Narrow framing** is the habit of treating events separately rather than pooling them, a mental-accounting question (p. 143). It matters because it can lead to choices that would be rejected if the events were seen together.
- **Kahneman and Lovallo's idea: managers combine bold forecasts with timid choices.** Bold forecasts come from the inside view (judging your own project from within, ignoring base rates for similar projects). Timid choices come from loss aversion, which a reward system can make worse (pp. 143–144).
- **A CEO would take all 23 independent positive-value projects, but only 3 of the 23 managers said they would.** One manager said he feared being fired after a failure, and Thaler blames narrow framing (pp. 145–146).
- **"Dumb principal" problems:** often the boss, not the agent, is at fault, because the boss does not reward decisions that were good given what was known when they were made (ex ante). Hindsight bias (the boss forgetting he also thought it was a good idea) makes this harder (p. 147).
- **Samuelson's argument.** If you refuse one bet (with a wealth proviso), you should refuse many. Thaler and Benartzi think the fault lies in refusing the single bet (pp. 150–151).
- **Myopic loss aversion** (loss aversion plus frequent evaluation) is offered as an explanation of the equity premium puzzle, with an implied evaluation period of about one year (pp. 151, 154).
- **Experiments:** the same data shown as annual vs 30-year returns changed the stock share from 40% to 90%. More frequent feedback lowered it from 70% to 41%. Israel's reporting change had the predicted effect (pp. 152–154).
- **Cab drivers** worked fewer hours on high-wage days, apparently following a daily income target. Experienced drivers did so less (pp. 155–156).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Setting and the question (pp. 142–143).** In 1991–92 Thaler, Camerer and Kahneman were visiting scholars at the Russell Sage Foundation. Busy schedules meant they could not recreate their earlier intense joint work (p. 142). Both Thaler and Kahneman were thinking about narrow framing. This is a mental-accounting question: when are transactions combined and when are they treated separately? Specifically: when do people get into trouble by treating events one at a time rather than as a portfolio? (p. 143)

**Kahneman and Lovallo: bold forecasts, timid choices (pp. 143–144).** Managerial decisions are driven by two biases that counter each other but need not cancel (p. 143).
- *Bold forecasts* come from the **inside view** (judging a project from within the team, ignoring base rates, i.e. average outcomes of similar projects) versus the **outside view**.
- Kahneman's curriculum team guessed 18–30 months. Their curriculum expert, taking the outside view, said that in his experience no group had finished a similar task in under seven years, and 40% of teams never finished (p. 143).
- The outside view, if carefully built from baseline data, is "far more reliable" than the inside view (p. 144).
- The inside view is so natural that even Kahneman fell for it. In fall 1996 he said his edited book would take six months. It appeared in 2000, almost four years later (p. 144).
- *Timid choices* come from loss aversion. In many companies a large gain brings a modest reward, while an equal-sized loss gets you fired. Then even a manager who starts risk neutral becomes highly risk averse (p. 144).

*Intuition note:* The inside view makes plans look easy, because it ignores base rates. Loss aversion, which a lopsided reward system can make worse, can make good gambles look dangerous. Thaler links the timid choices to narrow framing (judging each project alone).

**The executives' example (pp. 144–146).** Thaler asked 23 publication heads about a project with a 50% chance of a $2 million profit and a 50% chance of a $1 million loss. Its expected payoff is $500,000. A loss would not threaten the firm (p. 145).
- Only 3 of 23 would do it (p. 145).
- The CEO would take all of them if the projects were independent. Twenty-three projects have an expected gain of $11.5 million, and the chance of losing money overall is under 5% (p. 145).
- A manager explained: success brings a pat on the back and perhaps three months' income. Failure may get him fired (p. 145).
- Conclusion: narrow framing leaves the firm with too little risk. One solution is to pool investments and consider them as a package (pp. 145–146).

**Pharmaceutical firm (p. 146).** The firm treated R&D as a portfolio of lottery tickets. But a proposed pricing experiment to improve patient "compliance" (taking prescribed medicine) was rejected as too risky. Thaler judges the experiment's cost tiny relative to the company, and it only looked risky relative to one manager's budget. Narrow framing blocked experimentation (p. 146). A footnote reports that text reminders cut missed medication from 25% to 9% (Wald et al., 2014) (note * to p. 146, printed on p. 156).

**"Dumb principal" problems (p. 147).** Economics usually blames agents who maximize their own welfare. Thaler says the real culprit is often the boss. To get managers to take risks, they must be rewarded for decisions that were value-maximizing ex ante (with the information available then), even if they lose ex post. Hindsight bias makes this hard, because the boss may forget that he also thought it was a good idea (p. 147).

**Benartzi and the equity premium puzzle (pp. 147–149).**
- The **equity premium** is the return on equities minus the return on a risk-free asset such as short-term government bonds (p. 148). For 1889–1978 it was about 6% per year (p. 148).
- A premium of some size is expected from risk aversion. Mehra and Prescott (1985) asked how *large* the premium could be in standard theory. Their model's largest value was 0.35%, far below 6%. Investors would have to be implausibly risk averse to explain the 6% (p. 148).
- A footnote says the theory links the premium to the risk-free rate, and the real T-bill rate then was below 1% (note † to p. 148, printed on p. 156). A second footnote says growth of 1% doubles a portfolio in 70 years, 1.35% in 52 years, and 7% in 10 years (note ‡ to p. 148, printed on p. 157).

**Samuelson's bet (pp. 149–150).** Samuelson offered his colleague Brown a coin flip: win $200 on heads, lose $100 on tails. Brown refused because "I would feel the $100 loss more than the $200 gain", but said he would take 100 such bets (p. 149).
- **Samuelson's result:** with one proviso, if you won't play one bet, you shouldn't play several. The proviso is that your unwillingness to play a single bet is not sensitive to relatively small changes in wealth, specifically any wealth level you could reach by playing out all the bets. Here the range is a loss of up to $10,000 and a gain of up to $20,000 (p. 149).
- **Proof by backward induction** (start at the end and work back):
  - If bet 100 is optional, Brown refuses it.
  - Then bet 99 is effectively a single bet, so he refuses it too.
  - Continuing, he refuses even the first bet (pp. 149–150).
- A footnote says this relies on expected utility of wealth, where wealth is fungible. Mental-accounting effects such as the house money effect are excluded (p. 157n).
- **Samuelson's diagnosis:** Brown misunderstands the law of large numbers. He sees that losing money in 100 plays is unlikely (about 1 in 2,300), but ignores the small chance of losing a large sum (p. 150).

**Thaler and Benartzi's reversal (pp. 150–151).** They think Samuelson was half right. Brown's preferences are inconsistent, but the mistake is refusing the single bet.
- Brown expects to win $5,000 from the 100 bets. The chance of losing more than $1,000 is about 1 in 62,000 (p. 151).
- Rabin and Thaler: "A good lawyer could have you declared legally insane for turning down this gamble." (p. 151)
- So by Samuelson's own logic the single bet should not be refused. They call the phenomenon **myopic loss aversion**: you can only take the 100 bets by taking the first, and it is only thinking about the bet in isolation that fools you into refusing (p. 151).
- Applied to investing: with a 6% edge, the chance that stocks do worse than bonds over 20–30 years is small, but investors take too short-term a view (p. 151).

*Intuition note:* The same logic can be used in two directions. If you reject one bet, you should reject many, but if many are clearly good, you should accept one. Thaler says the accept-many conclusion is the sensible one.

**Experiment 1: USC employees (pp. 151–153).** New non-faculty employees at USC imagined a retirement plan with two unlabelled funds (stock index vs five-year government bonds, using 68 years of returns). Both groups saw identical data (p. 151).
- One group saw one-year returns and the other saw simulated 30-year average annual returns (Figure 9) (pp. 151–152).
- For Econs the format would not matter, but it did: 40% in stocks (annual) vs 90% (30-year) (p. 152).
- The authors say this contradicts Samuelson's hypothesis that people overestimate how much repetition reduces risk. When people see the actual data, they "love the riskier portfolio" (p. 153).

**Experiment 2: frequency of looking (p. 153).** Thaler, Tversky, Kahneman and Schwartz (1997) had Berkeley students manage a pretend endowment portfolio, with real earnings of $5–$35 depending on results. Those who saw results eight times per simulated year put 41% in stocks. Those who saw them once a year put 70% (p. 153).

**Natural experiment: Israel (pp. 153–154).** In 2010 Israeli funds changed their display from the most recent month's return to the past year's return (study by Maya Shaton). Afterwards investors moved more money into stocks, traded less, and chased recent winners less (pp. 153–154).

**Implications (p. 154).** The evaluation period at which investors would be indifferent between stocks and bonds, using historical data, is roughly one year, which Thaler finds plausible. Their analysis implies that the equity premium is high because investors look at their portfolios too often. His advice: hold a diversified, stock-heavy portfolio and avoid financial news. A footnote notes that stocks can fall 50%, which is why he endorses reducing the stock share with age (p. 154, n. p. 157).

**Cab drivers (pp. 154–156).** Drivers rent a cab for 12 hours at a flat fee and keep fares and tips. Some drivers said they used a target income strategy and quit when they reached it (pp. 154–155).
- Thaler, Camerer, Loewenstein and Babcock studied trip sheets from a cab company and the NYC Taxi and Limousine commissioner (p. 155).
- First, earnings later in the day could be predicted from earlier earnings, so high-wage and low-wage days exist. The central result: the higher the wage, the less drivers worked (p. 155).
- The authors do not claim a downward-sloping supply curve in general. They accept that more people will drive cabs if the wage doubles. The result concerns how long drivers work on days they have chosen to work (p. 155).
- More experienced drivers behaved more sensibly, driving more when wages were higher. The effect is therefore stronger for inexperienced drivers (p. 156).
- Tracking earnings monthly rather than daily would pay about 5% more with equal hours each day, and about 10% more by driving more on good days (p. 156).
- Thaler and his coauthors suspected that, especially for inexperienced drivers, the daily target acts as a self-control device that is easy to follow and justify (p. 156).
- A footnote says that if some Uber drivers behaved this way, it would limit the surge's effect on driver supply. The key question is whether enough drivers watch surge prices and start driving when they rise, and this is impossible to answer without Uber's data (note †† to p. 156, printed on p. 157).

## Toy example (ours)
A bet pays +$200 or −$100 with equal chance, as in Samuelson's bet. Look at three independent plays.
- One play: expected value 0.5×200 + 0.5×(−100) = +$50. Chance of a loss: 50%.
- Three plays: the number of wins k is 0–3, with probabilities 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8.
  - 3 wins: 3×200 = +$600.
  - 2 wins: 2×200 − 100 = +$300.
  - 1 win: 200 − 2×100 = $0.
  - 0 wins: −$300.
- Expected value: (1/8)(600) + (3/8)(300) + (3/8)(0) + (1/8)(−300) = 75 + 112.5 + 0 − 37.5 = +$150 = 3×$50.
- Chance of losing money: only 0 wins, so 1/8 = 12.5%, versus 50% for one play.

Someone who evaluates each play separately sees a 50% chance of loss and refuses. Someone who evaluates the three-play package sees a 12.5% chance of loss and accepts. Only the frame differs.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Narrow framing across projects ↔ diversification & covariance: pooling independent positive-value bets cuts the chance of a net loss.
- [structural-analogy] Dumb principal ↔ incomplete contracts: rewards cannot capture ex ante quality of decisions.
- [structural-analogy] Inside vs outside view ↔ conventional expectations: both concern judging the future from the current situation versus broader evidence.

## Questions this raises
- How would the 100-bet gamble look under a different loss-aversion model, and could the proviso on wealth fail for some people?
- How should an organization measure and reward ex ante decision quality when hindsight bias is strong?
- If the evaluation period of one year is plausible, how robust is that estimate to the choice of data period?
- Does target-income behavior disappear with experience, and what about workers without a learning environment?
