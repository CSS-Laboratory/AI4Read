# Value Theory (Thaler, ch. 4)

## L0 — One line
Thaler learns from an early draft of prospect theory that economics needs separate descriptive models of real human choice, built on a value function over gains and losses (not levels of wealth) with diminishing sensitivity and loss aversion.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks why standard economics predicts real behavior badly. In Thaler's reading, Kahneman and Tversky's draft points to the cause: economics uses one theory as both a normative and a descriptive model. Expected utility theory is a good guide to how one should decide, but it does not describe what people do. Their alternative judges outcomes as changes from a reference point. The resulting S-shaped value function has diminishing sensitivity for gains and losses, and losses weigh more than gains. This explains risk aversion for gains, risk seeking for losses, and the endowment effect. For Thaler it became a mission: build descriptive models of Humans.

**Prerequisites:** idea of utility and expected value; basic probability; right triangles (Pythagoras).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A normative theory says how to think correctly, meaning logically consistent with optimizing. A descriptive theory predicts what people do. Traditional economics uses one theory for both (pdf 36, 38).
- The railroad-track puzzle shows the gap: the Pythagorean answer is 29.7 feet, but the average guess is about 2 inches (pdf 37–38).
- Bernoulli's diminishing marginal utility of wealth gives risk aversion. Von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility theory rests on axioms such as transitivity, and Thaler calls it the right way to decide (pdf 38–40).
- Prospect theory aims at predicting real choices, not guiding rational ones. Thaler's "mission statement" is to build descriptive models that portray human behavior accurately (pdf 40).
- People experience life as changes (from the status quo or from expectations), not levels of wealth (pdf 41–42).
- The value function is S-shaped: diminishing sensitivity in both gains and losses, so people are risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses. This links to Weber-Fechner just-noticeable differences (pdf 43–44).
- The value function is steeper for losses than for gains (loss aversion), and this explains the endowment effect. Thaler calls loss aversion the most powerful tool in behavioral economics (pdf 44–45).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening.** Fischhoff tells Thaler that Kahneman and Tversky have a new decision-making project. Howard Kunreuther sends Thaler an early draft, titled "Value Theory", which later became "Prospect Theory". Thaler took two things from it: an organizing principle and a graph (pdf 36). A footnote reports Kahneman's reason for the renaming. "Value theory" was misleading, and they wanted a meaningless term that would gain meaning if the theory succeeded (pdf 45).

**Two kinds of theories.**
- *Normative* means "logically consistent, as prescribed by the optimizing model" of rational choice theory, not morally right. Thaler says this is the only way he will use the word in this book (pdf 36). The Pythagorean theorem is his example of a normative theory.
- *Railroad puzzle.* Two one-mile tracks lie end to end, fixed at the ends. Each expands by one inch and rises straight like a drawbridge. This gives a right triangle with base 1 mile and hypotenuse 1 mile + 1 inch, and the question is the altitude (pdf 37). Most people guess about the same as the expansion, or two or three inches. The actual answer is 29.7 feet (pdf 37).
- A rational choice theorist would use Pythagoras as both normative and descriptive model and predict about 30 feet. The average answer is about 2 inches, so this prediction is "terrible" (pdf 38).
- Economic theory, then and for most economists now, uses one theory for both purposes (pdf 38). Examples:
  - The theory of the firm: maximize profit, setting marginal (= incremental) cost equal to marginal revenue.
  - Becker's human capital theory: people choose education by correctly forecasting their earnings and enjoyment. Few students do this; many study what they enjoy most (pdf 38).
- Prospect theory broke from this and took on decision-making under uncertainty (pdf 38).
- *Bernoulli (1738).* He solved the St. Petersburg paradox by positing that utility rises with wealth at a decreasing rate (diminishing sensitivity). This gives risk aversion (pdf 38). Footnote: the coin-doubling gamble has infinite expected value, $\tfrac12\cdot 2+\tfrac14\cdot 4+\tfrac18\cdot 8+\dots$. Thaler offers a simpler solution: the other side cannot pay without limit. Forty heads in a row means a prize over $1 trillion, so if that would break the bank, the bet is worth no more than $40 (pdf 45).
- Figure 2 shows the concave curve. With wealth of $100,000, a sure $1,000 is preferred to a 50% chance of $2,000, because the second thousand is valued less than the first (pdf 39).
- *Expected utility theory* (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944) starts from axioms, mostly uncontroversial, such as transitivity (A over B and B over C implies A over C). They proved that if you want to satisfy the axioms, you must decide according to the theory. Thaler says he would use it for a mortgage or business decision, as he would use Pythagoras. For him it is the right way to decide (pdf 40).
- Prospect theory "had no pretense" of guiding rational choice. It is a theory of the behavior of Humans (pdf 40).
- Precedents. Simon coined "bounded rationality" but did not flesh it out. Baumol proposed that firms maximize sales subject to a minimum profit. Thaler thinks this may be a good descriptive model, and it may even be smart for CEOs, since CEO pay oddly seems to depend as much on a firm's size as on its profits. But it would still violate value maximization (pdf 40).
- *Takeaway 1:* build descriptive economic models that accurately portray human behavior (pdf 40).

*Intuition note:* Pythagoras is the right answer and a poor prediction of guesses. In the same way, a correct theory of rational choice need not predict real choices. Thaler's mission is therefore to build descriptive models that portray actual behavior accurately.

**A stunning graph.**
- Takeaway 2 is the *value function*. Kahneman and Tversky shift the focus from levels of wealth to changes in wealth, which Thaler calls a radical move (pdf 41). Humans experience changes. Example: you notice a room's temperature only if it differs from what you adapted to (pdf 41).
- Jane earns $80,000 and gets an unexpected $5,000 bonus. She thinks "Wow, an extra $5,000!", not about her lifetime wealth. The change can be from the status quo or from what was expected (pdf 42).
- Figure 3 is an S-shaped curve with the origin as reference point. Its upper part (gains) has the usual concave shape (pdf 42–43).
- The loss part also shows diminishing sensitivity. Losing $10 versus $20 feels much bigger than losing $1,300 versus $1,310 (pdf 43). In the standard model, losses move down the utility-of-wealth line, so each loss hurts more. (If you care less about increases, you care more about decreases.) Here the text says "figure 1", which seems to mean figure 2 (pdf 43).
- *Weber-Fechner Law:* the just-noticeable difference (JND) in a variable is proportional to its magnitude. Example: one extra ounce is unnoticed, but 2 versus 3 ounces of herbs is obvious (pdf 43).
- *Car Talk headlights:* a caller asked how both bulbs could fail at once. Tom's reply was "the Weber-Fechner Law". Thaler says the bulbs had not in fact failed together: one had been out unnoticed, and going from one bulb to zero is noticeable (pdf 43–44).
- Same logic for the List: people are more willing to drive ten minutes to save $10 on a $45 clock radio than on a $495 TV, where the saving would not be a JND (pdf 44).
- *Risk seeking for losses.* Both problems are identical in final wealth. Subjects were two different groups (pdf 44).
  - Problem 1 (richer by $300): sure gain of $100 [72%] versus 50% to gain $200 or $0 [28%].
  - Problem 2 (richer by $500): sure loss of $100 [36%] versus 50% to lose $200 or $0 [64%].
  - Added step: Problem 1 gives final wealth +$400 for sure versus +$500 or +$300 (relative to today). Problem 2 gives +$400 for sure versus +$300 or +$500. So the two problems are the same in levels.
  - Thaler's explanation: the second $100 loss hurts less than the first. People are ready to take the risk of losing more for the chance of getting back to no loss at all. Loss aversion adds to this (pdf 44).
- *Loss aversion.* At the origin the loss curve is steeper than the gain curve. Roughly, losses hurt about twice as much as gains please. This gives the endowment effect: Professor Rosett would not buy a bottle worth the same market price as one in his cellar (pdf 44–45). Thaler calls loss aversion "the single most powerful tool in the behavioral economist's arsenal" (pdf 45).

*Intuition note:* The curve gives three rules: judge changes, feel each extra dollar less, and feel losses more than gains. Together they predict the two choice patterns above and why giving something up feels costly.

## Toy example
Our own illustration, not from the text. Take $v(x)=\sqrt{x}$ for gains and $v(x)=-2\sqrt{-x}$ for losses (concave in both parts, with loss weight 2). Here $\sqrt{200}\approx 14.142$.
- Problem 1: the sure +100 gives $v=\sqrt{100}=10$. The gamble gives $0.5\cdot 14.142+0.5\cdot 0=7.071$. Since $10>7.071$, take the sure gain. This is risk aversion.
- Problem 2: the sure −100 gives $v=-2\cdot 10=-20$. The gamble gives $0.5\cdot(-2\cdot 14.142)+0=-14.142$. Since $-14.142>-20$, take the gamble. This is risk seeking.

These match the majority choices (72% and 64%). The function here is only illustrative; the text gives no formula.

## Concepts
- [[normative-vs-descriptive-theory]] (new) — normative: how to choose consistently; descriptive: how people do choose (pdf 36, 38)
- [[marginal reasoning]] — "marginal" means incremental; firms should equate marginal cost and marginal revenue (pdf 38)
- [[expected-utility-theory]] (new) — axiom-based theory of choice under risk, von Neumann–Morgenstern (pdf 39–40)
- [[prospect-theory]] (new) — descriptive theory of risky choice by Kahneman and Tversky (pdf 36, 40)
- [[value-function]] (new) — S-shaped curve over gains and losses from a reference point (pdf 41–42)
- [[diminishing-sensitivity]] (new) — each extra unit of a gain or loss matters less (pdf 38, 43)
- [[reference-dependence]] (new) — outcomes are judged as changes from the status quo or expectations (pdf 41–42)
- [[mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion]] — loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains; explains the endowment effect (pdf 44–45)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — Simon's term, not fleshed out (pdf 40)
- [[weber-fechner-law]] (new) — the just-noticeable difference is proportional to magnitude (pdf 43)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [metaphor] Pythagorean theorem as normative model ↔ economics: optimizing models as benchmarks — a correct rule that people do not follow intuitively.
- [structural-analogy] Weber-Fechner JND ↔ economics: diminishing sensitivity — both make the effect of a change depend on its size relative to a base.
- [structural-analogy] Bernoulli's concave utility ↔ risk aversion in finance (canon: mean-variance optimization) — concavity makes a sure amount preferred to a gamble with the same mean.

- What determines the reference point (status quo or expectation) in a given situation?
- Is the "about twice" ratio for loss aversion stable across people and sizes of stakes?
- Which descriptive models beyond sales maximization could replace profit maximization?
