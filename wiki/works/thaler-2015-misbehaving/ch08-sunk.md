# Sunk Costs

## L0 — One line
Humans often let money already spent and unrecoverable shape later choices, and Thaler explains this through mental accounting: an unused purchase feels like a loss, while a prepaid "investment" can make later consumption feel free.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Economic theory says sunk costs (spent money that cannot be recovered) should be ignored. Thaler asks why people nonetheless attend events, wear shoes, or play tennis in pain because of what they paid. His answer: in mental accounts, buying and then not using a good means "recognizing a loss", so using it closes the account. This effect may wear off over time. Surveys of wine buyers show inconsistent accounting: buying is an "investment", drinking is "free". In the first wine survey, many respondents did not give the opportunity-cost answer (what selling the bottle would bring). Thaler suggests firms may benefit from this pattern, as with fine wine, time-shares and membership fees. Thaler admits to these tendencies himself.

**Prerequisites:** opportunity cost; utility; transaction vs acquisition utility (previous chapter); the Econ/Human contrast.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Economists advise ignoring sunk costs, yet many people, even if they understand it, can find it hard to follow; the mistake is called the sunk cost fallacy (pp. 31–32).
- Mechanism: an unused ticket must be "recognized" as a loss in the mental books; using the item settles the account (p. 33).
- Sunk-cost effects can wear off. The evidence is gym attendance jumping after each bill and then tailing off, and theater attendance equalizing by spring (pp. 33–34).
- Sunk and opportunity costs can be intertwined; in the wine survey, over half said drinking an owned $75 bottle was free or saved money (pp. 35–36).
- In the second survey, "investment" was the most popular description of buying wine to lay away; combined with the first survey, Thaler sees inconsistent thinking, as if spending never happens (pp. 37–38).
- Firms can use this thinking: time-shares and membership fees may be framed as investments or sunk costs (p. 38).
- Behavioral problems can sometimes get behavioral solutions, such as Thaler's "outtakes" file (p. 39).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The chapter has no numbered sections; I follow its order.)

**Opening: Vince and the definition (pp. 31–33).** Money spent and unrecoverable is "sunk". Vince paid $1,000 for tennis, then got tennis elbow. He kept playing for three more months in pain because he "did not want to waste the membership fee". Thaler stipulates that Vince would decline a free game elsewhere, so the utility of playing is negative (p. 31).
- Friend Joyce made her daughter Cindy wear dresses already bought ("I bought those dresses…"). Thaler told her the money was gone and that ignoring sunk costs is rational. This relieved her guilt. Maya Bar-Hillel called Thaler a "clinical economist" (pp. 31–32).
- Textbooks call the error the sunk cost fallacy. Econs treat sunk costs as irrelevant; for Humans they linger as another SIF (supposedly irrelevant factor) (p. 32).
- Staw's "escalation of commitment": in his view, every thousand lives lost and every billion dollars spent made it harder to quit Vietnam. Thaler reports that "many people believe" the war continued because too much had been invested (p. 32).

**Why sunk costs matter (pp. 33–34).**
- Recall from the previous chapter: if a purchase gives no transaction (de)utility, the price is not felt as a loss. Later consumption gives acquisition utility and the account clears (pp. 32–33).
- If you skip the event, a $100 ticket "feels a lot like losing $100". Using the item lets you settle the account without a loss (p. 33).
- Shoes thought experiment (Thaler's claim about "most people"): the more you paid, the more pain you bear and the longer the shoes stay in the closet (p. 33).
- Gym: membership can help with self-control, because the fee "haunts" you and there is no immediate outlay when you go (p. 33).
- Gourville and Soman: attendance at a club billing twice a year jumps the month after the bill, then tails off. They call this "payment depreciation": sunk-cost effects wear off (p. 33).
- Arkes and Blumer: theater season-ticket buyers, already committed to full price, got a random small or large discount. Full-price payers attended more in the fall, but attendance was equal across the three groups by spring. Thaler offers two possible reasons: they felt they had their money's worth, or they forgot the price (p. 34).

*Intuition note:* Paying creates a "debit" in a mental ledger. Using the thing erases it; not using it makes the loss visible. Over time the effect can wear off, so the pull may weaken.

**Sunk and opportunity costs intertwined: coupons and wine (pp. 34–37).**
- Airport episode: Thaler had upgrade coupons, some free and some bought for $35. He gave Eldar Shafir his last one. Thaler said the coupon's origin was irrelevant, since he must buy more anyway. Eldar insisted it mattered: pay nothing if free, $35 if bought (pp. 34–35).
- Question: how long does the memory of a past purchase linger? Another motive was Professor Rosett, who drank his old wine but neither bought more nor sold any. Survey of readers of *Liquid Assets*: a Bordeaux case bought at $20/bottle, now ~$75 at auction; what is the cost of drinking one? (p. 35)

| Answer | Share |
|---|---|
| (a) $0, already paid | 30% |
| (b) $20 | 18% |
| (c) $20 plus interest | 7% |
| (d) $75, sale value | 20% |
| (e) −$55, saves money | 25% |

- Over half said drinking was free or saved money (p. 35). Theory's correct answer is $75, the opportunity cost. Most who chose it were economists; Thaler knows this because replies were not anonymous (names were required for a prize lottery) (p. 36).
- Variant: if the bottle were dropped and broken, a majority said it costs $75 (p. 36).
- Respondent comments: a retired engineer wanted to sell half after a price rise and drink the rest "free". Roman Weil said the cost is $75 less ~$15 selling costs, about $60, given plenty of inventory; with insufficient inventory, replacement cost of about $90 (p. 36).

*Intuition note:* The right cost of drinking an owned bottle is what you give up by not selling it. Many people instead look back at what they paid, or at their "gain".

**Second survey (pp. 36–38).** Bordeaux futures bought at $400/case, retail ~$500, not drunk for ten years. Scale 1 = strongly agree, 5 = strongly disagree, so a lower mean means more agreement:
- (a) like spending $400 on a getaway: mean 3.31
- (b) like a $400 investment gradually consumed: mean 1.94
- (c) like saving $100: mean 2.88

Thaler's reading: "investment" is most popular, "saving" second, "spending" last (p. 37). Combined with the first survey, the thinking is inconsistent: buying is an investment, drinking is free or saving. The paper is titled "Invest Now, Drink Later, Spend Never" (pp. 37–38).

**Business uses (p. 38).** Thaler notes this way of thinking is very good for the fine wine industry, since it removes the spending part of consumption. Time-shares: the payment (e.g. $10,000) is an "investment", the fee a nuisance, and vacations "free". Whether it makes sense for a family depends, in part, on how painful it is for them to spend money on vacations; Thaler says such investments are a way to disguise the cost of taking a vacation. Costco ($55/year) and Amazon Prime ($99/year): Thaler writes that it "seems likely" members see the Costco fee as an "investment", and that the Amazon fee "may well" be viewed that way, as a cost that does not "count" toward a particular purchase.

**Two confessions (p. 39).** Thaler says he is usually pretty good at ignoring sunk costs, especially purely monetary ones, but like most people can find it difficult to give up a project he has put a lot of work into. His "outtakes" file reduces the pain of cutting text: "once you understand a behavioral problem, you can sometimes invent a behavioral solution to it." He also does not want to know the auction price of his old wine, and calls himself Human.

*Footnote (p. 39):* the wine lottery drew 178 replies, about 42 cents per reply, with respondents paying their own postage.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A concert ticket cost Ann $100. Ben got a free one. Going would be unpleasant for both (utility −20). Assume that Ann feels skipping as a recognized loss of −100 (my illustration of the "mental books" idea).
- Ann: go = −20, skip = −100, so she goes.
- Ben: go = −20, skip = 0, so he skips.
- An Econ ignores the sunk $100 and skips: −20 < 0.
- Payment depreciation: if Ann's felt loss fades to −10 after months, then skip (−10) beats go (−20) and she stops attending.

## Concepts
- [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] (new) — letting unrecoverable past spending affect present choices (p. 32)
- [[mental-accounting]] (new; canon list item "mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion") — tracking purchases in mental books; unused purchases are "recognized" as losses (p. 33)
- [[transaction-utility]] (new) — the utility (or disutility) a purchase price itself can produce; the text only says that when a price produces none, the price is not felt as a loss, and refers to the previous chapter for the definition (pp. 32–33)
- [[opportunity-cost]] (new) — what you give up by using something, e.g. the $75 sale price (p. 36)
- [[payment-depreciation]] (new) — sunk-cost effect wearing off as time passes since payment (p. 33)
- [[escalation-of-commitment]] (new) — continued investment in a failing course because of past investment (Staw) (p. 32)
- [[econs-vs-humans]] (new) — Econs ignore sunk costs; Humans do not (pp. 32, 36)
