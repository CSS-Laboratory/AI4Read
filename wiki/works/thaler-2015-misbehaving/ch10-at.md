# At the Poker Table

## L0 — One line
Earlier gains or losses in a mental account change risk-taking: people gamble more with "house money" and, when behind, are especially willing to gamble when a bet offers a chance to break even.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why does how you are doing in tonight's poker game change how you play the next hand, when the stakes are tiny relative to wealth? Thaler combines poker observations with a lab study (with Eric Johnson). He finds two effects of mental accounts. After a gain, many people accept gambles they would normally refuse (the house money effect). After a loss, they take risks mainly when the gamble offers a chance to break even. Prospect theory's "risk-seeking for losses" does not fully capture this. Thaler links the pattern to bubbles, fund managers and rogue traders. Thaler adds that if rogue traders' risks were rational for them, management should watch losing employees.

**Prerequisites:** expected value; risk aversion vs risk seeking; reference point and prospect theory (as in the book's chapter 4); idea of a mental account.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Poker winnings or losses should not matter for later hands, yet some players behave differently when ahead or behind. The behavior is tied to a mental account, not to wealth (p. 46).
- Prospect theory predicts risk seeking in losses. Thaler says that explanation is incomplete: it is silent on why a bettor who is behind would not make a bigger bet with better odds of breaking even, since Thaler's impression is that players behind prefer small long-shot bets (p. 47).
- "House money" or "two-pocket" accounting treats winnings as not quite real money. This violates fungibility (p. 47–48).
- Johnson and Thaler tested this with choices where one randomly selected choice would "count", and favorable gambles were more likely to be played, so actual losses were very unlikely (p. 48).
- Results: after a $30 gain, 70% accept a fair ±$9 coin flip. After a $30 loss, only 40% do. But 60% gamble when the gamble offers a chance to recover the loss (p. 48–49).
- The effects arise whenever two reference points are salient, for example where you started and where you are now (p. 49).
- Thaler applies them to 1990s retirement investing, housing speculators, mutual fund managers and rogue traders (p. 49–50).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Poker observation (p. 46).** Cornell economists played low-stakes poker. Rarely did anyone win or lose more than $50. Thaler noticed that some players, especially those who reported results to a spouse, played differently when winning than when losing. Normatively, the night's result should be irrelevant to how a hand is played. Compare a $50 poker loss with a 50-cent fall in 100 shares: both are trivial relative to wealth, but only the poker loss changes behavior, and only while still playing (p. 46).

**Racetrack (p. 46–47).** The track takes about 17% of each dollar wagered, so bettors collectively lose 17% per race. By the last race most are "in the red" in their track account. The normative prediction is that this should hardly matter. Kahneman and Tversky cite a study showing that long-shot odds get worse on the last race. Thaler reads this as more people betting on the horses least likely to win (p. 46–47).

**Kahneman–Tversky explanation and its gap (p. 47).** They explain this by risk seeking over losses. Most people prefer a 50% chance of losing $200 (50% chance of $0) to a sure loss of $100. The reverse holds for gains: a sure $100 is preferred to a 50–50 chance at $0 or $200 (p. 47).

Thaler's objection: a bettor who is down $100 and wants to get back to zero could bet $2 on a 50-to-1 long shot. Or he could bet $100 on an even-money favorite and have a 50% chance of breaking even. If he is simply risk seeking, why not choose the latter? Prospect theory is silent here. Thaler's impression from poker is that players who are behind like small bets with a slim chance of a big win, such as drawing to an inside straight. They dislike big bets that risk a substantial increase in the loss, even though those bets have a higher chance of breaking even (p. 47).

> *Intuition note:* Thaler's impression is that a person behind wants to get back to zero, but dislikes bets that risk a much bigger loss. So the bets that seem to attract such players are those where a small amount is at stake and a win would erase the deficit.

**House money (p. 47–48).** Players who are ahead did not seem to treat winnings as "real money." Casino gamblers call this "gambling with the house's money." Thaler calls it "two-pocket" mental accounting. For example, a player brings $300 and is up $200. He keeps the $300 as "his" money and treats the $200 in chips as separate and ready to bet. "Easy come, easy go" applies. This blatantly violates fungibility, since money in either pocket spends equally well (p. 47–48).

**Experiment (p. 48–49).** Eric Johnson and Thaler wanted to replicate this in the lab. To avoid unethical real losses, subjects chose between sure things and gambles. Truthfully, one choice would be selected at random to "count." Favorable gambles were more likely to be played, so the chance of losing was tiny. Anyone who lost would have had to pay, for example by doing research assistance. No one lost (p. 48). A risk-averse Econ would take the sure thing in all three problems, since the gamble's expected outcome equals the sure thing (p. 48).

| Problem | Starting point | Gamble (a) | Sure option (b) | Choice (a) / (b) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | just won $30 | 50%: +$9; 50%: −$9 | no further gain or loss | 70% / 30% |
| 2 | just lost $30 | 50%: +$9; 50%: −$9 | no further gain or loss | 40% / 60% |
| 3 | just lost $30 | 33%: +$30; 67%: nothing | sure $10 | 60% / 40% |

(p. 48–49)

Thaler's reading (p. 49):
- Thaler says Problem 1 illustrates the house money effect. Subjects are normally risk averse for gains, so they would usually turn down the ±$9 flip. After a win, they accept it.
- Problems 2 and 3 show complex preferences when "behind." In Problem 2, a $30 loss does not produce risk taking, because there is no chance to break even. In Problem 3, there is such a chance, and a majority gamble.
- Footnote: prospect theory's risk seeking in losses may therefore fail if the gamble offers no chance to break even (p. 49).

> *Intuition note:* The same $30 loss leads to caution or risk taking depending on whether the available gamble can erase it. The loss alone does not decide.

**Everyday life (p. 49–50).** Thaler says the break-even and house money effects occur whenever two reference points are salient, such as where you started and where you are now (p. 49).
- *Bubbles.* The house money effect, together with extrapolating recent returns, facilitates bubbles. In the 1990s individual investors raised the share of new retirement contributions going to stocks. Part of the reasoning seemed to be that even if the market fell, they would lose only recent gains. Thaler says recently earned money should not feel like a smaller loss. Housing flippers in Scottsdale, Las Vegas and Miami had a similar "cushion." Highly leveraged ones lost more than house money, and many lost their homes (p. 49).
- *Professionals.* Mutual fund managers take more risk in the last quarter when trailing their benchmark (p. 49–50). Many rogue traders took on ever more risk at the end to break even. Thaler says this may be rational from the trader's view, since he risked losing his job. If so, management needs to watch employees who are losing money (p. 50).
- *Rule:* people threatened with big losses who have a chance to break even will be unusually willing to take risks, even if normally quite risk averse (p. 50).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
You are down $100 at the track. Assume fair odds, with no track take, to isolate the choice.
- **Option A:** bet $2 on a 50-to-1 long shot with win probability 1/51 ≈ 0.0196. A win pays $2 × 50 = $100, so you are back to $0. A loss costs $2 more, taking you to −$102. Expected change = (1/51)(100) − (50/51)(2) = 100/51 − 100/51 = $0.
- **Option B:** bet $100 on an even-money favorite with probability 0.5. A win gives +$100, so you are at $0. A loss gives −$100, so you are at −$200. Expected change = 0.5(100) − 0.5(100) = $0.

Both bets have the same expected value. B gives a 50% chance of breaking even and A only about 2%. But B risks $100 more, while A risks only $2 more. A pure "risk seeker" has no reason to prefer A. Thaler's impression is that players who are behind are attracted to A-type bets (p. 47).

## Concepts
- [[mental-accounting]] — money is tracked in separate accounts (poker night, racetrack day), and balances there affect choices (p. 46)
- [[house-money-effect]] (new) — after a gain, people take risks they would otherwise avoid, treating winnings as not "real" (p. 47–49)
- [[break-even-effect]] (new) — when behind, people take risks mainly if the gamble offers a chance to get back to zero (p. 49)
- [[reference-point]] (new) — a salient benchmark, such as where you started, against which outcomes feel like gains or losses (p. 49)
- [[fungibility-of-money]] (new) — the rule that money is interchangeable across "pockets" or accounts, violated by two-pocket accounting (p. 48)
- [[prospect-theory-risk-seeking-in-losses]] (new) — Kahneman–Tversky prediction that people prefer gambles to sure losses, which Thaler finds incomplete (p. 47, p. 49)

## Canonical instances
- [[cornell-poker-game]] — players behave differently when winning versus losing (p. 46)
- [[racetrack-last-race]] — long-shot odds worsen on the day's last race (p. 46–47)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] House money plus extrapolation of recent returns ↔ financial instability hypothesis: past gains encourage riskier positions, which feed booms.
- [structural-analogy] Rogue traders gambling to break even ↔ moral hazard: an agent facing personal downside takes hidden risks that management should monitor.

## Questions this raises
- Does the break-even effect hold with large real losses and no prior experimental framing, outside the lab?
- How do you tell irrational "house money" from rational responses to incentives, such as a trader's job risk?
- What determines which reference point (start of night, start of year, a benchmark) becomes salient?
