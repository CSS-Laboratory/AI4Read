# Game Shows

## L0 — One line
Data from two TV game shows (Deal or No Deal, Golden Balls) show that prospect-theory-type behavior, path dependence and substantial cooperation (about 40–50%) in a Prisoner's Dilemma remain when stakes are very high.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Critics said behavioral anomalies seen in the lab would vanish at "real" stakes. Thaler also notes that his earlier market-price evidence could not isolate the behavioral cause. Thaler and coauthors used game shows with very high stakes: Deal or No Deal contestants faced hundreds of thousands of dollars, and one Golden Balls pair played for about $175,000. On Deal or No Deal, contestants were only moderately risk averse and were path dependent: they took more risk when ahead, or when behind with a chance to break even. On Golden Balls, cooperation fell as stakes rose, but only to about 40–50%, the lab level. Students' lab replications gave similar results. The chapter gives evidence against the view that these behaviors are just artifacts of small or hypothetical stakes.

**Prerequisites:** expected value (probability-weighted average); risk aversion; the idea of prospect theory and the house money effect; the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- In the earlier market studies, price data could not pin down which bias caused a mispricing, and other (rational) explanations could not be ruled out. Game shows offered a way to study individual choices at very high stakes (pp. 251–252).
- Deal or No Deal offers sure "bank offers" versus continuing to open cases, so later rounds are difficult high-stakes risk choices that can be compared with the expected value of the remaining prizes (pp. 252–254).
- Contestants were only moderately risk averse. This gives no support to the idea that very high risk aversion could dissolve the equity premium puzzle (p. 254).
- Path dependence appeared strongly: gains and losses along the way changed risk taking, as in the house money effect and the "break even" effect (pp. 255–256).
- Public versus private tests with students found similar behavior. Students were more risk averse in front of a crowd, which surprised the author (p. 256).
- On Golden Balls, cooperation fell with stakes but remained at about the 40–50% lab level; the authors' "big peanuts" conjecture offers a possible explanation of why very low pots show higher cooperation (pp. 258–259).
- Cheap talk (talk with no penalty for lying) is mostly noise, but players who made an explicit promise to split were 30 percentage points more likely to split (p. 259).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Why game shows (pp. 251–252)
- Thaler says the real-world evidence (financial markets, football draft) had limits. It concerned market prices, not individual behavior, so the specific cause could not be identified. Cab drivers' and investors' behavior also could not exclude expected-utility explanations with biased beliefs (p. 251).
- Kahneman and Tversky's questions removed ambiguity but were hypothetical. Researchers in poor countries rarely used stakes of more than a few months' income (pp. 251–252).
- In 2005 at Erasmus University, Post, van den Assem and Baltussen showed Thaler their game-show project, with preliminary house money findings. He joined (p. 252).

### Deal or No Deal rules (pp. 252–254)
- The show is *Miljoenenjacht* in Dutch. Data came from the Dutch, German and US versions. The Dutch rules are described (p. 252).
- A board shows 26 amounts, from €0.01 to €5,000,000. The average Dutch contestant won over €225,000. Each of 26 briefcases holds one amount. The contestant keeps one case and may take its contents at the end (p. 252).
- He opens six other cases. Each opened amount leaves the board. He is then offered a certain "bank offer" and says "Deal" or "No deal". Further rounds open five, four, three, two, one, one, one, one cases, giving at most nine rounds (p. 253).
- In early rounds offers are a small fraction of the expected value (EV), the average of the remaining amounts. At the start EV is nearly €400,000. In round one offers are about 10% of EV. They can reach or exceed EV later, and average about three-quarters of EV by round six. Opening big prizes lowers EV and the offer (p. 254).
- Aim: compare expected utility with prospect theory, and test path dependence. Theory says only the current choice should matter; the path is a "SIF" (p. 254). The unit does not expand the abbreviation. A footnote says prospect theory was the clear winner (p. 261).

### Findings (pp. 254–256)
- Players were "moderately risk averse". Many rejected offers of 70% of EV with hundreds of thousands at stake. No Dutch player stopped before round four. Some economists said high risk aversion would dissolve the equity premium puzzle. The author says these results gave no support to that (p. 254).
- **Intuition note:** The author says a player with risk aversion high enough to explain the puzzle would never get that far into the game, yet no Dutch player stopped before round four. So the results give no support to the idea that very high risk aversion explains the equity premium puzzle.
- Earlier work (Thaler–Johnson) found people are less risk averse, even risk-seeking, in two cases: when ahead ("house money") and when behind with a chance to break even. Deal or No Deal contestants showed both tendencies (pp. 254–255).
- **Frank (Dutch):** After round one EV was over €380,000. In round two he opened four large prizes, so EV fell to about €64,000 and the offer was €8,000. At stage six the remaining prizes were €0.50, €10, €20, €10,000 and €500,000 (average €102,006). The offer was €75,000 (74% of EV). He said "No deal", then opened the €500,000 case, leaving EV €2,508. In the last round €10 and €10,000 remained. The offer was €6,000 (120% of EV). He refused and left with €10 (p. 255).
- **Susanne (German show, average win €20,602, top prize €250,000):** €100,000 and €150,000 remained. The offer was €125,000, exactly EV. She said "No deal", and won €150,000. The author says she was "undoubtedly" thinking she risked only €25,000 of "house money" (p. 255).
- The formal results show strong support for path dependence: choices reacted to earlier gains and losses (p. 255). The effect persisted at huge stakes (pp. 255–256).
- **Intuition note:** The same board of prizes can lead to different choices depending on how the contestant got there. That is what "path dependence" means here.

### Public versus private choice (p. 256)
- Worry: behavior on TV may differ from private behavior. Students played a simulated show with an emcee and a crowd. Payoffs were scaled down by 1,000 (large stakes) or 10,000 (small stakes), with biggest payoffs €5,000 and €500 (p. 256).
- Choices were not very different from the TV data. At lower stakes students were a bit less risk averse. Path dependence reappeared, with big winners and big losers both more risk-seeking (p. 256).
- Comparing with private computer play, the author expected more risk taking before a crowd. He found the opposite: students were more risk averse in front of the crowd. Otherwise results were similar (p. 256).

### Golden Balls and the Prisoner's Dilemma (pp. 256–259)
- Another objection concerned "other-regarding" behavior (Ultimatum and Dictator Games) at "real money" stakes (pp. 256–257).
- Golden Balls starts with four contestants and ends with two playing a final Prisoner's Dilemma for a pot. Each chooses "split" or "steal". Both split: each gets half. One steals: the stealer gets everything. Both steal: both get nothing (p. 257).
- In low-stakes lab experiments about 40–50% cooperate. The average jackpot was over $20,000, and one pair played for about $175,000. The sample is 287 pairs from the British show, which ran three years (p. 257).
- Figure 24 (cooperation by pot size, read from the image): 72% at $100, 65% at $250, 58% at $500, 54% at $1,000, then 59%, 59%, 52% at $1,500, $2,000, $2,500, then 50%, 51%, 47%, 46%, 49%, 43%, and 48% at $5,000 up to $100,000 (p. 258).
- Cooperation falls as stakes rise, but only to about 40–50%, the lab level. The author concludes there is no evidence that high low-stakes cooperation is unrepresentative of behavior at high stakes (p. 258).
- **Intuition note:** The decline comes from the very low pots, where cooperation was exceptionally high. At the highest pots the figure's note says players still cooperated about half the time (p. 258).
- **"Big peanuts" hypothesis (the authors' conjecture):** a sum feels small or large depending on context. With an average pot near $20,000, a $500 pot feels like peanuts, so why not be nice on TV? In a lab, $500 would be very large (p. 259). The authors suspect the same applies to Frank, who had been offered as much as €75,000 earlier and, in the last round, turned down a sure €6,000 to gamble (p. 259).

### Who splits? (p. 259)
- Among demographic variables, the only significant finding: young men are distinctly less likely to split (p. 259).
- Pre-decision speeches are "cheap talk" (no penalty for lying). The one reliable signal is an explicit promise to split, e.g. "I promise you I am going to split it, 120%". A player who does so is 30 percentage points more likely to split. The author links this to people lying more readily by omission than by commission (p. 259).

### Nick and Ibrahim (pp. 260–261)
- Golden Balls differs from the standard Prisoner's Dilemma: if you split and the other steals, you get zero, as if you had stolen. A footnote calls this a "weak" Prisoner's Dilemma (pp. 260–261).
- Nick told Ibrahim he would steal but then share the winnings. The host said no such promise is guaranteed, and Ibrahim asked why not both split. Ibrahim switched to "split" late. Nick also chose "split" (pp. 260–261).
- Ibrahim later said he had planned to steal until the last minute. By his own account, his story of a father who taught him to keep his word was invented: he said he never met his father (p. 261).

## Toy example
(Ours, not from the text.) A board has prizes €0, €10, €100, €1,000.
- Starting EV = (0 + 10 + 100 + 1,000)/4 = 1,110/4 = €277.50. An offer at 75% of EV would be 0.75 × 277.50 = €208.13.
- Suppose the contestant opens the €1,000 case. The board is €0, €10, €100, so EV = 110/3 = €36.67 and a 75% offer is €27.50.
- Expected utility says the choice depends only on this current board (offer €27.50 against a gamble over €0, €10, €100). Prospect theory with a reference point says a contestant who started near €208 may feel behind and gamble more. The chapter's Frank fits this pattern (p. 255).

## Concepts
- [[prospect-theory]] (new) — theory of choice with gains and losses relative to a reference point; compared with expected utility here (p. 254).
- [[house-money-effect]] (new) — more risk taking when one thinks one is ahead (p. 252).
- [[path-dependence]] (new) — earlier gains and losses influence current choices, though theory says they should not (p. 254).
- [[mental-accounting]] — term not defined in this unit; the author says the show is an ideal game to test it and prospect theory (p. 252).

- [[social-dilemma]] — Golden Balls final as a version of the Prisoner's Dilemma; in a game played once, the selfish rational strategy is for both to defect (p. 257).
- [[big-peanuts-hypothesis]] (new) — the authors' conjecture that a sum can seem small or large depending on context; a sum that feels like peanuts may make players willing to be nice (p. 259).
- [[cheap-talk]] (new) — costless statements; promises are mostly uninformative, but explicit promises predict splitting (p. 259).
- [[equity-premium-puzzle]] (new) — the author notes that high risk aversion was proposed to dissolve it; the game-show results do not support that (p. 254).

## Questions this raises
- If a win of €6,000 sounds large in a lab, how should "high stakes" be defined: absolute or relative to context?
- Why were students more risk averse before a crowd?
- How much of Golden Balls behavior is cooperative preference and how much is strategic posturing, given the weak Prisoner's Dilemma payoffs?
- Does the "moderately risk averse" finding generalize to non-TV settings?
