# Does the Stock Market Overreact?

## L0 — One line
Thaler and De Bondt used a psychology finding (extreme forecasts from flimsy data) to predict a new stock-market anomaly, and found that past Loser stocks later beat past Winner stocks, which the efficient market hypothesis (EMH: prices already reflect all available information) says should not happen.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler and De Bondt wanted a behavioral test that could predict something new in finance, not just explain an old fact. Kahneman and Tversky had shown that people make extreme predictions from weak information. Benjamin Graham's value investing and David Dreman's psychological reading of it suggested investors may overreact to recent performance. If so, extreme Winners should later disappoint and extreme Losers should recover, which the EMH rules out. Thaler and De Bondt ranked NYSE stocks over three to five years and compared Winner and Loser portfolios. Losers clearly beat Winners. The chapter also tells how the paper reached print despite the skepticism the authors expected from referees.

**Prerequisites:** price/earnings ratio; average return versus market return; the idea that prices already reflect public information; regression toward the mean.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Trading volume is high (about 5% monthly turnover). Thaler finds overconfidence a highly plausible explanation but impossible to prove, and a new explanation for an old fact is hard to prove in general. He and De Bondt therefore wanted a new prediction (p. 170).
- Kahneman–Tversky GPA experiment: forecasts from a sense-of-humor score were almost as extreme as forecasts from GPA decile. Thaler describes this as subjects "overreacting" to weak information (p. 171).
- Graham's Dow P/E table (1937–69) and Basu's and Banz's studies showed cheap or small stocks doing better. Thaler says the efficient market theory of the 1970s led academics to treat small-sample results like Graham's as anecdotes, and Basu and Banz wrote apologetic conclusions (pp. 173–174).
- Thaler says Dreman was the first to suggest an explicitly psychological explanation of the value effect, based on extrapolating the recent past (p. 174).
- Hypothesis: extreme Winners and Losers are overpriced or underpriced by stereotyping and extreme forecasts, so later returns regress toward the mean. The efficient market hypothesis forbids this (p. 175).
- Test: rank NYSE stocks over 3–5 years and compare the most extreme Winners and Losers afterwards. Losers did much better (p. 176).
- A lucky route into the Journal of Finance (Shefrin, Black) mattered: Thaler is convinced that otherwise publication would have taken years or not happened, since referees would have been highly skeptical (pp. 176–177).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Background (pp. 169–170).** Thaler met Werner De Bondt, a Belgian exchange student, at Cornell in fall 1978. He became De Bondt's thesis supervisor, though De Bondt's love was finance (p. 169). The plan was to add psychology to finance while using accepted financial-economics methods, so any result would be taken seriously (p. 169).

*Why a new prediction.* A behavioral explanation of an old effect, such as the equity risk premium, is hard to prove right (p. 170). Example: trading volume.
- A rational world would have hardly any trading. This is the "Groucho Marx theorem", or "no trade theorem". In the Tom and Jerry golf story, each asks why the other wants to trade, and the trade is called off (p. 170).
- Nobody takes the extreme version literally, but most financial economists agree, when pressed, that volume is surprisingly high: about 5% monthly turnover (p. 170).
- With overconfident investors, each thinks he is smarter than the other and volume emerges naturally. Thaler finds this highly plausible but impossible to prove (p. 170).

**The psychology input (p. 171, fig. p. 172).** Kahneman and Tversky asked subjects to predict students' GPA from one fact each. One group got the student's GPA decile (a decile is a 10%-band of the ranking). The other got a decile score on a "sense of humor" test (p. 171). A third condition, on mental concentration, gave results between the two (footnote, p. 177).
- Rational benchmark: GPA-decile forecasts should be much more extreme than humor-decile forecasts. Humor forecasts should stay close to the average GPA (p. 171).
- Actual result: humor forecasts were nearly as extreme. The top humor decile was predicted the same GPA as the top GPA decile (p. 171, fig. 11 on p. 172).
- Thaler's description: subjects "overreacted" to the humor information (p. 171).

*Intuition note.* People treat a weak clue almost as if it were a strong one. If investors do the same with recent company news, prices could move too far.

**Value investing and the academic reaction (pp. 172–174).**
- Thaler raises Keynes's claim that investors respond to "ephemeral and non-significant" day-to-day news (p. 172). He asks whether investors would behave like the GPA subjects, and treats the long-standing tradition of value investing as circumstantial evidence of overreaction (p. 172).
- Graham's measure is the price/earnings ratio (P/E): price per share divided by annual earnings per share. A high P/E implicitly forecasts fast earnings growth. A low P/E implicitly forecasts low or falling earnings (p. 173).
- Graham's table: from 1937 he ranked the 30 Dow stocks by P/E. A $10,000 stake in the 10 lowest-P/E stocks grew to $66,900 by 1969. The 10 highest-P/E stocks grew to $25,300. All 30 stocks grew to $44,000 (p. 173).
- Graham's implicit behavioral story: cheap stocks are unpopular, expensive ones fashionable, so a contrarian can beat the market, "although not all the time" (p. 173). The strategy did not work in 1917–33. Graham warned that mispricing from neglect or prejudice may persist "an inconveniently long time" (p. 173).
- Thaler adds that value investing did badly in the late-1990s technology bubble (p. 173).
- By the early 1980s most academics found Graham passé (p. 174). Digitised data (CRSP for prices, COMPUSTAT for accounting data) allowed bigger studies, so small hand-built results counted as anecdotes (p. 174).
- Thaler's reading: it was not so much that anyone had refuted Graham's claim as that the efficient market theory of the 1970s said value investing could not work (p. 174).
- Basu (late 1970s) supported Graham but ended with an apologetic conclusion about the 14-year period (p. 174). Banz (1981) found small firms beat large firms. He wrote that it was "not likely" due to market inefficiency but rather evidence of "pricing model misspecification" (p. 174).
- Dreman (1982, *The New Contrarian Investment Strategy*) is called by Thaler the first person to suggest an explicitly psychological explanation of the value effect, based on extrapolating the recent past. It made little impression on academics because it was a popular book (p. 174).

**The hypothesis (p. 175).**
- Suppose the P/E effect is caused by overreaction. High-P/E "growth" stocks are too high from over-optimistic forecasts. Low-P/E "value" stocks are too low from over-pessimism. Then later returns are regression toward the mean (p. 175). Example: a basketball player's record 50-point game is likely followed by fewer points (p. 175).
- Mechanism: years of success give a company an aura of being "good", and years of failure tag it "bad". Thaler calls this corporate stereotyping. Combined with overly extreme forecasts, it sets up mean reversion (p. 175).
- The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) has two components. "Price is right": prices do not diverge from intrinsic value. "No free lunch": you cannot beat the market, because the price already contains all information (p. 175). On the EMH's reasoning, past returns and P/E are known, so they cannot predict price changes (the text calls them SIFs). Mean reversion would therefore clearly violate the EMH (p. 175).

**The study (pp. 176–177).**
- Design: take all NYSE stocks and rank performance over a long period, for example 3–5 years. The best are "Winners", the worst "Losers". Compare a group of the biggest Winners and Losers (for example the most extreme 35 of each) going forward (p. 176).
- Predictions: under the EMH the two portfolios do equally well. Under overreaction, Losers beat Winners (p. 176).
- This would predict a new anomaly. It would also support "generalized overreaction", where the cause of the overreaction is not specified (p. 176).
- Result: with a look-back of at least about three years, Losers beat Winners, "much better". In one test, five years formed the portfolios. In the next five years Losers beat the market by about 30% and Winners trailed it by about 10% (p. 176).

*Intuition note.* Past price history alone, which the EMH calls useless, predicted later returns. That fits the idea that extreme past performance had pushed prices too far.

**Publication (pp. 176–177).** Shefrin organised an American Finance Association session. The annual Journal of Finance issue published selected session papers without formal peer review (pp. 176–177). Shefrin nominated both his own paper and theirs, and the AFA president Fischer Black published both (p. 177). The paper appeared in 1985. Thaler is convinced that without this back door the paper would have taken years to publish, or might not have been published at all, because everyone "knew" the results were wrong and referees would have been highly skeptical. He and De Bondt would also not have written an apologetic conclusion (p. 177).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four firms all have the same true long-run earnings growth: 6% a year. Their last five-year growth rates were A 12%, B 8%, C 4%, D 0%. The average is (12 + 8 + 4 + 0)/4 = 24/4 = 6%.
- Suppose overreacting investors extrapolate each firm's past fully, so they expect A to grow 12% and D 0%.
- Next period every firm actually grows 6%.
- Forecast error for Winner A: 6 − 12 = −6 points, a disappointment. For Loser D: 6 − 0 = +6 points, a pleasant surprise.
- So price news after ranking is bad for the Winner and good for the Loser. That is the Loser-beats-Winner pattern.
- A rational investor forecasting 6% for everyone would have errors of 0 for each firm, so past rank would predict nothing.

## Concepts
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — here two components: "price is right" and "no free lunch"; past returns and P/E cannot predict returns (p. 175)
- [[overreaction]] (new) — extreme forecasts from weak or ephemeral information; in markets, pushing prices too far up or down (pp. 171, 175)
- [[regression toward the mean]] (new) — extreme outcomes tend to be followed by less extreme ones; here the proposed source of Loser/Winner return reversal (p. 175)
- [[value investing / P/E effect]] (new) — in Graham's 1937–69 Dow data, low-P/E "cheap" stocks beat high-P/E stocks, though "not all the time" (p. 173)
- [[overconfidence]] (new) — each investor thinks he is smarter than the counterparty; Thaler finds this a highly plausible (but unprovable) explanation of high trading volume (p. 170)
- [[no-trade theorem]] (new) — in a rational world there would be hardly any trading, since no rational agent wants to buy what another rational agent is willing to sell; no one takes the extreme version literally (p. 170)
- [[anomaly]] (new) — an empirical pattern the EMH says should not occur, such as the value effect or the size effect, or the new Loser/Winner pattern (pp. 174–176)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — stereotyping companies as "good" or "bad" and extrapolating the recent past (pp. 174–175)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Regression toward the mean here ↔ statistics: extreme observations of a noisy variable move back toward the average. This is used as a story for prices, not a derived result.
- [structural-analogy] Banz's "pricing model misspecification" ↔ joint-hypothesis problem: a return anomaly can be blamed on the EMH or on the return model.
- [structural-analogy] Graham's warning that mispricing "may persist" ↔ limits to arbitrage: correcting a mispricing can be slow and risky.

## Questions this raises
- Does a Loser–Winner gap prove overreaction, or could it come from a missing risk factor (the "model misspecification" reply)?
- How long must the look-back period be, and does the effect also appear at short horizons?
- If Graham, Basu and Banz found real patterns, why did they not disappear once published?
- Does the evidence say what investors overreact to, given that "generalized overreaction" does not specify it?
