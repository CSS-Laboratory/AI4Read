# Anomalies

## L0 — One line
Following Kuhn's model, in which paradigms change only once experts believe there are many unexplained anomalies, Thaler used a regular journal column to document a broad list of them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Following Kuhn, Thaler notes that a few scattered unexplained facts are not enough to overturn a paradigm, and defenders can dismiss each with an ad hoc patch. He felt a whole series of anomalies, each needing its own patch, was required. The American Economic Association (AEA)'s new *Journal of Economic Perspectives* gave him a quarterly "Anomalies" column from 1987. He chose examples from many methods, including market data, to show that anomalies are not only lab results. Examples here are stock-market calendar effects and racetrack betting. The column was widely read. Its last installment appeared in 2006.

**Prerequisites:** expected return; the idea of a "paradigm"; basic logic of "if P then Q"; what an efficient market means.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Kuhn's model: paradigms change only when experts see a large number of anomalies the current paradigm cannot explain (p. 127).
- Defenders of the status quo pick results apart, find an "epicycle" (an ad hoc add-on) for each, and treat each anomaly as a one-off (p. 128). Thaler felt that a whole series of anomalies was needed (p. 128).
- The Wason four-card problem illustrates confirmation bias, the habit of looking for confirming evidence. This motivates a feature that reports disconfirming evidence (pp. 129–130).
- Thaler defines an anomaly as a result hard to rationalize within the paradigm, or one that needs implausible assumptions. To be considered for publication in the feature, proposed explanations must be falsifiable, at least in principle (p. 130).
- The column had large reach: about half of responding AEA members read it regularly, and Thaler says it reached over 5,000 economists (pp. 130–131).
- Only five of the first fourteen columns were mainly experimental, which fits his aim of dispelling the myth that anomalies occur only in the lab (p. 131).
- Calendar effects in stocks are anomalies for which Thaler had no explanation (p. 131).
- Racetrack returns differ across odds, contradicting the equal-expected-return prediction of an efficient market (pp. 131–132).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Kuhn and the strategy (pp. 127–128).**
- Thaler says Kuhn's model implies that paradigms change only once experts believe there are many unexplained anomalies. A few scattered facts are not enough (p. 127).
- He thought of his own work in this light only privately, because calling it part of a "revolution" would have seemed brash for a "promising" young academic (p. 127).
- Example of a paradigm shift: the Copernican revolution replaced the Ptolemaic view, in which everything revolved around the Earth. The geocentric system had long done a pretty good job of explaining planetary movements, albeit with numerous somewhat ad hoc modifications called epicycles (small circles on the main circular path) (pp. 127–128).
- At the Chicago conference, defenders of the status quo evoked a paradigm shift "with evident horror" (p. 128). Their method was to pick apart each result and find an "economics version of an epicycle." They treated each anomaly as a one-off puzzle (p. 128).
- Thaler's conclusion: a real shift would need a whole series of anomalies, each calling for its own ad hoc explanation (p. 128). He writes that he "felt" this was needed.

*Intuition note:* Defenders could always find an "epicycle" for any single odd fact. Thaler felt that only a whole series of anomalies, each needing its own ad hoc patch, could create a real shift.

**Origin of the column (pp. 128–129).**
- Hal Varian, an advisory editor of the new *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, and Thaler proposed an anomalies feature. Editor Joseph Stiglitz approved it, giving Thaler a platform four times a year (p. 128).
- The first installment (1987) opened with Kuhn's line that discovery begins with awareness that nature has violated "paradigm-induced expectations" (p. 129).

**Why a feature on anomalies? The four-card problem (p. 129).** This is Thaler's 1987 column text, based on Wason (1968).
- Four cards show A, B, 2, 3. The statement to test: every card with a vowel on one side has an even number on the other. Turn over as few cards as possible (p. 129).
- Typical class ranking, most to least turned over: A, 2, 3, B (p. 129).
- A is correctly chosen: an odd number behind it would falsify the statement (p. 129).
- The 2 is futile: turning it over can neither prove nor refute the statement (p. 129).
- The 3 must be turned: it can refute the statement. B must be turned too, since a vowel might be on its other side. The problem did not say numbers are always on one side and letters on the other, though solvers usually assume it (p. 129).
- Lesson 1: people tend to seek confirming rather than disconfirming evidence (the confirmation bias), shown by the 2 being chosen more than the 3. Lesson 2: unwarranted assumptions can strengthen this bias, shown by the unpopularity of B (p. 129).

*Intuition note:* To test "if vowel then even," look for cases that could break the rule. Turning a card that can only agree with it can neither prove nor refute the rule.

**The feature's rules (p. 130).**
- The feature reports "successful searches for disconfirming evidence" (p. 130).
- The paradigm, as Thaler states it: most behavior can be explained by stable, well-defined preferences and rational choices in markets that (eventually) clear. He writes "most (all?)" about how far this extends (p. 130).
- A result is anomalous if it is difficult to "rationalize" or needs implausible assumptions. "Difficult" and "implausible" are judgments, so readers may submit explanations (p. 130).
- To be considered for publication, proposed explanations must be falsifiable, at least in principle. A tax explanation should predict no anomaly in a no-tax country, for non-taxed agents, or before the tax existed. A transaction-cost explanation should predict the effect vanishes in an experiment without such costs (p. 130).

**Practicalities and reach (pp. 130–131).**
- Thaler wrote a column every quarter for nearly four years, each 10–12 pages with a "Commentary" section. He knew he could write on at least ten topics (p. 130).
- The work was time-consuming: he recruited specialist coauthors but wrote all the final versions. It took time from "real research" (p. 130).
- In an AEA survey, half the responding members said they read the feature "regularly." A typical specialized article is "probably lucky" to find 100 readers, whereas these reached over 5,000 economists (pp. 130–131).

**Choice of anomalies (p. 131).**
- Goal: a broad spectrum, many using market data, to dispel the myth that anomalies occur only in the lab. Of fourteen columns in the first four years, five were primarily experimental. Many concerned finance, the most disturbing to defenders of the paradigm (p. 131).
- Thaler did not have satisfactory behavioral explanations for every anomaly (p. 131).
- *Calendar effects* (first two columns): stocks tend to rise on Fridays and fall on Mondays. January is good, especially early January and for small companies. Days before holidays are good. He writes that all logical, and some illogical, explanations could be rejected, and that he had no explanation either (p. 131).
- *Racetrack betting* (pp. 131–132): in pari-mutuel systems the odds come from the money bet on each horse. The track removes a set share (typically about 17%) and winning bettors split the rest (p. 131).
- Efficiency prediction: if the track takes 17% and the betting market is efficient, every bet, from favorite to longshot, should have the same expected return of −17%; $100 bet returns $83 on average (p. 131).
- Data: an even-money favorite returns 90 cents per dollar. A 100-to-1 longshot returns about 14 cents (p. 132). Longshot returns are even worse in the last race of the day, which links to the breakeven effect of chapter 10 (p. 132).

*Intuition note:* If the crowd's odds were fair apart from the track's cut, all horses would lose the same 17% on average. Instead, longshot bettors lose much more.

**End of the column (p. 132).**
- Fourteen consecutive columns were collected as *The Winner's Curse* (itself a column title). A few more appeared irregularly; the last in 2006. The column was officially retired shortly after. Editor Andrei Shleifer declared the purpose served, which Thaler jokes meant he was "fired" (p. 132).
- Footnote: the editors handled refereeing, and Tim Taylor rewrote each article for non-specialists, letting authors opt out of changes (a default) (p. 132).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* A pool of $1,000 is bet on three horses. The track keeps 17%, so $830 is paid out.

| Horse | Money bet | Payout per $1 bet if it wins |
|---|---|---|
| A | $500 | 830/500 = 1.66 |
| B | $300 | 830/300 ≈ 2.7667 |
| C | $200 | 830/200 = 4.15 |

Suppose each horse's true win probability equals its share of the money: 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. Then the expected return per $1 is:
- A: 0.5 × 1.66 = 0.83
- B: 0.3 × 2.7667 = 0.83
- C: 0.2 × 4.15 = 0.83

Every bet returns 83 cents, i.e. −17%. This is the efficient-market benchmark. Thaler's anomaly is that real data deviate: an even-money favorite returns 90 cents and a 100-to-1 longshot only about 14 cents per dollar (p. 132).

## Concepts
- [[anomaly]] (new) — a result hard to rationalize within the paradigm, or one needing implausible assumptions (p. 130)
- [[paradigm-shift]] (new) — a rare break in which a field changes direction; in Kuhn's model it happens only once experts believe there are many unexplained anomalies (p. 127)
- [[confirmation-bias]] (new) — tendency to seek confirming rather than disconfirming evidence (p. 129)
- [[pari-mutuel-betting]] (new) — odds set by the money bet; the track removes a fixed share first (p. 131)
- [[calendar-effects]] (new) — day-of-week, January and pre-holiday patterns in stock returns (p. 131)
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — here, an efficient betting market implies equal expected returns on all bets (p. 131)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — not defined in this unit; only the paradigm's rationality assumptions appear (p. 130)
