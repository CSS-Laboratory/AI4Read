# Bargains and Rip-Offs

## L0 — One line
Besides the surplus from the thing bought, people also enjoy or dislike the deal itself (price versus a reference price), which Econs (the textbook-style decision makers) do not feel and sellers exploit.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks how real consumers value purchases, since the textbook method of comparing every alternative use of money is impossible to carry out. He proposes two kinds of utility. Acquisition utility is the standard consumer surplus. Transaction utility is the gap between the reference price and the price paid. A beach-beer survey shows people state different maximum prices for the same beer depending on where it is bought. Transaction utility can block good purchases and trigger wasteful ones. Sellers therefore create "deals" with fictional suggested prices and frequent sales. Retailers that cut back or dropped coupons and sales (Macy's, JC Penney) did badly. Walmart and Costco also sell transaction utility.

**Prerequisites:** opportunity cost; consumer surplus; loss aversion and the prospect-theory value function; the median.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The normative theory of choice (how people should choose) rests on opportunity costs. No one can fully carry it out, and many people consider only a few alternatives (pp. 24–25).
- Thaler's "pin factory" interviews of lower-middle-class households shaped his views on mental accounting (p. 25).
- Paying for a routine purchase is not felt as a loss. If it were, trading a $10 bill for two $5 bills would hurt (pp. 25–26).
- Acquisition utility equals consumer surplus. Transaction utility is the difference between the price paid and the reference price: negative for a "rip-off", positive for a "bargain". Econs feel only the first (p. 26).
- In the beach-beer survey the consumption is identical, yet median willingness to pay was $7.25 (resort) versus $4.10 (store). Thaler explains this through expectations, as transaction utility, and the place of purchase is a supposedly irrelevant factor (SIF) for Econs (p. 27).
- Transaction utility can deter welfare-enhancing purchases and induce wasteful ones (pp. 27–28).
- Sellers have an incentive to manipulate reference prices; goods marketed this way (rugs, mattresses) are bought infrequently and their quality is hard to assess (p. 28).
- Macy's and JC Penney failed when they cut coupons and "fake prices". Walmart and Costco succeed with genuinely low prices and by making shopping feel like bargain hunting (pp. 28–30).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 24).** Maya Bar-Hillel saw quilts with regular prices of $300 (king), $250 (queen) and $200 (double). All were on sale at $150 this week. She has a double bed, yet she bought the king size (p. 24).

**Opportunity costs and the limits of the normative theory (pp. 24–25).**
- Thaler recalls that all economic decisions are made through the lens of opportunity costs. If a ticket could be sold for $1,000, what you paid for it does not matter; going costs what the $1,000 could do otherwise (p. 24).
- Thaler calls this the right normative theory, and it is what Econs do. But anyone trying to apply it to every decision would be paralyzed, because the problem is too complex (pp. 24–25).
- Many people consider only a few alternatives (p. 25).
- Footnote: Mullainathan and Shafir report that the poor come closer to Econ behaviour on this dimension, because opportunity costs are salient for them. The constant worry takes a toll and may contribute to bad decisions such as rolling over payday loans (p. 30).

*The interviews (p. 25).* Thaler hired a student to interview local lower-middle-class families, because spending matters more when budgets are tight. The respondent was the household member handling the money, more often the wife. The aim was a general impression, not data for a paper. He says "This was my pin factory" and that the interviews greatly influenced his later writing on mental accounting (p. 25).

**When is a cost a loss? (pp. 25–26).**
- Prospect theory's value function is steeper for losses than for gains. Losses hurt about twice as much as gains please (p. 25).
- If paying $5 for a sandwich felt like a loss, life would be miserable. Even trading a $10 bill for two $5 bills would count as a loss. "Losing" each $5 would hurt more than receiving $10 pleases (pp. 25–26).
- So for routine transactions, a payment is clearly not felt as a loss (p. 25).

**Two kinds of utility (p. 26).**
- *Acquisition utility* is the utility of the object gained minus the opportunity cost of what is given up. It equals consumer surplus. For an Econ it is the whole story (p. 26).
- Examples: a thirsty person gets a windfall from a $1 water bottle. A quilt that fits a double bed is worth more to an Econ than one hanging two feet over each side (p. 26).
- *Transaction utility* is the difference between the price actually paid and the price normally expected, the *reference price*. The text does not state the order of subtraction; it calls the result "negative" for a rip-off and "positive" for a bargain. A sandwich at triple the usual price gives negative transaction utility. Maya's king quilt at the price of a smaller one gives positive (p. 26).

*Intuition note:* Acquisition utility asks "is the thing worth it?" Transaction utility asks "is this a good deal compared with what I expect?" Only the second depends on the reference price.

**The beach-beer survey (pp. 26–28).**
- Setup: executive-MBA students who were regular beer drinkers imagine lying on a beach, wanting a cold beer. A friend will buy it from a fancy resort hotel or a small rundown grocery store. He buys only if the price is at or below the stated limit. No bargaining is possible (p. 26).
- Design points: the consumption is identical, the respondent never enters or sees the establishment, so no ambience is consumed, and no negotiation exists, so the situation is incentive compatible (p. 27).
- Result: median willingness to pay (inflation-adjusted) was $7.25 for the resort and $4.10 for the store (p. 27).
- Thaler's explanation: people expect higher prices at a fancy hotel, so $7 there is "annoying but expected" while $7 at a bodega is an outrage. For Econs the place of purchase is a supposedly irrelevant factor (SIF) (p. 27).
- Econs do like bargains, such as 10-cent beers, but that pleasure is captured by acquisition utility (p. 27).
- Footnote: the median is the middle answer, with as many answers above as below (p. 30).

**Welfare effects (pp. 27–28).**
- Transaction utility can prevent welfare-enhancing purchases and induce wasteful ones (p. 27).
- Example: Dennis would pay $4 at the bodega but $7 at the hotel. Tom could buy at the store for $5 and say it was from the hotel, and Dennis would be happier. Only his distaste for overpaying stops him from agreeing to the purchase without Tom's subterfuge (p. 27).
- For comfortable people, negative transaction utility can block special experiences, and the overpayment is soon forgotten. Good deals can lure people into buying things of little value, like Maya's quilt (p. 28).

*Intuition note:* The feeling about the deal is separate from the benefit of the item. It can cut either way, so it can both block worthwhile purchases and induce wasteful ones.

**Sellers' responses (pp. 28–30).**
- Sellers manipulate reference prices, for example with a largely fictional "suggested retail price" (p. 28).
- Rugs, mattresses and at some retailers men's suits seem always on sale. Such goods are bought infrequently and their quality is hard to assess. The suggested price can suggest high quality (more perceived acquisition utility) and imply a bargain (transaction utility) (p. 28).
- Shoppers can get hooked on transaction utility. "Everyday low pricing" experiments usually fail (p. 28).
- Footnote: a study found that when Walmart entered a market, supermarkets using promotional pricing did significantly better in revenues and long-term viability than those using everyday low prices (p. 30).
- *Macy's:* in 2006–07 it targeted coupons, fearing a link to less prestigious retailers. It cut coupon use by 30% in spring 2007. Sales plummeted and it promised to restore coupons by the holiday season (pp. 28–29).
- *JC Penney (2012):* less than 1% of revenue came from full-price transactions. CEO Ron Johnson announced an end to "fake prices", ended coupon sales, and rounded .99 prices up to the nearest dollar. JC Penney claimed the effective price paid was the same (p. 29).
- Result: sales and stock price plummeted. Johnson was ousted a year later, coupons returned, and sales had not recovered by 2014. Thaler says consumers may have paid no more but lost transaction utility, and he only suggests ("maybe") that they disliked being told the prices were fake (p. 29).
- *Walmart and Costco* succeed with everyday low prices. They have not removed transaction utility; they convey that shopping is bargain hunting, for example Walmart's receipt-scanning "savings catcher" refund app. Unless Macy's and JC Penney gave up all pretensions of an upscale shopping experience, they could not compete with these low-cost providers in providing transaction utility (p. 29).

**Conclusion (pp. 29–30).** Hunting for bargains is fine, and savings on one purchase make another possible. Consumers should avoid buying unused things because the deal is too good. Businesses should know that everyone wants a good deal, even affluent Costco shoppers (pp. 29–30).

## Toy example
*(Our own example.)* A reference price for a jacket is $100.
- Store A sells it at $60. Store B sells it at $90. Suppose it is worth $95 to you.
- Acquisition utility: A gives 95 − 60 = 35. B gives 95 − 90 = 5.
- Transaction utility, as the chapter defines it (reference price minus price paid, signs as in the chapter's bargain/rip-off labels): A gives 100 − 60 = +40 (bargain). B gives 100 − 90 = +10.
- Now the reference price is $50 (you usually shop at a discounter). At $60 transaction utility is 50 − 60 = −10, a rip-off, even though acquisition utility is still 35. A person who weights the rip-off feeling heavily might refuse, while an Econ would buy.
