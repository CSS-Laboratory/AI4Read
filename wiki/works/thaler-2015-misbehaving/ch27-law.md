# Law Schooling

## L0 — One line
Thaler recounts presenting "behavioral law and economics" to Chicago's law-and-economics establishment, arguing that the endowment effect undercuts the Coase theorem even when transaction costs are essentially zero, and that anger over unfair offers is another reason its predictions often fail.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler, Christine Jolls and Cass Sunstein wrote a paper arguing that law and economics should be modified to allow for Humans who have three bounds: bounded rationality, bounded willpower and bounded self-interest. At a Chicago workshop, Judge Richard Posner and others objected. The biggest fight was over the Coase theorem (with no transaction costs, resources flow to their highest-valued use). Thaler's mug and token experiments show the endowment effect lowering trade even when transaction costs are essentially zero. A new Ultimatum Game, in which players supply the stakes, shows more rejection of unfair offers. A short, mild section on paternalism also drew anger. The chapter shows why behavioral findings threaten arguments in which markets alone sort things out.

**Prerequisites:** Ultimatum Game, endowment effect and sunk costs (earlier chapters); the idea of an "Econ" (a fully rational agent) and a "Human" (a real person with limits); paternalism (helping people make better choices); basic property rights.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Traditional law and economics was based exclusively on Econs, and many of its arguments depended implicitly on markets being left to sort things out. Thaler's team built a "three bounds" alternative (pp. 211–212).
- Orange, sticky parking tickets may raise the *perceived* chance of being caught without changing the actual chance. That conflicts with the assumption of correct beliefs about the probability of being caught (p. 214).
- Posner's evolution objection: if people evolved to behave a certain way, the behavior must be good for us "in some sense" and therefore rational. Thaler replies that behavioral economics documents conflicts with the standard model, and that the model needs changing to predict well (pp. 214–215).
- The Coase theorem says that with no transaction costs, resources flow to their highest-valued use whoever holds the legal right. Coase himself calls the no-transaction-cost assumption "very unrealistic" (pp. 215–216).
- Tokens traded as predicted, but mugs did not, because owners valued mugs about twice as much. Thaler says the token result rules out transaction costs as the explanation (pp. 220–221).
- Lott's suggestion to relabel the endowment effect as a "transaction cost" would make the theory untestable, in Thaler's view (p. 221).
- When players supplied the Ultimatum stakes themselves, Responders demanded more. Anger and unfairness also help explain why Coasean bargaining fails (pp. 222–223).
- Paternalism is approached in the mildest way, as "anti-antipaternalism," with the caveat that bureaucrats are also Humans (pp. 223–224).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Origin of the project (pp. 211–213).** Thaler spent 1994–95 at MIT Sloan, then moved to Chicago (p. 211). Orley Ashenfelter asked him for a talk on behavioral economics and law. Thaler knew no law, so he recruited Christine Jolls, who was finishing a PhD in economics and a Harvard law degree (p. 211). Traditional law and economics used "exclusively" Econs models. Many papers took pages to conclude that leaving markets alone is best, often relying implicitly on "the invisible handwave" (p. 211). Thaler's plan was to add the "three bounds" and see how arguments must change (p. 212). Cass Sunstein joined. The paper, "A Behavioral Approach to Law and Economics," was 76 pages with 220 footnotes (p. 212). Law reviews allow multiple simultaneous submissions. Stanford Law Review accepted first, and Thaler proposed a commentary by a leading law-and-economics figure (pp. 212–213). Posner was the obvious choice. He is considered by many to be the founder of modern law and economics, and he is a federal judge on the Seventh Circuit. Law and economics was primarily based on Chicago-style economics, so he had a considerable investment in it (p. 213). Posner sent a long, critical, emotional letter the day before the workshop (p. 213).

*Intuition note:* Many Econ-based articles reached "leave markets alone" conclusions. Thaler's team wanted to see how such arguments would have to change once Human limits are added.

**The workshop (pp. 214–215).**
- **Parking-ticket example.** Standard theory assumes correct beliefs, including about the chance of being caught, and that people weigh expected gains and losses. Brighter, more visible tickets might raise perceived probability "at almost no cost." If perception can be shifted without changing the actual probability, the same may hold for more serious crimes. Thaler says this thought was "pure heresy" to the audience (p. 214). A footnote (marker p. 214, text p. 224) says they later called this a nudge.
- **Posner's objection:** evolution may explain the "cognitive quirks" (Ultimatum rejections, attention to sunk costs). If humans evolved to behave so, he held, it must be good for us "in some sense" and therefore rational (pp. 214–215).
- **Thaler's reply:** evolution is accepted, but it need not feature in the economic analysis. We know people are loss averse without needing the evolutionary explanation. Behavioral economics highlights behaviors that conflict with the standard rational model. Unless the model is changed to say people attend to sunk costs, it "will make poor predictions" (p. 215). Posner called this "completely unscientific" (p. 215).

**The Coase theorem (pp. 215–216, 220–221).**
- **Statement.** With no transaction costs (people can easily trade), resources flow to their highest-valued use (p. 215). A footnote adds that the stakes must be small relative to the parties' wealth; Thaler says he ignores this proviso in the discussion (p. 224).
- **Roommate example.** Alexa will pay $5 a night to play loud music. Julia will pay $3 a night for silence. Hallie, the resident advisor, can give either one the right (p. 215).
  - If Julia holds the right to silence, Alexa pays Julia some amount between $3 and $5, and both are better off (p. 216).
  - If Alexa holds the right, Julia will not pay enough to stop her, since silence is worth less to Julia than the music is to Alexa (p. 216).
  - Either way Alexa plays her music. When transaction costs are low, the ruling decides only who pays (p. 216).
- **Why it matters.** Judges often decide who owns a right. If transaction costs are low, their decision will not determine which economic activities occur (p. 216). Coase called zero transaction costs "a very unrealistic assumption" (p. 216). The team wanted to show the result fails even when transaction costs are essentially zero (p. 216).
- **Experiments (figure 17, mug experiments of chapter 16).** The figure's pages carry no text in the supplied layer, so its contents are not reproduced here.
  - *Tokens.* Each subject has a personal cash redemption value for a token. The highest-valuation students ended up owning the tokens, as predicted. So transaction costs were not inhibiting trade in any meaningful way (p. 220).
  - *Mugs.* Every other student got a mug. By random assignment about half the mugs should trade under the theory. Trading volume was much lower, because owners valued mugs about twice as much as non-owners did. So the initial allocation did affect final ownership (p. 220).
- **Transaction-cost rejoinder.** Gertrude Lott asked whether transaction costs explain the low trading. Thaler answered that tokens carried the same transaction costs as mugs and traded as predicted (p. 221). **Lott's objection:** couldn't the endowment effect itself be called a transaction cost? **Thaler's reply:** transaction costs are the cost of doing a transaction, not the desire to do one. If preferences can be relabeled as costs, the theory is "untestable and worthless" (p. 221). Posner smiled and conceded Thaler was not the least scientific person in the room (p. 221).

*Intuition note:* The token market shows trade can work in this setting. The mug market shows trade falling well short of the prediction when owners value what they were handed. So the failure comes from preferences, not from trading costs.

**Ultimatum Game with own money (pp. 221–223).** Thaler suggests that clinging to cherished theories might be evidence of the sunk-cost fallacy, and then presents one new experiment (p. 221).
- *Design.* Students brought $5 each. A $10 pot was formed from the two $5 contributions. Each filled out a form for both roles, Proposer and Responder, and was then paired with an anonymous student holding the other role. The smallest offer allowed was 50 cents (pp. 221–222). The $5 is a sunk cost, a "SIF" (a term the chapter uses without defining it). If sunk costs don't matter, outcomes should match the standard game (p. 222).
- *Results.* Most Proposers offered at least 40% of $10, much as in earlier versions (p. 222). Responders became *less* consistent with income maximization:

| Group | Average minimum request |
|---|---|
| Earlier experiments (Kahneman, Knetsch, Thaler) | $1.94 |
| MIT MBA | $3.21 |
| Chicago MBA | $3.73 |
| Chicago law | $3.35 |

  Many Responders in all three groups demanded their full $5 back (p. 222). Making the game more "real" did not make Responders more self-interested (p. 222). A footnote says the experimenter "makes money" through rejected offers, and the authors always found a way to return the money to students, often by playing a beauty contest game (pp. 224–225).

*Intuition note:* One might expect players using their own money to act more rationally. Thaler found the opposite: Responders' minimum requests rose.

**Link to Coase: fairness and anger (pp. 222–223).**
- *Willow tree.* Thaler's neighbor wanted a willow tree removed. The cost was $1,000, about a month's income for Thaler. Thaler was ambivalent about the tree and would not pay. Applying the Coase theorem, he offered to let the neighbor remove it at his own expense. The neighbor found this outrageous and slammed the door (pp. 222–223).
- *General point.* Offended people may punish the other party at a cost to themselves. After a lawsuit, both sides are typically upset, especially the loser. The Coase theorem needs the loser to make an offer if he values the lost right more (p. 223).
- *Evidence.* Ward Farnsworth interviewed attorneys from over twenty civil cases in which injunctive relief was granted or denied after full litigation. In not a single case did the parties try to negotiate afterward (p. 223).

**Paternalism (pp. 223–224).** Chicago libertarianism rests on consumer sovereignty, the notion that people choose well and better than anyone else could for them. The bounds undercut this: if people err, someone could in principle help (p. 223). The team used Sunstein's term "anti-antipaternalism," which stops short of arguing for paternalism. It says the knee-jerk claim that nobody can help others decide better is undercut by research. A two-page section on this was followed by a longer one on "behavioral bureaucrats," stressing that bureaucrats are also biased Humans (pp. 223–224). Thaler says critics keep accusing them of ignoring this (p. 224).

**Aftermath and postscript (p. 224).** No key participant was converted, but the paper was going to cause a stir. Thaler cannot say what impact the paper had, only that it is frequently cited. The field now fills an 800-page handbook. Russell Korobkin is quoted: "The battle … has been won." Thaler prefers "mission launched" (p. 224).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four students have token-style values for a mug: $1, $2, $3, $4. Two mugs are given at random.
- *Coase prediction (no endowment effect).* The mugs end up with the two highest valuers ($3 and $4). Say the mugs start with the $1 and $2 students. Both mugs trade, so 2 trades.
- *Expected trades under random assignment.* The expected number of mugs held by the bottom two valuers is $2 \times \tfrac{2}{4} = 1$. This is half of the 2 mugs, matching "about half should trade" (p. 220).
- *Endowment effect, owners value mugs at double.* Owners' values become $2$ and $4$. Owner "$2$" sells to a buyer valuing $3$ or $4$, since $3>2$. Owner "$4$" will not sell to the remaining buyer valuing $3$ or $4$, since $3<4$ and $4=4$ gives no gain. So 1 trade, not 2, and one mug sits with a lower-valuing owner.

## Concepts
- [[coase-theorem]] (new) — with no transaction costs (and small stakes), resources flow to highest-valued use whoever holds the right (pp. 215–216, 224)
- [[transaction-costs]] (new) — the cost of doing a transaction, not the desire to do it (p. 221)
- [[endowment-effect]] — owners value goods more than non-owners; mug owners about twice as much (p. 220)
- [[three-bounds]] (new) — bounded rationality, bounded willpower, bounded self-interest (p. 212)
- [[bounded-rationality-heuristics]] — limited cognition, treated here as one of the three bounds (p. 212)
- [[ultimatum-game]] (new) — Proposer offers a split; Responder may reject, leaving both with nothing (pp. 215, 221–222; footnote pp. 224–225)
- [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] (new) — paying attention to sunk costs that should not matter; the $5 stake is called a SIF (pp. 215, 222)
- [[consumer-sovereignty]] (new) — the notion that people make good choices, and better ones than anyone else could make for them (p. 223)
- [[anti-antipaternalism]] (new) — Sunstein's term for the mild view that helping people choose better is not impossible (p. 223)
- [[nudge]] (new) — later name for changes like the orange ticket (p. 214, footnote)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Coase theorem with low transaction costs ↔ economics-finance: no-arbitrage reasoning that trade removes inefficiency, which fails when frictions exist.
- [metaphor] Posner's "evolved, so rational" ↔ economics-finance: adaptive markets hypothesis, where behavior is fit to an environment.
- [structural-analogy] Responders rejecting unfair offers ↔ economics-finance: social dilemmas, where costly punishment changes outcomes.

## Questions this raises
- If transaction costs are not the cause, how large is the endowment effect's effect on legal outcomes beyond mugs?
- Can perceived probabilities of punishment be shifted for serious crimes as for parking tickets?
- How can "anti-antipaternalism" be put into practice given biased bureaucrats?
- If a behavior has an evolutionary explanation, as Posner suggested, does that make it rational, or change how well a model predicts it?
