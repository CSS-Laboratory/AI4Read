# Supposedly Irrelevant Factors

## L0 — One line
Standard economic theory models people as perfectly optimizing "Econs", but real Humans are swayed by factors the theory calls irrelevant, so economics needs to take these factors seriously (behavioral economics).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler opens with a story. Students were happier with 96/137 than with 72/100, though the grade rule and the share of correct answers were about the same. He asks what this says about the economist's model of people. The model assumes optimizing, unbiased "Econs" plus equilibrium. He argues these premises are flawed. Yet he says Econ models remain useful starting points, and much empirical work does not depend on optimization. Models become dangerous when they make precise predictions that require everyone to be sophisticated, in policy on retirement saving or bubbles, for example. He proposes paying attention to "supposedly irrelevant factors" (SIFs). He then previews the book.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of rational choice under a budget; supply and demand equilibrium; what a randomized experiment is.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A hard exam with a low raw average (72/100) upset students even though grades were curved and announced. Changing the total to 137 points made them happy at no cost to grading (pdf 16–17).
- Thaler contrasts *homo economicus* ("Econ") with real Humans. Humans' "misbehavior" shows the model is wrong, not that people are defective (pdf 17).
- Economics gets its power from a unified core: Optimization + Equilibrium = Economics, with unbiased beliefs (rational expectations) (pdf 18).
- Three flaws: problems are often too hard to optimize; beliefs are biased (e.g. overconfidence); and many factors are left out (SIFs) (pdf 18–19).
- Evidence has grown from anecdote to high-stakes domains: retirement saving, mortgages, stock investing, and the 1987 crash, the tech bubble, the housing bubble and the crisis (pdf 20).
- Econ models are not to be discarded: they are starting points. They may approximate reality when problems are easy or actors are specialists. Much empirical work (randomized trials, natural experiments) does not assume optimization (pdf 20–21).
- An imprecise, directional prediction can be safe enough (the fertilizer example). Precise ones that assume universal sophistication can give bad policy advice (pdf 21).
- Behavioral economics is still economics, now with psychology added. It aims at better predictions and it has entered policy (UK team, 2010) and business (pdf 22).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**The exam story (pdf 16–17).**
- Thaler wrote a hard midterm to separate top, middle and bottom students. Scores were widely dispersed, but the average was 72/100, and students were in an uproar (pdf 16).
- The raw average had no effect on grades. The school's norm was a curve with average B or B+, and few grades below C. He had announced the mapping: above 80 gives A or A–, above 65 gives some B, only below 50 risks below C. Students were still unhappy (pdf 16).
- Fix: make the total 137 points. The next exam was slightly harder, with 70% of answers right, but the average was 96 points. Students were delighted, though no grade changed (pdf 17).
- He chose 137 for two reasons: the average lands in the 90s, and dividing by 137 in one's head is hard, so most students did not seem to bother converting to percentages. He later disclosed the practice in the syllabus, so he says it was not deception (pdf 17).
- An economist would see "misbehavior". No one should be happier with 96/137 (70%) than with 72/100 (pdf 17).

**Econs vs Humans (pdf 17–19).**
- The problem lies with the model, not with people. Economic models use a fictional *homo economicus*, an "Econ". Humans misbehave relative to it, so models predict badly. Virtually no economists saw the 2007–08 crisis coming, and many thought it could not happen. A footnote credits Robert Shiller with warning about housing prices (pdf 17–18).
- Economics is "powerful" in two ways: its policy influence (a near monopoly on policy advice), and its unified core theory, which other social sciences lack and which economists compare to physics (pdf 18).
- Core premise: people choose by optimizing. They pick the best affordable option, and their beliefs are unbiased ("rational expectations"). Example: if people starting new businesses on average believe their chance of succeeding is 75%, that should be a good estimate of the share that actually succeed. Econs are not overconfident (pdf 18).
- This is combined with equilibrium: in competitive markets, prices move until supply equals demand. Thaler's simplification: Optimization + Equilibrium = Economics (pdf 18).
- Three flaws (pdf 18–19):
  1. Optimization is often too hard. Even a grocery trip offers millions of affordable combinations. Careers, mortgages and spouses are harder, and observed failure rates make universal optimality hard to defend.
  2. Beliefs are not unbiased. Overconfidence is well established, and psychologists have documented many other biases.
  3. Many factors are omitted. Econs would ignore Sunday hunger when buying for Tuesday. They would ignore the price already paid when deciding whether to finish a meal. They would not care about the date of a gift, and would prefer cash.
- Adam Smith, before *The Wealth of Nations*, wrote a book on human "passions". Econs have none (pdf 19).

*Intuition note:* Thaler says the theory's intellectual power comes from a unified core built on a few premises. He argues those premises are flawed, so predictions built on them can be bad. The exam story is a small case where an "irrelevant" number (the total) mattered to students' mood.

**Evidence and what to keep (pdf 20–21).**
- Critiques were once brushed aside. Evidence has since "raised the stakes": poor choices in retirement saving, mortgages and stock investing, and the 19 October 1987 fall of over 20% in stock prices worldwide without substantive bad news. This was followed by the tech bubble, a housing bubble, and the global crisis (pdf 20).
- Thaler does not say to discard Econ theory. It remains a starting point. It may approximate reality when problems are easy or actors have specialized skills, but these cases are "the exception rather than the rule" (pdf 20).
- Much empirical work does not rely on optimization. Randomized control trials and natural experiments study treatments such as education, class size, job help, jail, moving neighborhoods and Medicaid. They can also test optimizing models (pdf 20–21).
- A directional prediction can be safe enough, because it is imprecise. Example: farmers use more fertilizer if its price falls. This is like apples falling down, not the law of gravity (pdf 21).
- Trouble comes from precise predictions that need everyone sophisticated. If farmers were all Econs, publishing research findings would be enough. Thaler says this is bad advice unless all farmers are Econs, and asks about peasant farmers in India or Africa. Similarly, someone who believes everyone saves just the right amount may conclude there is no reason to help people save (for example, with pension plans), and Thaler says this passes up the chance to make many people better off. A central banker who believes bubbles are impossible can make serious mistakes, as Greenspan has admitted happened to him (pdf 21).

*Intuition note:* A weak, directional claim (the effect goes one way) can stay safe even if people are imperfect. A precise claim that needs everyone to be sophisticated may not. Policy built on such a claim can pass up chances to make people better off (pdf 21).

**The proposal and the book (pdf 22–23).**
- Keep building abstract Econ models, but do not treat them as accurate descriptions or base policy on them. Pay attention to SIFs (pdf 22).
- Behavioral economics is "still economics", with psychology and other social sciences. It aims at better prediction. It is also "more interesting and more fun", the "un-dismal science". The UK Behavioural Insights Team was formed in 2010, and businesses are catching on (pdf 22).
- Plan: early years collecting odd examples; then mental accounting, self-control, fairness, finance; then taxi drivers, NFL drafting, game shows; finally Downing Street. The book is both chronological and topical. The early years include challenges from those who questioned the enterprise, Thaler mentions squabbles with traditionalists, and he advises stopping reading when it is no longer fun (pdf 23).

## Toy example (ours)
A student answers 70% of questions correctly.
- Exam A, 100 points: score = 0.70 × 100 = 70.
- Exam B, 137 points: score = 0.70 × 137 = 95.9, about 96.
- The percentage is 95.9 / 137 = 0.70 in both cases, so an Econ sees no difference.
- A student who reacts to the raw number sees 70 versus 96 and feels better on B. That is a supposedly irrelevant factor in action.

## Concepts
- [[econ-vs-human]] (new) — Econ: a fictional perfect optimizer with unbiased beliefs; Human: a real person who departs from it (pdf 17)
- [[supposedly-irrelevant-factors]] (new) — SIFs: factors that theory says should not matter but do affect behavior (pdf 19, 22)
- [[constrained-optimization]] (new) — choosing the best option the budget allows; the first core premise (pdf 18)
- [[rational expectations]] — beliefs are unbiased: what people on average believe about an outcome should be a good estimate of the actual rate (pdf 18)
- [[equilibrium]] — free prices adjust until supply equals demand (pdf 18)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — Thaler argues choice problems are often too hard to optimize; the term itself is not used here (pdf 18–19)
- [[behavioral-economics]] (new) — economics with strong injections of psychology and other social sciences (pdf 22)

## Canonical instances
- [[137-point-exam]] — raw score level changes mood although grades and (roughly) the share correct do not change (pdf 16–17)
- [[crash-of-1987]] — stock prices fell over 20% worldwide with no substantive bad news (pdf 20)
- [[fertilizer-and-farmers]] — a directional prediction is safe, but "publish the findings" policy assumes Econs (pdf 21)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] SIFs ↔ economics-finance: sunk-cost and mental-accounting effects — past price paid should not matter to the current decision (pdf 19).
- [metaphor] Econ theory as "physics" ↔ physics: a few core premises generate the rest. Thaler says economists draw this comparison (pdf 18).
- [structural-analogy] Randomized trials and natural experiments ↔ causal inference: they learn about treatment effects without assuming optimization (pdf 20–21).

## Questions this raises
- When are problems "easy" enough, or actors specialized enough, for Econ models to work well (pdf 20)?
- How can we tell a safe, directional prediction from a risky, precise one before it fails?
- If the 137-point trick works because students do not compute percentages, would it still work if they did?
- Which SIFs matter most in high-stakes domains such as retirement saving?
