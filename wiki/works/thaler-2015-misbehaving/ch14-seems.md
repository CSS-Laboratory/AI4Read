# What Seems Fair?

## L0 — One line
Ordinary people judge firms' prices and wage decisions by fairness norms tied to a reference point, and firms that break these norms risk losing long-term goodwill, even when standard theory says the action is correct.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks what firm actions people think are unfair. Telephone surveys of about 100 respondents per question show that price rises after a demand surge, such as snow shovels after a blizzard, are widely judged unfair. Fairness depends on framing, on the status quo (the usual terms) as a reference point (the standard against which a change is judged), and on whether the money goes to the seller. Thaler uses this to give a partial explanation of sticky wages (wages that do not fall enough in recessions). He then argues, from cases (First Chicago, Coca-Cola, iTunes, Uber, Next), that firms who plan long relationships should respect these norms. He does not know of any systematic study of whether firms obey the norms (p. 94).

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand price rises under a shortage; the idea of a reference point (endowment effect, loss); nominal versus real wages.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Surveys (about 100 respondents per question) test which firm actions count as "unfair". The aim is descriptive, not moral (pp. 90–91).
- Raising prices after a demand surge is judged unfair by most respondents, though standard theory says it allocates goods to those who value them most. MBA students judged it acceptable (p. 90).
- Who gets the money matters. An auction is far more acceptable if proceeds go to charity, but not for medicine in an epidemic (pp. 91–92).
- Fairness is related to the endowment effect (people feel entitled to the terms they are used to and treat any worsening as a loss). The usual terms of trade are a reference point, and a surcharge is worse than removing a discount (pp. 92–93).
- In the survey, most respondents judged a cut in the nominal wage (the money figure) unfair, but most judged a raise below inflation acceptable. Thaler offers this as part of a partial explanation for sticky wages (pp. 93–94).
- Firms that break the norms can be punished. Thaler says a large first mover runs considerable risks if competitors (the other firms) do not follow it (p. 97).
- Thaler advises firms seeking long-term goodwill to under-price at peak times, and advises Uber to cap surges (pp. 99–100).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Setup.** Thaler joined a project started by "Danny and Jack" (the page names Jack Knetsch) on what makes a transaction seem "fair", related to his "beer on the beach" question (p. 89). Through Knetsch's arrangement with the Canadian government, they faxed questions on Mondays and received answers from random samples of Ontario (later British Columbia) residents on Thursday nights (p. 89). Answers "completely fair" and "acceptable" were merged as "acceptable"; "somewhat unfair" and "very unfair" as "unfair". Each question had about 100 respondents (p. 90).

**Snow shovels.** A store raises its price from $15 to $20 the morning after a snowstorm. Result: acceptable 18%, unfair 82% (p. 90). Theory says the price should rise so that shovels go to those with the highest willingness to pay (p. 90). Thaler's MBA students answered with acceptable 76%, unfair 24%. He calls this Kahneman's "theory-induced blindness" (p. 90). The exercise is "experimental philosophy": it describes what people think fair and what makes them angry (p. 90). Many places have anti-gouging laws (p. 91).

*Intuition note:* Standard theory says higher prices after a shock are right. Most ordinary people feel they are being poked. Training in theory can make people forget how Humans react.

**Who gets the proceeds.** Cabbage Patch doll, one left a week before Christmas, sold by auction: acceptable 26%, unfair 74%. Adding "proceeds donated to UNICEF": acceptable 79% (p. 91). The unpopularity thus seems to come from the owner extracting every penny, not simply from the doll going to the affluent (p. 91). For the last package of medicine in a flu epidemic, people disliked the auction even with charity proceeds. Thaler suggests that for most people health care is a different category (pp. 91–92). He mentions that no country permits a free organ market, though Iran has a kidney market, and Alvin Roth's word "repugnant" (p. 92).

**Framing.** A dealer sells a scarce car at list price and then charges $200 above list: acceptable 29%, unfair 71%. A dealer who sold at $200 below list and now sells at list: acceptable 58%, unfair 42% (p. 92). Advice: set the highest intended price as the "regular" price, and call deviations "sales" or "discounts" (p. 92).

**Endowment effect and reference points.** Buyers and sellers feel entitled to the usual terms of trade and treat deterioration as a loss. This is strongest when something once free or included is now charged for (pp. 92–93). People still think firms may earn a reasonable profit. Raising prices because costs rose is "almost always" judged fair (p. 93).

*Intuition note:* The same final price can feel fair or unfair depending on what it is compared with. The status quo is the comparison.

**Sticky wages.** Theory of equilibrium suggests wages should fall in a recession so supply equals demand. In fact wages seem sticky (p. 93). A partial explanation: wage cuts make workers angry, so firms keep pay fixed and lay off surplus staff (p. 93). Survey pair (p. 93–94):
- Recession, no inflation, wages cut 7%: acceptable 38%, unfair 62%.
- Recession, 12% inflation, salaries raised 5%: acceptable 78%, unfair 22%.

Thaler says spending power is the same in the two cases, but a nominal cut is a loss and a raise below inflation is not (p. 94). He and some other economists felt central banks should have tolerated somewhat more inflation after the financial crisis. He says even 3% "might have" helped firms cut real wages and speed the jobs recovery (p. 94).

**Do firms obey the norms?** Thaler knows of no systematic study. He suspects most successful firms understand the norms and try to avoid seeming unfair (p. 94). The value of seeming fair should be especially high for firms that plan to sell to the same customers for a long time (p. 94). After Katrina, chains gave away supplies, while "temporary entrepreneurs" sold plywood at whatever the market paid. Both are profit-maximizing, but the chains are building a reputation with long-term payoffs (pp. 94–95).

**Firms that got it wrong.**
- *First Chicago (mid-1990s):* a $3 fee for using a teller when an ATM could do the job. The press and competitors attacked it ("Free Teller" sign, radio ad, Jay Leno). The fee was dropped only in December 2002, after a takeover (pp. 95–96).
- *Coca-Cola's Ivester:* he defended price-varying vending machines because Coke's utility "varies from moment to moment" (p. 96). The press said he had a "tin ear". Several actions contributed to his resignation, but this speech attracted the most attention (p. 96).
- *Whitney Houston (2012):* about twelve hours after her death, UK iTunes raised *The Ultimate Collection* from £4.99 to £7.99 (+60%) and another album from £7.99 to £9.99 (+25%). Fans were outraged. Digital copies cannot run out, which may have sharpened the anger (pp. 96–97). In the US, where prices did not spike, weekly album sales went from 1,700 to 101,000 and song downloads from 15,000 to 887,000 (p. 97). Thaler says a seller must trade short-term gain against possible long-term loss of goodwill, which is hard to measure (p. 97).

**Are firms always punished?** Airlines added fees one after another without evident irreparable harm. The key is whether competitors follow the first mover. If they do, customers may be peeved but have little choice if they must consume the product in question. A large first mover that violates fairness norms and is not followed "runs considerable risks" (p. 97).

**Uber.** Thaler's takeaway: demand spikes are a particularly bad time to appear greedy (p. 98). Surge pricing: prices rise with demand and customers are shown the multiple. Media reports mention multiples above ten (p. 98). **Uber's defense:** higher prices bring more drivers. **Thaler's reply:** he says it is hard to judge without Uber's internal data on driver supply, but the argument does not seem compelling "on the face of it". Drivers cannot easily join at short notice, and multiples as high as ten suggest supply responds slowly (p. 98). High multiples during a New York City blizzard attracted the attention of the New York State attorney general. New York has a law banning "unconscionable excessive prices" during an "abnormal disruption of the market" (pp. 98–99). The agreement: Uber looks up the highest multiples it charged on four different days in the sixty days before the disruption, and the fourth-highest then caps the surge during the emergency. Uber also offered to donate 20% of extra revenue to the Red Cross (p. 99). Thaler thinks Uber should have acted earlier on its own, and suggests a cap of about three times the usual fare. This is his "vague impression" of the range for hotel rooms and plane tickets (p. 99). Those services sell out at peak times, so owners are intentionally setting prices too low in peak season (p. 99).

**Under-pricing at peak.** A ski-lodge owner: "If you gouge them at Christmas they won't come back in March" (pp. 99–100). Next and Alinea in Chicago sell tickets with prices varying by day and time. The top price is only about 25% above the cheapest, so prime tables sell out (p. 100). Kokonas rejected advice to auction reservations. **Kokonas:** a business should not charge more than the good is worth, no matter how great the demand, even if the customer is willing to pay more. Kokonas believes such a customer will not come back and may share the disgruntled experience with other potential diners (p. 100). Thaler adds that restaurants wanting the long haul would be well advised to follow this (p. 100). A footnote reports that the NFL deliberately keeps Super Bowl ticket prices reasonable for its "ongoing relationship with fans" (p. 101).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An employer pays $1,000 a month.
- Case A: no inflation; the employer cuts pay to $950. Real pay = $950. Nominal change = −$50.
- Case B: 10% inflation; the employer raises pay to $1,045. Real pay = 1,045 / 1.10 = $950. Nominal change = +$45.

Real pay is the same ($950). Under Thaler's account, A shows a nominal drop, which workers treat as a loss, while B shows a nominal rise and is more acceptable (p. 94). Inflation therefore lets the employer reach the same real cut with less anger.

## Concepts
- [[mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion]] — fairness norms follow from the endowment effect: usual terms of trade become the reference point, and deterioration is a loss (p. 92)
- [[fairness norms]] (new) — shared views on which firm actions are acceptable; measured by survey (p. 90)
- [[reference point / status quo]] (new) — the usual price or wage against which changes are judged (p. 93)
- [[sticky wages]] (new) — wages that fall too little in recessions, partly because cuts anger workers (p. 93)
- [[nominal vs real wage cut]] (new) — a nominal cut is judged unfair; a below-inflation raise is judged acceptable (p. 94)
- [[theory-induced blindness]] (new) — Kahneman's notion; trained theory users forget how Humans react (p. 90)
- [[reputation / long-term goodwill]] (new) — Thaler says the value of seeming fair should be especially high for firms that sell to the same customers for a long time (p. 94)
- [[equilibrium]] — theory says falling labor demand should lower wages until the market clears (p. 93)

- [structural-analogy] Reputation for fair dealing ↔ repeated games: loss of future business disciplines short-term opportunism.

## Questions this raises
- How can one test whether firms actually obey fairness norms, given that Thaler knows of no systematic study (p. 94)?
- Is the reaction to a price rise different when supply is limited (shovels) than when it is not (downloads) (p. 97)?
- How much do fairness judgments change when all competitors adopt a fee (p. 97)?
- Would a surge cap such as Thaler's suggested three times work, given the lack of supply data (pp. 98–99)?
