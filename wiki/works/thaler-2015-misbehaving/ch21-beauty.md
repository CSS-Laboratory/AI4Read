# The Beauty Contest

## L0 — One line
Keynes's beauty contest, in which you must guess what average opinion will be, has the same structure as the guess-two-thirds-of-the-average game, and Thaler believes it remains an apt description of how financial markets work.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks why behavioral finance met doubt, and what Keynes's beauty contest says about markets. He splits the efficient market hypothesis (EMH) into two parts: price equals true value, and no one can reliably beat the market. He explains the 1970s confidence in the EMH. Keynes doubted that professionals act as "smart money". He likened investing to guessing average opinion. Thaler ran the same-structure guess-the-number game with Financial Times readers. Its only Nash equilibrium (no one wants to change if all pick it) is zero, yet the winning guess was 13. Good play depends on how deeply the others think. Thaler says money managers, too, are trying to buy what others will later value more.

**Prerequisites:** averages; the idea of a best response (Nash equilibrium); the basic idea of an efficient market.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The EMH has two somewhat related but conceptually distinct parts: the price-is-right part (price equals intrinsic value) and the no-free-lunch part (no reliable way to beat the market). (p. 160)
- Economists thought the price-is-right part could not be directly tested, because intrinsic value is not observable. Thaler says it is in many ways the more important part, since if prices are right there can never be bubbles. (p. 160)
- Thaler suggests confidence in the EMH was perhaps based as much on compelling logic as on data. New data (CRSP) and early tests, for example money managers doing no better than market averages, supported it. (pp. 161–162)
- Keynes thought emotions ("animal spirits") matter in investing, and that trivial short-run profit news had an excessive influence on markets. He thought professionals were more likely to ride irrational exuberance than to fight it. (p. 163)
- Keynes's newspaper beauty contest requires guessing what others will guess; investing is analogous (third, fourth and higher degrees). (pp. 163–164)
- In the two-thirds game the only Nash equilibrium is 0, but good play depends on the others' level of thinking. (pp. 164–165)
- In the 1997 FT contest the winning guess was 13. Guesses clustered at 0/1, 22 and 33, and the prank guesses were 99 and 100. (pp. 165–166)
- Even "value" and "growth" managers are really trying to buy what others will later value more. A manager's "long run" is only months or a few years. (p. 167)

### Doubt about behavioral finance, and the EMH (pp. 159–161)
- Financial markets were thought to be a place where foolish behavior would not move prices. The standard argument: a few smart people trade against the mistaken and "correct" prices (p. 159).
- Michael Jensen, a colleague of Thaler's, wrote that he believed no other proposition in economics has more solid empirical evidence supporting it than the EMH (p. 159).
- Eugene Fama coined the term (p. 159). The EMH has two somewhat related but conceptually distinct components (p. 160).
- **The price is right** (Thaler's label; he first heard the term from Cliff Smith): an asset sells for its true intrinsic value. If a firm's rational value is $100 million, its market cap is $100 million. This is thought to hold for single securities and for the overall market (p. 160).
- Economists felt safe because intrinsic value is unobservable, so they thought this component could not be tested directly. Fama tends not to emphasize it. Thaler says it is in many ways the more important part: if prices are right, there can never be bubbles (p. 160).
- A footnote notes that experimental economists have created bubbles in laboratory experiments, but financial economists put little credence in them, in part because the experiments give professionals no opportunity to intervene and correct the mispricing (p. 167).
- **No free lunch** (Thaler's label for the second component, the idea that there is no way to beat the market): it says that, because all publicly available information is reflected in current prices, it is impossible to reliably predict future prices and make a profit (p. 160).
- Argument: if a $30 stock will certainly soon be worth $35 and the information is public, many buy at once. The price jumps almost instantly to $35 and the opportunity vanishes (pp. 160–161).
- Jensen's PhD thesis showed that professional money managers do no better than simple market averages; Thaler adds that this remains true today (p. 161).

*Intuition note:* No free lunch says you cannot profit from news everyone already has. The price-is-right part is a different claim: price equals real value. Thaler calls it in many ways the more important part.

### How finance took off (pp. 161–163)
- Thaler offers as one possible explanation for the late arrival of the EMH that finance was slower to develop than other branches of economics. Before the 1970s, business-school finance was something of an academic wasteland, with courses often similar to accounting and little theory or rigorous empirical work (p. 161).
- Modern financial economics began with Markowitz, Miller and Sharpe. It took off because of cheap computing power and great data (p. 161).
- A $300,000 grant to the Chicago business school created a stock-price database from 1926 (CRSP). The first release was in 1964. Researchers included Miller, Fama, and students Jensen, Roll and Scholes (pp. 161–162).
- Fama's comprehensive review appeared in 1970. Eight years later Jensen's sentence declaring the EMH proven appeared, ironically in the preface to a special issue on anomalies (p. 162).
- Thaler's view: Jensen's and others' confidence was perhaps based as much on the compelling logic as on the data. Rational-expectations models were rising and Keynesian economics was declining; perhaps for this reason Keynes was no longer required reading. Thaler regrets this, since Keynes might have made the debate more even-handed (p. 162).

### Keynes as forerunner (pp. 162–164)
- Thaler finds the most insightful chapter of the *General Theory* is the one on financial markets. Thaler says Keynes's observations were based in part on his experience as an investor, including managing his college's portfolio (p. 162).
- Keynes thought "animal spirits" (emotions) matter for investment decisions (p. 163).
- He thought markets were more efficient early in the twentieth century, when managers owned most shares and knew what the firm was worth. As ownership dispersed, "real knowledge" in valuation "seriously declined" (p. 163).
- By the mid-1930s he judged that day-to-day profit fluctuations, "ephemeral and non-significant", had an "absurd" influence on the market (p. 163).
- Evidence he cited: ice-company shares are higher in summer, when sales are higher. In an efficient market prices reflect long-run value, so such a seasonal pattern is "verboten" by the EMH (p. 163). A footnote says a recent paper (Chang et al., 2014) finds support: seasonal firms have higher prices when their earnings are higher (p. 167).
- Keynes doubted professionals would act as the "smart money" the EMH defenders rely on. He thought they were more likely to ride a wave of exuberance than to fight it. One reason is that contrarianism is risky: he quotes worldly wisdom that failing conventionally is better for one's reputation than succeeding unconventionally (p. 163).
- **The analogy:** in a newspaper contest one picks the six prettiest faces out of a hundred photographs. The prize goes to the entrant closest to the average choice. So one must pick the faces one thinks others will pick, not one's own favorites. Keynes reached "the third degree", anticipating what average opinion expects average opinion to be, and says he believes some practice the fourth, fifth and higher degrees (pp. 163–164).

### The guessing game (pp. 164–166)
- Thaler believes Keynes's analogy remains an apt description of how financial markets work, and of the key role of behavioral factors, though it may be hard to grasp; he introduces the puzzle below to show its gist (p. 164).
- **Game:** guess a number from 0 to 100, as close as possible to two-thirds of the average guess. Example: guesses 20, 30, 40 give average 30, two-thirds is 20, so 20 wins (p. 164).
- **Levels of thinking** (Thaler's labels):
  - Zero-level: picks at random, so the average is 50.
  - First-level: expects 50 and guesses 33.
  - Second-level: expects 33 and guesses 22.
  - Third-level: expects 22 and guesses 15 (p. 164).
  - Thaler notes there is no convenient stopping point in this chain of thinking (p. 164).
- **Nash equilibrium:** a number such that, if everyone guessed it, no one would want to change. The only one is zero. If everyone guessed 3, you would want 2. If everyone guessed 2, you would want 1.33, and so on. Thaler says that if and only if all participants guessed zero would no one want to change (pp. 164–165).
- **The question worth asking:** who are the other players and how much math and game theory do they know? At a local bar, especially late in the evening, others are probably not thinking deeply, so a guess around 33 might make sense. Only at a conference of game theorists would one want a guess close to zero (p. 165).
- The game is formally identical to Keynes's contest: you guess what others think others think. Economists call it the "beauty contest". Rosemarie Nagel first studied it experimentally (p. 165).

*Intuition note:* The "right" answer, 0, is right only if everyone reasons all the way down. A good guess is a best reply to the actual crowd, not to a perfectly rational one.

### The Financial Times experiment, 1997 (pp. 165–167)
- The FT ran the game with British Airways tickets as the prize. The winning guess was 13 (p. 165).
- Figure 10 labels the spikes at 0 and 1 as too much economics training, 22 as second-level thinkers, 33 as first-level thinkers, and 99 and 100 as pranksters (p. 166).
- Many readers knew zero is the Nash equilibrium but wrongly thought it would win. Several guessed 1 to allow for a few dullards (p. 165).
- The guesses of 99 and 100 all came from one Oxford student residence, where one person filled in postcards for his housemates. Because each card had a different name, the entries were kept. They moved the winning guess from 12 to 13 (p. 166).
- Explanations that Thaler reports:
  - A poet guessed 0.
  - A Tory, having decided the world cannot be counted on to be rational, guessed 1: the answer should be 0, but Labour had won (p. 166).
  - A student guessed 7 because his father, who knows an average amount about numbers and markets, guessed 10. Thaler says the student underestimated his father: had he credited him with thinking one level beyond the average contestant, he might have won (p. 167).
  - A second poet guessed 10, reasoning that random guesses from 1 to 45 average about 23, so logic indicates 15, leaving 10 for himself (p. 167).
- Footnotes: the Nash equilibrium of zero does "a terrible job" as a descriptive theory and as advice, and a growing literature attempts to provide better descriptive models (p. 168). Some guessed 1 because of the wording "between 0 and 100", which Thaler later changed to "from" (p. 168).

### Back to money managers (p. 167)
- Thaler says the FT guessers, at various levels of sophistication, show that Keynes's analogy is still an apt description of what money managers try to do (p. 167).
- "Value" managers buy cheap stocks and "growth" managers buy fast-growing ones. But nobody seeks expensive or shrinking firms, so all are trying to buy stocks that other investors will later decide are worth more (p. 167).
- Buying a stock the market does not fully appreciate is fine as long as the market comes around sooner rather than later. Keynes: "In the long run, we are all dead." The typical long run for a portfolio manager is no more than a few years, maybe a few months (p. 167).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 10 players who guess 33 (six players), 22 (three players) and 0 (one player).
- Sum = 6·33 + 3·22 + 1·0 = 198 + 66 + 0 = 264.
- Average = 264/10 = 26.4. Target = (2/3)·26.4 = 17.6.
- Distances: |33 − 17.6| = 15.4, |22 − 17.6| = 4.4, |0 − 17.6| = 17.6. So 22 wins, the guess one step below the crowd's most common guess.
- If everyone iterated fully from 50, the guesses would be 50 → 33.3 → 22.2 → 14.8 → 9.9 → … → 0. Each step multiplies by 2/3, so the Nash number is 0. But here the winning guess was 22, not 0.

## Concepts
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — Thaler's two-part version: "price is right" plus "no free lunch" (pp. 159–160)
- [[price is right]] (new) — market price equals intrinsic value, so no bubbles are possible (p. 160)
- [[no free lunch]] (new) — public information is in prices, so reliable profitable prediction is impossible (p. 160)
- [[beauty contest / higher-order beliefs]] — forecasting what others expect others to expect; the number game has the same structure (pp. 164–165)
- [[Nash equilibrium]] — a number such that nobody wants to deviate if all choose it. Here it is only 0 (pp. 164–165)
- [[animal spirits]] — Keynes's emotions that influence investment decisions (p. 163)
- [[level-k thinking]] (new) — Thaler's zero-, first-, second-, third-level thinkers, each best-replying to the level below (p. 164)
- [[limits to arbitrage]] — the term is not used here. Keynes says contrarianism is risky and quotes worldly wisdom about reputation, one reason professionals may ride a wave rather than fight it (p. 163)
