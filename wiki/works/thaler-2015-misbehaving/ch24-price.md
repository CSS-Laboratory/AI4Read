# The Price Is Not Right

## L0 — One line
Thaler argues that Shiller's findings, together with the 1987 crash, suggest stock prices are too variable to be "right", yet Shiller's valuation-ratio signals are too imprecise to be a sure way to beat the market.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The efficient market hypothesis (EMH) says you can't beat the market and that prices are right. The chapter mainly looks at the second claim, then turns to the first. Shiller (1981) compared stock prices with the present value of later dividends (their worth today). He found prices moved far more than that benchmark. Thaler believes the October 1987 crash, a big fall on a day without important news, effectively settled the debate. Shiller's long-term price/earnings ratio and house-price data also have some predictive value, but only imprecise value. It is easier to see a possible bubble than to say when it will pop. Timing the market is rarely successful.

**Prerequisites:** present value (discounting); what a forecast is; price/earnings ratio; mean reversion.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A stock price should equal the market's expectation of the present value of future dividends, so it is a forecast (p. 183).
- A rational forecast cannot vary more than the thing forecast. Shiller found prices far more variable than the ex post (after the fact) present value of dividends, i.e. the dividends actually paid later, discounted back (pp. 183–185).
- Thaler believes the 1987 crash effectively settled the debate: prices fell over 20% on a day with no important news (pp. 185–186).
- Shiller's 1984 paper proposed that social dynamics, like fashion, may move stock prices. Later, "animal spirits" captured this (pp. 186–187).
- The price/earnings ratio (price over ten-year average earnings) has some predictive power, with a "Yes, but . . ." answer (pp. 187–188).
- The predictive power is imprecise. Shiller's 1996 warning came four years before the peak, so an investor who bet heavily on a fall in 1996 would have gone broke first (pp. 188–189).
- House prices and the price-to-rent ratio diverged from their benchmarks. A bubble was still not certain at the time (p. 189).
- Imprecise signals are not useless: the larger the divergence, the more seriously to take them (p. 190).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Setup: the two parts of the EMH.** The EMH has two parts: you can't beat the market (no free lunch), and prices are "right". Thaler and De Bondt mainly questioned the first. Shiller's 1981 paper addressed the second (p. 183).

**What should set a stock's price.** Take a foundation that buys a share and never sells it. Its only return is dividends. The share's value should then equal the present value of all future dividends, meaning the flow's worth after allowing that money later is worth less than money now (p. 183). If the stock is sold, the sale price, discounted, is added. For long holding periods this has a negligible effect (p. 191, footnote). Future dividends are unknown, so the price is the market's expectation of that present value (p. 183).

**Rule of rational forecasts.** Predictions cannot vary more than the thing being forecast (p. 183). Thaler's example is Singapore's daily high temperature: usually about 90°F, about 95°F on a very hot day, about 85°F on a "cold" day. A forecaster who says 50°F one day and 110°F the next breaks the rule (pp. 183–184).

*Intuition note:* A rational forecast should not swing more than the thing it forecasts. Thaler's picture is the drunken Singapore weather forecaster, wilder than the weather.

**Shiller's test.** Shiller collected stock prices and dividends back to 1871. For each year he computed an "ex post rational" forecast. He took the dividends actually paid afterwards and discounted them back to that year (p. 184). After removing the long-run upward trend, the present value of dividends was highly stable, "like the temperature in Singapore". Stock prices were highly variable (p. 184; figure 12, p. 185). His title asks whether prices move too much to be justified by later dividend changes. Thaler says that by figure 12 the answer is yes (p. 185).

**Reaction.** The results caused a "firestorm". Papers attacked them, one called the "Shiller Killer", and Kleidon's paper was presented at the Chicago conference of chapter 17. Economists still quibble over the right way to run the test (p. 185).

**October 1987.** Thaler believes the debate was effectively settled on Monday, October 19, 1987 (p. 185). Prices fell worldwide, starting in Hong Kong. In New York they fell over 20%, after a drop of more than 5% on the previous Friday (pp. 185–186). There was no important news that day. By comparison, the U.S. market fell 4.4% the day after Pearl Harbor (p. 186). The S&P 500 then rose 5.3% on Tuesday, rose 9.1% on Wednesday and fell 8.3% on Monday the 26th (p. 186). Thaler's argument is that in a rational world prices change only with news, and the only news was that prices were moving wildly. If prices are too variable, they are "in some sense" wrong. Thaler finds it hard to argue that the prices at the close on Thursday October 15 and the following Monday (more than 25% lower) can both be rational measures of intrinsic value, given the absence of news (p. 186).

*Intuition note:* If no news could change the value of future dividends between Thursday's close and Monday's close, a fall of over 25% makes it hard to say both prices were rational. This is Thaler's own judgement ("I believe"); he notes that economists still quibble over how to run Shiller's test (pp. 185–186).

**Toward a behavioral reading.** Shiller's 1981 paper was not psychological. Thaler read it behaviorally and urged Shiller, in a walk at Cornell in spring 1982, to think this way (p. 186). Two years later Shiller wrote "Stock Prices and Social Dynamics". It suggested that social phenomena may influence stock prices as much as fashion trends, like hemlines (pp. 186–187). Later, with Akerlof, he used Keynes's "animal spirits" for whimsical shifts in attitudes (pp. 186–187).

**The "no free lunch" side.** Value stocks, with low price/earnings (P/E) ratios or extreme past losses, predictably beat the market (p. 187). Does the same hold for the whole market? Thaler's answer is "Yes, but . . ." (p. 187). Shiller's method divides the index price (e.g. S&P 500) by earnings averaged over ten years. This smooths business-cycle swings (p. 187; figure 13, p. 188). With hindsight, Thaler sees in the chart a reversion to the mean: stocks looked cheap in the 1970s and eventually recovered. They looked expensive in the late 1990s and later crashed. So there appears to be some predictive power, but it is "not very precise" (p. 188).

**The "but".** In 1996 Shiller and Campbell warned the Federal Reserve Board that prices looked dangerously high. This led Greenspan to ask in a speech how one could know if investors had become "irrationally exuberant" (p. 188). Shiller's book of that name appeared in 2000, as the market began to slide (p. 188). The market peaked four years after his warning, so he "was wrong for a long time before he was right" (pp. 188–189). A heavy bet on a fall in 1996 would have gone broke first (p. 189). Thaler adds that he also thought tech stocks overpriced and predicted in 1999 a "Great Internet Stock Bubble". He says he would have written the same two years earlier (p. 191, footnote).

**Housing.** Shiller and Case built the Case–Shiller index from repeat sales of the same home. This controls for home quality and location. Earlier indicators were unreliable because the mix of homes sold each month could vary greatly and skew the average (p. 189). Real home prices (figure 14) grew modestly until the mid-1990s, then shot up (p. 189). The price-to-rent ratio had hovered around 20:1 for a long time, then diverged sharply (p. 189). Shiller warned of a bubble and was eventually right. At the time, one could not be sure whether much higher ratios were "the new normal" (p. 189).

**Conclusion.** Imprecision does not make the signals useless. A strong divergence from history in either direction has some predictive value. The further the divergence, the more seriously to take it (p. 190). Investors should be wary of overheated markets but should not expect to get rich by timing the market. Detecting a possible bubble is much easier than saying when it will pop. Market timers are rarely successful (p. 190).

*Intuition note:* A signal can carry some information and still be too imprecise to be a sure way to make money. Thaler says Shiller "was wrong for a long time before he was right", and a heavy bettor would have gone broke first (p. 189).

**Coda.** In 1991 Thaler and Shiller began a semiannual NBER behavioral finance workshop (p. 191).

## Toy example
(Ours, not from the text.) A stock will pay a dividend of 10 at the end of each of two years, and nothing after. Use a discount rate of 0%, so the present value is the sum of the dividends.
- Case A: dividends are 10 and 10, so the present value is 20.
- Case B: dividends are 10 and 30, so the present value is 40.

Before knowing which, the market expects 50% for each case: $0.5\times20+0.5\times40=30$. A rational price of 30 can later turn out to be 20 or 40, but never below 20 or above 40. A price that swung between 5 and 70 in the same years would vary more than the present value of dividends it forecasts (range 20–40). That is Shiller's comparison in miniature.

## Concepts
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — here two components: no free lunch, and prices are "right" (p. 183)
- [[excess volatility]] (new) — prices move more than the present value of later dividends can justify (pp. 184–185)
- [[present value of dividends]] (new) — future dividends discounted for the lower worth of later money; this is the rational stock value (p. 183)
- [[animal spirits]] — Keynes's term, used by Shiller and Akerlof for whimsical shifts in consumer and investor attitudes (pp. 186–187)
- [[mean reversion of valuation ratios]] (new) — Thaler says that, with hindsight, a market diverging from its historical P/E trend appears eventually to revert to the mean (p. 188)
- [[limits to arbitrage]] — our link: Thaler's point that an investor betting heavily on a fall in 1996 would have gone broke first resembles this idea, though he does not use the term (p. 189)
