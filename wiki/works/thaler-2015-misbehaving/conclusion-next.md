# Conclusion: What Is Next?

## L0 — One line
Thaler hopes economics becomes "evidence-based", building theory from observed behaviour of real people (Humans), and urges everyone to observe, collect data and speak up.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks where behavioral economics should go next. He says it has had most impact in finance, because finance has sharp theories and rich data. He wants the same in macroeconomics, where data are scarce and theories hard to falsify. He also points to development economics and education, where field experiments (randomized trials) test ideas. He then draws three lessons for economists and non-economists: observe, collect data, speak up. He is optimistic. Some of the best recent behavioral papers come from economists who do not call themselves "behavioral". He expects the label to vanish once all economics is "as behavioral as it needs to be".

**Prerequisites:** efficient market hypothesis; CAPM and beta; randomized control trial (random assignment to treatment or control); prospect theory; the book's terms "Econs" (fully rational agents), "Humans" (real people, as opposed to Econs), and "SIFs" (supposedly irrelevant factors); CAPM (capital asset pricing model, in which beta was once thought the only factor that matters, p. 299); the Palm/3Com story (told earlier in the book, not on these pages).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Behavioral economics is going mainstream; Thaler offers a "wish list", not forecasts (p. 298).
- Finance is where it has had most impact, thanks to tightly specified theories and abundant data. Thaler knows of no other field that would allow as clear a refutation of theory as the Palm/3Com story (p. 299).
- Debate in finance is kept (mostly) productive by its focus on data; CAPM has been rejected as an adequate description of stock prices (p. 299).
- Macroeconomics is the field Thaler most wants to change. It lacks easily falsifiable predictions and has little data, yet behavioral details (tax-cut design, failure risk for entrepreneurs) can still be studied (pp. 300–303).
- Prospect theory is the seminal evidence-based theory; field experiments in development and education extend the economist's toolkit beyond money incentives (pp. 303–305).
- Three lessons: observe, collect data, speak up. Good leaders must create environments in which employees feel evidence-based decisions will always be rewarded, whatever the outcome (pp. 305–307).
- Thaler expects behavioral economics to disappear as a separate field once all economists include relevant variables (p. 308).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The source has no numbered sections; headings follow its breaks.)

**Opening (pp. 298–299).** Behavioral economics is no longer fringe. Thaler will be American Economic Association president in 2015, with Robert Shiller his successor (p. 298). He offers a wish list, because, in his words, the only sensible prediction is that what happens will surely surprise us. Most wishes address economists; some address "consumers" of research such as managers and bureaucrats (p. 298).

**Taking stock: finance (pp. 299–300).**
- Behavioral approaches had their greatest impact in finance. This was unthinkable in 1980, since markets were thought the most efficient (p. 299).
- Two reasons: tightly specified theories, such as the law of one price, and data on thousands of stocks back to 1926 (p. 299). A footnote adds that finance offers the best chances to make money if markets misbehave, so many resources went into it (p. 299).
- Not all financial economists have dropped the efficient market hypothesis, but behavioral approaches are taken seriously, and on many issues the rational-versus-behavioral debate has dominated the literature for over two decades (p. 299).
- **Fama, as Thaler quotes him:** "we agree about the facts, we just disagree about the interpretation". **Thaler's statement of the facts:** CAPM has clearly been rejected as an adequate description of price movements; beta, once thought the only factor that mattered, does not appear to explain very much; many once-"irrelevant" factors now seem to matter, though why is controversial (p. 299).
- Thaler says the field appears to be converging on what he calls "evidence-based economics". Most economic theory is deduced from rational-choice axioms, and a theory of Econs cannot be empirically based because Econs do not exist (p. 299).
- Thaler says that in a world where one part of a company can sell for more than the entire company, handwaving will not suffice. Finance has taken the "limits of arbitrage" seriously. Aspiring "smart money" may profit more from riding a bubble than from betting on a return to sanity (p. 300). Following Kuhn, discovery starts with anomalies (p. 300).

*Intuition note:* Finance was a good test bed because the theory made sharp claims and the data could contradict them. Where theories make few falsifiable predictions and data are scarce, as in macroeconomics, "smoking gun" evidence is hard to find (p. 300).

**Macroeconomics (pp. 300–303).**
- It is the field Thaler most wants to see change, and it has had the least behavioral impact. Akerlof and Shiller tried for several years to run a behavioral macro meeting at the NBER but could not fill the program, and abandoned it (p. 300).
- One reason *may* be that macro theories make few falsifiable predictions and data are scarce, so "smoking gun" evidence is missing (p. 300).
- Economists disagree even on basic crisis advice for 2007–08. The left favours infrastructure spending; the right fears waste, debt and inflation and favours tax cuts. Each side blames the other's austerity level for the slow recovery. Randomized recession policies are unlikely, so the debate may never be settled (p. 301). (A footnote notes "natural" experiments such as the fall of the Berlin Wall (p. 301).)
- Still, lack of a core rational model does not mean behavioral ideas cannot help. "We should not need smoking guns" to start collecting evidence (p. 301).
- *Tax cut design.* For a Keynesian stimulus, whether the cut is a lump sum or spread over the year is a SIF. Thaler advises spreading it, since lump sums are more likely saved or used to pay debt (p. 301). A footnote cites Epley et al. (2006): people report a greater propensity to spend a tax cut labelled a "bonus" than a "rebate" (p. 301).
- *Supply-side repatriation holiday.* We need evidence on whether firms would invest, pay out or hoard the returned cash (pp. 301–302).
- *Encouraging start-ups.* Right and left favour tax or subsidy measures. Economists rarely discuss cushioning the downside when a business fails, which happens at least half the time. Since losses loom larger than gains, this might matter (p. 302).
- A footnote warns that many entrepreneurs are overconfident, and suggests training on base rates (p. 302).
- Jon Stewart, in an interview with Austan Goolsbee, proposed softening the damage of risk, for example when health insurance is tied to a job. Thaler says economists should not need a comedian for this. 97% of U.S. small business owners earn under $250,000 (pp. 302–303).

**Evidence-based theory and field experiments (pp. 303–305).**
- Development economics is probably, along with finance, where behavioral economists have the greatest impact, in part because the field has been revitalized by randomized trials in poor countries (p. 303).
- Prospect theory is the seminal evidence-based theory. Kahneman and Tversky collected data on what people do and built a parsimonious theory. Expected utility theory is a normative theory derived from axioms. Prospect theory has been tested on game show contestants, golf professionals and stock investors. Barberis, Laibson and Rabin also start from facts (p. 303).
- Experiments widen a toolkit that often had a single tool, monetary incentives. Treating all money as the same, and as the main motivator, is not realistic (p. 303).
- *Education (p. 304).* Evidence for financial incentives to parents, teachers or kids is weak.
  - Fryer: one intriguing finding suggests that rewarding inputs (homework) rather than outputs (grades) is effective.
  - Fryer, List, Levitt and Sadoff: teachers given an up-front bonus that must be returned if targets are missed improve student performance significantly more than teachers offered an end-of-year bonus for the same goals. Caveat: clawbacks are unpopular and may seem "unfair" (p. 304).
  - U.K. text-reminder trial: half the parents got texts 5, 3 and 1 days before a math test ("pre-informing"). Performance rose by about one extra month of schooling, and two months for the bottom quartile. Parents and students wanted to continue. Thaler says this belies the unsupported claim that nudges must be secret (p. 304).
- Field experiments are "perhaps the most powerful tool" for evidence-based economics (p. 305).

*Intuition note:* A theory built from observed behaviour can be tested against data. Experiments widen the economist's toolkit beyond monetary incentives and let economists test details such as the framing of a bonus or the use of reminders.

**Wish list for non-economists: three lessons (pp. 305–307).** Schools are old yet we do not know how to teach well, and Thaler asks, rhetorically, whether there is any reason to think we know the best way to run newer institutions such as corporations (p. 305).
- **Observe.** Behavioral economics began with simple observations: people eat too many nuts, keep mental accounts, make many mistakes (p. 305).
- **Collect data.** Anecdotes only illustrate. Overconfidence arises because people do not record their past wrong predictions, and confirmation bias leads them to seek only confirming evidence. The protection is data that can prove you wrong. Most organizations have an urgent need to learn how to learn: at the very least they should try new things and keep track of what happens, and even better would be to run experiments; Thaler says behavioral scientists are cheaper than lawyers or consultants (pp. 305–306).
- **Speak up.** In Gawande's account of the 1977 KLM crash (over 500 deaths), the second officer was too timid to question the captain; his warning was dismissed and he stayed quiet. On Everest, Rob Hall and Scott Fisher broke their own 1 p.m. turnaround rule and died, and no subordinate intervened (p. 306).
- Thaler's own speaking up was a risky path that suited his low opportunity costs (p. 307). Leaders must make sure employees feel that evidence-based decisions will always be rewarded, whatever the outcome. The cost to bosses is only bruised egos (p. 307).

**Closing optimism (pp. 307–308).**
- Some of the best recent behavioral papers come from economists who do not call themselves behavioral. Examples: Hastings and Shapiro on mental accounting of gasoline, and Chetty and team on Danish pension saving (p. 307).
- **Thaler's report of Chetty et al.:** tax-break incentives have virtually no effect on saving; 99% of the work is done by choice architecture such as the default saving rate, i.e. SIFs (p. 307).
- When all economists are open-minded, behavioral economics will disappear, and the Econ-only holdouts will wave "a white flag, rather than an invisible hand" (p. 308).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text; numbers invented.)* Test a text-reminder nudge. Randomly assign 4 students to texts and 4 to none.
- Treatment scores: 60, 70, 65, 75. Sum = 270, mean = 270/4 = 67.5.
- Control scores: 55, 65, 60, 70. Sum = 250, mean = 250/4 = 62.5.
- Estimated effect = 67.5 − 62.5 = 5 points.
Random assignment makes the groups comparable on average, so the gap is evidence for the nudge. Eight students is far too few to be convincing. Thaler's lesson is to collect "lots" of data, and data that could prove you wrong.

## Concepts
- [[evidence-based-economics]] (new) — theory built from observed behaviour and tested on data, not deduced from rationality axioms (p. 299)
- [[limits to arbitrage]] — prices can diverge from intrinsic value and "smart money" may not correct them (p. 300)
- [[efficient market hypothesis (weak/semi-strong/strong)]] — rational camp's view; still held by some financial economists (p. 299)
- [[CAPM & beta]] — rejected as an adequate description of stock price movements; beta does not appear to explain very much (p. 299)
- [[mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion]] — money is not treated as the same everywhere; losses loom larger than gains (pp. 302, 305)
- [[sif-supposedly-irrelevant-factor]] (new) — a detail the rational model ignores but that changes behaviour (p. 301)
- [[randomized-control-trial]] (new) — random assignment to treatment and control to learn what works (pp. 303–304)
- [[prospect-theory]] (new) — theory built from observed choices; seminal evidence-based theory (p. 303)
- [[nudge-choice-architecture]] (new) — design of defaults and reminders that changes behaviour; need not be secret (pp. 304, 307)
- [[confirmation-bias-overconfidence]] (new) — seeking only confirming evidence; defended against by recording data (pp. 305–306)
