# Misbehaving in the Real World (Interlude)

## L0 — One line
Behavioral ideas such as mental accounting helped a small ski resort price better, while GM's huge promotion sold many cars but its management declined to study why it worked.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks whether behavioral economics helps in practice. At Greek Peak, a struggling ski resort, he and the marketing director used mental accounting to raise revenue without losing customers: free extras, advance-sale ten-packs, and next-visit coupons. At General Motors, a 2.9% loan promotion sold many cars although, according to a Wall Street Journal reporter, it was worth less than the rebate. Thaler suggests buyers judged the loan by its low rate. GM then declined to study why it worked. His contrast: tiny Greek Peak reasoned more analytically than huge GM, and reluctance to test and learn is common.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of mental accounting (separate "accounts" for money), sunk costs (money already spent), reference point (a price used for comparison), transaction utility (the pleasure of a good deal), rebate (cash back after buying), and simple percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Greek Peak needed higher prices but faced Vermont resorts' prices as a reference point. The fix was gradual increases plus a better, cheap-to-provide experience (pp. 80–81).
- Removing a $1 fee on the racecourse and adding free clinics raised perceived value at trivial cost (pp. 80–81).
- Thaler explains the popularity of the ten-pack, sold at 40% off in advance, by transaction utility, decoupling the purchase from each ski day, and commitment (pp. 81–83).
- About 60% of ten-pack tickets were redeemed at a 60% price, so the resort effectively got full price, earlier (p. 82).
- "Secret sales" give away money from customers ready to pay full price. A coupon for the next visit feels generous and may bring customers back (p. 83).
- Rebates seemed more popular than equal price cuts, as mental accounting would lead one to expect. GM's 2.9% loan sold well though a journalist found it worth less than the rebate (pp. 84–86).
- GM set the rate in under an hour, nobody Thaler met said they were responsible for evaluating it, and management rejected Thaler's proposal to study it. Thaler calls this reluctance to experiment common (pp. 86–87).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 79)
Thaler says that if behavioral economics is to offer a more realistic description of behavior, it should help in practical settings. Early in his career he found mental accounting had business uses, especially in pricing (p. 79).

### Greek Peak (pp. 79–84)
**Problem.** The family-owned resort near Ithaca was in financial trouble. Low-snow winters and a tough economy forced heavy borrowing at high interest rates, so it had to raise revenue and cut debt or go bankrupt. Thaler was paid in barter: lift tickets and equipment for his kids (p. 79).

**Price dilemma.** A profitable price would be nearly the same as the prices of Vermont and New Hampshire resorts. Costs per skier were not much different, but Greek Peak had only five lifts and less terrain (p. 80). Thaler says the famous resorts' prices were a salient *reference point*, and customers would expect to pay less for an inferior product. Greek Peak's advantage was proximity: Vermont is a five-hour drive, and busloads came from cities to the south (p. 80).

**Plan.** Raise prices gradually over several years, avoiding a backlash from a sudden jump. Improve the experience, to partly justify the higher prices and make the purchase seem less of a rip-off (p. 80).
- The slalom racecourse charged $1. Paying with gloves on and a failing vending-style slot was a "damn nuisance." It earned perhaps a few thousand dollars a year, so Thaler suggested making it free (pp. 80–81).
- Idle instructors ran a free video clinic ("Free lessons!") (p. 81).

**Local market.** Students already bought a discounted six-pack of weekday tickets by October 15 (p. 81). For locals Greek Peak designed the **ten-pack**: five weekend and five weekday tickets, 40% off retail if bought by October 15. The aim was a deal that would not be available to the out-of-town skiers. For them the ticket is a small part of trip costs, so a few dollars more would hardly change whether they came (p. 81).

Thaler gives these reasons for its popularity:
1. 40% off gives a lot of transaction utility (p. 81).
2. Advance purchase decouples buying from the decision to ski. As with wine, the purchase is an "investment," so a spontaneous ski day feels free. From the resort's view this is better than free, because the customer's money is a sunk cost (pp. 81–82). Skiers want to use tickets to avoid waste and may bring a friend who pays full price (tickets were non-transferable) (p. 82).
3. Skiing is something people resolve to do more of. The ten-pack works like a gym membership as a commitment, which the "planners" like (p. 82).

After a few years, advance packages and season passes were a substantial share of revenue. The early cash removed the need to borrow until December and hedged against a warm, snowless winter (p. 82).

**The redemption result.** Michael found that 60% of ten-pack tickets were redeemed, and the packs sold at 60% of retail. "In essence" the resort sold tickets at full price and got the money months earlier (p. 82). Customers mostly repurchased, and those who used few tickets blamed themselves (p. 82). Some customers who ended with nearly all tickets unused asked about carrying them over and were politely told no. Al's offer: buy a ten-pack again and the old tickets stay valid. Thaler doubts many bought for that reason, but says customers did seem to appreciate the resort's effort to be "fair" (p. 83).

> *Intuition note.* At 60% redemption, the resort's real discount was close to zero. Customers paid early, and many tickets went unused. The "40% off" label pleased buyers while the resort's income per ticket actually used was about the full price.

**Early-season pricing.** Al used to set a price, often half off, by looking at the mountain. Thaler calls this a "secret sale": customers arrive ready to pay retail, so the discount gives away revenue. Thaler says a price cut makes sense only if it increases current sales or, perhaps, future sales by building customer loyalty (p. 83). The new rule: full price today plus a coupon for up to 50% off the next visit, depending on how many lifts were running. Customers expected full price, so the coupon seemed generous and might bring them back to buy lunch and a beer (p. 83). In a story Michael told, a man with a new ten-pack heard a seller explain the coupon to the person ahead of him. He put the ten-pack away and paid full price (pp. 83–84).

**Outcome.** A solid revenue base before the season got the resort out of debt and made it less dependent on snowfall. Greek Peak is still in business (p. 84).

**Footnotes.** (*) Retail price gave positive acquisition utility, especially to locals who skied a day and were home for dinner. The problem was perceived transaction utility. (†) Thaler's daughter Maggie answered "Sunk costs!" when he pointed to the money paid for her after-school ski programme. (‡) Michael has since died (p. 87).

> *Intuition note.* Customers judge a price against a reference (Vermont) and against the feeling of a good deal, not only against the value of the product. The resort worked on those feelings (free extras, a "deal") as well as on the price.

### My day at GM (pp. 84–87)
**Rebates.** Car makers had unsold old-model inventory each August. Chrysler introduced rebates in 1975, and Ford and GM followed (p. 84). Thaler says a rebate seemed more popular than an equal price cut, "as one might expect based on mental accounting." Example: a $14,800 list price cut to $14,500 is no big deal, but calling the $300 a rebate makes the consumer think of it separately and gives it more weight (p. 84). In New York this had a cost: tax was paid on the full $14,800, and the $300 check did not refund the 8% sales tax on it (p. 84). Rebates were losing their luster (p. 84).

**The loan.** GM offered a 2.9% car loan when market rates were 10% or more. Buyers could choose the rebate or the loan. Sales jumped, with reports of buyers sprawled on hoods (p. 85). A Wall Street Journal reporter had calculated that the loan was worth less than the rebate: using the rebate as a bigger down payment would save more money (p. 85). Thaler told Jay Russo, a Cornell colleague consulting for GM, that he might have a "simple psychological explanation." The rebate was a small share of the price, but the loan rate was under a third of the usual one. That sounds like a much better deal, and few people besides accountants and reporters would do the math, in an era before spreadsheets and home computers (p. 85).

**The visit.** After a written note, a GM marketing manager visited Ithaca for about an hour. Jay said he was there to "count your heads" (p. 85). Thaler was then invited to Detroit (p. 86). In the meetings, no one Thaler asked said they were responsible for evaluating a promotion that cut prices by hundreds of millions of dollars. The 2.9% rate emerged in under an hour: one manager suggested 4.9%, another 3.9%, and someone 2.9%. Roger Smith, the CEO, liked the sound of it (p. 86). Thaler proposed two things: find out why it worked, and plan for Ford and Chrysler copying it. Management rejected this and decided instead to plan production better to avoid excess inventory. Thaler reports that this plan would remove the need to evaluate the promotion, since there would be no more end-of-model-year sales (pp. 86–87).

**Moral.** Thaler was "astounded" that GM did not study its own promotion, and says Michael Cobb at Greek Peak thought more analytically. He calls the reluctance to experiment, test, evaluate and learn "all too common" in business and government. GM's inventory plan failed the next summer and, as far as he knows, every summer since: "Overconfidence is a powerful force" (p. 87).

> *Intuition note.* A big, expensive decision can be made by feel and then never checked. A small firm that tracks its results (like Greek Peak's redemption rate) can learn more than a large one that does not.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A resort's retail ticket is $50. A ten-pack at 60% of retail costs $0.60 × $50 × 10 = $300.
- If a buyer uses all 10 tickets, the resort earns $300 ÷ 10 = $30 per ticket used (60% of retail).
- If the buyer uses 6, the resort earns $300 ÷ 6 = $50 per ticket used (100% of retail), received months early.
- If the buyer uses 8, the resort earns $300 ÷ 8 = $37.50 (75%).

In general, revenue per ticket used = 0.6 ÷ (redemption share) of retail. This equals 1.0 exactly when 60% are redeemed, as at Greek Peak (p. 82). It ignores extra spending on lunch or friends' tickets.

## Concepts
- [[mental-accounting]] — here: people keep separate mental accounts, so framing the same money differently (rebate vs price cut; ten-pack "investment") changes behavior (pp. 80–82, 84).
- [[reference-point]] (new) — a salient price (Vermont lift tickets) against which a price is judged (p. 80).
- [[transaction-utility]] (new) — as used here, the sense of getting a good deal ("lots" with 40% off; low when the price seems high against bigger resorts). The footnote contrasts it with acquisition utility, the positive value of a ski day itself (pp. 81, 87).
- [[sunk-cost]] (new) — money already spent. It should not drive choices, but it spurs ten-pack holders to use tickets (pp. 82, 87).
- [[commitment-planner-doer]] (new) — people buy in advance to commit to a resolution, like a gym membership; the "planners" like it (p. 82).
- [[secret-sale]] (new) — a discount given to customers who were ready to pay full price (p. 83).
- [[overconfidence]] (new) — GM's belief that it could plan away excess inventory, despite repeated failure (p. 87).

## Canonical instances
- [[greek-peak-ten-pack]] — advance discount package whose low redemption (60%) made it near-full price (pp. 81–83).
- [[greek-peak-coupon-pricing]] — full price now plus a next-visit coupon replaces the secret sale (p. 83).
- [[gm-2.9-percent-loan]] — low-rate financing that sold many cars though worth less than the rebate, per a WSJ reporter's calculation (pp. 85–86).
- [[car-rebate-sales-tax]] — in New York, the rebate framing meant buyers paid sales tax on the full price (p. 84).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Ten-pack advance purchase ↔ finance: a commitment device that locks in future behavior and cash flow (a prepaid contract).
- [metaphor] GM's unexamined promotion ↔ agent-based modeling/inductive reasoning: firms that do not test hypotheses against experience cannot learn.
- [structural-analogy] Redemption below 100% ↔ insurance or gym memberships: revenue depends on customers using less than they bought.

## Questions this raises
- How much of the ten-pack's success came from transaction utility versus commitment? Could a field test separate them?
- Would the 60% redemption rate survive if customers were told about it?
- Why do firms avoid evaluating successful promotions, and what would an experiment at GM have looked like?
- Does the "fair" unused-ticket offer make customers loyal, or is it only a sign of goodwill?
