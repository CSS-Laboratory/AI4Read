# Save More Tomorrow

## L0 — One line
Retirement saving can be raised a lot by plan design that fits human biases (defaults, commitment now to save out of future raises), even though standard theory calls such design features irrelevant.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how to raise retirement saving when standard theory offers only one lever, the after-tax return, whose effect on saving has no clear sign. Thaler answers with automatic enrollment and Save More Tomorrow: workers commit now to raise their saving rate at future pay raises, so inertia works for the saver and loss aversion and present bias are blunted. In one firm, joiners' saving rate rose from 3.5% to 13.6% in about three and a half years. Danish data suggest the new saving is mostly genuinely new, and that 99% of the effect comes from automatic features and 1% from tax breaks. The chapter ends with the coining of "libertarian paternalism".

**Prerequisites:** the "Econ" vs "Human" contrast; default (what happens if you do nothing); IRA (a tax-sheltered personal retirement account); tax withholding (tax taken from paychecks in advance); loss aversion; present bias; the 401(k) as an employer retirement plan with optional employer match.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Standard theory implicitly predicts that no policy variable other than the after-tax return can matter, and it cannot say whether a higher return raises or lowers saving contributions (pp. 263–264; footnote p. 276).
- Thaler's early proposals: use tax refunds for IRA contributions, raise withholding so refunds are bigger, and make 401(k) enrollment the default (pp. 265–266).
- Economics predicts the default has no effect because it is a "supposedly irrelevant factor" (SIF). Madrian and Shea found that automatic enrollment raised first-year joining from 49% to 86% (pp. 266, 269).
- Employees seemed to treat defaults as suggestions. The 3% rate and money market fund (a low-risk, low-return fund) were picked to minimize the chance of the company being sued, and most employees stuck with them. After the change, fewer employees chose 6% and more chose 3% (pp. 269–270).
- Save More Tomorrow targets three obstacles: inertia, loss aversion and present bias. At Tarbox's firm, 78% of those offered it joined, and their saving rate rose from 3.5% to 13.6% (pp. 267–268, 271).
- Danish data: the saving is mostly new, and 99% of the effect comes from automatic features, 1% from tax breaks (p. 274).
- Mulligan asked whether this is paternalism. Thaler said the plan is voluntary and, struggling for words, blurted out "libertarian paternalism" (p. 275).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Why standard theory is of little help (pp. 263–264)
- Standard theory assumes people already save the right amount. Its theories (Friedman, Modigliani) implicitly predict that only the after-tax return can matter as a policy variable (p. 263).
- The effect of a higher return is ambiguous. Rewards rise, but a given nest egg needs less saving (pp. 263–264).
- Footnote: theory does predict the total nest egg rises. It does not say whether contributions rise or fall. Analogy: with a car twice as fuel-efficient, an Econ drives more miles but probably does not buy more fuel (p. 276).
- Empirical work was hard. Bernheim (2002) said that reviewing the literature is humbling (p. 264).
- Special accounts muddy tests, because withdrawal penalties and the "retirement" mental account may both help saving. Thaler says a definitive test came only recently (p. 264).

**Intuition note:** If the only lever has an unknown sign, theory cannot guide design. Behavioral economics adds many more levers ("all those SIFs", p. 264).

### The 1994 proposals (pp. 264–266)
- IRA limit: $2,000/year ($4,000 per couple). With a 30% marginal tax rate, a full contribution cuts taxes by $600 (p. 264).
- The contribution must be made before filing. But 90% of filers get a refund, averaging about $3,000, and arrive late (p. 265).
- Proposal 1: let taxpayers direct part of the refund into an IRA that counts for the return being filed (p. 265).
- Proposal 2: adjust withholding so refunds are larger unless people actively reduce withholding. Windfalls tend to be saved at a higher proportion than regular income, especially if sizable (p. 265).
- Thaler suspected that bigger withholding also improves compliance. This rests on loss aversion and risk seeking in losses. A study of 4 million Swedish returns found sharply higher claimed "other expenses" deductions when taxpayers would otherwise owe. Such small claims (under 20,000 kroner, about $2,600) are rejected over 90% of the time when audited (pp. 265–266).
- Proposal 3: make 401(k) enrollment the default. Thaler's reading of economics: it predicts no effect, since no Econ would skip large gains (employer match) over a few forms. For Humans, forms and investment choice are daunting, so the effect could be big (p. 266).
- McDonald's had tried this under the name "negative election" (p. 266).

### Designing Save More Tomorrow (pp. 266–268)
- Thaler spoke to Fidelity's retirement-plan clients. Firms were moving from employer-run pensions to defined contribution plans (p. 266).
- Method: list the main behavioral obstacles and design to overcome each (p. 267).
  - **Inertia:** people plan to save more "soon" but rarely change their rate (p. 267).
  - **Loss aversion:** people hate pay cuts, measured in nominal dollars (p. 267).
  - **Present bias:** people are more patient about the future than the present. The marshmallow choice (1 at 2 p.m. vs 3 at 2:15 p.m.) is easy to wait for if it is tomorrow's, hard if it is today's (p. 267).
- The plan: choose now to raise the saving rate at the next raise, and stay enrolled until opt-out or a cap. Tying increases to raises averts loss aversion. A future start mitigates present bias. Staying enrolled makes inertia work for the saver (p. 268).
- No company called. Mark Iwry (Treasury) led rulings that defined and approved "automatic enrollment" (p. 268).

### Madrian and Shea (pp. 268–270)
- One firm adopted automatic enrollment in June 1999. Madrian compared employees newly eligible in 1998 with those hired after the change (p. 269).
- First-year joining rose from 49% to 86%; only 14% opted out (p. 269).
- Downside: employees seemed to treat the defaults as suggestions (pp. 269–270).
  - The default was a 3% rate in a money market fund. That fund was then the only option approved by the Department of Labor (p. 269).
  - The 3% was influenced, unintentionally, by the wording of a June 1998 ruling. Footnote: Iwry chose a low rate to avoid opposition, and the "initial anchor stuck" (pp. 269, 276).
  - Before the change, many chose 6%, the end of the employer match. Afterwards, fewer chose 6% and more chose 3% (p. 270).
- Thaler concludes that firms using automatic enrollment should also deploy Save More Tomorrow (p. 270).

**Intuition note:** A default that the firm did not intend as advice seemed to be treated as advice. So the default's level may matter, not just its existence.

### The first Save More Tomorrow case (pp. 270–272)
- Brian Tarbox's client had too few lower-paid participants, which risked non-compliance with Department of Labor limits. He met each employee one-on-one (p. 270).
- His software often recommended the firm's 15% maximum. Employees laughed at a jump from 5% to 15% (p. 271).
- Step 1: advise a 5-point increase. Nearly three-quarters declined (p. 271).
- Step 2: offer Save More Tomorrow, with +3 points at each raise for up to four raises. 78% of those offered it accepted (p. 271).
- After four annual raises (about three and a half years), their saving rate went from 3.5% to 13.6% (p. 271).
- Figure 25 (saving rates of each group's participants, %, p. 272):

| Group | Initial | 1st raise | 2nd | 3rd | 4th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Declined advice | 6.6 | 6.5 | 6.8 | 6.6 | 6.2 |
| Took recommended rate | 4.4 | 9.1 | 8.9 | 8.7 | 8.8 |
| Joined Save More Tomorrow | 3.5 | 6.5 | 9.4 | 11.6 | 13.6 |
| Declined Save More Tomorrow | 6.1 | 6.3 | 6.2 | 6.1 | 5.9 |

- The 5-point group jumped in year one but "got stuck" as inertia set in (p. 271).
- Later implementations: take-up depended strongly on ease of learning and signing up. Seminars without on-the-spot sign-up have limited effect. Thaler suggests making such a plan the default. He says 10% is a bare minimum for those without other wealth, and 15% is better (pp. 272–273).

### Spread and evidence on net worth (pp. 273–274)
- Many firms use "automatic escalation", which delinks increases from raises. Thaler says this does not appear vital (p. 273).
- Aon Hewitt survey of the largest employers: by 2011, 56% used automatic enrollment and 51% offered escalation or Save More Tomorrow. The 2006 Pension Protection Act gave a small incentive (p. 273).
- Thaler and Benartzi estimate 4.1 million U.S. users by 2011, saving an extra $7.6 billion per year by 2013. The UK's scheme had an opt-out rate of about 12% (p. 273).
- The open question was whether the saving is offset elsewhere. Chetty and coauthors, using Danish wealth records, find two results (pp. 273–274):
  1. Most of the saving is "new". There is no discernible fall in other saving or rise in debt. Econs, who treat money as fungible, would offset it (p. 274).
  2. Only 1% of the increase is attributed to tax breaks and 99% to automatic features. The authors say automatic policies could have larger impacts at lower fiscal cost (p. 274).

**Intuition note:** In this study the nudged-in saving mostly stayed saved, which would surprise Econs, who treat money as fungible. The authors suggest that automatic policies could do more than tax subsidies.

### Mulligan and "libertarian paternalism" (pp. 274–275)
- Thaler's reading: an Econ would not join the plan, or would offset it elsewhere (pp. 274–275).
- **Objection (Casey Mulligan):** the authors might trick people into saving too much. Later: "isn't this paternalism?" (p. 275).
- **Thaler replies:** he conceded overshooting was possible but said it seemed unlikely given low U.S. saving, and the plan has a maximum rate. He adds that it seems better to overshoot than to undershoot, because a sixty-year-old with surplus has many remedies and one with too little has little time (p. 275).
- On paternalism: it normally means coercion, and this plan is voluntary. He blurted out "libertarian paternalism" (p. 275).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Pay is $50,000 and the saving rate is 3%. Taxes are ignored.
- Now: saving = 0.03 × 50,000 = 1,500. Take-home = 48,500.
- Raise of 4% to 52,000, and saving 6%: saving = 3,120. Take-home = 48,880, which is more than 48,500 (+380).
- Another 4% raise to 54,080, and saving 9%: saving = 4,867.20. Take-home = 49,212.80, which is more than 48,880.
- Saving rose from 1,500 to 4,867.20, yet the nominal paycheck never fell. This is the loss-aversion safeguard.
- Condition: if the first raise were only 2% (51,000), saving at 6% = 3,060 and take-home = 47,940, below 48,500. The safeguard holds only if the raise is large enough relative to the 3-point step.

## Concepts
- [[supposedly-irrelevant-factors]] (new) — features that standard theory says cannot matter but that change behavior, such as defaults and forms (pp. 264, 266).
- [[default-effect-automatic-enrollment]] (new) — enrollment unless one opts out; raised first-year joining from 49% to 86% in one firm; employees seemed to treat defaults as suggestions (pp. 266, 269–270).
- [[inertia]] (new) — procrastination and rarely changing saving options; a design can make it work for the saver (p. 267).
- [[present-bias]] (new) — more self-control about the future than about the present (p. 267).
- [[mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion]] — here, loss aversion is the dislike of paycheck cuts, measured in nominal dollars; the "retirement" mental account is also less tempting to dip into (pp. 264, 267).
- [[save-more-tomorrow]] (new) — commit now to raise saving rates at future raises, with opt-out and a cap (p. 268).
- [[libertarian-paternalism]] (new) — Thaler's off-the-cuff label for a voluntary (non-coercive) kind of paternalism, as in Save More Tomorrow (p. 275).

## Canonical instances
- [[madrian-shea-401k-default]] — default effect: 49% → 86% joining; 3% anchoring (pp. 269–270, 276).
- [[tarbox-save-more-tomorrow-firm]] — saving rate 3.5% → 13.6%; the advice-only group stalled (pp. 271–272).
- [[danish-saving-study-chetty]] — saving is mostly new; 99% automatic features, 1% tax (p. 274).
- [[marshmallow-delay-choice]] — present bias, 2 p.m. tomorrow vs 2:15 p.m. (p. 267).
- [[swedish-tax-deduction-study]] — loss aversion in tax filing (pp. 265–266).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Present bias ↔ commitment devices: committing now for a later raise resembles pre-commitment against one's future self.
- [structural-analogy] Default as signal ↔ asymmetric information: employees read a neutral default as the employer's advice.
- [metaphor] Inertia working "for us" ↔ equilibrium: a status quo can be a stable resting point, here a good one.

## Questions this raises
- If defaults are read as suggestions, how should the default level be set, and by whom?
- Does the Danish result (1% tax, 99% automatic) hold for people with other wealth or for other countries?
- Is a voluntary, opt-out design really "paternalism"? What distinguishes it from coercion?
- Why did firms adopt so slowly despite free consulting (pp. 268, 273)?
