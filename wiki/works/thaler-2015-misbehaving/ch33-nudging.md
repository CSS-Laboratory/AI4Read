# Nudging in the U.K.

## L0 — One line
Thaler argues that governments can use behavioral science to improve policy by letting departments set the agenda, making the wanted action easy, and testing interventions with randomized trials wherever possible.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler tells how *Nudge* led to the U.K. Behavioural Insights Team (BIT), and what its work teaches. The question is how governments can use behavioral science, and how much small changes can achieve. His answer: let departments set the agenda, make the wanted action easy, and test interventions with randomized controlled trials wherever possible. A tax-letter sentence about social norms sped up payments. An attic-cleanup offer shows barrier removal, and also the pitfalls of field trials. Small effects can matter at scale, but "one-click" interventions are the easiest wins. Nudges can also serve bad ends, so they should be transparent, true and tested.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a nudge (a behavioral policy tool of the kind advocated in the book *Nudge*); control group vs treatment group; percentage vs percentage points.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A short visit to London in 2008 moved *Nudge* from a book to a coalition government's plans; the BIT was created in 2010 and led by David Halpern (pp. 283–287).
- The BIT's mission was broad: impact in two policy areas, spread of behavioral understanding, and a tenfold return on its cost. Departments chose the agenda (p. 287).
- A tax-reminder experiment (about 120,000 taxpayers) found that "most people pay, and you are one of the few who haven't" worked best, adding over 5 percentage points paying within 23 days (p. 289).
- Two mantras: make it easy (Lewin's "unfreezing"), and evidence-based policy needs evidence, ideally RCTs (p. 290).
- The attic experiment gave only a hint (28 installations in total) but shows barrier removal and the risks of field RCTs (pp. 291–292).
- Small effects (1–2%) can be worth much at scale. The savings programs combined three ingredients that greatly raise the chance of success: likely benefit, agreement that change is good, and a one-click action (pp. 292–293).
- Reminders, such as texts, can be effective even when simple and transparent (p. 294).
- The movement spread to the U.S. and many countries. Thaler says there has been, up to now, very little actual economics in the BIT's work (its tools come mainly from psychology and other social sciences), and urges "nudge for good" (pp. 295–297).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The chapter has no numbered sections. The headings below are ours and follow its order.

### How the U.K. project began (pp. 283–286)
- In July 2008 Thaler was in London on his way to Cass Sunstein's wedding. Richard Reeves, soon director of the think tank Demos, had a copy of *Nudge* and put him in touch with Rohan Silva, who wanted him to meet Conservative Party staff (pp. 283–284).
- At the Houses of Parliament he met Silva, Steve Hilton and the MP Oliver Letwin. They thought the *Nudge* approach could support the party's rebranding under David Cameron and George Osborne, whose stated goal was a more progressive, pro-environment party (p. 284).
- Silva bought ten copies. Cameron saw the pile and, apparently liking the book, put it on a summer-reading list for Tory MPs. Thaler suspects Silva drafted that list (pp. 284–285).
- In spring 2009 Thaler met Sir Gus O'Donnell, the Cabinet Secretary (top civil servant), who was already a fan of behavioral economics (p. 285).
- After the May 2010 election, the Conservatives formed a coalition with the Liberal Democrats. Cameron became Prime Minister, Nick Clegg Deputy PM, and Reeves became Clegg's chief policy advisor. Silva and Hilton became senior policy advisors to the PM (p. 285).

### Setting up the Behavioural Insights Team (pp. 286–287)
- David Halpern was chosen to run it. He was a social scientist who had been chief analyst in Blair's strategy unit. This gave him government experience and nonpartisan credentials (p. 286).
- On a train to Paris, Hilton wanted the name "behavior change". Thaler and Halpern preferred "Behavioural Insights Team", which was chosen. Silva predicted that everyone would call it the "nudge unit" anyway (p. 286).
- Official mission: significant impact in at least two major policy areas; spread of behavioral understanding across government; at least a tenfold return on the unit's cost (p. 287).
- Method: ask each department what problems it faces, then brainstorm. Letting departments choose the agenda was vital, not lecturing them (p. 287).

### Case 1: HMRC tax letters (pp. 287–289)
- Taxpayers who file returns pay on January 31 and July 31. If the second payment is late, a reminder goes out, then letters, calls and legal action. Collection agencies and legal action are costly and annoy taxpayers, who are also voters (p. 288).
- Nick Down at HMRC had read Cialdini. His pilot used Cialdini's standard advice: tell people, if true, that most others comply. *Nudge* had reported a Minnesota study where "more than 90% paid on time" was the most effective message (p. 288).
- The pilot had no control group and varied several things at once, so it was not rigorous. The experiments were cheap: only the wording of letters already being sent changed (p. 288).
- The latest of three rounds (Michael Hallsworth and academics): nearly 120,000 taxpayers owing £351–£50,000. Everyone got a reminder explaining how to pay. Apart from the control, each letter added one sentence (p. 289). Examples:
  - "The great majority of people in the UK pay their taxes on time."
  - "The great majority of people in your local area pay their taxes on time."
  - "You are currently in the very small minority of people who have not paid their taxes on time."
- "The great majority" replaced "90%" because BIT could not confirm 90% for every locality. Thaler: ethical nudges must be transparent and true (p. 289).
- All versions helped. The best combined "most people pay" with "you are one of the few that hasn't". It raised payment within 23 days by over five percentage points. The experiment sped up £9 million of revenue in those 23 days (p. 289).
- Footnotes: 23 days is when the HMRC system sends another letter (p. 289, fn). By Thaler's definition, transparency means nothing is hidden and all study results are eventually released (p. 289, fn).

*Intuition note:* Adding one true sentence to a letter that was being sent anyway costs nothing. Even a few percentage points more payers can speed up millions of pounds when many people are reached (the £9 million was a speed-up, and the net saving is hard to calculate).

### Two mantras (p. 290)
1. Make it easy. Following Kurt Lewin, the first step in changing behavior is "unfreezing", e.g. removing barriers, however subtle (p. 290).
2. "We can't do evidence-based policy without evidence." Test interventions, where possible, with randomized controlled trials (RCTs): people are assigned at random to treatments, including a no-treatment control group. This is not always feasible (p. 290).
   - Footnote: Thaler knows of no RCT of Save More Tomorrow, because he and his colleagues could never get a company to agree to offer it only to randomly chosen employees. The closest was one company with trials at two plants and twenty-six control plants. Employees selected themselves into the education sessions, so interpretation was cautious (p. 290, fn).

### Case 2: attic insulation (pp. 290–292)
- About a third of British homes lacked adequate attic ("loft") insulation, though savings can repay the cost in as little as one year. Subsidies for owners and landlords drew little take-up (pp. 290–291).
- Homeowners said clutter made it too much trouble. BIT proposed packaging insulation with an attic-cleanup service. Two versions: at the installer's cost (£190) or at retail price (£271), on top of the £179 insulation (p. 291).
- Design: flyers to three similar neighborhoods, nearly 24,000 per neighborhood. Each neighborhood got one offer: discounted cleanup, retail cleanup, or the standard deal (control) (p. 291). Footnote: with only three neighborhoods this is not pure random assignment, because neighborhood differences could confound results (p. 291, fn).
- Result: only 28 attics were insulated in total: 3 (standard deal), 16 (cheap cleanup), 9 (retail cleanup). $3+16+9=28$ (added step). Thaler calls it "something between a scientific finding and a nifty anecdote" (pp. 291–292).
- The department did not replicate because take-up was low. Thaler includes the case for two reasons: it is a literal example of Lewin's barrier removal, and it shows the pitfalls of field RCTs (p. 292).
  - Field trials are expensive. Unlike in labs, the experimenter often cannot be on site and cannot easily retry. Errors can be reduced but never disappear (p. 292).
- In 2013 the U.K. established a What Works Network to encourage testing in every domain. Thaler says the most important legacy of the BIT may be to help nudge governments to test ideas before implementing them. He also says not every idea will work (p. 292).

### Small effects and "one-click" interventions (pp. 292–294)
- A 1–2% change can matter if the intervention is nearly costless. Thaler compares scoffing at it to the "big peanuts" fallacy; at billion-dollar stakes small percentages add up (pp. 292–293).
- Automatic enrollment and Save More Tomorrow can wrongly suggest that big impacts are easy. They had three ingredients (p. 293):
  1. Designers have good reason to think some people would benefit from changing behavior.
  2. The target population agrees change is desirable (surveys: a majority thought they should save more).
  3. The change takes one nearly costless action, or none. Thaler calls these "one-click" interventions.
- Many problems lack a one-click solution. Example: heavy weight loss. Thaler could not devise an "Eat Less Tomorrow" plan, and most diets fail long-run (p. 293).
- Concrete one-click case: long-term reversible contraception (IUD) for reducing teenage pregnancy. Trials with sexually active young women found failure under 1%, and no further action is needed. His advice: look for settings where a one-time action does the job; "invent one" if none exists (pp. 293–294).

*Intuition note:* Thaler says programs are much more likely to succeed when people would benefit, agree that change is good, and one easy step delivers it. For many problems, such as heavy weight loss, no such one-click solution exists.

### Reminders (p. 294)
- Texts can be effective without being creative or hidden. In Ghana, an RCT by Innovations for Poverty Action found texts helped people follow malaria-medication regimens. The most effective messages were brief: the reminder mattered, not extra information (p. 294).
- READY4K! sends parents of preschoolers parenting tips by text. The study found significant increases in parental literacy involvement at home and at school, and learning gains for children (p. 294).
- Reminders show that nudges can be gentle and transparent (p. 294, fn).

### BIT's later history (pp. 294–295)
- The BIT passed its two-year review and was renewed by the Cabinet Office in 2012. After the Admiralty Arch and the Treasury, in 2014 it was partially privatized. It is owned equally by the Cabinet Office, its employees and NESTA, with a five-year contract with the Cabinet Office. It has about fifty staff and also helps other governments, e.g. a tax-compliance study in Guatemala (pp. 294–295).

### The U.S. and beyond (pp. 295–296)
- Cass Sunstein ran OIRA (formed 1980 to assess the economic impact of regulations). He had no mandate or budget for RCTs but served somewhat as a "one-man" BIT (p. 295).
- In 2014 Maya Shankar created the White House Social and Behavioral Sciences Team (SBST). It began with six people, including staff from J-PAL and ideas42. In its first year it embedded a dozen behaviorally informed RCTs in federal programs, for example on veterans' benefits and student-loan repayment. The federal government then committed budget to expand it (pp. 295–296).
- A 2014 report of the Economic and Social Research Council says that 136 countries have used behavioral sciences in some policy, and 51 have "centrally directed policy initiatives" influenced by them (p. 296).
- Thaler notes that the report says "behavioral sciences". The BIT's work is often wrongly called behavioral economics. Up to now there has been very little actual economics in it; tools come mainly from psychology and other social sciences (p. 296).

### Closing: "nudge for good" (pp. 296–297)
- Nudges are tools that existed before the name. They can also be used to nudge people into a second mortgage and a spending binge. Bad actors can misuse behavioral findings (p. 296).
- Thaler's remedy: choose nudges based on science and test them rigorously. He notes Chicago has launched a behavioral insights team with ideas42 and urges readers to press their own governments to do the same (pp. 296–297).

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text.* A letter experiment with 1,000 randomly chosen late payers per group.
- Control letter: 400 of 1,000 pay within 23 days, a rate of $400/1000 = 40.0\%$.
- Norm-sentence letter: 450 of 1,000 pay, a rate of $450/1000 = 45.0\%$.
- Effect: $45.0 - 40.0 = 5.0$ percentage points. In relative terms $5.0/40.0 = 12.5\%$ more payers.
- Scale: if 100,000 people got the norm letter instead of the control, the expected extra payers within 23 days are $100{,}000 \times 0.05 = 5{,}000$. The sentence costs nothing extra, so the gain is almost free.
- Caution: with tiny counts, e.g. 3 vs 16 successes out of about 24,000 (as in the attic case), chance could drive the gap, so replication is needed.

## Concepts
- [[nudge]] (new) — the kind of behavioral policy tool advocated in the book *Nudge*; Thaler calls nudges "merely tools" (pp. 284, 296).
- [[randomized-controlled-trial]] (new) — people are randomly assigned to treatments, including a no-treatment control; Thaler's "gold standard" (p. 290).
- [[make-it-easy-unfreezing]] (new) — Lewin's idea that the first step in changing behavior is "unfreezing"; one way to unfreeze is to remove barriers (p. 290).
- [[social-norm-message]] (new) — telling people, if true, that most others comply with a norm (Cialdini) (p. 288).
- [[one-click-intervention]] (new) — a policy needing one nearly costless action, or none (p. 293).
- [[transparent-nudge]] (new) — Thaler's definition of transparency: nothing hidden and all study results eventually public (p. 289, fn); he also says ethical nudges must be true (p. 289).
- [[bounded-rationality-heuristics]] — the "Econs vs Humans" contrast: Econs would already have insulated their attics (p. 290).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Randomized controlled trial ↔ medicine: random assignment with a control group — Thaler notes the method comes from medical research.
- [structural-analogy] One-click intervention ↔ default options (automatic enrollment): the desired outcome occurs with no effort.
- [metaphor] Social-norm message ↔ conditional cooperators: Thaler's footnote links the idea to conditional cooperators, ch. 15 (p. 288, fn).
