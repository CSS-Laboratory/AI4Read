# Mugs

## L0 — One line
Thaler and colleagues built a market experiment with induced-value tokens as a control, and showed that people given coffee mugs demanded about twice what non-owners would pay, so trade volume fell far below the theory's prediction.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Plott and Smith doubted the evidence for the endowment effect (valuing a thing more once you own it). Their objections were that subjects may be confused, and that markets would remove the effect. Thaler, with Danny and Jack Knetsch, ran a market experiment. Tokens, whose value is assigned by the experimenter, served as a control. Theory predicts about half the goods change hands. Tokens did roughly that. Mugs and pens did not: owners' asking prices were about twice buyers' bids, and few trades happened, even with repeated rounds. Thaler reads this partly as loss aversion, plus inertia (status quo bias). It matters because it answers the standard "markets and learning will fix it" objection.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand; reservation price; random assignment; loss aversion (losses weigh more than equal gains); the idea of a prediction from "economic theory".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Knetsch–Sinden: those given a lottery ticket mostly kept it (82%), while only 38% of those given $3 bought one. Theory says the starting allocation should not matter (pp. 109–110).
- Plott and Smith objected: subjects may be confused and need learning, and markets would remove the effect. This set the design requirements (p. 110).
- Thaler and Danny's insight: if the endowment effect is real, it would lower the volume of trade. This gives a testable prediction (p. 110).
- Induced-value tokens (worthless outside the lab, with experimenter-assigned values) serve as a control. Thaler did not expect an endowment effect for them, so they test whether the market works and the subjects understand (pp. 110–114).
- With random assignment, theory predicts that about half the goods change hands. With tokens, the actual trades were 12, 11 and 10 out of a predicted ~11 (pp. 112–114).
- With mugs, trades were only 4, 1, 2, 2. Sellers' median reservation price was $5.25 and buyers' was $2.25–$2.75 (p. 114).
- Pens gave a similar result, and a roughly 2:1 selling-to-buying price ratio recurred across versions. Thaler links this to losses hurting about twice as much as gains please (pp. 114–115).
- The effect can be "instant". Thaler says loss aversion is part of the explanation, along with a related phenomenon, inertia, which Samuelson and Zeckhauser call status quo bias (p. 115).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**The challenge (pp. 109–110).** Alvin Roth organised a Pittsburgh conference on experimental methods. Vernon Smith and Charlie Plott said there was no convincing evidence for the endowment effect, which is the tendency to value a thing more once you own it. Thaler's evidence was a Knetsch–Sinden experiment. Half the subjects got $3 and half got lottery tickets. The lottery winner chooses between $50 cash and $70 in bookstore vouchers. Later, ticket holders could sell the ticket for $3 and money holders could buy one for $3 (p. 109).
- Both groups face the same question. Theory says the starting allocation should not matter (p. 109).
- Result: 82% of ticket holders kept the ticket. Only 38% of the others bought one (pp. 109–110). Thaler says this "could not be any stronger or clearer" (p. 110).

*Intuition note:* The ownership was randomly assigned, so tastes are unlikely to explain the gap. People tended to keep whatever they happened to start with.

**Critique and design goal (p. 110).**
- Smith and Plott's objections, from chapter 6's list, were (a) confusion, so subjects need a chance to learn, and (b) a market context would remove the misbehaviour (p. 110).
- After the talk with Charlie and Vernon, Thaler and Danny recognized that an endowment effect, if real, would reduce market trading volume. Owners keep their objects and non-owners are not keen to buy (p. 110).
- They added a market to the Knetsch study and used Smith's "induced value" method as a control. Subjects trade tokens that are worthless outside the lab. Each is told a personal value, redeemable at the end only if they hold a token. For example, Seth would get $2.25 and Kevin $3.75 for a token (p. 110).
- Thaler did not expect an endowment effect for tokens, "any more than" for a particular twenty-dollar bill (p. 110).

**How the token market works (pp. 111–112, Figure 7).**
- Example: 12 subjects with random values from $0.25 to $5.75, ranked from highest to lowest. Six tokens are handed out at random (pp. 111–112).
- Owners fill in forms saying "I will sell / will not sell" at each price. The lowest price at which they will sell is their *reservation price*. Example: a $4.25 valuation sells at $4.50 but not at $4, so the reservation price is $4.50 (p. 111).
- Buyers fill in similar forms (p. 111).
- If the market works well, theory predicts that the six highest-valuing subjects end up holding the tokens (p. 111).
- Market-clearing price: work inward from both ends. The price must lie between the reservation prices of the last pair to trade (Subjects 7 and 6). Prices move in 50-cent steps, so the price is $3 (p. 111).
- On average, the predicted volume of trade is half the tokens distributed (p. 112).

*Intuition note:* Random assignment puts about half the tokens in the wrong hands. Trade fixes this. So a market with no endowment effect should produce trade equal to about half the tokens.

**The same logic for real goods (p. 113).**
- With a chocolate bar, subjects judge their own value. Theory gives the same prediction, with about half the bars changing hands (p. 113).
- With an endowment effect, owners value the good more and trade volume falls (p. 113).

**Cornell token test, fall 1985 (pp. 113–114).**
- An advanced law-and-economics class had 44 students and 22 tokens. Subjects wrote reservation prices, and the exercise was practised three times (p. 113).
- The market rule is the standard supply-and-demand one. Rank sellers' asks from lowest to highest and buyers' bids from highest to lowest. Sale 1 happens if the top bid is above the lowest ask, sale 2 if the second bid is above the second ask, and so on. All trades occur at one price, where demand equals supply (pp. 113–114).
- Theory predicts about 11 trades. The three trials gave 12, 11 and 10. So the market worked and subjects understood the task (p. 114).

**The real-goods experiment (p. 114).**
- Goods: a Cornell coffee mug ($6) and a boxed ballpoint pen ($3.98, price tag left on). There were 22 of each (p. 114).
- A mug went to every other student. Everyone inspected a mug. There were four market rounds, with one picked at random to "count", to allow learning as Plott and Smith required (p. 114).
- Theory predicts about 11 trades. Thaler predicted significantly fewer (p. 114).
- Result: 4, 1, 2, 2 trades. The sellers' median reservation price was $5.25 in all four rounds. Buyers' median was $2.75 in one round and $2.25 in the others (p. 114).
- Pens, with the mug-less students as owners: 4–5 trades, and a price ratio again near 2:1 (pp. 114–115).
- Many versions run for critics and referees gave the same result. Buyers would pay about half of what sellers demanded, "even with markets and learning" (p. 115).
- Thaler concludes that losses are "roughly twice as painful as gains are pleasurable". He says this has been replicated many times (p. 115).

*Intuition note:* Tokens trade readily and mugs do not. So the market and the subjects were not the problem. The ownership of a real object itself changed how it was valued.

**Interpretation (p. 115).**
- Thaler says people stick with what they have, at least in part because of loss aversion. Giving up the mug feels like a loss (p. 115).
- The effect can be "instant", and in these experiments subjects had "owned" the mug for only a few minutes before trading (p. 115).
- Related is inertia, which Samuelson and Zeckhauser call *status quo bias*. People stay put unless there is good reason to switch, or perhaps even despite one (p. 115).
- Thaler says the two will often work together as forces that inhibit change. Thaler's example is displaced workers who face a job change plus the loss of friends, family and home (p. 115).
- Since the 1990 mugs paper there have been "dozens, perhaps hundreds" of follow-up studies, some critical, others exploring boundary conditions (the limits on when the effect appears). Nearly all of them share one feature: coffee mugs, with thousands of university-insignia mugs bought (pp. 115–116).
- Closing anecdote: Danny said he thought people stop being "promising" at about the time they turn forty. Thaler was thirty-nine (p. 116).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four students value a chocolate bar at $1, $2, $3, $4. Two bars are given to the two lowest-valuing students ($1 and $2).
- **Theory (no endowment effect):** asks are 1 and 2 and bids are 4 and 3. Sort bids high to low (4, 3) and asks low to high (1, 2). 4 > 1 gives sale 1, and 3 > 2 gives sale 2. Trades = 2.
- **Endowment effect (owners value their bar at double):** asks become 2 and 4, so sorted asks are 2, 4. Bids stay 4, 3. 4 > 2 gives sale 1, but 3 > 4 fails. Trades = 1.

The same people and values give half the trades once owners inflate their values.

## Concepts
- [[endowment-effect]] (new; part of canon "mental accounting, endowment effect, loss aversion") — people value what they own more than non-owners do, so owners tend to keep it, even after random allocation (pp. 109–110, 113)
- [[loss-aversion]] (new; same canon entry) — giving up an owned good is a loss; Thaler says losses hurt roughly twice as much as gains please (p. 115)
- [[status-quo-bias]] (new) — inertia, the tendency to keep what one has unless given reason to switch, or perhaps even despite one; Samuelson and Zeckhauser's term (p. 115)
- [[induced-value-method]] (new) — subjects get tokens, worthless outside the lab, with assigned redemption values (p. 110)
- [[reservation-price]] (new) — lowest price a seller accepts, or highest price a buyer pays (pp. 111, 113)
- [[equilibrium]] — here, the market-clearing price at which supply equals demand (pp. 111–112, 114)

## Canonical instances
- [[knetsch-sinden-lottery-experiment]] — 82% of ticket holders kept their tickets, against 38% of the others who bought one (pp. 109–110)
- [[induced-value-token-market]] — the control market; 12, 11, 10 trades against ~11 predicted (pp. 111–114)
- [[cornell-mug-experiment]] — 4, 1, 2, 2 trades; median sell $5.25 vs buy $2.25–$2.75 (p. 114)
- [[pen-experiment]] — 4–5 trades; price ratio again about 2:1 (pp. 114–115)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Low trade volume under the endowment effect ↔ market frictions: a wedge between buyers' and sellers' reservation prices shrinks trading
- [metaphor] Status quo bias as physical inertia ↔ physics: Thaler compares people sticking with what they have to a physical object at rest that stays that way unless something happens (p. 115)
- [structural-analogy] Induced-value tokens as a control ↔ experimental design: a condition where the effect should be absent checks that subjects and the market work

## Questions this raises
- If owners price at about twice buyers' bids, how would real markets (houses, jobs) behave?
- Why does ownership change value after only a few minutes ("instant endowment effect")?
- What are the "boundary conditions" of the effect, where it appears and where it does not? (p. 115)
- Would the tokens' null result survive if subjects could resell for different values?
