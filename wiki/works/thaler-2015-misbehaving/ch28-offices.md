# The Offices

## L0 — One line
A comic case study of how Chicago Booth's faculty assigned offices in a new building illustrates, in Thaler's telling, a possible endowment effect, the over-weighting of an easily measured number, and the anger that a ranking process with an unannounced number of bins caused (with some blame, he says, on an architect who was not told how offices would be assigned).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Booth faculty had to pick offices in a new building. The deans chose a "merit"-ranked draft, with the number of bins never announced, and some senior professors reacted with fury. Thaler tells the story as a participant. The rules left some ambiguity, and Thaler thinks some of the anger came from the explicit ranking. In one case a mock-draft pick seems to have been treated as owned. In hindsight Thaler thinks offices were grabbed on flawed heuristics and square footage was overweighted. He thinks more transparency might have helped, and he blames the architect a bit, since no one told him how offices would be assigned. This is a light anecdote, not an experiment.

**Prerequisites:** the endowment effect (people value what they own more than what they could acquire); heuristics (rules of thumb); basic market ideas such as "value buy".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Offices were allocated by a ranked draft, and trading offices or buying picks was ruled out (pdf 268). Thaler's phrase "picking order (and pecking order)" hints that the order itself became a source of conflict (pdf 268–269).
- A "merit" ranking in bins whose number was never announced, with random order inside each bin, was followed by anger among senior faculty. Thaler thinks some of the furor could have been mitigated by a more transparent process, for example by making the number of bins public (pdf 269, 272).
- Thaler suggests the endowment effect appeared even for offices picked in a labelled mock draft (pdf 270).
- Luigi Zingales, suspicious by nature, questioned the stated square footage of his chosen office and found it was wrong; other size mistakes then turned up. He had told no one beforehand, lest he lose his competitive advantage (pdf 271).
- In hindsight, Thaler says fifth-floor offices were grabbed early, perhaps because of a flawed "higher is better" heuristic (pdf 272).
- Thaler calls square footage the "overhyped commodity": if a spreadsheet of measurements is the only thing people stare at, he says, this factor is "bound to be overweighted" ("If there is a number, people will use it", pdf 272).
- Thaler puts a bit of the blame on the architect, because no one told him how offices would be assigned. He adds that the term "choice architect" had not yet been invented (pdf 272).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Setting (pdf 267).** In spring 2002 the Booth School was moving to a new building designed by Rafael Viñoly. Offices had to be assigned. The deans chose a draft: each person gets a time slot, then picks any open office, seeing all earlier picks. Seniority was rejected because "you are only as good as your last paper". A lottery was not seriously considered.

**The rules (pdf 268).**
- Order was by "merit", judged by Deputy Dean John Huizinga. He sorted faculty into bins and used random drawing within each bin. The number of bins was never announced (pdf 268).
- Each person had 15 minutes, with an architect's help. The building was only a steel cage, so offices could only be seen in drawings and a scale model (pdf 268).
- Offices could not be traded. The deans emphatically ruled out buying an earlier pick from a colleague, and did not auction picks. Thaler's comment is that faculty offices are "too sacred to sell" even at Chicago (pdf 268).

**The order leaks (pdf 269).** Anil Kashyap emailed everyone to collect time slots, and the order became clear within hours. All tenured full professors picked before associates, then assistants, then adjuncts. Order within the untenured groups looked random.

**Thaler's reconstruction of the senior order (pdf 269).** Huizinga has never revealed the method to Thaler (or, as far as Thaler knows, to anyone else); this is Thaler's "best guess", which Huizinga neither confirmed nor denied but allowed was right on the basic facts (pdf 272–273).
- Thaler *believes* there were three bins for full professors. Bin A had about a dozen stars or obvious senior figures, with at least one from each faculty group and several from finance. Bin B held most of the rest, and bin C held those no longer doing active research. Huizinga put himself last.
- Thaler believes bin A had several purposes; he names two. One was to reward contributors. The other was to scatter the stars, because the best offices are corners, which are far apart.
- The most distressed people were those in bin B who thought they belonged in bin A and then drew a late slot. "Archie" was the angriest (names given by first name only are fictitious: pdf 273). His colleague "Clyde" was in bin A and drew second pick (pdf 269–270).

**Reactions and mock drafts (pdf 270).** Archie thought the draft was rigged. Doug Diamond had first pick and Fama third. Thaler thought only Doug was truly happy with his spot. Kashyap organised email mock drafts, and the faculty demanded office sizes and thermostat information. About one in three offices had a thermostat. Thaler suggested installing "placebo" thermostats in the other offices, saying that from his own experience the real ones were no more effective. When a real pick took an office claimed in a mock draft, the loser protested "That was my office, you bastard!" Thaler's reading: it "seems" that the endowment effect can occur even for an office chosen in a clearly labelled practice exercise.

**Draft day (pdf 270–271).** Picks began at 8:30 a.m.
- At 1:15 Luigi Zingales doubted the stated square footage of his chosen office. The real plans showed it was 20 sq ft smaller than listed. Offices are mostly between 180 and 230 sq ft.
- He switched to a larger office and told others only after choosing, so as not to lose his advantage. Others then demanded remeasurement and found more errors. Huizinga was reached at a conference, and the draft was suspended around 3 p.m.
- After remeasurement, some early pickers found their offices had "shrunk". Huizinga ruled that the draft would restart, and people could switch only to offices not yet taken, even by later drafters. This caused more uproar. Huizinga then appeared in Groucho Marx glasses, which brought the house down, though less so for the angry.

*Intuition note.* In Thaler's telling, the draft made faculty care about where they were ranked as well as about the offices, and he thinks some of the furor came from the explicit ranking. He suggests that one recorded mock-draft choice was defended as the chooser's own.

**Postmortem (pdf 271–272).** The offices were mostly similar once built, apart from nine corner offices.
- *Fifth floor.* These were grabbed early, perhaps through a "higher is better" heuristic. There is no view advantage over the fourth floor, and the fifth is served by only one of three elevator banks, the busiest.
- *North face.* It has the best views, including the skyline, but was not picked early. Thaler's conditional: if north exposure was the "value buy" in this market, the overhyped commodity was square footage.
- *Square footage.* In that case it was the "overhyped commodity". Thaler says the difference between 190 and 210 sq ft is not noticeable, and most visitors do not realise that offices differ in size. If the only thing one stares at is a spreadsheet of measurements, he says, this factor is "bound to be overweighted".
- *Transparency.* Thaler thinks some of the furor could have been mitigated by a more transparent process; making the number of bins public "might have been a good idea" and would at least have reassured Clyde that he had not been deliberately slotted late (as printed; see Reading notes).
- *Architect.* Thaler puts "a bit of the blame" on Viñoly and his team: no one told the architect how offices would be assigned. Knowing it, he might have avoided corner offices. Thaler suggested shrinking Doug's office (fifth floor, northeast corner, the biggest) to make the first choice less obvious. But he was "only an architect"; the term "choice architect" had not yet been invented.

*Intuition note.* In Thaler's account, people may give too much weight to features that are easy to see and compare, such as a number on a spreadsheet, and unclear rules may leave losers suspicious (Archie thought the draft was rigged).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three offices, with a view score out of 10 and a size in sq ft. Suppose true value = view + 0.05 × (size − 180).
- A: view 3, size 210 → 3 + 0.05×30 = 3 + 1.5 = 4.5
- B: view 8, size 190 → 8 + 0.05×10 = 8 + 0.5 = 8.5
- C: view 5, size 200 → 5 + 0.05×20 = 5 + 1.0 = 6.0

A picker who looks only at the spreadsheet column "size" takes A (210), worth 4.5. A picker who weighs everything takes B, worth 8.5. The gap is 8.5 − 4.5 = 4.0 points. This shows why an overweighted salient number leaves the best "value buy" for later pickers.

## Concepts
- [[endowment effect]] — valuing what one has chosen or owns more; Thaler says it seems to appear even for a mock-draft office (pdf 270)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — rules of thumb; Thaler says fifth-floor offices were perhaps grabbed on a flawed "higher is better" heuristic (pdf 272)
- [[overweighting of salient numbers]] (new) — a visible number such as square footage gets too much weight in a choice (pdf 272)
- [[choice architecture]] (new) — Thaler says no one told the architect how offices would be assigned, and that the term "choice architect" was not yet invented (pdf 272)
- [[process transparency]] (new) — publishing rules, such as the number of bins, could reduce anger (pdf 272)

## Canonical instances
- [[booth-office-draft]] — a non-tradable draft whose number of bins was never announced, followed by fury and, after office-size errors were found, a restart (pdf 267–271)
- [[mock-draft-endowment]] — "That was my office, you bastard!" (pdf 270)
- [[square-footage-overweighting]] — Thaler says a 20 sq ft gap (190 vs 210) is not noticeable, yet square footage was "bound to be overweighted" (pdf 272)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Hidden bins and an opaque ranking ↔ economics-finance: rules vs. discretion, with the process itself generating conflict.
- [structural-analogy] Overweighting square footage ↔ bounded rationality: salient, measurable attributes dominate choices.
- [metaphor] Faculty offices "too sacred to sell" ↔ markets: some goods are not traded openly, even in a free-market culture.

## Questions this raises
- Would a lottery, an auction or a transparent seniority rule have avoided the conflict, or only moved it?
- Would the "placebo thermostat" idea work, and what are the ethics of such placebos?
- How can designers learn how their buildings or products will be used, including how offices will be assigned (which Thaler says no one told Viñoly)?
- How far can one generalise from one faculty's anecdote told by a participant?
