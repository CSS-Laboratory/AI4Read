# The Planner and the Doer

## L0 — One line
Thaler models self-control as a conflict between a far-sighted "planner" and a present-focused "doer", where the planner can influence the doer through rewards or penalties (in the energy-bar example, costly guilt) or through rules that limit his options (pp. 71–74).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How can economics explain people who plan one thing and then do another? Thaler draws on Strotz, Mischel and Ainslie. With Shefrin he builds an "as if" model: a planner who cares about all future periods and a series of selfish, short-lived doers. The planner can use guilt, which makes life less pleasurable, or rules; perfect rules would be better, but they may be unavailable and are inflexible by design. The chapter then notes that the beta–delta model of later authors is simpler and now more popular. It also gives Thaler's view that people are partly naïve about their own self-control problems: they underestimate them. This matters because self-control problems are important for many social problems (p. 77).

**Prerequisites:** discounting and time inconsistency (previous chapter); diminishing marginal utility; the idea of a principal and an agent.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Strotz found that only exponential discounting guarantees that a plan, once made, stays wanted; he worried this condition fails (p. 66).
- Two tools against temptation: remove cues, or commit by limiting your own choices (Odysseus, Strotz's pay schedule) (p. 67).
- Mischel's children waited longer when the treat was out of sight; waiting time later predicted life outcomes (p. 68).
- You would want to commit yourself only when you have good reason to believe that the later change of preferences will be a mistake (p. 69).
- Planner–doer model: a principal–agent structure inside one person, with a selfish doer each period (pp. 71–72).
- Guilt lowers the doer's pleasure; perfect rules do better but may be unavailable or inflexible (pp. 73–75).
- Mental accounting (keeping money in separate pots) is feasible as a self-control device because money is not treated as fungible, that is, as interchangeable (p. 75).
- The beta–delta model (which weights "now" far above "later") is simpler and has become the model of choice, although Thaler still finds planner–doer most useful; agents can be sophisticated (they know their bias), naïve (unaware of it), and, in Thaler's view, partly naïve (pp. 75–77).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Three sources of inspiration (pp. 66–70)
**Strotz (1955).** Strotz asked which preferences ensure that a person who makes a plan will not want to change it. Only one highly specific case, exponential discounting, guarantees time consistency. Like Samuelson, he worried that these conditions would not be met (p. 66).

**Odysseus and the Sirens.** Odysseus's plan had two parts (p. 67):
- The crew put wax in their ears. This removes the tempting cue.
- Odysseus is tied to the mast. This is a *commitment strategy*: limiting your own choices.

Strotz himself chose to be paid over twelve months instead of nine, "although I could use the interest!" (p. 67).

**Mischel's experiments.** A child (age four or five) could ring a bell for one treat now, or wait for three (p. 67).
- With the treats in view, the average wait was barely over a minute. With the treats out of sight it was eleven minutes. Thinking about something "fun" also helped (p. 68).
- Follow-up: about a third of roughly 500 original children were found. Waiting time predicted outcomes from SAT scores to career success to drug use. This was surprising because Mischel's own work showed that personality traits predict behavior poorly (p. 68).

**Ainslie (1975).** Most studies found that animals such as rats and pigeons show the same discounting pattern that leads to preference reversals in humans; Thaler says animals discount hyperbolically (p. 69). Ainslie lists strategies:
- Commitment.
- Raising the cost of temptation, for example a check that someone may cash if you are seen smoking.
- A "private side bet" with yourself (p. 69).

**Thaler's question.** If I know I will change my mind, when would I restrict my future choices? Only when I have good reason to think the later change of preferences will be a mistake. Eating the whole bowl of cashews spoils dinner, so removing the bowl is smart (pp. 69–70).

*Intuition note:* Thaler says you would want to restrict your own future choices only when you have good reason to believe the later change of preferences will be a mistake (p. 69). In Mischel's experiments the circumstances of waiting, such as whether the treat was in sight, mattered (p. 68).

### The two-self idea and the principal–agent form (pp. 70–72)
McIntosh's quote: self-control is paradoxical unless the psyche has more than one energy system, with some independence (p. 70). Thaler wondered whether he needed a model with two selves (p. 70). With Shefrin he proposed a metaphor: a forward-looking **planner** and a devil-may-care **doer** (p. 71).
- They rejected game theory because the doer is passive and non-strategic: he reacts to what is in front of him and consumes until sated (p. 71).
- They chose a **principal–agent model**. The principal delegates authority. The agent knows some things the principal does not, and it is too costly for the principal to monitor every action. The firm responds with rules and incentives, such as commission pay or receipts for expenses (pp. 71–72).

### Model assumptions (p. 72)
- There is a new doer each period. He is completely selfish and ignores later doers.
- The planner is completely altruistic and cares only about the utility of the series of doers.
- Her control is limited, especially when the doer is aroused by food, sex, alcohol or the urge to goof off.
- Her tools are (a) rewards or penalties that leave discretion, or (b) rules that limit options such as commitments.

### Harry and the energy bars (pp. 72–75)
Harry is alone in a cabin for ten days with ten energy bars (p. 72).
- The planner does not discount distant doers. Each doer has diminishing marginal utility and eats until the last bite adds no pleasure (p. 72).
- The planner's best outcome is one bar per day. This is consumption smoothing, as Econs would do under the life-cycle hypothesis (pp. 72–73).
- Ten programmable safes would achieve this (p. 73).
- With no intervention, suppose a doer stops at three bars. Days 1–3 use nine bars. On day 4 the doer eats the last bar. The rest of the week "is no fun" (p. 73).
- If no commitment strategy such as safes is available, the only other tool the planner has in the model is **guilt**, and imposing guilt is costly. In the example she cannot make the doer feel bad only after the first bar; she has to make each bite less pleasurable (p. 73).
- Figure 6 shows three happiness curves, peaking at three bars (no guilt), two bars (some guilt) and one bar (lots of guilt). Each level of guilt lowers happiness. Willpower requires effort (pp. 73–74).

*Intuition note:* Guilt works by making the doer enjoy his meal less. If perfect rules can be implemented, such as safes with one bar each, they achieve much more satisfaction than the guilt-induced diet (p. 74).

**Why not always use rules?** (pp. 74–75)
- Enforced rules may not be available. A healthy dinner delivery does not stop you from ordering pizza (p. 74).
- Rules are inflexible. With a nine-month pay schedule Strotz could buy a lawnmower on winter sale, but he then needs discipline to last through summer (pp. 74–75).
- Organizations face the same trade-off. A rulebook leaves low-level agents unable to do the obviously sensible but unanticipated thing (p. 75).
- Accounting in organizations and mental accounting in people, with envelopes, mason jars and retirement plans, also help control spending. The failure to treat pots of money as fungible (interchangeable) is what makes such accounting strategies feasible (p. 75).

### An "as if" model (p. 75)
Thaler and Shefrin did not believe there are two people in the head. A footnote suggested the planner may sit in the prefrontal cortex and the doer in the limbic system. Thaler says the planner can be read as Kahneman's System 2 and the doers as System 1. He adds that whether the model has a physiological basis does not matter for practical purposes (p. 75).

### The beta–delta model (pp. 75–77)
Laibson (1997) pioneered it. Rabin and O'Donoghue elaborated it. Its advantage is mathematical simplicity: it is the smallest possible modification of Samuelson's model that can capture the essential aspects of self-control (p. 76). Thaler still finds planner–doer the most useful way to think about self-control, but beta–delta has become the model of choice (p. 75).
- Simple version: no discounting among "later" periods, but anything "later" is worth only half of "now". A Wimbledon first-round match worth 100 this year is worth 50 next year or any later year. This is **present bias**, and it leads to time-inconsistent choices (p. 76).
- Laibson assumed **sophisticated** agents, who know their time preferences. O'Donoghue and Rabin assumed **naïve** agents, who are unaware of them (p. 76).
- Thaler shares the view of all three authors that the truth lies in between: partial naïveté. Most of us realize we have self-control problems but underestimate their severity (p. 76).
- Loewenstein's "hot–cold empathy gaps": in a cool mood we expect to eat healthy dinners. On Wednesday night the pizza and beer win. A planner may then need a rule such as "no midweek beer and pizza outings", and a way to enforce it (pp. 76–77).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ten bars, ten days, as with Harry. Suppose a doer's extra pleasure from the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th bar is 6, 4, 2, −2. He eats while the extra pleasure is positive, so he stops at 3 bars. Daily utility is 6+4+2 = 12.
- **No intervention:** days 1–3 give 3×12 = 36. Day 4 uses the last bar (6). Days 5–10 give 0 (we ignore hunger pain). Total = 42.
- **Safes, 1 bar per day:** 10×6 = 60.
- **Guilt of 5 per bar eaten:** the extra pleasures become 1, −1, −3, so he stops at 1 bar. Daily net utility is 6−5 = 1, so the total is 10×1 = 10.
- **Guilt of 3 per bar:** the extra pleasures become 3, 1, −1, so he eats 2 bars. (added step) Net daily utility is 6+4−2×3 = 4. Ten bars last 5 days, so the total is 5×4 = 20 (days 6–10 give 0).

Ranking: safes 60 > no intervention 42 > mild guilt 20 > strong guilt 10. In this toy both guilt levels score below doing nothing, but only because we ignore the pain of hunger on days 5–10. The toy is meant to show that guilt lowers the doer's pleasure, not that guilt never helps.

## Concepts
- [[time-inconsistency]] (new) — later choices contradict earlier plans; only exponential discounting guarantees consistency (p. 66)
- [[commitment-strategy]] (new) — limiting your own future options to prevent a later mistake (p. 67)
- [[planner-doer-model]] (new) — an "as if" model: a far-sighted planner and a series of selfish doers (p. 71)
- [[principal-agent-model]] (new) — a boss delegates to an agent who knows more and is costly to monitor (p. 71)
- [[hyperbolic-discounting]] (new) — the discounting pattern that produces preference reversals; also found in animals (p. 69)
- [[present-bias]] (new) — "now" is weighted far above any "later" (the beta–delta idea) (p. 76)
- [[sophisticated-vs-naive]] (new) — agents who know vs those who do not know their present bias (p. 76)
- [[hot-cold-empathy-gap]] (new) — in a cool mood we underestimate how much temptation will matter later (pp. 76–77)
- mental-accounting — separate money pots (envelopes, mason jars, retirement plans) serve as a control technique; the failure to treat money as fungible makes this feasible (p. 75)
- marginal reasoning — the doer stops when one more bite adds no pleasure (p. 72)
