# Football

## L0 — One line
NFL teams overpay for early draft picks, so Becker's idea that high stakes and competitive markets put "Econ-like" people in key jobs fails in this setting.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Becker conjectured that competition and division of labor push people who think like Econs (fully rational decision-makers) into the jobs that need it. Thaler and Massey test this on the NFL draft. Teams trade picks using a steep value "Chart". But a player's surplus (performance value minus pay) does not fall with draft order the way the Chart says. Within the first round, early picks give less surplus than later ones, so teams should trade down. Teams also discount next year's picks at an implied 136% a year. Arbitrage (profiting from a price gap) cannot correct this, and owners and coaches keep acting on "win now" and convention. The lesson: organizations are owned and run by Humans.

**Prerequisites:** efficient market hypothesis; opportunity cost; present bias; idea of a discount rate; limits to arbitrage.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Becker conjecture:** competitive labor markets put the people who can do the complex analysis into the jobs that need it. The chapter tests it with the NFL draft (pdf 274).
- **The Chart is steep and fits trades very well,** because every team uses it. It values the first pick at about five times the 33rd (pdf 277–278).
- **Surplus value = performance value minus compensation.** The surplus curve is far flatter than the Chart's value curve, and it rises through the first round (pdf 282–283).
- **Teams forecast player quality only weakly.** Of two consecutive picks at one position, the earlier player is better 52% of the time overall and 56% in round 1 (pdf 281).
- **The one-round-earlier rule for next year's picks implies a 136% annual discount rate.** Advice: trade down and "lend" picks (pdf 284).
- **Alternative explanations fail:** jersey sales, ticket sales, star appeal and superstar upside do not rescue the high price (pdf 284–285).
- **Limits to arbitrage:** a high pick cannot be sold short, so smart teams cannot push prices to value (pdf 285).
- **"Dumb principal" problem:** owners who want to win now are often at least as responsible as employees for the bad decisions. Fourth-down choices show the same pattern (pdf 288–289).
- **Conclusion:** the Becker conjecture may be less compelling higher up the managerial ladder (pdf 289–290).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Setup: the Becker conjecture (pdf 274)
Thaler wants studies of high-stakes decisions, to answer critics who say biases vanish when stakes are high. Becker's version: "division of labor" strongly weakens, if it does not remove, the effects of bounded rationality. Even if 90% of people cannot do the complex analysis, the 10% who can will end up in the jobs that require it. Thaler tests whether this holds for NFL owners, general managers and coaches. His spoiler: it doesn't (pdf 274).

### The NFL draft (pdf 275)
- Once a year, teams pick mostly college players. The worst record picks first and the champion picks last. There are seven rounds (pdf 275).
- For the initial contract (usually 4–5 years) a player can play only for the drafting team. Then he becomes a free agent (pdf 275).
- Teams may trade picks. The sample has over 400 trades, so one can infer how teams value picking earlier. Trades of this year's picks for future picks reveal time preferences (pdf 275).

### Hypothesis: early picks are overvalued (pdf 276–277)
- Example: in 1999 coach Mike Ditka said publicly he would be willing to trade all his picks to get Ricky Williams. The Saints moved from pick 12 to pick 5. They gave up all their picks that year plus their 1st- and 3rd-round picks the next year. Next year's pick turned out to be the second overall. Williams was very good but "not transformative", and Ditka was fired (pdf 276).
- Five psychological findings support the hypothesis (pdf 276–277):
  1. Overconfidence in telling players apart.
  2. Forecasts that are too extreme: scouts call too many players future superstars.
  3. The winner's curse: the winning bidder is often the one who most overvalues the object.
  4. The false consensus effect: people tend to think others share their preferences. A team in love with a player thinks other teams are too, so it jumps the line.
  5. Present bias: owners and coaches want to win now.
- Thaler therefore expects the market for picks to violate the efficient market hypothesis (pdf 277).

### Step 1: the market value of picks (pdf 277–279)
- Estimated from trades, the value curve is very steep. The first pick is worth about five times the 33rd, the first of round 2 (pdf 277).
- The trades lie very close to the fitted line (Fig. 18). The reason is that everyone uses the Chart, a table of relative pick values. Mike McCoy, an engineer and minority owner of the Cowboys, first eyeballed it from past trades, at coach Jimmy Johnson's request. It later spread across the league (pdf 278).
- Fig. 19 gives Chart values: pick 1 = 3,000, pick 2 = 2,600, pick 3 = 2,200, pick 12 = 1,200, pick 32 = 590 (pdf 279).
- McCoy said he never meant to say what values picks *should* have. Thaler and Massey ask whether the Chart's prices are "right" in the efficient-market sense (pdf 279).

### Step 2: what players cost (pdf 279–281)
- The NFL has a salary cap, an upper limit on a team's payroll. Every team has the same budget, so acquiring a star carries explicit opportunity costs. The only way to build a winner is to find players worth more than they cost (pdf 279–280).
- Rookie pay is set largely by the league. The "cap charge" is salary plus amortized signing bonus. It is also steep in draft order and fits well (Fig. 20, pdf 280).
- High picks are therefore expensive twice: in picks given up and in salary (pdf 281).
- Teams cannot ignore sunk costs. An expensive bust is pushed into the game, which hurts performance (pdf 281).

### Step 3: are early picks worth it? (pdf 281–284)
- Thought experiment: take two consecutive picks at the same position. Perfect forecasters would pick the better one 100% of the time, and no-skill teams 50% of the time. In reality the earlier player is better 52% of the time across the draft and 56% in round 1 (pdf 281). Footnote †: "better" is measured by games started, but finer measures give similar results (pdf 290).
- **Method.**
  - Follow each drafted player through his initial contract.
  - Value each player-year by the cost of an equivalent player (same position and quality) in years 6–8 of his contract. That player is paid the market rate.
  - Sum these values to get the player's **performance value** to the drafting team (pdf 281–282).
  - **Surplus value** = performance value − compensation. It is like the team's profit over the initial contract (pdf 282).
- Performance value slopes down in draft order, so teams have some ability to rate players (pdf 282).
- The surplus curve slopes upward throughout the first round: early picks are worth *less* surplus than later ones (Fig. 21, pdf 283).
- Fig. 22 puts the Chart and the surplus on one scale, with the first pick = 1. An efficient market would make the two curves identical (pdf 283–284).
- The Chart says that pick 1 trades for five early second-round picks. But each of those second-round picks yields more surplus than the first-round pick they are traded for. Thaler calls it the most blatant efficiency violation he has seen (pdf 284).

*Intuition note:* The price of a pick falls very fast with draft order, but the profit from the player does not. Teams pay for a precision in forecasting that they do not have. A 52% hit rate is close to a coin flip.

### Time preferences (pdf 284)
- Trades of this year's picks for next year's follow a rule of thumb: a pick in a given round this year gets a pick one round earlier next year. Detailed analysis confirms this.
- This rule implies teams discount the future at 136% per year, worse than a loan shark.
- Two pieces of advice follow: **trade down**, and **be a draft-pick banker** by lending picks this year for better picks next year (pdf 284). Footnote ‡: a really smart team could chain such trades round after round (pdf 291).

*Intuition note:* An implied discount rate shows how much more a team demands for waiting a year. At 136%, it demands far more than any normal borrowing rate would justify, which is what present bias looks like.

### Ruling out rival explanations (pdf 284–285)
- **Jersey sales:** teams share all jersey and official-product revenue equally (pdf 284).
- **Ticket sales:** teams have waiting lists, and no one comes to watch a bad player even if he is famous. Redoing the analysis with only offensive linemen, who are largely anonymous, gives the same picture (pdf 284–285).
- **Superstar chance:** the authors evaluated every possible two-for-one trade-down allowed by the Chart, for example pick 1 for picks 7 and 8, 4 and 12, or 2 and 50. Trading down gave a large increase in games started with no loss in all-star seasons (pdf 285).

### Why markets don't fix it (pdf 285)
This illustrates limits to arbitrage. A good team can do little except lend picks, and there is no way to sell a high pick short. So there is no arbitrage for smart teams, let alone outside investors. The best one can do is buy a bad team and improve its drafting by trading down (pdf 285).

### Case: Dan Snyder and the Redskins (pdf 285–287)
- Snyder's staff met the authors and seemed to grasp the two lessons. Yet the team moved up and traded next year's high pick for this year's lesser one. The reason given: "Mr. Snyder wanted to win now" (pdf 286).
- In 2012 the Redskins moved from pick 6 to pick 2 to get Robert Griffin III (RG3). They gave up that sixth pick, their 2013 first- and second-round picks, and their 2014 first-round pick. That 2014 pick became the second overall (pdf 286–287).
- RG3 did well in year 1, then was injured and declined. Thaler says one should not judge a trade by hindsight and that the Redskins were unlucky with injuries, but that is part of the point, which he sums up as "all your eggs in his basket" (pdf 287). Russell Wilson, taken in round 3, appeared better and less injury-prone (pdf 287).
- Footnote §: in 2014 the Rams sent out the players chosen with those picks as captains, and won 24–0 (pdf 291).
- Another team's staff split: analytic staff favored trading down, while the owner or a coach fell in love with a player. Extra picks from trading down were treated as "house money" and quickly traded for another "sure thing" (pdf 287).

### Dumb principals and fourth downs (pdf 288–289)
- Thaler says "agency problem" is often a mislabel here, because owners are at least as responsible as employees. The owner who wants to win now pushes managers to trade up. He compares the chapter-20 CEO case (pdf 288).
- Romer found teams do not "go for it" on fourth down enough. Burke extended this, and in 2013 the New York Times built a 4th Down Bot. Since Romer wrote his paper, going for it has marginally decreased. Draft strategy has not visibly changed either (pdf 288–289).
- Silver estimates bad fourth-down choices cost half a win a season, and the Times analysts two-thirds. The season has 16 games (pdf 289). Footnote ¶: Thaler thinks Silver's estimate may be conservative, because committing to go for it also changes third-down play-calling (pdf 291).
- Coaches follow convention because it will not be second-guessed. Thaler cites Keynes: following conventional wisdom keeps you from getting fired (pdf 289).

### Verdict on the Becker conjecture (pdf 289–290)
- It may hold at some level: competitive markets do channel people into suitable jobs (pdf 289).
- It may become less compelling higher up the ladder. Thaler cites the Peter Principle. Coach, chair and CEO jobs are multidimensional, and analytic skill may not be the key one (pdf 289–290).
- Salvage attempt: hire geeks. Thaler's hunch is that the more important a decision, the more managers rely on gut instinct (pdf 290).
- A necessary condition for good decisions is buy-in from the owner. The owner must also convince staff they will be rewarded for smart, unconventional chances, especially when they fail. Few teams manage this (pdf 290).

*Intuition note:* Owning a billion-dollar team does not make someone a careful probability thinker. The people who decide are Humans, so the market need not select Econs.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Chart values: pick 1 = 3,000, pick 7 = 1,500, pick 8 = 1,400 (pdf 279). The Chart makes pick 1 about equal to picks 7 + 8: 1,500 + 1,400 = 2,900 ≈ 3,000.

Suppose (our made-up numbers, in $ millions) the surplus is 1.0 for pick 1 and 1.2 for each of picks 7 and 8.
- Chart ratio, pick 7 : pick 1 = 1,500 / 3,000 = 0.5.
- Surplus ratio, pick 7 : pick 1 = 1.2 / 1.0 = 1.2.
- Trading pick 1 for picks 7 and 8 changes surplus from 1.0 to 1.2 + 1.2 = 2.4.

If the market were efficient, the two ratios would match. The mismatch is the trade-down opportunity.
