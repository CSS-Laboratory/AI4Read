# The List

## L0 — One line
Thaler's blackboard list of behaviours that conflict with rational-choice theory gained a framework when he read Kahneman and Tversky's argument that heuristics (rules of thumb) cause predictable errors, which are not random and so do not average out.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks what else people do that is inconsistent with standard rational-choice theory. He collects examples (ignoring sunk costs, which means money already spent; inconsistent valuation of time; and others) but does not know how to use them. Through the psychologist Baruch Fischhoff he finds Kahneman and Tversky's "Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." Its message is that limited brainpower leads people to use rules of thumb, which cause systematic errors. Thaler believes economists dismissed Simon's bounded rationality (people's limited ability to solve complex problems) as errors that cancel out. Kahneman and Tversky's errors do not cancel. Thaler sees each List item as a systematic bias in which a factor that theory calls irrelevant affects choices.

**Prerequisites:** expected-value and opportunity-cost thinking; the idea of an error term in a statistical model.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Thaler's List contains choices that violate rational-choice theory: ignoring sunk costs, inconsistent buying and selling prices, inconsistent valuation of time, and valuing fewer options over more (pdf 32).
- Hindsight bias (after the fact, we think we knew the outcome) makes it harder for CEOs to persuade managers to take risky projects with high expected gains, because the CEO wrongly thinks a failure should have been foreseen (pdf 33).
- Heuristics are rules of thumb that people use because time and brainpower are limited. They cause predictable errors (pdf 33–34).
- In Thaler's account, economists treated bounded rationality as an error term whose random errors cancel, so they felt it could be ignored (pdf 34).
- Kahneman and Tversky's key point is that the errors are not random, so they do not average out across people (pdf 34).
- Each List item is a systematic bias, and each features a supposedly irrelevant factor (SIF) that theory says should not matter (pdf 35).
- Much later behavioural economics has shown which SIFs are in fact highly relevant in predicting behaviour (pdf 35).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The List (pdf 31–32)
The gap between buying and selling prices (from the preceding chapter) made Thaler look for other behaviours inconsistent with rational choice. He wrote them on his office blackboard (pdf 31). The examples are as follows.
- **Tickets.** Thaler and Jeffrey get two free tickets to a basketball game in Buffalo, about a 1.5-hour drive from Rochester. A snowstorm arrives and they decide not to go. Jeffrey remarks that had they *bought* the expensive tickets, they would have braved the blizzard (pdf 31).
- **Lawn.** Stanley gets hay fever from mowing his lawn but will not pay a kid $10 to do it. He would also refuse to mow a neighbour's lawn for $20 (pdf 31).
- **Linnea.** The clerk mentions the same clock radio at $35 in a store ten minutes away, against $45 here, and Thaler asks whether she drives there to save $10. On another trip, she is offered the same ten-minute drive to save $10 on a $495 television ($485 elsewhere). Thaler says the answer is "likely different" (pdf 31).
- **Lee's sweater.** Lee judged a cashmere sweater too big an indulgence to buy. He is delighted when his wife gives it to him, though the couple pool all their money (pdf 32).
- **Cashews.** Thaler and his guests eat half a bowl of cashews in five minutes before dinner. Thaler hides the bowl and everyone is happy (pdf 32).

Thaler's diagnosis of each case (pdf 32):
- Jeffrey ignores the dictum "ignore sunk costs" (money already spent). The ticket price should not affect the decision to go.
- Stanley violates the precept that buying and selling prices should be about the same.
- Linnea does not value her time consistently if she spends ten minutes to save $10 on a small purchase but not on a large one.
- Lee feels better about the same-priced sweater when his wife made the decision.
- Removing the cashews removes an option, and to Econs (a term not defined on these pages; our gloss: the rational agents of theory) more choices are always preferred to fewer.

*Intuition note:* In each case something that should not matter (what was paid, whether one is buying or selling, who chose, the size of the purchase, an extra option) changes what the person does.

### Fischhoff and hindsight bias (pdf 32–33)
Thaler did not know what to do with the List. "Dumb stuff people do" was not an acceptable paper title (pdf 32). In summer 1976 he and Sherwin attended a conference near Monterey on the value of a life. There he met the psychologists Baruch Fischhoff and Paul Slovic (pdf 32). While driving Fischhoff to the airport, he learned that Fischhoff had done a PhD at the Hebrew University, working with Kahneman and Tversky. Fischhoff's thesis was on **hindsight bias**: after the fact, we think we always knew the outcome was likely (pdf 32). Thaler's example is that many people thought they had seen Obama's defeat of Clinton coming. They had not, and were misremembering (pdf 33).

Thaler thinks the bias matters for management. A CEO wants managers to take risky projects with high enough expected gains. Managers fear blame if a project fails, whether or not the decision was good ex ante. Hindsight bias worsens this because the CEO wrongly thinks the cause of the failure should have been foreseen. Thaler adds that we recognise this bias in others but not in ourselves (pdf 33).

### Heuristics and biases (pdf 33–34)
Fischhoff suggested reading his advisors' work. Thaler read their *Science* paper, "Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," in thirty minutes and says it changed his life (pdf 33).
- **Thesis:** humans have limited time and brainpower, so they use heuristics (rules of thumb) to make judgments (pdf 33).
- **Availability example:** to guess how common something is, we ask how easily we can think of instances. This works well in one's own community. It fails when the number of instances is not highly correlated with ease of recall. For example, people from most countries would likely say Dhruv is not a common name, but it is very common in India, a country with many people, so it is fairly common globally (pdf 33–34).
- **Big idea:** using heuristics causes people to make *predictable errors*. This gave Thaler a framework for his "helter-skelter" ideas (pdf 34).

### Simon and why economists ignored bounded rationality (pdf 34)
Herbert Simon, a polymath at Carnegie Mellon, wrote about "bounded rationality" before Kahneman and Tversky. By it he meant that people lack the cognitive ability to solve complex problems. Thaler thinks Simon had little impact on economics despite his Nobel Prize. Thaler believes many economists brushed bounded rationality aside as "true but unimportant."

Their reasoning, as Thaler reports it, was this. Models are imprecise anyway, and the statistical models handle imprecision with an **error term**. In the height example, a child's adult height is predicted from both parents' heights. The prediction is decent but imperfect, and the error term captures the gap. If errors are random (too high or too low equally often), they cancel out. Economists used this to justify ignoring bounded rationality and returning to the fully rational model (pdf 34).

**Kahneman and Tversky's red flag (Thaler's account):** the errors are not random. Example: most people guess that US gun deaths from homicide exceed those from suicide, but suicide gun deaths are almost twice as many. Even across many people, such errors do not average out to zero (pdf 34).

*Intuition note:* Random mistakes are like noise that cancels in a large sample. Mistakes that all lean the same way remain in the average and do not cancel.

### Systematic bias and SIFs (pdf 35)
Thaler concludes that each List item is an example of a systematic bias. Each also has a common structure. Theory says some key factor (the cashews, the ticket price) should not influence decisions. He calls these **supposedly irrelevant factors (SIFs)**. Much later behavioural work shows which SIFs matter for predicting behaviour, often using biases suggested by the 1974 paper. The resulting list now far exceeds the blackboard list. Thaler then read everything Kahneman and Tversky had written together (pdf 35).

Footnote on name order (pdf 35): Amos and Danny alternated whose name came first to signal equal partnership. Economics defaults to alphabetical order, while in psychology the order usually indicates relative contribution.

Other footnotes (pdf 35): the economics prize is not one of the original Nobel Prizes in Alfred Nobel's will, though it is awarded alongside them; and just having a gun in the house increases the risk that a member of the household will commit suicide.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Five people guess a true value of 100.
- **Random errors:** guesses 90, 110, 95, 105, 100 give errors −10, +10, −5, +5, 0. Sum = 0, so mean error = 0/5 = 0. The average guess is 500/5 = 100, which is right even though no individual guess is exact.
- **Systematic bias:** a shared heuristic leads everyone to guess low: 65, 75, 70, 70, 70. Errors are −35, −25, −30, −30, −30. Sum = −150, so mean error = −150/5 = −30. The average guess is 350/5 = 70.

More people would not fix the second case. The bias remains in the average, which is why predictable errors cannot be dismissed with an error term.

## Concepts
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — limited cognitive ability and time lead to rules of thumb. Simon used the term "bounded rationality," and Kahneman and Tversky link heuristics to predictable errors (pdf 33–34).
- [[systematic-bias]] (new) — error that is predictable in direction and does not average out across people (pdf 34–35).
- [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] (new) — letting money already spent affect a choice, against the dictum to ignore sunk costs (pdf 32).
- [[hindsight-bias]] (new) — after an outcome is known, believing one always thought it likely (pdf 32–33).
- [[availability-heuristic]] (new) — judging frequency by how easily instances come to mind (pdf 33–34).
- [[supposedly-irrelevant-factor]] (new) — a factor that theory says should not affect choice but does (SIF) (pdf 35).
- [[marginal reasoning]] — related only loosely: Linnea's inconsistent valuation of ten minutes against $10 is, in Thaler's words, "not valuing time consistently" (pdf 32).

## Canonical instances
- [[buffalo-tickets-example]] — Jeffrey says they would have braved the blizzard had they paid for the tickets, but with free tickets they stayed home (sunk cost) (pdf 31–32).
- [[linnea-clock-radio-tv]] — same $10 saving and same ten-minute drive, probably different decisions (pdf 31–32).
- [[cashew-bowl-example]] — removing the bowl (an option) leaves everyone happy, at odds with the statement that to Econs "more choices are always preferred" (pdf 32).
- [[dhruv-name-example]] — availability heuristic fails when examples are not correlated with true frequency (pdf 33–34).
- [[gun-deaths-homicide-vs-suicide]] — a predictable, non-random error in judgment (pdf 34).
