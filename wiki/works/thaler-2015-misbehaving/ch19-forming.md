# Forming a Team

## L0 — One line
Thaler's attempt to get psychologists and economists working together failed, but a small, flexible fund for training young economists (the Russell Sage summer camps) was, by Thaler's account, a huge success: he says it is largely the research of its graduates that turned behavioral economics into a vibrant part of mainstream economics.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Thaler asks how to grow a new field when no manual exists. Together with funder Eric Wanner, he first tried interdisciplinary meetings of psychologists and economists. Those meetings were not productive, and behavioral economics became mainly a field of economists who read psychology. The second effort worked: in 1992 the Russell Sage Foundation created the Behavioral Economics Roundtable, which ran two-week summer schools for graduate students from 1994 on. About 300 graduates exist by 2014, and some now teach the camps. Thaler says it is largely this cohort's research that has moved the field from a "quirky cult activity" into mainstream economics.

**Prerequisites:** The earlier chapters' contrast between "Econs" (fully rational agents) and "Humans" (real people); the Ultimatum and Dictator Games; no math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A new field needs a team, and fields usually grow without coordination: one paper, then imitators, conferences, and a journal. This is slow (p. 133).
- By the late 1980s only a handful of people thought of themselves as behavioral economists: Thaler, Loewenstein, Shiller and Camerer (p. 133).
- The first plan was joint meetings of psychologists and economists. They attracted stars but were not very productive (pp. 135–136).
- Thaler thinks the real problem was a general one: interdisciplinary meetings with high-level agendas tend to disappoint, because academics want to see actual results, and results presented in one field's style overwhelm or bore the other field (p. 136).
- The field turned out to be primarily one where economists read psychologists' work and then do research independently, though a handful of psychologists have collaborated successfully with economists. Thaler offers several possible reasons for the psychologists' lack of engagement (pp. 136–137).
- The Roundtable (1992, $100,000 a year to start) chose to spend its money on young scholars through summer camps (pp. 137–138).
- The camps are Thaler's evidence of success. The first year already included future leaders, and some alumni now teach the camps (pp. 139–140).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: why a team (pp. 133–134).** The "Anomalies" columns (earlier chapters) showed that many facts did not fit the traditional models. Thaler says that creating a new field "would require a team" and that no field manual existed (p. 133). Fields normally grow without coordination, e.g. game theory in the 1940s. A paper opens a topic, others follow, then conferences and a dedicated journal appear (p. 133). In the late 1980s, besides Thaler, only three people thought of themselves as behavioral economists: George Loewenstein, Robert Shiller and Colin Camerer (p. 133).

Camerer (met on the job market, nearly done with a Chicago PhD, not yet 21) has made many contributions, and two stand out (pp. 133–134).
- He more or less invented *behavioral game theory*: how people actually play games, versus standard game theory, which asks how Econs would play if they knew all others were Econs (p. 134).
- More recently he has been at the forefront of *neuro-economics*, which uses tools such as brain imaging (p. 134).

Thaler judged that the field would "flounder" without a critical mass of researchers with varied skills (p. 134).

*Intuition note:* Thaler saw that a few brilliant individuals were not enough. A field needs enough people with different skills, or it will flounder.

**Eric Wanner and the first plan (pp. 134–136).**
- Wanner was a program officer at the Sloan Foundation. He was a psychologist by training and, in Thaler's view, an economist by predilection (p. 134).
- Kahneman, who calls himself a pessimist, says he told Wanner he "could not see any way to honestly spend much money" on this (p. 134). Both Kahneman and Tversky sent Wanner to Thaler.
- Sloan then funded Thaler's year in Vancouver with Kahneman (p. 134).
- Wanner moved to the Russell Sage Foundation as president. Its mission is social policy (poverty, immigration). The board let him bring his behavioral agenda (pp. 134–135).
- First idea: occasional meetings of psychologists and economists, "hope that sparks would fly" (p. 135). Three groups were invited: distinguished psychologists, open-minded senior economists, and the few hard-core researchers (p. 135).
- The psychologists included Kahneman, Tversky, Mischel, Festinger and Schachter. The senior economists included Akerlof, Baumol, Schelling and Zeckhauser. The hard-core group was Camerer, Loewenstein, Shiller and Thaler. Shleifer came on Summers's suggestion (p. 135).
- The couple of meetings "did not turn out to be very productive" (p. 135). **Baumol** urged the group to move beyond "anomaly-mining" to a constructive agenda, but offered no suggestion for what that agenda should be (p. 135).

**Why the meetings failed, and what followed (pp. 136–137).** The following reasons are Thaler's own judgments.
- *Thaler's diagnosis* (introduced with "I think"): academics want to see actual results, not abstractions. Results presented in one field's manner overwhelm or bore the other field (p. 136). A footnote qualifies this: neuroscience has worked across fields, but around specific tools such as brain scans; Thaler does not want to say all such meetings are a waste, only that in his experience they have been disappointing (marker p. 136; footnote text p. 140).
- The stars' enthusiasm was "encouraging and misleading" (p. 136). It reinforced the belief that the field must be truly interdisciplinary (p. 136).
- *Outcome:* "That turned out to be a poor forecast" (p. 136). Behavioral economics is primarily a field where economists read psychologists and then do their own research (p. 136). Prelec and Shafir are notable exceptions (p. 136). Schachter tried stock-market research, grew frustrated with referees at mainstream journals, and abandoned it (p. 136).
- *Possible reasons psychologists did not engage* (p. 136–137):
  1. Few psychologists are attached to rational choice, so studying departures from it is not interesting. A typical reaction: "Of course people pay attention to sunk costs!"
  2. The psychology that behavioral economists use is not cutting-edge to psychologists.
  3. Applied problems have traditionally been low status in psychology (Cialdini is an exception).
- Behavioral economists have also not generated much new psychology. Most advances modify economic tools to fit Humans as well as Econs. Of the leaders, only Loewenstein has created much new psychology (p. 137).
- A footnote adds that judgment-and-decision-making, started by Kahneman and Tversky, still thrives (SJDM meetings draw over 500 scholars). Some marketing scholars also do behavioral work. But a typical behavioral-economics meeting has no psychologists (p. 141, n. †).

*Intuition note:* The plan did not work as hoped. Economists took ideas from psychology but mostly did their own research. Thaler offers several possible reasons (pp. 136–137) but does not name a single cause.

**The Behavioral Economics Roundtable (pp. 137–138).**
- Wanner stayed committed even if the field would consist almost entirely of economists. Russell Sage was too small to be the main funder, so he proposed a limited, "highly unusual" plan, which has been "a huge success" (p. 137).
- In 1992 the foundation formed the Roundtable with a modest budget, tasked with fostering the field's growth (p. 137).
- Initial members: Akerlof, Blinder, Camerer, Elster, Kahneman, Loewenstein, Schelling, Shiller, Tversky, Thaler. They could spend the money almost any way they wanted, "within reason" (p. 137).
- The budget began at $100,000 per year. Members chose to bring in young scholars through two-week summer programs for graduate students. No university then taught a graduate course in behavioral economics (p. 138).
- The official name was Russell Sage Foundation Summer Institutes in Behavioral Economics, called the "summer camps" (p. 138).

**The first camp, Berkeley, 1994 (pp. 138–139).**
- Organizers: Camerer, Kahneman, Thaler. Guests included Arrow, Ross and Plott (p. 138).
- Two recent PhDs were invited: Ernst Fehr and Matthew Rabin, each independently committed to behavioral careers (p. 138).
- **Fehr** (fairness; University of Zürich; also neuro-economics). His first notable paper was experimental: laboratory "firms" paying above the minimum wage got higher effort from "workers" (p. 138). This supports Akerlof's idea that employment contracts can partly be a gift exchange. If the employer treats the worker well, the gift is reciprocated with higher effort and lower turnover, which can make above-market wages profitable (p. 138).
- **Rabin**'s first behavioral paper was theoretical. Thaler calls it, at that time, the most important theory paper in behavioral economics since "Prospect Theory" (p. 138).
  - It was the first serious attempt to explain the apparent contradiction between the Dictator Game (people give money to an anonymous stranger) and the Ultimatum Game (people are mean to those who treat them unfairly) (pp. 138–139).
  - Rabin's answer hinges on reciprocity: we are nice to people who treat us nicely and mean to those who treat us badly (p. 139). The finding that people are "conditional cooperators" is consistent with the model (p. 139).
- More than 100 students applied, and thirty were chosen, "packed with the future stars of the field" (p. 139).

**Aftermath (pp. 139–140).**
- The camps continue in alternate years. Camerer and Loewenstein organized them for a while, and Laibson and Rabin have run the last several (p. 139).
- Laibson was a camper at the first one. Many current faculty are alumni, so the group is "becoming self-generating" (p. 139).
- Thaler makes no claim to have turned campers into stars. Laibson had already graduated from MIT and taken a Harvard job. The camps' primary accomplishment, he says, was to make it more likely that top young students seriously consider the field and to give them a network (pp. 139–140).
- First-year campers included Mullainathan (his interest in behavioral economics tipped him from computer science to economics; he founded the think tank ideas42), Odean (individual investor behavior), Chip Heath, Babcock and Jolls (p. 140).
- By the tenth camp (2014) there were about 300 graduates, many at top universities. It is largely their research, Thaler says, that has turned the field "from a quirky cult activity to a vibrant part of mainstream economics" (p. 140). Wanner is "founding funder" (p. 140).

*Intuition note:* The money did not buy a finished field. It bought a pipeline of well-trained young people who knew each other. Thaler's claim (p. 140) is that their later research made the field mainstream.

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Assume each camp takes 30 students, and no student attends twice. Thaler's "about 300 graduates" after ten camps (p. 140) is consistent with this assumption.
- After 1 camp: 1 × 30 = 30 graduates.
- After 10 camps: 10 × 30 = 300 graduates.
- Compare with the late 1980s core of 4 (Thaler plus three others, p. 133): 300 / 4 = 75 times larger.
- Now suppose, purely as an illustration, that each camp's faculty includes 2 former campers. Then over 10 camps, 10 × 2 = 20 faculty slots go to alumni. The pipeline has begun to staff itself, as Thaler describes for Laibson (p. 139).

## Concepts
- [[behavioral-game-theory]] (new) — study of how people actually play games, rather than how Econs would (p. 134)
- [[econs-vs-humans]] (new) — Thaler contrasts Econs (the agents of traditional models, who in standard game theory know that all others are Econs, p. 134) with Humans (real people); fuller definitions come from earlier chapters (p. 133)
- [[anomaly-mining]] (new) — Baumol's term for collecting departures from standard theory; he thought it had served its purpose (p. 135)
- [[reciprocity]] (new) — in Rabin's account, being nice to those who are nice to us and mean to those who are not (p. 139)
- [[gift-exchange-in-employment]] (new) — Akerlof's idea that good treatment by the employer is repaid with effort and lower turnover (p. 138)
- [[incomplete-contracts]] — related concept, not named in this unit (canon link only); the text says only that employment contracts could be viewed partially as a gift exchange (p. 138)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Summer camps seeding a field ↔ network growth: a small seeded core of connected members becomes self-sustaining as alumni become faculty.
- [metaphor] Fields growing from one paper to conferences to a journal ↔ narrative contagion (SIR): ideas spread person to person (the text says only that others "read about it" and join).

## Questions this raises
- Thaler says psychologists failed to engage for several possible reasons. Which of them, if any, can be tested?
- Is it only chance that cohorts built around shared problems and tools (neuroscience, p. 140 n.) worked, while meetings built around broad agendas did not?
- Thaler credits camp graduates' research for mainstream acceptance. What else might have contributed, and how would one tell?
- If economists mainly modify tools rather than create new psychology (p. 137), what limits does that put on the field?
