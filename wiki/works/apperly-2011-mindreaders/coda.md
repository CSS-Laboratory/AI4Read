# Coda

## L0 — One line
Apperly argues that, viewed across all the literatures on mindreading, a two-systems account (a high-level plus a low-level process, making complementary trade-offs between flexibility and efficiency) is worth its loss of parsimony (simplicity), and that he thinks there is every reason to expect it to hold beyond the epistemic states (perceptions, knowledge, beliefs) he studied.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The Coda looks back at the book's broad but narrow scope. Evidence on mindreading now comes from animals, infants, children, atypical development and adults, and it is richer but also more confusing. Apperly thinks the field has, if anything, underestimated how hard it is to infer mental states quickly. He defends his two-systems view against a simpler one-system view: separate literatures may seem to favour one system, but combined they do not. He admits that he said little about desires, intentions and emotions. He thinks this omission does not change the conclusion, because the flexibility–efficiency problem applies to those states too. He ends with the "tower without floors" image: he hopes to have laid a few floorboards.

**Prerequisites:** The book's idea of mindreading (attributing mental states such as beliefs); the contrast between flexible and efficient cognition; the idea of parsimony (preferring simpler explanations).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Mindreading research is broad and cross-disciplinary, but its evidence is now richer and more confusing, and debate is bitter (p. 182).
- Apperly thinks the field has underestimated the problems: little attention has gone to how mindreaders make abductive inferences (inferences to a best explanation) about mental states, and to how they do so fast enough for real-time social life (p. 182).
- Converging evidence helps. Infant mindreading looks less anomalous (less out of place) when set beside efficient mindreading in animals and adults (p. 182).
- Parsimony versus power: Apperly's contention is that the loss of parsimony in a two-systems account is more than repaid by explanatory power across literatures (p. 183).
- Other two-system accounts exist, but many separate mindreading from other social cognition. Apperly's point is that the flexibility–efficiency tension remains even within epistemic mindreading (p. 183).
- The account is admittedly incomplete: it says little about desires and intentions, and does not cover emotions and traits in detail (pp. 183–184).
- He argues the omission is not misleading, for two reasons: the same pattern of success and failure appears for those states, and the cognitive problems are analogous (p. 184).
- He expects the two-systems argument to hold for mindreading in general, and he leaves open how components are integrated (p. 184).

## L3 — Walkthrough

The Coda has no numbered sections. I follow its order of paragraphs.

### Scope and converging evidence (p. 182)
- Apperly calls the book's scope "at once unusually broad and unusually narrow" (p. 182). The breadth comes from the research itself. Researchers use non-human animals and children. They draw on atypical and typical development. They work with philosophers and linguists. Recent work adds infants, and adults studied with cognitive neuroscience and cognitive psychology methods (p. 182).
- Result: richer evidence, but "more complicated and confusing". The debates concern who can mindread, how they do it and how we can tell (p. 182).
- Author's view: "if anything" the scale of the problems has been underestimated. Little attention has gone to how mindreaders make *abductive inferences* about mental states (our gloss, not in the source: inference to a best explanation). Even less has gone to how they do this in time for fast-moving social interaction (p. 182).
- These problems loom as large for mindreading as for any cognitive ability. An adequate account must therefore explain how they are solved (p. 182).
- He hopes to have convinced readers that progress can be made on these questions by looking at the bigger picture from the many contributing fields, and by drawing on lessons from other topic areas (p. 182).
- Example of converging evidence. Infant mindreading might seem anomalous. He cites extensive research showing that language and executive function, which infants lack, are necessary for mindreading to develop in older children. But it looks less anomalous beside evidence of cognitively efficient mindreading in non-human animals and adults (p. 182).

**Intuition note.** A result that looks odd inside one research area may fit a pattern when you look at several areas. Apperly uses this to say we should judge theories against all the evidence together.

### Two systems versus one (p. 183)
- The "two-systems" view: human mindreading combines high-level and low-level processes. They make complementary trade-offs between flexibility and efficiency (p. 183).
- Parsimony (preferring simpler theories) should make us ask whether a one-system account would be better (p. 183).
- Apperly's reply:
  - Looking at each literature separately, a one-system account might seem to cover enough data to be preferable (p. 183).
  - Combined, the stack of "anomalous" findings becomes uncomfortably large (p. 183).
  - A one-system account built for infants or animals would be very different from one built for human adults. That makes one-system approaches look less parsimonious after all (p. 183).
  - **Contention:** the loss of parsimony is "more than compensated" by the gain in explanatory power across the literatures (p. 183).
  - He adds that two-system proposals are very common elsewhere in psychology, which he finds reassuring (p. 183).

**Intuition note.** A theory that looks simpler may lose that advantage if it would need very different versions for different groups (infants, animals, adults). Apperly's point is that one-system accounts then look less parsimonious after all.

### How this differs from other two-system accounts (p. 183)
- Other authors have proposed two-system accounts of social cognition (the text points to Apperly & Butterfill, 2009, for discussion) (p. 183).
- Many of them (chapter 7) separate mindreading proper from other social cognition, such as face or eye-gaze perception, empathy, or behaviour prediction without mindreading. Apperly accepts that social cognition is much more than ascribing mental states (p. 183).
- His claim: even when restricted to mindreading as such, and to epistemic mental states (perceptions, knowledge, beliefs), the tension between flexibility and efficiency remains. So the case for two systems remains. The narrow focus is therefore "critical" to his account (p. 183).

### The limits of the narrow focus (pp. 183–184)
- Apperly says the focus may be too narrow for a general account. He has hardly mentioned desires and intentions (p. 183).
- Some researchers distinguish "cognitive" states (his focus) from "affective" states such as emotions (the text cites Shamay-Tsoory & Aharon-Peretz, 2007). He says there is evidence that different states are understood at different times by infants and children, that they are understood to varying degrees by animals, and that their cognitive and neural bases "may be only partially similar" to those of epistemic states (pp. 183–184).
- Emotions, intentions, desires, traits and other person information are essential for a full account. In this sense his account is "significantly incomplete" (p. 184).
- Key question: does the simplification risk wrong conclusions about the cognitive basis of mindreading? His answer ("I think"): "a straightforward 'no'" (p. 184). Two reasons:
  1. The contradictory pattern of success and failure in children, infants and animals holds "just as surely" for desires, intentions and emotions. So the focus has not overestimated the complexity to be explained (p. 184).
  2. Theoretically, the cognitive problems of mindreading those other states are directly analogous. There are competing demands to be highly flexible and also fast and efficient. There is no more reason to expect a "cognitive miracle" for the other states than for epistemic ones (p. 184).
- Low-level mechanisms for intentions or emotions might differ from those for epistemic states. Mirror neurons and the "mirror system" are candidate efficient mechanisms. Despite some ambitious claims, they cannot explain the full richness of everyday thinking about these states (p. 184; he cites Decety & Grezes, 2006; Gallese & Goldman, 1998).
- Conclusion: he thinks there is "every reason to suppose" that the two-system argument holds for mindreading in general. Future work should study how the components are integrated into an ability that meets the demands of ongoing social activity and approximates the richness of the normative account of mindreading (p. 184).

**Intuition note.** Part of the argument rests on analogy (the same flexible-and-fast demands apply to the other mental states) and part on a claim about the evidence (the same pattern of success and failure). On that basis a two-system solution is expected to apply to all of them. The author presents this as a reasoned expectation ("I think").

### Closing image (p. 185)
- The preface compared the mindreading literatures to a tower with walls but no floors. Apperly still thinks this is accurate. Research will be more sound once its cognitive basis is explained. He hopes to have laid "a few floorboards" (p. 185).

## Toy example
*Ours, with invented numbers; not from the book.* Suppose six findings need explaining:
- F1, F2: fast, efficient mindreading in infants.
- F3: the same in an animal.
- F4: fast, efficient mindreading in adults.
- F5, F6: flexible, language-dependent mindreading in adults.

Compare three accounts:
- **One efficient system:** explains F1–F4 = 4 of 6. It leaves F5 and F6 as anomalies.
- **One flexible system:** explains F5 and F6 = 2 of 6. It leaves F1–F4 as anomalies.
- **Two systems:** efficient system covers F1–F4 (4), flexible system covers F5–F6 (2), total 6 of 6, using 2 mechanisms.

Each one-system account is simpler (1 mechanism) but leaves anomalies (2 and 4). To cover all six, the one-system version would need extra patches. Suppose each patch counts as one mechanism. The patched efficient system then needs 1 + 2 = 3 and the patched flexible system needs 1 + 4 = 5, both more than 2. This illustrates Apperly's point (with our invented numbers): after covering all the evidence, the "simpler" account may no longer be simpler.
