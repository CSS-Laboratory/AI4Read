# Evidence from children

## L0 — One line
Children's mindreading improves between ages 2 and 7 and is strongly associated with executive function and language and appears to be linked to social experience, but the child evidence does not clearly show it to be a separate faculty.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What do studies of children show about the cognitive basis of mindreading? Children usually pass standard false belief tasks around ages 3–5. They show some mindreading earlier, but passing does not guarantee success on related problems. False belief performance is linked to executive function, to language and social experience, and to non-mental tasks such as false signs and counterfactuals. Autism research does not clearly support strong specialization. Deafness research gives clearer evidence that language matters. The author concludes that false belief is not a single "true test" of mindreading. He also concludes that the child data leave important details uncertain, such as the executive function link.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a control group; correlation and "partialling out" a third variable; basic grammar (main clause vs embedded clause). Terms used above: a false belief task asks what someone will think or do when their belief is wrong; executive function = domain-general control of thought and action (inhibition, working memory, planning); a false sign is a sign that has gone out of date; a counterfactual question asks what would have been the case otherwise.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Mindreading studies need a **perspective difference**: the target must see, know or believe something different from the subject. Otherwise success could come from the subject's own mind (p. 11–12).
- Standard false belief tasks (unexpected transfer, unexpected contents) are passed mostly between 3 and 5 years. Younger children's errors are systematically egocentric, and task tweaks barely lower the age (p. 12–13).
- Mindreading does not begin or end at false belief. Younger children handle seeing, knowing and diverse beliefs; passing does not guarantee success on second-order (belief-about-belief), desire–belief, emotion and "Oedipus" (description-dependent) problems (p. 14–17).
- The concepts question has no easy answer, so treating false belief as the "one true test" seems arbitrary (p. 18).
- False belief performance correlates with false signs and counterfactuals, but not (once controlled) with "false" photographs. Its representational demands are neither unique to mindreading nor fully shared with all representation tasks (p. 19–22).
- Executive function (domain-general control of thought and action; especially conflict inhibition) correlates with false belief. Five accounts of why are discussed, and the child data leave the details uncertain (p. 22–26).
- Language and social experience appear to be causally involved, but which aspects matter is unclear. Deaf children whose parents are not fluent signers tend to show language and mindreading delays (p. 26–30, 32).
- Autism research shows mindreading can be impaired out of proportion to general intelligence. It does not show impairment independent of language or executive function, nor strong domain-specificity (p. 30–32).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 11)
The modern literature began with a question about chimpanzees (Premack & Woodruff, 1978). Most of the work since then is on human development, and many central themes and paradigms come from it (p. 11). The chapter covers typically developing children aged 2+, then atypical development. These studies set the benchmarks for theories, paradigms and (until recently) age of acquisition (p. 11). Infant evidence comes in chapter 3, and it is easier to appreciate against the older-child background (p. 11).

### Perspective differences as a key empirical device (p. 11–12)
Commentators on Premack and Woodruff (Bennett, Dennett, Pylyshyn) warned of a problem. We cannot be sure a subject is tracking another's mental states if the subject's own states coincide with the other's (p. 11). Apperly's example: suggesting the pub to someone already wanting beer would be weak evidence of mindreading skill. It is a better test if the person has a hangover (p. 11–12). So studies contrive a situation where the target sees, knows, wants, intends or believes something different from the subject (p. 12).

> **Intuition note.** If you and the target think the same thing, "correct" answers cannot show that you are tracking them. A difference between your view and theirs makes success a better sign that you are modelling *them*.

### Children's mindreading

**False belief tasks (p. 12–14).**
- Gopnik, Slaughter & Meltzoff (1994) diagnosed the field with "neurotic task fixation" (p. 12). False belief tasks are still the most used and most richly interpreted (p. 12).
- Almost all are variants of two procedures with a perspective difference (Figure 2.1; p. 12–13).
  - *Unexpected transfer* (Wimmer & Perner, 1983): Sally sees an object put in the round box and leaves. Andrew moves it to the square box. The child predicts where Sally will look or where she thinks it is, and also answers memory-check questions (p. 12).
  - *Unexpected content* (Hogrefe, Wimmer & Perner, 1986): a Smarties tube turns out to contain paperclips. The child judges what another child, who has not looked inside, will think is in it (p. 12).
- Wimmer and Perner found many children wrong until about 5 years. A meta-analysis of 178 studies (Wellman, Cross & Watson, 2001) suggests the chance of a correct answer changes more rapidly between 3 and 4 than between 4 and 5 (p. 12).
- Errors are not random. Children are systematically egocentric: they give the answer that is right from their own viewpoint. They say Sally will look in the true location, and that the new child will think there are paperclips (p. 12–13).
- The meta-analysis found that most methodological variants had little effect. Even reliable ones (e.g. stressing what the character would think or do *first of all*) never lowered the passing age by more than a few months (p. 13).
- Hence there is general agreement that 3-year-olds' difficulty is real. There is a general presumption that children who *pass* have the concept of belief (p. 13–14).

**Mindreading before success on false belief tasks (p. 14–15).**
- *Level-1 visual perspective-taking* is knowing that people with different lines of sight may see different things. Many 2-year-olds and most older children succeed (Flavell and colleagues; Figure 2.2a; p. 14).
- *Level-2 perspective-taking* is knowing that people may see the very same thing in different ways. Many 3-year-olds struggle with this (Figure 2.2b; p. 14–15).
- In their 4th year, children predict that people with different beliefs will act differently, provided the child does not know which belief is true. Example: John thinks the puppy is in the shed, Jane in the garage, and nobody knows who is right (Bartsch & Wellman, 1989; p. 15).
- In the 4th year they also judge that someone who "has not seen" what is in a box will not "know" (p. 15).
- Predicting actions from diverse beliefs seems somewhat easier than predicting ignorance from lack of perceptual access. Both are easier than false belief tasks (Wellman & Liu, 2004; p. 15).
- Conclusion: well before passing false belief, 2- and 3-year-olds (in some cases even younger infants) show some mindreading about seeing, knowing and believing (p. 15).

**Mindreading after success on false belief tasks (p. 15–17).** Passing a false belief task is no guarantee of success on related problems (p. 15).
- *Second-order false belief* (Figure 2.3): Sally peeps through a window while Andrew moves the object. Children who judge Sally's false belief are much less good at judging that Andrew falsely believes Sally has a false belief (Perner & Wimmer, 1985; p. 15–16).
  - Possibility 1: beliefs about beliefs need a later-developing conceptual understanding (p. 15).
  - Possibility 2: the concepts are essentially the same. The extra difficulty is memory and executive load from embedding and a more complex story (p. 15).
  - Simplified tasks let much younger children pass, which is consistent with the second view. These tasks are still harder than standard ones, which suggests there may be an additional conceptual component (p. 15).
- *Belief with desire.* If the object is undesirable and Sally must still open a box, children find it significantly harder to predict her choice than when she wants the object (p. 15–16).
- *Emotion.* Many 4-year-olds correctly predict the naive observer's belief that there are Smarties. They still judge he will be sad on seeing the box, and fail to predict his surprise at the paperclips (p. 16).
- *Oedipus problems.* Beliefs and knowledge are held under particular descriptions and not others (Frege; Quine; p. 16).
  - Oedipus did not have a false belief. He held his knowledge of Jocasta under "Jocasta" and "Queen of Thebes", but not under "my mother" (p. 16).
  - In Apperly's own work Heinz the puppet sees a die/eraser but does not feel it. So he knows it is a die, but not that it is an eraser (p. 17).
  - Five- and six-year-olds who passed false belief tasks and the comprehension checks still said Heinz *knew* there was an eraser in the box (p. 17).
  - They were as likely to predict that Heinz, who needed an eraser, would fetch the die/eraser as a known eraser (p. 17).
  - In studies with stories or real objects, with verbal or behavioural measures, and with false belief and dual-identity versions closely matched (Apperly & Robinson, 2003), children passing false belief do not necessarily pass Oedipus problems (p. 17).
  - Everyday example: Mr Jones is known to all as the teacher, but only some know him as Jenny's dad. A child who knows both must say "Mr Jones" to be understood by classmates (p. 17).
- Some authors say Oedipus problems are hard because they are second-order. The author rejects this, giving two objections (note 3, p. 33–34). First, difficulties extend to the extension of labels for objects with alternative identities (Apperly & Robinson, 2002). Second, on a normative account, understanding any representation already entails understanding description-relativity (note 3, p. 33–34).
- Conclusion: it is frequently claimed that 4-year-olds are at least qualitatively adult-like in mindreading. The author says the situation is "much less clear". They struggle with beliefs about beliefs, belief–desire interaction, emotional consequences, and description-relative representation (p. 17).
- Going forward, he treats conclusions from false belief tasks as generalizing fairly well to other mindreading abilities (p. 17).

> **Intuition note.** The evidence suggests mindreading is not one skill: different abilities show up at different ages. "Passing false belief" marks one point on a long road, not the finish line.

### Does mindreading depend on a specific set of concepts? (p. 18)
- Unless one is a behaviourist the short answer is "yes". Researchers often mean something stronger: do children understand perception, knowledge and belief *as such*? (p. 18)
- It is unclear when to credit a child with "knowledge". Candidates are sensitivity to mum's experience at 14 months, correct verbal judgements about lack of knowledge at 3 years, or Oedipus problems at 6 (p. 18).
- Or one might assume on theoretical grounds that the abilities would not develop without the concepts already in place (Fodor; Leslie) (p. 18).
- Treating false belief as the "one true test" seems "wholly arbitrary" given the diverse abilities (p. 18).
- He does not deny genuine, conceptual-flavoured change. Children get better at focusing on others' mental states, remember more of what others think and know, and make more sophisticated judgements (p. 18).

### Does mindreading pose unusual representational problems? (p. 18–19)
- It seems necessary for mindreading to keep some kind of log of others' perceptions, knowledge and beliefs, partitioned from one's own (p. 18).
- That log alone misses something. Sally's belief is *about* the same object and boxes as in our own picture of the world. This is what makes her belief out of step with what we know, and thereby false (p. 18–19).
- One view holds that these problems are unique, so nature supplied specialized innate architecture (Leslie, 1987, 1994a). Others note similar problems for different times, places, hypothetical states, signs and symbols (p. 19).
- Test: do children succeed on mindreading tasks at the same time as on non-mindreading tasks with similar representational demands? (p. 19). The debate returns in chapter 6 (p. 19).

**False beliefs, false signs and "false" photographs (p. 19–21).**
- Early comparisons used photographs (Figure 2.4a). Perner and Leekam (2008) summarize 12 studies (1990–2006) and find no overall difference between false belief and "false" photograph tasks. This is consistent with a general difficulty with representations (p. 19).
- However, correlations were small and were entirely non-significant once age and verbal ability were partialled out (p. 19–20).
- Theoretically the two tasks are poorly matched. The photograph accurately represents the earlier situation and is not "about" the new one, so it is not false. Sally's belief is about the current location, so it really is false (p. 20).
- *False sign* tasks match better (Figure 2.4b). The sign's function is to indicate the object's current location, so it becomes false when the object moves (p. 21, and note 7, p. 34).
- Children's accuracy on false belief and false sign is similar. Performance on the two is correlated (Sabbagh et al., 2006), and remains so when age is controlled (Leekam et al., 2008) (p. 21).
- The false belief–false sign correlation persists when "false" photo performance is controlled. The false belief–photo correlation is not significant when false sign is controlled (p. 21).
- Conclusions: the "false" photograph task fits the event sequence but not the need to reason about false representations. False belief performance is closely linked to false sign, which needs false-representation reasoning without mindreading. So the demands of false belief are quite specific, but not fully specific to mindreading (p. 21).

**Counterfactual reasoning (p. 21–22).**
- Counterfactual reasoning ("what would I have been doing had I not written this book?") treats a non-actual situation as an alternative to the current one. Future hypothetical thinking lacks this "aboutness" (p. 21).
- Within the Sally task: "Where would the object have been if Andrew had not moved it?" (counterfactual) vs "Where will it be if Andrew moves it back?" (future hypothetical) (p. 22).
- Riggs et al. (1998), also Perner et al. (2006) and Robinson & Beck (2000), found that 3- to 4-year-olds were similarly accurate on false belief and counterfactual questions. Performance was correlated even with verbal ability controlled. Future hypotheticals were significantly easier than matched counterfactuals (p. 22).
- Conclusion: false belief demands are not fully shared with future hypotheticals. They are shared with counterfactuals, so they are not fully specific to either mindreading or false representations (p. 22). Note 9 adds that counterfactual reasoning admits of degrees; this does not affect the conclusion (p. 34).

> **Intuition note.** Reasoning about what someone falsely believes is closely linked in performance to reasoning about a false sign or "what would have been". What they may share is thinking about a non-actual situation as an alternative to the current one.

### Mindreading processes: roles for executive function (p. 22–26)
- *Executive function* (EF) is a set of processes for flexible, goal-directed behaviour: working memory, response inhibition, resistance to interference, set-shifting, planning. It matters most in novel, ambiguous situations and is associated with frontal brain regions (p. 22).
- Anecdote: in bubble painting a 3-year-old sucks instead of blowing, though able to say what to do. This looks like a control problem, not a task-understanding problem (p. 22).
- Tests: gift delay and bear-dragon (inhibition), word/number span (working memory), card sorting switch (task-switching), forward planning, and the windows task. The windows task needs a counter-intuitive strategy and may need neither deception nor inhibition (p. 22–23; note 10, p. 34).
- Performance varies across children and improves from 3 to 5 years, as mindreading does (p. 23).
- False belief tasks could draw on EF in many ways: tracking events, holding Sally's belief alongside one's own knowledge, resisting interference, and responding from Sally's view (p. 23). Many studies find correlations with EF tests (p. 23).
- Conflict inhibition tasks correlate with false belief even when planning, working memory and delay are controlled. The reverse does not hold (p. 23). This does not say which aspects of false belief are demanding (p. 23).
- Accounts of the link:
  1. *Incidental demands* (Bloom & German, 2000): EF handles attending to and remembering the story and interpreting questions (p. 24).
     - Carlson and Moses (2001): control-question performance was strongly predicted by an EF battery (p. 24).
     - But children who passed the controls still varied on the critical false belief questions, and this variation still correlated with EF (p. 24).
  2. *Expression* accounts: reasoning about beliefs may need overcoming a default that beliefs are true, or a "reality bias" or "curse of knowledge" (p. 24).
     - Call and Tomasello's (1999) non-verbal "low-inhibition" task (Figure 2.5a): the child does not know the prize's location when taking Sally's false belief into account, so own-knowledge interference is much reduced (p. 24–25).
     - Children did no better than on standard false belief tasks (p. 24).
     - The author later describes how this manipulation greatly helps a patient with impaired EF (Samson et al., 2005) (p. 24).
     - So interference from one's own perspective is less clearly the limiting step for children (p. 24).
  3. *Emergence* accounts suggest EF is necessary for the developmental process of learning abstract concepts like belief, perhaps by letting children disengage from immediate objects of attention (p. 24–25).
  4. *Competence* accounts argue that reasoning about false beliefs requires the capacity (in working memory or other aspects of EF) to build mental representations of a certain complexity; until children have it they would be unable to reason about beliefs (p. 25).
  5. *Perner and Lang (1999)*: mindreading turned on oneself may drive EF development (p. 25).
     - Problems: it struggles to explain EF in young children who fail most mindreading tasks (p. 26).
     - Earlier EF predicts later mindreading, but not the reverse (p. 26).
     - Chinese children may be more advanced than American peers in EF but not in mindreading (Sabbagh et al., 2006) (p. 26).
- Conclusion: children's mindreading, and false belief especially, is strongly associated with EF. EF is deliberate and effortful, domain-general, and in short supply. Child data leave the details uncertain, so the accounts are revisited in later chapters (p. 26).

> **Intuition note.** Executive function is the mind's control system. It plausibly helps with several steps in false belief tasks, but a correlation alone cannot say which step or which direction.

### The role of language and social experience in mindreading (p. 26–30)
Success on false belief is related to performance on various language tests (Milligan, Astington & Dack, 2007). It is unclear which aspects of language matter most (p. 26).

**Syntax (p. 26–27).**
- De Villiers and de Villiers: mental-state talk ("think", "know") uses *embedded complement clauses*. In "John thought that Aldrin was the first man on the moon", the embedded clause can be false while the whole sentence is true (p. 26–27).
- They argue that learning this construction could give the representational basis for thinking about beliefs (p. 27).
- Test: tell the child "Sally thinks that the object is in the square box, but really it is in the round box" and ask "What does Sally think?". This needs no inference (p. 27).
- It is still surprisingly difficult for 3- to 4-year-olds, and not dramatically easier than inferential false belief tasks (p. 27).
- Circularity worry: the difficulty might lie in holding the false belief information in mind rather than in the syntax (p. 27).
  - Partial remedy (de Villiers & Pyers, 2002): items about remembering something false that Sally *said*, which gave similar results (p. 27).
  - The author thinks the circularity is only partially overcome, because saying and thinking are closely related (note 11, p. 34).
- The same children were tested repeatedly. Earlier embedded-complement performance predicted later false belief performance, whereas early false belief performance did not always predict later embedded-complement performance (p. 27). This suggests that understanding of the syntax may have a causal role (though see Perner et al., 2003) (p. 27).
- Other evidence: relative-clause comprehension correlates with false belief even controlling for similar sentences without the embedding relation (Smith, Apperly & White, 2003). But false belief also relates to syntax in general (Milligan et al., 2007) (p. 27).

**Semantics (p. 28).** Sally says she *thinks* the object is in the round box; Andrew says he *knows* it is in the square box. Choosing correctly depends on the meanings of "think" and "know". Moore, Pure and Furrow (1990) found improvement between 3 and 4 years, correlated with false belief performance. The study gives no evidence on causality or on specificity to mental verbs. Mindreading also relates to general semantic measures (p. 28).

**Pragmatics, conversation and social experience (p. 28–29).**
- Meaning depends on context and on what the speaker might think or intend (Grice). Adult accounts of pragmatics commonly presuppose mindreading, whereas developmental work treats conversation as a rich case of social interaction as a training ground (p. 28).
- Evidence: Harris (2005) argues conversation highlights perspective differences, supported by a training study (Lohmann & Tomasello, 2003). Parents' tendency to treat children as having independent mental states goes with better mindreading (p. 28). Siblings, especially older siblings, and more older children and adults in daily contact go with better mindreading (p. 28–29).
- Dunn and Brophy (2005) summarize that conversation amount, mental-state content, contextual relevance and relationship quality all appear to affect children's mindreading, current or later (p. 29).
- The mechanism is rarely spelled out. Commonly it is seen as helping acquire concepts, either by supplying evidence for induction or by introducing them interpersonally before internalization (Astington & Baird, 2005) (p. 29).
- Nelson (2005) sees the task as joining "a community of minds", a view the author says is interesting but not specified in terms of on-line processes. In chapter 6 he suggests such ideas may help with the "problem of relevance" (p. 29).

**Language as an impediment (p. 29).** Language demands might make tasks harder (Bloom & German, 2000). For common child tasks the author thinks the balance of evidence is against this. Reducing verbal load helped somewhat, especially for 2nd-order tasks. But Call and Tomasello's fully non-verbal task was no easier than standard tasks. Language also makes tasks comprehensible and fun for children (p. 29).

**Section summary (p. 30).** Between 3 and 5 years children also improve on non-inferential tasks that require remembering the false belief, and on understanding certainty in "know" and "think". Language and social experience appear causally involved. Some researchers think the relationship is so intertwined that it is a mistake to treat mindreading as an independent faculty (p. 30).

### Mindreading and atypical development (p. 30–32)
Only the subset of this research bearing on the cognitive basis of mindreading is covered (p. 30).

**Autism (p. 30–32).**
- *Definition used:* a developmental disorder diagnosed from impaired social abilities, impaired communication, and inflexible, repetitive non-social behaviour (p. 30). Headline findings are clear, the detailed picture is less so, and clear evidence on cognitive basis is limited (p. 30).
- *Headline 1:* as a group, children with autism do worse on false belief than children with similar intellectual disability but without autism (Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith, 1985). The finding is widely replicated and extends to other mindreading tasks. It shows mindreading can be impaired while general intelligence is relatively good, and led to claims that a mindreading impairment might cause autism (p. 30–31).
- *Headline 2:* language and mindreading are correlated in autism. But children with autism may need a verbal mental age of 9 to pass false belief tasks, perhaps via an atypical linguistic strategy. So it is unclear that this supports a general claim that language is necessary for mindreading (p. 31).
- *Headline 3:* EF impairments are found in autism. Evidence on whether they explain mindreading problems is "decidedly mixed". There are positive correlations, EF impairment without mindreading impairment (Ozonoff et al., 1991), and the reverse (Pellicano, 2007). A possible reason is that many different tasks measure each (p. 31).
- *Matched tasks:*
  - Early studies found children with autism failing false belief but doing much better on "false" photograph tasks (Leekam & Perner, 1991; Leslie & Thaiss, 1992). This seemed strong evidence for impairment independent of structurally identical reasoning and supported domain-specific module claims (p. 31–32).
  - Since photographs are not false (see above), better-matched tasks were used. Children with autism perform at similar rates on false belief, false sign (Bowler et al., 2005) and counterfactual reasoning (Grant, Riggs & Boucher, 2004) (p. 32).
- *Author's conclusion:* mindreading can be impaired out of proportion to general intelligence. But autism research gives no good reason to think it can be impaired out of proportion to all other processes such as language or EF (p. 32). So it does not support strong domain-specificity, nor give clear evidence on causal dependence on language and EF (p. 32). A significant subset with clinical social impairment pass many standard tasks, which should make us cautious about claiming autism is due to a lack of mindreading concepts (p. 32).

**Deafness (p. 32).**
- Deaf children of fluent signing parents (usually deaf themselves) have typical language development and pass mindreading tasks at similar ages to hearing peers. So deafness per se does not impair mindreading (p. 32).
- Deaf children of non-fluent signers tend to have language delay and also delay on mindreading tasks (Peterson & Siegal, 2000). It is debated whether this is general language delay, delay in specific syntax, or reduced conversational pragmatics experience (p. 32).
- On any account, such findings strengthen the evidence that language acquisition plays a causal role (p. 32).

### Summary (p. 33)
Child studies have been the mainstay of research for 30 years. Between ages 2 and 7 judgements become more sophisticated. Advances are related to language and EF development, and disruption to language or EF may lead to disruption in mindreading (p. 33). Two limits: theories concern early childhood and leave a gap from about 7 years to adulthood (to be addressed with older children and adults, chapter 7), and recent infant and animal evidence casts the language/EF link in a different light (chapter 3) (p. 33).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show why a perspective difference is needed and why errors can be "systematic" (p. 11–12).

Test two imaginary children (A, B) on 4 unexpected transfer stories, each with two boxes.
- Trials 1–2: *false belief* — the object is moved while Sally is away. Correct answer: the old box.
- Trials 3–4: *true belief* — Sally watches the move. Correct answer: the new box, which is the true location.

Child A always answers the true location. Trials 1–2: 0 correct of 2. Trials 3–4: 2 correct of 2. Total 2/4 = 50%.

Child B guesses at random, with probability ½ per trial. Expected correct: 2 × ½ = 1 on trials 1–2, and 2 × ½ = 1 on trials 3–4. Total 2/4 = 50%.

Totals are identical. The two children differ only on false belief trials: A scores 0 of 2, always wrong in the same way (egocentric), while B is at chance. On true belief trials alone, A scores 100% and would look like a mindreader. Only the perspective-difference trials expose A's strategy.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Egocentric errors on false belief tasks ↔ LLM evaluation: shortcut answers that look competent unless the test contains a perspective difference (Clever Hans effect, construct validity) — same logic of control trials.
- [structural-analogy] "Reality bias / curse of knowledge" ↔ judgement under hindsight or informational asymmetry in social science — both are projection of one's own information onto another.
- [metaphor] Oedipus problems ↔ referential opacity in logic: substituting co-referring descriptions inside "knows that…" changes truth.

## Questions this raises
- If false belief is not the "one true test", which battery of tasks would justify crediting a child with the concept of belief?
- Can the executive function link be separated into tracking the story, holding two perspectives and inhibiting one's own view? Which steps do child tasks actually tax?
- What would change if infants succeed on implicit belief tracking (chapter 3)? Would the language and EF findings above still tell us about the origin of mindreading?
- How could the causal role of language be shown more cleanly than by correlations, longitudinal prediction or deafness studies?
