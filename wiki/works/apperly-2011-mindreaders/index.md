# Apperly, I. (2011). *Mindreaders: The Cognitive Basis of "Theory of Mind"*. Hove: Psychology Press.

## In one paragraph
Apperly argues that research on "theory of mind" has been too narrow. It has focused on children acquiring mental-state concepts, as if nothing needed explaining afterwards. He asks instead which cognitive processes let us infer, hold in mind and use what others see, know and believe. He reviews evidence from children, infants and animals, brain studies and adults. The evidence looks contradictory: mindreading appears both fast and slow, automatic and effortful. He argues that full, open-ended mindreading would be computationally intractable. His solution is a two-systems account. Efficient but inflexible low-level processes work alongside flexible but effortful high-level reasoning. The low-level processes have "signature limits", which give an independent test of the account. This matters because it treats mindreading as something adults do effortfully and imperfectly, not only as a milestone children pass.

## The path through the work
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch01-introduction]] — Apperly argues that research on "theory of mind" has been too narrow (children, concepts, philosophical theories), and proposes to study the cognitive processes of mindreading in a way that must explain how it can be both flexible and fast.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch02-evidence]] — Children's mindreading improves between ages 2 and 7 and is strongly associated with executive function and language and appears to be linked to social experience, but the child evidence does not clearly show it to be a separate faculty.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch03-evidence]] — Infants and some animals behave as if they track what others see, know and (for infants) believe, but this evidence does not show that they grasp belief "as such", and its cognitive basis remains unclear.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch04-evidence]] — Brain imaging and brain-injury studies together suggest that mindreading is a function of a network of several partly domain-general processes, not of one specialised module.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch05-evidence]] — Adults pass simple mindreading tests, yet reaction-time, bias and dual-task studies show that many aspects of their mindreading are biased towards their own viewpoint and effortful, while simple Level-1 perspective-taking (what another can see) appears quick and relatively automatic.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch06-cognitive]] — Because full, open-ended mindreading would be computationally impossible, Apperly proposes that adults use two kinds of process: efficient but inflexible low-level modules and flexible but effortful high-level reasoning guided by scripts, narratives and norms.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/ch07-elaborating]] — Because efficient low-level mindreading must be inflexible, its "signature limits" (for example Level-1 but not Level-2 perspectives) give, Apperly argues, an independent test for telling low-level from high-level mindreading, which makes the two-systems account a potential explanation rather than a mere description.
- [[apperly-2011-mindreaders/coda]] — Apperly argues that, viewed across all the literatures on mindreading, a two-systems account (a high-level plus a low-level process, making complementary trade-offs between flexibility and efficiency) is worth its loss of parsimony (simplicity), and that he thinks there is every reason to expect it to hold beyond the epistemic states (perceptions, knowledge, beliefs) he studied.

## Big ideas across units
- **Mindreading is more than having concepts, and false belief is not the one "true test".** Mindreading does not begin or end at false belief. Having concepts says little about how people mindread. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch06.
- **Flexibility versus efficiency, and intractability.** The inverse, frame and abduction problems make unlimited mindreading impossible. This motivates the two-systems account, which the later chapters develop, test and defend. Units: ch01, ch06, ch07, coda.
- **Mindreading is not a single specialised module.** Child and autism data, brain imaging and patient studies point to several partly domain-general processes. Units: ch02, ch04, ch07.
- **Executive function and language.** Their role runs from the child correlations through the infant and animal data (little language or executive function) to adults' effortful use of mental states. Units: ch02, ch03, ch04, ch05, ch06, ch07.
- **Egocentric bias and resisting one's own perspective.** This shows up as the reality bias or curse of knowledge in children and adults, in right frontal patients, and as the cost of high-level processing. Units: ch02, ch04, ch05, ch06, ch07.
- **Level-1 versus Level-2 perspective-taking as a diagnostic.** Level-1 is quick and relatively automatic in adults and is seen in chimpanzees. This becomes the case study for the "signature limits" of low-level processing. Units: ch02, ch03, ch05, ch06, ch07.
- **Mindreading without adult concepts.** Infants and animals show mindreading-like behaviour without much language or executive function. Chapter 3 compares number cognition as a cautionary precedent, and chapters 6–7 use it as the worked two-systems analogy. Units: ch03, ch05, ch06, ch07, coda.
- **The normative account as an idealisation.** Philosophy's model of attitudes to propositions is treated as a working hypothesis. It is judged almost certainly wrong as a description of real mindreaders, and it also fits the animal data badly. Units: ch01, ch03, ch06, ch07.

## How to read it
- **Essential (also the catalogue's focus):** ch01 (the questions and the problems), ch02, ch03 and ch05 (the evidence), and ch06 (the two-systems account).
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch04 is more technical, with imaging and patient detail. Its main conclusion is in its L0 line. ch07 elaborates and applies the theory. Read at least its L0 and the "independent criteria" idea if you want to see why the account is more than a description. The coda is short and works as a summary.
- **Dependencies:**
  - ch02 introduces the false-belief tasks, the false-photograph control and Level-1/Level-2 perspective-taking. ch03, ch04 and ch05 all rely on these.
  - ch04 also assumes the ch02 material on controls.
  - ch06 draws on ch2–5 and returns to the flexibility and frame problems from ch01.
  - ch07 depends on ch06, and the coda on the whole argument.
