# Evidence from adults

## L0 — One line
Adults pass simple mindreading tests, yet reaction-time, bias and dual-task studies show that many aspects of their mindreading are biased towards their own viewpoint and effortful, while simple Level-1 perspective-taking (what another can see) appears quick and relatively automatic.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Adults "pass" most standard mindreading tests, so developmental researchers often ignore them. Apperly asks what finer measures (speed, errors, bias, interference) reveal about the cognitive basis of adult mindreading. Adults show egocentric bias (reality bias, curse of knowledge). Holding a belief in mind that conflicts with reality has a processing cost. Belief inferences do not appear to be automatic; they may be spontaneous, but only with some motivation. Level-1 visual perspective-taking appears quick and relatively automatic. Using mental-state information, as in communication games, is error-prone and appears to draw on executive function. Apperly proposes a starting taxonomy: inferring, holding in mind, and using mental states.

**Prerequisites:** false-belief tasks (unexpected transfer); reaction-time experiments (speed/accuracy trade-offs, interference); the idea of executive function (control, inhibition, working memory); Level-1 perspective-taking (knowing what another person can see).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Language researchers tend to assume mindreading is already solved, and social psychologists often study enduring traits rather than short-term beliefs. Apperly says the "mindreading module" idea names a need, not an explanation of speed (pp. 86–88).
- Adults show egocentric bias (judging from one's own view): their own knowledge leaks into judgements about others, and the effortful adjustment away from it succeeds to varying degrees (pp. 88–90).
- Holding someone's false belief in mind carries a processing cost when it conflicts with reality, affecting both belief and reality judgements (pp. 91–92).
- The evidence suggests belief inferences are not automatic. They may be made spontaneously, but spontaneous inferences need some motivation (pp. 92–95).
- Level-1 perspective-taking appears quick and relatively independent of executive control. The sign is "altercentric" interference: the avatar's view slows the participant's own judgement. Apperly sees some convergence with what infants and animals can do (pp. 95–99, 105–106).
- Using mental-state information is hard even for adults: false belief and negative desire slow prediction, and adults often fail to take a director's limited view into account (pp. 99–103).
- Executive function appears to play some role in adult mindreading. Individual-difference and dual-task (a second, concurrent task) studies with complex tasks cannot say which sub-process needs it, though the dots-task studies give more specific clues (pp. 103–106).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 86)
Developmental and comparative researchers typically ignore adults because adults pass most tests. Language researchers typically assume mindreading is a problem others have solved. Social psychologists have studied forms of mindreading for decades and get cross at being overlooked. Apperly therefore starts with work on communication and on social psychology (p. 86).

### Language and communication (pp. 86–87)
- Grice argued that communication needs more than encoding and decoding messages: it needs pragmatic inferences (judgements about intended meaning) based on what speakers and listeners know and intend (p. 86).
- Example: "the cat's been sick this week" means nothing useful if only syntax and semantics are used (p. 86).
- Clark and colleagues: communication works within a common ground of mutually known information. This entitles the listener to assume "the cat" is a cat both know about, and "this week" is a shared time-frame (p. 87).
- Several cats may be in common ground. Sperber and Wilson: listeners may expect speakers to be relevant. In a staff meeting, "the cat" is the head of department (the example's joke nickname), not a pet cat (p. 87).
- Cost: this appears to place a significant burden on mindreading, which seems necessary to generate, maintain and update what is mutually known and to calculate relevance. All of this must be done quickly enough to keep up with a conversation and efficiently enough that communication is not deprived of cognitive resources (p. 87).
- **Sperber and Wilson's proposal:** a fast, efficient mindreading module. **Apperly's reply:** they are surely right to identify a need for mindreading in communication, but their solution begs the interesting questions about how mindreading could be fast and efficient. A module does not explain speed. It presupposes that mindreading can be made tractable for fast computation, and nobody has yet shown how. The problem of relevance looms as large for mindreading as for communication; he returns to it in chapter 6 (p. 87).

*Intuition note:* Words underdetermine meaning, so listeners must work out what the speaker knows and means. Apperly's point is that naming a module does not explain how this is done quickly.

### Social psychology (pp. 87–88)
- Social psychologists have typically studied adults' inferences about traits and beliefs that are *enduring* (e.g. Bob is anxious). Often the inference comes from scant evidence or from target characteristics (race, class, age, gender), and they ask whether participants take account of mitigating context (p. 88).
- Example: participants are less likely to infer anxiety from fidgeting if the person was being asked embarrassing questions, but using such "situational constraints" appears to need executive resources (Gilbert, Pelham & Krull, 1988) (p. 88).
- Mindreading research has typically studied beliefs and desires that are short-term and normatively warranted. In a false-belief task it does not matter who Sally is: provided she is a sentient, rational agent she will necessarily reach the false belief, and it can change with further perceptual access (p. 88).
- It is not clear the simple judgements studied in mindreading have the same cognitive basis as social-psychology judgements. Apperly says there may be mindreading concepts and processes that add to social psychology. He suspects that mindreading researchers who move beyond simple cases might do well to examine social-psychology concepts (p. 88).
- Two lessons: (1) quick, busy or low-attention social judgements are more likely to rest on fast-and-frugal heuristics (social scripts, stereotypes), while taking account of context is effortful. (2) When judging what others feel, believe or know, participants often begin with their own state and adjust effortfully towards the target's; this is thought to produce egocentric reasoning biases (p. 88).

### Biases in adults' mindreading (pp. 88–90)
- Without time pressure adults tend not to err on simple tasks, so tasks are modified to be probabilistic or uncertain and then checked for systematic bias (pp. 88–89).
- **Mitchell et al. (1996):** videos in which Sally thought a jug held milk and was then told it held orange juice. Participants judged whether she would change her mind. They judged this *less* likely when they knew her original belief was true than when they knew it was false. Apperly notes this is illogical, since Sally's information is the same in both cases, and says the study suggests participants' own knowledge contaminated the judgement (p. 89).
- **Birch and Bloom (2007):** adapted the unexpected transfer task, with participants rating the probability that Sally would search in each location. In the critical conditions Sally always had a false belief. When participants knew exactly where the object was, they rated it less likely that she would search incorrectly than when they only knew it was elsewhere (p. 89).
- Mitchell et al. called the effect a reality bias; Birch and Bloom called it a curse of knowledge (p. 89).
- **Epley, Keysar et al. (2004):** privileged knowledge influenced how adults thought others would interpret ambiguous messages or make hard perceptual discriminations. Bias was larger under quick judgement and smaller with a financial incentive for accuracy. They proposed that adults adjust away from an egocentric starting point, and that the adjustment is effortful (p. 89).
- Apperly's reading: under uncertainty adults often use the heuristic "others are like me" and correct with varying degrees of success. This does not say what the adjustment is based on (p. 89).
- Some authors claim the main difference between infants and adults is the ability to muster the effort to overcome egocentric bias (Birch & Bloom, Fodor, Mitchell et al.). Apperly thinks this is likely one important development but very unlikely to be the whole story, not least because it offers no clear way of explaining why infants appear to do better than older children on false-belief tasks despite lacking executive resources (p. 90).

*Intuition note:* Adults seem to start from what they themselves know and then adjust. The bias was larger under quick judgement and smaller with a financial incentive for accuracy, so the adjustment looks effortful and not always complete.

### Speed and accuracy of mindreading (p. 90)
Adults' residual variation in speed and accuracy allows specific questions about the cognitive basis of mindreading. Apperly's own work has three motivations: keep tasks simple (for matched controls and fewer language or executive confounds); keep them close to developmental tasks; decompose mindreading into holding in mind, inferring, and using mental-state information (p. 90).

#### Holding in mind what people think: a non-inferential false belief task (pp. 90–92)
- Studies that simply *told* children about a false belief found that children often mis-remember it and report reality instead. It is unclear whether this reflects difficulty holding the belief in mind or difficulty with the embedded-clause syntax. A study of adults would resolve this, since adults have no syntax difficulty (pp. 90–91).
- **Task (Apperly, Back, Samson & France, 2008):** two briefly presented sentences, e.g. "He thinks the object on the table is red" and "Really, the object on the table is blue". Then a picture probe showed either the belief or the reality. Participants judged whether the picture matched the sentences (Figure 5.1) (p. 91).
- Baseline: sentences about unrelated aspects (one about the object on the table, the other about the object on the chair). The amount of information was similar. The only clear difference was belief–reality conflict in the false-belief condition (p. 91).
- Result: slower and/or more error-prone in the false-belief condition, on *both* belief and reality probes. So there was interference from knowledge of reality when judging belief, and from knowledge of the false belief when judging reality (pp. 91–92).
- A second experiment found this was not affected by the time available for reading the sentences, suggesting the difficulty was with holding conflicting information in mind, not with encoding it (p. 92).
- Interpretation: holding what someone thinks carries a detectable processing cost when it conflicts with what we know. Since the information was held for only seconds, Apperly thinks the interference seems likely to arise in working memory. He says it would be interesting to know whether longer retention intervals give similar effects. The result also gives indirect support for the view that children's errors come from holding false beliefs in mind rather than from syntax (p. 92).

*Intuition note:* Keeping "he thinks red" and "really blue" both in mind makes the two compete. The competition slows you down whichever one you are asked about.

### Mindreading inferences (pp. 92–99)
Apperly's two series of experiments on inference give results that appear to reflect the conflict in the literature on the role of cognitive control in mindreading (p. 92).

#### Evidence that belief inferences are not automatic (pp. 92–95)
- Claims that belief inferences are automatic (Friedman & Leslie, Sperber & Wilson, Stone et al.) are not based on direct evidence. Indirect evidence from text comprehension suggests the opposite: readers' inferences for a situation model are *spontaneous*, not automatic (p. 92).
- Example (McKoon & Ratcliff, 1986): after reading that a desperate woman ran to the car and jumped in, people tended to judge wrongly that the word "drive" had appeared. Such inferences are less likely if motivation for comprehension is low or if the inference needs information from larger stretches of text (pp. 92–93).
- Apperly sees no reason to suppose that inferences about Sally's belief are exceptions. An unpublished pilot study gave some consistent evidence. Further experiments used videos and pictures, which were easier to control (p. 93).
- **Incidental false belief task (Apperly, Riggs, Simpson, Chiavarino & Samson, 2006):** the logic is that if we automatically infer beliefs, we will do so even when unnecessary. Participants watched video sequences and had to monitor the hidden object's location, but not where Sally thought it was. Unpredictable probe questions then asked about the video, including Sally's belief (Figure 5.2) (p. 93).
- Result: responses were significantly slower to probes about Sally's false belief than to probes about the object's location. When participants were *told* to track Sally's belief, they answered belief probes as fast as location probes. So belief probes are not intrinsically slow, and the slowness in the first condition suggests Sally's belief was not automatically inferred and encoded (p. 93).
- **Back and Apperly (2010):** replicated this, with the same pattern when Sally's belief was true. Some authors propose true belief as a default setting for belief ascription (Fodor; Leslie & Thaiss). These findings suggested this may not be so (p. 93).
- An unexpected result (p. 95): belief probes took longer than reality probes, true or false belief. Also, responses to *either* probe type were slower when Sally's belief was false than true. This resembles the interference in the non-inferential task. For that explanation to apply, participants would have had to infer Sally's belief spontaneously on at least some trials. Apperly's reasoning: repeated belief probing may have made inferences more likely. If probing stopped, the false/true difference on reality probes should vanish, and that was the pattern observed (p. 95).
- Summary: attending to a stimulus that affords a belief inference does not mean one necessarily makes it. This shows as a cost when one is asked about the belief. Participants may infer spontaneously, and if they do, holding the belief interferes with reality judgements. Spontaneous inference needs some motivation, such as probe frequency in an experiment. Apperly is sure that people in real life are often motivated to infer thoughts, but says that without such motivation there is no evidence at all that beliefs are inferred. This fits other everyday inference and the general view of mindreading as a domain-general, effortful inferential process (p. 95).

*Intuition note:* Like reading between the lines, working out what someone believes happens when you care or are asked, not on every glance. When it does happen, the stored belief can interfere with thinking about reality.

#### Evidence that Level-1 visual perspective-taking is automatic (pp. 95–98)
- Claims that mindreading is automatic are well motivated. If infants and animals mindread and if mindreading serves on-line interaction, it should not depend heavily on strategic executive control. A good place to look for efficient mindreading is Level-1 perspective-taking (knowing what another sees). There is good evidence for it in infants and animals, and it has the closest link between observable cues (body orientation, gaze) and an epistemic state ("seeing") (pp. 95–96).
- **Task (Samson, Apperly, Braithwaite, Andrews & Bodley Scott, in press):** same rationale as above, opposite predictions. An avatar stood in a room with dots on the wall. He sometimes saw the same number of dots as the participant, sometimes fewer. Each trial had a perspective cue ("You / He"), then a content cue (a number), then the picture. Participants judged whether the number matched what was seen from the cued perspective (Figure 5.3, p. 96; p. 97).
- **Egocentric effect:** when judging the avatar's view, responses were slower and more error-prone when the participant saw more dots than the avatar (p. 97).
- **Altercentric effect:** when judging their *own* view, responses were slower and more error-prone when the avatar saw a different number than when he saw the same number. It appeared that participants might also be working out the avatar's perspective, which interfered (p. 97).
- Controls (p. 97): dot spacing held constant, with both effects still found. On a sub-set of incongruent trials the avatar saw no dots (so all dots were in one group behind him); trials where the avatar saw no dots gave the same results as the full data. Apperly says the effects therefore could not easily be explained by spacing or by difficulty counting dots.
- Cue-switching worry: because a cue told participants whose perspective to judge, interference from the avatar was only relatively weak evidence of automaticity; it might be a carry-over from trial-to-trial switching. A stronger test required participants to judge only their own perspective throughout. The effect was smaller but remained (p. 97).
- A grey bar of similar size in place of the avatar gave no similar effect, confirming the effect was not merely disruption of the dot array (pp. 97–98).
- Summary: participants computed the avatar's perspective when they did not need to, were never told to, and even though it made their own judgements harder. Altercentric interference may be an indirect or implicit test of perspective-taking (p. 98).
- The effects are compatible with evidence that people automatically process gaze direction, but go further by suggesting that we calculate what is seen. Apperly notes that automaticity is notoriously hard to demonstrate and these effects may not meet the strictest tests. For his purposes, what matters is that their characteristics appear very different from those found for belief reasoning (p. 98).

*Intuition note:* The avatar's view intruded even when it was irrelevant. This suggests that, at least for simple seeing, one computes what another sees quickly and with little control.

#### Comparison with infants and non-human animals (p. 98)
Infants and animals show evidence of tracking what an agent sees. Adults appear to do something analogous efficiently: quickly enough to interfere with on-line self-judgements, and relatively independently of cognitive control (reinforced by the dual-task study below). So there appears to be some convergence. The adult data appear to show something not yet shown in infants or animals: efficient calculation of mental *content* for the avatar. This matters because ascribing mental content is a critical criterion for telling mindreading from behaviour reasoning (chapter 3). Apperly returns to this in chapter 7 (p. 98).

#### Can mindreading inferences be both automatic and non-automatic? (pp. 98–99)
- Apperly thinks they can. Chapter 6 will argue there are good reasons why adults should have different ways of making mindreading inferences, with different flexibility–efficiency trade-offs (pp. 98–99).
- If this is correct, the incidental belief task could be seen as a laboratory model for mindreading inferences about belief, knowledge and perception as such. These would approximate the normative model (ascribing an attitude with propositional content), but the flexibility this entails must come with some requirements for cognitive effort (p. 99).
- The altercentric effect could be seen as a model for relatively effortless inferences. If this is correct, Apperly says we should predict that such inferences will not meet the normative model and might be made only from certain perceptual cues, for certain agents, with limited content. This is for further research (p. 99).

### Mindreading use (pp. 99–103)

#### Deductive inferences about beliefs and desires (pp. 99–101)
- In the unexpected transfer task, Sally's false belief predicts where she looks only together with her desire to find the object. Children find it significantly harder to predict behaviour when she has a false belief and a desire to avoid the object (p. 99).
- **Task (Apperly, Warren, Andrews, Grant & Todd, in press):** participants predicted which of two boxes Andrew would open. Food was hidden in one box. Three sentences gave the food's location, where he thought it was (true or false), and whether he liked it. This gave four combinations of true/false belief × positive/negative desire. Participants judged which box he would open (Figure 5.4). Catch trials asked where the food was (pp. 99–100).
- Result: adults were slower and made more errors with a false than a true belief. They were also slower with negative than positive desire. This pattern is directly analogous to that seen in the ages at which children first pass these combinations (p. 100).
- Design: there was no need for abductive inferences (inferring unobserved beliefs or desires), because those were given. Participants had to hold the information briefly and deduce Andrew's action (pp. 100–101).
- It is not possible to say whether the belief effect arose from holding the information in mind or from reasoning about Andrew's behaviour. The desire effect is easier to interpret: there is no particular reason to expect negative desires to be harder to hold, so we may suppose that at least the desire effect was due to differences in deducing his search behaviour (p. 101).
- Note 2: the common account of children's belief–desire difficulty places it in having to infer the belief (Leslie, German & Polizzi, 2005). That account cannot easily explain these findings (p. 107).

#### Using mindreading inferences in referential communication (pp. 101–103)
- **Keysar, Barr, Balin and Brauner (2000):** participants moved items on a grid on a "director's" instructions. The director could not see items in covered slots. Level-1 problems like this are solvable by 2-year-olds. For "move the small ball down one slot" the director cannot know about the smallest ball (y), so he must mean x. Adults frequently moved y, which best fit the instruction from their own view. Eye records showed they frequently considered the hidden object even when they went on to move the correct one (p. 101).
- Keysar, Lin and Barr (2003): same pattern when the director had a *false belief* about the identity of objects in closed slots (p. 101).
- **Apperly, Carroll et al. (in press):** a computer version reproduced the errors. Participants were no worse when explicitly required to switch perspectives, using a second director who shared their view. So adults' difficulty did not seem to be with switching between perspectives (p. 102).
- A non-mindreading condition (no director; participants followed a rule to avoid objects in closed slots) had the same grids, objects to avoid and instructions. It produced significantly fewer errors. This suggests that holding and using the speaker's perspective makes demands beyond the general demands of the task (pp. 102–103).
- Adults showed the ability to use speaker information but struggled to use it reliably. Other studies suggest better use in some circumstances (p. 103):
  - Nadig and Sedivy (2003): 5- and 6-year-olds on a 2×2 grid with one hidden object looked much more at referents the speaker could see.
  - Nilsen and Graham (2009): even 4-year-olds often chose the correct referent.
  - Hanna, Tanenhaus and Trueswell (2003): adults looked more at items both could see, but still looked at objects only they could see.
  - Eye-tracking showed sensitivity within 500 ms of the critical word's onset (Nadig & Sedivy, 2001), or even before onset (Barr, 2008) (p. 103).
- Note 3: Barr (2008) shows that adults pay less attention to privileged-ground objects early, but integrate this much more slowly with the director's instruction to resolve ambiguity (p. 107).
- Apperly's hypothesis: the best performance came with smaller arrays and a single hidden item, so it seems plausible that handling a speaker's ignorance of one item falls within general executive or attentional capacity, with errors increasing as complexity rises. He says such hypotheses must be tested directly (p. 103).

*Intuition note:* Apperly's hypothesis is that adults can work out what a speaker cannot see, but that this is easier with a simple display than a busy one, because busy displays make greater demands on general capacity. He says this still needs testing.

### Individual differences in executive function and mindreading (pp. 103–104)
- Maylor et al. (2002): an elderly deficit on a stories task (white lies, double-bluffs) was not explained by two executive measures. Apperly says the varied stories make it hard to identify common processes, so the null is hard to interpret (p. 104).
- German and Hehman (2006): stories varied systematically in beliefs (true/false) and desires (positive/negative). Performance correlated with working memory, processing speed and inhibitory control. In regression, processing speed and inhibitory control were more important. The task needed inferring beliefs, holding them with desires, and predicting action, so one cannot say which sub-process uses executive function (p. 104; note 4, p. 107).
- Qureshi (2009): adults' egocentric errors in the communication game correlated with executive tasks requiring holding in mind and selecting between alternative responses, but not with a variety of other inhibition and working-memory tests (p. 104).

### Using a secondary task to interfere with mindreading (pp. 104–106)
Dual-task methods (a concurrent executive task while mindreading) pose significant methodological challenges (p. 104).
- McKinnon and Moscovitch (2007): a concurrent working-memory task disproportionately impaired a story-based mindreading task (white lies, double-bluff) compared with a non-mindreading control (p. 105).
- Bull, Phillips and Conway (2008): tasks needing inhibition, switching and updating impaired both mindreading and non-mindreading trials (p. 105).
- Three difficulties (p. 105): (1) a secondary task may disrupt components unrelated to mindreading, as seems the case in Bull et al. (2) Executive function is multi-faceted, so it is unclear whether the two patterns conflict. (3) Complex story tasks are opaque about which mindreading processes are assessed. Apperly's own attempts with non-verbal false-belief tasks gave similar interference on belief and control trials, and he could not tell why (note 5, p. 107).
- **Qureshi, Apperly and Samson (2010):** the Level-1 dots task alone or with a secondary executive task (tap once for two beeps, twice for one beep; note 6, p. 107). The secondary task exaggerated the egocentric effect and also exaggerated the altercentric effect (p. 105).
- Reasoning: if irrelevant calculation of the avatar's view needed general executive resources, the secondary task should have reduced the altercentric effect. That it was exaggerated suggests that calculating the avatar's perspective is relatively automatic and relatively independent of general executive resources. Executive resources appear to be needed to *resolve interference* between self and other perspectives, both available automatically (pp. 105–106).

*Intuition note:* Loading the executive system did not stop the avatar's view being computed. It seems to have made the clash between "my view" and "his view" harder to sort out.

### Summary (p. 106)
- Adults can be informative subjects, as in psycholinguistics. They are cooperative and can do many trials, which allows methods unusable with others (p. 106).
- The studies provide clear evidence that many aspects of adult mindreading remain subject to systematic egocentric bias and are relatively effortful and demanding of executive resources. This shows in slower responses when adults are accurate, but adults also keep making errors on conceptually simple tasks. Having the conceptual capacity for a task is no guarantee it will be used when appropriate (p. 106).
- In contrast, at least some mindreading, such as Level-1 perspective-taking, is performed in a way that is cognitively efficient and relatively automatic. Apperly says there are many reasons to think mindreading must have these properties at least some of the time (p. 106).
- The chapter brings together a starting taxonomy, not definitive categories: processes for **inferring** mental states, **holding** them in mind, and **using** them to explain or predict behaviour. It allows more precise questions about task demands (p. 106).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Hypothetical Level-1 dots data for one participant who judges *their own* view. Reaction times (ms):
- Avatar sees the same number of dots (consistent): 600, 620, 640. Mean = (600 + 620 + 640) / 3 = 1860 / 3 = 620 ms.
- Avatar sees a different number (inconsistent): 680, 700, 720. Mean = (680 + 700 + 720) / 3 = 2100 / 3 = 700 ms.
- Altercentric effect = 700 − 620 = 80 ms.

Because the participant was asked only about their own view, the extra 80 ms suggests the avatar's view was computed anyway, as in the Samson et al. logic (p. 97). If a secondary task raised the inconsistent mean to 740 ms with the consistent mean unchanged, the effect would be 740 − 620 = 120 ms, "exaggerated", the direction reported by Qureshi et al. (p. 105). These numbers are invented.

## Concepts
- [[reality-bias-curse-of-knowledge]] — own true knowledge biases judgements of what another will think or do (Mitchell et al. call it reality bias; Birch & Bloom call it curse of knowledge) (p. 89)
- [[egocentric-bias]] (new) — judging others by starting from one's own state and adjusting effortfully, with varying degrees of success (pp. 88–89)
- [[perspective-taking-level-1-level-2]] — Level-1: knowing what another can see; used here via dots and grid tasks (pp. 96, 98, 101)
- [[altercentric-interference]] (new) — slowed self-judgements, apparently because the other's perspective is computed too (p. 97)
- [[common-ground]] (new) — mutually known information assumed by speakers and listeners in communication (pp. 86–87)
- [[non-inferential-false-belief-task]] (new) — false belief is told, not inferred, so only holding in mind is probed (p. 91)
- [[incidental-false-belief-task]] (new) — tests whether belief is inferred when not required (p. 93)
- [[automaticity-of-mindreading]] (new) — whether mindreading occurs without need or intent. Apperly distinguishes spontaneous from automatic inference, and finds belief inference to be spontaneous at most (pp. 92–95)
- [[implicit-vs-explicit-tom]] — here, altercentric interference is described as an indirect or implicit test of perspective-taking (p. 98)
- [[dual-task-method]] (new) — a concurrent task to tax executive function while mindreading (pp. 104–105)
- [[folk-psychology]] — touched on only briefly here: a false belief predicts action only together with a desire (p. 99)

## Canonical instances
- [[mitchell-1996-reality-bias]] — participants judged Sally less likely to change her mind when her belief was true (p. 89)
- [[birch-bloom-2007-curse-of-knowledge]] — knowing the exact object location reduced the judged chance Sally would search wrongly (p. 89)
- [[apperly-2008-non-inferential-fb]] — belief–reality conflict slowed both belief and reality probes (pp. 91–92)

- [[samson-dots-task]] — egocentric and altercentric effects in Level-1 perspective-taking (pp. 96–98)
- [[keysar-director-task]] — adults often fail to take the director's limited view into account (pp. 101–103)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] egocentric "start from self, adjust" ↔ judgement under uncertainty: anchoring and adjustment — both describe starting from an anchor and adjusting from it.
- [structural-analogy] altercentric and egocentric interference ↔ cognitive psychology: congruency effects — both slow responses when irrelevant information conflicts with the target.
- [structural-analogy] common ground and relevance ↔ pragmatics models such as the Rational Speech Act (not in this unit) — both treat meaning as inference about speaker knowledge.
- [metaphor] a mindreading module as a non-explanation of speed ↔ AI: naming a component is not specifying its algorithm.

## Questions this raises
- If Level-1 effects are efficient but carry only limited content, what exactly can the efficient system represent (which cues, agents, contents), as Apperly predicts (p. 99)?
- Which sub-process — inferring, holding, or using — needs executive function in tasks like German and Hehman's (p. 104)?
- Does the belief–reality interference persist when information must be held for much longer than seconds (p. 92)?
- How could infants' success on false-belief tasks be explained, given that adult false-belief handling is effortful (p. 90)?
