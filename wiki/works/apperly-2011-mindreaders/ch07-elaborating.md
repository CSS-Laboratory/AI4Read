# Elaborating and applying the two-systems theory

## L0 — One line
Because efficient low-level mindreading must be inflexible, its "signature limits" (for example Level-1 but not Level-2 perspectives) give, Apperly argues, an independent test for telling low-level from high-level mindreading, which makes the two-systems account a potential explanation rather than a mere description.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter 6 proposed two kinds of mindreading process: low-level (efficient, inflexible) and high-level (flexible, demanding). Is this more than a description? Apperly says it is, because low-level processes must have limits, and these limits give independent criteria for deciding which process is at work. He uses Level-1 versus Level-2 perspectives as a case study. He then applies the account to 3-year-olds' false-belief failures, development, older children, individual differences, autism, brain imaging and timing. Finally he compares it with other two-systems, modular, simulation and theory-theory accounts. His main objection to most of the last two is that they ignore the problem of identifying what is relevant.

**Prerequisites:** the chapter 6 distinction between low-level (modular) and high-level mindreading; Level-1 vs Level-2 perspective-taking; false-belief tasks; the idea of response-time "interference" effects; basic fMRI/EEG ideas.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Independent criteria.** Low-level mindreading must have hard limits. Finding such limits gives independent reasons to think a low-level process is at work, which Apperly argues answers the charge of circularity (pp. 143–144, 174).
- **Level-1 case study.** Adults show automatic "altercentric" interference from an avatar's Level-1 perspective, but no such interference was found for Level-2 perspectives, in one paradigm (pp. 145–148).
- **Convergence on limits.** As with the 3–4 item limit in number cognition, similar limits across adults, infants and animals would suggest that one shared module may be at work, and that general resources are not the explanation (pp. 149–153).
- **Why 3-year-olds fail.** Low-level processing may be immune to egocentric bias. Explicit judgements are assumed to recruit high-level processing, which is open to egocentric bias and needs resources (pp. 153–155).
- **Development.** Executive function is treated as part of mature mindreading competence, not just a gateway. Language may scaffold development. Apperly suggests that language and social experience help children become expert at identifying what is relevant (pp. 156–160).
- **Later development, individual differences, autism.** Older children mainly get faster and more accurate across all problems. Differences between people may come from executive function, social expertise or motivation (pp. 160–168).
- **Neuroscience.** High-level mindreading is a multi-region process. TPJ selectivity may reflect only the visible part of a much larger high-level process, or a low-level module triggered by stimuli it cannot process (pp. 168–171).
- **Other theories.** Earlier two-systems accounts are one-system, aimed at different targets, or underspecified. A single general mindreading module cannot support flexibility, and massive modularity does not explain efficiency. Simulation and theory theories neglect relevance (pp. 172–179).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 143)
Chapter 6 argued that adults have "low-level" processes (efficient, inflexible) and "high-level" processes (flexible, demanding). They also have much social knowledge and non-mindreading processes that underwrite everyday social cognition (p. 143). This chapter has two aims. The first is to see whether the model fits evidence that looked contradictory earlier. The second is to show the model is more than a description, for two reasons (p. 143):
- A complex model risks being too complex, able to fit any pattern.
- Explaining efficient mindreading by "efficient processes" looks circular unless independent criteria show which process is in use.

**Author's contention:** the independent criteria come from the fact that low-level processes must be limited (p. 143).

### Low-level processes for mindreading (p. 144)
Low-level processes gain efficiency through hard constraints on their input and on the operations done on it. If infants, animals or the efficient abilities of adults show such limits, that gives independent reasons for thinking a low-level modular process is at work. If the limits are similar across groups, the same module may be involved (p. 144). The analogy used throughout is number processing. Humans and animals have two efficient modules: precise enumeration of small sets, with a strict limit of 3–4 items, and approximate estimation of large sets, which gets less precise as set size grows (p. 144). The author illustrates the line of thought with his own work on Level-1 perspectives (p. 144).

### Level-1 perspectives as a case study

**Overview (p. 144).** The plan is to show the following:
- Automatic perspective processing is efficient, early-developing and limited to Level-1 perspectives.
- There is a prima facie case that infants and animals converge on similar limits.
- Such convergence adds to what we can say about the nature and origins of the process.

The author says details may be wrong; the case is a template.

**Processing characteristics in adults and children**

*Automatic and efficient? (p. 145).*
- Adults' explicit judgements about perspective are egocentric (biased toward their own view).
- The critical evidence is indirect. In Samson et al.'s task, adults found it harder to judge how many dots *they* saw when an avatar saw fewer dots than they did, compared with when the avatar saw all the dots. This is an "altercentric" (other-centred) interference effect.
- It occurred even though the avatar's view was never explicitly judged on other trials and participants could in principle have ignored the avatar.
- Qureshi et al. (2010) found that a simultaneous executive-function task did not interrupt the avatar's perspective processing. This suggests the processing is relatively automatic and cognitively efficient (p. 145).
- This fits a low-level, modular process for at least simple contents of "what someone sees" (p. 145).

*Original module or downward modularized process? (pp. 145–146).*
- Evidence from adults leaves provenance open.
  - An *original module* would have supported efficient Level-1 processing since infancy.
  - *Downward modularization* means that practice makes an effortful process efficient and relatively encapsulated.
- Original modularity predicts automaticity at all ages. Modularization predicts that it increases with age (p. 145).
- Surtees and Apperly (2010) tested children aged 6, 8 and 10, and adults. Older participants were faster, but equivalent altercentric effects appeared at all ages (p. 146).
- This gives no evidence of modularization, and is consistent with an original module. It cannot rule out that modularization was complete by age 6. A strong test needs methods for younger children (p. 146).
- The process would need inputs from upstream modules that identify agents, their eyes and hands, and where eyes look or hands touch. So Apperly describes not a module for Level-1 perspective-taking as a whole, but a module performing a specific and relatively late step in processing social stimuli (p. 146).
- Modules are invoked because encapsulation avoids burdens on memory and executive function, not out of commitment to nativism. Developmental change is still possible. The genetic versus environmental balance is an empirical question (p. 146).

*Processing limits? (pp. 146–148).*
- Modules gain efficiency at the cost of flexibility, so if the evidence is rightly read as showing an efficient perspective module, distinct limits must be expected. If the only limits were explicable by knowledge, experience or general capacity, that would count against a modular account (pp. 146–147).
- Apperly and Butterfill (2009) suggest that economy may come from representing relations between agents, objects and properties, rather than relations between agents and propositions (p. 147).
- **Prediction:** the low-level process handles Level-1 problems (what object or situation an agent sees). It also handles the "knowledge" and "false belief" tasks on which infants and animals succeed. It does not handle Level-2 problems (the particular way an agent sees something, e.g. an upside-down box), because these require an agent–proposition relation (p. 147).
- **Level-2 test** (Surtees, Apperly & Samson, 2010; Figure 7.1, p. 148):
  - A cartoon room has numerals on the wall or table. An avatar sits behind the table facing out, so he sees table digits rotated 180°. A "9" for the participant is a "6" for the avatar.
  - Symmetrical digits (0, 8) look the same to both and give the baseline (p. 147).
  - Children and adults showed *egocentric* interference when judging the avatar's view (slower and more error-prone on 6/9 than 0/8 trials).
  - They showed *no altercentric interference* when judging their own view (pp. 147–148).
- This is consistent with the low-level process not supporting the propositional content needed for Level-2 perspectives (p. 148).

> **Intuition note.** An efficient process buys speed by only accepting simple inputs. A way of seeing that is "Level-2-like" (as a 9, not a 6) needs richer content than "agent sees object". The avatar's Level-2 view does not slip into the participant's own judgement, which suggests the fast process cannot carry that richer content.

**The power of converging evidence on limits (pp. 148–153)**

*Caution (pp. 148–149).* The Level-2 result is a null result in one paradigm. It could reflect an incidental task feature. Or Level-2 processing might just be too demanding to be quick enough to cause interference. We also want to know whether the same process underlies infants' and animals' success, and how genes and environment shape it (pp. 148–149).

*Number modules (pp. 149–150).*
- Precise enumeration of small sets has a strict 3–4 item limit. It is found with many methods in human adults, human infants and non-human animals, so it is not an artefact of one paradigm.
- The limit is invariant across adults and infants, who differ in working memory and executive function. So it is not set by general capacity. It is a fact about a modular process (p. 149).
- This "signature limit" makes it possible to identify when behaviour is influenced by the module (only for numbers up to 3–4) and when it is not (p. 149).
- It helps decide whether an animal's number sensitivity is abstract enumeration or learned association with correlates like mass or surface area. If similar limits are seen in animals and humans, and the human data suggest abstract enumeration, the *parsimonious* conclusion is the same process in animals (p. 149).
- Stability of the limit across individuals in different environments suggests a high degree of genetic constraint. Stability across species in different niches suggests a relatively ancient genetic constraint (pp. 149–150).
- The limit is found in humans (Feigenson et al., 2004), macaques (Hauser & Carey, 2003) and fish (Dadda et al., 2009). It is "far from clear" what common environmental constraint could explain this, so a genetic origin seems more likely (p. 150).
- Note 4: this does not rule out environmental input to development. It adds that the environment would not have a critical role in constraining the module's architecture to a particular signature limit (p. 179).
- Note 3 offers a speculation. Grammatical number marking in languages (singular/dual/trial/plural) may reflect these cognitive limits. Evidential marking in languages (source of evidence) might reflect distinctions made by a low-level mindreading process (p. 179).

*Mindreading (pp. 150–153).*
- Researchers on infants and animals have seldom sought the *scope* of abilities. Showing surprising abilities counts as exciting, and pointing out limits as "curmudgeonly". Both are equally informative (p. 150).
- With caution about absence of evidence, chapters 2–3 give a prima facie case for convergence. Infants and animals show mindreading of some kind, but there is no evidence of sensitivity to Level-2 perspectives or to the particular way someone thinks about something (p. 150).
- Even the best infant false-belief evidence does not show that infants represent agents believing propositions. Tracking a Level-1 agent–object–property relation would suffice (p. 150).
- **Four benefits of granting convergence** (pp. 150–152):
  1. *Not a task artefact; not general resources.* Similar nulls from very different tasks and groups make an artefact less likely. Memory and executive resources vary widely across adults, infants and animals, so they are unlikely to explain the pattern. Being limited to Level-1 may be how a module is efficient. The signature limit would then mark when the module influences behaviour (pp. 150–151).
  2. *Evidence against deflationary explanations.*
     - **Deflationary view (Povinelli, Perner and colleagues):** infants and animals reason about behaviour in a sophisticated way but not about mental content (p. 151).
     - **Apperly's point:** this view may suit the infant and animal evidence, but it cannot explain the adult findings, because altercentric interference entails that adults process the avatar's mental content (the accounts were never meant to explain adult evidence). If adult limits converge with infant and animal limits, the same module may be at work. Then infants and animals may be processing the target's mental content and really mindread, albeit in the limited way the module allows (pp. 151–152).
  3. *Genes and environment.* Consistent limits across animals and adults in different learning environments would indicate similar processes under strong genetic constraint. Whether other species show the same limits is "an open question". Limits should be expected, and their nature will inform evolutionary origins (p. 152).
  4. *A tool for spotting the module in children and adults.* One place to look is communication. Evidence on adults using a speaker's visual perspective is mixed. A possible explanation is that taking the speaker's perspective is fast when the low-level module can do it, and not possible when Level-2 processing is needed (p. 152). Note 7 adds that Level-1/Level-2 cannot explain the mixed findings, since almost all those studies need only Level-1. The systematic difference is that participants succeed more when the number of objects in common and privileged ground is small. The module may also have a capacity limit of a few items (p. 180).
- **Summary.** Adults, infants and some animals *might* share a low-level process for Level-1 but not Level-2 perspectives. Its effects are likely to show in implicit measures (eye gaze, response-time interference), not in explicit judgements (p. 152).
- **General strategy, valid even if the Level-1 hypothesis is wrong:** "short of a cognitive miracle", efficient mindreading will bring inflexible limits. Limits not explained by lack of general resources or of learning opportunities suggest a low-level modular process (p. 152).
- Comparing limits across tasks and groups helps interpret infants' and animals' abilities. The author suspects other original mindreading modules may exist (p. 153).
- Note 5: not every efficient process is original. Automatic summing of symbolic digit strings depends on practice, and nobody expects it in untrained infants or animals. There the limits would point to modularization, not original modularity (pp. 179–180).
- Note 6: the Level-1/Level-2 distinction will probably not divide the cases exactly. There may be other arbitrary limits (p. 180).

> **Intuition note.** A limit that stays the same in an infant, an adult and a monkey is unlikely to be set by working memory, since these resources differ a lot between them. That makes it a good fingerprint of a specific process. Where limits are absent, behaviour may come from a different process.

### Why don't 3-year-olds pass false belief tasks? Egocentrism and explicit judgements (pp. 153–155)
- **The puzzle:** the low-level module is sophisticated enough for the Onishi and Baillargeon (2005) false-belief evidence. Yet 3-year-olds fail standard tasks that involve the same false belief about an object's location. How do infants avoid the egocentrism that dominates 3-year-olds' answers? (p. 153)
- The precocious evidence uses indirect measures (looking time, eye gaze). So does the adult evidence: altercentric interference, and listeners' eye movements in perspective-taking (Barr; Hanna & Brennan). Within the same 3-year-olds, explicit answers are wrong while anticipatory looking is correct (Clements & Perner, 1994; Ruffman et al., 2001) (p. 153).
- Calling low-level abilities "implicit" describes the pattern but does not explain it (pp. 153–154).
- **Explanation offered by Apperly:**
  - Modular processing behind an "informational firewall" may be free of influence from generally available knowledge. It may therefore be immune to egocentric bias, which is part of how it avoids needing executive resistance to interference (p. 154).
  - High-level mindreading builds a model of the target's mental states inside a model of the wider situation. This plausibly brings a tendency toward egocentric bias (p. 154).
  - **Assumption:** explicit judgements, as in standard tasks, necessarily recruit high-level mindreading, even when the problem is simple enough for the module. So success depends on having resources, even if the module processes the perspective correctly (p. 154).
  - **3-year-olds (Apperly's suggestion):** the eye movements show, on his account, the module processing the false belief, but the child cannot muster the resources to resist egocentric interference in the high-level process behind the explicit answer (p. 154).
  - Number analogy: children's incorrect explicit cardinality judgements versus infants' implicit abilities (Sarnecka & Lee, 2009) (p. 154).
- These cases are "informative anomalies", where the problem falls within the narrow range the module can handle. High-level processes are effortful and risk egocentrism, but give "an enormous extension" to what modules can do (pp. 154–155).
- Note 9: a child may fail a task for lack of executive resources rather than lack of the targeted mindreading ability (a modest claim of some performance accounts, e.g. Bloom & German, 2000). This does not bear on where the ability comes from (p. 180).

> **Intuition note.** The child may "know" the right answer in the fast system (the eyes go to the correct door) but speak from the slow system, which is pulled by what the child knows to be true. The explicit-task failure is then a failure of the slow system, not of the fast one.

### High-level mindreading (pp. 155–171)

**Division of labour (p. 155).** Most studies target high-level mindreading, which can mislead about its overall role. High-level mindreading is the best hope for explaining how we reason about diverse mental states as inferences to the best explanation (the normative account). But most everyday social work is *likely* underwritten by low-level processes plus social knowledge, since social life is regular enough for inflexible modules and for scripts, schemas, roles and habits. On Apperly's account, high-level mindreading is only made possible by richly structured social knowledge (p. 155). Note 8 adds that such suggestions about the critical but limited role of high-level mindreading are not new (e.g. Perner, 1991) (p. 180).

**Development (pp. 156–160).**
- The "theory of mind" view stresses acquiring concepts (belief, desire, knowledge). Apperly shifts emphasis away from this. He does not deny that concepts change. But it is unclear what owning a concept means or how it is put to use. And the view says little about older children and adults (p. 156).

*Executive function (pp. 156–158).*
- Two competing accounts: *emergence* (executive function is involved in development itself) versus *expression/competence* (it is involved in mindreading per se) (p. 156).
- Apperly says earlier chapters give strong evidence that executive function has a variety of roles in adult mindreading, as expression/competence accounts predict. This does not rule out emergence, which would need a unique developmental role not served in mature mindreading. No such evidence exists currently, but it may be found (pp. 156–157).
- Apperly says the adult evidence clearly indicates that at least one reason, and perhaps the only one, why executive function relates to mindreading in young children is that it is an integral part of mature mindreading (p. 157).
- The author rejects the premise of the question. Expression accounts presuppose concepts that children already have. He is doubtful about glossing infants' abilities in conceptual terms. He is also doubtful that a shortage of executive resources is the only thing separating infants from older children (p. 157).
- Instead, executive function development is an *enabling condition* for high-level mindreading. Children need enough capacity to maintain and elaborate a situation model that contains the background situation (reality or fiction) plus another person's perspective embedded in it. They also need control to avoid interference between the two (p. 157).
- Hence executive function is an integral part of children's mindreading competence. This draws on Perner (1991: increasingly sophisticated mental models) and Russell (1996: flexible thinking about alternative perspectives). Apperly adds the link to working memory and executive control, so the timetable of high-level mindreading is constrained by executive-function development (pp. 157–158).
- **Caution on child data (p. 158):** a link between a particular executive function and success on a task shows only that it was the *limiting factor* there. An executive function can be integral to mindreading without being the critical factor on a given task. So data from adults are informative even for those interested only in young children.

*Language and social experience (pp. 158–160).*
- Developmental data alone cannot say whether language is involved in emergence or is part of the mature mindreading ability (p. 159).
- Adult evidence: three patients with severe grammatical impairment (including PH, whose impairments extended to syntax that might matter for development) did very well on complex mindreading tasks that probably need high-level mindreading. So in adults, high-level mindreading does not appear critically dependent on grammatical language (p. 159).
- Grammar may then be scaffolding for emergence that can later be removed. Whether syntax effects can be separated from other language effects, or from general social interaction, is "hotly debated" (p. 159).
- Apperly suggests, as an alternative to the view that language and social interaction help children acquire mindreading concepts, that they make children *expert at understanding situations in a similar way to the people around them*. This is related to Hutto's narrative practices and Nelson's "community of minds". It does not reduce to them (pp. 159–160).
- Apperly believes the endowment of scripts and memories has a further role: identifying which information is likely to be relevant. The social origin of that information produces interpersonal agreement on relevance. This is the background for ad hoc ascription of mental states without running into intractable information processing (p. 160).

*Summary (p. 160).* Executive function enables increasingly sophisticated situation models. Expertise in identifying what is relevant, mediated by language and social interaction, lets relevant information be put into those models. Both continue to change after age 6, so both may explain continuing development (p. 160).

> **Intuition note.** Two ingredients are needed: a large enough "workspace" to hold reality and someone's view of it together, and good enough knowledge of what matters in a situation to fill that workspace sensibly. The first is enabled by executive development, the second built up through language and social experience.

**Changes in older children (pp. 160–163).**
- Little attention has been paid to change after standard tasks are passed. Work on "folk epistemology" exists, but it is unclear that these rarefied concepts explain the intuition that older children are just better at mindreading. Work on sarcasm and faux pas shows improvement between 6 and 9. This is hard to interpret if conceptual change is the engine, since it is not clear these need a more sophisticated concept of mental states (pp. 160–161).
- Three reasons to expect later change (p. 161):
  - Neural systems implicated in mindreading (mPFC, temporo-parietal regions) are among the latest to mature, with structural change into early adulthood. Apperly cautions that neural change may be consequence rather than cause.
  - Executive control and processing speed keep developing.
  - Children's social apprenticeship continues.
- *Late change on simple tasks* (Figure 7.2, p. 162). Two studies are compared: explicit Level-1 perspective judgements (Surtees & Apperly, 2010; ages 6, 8, 10, adults) and belief–desire reasoning (Apperly, Warren et al., in press; ages 6, 8, 11, adults). Three features (p. 163):
  1. Large age-related gains in speed of correct responses (Figure 7.2, p. 162; values read approximately from the bars: about 2,100–2,750 ms at age 6 down to roughly 750–1,100 ms in adults).
  2. Conflicting-perspective and false-belief conditions stay the most demanding into adulthood.
  3. Little evidence of disproportionate improvement on these harder problems. Change is mainly an overall gain in speed and accuracy, not a specific reduction in egocentrism.
- Note 11: an exception is Dumontheil, Apperly & Blakemore (2010), who found a disproportionate reduction in egocentrism between the oldest adolescents and adults (p. 180). Note 10: speed was the clearest variable, and accuracy showed analogous or non-contradictory effects (p. 180).
- Why speed matters: mindreading must arrive in time to be useful.
  - **Sam's party (p. 163):** Sam lists weekend plans without mentioning his birthday party. You might infer that he does not know of it; the point is whether you can do so quickly enough to avoid revealing the surprise.
  - Author's suspicion: faster high-level mindreading affects how far it is integrated with other activities and so how much it is used (p. 163).

**Individual differences (pp. 163–168).**
- The Empathising Questionnaire (EQ) mixes items on mindreading with items on social motivation, so it is hard to read in functional terms (pp. 163–164).

*Low-level (p. 164).* There is no evidence of variability in typical individuals. It might be low if processes are tightly constrained. But in number cognition, estimation accuracy for large sets varies across people and relates to symbolic number performance (Gilmore & Inglis, 2009). Similar effects may be found for mindreading (p. 164).

*High-level (pp. 164–166).*
- Depends on general resources, including executive control. Individual differences in these are well established, though relationships are likely complex (p. 164).
- Breadth and depth of social experience helps identify what is relevant. Visiting a country with a very different culture can give an extreme illustration of how dependent we are on this (pp. 164–165).
- **Matilda and the brioche (p. 165):** in a London park a posh child called Matilda asks for brioche to feed the ducks. The author assumes a stranger finds it funny for the same reasons he does. But there are other possible reactions: finding it funny as a mistake for "bread", or finding it offensive. Sharing a joke depends on agreeing about relevance. We move through different micro-cultures each day, each with its own scripts and norms (p. 165).
- Motivation may also vary. Apperly finds it puzzling that Baron-Cohen et al. contrast empathizing with systemizing, since on many accounts (Davidson, Dennett) the purpose of mindreading is to systematize the world of agents. From this perspective we might expect the best mindreaders to be high on both (p. 166).

*Autism (pp. 166–168).*
- Many researchers doubt a mindreading deficit is a general explanation of autism. High-functioning people often pass standard tests. Impairments in social interaction and communication still need explaining (p. 166).
- *Low-level.* Autism is strongly heritable, and mindreading is impaired out of proportion to general intellect. This suggests asking whether a mindreading module is impaired. The author rejects modularity as a *general* account of mindreading, but not a role for modular processes (p. 166).
- Standard-task difficulties in autism extend to matched control tasks, so the hypothesis looked unviable (pp. 166–167). Two recent studies (Senju et al., 2009, 2010) found impairment on an implicit, non-verbal, eye-movement false-belief task that typically developing 2-year-olds pass. Higher-functioning adults with autism were impaired on this task while performing typically on standard tasks (p. 167).
- It is "very plausible" this shows a deficit in a relatively low-level process related to mindreading. It also suggests low-level and high-level abilities are at least partially dissociable (p. 167). Note 12: whether it is a module for mindreading or some other low-level process remains open (p. 180).
- *High-level.* One interpretation (Senju et al., 2009): the social and communicative impairments all follow from a primary impairment in mindreading identified on the implicit false-belief task, and success on standard tasks reflects a coping strategy, as Happé suggested (p. 167).
- Apperly suggests a further factor. High-level mindreading uses generic situation-model building, which intelligent people with autism can do. But it also depends on expertise in identifying relevant information, built through social engagement. Differences in social ability and motivation, possibly compounded by growing up among relatives with similar traits, may reduce convergence with others on relevance. This could contribute to the difficulty of "setting the point" behind others' actions or words (pp. 167–168).

**Implications for the cognitive neuroscience of mindreading (pp. 168–171).**

*Multiple contributions (pp. 168–169).*
- High-level mindreading builds a situation model, with a model of what is in the target's head. It is shaped by background knowledge, judgements about the target, and open-ended monitoring that makes executive and memory demands. So it is unsurprising that it recruits many systems (p. 168). Note 13 addresses the apparent circularity of this argument (p. 180).
- Matches with regions: temporal poles for semantic knowledge such as scripts and norms. mPFC for ad hoc representations (problem-solving, episodic memory, introspection). Lateral prefrontal for inhibitory control. Bilateral TPJ for switching attention from salient stimuli, besides any more specific role (p. 168).
- Not every region is equally critical for each task. A patient with extensive mPFC damage passes standard tasks. A possible reading is that mPFC manages ill-structured everyday situations, whereas standard tasks are built to constrain interpretation (pp. 168–169).
- Activation also depends on what the control task demands. Temporal poles were not active in false belief vs "false" photograph stories. Either the simple stories need no integration with stored semantic knowledge, or both conditions need it equally. These can be tested by varying integration need independently of mindreading (p. 169). Note 14: Saxe's related work examined only regions identified by the localiser, so regions like temporal poles are not considered (p. 180).
- The primary question is not which region is the seat of mindreading but how multiple functions combine in an emergent phenomenon, tested with neuroimaging and neuropsychology using well-matched tasks (p. 169).

*Selective activity in TPJ (pp. 169–171).*
- R-TPJ shows selective activation for mental-state stimuli, and L-TPJ some selectivity for perspective differences. **Leslie's view (2005):** this indicates a mindreading module with its own neural seat (p. 170). Apperly offers two alternatives.
- **Alternative 1: R-TPJ reflects high-level demands.** Then its function is not "mindreading per se". Peeling away shared functions leaves something specific to mindreading, "but it will not be mindreading" (p. 170). What is left might be:
  - abstract semantic knowledge about mental states (note 15: this might be claimed as support for the idea that mindreading is having a theory of other minds, but such knowledge would be largely useless without flexible context-sensitive use, p. 181), or
  - social content modulating a generic function such as shifting attention.
  
  Either way, this tells us only about the visible part of a much larger set of processes (p. 170).
- **Alternative 2: R-TPJ reflects a low-level module.** This is less straightforward than it looks. Low-level modules would naturally be expected to be neurally localised, though this is not a strong requirement. But the tasks that best show R-TPJ selectivity require explicit responses to verbal stories, and the activity shows no sign of automaticity (Saxe, Schulz & Jiang, 2006). So if it is a module, it may be triggered by stimuli it cannot process as the task requires. The analogy is the approximate-number system being recruited for precise large-number arithmetic, though it is too imprecise to solve it (Dehaene, 2009) (pp. 170–171).

*Time course (pp. 171–172).*
- fMRI resolution is seconds, but adult mindreading response times are usually under one or two seconds and often a few hundred ms. EEG has millisecond resolution but poorer localization. fMRI gives a priori expectations of frontal, temporal and parietal sources. EEG also needs tasks that fix processes in time, so that one knows when to "start the clock" for ERPs (p. 171).
- Sabbagh & Taylor (2000) used short false-belief and "false" photograph stories and recorded from the response question. Scalp potentials differed within a few hundred ms, in left frontal and (less reliably) left parietal sites (p. 171).
- It is unclear what participants did at the test question: infer the mental state, or recall a state already inferred and encoded spontaneously. The method cannot tell these apart (pp. 171–172).
- Combining the behavioural methods of chapter 5 (which tease apart inferring, encoding, holding and using mental-state information) with EEG, MEG or TMS has great potential (p. 172; note 16, p. 181).

### Other theories (pp. 172–179)

**Other two-systems accounts (pp. 172–174).** Apperly and Butterfill (2009) said that existing accounts differ in three ways (p. 172):
1. *Superficial resemblance.* Leslie and colleagues, and Baillargeon and colleagues, describe sub-systems of a "theory of mind" module. They are compatible with the view here in that infants likely have more than one low-level process. But they characterize only multiple low-level processes, so they are "one-system" accounts and cannot fully explain mindreading (pp. 172–173).
2. *Different targets.* Tager-Flusberg & Sullivan (social cognition vs social perception), Gergely & Csibra (mindreading vs goal-directed action without mental states), Singer (mindreading vs empathy). These do not explain how ascription of the same mental states can be both efficient and flexible (p. 173).
3. *Underspecification.* Contrasts such as implicit/explicit (Dienes & Perner), intuitive/reflective (Keysers & Gazzola), levels of consciousness (Zelazo) and levels of representation (Karmiloff-Smith) may fit the low/high distinction. But they do not specify how these properties affect processing for mindreading (pp. 173–174).
- **Critics' charge:** Gigerenzer (2009) and Keren & Schul (2009) say two-systems accounts are often a "yin-yang list of dichotomies", a pseudo-explanation added after the data are known (p. 174).
- **Apperly's reply:** his account specifies independent criteria (signature limits) that follow from the theoretical analysis in chapter 6, where efficiency is traded for flexibility and low flexibility shows as limits. So it is a potential explanation, not just a description (p. 174).

**Modular accounts (pp. 174–176).**
- Apperly thinks some mindreading is done by modules in the sense of hard input and operation limits. They may be fast, automatic and relatively independent of general resources. Some may be strongly genetically constrained and neurally localised, while others may result from late modularization (pp. 174–175).
- But as a *general* account, modules have "exactly the wrong kinds of properties", since they cannot support open-ended, context-sensitive processing (p. 175).
- **Massive modularity** (Barrett & Kurzban; Carruthers; Pinker; Sperber): many modules with complementary restrictions, whose outputs feed further modular processing, might yield flexibility (p. 175). Carruthers (2006) ascribes mindreading to a *single* module, which Apperly thinks cannot work (p. 175).
- Massive modularity does not escape the flexibility–efficiency trade-off. Flexible processing needs repeated cycles through modules. This takes time, needs domain-general memory and probably executive control. So it could explain flexible mindreading but not efficient mindreading (pp. 175–176).
- Combining massive modularity for flexible mindreading with specialized efficient modules is possible in principle. The challenge is to generate predictions distinct from a non-modular account. It is "a world away" from the claim that mindreading per se is one domain-specific innate module (Baron-Cohen, Leslie, Segal), which Apperly thinks unlikely to be correct (p. 176).

**Simulation-theory and theory-theory (pp. 176–179; Figure 7.3, p. 177).**
- *Theory-theory:* mindreading depends on mental-state concepts and principles about their interactions. Initial information about the world and the target is run through reasoning steps to deduce a prediction of what the target will think, want or do. Apperly's account treats high-level mindreading as another reasoning problem, and he grants that humans have a conceptual grasp of mental states (p. 176).
- *Simulation-theory:* we use our own minds to simulate others. We marshal information about the target and their situation, and feed it into our own judgement and decision-making systems to produce judgements on the target's behalf, so no exhaustive theory is needed. The present account's emphasis on counterfactual or "imaginative" reasoning might comfort simulation theorists (p. 178). Note 17 says this comfort may be "false". Imagination drawing on first-person knowledge is compatible with theory-theory, since concepts and principles can operate over content drawn from first-person knowledge. Note 17 also cites Goldman's (2006) distinction of "theory-driven" from "process-driven" simulation. Apperly doubts that process-driven simulation describes most mindreading (p. 181).
- Both camps have struggled to generate distinctive empirical predictions. A theory converging on the same ground would be little advance (p. 178).
- **Main objection:** both debate mainly the *middle stage* of Figure 7.3, where starting information is processed by theory-driven reasoning or imagination. They neglect the computational problem of reasoning or imagination without intractability (chapter 6).
  - **Beer in the fridge (p. 178):** a target who thinks there is beer in the fridge and wants a beer may not go to the fridge (it is "just not clear" that she will necessarily), since logically she could do almost anything.
  - Identifying the target's starting state and situation raises the same plausibility and relevance problem. It is usually taken for granted, outside experiments that supply it. That it seems obvious is a testament to our ability to find relevant information, not an explanation of it (pp. 178–179).
  - Apperly's contention: how we do this should be at the centre of the psychological account. Most simulation and theory-theory accounts "dupe us" into thinking it is not a problem (p. 179).
  - Notes 18–19: process-driven simulation avoids the relevance problem only at the middle stage and faces it at the starting conditions. This is the kind of problem thought to underlie patient PF's difficulties (Samson et al., 2007) (p. 181).
- **Distinguishing features of Apperly's account (p. 179):**
  - We should be very worried by the potentially intractable computational problems.
  - People use two general kinds of process, low-level and high-level, which make mindreading tractable in different ways.

> **Intuition note.** Whether you reason by rules or by imagining yourself in the other person's position, you first need to know what to feed in: what the other person knows and what matters in their situation. Apperly says most such theories take this for granted, while on his account it is a central problem.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Purpose:** show how a "signature limit" works as an independent criterion, using invented numbers modelled on the dot-avatar logic.

Altercentric interference is the mean response time (RT) on trials where the avatar's view differs from the participant's, minus the mean RT where it is the same.

Invented data (ms), four trials per cell, participant judging their *own* view:
- **Level-1 task.** Inconsistent: 830, 810, 850, 790. Sum = 3,280; mean = 3,280 / 4 = 820. Consistent: 770, 750, 760, 760. Sum = 3,040; mean = 3,040 / 4 = 760. Interference = 820 − 760 = **60 ms**.
- **Level-2 task.** Inconsistent: 905, 895, 910, 890. Sum = 3,600; mean = 900. Consistent: 900, 905, 895, 900. Sum = 3,600; mean = 900. Interference = 900 − 900 = **0 ms**.

**Reading:** interference in Level-1 but not Level-2 matches the predicted signature limit. It would indicate a fast process that handles Level-1 content only. The criterion does not depend on how fast or efficient the participants are, since a fast overall RT alone would not show it. A caution from the chapter applies. A single 0 ms result is a null result in one paradigm and needs convergence from other tasks and groups (p. 148).

## Concepts
- [[two-systems-account]] — efficient-but-limited low-level processes plus flexible-but-costly high-level processes, with independent criteria from signature limits (pp. 143, 174)
- [[implicit-vs-explicit-tom]] — implicit measures (gaze, interference) may reveal low-level processing. Explicit judgements are assumed to recruit high-level processing (pp. 152–154)
- [[perspective-taking-level-1-level-2]] — Level-1: what an agent sees. Level-2: the particular way it appears (p. 147)
- [[modularity-tomm]] — here modules are informationally encapsulated, low-level and limited. Not a general account of mindreading, and not tied to nativism (pp. 146, 174–176)
- [[theory-theory]] — mindreading by concepts and principles about mental states. Apperly's account treats high-level mindreading as another kind of reasoning problem, so it is partly compatible (p. 176)
- [[simulation-theory]] — mindreading by running one's own judgement systems on the target's information. Apperly's account emphasises high-level mindreading as counterfactual or imaginative reasoning, which might comfort simulation theorists (p. 178)
- [[mentalizing-network]] — several regions (temporal poles, mPFC, lateral PFC, TPJ) are suggested to form a network for the flexible construction of situation models in high-level mindreading (p. 168)
- [[temporo-parietal-junction-tpj]] — R-TPJ selectivity read as part of high-level processing or as a low-level module triggered by unsuitable stimuli (p. 170)
- [[false-belief-vs-false-photograph-contrast]] — the localiser; its subtraction may leave only part of mindreading (pp. 169–170)
- [[mindblindness]] — the autism deficit is doubted as a general explanation. A low-level implicit false-belief deficit is reported (pp. 166–167)
- [[reality-bias-curse-of-knowledge]] — egocentric bias, suggested to afflict high-level model construction but not the module (p. 154)
- [[signature-limit]] (new) — a fixed limit on a module's processing (e.g. 3–4 items; Level-1 only) that marks its influence and does not follow from general resources (pp. 149, 151)
- [[altercentric-interference]] (new) — slowing of one's own-perspective judgement by an irrelevant other's perspective, an index of automatic processing (p. 145)
- [[original-module-vs-downward-modularization]] (new) — an efficient process present since infancy versus one made efficient by practice. Contrasting predictions about age (pp. 145–146)
- [[situation-model]] (new) — a model of the situation with the target's perspective embedded, the basis of high-level mindreading (p. 157)
- [[relevance-problem]] (new) — identifying what is plausible and relevant in a situation. Social knowledge and expertise help solve it (pp. 160, 178–179)
- [[massive-modularity]] (new) — many complementary modules chained together, which may give flexibility but not efficiency (pp. 175–176)
- [[emergence-vs-expression-accounts]] (new) — whether executive function is part of the developmental process or of mindreading itself (p. 156)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] signature limit as diagnostic of a specific process ↔ construct validity in LLM evaluation: a performance pattern used to test whether a model uses the same process as humans — both use an independent criterion rather than success alone.
