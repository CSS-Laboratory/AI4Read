# Evidence from neuroimaging and neuropsychology

## L0 — One line
Brain imaging and brain-injury studies together suggest that mindreading is a function of a network of several partly domain-general processes, not of one specialised module.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what fMRI and patient studies can tell us about the cognitive basis of mindreading. Imaging repeatedly finds a network (mPFC, TPJ, temporal poles, precuneus). Most of its regions are not specific to mindreading; right TPJ looks the most selective. Closely matched control tasks suggest that right TPJ tracks mental content, while left TPJ tracks perspectives in general. Patient studies add causal evidence: right lateral frontal damage can impair inhibiting one's own perspective. Left TPJ damage also impaired false-photograph tasks, not only belief tasks. Severe syntax impairment spared belief reasoning in three patients. Extensive mPFC damage spared simple mindreading in one patient. Each source has limits, but together they point to a multi-process network.

**Prerequisites:** the false-belief task, the false photograph control and Level-1/Level-2 perspective-taking (earlier chapters); the idea of an experimental control condition; the idea of a lesion (damaged brain area). Terms: fMRI = brain scan that tracks blood-oxygen changes; mPFC = medial prefrontal cortex; TPJ = temporo-parietal junction (L-/R- = left/right); localiser = a contrast used to pick regions of interest; domain-general = used for many tasks, not only mindreading; executive processes = control processes such as inhibition.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Imaging shows correlates of function, not that an activity pattern is causally necessary. Lesion studies allow stronger causal inferences, yet Apperly says just as much information about mindreading comes from patient studies as from imaging (pp. 58–59).
- A "mindreading network" (mPFC, TPJ, temporal poles, precuneus) is robust across stimuli and mental states. It is not specialised for mindreading, since these regions are also implicated in introspection, memory and reasoning (pp. 60–63).
- Well-matched controls (false photograph, false sign) divide the network. R-TPJ is selective for mental content; L-TPJ also responds to non-mental perspectives such as signs (pp. 64–67).
- Subtraction and localiser methods can be too restrictive. They may remove activity that is important for mindreading, such as executive and mPFC processes (pp. 67–71).
- Patient WBA suggests that right frontal executive processes are critically involved in resisting one's own perspective but may not be necessary for representing another's (pp. 76, 83).
- L-TPJ patients fail belief and false-photograph tasks alike. Patient PF's pattern suggests that L-TPJ does not simply "represent perspectives" (pp. 77–79).
- Three patients with selectively impaired syntax passed belief tasks, and one of them (PH) also passed a second-order task. Apperly concludes that grammatically structured representations are not necessary for thinking about beliefs (pp. 79–82, 84).
- Patient GT, with a large mPFC lesion, had intact simple mindreading. This challenges claims that mPFC is necessary, and fits mPFC mattering for novel or ill-structured problems (pp. 82–84).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 58–59)
- Social cognitive neuroscience has grown fast. Some people doubt that brain data can tell us anything about mindreading psychology. Apperly thinks this is "unduly pessimistic" but also sees reasons for caution (p. 58).
- Most of the informativeness of imaging depends on having clear hypotheses and good paradigms. Apperly argues that for mindreading, especially in adults, we are only just developing these (p. 58).
- Neuropsychology (study of brain-injured patients) has been eclipsed by imaging. This is a pity for two reasons (pp. 58–59):
  1. fMRI and PET give neural correlates. They do not show that an activity pattern is causally necessary. Injury patterns allow stronger causal inferences (p. 58).
  2. For sceptics of "neo-phrenology", lesion studies can be read as natural experiments on how psychological functions depend on each other. Example: three patients pass false-belief tasks despite severe grammar impairment. This is informative largely independently of the lesion location (all three had overlapping left fronto-temporal lesions) (p. 59).
- Overall picture: mindreading recruits a complex network. Some regions seem highly selective, but Apperly concludes that mindreading per se is a function of the network, not of specific, specialised regions (p. 59).

### Functional neuroimaging (pp. 59–72)

**Method (p. 59).** fMRI divides the brain into voxels (small cubic volumes) of 3–6 mm. It tracks blood oxygen, which lags neural activity. Temporal resolution is a few seconds, although a neuron may fire up to 200 times per second. Studies therefore tend to compare *where* activity occurs across conditions rather than fine timing. Most participants are adults, because scanning demands keeping still.

**Identifying activity of interest (pp. 59–60).** Task activity includes general perceptual and cognitive processing. The usual fix is subtraction: run a control task with the same general demands but no mindreading, then subtract. This works only if the tasks are well matched. What it reveals also depends on a good account of what mindreading involves (p. 60).

**The "Mindreading Network" (pp. 60–61).**
- Fletcher et al. (1995, PET) used verbal stories. In the mindreading story a robber drops his glove, a policeman tells him to stop, and the robber gives himself up. Readers presumably reason that the robber wrongly presumes the policeman knows he is a robber. Controls were "physical" stories (a burglar startles a cat, which sets off the alarm) and unconnected sentences (p. 60).
- Subtraction left four regions: medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), temporo-parietal junction (TPJ), temporal poles (TP) and precuneus/posterior cingulate (PC) (p. 60; Fig. 4.1, p. 61).
- The pattern is robust across cartoons, animations of squares and triangles, strategic games, and across beliefs, knowledge, desires and intentions (p. 60). This raises confidence that the network is not tied to visual or verbal tasks, or to false belief alone (p. 60).
- Apperly cites evidence that components of the network (especially mPFC) changed very recently in evolution, and that areas such as mPFC and TPJ are among the last to mature in development (p. 61).
- Some researchers call this the "theory of mind" or "mentalizing" network. Some take it as evidence for a specialised mindreading module (Gallagher & Frith, Leslie) (p. 61).
- Apperly's reply: in its strongest sense, this view would make imaging largely uninformative. If the network served only mindreading, and its parts were recruited equally for every kind of mindreading, then knowing it exists would tell us nothing about how mindreading works. Neither premise seems correct (pp. 61–62).

**Is the network specialised for mindreading? "No" (pp. 62–63).** Two ends of a spectrum:
- *Legrand & Ruby (2009):* the regions are also implicated in introspection, memory recall and reasoning in general, especially inductive reasoning and reasoning under vagueness or uncertainty. The regions are strongly interconnected. On this view mindreading is one of many domain-general functions (p. 62).
- *Frith (2007):* different regions have distinct but still domain-general, abstract functions (p. 62).
  - mPFC: orienting attention to incoming versus internally generated information, self-reflection, episodic memory, perhaps temporary event-script representations. Frith speculates that the mindreading part of mPFC may be most involved in "second-order" representations, perhaps also conscious experience.
  - TPJ: processing different perspectives, mental or spatial; perhaps related to reorienting attention.
  - Temporal poles: high-level semantic knowledge such as scripts.
- Frith's view assigns more specialisation than Legrand & Ruby's, but the two could add up to the same overall network. Either way, these regions are not a domain-specific module. The suggested roles hint at component processes of mindreading (p. 63).

*Intuition note:* A brain region that lights up during mindreading may also light up during memory or reasoning. So "active in mindreading" does not mean "used only for mindreading". The useful question is which general processes mindreading borrows.

**Is there functional differentiation within the network? "Yes", but most studies cannot show it (pp. 63–64).**
- Carrington & Bailey (2009) reviewed many studies. They found little systematic difference in activation by mental state, verbal versus non-verbal stimuli, paradigm, or explicit versus implicit mindreading (p. 63).
- Apperly gives reasons this may be too pessimistic (pp. 63–64):
  - Few studies compared different mindreading tasks within the same study and participants, so the conclusion rests on weak between-study comparisons.
  - Tasks are opaque. In Fletcher's story the obvious demand is judging the robber's false belief, but the task probably also involves intentions and emotions of both characters.
  - Controls may not match. The "physical" stories required inferences (the cat triggered the alarm), but these may not match the cascade of mindreading inferences.
- Progress came from simpler stimuli and tighter controls (p. 64).

**False beliefs versus "false" photographs (pp. 64–66).**
- Saxe & Kanwisher (2003, Exp. 2): participants read five story types (false belief, "false" photograph, desires, physical characteristics of people, physical scenes) and chose the word that completed a sentence. Examples are in Fig. 4.2 (pp. 64–65).
- False belief > "false" photo activated bilateral TPJ, PC and mPFC. Saxe and colleagues used this contrast as a *functional localiser*: it picks out regions selective for false beliefs, so later analyses can focus on them (p. 64).
- In Saxe & Kanwisher the criteria singled out left and right TPJ. These regions responded to desire stories but not to physical-scene or physical-characteristic stories. So they respond to mental states, not merely to people (p. 64).
- Further dissociations (pp. 64–65):
  - Saxe & Powell (2006): PC, and especially L- and R-TPJ, responded to stories about thoughts, not to stories about appearance or bodily sensations. mPFC showed little activity for any story type.
  - Saxe, Moran et al. (2006): a self-reflection task activated mPFC and PC but not TPJ.
  - Saxe & Wexler (2005), Young & Saxe (2008): R-TPJ was sensitive to when mental-state information appeared. It was also sensitive to re-using that information to reconcile it with background knowledge or to make a moral judgement. L-TPJ and PC showed a similar but less neat pattern. mPFC showed much less activation.
- Summary: R-TPJ, and less so L-TPJ and PC, respond selectively to written mental-state content. They respond to beliefs known to be true or false whether the belief is inferred or just described. This parallels 3- to 4-year-olds, who fail both inferring and simply remembering a told false belief. mPFC is indiscriminate or silent. This led to claims that R-TPJ may host a domain-specific module (e.g., Leslie, 2005) (p. 66).

**Further division: false signs and visual perspectives (pp. 66–67).**
- Caveat (from ch. 2): a "false" photograph is a physical, not mental, representation, and it is also not false. Perner et al. (2006) added a *false sign* condition (e.g., a sign to a monastery is turned to point at the golf course; Fig. 4.2). A false sign is genuinely false, so it creates a perspective difference for the participant, as a false belief does (p. 66).
- Their results (p. 66):
  - The false belief > "false" photo contrast replicated: bilateral TPJ, mPFC, PC, and middle and superior temporal gyri.
  - The R-TPJ regions from that contrast showed little activity on false-sign trials. This is consistent with R-TPJ being specific to mental states rather than perspective problems in general.
  - L-TPJ regions (and a small mPFC region) were more active for false signs than for "false" photos. This suggests these regions were not selective for mental states but were nonetheless selective for perspectives.
- Aichhorn et al. (2006) found the strongest activity in L-TPJ rather than R-TPJ during Level-2 visual perspective-taking (pp. 66–67).
- Apperly's summary (p. 67):
  - R-TPJ responds selectively to mental reasoning compared with closely matched tasks without mental content.
  - R-TPJ seems sensitive to *content* (e.g., a belief that the car is a Porsche). L-TPJ seems sensitive to the *form* of processing, so mindreading is one of several perspective-taking problems.
  - This resembles 3-year-olds, who talk about mental states but may fail false-belief tasks, which test understanding of beliefs as perspectives.
  - Mindreading may result from interactions among multiple systems, not from one homogeneous module. Even R-TPJ and L-TPJ together probably cover only some of its demands (p. 67).

*Intuition note:* The false-photo and false-sign controls act like pulling apart two ingredients of a false-belief task. One ingredient is "this is about a mind". The other is "this is a different view of the same facts". The two TPJs seem to track different ingredients.

**Limitations of subtractive methods and localisers (pp. 67–68).**
- The false belief − false photo (or false sign) subtraction is a precise instrument, perhaps "too precise" for some questions. It may subtract out processes that matter for reasoning about beliefs (p. 67).
- Functionally: belief reasoning often requires abductive and deductive inference and may well make demands on executive function. The control tasks are meant to match the form of thinking required, and so most, if not all, of the likely reasoning and executive demands. So studying what survives subtraction is not the obvious method for finding out which functions play a role (pp. 67–68).
- Neuroanatomically: localiser studies find a minor role for mPFC and none for the temporal poles, although other studies implicate both strongly. One possibility is that the less specific controls in those studies failed to remove irrelevant activity; another is that the localiser identifies only a subset of the systems involved (p. 68).

**Other investigations of the role of mPFC (pp. 68–70).**
- *Gilbert et al. (2007)* crossed attention type (perceptual maze versus imagined maze) with a mindreading manipulation. In non-mindreading blocks participants judged interval timing. In mindreading blocks they were told that an experimenter controlled the timing and judged afterwards whether the experimenter was helpful. The intervals were identical in both conditions (p. 68).
  - Findings: mindreading produced activation in mPFC and right temporal pole, not in either TPJ. Apperly notes it may be significant that the task needed only a general, somewhat emotive evaluation, not judgements about beliefs or desires (R-TPJ) or perspectives (L-TPJ) (pp. 68–69).
  - The mPFC regions for mindreading did not overlap with those for orienting attention to external stimuli (mindreading regions were more caudal, attention regions more rostral; note 9, p. 85).
  - Two readings: mPFC has a mindreading role independent of attention; or (the authors' alternative) mPFC orients attention in both cases and more caudal regions do so for social purposes. Apperly draws a parallel with R-TPJ, where nearby or overlapping regions serve mindreading and attention switching (p. 69).
- *Mitchell, Banaji & Macrae (2005):* participants rated how pleased a photographed person was (social), compared with rating facial symmetry (non-social). Ventral mPFC (also active in self-reflection studies) was selective for social judgements. In a post-test, it was proportionately more active for people rated similar to self (p. 69).
- *Mitchell, Macrae & Banaji (2006)* replicated this and found the inverse pattern in dorsal mPFC, more active for dissimilar others (p. 69). The authors read this as support for simulation theories; Apperly has argued the findings do not discriminate simulation from theory-theory (note 11, p. 85).
- Apperly's reading: the results highlight introspection as a possible source of information about others, applied depending on similarity to self (p. 69).
- Visual perspective-taking studies, where similarity-to-self is irrelevant, did not find mPFC activation (Aichhorn 2006; Vogeley 2004; Zacks 2003) (p. 70). So similarity-to-self may not be basic to all mindreading. It may be particularly important under uncertainty, narrowing the plausible set of thoughts (Jenkins & Mitchell, 2009; chapter 6) (p. 70).

**Is there life beyond the mindreading network? (pp. 70–71).**
- Peak activations for mindreading do not overlap with those for language or executive function (Frith & Frith; Saxe, Carey & Kanwisher), though some reviewers disagree. Apperly urges caution because the controls may be too restrictive (p. 70).
- *Saxe, Schulz & Jiang (2006)* ran the false belief − false photo localiser plus an executive localiser (response selection and inhibitory control; a stimulus–response incompatibility task, note 12, p. 85). There was almost no overlap. This fits either "mindreading does not need executive function" or "the subtraction removed executive activity" (p. 70).
- A further simple mindreading task, compared with a baseline using the same stimuli, activated every region of the inhibitory-control network. So mindreading also recruits regions selective for executive function. Looking only at the false belief − false photo localiser would have missed this (pp. 70–71).
- *Vogeley et al. (2001)* adapted Fletcher's stories so that the participant was an agent in them (e.g., the owner of the robbed shop). Only one region was selectively activated for mindreading judgements when the self-perspective was salient: right lateral prefrontal cortex. It is outside the mindreading network but regularly implicated in executive function. This indicates that mindreading may require inhibition of self-perspective, met by generic executive processes (p. 71).

*Intuition note:* A tight localiser is a good way to ask which regions are special to mindreading. It is not the obvious way to ask which regions mindreading uses. That second set can include general-purpose regions that also do other jobs.

**Time-course and automaticity (pp. 71–72).**
- fMRI is too slow for time-course questions (chapter 7). It can test automaticity: do stimuli that afford mindreading necessarily activate the network? (p. 71)
- Saxe, Schulz & Jiang (2006) used the same visual sequence in two conditions. An object moved into one box and then switched to another. A cartoon character faced the boxes during the first movement. She either saw the switch (true belief) or turned away (false belief) (p. 71).
  - *Mindreading condition:* judge which box the character thought held the object. This activated mindreading-network regions.
  - *Non-mindreading condition:* the same stimuli, with a facing/not-facing rule that was logically equivalent. No mindreading-related activation was observed, although the stimuli afforded mindreading and it was not required. This implies that such stimuli do not drive mindreading automatically (pp. 71–72).

**Conclusions from neuroimaging (p. 72).**
- A network is consistently involved in mindreading in adults. Its maturation may relate to children's success (Sabbagh et al., 2009).
- R-TPJ activates in a highly domain-specific way for mindreading content. It remains unclear whether this reflects processes unique to mindreading or a domain-general process (such as attention reorienting) applied to mental content.
- Other regions are clearly involved and are not unique to mindreading:
  - L-TPJ: the perspectival nature of mental states, and non-mental perspectives such as signs.
  - Right lateral prefrontal cortex: likely inhibiting self-perspective.
  - mPFC: introspection, strategic attention to external information, episodic memory, event scripts.
  - Temporal poles: personal and semantic memory, schematic representations, integration with emotional information.
- This supports the chapter 6 point that mindreading depends on general abilities to parse situations for relevant information (p. 72).

### Neuropsychological studies (pp. 73–84)

**Aims (p. 73).** Lesion studies can show whether a region is actually *necessary*, which overcomes the correlational nature of imaging. This matters for cognitive accounts only to the degree that we trust the region–function link. They can also test whether severe loss of a function (executive function, language) necessarily impairs mindreading. This does not require neat region–function mappings (p. 73).

**Can brain injury impair mindreading? (pp. 73–74).**
- Mindreading tasks make demands on language, memory and executive processing unrelated to mindreading. Brain injury often impairs social function and mindreading performance. The challenge is to separate the possible reasons for error (p. 73).
- Early studies used mildly impaired patients and complex, subtle tasks. Stone et al. (1998): frontal-lesion patients made few errors on first- and second-order false-belief tasks. Patients with orbito-frontal lesions did worse than controls on faux-pas stories (p. 73). Other studies used lies, irony, double bluff, white lies, persuasion, social misunderstandings and verbally explained cartoons (pp. 73–74).
- Each study reported mindreading impairment in patients compared with matched controls. They variously implicated the right hemisphere, bilateral orbito-frontal regions, left anterior regions, the amygdalae and temporal poles. Together they give no consistent picture (p. 74).
- Two impediments: mindreading demands are confounded with language, executive and memory demands, and the tasks are opaque about their underlying processes (p. 74).

**A task analysis of false belief tasks (pp. 74–75).** Apperly's group built simpler tasks from three considerations (p. 74):
1. *Incidental demands:* verbal presentation, following event sequences, keeping track of locations and who saw what. They reduced language and simplified sequences, and added matched control trials (p. 74).
2. *Specific demand:* the participant usually knows the true state of reality and must resist its interference when judging Sally. This corresponds to self-perspective inhibition ("executive expression" accounts, ch. 2). It is almost ubiquitous but not necessary, so they manipulated it (p. 75).
3. *Perspective-taking:* constant across tasks. The interest was whether patients showed impairment not due to the incidental demands or to self-perspective interference (p. 75).

**Manipulating self-perspective inhibition (p. 75).**
- *High-inhibition task:* a standard unexpected-transfer false-belief task. In false-belief, control and filler trials the participant always judges which box Sally will open. Warm-up trials establish this principle, after which test trials are language-free. Many trials allow comparison with chance (p. 75).
- *Low-inhibition task:* adapted from Call & Tomasello (1999). The participant must work out which of two boxes holds the object, using Sally's clue, which may reflect a true or false belief. The participant does not know where the object is, so there is less need to inhibit own knowledge (p. 75).

**Executive function and self-perspective inhibition (pp. 75–77).**
- In an initial study with the reality-unknown (low-inhibition) task (Apperly et al., 2004), 4 of 12 patients were not above chance on false-belief trials or on matched control trials. All four had frontal lesions and some impairment on memory and executive tests. This supports a role for executive function and frontal structures in meeting the incidental demands of false-belief tasks (pp. 75–76).
- *WBA* (large right frontal lesion, impaired working memory and executive function including inhibition) scored 11/12 on low-inhibition false-belief trials (p. 76). On the high-inhibition task he scored 1/12, below chance, while at ceiling on controls. This is the egocentric pattern of many 3-year-olds. His egocentric bias also appeared on a 3-option false-belief task, a football-supporter desire/emotion task and a Level-2 visual perspective-taking task (p. 76).
- His success on the low-inhibition task suggests he can take another's perspective. His difficulty lies in resisting interference from a salient own perspective. WBA's lesion includes right lateral prefrontal regions implicated in imaging. Apperly sees this as converging evidence that executive function and right prefrontal cortex are implicated in inhibiting self-perspective (p. 76).
- Exception: WBA made no egocentric errors on Level-1 visual perspective-taking, although self-perspective should be no less salient than at Level 2. This raises the possibility that Level-1 differs in its cognitive demands (pp. 76–77).

*Intuition note:* WBA's success on the low-inhibition task suggests he can represent what someone else thinks. His problem appeared when his own perspective was salient and in the way. The error is "responding from my view", not "being unable to represent your view".

**Domain-specificity and the role of L-TPJ (p. 77).**
- Of the initial 12 patients, three showed impaired belief reasoning despite ceiling performance on matched control trials. All three had overlapping lesions to left TPJ, converging with imaging (p. 77).
- A non-verbal false-photograph task was devised to match the low-inhibition task. A photo is taken showing the object in its original location, the boxes are swapped, and the photo is then given as a clue (Fig. 2.5b). The photo is genuinely false and behaves like a false sign (p. 77).
- All three L-TPJ patients showed the same impairment as on the false-belief task (poor on false photo trials, above chance on controls; Apperly et al., 2007). Note 15 reports that all 11 of the original 12 patients who could be tested showed a similar pattern on the two tasks (p. 85). Their errors were not due to general task difficulty, yet they generalised beyond mindreading to structurally similar tasks. This fits imaging (L-TPJ active for false belief, false sign and, less so, "false" photo) and a domain-general perspective-taking ability linked to L-TPJ (p. 77).

**There is more to perspective-taking than representing a perspective (pp. 77–79).**
- Apperly warns of an illusion of progress from replacing one poorly understood thing with another: calling L-TPJ the seat of perspective-taking or meta-representation would not explain how perspective-taking is achieved (pp. 77–78).
- *PF* (L-TPJ lesion, least general impairment) was above chance on the non-verbal high-inhibition task. So her difficulty is not with representing perspectives per se, nor with resisting self-perspective (p. 78). Note 16 gives a double dissociation: PF failed low-inhibition and passed high-inhibition, while WBA did the reverse. So it is difficult to argue that WBA's success on the low-inhibition task was just a consequence of that task being easier (p. 85).
- *3-choice false-belief task* (unexpected contents): a pizza box turns out to contain a passport, which is swapped for scissors. The character saw the passport but not the scissors. Two wrong answers are possible: egocentric (scissors) or appearance-based (pizza) (p. 78).
  - PF's errors were almost never egocentric. She often chose the box's appearance (pizza). WBA made strong egocentric errors on the same task, as do young children on a similar task (p. 78).
  - PF made all her errors in the first block and none in the second after a short break (p. 78).
- **Apperly's speculation:** the literature has focused on how to *hold* another's perspective in mind, and has overlooked how we work out what the other's perspective is, or how we decide to take it. The low-inhibition false-belief and false-photo tasks require deciding *when* to monitor perspectives. The 3-choice task requires deciding *what* information is relevant (past perceptual access versus current misleading appearance). PF's pattern suggests difficulty with both. The second block suggests she could eventually identify the relevant information (pp. 78–79).
- He suggests the 3-choice task models two everyday circumstances. In very novel situations, finding when and what takes effort (PF's first block). In highly familiar situations it is "just obvious" what others think (second block) (p. 79).
- It is unclear whether her difficulty comes from a high-level executive impairment, inaccessible social knowledge or something else. Her case does not support the claim that perspective per se is represented in L-TPJ (p. 79).

**The role of syntax in mindreading (pp. 79–82).**
- In the first study, five patients who passed control trials of the non-verbal task failed control trials of a verbal six-line-story task, so their verbal false-belief answers could not be interpreted. Language can burden patients' comprehension (p. 79).
- Previous work (Varley & Siegal, 2000; Varley, Siegal & Want, 2001) found two patients with disproportionately impaired syntax who seemed to pass false-belief tasks. Apperly's group added patient *PH* (left-sided lesion, impaired syntax, other language relatively spared) and tested him on fully non-verbal first- and second-order tasks (pp. 79–80).
- *Verbal stories without embedded complements:* PH made no errors on control trials and was above chance on false-belief questions (p. 80).
- *Embedded complement syntax* ("He thinks there's a red ball in the box but really there's a blue ball in the box"): PH judged the real colour correctly but was not above chance on what the man thought. A false belief inside an embedded complement clause was not understood (p. 80).
- PH had no difficulty choosing to believe a speaker who used more certain mental verbs ("assert") over less certain ones ("suppose"). He failed relative-clause and adverbial-clause sentences. He failed syntax tests passed by 3- to 4-year-olds, including the aspects implicated in mindreading development (p. 80).
- *Non-verbal tasks:* one error in total on the first-order tasks (low- and high-inhibition). A non-verbal *second-order* task (Fig. 4.3): Sally gives a clue to Granny and sees the boxes being swapped, but Granny does not. Granny falsely thinks that Sally has a false belief, so Granny searches the box Sally does *not* indicate. PH made no errors (p. 81).
- **Conclusion:** adults' ability to reason about beliefs can remain fundamentally intact despite severe syntax impairment. This follows for syntax only. PH's case gives no evidence on semantics (p. 81). His impairment still limits his access to much everyday talk about what people think. The case undermines claims that false-belief reasoning is fundamentally dependent on syntax (Carruthers, 2002; de Villiers & de Villiers, 2000) (pp. 81–82). The contrast with children is taken up in chapter 7 (p. 81).

*Intuition note:* PH cannot understand a sentence like "he thinks that…" but can still predict what a person with a false belief, or a belief about another's belief, will do. So reasoning about beliefs does not seem to need the grammar used to talk about beliefs.

**Is mPFC necessary for mindreading? (pp. 82–83).**
- mPFC is consistently active in imaging and often singled out as most important. Yet patient *GT* (Bird et al., 2004), with a very extensive bilateral mPFC lesion, showed no mindreading impairment (p. 82). She passed first-order false belief and stories with double bluffs, persuasion, white lies, norm violations and faux pas. She matched controls at attributing mental states to animated geometric shapes (p. 82).
- GT had non-social impairments: auditory working memory, autobiographical memory, planning and inflexibility on some executive tasks. Her inhibitory control was normal (p. 82).
- Comparison with WBA: WBA had a mindreading deficit (self-perspective inhibition) and a general non-social inhibition deficit, with a lesion extending to right lateral cortex. GT had neither deficit, and her lesion is largely bilateral medial PFC. The two cases are not contradictory (p. 82).
- Interpretation depends on lesion extent. Bird et al. caution that GT's lesion may not overlap every mPFC peak found in imaging. Still, a large part of mPFC is affected, which questions claims that mPFC is necessary (pp. 82–83).
- Two possible resolutions: mPFC is necessary for the *development* of mindreading but not for adult performance (Bird et al.); or it remains necessary in adults but not for the tasks used (p. 83).
- **Apperly's speculation (labelled as such):** mPFC processes (introspection, online event representations, redirecting attention) may matter most for novel or complex problems where social scripts give no straightforward answer. GT's tasks could be done with intact scripts and an intact ability to represent others' minds as distinct from one's own. Support: mPFC activity was higher when the belief or desire to infer was poorly specified (Jenkins & Mitchell, 2009) (p. 83).

**Conclusions from neuropsychology (pp. 83–84).**
- WBA converges with imaging: executive processes (right fronto-lateral cortex) are critical for resisting one's own perspective, but may not be necessary for representing another's (p. 83).
- Evidence suggests that L-TPJ is necessary for perspective-taking of all kinds and is not specific to mindreading. PF indicates that "represents perspective" may be an over-simplification, and hints at the hidden complexity of working out what people think and when this is useful (pp. 83–84).
- GT seems at odds with imaging results on mPFC in simple mindreading, but is consistent with mPFC mattering for complex, ill-structured problems (p. 84).
- No study has tested patients with lesions restricted to R-TPJ. Expectation: they should show highly specific mindreading disruption. TMS disruption of R-TPJ impaired moral decisions that depend on a protagonist's beliefs and intentions (Young et al., 2010). A direct mindreading test would be reassuring (p. 84).
- Three patients with selectively impaired syntax suggest that grammatically structured representations are not necessary for thinking about beliefs, even beliefs about beliefs. Whether the same holds for impaired semantics is an open question (p. 84).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Arbitrary activation units for three regions in four conditions: false belief (FB), "false" photo (FP), false sign (FS) and a plain baseline (B = 1 everywhere).

| Region | FB | FP | FS | FB−FP | FB−FS | FB−B |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | 6 | 2 | 2 | 4 | 4 | 5 |
| R2 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 0 | 0 | 4 |
| R3 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 3 | 0 | 3 |

Reading it with the chapter's logic:
- R1 survives both subtractions (6−2 = 4; 6−2 = 4). It is content-selective, like R-TPJ.
- R3 survives FB−FP (4−1 = 3) but not FB−FS (4−4 = 0). It responds to perspective differences in general, like L-TPJ.
- R2 vanishes in both subtractions (5−5 = 0). Against baseline it is clearly active (5−1 = 4). A localiser would miss it, as the chapter says of executive regions.
- Even R1 is only a *correlate*. To show that R1 or R2 is *necessary*, one would need patients with damage there, and R2's damage might impair FB and FP trials alike.

## Concepts
- [[mentalizing-network]] — the set of regions (mPFC, TPJ, temporal poles, precuneus) repeatedly active in mindreading tasks; here the claim is that it is not specific to mindreading (pp. 60–63)
- [[temporo-parietal-junction-tpj]] — here split into R-TPJ (mental content) and L-TPJ (perspectives in general, also signs) (pp. 66–67)
- [[medial-prefrontal-cortex-mpfc]] (new) — frontal midline region linked to introspection, attention direction, event scripts; its necessity for mindreading is questioned (pp. 62, 82–83)
- [[false-belief-vs-false-photograph-contrast]] — fMRI localiser separating belief representation from representation in general (p. 64)
- [[false-sign-task]] (new) — genuinely false physical representation (e.g., a turned sign); a closer control for perspective differences (p. 66)
- [[functional-localiser]] (new) — a contrast used to pick out regions of interest for further tests; may be too restrictive (pp. 64, 67–68)
- [[subtraction-method]] (new) — subtracting control-task activity to isolate mindreading-specific activity (pp. 59–60)
- [[neural-correlate-vs-necessity]] (new) — imaging shows correlates; only lesion evidence can show that a region or function is necessary (p. 58)
- [[self-perspective-inhibition]] (new) — resisting interference from one's own knowledge when judging another's view; linked to right lateral prefrontal cortex (pp. 71, 75–76)
- [[reality-bias-curse-of-knowledge]] — candidate link only: the chapter describes WBA's egocentric responding (judging from his own point of view) and does not use this label (p. 76)
- [[double-dissociation]] (new) — PF fails where WBA passes the low-inhibition task, and the reverse for the high-inhibition task (p. 85, note 16)
- [[perspective-taking-level-1-level-2]] — WBA erred at Level 2 but not Level 1 (pp. 76–77)
- [[first-order-false-belief]] / [[second-order-false-belief]] — PH passed non-verbal versions of both (p. 81)
- [[unexpected-transfer-task]] — the high-inhibition task is a standard version (p. 75)
- [[unexpected-contents-task]] — the 3-choice task extends it (p. 78)
- [[embedded-complement-syntax]] (new) — clause structure used to report mental states ("he thinks that…"); PH could not understand it (p. 80)
- [[modularity-tomm]] — the view that a specialised mindreading module exists; imaging evidence is argued not to support its strong form (pp. 61–63)
- [[simulation-theory]] / [[theory-theory]] — mentioned only in note 11: the mPFC similarity findings do not, in Apperly's view, discriminate them (p. 85)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Functional localiser (test only a pre-selected region set; note 6, p. 84) ↔ statistics: pre-registering a subset of hypotheses to cut the multiple-comparison correction. The restriction raises power but may exclude real effects.
- [structural-analogy] Over-matched control subtracting out what matters (p. 67) ↔ regression: controlling for a covariate that is part of the causal pathway removes the effect of interest.
- [structural-analogy] Self-perspective inhibition (p. 75) ↔ theory-of-mind canon, reality bias / curse of knowledge: one's own true knowledge pulling judgements about others' beliefs.
- [metaphor] Similarity-to-self narrowing the plausible thoughts of others under uncertainty (p. 70) ↔ Bayesian theory of mind: priors that constrain inference over mental states.

## Questions this raises
- If R-TPJ is the most content-selective region, what happens to mindreading in patients with lesions confined to R-TPJ? (p. 84)
- Is R-TPJ activity unique to mindreading, or is it domain-general attention applied to mental content? (pp. 69, 72)
- How do people decide *when* to take another's perspective and *what* information is relevant, and which brain systems do this? (pp. 78–79)
- If syntax is not needed for belief reasoning in adults, what role does it play in children's development (chapter 7), and what about semantics? (pp. 81, 84)
