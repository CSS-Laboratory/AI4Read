# Evidence from infants and non-human animals

## L0 — One line
Infants and some animals behave as if they track what others see, know and (for infants) believe, but this evidence does not show that they grasp belief "as such", and its cognitive basis remains unclear.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what indirect behavioural evidence from human infants and non-human animals (mainly chimpanzees, also scrub-jays) shows about mindreading. Infants (12–18 months in the studies reviewed) look and act as if they track others' knowledge and false beliefs. Chimpanzees seem to track what others see and have seen, but not false belief. Apperly argues that these abilities cannot rest on much language or executive function. Innate-concept and simpler-concept readings leave gaps; the behaviour-rule reading is not yet ruled out. None of the data shows an understanding of belief as such. The lesson is to study the limits of these abilities as well as their competence, because mindreading may be possible without the adult concepts.

**Prerequisites:** the unexpected transfer false-belief task (Sally and the object); the idea of a "concept"; "executive function" as flexible, effortful self-control; Level-1 vs Level-2 perspective-taking from chapter 2; "mindreading" = ascribing seeing, knowing and believing to others; violation-of-expectation = infants look longer at events that break a principle; the normative account = belief understood as a relation between an agent, an attitude and a proposition, with seeing, knowing and believing interdependent; convergent evolution = similar abilities evolving separately.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Infants and animals have little language and executive function, so we can assume that mindreading seen in them does not depend heavily on these resources. The measures are indirect (looking, requests, food competition) (p. 35, p. 39).
- Infants of 12–15 months look longer when an agent acts against her (false) belief, as in Onishi and Baillargeon's violation-of-expectation study. Other work shows sensitivity to knowledge, to what agents can see, and to false belief in helping at 18 months (pp. 36–38).
- Three readings of the infant data (innate concepts, simpler concepts such as "engagement", behavioural rules) are discussed. Apperly judges the innate and simpler-concept accounts incomplete, because they do not explain how infants use the concepts. The behavioural-rules reading says the evidence does not show mental-state reasoning; Apperly says evidence of coherence or of mental content could address it (pp. 39–43).
- Povinelli's argument holds that inferring a mental state from behaviour, or behaviour from a mental state, presupposes a behavioural rule, so one could map directly from behaviour to behaviour; he holds that the pure behaviour reading is the more parsimonious. Children differ because their abilities are coherent across tasks and they talk about mental content; infants have not yet been shown to meet these tests (pp. 41–43).
- Infant number cognition is the cautionary precedent: early "concepts" turned out to be specialised, limited processes. Mindreading in infants may be similar. Current data do not show a concept of belief as such (pp. 43–45).
- Chimpanzees show evidence of Level-1 perspective-taking and of tracking who has seen what. They show no evidence of Level-2, of understanding hearing leads to knowing, or of predicting a false-belief search (pp. 46–50).
- The view that chimpanzees understand perception and knowledge but not belief fits the normative account badly. On that account the concepts are interdependent, and understanding seeing would support both Level-1 and Level-2 perspective-taking (p. 52).
- Western scrub-jays show evidence that appears as strong as that from closer relatives; such abilities are generally assumed to reflect convergent evolution. The cognitive basis remains unexplained, but mindreading is possible "somehow" without much language or executive function (pp. 54–56).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 35)
Apperly groups infants and non-human animals for three reasons (p. 35):
- Both lack language and executive function. If mindreading is seen in them, we can assume it does not depend heavily on these resources.
- Both are studied with indirect behavioural measures, not explicit judgements, so they raise similar questions about what counts as evidence of mindreading.
- In other domains (number, space), infant–animal comparisons have been very informative. Apperly thinks the mindreading literature is only starting to use them.

### Human infants

#### Indirect measures (pp. 35–39)
**Gaze in 2–3-year-olds.** Garnham, Perner and colleagues gave 2- and 3-year-olds the unexpected transfer task. They recorded gaze when the experimenter asked "I wonder where she's going to look?" (p. 36).
- Few children judged correctly where Sally would look.
- Many incorrect-judging 3-year-olds nonetheless looked longer at the door from which Sally would exit towards the box where she wrongly believed the object was (p. 36).
- This was read as early "implicit" awareness of false belief. Supporting evidence: it did little to shake children's confidence in their wrong explicit answer (p. 36).
- It was absent in the younger group (age garbled in the text layer), so it could be seen as an early stage of the 3–4-year transition (p. 36).

**Southgate et al. (2007).** A non-verbal version showed 2-year-olds' looking anticipated Sally's incorrect search. Explicit judgements were not tested because no study has found 2-year-olds passing standard false-belief tasks. This widens the gap between explicit and indirect measures (p. 36).

**Requests and helping.**
- O'Neill (1996): a toy was put out of reach while the mother either attended or did not (out of the room, or eyes and ears covered). When she had not witnessed it, children were significantly more likely to point at the toy, name it or name its location. Their request strategy varied with the mother's knowledge state (pp. 36–37).
- Similar sensitivity at 12–14 months if the mother actively plays with the child and toy (Moll & Tomasello; Tomasello & Haberl) (p. 37).
- 18-month-olds take account of someone's false belief when helping (Buttelmann et al.) (p. 37).

**Violation-of-expectation paradigm.** An event is acted out so that its outcome either fits or violates a principle. If infants look longer at the violating outcome, the principle guides their expectations (p. 37).

**Onishi and Baillargeon (2005)** (Figure 3.1, p. 38) (p. 37):
- Fifteen-month-olds were first familiarised with Sally placing an object in one of two boxes (say the round one) and reaching to retrieve it.
- In the false-belief test, Sally puts the object in the round box and leaves. The object moves to the square box. Sally returns and searches in the round box (consistent with her belief) or the square box (inconsistent).
- Infants looked significantly longer when her search was inconsistent with her false belief.
- In a true-belief condition (Sally saw the move) the pattern reversed: they looked longer when she searched the wrong box.
- With controls, this suggests infants' expectations are sensitive to whether Sally's belief is true or false.

**Not an isolated result** (pp. 37–38):
- Surian et al. (2007) found similar results at 13 months.
- Song et al. (2008): infants expected a false belief could be corrected if Andrew told Sally where the object was.
- Song and Baillargeon (2008): similar expectations when Sally saw a misleading situation (a box looked as if it contained a doll but held a skunk).
- Luo and Baillargeon (2007) and Sodian et al. (2007): infants of 12–14 months take account of what an agent can or cannot see.

Apperly says the abilities found so far may turn out to be only a small part of what infants can do. The results fit naturalistic observations of rich infant–caregiver social behaviour. They are not far from the results on simple visual perspective-taking and on sensitivity to the mother's knowledge when communicating (pp. 38–39). He concludes that infants show sensitivity to others' mental states in at least some circumstances. The open question is how (p. 39).

*Intuition note.* Infants cannot answer questions, so researchers measure what they look at or do. Looking longer at "surprising" events suggests infants expected something else. The 15-month-old study used events closely modelled on the false-belief task given to 3–4-year-olds, but it measures looking, not explicit judgements.

#### Explanations of infants' abilities (pp. 39–45)
**Common ground.** Infants of 12–15 months have almost no productive language, very limited receptive language, and executive function different in quantity and kind from 3–4-year-olds. So language and executive function cannot feature in an explanation of what infants do. If infants have mindreading concepts, those resources played little role in acquiring them. Whatever processing yields infants' expectations cannot need much of them (p. 39).

**(a) Innate mindreading concepts?** (pp. 39–40)
- Onishi and Baillargeon, and others who already thought belief and knowledge must be innate (Fodor; Leslie; Surian et al.), favour this view (p. 39).
- Innateness does not explain how concepts are put to work. Previously these researchers assumed that using innate concepts often made significant demands on executive function.
- Leslie's version: the "theory of mind module" only generates candidate beliefs. Ascribing a particular false belief needs selection among them, which needs an executive capacity not developed until 3–4 years (pp. 39–40).
- Apperly's objection: nobody has explained why children's reasoning with an innate belief concept requires executive control while infants' does not. Non-verbal tasks do not help on their own, because removing verbal demands does not make false-belief tasks easier for 3–4-year-olds. So this reading "creates as many problems as it solves" (p. 40).

**(b) Simpler mindreading concepts?** (p. 40)
- **Moll and Tomasello, O'Neill:** younger children do not track the mother's knowledge as such but her "engagement" (O'Neill) or "experience" (Moll and Tomasello). This is a relation between agent and object or situation that co-varies with knowing in useful circumstances.
- It would not support judgements about whether she "knows" and would not meet normative standards for knowledge formation.
- **Apperly:** moving towards infants' abilities being distinct from older children's is a step in the right direction. But these accounts do not say why engagement or experience are easier to grasp or process than perception, knowledge and belief. They give new words, not a theory.

**(c) No mindreading concepts (and no mindreading)?** (pp. 40–42)
- **Perner and Ruffman (2005)**, inspired by Povinelli's scepticism about animals, suggest infants have (possibly innate) behavioural rules predicting future from current behaviour, with no mental-state step. Example rule: agents search for an object where they have looked at it. This would match Onishi and Baillargeon's looking pattern (p. 41).
- They extend this to the "engagement"/"experience" findings (p. 41).
- Apperly stresses it is an argument in principle, not just an empirical guess about rules. **Povinelli and Vonk (2004)** argue (Figure 3.2) that:
  1. Inferring a mental state from behaviour presupposes a behavioural rule linking observed behaviour (she looked at the object in the round box) to the unobservable state (she thinks it is in the round box).
  2. Predicting behaviour from a mental state presupposes a rule linking the state to a behaviour (she will approach the round box) (p. 41).
- **Conclusion drawn by Povinelli, Perner and Ruffman:** one could map directly from looking to search. Adding the mental state would be unparsimonious. The evidence does not entitle us to say infants reason about mental states, and parsimony favours behaviours only (p. 42).

*Intuition note.* The mentalist account has two links: behaviour → belief → behaviour. The sceptic argues that, since each link already needs a behavioural rule, one could map directly from looking to search behaviour. On this view, infants' looking patterns do not by themselves show mental-state ascription.

**Why not be equally sceptical about 3–4-year-olds?** (pp. 42–43) Early research settled this with two kinds of evidence:
- **Coherence.** Children pass diverse false-belief tasks, with beliefs induced by very different behaviours, around the same time (Perner, Leekam & Wimmer, 1987). They also pass false sign tasks, counterfactual reasoning and Level-2 perspective tasks around then. Apperly says it would be "truly surprising" if children learned a whole set of behavioural rules simultaneously, and that this coherence is "inexplicable" on a behavioural-rules account (p. 42).
- **Talk about mental content.** Children talk about what people see, think and know. This is clear evidence they ascribe content-bearing states. Behavioural rules go from behaviour to expected outcome without mental content as an intermediary. Apperly does not say language is necessary for mindreading (p. 42).

For infants, it is too early to judge coherence. They may be sensitive to a variety of causes and consequences of beliefs, but there is no evidence that they pass level-2, counterfactual or false-sign tasks. Verbal evidence of content is impossible; Apperly will discuss adults' interference effects (chapter 5) and how such evidence might bear on infants and animals (chapter 7) (p. 43).

**A lesson from number research** (pp. 43–44)
- Nativist researchers welcomed the infant findings. The excitement and debate stem from the view that later development just involves learning when to apply the concepts (p. 43).
- Number precedent: until the early 1980s, little evidence of numerical reasoning before counting at 2–3 years. Then preferential-looking and habituation studies found infants sensitive to the number of items in repeated sets (Starkey & Cooper), in short event sequences (Wynn), and to small vs large sets (Xu & Spelke). This led to claims that infants had number concepts after all (p. 43).
- Further research found infants process precise numbers only up to 4 items and discriminate large sets only if the size ratio is large enough. These limits are informative because they cannot be explained by the nature of numbers per se and do not appear to be due to limits on infants' intelligence, general processing capacity or executive function (p. 43). Note 2 says analogous findings hold in other domains, e.g. object knowledge (p. 56).
- Reasonable consensus: infants' number abilities depend on relatively specialised processes that are strictly limited. The limits are overcome only by learning a conventional counting and mathematical system. That takes years and makes significant demands on domain-general language and executive resources (p. 44).
- "Innate concept" of number is now a weaker, more interesting sense than first supposed. Apperly and Butterfill (2009) argue that something analogous may be true for mindreading, so we should attend to the limits of infants' abilities as well as their competence (p. 44).

**Are infants' abilities limited?** (pp. 44–45)
- Infants know less and have fewer processing resources. The question is whether, supposing they mindread, their mindreading is limited beyond what that explains. There is currently too little evidence to answer clearly (p. 44).
- The existing "false belief" findings could be explained by simpler abilities. Infants have simple notions of "perceptual access" including Level-1 perspective-taking (Sodian et al.), and track to some degree what an agent has accessed. Such a record might suffice for an expectation in many "false belief" situations (p. 44; see also note 3, p. 56).
- No evidence yet that infants pass Level-2 tasks. Level-2 corresponds, on many analyses, to the normative account of belief understanding: belief as a relation between an agent, the attitude of believing, and a propositional representation. Nor is there evidence that infants pass a false-belief task requiring ascription of propositional states (p. 44).
- **Negative conclusion:** current evidence does not support the claim that infants have a concept of belief as such. **Positive conclusion:** a concept of belief as such is not necessary for solving many problems that adults gloss as belief problems (pp. 44–45).
- Apperly warns the literature risks premature conclusions, partly because of the "neurotic task-fixation" that treated false-belief tasks as a litmus test. Whether infants are simple mindreaders or sophisticated behaviour readers, they may be more sensitive to others' minds than thought (p. 45).

*Intuition note.* Apperly suggests it is possible that an infant's record of what someone has seen is enough to form an expectation in many "false-belief" situations, without a concept of belief. The Level-2 and propositional-content tests are the ones infants have not yet been shown to pass.

### Non-human animals (pp. 45–56)

#### Introduction and limits on language and executive function (pp. 45–46)
- Lesson from the literature: "never say never". Abilities once thought uniquely human have repeatedly been found, in multiple species, some distant from humans. Interpretation is hotly contested (p. 45).
- Focus: perception, knowledge and belief, mostly in chimpanzees (and bonobos). Other species come later. Goals and intentions are set aside (note 5, pp. 56–57).
- Chimpanzees' language and executive function are probably less than a 3-year-old human's. Kanzi, after extensive training, has vocabulary and grammar comparable to a 2-year-old's (p. 46).
- **Reverse contingency task** (like the children's "windows" and "less-is-more" tasks): a choice between a bigger and a smaller reward, with the rule that the box pointed to is taken away. The best strategy is to point to the smaller. Chimpanzees take tens or hundreds of trials to choose the smaller box above chance, and sometimes stay below chance throughout. This resembles most 3-year-olds. Many 4-year-olds grasp the rule quickly (p. 46).

#### Perceiving (pp. 46–48)
- **Povinelli and Eddy (1996):** chimpanzees begging for food reliably gestured to a person facing them rather than one facing away. They failed to discriminate contrasts where both faced them: ears covered vs eyes covered, bucket beside the head vs over the head. 3-year-old children did well on similar tests. Conclusion: chimpanzees use a facing/not-facing cue and do not understand seeing as a mental state (pp. 46–47).
- **Kaminski et al. (2004):** chimpanzees may be sensitive to both body and face orientation, so the picture may be "unduly pessimistic". But only limited evidence of sensitivity to whether the person's eyes were open (p. 47).
- **Hare et al. (2000)** (Figure 3.3): a subject chimpanzee competed with a dominant one across a barrier. Food was either in common view or hidden from the dominant. Subjects approached hidden food more often than visible food (though see Karin-D'Arcy & Povinelli, 2002) (p. 47).
- **Hare, Call & Tomasello (2006):** chimpanzees approached food from the side that minimised being seen by a human competitor, whether because the human faced away or an opaque screen blocked that side (pp. 47–48).
- **Melis et al. (2006):** for hearing, chimpanzees preferred a silent tunnel to a noisy one, but not in a non-competitive control (p. 48).
- **Apperly's summary:** evidence of understanding "perception" in vision and hearing. They may not grasp all conditions for seeing (e.g. open eyes). They grasp that a competitor with a line of sight unobstructed and facing the food will behave differently from one without. This is broadly consistent with Level-1. No evidence of Level-2 or of understanding visual attention (looking without "seeing") (p. 48).

#### Knowing (pp. 48–50)
- **Hare, Call & Tomasello (2001)** (Figure 3.4): two barriers. In baseline both animals saw the food hidden. In the test only the subject saw it. At decision time the subject could see the food and the dominant could not in both conditions. Only what the dominant had seen differed. Subjects were more likely to approach when the dominant had not seen the hiding (pp. 48–49).
- **Alternative:** the animal might tag the food itself as "seen", "low quality" or "dangerous", with no enduring relation of the dominant knowing. A second experiment argues against this. The reluctance disappeared when the dominant who saw the hiding was replaced by another who had not seen it. So the strategy tracked a relation between the food and a particular dominant (p. 49).
- **Hearing and knowing:** Bräuer, Call & Tomasello (2008) found no discrimination between noisy and quiet hiding. If this reflects a general failure to grasp that hearing leads to knowing, it suggests chimpanzees' abilities may be less coherent than infants'. Infants show some understanding that a verbal message changes knowledge (Song & Baillargeon, 2008) (pp. 49–50).

#### Believing (p. 50)
- In Hare et al.'s (2001) "false belief" condition (Figure 3.5) the dominant saw food hidden behind one barrier but not its move to a second. The dominant would reliably search the wrong place. If the subject predicted this, it should have done better than in the "knowledge" condition, where the dominant's search was merely unpredictable.
- In fact subordinates were no more successful than in the knowledge condition (p. 50).
- Kaminski et al. (2008): chimpanzees discriminated competitors who did or did not know the food's location, but did not discriminate a false-belief competitor from a matched control (p. 50).

#### What do chimpanzees understand? (pp. 50–52)
- **Sceptical view** (Penn & Povinelli; Povinelli & Giambrone): first made about chimpanzees. A competitor who has looked at food is expected to approach it, and this does not warrant ascribing "seeing" (pp. 50–52).
- **Call & Tomasello (2008)** point to the relatively coherent pattern across tests and to the similarities with human infants. The result is a stalemate, each side calling its reading more parsimonious. Apperly returns to this in chapter 7 (p. 52).
- Call and Tomasello conclude chimpanzees understand perception and knowledge but not belief. If chimpanzees understand some mental states, this looks like a reasonable gloss (p. 52).
- **Apperly's objections from the normative account:**
  1. Where understanding is supposed, it falls qualitatively short. Normatively, understanding seeing involves a relation between agent, attitude and propositional content, and would support both Level-1 and Level-2. Evidence suggests only Level-1 (p. 52).
  2. On the normative account the meanings of mental-state concepts are interdependent (Davidson). So it is "simply incoherent" to say a chimpanzee understands perception and knowledge but not belief (p. 52).
- Even on the most generous reading, chimpanzees may understand only some important features of these states and their causes and consequences. So the data give no support for the view that mindreading depends on a clearly defined set of concepts, or that the normative account captures the natural divisions of everyday mindreading. Whatever chimpanzees do, it is not well captured by the normative account. This leaves open whether they reason about mental states of some kind (p. 52).

*Intuition note.* Apperly's point is about the yardstick. The adult, normative concepts of seeing, knowing and believing come as a package. Chimpanzee abilities look partial and uneven, so they should not be sorted into a list of which of seeing, knowing and believing they have.

#### Parallels with human infants? (p. 53)
- Call and Tomasello see similarities (Herrmann et al. emphasise differences). It is tempting to suppose shared mechanisms from common ancestry, with humans escaping their limits through cultural learning and language. Apperly discusses this in chapter 7 (p. 53).
- Two points where chimpanzees fall short of infants:
  1. Understanding hearing but not that hearing leads to knowing indicates a potential lack of coherence. Knowledge normally unifies results of several perceptual and inferential processes, and modality-specific understanding would fall short of that. No similar incoherence yet in infants, though this may reflect few studies.
  2. Infants form specific expectations (she will search in the round box) from what she "thinks". Chimpanzees do not make a specific prediction for a competitor with a false belief. Apperly recalls that there is no evidence infants reason about beliefs as such.
- "Never say never" remains the safest bet (p. 53).

#### Other non-human species (pp. 53–56)
- **Why look wider:** if several species in very different ecological niches show similar abilities, shared genes may matter. Abilities in distant species make common ancestry less likely and may indicate pressures behind convergent evolution (pp. 53–54).
- **Close(ish) relatives:** no evidence that other species exceed chimpanzees. Evidence of Level-1 perspective-taking in orang-utans, rhesus macaques, goats and dogs. Most of these live in complex social environments and so say little about genes vs environment. Orang-utans are solitary for much of life but grow up in close social contact for years (p. 54).
- **Western scrub-jays** cache food and vary caching by observation (p. 54):
  - With a competitor present they cache in darker places, in occluded places, and further from the competitor (Dally et al.). Possibly also according to whether the competitor can hear (Stulp et al.).
  - They track "who has seen". When alone, they prefer to recover caches a competitor observed (Emery & Clayton, 2001) (p. 55).
  - Clayton et al. (2007): all items were vulnerable because they had been observed, yet jays selectively retrieved items seen by the currently present competitor, rather than items cached while another, now absent, competitor watched. This suggests they encoded which competitor saw which items (p. 55).
- Evidence in other birds is more limited: rooks adapt retrieval to a competitor's gaze cues in a way that looks more sophisticated than chimpanzees (von Bayern & Emery) (p. 55).
- All of this remains vulnerable to the same sceptical arguments, but the jay evidence appears as strong as any from relatives of humans (p. 55).
- The last common ancestor of birds and humans (lizard-like amniote, ~300 million years ago) very probably could not take perspectives. So the abilities probably result from convergent evolution under analogous pressures. Corvids live in complex social groups with cooperation and competition. Homologous structures for "higher cognition" have been suggested despite different neuroanatomy (p. 55).

#### Summary (pp. 55–56)
- Several non-human species have abilities attuned to what others perceive and know, and likely more species would show them. These species act on another's current or past perceptual access, and may deny another access to it. Infants' abilities may be more coherent and extensive than those of non-humans (pp. 55–56).
- The cognitive basis is unclear. If the abilities rest on conceptual understanding of mental states, the concepts do not map simply to "perception" and "knowledge" of the normative account. If they rest on understanding behaviour only, we must ask how animals reason in a sophisticated way about behaviour (p. 56).
- Neither account explains how animals or infants think about agents without language and with little executive function. Apperly says it is not obvious a priori which account is more compatible with that. The point that stands is that such mindreading is possible, "somehow". This matters for chapter 6 on cognitive efficiency (p. 56).

#### Notes (pp. 56–57)
- Note 1: unclear whether infants treat Andrew's message as correcting or overwriting Sally's belief. Either way, their expectations are not based only on what she has seen (p. 56).
- Note 3: a full understanding of perception is more complex than that assumed for infants (the Oedipus example). Apperly supposes only that infants are sensitive to agents' exposure to situations and to some consequences of it for what agents "know" (p. 56).
- Note 4: scare-quotes on perception, knowledge and belief mark that these do not fully match adult understanding. "Understanding" does not imply reflective insight (p. 56).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two predictors of where Sally searches, with boxes round (R) and square (S):
- **Rule A (behavioural):** "Search where you last saw the object."
- **Rule B (mentalist):** "Infer belief = where she last saw it (if nothing else informed her), then predict search at the believed place."

| Trial | What happened | Rule A predicts | Rule B predicts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sally sees object in R, leaves; object moves to S | R | belief R → R |
| 2 | Sally watches the object move from R to S | S | belief S → S |
| 3 | As trial 1, but on return Andrew tells her "it's in S" | R | belief updated to S → S |

Trials 1 and 2: A and B agree in 2 of 2 trials, so infant looking in these trials cannot separate them. This is Povinelli's point. Trial 3: A fails unless an extra rule (one covering what she was told) is added. B needs only a belief that can be updated. A defender of A can keep adding rules, so the choice is not settled by trial 3 alone. The text notes (note 1, p. 56) that Song et al.'s result shows expectations are not based only on what Sally has seen.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] behavioural-rules vs mentalist accounts ↔ statistics: two models can predict the same data, and a latent middle variable adds nothing testable — both readings predict the same looking patterns.
- [structural-analogy] Povinelli's behaviour-only reading ↔ Clever Hans effect: apparent mental-state competence that may come from simple cues in the test.
- [metaphor] the finding that infants' number abilities were limited ↔ two-systems account: an efficient, limited system vs a flexible, costly one — Apperly hints at a similar mindreading story.
- [structural-analogy] Infants' success on indirect false-belief measures, with children's failure on explicit judgement ↔ implicit vs explicit ToM: indirect and explicit measures can come apart.

## Questions this raises
- What evidence could separate a behavioural-rules reading from a mental-state reading? Apperly points to coherence across tasks and ascription of mental content (chapters 5 and 7).
- What do "simpler" mindreading abilities look like, and why would they be efficient without language and executive function (chapter 6)?
- If infants track perceptual access and chimpanzees do not predict false-belief search, which task would show a real difference between them?
- Does the limit pattern in infant number cognition (about 4 items, ratio limits) have a mindreading counterpart?
