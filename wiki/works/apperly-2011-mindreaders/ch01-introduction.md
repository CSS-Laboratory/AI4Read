# Introduction: What is "mindreading", and what is the book's plan?

## L0 — One line
Apperly argues that research on "theory of mind" has been too narrow (children, concepts, philosophical theories), and proposes to study the cognitive processes of mindreading in a way that must explain how it can be both flexible and fast.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Apperly asks what cognitive processes let us operate in a world of agents who have perceptions, knowledge and beliefs. He argues that research has been narrow. It has focused on children acquiring mental-state concepts, as if nothing remained to explain afterwards. He prefers the neutral term "mindreading", focuses mostly on epistemic mental states (perception, knowledge, belief), and sets aside the philosophers' theory-theory versus simulation debate. He organises his questions around representations, processes and architectures. He flags two problems: mindreading seems to need both flexibility and efficiency, and it faces the "inverse" problem (goals are not easily read from actions) and the "frame" problem (which beliefs are relevant?). This prepares a two-systems account (two kinds of process) in chapter 6.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of beliefs and desires as causes of action; the false-belief task in outline; basic cognitive-psychology terms such as working memory.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Mindreading means inferring unobservable mental states (what others know, want, intend, believe) from what they do and say. It is interesting, surprising and puzzling at once (p. 1).
- Apperly's complaint is that most work studies 3- to 5-year-olds, and that common habits of thought treat having mental-state concepts as the end of the story. He wants a broader range of data, participant groups and kinds of cognitive process (p. 2).
- He thinks it plausible that adults, like ball-catchers, use rules of thumb and specialised processes, not only explicit reasoning about mental states (p. 3).
- He uses the neutral word "mindreading" because "theory of mind" implies that mindreading consists in having a theory, which he sees as at best one possibility among many (pp. 3–4).
- He restricts the case study to epistemic states (perception, knowledge, belief) and sets aside theory-theory versus simulation, because he finds that debate remarkably poor at generating testable predictions for states such as seeing, knowing and believing (pp. 4–5).
- He treats philosophy's normative model (attitudes towards propositions) as a working hypothesis that we mindread this way at least some of the time, but does not want to be tied exclusively to it. He suspects that much everyday mindreading, and possibly all of infants' and animals', deviates substantially from it (pp. 5–6).
- His questions concern representations, processing and architecture. Some researchers simply state answers to them (innate module versus slow conceptual learning) (pp. 6–7).
- Flexibility and efficiency demands conflict, which motivates a two-systems account. The inverse and frame problems make mindreading look computationally intractable in the general case (pp. 8–9).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### What is "mindreading"? (pp. 1–3)
- The mindreading discussed is not mystical. It is the everyday ability to think about what others know, want, intend and believe (p. 1).
- Reasons for **interest**: claims that it is uniquely human, that it underlies social interaction and communication, and that impaired mindreading may be part of the explanation of disorders such as schizophrenia and autism (p. 1).
- Reasons for **surprise**: apparently sophisticated mindreading in infants and some animals, alongside apparently poor mindreading in children and sometimes adults (p. 1).
- Reason for **mystery**: we have no direct access to others' mental states and must infer them from behaviour. How we do this raises questions that have only been partially addressed (p. 1).
- Apperly's aim is to defend the interest, but to suggest that some surprises and mysteries come from conceptualising the problem narrowly (p. 1).
- **Objection he reports from other researchers:** "Why are you studying adults? Don't they already have a theory of mind?" (p. 1). For him this reveals two habits (p. 2):
  - Work has focused almost exclusively on *when* children can reason about mental states.
  - Many hold that development consists of acquiring abstract mental-state concepts that form a "theory", so once young children pass simple tasks, "there is really nothing further to explain" (p. 2).
- **Apperly's reply:** this is baffling. Cognitive psychologists study adults even though young children already have abilities in number, space, time and physics. Adult literatures model the system children are developing, and he asks whether there is any reason for mindreading to be an exception (p. 2).
- Talk of "concepts" gives a deceptive impression of simplicity. There is little consensus on what having a concept means. He asks whether we really suppose that a child who fails a test is quite insensitive to how mental states govern behaviour, or that a child who passes has nothing further to learn (p. 2).
- He grants most researchers would reject such black-and-white views, but says common habits of thought run this way (p. 2).
- He keeps conceptual-change insights but holds that having key concepts "might be neither the beginning nor the end" of mindreading abilities (p. 2).
- **Starting point:** a world of agents whose behaviour can be understood by supposing mental states that interact by psychological laws (e.g. people act to satisfy desires on the basis of beliefs) (pp. 2–3). Note 1 (p. 10): whether such states are real or "useful fictions" does not matter here, since both sides agree mindreading involves thinking about them.
- Part of the answer to how we operate in this domain is that adult humans at least reason about mental states as such. This is "unlikely to be the full answer" (p. 3).
- **Analogy with physical objects (p. 3):**
  - Adults can reason about mass, velocity and force, but this is "demonstrably not" how people catch balls.
  - Note 2 (p. 10): fielders run so that gaze elevation to the ball rises at a decreasing rate and horizontal gaze angle rises at a constant rate (McLeod et al., 2003).
  - Everyday intuitions about balls follow rules of thumb. Note 3 (p. 10): many adults say a ball dropped by a moving person falls straight down, when its path is in fact parabolic (McCloskey, 1983).
  - So in the object domain people use tricks, rules of thumb and specialised processes that do not necessarily operate over the physics-class concepts. It seems plausible the same holds for mindreading (p. 3).

*Intuition note:* A suitably educated adult could work out the physics, but people do not catch balls that way. Apperly thinks it plausible that understanding minds likewise relies on tricks, rules of thumb and specialised processes, not only on explicit mental-state reasoning.

### Why "mindreading"? (pp. 3–4)
- "Theory of mind" is the usual term, and Apperly has used it. Alternatives are "folk psychology", "mentalizing" and "social cognition" (p. 2).
- He objects to it mainly because it implies that mindreading consists in *having* a theory, which is "at best" one possibility among many. It also suggests something one has rather than something one does. A lesser reason is that the phrase is linguistically awkward (p. 3).
- "Mindreading" is theoretically neutral. By analogy with reading text (where we go far beyond the words on the page), mindreaders often process perceptually accessible social stimuli and always have to make significant further inferences to the thoughts, desires, knowledge or intentions they cannot directly perceive (p. 4).
- "Social cognition" and "social understanding" are much too broad for his purposes. There is far more to social cognition than mindreading (p. 4).

### Epistemic mental states as a case study (p. 4)
- A full account would need all mental states (epistemic, emotional, motivational), since they combine as causes of behaviour. We are far from having the evidence for a full account, and work remains in conceiving its form (p. 4).
- Strategy: limit most discussion to epistemic states (perceptions, knowledge, belief). These sit at the centre of the literature and give the largest evidence base. The hope is that the account can serve as a model for other states (p. 4).

### Normative models and philosophical theories (pp. 4–6)
- Psychologists and philosophers have collaborated from the start. Premack and Woodruff (1978) asked whether chimpanzees have a "theory of mind". Philosophers' commentaries led to perspective-difference paradigms such as the false-belief task (Wimmer & Perner, 1983) (p. 4).
- **Normative model** (from philosophy of mind): agents hold attitudes (seeing, knowing, believing) towards propositional contents, and the mindreader reasons about these relations (p. 5).
- Example: you may predict that I will go back for an umbrella if you can judge that I will see that it is raining, that I know it will make me wet, and that I believe an umbrella is inside (p. 5).
- Philosophy also supplies two accounts of how mindreading is possible (p. 5):
  - **Theory-theory:** mindreading depends on mental-state concepts and principles about their interactions.
  - **Simulation theory:** mindreading may not need a fully specified theory if only we could use our own minds to model (simulate) others' minds.
- **Apperly's criticism:** the debate gave a sense of possible "flavours" but is "remarkably poor" at generating testable predictions for seeing, knowing and believing. Nobody has yet come up with a generalizable test for whether a problem was solved by simulation or theorising. His strategy is to set the debate aside and perhaps return later (p. 5).
- Note 4 (p. 10): a stronger case exists for simulation of *non-propositional* states (action schemas, emotions). It does not follow that the debate applies to propositional-attitude ascription.
- The normative model is a **working hypothesis**: we mindread roughly this way at least some of the time. He suspects the very best mindreading might only approximate it. He also suspects that much everyday mindreading, "possibly all" of infants' and animals', deviates substantially from it (pp. 5–6).

### Structure of the book (p. 6)
- Part 1 (chapters 2–5): identify questions about the cognitive basis of mindreading and review the literatures selectively. These cover adults, children, infants, atypical development, neuroscience and animals. Its distinctive features are breadth of evidence and a focus on cognitive processes (p. 6).
- Part 2 (chapters 6–7): chapter 6 builds a schematic account, and chapter 7 draws out implications for children, adults and animals (p. 6).
- Readers focused on theory may skip to chapters 6–7, taking his word for the evidence. The patterns across diverse tasks and groups are what justify the new look (p. 6).

### A cognitive psychologist's wish-list (pp. 6–7)
Three kinds of question, interrelated but able to vary with "substantial independence" (p. 6):
- **Representations:** Is mindreading "special"? Does it depend on specific concepts? Is the representational basis *domain-specific* (specialised) or *domain-general* (shared with other functions)? What role does language play? (p. 7)
- **Processes:** Is it effortful, using working memory, inhibition or executive function? Fast or slow? Automatic, or dependent on context? (p. 7)
- **Architecture:** Is it unitary? Are there sub-components (e.g. perception versus knowledge)? Is there a hierarchy of simple and complex systems, and what passes between them? Could communication or action planning have their own "on board" mindreading systems that do not talk to each other? (p. 7)
- These questions have been asked before, and some think they are answered. Some state that mindreading is an innate, fast, automatic, domain-specific module (Leslie, Sperber & Wilson, Stone et al.). Others state that it develops slowly through conceptual insights from domain-general learning (Gopnik & Meltzoff, Perner, Wellman) (p. 7).
- Apperly thinks these contradictory views derive in part from differing theoretical commitments, and also from the questions arising piecemeal. He wants to ask them systematically (p. 7).

### Unspoken problems at the heart of mindreading (pp. 8–9)
**Problem 1: contradictory demands.**
- *Jury example:* a juror must judge what the defendant saw, knew and intended (poison versus sugar, careless versus intending). This could involve any person, circumstance or information. We expect jurors to take their time, think carefully and discuss; viewed this way, mindreading would seem as flexible as any general reasoning process and potentially demanding of limited processing resources (p. 8).
- *Football example:* passing depends on what opponents can see, and faking a pass seems to depend on deliberately giving an opponent a false belief. Such judgements must be made extremely quickly, and it would seem very useful, if not essential, that they be efficient, so as not to interfere with other ongoing processes (pp. 8–9).
- So it seems that mindreading must be as flexible as any reasoning process and yet fast and efficient. In cognitive systems flexibility and efficiency "tend to be negatively correlated". Many accounts in other domains (social categorisation, number, reasoning) resolve this with **two systems** making complementary trade-offs. We should not be surprised if mindreading is not one kind of process, and chapter 6 will argue for a two-systems account (p. 9).

*Intuition note:* A juror is expected to take their time; a footballer must judge extremely quickly. One process that is both fully open-ended and instant is unlikely, so two kinds of process may share the work.

**Problem 2: deep conceptual problems.**
- **Inverse problem** (Csibra & Gergely, 2007): goals and actions are in many-to-many correspondence (the text says "many-to-one mappings", but its explanation describes both directions). One action could be evidence for many goals, and one goal could lead to many actions. This makes inference from behaviour to mental state hard (p. 9).
- **Frame problem / problem of relevance** (Pylyshyn, 1987): in general, what are the causes or consequences of a change in a system whose components are all potentially (though not necessarily) linked? For mindreading, an agent may have any number of beliefs, any of which might be relevant (p. 9).
- Apperly's inference: it follows that even a conscientious juror with unlimited time should not be able to judge guilt, since the problem is computationally intractable. Yet jurors decide and footballers pass. How this is possible is an objective of the second half (p. 9).

*Intuition note:* Seeing an action does not reveal one goal, and any belief might matter. In the general case, working out what someone thinks looks endless (computationally intractable). People manage, so how they do it needs explaining.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
The inverse problem. Suppose a person has three possible goals and three possible actions. Our assumed links (goal → actions that could serve it):
- A "get a drink": open fridge (1), take out milk (2).
- B "tidy the kitchen": open fridge (1), wipe shelf (3).
- C "check the fridge works": open fridge (1).

Goal → actions: A has 2, B has 2, C has 1. So one goal can lead to several actions.

Action → goals (reading the links backwards):
- Observe action 1: goals A, B, C all fit, so 3 goals.
- Observe action 2: only A fits, so 1 goal.
- Observe action 3: only B fits, so 1 goal.

Seeing "opens fridge" alone cannot tell the observer which of 3 goals is at work. More information is needed, such as context or later actions. This is the many-to-many mapping that Apperly says makes inference from behaviour hard (p. 9).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Mindreading of agents ↔ intuitive physics: both may use heuristics and specialised processes, not explicit laws — Apperly's ball analogy (p. 3)
- [structural-analogy] Inverse problem ↔ inverse planning / inverse reinforcement learning (not in this unit): inferring goals from actions is under-determined in both — similar problem, not shown here to be the same mathematics

- [metaphor] Reading text for meaning behind the words ↔ mindreading as inference beyond perceptible cues (p. 4)

## Questions this raises
- What evidence would show that a mindreading task is solved by fast, efficient processes rather than by slow, flexible reasoning?
- If both the inverse and frame problems make general mindreading intractable, which constraints let people succeed?
- Can an account built for epistemic states carry over to emotions, desires and intentions?
- Does restricting the study to propositional attitudes exclude infant and animal "mindreading" that deviates from the normative model?
