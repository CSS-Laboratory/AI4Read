# Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm

## L0 — One line
To frame is to select some aspects of perceived reality and make them more salient in a text so as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation and/or remedy.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Entman asks what "framing" means, since many fields use it but none gives a general statement of how frames sit in texts and shape thinking. His answer: framing is selection plus salience, and frames typically define problems, diagnose causes, make moral judgments and suggest remedies. Frames sit in at least four places: the communicator, the text, the receiver and the culture. What a frame leaves out may matter as much as what it includes. A clear concept, he argues, would help debates on audience autonomy, journalistic objectivity, content analysis and democratic theory. He also proposes that communication could become a "master discipline" that synthesizes scattered concepts like this one.

**Prerequisites:** The idea of a mental schema (stored cluster of ideas that guides processing); reading percentages in a survey; basic idea of majority voting.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Entman proposes turning communication's ostensible weakness (a supposedly deficient core) into a strength: it could bring together and synthesize ideas scattered across other disciplines and aspire to become a "master discipline" (p. 51).
- Framing = selection + salience, in service of a problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation and/or treatment recommendation (p. 52).
- Frames have at least four locations (communicator, text, receiver, culture), and a frame in a text may not include all four functions (p. 52–53).
- Salience arises from the interaction of text and receiver's schemata, so a frame detected in a text does not guarantee influence on audiences (p. 53).
- Most frames are defined by what they omit as well as include; Kahneman and Tversky's lives-saved/lives-lost experiment shows a framing effect on choice (p. 53–54).
- In political news, frames register the power of actors who competed to dominate the text; consensus on problem and cause can coexist with contest over remedies (p. 55).
- A consistent concept gives an operational definition of "dominant meaning" and cautions researchers against stressing fugitive oppositional readings (p. 56).
- Entman argues that a framing paradigm would help debates on objectivity norms, tally-based content analysis, and what "true" public opinion can be (p. 56–57).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: a fractured paradigm (p. 51–52)
- Entman responds to the proposition that communication lacks disciplinary status because of deficient core knowledge. He proposes to turn this ostensible weakness into a strength by bringing together insights and theories that would otherwise stay scattered in other disciplines (p. 51).
- Reason: with little interchange among disciplines, hypotheses discredited in one field may receive wide acceptance in another. Potential paradigms stay "fractured", with pieces here and there but no comprehensive statement (p. 51).
- Communication can aspire to become a "master discipline" that synthesizes related theories and concepts. This would require scholars to plumb other fields and feed their studies back to outside researchers, and it would also enhance the rigor of communication scholarship (p. 51).
- Framing is a case study. It is found across the social sciences and humanities, but nowhere is there a general statement showing exactly how frames are embedded in a text or how framing influences thinking (p. 51).
- Whatever its specific use, the concept consistently offers a way to describe the power of a communicating text, namely how influence over a consciousness is exerted by transferring information (p. 51–52).
- Framing is often defined casually, leaving much to tacit understanding. The aim is to make common tendencies in its uses explicit and to suggest a more precise and universal understanding (p. 52).

### Of Frames and Framing (p. 52–53)
- **Definition:** "Framing essentially involves selection and salience." To frame is to select some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more salient in a communicating text, so as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation for the item described (p. 52).
- Typically frames diagnose, evaluate and prescribe, a point explored most thoroughly by Gamson (1992) (p. 52).
- Example, the "cold war" frame in U.S. foreign news: certain events (say, civil wars) highlighted as problems; source identified (communist rebels); moral judgment (atheistic aggression); solution (U.S. support for the other side) (p. 52).
- Four functions: **define problems** (what a causal agent is doing, with what costs and benefits, usually in terms of common cultural values); **diagnose causes**; **make moral judgments**; **suggest remedies** (offer and justify treatments, predict likely effects) (p. 52).
- A single sentence may perform more than one function, although many sentences in a text may perform none; a frame in a particular text may not necessarily include all four (p. 52).
- At least four locations of frames (p. 52–53):
  - *Communicator*: conscious or unconscious framing judgments, guided by frames (often called schemata) that organize beliefs.
  - *Text*: frames are manifested by the presence or absence of keywords, stock phrases, stereotyped images, sources, and sentences that give thematically reinforcing clusters of facts or judgments.
  - *Receiver*: the frames guiding the receiver's thinking may or may not reflect the text's frames and the communicator's intention.
  - *Culture*: the stock of commonly invoked frames; culture might be defined as the empirically demonstrable set of common frames in the discourse and thinking of most people in a group.
- Framing in all four locations includes similar functions: selection and highlighting, and use of the highlighted elements to build an argument about problems, causes, evaluation and/or solution (p. 53).
- Intuition note: A frame is a pattern of emphasis that carries an argument. Similar selecting-and-arguing work happens in the speaker's head, the words on the page, the listener's head and the shared culture, though the listener's frames may differ from the text's.

### How Frames Work (p. 53–55)
- **Salience** = making a piece of information more noticeable, meaningful or memorable. Higher salience enhances the probability that receivers perceive it, discern meaning and process it, and store it in memory (p. 53).
- Texts can raise salience by placement, repetition, or association with familiar symbols. But even a single unillustrated mention in an obscure part of a text can be highly salient if it comports with a receiver's schemata. Conversely, an emphasized idea can be hard to notice, interpret or remember because of existing schemata (p. 53).
- Schemata, and closely related concepts such as categories, scripts or stereotypes, are mentally stored clusters of ideas that guide processing of information. Salience is a product of text–receiver interaction, so a frame detected by researchers in a text does not guarantee influence on audience thinking (p. 53).
- **Kahneman and Tversky (1984):** an unusual Asian disease expected to kill 600 people. Program A: 200 saved. Program B: 1/3 probability that 600 saved, 2/3 probability that none saved. Result: 72% chose A, 28% chose B (p. 53–54).
- Same situation framed by deaths: Program C: 400 die. Program D: 1/3 probability nobody dies, 2/3 probability 600 die. C was chosen by 22% (twin of A, chosen by 72%); D by 78% (twin of B, chosen by 28%). The framing reversed the percentages (p. 54).
- Entman's reading: the frame determines whether most people notice, and how they understand, remember, evaluate and act on a problem. The notion implies a common effect on large portions of the audience, though not likely a universal one (p. 54).
- Selecting and calling attention to some aspects logically means directing attention away from others. Most frames are defined by what they omit as well as include, and omissions may be as critical as inclusions in guiding the audience (p. 54).
- **Edelman (1993)**, quoted: the character, causes and consequences of any phenomenon become radically different with changes in what is displayed, what is repressed and how observations are classified; the social world is a "kaleidoscope of potential realities" (p. 54).
- Entman: responses are clearly affected if receivers process information about one interpretation and have little or incommensurable data on alternatives. This is why exclusion is as significant to outcomes as inclusion (p. 54).
- **Sniderman, Brody and Tetlock (1991)**, quoted: framing primes values differentially, establishing the salience of one or another. A majority supports rights of persons with AIDS when the question stresses civil liberties, and supports mandatory testing when it stresses public health (p. 54).
- The survey text supplies most people with the considerations they use (Zaller, 1992). Entman adds that a potential counterframing is often mostly or wholly absent from a text, although an audience member with a strong civil-liberties philosophy might reject mandatory testing even under a strictly public-health frame (p. 55).
- Intuition note: In the Asian-disease experiment (600 people at risk), the same options described as lives saved or as deaths gave reversed percentages (72% for A, 22% for its twin C), so the majority flipped. What a description leaves out may steer people as much as what it says.

### Frames in Political News (p. 55)
- Frames call attention to some aspects of reality while obscuring others, which might lead audiences to have different reactions. Politicians seeking support are thus compelled to compete with each other and with journalists over news frames (p. 55).
- Entman: framing plays a major role in exerting political power, and the frame in a news text is really the "imprint of power", registering the identity of actors or interests that competed to dominate the text (p. 55).
- Many news texts exhibit homogeneous framing at one level of analysis, yet competing frames at another. **Iraq pre-war debate:** a tacit consensus among U.S. elites not to argue for options such as negotiation. The news frame included only two remedies (war now; sanctions now with war likely later), while problem definitions, causal analyses and moral evaluations were homogeneous. Between the two remedies, elites contested the framing (p. 55).
- Self-reinforcing power: any critique going beyond those remedies breached the bounds of acceptable discourse and was unlikely to influence policy. By conventional journalistic standards such views were not newsworthy. Unpublicized, they could gain few adherents and have little effect on public opinion, so elites felt no pressure to widen the frame (p. 55).
- **Gamson (1992)**: a frame can exert great social power when encoded in a term like "affirmative action". Once a term is widely accepted, using another risks looking less credible or not being understood. So the power of a frame can be as great as that of language itself (p. 55).

### Benefits of a Consistent Concept of Framing (p. 55–58)
- The concept directs attention to how a communicated text exerts its power. A **research paradigm** here = a general theory that informs most scholarship on the operation and outcomes of a system of thought and action. The framing paradigm could be applied with similar benefits to public opinion and voting (political science), cognition (social psychology), and class, gender and race research (cultural studies, sociology) (p. 56).
- **1. Audience autonomy** (p. 56):
  - Framing gives an operational definition of *dominant meaning*: the problem, causal, evaluative and treatment interpretations with the highest probability of being noticed, processed and accepted by the most people.
  - To identify a meaning as dominant or preferred is to suggest a framing that the text most heavily supports and that is congruent with the most common audience schemata.
  - A framing paradigm cautions researchers not to take fugitive components of a message and show how they might be read against the dominant meaning. If the text stresses in many reinforcing ways that the glass is half full, social-science evidence suggests relatively few in the audience will conclude it is half empty.
  - To argue that polysemy enables counterframing, researchers must show that real-world audiences reframe the message, and that this is not a by-product of research conditions (e.g., a focus group in which one participant can lead the rest, or a highly suggestive interview protocol).
  - People can recall their own facts and make links not in the text. But Zaller, Kahneman and Tversky, and Iyengar, among others, suggest that on most matters of social or political interest people are not generally so well informed and cognitively active, so framing heavily influences their responses. Gamson describes conditions that can mitigate this influence.
- **2. Journalistic objectivity** (p. 56–57): journalists may follow "objective" rules and still convey a dominant framing that prevents most audience members from a balanced assessment. Lacking a common understanding of framing, journalists frequently allow skilled media manipulators to impose dominant frames. If educated to tell scattered oppositional facts from challenging a dominant frame, they might make two or more interpretations equally salient to an inattentive audience. This would require a far more active role for reporters and would give more balanced reporting than the formulaic objectivity norm (p. 57).
- **3. Content analysis** (p. 57): the major task of determining textual meaning should be to identify and describe frames. Coders often just tally messages judged positive or negative, neglect to measure salience and fail to gauge how the most salient clusters (the frames) relate to audience schemata. Unguided by a framing paradigm, content analysis may often yield data that misrepresent what audiences pick up.
- **4. Public opinion and democratic theory** (p. 57):
  - In Zaller's account, framing appears to be a central power in the democratic process, since elites control the framing of issues. A different frame, according to Zaller, can indicate a different public opinion in surveys and even in voting. His theory, with Kahneman and Tversky's, "seems" to raise radical doubts about democracy.
  - Entman's questions: if elites can determine the major manifestations of "true" public opinion available to government, what can true public opinion be? How can even sincere representatives respond correctly when the evidence of it appears so malleable?
  - **Example:** three ways to frame an issue yield 40%, 50% and 60% approval. Approving the 60% option is not axiomatically the most democratic response, because of the cyclical majority problem (Riker, 1986), which Entman says makes majority rule among several complex options mathematically impossible.
  - Determining which framed opinion is closest to the public's "real" sentiments appears futile, since it would require contending elites and citizens to agree which frame is most accurate, fair or complete.
  - A framing paradigm can illuminate, if not solve, such puzzles.
- Closing: the concept merits a book-length essay; this effort, limited by space, is a preliminary contribution. It also exemplifies how communication might develop, from its wide and eclectic approaches, a core of knowledge that could translate into research paradigms contributing to social theory (p. 57–58).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A short article on a new bus lane has six sentences about it. Entman's point 3 says that counting positive and negative statements ignores salience. Illustrative salience weights (our own, arbitrary): headline = 3, opening paragraph = 2, later paragraph = 1.
- Positive: headline ("Bus lane cuts commutes") = 3; opening line ("Trips are faster") = 2. Total 3 + 2 = 5. Count: 2 statements.
- Negative: four later sentences (cost, parking loss, etc.), each 1. Total 4 × 1 = 4. Count: 4 statements.
- Plain tally: 4 negative vs 2 positive, so "negative". Salience-weighted: 5 positive vs 4 negative, so "positive-leaning".
The tally and the weighted reading disagree. The salience-weighted reading is closer to Entman's idea of a frame: a cluster of emphasized elements, not a raw count.

## Questions this raises
- How can a researcher measure the salience of a frame in a text, and its fit with audience schemata, in practice?
- If it is hard to get agreement on which frame is most accurate, fair and complete, what basis is there for judging "balanced" coverage?
- What conditions (Gamson, 1992, mentioned only briefly) reduce framing effects on audiences?
- Does the Asian disease result show an effect of emphasis, or merely of logically equivalent wording?
