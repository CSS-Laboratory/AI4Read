# How citizens acquire information and convert it into public opinion

## L0 — One line
Survey answers are samples of the considerations (reasons) a person has received and accepted, and that person's political awareness shapes both reception and acceptance (the Receive–Accept–Sample, or RAS, model).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how citizens learn about politics, which lies mostly beyond their immediate experience, and how they turn that information into survey answers. Zaller answers with four axioms: Reception, Resistance, Accessibility and Response. Aware people receive more messages and tend to resist those that clash with their predispositions (prior political leanings), but only to the extent that they have cues (contextual hints about a message's partisan meaning) showing the clash. People answer by averaging the considerations (reasons) that are accessible at that moment. He admits the Response Axiom is too simple. He argues that radical simplification is needed for rigorous analysis of typical survey data. The rest of the book deduces patterns of mass opinion from these axioms.

**Prerequisites:** basic survey-research ideas (attitude, response); probability as a ratio of counts; the idea of elite discourse from Chapter 2.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A "consideration" is any reason that might push a person to decide an issue one way or the other. It blends belief and evaluation, and it need not be coldly rational (pp. 40–41).
- Messages are of two kinds. Persuasive messages give reasons. Cueing messages tell people the partisan or ideological meaning of those reasons (pp. 41–42).
- Reception Axiom: the more cognitively engaged a person is with an issue, the more likely he or she is to be exposed to and comprehend messages. It is measured mainly by general political knowledge (pp. 42–43).
- Resistance Axiom: people tend to resist messages inconsistent with their predispositions, but only to the extent that they have the contextual information to see the inconsistency. Awareness can be expected to enhance resistance only where the issue's link to predispositions is obscure, which Zaller says is extremely common (pp. 44, 47–48).
- Accessibility Axiom: considerations used more recently are retrieved faster, and long-unused ones may be forgotten (p. 48).
- Response Axiom: people average across the considerations that are accessible "at the top of the head", and do not normally canvass all they hold. Zaller defends this simplicity against richer models (pp. 49–50).
- Under simplifying assumptions, Prob(liberal response) = L/(L+C). Awareness shapes L and C through Reception and Resistance (pp. 51–52).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Aim of the chapter (p. 40).** Comprehensive analysis of public opinion requires attention to how people learn and how they convert information into opinions; the chapter proposes a model of both. The model is meant to be parsimonious and testable on typical survey data. Zaller says it is not fully accurate, but he claims it fits the survey evidence well. All the important features of his analysis derive from it (p. 40).

**Some definitions (pp. 40–42).**
- *Consideration*: any reason that might induce someone to decide a political issue one way or the other. It combines a belief about an object with an evaluation of the belief (pp. 40–41). The term is borrowed from Kelley (1983) (p. 40, n. 1). Example: "President Bush's plan to balance the federal budget is fair to all competing interests" (pp. 40–41).
- Even a hostile reaction to a "bum on the street" has a cognitive part: the person was seen as a "bum", not as someone like me who lost his job (p. 41). Zaller prefers "consideration" to "schema" because schema stresses cognition over affect (p. 41, n. 3).
- *Persuasive message*: an argument or image giving a reason for a position. If accepted, it becomes a consideration (p. 41). Messages and considerations need not be coldly rational. Feelings of security can count as considerations (p. 41).
- *Cueing message*: "contextual information" about the ideological or partisan implications of a persuasive message (p. 42). Following Converse (1964), cues let citizens relate messages to their predispositions and respond critically. A Republican is more likely to reject criticism of Bush's budget if she knows the critic is a Democrat (p. 42).
- Chapter 2 illustration: unaware hawks and doves could not respond in a partisan way on aid to the Contras, but they could on "combating communism in Central America", a cue they understood (p. 42).

**The model (p. 42).** There are four axioms. None is individually original, and none is claimed to be perfectly true. Zaller claims they are plausible approximations and that, taken together, they have novel and empirically correct implications (p. 42).

**A1. Reception Axiom.** The greater a person's cognitive engagement with an issue, the more likely he or she is to be exposed to and comprehend ("receive") political messages on it, both persuasive and cueing (p. 42).
- Why cognitive and not affective engagement? The model concerns cognitive processes, and affective engagement is likely to affect them only insofar as it leads to intellectual engagement (pp. 42–43). Also, survey measures of cognitive engagement outperform affective ones in explaining most aspects of public opinion. Knowledgeable people are substantially more stable in their attitude reports. Highly interested people are not significantly more stable (p. 43).
- Political interest correlates with turnout, slightly more strongly than knowledge does. So affective engagement can be important, but unless coupled with intellectual engagement it appears to have only limited effects on opinion per se (p. 43).
- "Cognitive engagement", "political attentiveness" and "political awareness" mean the same thing here. Awareness is measured mainly by a summary score on neutral factual knowledge tests (p. 43).
- This is a general, chronic measure. It assumes that people knowledgeable about politics are habitually attentive to most particular issues. Domain-specific measures would be preferable but are not available, and the loss from using general ones is, as far as Zaller can tell, minimal (p. 43).
- A1 says nothing about where messages come from, whether elite discourse, friends or neighbours (pp. 43–44). That elite discourse shapes mass opinion is an auxiliary assumption, not part of the formal model. It needs independent justification (p. 44).
- Zaller says discussion and media-use measures give no guarantee of capturing only one type of communication, so the sources cannot be separated with the available data. Discussion has no impact once habitual awareness is controlled. Media use is measured very unreliably (p. 44, n. 5).

*Intuition note:* People who pay more attention to politics catch more of what is said, including the labels that tell them who is speaking. Zaller suggests that interest alone has only limited effects unless it is coupled with attention and thought.

**A2. Resistance Axiom.** People tend to resist arguments inconsistent with their political predispositions, but only to the extent that they have the contextual information to perceive a relationship between the message and their predispositions (p. 44).
- Cues carry that information, and A1 says the probability of acquiring cues depends on awareness. So A1 and A2 together imply that resistance to predisposition-inconsistent messages rises with awareness. Inattentive people often "mistakenly" accept such messages (pp. 44–45).
- The postulate leaves no room for citizens to reason or deliberate. Informed people react mechanically to cues, and poorly informed people uncritically accept what they meet (p. 45).
- Footnote 6: the operational model does not require inattentive people to accept everything. It requires only that they be more accepting than highly aware people and unable to respond selectively on the basis of their predispositions. Estimated acceptance rates are in fact very high (Figures 7.4 and 10.1) (p. 45, n. 6).
- *Support from Converse.* Few people reason for themselves about how ideas "go together". They rely on elite contextual information, and awareness of it is likely to depend on general awareness (p. 45).
- *Support from McGuire (1969).* A message is judged fairer, more factual and more valid when ascribed to a high-credibility source (p. 45). McGuire goes on to note that people do not seem to learn more from credible sources, but tend to accept their opinion leadership more readily. He says the receiver can be regarded as a "lazy organism" who tries to master the content only when it is absolutely necessary to make a decision (p. 45).
- *Counter-evidence.* Rhine and Severance (1970): students ignored source credibility on a topic that engaged them (tuition) and used it on non-ego-involving topics (parks in a distant community) (p. 46).
- *Petty and Cacioppo experiment (p. 46).* Design: 2 message types (strong vs weak arguments for senior comprehensive exams) × 2 involvement conditions (exams start next year vs in ten years) × 2 sources (Princeton professor vs local high-school class).
  - Low-involvement students paid some attention to argument quality. They were most affected by source credibility, and were favourable only when the source was the Princeton professor (p. 46).
  - High-involvement students ignored the source and were powerfully influenced by argument strength. Petty and Cacioppo show this is because they had thought more about the arguments (p. 46).
- *Why the encouraging result is unlikely to generalise (p. 47).* Three reasons:
  1. The weak arguments were extremely weak, and real-life persuaders cannot be expected to take such pains. Citizens are likely to face two opposing sets of arguments that, compared with those in a typical experiment, will all be "strong".
  2. Politics in the contemporary United States is notoriously low key and uninvolving. In such conditions Petty and Cacioppo's work indicates that most people process "peripherally", using superficial cues such as source credibility.
  3. The students judged issues rooted in their own experience. Most political issues lie beyond the direct experience of even the most attentive people.
- Conclusion: according to Petty and Cacioppo and others, real-world conditions encourage reliance on peripheral cues, such as whether the advocate is liberal or conservative. Citizens normally respond on the basis of external cues, provided they are attentive enough to have learned them (p. 47).
- *Caveat (pp. 47–48).* The argument applies only where the contextual information is obscure. Anticommunists responded consistently on "stopping communism" but not on "aid to the Contras" (p. 48). A question on taxing social security benefits should show age differences independent of awareness, because nearly everyone can link it to nearness to retirement (p. 48).
- Obscurity depends on the issue (race is understood by most) or on question wording. The more abstract the link, the more knowledge it takes, or the longer the chain of reasoning, the more awareness matters. The simpler and more direct the link, the less it matters (p. 48). Zaller says obscurity is extremely common in politics, so the axiom keeps its bite (p. 48).

*Intuition note:* Aware people tend to resist unwelcome messages to the extent that they have cues, such as knowing who is speaking, that show the clash. When the link to one's interests is simple and direct, even unaware people can react, so awareness is likely to matter less.

**A3. Accessibility Axiom.** The more recently a consideration has been called to mind, the less time it takes to retrieve it or related considerations. Long-unused ones may become inaccessible, that is, forgotten (p. 48). Zaller takes this from cognitive psychology, where support is overwhelming and, as far as he can tell, undisputed (p. 48). The axiom does not say what makes considerations "related". Zaller implicitly relies on common understanding (pp. 48–49).

**A4. Response Axiom.** Individuals answer survey questions by averaging across the considerations that are immediately salient or accessible to them (p. 49).
- They do not normally canvass all relevant considerations. If one consideration is accessible they use it, and if several are they average them (p. 49).
- Different people can answer from different considerations, for example ideology, gut feelings or self-interest. This fits literature on interpersonal heterogeneity (p. 49).
- Zaller grants the axiom is too simple. Tourangeau and Rasinski (1988) propose four stages: interpret, canvass, integrate, map onto response options (p. 49). He says this may still be too complex for mass survey data. Their evidence is consistent with only one important step, retrieval of a dominant consideration, as with a conservative who thinks of "welfare cheats" (p. 49). Wyer and Srull's 43-postulate model is even more dubious for such data. Such models offer heuristic guidance, but rigorous analysis needs radical simplification (p. 50).
- *On-line processing* (Hastie and Park, 1986; Lodge, McGraw and Stroh, 1989) is where a threat to the Response Axiom, if there is one, comes from. People are said to update attitudes continuously and retrieve the stored attitude later (p. 50). Zaller says there are strong reasons for doubting that it holds generally for political attitudes, to be discussed after his own evidence is presented. He briefly notes two of the most important (p. 50):
  1. It is wildly unrealistic to expect citizens to update every relevant attitude on each piece of news.
  2. The on-line model, he says, is just a restatement of the conventional "true attitude" model, which he has argued is not capable of accommodating the evidence on mass political attitudes.
- Survey responses in the model may reasonably be called attitudes or opinions, since they represent people's true feelings at the moment of answering. They are not "true attitudes" in the technical sense, because they are not assumed to represent more than a single aspect of those feelings. "Opinion statement" or "attitude report" fits best (p. 50).

**The RAS model and how the book uses it (pp. 51–52).** Opinion statements result from receiving information, deciding whether to accept it, and sampling at the moment of answering: the Receive–Accept–Sample (RAS) model (p. 51).
- The method has three steps: derive the axioms' implications for specific information flows, check whether the evidence agrees, and add new evidence where needed (p. 51). Examples of flows: two equally intense competing streams, mostly one-sided messages, or shifting proportions (p. 51).
- At a few points the book supplements the axioms in nonfundamental ways, such as a functional form linking awareness to reception in Chapter 7. It introduces no significant new substantive claims (p. 51).
- *Preview formula.* From the Response Axiom, if nonresponse is ignored and every internalised consideration is equally likely to be sampled, then (equation unnumbered; the page text shows only the numerator, so the denominator is reconstructed, see Reading notes):
$$\text{Prob(Liberal response)}=\frac{L}{L+C}$$
where $L$ and $C$ are the numbers of liberal and conservative considerations in the person's mind. "Liberal" and "conservative" only label the direction of an idea (p. 52).
- The balance of $L$ and $C$ depends on the intensity of liberal and conservative information flows, on political awareness and on predispositions (p. 52). More aware people receive more messages (A1) but are more selective in accepting them (A2). So they tend to hold more considerations, which tend to be more consistent with each other and with their predispositions. Less aware people hold fewer and less consistent ones (p. 52).
- Consequently, more aware people are more likely to state an opinion, and more likely to state one consistent with their predispositions (p. 52). Changing information flows should change different segments of the public by different amounts, and even in different directions (p. 52).

*Intuition note:* Think of each mind as a bag of reasons, and each survey answer as one or a few draws from it. What gets in depends on what you hear and whether you resist it. What comes out depends on what is on top.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two citizens on one issue, with the model's simplifying assumptions (no nonresponse, equal sampling chance).
- Ana holds $L=3$, $C=1$: Prob(liberal) $=3/(3+1)=0.75$.
- Ben holds $L=2$, $C=2$: Prob(liberal) $=2/(2+2)=0.50$.
- A new conservative message stream adds 2 conservative considerations to Ana, assuming she accepts them. Now $L=3$, $C=3$: Prob $=3/6=0.50$. Her opinion statement shifts from 0.75 to 0.50 without any change in her predispositions.
- If Ana were highly aware and resisted the 2 messages through cue knowledge, she would keep $C=1$ and stay at 0.75.
This shows how reception and acceptance, not just stored attitudes, drive the answers.

## Concepts
- [[RAS-model]] — Receive–Accept–Sample: opinion statements come from received, accepted considerations sampled at answer time (new; related to the canon's Receive–Accept–Sample entry) (p. 51)
- [[consideration]] — any reason that might induce deciding an issue one way or the other; a blend of belief and evaluation (new) (p. 40)
- [[persuasive-message]] — argument or image giving a reason for a position; becomes a consideration if accepted (new) (p. 41)
- [[cueing-message]] — contextual information about the partisan or ideological meaning of a message; close to the canon's "heuristics / cues" but defined as part of elite discourse (new) (p. 42)
- [[political-awareness]] — cognitive engagement or attentiveness, measured mainly by general factual political knowledge (p. 43)
- [[reception-axiom]] — A1: more engagement makes reception of messages more likely (new) (p. 42)
- [[resistance-axiom]] — A2: people tend to resist inconsistent messages only to the extent that they have cue information (new) (p. 44)
- [[accessibility-axiom]] — A3: recent activation speeds retrieval of considerations (new) (p. 48)
- [[response-axiom]] — A4: answers average the accessible considerations (new) (p. 49)
- [[opinion-statement]] — a genuine survey response that is not a "true attitude" and may lack a long half-life (new) (p. 50)
- [[on-line-processing]] — rival view: attitudes are updated continuously and retrieved, which Zaller doubts holds generally for political attitudes (new) (p. 50)

## Canonical instances
- [[contra-aid-vs-communism]] — unaware hawks and doves respond partisanly to the familiar cue "communism" but not to "Contras" (pp. 42, 48)
- [[petty-cacioppo-experiment]] — 2×2×2 persuasion design: low involvement means attitudes are most strongly affected by source credibility, high involvement means they are influenced by argument strength (p. 46)
- [[social-security-tax-age]] — a transparent link to predispositions (age) means awareness matters little (p. 48)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Response Axiom (sampling from accessible considerations) ↔ survey methodology: response as a draw from a mix of considerations — answers vary by chance even if the mix is fixed.
- [metaphor] Source-credibility cues in low-involvement persuasion ↔ heuristics/cues (canon): shortcuts let people act as if informed.
- [structural-analogy] $L/(L+C)$ ↔ a proportion estimated from a sample: sampling probability equals the share of one type in the pool.

## Questions this raises
- How does the awareness–reception link take a precise functional form (promised for Chapter 7)? (p. 51)
- Can the auxiliary assumption that elite discourse (not peers) drives opinion be justified independently? (p. 44)
- When does on-line processing hold, and what evidence in later chapters favours the sampling model over it? (p. 50)
- Which issues and question wordings make contextual information "obscure", and so make awareness matter? (p. 48)
