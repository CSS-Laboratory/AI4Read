# Making it up as you go along (Zaller, ch. 5)

## L0 — One line
Because most people hold several conflicting considerations on an issue, their survey answers depend at least partly on whichever consideration is most salient at that moment, so question order, framing and priming change the answers.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do seemingly irrelevant features of a survey, such as question order, interviewer race or wording, change what people say? Zaller answers with two ideas from the RAS model: people are ambivalent, and they answer from the considerations at the top of their heads. Salience effects follow, including priming, which changes what predicts a response. Extra thought can disrupt reliability, apparently when it does not match normal thinking, and Zaller's own test of it was inconclusive. Zaller concludes that the public has no single "true attitude". Polling then helps frame opinion, and elites unavoidably frame issues for a public with contradictory feelings.

**Prerequisites:** Earlier chapters of the book (the RAS model, axioms A2–A4, the Ambivalence Deduction); regression with interaction terms; test–retest correlation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Question-order, race-of-interviewer, reference-group and TV-news effects share one mechanism: people answer partly from whatever ideas are immediately salient (pp. 77–79).
- Zaller treats question-order effects as evidence of ambivalence, not as methodological artifacts. The ambivalent should be most affected (p. 79; D15, p. 77).
- Priming changes which predispositions correlate with a response, not only the marginal distribution. Elite discourse, news stories and question frames can all do this (pp. 80–84).
- Frames can also inform. For the politically unaware, a framed Contra-aid question lets anticommunist feelings operate where the bare question did not (pp. 83–84).
- Political awareness raises critical capacity. Aware people are expected to give more extreme attitudes, which Zaller reads as a reliability effect, and extreme attitudes are reported faster (pp. 85–86).
- The "stop-and-think" test of extra thought gave mixed results. Zaller ends by saying that, it seems to him, D23 and D24 have not been adequately tested and are neither confirmed nor disconfirmed, since the probe may cut across normal thinking (pp. 86–91).
- Zaller sits between Converse and Achen. Unstable answers may express real but momentary feelings, yet no single true opinion exists (p. 94).
- If Zaller's view is accepted, pollsters count as part of the political elite, and from his perspective issue framing by elites is unavoidable (pp. 95–96).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: Bishop's experiment (p. 76)
- Bishop and colleagues began a survey with hard questions about the local congressman. Only 12% could recall a House vote of his (p. 76).
- Afterwards 45% of these respondents said they follow politics "only now and then" or "hardly at all". A group not asked the congressman questions gave 22% (p. 76).
- Zaller's generalisation: people are unsure of their opinions because outside interviews they are seldom asked to state them. In a hurried interview they "make up" reports and are heavily influenced by whatever is on top of their minds (p. 76).
- Chapter thesis: many regularities (race-of-interviewer, question-order, TV priming) are **salience effects**, a common mechanism not widely appreciated (pp. 76–77).

### Response effects (pp. 77–79)
- **Question order.** Earlier (Ch. 2): 37% would permit communist reporters in the US when asked alone, 73% when preceded by a question on US reporters in Russia (p. 77).
- Zaller does not claim people have no thoughts. Both wariness of communism and concern for reciprocity were probably present. Which one controlled the answer depended on which the questionnaire made salient (p. 77).
- Explanation by A3, the Accessibility Axiom. The more recently a consideration was activated, the more accessible it is. It is like ideas on top of a heap in memory (p. 77; D14).
- D15: people with a roughly even balance of considerations (ambivalent) should be most affected. Consistent people should not be affected (p. 77).
- Support: Tourangeau et al. (1989). People with mixed feelings who also called the issue very important showed "carryover effects"; others showed none. Carryover ranged from 4 and 8 points (abortion, welfare) to 34 and 36 points (Contra aid, defense spending). The average was 19 points (p. 78).
- Three effects with substantive interpretations (p. 78):
  - **Race of interviewer.** A 1986 NYT poll gave Reagan 37% approval among blacks. A Washington Post poll, using black interviewers who announced a study of black attitudes, gave 23%. Sussman argues the likely effect was to induce respondents to "think black".
  - **Reference groups.** Charters and Newcomb: Catholics gave answers more consistent with Church doctrine when a priest had briefly appeared in the room.
  - **TV priming.** Iyengar: after an "episodic" poverty story, people more often blame the poor for poverty than after a societal-framing story.
- The mechanism in each case is the tendency to respond at least partly on whatever ideas are immediately salient, and RAS (A3, A4) gives an explanation (D16–D18, p. 79). Footnote 1: temporary activation effects may last seconds to weeks (p. 79).
- **Electoral case, 1984.** Polls differed by up to 20 points on Reagan vs. Mondale, too much for sampling error. Lipset's explanation: Reagan did better when the preference question came before policy questions. This persisted into the fall (p. 79).
- Zaller finds the explanation plausible but not certain. Polls showed people liked "Reagan the man" more than his program, and the campaigns mirrored this (p. 79).
- Conclusion: question-order effects should be seen as showing a fundamental property of mass preferences. People are ambivalent, perhaps unconsciously, and decide by the most salient ideas (pp. 79–80).

> **Intuition note.** Think of many people as holding several reasons for and against an issue. A question that happens to remind them of one reason tips the answer. The people with balanced reasons are the ones who tip most easily.

### "Priming" as a type of salience effect (pp. 80–85)
- Salience effects can change both the marginal distribution of answers and the types of people giving a given answer. The second shows up as changed correlates of attitude reports (p. 80).
- Experimental evidence (Iyengar et al.): presidential evaluations correlate more strongly with defense-spending attitudes after news about defense. Naturalistic evidence (Krosnick and Kinder): the Iran-Contra scandal made Central America attitudes correlate more strongly with Reagan job ratings (p. 80).
- **RAS account of Iran-Contra** (stated as a simplified assumption). Before the scandal, media messages about Reagan concerned social welfare. Social welfare predispositions acted as "gatekeepers" (via A2) of which considerations were accepted, so they correlated strongly with job ratings (p. 80).
- Zaller then supposes that the media instead stressed Reagan's anticommunism and his flirting with illegality to fund the Contras; anticommunist predispositions would then be the gatekeepers and the engaged correlate (p. 81). Krosnick and Kinder's result is "exactly" this effect (p. 81).
- Question frames can do the same thing. A frame that raises social welfare considerations engages social welfare predispositions. A frame stressing anticommunism engages anticommunist ones (p. 81).
- **Affirmative action frames** (Kinder and Sanders, 1987 NES pilot). "Unfair advantage" wording engaged whites' symbolic racism. "Discriminates against whites" wording engaged group-competition attitudes. Overall opposition was unaffected, but which whites opposed changed (pp. 81–82).
- **Oil drilling in Alaska** (1989 NES pilot, three random forms: bare bones, foreign-dependency frame, economic-costs frame). In the first two forms, social class did not correlate with attitudes. In the economic-costs form, class became a strong correlate. Support also rose, mainly among the working class (p. 82). Footnote 2: environmentalism attitudes were more engaged in forms 2 and 3 than in form 1 (p. 82).
- Americans are therefore not unconcerned about economic costs. Few simply thought of them until prompted (pp. 82–83).
- **Frames as information: Contra aid** (1989 NES pilot, bare vs. framed question; Table 5.1). Few unaware people knew who the Contras were (Ch. 2). Zaller reports that on the bare question they treated it as domestic-style spending: favouring domestic spending went with favouring Contra aid. Anticommunism was uncorrelated with their answers (p. 83).
- On the framed question, the unaware recognised the anticommunism link. Domestic-spending attitudes no longer mattered, and anticommunism became a strong determinant. Aware people were unaffected by form (p. 83).

**Table 5.1** (Form = 1 for the communism frame; variables scored liberal; t-ratios in parentheses; p. 84):

| Form | −0.51 (2.25) | −0.29 (1.34) | 0.26 (1.46) |

Intercepts are 0.93, 0.93 and 0.04. For the low group, the framed-form slopes are $0.03+0.34=0.37$ for anticommunism and $-0.37+0.50=0.13$ for domestic spending (added step).

- Summary: priming can come from (1) months of elite discourse, (2) a recent news story, or (3) question framing. In all three cases, but especially the last, it may be explained by the assumption that people hold several opinions (Ambivalence Deduction) and report the one most recently made salient (D19, p. 84). Footnote 3: effects (1) and (2) may also involve forming some new considerations, which belongs to attitude change (p. 84).
- **Order-induced priming.** Milburn: asking people to say everything that comes to mind about a Liberal (Conservative) made later attitude reports more ideologically consistent than in a control group. This held only for people with liberal or conservative leanings. Price replicated this by simply asking for liberal–conservative self-placement, again only for those with a clear leaning (pp. 84–85). RAS explains this (D20, p. 85).

> **Intuition note.** The same person can be "conservative" on one answer and "liberal" on another. Which of their attitudes is behind the answer depends on what the question or the news just made salient. So a frame changes who the supporters are, not only how many there are.

### Effects of thought on the reliability of attitude reports (pp. 85–91)
- **Habitual awareness.** In RAS, awareness raises stability and the reliability of reports (Ch. 6). An uncritical person would tend to hold mutually cancelling considerations much of the time, so reports would tend to be centrist, most often among the less aware (p. 85).
- Awareness brings critical capacity and internally consistent considerations, so the more aware should be more noncentrist (D21). This is confirmed (Sidanius; Lusk and Judd) (p. 85).
- Zaller prefers to call this a *reliability* effect. There is no reason to think the unaware differ in underlying predispositions. They are only less able to convert them into noncentrist reports (p. 85).
- **D22.** By A3, recently activated considerations are found faster. Awareness implies more recent activation, so extreme attitudes should be reported faster. Judd et al. (1991) confirm this (pp. 85–86). Footnote 4: Judd et al. also find that the second of two related questions is answered faster, which RAS explains by accessibility (p. 86).
- **Short-term extra thought.** According to the model, extra thought cannot change the mix of considerations, which come from media and elite discourse. It can lengthen memory search and so raise the number of considerations used (p. 86).
- The 1987 NES pilot "stop-and-think" probes made respondents discuss what a question means before answering. Expectation: averaging over more considerations should give higher reliability. Two indicators of reliability are stability over time and predictive reliability, the correlation with related attitudes (D23, D24) (p. 86).
- **Predictive reliability test.** A left–right scale built from social welfare items was related to the target items (jobs, government services, aid to blacks) in a stop-and-think vs. a retrospective condition. The model was (p. 87):
$$\text{Response}=b_0+b_1\text{Form}+b_2\text{Ideology}+b_3\,\text{Form}\times\text{Ideology}$$
- For the top 40% on awareness, $b_3$ had the expected sign for all three items but was significant for only one. With coefficients constrained equal across items (Table 5.2), the ideology effect is twice as large in stop-and-think, which is significant (p. 87).

**Table 5.2** (t-ratios in parentheses; p. 87):

| | Low awareness | High awareness |
|---|---|---|
| Intercept | 0.01 | −.20 |
| Ideology | 0.89 (4.23) | 0.62 (4.43) |
| Form | −0.36 (1.71) | 0.00 (0.0) |
| Ideology × Form | −0.24 (0.89) | 0.62 (2.38) |
| N | 434 | 437 |

- For the bottom 40%, stop-and-think did not raise reliability and "may have reduced it slightly". This is confounded by an artifact. The retrospective condition had an explicit "no interest" option, so low-awareness no-opinion rates averaged 38% (retrospective) vs. 4% (stop-and-think). Stop-and-think respondents are a less selected group (pp. 87–88). Footnote 5: among the highest information quintile, 16% vs. 3% (p. 88).
- The artifact runs against the aware-group result, so that increase is likely real (p. 88).
- **Stability test (Table 5.3).** Stop-and-think was expected to raise stability. It left stability among the highly aware unchanged and greatly reduced it among middle- and low-awareness people (p. 88).

**Table 5.3**, test–retest correlations, retrospective → stop-and-think (p. 88):

| | Low | Middle | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job guarantees | .68 → .45 | .64 → .41 | .70 → .55 |
| Government services | .56 → .43 | .48 → .38 | .61 → .58 |
| Aid to blacks | .79 → .53 | .81 → .51 | .70 → .86 |

- D24 appears disconfirmed but is tainted by selection bias (and footnote 6: respondents must answer twice). Zaller proposes to accept the disconfirmation of D24 and the partial disconfirmation of D23 "as probably real". Part of the reason is the size of the apparent disconfirmation in Table 5.3; more important, the pattern is intelligible in light of psychological research and could have been predicted from it (pp. 88–89).
- **Wilson et al.** Asking people to "analyze the reasons" for their feelings often disrupts the predictive reliability of attitude reports (p. 89).
  - Poster experiment: students who analysed reasons before choosing were less satisfied weeks later. Reasons were easy to find for humor posters ("it's funny") and against Impressionist prints ("fuzzy images"), so they chose humor posters more. Controls overwhelmingly chose Impressionist prints and were more satisfied (p. 89).
  - Wilson's account: the reasons sound plausible but may not explain one's feelings, and people adopt the attitude the reasons imply (p. 89).
  - Footnote 7: Wilson's stability findings are not comparable, because his stability tests usually have reasons analysed at T2 but not at T1 (p. 89).
- These results tend, in a fairly direct way, to confirm RAS's central assertion that reports reflect top-of-head ideas. But RAS cannot explain why a larger base of considerations undermines reliability (pp. 89–90).
- Zaller's resolution (following Wilson): the problem is extra thought that does not mimic the normal memory search (p. 90).
- **Millar and Tesser.** Reasons analysis should predict instrumental behavior, and feelings analysis should predict consummatory behavior. Mismatches give low correlations, and they found this (p. 90; footnote 8 gives the puzzle design).
- The 1987 probe ("What kinds of things come to mind…") appears more likely to induce an analysis of reasons than of feelings. Sniderman, Brody and Tetlock find the less aware weight feelings more, and the more aware stress ideological principle. So the probe should disrupt the less aware and help (or not harm) the more aware, which is the pattern in Tables 5.2 and 5.3 (p. 91).
- Zaller's verdict: a method that mimics normal thinking is needed. Meanwhile D23 and D24 "cannot be said to have been adequately tested", being neither confirmed nor disconfirmed (p. 91). The psychological studies still support the central RAS claim, though not necessarily every implication (p. 91).
- Footnote 9: Wilson, Kraft and Dunn find reasons analysis disrupts the less aware but does not improve the more aware. Their "more aware" are the top half, versus the top 40% here (p. 91).

> **Intuition note.** Zaller's expectation is that more thought should help, but he argues the problem lies in thought that does not mimic the normal search. Forcing people to list reasons can bring up different, possibly unrepresentative ideas, and then the answer may predict attitudes and behavior based on a normal memory search worse.

### Conclusions (pp. 91–96)
**Nature of public opinion.**
- Two ideas do nearly all the theoretical work: the Ambivalence Deduction and the Response Axiom. They explain response instability and also salience effects such as question order, race of interviewer and framing (p. 92).
- Zaller says the Ambivalence Deduction is probably the more unfamiliar and hence potentially controversial. It contradicts the dominant theories (Converse and Achen) and violates commonsense notions of public opinion (p. 92).
- Why it is plausible: issues are posed as summary judgments, which aggregate diverse concerns. Nothing requires consistent feelings about each element, e.g. supporting abortion rights without feeling comfortable about killing fetuses. Contradictory reactions each can be genuine, and most people most of the time need not reconcile them (pp. 92–93).
- Few citizens are ideologically consistent across issues (Converse, 1964), and Zaller argues many are equally inconsistent across aspects of one issue (p. 93).
- Implication: people typically have "a series of partially independent and often inconsistent" attitudes, not true ones. Which is expressed depends on chance and question order and framing (p. 93).
- Example: before the Persian Gulf War, support for war varied across polling questions. Journalists said framing could push support for a hard line to 40–50% or to 70–80% (p. 93).
- Not everyone is ambivalent on every issue. Nonambivalence is explained by RAS, provided it comes with high attention (pp. 93–94).

**Public opinion and democracy.**
- Converse: much instability reflects "nonattitudes". Achen: Converse's thesis would mean democratic theory "loses its starting point". Achen's own finding was that most people have almost perfectly stable true attitudes, with vague questions causing the instability (p. 94).
- Zaller's position is between them. Like Converse, he sees much uncertainty, and the problem is deeper than vague questions. Like Achen, he rejects the idea that fluctuation is mostly random guessing (p. 94).
- Even opposite answers at different times may express real feelings about the issue as seen at the moment. Random fluctuation between sincerely held positions would still indicate the balance of feelings (p. 94). But Zaller denies any single true position, for individuals or the public (p. 94).
- What is measured as opinion always depends on framing and ordering. Frames do not distort a true opinion, because the public uses the question to decide what the issue is (p. 95).
- If this view is accepted, pollsters count as part of the political elite. Kinder and Sanders: survey designers and powerholders both choose how issues are framed (p. 95).
- Pollsters aim to be unobtrusive. In practice this means offering the major positions of the elite debate, which passes framing back to politicians, activists, journalists and media consultants (p. 95).
- Zaller claims a novel micro-explanation of why elite framing works. From his perspective framing is unavoidable: given a public with no fixed attitude, someone has to crystallise issues in a way that can lead to action (pp. 95–96).
- Arnold (1990): Congress takes contradictory actions (balanced budget vs. spending items; tax incentives vs. simplification), depending on how issue entrepreneurs frame an issue. Leaders play on contradictory ideas in people's minds (p. 96).
- The next five chapters turn to the content of opinion statements and how they form (p. 96).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ten people are asked whether to permit communist reporters. Four have only "against" considerations, and two have only "for" considerations. Four are ambivalent, with one "for" (reciprocity) and one "against" (wariness of communism). Assume each answers from the single most recently activated consideration.
- **Asked alone:** the wariness cue is on top for the ambivalent four, so they say no. Permit = 2/10 = 20%.
- **After a question on US reporters in Russia:** reciprocity is on top for the ambivalent four, so they say yes. Permit = (2 + 4)/10 = 60%.
- **Result:** the change is 40 points, and all of it comes from the ambivalent four. The consistent six do not move (cf. D15). Real data show a 36-point change (37% → 73%, p. 77).

## Concepts
- [[salience-effect]] (new) — answers shaped by the ideas most immediately salient; grouped under one mechanism (pp. 76–79)
- [[ambivalence-deduction]] (new) — people hold differing feelings toward aspects of an issue, so no single fixed attitude (pp. 84, 92)
- [[accessibility-axiom]] (new) — A3: the more recently a consideration was activated, the more accessible it is (p. 77)
- [[response-effect]] (new) — seemingly irrelevant questionnaire features that change responses; for Zaller, not mere artifacts (pp. 77–79)
- [[priming-agenda-setting]] — here, changing which predispositions correlate with responses by what is salient (pp. 80–81, 84)
- [[framing-effect]] — question frames raise some considerations, and can also inform the unaware (pp. 81–83)
- [[gatekeeper-predisposition]] (new) — predisposition regulating which considerations are accepted, via A2 (p. 80)
- [[ras-model]] (reuse of the RAS concept) — opinion as a sample of accepted considerations, answered from the top of the head (pp. 76, 80, 88)
- [[political-awareness]] — habitual engagement; raises critical capacity, extremity and reliability of reports (p. 85)
- [[extremity-effect]] (new) — the aware give more noncentrist reports, read by Zaller as a reliability effect (p. 85)
- [[stop-and-think-probe]] (new) — a probe to temporarily raise engagement. Results are mixed and the test is judged inadequate (pp. 86–91)
- [[non-attitudes]] — Converse's thesis, which Zaller rejects as a general account (p. 94)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Reports as averages of a sample of considerations ↔ statistics: the sample mean is a more reliable estimate with larger samples — this is the logic behind D23.
- [structural-analogy] Elites framing issues to reach a favourable majority (Arnold) ↔ social choice: agenda control when preferences are mixed — both turn on how the question is posed.
- [metaphor] Pollsters as part of the elite ↔ measurement reactivity: the instrument changes what is measured.

## Questions this raises
- If reported opinion depends on framing, what would count as a valid measure of public opinion? Zaller notes that measuring opinion is hard to defend as a neutral act and that framing choices seem consequential (p. 95).
- Can a stop-and-think probe be built that imitates normal thinking, so D23 and D24 can be tested fairly (p. 91)?
- If no true attitude exists, on what basis should leaders be responsive to the public (pp. 94–96)?
- The tests of D23 and D24 are confounded with differences in no-opinion rates. How could a design remove that selection problem (p. 88)?
