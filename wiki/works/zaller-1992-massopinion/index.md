# Zaller, J. R. (1992). *The Nature and Origins of Mass Opinion*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/CBO9780511818691

## In one paragraph
Zaller offers one general model of how people turn political information into opinions, to replace a field split into separate subliteratures. In the Receive–Accept–Sample (RAS) model, elites supply messages. Citizens receive them according to their political awareness. They accept or resist them according to their predispositions and the cues they have. A survey answer is then an average of the few considerations that are most accessible. This explains unstable answers, question-order and framing effects, mainstream and polarization patterns, and why attitude change can peak among the moderately aware. The model is tested on surveys about Vietnam, defense, Central America and elections. Zaller admits its simplifications and ends by asking whether this dependence on elites means elite domination.

## The path through the work
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch01-introduction]] — Zaller proposes one simple, general model of how people turn media information into political opinions, as a break with public opinion research that is split into domain-specific subliteratures.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch02-information]] — Opinions are built from information carried in elite discourse (received unevenly, depending on political awareness) and from predispositions; the author argues that survey answers are therefore often put together on the spot from whichever considerations come to mind first (the top-of-the-head ones), rather than read off fixed attitudes.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch03-citizens]] — Survey answers are samples of the considerations (reasons) a person has received and accepted, and that person's political awareness shapes both reception and acceptance (the Receive–Accept–Sample, or RAS, model).
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch04-coming]] — In Zaller's account, survey answers wobble over time because, on most issues, most people hold conflicting "considerations" (reasons bearing on the issue) and answer from whichever come to mind most readily; open-ended probes and earlier studies are presented as fitting this account.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch05-making]] — Because most people hold several conflicting considerations on an issue, their survey answers depend at least partly on whichever consideration is most salient at that moment, so question order, framing and priming change the answers.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch06-mainstream]] — The RAS model predicts that political awareness raises support for a policy when elites agree (mainstream effect) and pulls opposed partisans apart when elites disagree (polarization effect).
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch07-basic]] — In the RAS model, attitude change is a two-step process, reception then acceptance, and a logistic version of that product is estimated on survey data and agrees with the predicted patterns: aware people receive more but resist more, so change can rise or peak in the middle with awareness.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch08-tests]] — Zaller's reception–acceptance model, applied to a one-sided message, predicts different shapes of opinion change for different message intensities, issue familiarities and ages, and the available data roughly bear this out.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch09-two]] — Zaller argues that public opinion on an issue responds to the relative intensity of competing (pro and con) message flows, filtered by each citizen's awareness and values, and a two-message extension of his Receive–Accept–Sample (RAS) model of opinion formation fits Vietnam War opinion in 1964–70.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch10-information-p1]] — In 1978 House elections, highly aware outpartisans (voters of the party opposing the incumbent) resist the incumbent's dominant campaign mainly because they alone pick up the faint challenger campaign (countervalent resistance), more than because awareness itself makes them reject incoming messages (awareness-driven partisan resistance played a role, but not a large one).
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch10-information-p2]] — In elections, resistance to a dominant campaign comes mainly from countervalent (opposing) information in the message stream, not from individual predispositions alone, and the same reception–acceptance logic fits House, Senate, presidential and primary data.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch11-evaluating-p1]] — Zaller restates the RAS model, argues that elites plausibly lead mass opinion (though the model itself cannot prove it), and lists weaknesses in each axiom, among the most serious being that it has no theory of perception and no mechanism for integrating information.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch11-evaluating-p2]] — Zaller defends the core of his model (awareness interacts with predispositions to shape reception and acceptance of elite messages), admits its theoretical and statistical weak points, and suggests that the same few ideas can fruitfully be applied to presidential evaluations, trust in government (with limits) and support for authoritarian regimes.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch12-epilogue]] — Heavy dependence of mass opinion on elite messages is unlikely to be elite domination, if experts are diverse and motivated to solve problems, the press covers all expert views, politicians stay within expert opinion, and citizens can follow like-minded elites.
- [[zaller-1992-massopinion/ch13-measures]] — Zaller measures political awareness with neutral factual knowledge, built into long additive "catch-all" scales (which mix direct and converted items), because, on theoretical and empirical grounds, it was the preferred measure and almost always outperformed education, media use, participation and interest.

## Big ideas across units
- **Answers are samples of considerations, not readouts of fixed attitudes.** People hold conflicting considerations and answer from the most accessible ones. This explains instability, order and framing effects, and the claim that there is no single "true attitude." Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch04, ch05, ch11-p1.
- **Political awareness governs reception.** Awareness varies widely around a low average. It raises exposure to elite messages, and it is measured with neutral factual knowledge. Units: ch02, ch03, ch06, ch07, ch08, ch13.
- **Acceptance depends on predispositions and cues.** Resistance appears in three forms: partisan, inertial and countervalent. Countervalent resistance gets the strongest support; inertial resistance is doubtful. Units: ch03, ch07, ch08, ch10-p1, ch10-p2, ch11-p1, ch11-p2.
- **The pattern of elite discourse shapes what awareness does.** Elite consensus produces a mainstream effect. Elite division produces polarization, and also constraint across issues. Units: ch02, ch06, ch09, ch12.
- **Attitude change can be nonmonotonic.** Reception rises and acceptance falls with awareness, so change can peak among the moderately aware. The curves vary with message intensity, issue familiarity and age. Units: ch02, ch07, ch08, ch10-p1, ch10-p2, ch11-p2.
- **Opinion tracks the relative intensity of competing message flows.** The one-message model is extended to two messages for Vietnam, House, Senate, presidential and primary data. Units: ch08, ch09, ch10-p1, ch10-p2, ch11-p2.
- **Instability is treated as substantive, not as noise.** Zaller reads wobbling answers as ambivalence. He sees the measurement-error account as compatible with his model but not as its explanation. Units: ch02, ch04, ch05.
- **Elite influence, its limits and its normative question.** Elite messages are treated as exogenous, and the model itself cannot show elite influence. The epilogue asks when heavy dependence amounts to domination. Units: ch01, ch11-p1, ch11-p2, ch12.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch02 and ch03 (the ingredients and the four axioms), then ch07 (the reception × acceptance model). The catalog assigns Chs. 1–5 and 7, so ch01, ch04 and ch05 are also core. ch04 and ch05 show how the model explains instability and response effects.
- **Dependencies, from the stated prerequisites:**
  - ch03 builds on ch02's elite discourse.
  - ch04 needs ch03's axioms, and ch05 needs the earlier chapters and the Ambivalence Deduction.
  - ch06 uses the RAS model.
  - ch07 uses the axioms.
  - ch08 needs ch07's Equations 7.3 and 7.5.
  - ch09 needs the ch06 patterns and ch07–08.
  - ch10 (both parts) needs ch7–9.
  - ch11 needs the four axioms and the ch7–10 functions.
  - ch12 needs the elite-cue, awareness and mainstream/polarization ideas.
  - ch13 supports axiom A1.
- **Can skip on a first pass:** the technical material in ch08 (the typology), ch09 (the fitted two-message model and instrumental variables) and ch10-p1/p2 (election estimates and appendices). Also ch13, except for why factual knowledge measures awareness.
- **Worth reading selectively:** ch06 gives the clearest empirical payoff. ch11 is a self-critique, and ch12 is the normative conclusion.
