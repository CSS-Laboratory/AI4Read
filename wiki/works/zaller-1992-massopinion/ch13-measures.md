# Measures appendix: how Zaller measures political awareness and predispositions

## L0 — One line
Zaller measures political awareness with neutral factual knowledge, built into long additive "catch-all" scales (which mix direct and converted items), because, on theoretical and empirical grounds, it was the preferred measure and almost always outperformed education, media use, participation and interest.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The appendix asks how to measure political awareness, the book's key independent variable, and how to build the scales. Zaller argues on theoretical and empirical grounds that factual knowledge is the best measure. General scales apparently cost little compared with domain-specific (single-area) ones. Because many surveys lack enough knowledge items, he converts other items into knowledge tests: issue-location tests (placing candidates correctly on issue scales), recognition items (rating public figures) and interviewer ratings. He gives coding rules and survey-by-survey scale recipes. Predisposition measures are admitted to be much more uneven. This matters because effects attributed to awareness can depend greatly on how awareness is measured (p. 333).

**Prerequisites:** survey items and scales; correlation; reliability of a scale (alpha); the book's idea that awareness governs reception of political messages (axiom A1).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Estimated awareness effects can depend greatly on the measure used, so the measure needs justification (p. 333).
- Education, media exposure, participation and interest each have specific weaknesses as awareness measures (pp. 334–335; interest: p. 335).
- Factual knowledge captures learning that has occurred and is relatively immune to social-desirability bias (overstating what looks good). In Zaller's tests it was almost always the leading performer (pp. 335–336).
- Domain-specific knowledge scales are theoretically better but only modestly better empirically, so the book uses general scales (pp. 336–337).
- Many datasets lack enough knowledge items. Location tests, recognition items and interviewer ratings fill the gap (pp. 337–338).
- Coding rules: equal weights, additive scales, "Don't know" counted as wrong, mean imputation for missing data (p. 339).
- Predisposition measures are much more uneven and less adequate, and used opportunistically; Zaller names two excellent cases and notes NES value measures have been generally excellent since 1984 (p. 344).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Political knowledge: how to measure awareness (pp. 333–336)
Zaller has tested alternative awareness measures across datasets. The result: effects attributed to awareness can depend greatly on measurement (p. 333).

**Candidates.** NES offers participation, interest, media use, education and neutral factual knowledge (p. 333). The literature has no agreement on the best one. Footnote 1 (p. 333) says researchers seem to use whatever measure is handy. Converse himself used different measures in different writings: interest, media usage, "partisan political involvement" (p. 334).

**Weaknesses of the alternatives (author's argument).**
- *Education.* Only moderately correlated with interest and media exposure. Many educated people may have the skill but lack the motivation (p. 334).
- *Media exposure.*
  - Zaller and Price argue it is probably necessary to distinguish "low-brow" from "high-brow" media (only the latter carry the rich national and international news), but most surveys cannot make the distinction.
  - Different media are hard to calibrate against each other.
  - Self-reports suffer from differing self-rating standards and social-desirability exaggeration. Example: self-reports implied 40% of Americans hear National Public Radio several times a week, about 10 times NPR's own estimates (p. 334).
  - Some claimed Wall Street Journal readers later said they read it seven days a week. Such misreporting can damage models that must discriminate at the top of awareness (pp. 334–335).
  - Exposure is not reception of information, which the model requires (p. 335).
- *Participation.* A patronage worker and an issue-club activist look alike, and a person can be highly aware without participating (p. 335).
- *Interest.* Reported interest is readily affected by the context in which the question is asked (Bishop et al.), and interest requires respondents to estimate an inner state (p. 335).

**Why knowledge.** Knowledge tests capture ideas actually encountered, understood and stored (p. 335). Compared with the alternatives, they are relatively immune to social-desirability response sets and to context effects on interest questions. They need no self-estimate of behaviour or inner states: respondents pass or fail (p. 335). Footnote 2: guessing penalties did not improve the information scales.

**Empirical comparison.** Zaller tested education, media exposure, participation, interest and knowledge against criteria: attitude stability, attitude consistency, and the correlation of vote with party attachment. With Price he also tested reception of news stories and a reception–acceptance model of attitude change. Knowledge was "always a respectable competitor and almost always the leading performer"; no other measure came close overall. Luskin's narrower tests agree (pp. 335–336).

*Intuition note:* Knowledge comes closest to recording what a person has actually taken in. The other measures record skill, interest, behaviour or self-description, and Zaller points to specific weaknesses in each.

### Issues in measuring information (pp. 336–337)
**Global or domain-specific?** Axiom A1 says reception depends on attention to particular issues. So domain-specific measures should show stronger effects, and the evidence supports this (Iyengar; Zaller 1986; McGraw and Pinney) (p. 336). The practical problem is poor discriminant validity (scales for different domains should not correlate too highly). People attentive to one facet tend to attend to others. Iyengar achieved validity by dropping highly cross-correlated, face-valid items, which lowered reliability. Zaller's scales kept all face-valid items. Economics, foreign policy and minority-relations scales then intercorrelated about .70 (not corrected for unreliability). They beat a general scale in predicting criteria "but never by margins that approached statistical or substantive significance" (p. 336). His 1986 conclusion: information is "a relatively general trait that can be effectively measured with a general-purpose information scale" (p. 336). Price and Zaller (1990) reached a similar conclusion. The book therefore uses general scales almost exclusively, and the loss is "apparently slight" (p. 337).

### Types of information tests (pp. 337–339)
Across 24 ICPSR datasets, item number, quality and format vary. A few have good dedicated tests (NES 1986 and 1988, the Brazilian survey). A few have almost none (NES 1978 congressional, 1984 "rolling thunder" primaries, 1990 Senate). Most have too few good items for alpha ≥ .85 (p. 337). Footnote 4: a 3–4 item scale has reliability around .65 and can miss mild nonmonotonicity (Figure 8.2) or subtle features of attitude-change curves (Figure 8.5). Footnote 3: 1984 candidate-awareness items are rejected because they rise with campaign learning. The theory needs habitual awareness, not awareness that reacts to the current campaign (p. 337).

Three item types are used heavily:
1. **Location tests.**
   - Since 1968, NES respondents place themselves and political objects on issue scales.
   - An item can be used as a knowledge test when the correct relative placement is clear to an informed observer. Example: Bush on the pro-spending side of Dukakis on defense spending (pp. 337–338).
   - Judgment is needed. Carter versus Ford on women's rights is not obvious, so it is unusable (p. 338).
   - Footnote 5: pre-1970 "which party favors X" items had low reliability. Zaller suspects this is because they are one-shot items, whereas location items need three responses and so screen out guessers.
2. **Recognition items.** Respondents who say they are unfamiliar with a figure on a feeling thermometer fail. Because these are highly susceptible to social desirability, Zaller uses them sparingly (p. 338).
3. **Interviewer ratings** (five-point). At least for face-to-face surveys with considerable political content, a single rating performs about as well as 10–15 direct tests at predicting criteria. Zaller checked for bias favouring higher-status persons (whites, males) and was able to find none (p. 338).

**Catch-all versus direct tests.** In the 1985 NES pilot, a 27-item direct-knowledge scale and a catch-all scale showed essentially no difference in predicting attitude stability, consistency and issue salience (p. 339). Footnote 7: the catch-all scale had 22 items and alpha .87; the 27-item scale had .89.

*Intuition note:* In the one test reported (1985 NES pilot), a scale of converted items predicted three criteria about as well as a purpose-built knowledge test. Zaller also stresses that lengthy scales are needed (n.4).

### Coding conventions (p. 339)
1. Each discrete test counts equally. Multi-bit items count bit by bit: one point per Supreme Court justice named (up to four) in 1966, and one point per interviewer-rating level above the lowest.
2. Simple additive scales, with a few noted exceptions. Footnote 8: principal components gave small weights to skewed items, which Zaller regards as strong because they isolate the extremes of awareness.
3. "Don't know" counts as incorrect, including when a respondent was not asked for candidate placements because he had no opinion on the issue.
4. Persons with missing data are kept unless more than two-thirds of their responses are missing. They get the average score of the items with data.

Alpha reliabilities are about .80–.90, mostly about .85. The 1951, 1978 House and 1990 Senate scales are .60–.70. Zaller gives no per-scale reliabilities, because alpha assumes equal item difficulty and other measures are unavailable (pp. 339–340).

### Political predispositions (pp. 344–345)
Awareness measures are strong and comparable except in 1951 and 1956. Predisposition measures are "much more uneven and, on the whole, much less adequate" (p. 344). Two studies are good: the 1987 pilot (Hurwitz–Peffley foreign-policy measure) and the 1989 pilot (Conover–Feldman equality items). Elsewhere, value measures often missed the dimension on which opinion was changing. Chapter 2 justifies using whatever is available (p. 344). The only measure of general partisan orientation in the 1951 study was recalled 1948 vote. The 1956 study had good preference questions, but none that seem well suited to the predispositions underlying racial attitudes. Since 1984, NES has had generally excellent value measures (p. 344).

**Coding.** Items keep their full range. They are combined by principal-components weights, unless all items have the same range, in which case a simple additive index is used (p. 344).

**Scales.** 1956: domestic cluster (V32, V38, V53, V59) and foreign cluster (V35, V50, V68, V71), each scaled by principal components (pp. 344–345). 1964–70: see the Chapter 9 appendix. 1978: five items (p. 345). 1982: three economic items, and two defense items for the nuclear freeze (p. 345). 1984: four items (p. 345). 1987 pilot: hawk–dove scale (six Hurwitz–Peffley items plus two); social-welfare ideology scale (fourteen Conover–Feldman items plus two self-designations); government-spending scale (p. 345).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A respondent in a survey with the following items.
- Five one-point tests: correct, correct, "Don't know", wrong, missing.
- Naming Supreme Court justices: she names 2.
- Interviewer rating: 4 on the five-point scale.

Apply the coding rules, imputing only on the one-point items (our simplification).
- "Don't know" counts as wrong, so it scores 0. Items with data: 4 (correct, correct, DK, wrong). Points among them: 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 = 2.
- The missing item gets the average of the available items: 2/4 = 0.5. One-point block = 2 + 0.5 = 2.5.
- Justices: 1 point each, so 2 points.
- Rating 4 is three levels above the lowest, so 3 points.
- Additive total = 2.5 + 2 + 3 = 7.5. Maximum possible = 5 + 4 + 4 = 13.

Two-thirds rule: she is missing 1 of 5 one-point responses (20%), so she is kept.

## Concepts
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — in this book, habitual propensity to receive political information, best measured by factual knowledge (pp. 333, 335)
- [[knowledge-based-awareness-measure]] (new) — additive scale of neutral factual tests used as the awareness indicator (p. 335)
- [[location-test]] (new) — a correct relative placement of candidates, parties or groups on an issue scale, scored as knowledge (p. 337)
- [[recognition-item]] (new) — saying one is unfamiliar with a figure on a feeling thermometer counts as failing a knowledge test (p. 338)
- [[interviewer-rating-of-information]] (new) — five-point rating of apparent political information, one point per level above the lowest (pp. 338–339)
- [[social-desirability-response-set]] (new) — tendency to overstate socially desirable traits. Knowledge tests resist it better than self-reports (p. 335)
- [[domain-specific-vs-general-awareness]] (new) — scales for one issue area versus a general scale. Domain-specific scales are only modestly better here (pp. 336–337)
- [[alpha-reliability]] (new) — internal-consistency statistic. Zaller wants ≥ .85 and notes that its equal-difficulty assumption fails (pp. 337, 340)
- [[political-predispositions]] (new) — value-based predispositions used as predictors. Measured unevenly and often opportunistically (p. 344)
- [[text-as-data-validation]] — loosely related: choosing a measure by testing it against criterion variables (p. 335)

## Questions this raises
- How much do the book's substantive results change under another awareness measure, given the opening claim that effects depend on measurement (p. 333)?
- If domain-specific scales are only modestly better, is it because awareness is truly general or because the domain scales have poor discriminant validity (p. 336)?
- Could the opportunistic predisposition measures bias the tests of the mainstream effects (p. 344)?
