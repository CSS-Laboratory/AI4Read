# The mainstream and polarization effects

## L0 — One line
The RAS model predicts that political awareness raises support for a policy when elites agree (mainstream effect) and pulls opposed partisans apart when elites disagree (polarization effect).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Zaller asks how the flow of elite messages changes what political awareness does to public opinion. From the RAS model he derives two patterns. When elites are nearly unanimous, more aware citizens absorb more of the same message and support the policy more. When elites split along partisan lines, more aware citizens follow their own side's elites, so liberals and conservatives diverge. He tests this on Vietnam (1964, 1970), the 1990 Persian Gulf crisis, and eight 1972–76 National Election Study (NES) items. He also argues that the same logic explains why attitude constraint (consistent positions across issues) rises with awareness, and he judges it preferable to rival explanations based on education or internal mental structure.

**Prerequisites:** the RAS model (reception, acceptance, sampling of considerations) from earlier chapters; logistic regression, interaction terms, multicollinearity, F-test.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Under elite consensus, the more aware a person is, the more mainstream messages he or she internalizes, so support for the mainstream policy rises with awareness (D25) (p. 98).
- Under a balanced partisan split, aware liberals and conservatives take in mostly their own side's considerations, so attitude reports polarize as awareness rises (D26) (pp. 100–102).
- Evidence: the Vietnam War in 1964 (mainstream) versus 1970 (polarized), and the Gulf crisis before and after congressional criticism (pp. 102–104).
- A test on eight 1972–76 NES items supports both deductions. The awareness × ideology term is large on partisan items and trivial on mainstream items (pp. 108–113).
- Zaller says the logic of polarization is isomorphic with Converse's account of attitude constraint. Constraint across issues rises with awareness because the aware learn "what goes with what" from elites (D27, D28) (pp. 113–114).
- Zaller calls the idea that the aware resist all persuasion "highly dubious". Awareness does appear to produce resistance to governing authorities, but less by resistance per se than by increasing sensitivity to countervailing elites' messages (p. 100).
- Zaller prefers the RAS account to issue-by-issue explanations (education as sober thought, security, sophistication) because it covers more cases. It also stresses external sources of constraint, the pattern of elite division, without denying internal mental connections (pp. 114–116).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: Nixon and price controls (pp. 97–98)
- In 1971 Nixon announced wage and price controls. Commentators across the spectrum welcomed them (p. 97).
- Columbia survey of activists: Democrats were largely unmoved, since they already favored controls. Republican activists' support rose from 37% to 82%, about 45 points. Gallup showed the public as a whole about 10 points more favorable (p. 97).
- The chapter treats two ideal types. In the first, elites reach (near-)consensus and virtually all messages take one side. In the second, elites disagree along partisan or ideological lines and the flow is roughly even (p. 97). Price controls and Vietnam in 1964 illustrate the first; Vietnam in the late 1960s illustrates the second (p. 97). Messier cases come later (p. 98).

### Mainstream effect (pp. 98–100)
- **Setup.** Virtually all persuasive messages favor the policy, and no cueing message says it clashes with people's values (p. 98).
- **Deduction.** By A1, higher awareness means more reception of persuasive messages. With no cues, A2 gives no basis for resisting. So higher awareness means more mainstream considerations internalized and, all else equal, more support (D25) (p. 98).
- **Corollary.** The awareness–support correlation should be strongest when elite consensus is strongest and weaker when consensus is weaker or absent (D25) (p. 99).
- **Supporting authors.**
  - Key (1961): education indicates exposure to society's "official" values (p. 98).
  - Gamson and Modigliani (1966): information correlates with support for government foreign policy (pp. 98–99).
  - McClosky and Brill (1983) and Mueller (1973) make related arguments (p. 99).
  - Sniderman et al. (1991) on AIDS quarantine: Zaller *interprets* the education effect as internalizing a medical consensus (p. 99, n. 1).
  - Cain, Ferejohn, and Fiorina (1987): in Britain, college-educated people more often say representatives should follow the party line. In the US, college-educated people more often say they should vote their own opinions. The better educated adhere more to their own country's tradition (p. 99).
- **Consensus strength.** Mueller: the education–support correlation for Vietnam was strong early, when elites mostly supported the war, and weak later (p. 99). McClosky and Brill classified over 100 civil-liberties items by Court and elite support for the libertarian option. Where support was strong, college-attenders were on average 24 points more libertarian than people with less than high school. The education effect weakened as elite support fell, and was slightly negative on the few items where the pre-Rehnquist Court and most elites were antilibertarian (e.g. civil disobedience) (pp. 99–100).
- **Resistance.** Zaller says it is widely supposed that awareness breeds resistance to elite influence. His view is that this has some validity. Awareness mainly makes people resistant to governing authorities by raising sensitivity to countervailing elites, especially ideological opponents (e.g. faint antiwar messages in Chapter 9). He calls the notion that the aware resist all persuasion "highly dubious" (p. 100).
- **Scope.** The model is less useful for "motherhood issues" on which virtually everyone agrees (free elections, public schools). It is more useful where the public is reluctant to follow an elite consensus (tolerance of disliked groups, war when not directly threatened) (p. 100).

*Intuition note.* If everyone in the media says the same thing, the people who pay most attention hear it most, so, all else equal, they end up agreeing most. The prediction rests on exposure (A1) plus the absence of cues that would let anyone resist.

### Polarization effect (pp. 100–102)
- **Assumptions.** Elites are roughly evenly split. Each camp sends persuasive and cueing messages. All messages are equally intense, so a person at a given awareness level is equally likely to take in any one (p. 100).
- **Liberals.** Awareness raises reception of persuasive messages on both sides and of cues. Aware liberals have cues showing the ideological implications, so they are likely to reject conservative arguments. These cues will not, however, impede their internalization of liberal messages (pp. 100–101). Less aware liberals receive few messages and few, less accessible cues, so they are less selective (p. 101).
- **Result.** The most aware liberals fill their heads with many considerations favoring the liberal side. Less aware liberals hold fewer, less consistent ones. The same holds, mutatis mutandis, for conservatives (p. 101).
- **Expectation.** The ratio of ideologically consistent to inconsistent considerations should rise with awareness. Figure 4.1 (earlier chapter) shows consistent/total rising from about .5 among the least informed to about .80 among the most informed. Two slopes are significant at .01, the third at .10 (p. 101).
- **Caveat (n. 3).** On closer inspection the pattern holds only for liberals, and strongly. For conservatives there appears to be little change. Zaller says the reason appears to be unmet assumed conditions: mass opinion was not near 50–50 on any of the three options, as an equal elite division would imply (p. 101).
- **Attitude reports.** More aware liberals should more often recall liberal considerations and so support the liberal position. Less aware liberals should show higher no-opinion rates and less liberal endorsement when they do answer. The same holds for conservatives (p. 101).
- **Conclusion (D26).** With evenly divided elites and balanced messages, awareness polarizes attitude reports (pp. 101–102).

*Intuition note.* The aware both hear more and know which messages "belong" to their side. That lets them accept the friendly messages and reject the hostile ones. The less aware hear little and are less selective, so they hold a smaller, more mixed set of considerations and more often give no opinion.

### Empirical support (pp. 102–113)
**Earlier work (p. 102).** Belknap and Campbell (1951–2), *The American Voter*, Gamson and Modigliani (1966), and Chong, McClosky, and Zaller (1984) all found awareness associated with sharper liberal–conservative (or Democratic–Republican) polarization on issues where elites disagree. Zaller says their models (cognitive consistency, contested norms) rest on the same regularity (p. 102, n. 5).

**Vietnam, Figure 6.1 (p. 102–103).**
- In 1964, with elites nearly all behind the war, awareness raised support among both mass liberals and conservatives. In 1970, with elites deeply divided, awareness was associated with greater polarization (p. 102).
- Check on elite positions: a research assistant classified *Newsweek* and *Time* cover stories. Prowar outnumbered antiwar about 3 to 1 in 1964 and about 1 to 1 in 1970 (p. 102, n. 6).
- Definitions: liberals rate liberals at least 15 points above conservatives on feeling thermometers, and conservatives show the reverse. "Supporting the war" means "keep troops but try to end the fighting" or a stronger stand (p. 103). Source: 1964 and 1970 CPS.

**Persian Gulf (pp. 102–104, 106–107).**
- From the August 1990 invasion through the fall election, there was only light criticism of Bush. Two days after the election, Bush announced several hundred thousand more troops. This triggered strong congressional criticism, hearings, and later a vote. Democrats were the most salient critics and Republicans the most salient defenders (pp. 102–103).
- Prediction: mainstream pattern before the criticism, polarization after (p. 103).
- The 1990 NES began the day after the election and had over 250 interviews before the criticism started. Item: "did the right thing in sending troops… or should have stayed out" (p. 103).
- Method: maximum-likelihood logistic regression controlling for awareness, party, gender, race, and Jewish ethnicity (coefficients in Table 6.3). Partisans are simulated at ±1.3 on the −2…+2 party scale, and awareness runs −1.8 to +2.57 SD (p. 103, n. 7).
- Result (Fig. 6.2): little partisan polarization before the criticism, clear polarization after. It is less sharp than for Vietnam, probably because elite polarization was shorter and less intense (pp. 103–104).
- Item on options (pull out / more diplomacy / tighten embargo / tougher military action): all but pullout imply support for basic involvement. Virtually all elite critics accepted involvement, with the possible exception of the Black Caucus (p. 104). So awareness should go with rejecting pullout, and Fig. 6.3 confirms it (p. 105). Even among black Democratic women, rejection of pullout rises from about 54% to about 92% (pp. 105–106). Footnote 8: in simple linear regressions, blacks' intercept and slope are .55 and .047, whites' .78 and .022, and awareness rises at least as strongly among blacks (p. 106). Footnote 9: any time trend was toward less polarization, not significant (p. 106).
- "Tougher military action": Democrats should reject it. Republicans were unclear, since Bush's early position was embargo plus diplomacy. After the mid-December rejection of a January 12 Baghdad meeting, willingness to use force was clearly Bush policy, so polarization over force should grow (p. 106). Fig. 6.4 appears to support this: highly aware Democrats and Republicans were apparently more polarized after December 15, but the increase is not statistically significant. Zaller calls this "equivocal support" (p. 107).
- Time coding in Table 6.3: for "right thing," 0 through November 9 and 1 afterward. For "tougher action," 0 through December 15 (p. 117). The three time-interaction terms for "right thing" are jointly significant on an F-test at p < .01 (p. 117).

**Conventions of graphical analysis (box, p. 105).** Awareness goes on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis. Awareness spans about the 1st to the 98th percentile. Probabilities are plotted on 0–1.0. Party is coded −2 to +2, with all others at 0, and partisans simulated at ±1.3. Other predispositions are simulated over ranges given case by case.

**Key point (p. 107).** Awareness has important effects, but they differ across policies and time depending on elite positions as reflected in the media. Awareness goes with support for consensual policies and with polarization on divided ones (p. 107). Footnote 10: the opinion change here is more complicated than shown, and later chapters treat it (p. 107).

*Intuition note.* The same measure, awareness, produces opposite-looking patterns on one issue as the elite message flow changes. In 1964 aware liberals and conservatives moved together toward the war, and in 1970 they moved apart.

**Systematic test, 1972–74–76 NES panel (pp. 107–113).**
- *Items (Table 6.1, p. 108).* Five mainstream items:
  - stay home from world problems (isolationism);
  - China in the UN;
  - trade with Communist countries;
  - equal hotel/restaurant access for blacks;
  - an equal role for women.

  Three partisan items:
  - busing for integration;
  - government job guarantee;
  - Vietnam "right thing."
- *Validation.* Zaller chose items from his own judgment of elite positions. A research assistant, unaware of his expectations, rated the 1972 party platforms. Expectations were largely confirmed. The exception was equal accommodations: the Republican platform made no direct reference to it. Zaller still counts it as mainstream because of the bipartisan history of the 1964 Civil Rights Act and the collapse of Southern opposition (p. 108). Footnote 13: an inflation item was rated but had a ceiling effect (p. 108). Footnote 11: panel data were used because the study compared awareness measures, and political knowledge outperformed the alternatives (p. 107).
- *Model (p. 109).*
$$\text{Prob(Lib. Response)} = \text{Prob(Opinionation)} \times \text{Prob(Lib.} \mid \text{Opinionation)}$$
  Each part is estimated separately. The liberal-response part is a logit function of awareness, ideological self-designation, party ID, and race, age, income, and South. The initial specification also had Awareness × Ideology and Awareness × Party terms. Each of the eight issues has its own equation, with items coded 0, 0.5, 1 (p. 109). Footnote 15 gives the coding. For example, for busing the far conservative position (68% of respondents) is 0, the next is .5, and the other five scale points are 1 (p. 109). Footnote 14: ideology was averaged over three waves, with missing values filled from other waves or the sample mean. Dropping respondents with missing data would mainly remove less-informed people and hamper detection of the awareness effect on mainstream items (p. 109).
- *Expectations.* The awareness × values interactions should be strong for partisan policies and "anemic" for mainstream ones. Awareness should also have a clearly positive effect on mainstream support (p. 109).
- *Results (Table 6.2, p. 111; Fig. 6.5, p. 110).* Ideology × Awareness is large on the three partisan issues (0.18, 0.19, 0.30, all significant) and trivial on the mainstream issues. Party × Awareness is erratic but insignificant or small (p. 109). On partisan issues awareness is associated with more polarized scale scores on all three (p. 110). Awareness is also positive in the opinionation function for all eight items (p. 111). Figure 6.5 builds liberals at +6 on ideology and +1.3 on party, and conservatives at +2 and −1.3, with awareness ±2 SD (p. 110).
- *Multicollinearity.* For all five mainstream items, the awareness coefficient correlated at .96 or higher with the Awareness × Ideology coefficient. In some equations neither awareness, ideology, nor their interaction was significant (p. 110). Since collinearity lowers precision even when one variable's true effect is zero (n. 16), Zaller dropped the interactions and ran F-tests on residual sums of squares. For antiisolationism, women's rights, trade, and China in the UN, the interactions did not contribute significantly, and awareness became significantly positive in all four. For equal accommodations, Party × Awareness stayed significant, but with the nonsignificant ideology interaction omitted, awareness was positive (p. 112). Dropping the direct awareness term is not permissible while the interaction stays in (n. 17).
- *Women's role.* This is the one doubtful case. The effect on liberals is modest and the ideology effect is large (p. 112). Zaller offers these explanations:
  - It may not have been a mainstream issue, though he finds this less plausible.
  - Liberals started at 71% support, so a ceiling effect limits the slope.
  - 1972 was near the high-water mark of radical feminism (Mansbridge, 1986), which may have had spillover costs.
  - The Republican Party was about to withdraw its ERA support.

  The hypothesis is still upheld in that awareness has an important positive effect (p. 112).
- **Conclusion.** Both deductions are supported: awareness raises support under consensus and polarizes opposed value groups under division (pp. 112–113). Footnote 18: mainstream norms are set by what elites with regular media access say publicly, not by what all elites believe (p. 113).

### Attitude constraint and mass belief systems (pp. 113–114)
- Liberals on one issue tend to be liberal on others. This is usually explained by "attitude constraint," classically in Converse (1964) (p. 113).
- Zaller says the logic of polarization is isomorphic with Converse's account. According to Converse, as Zaller reports, ideologies come from a "minuscule" number of creative elites and diffuse imperfectly. In that account only the aware attend enough to learn "what goes with what," so constraint develops mainly among them (p. 113).
- In the RAS model, aware liberals and conservatives look to partisan elites and acquire consistent positions across issues (Fig. 6.5). Higher interitem correlations among the aware generalize this pattern (pp. 113–114). So the model explains both cross-issue consistency and its growth with awareness (D27, D28) (p. 114).
- Zaller says this account does not differ importantly from Converse's. It is "a somewhat more fully specified statement" of it (p. 114).
- Footnote 20: Lane and Marcus et al. criticize Converse for ignoring idiosyncratic attitude structures and equating consistency with sophistication. Zaller accepts the first point, saying awareness drives a socialization process that overrides idiosyncratic structures. He equates consistency with sophistication only in the "possibly perverse sense" of having absorbed conventions (p. 114).
- Chapter 9 treats constraint when message flow is unstable or unbalanced (p. 114).

*Intuition note.* Constraint, in this account, is learned from elite packages of positions. Those who follow elite talk most learn the packages best, so their issue positions line up most.

### Alternative explanations (pp. 114–116)
- **Single-issue explanations.**
  - Cantril (1944): the better informed correctly understand their true interests.
  - Stouffer (1954): the better educated give "sober second thought."
  - Allport (1954): they feel greater personal security.
  - Bobo and Licari (1989): education brings more sophisticated reasoning.
  - Hamilton (1968) and Hahn (1970): the educated support Vietnam through "upper-middle class authoritarianism."

  Zaller says each is perhaps plausible but none generalizes easily. The RAS explanation (more exposure to mainstream elite values) is more parsimonious. He admits the mainstream norms have not been established in some of these cases but believes they could be (pp. 114–115).
- **Jackman (1978).** She found education linked to liberalism on some race items (strict segregation) but not others (busing), and concluded that learning of democratic values is "superficial." The RAS reading is that the first item is mainstream and the second partisan (p. 115).
- **Sullivan et al. (n. 22).** They find the educated are not more tolerant once dislike of the target group is controlled. Zaller replies that the mainstream model predicts educational effects only for traditionally protected liberties such as political speech. Even if fear explains part of the effect, he says the resulting support is politically significant, and discounting fears may itself be a mainstream norm (pp. 114–115, n. 22).
- Duch and Gibson (n. 24): the education effect on tolerance varies across nations, which Zaller takes as a sign it reflects socialization (p. 115).
- **Constraint explanations** stress internal sources: schematic associations formed by thinking (Fiske and Kinder; Judd and Krosnick; Luskin). The RAS model does not deny these but explains consistency by external sources, the pattern of elite division. This lets it explain different awareness effects across contexts (Figs. 6.1, 6.5), random response variation and response effects among novices and experts alike, and attitude change (pp. 115–116).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assumptions, for illustration only: 10 messages exist. A person at a given awareness level receives each one with probability $r$. Cue reception also has probability $r$. A received message from the opposing side is rejected only if the cue is received. Awareness levels: low $r = 0.2$, high $r = 0.8$.

- **Mainstream:** all 10 messages are pro and there are no cues. Expected pro considerations: low $10 \times 0.2 = 2$, high $10 \times 0.8 = 8$. Support rises with awareness.
- **Polarized, liberal citizen:** 5 liberal and 5 conservative messages, all with cues available.
  - Low: liberal considerations $5 \times 0.2 = 1$. Conservative received $= 1$, accepted with probability $1 - 0.2 = 0.8$, giving $0.8$. Consistent share $= 1/(1+0.8) = 0.556$.
  - High: liberal $5 \times 0.8 = 4$. Conservative received $= 4$, accepted with probability $1 - 0.8 = 0.2$, giving $0.8$. Consistent share $= 4/(4+0.8) = 0.833$.

The consistent share rises from about .56 to about .83, and the total number of considerations rises from 1.8 to 4.8. This mirrors the shape (about .5 to .8) of the pattern described for Fig. 4.1, but the numbers are ours.

## Concepts
- [[mainstream-effect]] (new) — under elite consensus, awareness raises support for the consensual policy (D25) (p. 98)
- [[polarization-effect]] (new) — under balanced partisan elite division, awareness polarizes opposed value groups (D26) (p. 102)
- [[receive-accept-sample-ras-model]] — the model generating both effects; the chapter derives from it via A1 and A2 (p. 98)
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — attention to and knowledge of politics. Measured by knowledge, education or participation; Zaller's test finds knowledge best (pp. 100, 107)
- [[heuristics-cues]] — here "cueing messages" tell people whether a policy fits their values, enabling resistance (pp. 98, 100)
- [[belief-system-constraint]] — cross-issue consistency; in this account, learned from elites, rising with awareness (D27, D28) (pp. 113–114)
- [[elite-consensus-vs-division]] (new) — the model takes elite consensus as an initial condition (p. 112). The elites who count as shapers of opinion are those who have or control access to the mass media (p. 113, n. 18)
- [[non-attitudes]] — less aware people show higher no-opinion rates (p. 101); Zaller does not use the term here.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Aware partisans reject uncongenial messages through cues ↔ motivated skepticism: congenial claims are accepted and uncongenial ones scrutinized.
- [structural-analogy] Awareness × ideology interaction ↔ partisan sorting: party and ideology line up more among the engaged.
- [metaphor] Mainstream effect ↔ Condorcet-style aggregation: shared signals produce agreement. But the mechanism here is exposure, not independent accuracy.

## Questions this raises
- If elite consensus is the initial condition, how is it measured without circularity (the author's own judgment plus one rater)?
- What happens when message balance shifts over time (the later chapters)?
- Why did conservatives not show the predicted consistency gain in Fig. 4.1 (p. 101, n. 3)? Was it only the unmet 50–50 condition?
- Can "external" and "internal" accounts of constraint be separated empirically?
