# Information, predispositions, and opinion

## L0 — One line
Opinions are built from information carried in elite discourse (received unevenly, depending on political awareness) and from predispositions; the author argues that survey answers are therefore often put together on the spot from whichever considerations come to mind first (the top-of-the-head ones), rather than read off fixed attitudes.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Zaller asks how mass opinion forms and why survey answers are so unstable. He introduces three ingredients. Elite discourse (what politicians, journalists and experts say) supplies simplified, framed information. Political awareness (attention to politics plus understanding) varies greatly among citizens, around a low average. Predispositions (stable traits, mainly values) shape which messages people accept. He argues that random response instability and question-wording and order effects weigh against the conventional true-attitude view. In its place he proposes a question-answering model: people answer by averaging over a sample of accessible considerations, often just one or two. Elite discourse, awareness and values together shape which considerations are available. Chapter 3 formalises this.

**Prerequisites:** survey percentages and cross-tabs; correlation and "variance"; the idea of a nonmonotonic relationship (one that rises over part of the range and falls over the rest); basic probability for the toy example.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Citizens depend on elites for most of their political information. That information is selective and stereotyped (it comes in "frames", simplified pictures of what an issue is about), so opinion can rarely be treated as a straightforward response to "the facts" (pp. 6–8, 13–14).
- Whether the public gets a choice among frames matters. Under one-sided elite discourse the public tends to follow the elite view, and the most attentive are the most likely to adopt it. Under divided discourse people tend to follow the elites who share their ideology or party, and the most attentive mirror the elite split most sharply (pp. 8–9).
- Racial attitudes and defense spending illustrate this. Mass opinion appears to have moved with elite discourse and party cues, though the author offers these only as illustration (pp. 9–15).
- Political awareness varies hugely around a low mean. Most scholarly models implicitly ignore it, which the author wants to correct, and its effects can be nonmonotonic (incumbent example) (pp. 16–21).
- The author argues that awareness is best measured by neutral factual tests, but "information" in the book means non-neutral elite messages (pp. 21–22).
- Predispositions (especially values) are stable traits that regulate acceptance or rejection of messages. The impact of values depends on whether people have the contextual information to connect them to an issue (pp. 22–28).
- Values are domain-specific parts of a loosely unified left–right ideology. General ideology measures can be used when necessary, as a second-best (pp. 26–28).
- Random instability, response effects and wording effects undermine the "file drawer" view. A question-answering model of top-of-the-head considerations is proposed (pp. 28–39).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 6)
Every opinion is a "marriage of information and predisposition" (p. 6). Information gives a mental picture of an issue. Predisposition motivates a conclusion. The book argues that variation in elite-discourse information, individual attention to it, and individual predispositions jointly determine the contours of public opinion (p. 6). This chapter defines the terms and builds intuition for the technical core, which starts in Chapter 3 (p. 6).

### Information and elite discourse (pp. 6–16)

**Dependence on elites (pp. 6–7).**
- Citizens depend on unseen others for most of their information about the wider world (p. 6). Lippmann: our opinions cover more than we can observe directly, so they must be pieced together from what others report and what we imagine (p. 6).
- These others are mostly political elites: politicians, officials, journalists, some activists, experts. Even friends and family may relay ideas that originated among elites (p. 6).
- The information reaching the public is highly selective and stereotyped (p. 7). Graber found citizens grumble about oversimplified TV news but will not read more detailed versions (p. 7).

**Stereotypes and frames (pp. 7–8).**
- Lippmann's news stereotypes are today usually called *frames of reference*. His example is Miss Sherwin of Gopher Prairie, who cannot imagine three million men and pictures World War I as a personal duel between Joffre and the Kaiser (p. 7).
- Lippmann doubted that individuals create such mental pictures themselves. We perceive what our culture has already defined, in the form it has stereotyped (p. 7).
- Some stereotypes are permanent (the corrupt politician). Others are recent creations. Examples are unisex toilets in the Equal Rights Amendment debate (Mansbridge), the Pro-Choice and Pro-Life labels (Luker), and "the homeless" (pp. 7–8).
- Frames determine what the public thinks it is becoming informed about, which often determines how it takes sides (p. 8). They matter even for matters people can observe. Iyengar uses experimental evidence to argue that episodic versus society-wide TV framing of poverty changes blame attribution and support for programs (p. 8).
- A central question is whether the public is given a choice between frames. Without a choice it can do little but follow the elite consensus (p. 8).

**Vietnam illustration (p. 8).**
- Early on, the public had one frame: containing Communism. Support was very strong, and the most media-exposed supported the "official line" most strongly (p. 8).
- Later, coverage framed the war as a civil war, inessential to US security and perhaps unwinnable. Supportive coverage continued but no longer had near-monopoly status (p. 8).
- Support weakened greatly (the author shows this in Chapter 9). Heavy exposure now went with polarisation. Attentive liberals moved toward liberal elites and attentive conservatives toward conservative elites (p. 8).
- General statement: when elites uphold a clear picture, the public tends to see events that way, with the most attentive most likely to adopt it. When elites divide, people tend to follow elites sharing their ideological or partisan predisposition, and the most attentive mirror the elite division most sharply (pp. 8–9).
- The author calls these claims "obviously quite strong". He uses the racial-attitudes story only to illustrate his general vision, not to convince anyone of its final correctness (p. 9).

*Intuition note.* People often see issues through frames supplied by their culture and by elites. When only one frame is on offer, the public tends to follow it, and the attentive are the most likely to do so. When elites split, people tend to follow the side that shares their own politics, and the attentive mirror the split most sharply, so exposure can increase polarisation.

**Elite discourse and racial attitudes (pp. 9–13).**
- Around 1900 virtually all white elites accepted some notion of racial inferiority, and these ideas had the support of the science of the period (p. 9). Efforts to mobilise white support for black equality were bound to fail (p. 9).
- By 1930 elite attitudes seemed to be shifting. Hoover's nominee John Parker was rejected for the Supreme Court largely because of a ten-year-old racist speech (p. 9). Race was apparently still not a major public issue in the 1930s (p. 9).
- **Myrdal's account (as reported by the author):** neither black material conditions nor discrimination differed much in 1940 from just after the Civil War. Yet Myrdal predicted great racial progress, which the author judges proved extraordinarily prescient. By Myrdal's account, white attitudes changed before conditions did (pp. 9–10).
- Myrdal located the explanation in intellectual developments. Scientists discredited racial-inferiority claims. The author's example is Carl Brigham, who in 1923 claimed Nordic intellectual superiority had been demonstrated and in 1930 said his own study was "without foundation" (p. 10).
- Psychologists then turned to the effects of white prejudice and the origins of prejudice (p. 10). Footnotes: Degler argues the new research was ideologically motivated but makes no argument that fraud or dishonesty was present (note 1). The author notes that, so far as he can tell, the possibility that white prejudice partly came from earlier racist elite discourse was not given serious consideration (note 2) (p. 10).
- Elite explanations of inequality shifted from racial inferiority to individual effort or, in the liberal version, white discrimination (pp. 10–11).
- Because opinion data begin only in the late 1930s, the effects on public opinion cannot be fully documented. Three points are reasonably clear (p. 11):
  1. *Mass shift toward racial equality.* The share of whites saying blacks should have equal chances at any kind of job rose from 45% (1944) to 97% (1972). The share favouring the same schools rose from 32% (1942) to 90% (1982). The shift roughly coincides with the elite shift, but resistance to government action and to compensation for past discrimination remains (p. 11).
  2. *Exposure.* The better educated, who are the most exposed to elite discourse, back the consensual elite ideas (equality, laws against segregated restaurants) but are not especially supportive of the elite-contested ones (affirmative action, busing) (p. 11).
  3. *Partisan cues* (Carmines and Stimson), below (p. 11).
- **Partisan cues (pp. 11–13, Fig. 2.1).** In the 1950s and early 1960s elite Democrats and Republicans showed no consistent partisan difference. Republican congressmen were more liberal than Democrats on race in Fig. 2.1, and the public did not differ by party (pp. 11–12).
- From late 1963, the Democrats became the party of racial liberalism (Johnson) and the Republicans the more conservative party (Goldwater 1964). Congressional voting followed party lines (p. 12).
- Rank-and-file partisans began to polarise from 1964, the result, it would seem, of the change in leadership cues (p. 13). A footnote says the first mass polarisation appears in a November 1963 Harris poll (p. 13).
- *Ambiguity:* polarisation could come from reshuffled party loyalties (the party conversion thesis) or from existing partisans changing opinion (the opinion leadership thesis) (p. 13). It appears both were at work: Petrocik shows the Democrats lost Southern whites and gained blacks, and Gerber and Jackson show opinion change toward the new party cues (p. 13).
- The second matters most here. If elite cues can change racial opinions, which appear among the most deeply felt, they can probably affect most other types of opinion as well (p. 13).

**Conceptualizing and measuring elite discourse (pp. 13–15).**
- Elite "information" consists of what may be assumed to be sincere attempts to capture what matters, but it is never "just information". It is selective and embedded in stereotyped frames (p. 13). Public opinion can therefore rarely be considered a straightforward response to "the facts" (p. 13).
- Lippmann: it is more reasonable to hold that public opinion is a moralized and codified version of the facts, not a moral judgment of a group of facts (p. 14).
- In this book, information carried in elite discourse means stereotypes, frames and elite leadership cues that let citizens form opinions (p. 14). The author's measures are simple. Often a dichotomy: monolithic elite view versus important disagreement (Chapter 6). Sometimes counts of media reports and the direction each pushes opinion (p. 14). He cites much research showing that even simple story counts suffice to show a close relationship between elite discourse and mass opinion (p. 14).
- **Defense spending example (pp. 14–15).**
  - Newsweek's 24 months before the 1980 election: 57 stories on defense spending, 46 wholly or mainly favouring more spending (p. 15).
  - The 24 months after: 60 stories, 40 opposed. Coverage swung from about four-to-one in favour of more spending to two-to-one in favour of cuts (p. 15).
  - Opinion moved in tandem. As late as 1975 only about 10% said too little was spent. By early 1981 a slight majority did. Then support for more spending fell by more than 30 points within a year (p. 15).

*Intuition note.* Because elites frame and select, public opinion appears to follow the mix of messages. When the mix of media stories changed, opinion appears to have changed quickly. In the author's view, simple story counts were enough to show this.

### Mass attention to elite discourse (pp. 16–22)

**Rational ignorance and its spread (pp. 16–18).**
- Most Americans are "rationally ignorant" (Downs) but differ greatly in degree (p. 16).
- A small but important minority is highly informed. About 1.9% could name as many as half of the Supreme Court members in an NES survey, and none of 1,500 named all nine in 1966 (p. 16, note 5).
- At the other end, in late 1986, 24% did not recognise George Bush's name or could not say his office (p. 16).
- Most people fall between and quickly forget. About three weeks after House Speaker Wright resigned amid scandal, only 45% could give any reason (p. 17).
- After the *Webster* abortion decision, about 50% could say anything about the ruling just afterwards, falling to about 35% over the next weeks (p. 17).
- **Fig. 2.2** (estimated diffusion, from the Price–Zaller model): upward of 95% of the most aware knew the ruling within three days. Almost none at the low end did (p. 18).
- Strongly pro-choice persons had only a rather modest informational advantage. Men and women, and women of childbearing age, did not differ (p. 18). The author says that learning about matters especially important to oneself does occur, but the tendency appears not to be very great or widespread (p. 18).
- Two main points: people vary greatly in general attentiveness regardless of issue, and average information is low. In short: *high variance in political awareness around a generally low mean* (p. 18).
- Familiar as this is, most of the time scholars build models that implicitly assume citizens are about equally informed and differ mainly in preferences and interests. A corrective is one aim of the book (p. 18).

**Example: the congressional personal vote (pp. 18–21).**
- Incumbents' average winning margins have risen. Less than half can recall their representative's name, but about 80% can recognise it (Mann and Wolfinger's claim: much of the advantage arises in the booth) (p. 19).
- Preview of Chapter 10: the most aware are most exposed to the incumbent's self-promotion but also best able to scrutinise it. They support or reject the incumbent according to party and values, however hard he campaigns (p. 19).
- The least aware get little information and are relatively unaffected. The moderately aware are exposed but lack the resources to resist, so they are the most susceptible (p. 19).
- **Fig. 2.3** (1978 NES, contested seats, outpartisans): defection to the incumbent is markedly higher among the moderately aware than at either extreme (p. 20). The case numbers are small, for the reasons in note 9 (p. 20).
- The relationship is *nonmonotonic*: positive over part of the range and negative over the rest (p. 20). A linear check would find little of interest (p. 20). Awareness effects are often strongly nonlinear, which makes them hard to detect and model (p. 20).
- Further complications: public opinion is sometimes shaped by monolithically one-sided discourse but more often by multiple, typically conflicting information flows of differing intensity, so awareness must be modelled as mediating exposure to each (pp. 20–21). Opinion formation is multistage. Awareness raises exposure but also the capacity to react critically (p. 21). These effects may cross-cut (the incumbent case) but need not. In some cases the most aware are easiest to persuade, in others the very inattentive (p. 21).
- The author says that systematically explaining these patterns will be the most important single contribution of the book (p. 21).

*Intuition note.* Awareness does two jobs. It lets messages in, and it lets people react critically to them. Where the two effects cross-cut, as in the congressional case, the middle of the awareness scale can be the most persuadable. The author says this need not always be so (p. 21).

**Conceptualizing and measuring awareness (pp. 21–22).**
- *Political awareness* = the extent to which a person pays attention to politics and understands what they encounter. Attention alone is not enough (p. 21). It denotes cognitive rather than affective engagement. Alternative labels in the literature include expertise, sophistication and involvement. The author prefers "awareness" because it captures reception and comprehension (p. 21).
- Other indicators are media exposure, participation, education and self-described interest. He argues (Measurement Appendix) that simple tests of neutral factual information are best, because they capture what has actually reached people's minds. Examples are which party controls the House, or whether Mainland China is in the UN (pp. 21–22).
- Those tests are neutral. Elite "information" is not: it is framed for partisan purposes and can reasonably be disputed, and some assertions cannot be fully verified, such as the claim that abortion signals moral decline (p. 22). Non-neutral information is indistinguishable from political argumentation. Neutral facts matter because they measure exposure to it (p. 22).
- Convention from here on: "information" means the non-neutral sense. The tests are called tests of "political awareness" or "knowledge" (p. 22).

### Political predispositions (pp. 22–28)

**Definition and status (pp. 22–23).**
- Exposure differences, by themselves, explain only part of opinion variance. Citizens also have interests, values and experiences that affect willingness to accept or resist persuasion (p. 22).
- *Political predispositions* = stable, individual-level traits that regulate acceptance or non-acceptance of the communications a person receives (p. 22). They are the critical intervening variable between communications and stated preferences (p. 23).
- Their sources are beyond the book's scope. They are assumed to be at least partly a distillation of lifetime experience. They also partly depend on social and economic location and, probably at least as strongly, on personality factors and tastes (p. 23).
- Elites are *not* assumed to shape predispositions in the short run. Long-run elite influence is likely to exist to some degree, but for the study of short-term opinion formation it can be safely neglected (p. 23). Footnote 11 says elite ideas, once internalised, can affect later susceptibility without any intervening effect on values (p. 23).
- Values get the most attention because they seem to have a stronger, more pervasive effect than other predispositions. Race and party attachment are also important, but race has too little variance to be central (p. 23, note 12).
- Values are, in the words of Kinder and Sears, enduring general standards that hold a more central position than attitudes (Kinder and Sears; Rokeach) (p. 23). Example: a person strongly attached to economic individualism would, all else equal, be more likely to reject an argument for higher taxes to pay for social welfare spending than someone less attached (p. 23).

**Two weaknesses of the values literature (pp. 23–26).**
1. *It largely fails to take systematic account of awareness (pp. 24–26).*
   - Hurwitz and Peffley's view: ignorant citizens fall back on core values and general postures (p. 24). **Author's reply:** an excellent point, but citizens must still have some minimal information to see a value's relevance, and that is easily absent (p. 24).
   - **Fig. 2.4** (1987 NES pilot): hawks are those valuing military strength, an aggressive posture and uncompromising opposition to communism. Doves reject those positions (p. 24).
   - *Aid to Contras:* 42% of the most aware hawks but 3% of the most aware doves favoured increased aid. Differences were only modest in the middle third and absent at the bottom (p. 24).
   - *Sending US troops to Central America to stop communism's spread:* sharp hawk–dove differences at all awareness levels (p. 25).
   - Likely explanation (the author's): the Contra question requires knowing who the Contras are (only about half knew which side the US supported, 1985 commercial surveys). The troops question mentions communism and so makes the value implications clear (p. 25).
   - Conclusion: the impact of value predispositions always depends on whether people have the contextual information to translate values into support for policies or candidates, and having it can never be taken for granted. This contingency underlies the whole study (pp. 25–26).
2. *The relation of value dimensions to each other and to ideology is unspecified (p. 26).*
   - Many people respond to disparate value dimensions (economic individualism, attitudes toward communists, tolerance, race, sexual freedom, religious authority) as if organised by a left–right dimension. Correlations are never so strong as to imply a single dimension but are always at least moderately strong, sometimes quite strong among the highly aware (p. 26).
   - **Definitions.** *Values* = domain-specific organising principles. *Ideology* = a more general left–right scheme organising disparate concerns, including value or issue dimensions (p. 26).
   - Two novelties: value dimensions are correlated dimensions of a master concept, and ideology is a constellation of related value dimensions rather than strictly unidimensional (p. 26).
   - Analogy (the author's, "in a certain respect") to intelligence: people are better at some tasks than others, but someone very high on one dimension tends to be at least fairly high on others. Similarly it is unusual to be very liberal on one dimension and extremely conservative on another. The tendency is clear but not overpowering (pp. 26–27). Libertarians are an exception that studies routinely ignore (p. 27, note 14).

**Measurement implications (pp. 27–28).**
- First, whenever possible use domain-specific value measures rather than general ideology, because a tailored indicator is less likely to miss reactions to a given issue (p. 27).
- Second, since values are to a significant extent organised by general orientation, omnibus left–right measures can be used when necessary. Few high-quality datasets capture opinion change, and some have only liberal–conservative self-identification (p. 27). He builds the best general measure available and hopes that one such measure is almost as good as another (p. 27).
- This practice is conservative: if the measure misses the relevant predisposition, relationships with values will tend to look weaker than they are (p. 27).
- "Liberal" and "conservative" mean only being closer to one pole of a value dimension or of the constellation, not doctrinaire ideology (p. 28). For this book values and ideology have the same theoretical status: indicators of predispositions to accept or reject communications (p. 28).

*Intuition note.* A value can only guide an opinion if the person knows enough to see that it applies. The author's likely explanation of the Contra versus troops contrast is that the Contra item needs knowledge the unaware often lack, while the troops item mentions communism and so carries the cue itself (pp. 24–25).

### What is an opinion? (pp. 28–39)

**Mueller's caveats and the evidence (pp. 28–30).**
- Mueller describes the interview as an odd social situation in which respondents may pronounce on topics they have never thought about (p. 28). The author adds that the consequences are quite predictable: opinion statements look rough and superficial. They vacillate across repeated interviews. Trivial changes in question order or response options shift aggregate opinion by 5–10 points, occasionally twice that. Wording changes regularly have large effects (p. 28).
- Examples (pp. 28–29):
  - Rasinski: 68% say too little is spent on halting the rising crime rate versus 55% on "law enforcement". 68% on "protecting social security" versus 53% on "social security".
  - 45% would "not allow" a communist speech, but only 20% would "forbid" it (Schuman and Presser).
  - A 1983 *New York Times* poll found support for a nuclear weapons "freeze" ranging from 18% to 83% depending on how the issue was framed.
- Analysts rarely show such evidence because they fear losing their audience. A more important reason, the author believes, is that nobody knows what to make of it, so they set it aside for methodologists (p. 29). The author will instead make a theory of why these problems exist an integral part of the analysis, admitting it is a gamble on an uncertain foundation (p. 30).

**Over-time instability (pp. 30–32).**
- **Table 2.1** (1980 NES panel, January and June). On cooperation with Russia: 60% of those "tougher" in January stayed so in June. Only 24% of the "middle" stayed. Overall only 50% gave the same answer. Chance would produce roughly 32% given his recoding (p. 30). On government services, some 55 percent gave the same answer (p. 30).
- Genuine opinion change is not the main explanation. Opinion at the third interview can typically be predicted as well from the first interview as from the second, which would not hold if changes were systematic (p. 30). Hence the generally accepted conclusion is that the instability is predominantly chance variation (pp. 30–31).
- **Converse (1964):** instability is due mainly to people without strong feelings who choose, often randomly, among the options. He suggested that large portions of an electorate lack meaningful beliefs even on long-contested issues (p. 31).
- **Measurement-error school (Achen, Erikson, others):** people have stable "true attitudes" and fluctuation is "measurement error". If error is large, correlations are deflated, so researchers reinflate them by standard psychometric corrections (p. 31). The author comments that this renders instability not only innocuous but invisible (p. 31).
- **Author's critique.**
  - Converse's thesis was an extreme claim meant for certain highly abstract issues. Later work (Converse and Markus) allows opinions to be more or less "crystallized", but nobody has said what crystallization consists of, so it remains more metaphor than testable theory (p. 31).
  - Measurement error is estimated at one-half to three-quarters of the variance of opinion items, yet researchers are remarkably incurious about what it consists of. The author says it is closer to a euphemism for "unexplained variance" than to a well-understood phenomenon (p. 32).

**Response effects (pp. 32–33).**
- Besides random error there is *systematic* error: reactions to question context, response order and trivial wording changes, called *response effects* (p. 32).
- **Communist reporters experiment.** 37% would allow Communist reporters in the US. When first asked whether US reporters should be allowed in Russia, 73% would allow them. Schuman and Presser: a norm of reciprocity is made salient, and the reporter questions have two meanings, an attitude toward an object and one toward a norm (p. 32).
- The author notes this implicitly abandons the idea of a single fixed opinion. People hold at least two considerations and answer on the basis of the one the questionnaire makes salient (p. 32).
- Other findings: self-reported interest in politics drops after obscure-issue questions, abortion opinion depends on the preceding items, and open-ended and closed questions yield different answers (pp. 32–33).

**Question-wording effects (pp. 33–34).**
- Order effects rarely exceed 10–15 points. Substantive wording changes produce larger, more reliable effects. These are usually treated as unworrying, on the thought that different questions deserve different answers (p. 33).
- The author doubts that this complacency is warranted. Adding a politician's endorsement to a policy question is likely to change responses. Under the "true attitude" model that should not happen, so many respondents appear to be making up, or at least editing, their opinions as they go (p. 33).
- **Mueller on Korea:** support was higher when the antiwar option required confessing a mistake (no size given), and consistently 15–20 points greater when the war was described as necessary to stop communism. In fall 1953, about 38% said the war was worth fighting, but 64% said the US did the right thing in sending troops to stop the Communist invasion (p. 33).
- **Objection (that different questions were asked):** it does not seem credible to the author. The issue was the same, so anyone with a fixed opinion should have been able to express it either way (p. 34).
- **Tversky and Kahneman, "Asian disease":** 600 expected deaths. Program A saves 200 for certain, program B has a one-third chance of saving 600. 72% chose A. In loss wording (C: 400 die; D: one-third chance nobody dies, two-thirds chance 600 die) only 22% chose the certain loss, a 50-point drop. The denotative meanings are identical (p. 34).

**The need for a model of the survey response (pp. 34–36).**
- Two kinds of evidence count against "true attitudes": 50–75% of the variance in typical items is random "error", too large to be comfortably ascribed to vague wording, and there are large systematic wording and order effects (p. 34). These findings seriously undermine the view that surveys are passive measures of what the public really believes (p. 35).
- Survey methodologists and social psychologists have turned to a "question-answering process". Wilson and Hodges contrast the traditional "file drawer" model with attitudes as "temporary construction" from a large, conflicted "database". Tourangeau and Rasinski propose a general model (p. 35).
- Much of this work addresses response effects but not random instability or how people form opinions from political information. A broader model is needed, and this book offers one (p. 35).
- **The model (Zaller 1984b), in outline.**
  - People are continuously exposed to political news, much of it pushing opinion one way or the other. Because attention is generally low, most people on most issues are relatively uncritical about what they internalise. They accumulate large stores of only partly consistent ideas, arguments and considerations (p. 36).
  - Asked a survey question, they call to mind as many of these ideas as are immediately accessible and choose in haste, typically on one or perhaps two considerations at the top of the head (p. 36; term from Taylor and Fiske).
  - So survey responses are a function of immediately accessible considerations. The flow of elite discourse determines which are salient (p. 36).
  - Instability arises because different considerations are salient at different times. Question order or wording changes alter salience systematically (p. 36).
  - *Illustration:* defense spending during the cold war. Someone might worry about Pentagon waste and about the Soviet threat, without recognising the trade-off. Asked unexpectedly, they do not canvass their minds fully. A TV scandal the night before might lead to one answer, an earlier question about Soviet aggression might lead to another (p. 36).
- A further feature: the more aware are more selective about what they internalise and more likely to reject ideas inconsistent with their values. Their stored ideas are therefore more consistent, and their closed-ended answers show more stability and more ideological consistency (p. 37).

*Intuition note.* The model replaces the picture of one fixed opinion measured with noise by a picture of many stored considerations, a few of which are sampled each time. Random flip-flops and wording effects both follow, because what gets sampled can change by chance or by the questionnaire.

**Background of the question-answering model (pp. 37–39).**
- Mathematical psychologists (Atkinson, Bower and Crothers) treat the mind as a bin of interpretive constructs searched stochastically. The chosen construct fixes the understanding and response (p. 37).
- Social cognition research: a *schema* organises prior information around a central idea and guides interpretation. People typically have several schemata for one phenomenon (being introduced to a professor versus a mother of three). Tesser: there is not a single attitude toward an object but any number, depending on the schema tuned in (p. 37).
- If people have multiple potential opinions, how do they decide? Psychologists' models are fearfully complicated (Wyer and Srull) (p. 38). One point appears reasonably clear: people rarely take the time to canvass all relevant thoughts. They appear to decide on whatever ideas are immediately accessible (Taylor and Fiske; Wyer and Srull say people retrieve only a small subset, apparently assuming it is representative) (p. 38).
- Yet other data make it clear that people may often use many diverse pieces of information: Kelley's voters decide as if summing likes and dislikes, and Anderson's information-averaging models (p. 38).
- **The author's reconciliation:** individuals decide by *averaging over a nonrandom but stochastic sample of relevant considerations*, with sample size varying from one to large. Size and composition depend on contextual and motivational factors, such as what the questionnaire made salient and how much attention the person generally pays to the subject (pp. 38–39).

### Summary (p. 39)
The study examines interactions among three classes of variables: aggregate variation in elite-discourse information (including elite cues on how to evaluate it), individual differences in attention to this discourse, and individual differences in values. Together they determine the mix of considerations in people's heads. Those at the top of the head at the moment of the survey question determine the response. The model is presented in Chapter 3 (p. 39).

## Toy example
*(Our own illustration, not from the text. We use uniform random sampling, a simplification of the author's "nonrandom but stochastic" sampling.)*

A respondent has four stored considerations about an issue: three pro (+1 each) and one con (−1). When asked, they sample some considerations, average them, and say "support" if the average is above 0.

- **Sample size 1.**
  - P(sampled consideration is pro) = 3/4. So P(support) = 3/4.
  - Chance of giving the same answer in two independent interviews = (3/4)(3/4) + (1/4)(1/4) = 9/16 + 1/16 = 10/16 = 62.5%.
  - A person with 2 pro and 2 con would have P(support) = 1/2, and consistency = 1/4 + 1/4 = 50%.
- **Order effect.** Suppose an earlier question makes the con consideration salient, so it is the one sampled. The answer is "oppose" (average −1), with no change in the person's stored considerations.
- **Sample size 3, drawn without replacement from the 4.**
  - There are 4 possible samples. Omit the con: average (1+1+1)/3 = +1. Omit one pro (3 ways): average (1+1−1)/3 = +1/3.
  - All four averages are above 0, so P(support) = 1 and consistency = 100%.
- **Sample size 2 (for contrast).** There are 6 pairs. 3 pro–pro pairs average +1 (support). 3 pro–con pairs average 0, which is not above 0 (oppose). So P(support) = 1/2 and consistency = 50%, lower than at size 1.
- Lesson: with the same stored considerations, an answer based on one consideration flips with sampling or question context. In this example the size-3 sample happens to be fully stable, but a bigger sample is not always more stable (size 2 is less so). This is only a loose echo of the chapter's point that the sample size can range from one to large (p. 39).

## Concepts
- [[elite-discourse]] (new) — the stereotypes, frames and leadership cues from political elites that give citizens their "information" (p. 14)
- [[frames-of-reference]] (new; cf. [[framing effect]]) — Lippmann's stereotypes, culturally given or elite-supplied, that determine what the public thinks an issue is about (pp. 7–8)
- [[political awareness / sophistication]] — attention to politics plus comprehension, which the author argues is best measured by neutral factual tests. It varies hugely around a low mean (pp. 18, 21–22)
- [[rational ignorance]] — Downs's phrase for the low political information of most Americans, who still differ greatly in degree (p. 16)
- [[heuristics / cues]] — here, elite partisan cues (e.g., party leaders' racial positions) to which the public was responsive; the text does not use the word heuristic (pp. 11–13)
- [[political-predispositions]] (new) — stable individual traits that regulate acceptance or rejection of communications. Elites are not assumed to shape them in the short run (pp. 22–23)
- [[values-vs-ideology]] (new) — values are domain-specific organising principles. Ideology is a general left–right scheme of correlated value dimensions (p. 26)
- [[nonmonotonic-awareness-effect]] (new) — the association with awareness is positive over part of the range and negative over the rest (p. 20)
- [[non-attitudes]] — Converse's claim that instability stems mainly from people lacking meaningful beliefs. The author calls it extreme (meant for certain highly abstract issues) and says "crystallization" remains more a metaphor than a testable theory (p. 31)
- [[measurement-error-theory]] (new) — instability treated as noise around stable true attitudes, justifying psychometric corrections. The author says its "error" is closer to a euphemism for unexplained variance than to a well-understood phenomenon (pp. 31–32)
- [[response-effects]] (new) — systematic changes in answers from question order, context or trivial wording (p. 32)
- [[consideration]] (new) — an idea, argument or reason stored in memory that may be sampled to answer a question (p. 36)
- [[top-of-the-head-response]] (new) — choice made on the one or perhaps two immediately accessible considerations (p. 36)
- [[question-answering-model]] (new) — only previewed here: opinion reports are built from a sample of accessible considerations, shaped by elite discourse, awareness and values. The formal model comes in Chapter 3 (pp. 35–39)

## Canonical instances
- [[vietnam-elite-consensus-and-split]] — one frame early (support strongest among the most exposed). Later split frames, with exposure polarising attentive liberals and conservatives (p. 8)
- [[racial-attitudes-elite-shift]] — by Myrdal's account, scientific and elite change preceded white attitude change. Party cues from 1963–64 apparently polarised partisans (pp. 9–13)
- [[newsweek-defense-spending-1980-82]] — story counts swing 46/57 pro, then 40/60 anti, with opinion moving in tandem (p. 15)
- [[webster-diffusion]] — news of the 1989 abortion ruling diffused by awareness (Fig. 2.2) (pp. 17–18)
- [[incumbent-personal-vote]] — nonmonotonic defection by awareness (Fig. 2.3) (pp. 19–20)
- [[contra-aid-vs-troops]] — value effects depend on awareness for one item but not for another (Fig. 2.4) (pp. 24–25)
- [[asian-disease-framing]] — 72% versus 22% for equivalent options under gain versus loss wording (p. 34)
- [[communist-reporters-order-effect]] — 37% versus 73% depending on a preceding reciprocity question (p. 32)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Awareness as both exposure and resistance ↔ political-science field: [[political awareness / sophistication]] moderating both persuasion and resistance — a two-stage filter can produce a nonmonotonic response.
- [structural-analogy] Top-of-the-head considerations ↔ [[framing effect]] / [[priming / agenda-setting]]: making a consideration salient changes the answer without changing underlying facts.
- [structural-analogy] Elite cues followed by partisans ↔ [[heuristics / cues]]: people respond to party-labelled signals.
- [structural-analogy] Counting media stories by direction as a simple measure of elite discourse ↔ [[text as data / validation]]: crude coded counts, justified by their relation to opinion.
- [metaphor] Values/ideology dimensionality ↔ intelligence dimensions: author's own analogy of correlated but distinct dimensions (pp. 26–27).

## Questions this raises
- If much of the measured "error" reflects sampling of different considerations, can its share of the variance (one-half to three-quarters) be predicted from awareness and the mix of elite messages?
- How would one tell the party-conversion and opinion-leadership theses apart in individual-level panel data?
- Does a nonmonotonic awareness effect hold for other kinds of messages beyond the incumbent campaign?
- How reliably can general ideology measures stand in for domain-specific values without systematically understating effects?
