# Coming to terms with response instability

## L0 — One line
In Zaller's account, survey answers wobble over time because, on most issues, most people hold conflicting "considerations" (reasons bearing on the issue) and answer from whichever come to mind most readily; open-ended probes and earlier studies are presented as fitting this account.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people give different answers to the same survey question weeks apart? Zaller uses the axioms of his Receive-Accept-Sample (RAS) model to deduce thirteen testable implications. He checks them with open-ended data on what came to people's minds (from the 1987 National Election Studies pilot) and with earlier studies. Ambivalence is common. Open-ended remarks predict fixed-choice (closed-ended) answers. Instability is mostly chance variation around a stable average. It is smaller for more politically aware people and for people whose considerations are consistent. Zaller says the measurement-error explanation is compatible with his model, but he treats instability as substantive evidence of ambivalence, not as noise.

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 3's RAS axioms; Pearson correlation; standardized regression coefficients; proportions and significance levels; the idea of a panel survey (same people, repeated waves).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A respondent who flipped on government services, with no sign of doing so knowingly, motivates Zaller's claim that most people appear to lack "just one attitude" and to answer from whichever considerations are salient (pp. 53–54).
- The 1987 pilot study's open-ended probes are an attempt to measure considerations directly. They have real limits: coder error, a small sample, selective attrition, and a one-month gap (pp. 55–57).
- D1 (ambivalence): with low awareness and balanced information, people are likely to hold conflicting considerations. About 36–49% show it on retrospective probes (open-ended questions asked after the answer) and about 75% on stop-and-think probes (asked before the answer) (pp. 59–62).
- D2: the direction of open-ended remarks correlates sizably with the closed-ended answer (averages of about .4–.5 for stop-and-think probes and .75–.8 for retrospective probes). This differs from the on-line personality-evaluation findings (pp. 62–64).
- D3–D4: instability is mostly chance variation around a stable central tendency (a stable average position), and it is lower for more aware people (D5–D7), for issues with clearer party cues (signals of party positions) (D8), and for close-to-home issues (D9) (pp. 64–68).
- Issue importance does not by itself reduce instability, because importance is only weakly tied to cognitive engagement. The model is driven by engagement, not by concern (pp. 68–69).
- D11: in a stable environment, long-run equilibrium points (each person's stable average position) should stay put. Awareness does not predict systematic change over widely spaced waves (pp. 69–70).
- D12–D13: changes in considerations track changes in answers, and more consistent considerations mean more stable answers. The weaker-than-expected fit is most likely due to coding error (pp. 70–73).
- Zaller says nothing in the RAS model is strictly inconsistent with Achen's measurement-error account, but the RAS model treats variation as substantive (pp. 74–75).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the vacillating teacher (pp. 53–55)
- **Design.** The 1987 NES pilot asked a standard question on cutting versus increasing government services. Before answering, respondents said what came to mind about cuts and about increases. The same questions were repeated four weeks later (p. 53).
- **The case.** A self-described teacher argued emphatically for more services, citing an education crisis. Four weeks later this person favored cuts, spoke just as emphatically about big government, and did not mention education. Case IDs were correct. The same respondent was stable on two other items (p. 54).
- **Conclusion drawn.** The respondent turned out to be representative of many others, according to Zaller: most citizens appeared not to have "just one attitude" and to use whichever attitude was most salient (p. 54).
- **Model.** People hold many frequently inconsistent considerations and answer from whichever are most accessible at the moment (p. 54). Highly engaged people may form homogeneous considerations, as the RAS model predicts (pp. 54–55). For most people on most issues the inconsistencies stay unresolved. Salience then depends partly on chance (last night's TV) and partly on systematic factors such as framing and question order (p. 55).

### The 1987 pilot study (pp. 55–57)
- **Sample.** Two-wave telephone survey of 1986 NES participants. Wave 1 had 450 respondents in early summer, and 357 were reinterviewed a month later (p. 55).
- **Items.** Federal job guarantees, government services, and aid to blacks (p. 55).
- **Two probe types.** A random half got *retrospective* probes after answering (asking what ideas had come to mind). These aim at a "memory dump" of what was on the mind at the moment of response (p. 56). The other half got *stop-and-think* (prospective) probes before answering. These aim to induce more careful thought, which might make responses more reliable, and to elicit the full range of relevant considerations (pp. 55–56).
- **Coding.** Up to four comments were coded per probe, averaging about four codable comments per item. Main variables were "directional thrust" (three-quarters of comments got a clear one; about 8% were uncodable) and a frame-of-reference code with more than 140 categories (p. 56). About one-tenth of interviews were double-coded, with coders differing on 10–15% of cases (p. 56).
- **Limitations (author's list).**
  - Coder disagreement, though within the normal range, is high enough to seriously undermine precise tests of certain hypotheses (p. 57).
  - Consecutive same-side remarks may be one consideration or several, so counts are approximate (p. 57).
  - There were just over a hundred complete cases on the retrospective side (p. 57).
  - Less aware people drop out more. Despite oversampling, the low-information survivors are a selected group, probably more interested in the issue (p. 57).
  - The one-month gap yields less instability than usual. These last three problems (small sample, selective attrition, short gap) all reduce statistical power to study instability (p. 57).
- The author calls these the best data of which he is aware, in fact the only available data, for testing certain expectations from the RAS model (p. 57).

### First deductions from the model (pp. 57–64)
**RAS axioms (Table 4.1, p. 58).**
- A1 Reception: more cognitive engagement means more likely to receive political messages.
- A2 Resistance: people tend to resist messages inconsistent with their predispositions, but only to the extent that they have the contextual information to see the link.
- A3 Accessibility: the more recently a consideration was thought about, the faster it is retrieved.
- A4 Response: people answer by averaging across the considerations immediately salient to them.

**Assumed environment.** Moderately intense (no dominating headline story, no obscure one) and temporally stable information flows favoring both liberal and conservative sides. Changing flows produce attitude change, which later chapters treat (p. 58).

**D1, Ambivalence Deduction.** By A2, resistance requires contextual information. Most Americans have low political awareness (Chapter 2). So they are unlikely to show high resistance to arguments against their predispositions. With balanced communications, they are likely to internalize contradictory arguments and form considerations on both sides (p. 59).
- *Hochschild (1981)*, 28 depth interviews: Vincent Sartori cannot decide on guaranteed incomes because he cannot decide how much weight to give productivity. Hochschild found that, given the chance to talk, people qualify, deny, retract or give up in frustration rather than make simple statements (p. 59). Depth interviews can be criticized for aggressive probing (p. 60).
- *Four pilot measures (p. 60–61):*
  - **Conflict count:** the number of opposing remarks that can be paired. Two pro and two con gives 2; three pro and two con also gives 2. Any score above 0 means internal conflict.
  - **Spontaneous ambivalence codes**, e.g. "depends", or saying both sides have merit.
  - **Star codes**, for a single remark with two opposing elements.
  - **Total indications:** the number of the three indices on which a person scores 1 or more.
  - All were computed across both waves (p. 61).
- *Table 4.2 (p. 61), % with total indications ≥ 1 (jobs / services / blacks):*

| Probe | Jobs | Services | Blacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrospective | 40 | 49 | 36 |
| Stop-and-think | 74 | 76 | 75 |

- The author reads this as 36–49% ambivalent on retrospective probes and about 75% on stop-and-think probes. He calls the retrospective figure surely an understatement and the stop-and-think figure still probably one (pp. 61–62).

*Intuition note.* If people hear balanced arguments on both sides and are unlikely to filter them well, they tend to end up with reasons for both answers. Zaller reads the pilot data as showing that this is common.

**D2.** If A4 holds, the direction of the open-ended remarks should predict the closed-ended answer (p. 62). Otherwise the ambivalence would be inconsequential (p. 62). Hastie and Park (1986), on personality evaluation, turned up many cases of no correlation or occasionally even a negative one; the explanation given is that people form evaluations "on-line" and store them. RAS assumes that on most political issues people lack preformed long-term attitudes (p. 62).
- *Index (footnote 12):* $\dfrac{\text{liberal} - \text{conservative}}{\text{liberal} + \text{conservative} + \text{ambivalent}}$ (p. 63).
- *Table 4.3, Pearson correlations (p. 63).*
  - Stop-and-think: per-wave correlations average .42 over the three items. Combined across waves they average about .51.
  - Retrospective: per-wave correlations average .75. Combined they average .80.
  - Example cells: jobs, retrospective, wave 1, .79 (N = 126); jobs, stop-and-think, wave 1, .39 (N = 212).
- Stop-and-think is the stronger evidence, since respondents cannot be justifying an answer already given (pp. 63–64). Zaller does not claim D2 is surprising or definitive (p. 64).
- **Objection (McGraw, Lodge and Stroh, 1990):** the correlation could arise in many ways, including post hoc rationalization. **Zaller replies:** the objection is well founded, but D2 is one of about thirty deductions, and no obvious alternative explains the whole range (footnote 13, p. 64).

### Response instability (pp. 64–75)
**D3.** Conflicting considerations plus A4 imply considerable variability in responses. Zaller cites it as strongly confirmed in many studies (p. 64).

**D4.** If information flow is steady, the average balance of considerations is roughly the same over time. What varies is which consideration is on top. So expect chance variation around a stable central tendency (p. 64). Studies that separate chance from long-term change find that instability is "almost exclusively" chance variation around a stable tendency, with Krosnick (1988) the exception. For example, a long-term score of 5 may fluctuate between 3 and 7 (p. 65).

*Intuition note.* When information flow is steady, think of each interview as a draw from a fixed pool of reasons (a simplification: A4 speaks of averaging). The pool does not change, but the draw does.

**D5–D7 (awareness).** More aware people are more likely (A1) to have the cueing messages to reject inconsistent information (D5). So they are more likely to form homogeneous considerations (D6). That homogeneity should bring greater stability over time (D7) (p. 65).
- *D5, Table 4.4 (1988 NES, p. 66).* Highly aware respondents were much more likely to know the parties' and leaders' positions on job guarantees. For example, the share saying Dukakis favors guarantees rises from 25% in the lowest group to 89% in the highest. Reagan falls from 9% to 4%. A similar pattern holds for defense spending.
- *D6, Figure 4.1 (pp. 66–67).*
  - Consistency $=\dfrac{\sum \text{consistent remarks}}{\sum \text{consistent} + \sum \text{inconsistent}}$, running from 0 to 1.
  - Low-awareness respondents are about as likely to mention inconsistent as consistent considerations. Highly aware respondents show a fair degree of consistency, though not enough to violate the ambivalence deduction. Two of the three relationships are significant at .01 and one at .10 (footnote 14, p. 67).
- *D7 (p. 67).* Three studies found that more aware people have less random instability: Dean and Moran (1977), Feldman (1989), Zaller (1991). Achen (1975) and Erikson (1979) found no relationship.

**D8 (party cues).** If awareness helps via cues, the public should be more stable on issues where partisan elites divide clearly (p. 67). Niemi and Westholm (1984, US and Sweden) find more instability where party cues are weaker. They measure cue clarity by the party-attachment/issue relationship, which Zaller notes is indirect (p. 67, footnote 15).

**D9–D10 (attention).** Awareness stands in for attention to an issue. So close-to-home issues should show less variability, which race, abortion and drugs seem to support (D9) (p. 68). Issue-public members, who care more, should also be more stable (D10). Converse (1964) and Schuman and Presser (1981) seemed to confirm this. Krosnick (1988) found the opposite: people who care more show more random variability (p. 68).
- *Zaller's diagnosis (1980 NES).* People rating an issue extremely important were hardly more knowledgeable. For example, someone rating Russia toughness 100 versus 25 was only about 13 points more likely to know Reagan was to Carter's right. For government services there was no relationship (p. 68). Krosnick et al. (1992) confirm that the knowledge gain is only modest (footnote 16, p. 68).
- Without the intervening cognitive engagement, the RAS model gives no expectation of more stability (p. 69). The model is driven by cognitive engagement, not by concern or feeling about an issue (p. 69).
- *Artifact (footnote 17).* People calling an issue unimportant may pick the scale midpoint as a "no opinion" answer and stay there. Recoding to three points gave a slightly negative importance–error link, as expected, but biases in the opposite direction. Krosnick's is the only one of eight studies to find that a significant part of instability is true change (p. 69).

**D11 (equilibrium).** Less aware people show more chance fluctuation, but not more systematic change over the long run in a stable environment (p. 69). In the model, all individuals (idealized as information processors) must remain near their equilibrium points, so these points should stay constant while the environment is stable (p. 69).
- Short-term "lumpiness" (unemployment, a news program) can shift salience for days or months, then countervailing messages should restore the long-term mean (pp. 69–70). Regression back to the mean could look like gradual change if waves are closely spaced. So the expectation would hold only for fairly widely spaced measurements (p. 70).
- *Evidence.* In the 1972–74–76 NES panel (two-year gaps), Wiley–Wiley stability coefficients were near 1.0 and did not vary with awareness (Zaller, 1986) (p. 70).

**D12 (direct test of the mechanism).** Changes in the direction of remarks should go with changes in closed-ended answers (p. 70). Model:
$$\text{Attitude}_2 = b_0 + b_1\,\text{Attitude}_1 + b_2\,\text{Considerations}_1 + b_3\,\text{Considerations}_2$$
A large $b_3$ with a moderate $b_2$ would show that immediately salient considerations explain departures from the first answer (p. 70).
- *Table 4.5 (standardized, p. 71), jobs / services / blacks.*
  - Retrospective: Considerations$_1$ is −.04 / .05 / .04, none significant. Considerations$_2$ is .38 / .36 / .35, all significant.
  - Stop-and-think: Considerations$_1$ is .12 / .03 / .15 (the last at p<.10). Considerations$_2$ is .16 / .24 / .15, all significant.
- The author says the data tend to fit this pattern (p. 70).

**D13.** Define consistency of a person's remarks across both waves:
$$\frac{\left|\sum \text{liberal} - \sum \text{conservative}\right|}{\sum \text{liberal} + \sum \text{conservative} + \sum \text{ambivalent}}$$
A score of 1.0 means all remarks lean one way, and such persons should be perfectly stable. A score of 0 means evenly balanced remarks, and such persons should be stable only about 50% of the time, as chance alone would give (p. 71).
- *Table 4.6 (p. 72), proportion stable, lowest (.00) and highest (1.00) consistency band.*

| Item and probe | .00 | 1.00 |
|---|---|---|
| Jobs, retrospective | .50 | .90 |
| Services, retrospective | .59 | .87 |
| Blacks, retrospective | .57 | .96 |
| Jobs, stop-and-think | .63 | .88 |
| Services, stop-and-think | .54 | .73 |
| Blacks, stop-and-think | .57 | .88 |

- Five of six tests are significant, and the sixth has p = .07 (p. 71). Stability does not run from 0.50 up to 1.0, especially on the stop-and-think side (p. 71).
- *Most likely explanation: coding error.* Items with more directionally uncodable remarks (from 2% to 16%) should show larger shortfalls (p. 72).
- *Figure 4.2 (p. 73).* The consistency–stability slope falls with the proportion uncodable (logarithmic fit $r^2=0.93$, $p<.05$). Stop-and-think items have more uncodable remarks (difference significant at .01 for each item). This likely explains their weaker effects (p. 73).
- Despite measurement error, a small sample and abnormally low base rates of instability, the author judges the results probably about as strong as can be expected (p. 73).
- *Within versus across interviews.* A one-wave consistency measure has almost no capacity to predict instability across waves (p. 74). This suggests that the conflict most responsible for instability is largely unnoticed by respondents because it occurs across interviews. The teacher may, after adopting the "bloated government" view, have blocked the education view (p. 74). This fits A4's hasty, incomplete memory search (p. 74). Tourangeau and Rasinski (1988) distinguish question interpretation from retrieval. Zaller says the results suggest interpretation is the more important source (footnote 23, p. 74).

*Intuition note.* Mixed considerations predict unstable answers, but measurement noise in coding blurs the link. Zaller suggests that the conflict that matters most arises across interviews, so respondents are probably unaware of it.

### Assessment against the measurement-error view (pp. 74–75)
- The author calls the nine deductions on instability among the most significant in the book. He says most of the findings are well established and that the explanation is what is new (p. 74).
- **Achen (1975):** instability is mainly measurement error, because vague survey language makes it hard to map attitudes onto response categories (p. 74).
- Zaller says nothing in RAS is "strictly inconsistent" with this. Both accept that ordinary language does not force people to think about an issue the same way each time. Footnote 24 adds that issues may be multidimensional (p. 74).
- *Two examples (pp. 74–75).*
  - Job guarantee: full-employment economy (most favor) versus Swedish-style welfare state (most oppose). The question is also double-barreled.
  - Defense spending: fear of enemies versus anger at Pentagon waste, or alternatively vague wording.
- **Fundamental difference.** In measurement-error models, error is noise and tells us about language. In RAS, variation is rooted in ambivalence, with implications for priming, question-order effects and attitude change (p. 75).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume a person's pool holds 5 considerations on one issue: 3 liberal and 2 conservative. In each interview the person draws one consideration at random and answers in its direction. This is our simplification of A4, which actually speaks of averaging.
- Probability of a liberal draw: $p = 3/5 = 0.6$.
- Probability of the same answer at both interviews: $0.6^2 + 0.4^2 = 0.36 + 0.16 = 0.52$.
- Consistency (D13 formula, no ambivalent remarks): $|3-2|/(3+2) = 0.2$.
- Pool 4:1: $p=0.8$, stability $=0.64+0.04=0.68$, consistency $=3/5=0.6$.
- Balanced pool: stability $=0.25+0.25=0.50$, matching D13's chance level. A one-sided pool gives $1.0$.
- (added step) In general, stability $=(1+c^2)/2$ for consistency $c$. Check: $c=0.2$ gives $0.52$; $c=0.6$ gives $0.68$.

## Concepts
- [[receive-accept-sample-ras-model]] — axioms A1–A4: reception, resistance, accessibility and response; treated as an information-processing model (p. 58)
- [[consideration]] (new) — a reason or idea bearing on an issue; people hold many, often conflicting ones, and respond from those salient (p. 54)
- [[ambivalence]] (new) — holding considerations on both sides of an issue; measured by paired opposing remarks, ambivalence codes and star codes (pp. 59–61)
- [[response-instability]] (new) — change over time in answers to the same closed-ended item; here mostly chance variation around a stable mean (pp. 64–65)
- [[stop-and-think-probe]] (new) — open-ended probe asked before answering, to elicit considerations (p. 56)
- [[retrospective-probe]] (new) — open-ended probe asked after answering, a "memory dump" (p. 56)
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — a proxy for cognitive engagement; raises reception of cues, resistance and consistency of considerations (pp. 65, 68)
- [[heuristics-cues]] — party and leader positions as cueing messages that let people reject inconsistent arguments (pp. 65–67)
- [[issue-public]] (new) — people who care strongly about an issue; caring alone does not guarantee engagement (p. 68)
- [[measurement-error-model]] (new) — Achen's view that instability is mainly noise from vague question wording (pp. 74–75)
- [[equilibrium-point]] (new) — the stable average of a person's considerations under steady information flow (p. 69)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Instability from sampling a fixed pool of considerations ↔ psychometrics/statistics: random draws around a stable true score — one-sample draws give $(1+c^2)/2$ in our toy, not the author's model.
- [structural-analogy] Coding error attenuating the consistency–stability slope (Fig. 4.2) ↔ text as data / validation: unreliable human codes weaken measured relationships.
- [metaphor] Salience-dependent answers ↔ framing effect and priming: the author says the ambivalence account has implications for priming and question order (p. 75).

## Questions this raises
- How does the model handle cases of true attitude change when information flows shift? The author defers this to later chapters (pp. 58, 69).
- Can one separate considerations from rationalization? The author concedes the objection to D2 (p. 64).
- If coding error weakens D13 tests, would a better coding scheme or a larger, less selected sample show the predicted 0.5–1.0 range?
- Is the "interpretation versus retrieval" suggestion (footnote 23) testable by experiments that fix question wording?
