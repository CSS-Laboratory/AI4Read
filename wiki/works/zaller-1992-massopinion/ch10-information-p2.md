# Information flow and electoral choice (part 2/2): comparing elections, the 1984 primaries, appendices

## L0 — One line
In elections, resistance to a dominant campaign comes mainly from countervalent (opposing) information in the message stream, not from individual predispositions alone, and the same reception–acceptance logic fits House, Senate, presidential and primary data.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can the RAS model of House elections be extended to other contests? Zaller compares House, Senate and 1984 presidential defection to incumbents. Nonmonotonic (rise-then-fall) defection by awareness appears strongly in House races and is essentially absent in the other cases. Defection is highest in low-intensity races and lowest in presidential races. A one-message model cannot explain this. Both sides of the message flow matter: more intense races bring more volume and more balance, so candidate evaluations polarize along party lines. The 1984 Mondale–Hart primaries show that awareness-based resistance is not automatic: the most aware resisted only later, once there was two-sided information and some stored considerations. Appendices give the Senate estimates and the primary model.

**Prerequisites:** Reception–acceptance logic of the RAS model (Chapters 7–8: probability of receiving a message × probability of accepting it); logit-type regression; party identification; the idea of "inpartisans" and "outpartisans" relative to an incumbent.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Zaller expects the same basic diffusion-and-influence processes across election types, so voting should look fundamentally similar once differences in information flow are allowed for. House races are the limiting case with the least information (p. 248).
- Nonmonotonic defection by awareness is strong in House races but essentially absent in Senate and presidential races. Even Senate campaigns now reach the least aware stratum; most House races do not (p. 249).
- Peak defection occurs in low-intensity races and the lowest in presidential races. A one-message model cannot explain this, so both sides of the flow must be modelled (p. 250).
- More intense races give more balanced and larger communication flows. Even a constant partisan bias in acceptance then produces more polarized net evaluations (p. 250–51).
- More aware partisans are more polarized mainly because they receive more information, not because they are much more selective (p. 252).
- Resistance to dominant campaigns needs countervalent information; predispositions alone explain little (p. 252–53).
- In the 1984 primaries, the most aware Democrats were first the most influenceable and only later resisted. Resistance is not automatic (p. 255–58).
- The primary dynamics are fitted by a nested sequence of reception–acceptance conversions (Appendix B), with correlation .92 between model and data (p. 262–64).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### House elections in comparative perspective (pp. 248–253)

**Rationale.** Most electoral analysis studies a single type of election. Because the House analysis uses the general RAS model, Zaller extends it to other cases. He expects the basic processes of information diffusion and influence to be the same, so voting across election types should look "fundamentally similar" once information-flow differences are accounted for (p. 248). House elections give voters the least information of any national election, so they are a limiting case. The question is what happens to choice as campaign intensity, and so candidate information, rises (p. 248).

**Data (Figure 10.6).** Defection to the incumbent is compared across four settings (p. 248):
- House races: as in the right panel of Figure 10.2, net of seniority held at its minimum.
- Senate races: 1990 NES Senate study, 25 races with an incumbent seeking reelection against a challenger from the opposition party. Same model and procedures as for the House. Estimates are for modal partisans.
- Low-intensity races sit at about the 10th percentile of campaign intensity and high-intensity races at about the 90th. The incumbent is assumed to have just completed a first term (p. 248).
- Footnote 16: spending is divided by the number of districts in the state. Low intensity means incumbent spending of $1.5 million per district, challenger spending of $500,000 and a media intensity score of 0.05. High intensity means $10 million for each candidate and media intensity .75 (p. 248).
- Presidential: 1984 defection of Democrats to Reagan, estimated by a simple polynomial regression (p. 249).

**Result 1: nonmonotonicity.** The rise-then-fall pattern of defection by awareness shows up strongly in House races and is essentially absent elsewhere (p. 249). This is no problem for RAS, because nonmonotonicity is only one possible outcome of reception–acceptance. If the change-inducing message is intense enough to reach the least aware stratum, the unaware can be more susceptible than anyone else (p. 249). Dryer (1971) found that the 1952 nonmonotonicity Converse discovered never reappeared in later presidential contests. Defection has been monotonically negative in awareness, because short-term stimuli had "effectively penetrated all segments of the electorate" (Dryer, quoted p. 249). Figure 10.6 adds that even Senate campaigns now penetrate to the least aware stratum, but most House elections do not (p. 249).

**Result 2: a puzzle for one-message logic.** Peak defection occurs in House races and low-intensity Senate races, which have low intensity. The lowest defection occurs in the presidential race, which has the highest intensity (p. 250). Zaller says nothing in his typology of attitude change (Figure 8.2), or any other manipulation of one-message logic, can explain this. Within House races, higher-intensity races also show lower defection (footnote 17: mean 41% vs. 59%, $t = 2.87$, $p < .01$). To explain the pattern, both sides of the communication flow must be considered (p. 250).

*Intuition note.* A model with one message cannot explain why defection is lowest in the most intense race. Once the challenger's message also gets through, the flow is both larger and more balanced, and both help polarize evaluations along party lines (balance gives each side a basis for a partisan evaluation; volume lets a constant bias add up).

**Candidate evaluations (Figure 10.7).** Net like/dislike scores (the net of four candidate-consideration measures) are shown for all three election types. House values come from Equation 10.1 and Table 10.4. Senate values are simulated with the same procedure (Table 10.7). Presidential values are raw means by awareness (p. 250). The pattern: more intense campaigns, across and within election types, make inpartisans' and outpartisans' evaluations more polarized along party lines. Since candidate considerations powerfully affect votes (Table 10.3), this yields more party-line voting and lower defection (p. 250).

**Why volume matters.** More intense races bring both a more evenly balanced flow and a larger volume (p. 250). Balance gives each side the informational basis for a partisan evaluation. Volume matters because partisans accept more congenial than uncongenial messages. Zaller's illustrative assumption (not an estimate) is 67% acceptance for congenial and 33% for uncongenial messages, roughly in line with the rates implied for median respondents by Table 10.4 (p. 251):
- A Democrat exposed to 3 pro-Democratic and 3 pro-Republican messages accepts 2 and 1, for an expected net of $+1$.
- With 6 of each, he accepts 4 and 2, so the net is $+2$.
- In general, the more messages a consistent bias operates on, the larger the expected net score. Republicans are driven the same way in the opposite direction (p. 251).

**Awareness and polarization.** Highly aware partisans ingest more campaign information, so they are more polarized in net considerations (p. 252). Zaller stresses that the main reason is not that the aware are more selective about which messages they accept ("though there may be some tendency"). They ingest much more information and process it with almost the same partisan bias (p. 252).

**House exception.** House defection is nonmonotonic because the amount of candidate information reaching less aware voters is lower than in any other race, "fairly close to nil" (the simulated mean is 0.13 remarks across both candidates in the lowest awareness group, low-intensity, low-seniority race; footnote 18). Voters undisturbed by new information do not defect (p. 252).

**Modified Converse account (p. 252).**
1. Voters at the lowest awareness in the lowest-intensity races stay loyal because they get almost no new information.
2. As information rises from nil to some, instability peaks. These are middle-awareness voters in low-intensity House races and low-awareness voters in low-intensity Senate races. Most of what they get is proincumbent information, which they have little inertial capacity to resist.
3. As information reaching outpartisans keeps rising, instability falls, reaching almost no defection among the highly aware in presidential races.
4. Inertial resistance is only a small part of that decline. The main reason is higher volumes of more balanced information, which polarize evaluations and raise loyalty.

**Theoretical conclusion.** Resistance depends on internal factors (stored considerations, values and attachments, awareness), but these predispositions "cannot, by themselves, explain very much." They must be nourished by exposure to a countervalent information flow (p. 252–53). Given two competing sets of information, people can generally choose by partisanship and values even if they score low on awareness. Under one-sided flow, as in low-key House and Senate races, their capacity for critical resistance appears limited (p. 253). Zaller concludes that the most important source of resistance to dominant campaigns is countervalent information in the overall stream. This is certain for elections and, per evidence from the mainstream model, "perhaps" for other contexts (p. 253).

*Intuition note.* A strong party loyalty is like a shield that works only when something is pushing against it. Under a one-sided message flow, even a loyal citizen's capacity for critical resistance appears quite limited.

### The dynamics of presidential primaries (pp. 253–258)

**Motivation (p. 253).** In partisan elections most votes are so firmly tied to party identification that change is hard to observe. Primaries have the opposite problem: preferences sometimes shift so fast they are hard to pin down. This tests whether the same account works in a "free-wheeling" election.

**Background (pp. 253–54).** In early 1984, Mondale looked almost unopposed. A New York Times poll showed him further ahead than any candidate had been at that point. Hart then rose in Iowa (second to Mondale), trounced Mondale in New Hampshire and received massive publicity. He then faced jokes and the "Where's the beef?" question. Hart and Mondale fought through late March–May, and Mondale prevailed (p. 254).

**Data and model (p. 254).** The NES ran a continuous survey, only about 45 Democrats per week, giving "slightly fuzzy" snapshots. Zaller split the season into four periods:
1. January–early February (Mondale seems locked up, Hart a blip).
2. Last two weeks of February (Hart builds momentum in Iowa and New Hampshire).
3. Three weeks from just after New Hampshire to just before the Illinois primary (Hart surge, but first questions about character and "new issues").
4. Late March to season's end (many minor ups and downs, Mondale wins).

**Period 1 (pp. 254, 256).** Support for Mondale rises steadily with awareness. Hart has minimal support, concentrated among the most aware, who were the only ones attentive enough to know a "second tier" candidate (p. 254; footnote 19: Sam Popkin, in oral remarks, is cited as noting that the politically aware are often "leading indicators" of future change). That the most aware were most susceptible to the dominant Mondale campaign "shouldn't" be surprising. If awareness brings resistance because it proxies for alternative information (stored considerations, cues, countervalent flows), there is no reason to expect resistance here. Democrats had no bad memories of Mondale, national elites provided no opinion leadership against him, and the Hart campaign was too weak to be countervalent. Influence should then depend wholly on reception (p. 256).

**Period 2 (p. 256).** Hart's esoteric message reached only the most aware quartile, with no resistance (one-sidedly positive coverage, no prior information). The Mondale message, still more intense, converted the moderately aware, who were just tuning in. It could not convert the most aware, who were in direct competition with the Hart campaign. The sharp nonmonotonicity in Mondale support was not resistance. The most aware were deserting Mondale for Hart, and the less aware had not yet heard of Hart's rise (p. 256).

**Period 3 (pp. 256–57).** Hart gained no more among highly aware Democrats and Mondale lost none there. Highly aware persons now resisted, "presumably" because they had enough information about both candidates not to be blown about by every turn. Hart's publicity reached moderately aware Democrats, who deserted Mondale in large numbers. The largest swings were among the moderately aware, and this surge nearly drove Mondale from the race (pp. 256–57).

**Period 4 (p. 257).** Moderately aware Democrats swung back to Mondale as his success grew. The most aware largely held their ground, "presumably" anchored in stored information about the two candidates. The least aware were also fairly stable, most unable to form a preference. This left the moderately aware most susceptible (p. 257). Footnote 20: Bartels (1988) and Brady and Johnston (1987) also stress that voters learn during primaries.

**Model fit (p. 257).** No likes/dislikes data exist to check the microdynamics. Zaller fits the reception–acceptance model to the Figure 10.8 data (Appendix B). The model "fits the data extremely well and confirms my basic account in every important respect." Figure 10.9 gives estimated preference change among Democrats: switching to Hart after Iowa rises with awareness, while switching to Hart after New Hampshire and to Mondale late peak at moderate awareness (p. 257).

**Summary on primaries (pp. 257–58).** Awareness-induced resistance is "not automatic." It depends on access to alternative communications, either stored considerations from past campaigns or current countervalent messages or cues. In early 1984 the most aware had no basis for resistance and were more reactive than any other group. Only later, with two-sided flow and some inertial considerations, were the most aware most resistant (pp. 257–58).

*Intuition note.* The same people who look "stubborn" in one election can look "gullible" in another. The difference is whether they have opposing information to lean on. Awareness lets people take in news. It protects them only if the news includes a competing story or they already hold relevant memories.

### Concluding remarks (p. 258)
The chapter is only incidentally about elections. Its aim is to show that summary political decisions, whether votes or closed-ended survey answers, rest on considerations formed from competing influences. For the first time there is abundant measurable variability in the intensity of opposing messages, together with measures of the underlying considerations. This allows direct observation of inertial resistance (party likes and dislikes) and, more important for elections, countervalent resistance (prochallenger and antiincumbent flows). The theoretical machinery is "exactly the same" as in earlier analyses (p. 258).

### Appendix A: Defections in Senate elections (pp. 258–262)

**Variables (pp. 258–59).**
- Awareness: the Senate study had an "unusually weak selection of awareness items," so Zaller built the best measure he could (p. 258).
- Campaign intensity: a state-level media measure built from the question on how many Senate-campaign stories respondents read, saw or heard (a good many, one or two, none). Range 0 to 0.74 (p. 258).
- Political values: party identification, plus policy distance from the incumbent. Distance is built from summed ADA and ACU ratings for the prior year, standardized and subtracted from standardized ideological self-description. It is coded so that, if the Democrat is left of the Republican, higher scores mean greater affinity to the challenger (p. 259).

**Table 10.6 (p. 259; outpartisan vote for incumbent, Equation 10.1, maximum likelihood, $N = 450$).** Selected coefficients (standard errors):
- Reception: intercept 0.19; awareness 0.40 (.39); incumbent spending 0.015 (.008); challenger spending $-.012$ (.008); media coverage $-2.65$ (1.42); seniority 1.14 (1.41); seniority × challenger spending $-0.0035$ (.0031).
- Acceptance: intercept 4.05; awareness $-1.31$ (.50); partisan strength $-1.18$ (.57); policy distance $-0.70$ (.31); challenger spending 0.001 (.001).

Seniority effects are smaller than in the House and not statistically significant. They follow the same pattern: a positive main effect and a negative interaction with challenger spending (pp. 259–60).

**Figure 10.6 settings (p. 260).** Awareness runs from $-1.9$ SD to $+1.86$ SD (98 percent of the scale). Idealized outpartisans have a party identification score of 1.3 and a policy distance of $+2$ (p. 260). Predispositions matter (p. 261):
- Among voters ideologically closest to the incumbent, defection actually increases with awareness.
- Among the most distant, the decline with awareness is steeper than shown.
- So the defection patterns resemble the Figure 10.1 curves, "except for a more intense message."

**Table 10.7 (p. 260; likes and dislikes, 1990 Senate, $N = 1866$).** Four dependent variables: incumbent likes, incumbent dislikes, challenger likes and challenger dislikes. Each has a reception and an acceptance function. Awareness has positive reception coefficients throughout (1.00, 0.82, 0.62, 0.94). There is no awareness term in any acceptance function.

**Method for Figure 10.7 (p. 261).**
- Reception function: awareness, turnout (purged of campaign-intensity effects), logged incumbent and challenger spending, logged seniority and media coverage. The challenger models add the challenger's prior electoral experience.
- Acceptance function: party attachment and policy distance.
- Policy distance in the likes/dislikes models is absolute distance, whereas signed left–right distance affects vote choice. For example, a conservative to the right of a conservative senator may dislike him or her without voting against him or her.
- Budget-priority items were combined into a standardized scale scored so low means affinity with the incumbent. Distance from the challenger uses standardized ideological self-identification.

**Awareness dropped from the acceptance function (p. 261).** When included, it took large positive coefficients (less resistance among the aware) while the reception coefficient turned negative (less reception among the aware). Zaller says he does not understand why Equation 10.1 "behaved nonsensically," but dropping awareness from acceptance restored sense. Footnote 22 notes a similar tendency in House estimates without the loquacity measure. A possible explanation is that the model is not identified if the true acceptance coefficient on awareness is zero.

**Idealized values (pp. 261–62).** Same as in the House analysis: party distance scores 1.3 and $-1.3$, absolute policy distance 2 and 0, budget attitude scores 1 and $-1$, and ideological distance from the challenger 1 and $-1$ for outpartisans and inpartisans respectively.

### Appendix B: Modeling the Mondale–Hart contest (pp. 262–64)

**Equations.** $H_t$ and $M_t$ are support for Hart and Mondale at time $t$. $P_{HC_t}$ and $P_{MC_t}$ are the proportions converting to Hart or Mondale in period $t$, each a function of awareness (p. 262):

$$H_1 = P_{HC_1} \tag{10.4a}$$
$$M_1 = P_{MC_1} \tag{10.4b}$$

Period 2 (a proportion converts to Hart, following Equation 7.7):

$$H_2 = H_1 + P_{HC_2}(1 - H_1) \tag{10.4c}$$
$$M_2 = M_1 - P_{HC_2} M_1 \tag{10.4d}$$

Hart's support equals initial support plus the converted share of initial nonsupporters. Mondale's equals initial support minus the share lost to Hart (p. 262). Periods 3 and 4:

$$H_3 = H_2 + P_{HC_3}(1 - H_2) \tag{10.4e}$$
$$M_3 = M_2 - P_{HC_3} M_2 \tag{10.4f}$$
$$H_4 = H_3 - P_{MC_4} H_3 \tag{10.4g}$$
$$M_4 = M_3 + P_{MC_4}(1 - M_3) \tag{10.4h}$$

Each $P_C$ term is a reception–acceptance process as in Equation 7.5 (p. 262). Equations 10.4a–h are nested, so earlier terms recur later. After substitution (p. 262–63):

$$H_2 = [P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})]$$
$$H_3 = [P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})] + P_{HC_3}\,(1 - [P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})])$$
$$H_4 = \big[[P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})] + P_{HC_3}(1 - [P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})])\big] - P_{MC_4}\big[[P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})] + P_{HC_3}(1 - [P_{HC_1} + P_{HC_2}(1 - P_{HC_1})])\big]$$

The Mondale equations take a comparable form (p. 263).

*(added step)* In the gain equations (10.4c, e, h), new support = old support + (converting share × the part of the electorate not yet supporting that candidate). In the loss equations (10.4d, f, g), new support = old support − (converting share × old support).

**Parameters and constraints (pp. 263–64).** There are five $P_C$ terms ($P_{HC_1}, P_{MC_1}, P_{HC_2}, P_{HC_3}, P_{MC_4}$). Each has four parameters (reception and acceptance intercepts plus awareness coefficients in both), so 20 in total. The data are 40 aggregate means (4 periods × 2 candidates × 5 awareness groups). Constraints (pp. 263–64):
- Awareness has the same effect in all reception functions. Intercepts absorb differences in message intensity.
- Acceptance functions are dropped for Equations 10.4a–d, because they added nothing. Footnote 23: estimates for $b_0$ tended toward $+\infty$, "indicating no resistance." Awareness is also dropped from the acceptance function of 10.4b.
- Awareness has the same acceptance effect in 10.4e–h. Separate coefficients would be preferable, but none could approach significance.
- This leaves 10 coefficients for 40 data points. A nonlinear regression estimated all equations simultaneously.

**Table 10.8 (p. 263).** Reception intercepts: Hart1 $-4.92$ (2.12), Mondale1 1.17 (.64), Hart2 $-2.59$ (.50), Hart3 0.32 (.40), Mondale4 $-0.44$ (.41). Acceptance intercepts: Mondale1 $-0.02$ (.27), Hart3 2.44 (1.46), Mondale4 2.41 (1.62). Awareness: reception 1.17 (0.80), acceptance $-2.87$ (1.43), applying to acceptance only in periods 3 and 4.

**Fit (p. 264).** Several intercept terms fall short of significance. Zaller says this is not worrisome because most of the terms are intercept shifts that rest on only five or ten data points. The awareness coefficients on both reception and acceptance reach "some degree" of significance. Footnote 24: with unequal cell sizes, significance tests are "only roughly suggestive." The correlation between model and raw data is .92 ($r^2 = .84$), which, given sampling error in the raw data and the constraints on the model, Zaller says "would seem" to be as much accuracy as could be hoped (p. 264).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Main idea: with the same partisan acceptance bias, more volume and more balance in the message flow produce more polarized net evaluations.

Assume a Democrat accepts 80% of congenial (pro-Democratic) messages and 20% of uncongenial ones. Net = accepted congenial − accepted uncongenial.
- Low volume, balanced: 2 of each type. $2(0.8) - 2(0.2) = 1.6 - 0.4 = +1.2$.
- High volume, balanced: 10 of each type. $10(0.8) - 10(0.2) = 8 - 2 = +6.0$. The bias is the same, but the net is five times larger.
- One-sided flow: a Republican (outpartisan to a Democratic incumbent) hears 6 pro-incumbent messages and 0 pro-challenger. He accepts 20% of the uncongenial ones, so the net is $6(0.2) = +1.2$ toward the incumbent.
- Balanced flow of 6 and 6: $6(0.8) - 6(0.2) = 4.8 - 1.2 = +3.6$ toward the challenger.

Same person, same bias: the one-sided flow pulls him toward the incumbent, the balanced flow gives a strongly partisan evaluation.

## Concepts
- [[receive-accept-sample-ras-model]] — opinion formed from messages received, then accepted or resisted. Extended here to Senate, presidential and primary data (p. 248).
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — raises reception of messages. Whether it also brings resistance depends on access to alternative communications: stored considerations or current countervalent messages and cues (p. 252–53, 256–58).
- [[countervalent-information]] (new) — opposing messages in the flow. Zaller's most important source of resistance to dominant campaigns, certainly in elections and perhaps in other contexts (p. 253).
- [[inertial-resistance]] (new) — resistance from stored considerations such as party likes and dislikes (p. 258).
- [[campaign-intensity]] (new) — volume and balance of candidate information. Measured here from spending and media coverage (p. 248, 258).
- [[nonmonotonic-reception-acceptance]] (new) — influence rises then falls with awareness. Can arise when the message is too weak to reach the least aware; if it is intense enough to reach them, they can be the most susceptible (p. 249, 252).
- [[outpartisan-defection]] (new) — outpartisans' vote for the incumbent. Peaks as information rises from nearly nil to some, and falls as information keeps rising and becomes more balanced (p. 252).
- [[partisan-perceptual-bias]] — here a constant tendency to accept congenial and resist uncongenial messages, assumed at 67/33 for illustration (p. 251).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Convert-the-remaining-pool recurrence in 10.4c–h ↔ diffusion/adoption models: in the gain equations each period adds a share of those not yet converted, which resembles a cumulative adoption curve (the loss equations subtract a share of current supporters).
- [structural-analogy] Net score rising with volume under constant bias ↔ Condorcet jury theorem: repeated signals with a fixed lean accumulate (here a partisan lean, not accuracy).
- [metaphor] Awareness-linked "leading indicators" (footnote 19) ↔ early adopters in opinion change.

## Questions this raises
- If awareness does not itself create resistance, what other variables (such as stored considerations or cues) should be measured directly rather than proxied by awareness?
- Zaller notes awareness behaved oddly in the Senate acceptance function and offers an identification worry. How should one test whether reception and acceptance effects of the same variable can be separated?
- How sensitive are the 1984 primary conclusions to using about 45 respondents per week and 40 aggregate means to fit 10 coefficients?
- Does the "countervalent information" conclusion hold for non-electoral issues, as Zaller suggests "perhaps" (p. 253)?
