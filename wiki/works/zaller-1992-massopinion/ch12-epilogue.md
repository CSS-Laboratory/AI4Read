# Epilogue: The question of elite domination of public opinion

## L0 — One line
Heavy dependence of mass opinion on elite messages is unlikely to be elite domination, if experts are diverse and motivated to solve problems, the press covers all expert views, politicians stay within expert opinion, and citizens can follow like-minded elites.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Zaller asks whether his finding that citizens depend heavily on elite messages means elites dominate public opinion. He defines domination as elites inducing opinions that citizens would not hold if they knew the best available information and analysis. A thought experiment, "Purple Land," lists five conditions under which heavy dependence is unlikely to amount to domination. He then argues, with cases such as homosexuality and nuclear winter, that the American system at least partially meets some of these conditions. His claim is that the question can be studied by examining how cues are produced and spread, not by judging whether elites are correct. He says the question remains open and needs more evidence.

**Prerequisites:** the book's earlier model of mass opinion (elite cues, political awareness, "mainstream" vs "polarization" patterns); basic ideas about the press and ideology.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- "Minimal media effects" gave way to evidence of substantial cumulative effects, so worry about elite manipulation is again legitimate (pp. 310–311).
- Elite domination is defined without needing to know who is "correct": elites induce opinions citizens would not hold under the best available information and analysis (p. 313).
- Purple Land lists five conditions that make domination unlikely despite heavy dependence on elites; they can be tested by studying how cues are generated and spread (p. 314).
- The press covers news from "all legitimate points of view," where legitimacy comes partly from accredited experts, not only officials. The post-1974 shift in coverage of homosexuality illustrates this (pp. 316–319).
- What matters is procedural correctness of expert communities: ideological diversity and incentives to solve problems; conventional knowledge strong enough to compel agreement is a further key issue (pp. 325–327).
- Expert agreement produces consensus; expert disagreement produces public polarization, strongest among the most attentive. Ideology links citizens to like-minded specialists (p. 327).
- Information generation precedes political control of it. Government is often a proximate, not original, source (pp. 328–330).
- One reason (Zaller's third) to involve the public is that a few specialists cannot check authority alone; their hope is to publicize and politicize disagreements so citizens can weigh in. The real problem is securing vigorous competition among ideas (pp. 331–332).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction: from "minimal effects" to renewed concern (pp. 310–311)
- Observers in the 1930s–40s feared mass media would bring totalitarian domination. Early survey research found media effects small relative to those fears. Media mostly reinforced and activated existing opinion, and community leaders mediated much of their impact in a "two-step flow" (p. 310).
- Recent studies with varied methods find substantial effects. No single story may matter, but the cumulative effect of many stories over months or years may be large. The "hypodermic needle" model is not revived (pp. 310–311).
- So complacency about elite manipulation has lost its academic warrant. Zaller takes up the issue using his earlier categories (p. 311).

### Defining elite domination (pp. 311–313)
- On first inspection the book's argument is discouraging. Many citizens are too inattentive to respond critically. The attentive minority respond to new issues mainly via the partisanship and ideology of message sources (p. 311).
- If domination means any case where the public moves in the direction of elite information and cues, there is little to argue about; the evidence is abundant. Zaller asks how it could be otherwise, since the public must regularly have opinions about matters "out of reach, out of sight, out of mind" (p. 311).
- **Page and Shapiro's framing:** public preferences are "authentic" to the extent that information and interpretations help the public reach the choices it would make if fully informed. Those who supply correct, helpful information *educate*. Those who supply erroneous or biased information *mislead*. Those who do so consciously and deliberately *manipulate* (p. 312).
- **Zaller's objection:** as Page and Shapiro themselves acknowledge, this framing needs independent knowledge of which information is correct, which is largely unavailable. He is reluctant to call Reagan's Soviet rhetoric manipulation, as Page and Shapiro do. Such judgments are inherently political, and one should not assume one's own judgments rise above partisanship (p. 312).
- He nonetheless keeps the "fully informed" idea. If it means all *available* information, fully informed judgments need not always be correct, and fully informed people may still disagree. So opposing groups can disagree without charging each other with manipulation (p. 312).
- **Definition:** elite domination is a situation in which elites induce citizens to hold opinions they would not hold if aware of the best available information and analysis (p. 313). It still depends on judging what the public would believe if fully informed, but Zaller thinks this is surmountable (p. 313).

*Intuition note:* Zaller changes the test. The question is not "are the elites right?" but "would citizens who studied the issue carefully, with the best available analysis, disagree with the elites?"

### The parable of Purple Land (pp. 313–314)
- Blues and reds share many values but evaluate policies differently. Each hires experts of its own color and tells them to debate one another. Experts win large prizes (status, research support, recognition) for convincing other experts (p. 313).
- Politicians and activists are pragmatic and rarely originate ideas. They take ideas from congenial experts without hard questions, and they rarely go beyond expert discourse (p. 313).
- Citizens are uninterested. Communication specialists keep them informed in simple terms. Few notice who endorses what, and these few mechanically adopt the opinion of their own type of elite. The rest voice whatever is on top of their head. No citizen thinks for himself or herself (pp. 313–314).
- **Zaller's verdict:** there is no elite domination. When experts agree, citizens could be sure that a lifetime of study would not take them far from the experts' conclusion. When experts disagree, citizens following their own type could be confident that closer study would more likely lead to their own subcommunity's conclusion (p. 314).
- **Five conditions** under which heavy dependence is unlikely to be domination (p. 314):
  1. Experts' predispositions differ in ways that parallel the public's, so experts examine issues from all viewpoints.
  2. Institutional incentives push experts to develop effective solutions.
  3. The press gives ample coverage of all expert viewpoints ("expert" broadly construed).
  4. Politicians and activists stay within the parameters of expert opinion.
  5. In elite disagreement, citizens can align with the faction sharing their predispositions.
- These conditions can be studied empirically without any special insight into elites' "correctness"; one need only study how cues are generated and diffused (p. 314). The rest of the chapter aims to show what issues and evidence are needed, not to settle the question (p. 314).

*Intuition note:* In Purple Land citizens are as uncritical as in the book's model, yet there is no elite domination in Zaller's sense. Safeguards sit in the elite system, not in citizens' own thinking.

### The political communications system of the United States (pp. 315–331)

**The press (pp. 315–319).**
- Reporters regard as newsworthy what their "legitimate" sources say is newsworthy (Cohen; Sigal). One reason is that journalists "go native." A more basic reason is that they lack the time, training and often the inclination for primary investigation (p. 315).
- Zaller's remark: journalists are never afraid to say what *other people* think is true. Given pressures to suppress truth, publicizing a wide range of sources can be a valuable service (p. 315).
- **Zaller's extension:** the press is not mechanically tied to named sources. It tries to cover news from "all legitimate points of view." Legitimacy is conferred by officials and interest-group leaders, and also by specialists accredited by mainstream institutions (government, universities, research institutes, major publishers). If a significant fraction of mainstream experts holds a view, the press will look for ways to base reports on it (p. 316).
- Example: Morely Safer's CBS story of a marine burning a Vietnamese hut was antiwar in implication. Zaller doubts Safer checked any particular source on how to frame it, but says he reflected a view held by a significant fraction of foreign policy experts (p. 316).
- He says he goes beyond the existing literature, which stresses particular sources, mostly officials. The broader dependence is harder to document but likely present (p. 316).
- **Case: homosexuality.** While the American Psychiatric Association (APA) held homosexuality to be a disease, coverage was dominated by antihomosexual stereotypes (the 1950 *New York Times Index* pointed to "perversion and scandal"). After the APA's 1974 membership poll declared it no longer a disease, the press began to use a "civil rights" frame alongside the old "vice" frame (pp. 316–317).
- Network TV news carried 14 minutes of stories treating homosexuals as a minority seeking rights in the five years before the vote and 135 minutes in the next five, nearly tenfold (p. 317). Figure 12.1 shows story counts. As read from the image, they are low (about 1–4 per year) in 1969–73 and higher from 1974, peaking near 35 in 1978 (p. 316).
- The new coverage fell mostly into three groups: the Matlovich lawsuit, state and local referenda such as Dade County's, and human-interest stories on campus gay organizations (p. 317).
- **Objection (considered by Zaller):** what really changed was homosexuals' militancy. **Reply:** militancy and visibility contributed, but were not the whole explanation. Nothing in the movement compelled the sympathetic campus stories (p. 317).
- Expert opinion was mostly "deep background." Reporters now saw homosexuals as ordinary citizens suffering prejudice, a view Zaller says originated among psychiatric experts (p. 318). Cronkite's 1972 item mildly ridiculed gay activists; his 1974 introduction spoke of a fight for "acceptance and respectability." Matlovich was the first dismissed homosexual to get extensive, respectful evening-news coverage, soon after the APA's declaration (p. 318).
- The link to the APA vote is not mechanical, but the change is "best understood" as reflecting the press's sensitivity to expert authority (p. 318). Politicians also changed: in 1978 Brown, Reagan and Carter opposed a California initiative on firing homosexual teachers (pp. 318–319). Zaller says this is hard to imagine under earlier expert unanimity (p. 319).
- Footnote 6: this resembles Bennett's indexing hypothesis, but with a broader range of influential sources (p. 319).

**Experts (pp. 319–328).**
- Zaller, citing V. O. Key: the press is not a wielder of great power on its own initiative; media managers are like transport managers, not responsible for what flows through their channels (p. 319). So one must study how expert communities generate and spread knowledge claims: whether they are ideologically diverse, open to challenge, and institutionally supportive of serious attempts to solve problems. Zaller finds little scholarship on this (p. 319).
- **Nuclear winter anecdote** (Zaller organized a speaker program; quotations approximate). A liberal expert refused at first to moderate a talk by a conservative nuclear-war expert, then agreed. Meeting, they found both thought nuclear winter "a nonstarter," and discussed incineration rates and other technical parameters. Soon after, the topic left the popular media (pp. 320–321).
- Zaller draws three points (p. 321):
  - Ideological diversity exists, yet agreement was reached.
  - Strong ideological differences are not an insuperable barrier to rational discussion; the key appears to be conventional scientific knowledge (physics, chemistry) accepted by both, which apparently can lead users to conclusions they are predisposed against.
  - The press apparently lost interest once experts on both sides agreed, despite a presumably strong market for such stories.
- He cannot say how often this holds elsewhere. One can imagine expert communities that enforce homogeneity, are too polarized to talk, or lack strong conventional knowledge (p. 321).
- **Expert side of the homosexuality case.**
  - Early rights groups achieved almost nothing lasting in about 70 years, until the 1930s (Lauritsen and Thorstad). Homosexuals had no allies inside the system, and many internalized the "sick" label (pp. 321–322).
  - From the late 1940s, researchers studied homosexuals leading ordinary lives, not prisoners or patients (Bayer). Kinsey argued that his incidence data challenge the view that homosexual activity itself shows a psychopathic personality (p. 322).
  - Hooker, early 1950s: standard adjustment tests on matched samples of homosexual and nonhomosexual men found no differences in overall adjustment. These were Rorschach tests interpreted by judges blind to group. Zaller treats this as an application of "neutral scientific knowledge" (p. 322).
  - Hooker was rewarded, not penalized, by psychiatry and politics. Psychiatry was no longer monolithic, though traditional therapists kept treating homosexuality as disease. Zaller says that, given the press rule above, this sufficed for change in public discussion (p. 323).
  - Homosexuals were the earliest attentive audience. Their first response was cautious interest; Zaller says it was probably inevitable that they would eventually gravitate toward the "normal" view. Kameny won a hedged 1965 Mattachine resolution (p. 323). Later they rejected psychiatric authority, while friendly experts stayed useful allies (pp. 323–324).
  - Zaller says this conforms to the "mainstream" and "polarization" models. Under a mainstream norm they stayed "in the closet," and the norm was often internalized. Offered a friendlier expert view, they allied with it (p. 324).
  - Militant protests (1970 APA meeting) pushed the APA to think harder. The trustees' and then the membership's vote to delete homosexuality involved a large political component. Yet some trustees were convinced, and others privately thought it a disorder but admitted that evidence for it was lacking. Zaller concludes standard psychiatric evidence "appeared to count" (p. 324).
  - The profession faced a dilemma: remove homosexuality because standard assessment found no problems, or devalue the standard techniques that underpin its authority elsewhere. Zaller says this must in the end have been an easy choice (pp. 324–325).
- **Procedural correctness.** Zaller does not evaluate whether psychiatrists were right. What matters is whether expert communities are ideologically diverse and have institutional incentives pushing members to resolve pressing problems. Psychiatry deserves a favorable evaluation: an untenured researcher opened the first crack in a power structure in the mid-1950s, which Zaller credits to institutional arrangements, the most important (he suggests) being standard assessment techniques (p. 325).
- **Toward a general account** (p. 325): key issues are (a) recruitment bias, (b) professional incentive structures, and (c) conventional knowledge strong enough to compel agreement among differently predisposed users.
  - Recruitment: people of different ideologies are drawn to different disciplines (Lipset and Ladd). Whether this yields homogeneity or pressure on minorities is unanswered. To the extent recruitment limits who becomes an expert, elite discourse narrows and domination becomes a danger (p. 325).
  - Incentives: in medical research, status, support and salary depend on research value as judged by peers. One need posit incentives, not altruism (p. 326). AIDS anecdote (a sociologist's report): researchers who hoped the virus was hard to transmit were quickly absorbed in winning "the big prize" (p. 326). How widely such incentives operate is an open empirical question (p. 326).
  - Predispositions still shape experts' conclusions, as in social science. But to keep standing, experts must justify conclusions in arguments acceptable to those who do not share their predispositions (pp. 326–327).
- **Ideological bias in pluralistic communities may have salutary effects** (p. 327). Issues are scrutinized by differently motivated persons. If they agree, nonspecialists, as very often happens, can have some confidence that the answer is sound or the best available for now. If not, they air disagreements publicly, alerting citizens to uncertainty (p. 327).
- **Consensus and polarization.** If specialists agree, every source says roughly the same: an "elite consensus" and "mainstream norm," most strongly supported by the most attentive. If predispositions produce disagreement, journalists publicize it, often in good-versus-evil terms. Politicians linked to like-minded specialists also disagree. The public polarizes along the same lines, most strongly among the most attentive (p. 327).
- **Ideology** is here a way for citizens to connect with knowledgeable specialists who share their predispositions. It is not a unified product of one thinker, but an agglomeration of views of specialists sharing a predispositional bent. When experts change, mass belief systems tend to follow, with lags. Citizens need only recognize which elites share their predispositions and take cues from them (pp. 327–328).
- **Summary of the idealized system** (p. 328): ideas develop among specialists and diffuse down via politicians and media. It neglects institutions, partisan competition and censorship. It sets out conditions that, if met, would greatly mitigate the objectionable effects of dependence. Some are at least partially met in the U.S.: expert communities sometimes operating within salutary incentives, and a press attentive to them (p. 328).

*Intuition note:* Ideology works like a switchboard. A citizen who knows "my side" can pick up expert views without understanding the technical debate.

**Politics and information (pp. 328–331).**
- Zaller treats information generation as primary because political information precedes political action. Even government's attempts to manipulate rest on a view of the world supplied by specialists, and much of the information system lies outside government (pp. 328–329).
- Most accounts, based on studies of press sources, stress government generation and manipulation of information. Zaller says they focus on what is easy to observe. Officials may be the proximate source without being the creator, and they cannot always control framing or the press's interests (p. 329).
- **Church–Morgenthau dinner** (Broder 1984): early in the Vietnam War, Senator Church and Hans Morgenthau tried to make skeptical reporters reexamine assumptions. Zaller says the details are unimportant; what matters is Church's (or perhaps Morgenthau's) apparently self-conscious effort to influence reporters through academic viewpoints. Books are, he says, probably at least as important: Bernard Fall's work was apparently read by most journalists in Vietnam, which framed Vietnam as anti-imperialism and nationalism (pp. 329–330, fn. 15).
- Government does color and censor, and "politics" can override experts in the short run. But partisan corruption should not be exaggerated (p. 330).
- **AIDS:** despite backlash potential, no partisan backlash issue arose. Both parties accepted public education plus research. Disagreement concerned speed and funding. Zaller asks rhetorically whether anyone can doubt that expert disagreement on strategy (e.g., quarantine) would have made it instantly partisan. The implied answer is that no one can reasonably doubt it (p. 330).
- **Race:** early-century expert opinion was predominantly racist (Ch. 2). In the 1920s it underwent "a virtual revolution," and by 1930 a new consensus minimizing group differences in intelligence was dominant. Zaller says it is at least arguable that this consensus is the reason the white power structure began at last to see blacks' point (Myrdal) (p. 331).
- Authorities and others can short-circuit the system, and expert communities have their own dysfunctions. The literature stresses breakdowns, giving a better idea of how the system fails than of how it works. Zaller directs attention to policy specialists relatively independent of authority, who raise and frame issues and are tied to the public through press, politicians and shared predispositions (p. 331).

### Concluding remarks (pp. 331–332)
- **Objection:** if only specialists can conduct political debate, why involve the public? Zaller's answers: it is the people's right; participation is a value (Mill). He does not find either answer attractive if the public regularly made morally abhorrent or technically stupid decisions. His third reason: unchecked government "invariably goes astray" (p. 331).
- A few specialists cannot check authority; they can be silenced. Their only hope is to publicize and politicize disagreements so citizens can weigh in. Zaller sees no obvious reason to think citizens wiser than specialists, so long as specialists are broadly recruited and free to operate under salutary incentives. What matters is citizens' collective ability to pressure and remove leaders, more than collective wisdom (p. 332).
- This assumes public opinion can check authorities. He does not find that assumption problematic given partisan competition. The real problem, he says, is guaranteeing an equally vigorous competition among opposing ideas (p. 332).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 8 attentive citizens: 4 red, 4 blue. The inattentive are left out, since the text says they voice top-of-head ideas. Six experts: 3 red, 3 blue. The press reports every expert view. Attentive citizens adopt the view of experts who share their color.
- **Case 1, consensus:** all 6 experts say "yes" to a policy. Support among attentive reds is 4/4 = 100%; among attentive blues 4/4 = 100%. Gap = 100 − 100 = 0 points: a mainstream norm.
- **Case 2, disagreement:** red experts say "yes" (3 of 3), blue experts say "no" (3 of 3). Attentive reds: 4/4 = 100% yes. Attentive blues: 0/4 = 0% yes. Gap = 100 − 0 = 100 points: polarization.
- **Zaller's test:** domination would exist only if, in case 2, careful study would lead reds to "no" or blues to "yes". If reds who studied the issue would still find the red experts right (and likewise blues), there is no domination, even though all 8 citizens just followed cues.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Zaller's cue-taking from like-minded elites ↔ political-science: Lupia–McCubbins conditions for persuasion — a listener can rely on a speaker who is knowledgeable and shares interests.
- [structural-analogy] Experts justifying conclusions despite predispositions ↔ political-science: motivated reasoning — preconceptions shape conclusions, but pluralism and shared standards constrain them.
- [structural-analogy] Press covering "all legitimate points of view" ↔ political-science: agenda-setting/framing — the menu of frames is set upstream of the media.

## Questions this raises
- How could one measure empirically the five Purple Land conditions (e.g., ideological diversity of experts, or press coverage of all expert views)?
- Which issues lack conventional knowledge strong enough to compel agreement, and what does elite domination look like there?
- What happens when experts are recruited homogeneously, so that the press covers all "legitimate" views but the range is narrow?
- Does the nuclear winter or AIDS pattern (expert agreement ends public attention) generalize beyond issues where physical or medical science applies?
