# Evaluating the RAS model and looking toward future research (part 2/2)

## L0 — One line
Zaller defends the core of his model (awareness interacts with predispositions to shape reception and acceptance of elite messages), admits its theoretical and statistical weak points, and suggests that the same few ideas can fruitfully be applied to presidential evaluations, trust in government (with limits) and support for authoritarian regimes.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part asks how well Zaller's reception–acceptance models stand up and where the framework could go. He concedes several weaknesses, including these. Attitude change is analysed only as a dichotomy. Inertial resistance (old views resisting new messages) has weak support. The model assumes people never think for themselves, though predispositions give some autonomy. Estimates are statistically fragile because predictors overlap (multicollinearity) and key subgroups are small. He still defends the awareness × predisposition core. He then applies the model to Reagan's character in 1984, to trust in government, and to support for authoritarian regimes (Brazil, with comparisons). He closes by calling the model approximate and provisional, but a parsimonious basis for synthesis in a fragmented field.

**Prerequisites:** the four RAS axioms and the reception/acceptance functions of Chs. 7–10; response probability as a share of considerations; multicollinearity; nonmonotonic curves.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Attitude change should be a shift in the balance of considerations. Zaller's analyses only use dichotomous switching (people either move to the opposite position or stay), and of the two graded measures he tries, the second has "no more theoretical warrant" than the first (pp. 282–284).
- Of the three resistance mechanisms (inertial, countervalent, partisan), countervalent resistance (the aware more often hear weak opposing messages) seems to have the strongest support, and partisan resistance (rejecting ideas that clash with one's values) seems firm. Inertial resistance (a large store of old considerations resisting change) is doubtful and may need elite validation; Zaller concludes that citizens' capacity for autonomous resistance is very distinctly limited (pp. 285–287).
- The model sets Prob(acceptance | no reception) to zero, but predispositions give citizens some autonomy. Zaller is less confident that the model captures how less attentive people form opinions (pp. 287–289).
- One-message models give clear predictions but fail in some cases. Two-message models explain those failures; readers may find them cumbersome, but Zaller defends them and regrets that no master model links the two versions (pp. 289–290).
- Weak statistical precision comes from multicollinearity (heavily overlapping predictors), small critical subgroups and subtle effects. These are testing problems, not problems of the RAS model itself (pp. 290–295).
- Applied to Reagan's character, the model shows strong partisan–awareness interaction and one nonmonotonic case (knowledgeability: support rises, then falls, with awareness), which Zaller thinks is best explained by a weaker countervailing message (pp. 296–299).
- Trust in government follows the model only where a change-inducing message with a clear ideological colour can be demonstrated or plausibly assumed (pp. 300–301).
- In Brazil, support for the regime rises with awareness and then falls. The F-scale (a personality test of authoritarian leanings) shows an important link to support only among the highly aware. A typology covers other regimes (pp. 301–308).
- Zaller reduces the model to two ideas: people hold poorly integrated considerations, and awareness interacts with predispositions. He calls it provisional (pp. 308–309).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening of this part: the RAIS wish (p. 281)
- Zaller says the most desirable model would be Receive–Accept–**Integrate**–Sample. In it, the outcome of earlier processing affects reception, acceptance and integration of later information (p. 281).
- He doubts that this elaboration will be fruitful until surveys can measure more than "likes" and "dislikes". That would include the volume and complexity of discrete cognitions, and where one cognitive structure's thoughts end and another's begin. He sees no such measurement in immediate prospect, and sees no reason for researchers to make these elaborations until the data needed to test them become available (p. 281).

### Models of the reception-acceptance process (pp. 281–295)
**Core idea.** Reception of persuasive messages rises with attentiveness, and so does capacity to resist uncongenial ones. The idea comes from Converse and McGuire (1960s) and underlies the Chapter 8 typology (p. 281). Zaller says it is "virtually never safe" to ignore individual differences in reception. The awareness effect on acceptance is less universal: it seems to wash out in high-intensity partisan elections (p. 281).

**Theoretical issues: graded attitude change (pp. 282–284).**
- In the RAS model attitude change is a change in the balance of positive and negative considerations, not a conversion. Zaller's analyses, however, only treat the dichotomous case: people either switch to the opposite position or stay (p. 282).
- 0–1 scoring has a technical merit. By Zaller's claim it is the only one-dimensional metric that does not treat "Don't know" as missing data. Dropping them would distort awareness effects, because "Don't know" respondents are less aware. Example: most early Vietnam attitude change was the conversion of no-opinion respondents into war supporters (p. 282).
- Strength or extremity of attitudes carries information too. Table 11.2 tries two measures on Central America (7-point scale, 1986 and 1987 NES; four awareness levels; "Don't know" omitted) (pp. 282–283).
  - **Method one**: mean difference. High-awareness hawks start at 5.26 and rise to 5.60 (difference 0.34). High-awareness doves rise from 2.27 to 3.19 (0.92). The pattern is "radically different" from the earlier analysis (pp. 282–283).
  - The problem is floor and ceiling effects. Aware hawks sit at 5–7 and have little room to move right. Aware doves sit at 1–2 and have much room (p. 283).
  - **Method two**, change relative to opportunity for change:
$$\text{Change}=\frac{\text{Time}_2-\text{Time}_1}{7-\text{Time}_1}$$
  Respondents at 7 in the first interview are dropped, since rightward movement is impossible for them (p. 284). Results are "somewhat closer" to the earlier analysis. For example, the high-awareness hawk cell is 0.18 and the high-awareness dove cell is 0.16 (pp. 283–284).
  - The measure takes ceilings into account but, Zaller says, has no more theoretical warrant than method one. Neither links the probability of switching to graduated change in the mix of considerations (p. 284).
- Footnote 7 explains the leftover anomaly (high-awareness doves). Two of 26 individuals moved from 1 to 7. Both then volunteered that Contra aid should be cut off. Zaller thinks a scale error is most likely, since the 1987 interview was by telephone (p. 284).
- **Suggested linkage (Zaller's argument).** Suppose communications so strongly favour the liberal side that even conservatives take it on a dichotomous item. Their support would be ambivalent. Intensified conservative messages would then tip many conservatives across the threshold. Liberals, less ambivalent, would switch less. This holds even if both groups formed identical new considerations. The most aware would switch most, because of heavier exposure and probably greater prior ambivalence (p. 284).
- *Intuition note:* Equal change in what people think can produce unequal switching, because people start at different distances from the 0/1 threshold. Zaller thinks an argument of this kind could be a starting point for future research on graded measures (p. 284).

**Theoretical issues: why awareness brings resistance (pp. 285–287).**
- Zaller proposes three mechanisms: inertial, countervalent, partisan (p. 285).
  - **Countervalent resistance** (the aware more often receive low-intensity countervalent flows) seems to have the strongest empirical support. The Vietnam and House election patterns are hard to explain without it (p. 285).
  - **Partisan resistance** (rejecting ideas seen as inconsistent with one's values) "likewise" seems firmly founded (p. 285).
  - **Inertial resistance** (a large store of initial considerations resists change) is open to question (p. 285).
- *Theory.* $C_1, D_1$ are countervalent and dominant considerations at the start; $C_2, D_2$ are those acquired later. The expected change in response probability involves the term
$$\frac{(C_1+C_2)}{(C_1+C_2)+(D_1+D_2)}.$$
  The author calls the whole expression Equation 7.1; its first term is garbled in the text layer [first term unreadable, p. 285; see Reading notes]. If inertial resistance were true, the derivative with respect to $C_1$ should be positive. It is a complicated function with no clear relation between $C_1$ and resistance. Footnote 9 reports numerical checks: $C_1$ is often associated with resistance, but never very strongly. The derivatives with respect to $C_2$ and $D_2$ (countervalent and partisan resistance) have the expected signs (p. 285).
- *Empirical support.* There was some in House elections, and perhaps in older sophisticated partisans' loyalty to Korea/Vietnam ideas (pp. 285–286).
- *Empirical problems.*
  - On Nixon's China initiative, Zaller searched for a nonmonotonic awareness effect among older conservatives and found none (p. 286).
  - Republican activists moved "breathtakingly" fast to wage and price controls in 1971 with scarcely any resistance (p. 286).
- These cases give Zaller the impression that inertial resistance is less important than the other two; it should be kept alive but treated "with reserve" (p. 286).
- **Conjecture.** Inertial resistance may need nourishment from current communications. Where it appeared (Korea, anti-Vietnam ideas) elite discourse backed the old attitudes. In the China case there was virtually no elite opposition. Elite validation might be insufficient to implant a new idea but adequate to awaken preexisting ones (pp. 286–287).
- Conclusion: partisan and countervalent resistance depend on elite discourse, and perhaps inertial resistance does too. "The capacity of citizens for autonomous resistance to dominant elite communications is very distinctly limited" (p. 287).
- *Intuition note:* On Zaller's conjecture, even inertial resistance may depend on some signal from elite discourse. Absent minimal elite validation, old feelings may be swamped (pp. 286–287).

**Theoretical issues: no independent thought (pp. 287–289).**
- The model implicitly sets
$$\text{Prob(Attitude change)}=\text{Prob(Reception)}\times\text{Prob(Acceptance}\mid\text{Reception)}+\text{Prob(Acceptance}\mid\text{No reception)},$$
with the last term constrained always to be zero (p. 287).
- Predispositions (from socialization, experience, interest, personality or reasoned choice) are internally motivated influences that give citizens some autonomy. Zaller rejects the charge that individuals are "passive receptacles". His claim is that citizens pick and choose, on the basis of predispositions, from the elite-sponsored alternatives they have been exposed to. To the extent they are attentive, they align with leaders or groups who share their predispositions and have more information (p. 287).
- *Vietnam example.* Educated liberals found it hard to turn against the war as liberal elites did. Zaller suggests this might simply be ambivalence brought about by inertial resistance, stronger among older liberals with large stores of anti-communist considerations. He thinks the model does no obvious injustice here (p. 288).
- *Less attentive people.* Zaller is less confident about them (p. 288). Their reports are unreliable and poorly correlated with predispositions, and sometimes "mistaken". In Ch. 5, inattentive respondents answered a bare Contra-aid question as if it were about government spending in general. This produced a wrong-way correlation with ideology, which disappeared when the Contras were described as anti-communist (p. 288).
- Predispositions probably also operate among the less aware, perhaps more directly. **Sniderman, Brody and Tetlock (1991)** report that group affect strongly influences the poorly educated, while ideology dominates among the better educated (p. 288).
- Opinion based on intergroup feelings need not break the model's dynamics. Cues and information might come from informal subcultures and spread by word of mouth, and could contradict elite channels. Backlashes may more often start in the public, but politicians sometimes stir them up. On elite lead versus follow, Zaller has no general answer; he sees reason to believe that in the cases studied elites "predominately led" rather than followed (p. 289).

**Methodological issues (pp. 289–295).**
- *One versus two messages.* The one-message model gives surprisingly accurate predictions under a range of conditions. Yet the cross-cutting liberal movements early in Vietnam and the inverse link between campaign intensity and aggregate defection require two-message modelling (pp. 289–290).
- Zaller suspects some readers will be frustrated, because two-message models are statistically cumbersome, impose data requirements that can rarely be met, and give no clear predictions. He answers that disparagement is unwarranted: they cannot readily be used deductively, but they support rigorous statistical modelling and explain why the one-message model fails, in a way that vindicates the reception-acceptance principles. The real criticism, Zaller says, is that the two versions are poorly connected; he could not formulate a master model (p. 290).
- *Robustness.* Some results are fragile: large standard errors and constrained parameters, sometimes on the two key effects (awareness on reception and on resistance) (p. 290).
  - Remark 1: contemporary statistical theory (Achen, 1983) stresses stability across datasets, substantive magnitude and theoretical pattern over single-test significance. By those criteria Zaller judges Chs. 7–10 strong (p. 290).
  - Remark 2: the imprecision is nonetheless real (p. 291). Three sources: (p. 291)
    1. *Interactive structure.* Awareness enters the one-message model (Eq. 7.7) four times: in the reception and acceptance functions of both baseline and change models. In the two-message model it enters the reception function for both messages, the acceptance function for both, and the recall function. Predispositions enter twice. This yields multicollinearity.
    2. *Small critical subgroups.* The resistance estimate depends on people both heavily exposed and predisposed to resist. These may be about half of the top 10% on awareness, i.e. about 5% of the sample.
    3. *Subtle effects.* Fig. 11.1 shows that dropping awareness from the acceptance function changes the Central America curves only a little (baseline coefficients fixed at Table 7.4 values, footnote 10). Even the reception effect can be invisible: Fig. 10.6 shows no hint of a positive awareness effect on reception for presidential defection and little evidence of strong reception effects for Senate defection, though other evidence shows large reception differences (pp. 291–292).
- None of these is a difficulty of the RAS model itself. The task is to test it so that expected effects can show up in noisy data (p. 292).
- **Recommendations** (pp. 292–295):
  - *Reliable measures.* Simple political-knowledge tests seem to discriminate better than other awareness measures. In every comparison Zaller could make, other measures gave weaker effects (pp. 292–293). Disciplines favour different measures: political scientists interest, communication specialists media exposure, sociologists education, psychologists knowledge (p. 293). Three- or four-item scales of middling difficulty discriminate poorly at the extremes; items should range from easy to hard (p. 293). Footnote 12 notes that very easy or hard items give misleadingly low item-to-total correlations; researchers should either ignore those correlations or use nonlinear measures such as item-to-total logit or probit coefficients (p. 293). The Central America results depended on an eight-item militarism scale, available by luck (p. 293).
  - *Survey size.* Zaller thinks the two-wave survey of 360 persons is probably near the minimum number needed. It had a 22-item awareness scale (alpha .87), and still the coefficients were barely significant after constraints (p. 293).
  - *Variance in information flow.* This is what matters most. Use cases with aggregate variation in flow, such as Vietnam over six years (Ch. 9) and 77 House campaigns (Ch. 10). Erbring et al. (1980), MacKuen (1984) and Bartels (1988) are further examples (p. 294).
  - *Modelling technology.* The four axioms do not require complicated nonlinear models, but given limited data, nonlinear models that capture the awareness–predisposition–information interaction are essential. Linear and interactive linear models would have done much worse. The operational forms are not claimed to be ideal (p. 294).
  - *Measuring information flow.* Zaller did not measure it precisely, even for Vietnam story counts. Message intensity also depends on audience interest, so the contributions of media attention and mass receptivity should eventually be separated. Meanwhile cross-case tests rely on judgment and are "somewhat ad hoc" (pp. 294–295).

### Parsimony as a value (pp. 295–309)
- Zaller opposes the "centrifugal urge" to build new concepts for each problem. For a given set of phenomena, the more concepts and distinctions used to explain them, the less well they are understood, all else equal (p. 295). He suggests three topics for the RAS model (p. 295).

**Evaluation of presidential character (pp. 295–299).**
- Stokes (1966) argued that assessments of candidates' personal qualities best explain interelection swings. Later work studies emotions or trait evaluations (integrity, competence), mostly their factor structure. It assumes all citizens attend enough to form structured reactions (pp. 295–296).
- Zaller's approach: treat character "information" as a campaign message, received and accepted according to awareness, ideology and partisanship (p. 296).
- Test: 1984 Reagan, via NES items. Traits: "Moral", "Knowledgeable" (coded 1 for "extremely"). Emotions: "proud" and a negative-emotion item (coded 1 for "yes"). The model is Equation 7.5, estimated by maximum likelihood (pp. 296–298).

| Coefficient (SE) | Knowledgeable | Moral | Pride | Fear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reception: awareness | 0.74 (.28) | 1.59 (.75) | 1.52 (.36) | 0.96 (.35) |
| Acceptance: awareness | −1.25 (.24) | −0.17 (.17) | −0.21 (.13) | 0.20 (.21) |
| Acceptance: party (−2 to +2) | −0.56 (.11) | −0.52 (.08) | −0.88 (.11) | 0.66 (.12) |

  Source: Table 11.3 (p. 298). Variables are coded liberal high; N is about 1,920–1,990.
- Results: assessments are highly partisan. Partisanship has less effect among the less aware and more among the highly informed (p. 297). Knowledgeability is the exception. Republican support peaks at moderate awareness, a strong nonmonotonic pattern "in this case but no others" (p. 297, Fig. 11.2).
- Zaller's explanation: a dominant media-crafted image of Reagan as commanding leader, and a less intense countervailing message in op-ed and news-analysis pieces (uninformed, uninterested in details). The latter reached only the most aware, who partly resisted. Awareness has an acceptance effect only in the knowledgeability model, consistent with the Ch. 7 claim that awareness proxies exposure to weaker countervalent flows (pp. 297–298).
- Message intensities cannot be measured directly, since there is no easy way to measure them directly, the validity of this explanation cannot be checked beyond "an appeal to the reader's intuition". The broader claim is that character opinion follows the same dynamics as other opinion (pp. 298–299).
- *Intuition note:* A group can look like the best-informed supporters and still be the first to doubt, because they alone hear the quiet criticism (p. 298).

**Trust in government (pp. 299–301).**
- Trust fell from about 1965. If it is a "mood", the answer would probably be no. If it is the spread of a "liberal alienation" message, the answer would be yes (p. 300).
- Citrin (1964–72 CPS data; Fig. 11.3) found that the decline followed the liberal-message pattern. Among liberals the relation of awareness to change is roughly linear and positive; among conservatives it is sharply nonmonotonic. Over other periods Citrin found little support, perhaps because changes were small (hard to see against measurement error) or because no flow had a clear ideological valence (p. 300).
- Zaller's key point: the model applies only where particular change-inducing messages with distinct ideological colorations can be demonstrated or plausibly assumed (p. 301).

**Popular support for authoritarian regimes (pp. 301–308).**
- With Geddes, Zaller applied the model to Brazil (early 1970s). Expectation: people in the broad middle of awareness show most support, since the most aware receive most propaganda but resist most (pp. 301–302).
- Finding: support for authoritarian policies rises with awareness until about the 90th percentile, then flattens and reverses (p. 302). There was no nonmonotonicity where the regime continued mainstream policies of the earlier democracy; it appeared only where it departed from them, e.g. press censorship (p. 302).
- **F-scale** (Fig. 11.4; 8-item support scale). Both F-scale and awareness raise support, in a non-straightforward way. Low-awareness persons do not reliably support the regime even when authoritarian by the F-scale, probably because they do not know the government's policies. Moderately aware persons support more but show little ideological consistency, responding uncritically to the dominant message. Only the highly aware show an important predisposition–support relation (pp. 302–303).
- Zaller calls these results an important qualification of *The Authoritarian Personality* theory. They suggest that exposure to propaganda may matter at least as much as personality, and further suggest that personality traits are just predispositions whose effect depends on interaction with awareness (p. 303).
- Education: better-educated Brazilians were more resistant given reception, perhaps through uncensored countervalent communications. Age had no discernible effect, perhaps because two effects cancelled (democratic norms versus preference for order) (p. 304).
- **Typology (Fig. 11.5).** Patterns should vary with regime effort to indoctrinate (high/low) and citizens' access to alternative values (some/none). Examples marked with question marks: Nazi Germany (high, some), Brazil (low, some), North Korea (high, none), U.S. mainstream issues (low, none; Ch. 6) (pp. 304–305). With high effort, even the least informed may show moderate support. With no access to alternatives, the decline among the most aware should be weaker or absent (p. 304). Footnote 17 says Gorbachev-era policies were a partial exception to the assumption that regimes espouse authoritarian policies (p. 304).
- For ineffective governments, more resistance is expected, giving gently nonmonotonic or flat/negative patterns. In Brazil, support collapsed first among the highly educated and involved when the economy faltered (footnote 18) (pp. 305–306).
- *Eastern bloc evidence* (suggestive, "smattering"):
  - Poland: support for martial law was greatest at low-to-middle education in 1984. By 1985, when many poorly educated gave no opinion, it was greatest at middle education (p. 306).
  - USSR (Roeder): support rose with education except at the highest levels (p. 306).
  - Soviet Afghan War: after increased government television coverage, approval rose 1984–86 among hesitant groups (young, uneducated, Central Asian). Opposition grew in Moscow, Leningrad, among graduates and party members (pp. 306–307). Siberia: "Don't know" fell from 59% to 31%, support rose from 24% to 51%, opposition unchanged. Moscow/Leningrad: opposition rose from 20% to 35% (p. 307).
  - Zaller sees a parallel to Vietnam 1964–66. The only evidence on the source of the anti-war message is that antiwar Soviets most often named word of mouth (p. 307).
- **Caveat.** Support built by one-sided communication can collapse once two-sided communication is allowed. In Brazil in 1974, opposition candidates were given limited media access shortly before the election and support for official candidates plummeted (p. 307).
- Changeable opinion is not insignificant: mass opinion may be superficial, but it is "directly attached to the levers of government" (pp. 307–308). Mobilization from opposition networks (Philippines, South Korea) and Romania 1989 are not clearly explained by the RAS model (p. 308).

### Concluding remarks (pp. 308–309)
- The framework involves four variables: aggregate message intensity; individual awareness; individual predispositions; and aggregate accidental factors that make considerations salient (p. 308).
- Zaller says the general model seems to work approximately equally well in a wide range of contexts, from elections to foreign wars to presidential character (p. 308).
- It reduces to two main ideas: (1) people on most issues have poorly integrated considerations, not "true attitudes"; (2) awareness–predisposition interaction is fundamental. The second was neglected in the 1960s and 1970s and is now getting more attention (p. 308–309).
- Standard studies still assume citizens react solely on their values and interests (p. 309).
- The model is approximate and provisional. It cannot handle integration of information into mental structures or the influence of past experience. The operational forms (Eqs. 7.7 and 9.8) are not claimed ideal (p. 309).
- Its value is synthesis across specialists who rarely communicate. The proper response to diversity is "to redouble the effort to find strong models" (p. 309).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Part A: the change-relative-to-opportunity score** $\frac{T_2-T_1}{7-T_1}$.
- Hawk A: 6 → 7. Raw difference 1. Score $=(7-6)/(7-6)=1/1=1$.
- Dove B: 2 → 4. Raw difference 2. Score $=(4-2)/(7-2)=2/5=0.4$.
- Respondent C at 7 → 7 is dropped (the denominator would be 0).
- Raw differences rank B above A, but A used all of the room available to him. The ceiling-adjusted score ranks A above B.

**Part B: ambivalence and switching.** Use a simple share rule: P(liberal answer) = L/(L+K), with L and K the numbers of liberal and conservative considerations (the form of the Eq. 7.1 term).
- Conservative: L=6, K=5. P = 6/11 = 0.545, a weak liberal lean.
- Liberal: L=10, K=1. P = 10/11 = 0.909.
- Both gain 4 conservative considerations. Conservative: 6/(6+9) = 6/15 = 0.40 (now below 0.5). Liberal: 10/(10+5) = 10/15 = 0.667 (still above 0.5).
- Identical new input moves the ambivalent conservative across the threshold but not the liberal.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dichotomous switching with latent ambivalence ↔ threshold/cascade models: the same push moves people who sit nearer the threshold (p. 284).
- [metaphor] Inertial resistance needing elite validation ↔ path dependence: old commitments constrain change only while something sustains them (pp. 286–287).
- [structural-analogy] Awareness as proxy for exposure to weak countervalent flows ↔ conditions for persuasion: acceptance depends on who is heard and believed (pp. 297–298).

## Questions this raises
- Can a single model with explicit simplifying assumptions link the one-message and two-message versions, as Zaller wishes (p. 290)?
- How could survey measures capture the number and organisation of considerations needed for an "Integrate" stage (p. 281)?
- If opinion built on one-sided communication collapses when two-sided flow is allowed, how stable is "mass opinion" elsewhere (p. 307)?
- When no message with an ideological colour can be identified (trust after 1972), what model should replace the RAS (pp. 300–301)?
