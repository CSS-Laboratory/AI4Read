# Evaluating the RAS model (part 1): restatement, "who leads whom?", and a critique of the four axioms

## L0 — One line
Zaller restates the RAS model, argues that elites plausibly lead mass opinion (though the model itself cannot prove it), and lists weaknesses in each axiom, among the most serious being that it has no theory of perception and no mechanism for integrating information.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Zaller asks how good the RAS model is. It does well on breadth and parsimony, but those were bought by simplification. He restates the argument. He then takes up the "who leads whom?" problem: elite communications are treated as exogenous (taken as given), defended with cases and an anticipation argument. The RAS model itself is agnostic about the source of messages, so a good fit does not, by itself, show anything about elite influence. Finally he criticises the axioms. The reception and resistance axioms ignore how prior ideas shape perception. The accessibility axiom leaves memory search undeveloped. The response axiom ignores attitude extremity and on-line processing. The model also has no mechanism for integrating information.

**Prerequisites:** the four RAS axioms (A1 Reception, A2 Resistance, A3 Accessibility, A4 Response) from earlier chapters; the idea of a survey "consideration"; basic survey vocabulary; an exogenous variable (taken as given, not explained within the model); on-line judgment (forming an evaluation as information arrives) versus memory-based judgment (building it from ideas recalled when asked).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Public opinion has two senses: citizens' real hopes and reactions, and the survey answers that mostly exist only when a pollster asks. RAS addresses both. Its strength is joining an information-diffusion theory (A1–A2) to a response theory (A3–A4) (p. 265).
- Resistance to persuasion depends heavily on countervalent communications (opposing messages); inertial resistance (resistance from stored information alone) looked least important in the one case where all three mechanisms could be compared (p. 267).
- Elite communications are treated as exogenous (taken as given, not explained by mass opinion). Zaller justifies this by case evidence and by the point that elites often anticipate future opinion rather than follow current polls (pp. 268–272).
- RAS is a generic diffusion model. It fits elite-driven and word-of-mouth diffusion alike, so a good fit does not, by itself, indicate anything about elite influence (pp. 272–273).
- A1 and A2 neglect that prior ideas shape what people perceive. Perception and acceptance may be one schema-driven step (driven by prior mental frameworks), and the lack of a perception theory is called "one of the greatest deficiencies" (pp. 274–276).
- A3 is perhaps the most defensible axiom; A4 is the most contestable (pp. 276–277).
- A4 omits attitude extremity. A test on Reagan ratings raises the possibility that extremity is a fairly simple function of the number and direction of accessible considerations (p. 278).
- Zaller defends memory-based responding against the on-line model, then concedes that people likely do "bounded on-line processing", updating specific evaluations as information arrives (p. 279). He concedes that RAS has no mechanism for integrating information (p. 280).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 265)
- Zaller asks whether public opinion exists with no pollster present. If it means citizens' hopes, fears and reactions, yes. If it means citizens privately thinking "I strongly approve of the way George Bush is doing his job", then most measured opinion exists only when a pollster is present (p. 265).
- RAS concerns both: the "considerations" formed from political communications, and how these are turned into survey responses (p. 265).
- On empirical breadth and parsimony the model looks good. Its main feature is the union of information diffusion through a differentially attentive population (A1, A2) with a theory of turning information into survey responses (A3, A4). The model "has the vice of its virtues": breadth and parsimony cost simplification and omission (p. 265).
- The chapter plan is a recapitulation, then weaknesses, then improvements, then an assessment of value (p. 266).

### The basic argument restated (pp. 266–267)
- Campaigns carry several conflicting message streams. Higher habitual political awareness makes reception more likely. It also makes resistance to value- or party-inconsistent information more likely, under certain circumstances (p. 266).
- Internalised communications become considerations, meaning reasons for taking a side. Survey answers rest on the considerations most salient at the moment. Instability arises because the top-of-the-head contents vary stochastically (p. 266).
- Responses are not purely random. Message exposure and selective acceptance fix a systematic range within which a person vacillates. Which idea is on top also depends on news that day, question wording or order, and recent experiences (p. 266).
- Attitude change means a change in long-term response probabilities. It comes from a changed mix of ideas, not a sudden conversion (p. 266).
- A message's effect depends on the ideas already in the mind and on concurrent opposing ideas. A person with a large inertial mass of stored information, or with countervalent sources, is unlikely to be much moved by any single dominant-campaign message. A person with little prior information and little access to alternative flows is likely to be moved a lot (pp. 266–267).
- Values regulate acquisition of countervalent and inertial considerations and internalisation of dominant messages. Partisan resistance tends to be stronger among the highly aware, who in many situations are more likely to hold the cueing messages needed to see an issue's implications for their values (p. 267).
- Who is most persuadable varies with the situation (p. 267):
  - the least aware, when information flow is very intense (presidential elections);
  - the moderately aware, when messages are moderately intense and partisan orientations are active (House elections, presidential popularity, the later Vietnam War);
  - the most aware, when there is little basis for partisan or ideological resistance (early 1984 primaries) or little access to countervalent information (early Vietnam War).
- These differences come from differences in exposure, above all two-sided flows and the relative intensity of opposing messages, "not" from differences in individual psychology across contexts (p. 267).
- Resistance depends very heavily on countervalent communications (opposing information or cues from oppositional elites). Inertial resistance seemed the least important of the three mechanisms in the only case that allowed all three to be examined, electoral attitudes (p. 267).

*Intuition note.* The same people can look easy or hard to persuade depending on what messages surround them. The variation comes from the information environment. Awareness matters because it makes a person more likely to receive messages and, in many situations, more likely to have the cues needed to resist them.

### Who leads whom? (pp. 268–274)
- **The problem.** A leader at the head of a crowd may be following it (the revolutionary-in-the-café story). Zaller treats communication intensities as exogenous. His working assumption is that elite communications shape mass opinion, not vice versa. One check: in the House-election analysis he controlled for incumbents' prior margins and found essentially no reason for concern (pp. 268, 268 n. 2).
- **Ground 1: in several cases the elite stimulus was clearly mainly or wholly exogenous.**
  - *Iran-Contra.* Reagan's popularity was high and he had no serious problems on the horizon. Congressional attacks thus came in spite of his standing, not because it was low (p. 268).
  - *Brown v. Board.* Inside accounts say the Court saw the public as largely hostile. The NAACP had minimal political clout. The decision appears primarily motivated by the justices' moral convictions (p. 268).
  - *Nuclear freeze.* Almost certainly, many politicians joined only because the freeze looked popular. Its impetus, however, came from arms-control professionals who were losing internal debates and wanted public support (p. 269).
  - *Late-1982 economic optimism.* This came from hopeful statements by Reagan and business leaders and from technical reports, not from prior public belief (p. 269).
  - *Persian Gulf War.* Few Americans knew Kuwait existed in August 1990, yet within two weeks support for using troops topped 80 percent. There was no communist threat, and most expected heavy casualties. No polls were apparently taken between the invasion and Bush's declaration, so there is no empirical basis for ruling out a public groundswell. Zaller thinks few informed observers are likely to take it seriously (p. 269).
  - The general point is that the direction of influence is hard to settle in the abstract, but plausible judgments are often possible case by case (p. 269).
- **Ground 2: anticipation (V. O. Key).** Even when politicians act on perceived public pressure, it is often the anticipation of that pressure, not actual pressure, that matters. On Key's view, elites respond to the opinion an opponent might call into existence at the next election, not to opinion in current polls. Politicians who slavishly follow existing opinion tend to fare poorly (p. 270).
  - *Nixon's 1971 wage-price controls.* Calculated to head off election-year criticism, so Nixon was responding. He was also leading, since his speech moved opinion towards support for controls (p. 270).
  - *Johnson and Vietnam.* Johnson knew the military difficulties and knew there was no groundswell for sending troops. He feared the charge of "losing Vietnam", which could doom the Great Society and revive McCarthyism. He followed opinion by anticipating a harsh retrospective judgment. He led it by having to mobilise support for unpopular policies (p. 270).
  - *Antiwar politicians.* Mainstream opposition began in 1966, at the high tide of support for the war. Fulbright, from Arkansas, opposed it against his political staff's advice and on the advice of his foreign-policy staff. Tip O'Neill's opposition rested on what military authorities privately told him, despite his belief that opposition was political suicide (pp. 270–271).
  - *Media.* Reporting usually reflects what sources say. Mohr's account (p. 271) is that the Vietnam debate was not essentially a quarrel between press and officials but one between factions inside the U.S. Mission. Sources were mostly in the military, CIA and State Department (p. 271).
  - *Tet.* Perhaps the most important exception: the press painted an apparent U.S. victory as a defeat. This seems to reflect the press's surprise at the Viet Cong's credible offensive, not public opinion, which still largely supported the war (p. 271).
- **Elite responsiveness is real.** Zaller says marginal drops in public support probably made it incrementally safer for predisposed politicians to go public, though he admits it is hard to see how anyone could really be sure. Robert Kennedy's late entry after McCarthy's New Hampshire showing may be an example. The elite press was carrying antiwar information in 1965–66, before declining support made it safe (p. 272).
- **Three arguments (put as possibilities) that such dynamics are consistent with elite leadership** (p. 272):
  1. Weakening support gives elites discretion, and those predisposed by ideology or analysis use it. Elites act autonomously but only once it is safe.
  2. A differentially autonomous elite: responsive politicians versus autonomous experts, officers and officials who use the media to create pressure that politicians then accede to.
  3. A weak form (Zaller: "much too weak"): antiwar elites merely responded to the public, and their messages then diffused by reception–acceptance.
- **What the model can and cannot show.** Strictly, the modelling cannot support even the weak argument for elite influence. RAS is "entirely agnostic" about the sources of communications. The model is consistent with elites having no influence at all and communications spreading by personal contact "like germs". It applies so long as awareness is positively associated with reception and negatively with acceptance. This may be part of why it fits well (pp. 272–273).
- RAS is a generic diffusion model. Hypothetically it could describe fashions, beliefs or diseases. Example (Zaller's, put as a possibility): young adults might have high exposure but resist, the old might be vulnerable but reclusive, so the middle-aged might be hit hardest. Raw CDC data suggest AIDS incidence was, at least for a time, nonmonotonic in age (p. 273).
- A close fit therefore does not, by itself, indicate anything about the extent of elite influence. Reasons to think elite influence is what diffuses must come independently of the model (Zaller points to pp. 43–44 of the book, outside this unit). Footnote 5 notes that the classic personal-influence studies give no mechanism for interpersonal communication to change mass opinion independently of elites (p. 273).
- **Conclusion.** The full account has elites with some autonomous ideas, split by party, watching the public, trying to lead and follow at once. The question is how much, which elites, and under what circumstances. The aim was to show what the formal models do and do not prove, and to make substantial autonomous elite leadership plausible (pp. 273–274).

*Intuition note.* RAS describes how a message spreads and is resisted, not who wrote it. So fitting the diffusion pattern does not, by itself, show that elites started the message. That claim needs separate evidence, which Zaller says he has supplied (here and in Chapter 2).

### Critical review of the axioms (pp. 274–280)

**A1, Reception (p. 274).**
- Zaller says A1 seems "initially unexceptionable", but asks what is actually received. Prior knowledge may change what is received, in at least two ways (p. 274):
  - *Comprehension.* More people learned of Oliver North's sentencing than why Speaker Jim Wright resigned, despite Wright's longer and fuller coverage. One poll suggested almost as many knew Fawn Hall's name as knew why Wright resigned (p. 274).
  - *Perception* (more speculative). The B2 maiden flight may be seen as pro-B2 by some and as "an obviously useless batmobile" by others (p. 274).
- In both cases differences stem from prior ideas or schemata. RAS makes no allowance for this (p. 274).

**A2, Resistance (pp. 274–276).**
- A2 says people resist only to the extent they have an appropriate cueing message, whose origin is at least in principle separate from the persuasive message. It follows Converse's "what goes with what" (p. 274).
- Zaller has no direct evidence that possession of contextual information explains the greater resistance of the aware. His support is indirect (pp. 275–276):
  - past research showing that source cues strongly affect responses;
  - the Vietnam War: under elite consensus, awareness meant more support and no resistance among the most aware. After elite disagreement in 1966, sophisticated liberals resisted prowar messages and accepted antiwar ones;
  - Chapter 6 cases where awareness did or did not go with polarisation depending on elite cues, notably recognition of Communist China in 1972, where awareness was not tied to resistance;
  - contested elections, the one type of case where the whole public, not only the aware, relies on partisan cues because all citizens are about equally partisan.
- Zaller's explanation for the pattern is that cues are subtle on typical partisan issues (job guarantees), obvious in partisan elections, and absent on mainstream issues. He sees no alternative explanation for the moment. It is therefore reasonable to accept that, "in many but not all situations", awareness brings knowledge of what goes with what (p. 276).
- **Conceptual criticism.** Someone who has already accepted dovish stories may see the B2 as a batmobile directly, so perception and evaluation form one schema-driven step. Zaller grants it may reasonably be objected that RAS's sharp separation of reception from acceptance is a purely theoretical distinction (p. 276).
- Zaller's alternative is a theory where prior cued communications affect both the chance of receiving a message and how it is perceived. Perception is theory-laden, so grafting a perception theory onto RAS would be valuable. Its absence is "one of the greatest deficiencies" of the model, in his opinion (p. 276).

**A3, Accessibility (pp. 276–277).**
- A3 is perhaps the most defensible. Recent or frequent use aids recall, which experimental psychology and political-attitude research support (p. 276).
- There is more to accessibility than recency. People recall different considerations when asked for reasons than when asked for feelings (Wilson et al.; Millar and Tesser). Mood, social context and other recent activity probably matter too (pp. 276–277).
- Zaller finessed this by assuming much simple randomness. He judges that acceptable as an initial strategy. Future work on memory search might explain more of the instability over time (p. 277).
- A3 lets one idea raise the accessibility of related ideas, but RAS does not say what makes ideas related. Relatedness might depend less on logic or language than on elite cues and the environment. A3 needs less tampering than the other axioms (p. 277).

**A4, Response (pp. 277–279).**
- A4 is "the most contestable". It has no place for attitude strength or extremity. Zaller says the binary approach is not inherently wrong, since opinion often enters politics as a single percentage and U.S. elections aggregate 0–1 choices (p. 277).
- Proposal: extremity varies with the net directional thrust of considerations and their total number. "A preponderance of mutually consistent considerations" would produce extreme reports (p. 277).
- Test, using the 1984 NES: likes and dislikes about Reagan as a proxy for salient considerations, and the 100-point thermometer as a proxy for extremity (p. 278).

**Table 11.1** (printed headings; the last two intervals overlap at 75):

| Reagan rating | 0–25 | 26–35 | 36–45 | 46–55 | 56–65 | 66–75 | 75–100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Avg. net likes/dislikes | −2.75 | −2.09 | −1.78 | −0.91 | −0.52 | 0.39 | 1.90 |
| Avg. sum likes/dislikes | 3.02 | 2.97 | 2.68 | 1.76 | 2.69 | 2.98 | 3.14 |
| N | 287 | 123 | 146 | 234 | 245 | 419 | 757 |

- Net rises steadily with warmth. The sum is nonmonotonic (lowest, 1.76, at 46–55; higher at both ends). Those with the most likes and dislikes rated Reagan most extremely. This suggests that extremity may be a fairly simple function of the number and direction of accessible considerations (p. 278).
- **On-line objection.** Researchers (Hastie and Park; Lodge, McGraw and Stroh; McGraw and Pinney) say judgments are formed on-line as information arrives and then simply retrieved. This conflicts with A4's memory-based construction (p. 278).
- **Zaller's reply.**
  - Hastie and Park's evidence is mostly nonpolitical, especially personality evaluation, which people do effortlessly. Political controversies may be the opposite (pp. 278–279).
  - Hastie and Park themselves specify that memory-based judgment occurs when people meet unimportant information and are then unexpectedly asked for an opinion. Zaller suggests this is the norm in politics, since issues are too numerous, multidimensional and obscure for on-line processing (p. 279).
  - The strongest evidence is chance variation over time and effects of question order, which the on-line logic would make irrelevant. Even attitudes about well-known politicians seem to depend on top-of-the-head ideas (Wilson, Kraft and Dunn). The on-line model is thus a variant of the old "true attitude" view (p. 279).
- **Concession.** A4 is only a rough first approximation. People likely do "bounded on-line processing": they update particular evaluations but may fail to integrate them into a fresh global attitude unless an unusual event such as a survey calls for it. For example, they update judgments about welfare families and about homeless needs, but not their global attitude to welfare. Asked on a survey for a global judgment, they have no up-to-date global evaluation to retrieve and must judge on the spot from the updated considerations that come to mind. A4 is thus "underspecified" rather than wrong (p. 279).

**Integration (p. 280).**
- A general weakness: RAS has no mechanism for integrating information. Once internalised, each item is another atomised consideration in a mental "bin" (p. 280).
- This is a drastic simplification. Total disconnectedness may hold for some segments of the public (Luskin), but some people, and probably most, build complex cognitions that grow with new information. That might raise their chronic accessibility and play a role in perceiving and scrutinising messages (p. 280).
- The model also has no provision for multiple reception of the same message. Re-reception might create more considerations, raise salience, or aid integration. Zaller says the answer is probably yes to all of these (p. 280).
- He left these out because there was no pressing need; the current machinery explains much of the explainable variance. Over the longer run, a fifth axiom might say that thought builds integrated schemata. These might neutralise opposing, less developed considerations and so reduce response instability, and might shape processing of new communications (p. 280).
- Caution: do not take integrated structures too far. Most political ideas are not integrated, opposing ideas coexist, and there is a large element of chance in which idea comes to the top of the head (p. 280).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take the diffusion point on p. 273. Three awareness groups, with invented numbers. Let "message internalised" = P(receive) × P(accept | received), a simple product we assume for illustration.

| Group | P(receive) | P(accept given received) | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.2 | 0.9 | 0.2 × 0.9 = 0.18 |
| Middle | 0.6 | 0.5 | 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.30 |
| High | 0.9 | 0.1 | 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.09 |

Reception rises with awareness and acceptance falls, so the middle group is most affected (0.30 > 0.18 > 0.09). The same numbers hold whether the message travels via elite media or by word of mouth. A good fit to such a pattern therefore cannot tell us who originated the message.

## Concepts
- [[receive-accept-sample-ras-model]] — Zaller's model: reception, acceptance, accessibility, averaging response; here also a "generic diffusion model" (pp. 265, 273)
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — habitual attention to politics; raises reception and, given cues, resistance (p. 266)
- [[elite-opinion-leadership]] (new) — elite communications shaping mass opinion; a working assumption, defended case by case (p. 268)
- [[anticipatory-influence]] (new) — elites respond to opinion an opponent might create later, not current polls (Key) (p. 270)
- [[cueing-message]] (new; related to [[heuristics-cues]] but here contextual "what goes with what" information, after Converse; see also [[belief-system-constraint]]) (p. 274)
- [[partisan-resistance]] (new) — rejecting messages that conflict with one's party or ideology; tends to be stronger among the aware (p. 267)
- [[countervalent-communication]] (new) — opposing information or oppositional-elite cues that enable resistance (p. 267)
- [[inertial-resistance]] (new) — resistance from stored information alone; seemed the least important of three mechanisms in electoral attitudes (p. 267)
- [[on-line-vs-memory-based-judgment]] (new) — evaluation updated at encounter versus built from accessible ideas at response; Zaller defends memory-based judgment as a rough first approximation but allows bounded on-line processing (pp. 278–279)
- [[attitude-extremity]] (new) — Zaller suggests it varies with net direction and number of salient considerations (p. 277)
- [[schema-driven-perception]] (new) — prior ideas shaping what one notices and how one reads it (p. 276)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] RAS reception × resistance ↔ epidemiology: exposure × susceptibility giving nonmonotonic incidence by age — same formal shape, as Zaller notes (p. 273).
- [structural-analogy] Cueing messages and awareness ↔ Lupia–McCubbins conditions for persuasion: how listeners judge sources.
- [metaphor] Top-of-the-head responding ↔ the on-line vs memory-based debate in social psychology.
- [structural-analogy] Elites anticipating a challenger's mobilisation ↔ principal–agent logic: agents act on the principal's likely future judgment.

## Questions this raises
- What independent evidence could separate elite-originated messages from word-of-mouth diffusion?
- How would a perception theory change A1–A2 predictions about who resists?
- Does extremity follow net and sum of considerations for issues as well as for politicians' ratings?
- How much of response instability would better models of memory search explain?
