# The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media (McCombs & Shaw, 1972)

## L0 — One line
In a small 1968 Chapel Hill study, the issues voters not yet definitely decided called important matched the issues the news media stressed (r ≈ +.97), which the authors read as supporting, but not proving, a media agenda-setting function.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The authors ask whether media set the "agenda" (which issues voters see as important), even if media change attitudes little. They interviewed 100 Chapel Hill voters not yet definitely decided in the 1968 presidential campaign. They content-analysed the nine news media these voters used. Media emphasis on issues correlated very strongly with voters' emphasis (+.967 for major, i.e. prominent, items; +.979 for minor). Voters' judgments seemed to follow all the news, not just news about a favoured candidate. This is better explained by agenda-setting than by selective perception (reading mainly what favours one's own side). The authors say this does not prove the hypothesis. It is a whole-group test; later work must go to the individual level.

**Prerequisites:** correlation coefficient; percentages and tables; the idea of content analysis (counting coded categories in media texts).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Hypothesis:** media may have little effect on the direction or intensity of attitudes, but it is hypothesized that they set the campaign agenda by influencing the *salience* of issues, i.e. how important issues seem (p. 177).
- **Design:** voters' answers to "what are the key issues?" were matched with a content analysis of the media those voters used, split into "major" (prominent) and "minor" (smaller) items (pp. 177–179).
- **Main result:** correlations between media emphasis and voters' issue emphasis were +.967 (major items) and +.979 (minor items) (pp. 180–181).
- **Test against selective perception:** selective perception means attending mainly to material favouring one's own party. Voters usually matched total news better than news about their own party. This was true in 18 of 24 comparisons (p. 182).
- **Media agree but imperfectly:** agreement tends to be highest among like media and, the authors suggest, is strongest on major items. The picture of the campaign shown in the media (the "pseudo-environment") is an imperfect copy of it (pp. 183–184).
- **Cautious inference:** the correlations do not prove agenda-setting. The authors argue that the "spurious" and "media follow audience" explanations are less plausible (pp. 184–185).
- **Side study:** high "salience of affect" (how far a voter's talk about an issue is pro/con rather than factual) went with less media use on one's most important issue, especially among the politically interested (pp. 186–187).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 176–177)
- Candidates now reach voters mainly through mass media. For many people the media are the only contact with politics (p. 176).
- The authors say evidence that media deeply change attitudes in a campaign is "far from conclusive". Evidence that voters *learn* from campaign information is much stronger (p. 176).
- **Berelson (cited):** many "hear" but few "listen". The people with the most media exposure are most likely to know where candidates stand (p. 177). Trenaman and McQuail found the same in the 1959 English election (p. 177).
- **Lang and Lang (cited):** the media "force attention to certain issues" and suggest what people should think about (p. 177).
- **Cohen (cited):** the press is "stunningly successful in telling its readers what to think about" (p. 177).
- **Hypothesis (authors):** media may have little influence on the *direction or intensity* of attitudes, but they set the campaign agenda by influencing the *salience* of attitudes toward issues (p. 177).
- **Intuition note:** the claim is about which topics seem important, not about what people conclude on them. Media could be weak at persuasion and still strong at steering attention.

### Method (pp. 177–179)
- **Sample:** respondents were drawn randomly from registered-voter lists in five Chapel Hill precincts described as representative. One community was chosen to control regional differences and variation in media performance (p. 177–178).
- **Interviews:** 100 interviews, 18 Sept–6 Oct 1968. A filter question kept only voters not yet definitely decided, presumably those most open to campaign information (p. 178).
- **Question:** adapted from Trenaman and McQuail. It asked about the two or three main things government should focus on, "regardless of what politicians say" (p. 178, n. 9).
- **Media sampled:** a spring 1968 pretest found nine sources supplied almost all the political information. They were Durham Morning Herald, Durham Sun, Raleigh News and Observer, Raleigh Times, New York Times, Time, Newsweek, and NBC and CBS evening news (p. 178).
- **Coding:** voter answers and media content (12 Sept–6 Oct) were coded into 15 categories. Media items were split into "major" and "minor" (p. 178). Intercoder reliability was above .90 (p. 178, n. 10).
- **"Major" items:**
  - TV: a story of 45 seconds or more, or one of the three lead stories.
  - Newspapers: a lead front-page story, or a story under a three-column headline with at least one-third (minimum five paragraphs) on political news.
  - News magazines: a story over one column, or the lead item of the news section.
  - Editorial pages: the lead editorial, or items at least one-third (five paragraphs) on the campaign.
  - "Minor" items are political stories smaller in space, time or display (pp. 178–179).

### Findings (pp. 179–184)
**What the media carried (Tables 1–2).**
- Table 1 covers 558 major items. Much coverage was campaign analysis, not issues: 28% overall. For Wallace, Humphrey and Nixon it was 35%, 30% and 25%. This "may give pause" to those who think campaign news is mainly about issues (pp. 179–180).
- Among major items about each candidate, Agnew apparently spent more time on Humphrey (22%) than Nixon did (11%) (p. 180). Minor-item emphasis closely paralleled major-item emphasis (p. 180).
- Table 2, by party: Humphrey/Muskie emphasised foreign policy (64% total) far more than Nixon/Agnew (38%) or Wallace/Lemay (26%). Wallace/Lemay emphasised law and order (52% total), against 32% for Nixon/Agnew and 23% for Humphrey/Muskie (p. 180).

**Main result.**
- Voters were asked to judge issues independently of politicians. The authors say the media appear to have exerted considerable impact on voters' judgments: the correlation between media major-item emphasis and voters' judgments was +.967. For minor items it was +.979 (pp. 180–181).
$$r_{\text{major}} = +.967, \qquad r_{\text{minor}} = +.979$$
- Although the candidates stressed very different issues, voters' judgments seem to reflect the *composite* of the media coverage. The authors infer that voters pay some attention to all political news (p. 181).
- **Intuition note:** the correlation compares two sets of issue emphasis, the media's and the voters'. A value near 1 means issues the media stressed most were, broadly, the ones voters named most. By itself it does not show which caused which.

**Agenda-setting vs selective perception (Table 3).**
- The composite could hide individual differences. The 45 respondents who leaned (without committing) to Humphrey (D), Nixon (R) or Wallace (W) were analysed separately for four media (p. 181).
- Each group's issue frequencies were correlated with (a) all news and (b) news about their own party. This was done for major and minor items. Example: for Democrats and the New York Times, r = .89 with all major news and .79 with Democratic-oriented news only (p. 182).
- **Logic of the test:**
  - If voters read selectively, correlations with own-party news should be strongest.
  - If voters attend to all news, correlations with total media content should be strongest. That would support agenda-setting.
- **Result:** 18 of 24 comparisons favoured all news over own-party news. This is "better explained" by agenda-setting than by selective perception (p. 182).

**Variation across media (Tables 4–5).**
- Table 4 covers all respondents. Voter–media correlations vary widely. Major items: New York Times .96, Raleigh News and Observer .91, Durham Morning Herald .94, but Newsweek and Time only .30. CBS had .63 (p. 183).
- The authors say the Table 5 correlations (media with media) might be interpreted as reliability coefficients: how far media agree on what matters. To the extent they are below 1, the media "pseudo-environment" is an imperfect representation of the 1968 campaign (p. 183).
- **Two factors reducing consensus:**
  - Media differ in form. Newspapers are daily with much space, TV is daily with severe time limits, and newsmagazines are weekly and less timely. The highest correlations tend to be among like media and the lowest between different media (p. 184).
  - Media have points of view. Still, high correlations (especially among like media, and on major items) suggest shared news values. The authors say wire services "doubtless" influence these norms. Minor items leave more room for interpretation; a newspaper uses only about 15% of available material (p. 184).
- **Conclusion:** the political world is reproduced imperfectly by each medium. Voters nonetheless share the media's composite definition of importance, which "strongly suggests" agenda-setting (p. 184).

### Discussion (pp. 184–187)
- **Limits (authors):** the correlations do not prove agenda-setting. They fit the conditions that must hold if it occurs. The unit of analysis is the aggregate (Chapel Hill voters as a group against several media). The authors say later research must move from this societal level to matching individual attitudes with individual media use (p. 184–185).
- **Refinements:** efforts were made to match respondent attitudes only with media actually used by Chapel Hill voters. The test also contrasted agenda-setting with selective perception (p. 185).
- **Alternative 1, spurious correlation (both follow the same events):** this assumes voters have other ways to observe politics. The authors reject that. Few see candidates, and interpersonal information is mostly relayed from media news. Media are the best and only easily available approximation of political reality for most people (p. 185).
- **Alternative 2, media matched to audience interests:** many studies find journalists' and audiences' news values diverge sharply, so a near-perfect fit would be remarkable. Five of the nine media are national, and none of the other four originates in Chapel Hill. The authors find it likelier that media prevailed in major coverage (p. 185, incl. n. 13).

**Psychological side study: salience of affect (pp. 185–187).**
- Respondents' open-ended answers were coded as "all affect", "affect dominant", "some affect but not dominant", or "no affect at all". Affect means a pro/con, liking/disliking orientation. Coder reliability was above .90 (p. 186, nn. 15–16).
- **Hypothesis:** salience of affect (a "cognitive style") will *index* differences in communication behaviour. The authors avoid causal language here (p. 186).
- They argue it sits closer to the end of *The American Voter*'s "funnel" than education or political interest, which are further back. It was not significantly correlated with those two (p. 186).
- **Table 6 (media use on one's most important issue, past 24 hours):**

| Medium | Low interest, high affect (N=40) | Low interest, low affect (N=17) | High interest, high affect (N=25) | High interest, low affect (N=12) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Newspapers | 27.5% | 35.4% | 36.0% | 58.3% |
| TV | 15.0% | 17.7% | 20.0% | 41.7% |

  Radio, news magazines and talk are in Table 6 on p. 186.
- Among the politically interested, 36% of high-affect respondents recalled reading about their top issue in a newspaper, against 58.3% of low-affect respondents (p. 187). High affect "tends to block" media use for further information. Strictly, high-affect respondents "do not recall" recent information (p. 187).
- No statistics were run because some cells are small. The authors say the pattern is consistent across all media (p. 186, n. 18).
- **Closing claim:** future work must consider both psychological and sociological variables. Agenda-setting looks useful for studying political consensus (p. 187).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three issues; percent of news emphasis vs. percent of voters naming each as important.

| Issue | All-news emphasis | Own-party news emphasis | Voters |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 20 | 60 |
| B | 30 | 50 | 25 |
| C | 20 | 30 | 15 |

Each column has mean 33.33.
- **Deviations:** all-news (16.67, −3.33, −13.33); voters (26.67, −8.33, −18.33).
- **Products:** 444.4 + 27.8 + 244.4 = 716.7.
- **Sums of squares:** all-news = 277.8 + 11.1 + 177.8 = 466.7; voters = 711.1 + 69.4 + 336.1 = 1116.7.
- $r = 716.7/\sqrt{466.7 \times 1116.7} = 716.7/721.9 \approx +.99$.
- **Own-party news:** deviations (−13.33, 16.67, −3.33). Products: −355.6 − 138.9 + 61.1 = −433.3. Own-party sum of squares is 466.7. $r = -433.3/721.9 \approx -.60$.

All-news matches voters better than own-party news does. This is the pattern McCombs and Shaw read as agenda-setting. With only three issues, the correlation is fragile.

## Concepts
- [[agenda-setting]] — media emphasis shapes the salience of issues for the public; a hypothesis here, not proved (pp. 177, 184)
- [[limited-effects-paradigm]] — the paper's background: evidence that media change attitudes is "far from conclusive" (p. 176)
- [[selective-exposure]] — the authors' term is "selective perception": voters attend mainly to material favouring their own party (p. 182)
- [[pseudo-environment]] — the picture of the campaign reflected in media, an imperfect copy of the real campaign (p. 183)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Agenda-setting as salience transfer ↔ framing: media emphasis changes which considerations people use — both concern emphasis, but this paper tests only issue salience.
- [metaphor] "Pseudo-environment" ↔ the idea that people act on mediated pictures of reality — the paper uses the term in passing.
- [structural-analogy] Correlating two sets of issue emphasis ↔ agreement measures in other fields — a similar idea (compare two profiles), not tied to these data.

## Questions this raises
- With only aggregate data, how could one show that media emphasis *causes* voter salience rather than the reverse?
- Would a sample of undecided voters in one community generalise to committed voters or other places?
- Why are the Newsweek and Time correlations with voters so low (.30 on major items) while the New York Times is so high?
- Does "salience of affect" really block media use, or do high-affect voters simply not recall it?
