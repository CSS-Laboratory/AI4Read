# Norms, Surprises, and Causes

## L0 — One line
System 1 automatically keeps a model of what is normal and instantly builds causal and intentional stories, and we are prone to apply causal thinking where statistical thinking is needed.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how System 1 decides what is normal and why we see causes so easily. Answer: System 1 links ideas that occur together. Surprise shows where this model of the world is violated. A single event can make a repeat feel normal. System 1 also builds causal stories at once, even when the "explanation" is empty. Michotte and Heider–Simmel suggest that we perceive physical and intentional causality directly, and that infants show signs of this too. This matters because System 1 cannot reason statistically; System 2 can learn to, but few people are trained. The author uses agent metaphors for the two systems because they fit how we think about causes.

**Prerequisites:** The earlier System 1 / System 2 distinction and the idea of associative activation (ideas that co-occur become linked); no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- System 1's main job is to maintain a model of what is normal in your world, built from associations among events that co-occur (pdf 72).
- Surprise is the most sensitive sign of what we expect. Active expectations are conscious; passive ones are events that are normal but not probable enough to wait for (pdf 72).
- One incident can make a recurrence feel normal, even when it is less probable. The soup example shows that events seem normal because they recall the first episode (pdf 73).
- Violations of norms are detected extremely fast, and categories carry norms for typical values and for plausible range (pdf 74–75).
- Causal connections are found automatically; in the Fred example System 2 accepted the offered cause. An explanation that fits two opposite outcomes explains nothing (pdf 75–76).
- Michotte and Heider–Simmel suggest we perceive physical and intentional causality directly, and infant evidence suggests this is present early (pdf 76–77).
- Bloom claims that our inborn readiness to separate physical and intentional causality explains the near universality of religious belief (pdf 77).
- People are prone to apply causal intuition wrongly where statistical reasoning is needed, and System 1 lacks that mode (pdf 77–78).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening.** The author describes the mind as a powerful associative computer. Spreading activation is automatic, but System 2 can somewhat control memory search and can set up the detection of events (pdf 72).

**Assessing Normality (pdf 72–75)**
- System 1 maintains and updates a model of your personal world, representing what is normal. The links join circumstances, events, actions and outcomes that co-occur regularly. The model shapes how you interpret the present and what you expect (pdf 72).
- Two kinds of surprise. *Active* expectation: you consciously wait for something (the child's return, a familiar voice). Not occurring is surprising. *Passive* expectation: events that are normal but not probable enough to be actively expected. Their occurrence is not surprising (pdf 72).
- **Jon example.** The author and his wife met a psychologist acquaintance, Jon, at a 40-room island resort. Two weeks later Jon sat next to them in a London theater. They felt it was the more remarkable coincidence, yet they were less surprised. Jon had become "the psychologist who shows up when we travel abroad." System 2 knew this was ludicrous, but System 1 made it seem almost normal. Meeting any other acquaintance there would have been more surprising, although by probability Jon was less likely than any one of hundreds of acquaintances (pdf 72–73).
- **Burning cars.** The couple saw a car on fire on their weekly New York–Princeton drive, and another burning on the same stretch a week later. They were less surprised the second time. Because the circumstances recurred, the second incident was enough to create an *active* expectation. They stayed prepared for months, perhaps years, but never saw another (pdf 73).
- **Norm theory (Kahneman and Miller).** In their restaurant example a guest winces at the soup. Many events then seem more normal (a startle at the waiter's touch, another guest's stifled cry). The reason is not necessarily that they confirm advance expectations. They recruit the original episode from memory and are interpreted together with it (pdf 73–74). A second abnormal event retrieves the first, and both fit a pattern (a tense guest). If another customer rejects the soup, the two surprises link and the soup is blamed (pdf 74).
- **Moses illusion.** "How many animals of each kind did Moses take into the ark?" Few people notice the error (Noah). The ark sets up a biblical context in which Moses is not abnormal. Moses and Noah also share a vowel sound and syllable count, which helps; you unconsciously detect associative coherence between “Moses” and “ark” and so accept the question. With George W. Bush there would be no illusion (pdf 74).
- **Detecting anomalies.** The word "cement" in a sentence is flagged at once as out of place. Brain responses to odd words appear within two-tenths of a second. The same response occurs for a male voice saying he is pregnant, or an upper-class voice reporting a large tattoo. This requires much world knowledge to be applied instantly (pdf 74).
- **Norms enable communication.** "A table" means a normal table. Categories have norms for typical values and for range. In "the large mouse climbed over the trunk of the very small elephant," we imagine a mouse smaller than a shoe on an elephant larger than a sofa. System 1 has access to category norms, including the range of plausible values (pdf 74–75).

*Intuition note:* System 1's sense of normal follows what has been linked together in memory, not only what is probable. One odd event can make later events that recruit it feel normal, even when they are less probable.

**Seeing Causes and Intentions (pdf 75–78)**
- **Fred.** "Fred's parents arrived late. The caterers were expected soon. Fred was angry." You link anger to lateness, not to the caterers. A coherent story is built automatically; System 2 accepts it (pdf 75).
- **Taleb's bond story.** On the day of Saddam Hussein's capture, bond prices rose, and a headline said that the capture might not curb terrorism. Half an hour later prices fell, and the headline said the capture boosted the allure of risky assets. Hussein's capture was destined to be the explanation of whatever happened (pdf 75). The author's conclusion: a statement that can explain two contradictory outcomes explains nothing at all. The headlines satisfy our need for coherence (pdf 76).
- **Jane's wallet.** In a surprise recall test, "pickpocket" was more strongly associated with the story than "sights," although only "sights" was in the sentence. Lost wallet, New York and crowds jointly evoke the pickpocket explanation (pdf 76).
- **Michotte (book published 1945).** The accepted view (back to Hume) was that we infer physical causality from repeated correlations. Michotte argued that we see causality as directly as color. In his displays, one drawn square touches another, which starts moving at once. Observers know there is no real contact but report an "illusion of causality" (the second is "launched"). Six-month-old infants appear to see cause and effect and show surprise when the sequence is altered. Thus impressions of causality do not depend on reasoning about causal patterns and are products of System 1 (pdf 76–77).
- **Heider and Simmel (1944).** Their 1:40 film shows two triangles and a circle moving around a house shape. Viewers see a bully, a terrified circle and an alliance. The author says only people with autism do not experience this. Infants under one year identify bullies and victims and expect a pursuer to follow the most direct path (pdf 77).
- **Freely willed action** is experienced as separate from physical causality, caused by a "disembodied you" (pdf 77).
- **Bloom's claim (as reported).** Our inborn separation of physical and intentional causality makes it possible to envision "soulless bodies and bodiless souls." Bloom's provocative claim is that this readiness explains the near universality of religious belief: the two modes of causation make it natural to accept two central beliefs of many religions (an immaterial divinity as ultimate cause of the physical world, and immortal souls that temporarily control bodies). In Bloom's view the two concepts of causality were shaped separately by evolution (pdf 77).
- **Statistical thinking.** The author says people are prone to apply causal thinking inappropriately to situations that need statistical reasoning. Statistical thinking derives conclusions about individual cases from properties of categories and ensembles. System 1 cannot do this; System 2 can learn it, but few people are trained (pdf 77–78).
- **Why the agent metaphors.** The author describes System 1 sometimes as an agent and sometimes as an associative machine. Both are fictions, used because they fit how we think about causes (mental economy), as with Heider's triangles (pdf 78).

*Intuition note:* We read stories into scenes: a lost wallet becomes a pickpocket and a market move gets a headline explanation. This happens automatically, and the story can feel convincing even when, as with the two headlines, it explains nothing.

**Speaking of Norms and Causes (pdf 78).** Three sample remarks: a repeat experience feels normal with little repetition; consider the whole range of normal reactions, not only the average; someone who cannot accept bad luck needs a causal story and will end up thinking someone sabotaged her work (pdf 78).

## Toy example (ours, with assumed numbers)
Assume the person in the next seat is certainly one of your 100 acquaintances, each equally likely.
- P(a particular person, e.g. Jon) = 1/100 = 0.01.
- P(some acquaintance) = 100 × (1/100) = 1.
- So Jon is 1/100 as likely as "some acquaintance."

By probability, Jon is no more likely than any other single acquaintance. Yet after the island meeting, the author says Jon would feel more normal than any other acquaintance (pdf 73). Probability counts equal chances; System 1's felt normality follows the memory link between Jon and "traveling abroad." This shows normality and probability coming apart.

## Concepts
- [[norm-theory]] (new) — events feel normal when they recruit and fit earlier episodes in memory, not only when they were expected (pdf 73).
- [[surprise]] (new) — the sign that something violates an active expectation or what the model treats as normal (pdf 72, 74).
- [[associative-coherence]] (new) — ideas that co-occur and fit together are accepted quickly, e.g. "Moses" with "ark" (pdf 74).
- [[category-norms]] (new) — typical value plus plausible range stored for each category (pdf 75).
- [[automatic-causal-search]] (new) — System 1 builds causal stories at once; in the Fred example System 2 accepted the story (pdf 75–76).
- [[illusion-of-causality]] (new) — Michotte: we directly see one moving object "launching" another (pdf 76).
- [[intentional-causality]] (new) — we readily attribute agency, personality and intentions to moving shapes (pdf 77).
- [[statistical-thinking]] (new) — reasoning from categories and ensembles to individual cases; System 1 lacks it, System 2 can learn it (pdf 78).

## Questions this raises

- What training lets System 2 reason statistically, and how much does it help against automatic causal impressions?
