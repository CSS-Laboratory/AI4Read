# Linda: Less Is More

## L0 — One line
People often rate a more detailed, more plausible story (feminist bank teller) as more probable than a broader event that contains it (bank teller), in part because intuition judges by coherence and a lazy System 2 often fails to check the logic.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman and Tversky asked whether logic or representativeness (similarity to a stereotype) wins when the two conflict. In the Linda problem, most people, including statistically trained ones, ranked "feminist bank teller" as more probable than "bank teller". This is the conjunction fallacy: rating "A and B" more probable than "A". It shrank in some settings, such as a short direct comparison or a "how many of 100?" wording. The "less is more" pattern (a bigger set valued lower) also appears in Hsee's dinnerware prices; seeing both sets together (joint evaluation) fixed that error, but not the Linda error. Kahneman cites a lazy System 2 and plausibility replacing probability as part of the story. He also describes how Linda drew critics.

**Prerequisites:** Venn diagrams (subsets); basic probability; System 1 / System 2 as used earlier in the book; between- vs within-subject experiments.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A subset event cannot be more probable than the set containing it. Many people still say it is, which is the conjunction fallacy (pdf 154–155).
- Rankings by resemblance and by probability were identical, so probability judgments tracked representativeness (pdf 154, 156).
- Expertise did not protect much: 89% of undergraduates and 85% of decision-science doctoral students made the error in the eight-item version (pdf 154–155).
- Direct comparison helps in some cases. In the short version, 64% of social-science graduate students answered correctly, versus 15% with eight items (pdf 155–156).
- Hsee's dinnerware and List's baseball cards show the same "less is more" pattern: in single evaluation, value seems to be averaged rather than added (pdf 157–158).
- Joint evaluation removes the error in the dinnerware case but not in the Linda case, because the sum-like nature of probability is less obvious than that of money (pdf 158).
- Frequency wording (asking how many of 100 people) cut errors from 65% to 25% in one problem (pdf 160).
- Kahneman says the laziness of System 2 is part of the story: plausibility was enough for it to endorse a conjunction (pdf 160–161). Critics then focused on the conjunction fallacy, which he calls unique as a clash between intuition and logic (pdf 161–162).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The Linda problem (pdf 153–154)
- Purpose: Kahneman and Tversky made up the problem to provide what they intended as conclusive evidence of the role of heuristics in judgment and their incompatibility with logic (pdf 153).
- Description: Linda is 31, single, outspoken, very bright, a philosophy major, concerned with discrimination and social justice, and at antinuclear demonstrations (pdf 153).
- Eight scenarios were listed. The two critical ones were bank teller and bank teller who is active in the feminist movement (pdf 153).
- Everyone agrees Linda fits "feminist bank teller" better than "bank teller", since the added detail makes a more coherent story (pdf 153–154).
- Logic: the set of feminist bank tellers is wholly inside the set of bank tellers, so P(feminist teller) < P(teller). Specifying an event in more detail can only lower its probability (pdf 154).

**Intuition note:** Adding a detail can make a story feel more likely while making it less likely to be true.

### Experiments (pdf 154–156)
- Between-subjects first: each person saw seven outcomes with only one critical item. Mean rankings by resemblance and by likelihood were identical, and "feminist bank teller" ranked higher in both (pdf 154).
- Then a within-subject design: "bank teller" sixth, "feminist bank teller" last. The authors expected people to notice the relation and follow logic (pdf 154).
- About ten early questionnaires all ranked "feminist bank teller" above "bank teller" (pdf 154). Later, 89% of undergraduates violated the logic (pdf 154).
- 85% of Stanford doctoral students in decision science, all of whom had taken several advanced courses in probability, statistics and decision theory, did the same (pdf 154–155).
- Kahneman calls this a failure of System 2: both outcomes were in the same ranking, so participants had a fair opportunity to detect the relevant logical rule, and they did not take it (pdf 154).
- Short version (a direct question on which of the two alternatives is more probable): about 85–90% of undergraduates at several major universities chose the feminist bank teller (pdf 155). When Kahneman asked a class whether they realised they had violated a logical rule, someone shouted "So what?"; a graduate student said she thought she was only being asked for her opinion (pdf 155).
- Conjunction fallacy (as Kahneman and Tversky introduced it): judging a conjunction of two events more probable than one of them, in direct comparison (pdf 155).
- Gould's "homunculus" kept shouting that she can't just be a bank teller, even though he knew the answer. Kahneman reads this as System 1 (pdf 155).
- Correct answers: 64% of social-science graduate students at Stanford and Berkeley on the short version, versus 15% of a similar group on the eight-item version (pdf 155).
- Kahneman's explanation: in the long version an intervening item separated the two outcomes, so each was judged independently. The short version forced a comparison that mobilised System 2 (pdf 155–156). The 36% who still erred were not studied (pdf 156).

**Intuition note:** Putting the two options side by side can prompt a logical check. It did so for most of the sophisticated graduate students on the short version, but many other respondents still erred.

### Plausibility versus probability (pdf 156–157)
- Representativeness belongs to a cluster of closely related assessments that are likely to be generated together. The most representative outcomes produce the most coherent stories. The most coherent story is not necessarily the most probable, but it is plausible, and the unwary easily confuse coherence, plausibility and probability (pdf 156).
- Forecasting scenarios (different groups judged each): a flood somewhere in North America in which more than 1,000 people drown, versus a California earthquake causing a flood in which more than 1,000 drown. The second is more plausible but certainly less probable. Probability judgments were higher for the richer one (pdf 156).
- Control questions: Mark has hair vs Mark has blond hair; Jane is a teacher vs Jane is a teacher and walks to work. The structure is the same, but there is no fallacy. The detailed option is not more plausible, so there is no competing intuition and logic prevails (pdf 156–157).

### Less is more, even in joint evaluation (pdf 157–158)
- Hsee's dinnerware: Set A has 40 pieces (24 intact in three sets of 8, plus 8 cups with 2 broken and 8 saucers with 7 broken). Set B has 24 pieces, all intact (pdf 157).
- Joint evaluation (within-subject): Set A $32 vs Set B $30. Single evaluation (between-subjects): Set B $33 vs Set A $23 (pdf 157).
- Explanation: sets are represented by norms and prototypes, so the average quality of the dishes dominates. Set A's average is lower because of the broken dishes. This is "less is more" (pdf 157).
- List found the same in a real baseball-card market. Ten high-value cards were auctioned alone and with three modest cards added. The larger set was valued more in joint evaluation and less in single evaluation (pdf 158).
- Economic value is a sum-like variable, so adding a positively valued item can only increase it (pdf 158). Probability is sum-like too:
$$P(\text{Linda is a teller}) = P(\text{feminist teller}) + P(\text{non-feminist teller})$$
- System 1 averages rather than adds. Removing the non-feminist tellers raises the subjective probability, which gives the less-is-more pattern in single evaluation (pdf 158).
- Joint evaluation removes the error in Hsee's experiment but not in Linda, because the sum-like nature is less obvious for probability than for money (pdf 158).

**Intuition note:** Money obviously adds up, so seeing both sets together leads people to apply the logic that more dishes add value. Probability adds up too, but people do not feel that as clearly.

### Other conjunction errors (pdf 158–160)
- Wimbledon (Borg): A win the match; B lose the first set; C lose the first set but win the match; D win the first set but lose the match. B includes C, so P(B) must be higher than P(C). Yet 72% gave B a lower probability than C, and C is the more plausible story (pdf 158).
- Nonverbal die test: a die with 4 green and 2 red faces, rolled 20 times. Participants chose a sequence to win $25 and "probability" was never mentioned (pdf 159).
  - Sequences: 1. RGRRR, 2. GRGRRR, 3. GRRRRR.
  - Sequence 2 is sequence 1 with a G added at the start, so it can only be less likely. Almost two-thirds still preferred 2 over 1 (pdf 159).
  - Shown arguments for both choices, a large majority found the correct argument (for 1) more convincing (pdf 159).
- Frequency format: two groups saw variants of a medical-survey problem. Error was 65% with "What percentage…" and 25% with the version asking how many of the 100 participants (pdf 160).
  - Kahneman's likely explanation: "100 individuals" evokes a spatial picture of people sorting into groups, in which inclusion is obvious. "How many?" makes you think of individuals; "what percentage?" does not (pdf 160).

### What it says about System 2 (pdf 160–161)
- System 2 is "not impressively alert". Participants certainly "knew" Venn-diagram logic, but did not apply it reliably with all the information in front of them (pdf 160).
- The conjunction looked plausible, and that sufficed for System 2 to endorse it (pdf 161).
- Kahneman believes most subjects would avoid the error with indefinite time, a real stake and instructions to be sure. Instead the answer did not matter to them, they spent very little time, and they answered as if they were only being asked for an opinion (pdf 161).
- Contrast: in the dinnerware problem intuition rules in single evaluation and logic in joint evaluation. In Linda, intuition often beats logic even in joint evaluation (pdf 161).

### The controversy (pdf 161–162)
- **Critics' view (as reported by Kahneman):** researchers found instructions and hints that reduced the fallacy, and some argued that in the Linda context it is reasonable to read "probability" as "plausibility". These arguments were sometimes extended to suggest that the whole enterprise was misguided: if one salient illusion could be weakened or explained away, others could be as well (pdf 161).
- **Kahneman's reply:** this overlooks the conjunction fallacy's unique status as a conflict of intuition and logic. The between-subjects evidence for heuristics was not challenged, only not addressed (pdf 161).
- Net effect: more public visibility and a small dent in credibility among scholars (pdf 161–162).
- Kahneman says lawyers use two styles: raising doubts about the strongest arguments for a case, or focusing on the weakest part of a witness's testimony. A focus on weaknesses is also normal in political debate. He does not believe this is appropriate in science, but he has come to accept that social-science norms do not prohibit the political style (pdf 162).
- Asked why he and others focused on the conjunction fallacy rather than on findings that supported Kahneman's position more strongly, Hertwig (a persistent critic of the Linda problem) answered that it was "more interesting" (pdf 162).

## Toy example (ours)
**Conjunction.** Imagine 100 women who fit Linda's description. Suppose 5 are bank tellers: 3 feminists and 2 non-feminists.
- P(teller) = 5/100 = 0.05.
- P(feminist teller) = 3/100 = 0.03.
- Check the sum: 0.03 + 0.02 = 0.05.
- The feminist tellers are a subset, so 0.03 < 0.05, however well "feminist" fits her.

**Averaging vs adding.** Say each intact piece is worth $2 and a broken piece $0.
- Set B (24 intact): total $48, average $48/24 = $2.
- Set A (31 intact, 9 broken out of 40): total $62, average $62/40 = $1.55.
- Adding favours A ($62 > $48). Averaging favours B ($2 > $1.55). Single evaluation behaves like the average.

## Concepts
- [[conjunction-fallacy]] (new) — judging "A and B" more probable than "A" in direct comparison (pdf 155)
- [[representativeness / availability / anchoring]] — here, judging probability by similarity to a stereotype; rankings by probability matched rankings by resemblance (pdf 154, 156)
- [[heuristic]] — the Linda problem was meant as evidence of heuristics conflicting with logic (pdf 153)
- [[joint-vs-single-evaluation]] (new) — judging options together (within-subject) or one at a time (between-subjects) (pdf 157)
- [[sum-like-variable]] (new) — a quantity, such as money or probability, that adds up over parts, so adding a part cannot lower it (pdf 158)
- [[less-is-more-effect]] (new) — a set or event with extra items is valued or rated lower than the smaller one (pdf 157)
- [[frequency-representation]] (new) — posing a question as how many of 100 people, so that inclusion is easy to see (pdf 160)
- [[plausibility-vs-probability]] (new) — coherent stories are plausible but not necessarily probable, and the two get confused (pdf 156)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Sum-like probability, P(teller) = P(feminist teller) + P(non-feminist teller) ↔ probability theory: additivity over disjoint events. The unit states this decomposition.
- [structural-analogy] Less-is-more in single evaluation ↔ utility function: a value that fails to rise when a good is added violates monotonicity.
- [structural-analogy] "How many?" vs "What percentage?" ↔ framing effect: equivalent descriptions give different error rates.

## Questions this raises
- Why did 36% of the knowledgeable group still err in the short version? The text says this was not explored (pdf 156).
- Is the answer to "probable?" really driven by plausibility, or do some people read "probability" differently, as critics suggest (pdf 161)?
- What makes some comparisons trigger System 2 (the short Linda, "how many of 100") while others do not?
- Would payment or unlimited time remove the error? Kahneman only says he believes so (pdf 161).
