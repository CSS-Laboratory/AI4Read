# Anchors

## L0 — One line
Considering a number before estimating an unknown quantity pulls the estimate toward that number, through two mechanisms: deliberate but insufficient adjustment (System 2) and automatic priming (System 1).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do estimates move toward a number we were just shown, even a random one? Kahneman says this is the anchoring effect. It has two sources. One is System 2 starting at the anchor and adjusting too little. The other is System 1 priming: the anchor automatically brings to mind compatible information, so the estimate rests on a biased sample of ideas. The effect can be measured (55% of the anchor gap in the main example, which the author calls typical) and appears in experts, in money decisions and in courtrooms. People deny being influenced. It matters because some people exploit anchors in negotiation and marketing, and Kahneman advises assuming that any number on the table affects you; "thinking the opposite" may also help.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction (fast automatic vs slow effortful thinking); priming; percentages and averages.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Anchoring effect: an estimate stays close to a value the person considered first, even when that value is a rigged wheel spin (pdf 118).
- Two mechanisms: System 2 adjustment from an anchor that stops too early, and System 1 priming that makes anchor-compatible information easier to retrieve (pdf 119).
- Evidence for adjustment: head-shaking increases movement from the anchor, and cognitive load or mild drunkenness reduces it (pdf 120).
- Evidence for priming: a high or low temperature or car-price anchor primes different words and brands, so estimates draw on biased memories (pdf 121–122).
- The anchoring index = (difference between group estimates) / (difference between anchors). It is typically around 55%, and is similar for experts and for random anchors (pdf 122–124).
- Anchors work even when obviously random, so people's belief that an anchor is informative is not the cause (pdf 124).
- Anchors have practical effects and can be exploited (rationing signs, first offers, damage caps). Defences include "thinking the opposite" (pdf 124–125).
- System 2 cannot see anchoring's influence, so the safe rule is to assume any number on the table has anchored you (pdf 126).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening experiment (pdf 118)
Kahneman and Amos Tversky rigged a 0–100 wheel of fortune to stop only at 10 or 65. Oregon students wrote down the number and then answered two questions: whether the percentage of African nations among UN members was larger or smaller than that number, and their best guess of the percentage (pdf 118).
- Mean estimates were 25% (after 10) and 45% (after 65) (pdf 118).
- Anchoring effect: people consider a value for an unknown quantity before estimating it, and the estimate stays close to that value (pdf 118).
- Examples: an age-at-death question with a 114 or a 35 anchor (Gandhi), and the asking price of a house (pdf 118).
- This experiment was the first demonstration of anchoring's absurdity, because the number was obviously uninformative (pdf 118).
- Kahneman and Tversky disagreed on the mechanism. Later work showed both were right: one mechanism for each system (pdf 119).

*Intuition note:* A number that should be ignored still drags the guess toward it. The rest of the chapter asks why.

### Anchoring as adjustment (System 2) (pdf 119–120)
- Adjust-and-anchor heuristic (the idea Tversky liked): start from the anchor, judge whether it is too high or too low, and move gradually. Adjustment typically ends too soon, because people stop when no longer certain they should go farther (pdf 119).
- Evidence came from Shafir, Gilovich and their students (pdf 119).
- Line demonstration: draw a 2½-inch line upward from the page bottom, then another downward from the top to 2½ inches above the bottom. The first is likely the shorter. Each stops near the near edge of a region of uncertainty (pdf 119).
- Le Boeuf and Shafir's everyday cases: driving too fast after leaving a highway, and a child turning loud music down but not enough (pdf 119–120).
- Questions like "When did Washington become president?" bring a wrong anchor to mind, but you know the direction of the right answer. You adjust by finding arguments to move away (pdf 120).
- Epley and Gilovich: head-shakers move farther from the anchor, nodders show enhanced anchoring. Adjustment is less when memory is loaded with digits or the person is slightly drunk (pdf 120).
- Kahneman's conclusion: insufficient adjustment is a failure of a weak or lazy System 2. Tversky was right for at least some cases, those with deliberate adjustment in a specified direction (pdf 120).

*Intuition note:* Adjusting is work, and tired or distracted people do less of it. So they end up nearer the anchor.

### Anchoring as priming effect (System 1) (pdf 120–122)
- Kahneman: in most cases of anchoring there is no subjective experience of adjustment. You probably did not adjust down from 144 for Gandhi's age, yet the absurd number still affects the estimate (pdf 120–121).
- Kahneman's hunch was that anchoring is suggestion (causing someone to feel something by bringing it to mind). Tversky objected that suggestion was unexplained. It is now explained as a priming effect that selectively evokes compatible evidence (pdf 121).
- System 1 tries to make sentences true, so it builds a world in which the anchor is the true number. This is associative coherence (pdf 121).
- Mussweiler and Strack, temperature: the anchor was 20°C (68°F) or 5°C (40°F) for Germany's annual mean temperature. The high anchor made summer words (sun, beach) easier to recognise, and the low anchor made winter words (frost, ski) easier (pdf 121).
- Estimates draw on these biased samples of ideas, so they are biased too (pdf 121–122).
- Cars: a high anchor primed luxury brands (Mercedes, Audi), and a low anchor primed mass-market brands (Volkswagen) (pdf 122).

*Intuition note:* The anchor does not tell you the answer. It changes which memories come to mind first, and your estimate is built from those memories.

### The anchoring index (pdf 122–124)
Anchoring can be measured. Exploratorium visitors were asked about the tallest redwood, with anchors of 1,200 feet (high) and 180 feet (low) (pdf 122).
- Anchor difference: $1{,}200-180=1{,}020$ feet. Mean estimates were 844 and 282 feet, a difference of 562 (pdf 122).
$$\text{anchoring index}=\frac{562}{1{,}020}\approx 55\%$$
- 100% means slavishly adopting the anchor, and 0% means ignoring it entirely. 55% is typical (pdf 122).
- Real-estate agents saw an asking price well above or well below the listed price of a real house. They denied any influence, but the index was 41%. Business students scored 48%, but admitted being influenced (pdf 122–123).
- Seabird donations: with no anchor, the mean was $64. With a $5 anchor it was $20, and with a $400 anchor $143. The difference was $123, and the effect was "above 30%": a $100 higher request raised mean willingness to pay by about $30 (pdf 123). (added step: $123/(400−5)=123/395≈31%.)
- Marseilles pollution study: effect over 50%. Online "buy now" prices and art-auction estimates also act as anchors (pdf 123).
- Anchoring may look reasonable, since an anchor can be a hint. But random anchors work as well as potentially informative ones. The wheel of fortune index was 44%, and the last digits of a Social Security number also worked as anchors. Hence people are not anchored because they believe the number is informative (pdf 124).
- Judges (over 15 years' experience) read a shoplifting case and rolled loaded dice that gave 3 or 9. Those who rolled 9 gave 8 months on average, and those who rolled 3 gave 5 months, an effect of 50% (pdf 124). (added step: (8−5)/(9−3)=0.5.)

*Intuition note:* The index asks what share of the gap between anchors shows up in the gap between estimates. Even experts and obviously random numbers give large shares.

### Uses and abuses of anchors (pdf 124–125)
- Campbell's soup, Sioux City: with a "limit of 12 per person" sign, shoppers bought an average of 7 cans, twice as many as with no limit. Anchoring is not the sole explanation, since rationing also signals urgency. But 12 would anchor even if a roulette wheel produced it (pdf 124–125).
- In single-issue negotiation, such as price alone, moving first is an advantage, and the first number is a powerful anchor (pdf 125).
- Kahneman's advice to his negotiation students: do not answer an outrageous offer with an equally outrageous counteroffer. Make a scene or threaten to walk out, and make it clear that you will not negotiate with that number on the table (pdf 125).
- Galinsky and Mussweiler: negotiators told to search memory for arguments against the anchor reduced or eliminated the effect. Examples were focusing on the opponent's minimal acceptable offer or on the opponent's costs of no agreement. "Thinking the opposite" may be a good defence (pdf 125).
- Damage caps: a $1 million cap would remove larger awards but also pull up many awards that would otherwise be much smaller. It would almost certainly benefit serious offenders and large firms much more than small ones (pdf 125).

### Anchoring and the two systems (pdf 126)
- Anchoring is studied in tasks that System 2 finishes, but System 2 works on data retrieved automatically by System 1. So it has no control over, and no knowledge of, the anchor's effect. Participants given random or absurd anchors confidently deny being affected, and they are wrong (pdf 126).
- As with the law of small numbers, a message has much the same effect on the associative system whether or not it is reliable (WYSIATI, "what you see is all there is"), unless it is immediately rejected as a lie. A random anchor is an extreme case (pdf 126).
- Priming research's main moral: thoughts and behaviour are influenced by the momentary environment more than we know or want. Anchoring is threatening in the same way. You are aware of the anchor, but you do not know how it guides and constrains your thinking, because you cannot imagine how you would have thought if the anchor had been different or absent (pdf 126).
- Advice: assume any number on the table has anchored you, and if stakes are high, mobilise System 2 against it (pdf 126).

### "Speaking of Anchors" (pdf 127)
Four sample remarks: set aside a target firm's revenue plan, avoid anchoring on best-case plans, aim to get the other side anchored on your number, and a defence lawyer's low damages figure anchored a judge (pdf 127).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Question: "How many students are in this school?" Low-anchor group is asked "more or less than 200?", and high-anchor group "more or less than 1,000?". Suppose mean answers are 500 and 700.
- Anchor difference: 1,000 − 200 = 800.
- Estimate difference: 700 − 500 = 200.
- Index = 200/800 = 0.25, i.e. 25%.
Reading: the groups' answers differ by a quarter of the anchor gap. If everyone had copied the anchor, the answers would have been 200 and 1,000, an index of 100%. If anchors were ignored, the answers would have been equal, an index of 0%.

## Questions this raises
- When both mechanisms operate on the same question, how much does each contribute to the 55%?
- Why do students admit anchoring while professionals deny it, if both are equally affected?
- Can "thinking the opposite" be taught as a habit, and does it help under cognitive load?
- In negotiation, how should a first mover choose an anchor, given the risk of an offer that is too extreme?
