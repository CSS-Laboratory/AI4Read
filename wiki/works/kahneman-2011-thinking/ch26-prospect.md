# Prospect Theory

## L0 — One line
Bernoulli's utility-of-wealth model lacks a reference point; prospect theory adds it, so outcomes are valued as gains and losses, with diminishing sensitivity and loss aversion.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why does the utility-of-wealth model fail to predict choices? Kahneman says it has no reference point. People judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point. Sensitivity falls as amounts grow, and losses loom larger than gains. The result is usually risk aversion for gains and in mixed gambles, but risk seeking when all options are bad. Two problems with identical final wealth got opposite majority choices. Rabin's theorem shows that utility of wealth cannot explain small-stakes loss aversion. Prospect theory has blind spots of its own: it cannot handle disappointment or regret. Kahneman says many scholars accepted it because its added concepts predicted new facts that turned out true.

**Prerequisites:** expected value of a gamble; the idea of "utility of wealth" (Bernoulli's model, assumed from earlier chapters); reading slopes of a curve; System 1 (the automatic mode of thinking, from earlier chapters); a *reference point* (the starting state from which a gain or loss is measured); a *mixed gamble* (one with both a possible loss and a possible gain).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Utility theory values a gain as the difference between utilities of two *states of wealth*. In that theory, gains and losses are allowed to differ only in sign (pdf 270–271).
- Prospect theory makes *changes* from a reference point (gains and losses) the carriers of value, that is, the things value attaches to. The reference point is the missing variable, and it makes the theory more complex (pdf 272–273).
- Problems 3 and 4 have identical final wealth but opposite majority choices. This is Kahneman's decisive counterexample to Bernoulli (pdf 272).
- Three System 1 features: reference-point evaluation, diminishing sensitivity, loss aversion. Figure 10's S-shaped value function illustrates them (pdf 273–274).
- Loss aversion ratio is usually 1.5–2.5. Mixed gambles therefore produce strong risk aversion (pdf 276–277).
- Diminishing sensitivity makes people risk seeking when a sure loss faces a larger probable loss. This does not contradict loss aversion (pdf 277).
- Rabin (2000): within utility theory, rejecting a small favorable gamble commits you to absurd risk aversion on some larger gamble (pdf 277).
- Prospect theory itself cannot handle disappointment and fails to allow for regret. Many scholars accepted it because its extra concepts predicted new facts that turned out true, not because it is "true" (pdf 279–280).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: how the flaw was found (pdf 270–271)
- Kahneman read about experiments that measured the utility of money using gambles of a few pennies. Such experiments modify wealth by less than a dollar (pdf 270).
- He asked whether it is plausible that people judge such gambles by tiny wealth differences. Psychophysics (the study of how stimuli are perceived) suggested asking about wealth directly, not about changes of wealth (pdf 270).
- Amos remembered that the economist Harry Markowitz had attached utilities to *changes* of wealth. They concluded their theory would define outcomes as gains and losses (pdf 270).
- The old idea then seemed absurd. They could not assess their own wealth within tens of thousands of dollars. Kahneman calls the earlier failure to see this *theory-induced blindness* (pdf 270).
- In utility theory the utility of a gain is a difference between two states of wealth. For example, +$500 at $1 million is $u(1{,}000{,}500)-u(1{,}000{,}000)$. Losing $500 is the reverse difference, so gains and losses differ only in sign. The theory cannot say that losing $500 hurts more than winning $500 pleases (pdf 270–271).

**Intuition note.** In Bernoulli's model only your final wealth matters. Kahneman argues people react to the change itself.

### Gains versus losses: Problems 1–2 (pdf 271)
- Problem 1: $900 for sure, or 90% chance of $1,000. Most people choose the sure thing (risk averse). The value of $900 is more than 90% of the value of $1,000.
- Problem 2: lose $900 for sure, or 90% chance to lose $1,000. Most people take the gamble (risk seeking). The value of losing $900 is much more than 90% of the value of losing $1,000.
- Kahneman says the evaluation of probabilities (90% versus 100%) also contributes; this is deferred to later.
- Others had noticed risk seeking when all options are bad. Theory-induced blindness meant the fact was ignored (pdf 271).

### Problems 3–4: the counterexample (pdf 272–273)
- Problem 3: given $1,000, choose 50% win $1,000 or $500 sure. Problem 4: given $2,000, choose 50% lose $1,000 or lose $500 sure.
- In final wealth both are the same choice. You have either a sure +$1,500, or equal chances of +$1,000 or +$2,000 (pdf 272).
- Bernoulli's theory predicts similar choices. A large majority took the sure thing in Problem 3 and the gamble in Problem 4. The reference point is +$1,000 in Problem 3 and +$2,000 in Problem 4. So +$1,500 is a $500 gain in the first and a loss in the second (pdf 272).
- The gift is barely noticed because it is part of the reference point, and reference points are generally ignored. Risk attitudes would not change if your net worth were a few thousand dollars higher or lower (unless abjectly poor). You just like winning and dislike losing, and you almost certainly dislike losing more than you like winning (pdf 273).
- Prospect theory needs the state *and* the reference state, so it is more complex. In science complexity is considered a cost, which must be justified by a sufficiently rich set of new and (preferably) interesting predictions of facts the existing theory cannot explain (pdf 273).

**Intuition note.** Same final wealth, different starting point for judging, different choice. People barely notice the gift because it is part of the reference point; the choice then depends on whether an option counts as a gain or a loss relative to that reference point.

### Three features of System 1 (pdf 273–274)
1. **Reference point (adaptation level).** Three-bowl demonstration: after cold and warm water, the same middle bowl feels hot to one hand and cold to the other. For money the reference is usually the status quo, but it may be an expected outcome or one you feel entitled to, like a colleague's raise. Better is a gain; worse is a loss.
2. **Diminishing sensitivity.** A weak light matters in a dark room, not in a bright one. The difference between $900 and $1,000 feels smaller than that between $100 and $200.
3. **Loss aversion.** When directly weighed against each other, losses loom larger than gains. Kahneman links this to an evolutionary history: organisms that treat threats as more urgent than opportunities survive better.

- Figure 10 plots psychological value against dollar amount. It is S-shaped (diminishing sensitivity on both sides). The slope changes abruptly at the reference point, with a steeper loss side: this is loss aversion (pdf 274–275).

### Loss Aversion (pdf 275–277)
- Mixed prospects hold both a possible loss and a possible gain. Problem 5: tails lose $100, heads win $150. The expected value is positive, yet most people reject it (pdf 275–276).
- The rejection is an act of System 2, but its inputs are System 1 emotions. For most people, fear of losing $100 is stronger than hope of gaining $150 (pdf 276).
- Test: what is the smallest gain that balances an equal chance to lose $100? For many people, about $200. The estimated loss aversion ratio is usually 1.5–2.5, and some people are much more loss averse than others (pdf 276).
- Professional risk takers are more tolerant of losses, "probably" because they do not respond emotionally to every fluctuation. Participants told to "think like a trader" became less loss averse, and a physiological index of arousal to losses fell sharply (pdf 276).
- Self-test with 50–50 gambles: answer $10 means indifference to risk; below $10, risk seeking; above, loss averse. Try losses of $10, $500, $2,000. The coefficient tends to rise with stakes, "but not dramatically." For ruinous losses it is very large and may even be infinite (pdf 276).
- **Two claims, no contradiction (pdf 277).** In mixed gambles, loss aversion causes extremely risk-averse choices (the loss looms about twice as large, as the slopes show). In bad choices (sure loss versus a larger probable loss), the bending of the curve causes risk seeking. Kahneman calls these two insights "the essence of prospect theory".
- Loss aversion is strong even when the amount at risk is tiny relative to wealth. Kahneman says this is why Figure 10 shows an abrupt slope change at the reference point. He asks whether attitudes to states of wealth could plausibly explain such extreme aversion to small risks, and calls it a striking example of theory-induced blindness that this flaw in Bernoulli's theory escaped scholarly notice for more than 250 years (pdf 277).
- **Rabin's theorem (2000).** Kahneman reports: anyone who rejects a favorable small-stakes gamble is mathematically committed to a foolish degree of risk aversion for some larger gamble. Example: most Humans (his term for the agents of prospect theory) reject "50% lose \$100, 50% win \$200". According to utility theory, such a person will also reject "50% lose \$200, 50% win \$20,000". Kahneman says no one in their right mind would reject that (pdf 277).
- Rabin and Thaler ended an article by saying that "expected utility is an ex-hypothesis", echoing a Monty Python sketch. Many economists saw this as blasphemy; Kahneman finds the humor legitimate (pdf 277–278).

**Intuition note.** Rabin's theorem, as Kahneman reports it, says that within utility theory rejecting a small good bet commits you to rejecting a much larger and clearly attractive one. Kahneman's own account is different: loss aversion is considerable even when the amount at risk is minuscule relative to wealth, hence the abrupt change of slope at the reference point.

### Blind Spots of Prospect Theory (pdf 278–280)
- **Why textbooks omit it (pdf 278).** Kahneman says the standard results of introductory economics are most easily explained by assuming that Econs (the agents of the standard model) make no foolish mistakes. He calls this assumption "truly necessary" for the standard results. He finds omission reasonable, since the basic tools are hard enough. He adds that prospect theory's departures from rationality are often irrelevant to economic predictions. In some contexts they matter: Humans follow the immediate emotional impact of gains and losses, not long-term wealth prospects.
- **Flaws of its own (pdf 278–279).** Kahneman says theory-induced blindness is not restricted to expected utility theory: prospect theory has flaws of its own, and blindness to them has contributed to its acceptance as the main alternative to utility theory.
- **Reference value of zero (pdf 279).** Prospects: A, one in a million to win $1 million; B, 90% win $12, else nothing; C, 90% win $1 million, else nothing. Prospect theory gives "win nothing" the same value (zero) in all three. In A and B it is a nonevent. In C it is intensely disappointing: the high probability of a large win sets a tentative new reference point, so nothing feels like a large loss. Prospect theory cannot handle disappointment.
- **Regret (pdf 279–280).** Both prospect theory and utility theory assume each option is evaluated separately and independently. Problem 6: 90% win $1 million or $50 sure. Problem 7: 90% win $1 million or $150,000 sure. Failing to win hurts in both, but in Problem 7 the pain is compounded by regret over spurning a sure $150,000. In regret, how an outcome feels depends on an option you could have chosen but did not.
- **Why regret models had less influence (pdf 280).** The emotions are real, but regret theories make few striking predictions that distinguish them from prospect theory, which is simpler. Scientists treat theories as a bag of tools. Prospect theory was accepted by many scholars not because it is 'true' but because its new concepts, notably reference point and loss aversion, were worth the trouble: they yielded new predictions that turned out to be true. Kahneman: "We were lucky."

**Intuition note.** A better description of feelings does not guarantee a better theory. Extra complexity is worth it only if it predicts something new that is true.

### Speaking of Prospect Theory (pdf 280)
Three sample sentences: extreme loss aversion makes someone refuse very favorable bets; a wealthy person's strong reaction to trivial gains and losses makes no sense; weighing losses about twice as much as gains is normal.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrative value function: $v(x)=x$ for gains, $v(x)=2x$ for losses (loss aversion ratio 2, in the book's 1.5–2.5 range).
- Problem 5: $0.5\cdot v(150)+0.5\cdot v(-100)=75+0.5\cdot(-200)=75-100=-25<0$. Reject, although expected value is $0.5\cdot150-0.5\cdot100=+25$.
- Smallest balancing gain $G$: $0.5G-0.5\cdot200=0\Rightarrow G=200$, matching "about \$200".

Now add diminishing sensitivity: $v(x)=\sqrt{x}$ for gains and $v(x)=-2\sqrt{|x|}$ for losses.
- Problem 3 (reference = wealth + gift $1,000): sure +$500 gives $\sqrt{500}\approx22.36$. The gamble gives $0.5\sqrt{1000}+0.5\cdot0\approx15.81$. The sure thing wins.
- Problem 4 (reference = wealth + gift $2,000): sure −$500 gives $-2\sqrt{500}\approx-44.72$. The gamble gives $0.5\cdot(-2\sqrt{1000})\approx-31.62$. The gamble wins.

The same final wealth gives opposite choices, as in the text.

## Concepts
- [[reference-dependence]] — outcomes are coded as gains or losses from a reference point (status quo, expectation or entitlement), not as final wealth (pdf 272–274)
- [[loss-aversion]] — losses loom larger than equal gains; the ratio is usually 1.5–2.5 (pdf 274, 276)
- [[diminishing-sensitivity]] (new) — each extra unit of gain or loss matters less as amounts grow; gives the S-shape (pdf 274)
- [[prospect-theory]] (new) — the theory Kahneman developed with Amos, valuing gains and losses relative to a reference point (pdf 273)
- [[theory-induced-blindness]] (new) — failing to notice flaws in an accepted theory, such as Bernoulli's (pdf 270)
- [[risk-aversion]] — preferring a sure gain; here also strong in mixed gambles because of loss aversion (pdf 271, 277)
- risk seeking (new id: [[risk-seeking]]) — preferring a gamble to a sure loss when all options are bad (pdf 271)
- [[expected-utility]] — the utility-of-wealth account that Rabin's theorem attacks (pdf 277)
- [[utility-function]] — in Bernoulli's model, value attaches to states of wealth (pdf 270)
- [[regret-and-disappointment]] (new) — emotions prospect theory ignores: outcome feelings depend on expectations or on rejected options (pdf 279–280)

- [structural-analogy] Loss aversion (steeper loss slope) ↔ asymmetric loss functions in statistics: errors in one direction cost more than in the other.
