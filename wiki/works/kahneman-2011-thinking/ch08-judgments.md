# How Judgments Happen

## L0 — One line
System 1 continuously computes basic assessments, matches intensities across dimensions and over-computes ("mental shotgun"), so an easier assessment can readily stand in for a harder question.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do intuitive judgments arise? System 1 runs basic assessments all the time (threat, similarity, causality, average length) with little effort. When a question is hard, a related basic assessment can stand in for it. Kahneman calls this the essential idea of the heuristics and biases approach. Two other System 1 features help: it matches intensity across different dimensions, and its "mental shotgun" computes more than was asked. System 1 handles averages well but tends to ignore the size of a set, so it deals poorly with sums. Face-based judgments of competence are one example: they have some influence on votes, though no evidence links those features to performance in office.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction from earlier chapters (fast automatic vs. slow effortful thinking); average vs. sum.

## L2 — Key ideas
- System 2 answers questions by directing attention and searching memory. System 1 monitors continuously and produces assessments without intention. These are easily substituted for harder questions (pdf 89).
- Evolution shaped System 1 to keep assessing threat, opportunity and whether everything is normal. Good mood and cognitive ease are the human equivalents of assessments of safety and familiarity (pdf 89).
- Face reading is a basic assessment put to modern use. In about 70% of the races studied (senator, congressman, governor), the winner was the candidate whose face got the higher competence rating; the effect was about three times larger for politically uninformed, TV-prone voters (pdf 90–91).
- System 1 represents categories by a prototype or typical exemplars. It deals well with averages but tends to ignore set size, so it handles sum-like variables poorly, as the bird-rescue payments show (pdf 92–93).
- Intensity matching lets System 1 map one scale onto another (Julie's reading vs. height). Predicting by such matching is natural but statistically wrong (pdf 93–94).
- The mental shotgun: intending one computation triggers others. This slows performance when the extra computation conflicts with the task (pdf 94–95).
- Not every attribute is assessed. Confirming exact counts and computing totals needs System 2 (pdf 91–92).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (no heading).** You can answer any number of questions and evaluate any number of attributes (pdf 89). Questions go to System 2, which directs attention and searches memory. System 1 monitors inside and outside the mind and generates assessments without specific intention and with little or no effort (pdf 89). These basic assessments are easily substituted for harder questions; Kahneman calls this the essential idea of the heuristics and biases approach (pdf 89). Two more features support substitution:
- Translating values across dimensions, e.g. "If Sam were as tall as he is intelligent, how tall would he be?" (pdf 89).
- The mental shotgun: a System 2 intention to answer a question automatically triggers other computations, including basic assessments (pdf 89).

**Basic Assessments.** Evolution shaped System 1 to provide continuous assessment of the main problems an organism must solve to survive: how things are going, threat or opportunity, normal or not, approach or avoid. Humans have inherited these mechanisms, and they are still running (pdf 89).

*Faces.* Todorov showed that one glance at a face yields two assessments (pdf 90):
- Dominance, cued by face shape (e.g. a square chin).
- Trustworthiness or intent, cued by expression (smile or frown).

Accuracy is imperfect (round chins are not reliable signs of meekness; smiles can be faked), but even imperfect assessment gives a survival advantage (pdf 90).

*Voting.* Students rated campaign portraits of politicians, sometimes seen for only one-tenth of a second, without political context. In about 70% of races for senator, congressman and governor, the winner was the candidate whose face got the higher competence rating (pdf 90). Later studies confirmed this in Finland, England (zoning boards), Australia, Germany and Mexico. In Todorov's study, competence ratings were far more predictive of outcomes than likability ratings, which Kahneman found surprising (pdf 90). People judge competence by combining strength and trustworthiness: a strong chin plus a slight confident smile. There is no evidence these features predict performance in office. Brain studies show a stronger negative emotional response to losing candidates (pdf 90). Kahneman calls this a *judgment heuristic*: voters want to know how good a candidate will be, and fall back on a simpler assessment that is quick, automatic and available (pdf 90–91).

*Who is affected.* Among politically uninformed voters who watch much television, the effect of facial competence was about three times larger than among better-informed, lower-TV voters. So System 1's weight in decisions differs between people (pdf 91).

*Language.* System 1 understands language. Its routine assessments include similarity, representativeness, causality, and availability of associations. They run even without a task (pdf 91).

*Not everything is assessed (figure 7).* A glance shows that two towers are equally tall and more similar to each other than the left tower is to the array of blocks in the middle. It does not show that the left tower has as many blocks as the floor array, nor the height of a tower built from them. Counting and comparing is System 2 work (pdf 91).

> *Intuition note:* System 1 delivers some answers for free (similarity, danger, a face's "look") and none for others (the total of a set, exact counts of larger sets). Such free answers can be what we fall back on when a question is hard.

**Sets and Prototypes.** *Average length of the lines in figure 8* is easy. A fraction of a second suffices to register it with considerable precision, even when the person is busy with a memory task. People can adjust another line to match it accurately without stating it in inches. Number of objects is also registered: precisely for four or fewer, crudely for more (pdf 92). *Total length* is different: System 1 has no suggestion. System 2 must estimate the average, estimate or count the lines, and multiply (pdf 92). Reason: System 1 represents categories by a prototype or typical exemplars, so it "deals well with averages but poorly with sums". Category size tends to be ignored for sum-like variables (pdf 92–93).

*Exxon Valdez experiment.* Groups stated willingness to pay for nets protecting 2,000, 20,000 or 200,000 birds. If saving birds is an economic good, it should be sum-like, so 200,000 birds should be worth much more than 2,000. Average answers were $80, $78 and $88. Participants responded to a prototype, the image of a helpless bird drowning in oil. Near-complete neglect of quantity in emotional contexts has been confirmed many times (pdf 93).

> *Intuition note:* A mind that stores one typical image of a bird in trouble has the same picture whatever the number of birds. So quantity can have very little effect on the answer.

**Intensity Matching.** Questions about happiness, a president's popularity, punishment or a politician's prospects all refer to a dimension of intensity that allows "more" (pdf 93). System 1 can match intensities across dimensions. If crimes were colors, murder would be a deeper red than theft; if they were music, mass murder would be fortissimo and unpaid parking tickets a faint pianissimo. In classic experiments people adjusted loudness to the severity of crimes, and others adjusted it to the severity of punishments (pdf 93). One tone much louder than the other (crime vs. punishment) would give a sense of injustice (pdf 93).

*Julie example:* Julie read fluently at age four. Matching her precocity to a man's height: 6 ft is obviously too little and 7 ft probably too much; reading at fifteen months would be extraordinary, perhaps like 7'8". The same can be done for income, crime severity and GPA. Matches are close across people in the same cultural milieu (pdf 94). Kahneman says people predicting her GPA translate from one scale to another and pick the matching GPA. He promises later to show why this is statistically wrong, though natural to System 1 and also acceptable to System 2 for most people except statisticians (pdf 94).

> *Intuition note:* Intensity is a shared "volume knob" across topics. This makes answers to odd questions come easily, but easy does not mean statistically valid.

**The Mental Shotgun.** Some System 1 computations are routine and continuous (building a 3-D view of the visual field, monitoring for violated expectations). Others occur only when intended (judging your happiness or the president's prospects) (pdf 94). Control over intended computations is imprecise: we often compute more than we need (pdf 94–95).
- *Rhymes:* People heard word pairs and pressed a key when they rhymed. VOTE–GOAT rhymes but is spelled differently. Participants were distinctly slower on it than on VOTE–NOTE, although the instructions required only a comparison of sounds. Kahneman calls the extra spelling comparison superfluous and even detrimental to the main task (pdf 95).
- *Metaphors:* People judged sentences as literally true or false. All three sentences ("Some roads are snakes", "Some jobs are snakes", "Some jobs are jails") are literally false, but "Some jobs are snakes" is more obviously false; the other two can be metaphorically true, and reaction times showed a substantial difference. The correct answer prevailed, but the conflict disrupted performance (pdf 95).

Next chapter: shotgun plus intensity matching explains intuitive judgments about things we know little about (pdf 95).

> *Intuition note:* Asking System 1 one question sets off several computations, like scattered pellets. When the extra answer conflicts with the task, it disrupts performance.

**Speaking of Judgment (pdf 96).** Four example remarks: attractiveness is judged automatically; brain circuits judge dominance from face shape; punishment will not feel just unless its intensity matches the crime; and a mental-shotgun case where someone asked about a company's finances cannot forget that he likes the product.

## Toy example (ours)
Two sets of lines, each with average length 6 cm.
- Set A: 4, 6, 8 cm. Count = 3. Total = 3 × 6 = 18 cm.
- Set B: 4, 6, 8, 4, 6, 8 cm. Average = (4+6+8+4+6+8)/6 = 36/6 = 6 cm. Count = 6. Total = 6 × 6 = 36 cm.

A glance gives the same average impression for both (System 1's prototype). The totals differ by a factor of 2 because the count differs. A sum-sensitive judge would pay twice as much for Set B's total. By the same logic, if the $80 for 2,000 birds were proportional to the number saved, 200,000 birds would be worth 100 × $80 = $8,000. The observed answer was $88 (pdf 93).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Scope insensitivity to quantity (bird nets) ↔ decision-game-theory: expected-utility valuation that should scale with outcome size — the observed answers do not scale.
- [metaphor] Mental shotgun ↔ interference effects in cognitive tasks: an irrelevant dimension slows a main task.
