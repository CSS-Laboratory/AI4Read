# Rare Events

## L0 — One line
A rare event is often overestimated and overweighted when it gets focused attention (through vividness, concrete format or explicit description), and, when it is not overweighted, neglected.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks when people overweight rare events and when they ignore them. Early prospect theory said only that unlikely events are "ignored or overweighted". His answer rests on System 1: focus on an event, in confirmatory mode, makes it easier to imagine, so its probability tends to be overestimated and its decision weight rises. Vivid outcomes, frequency formats ("1 in 1,000") and explicit descriptions strengthen this. Denominator neglect (judging by the count of winning cases and ignoring the total) is one result. When a rare event is not explicitly in mind, as in choice from experience, it is not overweighted and is often underweighted. Terrorism, lotteries, risk communication and courtroom framing all illustrate or exploit these effects.

**Prerequisites:** probability and expected value; the idea of System 1 (fast, automatic) and System 2 (slow, effortful); the idea of a decision weight from prospect theory. Terms used below: confirmatory mode (looking for evidence that a statement is true); cognitive ease (how easily something comes to mind); choice from experience (choosing after seeing outcomes repeated, not after reading a description).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Fear of a rare danger (a bus bomb) can be strong, automatic and insensitive to the exact probability, even when System 2 knows the risk is tiny (pdf 314).
- Overestimating probability and overweighting in decisions are distinct, but the same mechanisms drive both: focused attention, confirmation bias, cognitive ease (pdf 315–316).
- A rare event's probability is most likely to be overestimated when the alternative is not fully specified (diffuse). Fans' probabilities for eight teams summed to 240% (pdf 316–317).
- Kahneman believes a vivid representation of the outcome, emotional or not, reduces the role of probability. The Princeton results suggest that emotional intensity alone is not the explanation (pdf 318–320).
- Denominator neglect: attention goes to the winning cases, so "8 in 100" can look better than "1 in 10". Frequency formats raise the weight of low-probability events (pdf 320–322).
- Format can be exploited by advocates and lawyers (pdf 322).
- In choice from experience, rare events are not overweighted and underweighting is common, partly because many people never see the rare event. Kahneman finds it far more plausible that global impressions pool rare events away, unless a rare event is extreme enough to come to mind separately (pdf 323–324).
- A rare event is overweighted if it specifically attracts attention. When there is no overweighting, there is neglect (pdf 324–325).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: bus bombings and lotteries
- Israel had 23 bus bombings between December 2001 and September 2004, with 236 deaths. About 1.3 million people rode buses daily. The risk to a traveler was tiny, yet people avoided buses or scanned fellow riders anxiously (pdf 314).
- Kahneman himself disliked stopping next to a bus at a red light, though he knew he was more likely to be hurt in a driving accident. His avoidance was not rational concern for survival. Being near a bus brought unpleasant thoughts of bombs, and he wanted to think of something else (pdf 314).
- Terrorism works by an **availability cascade**: a very vivid image of death, reinforced by media and conversation, becomes highly accessible. The arousal is associative, automatic and uncontrolled. System 2 may "know" the probability is low, but System 1 cannot be turned off (pdf 314).
- The emotion is disproportionate and also insensitive to the exact probability. Kahneman asks whether residents of a city told of one bomber, whose risk is lower by half than a city told of two, feel much safer. The question is rhetorical; he implies the answer is no (pdf 314).
- High-prize lotteries work similarly. The prize fantasy is shared and reinforced in conversation, and buying a ticket is rewarded at once by pleasant fantasy. In both cases only possibility matters, not probability (pdf 314–315).
- Original prospect theory said "highly unlikely events are either ignored or overweighted". It did not say when each occurs, or give a psychological interpretation. Kahneman's current view draws on research on emotion and vividness (pdf 315). He says overweighting of unlikely outcomes is rooted in System 1 features: emotion and vividness influence fluency, availability and probability judgments, and so account for the excessive response to the few rare events we do not ignore (pdf 315).

### Overestimation and Overweighting
- Two related questions: the probability that the next US president is a third-party candidate, and the price you would pay for a $1,000 bet on it. The first asks for a probability judgment, the second for a decision weight (pdf 315).
- Oversimplified answers (to be qualified): people overestimate probabilities of unlikely events, and people overweight unlikely events in decisions. These are distinct phenomena with the same mechanisms: focused attention, confirmation bias, cognitive ease (pdf 315–316).
- Specific descriptions trigger System 1 in confirmatory mode: it retrieves evidence and images that make the statement true. The process is biased but is not fantasy: people look for a plausible scenario. The probability judgment is ultimately set by the fluency with which a plausible scenario comes to mind (pdf 316).
- If the target event is very likely, attention goes to its alternative. For "baby released within three days", you focus on possible problems. Availability is likely evoked and, in confirmatory mode, the estimate of problems is probably too high (pdf 316).
- A rare event is most likely overestimated when the alternative is not fully specified (pdf 316).
- **Fox's NBA study.** Fans estimated each of eight teams' chances of winning the playoffs. For each team, the alternative (seven other teams) is diffuse. The eight judgments summed to 240%, though they must sum to 100% (pdf 316–317).
- When the question was East vs. West conference, both events were equally specific and the judgments summed to 100% (pdf 317).
- Fans also gave cash equivalents (a sure amount as attractive as the bet) for bets paying $160. The eight cash equivalents summed to $287. An average participant who took all eight bets would be guaranteed a loss of $127, since $287 − $160 = $127. The participants surely knew there were eight teams, yet they overweighted (pdf 317).
- Kahneman applies this to the planning fallacy: success is specific and easy to imagine, failure is diffuse. Entrepreneurs and investors are prone both to overestimate and to overweight (pdf 317).

*Intuition note:* When you think hard about one possible outcome, your mind builds a story for it and neglects the vague "everything else". Each outcome can therefore get too much probability, and people may pay too much to bet on it (as the fans did).

### Vivid Outcomes
- Utility theory: decision weight equals probability (100 for a sure thing, 90 for 90%, nine times the weight of 10%). Prospect theory: variations in probability have less effect on weights. In an earlier experiment the weight of a 90% chance was 71.2 and of a 10% chance 18.6. The probability ratio is 9.0 but the weight ratio is only 3.83, which indicates insufficient sensitivity to probability in that range (pdf 317–318).
- Both theories say the weight depends on probability, not on the outcome. Kahneman says this theoretical prediction turns out to be wrong (pdf 318).
- **Chicago team** ("Money, Kisses, and Electric Shocks"): gambles with emotional outcomes (kissing a movie star, a painful but not dangerous shock) were valued much less sensitively to probability than cash gambles. Other researchers, using physiological measures such as heart rate, found that fear of an impending shock was essentially uncorrelated with its probability. The Chicago team proposed that "affect-laden imagery" overwhelms probability (pdf 318).
- **Princeton team**, ten years later, argued that low sensitivity is normal and money gambles are the exception. A money gamble has a definite expected value that serves as an anchor. Example: an 84% chance of $59 has expected value $49.56, so a cash equivalent near $50 is easy to find. For "84% chance of a dozen red roses" there is no anchor. The gap between 84% and 21% gambles was much larger for money than for roses (pdf 318).
- Willingness to pay to avoid painting an apartment vs. cleaning dormitory bathroom stalls (21% or 84% chance): the second is more emotional, yet the weights did not differ. Evidently, the intensity of emotion is not the answer (pdf 319).
- Adding an explicit price ("roses. Value $59") did not restore sensitivity to probability. People thinking of roses did not use the price as an anchor (pdf 319).
- **Kahneman's hypothesis:** a rich, vivid representation of the outcome, emotional or not, reduces the role of probability. He predicts, with "reasonably high confidence", that a vivid but irrelevant detail on a money outcome has the same effect. His example is $59 vs. $59 in a large blue cardboard envelope next Monday morning (pdf 319–320). The text states this as a prediction, not a result.
- Cognitive ease also feeds the certainty effect: with a vivid image of the event, its non-occurrence is also vivid and overweighted. Together, an enhanced possibility effect and an enhanced certainty effect leave little room for weights to change between 21% and 84% (pdf 320).

*Intuition note:* On Kahneman's hypothesis, a vivid picture of the prize makes both "I might win" and "I might not win" feel real. So the exact chance matters less to the feeling than it should.

### Vivid Probabilities
- **Urn experiment.** Urn A has 1 red marble in 10 (10%). Urn B has 8 red in 100 (8%). About 30%–40% of students choose B. Epstein reads this as the superficial processing of System 1 (pdf 320).
- Following Slovic, Kahneman calls the bias **denominator neglect**: attention is on the winning marbles, and the non-winners are not weighed with equal care. He pictures one red marble in a vague white background for the small urn and eight red ones for the large urn, a more hopeful image (pdf 320).
- The same applies to certainty. With a 90% chance of winning, the event of not winning is more salient if 10 of 100 marbles are "losers" than if 1 of 10 is (pdf 321).
- Vaccine: a described 0.001% risk of permanent disability looks small. The wording one of 100,000 vaccinated children will be permanently disabled calls up an image of an individual child. Low-probability events are much more heavily weighted in relative-frequency format (how many) than in abstract terms (how likely). System 1 handles individuals better than categories (pdf 321).
- A disease described as killing 1,286 people out of every 10,000 was judged more dangerous than one described as killing 24.14% of the population, although the first risk is only about half as large. It was also judged more dangerous than one described as killing 24.4 out of 100 (pdf 321).
- Kahneman says the effect would surely be reduced or eliminated if people compared the formulations directly, which calls for System 2. But life is usually a between-subjects experiment, he says: we see one formulation at a time (pdf 321).
- **Mr. Jones.** Forensic professionals judged whether to discharge a patient with a history of violence. The frequency format (of every 100 patients similar to Mr. Jones, 10 are estimated to commit violence) led to 41% denying discharge, vs. 21% for the "10% probability" format (pdf 321–322).
- Manipulation: Slovic and colleagues cite an article on homicides (about 1,000 a year) by seriously mentally ill people who are not taking their medication. The text lists several ways of stating the same fact (1,000 out of 273,000,000 Americans; 0.00036%; less than one-thirtieth the number of suicide deaths). Slovic says these advocates are open that they want to frighten the public, hoping this raises funding for mental health services (pdf 322).
- DNA evidence: saying a false match occurs in 1 of 1,000 capital cases invites the jury to imagine the man before them wrongly convicted. The prosecutor will prefer the abstract frame, "0.1%" (pdf 322).

*Intuition note:* "1 in 1,000" puts one concrete victim in your mind and hides the 999 who are fine. "0.1%" is just a number. The same fact therefore feels different.

### Decisions from Global Impressions
- The evidence suggests the hypothesis that focal attention and salience contribute to both overestimation and overweighting. Salience rises with mere mention, vividness and format. There are exceptions: an erroneous theory may make an event seem impossible, or you may be unable to imagine how it could happen. Kahneman calls the bias large and robust, though not absolute (pdf 322–323).
- **Choice from experience** vs. choice from description. In the experiments the participant presses one of two buttons, each producing a reward or nothing at random. Expected values are about equal and one button is riskier. In choice from experience she sees many trials first. In choice from description she reads the prospect (e.g., a 5% chance to win $12). Description gives the possibility effect. In experience, overweighting is "never observed" and underweighting is common (pdf 323).
- Everyday cases of experience: a restaurant that is usually good, a moody friend, earthquakes in California. The experiments suggest rare events are not overweighted in decisions like these (pdf 323).
- Interpretation is "not yet settled", but there is general agreement on one major cause of underweighting: many participants never experience the rare event. Most Californians have never felt a major earthquake, and no banker in 2007 had lived through a devastating financial crisis. Hertwig and Erev say rare events receive less impact than their probabilities deserve (pdf 323–324).
- Underweighting also occurs after the rare event has been experienced. **Adele and Brian:** Adele is consistently helpful. Brian is usually a bit less so but occasionally extremely generous. Two views are given (pdf 324):
  - *View 1:* a choice between two gambles, in which the possibility effect overweights the rare good outcome and favors Brian.
  - *View 2:* a choice between global impressions. Good and bad experiences are pooled, and the norm is biased toward typical and recent instances, favoring Adele. The exception is a rare event so extreme that it comes to mind separately (e.g., Brian once verbally abused a colleague).
- Kahneman finds View 2 "far more plausible" in a two-system mind. System 1 forms global representations with an emotional attitude and an approach/avoid tendency. The buttons likewise develop integrated "personalities" (pdf 324).
- **Summary of conditions.** A rare event's probability will often (not always) be overestimated, because of the confirmatory bias of memory. It will be overweighted if it specifically attracts attention. Explicit description effectively guarantees separate attention. Obsessive concerns, vivid images, concrete representations ("1 of 1,000") and explicit reminders all contribute to overweighting. When there is no overweighting, there will be neglect. For rare probabilities, he says, our mind is not designed to get things quite right (pdf 324–325).

*Intuition note:* A rare event is weighted too much when something pushes it to the front of attention, and, when there is no overweighting, it is neglected. Kahneman says our mind is not designed to get rare probabilities quite right, which is not good news for dangers nobody has experienced yet.

### Speaking of Rare Events
- Four sample remarks restate the chapter: vivid tsunami images inflate tourists' probabilities; a disaster cycle of exaggeration, then neglect; list specific alternatives that sum to 100%; describing a risk as 1 death per 1,000 counts on denominator neglect (pdf 325).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take the chapter's urn pair and its frequency rewording.
1. Urn A: 1 red in 10, so 1/10 = 0.10. Urn B: 8 red in 100, so 8/100 = 0.08. A is better, since 0.10 > 0.08.
2. A chooser who attends only to the numerators compares 1 with 8 and picks B. That is denominator neglect.
3. Reword a risk: 0.001% = 0.001/100 = 1/100,000. The text predicts that "1 in 100,000" feels larger than "0.001%", though the quantity is identical.
4. Sum check for the Fox pattern: if all eight teams were equally likely, a fair $160 bet on each would be worth 160 × (1/8) = $20. The fans' average was $287 / 8 ≈ $35.88 per bet (added step).

## Concepts
- [[probability weighting]] — here, rare outcomes get a decision weight above their probability when they capture attention; underweighted or ignored otherwise (pdf 315, 325)
- [[decision weight]] (new) — the weight a possibility carries in a choice; in prospect theory it is not equal to its probability (pdf 317)
- [[possibility effect]] (new) — rare outcomes are overweighted relative to their probability (pdf 323; see also pdf 320)
- [[certainty effect]] (new) — the chance of not getting an outcome is overweighted when the outcome is nearly sure (pdf 320)
- [[denominator neglect]] (new) — attention to the winning count and neglect of the total, so "8 in 100" can look better than "1 in 10" (pdf 320)
- [[availability cascade]] (new) — a vivid image reinforced by media and talk becomes highly accessible and drives behavior (pdf 314)
- [[representativeness / availability / anchoring]] — availability here is judging probability by the ease with which scenarios come to mind; anchoring is the vague expected value used for cash equivalents (pdf 316, 318)
- [[framing effect]] — the same risk described as a percentage or as "x in n" gives different weights and choices (pdf 321–322)
- [[choice from experience]] (new) — choosing after repeated sampling of outcomes; rare events are not overweighted, and underweighting is common (pdf 323)
- [[heuristic]] — the availability heuristic as used here: probability judged by how many scenarios come to mind and how easily (pdf 316)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Overweighting what is in focus ↔ decision-game-theory: a focal event with a diffuse alternative breaks additivity of probabilities, violating the rule that probabilities sum to 1.
- [metaphor] Choice from experience vs. description ↔ learning in games: values learned from sampled payoffs versus values read from stated payoff tables.
- [structural-analogy] Format manipulation of risk ↔ mechanism design: the presentation of information changes behavior without changing the underlying facts.
