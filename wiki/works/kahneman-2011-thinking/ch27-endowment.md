# The Endowment Effect

## L0 — One line
People often demand more for a good they own and use than they would pay for it, because losses from a reference point weigh more than equal gains, so choices depend on the status quo and not only on the final state.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Standard indifference curves (lines of equally desirable combinations) ignore where you currently stand. Kahneman points to what is missing. He argues that the reference point matters, and loss aversion (losses hurting more than equal gains please) biases choice toward the status quo. Thaler used prospect theory to explain why a wine owner was very reluctant to sell at $100 yet would never pay more than $35. In mug experiments, sellers set a price about twice what buyers or choosers set. The effect is expected for goods held "for use", not for goods held "for exchange". It disappeared with trading experience in one test, and the poor may be a further exception. It matters because it contradicts the assumption of stable tastes.

**Prerequisites:** indifference and utility; the prospect-theory value function from the previous chapter (reference point, steeper for losses than for gains).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The standard indifference map leaves out the individual's current position, the reference point. Kahneman calls this theory-induced blindness and compares it to Bernoulli's error (pdf 282).
- Albert and Ben are "hedonic twins" who start indifferent between a raise and extra leisure. After they are assigned one each, prospect theory predicts that loss aversion makes both prefer to stay put. Tastes therefore vary with the reference point, and choice is biased toward the status quo (pdf 283).
- Professor R's gap between his selling price ($100) and his buying price ($35) is inconsistent with a single value per bottle. Thaler named the pattern the endowment effect (pdf 284).
- Prospect theory offers an explanation of the gap. Giving up a good is a loss and getting it is a gain, and the loss function is steeper (pdf 285).
- The effect is not universal. There is no loss aversion in routine trading of goods held "for exchange", such as money or a merchant's shoes. Wine and Super Bowl tickets are goods held "for use", where the effect appears; leisure and the living standard that income supports are also not intended for sale (pdf 285–286).
- In the mug experiments, the Sellers' average price was $7.12, the Choosers' $3.12 and the Buyers' $2.87. Trades were fewer than half the predicted number. The Sellers' value was a bit more than twice the others', a ratio very close to the loss aversion coefficient in risky choice (pdf 287–288).
- Reference points are labile, and the effect can be eliminated by changing the reference point. In List's test, experienced card traders showed no trace of it, while novices did. It also disappeared in some of Knetsch's manipulations. Poverty and culture may also remove or change it (pdf 289–290).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening section: the missing reference point (pdf 281–284)
- **Figure 11** is an "indifference map" for income (dollars per year) and leisure (days per year). Each indifference curve joins combinations that have equal utility (pdf 281).
- Convex curves mean diminishing marginal utility: the more leisure you have, the less an extra day matters, and the same holds for income. The curves would turn into parallel straight lines if people were willing to trade days for income at the same price regardless of how much income and vacation time they have (pdf 281).
- Points on one curve are equally attractive, so no incentive is needed to move between them (pdf 281–282).
- **What is missing:** the person's current income and leisure, such as the terms of a salaried job. This is the reference point or status quo, and the figure does not show it (pdf 282).
- Kahneman says this is "Bernoulli's error all over again". The map implicitly assumes utility depends only on the present situation and that the past is irrelevant (pdf 282).
- Evidence that the reference point matters:
  - In labor negotiations, the existing contract is the reference point and concessions hurt (pdf 282).
  - Unemployed workers' minimal acceptable wage averages 90% of their previous wage and falls by less than 10% over a year (pdf 282).
- **Albert and Ben** start at point 1 with identical tastes. They can have a $10,000 raise (A) or an extra day of paid vacation each month (B), and they toss a coin. Albert gets A and Ben gets B (pdf 282–283).
  - **Standard theory:** A and B are equally attractive, so little or no incentive is needed to switch (pdf 283).
  - **Prospect theory:** both twins definitely prefer to remain where they are (pdf 283).
  - From point 1, Albert saw "raise of $10,000" and "12 extra days of vacation" as equally attractive. From A, the choice becomes: stay at A with no gain or loss, or move to B for 12 extra days and a $10,000 salary cut (pdf 283).
  - Even if 12 days are as impressive as a $10,000 gain, they do not compensate for a $10,000 loss. Ben's case is the same: for him, the loss of leisure outweighs the extra income (pdf 283).
- Two lessons: (1) tastes are not fixed and vary with the reference point; (2) disadvantages loom larger than advantages, which biases choice toward the status quo (pdf 283).
- Loss aversion does not mean you never change. A benefit can exceed even the overweighted losses. It implies only that choices are strongly biased toward the reference situation, and generally toward small rather than large changes (pdf 283).
- Indifference maps and Bernoulli's wealth states share one mistaken assumption: that utility depends only on the state and is not affected by history (pdf 283–284).

*Intuition note:* The same two options feel different depending on which one you hold. Moving away from what you have counts as a loss, and losses weigh more.

### Section: The Endowment Effect (pdf 284–288)
- **Origin.** Thaler, a graduate student at Rochester in the early 1970s, collected behavior that rational-choice theory could not explain (pdf 284).
- **Professor R** (Richard Rosett) was very reluctant to sell a bottle from his collection, even at $100 (1975 dollars), but would never pay more than $35 for a bottle of that quality. At prices between $35 and $100, he would neither buy nor sell (pdf 284).
- Theory expects one value for the bottle. If it is worth $50 to him, he should be willing to sell for any amount above $50 and to pay any amount up to $50. Instead his minimum selling price ($100) was much higher than his maximum buying price ($35), so "owning the good appeared to increase its value" (pdf 284).
- **Concert ticket example (the author's):** you paid $200 and would have paid up to $500. Others now offer $3,000. If you resemble most of the audience at sold-out events, you do not sell (pdf 284).
- **Thaler's insight:** he obtained an early draft of prospect theory. Prospect theory suggested that willingness to buy or sell depends on whether you own the item now. An owner considers the pain of giving it up, and a non-owner considers the pleasure of getting it. Because the value function is steeper for losses, the two values are unequal (pdf 285).
- **Not universal.** Changing a $5 bill into five singles produces no sense of loss. The merchant selling shoes certainly feels no loss, and you probably do not feel paying as a loss, because both the shoes and the money are held "for exchange". Wine and Super Bowl tickets are held "for use". Leisure and the living standard your income supports are also not intended for sale or exchange (pdf 285–286).
- **Design.** Knetsch, Thaler and Kahneman borrowed Vernon Smith's token markets. Tokens are redeemed for cash at values that differ across people, and trading follows supply and demand. Theory predicts both the price and the number of trades. If tokens go randomly to half the participants, half should change hands (pdf 286).
  - Token rounds replicated Smith's results, with trades typically very close or identical to the predicted number (pdf 286).
- **Mug markets.** The mugs carried a university insignia and were worth about $6. They went randomly to half the participants (Sellers). The others (Buyers) paid with their own money (pdf 287).
  - The average selling price was about double the average buying price. Trades were fewer than half the predicted number (pdf 287).
- **Choosers** could receive either a mug or a sum of money and named the amount equally desirable. The averages were:

| Group | Average price |
|---|---|
| Sellers | $7.12 |
| Choosers | $3.12 |
| Buyers | $2.87 |

- Sellers and Choosers face the same two options (mug or money) with identical long-term effects. Only the emotion of the moment differs. Kahneman links the Sellers' high price to reluctance to give up owned things and says loss aversion is built into System 1's automatic evaluations (pdf 287).
- Buyers and Choosers set similar values, as expected if Buyers do not experience spending money as a loss (pdf 287).
- **Brain imaging:** selling goods one would normally use activates regions associated with disgust and pain. Buying does so only when prices are perceived as too high. Buying at especially low prices appears to be pleasurable (pdf 287–288).
- The Sellers' value is a bit more than twice that of Choosers and Buyers. This ratio is very close to the loss aversion coefficient in risky choice, as expected if one value function applies to riskless and risky decisions (pdf 288).
- A ratio of about 2:1 appears in other domains. For household purchases of eggs, orange juice or fish, the effect of price rises is about twice that of price drops, in contrast to the predictions of economic theory (pdf 288).
- The mugs experiment has remained the standard demonstration of the endowment effect (pdf 288).
- **Knetsch's pen/chocolate test:** classes received a gift (an expensive pen or a Swiss chocolate bar), and only about 10% chose to swap for the other gift (pdf 288).

*Intuition note:* Sellers and Choosers face the same two options, so the difference between them is not about what the mug offers. Choosers value the mug at less than half the Sellers' price. The gap reflects the Sellers' reluctance to give up something they own.

### Section: Thinking Like a Trader (pdf 288–291)
- Prospect theory's basic ideas are that reference points exist and losses loom larger than equal gains (pdf 288).
- **Boston condos in a downturn:** owners who bought at a higher price had a higher reference point. They set higher prices, took longer to sell and eventually received more money. A rational agent would treat the purchase price as irrelevant history (pdf 288).
- The original gap between selling and buying prices (or, more convincingly, between selling and choosing) was important in the early acceptance of reference points and loss aversion. Reference points are labile, especially in unusual laboratory situations, and the endowment effect can be eliminated by changing the reference point (pdf 289).
- No effect is expected when owners see goods as carriers of value for future exchange (pdf 289).
- **List's baseball-card studies:** novice traders were reluctant to part with their cards, and this reluctance eventually disappeared with trading experience. List also found a large effect of experience for new goods. In a replication of Knetsch's design, only 18% of inexperienced traders swapped a mug for chocolate (or the reverse) and 48% of experienced traders swapped. Experienced traders "showed no trace" of an endowment effect (pdf 289).
- **Knetsch:** the effect appeared only if participants had physical possession of the good for a while before trading was mentioned (pdf 289). The author notes that the different methodological concerns of experimental economists and psychologists have been much in evidence in the debate about the effect (pdf 289).
- Veteran traders have apparently learned to ask "How much do I want to have that mug, compared with other things I could have instead?" With that question the asymmetry between getting and giving up is irrelevant (pdf 289).
- **The poor** (a group where the effect is not expected): living below the reference point puts them always "in the losses". Small amounts of money are perceived as a reduced loss, not as a gain. Costs are losses, since money spent on one good is the loss of another good (pdf 289–290).
- Some people find spending painful even when objectively well-off. There may be cultural differences in attitudes toward spending, which may explain why buying and selling prices diverge substantially among US students but much less among English students. Kahneman concludes that "much remains to be learned" (pdf 290).
- The closing "Speaking Of" quotes give everyday uses, such as an office-choice example, stalled negotiations, demand falling when prices rise, a reluctance to sell a house for less than was paid, and a miser who treats each dollar spent as a loss (pdf 290–291).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume a simple value function with loss weight 2, close to the ~2:1 ratio mentioned (pdf 288). Gains count at face value and losses count double.
- From point 1, Albert values +$10,000 and +12 days equally, say +10 units each.
- At A, moving to B gives +12 days = +10 units and a $10,000 cut = −(2 × 10) = −20 units. The net is 10 − 20 = −10, so he stays.
- Ben at B, moving to A, gets +$10,000 = +10 units and loses 12 days = −20 units. The net is −10, so he stays.

Both twins stay, although the two jobs were equally attractive before the assignment.

## Concepts
- [[reference-dependence]] — outcomes are judged as gains or losses relative to the current position or status quo (pdf 282)
- [[loss-aversion]] — a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases; loss-side slope is steeper, roughly 2:1 here (pdf 285, 288)
- [[utility-function]] — here the indifference map's idea of utility is criticized for depending only on the present state (pdf 281–282)
- [[preference-relation]] — indifference curves list equally desirable combinations; the author says they wrongly ignore the reference point (pdf 281–282)
- [[endowment-effect]] (new) — owning a good appears to raise the price you demand for it above what you would pay (pdf 284)
- [[status-quo-bias]] (new) — choices strongly favor the reference situation because the disadvantages of change loom larger (pdf 283)
- [[held-for-use-vs-exchange]] (new) — the effect is expected for goods held to enjoy, and not expected for goods held to trade (money, a merchant's stock) (pdf 286, 289)
- [[theory-induced-blindness]] (new) — the power and elegance of a model can blind scholars to what it omits, here the reference point (pdf 282)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Endowment-effect gap between willingness to accept and willingness to pay ↔ decision-game-theory: path-dependent preferences that break the stable-preference assumption behind utility maximization — both break "tastes are fixed".
- [metaphor] Veteran traders asking "compared with other things I could have" ↔ bounded rationality/ecological rationality: experience tuning a mental habit to its environment.
- [structural-analogy] Poor people's choices "between losses" ↔ framing effect: the same budget decision feels different depending on whether options are coded as gains or losses.

## Questions this raises
- If reference points are labile, what determines the reference point in a given situation, and how could one measure it?
- Does the 2:1 seller-to-chooser ratio really come from the same loss aversion coefficient as in risky choice, or does it only look similar?
- Why might the effect differ between US and English students, and what other cultural factors could matter?
- If experienced traders show no effect, does market experience remove loss aversion itself or only change the question being asked?
