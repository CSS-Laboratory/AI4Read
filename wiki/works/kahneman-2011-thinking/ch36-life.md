# Life as a Story

## L0 — One line
We judge whole lives, and we choose whether to repeat experiences, by the story we remember (its peaks and its ending), while the length of the experience is largely ignored.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks why we care so much about how a life or an episode ends. His answer: the remembering self (the self that keeps stories) builds stories, and a story is about significant moments, not time passing. So duration neglect (ignoring length) and the peak-end rule (judging by peaks and the end) apply to whole lives, not only to short episodes. In Diener's study of "Jen", doubling her life span had no effect on the judgments, and adding five mildly happy years made her life look worse. Students' decisions about repeating a vacation followed their final evaluation. Kahneman's informal thought experiments suggest that many people care little about their amnesic (memory-wiped) experiencing self (the self that lives moments).

**Prerequisites:** sum vs. average; the idea of an "experiencing self" and a "remembering self" (the text refers back to the cold-hand experiment, pdf 380).

## L2 — Key ideas
- A story is about significant events and memorable moments, not time passing. Duration neglect is therefore normal in stories, and the ending often defines a story's character (pdf 377).
- We care about the narrative quality of other people's lives, even after they are dead, and not only about their feelings (pdf 377–378).
- In Diener's Jen study, doubling the length of her life had no effect whatsoever on judged desirability or on judged total happiness. The life seems to be represented by a prototypical slice of time, not a sum (pdf 378).
- Less-is-more effect: adding 5 slightly happy years to a very happy life lowered judged total happiness. This held within subjects too, and with older respondents (pdf 378–379).
- Labor and vacations seem to show that duration matters. Kahneman says this is only because the quality of the end changes with the length (pdf 379).
- Spring-break intentions to repeat were entirely determined by the final evaluation, even when it misrepresented the diaries. People choose by memory (pdf 379–380).
- Kahneman's informal thought experiments (amnesia potion, painful operation): people often show little concern for their experiencing self (pdf 380).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: La Traviata (pdf 377).**
- Violetta lies dying in the last act. Her lover rushes to Paris to see her. The audience feels intense tension about whether he arrives in time (pdf 377).
- Kahneman realised he did not care about the *length* of her life. Dying at 27 instead of 28 would not have moved him (pdf 377).
- The joy of the reunion would be the same whether the lovers had 10 minutes or a week. If the lover had come too late, it would be "an altogether different story" (pdf 377).
- Conclusion: "A story is about significant events and memorable moments, not about time passing." Duration neglect is normal in a story, and the ending often defines its character. The same features appear in memories of colonoscopies, vacations and films. The remembering self composes stories and keeps them (pdf 377).
- Examples of caring about stories: we want to know whether an estranged mother and daughter reconciled before she died. We pity a man who died believing in his wife's love, though he lived happily, when we hear that she had a lover for many years and stayed with him only for his money. We feel for a scientist whose discovery was proved false after her death (pdf 377–378).
- We also care intensely that our own life is a good story with a decent hero (pdf 378).

**Diener's study of Jen (pdf 378–379).**
- Setup: Jen is a never-married woman with no children, who dies instantly and painlessly in a car accident. She is extremely happy throughout her life, which lasts either 30 or 60 years. A second version adds 5 years, so she dies at 35 or 65. The extra years are pleasant, but less so than before (pdf 378).
- Participants answered two questions: how desirable her life was as a whole, and how much total happiness or unhappiness she experienced (pdf 378).
- Between-subjects result: doubling the duration had no effect whatsoever on either judgment (the text layer is garbled just before "no effect", pdf 378). Her life seems represented by a prototypical slice of time, not a sequence of time slices. "Total happiness" was the happiness of a typical period, not the sum (or integral) over her life (pdf 378).
- Less-is-more: adding 5 "slightly happy" years to a very happy life caused a substantial drop in judged total happiness. Kahneman reads this as strong evidence that an average (prototype) replaced a sum (pdf 378).
- Within-subject version: each participant made both judgments in immediate succession. Kahneman did not believe reasonable people would say that adding slightly happy years makes a life substantially worse. He was wrong, and the intuition was "overwhelming" (pdf 378).
- The pattern seemed absurd, so Diener's team first suspected the young participants' folly. The pattern did not change when students' parents and older friends answered (pdf 378–379).
- Conclusion: in intuitive evaluation of entire lives as well as brief episodes, peaks and ends matter but duration does not (pdf 379).

*Intuition note:* People judge a life like a photo of a typical moment, not like a running total. Extra years of "okay" happiness pull the typical moment down, so the life looks worse even though nothing was taken away.

**Objection: labor and vacations (pdf 379).**
- **Objection:** we all share the intuition that labor is much worse at 24 hours than at 6, and that 6 days at a good resort beat 3. Duration seems to matter (pdf 379).
- **Kahneman replies:** duration appears to matter only because the quality of the end changes with length. The mother is more depleted after 24 hours. The vacationer is more rested after 6 days. What truly matters is the progressive deterioration or improvement of the experience and how the person feels at the end (pdf 379).

**Amnesic Vacations (pdf 379–380).**
- Resorts sell restorative relaxation. Tourism helps people construct stories and collect memories. Frenetic picture-taking suggests that storing memories is often an important goal. The photographer treats the scene as a future memory to be designed, which is not necessarily best for the experiencing self (pdf 379).
- We often evaluate touristic vacations by the story and memories we expect to store. "Memorable" is a common word for highlights. Declaring a moment unforgettable gives a self-consciously memorable experience a weight and significance it would not otherwise have (pdf 379).
- Diener's spring-break study: students kept daily diaries with daily evaluations, gave a global rating at the end, and said whether they would repeat the vacation. Statistical analysis found that intentions were *entirely* determined by the final evaluation, even when it did not accurately represent the diary record. Right or wrong, people choose by memory (pdf 379–380).
- Thought experiment 1 (not formally studied; Kahneman reports informal impressions): all pictures and videos are destroyed and a potion wipes your memory of the vacation. Eliminating memories greatly reduces the value of the experience (pdf 380).
  - Some people treat themselves like any amnesic and return to a place where they were happy before.
  - Some say they would not bother to go at all, which Kahneman takes to reveal that they care only about the remembering self, and less about their amnesic experiencing self than about an amnesic stranger.
  - Many would not send themselves or another amnesic to climb mountains or trek through jungle. Those experiences are mostly painful in real time and gain value from the expected memory (pdf 380).
- Thought experiment 2: a painful operation while conscious, with a drug that wipes the memory. In his informal observation, most people are "remarkably indifferent" to the pains of their experiencing self. Kahneman feels pity for that self, but no more than for a stranger in pain. He concludes: "I am my remembering self," and the experiencing self is like a stranger to him (pdf 380).

*Intuition note:* When memory will be erased, Kahneman's informal impression is that the experience loses much of its value for many people, and some would not go at all. This fits the idea that, for these people, the self that plans and chooses cares mostly about the story to be remembered.

**Speaking of Life as a Story (pdf 381).** Kahneman lists sample remarks. One example: an Alzheimer's patient no longer keeps a narrative of her life, but her experiencing self is still sensitive to beauty and gentleness (pdf 381).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Life A: 10 years at happiness 8. Life B: the same 10 years plus 5 years at happiness 6.
- Sum: A = 10 × 8 = 80. B = 80 + 5 × 6 = 110. By sum, B is better.
- Average ("typical period", our simplification of the prototype): A = 80/10 = 8. B = (80 + 30)/15 = 110/15 ≈ 7.33. By average, B is worse.
- Judges who use a prototype rank A above B, which is the less-is-more pattern found with Jen (pdf 378).

## Concepts
- [[duration-neglect]] (new) — the length of an experience barely affects how it is judged or remembered (pdf 377)
- [[peak-end-rule]] (new) — evaluations are driven by peak moments and the end, not the total (pdf 378)
- [[remembering-self]] (new) — the self that composes and keeps stories, and decides by memory (pdf 377, 380)
- [[experiencing-self]] (new) — the self that lives the moments; to the author it is "like a stranger" (pdf 380)
- [[less-is-more-effect]] (new) — adding mildly positive time lowers the judged value of a whole, a sign of average replacing sum (pdf 378)
- [[prototype-substitution]] (new) — a typical slice of time stands in for the whole life or episode (pdf 378)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] prototype replacing a sum ↔ representativeness (HS canon): judging by similarity to a typical case, not by full information.
- [metaphor] designing a "future memory" while on holiday ↔ framing effect: how an experience is packaged shapes its valuation.

## Questions this raises
- If people choose by memory, should policy aim at the remembered story or at experienced well-being?
- Why do within-subject comparisons, which make the inconsistency visible, not remove the less-is-more judgment?
- How would the thought-experiment answers change in a formal study, not just informal impressions?
