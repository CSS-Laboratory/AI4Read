# The Characters of the Story

## L0 — One line
Kahneman introduces two "characters" of the mind, a fast automatic System 1 and a slow effortful System 2, as useful fictions for describing how intuition, effort, error and self-control interact.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how to talk about two very different kinds of thinking: the instant impression you get from an angry face, and the effortful work of multiplying 17 × 24. Kahneman adopts the labels System 1 (automatic, fast) and System 2 (attention-demanding, slow). System 1 supplies impressions that System 2 usually endorses. System 2 takes over when things get difficult, and it also controls impulses. System 1 has systematic errors (biases) and cannot be switched off. So errors of intuition are often hard to prevent. The two systems are fictions, which Kahneman finds useful because stories about agents are easier to follow. The chapter sets the vocabulary for the rest of the book.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday experience of attention, effort and optical illusions.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Fast thinking (reading an angry face) happens to you, without effort or sense of control. Slow thinking (17 × 24) is deliberate, effortful and shows in the body, for example in dilated pupils. (pdf 21–22)
- System 1 works automatically and quickly. System 2 allocates attention to effortful activities and is often associated with agency, choice and concentration. The terms come from Stanovich and West. (pdf 22)
- Attention is a limited budget. Effortful tasks interfere with each other, and intense focus can make people blind to the obvious, as in the gorilla video. (pdf 25–26)
- Most of the time System 2 adopts System 1's suggestions with little change. It takes over when System 1 has no answer, when something surprising happens, or when an error is about to be made. (pdf 26)
- System 1 is usually accurate, but it has biases, cannot be turned off, and has little grasp of logic and statistics. (pdf 27)
- Conflict between an automatic response and an intention shows that System 2's job includes self-control. (pdf 27–28)
- Cognitive illusions behave like the Müller-Lyer illusion: knowing the truth does not remove the impression. Constant vigilance is impractical, so the best we can do is a compromise. (pdf 29–30)
- The systems are fictitious characters. The sentences about them are descriptions, not explanations, and are used because agent-stories are easier to process. (pdf 30–31)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: fast and slow thinking
- **Angry face (Figure 1).** You see at once that the woman is angry, and you sense she is about to say unkind words. This happens automatically; it "just happened to you". The author calls it fast thinking. (pdf 21)
- **17 × 24.** You instantly recognise a multiplication problem and feel that 12,609 and 123 are implausible. You cannot be sure 568 is wrong without working, and no precise answer comes to mind. You can choose whether to compute. (pdf 21–22)
- **Doing the sum** means retrieving the school procedure and running it while holding intermediate results in memory. It is effortful, deliberate and orderly, a prototype of slow thinking. Muscles tense, blood pressure and heart rate rise, and pupils dilate; they return to normal when you finish or give up. The answer is 408. (pdf 22)

*Intuition note:* some mental events come to you uninvited, and others you must do on purpose. The second kind costs effort that the body shows.

### Two Systems
- Kahneman adopts Stanovich and West's labels. **System 1** operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control. **System 2** allocates attention to effortful mental activities, including complex computation. It is often associated with agency, choice and concentration. (pdf 22)
- We identify ourselves with System 2, the conscious reasoning self. Yet in the author's telling System 1 is the hero of the book. It originates the impressions and feelings that are the main sources of System 2's explicit beliefs and choices. Only System 2 can build thoughts in an orderly series of steps. Sometimes System 2 overrules System 1's impulses and associations. (pdf 22–23)
- **System 1 examples:** detecting that one object is more distant, orienting to a sudden sound, completing "bread and…", answering 2 + 2, understanding simple sentences, a chess master finding a strong move, and recognising that a meek, tidy person with a passion for detail resembles an occupational stereotype. (pdf 23)
- System 1 includes innate skills shared with animals, such as avoiding losses and fearing spiders. It also includes skills that became automatic through long practice, such as reading and social nuance. Some skills are expert-only (chess), others widely shared. (pdf 23–24)
- Some System 1 actions are involuntary: you cannot help understanding a simple sentence or knowing 2 + 2 = 4. Others, like chewing, can be controlled but normally run on automatic pilot. Attention is shared: a loud sound is oriented to by System 1, which mobilises System 2's voluntary attention. (pdf 24)
- **System 2 examples:** bracing for the starter gun, following one voice in a noisy room, counting the letter *a* on a page, parking in a narrow space, filling in a tax form, checking a complex logical argument. All require attention and are disrupted when attention is drawn away. (pdf 24)
- System 2 has some ability to change how System 1 works by programming attention and memory, for example by setting yourself to look for a white-haired woman at a station. Keeping such a set needs continuous effort. (pdf 24–25)
- **Limited attention budget.** If you try to go beyond it, you fail. Effortful activities interfere with each other, so you cannot compute 17 × 24 while turning left into dense traffic. You can do several things at once only if they are easy, such as talking on an empty highway. (pdf 25)
- **Invisible gorilla** (Chabris and Simons). Viewers count passes by the white-shirted team while ignoring the black team. A gorilla-suited woman crosses for 9 seconds. About half of the many thousands of viewers notice nothing. The counting task, especially the instruction to ignore one team, causes the blindness. Nobody watching without that task misses her. (pdf 25)
- Seeing and orienting are automatic System 1 functions, but they need some attention. Non-noticers are initially sure the gorilla was not there. The author's lesson is that we can be blind to the obvious, and blind to our blindness. (pdf 25–26)

*Intuition note:* attention works like a small fixed budget. Spend it all on one demanding task and even a striking event may go unseen.

### Plot Synopsis
- Both systems are active whenever we are awake. System 2 is normally in a low-effort mode. System 1 continuously offers impressions, intuitions, intentions and feelings. If System 2 endorses them they become beliefs and voluntary actions. When all goes smoothly, which is most of the time, System 2 adopts them with little or no modification. (pdf 26)
- System 2 is mobilised when (a) System 1 has no answer, as with 17 × 24; (b) something violates System 1's model of the world ("lamps do not jump, cats do not bark"), which produces surprise and a search for an explanatory story; (c) it detects an error about to be made. It also monitors your behaviour. In summary, System 2 takes over when things get difficult, and it normally has the last word. (pdf 26)
- The division of labour minimises effort and optimises performance. It works well most of the time because System 1 is generally very good at what it does: its models of familiar situations and its short-term predictions are usually accurate. But System 1 has **biases**, systematic errors in specified circumstances. It sometimes answers an easier question than the one asked, it has little understanding of logic and statistics, and it cannot be turned off. You will read a word in a language you know unless your attention is totally elsewhere. (pdf 26–27)

*Intuition note:* System 1 proposes and System 2 usually approves. Errors arise when System 1 proposes something wrong and System 2 has no clue to the error or does not monitor closely enough.

### Conflict
- **Figure 2 task.** Words are printed in two columns. Task 1: say whether each word is upper- or lowercase. Task 2: say whether each is printed left or right of center. In the figure (read from the page image), the left column holds the words LEFT/RIGHT and the right column holds upper/lower, each printed in various positions and in either case. (pdf 27)
- In task 1 the left column was easy and the right column slowed you down. In task 2 the left column was hard and the right column easy. You set yourself for the task words, and in the conflicting column they could not be ignored. Overcoming the competing response was a strain. (pdf 27–28)
- Other cases of automatic reaction versus intention: not staring at an oddly dressed couple, forcing attention onto a boring book, and steering into a skid without braking. The author concludes that one task of System 2 is to overcome System 1's impulses, so System 2 is in charge of self-control. (pdf 28)

### Illusions
- **Müller-Lyer (Figure 3).** Two horizontal lines with fins look different in length, but a ruler shows they are identical. After measuring, System 2 believes they are equal, but you still *see* the bottom line as longer. You cannot decide to see them equal. (pdf 28–29)
- The only defence is to learn to mistrust impressions of line length when fins are attached, which requires recognising the pattern and recalling what you know. If you can do this, you will never again be fooled by the illusion, but you will still see one line as longer. (pdf 29)
- **Cognitive illusions** are illusions of thought. Kahneman's psychotherapy teacher warned (teacher's voice): a patient who describes many failed treatments and quickly decides you are different, so that you feel sure you can help, is "most likely a psychopath"; throw him out. Years later Kahneman learned this was a warning against psychopathic charm, and the leading authority on psychopathy confirmed the advice was sound. (pdf 29–30)
- The analogy: the teacher took it for granted that the sympathy we would feel would not be under our control and would arise from System 1. The teaching was not to distrust feelings generally. A strong attraction to a patient with repeated failed treatments is a danger sign, like the fins. (pdf 30)
- **Can illusions be overcome?** The message of these examples is not encouraging. Because System 1 cannot be turned off, errors of intuition are often hard to prevent. Biases cannot always be avoided, because System 2 may have no clue to the error. Even with cues, prevention takes enhanced monitoring and effort from System 2. Continuous vigilance is impractical, and System 2 is too slow to replace System 1 for routine decisions. The compromise is to recognise error-prone situations and try harder when stakes are high. The book's premise is that it is easier to recognise other people's mistakes than our own. (pdf 30)

*Intuition note:* knowing the truth changes what you believe, but not always what you perceive or feel. So the practical defence is a learned rule: in this pattern, do not trust the impression.

### Useful Fictions
- **Objection (the author's professional colleagues):** saying "System 2 calculates products" is a sin, like "The butler steals the petty cash", because it seems to explain a person's thoughts and actions by little people in the head. They rightly question whether such a sentence explains how products are calculated. (pdf 30)
- **Kahneman replies:** the sentence is a description, not an explanation. It is shorthand, for example, for saying that mental arithmetic is a voluntary activity that takes effort, should not be done while making a left turn, and goes with dilated pupils and a faster heart rate. (pdf 30–31)
- Likewise, "System 2 prevented James from reacting foolishly" means James would have been more aggressive had his capacity for effortful control been disrupted, for example by drink. (pdf 31)
- The systems are "fictitious characters": not systems in the standard sense, and no one part of the brain is their home. They are useful because a sentence is understood more easily when it says what an agent does than what something is. The mind, especially System 1, appears to have a special aptitude for stories about active agents. The names "System 1/2" are short nicknames that take less working memory than "automatic system" and "effortful system". Anything occupying working memory reduces the ability to think. (pdf 31)

### Speaking of System 1 and System 2
Three sample sentences close the chapter, for example "He had an impression, but some of his impressions are illusions." (pdf 32)

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text; costs are invented for illustration.)* Model attention as a budget of 10 units. Say driving on an empty highway costs 1, chatting with a passenger costs 2, computing 17 × 24 costs 8, and turning left into dense traffic costs 7.
- Highway + chat: 1 + 2 = 3 ≤ 10, so both can be done together (easy, undemanding tasks, pdf 25).
- 17 × 24 + left turn: 8 + 7 = 15 > 10, so the budget is exceeded and performance fails. This matches the author's remark that you could not compute 17 × 24 while making a left turn into dense traffic (pdf 25).

The text gives no numbers. It only says the budget is limited and effortful tasks interfere.

## Concepts
- [[system-1]] (new) — automatic, fast, effortless, no sense of voluntary control; source of impressions, intuitions and feelings (pdf 22–23)
- [[system-2]] (new) — allocates attention to effortful activity; often associated with agency, choice and concentration; in charge of self-control; usually endorses System 1 (pdf 22, 26, 28)
- [[attention-budget]] (new) — limited capacity of attention; effortful tasks interfere, easy ones can be combined (pdf 25)
- [[bias]] (new) — systematic error System 1 is prone to make in specified circumstances (pdf 27)
- [[cognitive-illusion]] (new) — illusion of thought; the author compares it closely to Müller-Lyer, where the impression persists after you know the truth (pdf 29–30)
- [[self-control-conflict]] (new) — clash between an automatic response and an intention; System 2 resolves it with effort (pdf 28)
- [[heuristic]] — not defined in this unit; the chapter only says System 1 sometimes answers an easier question than the one asked (pdf 27)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] System 2 monitoring and overruling System 1 impulses ↔ decision-game-theory: bounded rationality, where limited attention constrains choice — both treat effort and attention as scarce.
- [metaphor] System 2 as a self-controller of System 1 ↔ decision-game-theory: commitment devices that limit one's own options — both concern guarding against one's own impulses.

## Questions this raises
- If System 1's biases show up only in "specified circumstances", which circumstances are they, and can they be predicted?
- How far can learned rules (such as mistrusting line-length impressions when fins are present) reduce errors without constant vigilance?
- What can be a test for a statement like "System 2 prevented James…", if the systems are only fictions?
- How does a fixed attention budget connect to how people judge and choose?
