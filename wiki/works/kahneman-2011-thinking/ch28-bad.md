# Bad Events

## L0 — One line
Losses loom larger than gains, as part of a broad "bad is stronger than good" pattern, and this one idea predicts surprising behavior in sport, bargaining, reform, fairness judgments and law.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Loss aversion sounds like common sense. Kahneman shows what follows when outcomes are judged as gains or losses against a reference point (the status quo or a goal). He places it inside a broader negativity dominance (bad outweighs good) in brain and behavior. Golfers putt better to avoid a bogey (one over par) than to make a birdie (one under par). Negotiators treat concessions as losses. Reform plans end up costlier and less effective when losers have influence. People generally judge a firm unfair for imposing losses relative to the earlier price or wage. Courts often separate actual losses from foregone gains (gains not obtained). The unit matters because one simple concept yields precise, non-obvious predictions across very different settings.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a reference point (gain vs. loss relative to it); prospect theory only by name.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Loss aversion is the author's pick for psychology's most significant contribution to behavioral economics. It is obvious in outline but yields specific, surprising predictions (pdf 292).
- Negativity dominance: threats get priority in the brain, and the bad outweighs the good in many domains. Loss aversion is one of its manifestations (pdf 293).
- Goals act as reference points. Failing a goal is felt more strongly than exceeding it is enjoyed (pdf 294).
- Golf evidence: more than 2.5 million putts show a 3.6% difference in success rate, higher when putting for par than for a birdie (pdf 295).
- Negotiations and reforms: concessions are losses to the giver, so agreements are hard to reach and potential losers, if they have political influence, resist harder than potential winners (pdf 296–297).
- Fairness: the existing price or wage is an entitlement. Imposing losses relative to it is generally judged unfair (unless the firm is protecting its own entitlement), while not sharing gains is not branded unfair (pdf 297–299).
- Law distinguishes actual losses from foregone gains. This may be justified by the asymmetry in well-being (Zamir) (pdf 300).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pdf 292).** The idea that outcomes are judged as gains and losses, with losses looming larger, "surprises no one." Kahneman says "we know more than our grandmothers did": loss aversion now sits inside a two-systems model where negativity and escape dominate positivity and approach. He lists consequences: only out-of-pocket losses are compensated in transport; large reforms often fail; golfers putt more accurately for par than for a birdie (pdf 292).

**Negativity Dominance (pdf 292–294).**
- Figure 12 shows frightened eyes versus happy eyes. Each picture was flashed for less than 2/100 of a second and then masked. No observer consciously knew they had seen eyes. Yet the amygdala (the brain's "threat center") responded strongly to the threatening picture (pdf 292).
- The information probably travels by a fast channel that bypasses the visual cortex. Angry schematic faces are processed faster than happy ones. An angry face reportedly "pops out" of a happy crowd, but not the reverse. No comparably rapid mechanism for good news has been detected. Threats are privileged over opportunities (pdf 293).
- Symbolic threats work too. Bad words (war, crime) attract attention faster than happy words (peace, love). Registering a disagreeable opinion sentence takes under a quarter of a second (pdf 293).
- **Rozin:** one cockroach wrecks a bowl of cherries, but a cherry does nothing for a bowl of cockroaches. Loss aversion is one manifestation of broad negativity dominance (pdf 293).
- A paper titled "Bad Is Stronger Than Good" (other scholars) summarizes evidence that bad emotions, parents and feedback have more impact than good ones, bad information is processed more thoroughly, and bad impressions and stereotypes form faster and resist disconfirmation more (pdf 293–294).
- **Gottman:** a stable relationship requires good interactions to outnumber bad ones by at least 5 to 1 (his estimate). A friendship built over years can be ruined by one action (pdf 294).
- Some good/bad distinctions are hardwired (pain bad, sweet good, up to a point). Often the boundary is a reference point that changes with circumstances. **Cabanac** would call finding shelter behind a rock in a cold storm intensely pleasurable, because it signals improvement. The relief fades (pdf 294).

*Intuition note:* The brain seems built to react to danger first. Across eyes, words and relationships, the text shows the bad often counting for more than the good, and loss aversion is one case of this pattern.

**Goals are Reference Points (pdf 294–296).**
- Loss aversion is the relative strength of two motives: avoiding losses is stronger than achieving gains. A reference point may be the status quo or a future goal. Missing the goal is a loss and exceeding it a gain. Aversion to failing is much stronger than the desire to exceed (pdf 294).
- **Cabdrivers.** Their controlling goal is typically a daily earnings target. Economic logic says to work long hours on rainy days (when fares are plentiful) and take leisure on mild days. Loss aversion suggests the opposite. Drivers with a fixed daily target will work more when business is slow and go home early on rainy days (pdf 294–295). This is a prediction in the text; no data are reported here.
- **Golf (Pope and Schweitzer).** Par is the reference point: a birdie is a gain and a bogey a loss. Putting for par avoids a loss. Missing a birdie putt is a foregone gain. Prediction: players try harder for par (pdf 295).
- Result from more than 2.5 million putts: at every distance, success was higher for par than for a birdie. The difference was 3.6%. If Tiger Woods in his best years had putted for birdies as well as for par, his average score would have improved by one stroke and his earnings by almost $1 million per season. Players do not consciously slack off. Their aversion to a bogey apparently adds concentration (pdf 295).
- The concept led to a precise, nonintuitive hypothesis and a finding that surprised everyone, including golfers (pdf 295–296).

*Intuition note:* The same stroke is worth the same on the scorecard, yet in this study players apparently concentrate a little more to avoid a bogey (a "loss") than to make a birdie (a "gain").

**Defending the Status Quo (pdf 296–297).**
- In negotiations, especially renegotiations of existing contracts, existing terms are reference points, and any change is seen as a concession. Your concessions are my gains but your losses. They hurt you more than they please me, so you value them more than I do, and the same holds in reverse. Agreement is hard (pdf 296).
- Shrinking pies require allocating losses and are especially hard. People are more easygoing over an expanding pie (pdf 296).
- Negotiators send messages to set a reference point and anchor the other side. They may pretend attachment to a good they see as a bargaining chip. Under a norm of reciprocity, a "painful" concession invites an equally painful one (pdf 296).
- Animals fight harder to prevent losses than to gain. When a territory holder is challenged by a rival, the owner almost always wins, usually within seconds (a biologist's observation) (pdf 296).
- Reforms: plans usually produce many winners and some losers with overall improvement. If the affected parties have political influence, potential losers are more active and determined than winners. The outcome is biased toward them, so it is costlier and less effective. Grandfather clauses protect current stakeholders. Loss aversion is a conservative force keeping life near the reference point. It favors minimal change for institutions and individuals, helping keep us stable in neighborhood, marriage and job (pdf 296–297).

**Loss Aversion in the Law (pdf 297–300).**
- Kahneman, Thaler and Knetsch studied fairness through weekly telephone-survey questionnaires in Vancouver/Toronto. Question: does the opprobrium (public disapproval) attached to unfairness constrain profit seeking? It does (pdf 297).
- Rule found: the existing wage, price or rent is a reference point with the nature of an entitlement. The public considers it unfair for a firm to impose losses on customers or workers relative to the reference transaction, unless it must do so to protect its own entitlement (pdf 297).
- **Snow shovels:** $15 raised to $20 after a snowstorm. 82% rated this Unfair or Very Unfair. The price rise is seen as a loss imposed "because it can" (pdf 298).
- **Photocopy shop (1984 dollars, about 100% inflation since):** cutting an employee's wage from $9 to $7 when unemployment is high: 83% Unfair or Very Unfair. Hiring a replacement at $7 after the employee leaves: 73% Acceptable. The entitlement is personal to the current worker (pdf 298).
- Dual entitlements: a firm facing a threatened loss may pass it on. Most respondents did not think it unfair to cut wages when profitability is falling (pdf 298–299).
- Gains follow different rules. A firm with lower costs need not share the bonanza. Generosity is liked, but not sharing is not branded unfair. Respondents showed indignation only when a firm exploited its power to break informal contracts with workers or customers and impose a loss on others to increase its profit (pdf 299).
- The paper went to the *American Economic Review* against what was then accepted wisdom among many economists, that self-interest rules and fairness concerns are generally irrelevant. It is often cited and its conclusions "have stood the test of time." Later research supports reference-dependent fairness and shows economic significance (pdf 299).
- Catalog study: customers who learned that the merchant now charged less for a product they had bought at a higher price cut future purchases by 15%, an average loss of $90 per customer. Those who bought more items at higher prices reacted most, and the losses far exceeded the gains from the extra purchases (pdf 299). Employers who violate fairness rules are punished by reduced productivity, and merchants with unfair pricing can expect lost sales. Unfairly imposing losses can also be risky if the victims can retaliate (pdf 299).
- Strangers who see unfair behavior often join in the punishment. In MRI studies, altruistic punishment is accompanied by more activity in "pleasure centers." It "could well be the glue that holds societies together" (author's speculation). But brains do not reward generosity as reliably as they punish meanness: again an asymmetry (pdf 299–300).
- Law: **Cohen and Knetsch** found a sharp distinction between actual losses and foregone gains. A merchant whose goods are lost in transit may recover costs but is unlikely to recover lost profits. "Possession is nine-tenths of the law" confirms the reference point's moral status. **Zamir** makes the "provocative point" that the distinction may be justified by asymmetrical effects on well-being: if people who lose suffer more than those who merely fail to gain, they may also deserve more protection from the law (pdf 300).

**Speaking of Losses (pdf 300–301).** Sample sentences: a reform will fail because losers fight harder; each negotiator thinks the other's concessions are less painful, and both are wrong; renegotiation is easier if the pie is seen as expanding; tenants feel entitled to current terms; clients accept price rises when they know costs rose (pdf 300–301).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A cabdriver has a fixed daily target of $120. On rainy days she earns $30 per hour; on mild days $15 per hour.
- Target-driven: rainy day $120 ÷ $30 = 4 hours. Mild day $120 ÷ $15 = 8 hours.
- Economic logic: an hour on a rainy day earns twice as much as on a mild day, so she should work *more* on rainy days. Target-driven behavior gives the reverse: 4 hours versus 8.
- Total over the two days: 4 + 8 = 12 hours for $240. Working 8 hours on the rainy day and 4 on the mild day would earn 8 × $30 + 4 × $15 = $240 + $60 = $300 in the same 12 hours.

This is an illustration of the text's prediction, not data.

## Concepts
- [[loss-aversion]] — we are driven more strongly to avoid losses than to achieve gains (pdf 294)
- [[reference-dependence]] — good vs. bad is judged relative to a status quo, goal, par, or existing price (pdf 294)
- [[negativity-dominance]] (new) — broad pattern: bad outweighs good in attention, impact, and processing; loss aversion is one case (pdf 293)
- [[prospect-theory]] (new) — theory the author uses to predict that par putts count more than birdie putts (pdf 295)
- [[dual-entitlement]] (new) — the firm is entitled to its current profit and individuals to their current terms (wage, price, rent); imposing losses on them to increase profit is unfair (pdf 298–299)
- [[altruistic-punishment]] (new) — strangers punish unfair behavior toward others; brain activity suggests it is rewarding (pdf 300)
- [[framing-effect]] — not named here; the status quo as reference shapes how a change is judged (pdf 296)
