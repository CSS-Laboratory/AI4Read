# Frames and Reality

## L0 — One line
Logically equivalent descriptions of a choice can produce different decisions, because System 1 reacts to words and not to the underlying reality, so preferences are often attached to frames and not to states of the world.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks whether people's choices depend on the words used to describe the same outcome. They do. Gain/loss wording (keep £20 vs lose £30), survival vs mortality, lives saved vs lives lost, and tax "reduction" vs "surcharge" all shift choices or reactions. Experts are affected too. In the cases studied, no underlying preference is being distorted, because the intuitions themselves are about the description. Some frames are still better than others: broad accounts and gallons-per-mile lead to better decisions, and the organ-donation default matters hugely. This matters for policy, and it counts against the rational-agent model, which assumes choices are reality-bound.

**Prerequisites:** System 1 / System 2 (fast, automatic thinking vs slow, effortful thinking); expected value; basic prospect-theory idea that people tend to prefer a sure gain to a gamble, but tend to gamble when both outcomes are bad.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Two meanings of "meaning."** Equivalent sentences ("Italy won" / "France lost") have identical truth conditions but evoke different associations. Econs follow the first; Humans react to the second (pdf 354).
- **Emotional framing leaks into choice.** Costs feel less bad than losses, and survival sounds better than mortality. Physicians were as susceptible as laypeople (pdf 355, 357–358).
- **Neuro evidence.** Frame-conforming choices went with more amygdala activity. Choosing the sure thing despite the LOSE label went with more anterior cingulate activity. The least frame-susceptible ("rational") subjects showed more activity in a frontal area, and they were not the ones with the strongest neural evidence of conflict (pdf 357).
- **Risk attitudes are not reality-bound.** In the Asian disease problem, people tend to be risk averse when outcomes are framed as gains and risk seeking when both outcomes are framed as losses. Public-health professionals showed the same pattern (pdf 359).
- **No hidden true preference.** Confronted with their inconsistency, people usually fall into embarrassed silence. Moral intuitions attach to descriptions, not to substance (pdf 359–361).
- **Some frames are better.** Broader accounts help with sunk costs, gallons-per-mile corrects the mpg illusion, and the defaults in organ-donation forms matter hugely (pdf 361–364).
- **Organ donation is best explained by System 2 laziness.** Large national differences in donation rates are attributed to opt-out vs opt-in forms. This counts against the rational-agent theory (pdf 363–364).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pdf 354)
- **Logical meaning:** "Italy won" and "France lost" designate the same state of the world, so their truth conditions are identical. Econs' preferences are reality-bound: they are about states of the world, not the words describing them (pdf 354).
- **Associative meaning:** What happens in your associative machinery when you understand a sentence. "Italy won" evokes the Italian team's play. "France lost" evokes French errors, such as Zidane's head butt (pdf 354).
- Because equivalent statements evoke different reactions, Humans cannot be as reliably rational as Econs (pdf 354).

*Intuition note:* A mind that reacts to associations, not only to logic, will treat two descriptions of one fact as two different things. This is the root of the emotional framing effects below (the organ-donation effect is explained differently, by System 2 laziness).

### Emotional Framing (pdf 354–358)
- **Framing effects** (Kahneman and Tversky's label) are unjustified influences of formulation on beliefs and preferences (pdf 354).
- **Two-gamble example.** (a) Accept a gamble with a 10% chance to win $95 and a 90% chance to lose $5? (b) Pay $5 for a lottery with a 10% chance to win $100 and a 90% chance of nothing? Both leave you richer by $95 or poorer by $5. Version (b) draws many more "yes" answers. Losses evoke stronger negative feelings than costs (pdf 354–355).
- **Thaler's "costs are not losses."** After failing to ban differential pricing, the credit-card lobby's fallback was to call the difference a cash discount, not a credit surcharge. People more readily forgo a discount than pay a surcharge. The two may be economically equivalent but are not emotionally equivalent (pdf 355).
- **UCL neuroimaging study** (Figure 14). Each trial first tells the subject to imagine receiving, for example, £50. The subject then chooses between a sure outcome and a wheel gamble: white pays the whole amount, black pays nothing. The sure outcome equals the gamble's expected value, here £20. It is framed either as KEEP £20 or as LOSE £30 (pdf 355–356).
  - A reality-bound Econ would respond the same way to both frames. System 1 is expected to be biased toward the sure option under KEEP and against it under LOSE (pdf 356).
  - Trials were sorted afterwards. "Conforming" trials are those with the sure thing chosen under KEEP, or the gamble under LOSE. The rest are "non-conforming" (pdf 356).
- **Three findings** (pdf 357):
  1. The amygdala (linked to emotional arousal) was most likely active when choices conformed to the frame. It is "a likely suspect" for involvement in System 1.
  2. The anterior cingulate (linked to conflict and self-control) was more active when subjects chose the sure thing despite the LOSE label. Resisting System 1 apparently involves conflict.
  3. The least frame-susceptible ("most rational") subjects showed enhanced activity in a frontal area that combines emotion and reasoning. They were not those with the strongest neural evidence of conflict. It appears they were (often, not always) reality-bound with little conflict.
- **Lung-cancer study** (Tversky with Harvard colleagues). Physicians compared surgery and radiation. Five-year survival favors surgery, but surgery is riskier in the short term. One group read a 90% one-month survival rate; the other read a 10% mortality rate in the first month. In the survival frame 84% chose surgery. In the mortality frame 50% favored radiation. Physicians were as susceptible as hospital patients and business-school students (pdf 357–358).
- **Why the brain study differs from the survey.** Brain-study participants saw both frames many times, so they could notice the distraction and translate LOSE into its KEEP equivalent. The few who managed this were probably among the "rational" agents. The physicians had no reason to suspect that the other frame would change their choice. Reframing is effortful, and System 2 is normally lazy, so we rarely discover how frame-bound our preferences are (pdf 358).

*Intuition note:* Equivalent options feel different because the words carry emotion. You rarely notice this unless you see both versions, and most people seldom get the chance.

### Empty Intuitions (pdf 358–361)
- **Asian disease problem.** A disease is expected to kill 600 people.
  - Frame 1: A saves 200 for sure. B has a 1/3 chance that 600 are saved and a 2/3 chance that none are saved. A substantial majority choose A (pdf 358–359).
  - Frame 2: A′ means 400 die for sure. B′ has a 1/3 chance that nobody dies and a 2/3 chance that 600 die. A large majority choose the gamble B′ (pdf 359).
  - A = A′ and B = B′ in consequences (pdf 359).
- **Link to prospect theory.** Decision makers tend to be risk averse when outcomes are good and risk seeking when both outcomes are negative. This was established for money. The disease problem shows the same rule for lives saved or lost, so risk attitudes are not reality-bound (pdf 359).
- **Public-health professionals** given the problem by Tversky were also susceptible to the framing. Kahneman says we must get used to the idea that important decisions are influenced, "if not governed," by System 1 (pdf 359).
- **Confronted with the inconsistency,** people usually respond with embarrassed silence. The original intuitions came from System 1 and had no more moral basis than the preference for keeping £20. System 2 has no moral intuitions of its own to answer (pdf 359–360).
- **Schelling's tax example** (from *Choice and Consequence*; framing was not his main concern) (pdf 360):
  - **Students' view 1:** A child exemption larger for the rich than for the poor is completely unacceptable.
  - **Reframe:** Make a two-child family the default. Families with fewer children then pay a surcharge.
  - **Students' view 2:** The childless poor should not pay as large a surcharge as the childless rich. They rejected this just as vehemently.
  - **Schelling's argument:** The tax gap between childless and two-child families is a "reduction" in version 1 and an "increase" in version 2. If you want the poor to receive the same (or greater) benefit as the rich for having children, then you must want the poor to pay at least the same penalty as the rich for being childless. So both proposals cannot be rejected logically (pdf 360).
- **Kahneman's conclusion.** "When in doubt, favor the poor" is a System 1 rule that gives contradictory answers depending on the frame. Asked how the tax code should actually treat families, readers are likely dumbfounded. Our moral feelings attach to frames, not to reality (pdf 360–361).
- **Key claim.** Framing should not be viewed as masking or distorting an underlying preference. In these cases no underlying preference is distorted: preferences are about framed problems, moral intuitions about descriptions (pdf 361).

*Intuition note:* If different wordings flip your answer and you cannot say which answer is right, the author concludes, for his examples, that no underlying preference was being masked by the frame.

### Good Frames (pdf 361–364)
- **Theater tickets.** Version 1: the woman lost two $80 tickets already bought. Version 2: she lost $160 cash meant for the tickets and could use a credit card. Most think she will not rebuy after losing tickets, but will charge tickets after losing cash (pdf 361–362).
  - **Explanation:** mental accounting and the sunk-cost fallacy. Lost tickets are posted to the play's account, so the cost seems doubled. Lost cash is charged to "general revenue" (pdf 362).
  - **Better frame:** the cash version. The loss is sunk either way, and only current options matter. Advice: "Would you have bought tickets if you had lost the equivalent amount of cash?" Broader frames and inclusive accounts generally lead to more rational decisions (pdf 362).
- **MPG illusion** (Larrick and Soll, *Science*, 2008). Adam moves from 12 to 14 mpg; Beth from 30 to 40 mpg. Most think Beth's change matters more (pdf 362–363). At 10,000 miles each (pdf 363):
  $$\text{Adam: } 833 \to 714 \text{ gal, saving } 119 \text{ gal;}\qquad \text{Beth: } 333 \to 250 \text{ gal, saving } 83 \text{ gal.}$$
  Gallons-per-mile (or liters per 100 km) is the right frame. The sticker on new US cars from 2013 will include it, in small print (pdf 363).
- **Organ donation** (pdf 363–364):
  - Donation rates (2003 article): close to 100% in Austria vs 12% in Germany; 86% in Sweden vs 4% in Denmark (pdf 363).
  - High-donation countries use an opt-out form; low-donation countries use opt-in. The best single predictor is the default option (pdf 363–364).
  - Unlike other framing effects traced to System 1, this one is best explained by System 2's laziness. If people have not already decided, they must make the effort to think. Kahneman imagines boxes with "2 + 2 = ?" vs "13 × 37 = ?" and expects donations to be swayed (pdf 364).
  - If you value a large supply of organs, you will not be neutral between formulations that yield almost 100% and 4% (pdf 364).
- **Wider verdict.** An important choice is controlled by an inconsequential feature of the situation. A theory must say some events are impossible, and observing them falsifies it. Kahneman counts this as a point against the rational-agent theory. Theories can survive long after evidence falsifies them, and the rational-agent model did survive. Believers assume formulation cannot determine preferences on significant problems, so they do not investigate it. Skeptics are sensitive to such factors (pdf 364).

*Intuition note:* Once the role of formulation is acknowledged, the question is which formulation to adopt; the author's good examples are frames that put the real consequences in view (broad accounts, gallons per mile) and a default that gives the outcome we want.

### Speaking of Frames and Reality (pdf 365)
Four sample remarks: frame the outcome as money kept, not lost; change the reference point ("imagine we did not own it"); charge the loss to "general revenue"; opt-out vs opt-in mailing lists (pdf 365).

## Toy example (ours)
A shop sells an item for $12 on credit and $10 in cash.
- Frame A (a cash discount of $2 off the $12 list price): the cash payer pays 12 − 2 = $10.
- Frame B (a credit surcharge of $2 added to the $10 list price): the credit payer pays 10 + 2 = $12.

Both frames give $10 for cash and $12 for credit, so an Econ is indifferent. Per the book's cash-discount example (pdf 355), people forgo a discount more readily than they pay a surcharge, so Frame B should provoke more resistance. Same facts, different feeling.

Gamble check (same logic as pdf 354): a gamble with a 20% chance to win $48 and an 80% chance to lose $2, and a $2 ticket with a 20% chance of $50, both give +48 or −2 (50 − 2 = 48; 0 − 2 = −2).

## Concepts
- [[framing-effect]] — unjustified influence of formulation on beliefs and preferences (pdf 354)
- [[reference-dependence]] — outcomes judged relative to a reference point, such as keeping vs losing, or a tax default (pdf 360–361)
- [[risk-aversion]] — tending to prefer the sure thing when outcomes are good; Kahneman adds that people tend to be risk seeking when both outcomes are negative (pdf 359)
- [[heuristic]] — System 1's quick rule, such as "favor the poor," which can contradict itself (pdf 360–361)
- [[system-1-system-2]] (new) — System 1 reacts automatically to emotional words; System 2 is effortful and lazy (pdf 356, 358)
- [[econs-vs-humans]] (new) — Econs have reality-bound preferences; Humans' are frame-bound (pdf 354)
- [[reality-bound-preferences]] (new) — preferences that depend only on states of the world, not on descriptions (pdf 354)
- [[mental-accounting]] (new) — a loss is posted to a specific account, and its significance depends on the account (pdf 362)
- [[sunk-cost-fallacy]] (new) — letting past, unrecoverable costs influence current decisions (pdf 362)
- [[default-effect]] (new) — the preset option strongly predicts choices (pdf 363–364)
- [[neuroeconomics]] (new) — study of what the brain does during decisions (pdf 356)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Framing effect ↔ decision theory: description invariance. Equivalent descriptions of an option should yield the same choice, and framing violates this.
- [structural-analogy] Default effect ↔ mechanism design: the choice rule (opt-in vs opt-out) shapes outcomes even when the options are unchanged.
- [structural-analogy] MPG vs gallons-per-mile ↔ nonlinear transformations: the reciprocal scale changes which differences look large (12→14 mpg saves more than 30→40).
- [metaphor] Gain/loss frame reversal ↔ prospect theory's reflection of risk attitude around a reference point.

## Questions this raises
- If no underlying preference exists in cases like Schelling's, how should policy makers decide which frame to use?
- Which framing effects are due to System 1 emotion and which to System 2 laziness? Do they need different remedies?
- How can people be trained to reframe, and does repeated exposure to both frames (as in the brain study) reliably produce "rational" choices?
- If skeptics of rationality choose the default for the public, how can that be reconciled with respecting individual preferences?
