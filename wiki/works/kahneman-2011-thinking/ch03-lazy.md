# The Lazy Controller

## L0 — One line
System 2 (slow, deliberate thinking) draws on a limited pool of effort that it also uses for self-control, so it tends to be lazy and often endorses the quick answers of System 1 without checking them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do smart people accept wrong intuitive answers? Kahneman argues that effortful thinking and self-control are both mental work and share one limited budget. When System 2 is busy or depleted, System 1 has more influence on behavior. Even when System 2 is free, many people do not check their intuitions. The bat-and-ball puzzle, the roses syllogism and the Michigan murder question all show this. People who check more are called "engaged" (Stanovich: more "rational"). Self-control at age four predicts later intelligence, and low scorers on the Cognitive Reflection Test are more impulsive. Lazy checking is a recurring source of error in the book.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Law of least effort.** Kahneman finds a "stroll" speed in walking and a similar easy speed for System 2. Beyond that speed, thinking and walking compete for the same limited resource. People avoid effortful thinking when they can. (pdf 41–42)
- **Self-control and deliberate thought draw on the same budget.** Keeping a coherent train of thought needs discipline. Flow is the exception: deep absorption needs no self-control. (pdf 42)
- **Cognitive load weakens self-control.** With a list of digits in memory, people more often pick chocolate cake over fruit salad. System 1 has more influence when System 2 is busy. (pdf 42–43)
- **Ego depletion.** An act of self-control makes the next one harder. Unlike cognitive load, it is partly a loss of motivation, so a strong incentive can overcome it. (pdf 43–44)
- **Glucose.** Baumeister's group links mental effort to blood glucose. In their experiment, glucose lemonade prevented the depletion effect and Splenda lemonade did not. The parole-judges study shows a related pattern in real decisions. (pdf 44–45)
- **System 2 as a lazy monitor.** One job of System 2 is to check what System 1 suggests. In the bat-and-ball puzzle, more than 50% of students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton gave the intuitive wrong answer. (pdf 45–46)
- **When we believe a conclusion, we accept weak arguments for it.** The roses syllogism shows this. (pdf 46–47)
- **Failures of effort, not of ability.** Students who fail these minitests can solve harder problems. The cause is, at least to some extent, insufficient motivation. (pdf 48)
- **Self-control, intelligence and the Cognitive Reflection Test.** Waiting children (Mischel) later had better executive control and higher intelligence scores. Low CRT scorers are more impulsive and impatient. (pdf 48–49)
- **Intelligence is not rationality (Stanovich).** The "algorithmic mind" (brain power) is separate from the "reflective mind" (rationality). The bat-and-ball question predicts susceptibility to error somewhat better than IQ tests. (pdf 50)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the stroll and the limits of System 2 (untitled section)
- Kahneman walks four miles in the Berkeley hills. A speed of about 17 minutes per mile feels like a stroll: some physical effort, but no strain and no conflict, and he can think and work while walking. (pdf 41)
- System 2 also has a natural speed. Routine monitoring, small decisions while driving, reading a newspaper and polite chat all take little effort. (pdf 41)
- At the extremes, walking and thinking compete for System 2's limited resources. Test: ask a friend you are walking with to compute 23 × 78 in his head right away. He will almost certainly stop walking. Heavy demands on short-term memory cannot be combined with other effort. (pdf 41)
- Not all slow thinking is intense. Kahneman did "the best thinking of my life" on leisurely walks with Amos. (pdf 41)
- Faster walking brings a sharp drop in his ability to think coherently. At his top sustainable speed, about 14 minutes per mile, he does not try to think of anything else. He needs both physical effort and a mental effort of self-control to resist the urge to slow down. Conclusion: self-control and deliberate thought apparently draw on the same limited budget of effort. (pdf 41–42)
- For most people, keeping a coherent train of thought also needs self-control. Frequent task switching and speeded-up mental work are probably not pleasant, and people avoid them. This is why the law of least effort is a law. His own habit of checking e-mail and the refrigerator while writing shows an urge to escape. (pdf 42)
- **Flow.** Csikszentmihalyi named the state of "effortless concentration so deep" that people lose their sense of time, of themselves and of their problems. He called it an "optimal experience". Painting, racing motorcycles and even writing can induce it. (pdf 42)
- Flow separates two forms of effort: concentration on the task, and deliberate control of attention. Riding a motorcycle at 150 mph or playing competitive chess is very effortful. In flow, though, keeping attention focused needs no self-control, which frees resources for the task. (pdf 42)

> **Intuition note.** There are two kinds of "trying hard": doing the task, and forcing yourself to keep at it. Flow removes the second kind.

### The Busy and Depleted System 2
- Self-control and cognitive effort are both forms of mental work. People facing a demanding cognitive task and a temptation at the same time are more likely to give in. (pdf 42)
- Example: keep seven digits in mind, as the top priority, for a minute or two. You are then offered chocolate cake or fruit salad. You are more likely to choose the cake. "System 1 has more influence on behavior when System 2 is busy, and it has a sweet tooth." (pdf 42–43)
- Cognitively busy people are also more likely to make selfish choices, use sexist language and make superficial social judgments. Other causes of weaker self-control: a few drinks, a sleepless night, being at the wrong time of day for your type (morning or night person), and anxious over-concern with performance that fills short-term memory with pointless thoughts. Conclusion: controlling thoughts and behavior is one of the tasks of System 2. (pdf 43)
- **Ego depletion.** Baumeister and colleagues show that voluntary effort of all kinds (cognitive, emotional, physical) draws at least partly on a shared pool of mental energy. Their experiments use *successive* tasks. If you had to force yourself to do something, you are less willing or able to exert self-control on the next challenge. This is named ego depletion. (pdf 43)
- Two demonstrations (pdf 43):
  - People who suppress their emotional reaction to a charged film later hold a dynamometer grip for less time.
  - People who ate radishes and celery while resisting chocolate and cookies later gave up sooner on a difficult cognitive task.
- Tasks known to deplete (all involve conflict and suppressing a natural tendency) (pdf 43–44):
  - avoiding the thought of white bears
  - inhibiting the emotional response to a stirring film
  - making a series of choices that involve conflict
  - trying to impress others
  - responding kindly to a partner's bad behavior
  - interacting with a person of a different race (for prejudiced individuals)
- Signs of depletion (pdf 44):
  - deviating from one's diet
  - overspending on impulsive purchases
  - reacting aggressively to provocation
  - persisting less time in a handgrip task
  - performing poorly in cognitive tasks and logical decision making
- **Depletion is not the same as cognitive load.** Ego depletion is at least partly a loss of motivation. After one act of self-control you do not feel like making another effort, though you could if you had to. In several experiments a strong incentive let people resist depletion. By contrast, when six digits must be held in memory during a task, more effort is not an option. (pdf 44)
- **Glucose.** Baumeister's team found that mental energy is "more than a mere metaphor". The nervous system uses a lot of glucose. Hard reasoning or self-control lowers blood glucose, like a sprinter drawing down glucose stored in her muscles. The implication, confirmed in several experiments, is that glucose can undo depletion. (pdf 44)
- **Experiment (pdf 44–45).**
  1. Volunteers watched a silent film of a woman being interviewed and interpreted her body language, while ignoring words crossing the screen. They refocused if distracted. This act of self-control is known to cause depletion.
  2. All drank lemonade, sweetened with glucose for half and with Splenda for the rest.
  3. All then did a task where an intuitive response must be overcome to get the right answer. Intuitive errors are normally much more frequent in depleted people.
  4. Result: Splenda drinkers showed the depletion effect, and glucose drinkers did not.
- Kahneman says it will take time to learn whether glucose-depleting tasks also cause the arousal seen in pupil size and heart rate. (pdf 45)
- **Parole judges (PNAS study) (pdf 45).**
  - Eight Israeli judges reviewed parole applications in random order, about 6 minutes each. The default is denial, and only 35% are approved.
  - The authors plotted approval rate against time since the last food break (three breaks a day).
  - The approval rate spikes to about 65% after each meal, then falls steadily over about two hours to about zero just before the next meal.
  - The authors checked many alternative explanations. The best account: tired and hungry judges fall back on the easier default of denial. Fatigue and hunger probably both play a role.

> **Intuition note.** Effort and willpower behave like a tank that empties. When it is low, the easy default wins. The easy default is the intuitive answer, cake, or "no".

### The Lazy System 2
- One main function of System 2 is to monitor and control thoughts and actions "suggested" by System 1. It lets some be expressed in behavior and suppresses or modifies others. (pdf 45)
- **Bat-and-ball puzzle.** A bat and ball cost $1.10. The bat costs one dollar more than the ball. How much is the ball? (pdf 45)
  - The intuitive answer is 10¢. It is appealing and wrong. If the ball were 10¢, the bat would be $1.10 and the total would be $1.20. (pdf 46)
  - The correct answer is 5¢. (pdf 46)
  - (added step) Let $b$ be the ball's price in dollars. The bat costs $b + 1$. Total: $b + (b+1) = 1.10$, so $2b = 0.10$ and $b = 0.05$. Check: $0.05 + 1.05 = 1.10$.
  - People who got the right answer probably also felt the pull of 10¢ and resisted it. (pdf 46)
  - Frederick's reasoning: anyone who says 10¢ did not actively check. Their System 2 endorsed an answer it could have rejected with a small effort. They also missed a social cue, since nobody puts a puzzle with an obvious answer in a questionnaire. Checking costs only a few seconds, so the failure to check is remarkable. (pdf 46)
  - Results: more than 50% of Harvard, MIT and Princeton students gave the intuitive answer. At less selective universities the failure rate was above 80%. This is a recurrent theme: many people are overconfident and trust their intuitions too much, and they find effort mildly unpleasant. (pdf 46)
- **Roses syllogism.** Premises: "All roses are flowers." "Some flowers fade quickly." Conclusion: "Therefore some roses fade quickly." (pdf 46)
  - A large majority of college students call it valid. It is not, because the quickly fading flowers might include no roses. (pdf 46)
  - A plausible answer comes at once and the idea "it's true, it's true!" blocks checking the logic. (pdf 47)
  - Implication: when people believe a conclusion, they readily believe unsound arguments for it. "If System 1 is involved, the conclusion comes first and the arguments follow." (pdf 47)
- **Michigan murders.** "How many murders occur in the state of Michigan in one year?" (Frederick). (pdf 47)
  - The trick is whether you remember Detroit, a high-crime city, is in Michigan. Students know it is the largest city in Michigan, but known facts do not always come to mind. (pdf 47)
  - Those who remember Detroit give higher estimates. A majority did not think of it. The average guess for Michigan was lower than the average guess for Detroit alone. (pdf 47)
  - Blame is shared. Retrieval from memory is automatic (System 1) and differs between people (residents, geography buffs, more intelligent people have richer representations). Intelligence is "also the ability to find relevant material in memory and to deploy attention when needed". Deliberate search and checking belong to System 2 and vary among individuals. (pdf 47)
- **Common thread.** Failing these minitests is partly a lack of motivation. Students admitted to good universities can reason through them and solve much harder problems. Their ease in being "satisfied enough to stop thinking" is troubling. "Lazy" is harsh but not unfair. Those who avoid intellectual sloth are "engaged": more alert, more active, more skeptical about intuitions. Stanovich would call them more rational. (pdf 48)

> **Intuition note.** The danger is not that System 2 cannot find the answer. It often does not start looking, because an answer already feels right.

### Intelligence, Control, Rationality
- Researchers ask a correlation question: if people were ranked by self-control and by cognitive aptitude, would the rankings be similar? (pdf 48)
- **Mischel's delayed-gratification experiment (with an Oreo and cookies).** (pdf 48–49)
  - Four-year-olds chose between one cookie available at any time (by ringing a bell) and two cookies if they waited 15 minutes alone in a bare room.
  - About half waited the full 15 minutes, mainly by keeping attention away from the reward.
  - Ten or fifteen years later the resisters had better executive control, especially in reallocating attention. As young adults they were less likely to take drugs. They also had substantially higher intelligence scores.
- **Oregon training study.** Children aged 4–6 played attention-demanding computer games in five 40-minute sessions. In one game they moved a cartoon cat to a shrinking grassy area while avoiding a growing muddy area. Attention training improved executive control, and nonverbal intelligence scores also improved, with the gain lasting several months. The same group found specific genes involved in attention control, an effect of parenting techniques, and a close link between control of attention and control of emotions. (pdf 49)
- **Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT).** Frederick built it from the bat-and-ball problem plus two other questions that invite a compelling wrong intuitive answer. (The text says the questions are "shown here", but they are not in the pages given.) (pdf 49)
  - Low scorers have a weak supervisory System 2. They answer with the first idea and will not invest effort to check. (pdf 49)
  - They are also impulsive, impatient and keen on immediate gratification. (pdf 49)
  - 63% of the intuitive respondents prefer $3,400 this month to $3,800 next month. Only 37% of those who solve all three puzzles prefer the smaller sooner amount. (pdf 49)
  - For overnight delivery of a book, low CRT scorers will pay twice as much as high scorers. (pdf 49)
  - Frederick's findings suggest the "characters of our psychodrama" have different "personalities". System 1 is impulsive and intuitive. System 2 is capable of reasoning and cautious, but for some people also lazy. Some people are more like their System 2 and others closer to their System 1. The CRT is "one of the better predictors of lazy thinking". (pdf 49–50)
- **Stanovich and West.** They introduced the terms System 1 and System 2 (now they prefer Type 1 and Type 2 processes). They ask what makes some people more susceptible to judgment biases. His book *Rationality and the Reflective Mind* splits System 2 into two "minds". (pdf 50)
  - The *algorithmic* mind does slow, demanding computation. It is the brain power measured by intelligence tests and by quick task switching.
  - High intelligence does not make people immune to bias. Another ability, *rationality*, is involved. It is similar to Kahneman's "engaged" person. Superficial or lazy thinking is a flaw of the reflective mind, a failure of rationality.
  - Support: the bat-and-ball question and similar ones are somewhat better indicators of susceptibility to cognitive errors than conventional intelligence measures such as IQ tests.
  - Kahneman: "Time will tell" whether the intelligence/rationality distinction leads to new discoveries.

> **Intuition note.** Being able to think hard and being willing to think hard are different. Rationality, in this sense, is the willingness part.

### Speaking of Control (closing quotations, pdf 50–51)
Four sample sentences for everyday use: a person in flow does not struggle to stay on task. A depleted person falls back on standard procedures. A speaker who did not check whether he made sense may have a lazy System 2 or may be tired. Someone who says the first thing that comes to mind may also have trouble delaying gratification, which suggests a weak System 2. (pdf 50–51)

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**A. Same trap, new numbers.** A pen and a notebook cost $2.20 together. The notebook costs $2.00 more than the pen. What does the pen cost?
- Intuition: subtract $2.00 from $2.20 and say the pen costs 20¢.
- Check: pen 0.20, notebook 2.20, total 2.40, not 2.20. So 20¢ fails.
- Solve: let $p$ be the pen's price. The notebook costs $p + 2.00$. Then $p + (p + 2.00) = 2.20$, so $2p = 0.20$ and $p = 0.10$.
- Check: pen 0.10, notebook 2.10, total 2.20. ✓. The pen costs 10¢.
- The check takes one addition. This is the cheap step that, according to Kahneman, lazy System 2 skips.

**B. A counter-model for the roses syllogism.** Suppose there are two flowers: a rose R and a tulip T. T fades quickly and R does not.
- "All roses are flowers": true.
- "Some flowers fade quickly": true, since T does.
- "Some roses fade quickly": false, since R does not.
- Both premises are true and the conclusion is false, so the argument is invalid.

**C. The delay choice in numbers.** Taking $3,800 next month instead of $3,400 now gives an extra $400 for waiting one month. That is $400 / $3,400 ≈ 0.1176, about 11.8% in one month. Preferring the $3,400 means being unwilling to wait for that return.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Law of least effort and "satisfied enough to stop thinking" ↔ decision theory: satisficing under bounded rationality — both describe stopping search at an acceptable answer because search is costly.
- [structural-analogy] Cost of checking vs. benefit of avoiding an error ↔ decision theory: costly information acquisition — checking is a small-cost action that many people do not take.
- [structural-analogy] Preference for $3,400 now over $3,800 next month ↔ economics: time discounting and impatience — here linked to impulsive intuitive thinking.
- [metaphor] "Mental energy" / glucose as a depleting budget ↔ economics: scarce resource budget constraint — effort is treated like a budget, but the text does not give a formal model.
- [structural-analogy] Judges' default of denial when depleted ↔ game theory and decisions: defaults and status-quo bias in choice under fatigue.

## Questions this raises
- If System 2 is lazy by default, which situations or incentives reliably get people to check? (The text says a strong incentive helped against ego depletion, pdf 44.)
- How much of the CRT's predictive power is willingness versus ability? The text says it predicts error better than IQ tests, but only "somewhat" (pdf 50).
- Could more attention training (as in the Oregon study) raise rationality as well as test scores, and for how long?
- Do the glucose and ego-depletion findings hold up when other researchers test them, and does the parole result reflect hunger or fatigue? (The text says "both ... probably play a role", pdf 45.)
