# The Lazy Controller

## L0 — One line
Slow, effortful thinking (System 2) and self-control seem to draw on one limited effort budget, and people often accept an intuitive answer without checking it.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why does careful thinking so often not happen? Kahneman suggests that System 2 (slow, effortful thinking) and self-control apparently draw on the same limited budget of effort. When System 2 is busy or depleted, System 1 (fast intuition) has more influence. Many people, even at top universities, accept an intuitive answer without checking it, as in the bat-and-ball puzzle. Low scorers on a checking test are also more impulsive. Kahneman reports Stanovich's view that rationality (being "engaged") differs from intelligence. This matters because failures on simple puzzles like these seem to come, at least to some extent, from low motivation to check, not only from inability.

**Prerequisites:** The System 1 / System 2 distinction (fast automatic vs slow effortful thinking); basic algebra for the bat-and-ball puzzle.

## L2 — Key ideas
- System 2 has a "natural speed". Strolling and light thinking fit together, but at the extremes (heavy mental load, fast walking) they appear to compete (pdf 41–42).
- Self-control and deliberate thought apparently draw on the same limited budget of effort. Kahneman also suspects, without a systematic survey, that people avoid speeded-up or task-switching mental work when possible, and says this is how the law of least effort comes to be a law (pdf 42).
- A busy System 2 (e.g. holding digits in memory) leaves System 1 more influence, for example in choosing cake over fruit (pdf 42–43).
- Ego depletion: after one act of self-control, people are less willing or able to exert it again. It is at least partly a loss of motivation, so it differs from cognitive busyness. Glucose experiments and the Israeli parole-judge data are offered as support (pdf 43–45).
- The bat-and-ball puzzle, the roses syllogism (a short logic argument) and the Michigan murder question suggest that System 2 often endorses intuition without checking, even among selective-university students; Kahneman says failure to check seems at least to some extent a matter of motivation (pdf 45–48).
- Self-control in four-year-olds goes with later executive control (managing attention in thinking tasks) and intelligence scores. In an attention-training study with children aged 4–6, training improved executive control and nonverbal intelligence scores (pdf 48–49).
- Frederick's Cognitive Reflection Test: low scorers are more impulsive and more impatient than high scorers (pdf 49).
- Stanovich separates intelligence (the algorithmic mind: slow, demanding computation) from rationality (linked to the reflective mind, where lazy thinking is a flaw). Kahneman presents this as an attractive idea whose usefulness "time will tell" (pdf 50).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the stroll and the effort budget (pdf 41–42)
- Kahneman walks at about 17 minutes per mile, which he experiences as a stroll with no strain. He can think and work at that speed (pdf 41).
- System 2 also has a natural speed. Routine monitoring, small driving decisions and pleasantries take little effort (pdf 41).
- At the extremes, walking and thinking appear to compete for limited System 2 resources. Test: ask a friend to compute 23 × 78 while walking, and he will almost certainly stop. Kahneman can think while strolling but cannot do work that heavily loads short-term memory (pdf 41).
- At his fastest sustainable pace (about 14 min/mile) he does not try to think. He must also use self-control to resist slowing down. His conclusion: "Self-control and deliberate thought apparently draw on the same limited budget of effort" (pdf 42).
- Coherent thought usually needs self-control. Kahneman suspects, without a systematic survey, that frequent task-switching and speeded-up mental work are not intrinsically pleasurable, and that people avoid them when possible (pdf 42).
- **Flow** (Csikszentmihalyi): "a state of effortless concentration so deep that they lose their sense of time, of themselves, of their problems" (pdf 42). It separates two forms of effort, concentration on the task and deliberate control of attention. In flow, focus needs no self-control even when the task is very effortful (pdf 42).

*Intuition note:* Effortful thinking and willpower seem to come from one tank. Flow is the case where you spend effort on the task but none on forcing yourself to stay with it.

### The Busy and Depleted System 2 (pdf 42–45)
- Both self-control and cognitive effort are described as forms of mental work. People who face a demanding task and a temptation at once are more likely to yield. Example: holding seven digits in memory, you are more likely, on the evidence, to pick chocolate cake over fruit salad (pdf 42–43).
- Cognitively busy people are also more likely to make selfish choices, use sexist language and make superficial social judgments. Drinks, a sleepless night and being at the wrong time of day also weaken self-control (pdf 43).
- **Ego depletion** (Baumeister): all voluntary effort, whether cognitive, emotional or physical, draws at least partly on a shared pool of mental energy. Using successive tasks, they found that after forcing yourself to do something you are less willing or able to exert self-control next (pdf 43).
  - Stifling emotion during a film is followed by worse performance on a grip-endurance test (pdf 43).
  - Resisting chocolate and cookies while eating radishes and celery is followed by earlier giving up on a hard cognitive task (pdf 43).
- Depleting tasks all involve conflict and suppressing a natural tendency. Examples: avoiding thoughts of white bears, making a series of conflicted choices, trying to impress others. Signs of depletion include diet lapses, impulsive overspending, aggression and poorer logical decisions (pdf 43–44).
- Ego depletion is at least partly a loss of motivation: people resisted it when given a strong incentive. Cognitive load is different, since increasing effort is not an option when you must keep six digits in short-term memory while performing a task. So "Ego depletion is not the same mental state as cognitive busyness" (pdf 44).
- Glucose. The nervous system uses much glucose, and effortful mental activity appears especially costly. Blood glucose drops during hard reasoning or self-control, so Baumeister's group tested whether glucose could undo depletion (pdf 44).
  - Volunteers watched a silent interview film and had to ignore words crossing the screen, a depleting task. Then half drank glucose-sweetened lemonade and half drank Splenda-sweetened lemonade (pdf 44–45).
  - On a task requiring them to override an intuitive response, the Splenda group showed the usual depletion. The glucose group did not (pdf 45).
  - Whether glucose-depleting tasks also cause the arousal seen in pupil size and heart rate is described as still unknown (pdf 45).
- Parole judges. Eight Israeli judges reviewed cases in random order, about 6 minutes each. Only 35% of requests were approved, and denial is the default. Approval spikes to about 65% after each food break and falls steadily to about zero just before the next meal (pdf 45).
  - The authors checked many alternative explanations. The best account is that tired and hungry judges fall back on the easier default of denial. Kahneman says fatigue and hunger "probably" both play a role (pdf 45).

*Intuition note:* When System 2 is loaded or worn down, the easy default (cake, "deny parole", quitting) wins more often. Kahneman gives the glucose result and the judges' data as evidence that this has a physical side.

### The Lazy System 2 (pdf 45–48)
- A main function of System 2 is to monitor and control what System 1 suggests, letting some of it show in behavior and suppressing or changing the rest (pdf 45).
- **Bat and ball:** together they cost $1.10, and the bat costs $1 more than the ball. The intuitive answer is 10¢, which is wrong, since 10¢ + $1.10 = $1.20. The correct answer is 5¢ (pdf 45–46).
  - (added step) With ball $x$, bat $x + 1.00$: $2x + 1.00 = 1.10$, so $x = 0.05$.
  - Frederick's logic: anyone answering 10¢ did not check, although checking costs only a few seconds of effort. They also missed the social cue that no questionnaire would include a puzzle with such an obvious answer (pdf 46).
  - More than 50% of students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton gave the intuitive wrong answer. At less selective universities the failure rate exceeded 80% (pdf 46).
  - This is the first appearance of a recurrent theme: many people are overconfident and trust intuition too much, and apparently find cognitive effort at least mildly unpleasant (pdf 46).
- **Syllogism:** "All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly. Therefore some roses fade quickly." A large majority of college students endorse it. It is invalid, since there may be no roses among the quickly fading flowers (pdf 46). The feeling "it's true, it's true!" makes checking hard. Kahneman says the experiment suggests that people who believe a conclusion are very likely to accept arguments that seem to support it; if System 1 is involved, the conclusion comes first and the arguments follow (pdf 47).
- **Michigan murders** (Frederick): the trick is remembering that Detroit is in Michigan. A majority of respondents did not think of Detroit, and those who did gave higher estimates. The average guess by people asked about Michigan was lower than the guesses of a similar group asked about Detroit alone (pdf 47).
  - Blame is shared. Whether Detroit comes to mind is partly automatic memory (System 1), and people differ in how rich their representations are. The extent of deliberate checking and memory search is a System 2 trait that varies among people (pdf 47).
  - Kahneman says intelligence is not only the ability to reason; it also includes finding relevant material in memory and deploying attention (pdf 47).
- Common to all three: failure seems at least partly due to insufficient motivation. Admitted students can solve far harder problems. Kahneman calls the "lazy" label harsh but says it does not seem to be unfair. He calls the opposite type "engaged," and notes Stanovich would call them more rational (pdf 48).

*Intuition note:* Failing these puzzles seems, at least in part, not to be about inability: admitted students can solve them. System 2 often accepts a plausible answer without spending a few seconds to check.

### Intelligence, Control, Rationality (pdf 48–50)
- **Mischel's delay test:** four-year-olds chose between one Oreo now and two cookies after waiting 15 minutes alone. About half waited, mainly by keeping attention away from the reward (pdf 48). Ten or fifteen years later, resisters had higher executive control, especially in reallocating attention. As young adults they were less likely to take drugs and had substantially higher intelligence scores (pdf 48–49).
- **Oregon training study:** children aged 4–6 played attention-demanding computer games in five 40-minute sessions. Executive control improved, nonverbal intelligence scores improved, and the gain lasted several months. The same group's other work identified genes involved, found an effect of parenting, and found a close link between control of attention and control of emotion (pdf 49).
- **Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT, Frederick):** bat-and-ball plus two other questions that also invite a compelling wrong answer. The two others are not on these pages. Low scorers are prone to answer with the first idea and unwilling to check (pdf 49).
  - They are also more impulsive and impatient. 63% of intuitive respondents prefer $3,400 this month to $3,800 next month, against 37% of those who solved all three correctly (pdf 49).
  - Low scorers would pay twice as much for overnight book delivery as high scorers (pdf 49).
  - Kahneman says Frederick's findings suggest that System 1 is impulsive and intuitive, while System 2 is capable of reasoning and cautious, but for some people also lazy. The test is "one of the better predictors" of lazy thinking (pdf 49–50).
- **Stanovich:** he and West introduced "System 1/2" (now "Type 1/2" processes). He splits System 2 into an *algorithmic mind* (slow computation, what intelligence tests measure) and a *reflective mind* (rationality). He argues that high intelligence does not make people immune to bias, and that lazy thinking is a failure of rationality. He and colleagues found the bat-and-ball question and similar ones to be "somewhat better" indicators of susceptibility to cognitive errors than IQ tests. Kahneman says time will tell whether the distinction leads to new discoveries (pdf 50).

*Intuition note:* Being good at computing and being disposed to check your own intuitions seem to be different things. Kahneman reports that Stanovich and colleagues found the bat-and-ball question and similar ones to be somewhat better indicators of susceptibility to cognitive errors than IQ tests.

### Speaking of Control (pdf 50–51)
Four sample sentences show how to use the ideas: flow, depleted ego leading to routine procedures, a lazy System 2, and impulsive "first thing that comes to mind" speech said to probably go with trouble delaying gratification (pdf 50–51).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A pen and an eraser cost $2.20 together. The pen costs $2.00 more than the eraser. Intuition says the eraser costs 20¢. Work it out as System 2 would:
1. If eraser = 0.20, pen = 0.20 + 2.00 = 2.20.
2. Total = 0.20 + 2.20 = 2.40, which is not 2.20. So the intuition fails.
3. Let eraser = $x$. Then $x + (x + 2.00) = 2.20$, so $2x = 0.20$ and $x = 0.10$.
4. Verify: eraser 0.10, pen 2.10, total 2.20 ✓.

The check takes a few seconds. This is the cost the chapter says many people do not pay.

## Concepts
- [[system-1-system-2]] (new) — fast, automatic intuition vs slow, effortful, controlling thought (pdf 45)
- [[law-of-least-effort]] (new) — people avoid effortful thinking when they can; Kahneman suspects this, without a systematic survey (pdf 42)
- [[ego-depletion]] (new) — after an act of self-control, later self-control or effort is weaker, partly through lost motivation (pdf 43–44)
- [[cognitive-load]] (new) — a mentally busy System 2 gives System 1 more influence; unlike depletion, more effort is not an option (pdf 42–44)
- [[flow]] (new) — effortless deep concentration where no self-control is needed (pdf 42)
- [[cognitive-reflection-test]] (new) — three puzzles with a compelling wrong intuitive answer; Kahneman calls it one of the better predictors of lazy thinking (pdf 49–50)
- [[delay-of-gratification]] (new) — choosing a larger later reward over a smaller immediate one (pdf 48–49)
- [[rationality-vs-intelligence]] (new) — Stanovich: rationality (reflective mind) is distinct from intelligence (algorithmic mind) (pdf 50)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Intuitive choice of $3,400 now over $3,800 later ↔ decision theory: impatience in intertemporal choice — both concern preferring sooner, smaller rewards.
- [structural-analogy] Judges defaulting to "deny" when tired ↔ bounded rationality: limited resources push toward simple decision rules.
- [metaphor] Effort "budget" or "tank" ↔ economics: scarce resource allocated between uses (Baumeister, as reported in the chapter, says the glucose findings show the energy idea is "more than a mere metaphor", pdf 44).

## Questions this raises
- If ego depletion is partly lost motivation, how large a reward is enough to restore effort, and does the same hold for cognitive load?
- Which people and situations count as "engaged", and can checking be taught or only trained, as in the attention games?
- How far do the parole-judge data and lemonade results support a causal glucose effect rather than other explanations?
- If rationality differs from intelligence, how should it be measured beyond the CRT?
