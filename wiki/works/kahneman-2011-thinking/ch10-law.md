# The Law of Small Numbers

## L0 — One line
Small samples produce extreme results more often than large ones, but our mind is not adequately sensitive to sample size and is quick to seek causes for what is only chance (pdf 109, 112, 116).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do the counties with the highest kidney-cancer rates and those with the lowest look alike (rural, thinly populated)? Because small samples vary more, not because of any cause. Kahneman argues that people, even trained researchers, are not adequately sensitive to sample size. The mind favours certainty over doubt, pays more attention to the content of a message than to its reliability, and is quick to see pattern and cause in random sequences. Examples include the "hot hand" (a supposed scoring streak in basketball), air-force squadron losses, and the investment in small schools. The lesson he and Tversky gave researchers: treat statistical intuitions with suspicion and compute whenever possible (pdf 108–116).

**Prerequisites:** percentages; probability of independent events; the idea of an average and a sample drawn from a population.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Extreme outcomes, both high and low, are more likely in small samples than in large ones. This explains the kidney-cancer pattern without any cause (pdf 109).
- "Large samples are more precise" and "small samples yield extreme results more often" mean the same thing, but for many readers the second is harder to feel (pdf 110).
- Psychologists commonly, Kahneman included, chose samples that were too small; an article he read said this exposed them to a 50% risk of failing to confirm true hypotheses (pdf 111).
- We pay more attention to the story in a message than to its reliability. System 1 (the automatic, effortless mode of thinking) is not prone to doubt; System 2 (the deliberate, effortful mode) can doubt, but sustaining doubt is harder work than sliding into certainty (pdf 112–113).
- Our liking for causal thinking can make us judge random sequences as non-random. Examples: birth sequences, London bombing, Israeli squadrons (pdf 113–114).
- Kahneman, citing a study of shot sequences, calls the hot hand in basketball a cognitive illusion. He raises the same error for judging investment advisers and CEOs (pdf 115).
- Small schools are not better on average, only more variable. A very large investment was based on this error (pdf 116).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: kidney cancer in 3,141 counties (pdf 108–110).**
- Counties with the lowest kidney-cancer incidence are mostly rural, sparsely populated, and in traditionally Republican states of the Midwest, South and West. Readers tend to infer a clean-living explanation (pdf 108).
- The counties with the highest incidence have the same description. Wainer and Zwerling, tongue-in-cheek, offer a poverty explanation for these. A rural lifestyle cannot explain both extremes (pdf 108).
- Kahneman's key factor: rural counties have small populations. System 1 finds causal links automatically, sometimes even spurious ones. It is inept with "merely statistical" facts, which change the probability of outcomes but do not cause them (pdf 108).
- Urn illustration: an urn is half red, half white marbles. Draw 4, record the reds, replace, repeat. "2 red, 2 white" occurs almost exactly 6 times as often as "4 red" or as "4 white" (pdf 109).
- Jack draws 4 marbles per trial, Jill draws 7. Both record homogeneous samples (all one colour). Jack sees them more often by a factor of 8 (expected 12.5% vs 1.56%) (pdf 109).
- Counties are samples from a giant urn; rural samples are smaller, so extreme rates, high or low, are most likely there (pdf 109).
- Conclusion: the explanation is statistical, not causal. Small population neither causes nor prevents cancer; it only allows the rate to be much higher or lower. "There is nothing to explain." Next year the same general pattern of extremes would appear in small samples, but counties with high rates last year would not necessarily be high again. If so, the differences are artifacts: observations produced entirely by the research method, here sample size (pdf 109–110).
- *Intuition note:* A small group is easily pushed to an extreme by a few lucky or unlucky cases. A big group averages the luck out. So extremes at both ends can be accidents of sampling rather than facts about the places (pdf 109).
- Kahneman suggests readers know the law of large numbers only loosely. "Sparsely populated" did not stand out, and the 4-versus-7 gap may have surprised them. Even sophisticated researchers had poor intuitions in his first joint study with Amos Tversky (pdf 110).

**The Law of Small Numbers (pdf 110–112).**
- The collaboration began with a debate about whether people without statistical training are good "intuitive statisticians." Kahneman had recently found that he was not (pdf 110).
- Sampling variation is a costly obstacle for researchers. Example: to confirm that six-year-old girls have a larger average vocabulary than boys (true in the population), a small sample may by luck give an inconclusive result or even favour boys. A sufficiently large sample is "the only way" to reduce this risk (pdf 111).
- The risk can be estimated by a fairly simple procedure, but psychologists usually use judgment. An article Kahneman read said they commonly chose samples so small that they faced a 50% risk of failing to confirm true hypotheses. A plausible explanation: intuitive misconceptions about sampling variation (pdf 111).
- Kahneman himself had chosen samples by tradition and intuition, though he taught statistics. His odd results were artifacts of his method (pdf 111).
- He and Tversky built a questionnaire on realistic research situations (sample sizes, risks of failure, advice to students) and gave it to sophisticated participants, including authors of two statistical textbooks. The results are not given in the text layer of these pages (pdf 111–112).
- Their article was titled "Belief in the Law of Small Numbers." The law of small numbers is the tongue-in-cheek claim that the law of large numbers applies to small numbers as well. They recommended treating statistical intuitions "with proper suspicion" and replacing "impression formation by computation whenever possible" (pdf 112).

**A Bias of Confidence Over Doubt (pdf 112–113).**
- Example: "In a telephone poll of 300 seniors, 60% support the president." The gist is "elderly support president." Sample size is background, so unless you are a professional you may not react very differently to 150 and 3,000. People are not adequately sensitive to sample size (pdf 112).
- A message has a story and a source. If unreliability is obvious (a partisan, flawed poll), you reject it. Whether System 1 separates degrees of belief, such as *New York Times* vs. watercooler, is doubtful: the author says the principle of WYSIATI (named here, not explained on these pages) suggests that it cannot (pdf 112).
- System 1 is not prone to doubt; it suppresses ambiguity. System 2 can hold incompatible possibilities, but sustaining doubt is harder than sliding into certainty. The law of small numbers is a manifestation of a general bias favouring certainty over doubt (pdf 112–113).
- Related: we exaggerate coherence, as in the halo effect. A machine for jumping to conclusions acts as if it believed in small numbers (pdf 113).
- *Intuition note:* The mind builds a neat story from scraps. It rarely asks how good the scraps are.

**Cause and Chance (pdf 113–116).**
- The statistical view relates an event to what could have happened instead: chance selected it among alternatives (pdf 113).
- Six births in sequence: BBBGGG, GGGGGG, BGBBGB. Events are independent and B and G are about equally likely, so all three are equally likely. Intuition judges BGBBGB much more likely, because only it looks random (pdf 113). (added step: if B and G were exactly equally likely, each specific sequence would have probability $(1/2)^6 = 1/64$.)
- We are pattern seekers. Causal vigilance may have had evolutionary advantages, such as noticing an apparent increase in lions even if it is random fluctuation (pdf 114).
- London bombing (Feller): hit maps showed conspicuous gaps. Some suspected spies in unharmed areas. Analysis showed the pattern was typical of randomness. Feller: randomness appears "as regularity or tendency to cluster" to the untrained eye (pdf 114).
- Yom Kippur War: one squadron lost four planes, the other none, from the same base. No operational difference was found. Kahneman advised accepting blind luck and stopping the pilot interviews, since a random search for a cause was hopeless and pilots should not be made to feel at fault (pdf 114–115).
- Hot hand (Tversky, Gilovich, Vallone): players, coaches and fans believe in it. Analysis of thousands of shot sequences found none in professional basketball. Shot sequences satisfy all tests of randomness, though some players are more accurate. Red Auerbach's response: "Who is this guy? So he makes a study. I couldn't care less." (pdf 115).
- Questions about investment advisers and CEOs follow. Following intuition, you will more often than not misclassify a random event as systematic (pdf 115).
- *Intuition note:* A streak is what randomness often looks like. Waiting for more data protects against crediting luck as skill (pdf 114–115; the closing quotes on pdf 117 point the same way).

**Small schools (pdf 115–116).**
- Wainer and Zwerling wrote about the Gates Foundation's roughly $1.7 billion investment in small schools. In 1,662 Pennsylvania schools, 6 of the top 50 were small, an overrepresentation by a factor of 4. Other institutions joined (pdf 115–116).
- A causal story (more personal attention) is easy, but the facts are wrong. Bad schools also tend to be smaller than average. Small schools are not better on average, only more variable. If anything, say Wainer and Zwerling, large schools tend to do better, especially in higher grades (pdf 116).

**Two larger stories (pdf 116).**
- (1) We attend more to message content than to reliability, so our view of the world is simpler and more coherent than the data justify. (2) Many facts are due to chance, including accidents of sampling. Causal explanations of chance events are inevitably wrong (pdf 116).

**Speaking of the Law of Small Numbers (pdf 117).**
- Four sample remarks: three successful films are too early to declare a CEO has a hot hand; do not believe a new trader is a genius before a statistician estimates how likely his streak is to be chance; a sample too small to support inferences should not be followed (the law of small numbers); results can be kept secret until the sample is sufficiently large, to avoid pressure for a premature conclusion (pdf 117).

## Toy example (ours)
A true disease rate of 50% is spread by chance. A "town" is a sample of people; "extreme" means 0% or 100% ill.
- Town of 2 people: 4 equally likely outcomes (HH, HT, TH, TT, with H = ill). Extreme: HH and TT = 2 of 4 = 50%.
- Town of 6 people: $2^6 = 64$ outcomes; extreme: all ill or none ill = 2 of 64 = 3.125%.
- Ratio: $50 / 3.125 = 16$. Tiny towns hold both the "cleanest" and "sickest" rates, though every person had the same 50% risk.

## Concepts
- law-of-large-numbers (new) — results of large samples are more precise and deserve more trust than those of small samples (pdf 110)
- law-of-small-numbers (new) — the tongue-in-cheek belief that the law of large numbers also holds for small samples (pdf 112)
- sampling-variation (new) — differences between samples drawn from the same population by chance (pdf 111)
- artifact (new) — an observation produced entirely by the research method, such as sample size (pdf 110)
- sample-size-insensitivity (new) — not reacting adequately to how large a sample is (pdf 112)
- WYSIATI (new) — a principle named in the text (not spelled out on these pages) that the author invokes to suggest System 1 cannot distinguish degrees of belief (pdf 112)
- causal-thinking-bias (new) — automatic search for causes, including for random events (pdf 108, 113)
- hot-hand-illusion (new) — belief in streaks in random sequences of shots (pdf 115)
- halo-effect (new) — feeling that we know a person about whom we know little (pdf 113)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Small samples being more variable ↔ bias–variance trade-off: sample size controls how much results vary across samples.
- [structural-analogy] Last year's extreme counties need not be extreme this year ↔ regression to the mean (a term this unit does not use).
- [structural-analogy] Belief that small samples resemble the population ↔ heuristic / representativeness: judging by similarity rather than by computation.
