# Answering an Easier Question

## L0 — One line
When a hard question has no quick answer, System 1 answers a related easier question instead, and you may not notice the swap.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do people form intuitive opinions on hard questions, such as how happy they are or how far a political candidate will go? Kahneman proposes substitution. System 1 finds an easier, related question (the heuristic question), answers it, and fits that answer to the original (target) question by matching intensities. A lazy System 2 often endorses the result. Three examples: a size illusion in a perspective drawing, the effect of question order on reported happiness, and Slovic's affect heuristic (likes and dislikes shape beliefs). Judgments made by substitution are biased in predictable ways. The chapter ends with a list of System 1's characteristics.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction (fast automatic vs. slow effortful thinking); correlation as a measure of how two answers go together.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Substitution: if no good answer to a hard target question comes quickly, System 1 answers an easier heuristic question instead (pdf 97).
- The "mental shotgun" is imprecise control over which questions we answer. Together with intensity matching, its automatic processes make the heuristic question very likely to be evoked (pdf 99); this differs from Pólya's heuristics, which System 2 deliberately implements (pdf 98).
- Intensity matching converts the heuristic answer to the target's scale, for example feelings about dolphins into dollars (pdf 99).
- A lazy System 2 often endorses the heuristic answer without checking it. You may not notice the swap or that the question was hard (pdf 99).
- 3-D size illusion: the impression of 3-D size dictates the judgment of 2-D size, and you cannot help it. This is a true illusion, not a misunderstanding of the question (pdf 100–101).
- German students' survey: asking about dates first made dating and happiness answers strongly correlated; asking about happiness first gave about zero correlation (pdf 101–102).
- Affect heuristic (Slovic): likes and dislikes determine beliefs about benefits and risks. In the context of attitudes, System 2 is more of an endorser than an enforcer (pdf 103).
- The closing list of System 1 traits includes "computes more than intended (mental shotgun)" and "sometimes substitutes an easier question" (pdf 104–105).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pdf 97)
You are rarely stumped. You have intuitive opinions on almost everything, often resting on evidence you can neither explain nor defend. Hard-to-answer questions such as 17 × 24 are the rare exception (pdf 97).

### Substituting Questions (pdf 97–99)
- **Account (Kahneman's):** if a satisfactory answer to a hard question is not found quickly, System 1 finds a related easier question and answers it. This is *substitution* (pdf 97).
- **Target question:** the assessment you intend to produce. **Heuristic question:** the simpler question you answer instead (pdf 97).
- **Heuristic** (technical definition): a simple procedure that helps find adequate, though often imperfect, answers to difficult questions (pdf 97).
- **Origin:** the idea arose early in Kahneman's work with Amos [Tversky] and was the core of the heuristics-and-biases approach. They asked how people manage to judge probability without knowing precisely what it is. Kahneman and Tversky's answer was that, when called upon to judge probability, people actually judge something else and believe they have judged probability (pdf 97).
- **Pólya:** substitution can be a good strategy: "find" an easier problem you can solve. But Pólya's heuristics are deliberate System 2 procedures. The heuristics here are not chosen; they result from the mental shotgun (pdf 97–98).
- **Table 1** (pdf 98). Each target question is paired with its heuristic question:

| Target | Heuristic |
|---|---|
| How much would you contribute to save an endangered species? | How much emotion do I feel when I think of dying dolphins? |
| How happy are you with your life these days? | What is my mood right now? |
| How popular is the president right now? | How popular will the president be six months from now? |
| How should financial advisers who prey on the elderly be punished? | How much anger do I feel when I think of financial predators? |
| How far will this woman running in the primary go in politics? | Does this woman look like a political winner? |

- Answering the target questions seriously would raise other hard issues, such as the meaning of happiness, and is "completely impractical" (pdf 98). The heuristic alternative sometimes works fairly well and sometimes leads to serious errors (pdf 98).
- The heuristic question is very likely to be evoked and very easily answered, so it gives an off-the-shelf answer (pdf 99).
- **Fitting the answer:** the answers need to be fitted to the original questions. Another System 1 capability, *intensity matching*, solves this. Feelings and contribution dollars are both intensity scales, so the matching dollar amount comes to mind. Political skill (pathetic to extraordinary) maps onto political success (from being defeated in the primary to someday becoming president) (pdf 99).
- Sometimes a heuristic answer is endorsed by System 2. System 2 can reject or modify it, but a lazy System 2 often endorses it without much scrutiny. You may not notice that you did not answer the question asked, or that it was difficult (pdf 99).

*Intuition note:* When no good answer comes quickly, a hard question can get replaced by an easy one whose answer already sits in your mind. A feeling is then converted to the scale the question wants. Because an intuitive answer comes readily, you are not stumped and may not notice that the question was hard.

### The 3-D Heuristic (pdf 99–101)
- **Figure 9:** a corridor drawn in perspective with three men. Question: as printed, is the right figure larger than the left? The quick answer is yes, but a ruler shows the figures are exactly the same size (pdf 100).
- Your perceptual system automatically reads the picture as a 3-D scene, where the right figure is farther away and much larger. Only visual artists and experienced photographers have learned to see the drawing as an object on the page (pdf 100–101).
- For most people, the dominant impression of 3-D size dictates the judgment of 2-D size. This is substitution (pdf 101).
- **Not a misunderstanding:** you knew the question was about size on the page. Asked to estimate the size, your answer would be in inches, not feet. You were influenced by the answer to an unasked question, "How tall are the three people?" (pdf 101).
- The substitution is automatic, and the depth cues are irrelevant to the task but cannot be ignored. The associated bias is that objects that look more distant also look larger on the page (pdf 101).
- Kahneman concludes that a judgment based on substitution will inevitably be biased in predictable ways (pdf 101). Here it happens so deep in perception that you cannot help it (pdf 101).

*Intuition note:* You know what the question asks, yet an automatic process answers a different question first. The depth cues are irrelevant to the task, yet you could not ignore them.

### The Mood Heuristic for Happiness (pdf 101–102)
- **Survey of German students** with two questions: "How happy are you these days?" and "How many dates did you have last month?" (pdf 101).
- **Order happiness → dates:** the correlation between the answers was about zero. **Order dates → happiness:** the correlation was about as high as correlations between psychological measures can get (pdf 101–102).
- **Explanation:** dating is apparently not the center of their lives (hence the zero correlation in the first order). But the dating question aroused an emotional reaction. Students with many dates were reminded of a happy aspect of their lives, and students with none of loneliness and rejection. That emotion was still present when the happiness question came (pdf 102).
- "Happiness these days" is not an easy assessment, and a good answer needs thinking. The students already had a ready answer to a related question, how happy they were with their love life, and substituted it (pdf 102).
- They are not confused. Asked, they would say romantic life and life as a whole are different. But they were asked how happy they were, and System 1 had a ready answer (pdf 102).
- The same pattern appears when a question on relations with parents or on finances comes just before the happiness question. Satisfaction in that domain dominates the happiness report. Any emotionally significant question that alters mood would have the same effect. Kahneman links this to WYSIATI: the present state of mind looms large in evaluating happiness (pdf 102).

*Intuition note:* The order of questions changed the answers because it changed which feeling was currently in mind. The measured link between dating and happiness depends on whether dating was raised first.

### The Affect Heuristic (pdf 102–103)
- **Slovic's affect heuristic:** people let their likes and dislikes determine their beliefs about the world (pdf 102).
- Political preference determines which arguments seem compelling. People who like a health policy believe its benefits are substantial and its costs manageable. Hawks probably think other nations are weak, and doves probably think they are strong (pdf 102–103).
- Attitudes toward irradiated food, red meat, nuclear power, tattoos or motorcycles drive beliefs about their benefits and risks. If you dislike one, you probably believe its risks are high and its benefits negligible (pdf 103).
- Minds are not completely closed. Beliefs and even attitudes may change a little if you learn a risk is smaller than you thought. That information will also improve your view of the benefits, even if nothing was said about benefits (pdf 103).
- **A new side of System 2:** earlier it was described as a more or less acquiescent monitor, and also as active in memory search, computation and choice. In examples such as the bat-and-ball problem (a puzzle from earlier in the book) it appeared to be ultimately in charge, able to resist System 1, and self-criticism is one of its functions. For attitudes it is more an apologist for System 1's emotions, an "endorser rather than an enforcer." Its search for information is mostly limited to what is consistent with existing beliefs (pdf 103).

*Intuition note:* Here the conclusion comes first and the arguments follow. A mind that feels positive about something finds both its low risks and its high benefits believable.

### Speaking of Substitution and Heuristics (pdf 103–104)
Example remarks include a warning that the question faced is whether a candidate can succeed, while the question seemingly answered is whether she interviews well. Another notes that last year's performance is being used as a heuristic to predict a firm's future value and asks whether that heuristic is good enough. (pdf 103–104).

### Characteristics of System 1 (pdf 104–105)
- The author says the list is meant to give an intuitive sense of the "personality" of the fictitious System 1 (pdf 104).
- Traits are listed: it generates impressions and feelings, which become beliefs and attitudes when System 2 endorses them. It works automatically and fast, creates coherent patterns of activated ideas, and links cognitive ease to illusions of truth. It infers causes, neglects ambiguity, is biased to believe and confirm, and focuses on existing evidence (WYSIATI) (pdf 104–105).
- It also matches intensities across scales, "computes more than intended (mental shotgun)," and sometimes substitutes an easier question (pdf 105).
- Further items: more sensitive to changes than to states, overweights low probabilities, diminishing sensitivity to quantity, responds more strongly to losses than to gains, and frames decisions narrowly. In the text layer, asterisks appear next to these items (pdf 105). These pages do not explain the asterisks.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four students. Scale 1–10 for happiness. The invented numbers show how substitution produces the order effect.
- Dates last month: 0, 5, 0, 5. Mean = 10/4 = 2.5. Deviations: −2.5, +2.5, −2.5, +2.5.
- **Happiness asked first** (students think about life broadly): answers 6, 6, 7, 7. Mean = 6.5. Deviations: −0.5, −0.5, +0.5, +0.5.
  - Products of deviations: 1.25, −1.25, −1.25, 1.25. Sum = 0. Correlation = 0.
- **Dates asked first** (students answer with their love-life feeling): no dates → 3, many dates → 8, so answers are 3, 8, 3, 8. Mean = 5.5. Deviations: −2.5, +2.5, −2.5, +2.5.
  - Products: 6.25 × 4 = 25. Sum of squares of dates = 25, of happiness = 25.
  - Correlation = 25 / √(25 × 25) = 1.
Same students, same dates; only the heuristic question in mind changed.

## Concepts
- [[substitution]] (new) — answering an easier, related question in place of a hard target question (pdf 97)
- [[target-question]] (new) — the assessment you intend to produce (pdf 97)
- [[heuristic-question]] (new) — the simpler question actually answered (pdf 97)
- [[heuristic]] — a simple procedure giving adequate, often imperfect answers to hard questions. Pólya's heuristics are chosen by System 2; those here are not chosen (pdf 97–98)
- [[mental-shotgun]] (new) — imprecise control over targeting responses; the mind computes more than intended (pdf 98, 105)
- [[intensity-matching]] (new) — System 1 maps a feeling or impression onto another scale of the same intensity (pdf 99)
- [[affect-heuristic]] (new) — Slovic's idea: likes and dislikes determine beliefs about risks and benefits (pdf 102)
- [[system-1-system-2]] (new) — fast automatic System 1 vs. effortful System 2, here often an endorser of System 1 (pdf 99, 103)
- [[wysiati]] (new) — System 1 focuses on existing evidence and ignores absent evidence; cited for the present mood's weight (pdf 102, 104–105)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Substitution (answer an easier question) ↔ decision-game-theory: bounded rationality / satisficing — both are shortcuts under limited effort, but the chapter does not link them.
- [structural-analogy] Affect heuristic (feelings fix beliefs about risk/benefit) ↔ decision-game-theory: framing effect — an irrelevant factor changes judgments.
- [metaphor] Question-order effect ↔ measurement in the social sciences: survey design — the order of items can change what a response measures.

## Questions this raises
- When is the heuristic answer good enough, and how could one tell? The text says only that heuristics sometimes work fairly well and sometimes lead to serious errors (pdf 98).
- What would make a lazy System 2 scrutinize an intuitive answer? The chapter gives only example remarks, such as asking whether we still remember the question we are trying to answer (pdf 103).
- How could survey designers avoid order effects like the dating and happiness case?
- How general is the affect heuristic beyond the examples (health policy, nuclear power)?
