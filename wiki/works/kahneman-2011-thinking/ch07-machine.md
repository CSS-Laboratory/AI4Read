# A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions

## L0 — One line
System 1 quickly builds the best story it can from whatever information is active, hides ambiguity and doubt, and so jumps to conclusions (WYSIATI: what you see is all there is).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do intuitive judgments feel certain when the evidence is thin? Kahneman says System 1, the fast automatic mind, resolves ambiguity silently, tends to believe first, and lets first impressions colour later information (the halo effect). It builds a story only from the ideas currently active, so the amount and quality of evidence barely matter. Confidence mostly follows how coherent the story is. System 2, the slow effortful mind, does the doubting, but it is sometimes busy and often lazy. The unit also gives a practical rule: collect judgments independently ("decorrelate error"). WYSIATI is used later to explain overconfidence, framing effects and base-rate neglect.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction; the idea of an average of noisy estimates; basic sense of "bias" versus random error.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Jumping to conclusions is efficient when conclusions are likely correct, errors are cheap, and time is saved. It is risky when the situation is unfamiliar, the stakes high and time short (pdf 79).
- System 1 resolves ambiguity by a bet guided by experience, recent events and context. It keeps no record of rejected alternatives, so doubt is left to System 2 (pdf 79–80).
- Understanding a statement starts with an attempt to believe it (Gilbert). Unbelieving is System 2's job, so a busy System 2 leaves us gullible. Hypothesis testing also tends to seek confirming evidence (pdf 80–81).
- The halo effect makes us like or dislike everything about a person, and gives first impressions extra weight. Asch's Alan and Ben and Kahneman's essay grading illustrate it (pdf 81–83).
- Decorrelate error: independent judgments average out noise, but shared bias is not reduced by pooling, and mutual influence lowers the precision of the group estimate. This gives rules for witnesses and meetings (pdf 83–84).
- WYSIATI: System 1 represents only activated ideas, and success means coherence, not completeness. One-sided evidence raised confidence even when people knew it was one-sided (pdf 85–86).
- WYSIATI is invoked for overconfidence, framing effects and base-rate neglect (pdf 87).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pdf 79)
Kahneman recalls Danny Kaye's line about "jumping to conclusions" and says he believes it is an apt description of how System 1 functions (pdf 79). Jumping is efficient if conclusions are likely correct, occasional mistakes are acceptable, and much time and effort is saved. It is risky if the situation is unfamiliar, the stakes high, and no time exists to collect information. Intuitive errors are probable there, and a deliberate System 2 intervention may prevent them (pdf 79).

### Neglect of ambiguity and suppression of doubt (pdf 79–80)
- Figure 6 shows three ambiguous exhibits. The middle shape in "A ? C" and in "12 ? 14" is identical, yet it is read as B in letters and as 13 in numbers. The context helps determine the reading, and you do not notice the ambiguity (pdf 79).
- For the sentence about Ann and the bank, you probably imagined money and tellers. After an earlier sentence about floating gently down a river, you would imagine a different scene. With no explicit context, System 1 generated a likely one, and you were not aware of the choice (pdf 79–80).
- When uncertain, System 1 bets, guided by experience. Recent events and current context weigh most. If nothing recent comes to mind, more distant memories govern, e.g. you sang your ABCs, not "A13Cs" (pdf 80).
- Key point: a definite choice was made without your knowing it. System 1 does not track rejected alternatives or even that there were any. Conscious doubt requires holding incompatible interpretations at once, which takes effort and belongs to System 2 (pdf 80).

*Intuition note:* You never see the ambiguity because the mind settles on one reading without telling you. Doubt is something extra that costs effort.

### A bias to believe and confirm (pdf 80–81)
- **Gilbert's view** (traced to Spinoza): understanding a statement begins with an attempt to believe it. This is an automatic System 1 operation that builds the best possible interpretation. Even nonsense such as "whitefish eat candy" evokes some initial belief (pdf 80).
- Unbelieving is, on Gilbert's view, a System 2 operation. In his experiment, participants saw nonsense assertions ("a dinca is a flame") followed by "true" or "false", and were later tested on which had been labelled "true". Some had to hold digits in memory. This selectively made it hard to "unbelieve" false sentences, and they later thought many false ones were true (pdf 80–81).
- Kahneman's moral: when System 2 is otherwise engaged, we will believe almost anything. System 1 is gullible and biased to believe, and System 2 is sometimes busy and often lazy. There is evidence that tired, depleted people are more influenced by empty persuasive messages such as commercials (pdf 81).
- Confirmation bias: "Is Sam friendly?" brings to mind different behaviour than "Is Sam unfriendly?" System 2 also uses a positive test strategy, searching for confirming evidence. This runs against the philosophers' advice to try to refute hypotheses, and scientists often do it too (pdf 81).
- System 1's confirmatory bias favours uncritical acceptance and exaggeration of the likelihood of extreme, improbable events. Asked about a tsunami hitting California within thirty years, you picture tsunamis and are prone to overestimate the probability (pdf 81).

*Intuition note:* On Gilbert's view, to understand a claim you first work out what it would mean if it were true. This automatic step amounts to an attempt to believe it, and unbelieving is a System 2 job.

### Exaggerated emotional coherence (halo effect) (pdf 81–83)
- Definition: the tendency to like or dislike everything about a person, including unobserved traits. It is one of the ways System 1's picture of the world is simpler and more coherent than the real thing (pdf 81).
- Joan: you find her personable. Asked about her generosity, you know virtually nothing, but liking her and liking generosity predispose you to believe she is generous. Then you probably like her even better (pdf 81–82).
- **Asch's study:** Alan is listed as intelligent—industrious—impulsive—critical—stubborn—envious; Ben has the same traits in reverse order. Most people view Alan more favourably. Early traits change the meaning of later ones: the stubbornness of an intelligent person seems justified, while intelligence in an envious, stubborn person seems more dangerous. Kahneman calls the halo effect also an example of suppressed ambiguity: like "bank", "stubborn" is ambiguous and is read to fit the context (pdf 82).
- In one study, people considered Alan's first three adjectives, then the last three as belonging to another person. Most then thought it impossible for all six to describe one person (pdf 82).
- Sequence is often chance but matters, because the halo effect raises the weight of first impressions, sometimes until later information is mostly wasted (pdf 82).
- **Kahneman's grading.** He first read each student's essays in one booklet in succession. He noticed his evaluations were strikingly homogeneous, and suspected the first essay had a disproportionate effect. He gave the benefit of the doubt on later vague statements after a high first score. A student with one strong and one weak essay got different grades depending on the order, although he had said the essays had equal weight (pdf 82–83).
- New procedure: score all students' answers to question 1, then question 2, and so on, writing scores on the booklet's inside back page. His confidence dropped. He found large discrepancies within a student and felt tempted to change the not-yet-written grade, which he tried not to do (pdf 83).
- His conclusion: the lower confidence was a good sign. The earlier consistency was spurious. It gave cognitive ease and let a lazy System 2 accept the final grade. The inconsistency was real, reflecting that one question is an inadequate measure and that his grading was unreliable (pdf 83).

### Decorrelate error (pdf 83–84)
- General principle: decorrelate error! In the pennies-in-a-jar task (Surowiecki), individuals do poorly but the average of many judgments does remarkably well. Individuals' errors are independent and, absent systematic bias, tend to average to zero (pdf 83–84).
- This works well only when observations are independent and errors uncorrelated. A shared bias is not reduced by aggregation. Letting observers influence each other effectively reduces the sample size and the group estimate's precision (pdf 84).
- Applications: witnesses are kept from discussing events before testifying, to stop unbiased witnesses influencing each other as well as collusion. For meetings, have members write a brief position before discussion. Open discussion gives too much weight to early, assertive speakers (pdf 84).

*Intuition note:* Averaging cancels random mistakes, not shared ones. If people influence each other, the group acts like a smaller sample.

### What you see is all there is (WYSIATI) (pdf 84–87)
- The associative machine represents only activated ideas. Information not retrieved (even unconsciously) might as well not exist, in Kahneman's words. System 1 builds the best story from active ideas and cannot allow for information it lacks (pdf 85).
- Its measure of success is the coherence of the story. The amount and quality of data are largely irrelevant. When information is scarce, System 1 is a machine for jumping to conclusions (pdf 85).
- Example: asked whether Mindik will be a good leader, given only that she is intelligent and strong, you quickly answer yes. But the next two adjectives could have been corrupt and cruel. You did not ask what you would need to know. The story is revised if new information arrives, but a bias favouring the first impression remains (pdf 85).
- A coherence-seeking System 1 plus a lazy System 2 means System 2 will endorse many intuitive beliefs. System 2 can be systematic (e.g. buying a home), but System 1 is expected to influence even the more careful decisions, and its input never ceases (pdf 85).
- WYSIATI: System 1 is radically insensitive to both the quality and the quantity of the information behind impressions (pdf 85–86).
- **Study by Tversky and two graduate students** (pdf 86): participants read a legal scenario (David Thornton, a union representative, arrested at Thrifty Drug and suing for false arrest). Some then heard a lawyer for one side, some both sides. The lawyers added no useful information beyond the story. Participants knew the setup, and one-sided groups could have generated the other side's argument. Still, one-sided evidence had a pronounced effect on judgments. One-sided groups were also more confident than those who saw both sides (pdf 86).
- Kahneman's reading: the result is just what one would expect if confidence is determined by the coherence of the story; consistency matters for a good story, not completeness. Knowing little often makes it easier to fit everything into a pattern (pdf 86–87).
- WYSIATI aids coherence and the cognitive ease that makes us accept statements as true. It explains fast thinking, and the story is often close enough to reality for reasonable action (pdf 87). It is invoked for (pdf 87):
  - **Overconfidence:** confidence depends mostly on story quality, even with little evidence. We fail to allow that critical evidence may be missing.
  - **Framing effects:** A 90% one-month survival rate after surgery is more reassuring than a 10% one-month mortality rate. Cold cuts called 90% fat-free are more attractive than the same cuts called 10% fat. The equivalence is transparent, but an individual normally sees only one version.
  - **Base-rate neglect:** the vivid Steve description is salient, and the statistical fact that there are more male farmers than male librarians almost certainly did not come to mind.

*Intuition note:* The mind judges how well the pieces it has fit together, not how many pieces are missing. A small, tidy set of facts can therefore feel more convincing than a large, messy one.

### Speaking of jumping to conclusions (pdf 88)
Four sample remarks apply the ideas: halo effect from a good presentation, decorrelating errors by separate judgments before discussion, a decision based on one consultant's report, and avoiding extra information that might spoil the story (pdf 88).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A simplified model of WYSIATI. Give each trait a leadership score: intelligent = +1, strong = +1, corrupt = −2, cruel = −2. Answer "good leader" if the sum of known traits is above 0.
- Known: intelligent, strong. Sum = 1 + 1 = +2, so "yes".
- Add corrupt and cruel: sum = 2 + (−2) + (−2) = −2, so "no".

The first answer was a bet on only two traits and was reversed by new information. In the unit's account, confidence depends mostly on the quality of the story one can tell, even with little evidence (pdf 87). The toy captures only the story-building step, not the lasting first-impression bias.

## Concepts
- [[wysiati]] (new) — System 1 builds a coherent story from only the activated information and ignores what is missing (pdf 85)
- [[suppression-of-ambiguity]] (new) — a single reading is chosen silently and the alternatives are not kept or noticed (pdf 79–80)
- [[bias-to-believe]] (new) — understanding starts with an attempt to believe; unbelieving is a System 2 task (Gilbert) (pdf 80)
- [[confirmation-bias]] (new) — memory and hypothesis tests favour evidence compatible with current beliefs; "positive test strategy" (pdf 81)
- [[halo-effect]] (new) — liking or disliking all aspects of a person, including unobserved ones, and overweighting first impressions (pdf 81–82)
- [[decorrelate-error]] (new) — keep judgments independent so random errors cancel when pooled; shared bias is not removed (pdf 83–84)
- [[overconfidence]] (new) — confidence depends mostly on the quality of the story; the quantity and quality of evidence count for little (pdf 87)
- [[framing effect]] — different ways of presenting the same information (e.g. 90% survival versus 10% mortality) often evoke different emotions (pdf 87)
- [[base-rate-neglect]] (new) — a vivid description crowds out the statistical fact about group sizes (pdf 87)
- [[heuristic]] — here, System 1's quick bet from context and experience, efficient but risky in unfamiliar, high-stakes settings (pdf 79)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] decorrelate error ↔ statistics: averaging independent noisy estimates reduces variance, but a shared bias remains — the unit gives the idea verbally, with no formulas.
- [structural-analogy] framing effects ↔ decision theory: equivalent options described differently get different choices, which conflicts with preference depending only on outcomes.
- [metaphor] WYSIATI ↔ game theory: a player who reasons only from activated information resembles limited-depth reasoning about others (canon link; not stated in this unit).

## Questions this raises
- When does System 2 actually step in? The unit says only that it may, in unfamiliar, high-stakes situations (pdf 79).

- How can one deliberately ask "what would I need to know?" before judging, as Kahneman says we do not (pdf 85)?
