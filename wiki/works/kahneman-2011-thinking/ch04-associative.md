# The Associative Machine

## L0 — One line
System 1 works by associative activation: an idea triggers many linked ideas, feelings and bodily reactions, so unnoticed primes (cues that make related ideas easier to evoke) can change what you think, feel and do.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How does System 1 make sense of the world so quickly? Kahneman answers that ideas sit in an associative memory network. Activating one idea spreads activation to many others, producing a coherent pattern of thoughts, emotions and body reactions. Priming studies show this activation can change behaviour: walking speed, cartoon ratings, voting, helping, paying for coffee. People are unaware of these effects. The author says the effects are robust but not necessarily large. His main point is that readers must accept the results as true about themselves, even though they feel nothing like them. System 1 is also the source of many later-discussed intuitive errors.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction from earlier chapters; no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Two odd words ("Bananas Vomit") set off a fast, automatic cascade: memories, disgust, bodily reaction, a causal story, mild surprise (pdf 52–53).
- Associative activation is coherent: ideas, emotions, expressions and actions reinforce each other (pdf 52–53).
- Ideas are nodes in an associative memory network with several kinds of links. Many ideas are active at once, and most of this work is hidden from consciousness (pdf 53–54).
- Priming means an idea makes related ideas easier to evoke. It reaches actions and emotions, not just words (pdf 54–55).
- Priming links run both ways between mind and body: old-age words slow walking, slow walking makes old-age words quicker to recognise. Smiling, frowning and nodding shift judgments (pdf 55–56).
- Reported priming effects: school polling places, money cues (independence, selfishness), shame cues (cleansing), watching-eyes posters (more payment) (pdf 56–59).
- The effects are "robust but not necessarily large", yet a few percent could tip an election. Disbelief "is not an option" (pdf 58).
- System 1 supplies impressions, impulses and the model of what is normal. Your subjective experience is largely the story System 2 tells itself (pdf 58–60).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the "Bananas Vomit" demonstration (pdf 52–53)
- Reading two words quickly causes unpleasant images, a slight disgust expression, faster heart rate, raised arm hair, sweat. This is an attenuated version of reacting to the real event, and it is automatic (pdf 52).
- Your mind assumes a time order and a cause (bananas caused sickness). You feel a brief aversion to bananas. You are readier to recognise related words (*sick, stink, nausea*; *yellow, fruit*, perhaps *apple, berries*). You notice the pairing is unusual and feel mild surprise (pdf 52).
- Kahneman names the process **associative activation**: evoked ideas trigger many others in a spreading cascade (pdf 52). Its essential feature is **coherence**. Word → memories → emotions → facial expression and avoidance → stronger feelings → compatible ideas. The pattern is "associatively coherent" (pdf 52–53).
- In about a second System 1 linked the words in a causal story, judged the threat, prepared you for newly likely events and assessed the surprise (pdf 53).
- It treated the word pair as a representation of reality. Kahneman, citing cognitive scientists, says cognition is embodied: "you think with your body, not only with your brain" (pdf 53).

*Intuition note:* Your mind does not wait for you to decide to react. A word starts a chain of memory, feeling and body response that supports itself, and, in Kahneman's words, you did not will it and could not stop it.

### Associative memory (pdf 53–54)
- Hume (1748) reduced the principles of association to three: resemblance, contiguity in time and place, and causality. Kahneman says the modern concept has changed radically, but these three principles are still a good starting point (pdf 53).
- Kahneman takes an expansive view of "idea": concrete or abstract, expressed as a word or a clenched fist. Ideas are nodes in a vast network, **associative memory**. Link types: cause–effect (virus → cold), thing–property (lime → green), thing–category (banana → fruit) (pdf 53).
- Beyond Hume: a great deal happens at once, and each activated idea activates many others. Only a few activated ideas register in consciousness. Kahneman says this limited access to our own minds is hard to accept but true: you know far less about yourself than you feel you do (pdf 53–54).

### The Marvels of Priming (pdf 54–56)
- The old method was to ask for the first word that comes to mind (e.g., for DAY: "night", "sunny", "long"). In the 1980s psychologists found that seeing a word measurably changes how easily related words are evoked (pdf 54).
- Example: after EAT you are more likely to complete SO_P as SOUP. After WASH you are more likely to complete it as SOAP. This is a **priming effect** (pdf 54).
- Priming also makes you quicker to recognise a whispered or blurry SOUP. It spreads to *fork, hungry, fat, diet, cookie*, and a recent wobbly table primes *wobbly*. Primed ideas prime others more weakly, so activation spreads in ripples through a small part of the network (pdf 54).
- Priming is not restricted to concepts and words. Kahneman says you must accept that your actions and emotions can be primed by events you are not aware of (pdf 54).
- **Florida effect (Bargh and colleagues):** NYU students, mostly aged 18–22, built four-word sentences from five words. For one group, half the scrambled sentences contained elderly-related words (*Florida, forgetful, bald, gray, wrinkle*). These students then walked down the corridor significantly more slowly than the others (pdf 54–55).
- Two stages of priming: words → thoughts of old age (the word "old" never appears) → slow walking. No student noticed the common theme, and all insisted nothing could have influenced them (pdf 55). This is the **ideomotor effect**: an idea influences an action (pdf 55).
- Kahneman adds that reading the paragraph primed the reader too: you would have been slightly slower than usual to rise from a chair, unless you dislike the elderly, in which case research suggests you might have been slightly faster (pdf 55).
- **Reverse direction (German study):** students walked for 5 minutes at 30 steps per minute, about one-third their normal pace. Afterwards they were much quicker to recognise old-age words (*forgetful, old, lonely*). Reciprocal links tend to produce coherent reactions (pdf 55).
- Other reciprocal links: amusement and smiling. Students holding a pencil in a smile-shaped or frown-shaped way rated *Far Side* cartoons; the "smiling" group found them funnier (pdf 55–56). Frowning (eyebrows squeezed) enhanced emotional response to upsetting pictures (pdf 56).
- Headphone study: participants nodded or shook their heads while hearing radio editorials. Nodders tended to accept the message and shakers tended to reject it, with no awareness (pdf 56). Kahneman concludes that the common advice to act calm and kind regardless of how you feel is very good advice: you are likely to be rewarded by actually feeling that way (pdf 56).

*Intuition note:* Mind and body are linked both ways. Putting your face or body into a pose can nudge the matching thought or feeling, just as the thought can nudge the pose.

### Primes That Guide Us (pdf 56–58)
- Kahneman says priming studies threaten our self-image as conscious, autonomous authors of our judgments and choices. Voting should not be affected by the polling station's location, he says, but it is. He adds that the effects of priming can reach into every corner of our lives (pdf 56).
- **Voting:** In Arizona precincts in 2000, support for school-funding propositions was significantly greater when the polling station was in a school than in a nearby location. A separate experiment found that images of classrooms and lockers also raised support. The image effect was larger than the difference between parents and other voters (pdf 56).
- **Money primes** (word tasks, Monopoly money, a dollar-bill screen saver; Vohs): primed people persevered almost twice as long on a hard problem before asking for help, were much less willing to help a confused student, and picked up fewer dropped pencils. Before a get-acquainted conversation with another person they set up two chairs much farther apart than non-primed peers (118 vs. 80 cm; the text layer is garbled in this sentence). Money-primed undergraduates also showed a greater preference for being alone (pdf 57).
- Kahneman's reading: the idea of money primes individualism, a reluctance to be involved with, depend on, or accept demands from others. He says Vohs left the implications to her readers, and that her findings suggest living in a culture full of money reminders may shape our behaviour and attitudes in ways we do not know about and may not be proud of (pdf 57).
- **Leader portraits (Kahneman's rhetorical question; no study is cited here):** he asks whether anyone can doubt that the ubiquitous portraits of the leader in dictatorial societies both convey the feeling of being watched and actually reduce spontaneous thought and independent action. The question implies he thinks not (pdf 57).
- Kahneman says the evidence of priming studies suggests that reminding people of their mortality increases the appeal of authoritarian ideas, which may become reassuring in the context of the terror of death (pdf 57).
- **Lady Macbeth effect:** after recalling a shameful act, people more often complete W__H and S__P as WASH and SOAP (not WISH, SOUP). After thinking about stabbing a coworker in the back, they were more inclined to buy soap, disinfectant or detergent than batteries, juice or candy bars (pdf 57–58). After lying by phone, people preferred mouthwash to soap; after lying by e-mail, soap to mouthwash (pdf 58).
- **Disbelief and its answer:** audiences often disbelieve, because System 2 believes it is in charge and knows its reasons. Kahneman voices the questions he expects from the reader: how can such trivial manipulations have such large effects, and are we completely at the mercy of whatever primes the environment provides? To the second his answer is of course not; the effects are robust but not necessarily large. Among a hundred voters only a few, with uncertain preferences, would vote differently, but a few percent could tip an election (pdf 58).
- Disbelief "is not an option": the results are not made up or statistical flukes, and you must accept that the major conclusions are true, and true about you as well. Your subjective experience is largely the story System 2 tells itself, and priming happens in System 1 without conscious access (pdf 58).
- **Honesty-box study (British university office kitchen):** for ten weeks a new poster hung above the price list, alternating flowers and watching eyes. Week 1 (eyes): average contribution 70 pence per litre of milk. Week 2 (flowers): about 15 pence. Over the whole period contributions were almost three times as high in eye weeks as in flower weeks, with no one commenting (pdf 58–59, figure 4).

*Intuition note:* A purely symbolic reminder of being watched, even a picture of eyes, was followed by higher payments, and the text says this happened without any awareness. Kahneman then asks whether you now believe you would fall into the same pattern.

### Closing and "Speaking of Priming" (pdf 59–60)
- Citing Timothy Wilson's *Strangers to Ourselves*, Kahneman says you have now met that "stranger in you", which may be in control of much of what you do although you rarely glimpse it. System 1 provides impressions that often become beliefs and impulses that often become choices (pdf 59).
- It links the present to the recent past and near-future expectations, holds the model of the world that tags events as normal or surprising, and gives rapid, often precise intuitive judgments, mostly without awareness. It is also the origin of many systematic errors, to be seen in later chapters (pdf 59–60).
- The chapter ends with sample sentences (pdf 60). One says that the world makes much less sense than you think, and that the coherence comes mostly from the way your mind works.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Model readiness to complete SO_P as a score; both fillers start at 1.0. Seeing EAT adds 0.8 to SOUP and nothing to SOAP.
- SOUP = 1.0 + 0.8 = 1.8; SOAP = 1.0.
- If choice chance is proportional to score: P(SOUP) = 1.8 / (1.8 + 1.0) = 1.8 / 2.8 ≈ 0.64; P(SOAP) ≈ 0.36.
- With no prime: 1.0 / 2.0 = 0.50 each. If WASH added 0.8 to SOAP instead, the numbers would reverse (P(SOAP) ≈ 0.64).
- Spreading: if EAT → FORK adds 0.8 and FORK → SPOON has link strength 0.5, SPOON gets only 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4, a weaker ripple.
This is a simplified illustration of associative activation.

## Concepts
- [[associative-activation]] (new) — evoked ideas trigger many others in a spreading, mostly unconscious cascade (pdf 52)
- [[associative-coherence]] (new) — ideas, emotions, expressions and actions mutually reinforce one another (pdf 52–53)
- [[associative-memory]] (new) — network of idea-nodes linked by cause, property and category (pdf 53)
- [[priming-effect]] (new) — an idea makes related ideas easier to evoke, with or without awareness (pdf 54)
- [[ideomotor-effect]] (new) — an idea influences an action, e.g. old-age words and slow walking (pdf 55)
- [[system-1]] (new) — the automatic, fast process behind associations, impressions and impulses (pdf 52, 59)
- [[embodied-cognition]] (new) — thinking involves the body, not only the brain (pdf 53)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Associative memory network with spreading activation ↔ network science: activation diffusing over a graph with weaker signals at greater distance.
- [metaphor] Priming ↔ framing effect: an unnoticed context changes the response to a logically unchanged task.
- [structural-analogy] Eyes poster and honesty box ↔ game theory: the shadow of being observed changes the payoff to defecting, though here the cue is only symbolic.

## Questions this raises
- If Kahneman says priming effects are robust but not necessarily large (pdf 58), how can one measure their size and tell them from flukes?
- Which of the primed behaviours happen in real choices outside the laboratory?
- Kahneman says our subjective experience consists largely of the story System 2 tells itself (pdf 58). How could we ever check our own choices for hidden primes?
- Why does System 1 produce systematic errors (pdf 60) if its model of the world is often precise?
