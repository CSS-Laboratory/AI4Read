# The Engine of Capitalism

## L0 — One line
Optimism, helped by WYSIATI, competition neglect and social pressure to look confident, can sometimes lead people and firms to take risks they would avoid if they knew the odds; it helps action and resilience, and a premortem is a partial remedy (pdf 248, 256–257).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people and firms take risks the odds do not justify? Kahneman says optimistic bias is normal and may be the most significant cognitive bias for decisions. Several factors feed it: wishful thinking, WYSIATI (judging from the information at hand), competition neglect (ignoring rivals' plans), the above-average effect (rating oneself above average) and overconfidence. Social pressure then rewards confident experts. The evidence comes from entrepreneurs, inventors, CEOs, CFOs and physicians. Optimism has real benefits, mainly resilience, and risk-taking may help the economy even when most risk takers lose. Attempts to train overconfidence away have had only a few modest successes. Gary Klein's premortem (a team imagines its plan failed) is offered as a partial remedy for organizations.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and simple probability; the idea of System 1 (fast, automatic thinking) and WYSIATI (the habit of judging only from the information at hand).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Optimistic bias is pervasive and may be the most significant cognitive bias for decisions; a mild form brings benefits, but only if the person keeps track of reality (pdf 248).
- Entrepreneurs tend to believe that base rates (how often something succeeds in general) do not apply to them. About 35% of US small businesses survive five years, yet 81% of surveyed entrepreneurs put their own odds at 7 in 10 or higher (pdf 249).
- Inventors given a clear "hopeless" verdict often persisted. Of those who continued, the average inventor doubled the initial loss before quitting (pdf 250).
- Optimistic CEOs, measured by their personal stock holdings, were more likely to overpay and make value-destroying mergers. They took more risk when more of their own money was at stake (pdf 250–251).
- Competition neglect and the above-average effect are produced by WYSIATI and substitution of an easier question. Competition neglect leads to excess entry (pdf 252–253).
- CFOs' forecasts of the S&P (a US stock index) had a correlation slightly below zero with outcomes, and their 80% intervals (ranges they gave an 80% chance of containing the result) missed 67% of the time. Social pressure favoring confidence helps explain such overconfidence (pdf 254–255).
- Optimism supports resilience and implementation. The author doubts that training removes overconfidence (pdf 256–257).
- Klein's premortem asks a group to write the history of an imagined disaster. It counters groupthink (a team suppressing doubt once a decision seems made) and is a partial remedy only (pdf 257).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening.** The planning fallacy is one of many forms of a pervasive optimistic bias. We see the world as more benign, ourselves as better, and our goals as more achievable than they are, and we exaggerate our ability to forecast (pdf 248). Because the bias is both a blessing and a risk, an optimist has reason to be both happy and wary (pdf 248).

**Optimists.**
- An optimistic attitude is largely inherited. Optimists are cheerful, resilient, less likely to be depressed, healthier, and likely to live longer (pdf 248).
- People who overstate their life expectancy work longer hours, expect higher income, remarry more often after divorce, and bet more on individual stocks (pdf 248).
- The blessings go only to people who are *mildly* biased and keep track of reality (pdf 248).
- Optimists shape our lives: inventors, entrepreneurs, political and military leaders. They sought challenges, were talented and almost certainly luckier than they acknowledge, and success confirmed their confidence. A survey of small-business founders found entrepreneurs more sanguine than midlevel managers about life in general (pdf 248–249).
- Hypothesis: the people with the greatest influence on others are likely to be optimistic, overconfident, and to take more risk than they realize (pdf 249).
- The evidence suggests that optimistic bias plays a role, sometimes the dominant one, whenever individuals or institutions voluntarily take significant risks. Risk takers often underestimate the odds. The text layer says they "do invest sufficient effort" to learn them; context implies *do not* (pdf 249).
- Optimism helps get resources and raise morale, so when action is needed, optimism, even of a mildly delusional kind, may be a good thing (pdf 249).

*Intuition note:* A little optimism helps people act and keep going. The cost is that decision makers may not see the risk they run. Both effects come from the same bias.

**Entrepreneurial Delusions.**
- About 35% of US small businesses survive five years (pdf 249).
- Entrepreneurs' average estimate of success for any business like their own was 60%, almost double the true value. 81% put their own odds at 7 in 10 or higher, and 33% said their chance of failing was zero (pdf 249).
- Imagined restaurant owner (the author's example): 60% of new restaurants are out of business after three years. The author says the outside view probably did not occur to her (pdf 249).
- Åstebro's data come from Canada's Inventor's Assistance Program. It rates inventions on 37 criteria and gives a letter grade. D and E predict failure and were given to over 70% of inventions (pdf 249–250).
- Forecasts of failure were accurate: of 411 projects with the lowest grade, only 5 reached commercialization and none was successful (pdf 250).
- About half of the inventors quit after a clear failure grade. 47% continued, and on average they doubled their initial losses. Persistence was relatively common among inventors scoring high on an optimism measure (pdf 250).
- Private invention returns were small, lower than the returns on private equity and on high-risk securities. People with the same qualifications earn more on average by selling their skills to employers than by self-employment. Kahneman says the evidence suggests that optimism is widespread, stubborn, and costly (pdf 250).
- Most people believe they are superior to most others on most desirable traits, and will bet small sums on it in the lab (pdf 250).
- Large mergers: the stock market usually marks down the acquirer, since integrating large firms fails more often than it succeeds. The "hubris hypothesis" explains such mergers by executives being less competent than they think (pdf 250).
- Malmendier and Tate classed CEOs as optimistic by the company stock they personally own. Such CEOs took on debt rather than issuing equity and were more likely to overpay for targets (pdf 250).
- The acquirer's stock suffered substantially more in mergers by these CEOs. So the market apparently identifies overconfident CEOs (pdf 250–251).
- This clears CEOs of betting other people's money: they take *greater* risks when they have more at stake (pdf 251).
- Firms with award-winning CEOs later underperform in stock and operations. CEO pay rises, outside activities grow, and earnings management is more likely (pdf 251).
- Vancouver Island motel: a young couple bought a motel that "six or seven previous owners" had failed to run profitably. They planned a loan for a restaurant and felt no need to explain why they would succeed (pdf 251).
- Optimistic risk-taking surely contributes to the economic dynamism of capitalism, even if most risk takers end up disappointed. Coelho raises a policy problem: should government lend to would-be entrepreneurs who will probably go bankrupt? Many behavioral economists accept libertarian-paternalist nudges that raise saving rates, but the question of whether and how government should support small business has no equally satisfying answer (pdf 251–252).

*Intuition note:* The failure rate is public, but each founder treats it as a fact about other people. Inventors and CEOs show the cost: some inventors kept going after clear bad news, and optimistic CEOs were more likely to make value-destroying mergers. The author says there is no evidence that risk takers have an unusual appetite for gambles; they are merely less aware of risks (pdf 256).

**Competition Neglect.**
- Emotion (wishful thinking) is only part of the story. Cognitive biases, notably WYSIATI, play an important role (pdf 252). Four linked habits:
  - We focus on our goal, anchor on our plan, and neglect base rates.
  - We focus on what we can do and neglect others' plans and skills.
  - We credit skill and neglect luck, which gives an illusion of control.
  - We focus on what we know and neglect what we do not know (pdf 252).
- The finding that 90% of drivers believe they are better than average is now read as a cognitive bias, not self-aggrandizement (pdf 252).
- Two questions: whether you are a good driver is easy to answer. Whether you are better than average needs an estimate of the average, so people answer the easier question (substitution) (pdf 252).
- Evidence: for tasks people find difficult, such as starting conversations with strangers, they readily rate themselves below average. The author's upshot: people tend to be overly optimistic about relative standing in activities where they do moderately well (pdf 252–253).
- Founders asked how much the outcome depends on what they do in their firm answered at least 80% in the author's small sample. The author says they are surely wrong: the outcome depends as much on competitors and market changes (pdf 253).
- Camerer and Lovallo coined "competition neglect." A Disney Studios chairman explained why big films open on the same days: "Hubris," because each studio thinks only about its own business (pdf 253).
- The author reads this as substitution. The needed question is how many people will see our film given what others do. The studios instead asked whether they had a good film and a good marketing organization (pdf 253).
- WYSIATI plus substitution produce competition neglect and the above-average effect. Excess entry means more competitors than the market can profitably sustain, so their average outcome is a loss. The economy as a whole may still gain. Dosi and Lovallo call failed firms that signal new markets "optimistic martyrs" (pdf 253–254).

*Intuition note:* Each entrant answers an easy question about itself and skips the hard one about the whole market. When every entrant does this, the market holds too many firms. The failures can still be useful to others.

**Overconfidence.**
- Duke CFO survey: 11,600 forecasts of next-year S&P returns. The correlation with true values was slightly less than zero. The CFOs apparently did not know their forecasts were worthless (pdf 254).
- Each CFO also gave a value 90% sure to be too high and one 90% sure to be too low. This is an "80% confidence interval." Outcomes outside it are "surprises," and about 20% are expected (pdf 254).
- Actual surprises: 67%, more than 3 times the expected rate (pdf 254).
- Overconfidence is another WYSIATI effect. We use the information that comes to mind and build a coherent story. Allowing for what never came to mind is impossible (pdf 254).
- To keep surprises at 20%, CFOs should have said every year: 80% chance the S&P return will be between −10% and +30%. That interval is more than 4 times wider than those they stated (pdf 254).
- Social reasons: a CFO giving that interval would be laughed at. A wide interval confesses ignorance, which is socially unacceptable for a paid expert. Truman's wish for a "one-armed economist" is the example (pdf 254–255).
- The CFOs most confident about the S&P were also overconfident about their own firm, which took more risk. Taleb is cited: inadequate appreciation of uncertainty inevitably leads economic agents to take risks they should avoid (pdf 255).
- Optimism is rewarded in markets. The author draws a lesson from the financial crisis: competition among experts and organizations can favor collective blindness to risk (pdf 255).
- Tetlock: the most overconfident experts were the most likely to be invited on news shows (pdf 255).
- Medicine: in an ICU autopsy study, clinicians "completely certain" of the diagnosis were wrong 40% of the time. A quoted passage (its speaker is not named on the page) says that appearing unsure is considered a weakness and that confidence is valued over uncertainty (pdf 255).
- The author calls an unbiased appreciation of uncertainty a cornerstone of rationality, but not what people and organizations want. Acting on pretended knowledge is often the preferred solution (pdf 255).
- Emotional, cognitive and social factors combine and sometimes lead people to take risks they would avoid if they knew the odds. The author says there is no evidence that risk takers have an unusual appetite for high-stakes gambles; they are less aware of risks. Lovallo and Kahneman: "bold forecasts and timid decisions" (pdf 256).

*Intuition note:* A calibrated 80% interval should be missed about one time in five. The CFOs missed two times in three, so their ranges were far too narrow. Honest ranges would have been more than four times wider, but such wide ranges sound like a confession of ignorance.

**Optimism and implementation (untitled section).**
- The author says the effects of high optimism on decision making are, at best, a mixed blessing, but its contribution to good implementation is certainly positive. The main benefit is resilience after setbacks (pdf 256).
- Seligman: an "optimistic explanation style" takes credit for successes but little blame for failures. It can be taught at least to some extent, for example to cold-call insurance sellers. After a door is slammed in a salesperson's face, blaming the customer is clearly better for resilience than blaming oneself (pdf 256).
- The author *believes* (not shown) that science needs a form of optimism: he has yet to meet a successful scientist who cannot exaggerate the importance of their work (pdf 256).

**The Premortem: A Partial Remedy.**
- The author is not optimistic about training. Reports of success are few and modest. Royal Dutch Shell geologists became less overconfident after training with many past cases of known outcome. Considering competing hypotheses mitigated, but did not eliminate, overconfidence (pdf 256).
- Reason: overconfidence follows from System 1 features that can be tamed but not vanquished. Subjective confidence reflects the coherence of the story, not the quality and amount of supporting information (pdf 256–257).
- Organizations may tame optimism better than individuals can (pdf 257).
- Klein's premortem: when a decision is almost made but not formally committed, gather knowledgeable people. The leader asks them to imagine it is a year later, the plan was carried out as it stands, and the outcome was a disaster, and to write a brief history of that disaster in 5 to 10 minutes (pdf 257).
- Two advantages: it overcomes groupthink once a decision seems made, and it directs the imagination of knowledgeable people in a much-needed direction (pdf 257).
- The problem it targets: as a team converges, public doubts are gradually suppressed and eventually come to be treated as flawed loyalty, which feeds overconfidence in a group where only supporters of the decision have a [word cut off by garbling]. The sentence about the premortem's limits is partly garbled in the text layer; the legible part says it does not provide complete protection against nasty surprises but goes some way toward reducing the damage from plans subject to WYSIATI and uncritical optimism (pdf 257).

*Intuition note:* Once a decision seems made, public doubt tends to be treated as disloyalty. The premortem asks people to imagine failure, which points their imagination at threats the team may have neglected.

**Speaking of Optimism.** The chapter ends with sample remarks: "illusion of control," "competitor neglect," "overconfidence," and a proposal to hold a premortem (pdf 257–258).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A manager gives 10 yearly sales forecasts, each as an 80% interval. A well-calibrated person should see about 20% surprises: 20% × 10 = 2.

Suppose 7 actual results fall outside the intervals. Surprise rate = 7 / 10 = 70%. That is 70 / 20 = 3.5 times the expected rate, similar to the CFOs' 67%.

To catch about 8 of 10 outcomes, suppose each range must be 4 times wider. A stated width of ±5 would become ±20.

Competition neglect uses the same logic. If a weekend can profitably hold 3 films but 5 open, 2 more than the market supports, each studio's own-film question (whether our own film is good) never sees that count.

## Questions this raises
- If optimists are mostly the people who shape decisions, when do the benefits (resilience, resources, morale) outweigh the costs of ignoring base rates?
- Is competition neglect better described as a limit on what a decision maker can know, or as a failure to ask the right question?
- Can a premortem work when the leader's view is already clear? What would show that it reduces overconfidence?
- Should governments support businesses that will probably fail, given their possible benefit to the economy as a whole?
