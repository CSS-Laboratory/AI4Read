# Experienced Well-Being

## L0 — One line
Well-being can be measured by how people feel moment to moment (the experiencing self), and this differs from how satisfied they say they are with life (the remembering self).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Standard happiness research asks one global life-satisfaction question, which addresses the remembering self. Kahneman instead focuses on the experiencing self and tries to measure its feelings. His team built the Day Reconstruction Method and the U-index, the share of time spent in an unpleasant state. Mood depends mostly on the current situation and on attention. Situation, social contact and health shape daily feelings; education raises life evaluation but not experienced well-being. Income above about $75,000 (high-cost areas) adds life satisfaction but, on average, no experienced well-being. His conclusion: life satisfaction is not a flawed measure of experience but "something else entirely." Surveys of time use and feelings can inform social policy.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and averages; the idea of a survey rating scale; the experiencing vs. remembering self (from the colonoscopy studies, only mentioned here).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Global life satisfaction is a question to the remembering self. The author was suspicious of it as a valid measure of well-being, given his earlier work on mistaken memories (pdf 382).
- The author proposed that Helen's month counts as happy if her time use was like this: mostly activities she would rather continue, little time she wants to escape, not much time in a neutral state (pdf 382).
- A continuous record of feelings is impossible, so the team used experience sampling and then the Day Reconstruction Method (DRM), which was meant to approximate experience sampling and weights episodes by duration (pdf 383–384).
- The U-index is the percentage of time in an unpleasant state. Its appeal is that it is based on time rather than on a rating scale, though unpleasant episodes are identified from ratings (pdf 384).
- Mood depends mainly on the current situation and on what one attends to; temperament and overall happiness also matter, but mood at work is largely unaffected by the factors behind general job satisfaction (pdf 385).
- Time use is partly controllable, so individuals and policy may be able to lower the U-index (pdf 385–386).
- Education, ill health, children, religion and income affect life evaluation and daily feelings differently (pdf 386–387).
- Poverty makes people miserable. Income above about $75,000 raises life satisfaction but not experienced well-being on average (pdf 387).
- Life evaluation and experience may be related, but they are different (pdf 388).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The starting point: a question for the remembering self
- Most well-being knowledge came from answers to variants of: "All things considered, how satisfied are you with your life as a whole these days?" This addresses the remembering self (pdf 382).
- Because the remembering self had been a poor witness in the colonoscopy and cold-hand experiments, Kahneman focused on the experiencing self (pdf 382).
- Proposal: "Helen was happy in the month of March" if she spent most of her time in activities she would rather continue than stop, little time in situations she wished to escape, and not too much time in a neutral state, where she would not care either way (pdf 382).
- Examples of wanting to continue: *flow* (Csikszentmihalyi's term for total absorption in a task), and the author's childhood crying when interrupted at play. Resistance to interruption signals a good time (pdf 382).
- Method: evaluate a profile of experienced well-being over successive moments, as with the two colonoscopy patients, following Edgeworth's hedonimeter (pdf 382–383).
- The author was first inclined to dismiss the remembering self as error-prone. He suspected this was too extreme, "which it turned out to be" (pdf 383).

### Measuring experience
- A continuous record is impossible, because a person cannot live normally while reporting. The closest option is *experience sampling*: a phone beeps at random times and asks about activity, company and feeling intensity (happiness, tension, anger, worry, engagement, pain and others) (pdf 383).
- It is expensive and burdensome. The team therefore built the *Day Reconstruction Method (DRM)*, hoping to approximate experience sampling (pdf 383).
- DRM procedure: in a two-hour session (all women in early studies), participants relive the previous day as film-like episodes. For each episode they pick activities and the main one attended to, list companions, and rate feelings on 0–6 scales (0 = absent, 6 = most intense) (pdf 383).
- It draws on evidence that people who recall a situation in detail can relive its feelings. The team assumed participants would fairly accurately recover the feeling of a *prototypical moment* of each episode. The author reports that several comparisons with experience sampling confirmed validity (pdf 383).
- Episode start and end times allow a *duration-weighted* measure of feeling over the waking day: longer episodes count more (pdf 383–384). Life-satisfaction questions in the same questionnaire were read as the remembering self's satisfaction (pdf 384). The DRM was used with several thousand women in the United States, France and Denmark (pdf 384).

### The U-index
- Moments mix many positive and negative feelings, but most can be classed as positive or negative overall (pdf 384).
- An episode is *unpleasant* if a negative feeling is rated higher than all the positive feelings. Share of time unpleasant: American women about 19%, French 16%, Danish 14% (pdf 384).
- **U-index** = percentage of time spent in an unpleasant state. Example: 4 hours of a 16-hour waking day gives 25% (pdf 384).
- Its appeal is that it rests on time, not on a rating scale. A fall from 20% to 18% means total time in discomfort or pain fell by a tenth (pdf 384).
- Inequality: about half of participants had a whole day with no unpleasant episode. A significant minority had much distress. It appears a small fraction suffers most, whether from illness, temperament or misfortune (pdf 384).
- By activity (1,000 American women, a Midwestern city): morning commute 29%, work 27%, child care 24%, housework 18%, socializing 12%, TV 12%, sex 5% (pdf 384).
- Weekdays were about 6% higher than weekends, mostly because weekends bring less disliked activity and no work stress (pdf 384–385).
- Surprise: time with children was slightly less enjoyable than housework for American women. French women spend less time with children but enjoy it more, perhaps because of more child care access and less driving (pdf 385).

*Intuition note:* The U-index asks one simple question: for what share of the day did bad feelings beat all good ones? Counting time, not scale points, lets us say things like "a tenth less suffering."

### What drives mood
- Mood at a moment depends on temperament and overall happiness too, but primarily on the current situation. Mood at work is largely unaffected by factors behind general job satisfaction, such as benefits and status. More important are situational factors: the opportunity to socialize with coworkers, loud noise, time pressure, and the immediate presence of a boss (in the first study, the only thing that was worse than being alone) (pdf 385).
- *Attention* is key. We feel what we attend to, which is normally the present activity. Exceptions: being in love or grieving can dominate experience (pdf 385).
- Example: French and American women ate for about the same time. For French women eating was twice as likely to be focal, and Americans, who combined eating with other things, got diluted pleasure (pdf 385).
- Implications: few individuals can will themselves into a sunnier disposition, but some may be able to arrange less commuting and more time with liked people. Another option is to shift time from passive leisure (TV) to active leisure (socializing, exercise) (pdf 385).
- Socially, better transport, child care and socializing opportunities for the elderly *may* be relatively efficient ways to cut the U-index. Even 1% would mean millions of hours of avoided suffering (pdf 385–386). Alan Krueger led the effort to bring the method into national statistics (pdf 386).

### Large surveys: Gallup
- Experienced-well-being questions now appear in national surveys, and the Gallup World Poll covers millions of respondents in over 150 countries. They ask about the previous day's emotions, in less detail than the DRM (pdf 386).
- Findings confirm the role of situation, health and social contact. A headache makes people miserable. The second best predictor of a day's feelings is contact with friends or relatives. The author says it is only a slight exaggeration to say that "happiness is the experience of spending time with people you love and who love you" (pdf 386).
- Life evaluation is measured by the Cantril ladder (steps 0 = worst possible life to 10 = best possible life) (pdf 386).
- Differences between the two aspects (pdf 386–387):
  - More education is associated with higher life evaluation but not with greater experienced well-being. In the US the more educated tend to report more stress.
  - Ill health hurts experience much more than evaluation.
  - Living with children costs daily feelings (stress, anger) more than evaluation.
  - Religious participation helps positive affect and stress more than evaluation, but not depression or worry.

### Money
- From more than 450,000 responses to the Gallup-Healthways Well-Being Index (daily, 1,000 Americans): being poor makes one miserable, and being rich *may* enhance life satisfaction but does not, on average, improve experienced well-being (pdf 387).
- Poverty amplifies misfortune. A headache raises the share reporting sadness and worry from 19% to 38% in the top two-thirds of incomes, and from 38% to 70% in the poorest tenth. Divorce, loneliness and the weekend effect also differ for the very poor (pdf 387).
- Satiation level: about $75,000 household income in high-cost areas, possibly less where living is cheaper. Beyond it the average gain in experienced well-being was "precisely zero" (pdf 387).
- Offered explanation (plausible, not proven): higher income may reduce the ability to enjoy small pleasures. Suggestive evidence: priming students with wealth reduced the pleasure their faces showed eating chocolate (pdf 387).
- Income raises life satisfaction well beyond the point where it stops affecting experience (pdf 387).

### Conclusion
- As with colonoscopies, evaluations of life and actual experience may be related but are different. Life satisfaction is not a flawed measure of experienced well-being, as the author once thought. It is "something else entirely" (pdf 388).
- The chapter ends with three sample remarks (illustrative lines, not findings): policy should aim to reduce human suffering and lower the U-index, with depression and extreme poverty a priority; the easiest way to increase happiness is to control your use of time; beyond the satiation level of income, you can buy more pleasurable experiences but lose some ability to enjoy cheaper ones (pdf 388).

*Intuition note:* Asking "how is your life?" and recording "how did yesterday feel?" give different answers. Education and money improve the first more than the second. Health, time use and company shape the second more.

## Toy example (ours)
A 16-hour waking day, ratings on the 0–6 scale. An episode is unpleasant only if its negative rating is higher than *all* its positive ratings.

| Episode | Hours | Negative | Highest positive | Unpleasant? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commute | 1 | 4 | 2 | yes (4 > 2) |
| Work | 7 | 3 | 3 | no (tie, not higher) |
| Lunch with friends | 1 | 0 | 5 | no |
| Housework | 2 | 4 | 1 | yes |
| TV | 3 | 1 | 3 | no |
| Dinner | 2 | 0 | 4 | no |

Total hours: 1+7+1+2+3+2 = 16. Unpleasant hours: 1+2 = 3. U-index = 3/16 = 18.75%. Counting episodes instead gives 2/6 ≈ 33%, which shows why duration weighting matters. Halving the commute to 0.5 h, with the freed 0.5 h going to lunch, gives 2.5/16 = 15.6%.

## Concepts
- [[experiencing-self-vs-remembering-self]] (new) — the self that feels moments vs. the self that evaluates and recalls life (pdf 382)
- [[experienced-well-being]] (new) — well-being as the profile of feelings over successive moments, judged by time spent in wanted vs. unwanted states (pdf 382)
- [[flow]] (new) — total absorption in a task; interruptions unwelcome (pdf 382)
- [[experience-sampling]] (new) — random phone beeps asking what a person is doing and feeling (pdf 383)
- [[day-reconstruction-method]] (new) — recall the prior day as episodes, then rate each episode's feelings (pdf 383)
- [[u-index]] (new) — percentage of time spent in episodes where a negative feeling is rated higher than all positive feelings (pdf 384)
- [[life-evaluation]] (new) — the judgment people make when they evaluate their life, e.g. the Cantril ladder (pdf 386)
- [[satiation-level-of-income]] (new) — income (~$75,000 in high-cost areas) beyond which average experienced well-being no longer rises (pdf 387)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] U-index ↔ statistics: a time-weighted proportion estimated by sampling moments — random beeps estimate the fraction of time in a state.
- [metaphor] Attention-dependent pleasure ↔ bounded rationality: limited attention shapes what we experience, as it shapes what we decide.

## Questions this raises
- How well does the DRM, which relies on recall, match experience sampling, given the remembering self's errors?
- Why might the satiation level differ by cost of living, and how was $75,000 estimated?
- If the U-index rests on "negative higher than all positives," how sensitive are results to that cut-off?
- Should policy aim at experienced well-being, life satisfaction, or both, when they diverge?
