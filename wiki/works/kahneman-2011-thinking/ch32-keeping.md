# Keeping Score

## L0 — One line
People keep emotional "scores" in their heads (mental accounts, regret, responsibility), and these scores push them into costly choices that a purely rational agent would not make.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks why people act against their own wealth or safety even when the money is not what motivates them. His answer: much of what drives us is self-administered emotional reward and punishment. Mental accounts (separate mental "books") lead to the disposition effect (selling winners, keeping losers) and the sunk-cost fallacy. Anticipated regret favours defaults and conventional choices. Strong responsibility feelings make many people refuse "taboo tradeoffs", such as accepting a tiny extra risk to a child for money. These tendencies harm individuals' wealth, sound policy and society. The emotions are nevertheless real, so Kahneman suggests adjusting to them rather than ignoring them. Gilbert and colleagues claim that we generally overestimate how much regret we will feel.

**Prerequisites:** loss aversion (losses weigh more than gains); the idea of a "fourfold pattern" of risk attitudes (mentioned, not explained here); the Econ/Human contrast (an Econ is a perfectly rational agent in economic theory).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Money often stands in for self-regard; these rewards and punishments are "in our heads", and the ultimate currency is often emotional (pdf 334).
- Mental accounts (separate mental "books" for money and outcomes) are a form of narrow framing (judging each account on its own). The investor wants to close each account at a gain (pdf 334–336).
- The disposition effect (selling winners, not losers) is costly: taxes and short-run momentum favour selling losers. Experienced investors are less prone to it (pdf 336).
- Sunk-cost fallacy: putting more resources into a losing project when better investments are available, rather than accept the humiliation of closing the account. Organisations suffer from it through the agency problem (a manager's incentives differ from the firm's goals). Research suggests that at least in some contexts it can be overcome, for example after it is taught as a mistake in courses (pdf 337).
- Regret depends on how easily we can imagine the alternative. Unusual events are easier to undo in imagination and so bring more regret; departures from the default bring more regret and blame. Together this favours conventional, risk-averse choices (pdf 338–340).
- Responsibility amplifies loss aversion. The "taboo tradeoff" and the precautionary principle (a rule that prohibits any action that might cause harm) are examples, and they can be inefficient (pdf 340–342).
- Practical advice: be explicit about regret beforehand, guard against hindsight, and note Gilbert and colleagues' claim that we may overpredict regret (pdf 343).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pdf 334)
Except for the very poor, the main motivators of money-seeking are not necessarily economic. Money is a proxy for points on a scale of self-regard and achievement. We "keep score" of these internal rewards and punishments. As a result we refuse to cut losses that would admit failure, we avoid actions that could cause regret, and we draw a sharp but illusory line between omission and commission, because responsibility feels greater for one than the other. This emotional "currency" creates conflicts of interest when someone acts as an agent for an organisation (pdf 334).

### Mental Accounts (pdf 334–337)
- **Mental accounts** (a topic Richard Thaler has studied for many years): we sort money and outcomes into separate accounts, some physical, some only mental. Examples are spending money, general savings and education savings. They are also used for self-control, such as budgets. Sometimes we pay for self-control, e.g. holding savings while carrying credit-card debt. For Humans they are narrow framing that keeps life manageable for a finite mind. Econs take a comprehensive view of outcomes and are driven by external incentives (pdf 334).
- Golf: pros putt better to avoid a bogey than to make a birdie. One conclusion is that the best golfers keep a separate account for each hole (pdf 334–335).
- **Blizzard example (Thaler):** two fans plan to drive 40 miles to a game, one having paid for his ticket and one having got it free. Who is more likely to drive into a blizzard? The answer is immediate: the one who paid. Explanation: missing the game closes the account at a negative balance, which is more negative for the buyer, who is out of pocket as well (pdf 335). This is a tacit System 1 emotional calculation. An Econ treats the ticket cost as sunk. To act like an Econ, System 2 would have to be aware of the counterfactual possibility: would I still drive into this snowstorm if I had got the ticket free from a friend? (pdf 335).
- **Disposition effect:** you need money for your daughter's wedding and will have to sell some stock. Blueberry Tiles shows a $5,000 gain and Tiffany Motors, an equal investment, a $5,000 loss; both values have been stable in recent weeks. Framed as pleasure versus pain, you will "certainly" sell the winner. Finance research documents a massive preference for selling winners (pdf 335–336).
- It is narrow framing: one account per share, each to be closed at a gain. A rational agent would view the whole portfolio and sell the stock least likely to do well (pdf 336).
- Kahneman corrects Amos's view that the purchase price is irrelevant. It does matter, because of taxes (US): realising losses reduces taxes and selling winners exposes you to them. Investors sell more losers in December. Recent winners also tend to keep gaining for a short while. The expected after-tax extra return of selling Tiffany rather than Blueberry is 3.4% over the next year (pdf 336).
- Experienced investors, using System 2, are less susceptible than novices (pdf 336).
- **Sunk-cost fallacy:** investing more in a losing account when better investments exist. A rational decision maker looks only at future consequences. Example: $50 million already spent on a late project. Another $60 million is needed, or the same sum could go into a new, more promising project. Companies often "drive into the blizzard". This is the top-right cell of the fourfold pattern: a sure loss versus an unfavourable gamble, which is often unwisely preferred (pdf 337).
- **Agency problem:** escalation is a mistake for the firm but not necessarily for the executive who "owns" the project, whose record would be stained. Boards often replace such a CEO. They do not necessarily think the new one is more competent. They know she lacks the same mental accounts (pdf 337).
- The fallacy keeps people too long in poor jobs, unhappy marriages and doomed research. Evidence suggests graduate students in economics and business are more willing to walk away from failing projects (pdf 337).

*Intuition note:* We treat each past decision as an unfinished account that we want to end well. This pulls attention to the past, although a rational decision maker looks only at future consequences.

### Regret (pdf 338–340)
- Regret is an emotion and a self-administered punishment. It is strongest when you can easily imagine doing something else. It is a counterfactual emotion, triggered by available alternatives to reality. Unusual events are easier to undo in imagination, because associative memory holds a picture of the normal world (pdf 338).
- **Hitchhikers:** Mr. Brown rarely picks up hitchhikers and Mr. Smith often does; both were robbed. Who feels more regret? Brown 88%, Smith 12%. Who will be criticised more? Brown 23%, Smith 77% (pdf 338–339). Regret and blame both come from comparison with a norm, but the norms differ. Regret compares with what you usually do. Blame compares with conventional standards of reasonable behaviour (pdf 339).
- **Paul and George:** both now own stock A and would be $1,200 better off with B. Paul considered switching and stayed (inaction). George switched (action), as the text implies; the line is garbled in the source. Who feels greater regret? Paul 8%, George 92%. The situations are objectively identical (pdf 339).
- People expect stronger emotional reactions, including regret and blame, to outcomes produced by action than by inaction. This has been verified for gambling. The asymmetry is at least as strong for losses (pdf 339).
- The key is the **default** and not commission versus omission. Deviating from the default makes the norm easy to imagine. Not selling a stock is the default; greeting a colleague is the default, so failing to greet is a deviation (pdf 339–340).
- Blackjack simulation: whether players were asked "Do you wish to hit?" or "Do you wish to stand?", saying yes brought more regret after a bad outcome. A coach whose team lost badly is expected to change something, and failure to do so brings blame (pdf 340).
- The asymmetry favours conventional, risk-averse choices. Consumers reminded of regret prefer brand names over generics. Fund managers clean up unconventional stocks near year-end. A physician who prescribes an unusual treatment (evidence inconclusive) faces a substantial risk of regret, blame and perhaps litigation. The potential benefit to reputation is smaller than the potential cost, because success is generally a more normal outcome than failure (pdf 340).

*Intuition note:* Regret is intense when you can easily picture the "normal" alternative. Deviating from the default makes that picture easy to build, so anticipated regret favours the conventional.

### Responsibility (pdf 340–343)
- Losses are weighted about twice as much as gains in several contexts (gambles, the endowment effect, price changes). The coefficient is much higher in some situations: you may be more loss averse for important matters such as health, and reluctance to "sell" important endowments increases dramatically when you might be responsible for an awful outcome (pdf 340–341).
- **Thaler's vaccine:** the disease has a 1/1,000 chance and brings a quick painless death. Most people pay a significant but limited amount for a vaccine. As paid volunteers who accept a 1/1,000 chance of the disease (with no vaccine), they demand far more. Thaler reported informally a typical ratio of about 50:1. Two reasons are given: selling your health is not seen as legitimate, and you would be responsible for a bad outcome and could have stayed with the default (pdf 341).
- **Insecticide survey:** risk of child poisoning rises from 15 to 16 per 10,000 bottles for a cheaper product. More than two-thirds would not buy it at any price. The minority who named a discount asked for a significantly higher amount than they would pay for a far larger safety improvement (pdf 341–342).
- Kahneman calls this attitude incoherent and potentially damaging. The "keeping-my-child-safe" account has a limited budget, and money saved could buy a safer car seat or socket covers. The resistance may stem more from a selfish fear of regret than from a wish to optimise safety (pdf 342).
- **Precautionary principle** (especially in Europe): it prohibits any action that might cause harm and puts the whole burden of proof on the actor. Sunstein notes it is costly and, strictly interpreted, paralysing. It would have blocked innovations such as antibiotics and X-rays (pdf 342). Kahneman says the strong version is untenable but rooted in a System 1 moral intuition. The dilemma has no simple solution (pdf 342–343).

*Intuition note:* When a trade means "selling" something you are not supposed to sell, such as health, the price asked can be extreme, partly because you would be responsible for a bad outcome and could have stayed with the default. Kahneman says an absolute attitude of this kind is potentially damaging to the safety of those we wish to protect.

### Closing advice (pdf 343)
- Self-administered punishments (and occasional rewards) are costly to Humans: they lead to actions detrimental to individual wealth, sound policy and society's welfare. But the emotions of regret and moral responsibility are real, and the fact that Econs lack them may not be relevant (pdf 343).
- Regret susceptibility is "a fact of life" to adjust to. A rich, cautious investor may afford a portfolio that minimises regret rather than maximises wealth (pdf 343).
- Precautions: be explicit about anticipating regret; if you can remember that you considered it carefully before deciding, you are likely to experience less of it. Beware hindsight bias, which comes with regret. Kahneman's own policy is to be very thorough or completely casual, because hindsight is worst when you think "just a little" (pdf 343).
- Gilbert and colleagues claim we anticipate more regret than we will feel, since we underestimate our "psychological immune system". Their recommendation is not to put too much weight on regret, since even if you have some, it will hurt less than you now think (pdf 343).

### Speaking of Keeping Score (pdf 343–344)
- Four example remarks close the chapter: someone keeps separate mental accounts for cash and credit purchases ("money is money"); a couple hold a stock only to avoid closing the account at a loss (the disposition effect); a couple never try a new dish, to avoid regret; and a buyer cannot choose the cheaper car seat because it feels like a taboo tradeoff (pdf 343–344).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assume a 20% tax rate, and that losses can be used to cut taxes at that rate. Two stocks are each worth $10,000 now. Stock W was bought for $5,000 (gain $5,000). Stock L was bought for $15,000 (loss $5,000). You must raise cash by selling one whole stock.
- Sell W: tax = 0.20 × 5,000 = $1,000, so net cash = 10,000 − 1,000 = $9,000.
- Sell L: tax saving = 0.20 × 5,000 = $1,000, so net cash-equivalent = 10,000 + 1,000 = $11,000.
- Difference: 11,000 − 9,000 = $2,000 in favour of selling L, before any future returns.

The mental-account view only sees "close W: success +5,000; close L: failure −5,000", so it picks W and gives up the $2,000. This illustrates the disposition effect. The $2,000 is our own number, unrelated to the 3.4% in the text.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Sunk-cost fallacy ↔ backward induction: both say only future payoffs should guide the current choice, and past costs should be ignored.
- [structural-analogy] Agency problem (CEO's incentives versus the firm's) ↔ mechanism design: aligning an agent's incentives with the principal's goal.
- [metaphor] Mental accounts and defaults ↔ focal points: both are salient reference structures that guide choices.

## Questions this raises
- If a board replaces a CEO to get rid of sunk-cost thinking, what other signals could force a firm to ignore sunk costs?
- How can one design a rule against harm, such as the precautionary principle, that does not give regret and moral intuition too much weight?
