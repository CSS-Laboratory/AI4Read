# Intuitions vs. Formulas

## L0 — One line
In uncertain ("low-validity") environments, simple formulas match or beat expert judgment, so intuition should be used only after objective information has been collected and scored in a disciplined way.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether expert intuition can beat simple prediction rules. Kahneman reports that in about two hundred studies the rules matched or beat experts, and no exception has been convincingly documented. Meehl suspected that experts try to be too clever, and they are also inconsistent. Equally weighted sums of a few scores can do about as well as optimally weighted regression. Many people still resist algorithms, partly because they prefer the "natural" and partly because the cause of a mistake matters to them. Kahneman's army-interview redesign gives a recipe. Score separate traits from factual questions, combine them by formula, and let intuition add a final score only after that. The lesson is neither "trust intuition" nor "dismiss it".

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a correlation (.90 is strong); System 1 as fast, automatic thinking; the idea of a weighted sum; multiple regression as a standard way to choose the weights.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Meehl's review and its successors: about 60% of roughly 200 studies favour algorithms significantly, the rest are ties, and a tie is "tantamount to a win" for the rules, which are normally much less expensive (pdf 216).
- Wine prices: Ashenfelter's three-weather-variable formula correlates above .90 with actual prices and beats current prices of young wines (pdf 217–218).
- Reasons experts lose (the first is Meehl's suspicion): they favour complex combinations of cues and overrule formulas, and they are inconsistent (e.g., radiologists contradict themselves 20% of the time) (pdf 218).
- Inconsistency is probably due to System 1's context dependency. Formulas give the same output for the same input, and when predictability is poor, inconsistency is destructive of any predictive validity (pdf 218–219).
- Dawes: equal-weight formulas are likely as accurate on new cases as optimal regression, so useful algorithms can be developed without prior statistical research. The Apgar score shows how much such a rule can achieve (pdf 219–220).
- Kahneman links resistance to algorithms to clinicians' illusion of skill, a strong preference many people have for the natural, and a stubborn psychological reality: for most people the cause of a mistake matters (pdf 221–222).
- Kahneman's army interview: separate trait ratings from factual questions, combined by formula, beat global judgments. The "close your eyes" intuition did as well as the six-rating sum, and Kahneman then gave it equal weight with that sum (pdf 224–225).
- A do-it-yourself hiring procedure applies this: about six traits, factual questions, one trait at a time, sum the scores, and hire the top total (pdf 225–226).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Meehl's "disturbing little book" (pdf 216–217)
- Meehl reviewed 20 studies comparing clinical predictions (subjective impressions of trained professionals) with statistical predictions (a few scores combined by a rule) (pdf 216).
- Typical study: counselors interviewed freshmen for 45 minutes and also had high school grades, several aptitude tests and a four-page personal statement. The formula used only high school grades and one aptitude test, yet it was more accurate than 11 of the 14 counselors (pdf 216).
- Similar results appeared for parole violations, success in pilot training and criminal recidivism (pdf 216).
- Today about 200 comparisons exist. About 60% show significantly better accuracy for algorithms. The others are draws, and Kahneman says a tie is "tantamount to a win" because rules are cheaper. No exception has been convincingly documented (pdf 216).
- Outcomes covered include cancer-patient longevity, credit risk, foster-parent suitability, and Bordeaux prices (pdf 217). Kahneman calls these domains "low-validity environments": they involve much uncertainty and unpredictability. In every case listed, a simple algorithm matched or exceeded the experts (pdf 217).
- Meehl, thirty years on, said no social-science controversy has such a large and diverse body of studies pointing so uniformly in one direction (pdf 217).

**Intuition note:** Even when the rule uses less information than the expert has, it usually does at least as well. In these unpredictable fields, the extra information in the expert's head (the interview, the taste of the wine) did not reliably produce a better forecast.

### Ashenfelter and Bordeaux (pdf 217–218)
- Aim: predict future prices of fine Bordeaux from information available in the vintage year. Bottles filled twelve months apart can differ in value by a factor of 10 or more (pdf 217).
- Vintage effects are generally agreed to come only from weather. Ashenfelter turned that knowledge into a formula using three variables: average summer growing-season temperature, rain at harvest-time, and total rain in the previous winter (pdf 217).
- It forecasts years and even decades ahead, and more accurately than the current prices of young wines. The correlation with actual prices is above .90 (pdf 217–218).
- Kahneman says this challenges the experts who shape early prices. He also says it challenges economic theory, under which prices should reflect all available information (pdf 217).

### Why experts are inferior to algorithms (pdf 218–219)
1. **Cleverness.** Meehl suspected experts try to be clever and consider complex combinations of features. Complexity may work in an odd case, but more often than not it reduces validity. Several studies found people do worse than the formula even when they are given its score, because they feel they hold extra information (pdf 218).
2. **Broken-leg rule.** Meehl's thought experiment: a formula predicts who will go to the movies tonight. It is proper to disregard it if you learn the person broke a leg today. Broken legs are very rare and decisive. Meehl held there are few circumstances where substituting judgment for a formula is a good idea (pdf 218).
3. **Inconsistency.** Humans are "incorrigibly inconsistent" when summarising complex information. Radiologists contradict themselves 20% of the time on the same chest X-ray. A study of 101 auditors found similar inconsistency. A review of 41 studies suggests this level is typical, even within minutes. Unreliable judgments cannot be valid predictors (pdf 218).
4. **Cause (Kahneman's "probably").** Inconsistency is probably due to System 1's context dependency. Examples: a cool breeze may make you slightly more positive, and a convict's parole prospects may change significantly between successive food breaks in the judges' schedule. We have little direct knowledge of what goes on in our minds, so we never know that we might have judged differently. Formulas always return the same answer for the same input, and when predictability is poor, inconsistency destroys validity (pdf 218–219).

**Conclusion (Kahneman):** the research suggests that, to maximise predictive accuracy, final decisions should be left to formulas, especially in low-validity environments (pdf 219). Kahneman says the evidence is fragmentary but gives solid grounds for a conjecture: conducting an interview is likely to diminish the accuracy of medical-school selection if the interviewers also make the final decision. Overconfident interviewers overweight their impressions. Likewise, the information that wine experts get by tasting young wine almost certainly makes things worse rather than better (pdf 219).

**Intuition note:** A formula is a judge who never has a bad day and never falls for a charming candidate. When predictability is poor, inconsistency is destructive of any predictive validity, so steadiness matters a great deal.

### Dawes and improper linear models (pdf 219–220)
- Multiple regression is the dominant statistical practice in the social sciences for weighting predictors; Kahneman calls its logic "unassailable": it finds the optimal weighted combination (pdf 219).
- Dawes observed that it adds little or no value. Pick predictors with some validity, make them comparable (standard scores or ranks), and weight them equally. This is likely just as accurate on new cases as the regression formula that was optimal in the original sample (pdf 219).
- Later research found equal weights are often superior, because they are not affected by accidents of sampling (pdf 219).
- Implication: useful algorithms can be built with no prior statistical research (pdf 219–220). Dawes's example: marital stability is well predicted by frequency of lovemaking minus frequency of quarrels (pdf 220).
- A back-of-envelope algorithm is often good enough to compete with an optimally weighted formula, and certainly to outdo expert judgment (pdf 220).

**Intuition note:** Fine-tuned weights can fit the quirks of one sample. Equal weights are not affected by such accidents of sampling, so they are likely to be as good as, or often better than, fitted weights on new cases.

### The Apgar score (pdf 220)
- Before 1953, clinicians judged newborn distress with different cues and no standard, and danger signs were often missed (pdf 220).
- Apgar listed five variables (heart rate, respiration, reflex, muscle tone, colour), each scored 0, 1 or 2. She first rated infants one minute after birth. A baby scoring 8 or above was likely in good shape; one scoring 4 or below was probably in need of immediate intervention (pdf 220).
- It gave delivery rooms consistent standards and is credited with an important contribution to reducing infant mortality. Kahneman says it has saved the lives of hundreds of thousands of infants. It is still used daily (pdf 220).

### The hostility to algorithms (pdf 221–223)
- **Clinicians' illusion of skill (Kahneman's view).** Therapists make many short-term predictions within sessions and often get instant feedback. The tasks at which they fail typically require long-term predictions, where feedback takes years and even the best formulas do only modestly well. The boundary of their skill is not obvious to them (pdf 221).
- **Moral language.** Meehl recorded critics calling statistics "mechanical… dead… blind" and clinical judgment "dynamic… holistic… living" (pdf 221).
- **Preference for the natural.** Most people prefer an organic apple even when told it is identical in taste and nutrition. The French wine world's reaction to Ashenfelter was, per *The New York Times*, between "violent and hysterical". One critic said: "It is like judging movies without actually seeing them" (pdf 222).
- **Consequential decisions.** Meehl wrote of clinicians' "horror" at a treatable case misclassified by a "blind, mechanical" equation. Meehl and others argue it is unethical to rely on intuition when an algorithm makes fewer mistakes. Kahneman says this runs against a stubborn reality: for most people the cause of a mistake matters (pdf 222).
- Kahneman says that when a human competes with a machine, our sympathies lie with the human, and that the aversion is rooted in a strong preference many people have for the natural over the synthetic (pdf 221–222).
- Kahneman says the hostility will probably soften as algorithms spread (recommendations, credit limits, cholesterol ratios, sports decisions) (pdf 222–223).

### Learning from Meehl (pdf 223–225)
- In 1955, aged 21, Kahneman had to set up an interview system for the whole Israeli army. Existing interviews (15–20 minutes, by young draftees seeking a general impression) were almost useless at predicting success (pdf 223).
- Following Meehl, he had interviewers collect specific facts about the recruit's life in his normal environment, and no longer let their global evaluations determine the final decision. They rated six traits separately (e.g., "responsibility", "sociability", "masculine pride"). Each trait had factual questions (jobs held, punctuality, contact with friends, sports). Traits were rated in fixed order on a five-point scale, to combat the halo effect (favourable first impressions colouring later judgments). A formula computed the final score (pdf 224).
- The interviewers protested ("You are turning us into robots!"). Kahneman compromised: after the interview, they closed their eyes, imagined the recruit as a soldier and gave a 1–5 score (pdf 224).
- Several hundred interviews were validated against commanders' evaluations. The sum of the six ratings predicted much more accurately than the old global method, though far from perfectly: from "completely useless" to "moderately useful" (pdf 224–225).
- Surprise: the "close your eyes" score did just as well as the six-rating sum. Kahneman gave it equal weight with that sum. Lessons: intuition adds value, but only after disciplined collection of objective information and disciplined scoring of separate traits. Do not simply trust intuition, "but do not dismiss it, either" (pdf 225).
- Some forty-five years later, the unit's commanding officer described a system little changed. Research indicated it still worked well, and she ended with "Close your eyes" (pdf 225).

### Do it yourself (pdf 225–226)
Kahneman's hiring procedure for a sales representative:
1. Choose about six prerequisite traits that are as independent as possible and assessable through a few factual questions (pdf 225).
2. Write questions and a 1–5 scoring guide for each. Preparation takes about half an hour (pdf 225).
3. Collect information one trait at a time, scoring each before the next (pdf 225–226).
4. Add the six scores. Do not do a "close your eyes" when you make the final decision. Hire the highest total even if you like another candidate better, and resist inventing "broken legs" (pdf 226).
- Kahneman says a vast amount of research promises you are more likely to find the best candidate this way than by overall intuition ("I looked into his eyes…") (pdf 226).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Three candidates are scored 1–5 on six traits, each scored separately in a fixed order.

| Candidate | Scores | Sum |
|---|---|---|
| A | 4+3+5+4+3+4 | 23 |
| B | 5+5+3+4+4+3 | 24 |
| C | 3+4+4+4+4+4 | 23 |

The equal-weight rule picks B, even if the interviewer "felt" A was best. If the interviewer's gut score is allowed, Kahneman's army rule gives it the same weight as the six-rating sum. Writing $S$ for the sum of the six ratings and $G$ for the 1–5 gut score, one way (our reading) to give $G$ the same weight as $S$ is to put it on the same scale as $S$ by multiplying by 6, since $S$ is a sum of six 1–5 ratings: final score $= S + 6G$. This is the "close your eyes" step, kept as one extra input. The do-it-yourself hiring procedure, by contrast, drops it.

## Concepts
- [[algorithm-vs-expert-judgment]] (new) — comparison of rule-based and subjective prediction; rules match or win in the studies reviewed (pdf 216).
- [[low-validity-environment]] (new) — a domain with a significant degree of uncertainty and unpredictability (pdf 217).
- [[broken-leg-rule]] (new) — disregarding a formula is proper when information is both very rare and decisive, as with a broken leg; Meehl saw few such circumstances (pdf 218).
- [[equal-weighting-formula]] (new) — combine standardised predictors with equal weights; often as good as regression on new cases (pdf 219).
- [[illusion-of-skill]] (new) — here, clinicians' confidence in long-term prediction built on success at short-term predictions (pdf 221).
- [[halo-effect]] (new) — favourable first impressions influence later judgments; Kahneman used factual questions and a fixed trait-by-trait order to combat it (pdf 224).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Equal weights beating optimal regression on new cases ↔ bias–variance trade-off: a simpler model can predict better because it is less affected by sampling accidents.
- [structural-analogy] Experts' inconsistent summary judgments ↔ noise in measurement: same input, different outputs lowers any possible validity.
- [metaphor] "Close your eyes" intuition as a late, bounded input ↔ forecasting: a judgmental adjustment layered on top of a model's output rather than replacing it.

## Questions this raises
- What makes an environment "low-validity", and could an environment be regular enough for expert intuition to win?
- How are the equal-weight predictors chosen, and what if two of them overlap strongly (the unit advises choosing independent traits)?
- Why did the "close your eyes" score add value here, given that intuition is usually inconsistent?
- How should one decide when a case is a true "broken leg" and not just a wish to override the formula?
