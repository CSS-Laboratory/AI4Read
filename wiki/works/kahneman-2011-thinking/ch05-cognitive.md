# Cognitive Ease

## L0 — One line
System 1 keeps a "dial" of cognitive ease, and many unrelated causes (clear font, repetition, good mood, priming) move it in the same way, so we can mistake ease for familiarity, truth, liking and coherence.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks how the feeling of "this is easy" shapes judgment. His answer: System 1 tracks ease versus strain. Ease can come from a clear font, repetition, priming or a good mood. Because the causes are interchangeable, people often cannot tell which one produced the feeling. They may take ease as a sign that something is familiar, true, good or coherent. Strain instead tends to mobilize System 2: people become more vigilant and analytic, and less intuitive and creative. This matters because predictable errors follow: false statements are more likely to be accepted after repetition, and bad fonts can improve reasoning. Ease also gives practical advice for persuasion, and a caution for your own thinking.

**Prerequisites:** The System 1 / System 2 distinction (fast automatic vs. slow effortful thinking); the idea of priming (a recent idea makes related ideas easier to reach); reading simple percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A single "cognitive ease" dial, run by System 1, signals whether things are going well; strain signals a problem that needs System 2 (pdf 61).
- Different causes of ease are interchangeable, so people cannot easily trace the feeling to its source (pdf 63, 66).
- Familiarity is partly an illusion: a word seen before is easier to see, and that ease feels like "pastness" (pdf 62–63).
- Judgments based on ease produce predictable illusions of truth. Repetition is a reliable way to make falsehoods believed (pdf 64).
- Persuasion advice follows from this: be legible, be simple, be rhymed, use pronounceable sources. Such devices help little if the message is obviously nonsensical (pdf 64–66).
- Strain tends to mobilize System 2: in one experiment, a barely legible font reduced the share of students making at least one mistake on the Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) from 90% to 35% (pdf 66–67).
- Ease appears to be associated with good feelings. Repeated exposure raises liking (the mere exposure effect), even for stimuli never consciously seen (pdf 67–68).
- Good mood, intuition, creativity and gullibility form one cluster; sadness, vigilance and analysis form the other. Ease-linked emotion is the basis of intuitions of coherence (pdf 70–71).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Cognitive Ease (pdf 61–62)
- Kahneman's picture: System 1 automatically keeps answers to questions such as "Is there a threat?" and "Is more effort needed?" Think of a cockpit with dials (pdf 61).
- One dial runs from "Easy" to "Strained." Easy means no threat and no need for effort; strained means a problem, requiring more System 2 effort (pdf 61).
- Figure 5: repeated experience, clear display, a primed idea and good mood all lead to ease. Ease leads to feeling familiar, true, good and effortless (pdf 61).
- Other causes: a pencil held crosswise in the mouth (forcing a "smile") induces ease. A poor font, faint colors, complicated wording, bad mood and frowning induce strain (pdf 61).
- Causes are interchangeable. In ease you are probably in a good mood, trust your intuitions and think casually and superficially. In strain you are more likely to be vigilant and suspicious and to make fewer errors, but you are also less intuitive and less creative than usual (pdf 62).

*Intuition note:* The brain has one summary feeling for "all is smooth." It has no simple way to say whether the smoothness came from the world or from print quality.

### Illusions of Remembering (pdf 62–63)
- Made-up names such as "David Stenbill" are remembered as non-famous for a short time. After a few days you may judge such a name famous in a list of celebrity names. Jacoby titled his demonstration "Becoming Famous Overnight" (pdf 62).
- For truly famous people you have a mental file. For Stenbill you have only a sense of familiarity (pdf 62).
- Jacoby: familiarity has a quality of "pastness" that seems to reflect prior experience. Kahneman calls this an illusion (pdf 62).
- Explanation: a word seen before is easier to see again. People identify it better when it is brief or masked, and read it a few hundredths of a second faster. This ease gives the impression of familiarity (pdf 63).
- Test suggested by Figure 5: a new word is more likely to feel familiar if it is unconsciously primed or shown in sharper contrast. The link also runs the other way: a recently seen word looks clearer in a blurred list (pdf 63).

*Intuition note:* "I have seen this" and "this is easy to see" feel the same. So things that make a word easier to see, such as priming or sharper contrast, can make it feel more familiar.

### Illusions of Truth (pdf 63–64)
- For statements like "New York is a large city…," related information comes quickly and mostly points one way (pdf 63). "A chicken has three legs" is more obviously false than "…four legs," because association delivers that many animals have four legs. System 2 sifts this (pdf 63).
- Kahneman's driving-test practice: when no good answer came to mind, he went by ease. Familiar meant probably true; new or improbably extreme meant rejected. System 1 produces the familiarity impression; System 2 relies on it for true/false (pdf 63–64).
- Lesson of Figure 5: predictable illusions inevitably occur if a judgment rests on ease or strain. Anything that eases the associative machine biases beliefs (pdf 64).
- Frequent repetition is a reliable way to make people believe falsehoods. Repeating a phrase alone is enough: people exposed to "the body temperature of a chicken" were more likely to accept "…is 144°" (pdf 64).
- If you cannot recall the source and have no way to relate the statement to other things you know, you have no option but to go with ease (pdf 64).

*Intuition note:* Hearing a claim again makes it smoother to process. With nothing else to check against, smoothness gets taken as evidence.

### How to Write a Persuasive Message (pdf 64–66)
Principle: reduce cognitive strain (pdf 64).
- Maximize legibility. "Born in 1892" is more likely believed than "born in 1887"; both are false (Hitler was born in 1889). Use high-contrast paper. Bright blue or red text is more believable than middling green, yellow or pale blue (pdf 64).
- Avoid complex language where simpler will do. Oppenheimer found that pretentious wording for familiar ideas is taken as a sign of poor intelligence and low credibility (pdf 65).
- Make it memorable. Rhyming aphorisms (e.g., "Woes unite foes") were judged more insightful than non-rhyming versions ("Woes unite enemies") (pdf 65).
- Quote a source with an easy name. In a Turkish-company stock experiment, reports came from an easy-named firm (Artan) and a hard-named firm (Taahhut). Observers weighted Artan much more. The best procedure would have been to average the two reports (pdf 65).
- Limit: these devices do not help much if the message is obviously nonsensical or contradicts known facts. Psychologists do not think people are stupid or infinitely gullible (pdf 65–66).
- Why ease misleads: truth feels easy when it is linked to your beliefs or comes from a trusted source. But other causes (font, rhythm) feel the same and are hard to trace. People can overcome them when strongly motivated. On most occasions the lazy System 2 follows System 1 (pdf 66).

*Intuition note:* Being true is not necessarily enough for a message to be believed, so ease can help it. The same shortcut can work on you, which is why the closing remarks advise re-thinking repeated claims.

### Strain and Effort (pdf 66–67)
- The link is reciprocal. Effortful System 2 work causes strain, and strain, whatever its source, tends to mobilize System 2 and move people from a casual intuitive mode to a more analytic one (pdf 66).
- The Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT) by Shane Frederick has three items, each chosen to evoke an immediate intuitive wrong answer. They are the bat-and-ball problem, the 5 machines/5 widgets problem (100 or 5 minutes) and the lily-pad problem (24 or 47 days) (pdf 66–67). The answers are in a footnote not included in these pages (pdf 67).
- Experiment: 40 Princeton students; half saw the CRT in small, washed-out gray print (legible but straining). 90% in the normal font made at least one mistake, versus 35% in the barely legible font (pdf 67).
- Kahneman's conclusion: strain mobilizes System 2, which is more likely to reject System 1's intuitive answer (pdf 67).

*Intuition note:* A harder-to-read page makes the mind "sit up." The extra effort catches some quick wrong answers.

### The Pleasure of Cognitive Ease (pdf 67–69)
- "Mind at Ease Puts a Smile on the Face": pictures made easier by a subliminal outline just before them produced a faint smile and relaxed brows, measured by facial-muscle electrical activity. Kahneman reads this as ease being associated with good feelings in System 1 (pdf 67).
- Easy names favor stocks: pronounceable company names do better in the first week after issue, though the effect disappears over time. Pronounceable tickers (KAR, LUNMOO) outperform tongue-twisting ones (PXG, RDO) and appear to keep a small advantage. Swiss investors expect fluent-named stocks to earn higher returns (pdf 67).
- Zajonc's mere exposure effect: repeating an arbitrary stimulus produces mild affection for it (pdf 67).
- Michigan student-newspaper experiment: five Turkish-sounding words were shown once, or 2, 5, 10 or 25 times. Frequent words were rated much more favorably as meaning "good." The finding was also confirmed with Chinese ideographs, faces and random polygons (pdf 68).
- The effect does not depend on consciousness: it occurs when stimuli are shown too fast to be noticed, and is stronger for stimuli never consciously seen (pdf 68).
- Zajonc's claim: novelty prompts caution; if repetition is followed by nothing bad, the stimulus becomes a safety signal. Chicks hatched from eggs exposed to a tone gave fewer distress calls to that tone. Zajonc said repeated exposure underlies social attachment and cohesion (pdf 68–69).

*Intuition note:* Zajonc's idea is that something seen often without harm becomes a safety signal, and safety is good. The effect itself occurs even when you do not know you saw the stimulus.

### Ease, Mood, and Intuition (pdf 69–71)
- Mednick (around 1960) thought creativity is associative memory that works exceptionally well. His Remote Association Test (RAT): find a word linked to three given words (cottage, Swiss, cake → cheese). Dive, light, rocket → sky was found by fewer than 20% of students in 15 seconds. Some triads (dream, ball, book) have no solution (pdf 69).
- German studies: mood was manipulated by recalling happy or sad episodes. Subjects judged within 2 seconds whether a triad was linked, too fast to find the answer (pdf 69–70).
- Result 1: guesses are much more accurate than chance. Kahneman infers a faint signal of coherence from the associative machine before the association is retrieved. Priming, a clear font and pre-exposure raise the tendency to call words linked (pdf 70).
- Result 2: good mood more than doubled accuracy on an "intuition index." Unhappy subjects did no better than random (pdf 70).
- Kahneman's reading: good mood, intuition, creativity, gullibility and reliance on System 1 form a cluster. Sadness, vigilance, suspicion, analysis and effort form another. A happy mood loosens System 2's control (pdf 70).
- A coherent triad (salt, deep, foam → sea) tends to produce a slight smile, even with no instructions about associates (pdf 70).
- Correlation is not causation: a cover story that music influences emotions eliminated the intuition of coherence. So the brief emotional response to a triad is the basis of coherence judgments. System 1 does not link emotion to the words when the emotion is expected (pdf 71). Kahneman calls this research robust and extremely surprising (pdf 71).

*Intuition note:* The feeling "these fit together" seems to come from a small pleasant reaction. When people were given another explanation for their good feeling, the intuition of coherence disappeared.

### Speaking of Cognitive Ease (pdf 71)
Four sample remarks: don't reject a plan for its font; repeated claims need re-thinking; familiarity breeds liking (mere exposure); good mood means weaker System 2, so be extra careful (pdf 71).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A simplified "ease-meter." Assume a reader gives each item an ease score = 2 (baseline) + 3 if seen before + 1 if shown sharply. The reader calls it "famous" if the score is at least 5.
- Name A, new, blurry: 2 → not famous.
- Name B, new, sharp: 2 + 1 = 3 → not famous.
- Name C, seen before, blurry: 2 + 3 = 5 → "famous."
- Name D, seen before, sharp: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 → "famous."

Names C and D were only seen on a made-up list, so they are not actually famous. The reader is wrong because ease from past exposure was read as fame. The numbers here are invented.

## Concepts
- [[cognitive-ease]] (new) — System 1's feeling of "easy" versus "strained," tracked as a dial; causes interchangeable (pdf 61–62)
- [[cognitive-strain]] (new) — the feeling that effort or an unmet demand is present; mobilizes System 2 (pdf 61, 66)
- [[system-1-system-2]] (new) — fast automatic process vs. lazy effortful process, assumed from earlier chapters (pdf 61; "lazy" System 2 on pdf 65–66)

- [[mere-exposure-effect]] (new) — mild liking grows with repeated exposure to an arbitrary stimulus (pdf 67–68)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] fluency as cue for truth ↔ judgment under uncertainty: availability heuristic (canon link; not stated in this unit) — both judge by how easily something comes to mind.
- [structural-analogy] mere exposure as safety signal ↔ evolutionary reasoning about adaptive caution — Zajonc's claim that novelty prompts fear and safe repetition removes it.
- [metaphor] a cockpit dial for ease ↔ bounded rationality: System 2's effort is conserved, so System 1's signals decide when to engage it.

## Questions this raises
- If ease cannot be traced to its source, what practical checks help us tell truth from fluency?
- Is the effect of strain on accuracy limited to puzzles with a tempting wrong intuition, like the CRT?
- Does Zajonc's safety-signal explanation fit the finding that the effect is stronger for stimuli never consciously seen?
- How large and lasting are the stock-name effects, given that the first-week advantage fades?
