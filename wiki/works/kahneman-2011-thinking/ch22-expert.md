# Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It?

## L0 — One line
Expert intuition is likely to be valid when the environment is regular enough to be predictable and the expert has had enough practice with good feedback; the expert's own confidence is not a reliable guide.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman, the more skeptical, and Gary Klein, the more trusting, ask: when can you trust a professional who claims an intuition? They agree that intuition is recognition, learned from stored experience. They also agree that confidence is a poor guide; System 1 (fast, automatic thinking) can answer an easier question instead and give a coherent but wrong answer with high confidence. So it usually works better to ask where the intuition came from. Trust it if the environment is regular and the person had a chance to learn it through prolonged practice with quick, clear feedback. Where cues are absent, as in long-term forecasting, hits come from luck or lies. Experts may not know where their skill ends.

**Prerequisites:** System 1 / System 2; substitution of an easier question; the illusion of validity; Meehl's result that simple algorithms often beat clinical judgment.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Adversarial collaboration (opponents co-write a paper on their dispute) with Klein: they found no real issues of disagreement, though they did not fully agree. Kahneman offers it as another way to deal with disagreements, instead of hostile exchanges. (pdf 227–228)
- Klein's recognition-primed decision model: System 1 offers a plausible option, System 2 simulates it, and the option is kept, modified or replaced. (pdf 229)
- Simon's definition: intuition is recognition and nothing else. Knowing without knowing how is normal mental life, not magic. (pdf 229–230)
- Expertise is a large collection of miniskills and takes long practice, for example at least 10,000 hours for top chess. (pdf 231)
- Confidence is not a reliable guide to validity, because cognitive ease (the story comes easily to mind), coherence (it has no contradiction) and WYSIATI (ignoring what you do not know) produce confidence too easily. (pdf 232)
- Two conditions for skill: a sufficiently regular environment, and prolonged practice to learn it. Without stable regularities, intuition cannot be trusted. (pdf 232–234)
- Whether a professional can develop intuitive skill depends essentially on the quality and speed of feedback, plus enough practice. Anesthesiologists, for example, get better feedback than radiologists. (pdf 234–235)
- Experts may not know the limits of their expertise, which helps explain why they are often overconfident. Judge an intuition mostly by its provenance (where it came from: the environment and the person's learning history), which usually works better than inspecting the intuition itself. (pdf 235)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: adversarial collaboration (pdf 227–228)
- Kahneman finds hostile journal exchanges (critique, reply, rejoinder) a waste of time. He tried "adversarial collaborations": opponents co-write a paper and sometimes do joint research, with an arbiter if tension is high. (pdf 227)
- His best one was with Gary Klein, leader of the Naturalistic Decision Making (NDM) school. NDM people study experts at work in real organizations. They reject the focus on biases and distrust replacing human judgment with rigid algorithms. (pdf 227)
- Kahneman never believed intuition is always misguided and admired Klein's firefighter studies. The question was: when can you trust an experienced professional who claims an intuition? Klein was more disposed to trust, Kahneman to doubt. (pdf 227–228)
- It took seven or eight years. The joint paper is titled "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree". They did not meet real issues on which they disagreed, but they did not really agree either. (pdf 228)

### Marvels and Flaws (pdf 228)
- Gladwell's *Blink* opens with art experts who felt a kouros statue was fake without knowing why. Readers remember it as a triumph of intuition. (pdf 228)
- Klein and Kahneman both reject the implied conclusion that a systematic search for the cue would have failed. Properly done, it would probably have succeeded. (pdf 228)
- Gladwell himself also describes a failure: voters chose Harding, who looked like a strong leader, with no other reason to believe he was one. Kahneman reads this as substitution of one question for another and says to expect such an intuition to be held with confidence. (pdf 228)

### Intuition as Recognition (pdf 228–230)
- Kahneman's views came from the illusion of validity and Meehl's work. Klein's came from studying fireground commanders. (pdf 228–229)
- **Klein's finding:** commanders usually generated a single option, not a pair. They drew it from patterns built over more than a decade of real and virtual experience and mentally simulated it. If it was fine they used it; if it had shortcomings they modified it; if it could not easily be modified they moved to the next most plausible option. (pdf 229)
- This is the recognition-primed decision (RPD) model. Phase 1: associative memory (System 1) brings a tentative plan to mind. Phase 2: deliberate mental simulation (System 2). The model also fits chess. (pdf 229)
- It develops Simon's idea: "The situation has provided a cue… Intuition is nothing more and nothing less than recognition." (pdf 229)
- Consequence: the firefighter who "knows" a house will collapse is like us recognizing a friend. Knowing without knowing how is the norm of mental life. (pdf 229–230)

> **Intuition note.** An intuition is memory answering a cue. Part of its apparent magic is that we cannot see the memory at work; but we cannot see it at work in ordinary recognition either.

### Acquiring Skill (pdf 230–231)
- Some emotional learning is fast. One experience is often enough to create a lasting fear, as with Kahneman's memory of road rage at the San Francisco airport ramp. Fear can also be learned from words, as with a young platoon commander taught to see a narrowing ravine as an ambush site. Unease without a conscious memory of its cause is labeled an intuition, in hindsight, if a bad experience follows. This emotional learning is closely related to Pavlov's conditioning, whose dogs learned a "learned hope". (pdf 230)
- Expertise in complex tasks is slow because it is a large collection of miniskills. Chess masters need at least 10,000 hours of dedicated practice (about 6 years at 5 hours a day) and learn thousands of configurations. (pdf 231)
- Analogy with reading: chess patterns are like letters, a position is a long word or sentence. A skilled reader can read "Jabberwocky" fluently, and a master can handle a position never seen before. Chess is harder because its "alphabet" is larger and its "words" are longer. (pdf 231)

### The Environment of Skill (pdf 232–234)
- Klein and Kahneman agreed on the nature and acquisition of intuitive skill. They differed on trust partly because they had different experts in mind. Klein thought of fireground commanders and clinical nurses. Kahneman thought of clinicians, stock pickers and political scientists making long-term forecasts. (pdf 232)
- **Klein's view:** true experts know the limits of their knowledge. **Kahneman's view:** there are many pseudo-experts who do not know that they do not know, and confidence is commonly too high and often uninformative. (pdf 232)
- Confidence comes from cognitive ease and coherence. The associative machine suppresses doubt, and a mind following WYSIATI reaches high confidence by ignoring what it does not know. Agreed principle: confidence in intuition is not a reliable guide to validity. (pdf 232)
- **Two conditions for acquiring skill:**
  1. an environment regular enough to be predictable;
  2. an opportunity to learn the regularities through prolonged practice.

  When both hold, intuitions are likely to be skilled. (pdf 232)
- Chess is extremely regular. Bridge and poker have robust statistical regularities. Physicians, nurses, athletes and firefighters face complex but fundamentally orderly situations. Their System 1 uses valid cues that System 2 may not be able to name. (pdf 232–233)
- Stock pickers and political scientists forecasting long term work in a *zero-validity* environment. (pdf 233)
- **Hogarth's "wicked" environments:** professionals are likely to learn the wrong lessons. Example: an early-twentieth-century physician had hunches about patients about to develop typhoid, but he spread it by palpating tongues without washing his hands. His predictions were accurate, but not because of intuition. (pdf 233)
- Meehl's clinicians worked in low-validity situations, not through lack of talent. The best algorithms beat them but were never very accurate. No "smoking gun" appeared, meaning no case where clinicians missed a highly valid cue that the algorithm caught. This is unlikely because if a strong cue exists, humans will find it given a decent opportunity. (pdf 233)
- Algorithms win in noisy environments because they detect weakly valid cues more often and use them consistently. (pdf 233)
- It is wrong to blame anyone for failing to forecast in an unpredictable world, but it seems fair to blame professionals for believing they can succeed in an impossible task. Without valid cues, intuitive hits are due to luck or lies. Rule: intuition cannot be trusted in the absence of stable regularities. (pdf 233–234)

> **Intuition note.** A mind can learn only regularities that exist. If outcomes are driven by chance, practice can produce confidence but not skill.

### Feedback and Practice (pdf 234–235)
- Braking on curves is learned under ideal conditions: immediate, unambiguous feedback and varied curves. A harbor pilot's environment is as regular, but long delays between action and outcome make skill hard to acquire. Opportunity to develop intuitive expertise depends essentially on the quality and speed of feedback, and on enough practice. (pdf 234)
- Expertise is a collection of skills. A person can be expert at some tasks and a novice at others. Surgeons are better at some operations. (pdf 234)
- Psychotherapists get quick feedback on reactions in the session, so they can learn the right words and tone. They get sparse, delayed or ambiguous feedback on which general treatment suits which patient. (pdf 234)
- Anesthesiologists get fast feedback. Radiologists get little information on accuracy or missed pathologies. So when an anesthesiologist says she has a feeling that something is wrong, everyone in the operating room should be prepared for an emergency. (pdf 234–235)
- Experts may not know their limits. A therapist skilled at short-term anticipation may wrongly think she can predict next year's outcome. The same holds for the financial expert on stock picking and the Middle East expert on the future. Unrecognized limits help explain overconfidence. (pdf 235)

### Evaluating Validity (pdf 235–236)
- **Joint answer:** for the most part one can tell likely-valid from likely-bogus intuitions. As with art authenticity, provenance usually beats inspecting the piece itself. Trust intuitions if the environment is regular and the judge had a chance to learn it. (pdf 235)
- Even in a regular environment, a learner (such as a talented young chess player) makes some mistakes with great confidence. Always ask whether there was an adequate opportunity to learn the cues. (pdf 235)
- In low-validity environments, heuristics apply. System 1 is often able to answer a hard question by substitution, for example judging a company's future by the executives' energy and competence. The answer may pass System 2's lax review and feel like expert confidence. So confidence is not a good diagnostic of accuracy. (pdf 235–236)
- Kahneman says the solution's contours were clear from the start: fireground commanders and pediatric nurses on the valid side, Meehl's specialties, stock pickers and pundits on the other. Once a project ends reasonably well, the main conclusion seems to have been obvious all along. (pdf 236)
- They disagreed less than expected, but differences of attitude and taste remained. Klein winces at "bias" and enjoys stories of absurd algorithm decisions. Kahneman tends to see algorithm failures as chances to improve them and finds more pleasure than Klein does in the comeuppance of arrogant experts in zero-validity situations. Intellectual agreement matters more than these. (pdf 236)

> **Intuition note.** Do not ask how sure the person is. Ask what world she learned in, and how often that world told her whether she was right.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Zero-validity world: a "forecaster" predicts a fair coin flip, so every prediction has probability 1/2 of being right, whatever method is used.
- Chance of 5 correct calls in a row: $(1/2)^5 = 1/32 \approx 3.1\%$.
- Suppose 32 confident forecasters each make 5 calls. The expected number with a perfect streak is $32 \times 1/32 = 1$.
- On average one forecaster will look gifted and feel confident, but the hits are luck.
- Contrast braking on curves: feedback comes after each bend, so errors are corrected and skill is real.

The streak does not show skill. By the chapter's rule, skill needs a regular environment plus prolonged practice with feedback.

## Questions this raises
- How can one measure the "validity" of an environment in practice, before relying on someone's intuition there?
- If confidence is uninformative, what track-record evidence is enough to show that an expert had a real chance to learn?
- Can feedback be added to slow or ambiguous domains (such as long-term treatment outcomes) so that skill can develop?
- When System 2 only checks a plausible answer leniently, what procedures could force a stronger check?
