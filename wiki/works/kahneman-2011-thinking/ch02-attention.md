# Attention and Effort

## L0 — One line
Effortful System 2 thinking uses a limited, measurable supply of mental effort, which pupil size tracks, and, under a "law of least effort", people tend to gravitate to the least demanding way of reaching a goal.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks what makes some mental operations effortful and how effort can be measured. In Beatty's experiments, pupil size rose and fell second by second with the demands of paced tasks such as Add-1 and Add-3. Mental effort behaves like electricity use: we choose the task, but the task draws what it needs, up to a limit. Under overload, System 2 protects the priority task and other things may go unseen. Effort is needed to hold several ideas in memory, to switch tasks and to work under time pressure. Because effort is a cost, people drift to the least demanding way of reaching a goal. This matters because System 1 often guides what System 2 believes it chose.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction from chapter 1 (fast automatic vs. slow effortful thinking); short-term memory; reading a simple time curve.

## L2 — Key ideas
- System 2's operations are effortful, and it is lazy, so System 1 often guides what System 2 believes it chose. Some vital tasks can be done only by System 2, because they need effort and self-control (pdf 33).
- Pupil size is a second-by-second index of mental effort. In paced tasks it follows an inverted V and peaks at maximum effort (pdf 34–35).
- System 2's life normally runs at the pace of a comfortable walk; Add-1 and Add-3 are "sprints" and casual chatting is a "stroll". In one casual observation, ordinary conversation apparently demanded little or no effort (pdf 35).
- Effort is limited and allocated selectively. Under overload System 2 protects the priority task, so a letter K shown at peak load was missed almost half the time (pdf 36).
- Skill and talent lower the effort a task needs. Under the "law of least effort", people drift toward the least demanding route to a goal (pdf 37).
- Effort is needed for holding several ideas that need separate actions or a rule, for task switching, and for time pressure (pdf 37–39).
- People avoid overload by splitting work into easy steps and storing intermediate results in long-term memory or on paper (pdf 39).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pdf 33)
- Kahneman casts System 2 as a supporting character who thinks she is the hero. Its defining feature is that its operations are effortful. One main trait is laziness, a reluctance to invest more effort than necessary.
- So System 2's thoughts and actions are often guided by System 1. Yet some vital tasks only System 2 can do, because they need effort and self-control that overcome System 1's impulses (pdf 33).

### Mental Effort: the Add-1 task (pdf 33)
- Method: write 4-digit strings on index cards and keep a steady beat (e.g. 1 per second). Read the digits aloud, wait two beats, then report the string with every digit incremented by 1. Example: 5294 → 6305. (Our added step: 9 becomes 0, as the example shows.)
- Few people can cope with more than four digits. Add-3 is harder (pdf 33).
- Filming your pupils while you work gives "a faithful record" of how hard you worked (pdf 33).

### Hess, Beatty and the pupil (pdf 34–35)
- Hess reported that pupils dilate with interest, and that they dilate substantially during two-digit multiplication, more for hard problems than easy ones. His observations indicated that the response to effort is distinct from emotional arousal (pdf 34).
- Kahneman and Beatty used a chin rest and camera, with an infrared flash on each metronome beat (one picture per second). They studied paced tasks, where they knew exactly what was in the subject's mind (pdf 34).
- Result: pupil size varied second by second as an inverted V. Effort builds with each digit heard, peaks during and just after the pause, and relaxes as short-term memory is unloaded. Longer strings gave larger dilations (pdf 34).
- Add-1 with four digits caused a larger dilation than holding seven digits for recall (pdf 35).
- In the first 5 seconds of Add-3 the pupil dilates by about 50% of its original area and heart rate rises about 7 beats per minute. This is "as hard as people can work"; they give up if more is asked (pdf 35).
- With more digits than could be remembered, pupils stopped dilating or shrank (pdf 35).
- In mental multiplication the pupil stayed large while the person worked and contracted at once on solving or giving up. This let the team tell when someone quit (pdf 35).
- Casual observation, of one woman: during ordinary conversation the pupil stayed small, and the talk apparently demanded little or no effort, no more than retaining two or three digits. Kahneman concluded that the study tasks were exceptionally effortful (pdf 35).
- Image: System 2 normally works at a comfortable walk, sometimes jogs, rarely sprints. Add-1 and Add-3 are sprints (pdf 35).

**Intuition note:** The pupil works like a live gauge of how hard the mind is working. It reads high while you struggle and drops the moment you stop.

### Effort and blindness (pdf 35–36)
- Subjects did Add-1 while letters flashed, and had to say afterwards whether a K had appeared. They gave the digit task complete priority (pdf 36).
- A K near the start or end was almost never missed. At peak effort it was missed almost half the time, even though the eye was wide open and staring at it. Detection failures followed the same inverted V as the pupil (pdf 36).

### The electricity-meter analogy (pdf 36)
- Pupils indicate the current rate of mental energy use, like an electricity meter. We decide what to do, but have limited control over the effort it takes. Even a life-or-death motive cannot make 4-digit memory as effortful as Add-3 on the same digits (pdf 36).
- Unlike a tripping breaker, which cuts all devices at once, System 2's response to overload is selective. It protects the most important activity and allocates "spare capacity" second by second to others (pdf 36).
- The timing of the target letter did not affect the main task, which shows subjects followed the priority instruction (pdf 36).
- Attention allocation was shaped by a long evolutionary history, and responding quickly to threats aided survival. In emergencies System 1 takes over and gives total priority to self-protection, as in a skid on an oil slick (pdf 36–37).

**Intuition note:** The mind does not shut down when overloaded. It protects the main task and gives only spare capacity to the rest, so something right in front of you can go unseen.

### Skill and the law of least effort (pdf 37)
- Beatty went on to become the leading authority on "cognitive pupillometry"; Kahneman wrote *Attention and Effort* (pdf 37).
- As skill grows, a task's energy demand falls and fewer brain regions are involved. Highly intelligent people need less effort for the same problems, as shown by pupil size and brain activity (pdf 37).
- The law of least effort: if several ways reach the same goal, people will eventually gravitate to the least demanding one. Effort is a cost, and skill acquisition is driven by the balance of benefits and costs (pdf 37).
- Ranking of tasks, from the pupil:
  - Holding one or two digits, or learning to link a word to a digit (3 = door): effects only 5% of the increase in pupil diameter seen with Add-3.
  - Telling apart the pitch of two tones: larger dilations.
  - Inhibiting the tendency to read distracting words: moderate effort.
  - Short-term memory for six or seven digits: more effortful.
  - Saying aloud your phone number or a birthday: brief but significant effort.
  - Two-digit multiplication and Add-3: near most people's limit (pdf 37).

### What makes operations demanding (pdf 37–39)
- Kahneman gives "tentative answers" (pdf 37).
- Effort is needed to hold several ideas in memory that need separate actions or must be combined by a rule. Examples are a shopping list, choosing fish or veal, or combining a survey result with a small sample size (pdf 37–38).
- Only System 2 can follow rules, compare objects on several attributes and make deliberate choices. System 1 detects simple relations and integrates information about one thing. It does not handle several distinct topics at once and is not adept with purely statistical information (pdf 38).
- Example: System 1 sees that a "meek and tidy soul" resembles a librarian. Combining that with the small number of librarians takes System 2, and only if it knows how, "which is true of few people" (pdf 38).
- Task sets: System 2 can program memory to obey an instruction that overrides habit (e.g. count the letter f on a page). Setting up and carrying out are both effortful. "Executive control" is the adoption and ending of task sets. The prefrontal area, more developed in humans than other primates, is involved (pdf 38).
- Switching tasks is effortful, especially under time pressure. Example: counting commas right after counting f's is harder because you must overcome the newly acquired tendency to focus on f. In Add-3 you must hold digits in three roles at once: queued, being transformed, and already transformed (pdf 38).
- Working-memory tests require switching between two demanding tasks. Good performers tend to do well on intelligence tests, but attention control also predicts air traffic controller and Israeli Air Force pilot performance beyond intelligence (pdf 38–39).
- Time pressure drives effort: memory decays, so you must rehearse before information is lost, like a juggler. "The most effortful forms of slow thinking are those that require you to think fast" (pdf 39).
- Few daily tasks are as demanding as Add-3 or storing six digits. We avoid overload by splitting tasks into easy steps and putting intermediate results into long-term memory or on paper (pdf 39).
- "Speaking of Attention and Effort": four sample remarks. They cover not solving a pupil-dilating task while driving, the law of least effort making someone think as little as possible, a person who was focused elsewhere and did not hear a message, and a System 1 intuition that means starting over with deliberate memory search (pdf 39–40).

**Intuition note:** A lot of hard thinking is about keeping several things in mind before they fade. We usually avoid overload by splitting a task into easy steps and storing results in long-term memory or on paper.

## Toy example (ours)
Add-3 on 5294 (each digit +3, 9+3 = 12 → 2): 5+3 = 8, 2+3 = 5, 9+3 = 2, 4+3 = 7 → **8527**.

Load count while transforming the 3rd digit: queue = the 4th digit (1 item); in process = the 3rd (1); already done = 8 and 5 (2). That is 4 items, all held at once, while the beat keeps going.

Least-effort version: 47 × 6 as one step is hard to hold in mind. On paper, 40×6 = 240 and 7×6 = 42, so 240 + 42 = 282. At each moment you hold only one or two numbers.

## Questions this raises
- If System 2 is lazy, in which everyday judgements do we accept System 1's answer without checking?
- How far does pupil dilation measure effort for tasks unlike paced digit tasks, such as self-paced reasoning?
- Does splitting a problem into steps always help, or can it add its own switching costs?
- How can a decision-maker be helped to bring in statistical information such as small sample size, which System 1 handles poorly?
