# The Outside View

## L0 — One line
Forecasts built from the details of one's own project (the inside view) tend to be too optimistic. Starting from the statistics of similar past projects (the outside view) gives a better indication of the realistic range when the reference class is well chosen, but people tend to ignore those statistics.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman describes a team whose curriculum expert knew the history of similar projects, yet the team forecast its own project far too optimistically. In his curriculum team, the inside view gave about two years. The expert's informal survey of comparable teams suggested 7–10 years for those that finished, and about a 40% chance of failure. The team ignored this and carried on; the book took eight years and was never used. Kahneman names the planning fallacy and irrational perseverance. His remedy is reference class forecasting: pick a class of similar cases, take its statistics as the baseline, then adjust for case-specific facts. The chapter matters because the same optimism affects budgets, plans and risk taking.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a base rate (how common something is in a category); averages and percentages; anchoring on a starting number.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Two ways to forecast: the inside view (own circumstances) and the outside view (statistics of similar cases). The team spontaneously used the inside view (pdf 240).
- The planning fallacy: forecasts close to best-case scenarios that could be improved by consulting similar cases. It appears in buildings, rail projects and kitchen remodels (pdf 242–243).
- The inside view fails partly because of "unknown unknowns". Many unforeseeable events can derail a big project, so something going wrong is likely overall (pdf 240).
- People who hold case information rarely feel they need class statistics, and "pallid" statistics are routinely discarded when they clash with personal impressions (pdf 241–242).
- Remedy, reference class forecasting: identify the class, take its statistics as the baseline, adjust if there are particular reasons to expect more or less optimism in this case (pdf 244).
- Optimism, not only favourable odds, may drive risk taking. Executives may pursue projects because they overrate their chances (pdf 245).
- Irrational perseverance (failing to abandon a project) and the sunk-cost fallacy (carrying on because effort is already invested) are named as reasons the team kept going. Kahneman calls this his own failure as leader (pdf 245–246).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening episode (pdf 238–239).** Kahneman led a team writing a high-school textbook on judgment and decision making. It included Seymour Fox, a curriculum expert. After about a year, Kahneman collected private estimates of the time to a finished draft. They were "narrowly centered around two years", from 1.5 to 2.5 years (pdf 238). Private collection, not open discussion, uses the group's knowledge better (pdf 238).
- He asked Seymour about similar teams that had built a curriculum from scratch, at the same stage of progress.
- Seymour: a substantial fraction (about 40%) never finished. Asked about those that finished, he could think of none that took less than seven years or more than ten (pdf 238–239).
- He ranked the team "below average, but not by much" (pdf 239).
- Seymour's own earlier estimate had matched the optimistic group. His knowledge of other teams was not connected to his forecast (pdf 239).
- The team treated the statistics as base rates usually are: "noted and promptly set aside". A causal inference was available: if so many fail, writing a curriculum is harder than thought. It clashed with their experience of good progress (pdf 239).
- They should have quit, but carried on. The book was finished eight years later and never used (pdf 239).
- Three lessons: the inside/outside distinction, the planning fallacy, and irrational perseverance (pdf 240).

*Intuition note:* Once Seymour gave the facts, the team noted them but did not act on them. A smooth plan felt more real than a statistic about strangers.

**Drawn to the Inside View (pdf 240–242).**
- The inside view uses the information in front of you (Kahneman's abbreviation WYSIATI). The first chapters were probably easier, and commitment was probably at its peak (pdf 240).
- Unknown unknowns (divorces, illnesses, bureaucratic crises) could not be anticipated by the team that day. They cause delays and long stalls. Other teams presumably could not foresee theirs either (pdf 240).
- The baseline prediction is what you would predict knowing only the category. It is the anchor for further adjustment (pdf 241).
  - Example: for a woman known only to live in New York City, guess the city's average female height. If her son is the starting center of his high-school basketball team, the estimate moves away from the mean in the appropriate direction (pdf 241).
- Seymour's comparison suggested the team's outlook was slightly worse than the baseline prediction, which was already grim (pdf 241).
- The accuracy of the outside view here was "surely a fluke". The argument for it is general: with a properly chosen reference class it indicates the ballpark, and may show that inside-view forecasts are not close (pdf 241).
- Like the participants in the Tom W experiment, Seymour knew the base rate but did not think of applying it (pdf 241).
- Nisbett and Borgida's students ignored global results when they had a little case information. The outside view "doesn't stand a chance" against the inside view (pdf 241–242).
- Kahneman's lawyer cousin answered that "every case is unique". Medicine, too, shows some remaining ambivalence about statistics and checklists (pdf 242).

*Intuition note:* Having details about one case makes you feel statistics about the class are unnecessary. That feeling is the problem.

**The Planning Fallacy (pdf 242–243).** Kahneman and Amos coined the term for plans and forecasts that are unrealistically close to best-case scenarios and could be improved by consulting statistics of similar cases (pdf 242).
- Scottish Parliament building: estimate of up to £40m (July 1997); £109m (June 1999); £195m cap (April 2000); £241m (November 2001); £294.6m (end 2002); £375.8m (June 2003); about £431m final cost, completed 2004 (pdf 242–243).
- A 2005 study of rail projects from 1969 to 1998 found passenger forecasts too high in more than 90% of cases. On average, ridership was overestimated by 106% and cost overrun was 45%. Forecasts did not improve over thirty years (pdf 243).
- A 2002 survey of kitchen remodelers: expected $18,658, paid $38,769 on average (pdf 243).
- Optimism is not the only cause. Contractors of kitchen renovations and weapon systems readily admit they routinely make most of their profit on additions to the plan; in these cases the forecasting failure reflects customers' inability to imagine how their wishes will escalate (pdf 243).
- Some initial budgets are not innocent. Authors want approval and know that projects are rarely abandoned over overruns. Decision makers who do not recognize the need for an outside view commit a planning fallacy (pdf 243).

**Mitigating the Planning Fallacy (pdf 243–245).** Flyvbjerg (quoted): the tendency to underweight or ignore distributional information is "perhaps the major source of error in forecasting". Planners should make every effort to frame the problem so as to facilitate using all available distributional information (pdf 243–244); Kahneman says this may be considered the single most important piece of advice for improving forecasting methods (pdf 244). The cure is the outside view, now called reference class forecasting. It uses a large database of plans and outcomes for hundreds of projects (pdf 244). Steps (pdf 244):
1. Identify an appropriate reference class.
2. Obtain the class statistics (e.g. percentage of budget overrun) and form a baseline prediction.
3. Adjust the baseline using case-specific information, if there are particular reasons to expect the optimistic bias to be more or less pronounced here.

- Decision makers may add a budget reserve, but such reserves often become self-fulfilling prophecies. An official told Flyvbjerg a reserve is to contractors "as red meat is to lions" (pdf 244).
- A well-run organization rewards precise execution and penalizes planners for failing to anticipate difficulties and for failing to allow for difficulties they could not have anticipated, the unknown unknowns (pdf 244–245).

**Decisions and Errors (pdf 245).** With Dan Lovallo, Kahneman sketched a theory in which optimistic bias is a significant source of risk taking.
- **Standard rational model:** people take risks because the odds are favourable.
- **Kahneman–Lovallo alternative:** executives under the planning fallacy overestimate benefits, underestimate costs, and tell success stories. They then pursue projects unlikely to meet budget, time or returns, or even to be completed.
- People "often (but not always)" take risks from over-optimism. This "probably contributes" to why people litigate, start wars and open small businesses (pdf 245).

**Failing a Test (pdf 245–246).** Kahneman now sees himself as the "chief dunce and inept leader". The project was his initiative, so he was responsible for ensuring major problems were discussed (pdf 245).
- He was cured of the planning fallacy on hearing Seymour. His failure was different: nobody pressed the question, and they went on without any explicit forecast (pdf 245–246).
- Sunk costs (effort already invested) and embarrassment about quitting added to this. It was no crisis, only new facts about people they did not know; the outside view was much easier to ignore than bad news about their own effort. He calls the state "lethargy" (pdf 246).
- He now looks for the outside view, but "it will never be the natural thing to do" (pdf 246).

**Speaking of the Outside View (pdf 246–247).** Sample sentences:
- Someone who looks only at his own case and not at other cases is taking an inside view.
- Someone who assumes a best case, when there are too many ways for a plan to fail to foresee them all, is a victim of the planning fallacy.
- A malpractice-case baseline prediction: how many such cases succeed or settle, and for what amounts? Is this case stronger or weaker than similar ones?
- Added investment out of unwillingness to admit failure is the sunk-cost fallacy.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A team forecasts that its software project will cost 200 (thousand). That is the inside view.
- Reference class: five similar past projects, each budgeted at 100. Actual costs were 130, 150, 120, 160, 140.
- Overruns: 30%, 50%, 20%, 60%, 40%.
- Mean overrun = (30 + 50 + 20 + 60 + 40) / 5 = 200 / 5 = 40%.
- Baseline prediction = 200 × 1.40 = 280.
- Adjustment: if the team has a specific reason to be somewhat worse than the class, e.g. less experience, it moves the forecast above 280, say to 300. It does not move below 280 without a specific reason.
- The inside view (200) is far from even the baseline (280). That gap is what the outside view reveals.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Baseline prediction plus adjustment ↔ Bayesian updating: the class base rate is the prior and case information updates it.
- [structural-analogy] Outside view ↔ statistics: using the reference-class mean as the best guess when case features are weakly informative.
- [metaphor] Optimism-driven risk taking ↔ expected utility: the chapter contrasts it with "favourable odds" as the cause of risk taking.

## Questions this raises
- How should one choose the reference class when several are plausible?
- How much should case-specific information move the baseline, and in which direction?
- How can organizations make decision makers use the outside view when it is easy to ignore?
- How would one test the claim that optimism helps explain litigation, wars and small-business founding?
