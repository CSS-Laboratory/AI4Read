# The Science of Availability

## L0 — One line
When people judge how common something is, they often report how easily examples come to mind, and this fluency can outweigh the number or content of the examples; the effect is weaker when System 2 is more engaged and is no longer found when the fluency is given a spurious explanation.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do people estimate the frequency of a category? Kahneman describes the availability heuristic: they judge by the ease with which instances come to mind. This substitutes one question for another, which Kahneman says inevitably produces systematic errors. Salient, dramatic or personal events are easy to recall. Schwarz's experiments show that the felt ease of retrieval can outweigh the amount retrieved. The effect disappears when the difficulty is given a spurious explanation, and it weakens when people are personally involved or more vigilant. Knowing the bias can help in some cases, such as credit allocation in marriages and teams.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction (fast automatic vs. slow effortful thinking); the idea of a heuristic as a mental shortcut.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Availability heuristic: frequency is judged by "the ease with which instances come to mind". It works by substituting one question for another, which produces systematic errors (pdf 128–129).
- No instances need to be retrieved to sense ease. Judging two letter sets by word-making possibilities is one example (pdf 128).
- To find biases, list factors other than frequency that make instances easy to retrieve: salience, drama, personal experience (pdf 129).
- Spouses' self-estimated shares of housework and other joint tasks add up to more than 100%, because one's own efforts are more available. Awareness of this can sometimes defuse credit conflicts (pdf 129–130).
- Schwarz's studies: listing twelve instances makes people rate themselves lower than listing six. Fluency trumped number retrieved (pdf 131).
- The fluency effect is better called an "unexplained unavailability" heuristic: it rests on surprise, and a spurious explanation removes it (pdf 132).
- No complex reasoning is needed: System 1 sets expectations, is surprised when they are violated, and looks for possible causes. System 2 can reset those expectations (pdf 133).
- People who are more engaged, involved or vigilant use content more. Others "go with the flow" and follow fluency (pdf 133–134).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Origins (pdf 128).** Kahneman and Tversky worked on judgment heuristics in Eugene, Oregon, in 1971–72, hosted by Paul Slovic. They asked what people do when estimating the frequency of a category. Answer: instances are retrieved from memory, and if retrieval is easy and fluent, the category is judged large. The definition is judging frequency by "the ease with which instances come to mind". They did not then ask whether this is deliberate or automatic. Kahneman says both systems are involved. Question: how many instances must be retrieved to sense ease? Answer: none. In the example, one letter set (XUZONLCJM versus TAPCERHOB) obviously yields many more words than the other, probably by a factor of 10 or more. News-frequency of countries is another case (pdf 128).

**Substitution and sources of bias (pdf 129).** The heuristic substitutes the ease of retrieval for the size of the category. Kahneman says such substitution "inevitably" produces systematic errors. His method for finding biases: list factors other than frequency that make instances easy to retrieve. Each is a potential bias.
- Salient events (Hollywood divorces, politicians' sex scandals) make you likely to exaggerate their frequency.
- A dramatic event temporarily raises its category's availability (a publicized plane crash, a burning car).
- Personal experiences, pictures and vivid examples are more available than others' incidents, words or statistics.

Resisting these is possible but tiresome. It requires asking, for instance, whether a belief about teenage thefts rests on a few recent local cases (pdf 129). He says the effort is sometimes worth it.

**Spouses' contributions (pdf 129–130).** Spouses estimated their personal percentage contribution to tidying, garbage and similar tasks. As expected, the self-assessments summed to more than 100%. Explanation: each spouse remembers their own efforts more clearly. The bias is not necessarily self-serving, since spouses also overestimated their contribution to causing quarrels, though less than their contribution to desirable outcomes. The same bias contributes to team members feeling they did more than their share. Kahneman is generally not optimistic about personal control of biases, but calls this "an exception". Credit-allocation situations are easy to identify, and noting that there is usually more than 100% credit to go around is sometimes enough to defuse tension (pdf 130).

*Intuition note:* Each person remembers their own efforts more clearly than the other person's. So each one's own share feels bigger, and the estimates add up to more than 100%.

**The Psychology of Availability: Schwarz's experiments (pdf 130–132).**
- Task: list six (or twelve) instances of assertive behavior, then rate your own assertiveness. Two determinants compete: the number of instances retrieved and the ease with which they come (pdf 130–131).
- Kahneman asks "Which will count more?" The text answers: ease. People who listed twelve rated themselves as less assertive than those who listed six. People asked for twelve non-assertive instances ended up thinking themselves quite assertive (pdf 131).
- Smilers and frowners: all listed six instances. Smilers contracted the zygomaticus muscle (a light smile); frowners furrowed the brow, which Kahneman says normally accompanies cognitive strain (instructed frowning increases it). Frowners had more difficulty and rated themselves relatively lacking in assertiveness (pdf 131).
- Other paradoxical findings (pdf 131–132): people believe they use their bicycles less after recalling many instances of use; they are less confident in a choice after producing more supporting arguments; they are less confident an event was avoidable after listing more ways to avoid it; and they are less impressed by a car after listing many advantages.
- A UCLA professor asked students to list ways to improve a course. Those asked for more improvements rated the course higher (pdf 132).

*Intuition note:* Struggling to finish a long list feels like evidence against the claim, even though the list itself supports the claim.

**When the paradox reverses (pdf 132–133).** People sometimes go by content instead of ease. Kahneman states that you truly understand a pattern when you know how to reverse it. Retrieval gets harder as the task goes on, and the drop between six and twelve appears to be steeper than participants expected. They seem to infer that if retrieval is harder than expected, they cannot be very assertive. The inference rests on surprise, so the heuristic is better described as an "unexplained unavailability" heuristic (pdf 132).
- Reversal by explanation: participants were told background music would affect memory performance. Those told it would make retrieval harder rated themselves equally assertive after twelve as after six instances. Spurious explanations such as text-box shapes or screen color worked as well (pdf 132).
- Is this chain of reasoning too complex for System 1? Kahneman says no complex reasoning is needed. System 1 sets expectations, is surprised when they are violated, and usually looks for a cause among recent surprises. System 2 can reset System 1's expectations on the fly. Example: if told the boy next door often wears a top hat, you are less surprised to see it. In the experiment the music is a possible cause, so the difficulty is no longer a surprise (pdf 133).

*Intuition note:* In these experiments the effect of low fluency rests on surprise: retrieval was harder than expected. A ready explanation for the difficulty removes the surprise, and the effect disappears.

**Involvement and vigilance (pdf 133–134).** In a cardiac-risk study, students recalled three or eight risky (or protective) behaviors. Students with no family history of heart disease were casual and followed fluency. Those who struggled to find eight risky behaviors felt relatively safe, and those who struggled to find protective ones felt at risk. Students with a family history showed the opposite pattern, following content. They were also more likely to feel their future behavior would be affected by the experience of evaluating their risk (pdf 133–134).

Kahneman concludes that ease of retrieval is a System 1 heuristic, replaced by a focus on content when System 2 is more engaged. Multiple lines of evidence suggest System 1 users are more susceptible. People "go with the flow" (follow ease more than content) when they are (pdf 134):
- doing another effortful task at the same time;
- in a good mood after recalling a happy episode;
- low on a depression scale;
- knowledgeable novices rather than true experts;
- high on a scale of faith in intuition;
- powerful, or made to feel powerful.

Kahneman finds the last finding intriguing. The authors open their article with a quote from George W. Bush, "I've just got to know how I feel" (pdf 134). The authors go on to show that reliance on intuition is only in part a personality trait: merely reminding people of a time when they had power raises their apparent trust in their own intuition (pdf 134).

*Intuition note:* Ease of recall is a quick System 1 signal that people follow more when vigilance is low. When they are more engaged, or personally involved, they are more likely to look at what they actually recalled.

**Speaking of Availability (pdf 134–135).** Sample remarks in which the bias is named: preferring the train after two plane crashes; underestimating indoor pollution because of few media stories; spy movies producing a sense of conspiracies; a CEO with several successes finding failure hard to imagine, hence overconfident.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ana lists instances of her assertiveness. Assume she expects the k-th instance to take about k seconds.
- Six-instance task: actual times 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3 s. The 6th takes 3 s against an expected 6 s: 3 − 6 = −3, so it was easier than expected. Rule: no surprise, so she feels assertive.
- Twelve-instance task: actual times 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15 s. The 12th takes 15 s against an expected 12 s: 15 − 12 = +3, so it was harder than expected. She is surprised and concludes "I'm not very assertive", though she has retrieved 12 > 6 instances.
- Music condition: she was told music would slow her down. The +3 s is attributed to the music and ignored, so she rates herself as she did after six.

The numbers and the surprise rule are invented only to show the pattern. The source reports the outcomes, not these quantities.

## Concepts
- [[representativeness / availability / anchoring]] — here only availability: judging frequency by ease of retrieval (pdf 128)
- [[heuristic]] — a judgment shortcut that substitutes one question for another (pdf 129)
- [[availability-heuristic]] (new) — the specific heuristic; Kahneman says it involves both systems (pdf 128)
- [[fluency-of-retrieval]] (new) — felt ease of recalling instances; can outweigh how many were recalled (pdf 131)
- [[unexplained-unavailability]] (new) — Kahneman's better description: lower-than-expected fluency, unexplained, drives the judgment (pdf 132)
- [[substitution]] (new) — answering a different question (e.g. ease of recall) in place of the one asked (pdf 129)
- [[debiasing]] (new) — reducing a bias, possible for credit allocation (pdf 130)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Availability from fluency ↔ decision theory: judging probability from a feature unrelated to true frequency — salience acts as a biased estimator.
- [metaphor] Attribution of low fluency to music ↔ causal reasoning: a "discounting" explanation removes the evidential value of a signal.

## Questions this raises
- How large is the availability effect compared with the true-frequency signal in real life (for example, media coverage and risk judgments)?
- Which conditions make people switch from fluency to content? Is it System 2 engagement, involvement, or something else?
- Can team or marriage "more than 100%" debiasing be extended to other settings where credit or blame is assigned?
- How reliable is the experimenters' reading of "surprise" as the mechanism?
