# Availability, Emotion, and Risk

## L0 — One line
Judgments of risk are shaped by what comes easily and vividly to mind, so media-driven "availability cascades" can distort public policy, and Kahneman argues that policy should still respect public fear.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How do ease of recall and emotion shape our views of risk? Kahneman reports that people misjudge causes of death because media coverage makes some easy to recall. Slovic's affect heuristic lets feelings answer hard questions about risk and benefit. Kuran and Sunstein's availability cascade shows how media and public worry can feed each other until policy shifts (Love Canal, Alar). Slovic and Sunstein disagree about how far experts, rather than the public, should be trusted on risk. Kahneman agrees with both: cascades distort priorities, but fear is itself painful and debilitating, and policies the public rejects are unworkable in a democracy. Kahneman says psychology should inform the design of risk policy.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of heuristics (mental shortcuts) and of System 1 (fast, automatic thinking) vs System 2 (slow, effortful thinking); basic ratios and frequencies.

## L2 — Key ideas
- After disasters, insurance and protective action rise, then fade as memory fades; protection is usually set to the worst disaster already experienced (pdf 136).
- Estimates of causes of death are warped by media coverage, which is itself biased toward novelty and poignancy (pdf 136–137).
- Easy-to-recall risks and fear reinforce each other. The affect heuristic lets the answer to an easy question about feelings stand in for a much harder question about beliefs (pdf 137).
- In Slovic's study, a message praising a technology's benefits also lowered its perceived risk, with no new evidence about risk (pdf 138).
- Slovic: the public's richer view of risk can reflect a genuine conflict of values, and "objective risk" does not exist. Sunstein: experts, using lives and dollars, should guard against populist excess (pdf 138–140).
- An availability cascade is a self-sustaining media–public loop that can end in panic and policy change. Love Canal and Alar are the examples (pdf 140–142).
- Probability neglect: we imagine the harm (the numerator) and give too little thought to its probability (the denominator). Terrorists are the most significant practitioners of inducing availability cascades (pdf 142).
- Kahneman's verdict: cascades distort priorities, yet fear should not be ignored, and policy should combine expert knowledge with public intuition (pdf 142–143).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: availability and disasters
- Kunreuther noticed that availability helps explain insurance purchase and protection after disasters. After each significant earthquake, Californians are for a while diligent about insurance and protection (pdf 136).
- As memories dim, worry and diligence dim too. This helps explain recurring cycles of disaster, concern and complacency (pdf 136).
- Protection is usually designed to be adequate for the worst disaster actually experienced. Since pharaonic Egypt, societies have tracked river high-water marks and prepared accordingly, *apparently* assuming floods will not rise higher. Images of worse disasters do not come easily to mind (pdf 136).

*Intuition note:* We prepare for what we can picture. A vivid recent disaster raises worry; fading memory lowers it.

### Availability and Affect
- Slovic, Lichtenstein and Fischhoff asked people which of two causes of death is more frequent, and by what ratio. Answers were compared with health statistics (pdf 136).
- Findings (pdf 136–137):
  - Strokes cause almost twice as many deaths as all accidents combined, but 80% judged accidental death more likely.
  - Tornadoes were seen as deadlier than asthma, which causes 20 times more deaths.
  - Lightning was judged less likely than botulism to kill, though it is 52 times more frequent.
  - Disease deaths are 18 times accidental deaths, but the two were judged about equally likely.
  - Accidents were judged over 300 times more likely than diabetes; the true ratio is given as 1:4.
- Kahneman's reading: estimates are warped by media coverage, which is biased toward novelty and poignancy. Media both shape and are shaped by public interest. Unusual events get disproportionate attention and so seem less unusual (pdf 137).
- The estimates are an almost direct representation of activation in associative memory, and an example of substitution. Slovic's deeper insight: ease of recall and emotional reaction are inextricably linked, and vivid, fluent danger thoughts exacerbate fear (pdf 137).
- **Affect heuristic** (Slovic): people judge by consulting emotions (Do I like it? Hate it? How strongly?), often unknowingly. It substitutes an easy question (How do I feel?) for a hard one (What do I think?) (pdf 137).
- Damasio proposed that emotional evaluations and bodily approach/avoidance states play a central role in guiding decisions. He and colleagues observed that people lacking appropriate emotions (sometimes from brain damage) decide worse (pdf 137–138).
- Technology survey: Slovic's team surveyed opinions about technologies (fluoridation, chemical plants, preservatives, cars). The text layer is truncated here, so the initial benefit/risk listing is not fully visible (pdf 138). Respondents then read arguments stressing either benefits or low risks (pdf 138).
  - Benefit messages changed beliefs about *risk*, and mild-risk messages raised the view of *benefits*, though no relevant evidence was given (pdf 138).
  - Kahneman, citing Haidt: "The emotional tail wags the rational dog." The affect heuristic builds a tidier world with no real trade-offs (pdf 138).

*Intuition note:* In this study, a technology that people came to like more was also seen as less risky, without new evidence about risk. Feeling seems to shape belief here.

### The Public and the Experts
- Slovic's picture of the citizen is unflattering: guided by emotion, swayed by trivia, inadequately sensitive to differences between low and negligibly low probabilities. Experts show similar biases in weaker form, but their risk preferences often differ from the public's (pdf 138).
- Part of the gap is lay bias. Part is a genuine conflict of values: experts count lives or life-years lost; the public distinguishes "good" from "bad" deaths, or accidents from voluntary activities such as skiing. Slovic says the public's concept of risk is richer and resists rule by experts (pdf 138–139).
- **Slovic's view:** "real risk" or "objective risk" does not exist. Risk is a human invention. He lists nine ways to define the mortality risk of a toxic release, from death per million people to death per million dollars of product produced. The choice of measure may reflect a preferred outcome, so "defining risk is thus an exercise in power" (pdf 139).
- **Sunstein's view:** regulation should rest on rational cost–benefit weighing, in lives (or life-years) saved and dollar cost, both objectively measurable. He thinks US priorities reflect public pressure more than analysis. He is not persuaded that risk is subjective, though many aspects are debatable (pdf 139–140).
- Sunstein: lawmakers and regulators may be overly responsive to irrational public concern, and they share citizens' biases (pdf 140).
- **Availability cascade** (Sunstein and Kuran): a self-sustaining chain of events that may start from media reports of a relatively minor event and lead to public panic and large-scale government action. The loop (pdf 140):
  1. A media story may catch the attention of part of the public, who become worried.
  2. The reaction becomes news, which adds coverage and concern.
  3. "Availability entrepreneurs" may speed it up.
  4. Media compete for headlines, and doubters attract mostly hostile attention.
  5. The issue becomes politically important and other risks fade.
- Kuran and Sunstein's comment, about the social context: "all heuristics are equal, but availability is more equal than the others". The importance of an idea is often judged by the fluency and emotional charge with which it comes to mind (pdf 140).
- **Love Canal** (1979): buried toxic waste surfaced, contaminating water above standard limits. Lois Gibbs tried to sustain interest in the problem. At the peak there were daily stories, scientists who said the danger was overstated were ignored or shouted down, and baby-size coffins were paraded. Many residents were relocated and the Superfund (CERCLA) followed. It was costly, and some claim the money could have saved more lives elsewhere. Opinion is still divided, and claims of health damage appear not to have been substantiated. CERCLA is considered a significant achievement of environmental legislation. Kuran and Sunstein wrote the story up almost as a pseudo-event, while environmentalists still speak of the "Love Canal disaster" (pdf 141).
- **Alar** (1989): a growth-regulating spray for apples. Press stories said huge doses caused tumors in rats and mice. Coverage fed fear, which fed coverage. Meryl Streep testified, the apple industry lost heavily, the product was withdrawn and banned. Later research confirmed that the substance might pose a very small risk as a possible carcinogen. Kahneman calls it an enormous overreaction, probably harmful on net because fewer apples were eaten (pdf 141–142).
- **Probability neglect** (Sunstein's term): we ignore small risks or weight them far too much, nothing in between. As Slovic argued, concern is not adequately sensitive to the probability of harm. We imagine the numerator, not the denominator. Combined with cascades, this "inevitably" exaggerates minor threats (pdf 142).
- Terrorists are, in today's world, the most significant practitioners of inducing availability cascades. Casualties from terror attacks are small relative to other causes (with exceptions such as 9/11), and in Israel weekly casualties almost never came close to traffic deaths. Gruesome repeated images keep people on edge. "Terrorism speaks directly to System 1" (pdf 142).
- **Kahneman's position:**
  - Cascades are real and distort priorities (pdf 142).
  - Sunstein would insulate decision makers and let impartial experts allocate resources. Slovic trusts experts much less and the public somewhat more than Sunstein does, and notes that insulation yields policies the public rejects (pdf 142).
  - Kahneman shares both concerns. Rational or not, fear is painful and debilitating, and policy makers must endeavor to protect the public from fear, not only from real dangers (pdf 142).
  - Cascades may help by raising attention to risk classes and enlarging the risk budget; Love Canal may have caused excessive resources to go to toxic waste, but it also had a more general effect of raising the priority of environmental concerns. Democracy is messy, in part because availability and affect heuristics are biased, though they generally point in the right direction (pdf 143).
  - Policy should combine expert knowledge with the public's emotions and intuitions (pdf 143).

*Intuition note:* Both sides have a point. Experts can take a broad view of all risks, but insulating them from public feeling can produce policies the public rejects.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A simplified cascade. Suppose each news story adds 2 "concern points", and each concern point triggers 0.6 new stories next week.
- Stories next week = 0.6 × (2 × stories this week) = 1.2 × stories this week.
- Week 1: 10 stories, concern 20. Week 2: 12 stories, concern 24. Week 3: 14.4 stories, concern 28.8. Week 4: 17.28 stories, concern 34.56. Coverage grows by 20% a week.
- If each point triggered only 0.4 stories, the factor would be 0.8: 10, 8, 6.4 stories, so the story fades.
The point is that a loop gain above 1 sustains a cascade, whatever the real danger. This model is only an illustration; Kahneman gives no such numbers.

## Questions this raises
- How can one tell a justified alarm from an availability cascade while it is happening?
- Could institutions use cascades on purpose to direct attention to real but neglected risks?
- Which measure of risk should a policy use when "lives lost" and "good vs. bad deaths" conflict?
- How large is the cost of fear itself, compared with the cost of the danger?
