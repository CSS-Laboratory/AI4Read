# Tom W's Specialty

## L0 — One line
Asked how probable something is, people often answer how similar it is to a stereotype, and so ignore base rates and the quality of the evidence.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman and Tversky gave people a personality sketch of "Tom W" and asked which graduate field he most likely studies. The sketch fits small fields (computer science) well and large ones poorly. Statistically trained students' probability rankings did not differ from their rankings by similarity to the stereotype. They neglected base rates (how common each field is) and ignored doubts about the sketch. Kahneman calls this substitution of an easy question for a hard one. Bayesian reasoning (how to change your mind given evidence) says: anchor on a plausible base rate and doubt how diagnostic the evidence is (how far it favours one hypothesis over another). The chapter gives everyday examples of the same error.

**Prerequisites:** proportions and percentages; probability as a number between 0 and 1; the idea of odds. System 1 / System 2 (fast automatic vs. slow effortful thinking) are used as known from earlier chapters.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A base rate is the proportion of a category in the population (here, the share of each graduate field). With no information about the individual, it is the natural basis for a guess (pdf 144).
- The Tom W design uses three tasks: a no-information likelihood ranking (where base rates decide), a ranking by similarity to a personality sketch, and a ranking by probability. The sketch was built to fit small fields (pdf 145–146).
- Psychology graduate students' probability rankings did not differ from their similarity rankings. Kahneman calls this substitution of an easier question for the asked one (pdf 147).
- Probability is a vague notion for laypeople, and a probability question activates a "mental shotgun" that evokes answers to easier questions. One of them is automatic representativeness (similarity to a stereotype) (pdf 148).
- Representativeness often works, but it has two "sins": over-predicting unlikely events (low base rate), and insensitivity to evidence quality (pdf 149–150).
- Base-rate neglect is not total. Instructions ("think like a statistician") and frowning increased the use of base rates. The frowning result suggests, at least among Harvard undergraduates, that laziness of System 2 rather than ignorance explains the neglect (pdf 149–150).
- Bayes's rule combines the prior (base rate) with the diagnosticity of the evidence (how far it favours the hypothesis over the alternative). The correct Tom W answer stays very close to the base rates (pdf 151).
- Two disciplines: anchor on a plausible base rate, and question the diagnosticity of the evidence (pdf 152).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the base-rate task (pdf 144)
Rank nine graduate fields by how likely Tom W is to be a student in each: business administration, computer science, engineering, humanities and education, law, medicine, library science, physical and life sciences, social science and social work. Tom W is treated like a marble drawn at random from an urn. To judge the colour of a marble you need the number of marbles of each colour. The proportion of a kind is its **base rate**. Without other information, you go by enrollment sizes, so humanities and education beats computer science or library science (pdf 144).

### The similarity task (pdf 145–146)
A psychologist's sketch, written in Tom's high-school senior year from tests "of uncertain validity", describes him. He has high intelligence but lacks true creativity. He needs order and neat systems. His writing is dull, with corny puns and sci-fi flashes. He has a strong drive for competence, seems to have little feel or sympathy for others, is self-centered, and has a deep moral sense (pdf 144–145).

Participants rank the fields by how similar the sketch is to the typical student in each. The average ordering in the early 1970s was (pdf 145):
1. computer science
2. engineering
3. business administration
4. physical and life sciences
5. library science
6. law
7. medicine
8. humanities and education
9. social science and social work

Kahneman says the ranking needs System 2, but the planted hints ("corny puns") trigger an automatic System 1 association with a stereotype (pdf 146). Tom W is a good fit to small groups and a much poorer fit to the largest, so he was designed as an "anti-base-rate" character (pdf 146). *Reading note:* one sentence of the source is garbled here (see Reading notes).

### Predicting by representativeness (pdf 146–148)
**Task 3** was given to graduate students in psychology: rank the fields by the probability that Tom W is a student in each. Kahneman and Tversky collected answers to this question from 114 psychology graduate students at three major universities, all of whom had taken several courses in statistics (pdf 146–147). The group knew the base rates and knew the source of the sketch was not highly trustworthy (pdf 146). Kahneman and Tversky expected them to focus exclusively on similarity ("representativeness") and to rank computer science high (pdf 146).

- Robyn Dawes, a sophisticated statistician and sceptic about intuitive judgment, said tentatively "computer scientist?". He recognised his mistake as soon as "base rate" was mentioned (pdf 146–147).
- The 114 students' probability rankings did not differ from their similarity ratings. Substitution was perfect: no sign they did anything other than judge representativeness (pdf 147).
- The error is serious because similarity and probability are not constrained by the same logical rules. Similarity may legitimately ignore base rates and doubts about accuracy. Anyone who ignores base rates and evidence quality in probability assessments will certainly make mistakes (pdf 147).

*Intuition note:* The hard question ("how probable?") is silently swapped for an easy one ("how much does he fit the picture?"). The answer feels like a probability, but it is only a similarity (pdf 147).

Logicians have developed competing, very precise definitions of probability. For many experts it is a degree of belief. For laypeople it is a vague notion linked to uncertainty, propensity, plausibility and surprise. Nobody asks what "probability" means, but they would ask about an invented word such as "globability" (pdf 147–148). A probability question activates a "mental shotgun" that evokes answers to easier questions. One is an automatic representativeness judgment, as with the funny false claim that Elvis's parents wanted him to be a dentist (pdf 148). Other examples are "you can see she is a winner" and judging leaders by their chins (pdf 148). Prediction by representativeness, although common, is not statistically optimal. In *Moneyball*, Billy Beane picked players by past statistics instead of by scouts' view of their build and look. The players were cheap, and the team got excellent results at low cost (pdf 148).

### The sins of representativeness (pdf 148–151)
Representativeness has virtues. Its impressions are often, indeed usually, more accurate than chance guesses. Examples: friendly-acting people are in fact friendly on most occasions, and a tall, thin athlete is more likely to play basketball than football (pdf 148–149). But where stereotypes are false it will mislead, especially if it makes people neglect base rates that point the other way. Even when it has some validity, exclusive reliance on it is associated with "grave sins against statistical logic" (pdf 149).

**Sin 1: over-predicting unlikely (low base-rate) events** (pdf 149).
- A woman reading the *New York Times* on the subway: representativeness says "PhD", but this is not necessarily wise. Many more non-graduates than PhDs ride the subway, so "no college degree" deserves serious consideration (pdf 149).
- Told a woman is "a shy poetry lover", you should opt for business administration over Chinese literature. Even if every female student of Chinese literature is shy and loves poetry, it is almost certain that there are more bashful poetry lovers in the much larger population of business students (pdf 149).
- People without statistical training can use base rates under some conditions. When Tom W's sketch is absent, it is obvious to everyone that the probability is the base rate. Concern for base rates evidently disappears as soon as his personality is described (pdf 149).
- **Author's correction:** Kahneman and Tversky first believed base-rate information will always be neglected when information about the specific case is available. That conclusion was too strong. In many experiments where base rates are given explicitly, many participants are influenced by them, although case information is almost always weighted more than mere statistics. Instructing people to "think like a statistician" enhanced base-rate use, and "think like a clinician" had the opposite effect (Schwarz and colleagues) (pdf 149–150).

**The cheek experiment** (pdf 150). Harvard undergraduates did the Tom W task while puffing out their cheeks or frowning. Frowning generally increases the vigilance of System 2. The puffers relied exclusively on representativeness and ignored base rates. The frowners showed some sensitivity to base rates.

*Intuition note:* Both systems are to blame. System 1 offers the false intuition, and System 2 endorses it. Because frowning helped, laziness (rather than ignorance) seems to be the proper explanation of neglect, at least among these students (pdf 150).

**Sin 2: insensitivity to the quality of evidence** (pdf 150–151). Kahneman calls the rule of System 1 WYSIATI (the abbreviation is not spelled out on these pages). Unless you decide immediately to reject evidence, System 1 automatically processes the available information as if it were true. The line "little feel and little sympathy" was probably enough to convince most readers that Tom is very unlikely to be in social science or social work, although they were told the description should not be trusted. You surely understand in principle that worthless information should be treated like no information, but WYSIATI makes this hard to apply. When in doubt, keep probabilities close to the base rate. This takes significant effort of self-monitoring and self-control (pdf 150–151).

**Correct answer to Tom W.** Stay very close to prior beliefs. Slightly reduce the high probabilities of well-populated fields (humanities and education; social science and social work). Slightly raise the low probabilities of rare fields (library science, computer science). The little evidence is not trustworthy, so base rates should dominate (pdf 151).

### How to discipline intuition (pdf 151–152)
Beliefs are subjective, but they should be constrained by the logic of probability (pdf 151):
- A 40% chance of rain tomorrow requires a 60% chance of no rain, and rules out a 50% chance of rain tomorrow morning.
- P(elected) = 0.30 and P(reelected | elected) = 0.80 require $0.30 \times 0.80 = 0.24$ for being elected twice in a row (pdf 151).

Bayesian statistics supplies the relevant rules. Bayes's rule combines the prior (here the base rate) with the **diagnosticity** of the evidence, the degree to which it favours the hypothesis over the alternative (pdf 151). The author's numbers: base rate 3% in computer science, and the description 4 times more likely for a computer-science student than for students in other fields. Then the rule says the probability is now 11%. With a base rate of 80%, it is 94.1% (pdf 151).

*(added step, odds form; the author gives only the results)*
- Prior odds are $0.03/0.97$. Multiplying by 4 gives $0.12/0.97$, and the probability is $0.12/(0.12+0.97) = 0.12/1.09 \approx 0.110$.
- For 80%: odds $= 4$, times 4 $= 16$, and probability $= 16/17 \approx 0.941$.

Two ideas to keep: (1) base rates matter even with evidence about the case, which is often not intuitively obvious. (2) Intuitive impressions of diagnosticity are often exaggerated. The combination of WYSIATI and associative coherence tends to make us believe the stories we spin for ourselves (pdf 151–152). The two rules:
- Anchor your judgment of the probability of an outcome on a plausible base rate.
- Question the diagnosticity of your evidence (pdf 152).

Kahneman says he was never taught how to do this and still finds it unnatural (pdf 152).

*Intuition note:* Start from how common the thing is, then move only as far as the evidence really justifies. Weak evidence moves you only a little (pdf 151–152).

### Speaking of Representativeness (pdf 152)
The chapter ends with sample remarks that a speaker might make. Examples: a well-trimmed lawn does not show a company is well managed; a start-up that looks unstoppable still faces a very low industry base rate of success; predicting rare events from weak evidence is a repeated mistake; and one should allow for uncertainty even about damning reports (pdf 152).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A population of 100 graduate students: 10 in computer science (CS) and 90 in humanities. Suppose a sketch fits 80% of CS students and 20% of humanities students. This is a likelihood ratio of $0.80/0.20 = 4$, as in the author's case.
- Students fitting the sketch: CS $= 0.80 \times 10 = 8$; humanities $= 0.20 \times 90 = 18$.
- $P(\text{CS} \mid \text{fits}) = 8/(8+18) = 8/26 \approx 0.31$.
- Check with odds: prior odds $10/90 = 1/9$; times 4 gives $4/9$; probability $= 4/13 \approx 0.31$.

Even though the sketch is much more typical of CS, humanities is still more likely (18 vs. 8). Ranking by similarity alone would put CS first, which is the Tom W error.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Bayes's rule with prior and likelihood ratio ↔ Bayesian statistics generally: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio (our added step reproduces the author's 3% → 11% example).
- [structural-analogy] Base-rate neglect ↔ bias–variance trade-off: relying only on the vivid specific evidence over-weights noisy data, while a prior stabilises estimates.
- [metaphor] Substitution of similarity for probability ↔ bounded rationality: the mind uses a cheap answerable question instead of the costly one.

## Questions this raises
- How can one judge a plausible base rate when the "reference class" is unclear?
- How does one estimate diagnosticity (the "4 times more likely") in real cases?
- When frowning helps, is the gain real accuracy or only more effortful answers?
- When is similarity a good proxy for probability, given the author's admitted virtues of representativeness?
