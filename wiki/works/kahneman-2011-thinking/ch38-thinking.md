# Thinking About Life

## L0 — One line
When people judge their life or predict their happiness, they often use a quick substitute (mood, a salient thought, one aspect such as climate), so "nothing in life is as important as you think it is when you are thinking about it" (pdf 393).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why does life satisfaction jump around marriage and then fall (Figure 16)? Kahneman suggests that people answer the question about their life by substituting an easier question, so reported satisfaction follows what comes to mind. It need not follow how happy people are moment by moment. The same mechanism, the focusing illusion, makes people exaggerate how much a place, car, or condition affects well-being. It also makes them miss adaptation, which is attention fading. Goals also matter for satisfaction, so well-being needs a hybrid definition covering the experiencing and the remembering self. The chapter ends by suggesting that the mind is not well designed for processing time, which can distort forecasts and choices.

**Prerequisites:** the experiencing self vs. remembering self; System 1 and substitution; WYSIATI (an acronym used on these pages without being spelled out); duration neglect; the idea of a heuristic.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Figure 16 (satisfaction rises before marriage, peaks at the wedding, then falls) is usually read as adaptation. Kahneman offers a heuristic reading: it may track how likely people are to think of the marriage (pdf 389–391).
- Global life-satisfaction questions are probably answered by substitution, e.g. by current mood, so they should be treated with caution (pdf 390).
- In the DRM studies, experienced well-being was on average unaffected by marriage, because marriage changes some aspects of life for the better and others for the worse. A disposition for well-being is also heritable (pdf 391).
- Goals set at 17–18 were associated with later income and with how much income mattered for satisfaction twenty years later. In part because of such findings, Kahneman adopts a hybrid view of well-being (pdf 392–393).
- Focusing illusion: any aspect you attend to looms large in a global judgment. Californian and Midwestern students did not differ in life satisfaction, yet both groups believed Californians are happier (pdf 393–394).
- Adaptation consists largely of thinking less about a situation. In Cohn's survey, respondents who did not know a paraplegic did not forecast this (pdf 395–396).
- Miswanting: the focusing illusion favors purchases that excite at first (a car) over lasting attention-demanding activities (pdf 396–397).
- Both the remembering self and prospect-theory forecasts neglect time. The word "happiness" has no simple meaning (pdf 397–398).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Figure 16 and two readings (pdf 389–391)
- **Data.** The German Socio-Economic Panel asks the same respondents about life satisfaction every year. They also report major life changes. Figure 16 plots satisfaction from −4 to 5+ years around marriage (pdf 389).
- **Shape in the image.** Satisfaction is roughly flat at about 0.3 in years −4 to −2. It peaks at about 0.6 at year 0, then falls, with small wobbles, to about 0.2 at 5+. These values are my reading of the plotted points (pdf 389).
- **Affective forecasting.** Gilbert and Wilson's term for errors in predicting one's future feelings. Couples know divorce is common but do not believe the statistics apply to them (pdf 389).
- **Common reading.** The graph traces adaptation: early joys fade as marriage becomes routine (pdf 389).
- **Kahneman's alternative.**
  - A life-evaluation question is not as easy as asking for a telephone number. Some people have a ready-made answer. Probably most do not, and they automatically substitute an easier question. System 1 is at work (pdf 390).
  - Examples: students asked about dating reported "happiness these days" as if dating were all that mattered. In Schwarz's experiment, half the subjects found a dime planted on a copier, and their reported life satisfaction improved markedly (pdf 390).
  - Beyond mood, the answer is set by a small sample of highly available ideas, not a careful weighting of life domains (pdf 390).
  - Newlyweds and soon-to-be-married people will probably recall the marriage. Marriage is almost always voluntary in the US, so almost everyone reminded of it is happy about it. Salience falls as novelty wanes (pdf 390–391).
  - So Figure 16 can be read as the likelihood of thinking about the marriage (pdf 391).
- **Consequence.** If the surge reflects a heuristic, little can be learned from it about happiness or adaptation. Being happy when reminded of the marriage does not make people happier the rest of the time (pdf 391).
- **DRM evidence.** Experienced well-being showed no overall difference between women with and without a mate. Partnered women spend less time alone and much less with friends. They spend more time making love, but also more on housework, cooking, and childcare. Marriage changes some things for the better and some for the worse (pdf 391).

*Intuition note:* A survey answer is often a snapshot of what the mind picked up in those few seconds. The wedding "peak" may measure thoughts about the wedding, not a long glow of happiness.

### Why circumstances correlate weakly with well-being (pdf 391–393)
- Both experienced happiness and life satisfaction are largely determined by temperament genetics. A disposition for well-being is as heritable as height or intelligence, shown by studies of twins separated at birth (pdf 391).
- Correlations are also low when effects balance (marriage). For high income, effects are generally positive but vary because people differ in how much they care about money (pdf 391–392).
- **Goals study.** About 12,000 people who started elite higher education in 1976 rated the goal of being very well-off financially on a 4-point scale at age 17–18. They were surveyed again in 1995–1997 on income and global life satisfaction (pdf 392).
  - Each extra point on the scale went with over $14,000 of job income (1995 dollars) among 597 medical professionals. It went with over $12,000 of household income among nonworking married women (pdf 392).
  - Comparing households above $200,000 with those below $50,000, income raised life satisfaction by .57 on a 5-point scale for those who called money essential, and only .12 for those who called it not important (pdf 392).
  - Those who wanted money and got it were significantly more satisfied than average. Those who wanted it and did not were significantly more dissatisfied. Setting especially difficult goals is one recipe for a dissatisfied adulthood. The least promising goal, measured by life satisfaction 20 years later, was becoming accomplished in a performing art (pdf 392).
- **Conclusion.** In part because of these findings, Kahneman changed his mind: an exclusive focus on experienced well-being is not tenable, and ignoring how people feel as they live is also untenable. He accepts a hybrid view covering both selves (pdf 392–393).

*Intuition note:* What people want shapes what they will count as success. The same income can please one person and disappoint another.

### The focusing illusion (pdf 393–396)
- **Definition.** From the speed of the answers and the effect of mood, Kahneman infers that people must be using heuristics (examples of substitution and WYSIATI). Any attended aspect looms large. Kahneman's one-line statement: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is when you are thinking about it." (pdf 393)
- **Origin.** In a family debate, Kahneman's wife claimed people are happier in California. He argued climate is not an important determinant of well-being (pdf 393).
- **California study** (with David Schkade). Students at state universities in California, Ohio, and Michigan either reported their own satisfaction or predicted that of someone with similar interests and values living elsewhere (pdf 393–394).
  - Californians enjoyed their climate and Midwesterners despised theirs. Yet there was "no difference whatsoever" in life satisfaction. Both groups wrongly believed Californians were happier, because they overweighted climate (pdf 394).
- **Car example.** Asked how much pleasure they get from their car, people effectively answer how much pleasure they get when they think about it. People rarely think about their car while driving, so they neglect duration and exaggerate the pleasure (pdf 394).
- **Californians.** Californians spend little time attending to the climate. A long-term resident is unlikely to be reminded of it; for someone who has always lived there and rarely travels, California is like having ten toes: nice, but not much thought about. Thoughts of an aspect are more salient if a contrasting alternative is available (pdf 394–395).
- **Recent movers.** A recent mover from Ohio is reminded of the move and the contrast. He will report himself happier whether or not he is. The illusion can make people wrong about their present well-being, others' happiness, and their own future happiness (pdf 395).
- **Paraplegics.**
  - Guesses based on a currently attending victim fit the early days. But attention is withdrawn from a new situation as it becomes familiar (pdf 395).
  - Exceptions: chronic pain, constant loud noise, and severe depression, which keep attracting attention, so there is no adaptation to them (pdf 395).
  - Paraplegics are in a fairly good mood more than half the time by one month after the accident. They work, read, enjoy jokes, and so on. Adaptation largely consists of thinking less about the situation. Most long-term circumstances are "part-time states" (pdf 395–396).
- **Cohn's survey.** Respondents estimated the share of time paraplegics spend in a bad mood (pdf 396).

| Accident was… | Knew a paraplegic | Did not know one |
|---|---|---|
| 1 month earlier | 75% | 70% |
| 1 year earlier | 41% | 68% |

  - Evidently, those who knew one had seen attention withdraw; the others did not forecast the adaptation. Judgments about lottery winners showed the same pattern (pdf 396).
- **Chronic conditions.** Kahneman expects the life satisfaction of paraplegics and others with chronic, burdensome conditions to be low relative to their experienced well-being, because being asked to evaluate their lives will inevitably remind them of others' lives and their old life (pdf 396).
  - Colostomy patients show no difference in experienced happiness from healthy people. Yet they would trade away years of life, and patients whose colostomy was reversed recall it as awful. It appears that the remembering self is subject to a massive focusing illusion about a life that the experiencing self endures quite comfortably (pdf 396).
- **Miswanting.** Gilbert and Wilson's word for bad choices from affective-forecasting errors. Focalism is their name for the focusing illusion. It leads us to exaggerate the effect of purchases or changed circumstances (pdf 396–397).
- **Car vs. group.** Attention to a car will fade, while a weekly poker or book club will keep drawing attention, as will tennis or the cello. By WYSIATI, we are likely to exaggerate the long-term benefits of the car but not of the social group. The illusion biases us toward goods and experiences that are initially exciting, and time is neglected (pdf 397).

*Intuition note:* We judge a thing while thinking about it, but we live with it mostly while thinking about other things. So we overrate what is exciting at first and underrate what keeps holding attention.

### Time and Time Again (pdf 397–398)
- The experiencing self's episode value (a "hedonimeter total") is the sum of moment values. The remembering self instead represents episodes by a few critical moments, especially the beginning, the peak, and the end, and neglects duration (cold-hand situation, Violetta's story) (pdf 397).
- In prospect theory a state is represented by the transition to it, and adaptation is neglected. The same holds for forecasts about chronic disease and for the focusing illusion (pdf 397).
- "The mind is good with stories, but it does not appear to be well designed for the processing of time." Happiness "does not have a simple meaning" (pdf 397–398).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A person rates her pleasure from her car as 8 (scale 0–10, where 0 = no pleasure from the car).
1. Divide her day into 10 equal periods. She thinks about the car in only 1 of them (assumed).
2. In that period the car adds 8. In the other 9 it adds 0.
3. Actual average contribution: (8×1 + 0×9)/10 = 0.8.
4. Her answer of 8 equals the value *when she thinks about it*. It ignores the 9 periods without thought, which is duration neglect. She overestimates the car's effect by a factor of 8/0.8 = 10.
5. A weekly club draws attention in, say, 4 of 10 periods at value 5. Actual contribution: (5×4)/10 = 2.0. Judged as 5, it is overestimated by 2.5 times, far less than the car.

## Concepts
- [[affective-forecasting]] (new) — predicting one's future feelings; for many people marriage reflects a massive error of it (pdf 389)
- [[substitution]] (new) — answering an easier question in place of the hard one asked, automatically (pdf 390)
- [[heuristic]] — here, mood or a few available ideas stand in for a careful evaluation of life (pdf 390)
- [[representativeness / availability / anchoring]] — here only availability: a few highly available ideas set the answer (pdf 390)

- [[wysiati]] (new) — acronym used here without expansion; the pages describe it as too much weight on the attended aspect, too little on the rest (pdf 394)
- [[experiencing-vs-remembering-self]] (new) — moment-by-moment well-being vs. evaluation of life; both are counted in the hybrid view (pdf 393)
- [[duration-neglect]] (new) — ignoring how long an experience or state lasts (pdf 394, 397)

- [[miswanting]] (new) — bad choices that arise from errors of affective forecasting (pdf 396)
- [[reference dependence]] — only touched on: prospect theory represents a state by the transition to it (pdf 397)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Substitution of an easy question ↔ heuristics in judgment: the same replacement of a hard evaluation by a simpler cue.
- [structural-analogy] Time-neglecting remembering self and prospect-theory "transition" coding ↔ reference dependence: both represent a state by a thin slice of time.
- [metaphor] Focusing illusion ↔ WYSIATI in forecasting: only the attended moments get weight.
