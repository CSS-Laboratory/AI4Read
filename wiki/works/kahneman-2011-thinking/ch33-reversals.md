# Reversals

## L0 — One line
People can judge the same options differently when they see them one at a time than when they see them together, so their single-evaluation judgments may not be consistent with principles they endorse when they compare the options.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks why the same options get different rankings when judged one at a time (single evaluation) or side by side (joint evaluation). His answer: in single evaluation, the emotional reactions of System 1, for example to a poignant story or to a prize, are much more likely to determine the judgment. Joint evaluation brings out features, such as safety or human versus animal, that people accept on reflection. The result is a preference reversal. Judgments are coherent within a category and may be incoherent across categories. This challenged economic theory. It also matters for law: jurors assessing punitive damages are reportedly barred from considering other cases.

**Prerequisites:** the System 1 / System 2 distinction; anchoring; the idea that rational choice means a stable ranking of options.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Single evaluation (one option at a time) and joint evaluation (options compared) can give opposite judgments. Reversals appear in compensation, bets, charity and punitive damages (pdf 345–352).
- In single evaluation, System 1 uses substitution (answering an easier question) and intensity matching (mapping a feeling's strength onto a scale such as dollars) to turn a feeling into a dollar value. Joint evaluation involves System 2 and brings out features that were not salient (pdf 346–347).
- Life is mostly lived in single-evaluation mode (with WYSIATI: only what is in view counts, and contrasting alternatives are absent), so reflective moral beliefs do not necessarily govern emotional reactions (pdf 346).
- Lichtenstein and Slovic's bet reversal drew in economists. Grether and Plott tried to discredit it and instead confirmed it (pdf 346–348).
- Judgments are coherent within a category and potentially incoherent across categories, because each object recruits its own norm (pdf 348–349).
- Hsee's evaluability hypothesis: in Hsee's dictionary example, the number of entries gets no weight in single evaluation because it is not evaluable on its own, but it counts in joint evaluation (pdf 351).
- Rationality is generally served by broader frames, and there is a presumption that comparative judgment is more likely to be stable than single evaluation, except where someone who controls what you see has a vested interest (pdf 352).
- Kahneman reports incoherence in the administration of justice: jurors assessing punitive damages are prohibited from considering other cases, and agency penalties are sensible within each agency but incoherent across agencies (pdf 352–353).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the victim-compensation scenarios (pdf 345–346)
- The case: a man loses the use of his right arm in a shooting during a store robbery. In scenario (i) it happens in his regular store. In scenario (ii) his regular store is closed for a funeral, so he is shot in the other store (pdf 345).
- **Joint evaluation:** there is "almost universal agreement" that compensation should be equal, because location is not a relevant factor. Kahneman says this comparison engages System 2 (pdf 345).
- **Single evaluation** (Miller and McFarland, between-subjects design: each participant sees one scenario): the victim gets a much larger award when shot in the store he rarely visited (pdf 345).
- Explanation: *poignancy* (a close cousin of regret) is a counterfactual feeling, evoked because the thought "if only he had shopped at his regular store" comes readily to mind. *Substitution* (answering an easier question instead) and *intensity matching* (mapping a feeling's strength onto a scale such as dollars) turn it into a dollar gap (pdf 345–346).
- The principle "poignancy is not legitimate" matters only when both scenarios are seen together. Normal life is between-subjects, and WYSIATI applies. So reflective beliefs do not necessarily govern emotional reactions, and moral intuitions are not internally consistent (pdf 346).

*Intuition note:* In this example, a principle people hold became relevant only when both cases were seen together. Seeing the contrast brought it to mind; seeing one case did not.

### Challenging Economics (pdf 346–348)
- **Bets** (roulette wheel, 36 sectors; slightly simplified): Bet A: 11/36 to win $160, 25/36 to lose $15. Bet B: 35/36 to win $40, 1/36 to lose $10 (pdf 346).
- In choice, the safer B is clearly more popular. In single evaluation ("lowest price at which you would sell it"), Kahneman conjectures the prize is salient and the value is anchored on it. The results support this, and the selling price is higher for A than for B (pdf 346–347).
- This is a **preference reversal**: B is chosen over A, but A is valued higher when each is priced alone. In joint evaluation, attention goes to A being much less safe. The features driving single evaluation (poignancy, anchoring on the prize) are suppressed or irrelevant in joint evaluation (pdf 347).
- In a within-subject version, participants price both bets and also choose, and they are unaware of the inconsistency. In Lichtenstein's 1968 interview, a participant repeatedly pays to swap the bet he chose for the one he rejected (pdf 347).
- Rational "Econs" would not show reversals, so this challenged the rational-agent model (pdf 347).
- **Grether and Plott** (*American Economic Review*) said their experiments were "designed to discredit the psychologists' works as applied to economics" (pdf 347). They tested thirteen explanations, including that psychologists ran the experiment. Only one survived: the psychologists were right. It is the least satisfactory for standard theory because it lets choice depend on context (pdf 347–348).
- Kahneman says theoretical beliefs are robust. The report had little direct effect on economists' convictions, but it made them more willing to take psychological research seriously (pdf 348).

*Intuition note:* Pricing a bet alone pulls your eye to its biggest number. Choosing between two bets pulls your eye to how risky each is.

### Categories (pdf 348–351)
- **Norms:** System 1 retrieves a norm automatically. John (6) at 5' is very tall, and Jim (16) at 5'1" is not. Asked "Is John as tall as Jim?", you say no. Here single and joint evaluation do not clash (pdf 348).
- **Main claim:** judgments and preferences are coherent within categories and potentially incoherent across categories. Apples vs peaches (same category, fruit) gives the same ranking in single and joint evaluation. Apples vs steak has no stable answer, because they do not fill the same need (pdf 349).
- **Dolphins vs farmworkers** (charity appeals): the dolphin appeal evokes endangered species, where dolphins rank high. System 1 matches the intensity of liking to a dollar scale of contributions (pdf 349–350). The skin-cancer appeal probably evokes public-health issues, where it probably ranks lower. In single evaluation dolphins drew somewhat larger contributions (pdf 350).
- In joint evaluation, "human vs. animal" becomes salient, and people show a solid preference for farmworkers and a willingness to contribute substantially more to them (pdf 351). The two judgments are not consistent (pdf 351).
- **Hsee's dictionaries** (both 1993): A has 10,000 entries and is like new. B has 20,000 entries and a torn cover but is otherwise like new. Single evaluation favours A, and joint evaluation favours B (pdf 351). *Evaluability hypothesis:* the number of entries gets no weight alone because it is not evaluable on its own. Jointly, B's advantage is obvious and entries matter far more than the cover (pdf 351).

*Intuition note:* A number such as "10,000 entries" means little without something to compare it to. Side by side, it suddenly matters.

### Unjust Reversals (pdf 351–353)
- **Mock jurors** (Texas jury rolls) assessed punitive damages in paired cases, one personal injury and one financial loss. Half judged case 1 first (single evaluation) and then compared the two. The other half saw the reverse order (pdf 351–352).
- Case 1: a child suffers moderate burns when pajamas that were not adequately fire resistant catch fire. Case 2: a bank's unscrupulous dealings cause another bank a $10 million loss (pdf 351–352).
- Single evaluation: higher damages for the bank than for the child, presumably because the loss size is a high anchor. Joint evaluation: sympathy for the individual victim prevails and the child's award surpasses the bank's. The bank's award stays anchored on its loss. Averaged over several pairs, personal-injury awards were more than twice as large in joint as in single evaluation (pdf 352).
- Single-evaluation jurors could not anticipate that the child's award would look inadequate next to a large bank award (pdf 352).
- **Which mode is better?** Kahneman says broader frames generally serve rationality. There is a presumption that comparative judgment, which involves System 2, is more stable than single evaluation. He warns against joint evaluation when someone controlling what you see has a vested interest, as with salespeople (pdf 352).
- Per Sunstein, jurors assessing punitive damages are explicitly prohibited from considering other cases. Kahneman says the law "favors single evaluation", contrary to psychological common sense (pdf 352).
- **Agency penalties** (Sunstein): within categories, penalties are "extremely sensible" in that more serious harms are punished more severely. Across agencies they vary in ways that reflect politics and history more than global fairness. A "serious violation" of worker-safety rules is capped at $7,000, while a Wild Bird Conservation Act violation can bring up to $25,000. The system is coherent within agencies but incoherent globally (pdf 353).

*Intuition note:* Each agency's penalty scale is sensible by itself. The oddness appears only when a rule from one agency is put next to a rule from another.

### Speaking of Reversals (pdf 353)
Closing sample remarks: broadening the frame tends to give more reasonable decisions, and isolated cases tend to follow System 1's emotional reaction. One remark says BTU units meant nothing until the speaker saw how much air-conditioning units vary, so joint evaluation was essential. Another remark says: you call a speech outstanding because you compared it to her other speeches, but compared to others she was still inferior (pdf 353).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two used laptops, scored by a judge whose rule is: in single evaluation, only "condition" is evaluable. Memory (8 GB or 16 GB) means nothing alone. Jointly, the judge sees both attributes and counts memory.
- Condition score: like new = 10, scratched lid = 7.
- Memory score (joint only): 8 GB = 4, 16 GB = 8.
- Laptop X (like new, 8 GB), Laptop Y (scratched, 16 GB).
- Single: X = 10, Y = 7, so X is valued higher.
- Joint: X = 10 + 4 = 14, Y = 7 + 8 = 15, so Y is valued higher.
The ranking flips because a heavily weighted attribute becomes usable only in comparison, as in Hsee's dictionaries.

## Concepts
- [[preference-reversal]] (new) — choosing X over Y, yet valuing Y above X when each is valued alone (pdf 347)
- [[single-vs-joint-evaluation]] (new) — judging one option alone (between-subjects) versus comparing options together (within-subject) (pdf 345)
- [[system-1-system-2]] (new) — fast, emotional System 1 versus slow, effortful System 2; comparison calls on System 2 (pdf 347)
- [[intensity-matching]] (new) — mapping the strength of a feeling onto another scale, such as dollars (pdf 346)
- [[substitution]] (new) — answering an easier question in place of a harder one (pdf 346, 350)
- [[evaluability-hypothesis]] (new) — an attribute that is not evaluable on its own, such as the number of entries, gets no weight in single evaluation (Hsee) (pdf 351)
- [[wysiati]] (new) — only the information at hand counts; contrasting alternatives are absent (pdf 346)
- anchoring (from [[representativeness / availability / anchoring]]) — a salient number, such as a prize or a loss, pulls the valuation (pdf 346, 352)
- [[reference-class-coherence]] (new) — judgments are coherent within a category (norm) and potentially incoherent across categories (pdf 349)
- [[framing effect]] — here, narrow single versus broad joint frames change judgments (pdf 351–352)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Preference reversal ↔ preference relation (decision theory): a stable complete ranking is assumed, and reversals show rankings that depend on how options are presented.
- [structural-analogy] Context-dependent choice (Grether and Plott's surviving hypothesis) ↔ framing effect: the same options are judged differently depending on the frame.
- [metaphor] Coherent within agencies, incoherent globally ↔ locally consistent rules that conflict when combined: each rule works alone, and contradictions show only under joint inspection.

## Questions this raises
- When single and joint judgments conflict, why is the joint one the better guide, and when is it not (for example, under manipulation by a salesperson)?
- Could courts give jurors comparison cases without introducing new biases?
- How do people decide which category's norm an object evokes, and can this be changed?
- Why did Grether and Plott's confirmation change economists' beliefs so little?
