# Introduction (Thinking, Fast and Slow)

## L0 — One line
People's judgments and choices can contain predictable errors (biases), many of which Kahneman and Tversky linked to the mind's mental shortcuts (heuristics), and a richer vocabulary for these errors may help us notice them and, in at least some cases, limit the damage.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kahneman asks how we can better identify and understand systematic errors of judgment and choice. He tells how his work with Amos Tversky showed that intuitive thinking can rely on heuristics, such as judging by resemblance or by ease of recall. They traced the resulting predictable biases to the design of cognition rather than to emotion, although he now gives emotion a larger role. He adds that expert intuition is a different, accurate source of fast judgment, based on practice and recognition. When a question is hard, we often answer an easier one instead. He introduces System 1 (fast) and System 2 (slow) and previews five parts. The aim is better "watercooler" talk about errors, so that we can notice them in others and eventually in ourselves.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday ideas of probability, sample size and "base rate" (how common something is in a population).

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Vocabulary as a tool.** Naming biases (e.g. the halo effect) makes them easier to anticipate and recognize, as disease labels help a physician (pdf 7).
- **Focus on error is not contempt for intelligence.** Most judgments are appropriate most of the time, and confidence in intuition is usually justified, but not always: we are often confident even when wrong (pdf 8).
- **Heuristics and biases.** Using shortcuts such as resemblance and availability produces predictable, systematic errors, shown by short demonstrations (Steve, the letter K) (pdf 10–11).
- **Challenge to 1970s assumptions.** The work traced errors to the design of cognition rather than to emotion, and questioned the view that people are generally rational (pdf 11–12).
- **From judgment to choice.** The same method applied to gambles led to prospect theory, a foundation of behavioral economics (pdf 13).
- **Two sources of intuition.** Accurate expert intuition comes from long practice and recognition; heuristic intuition comes from substituting an easier question (pdf 14–16).
- **Two systems.** Fast thinking is System 1 and slow thinking is System 2; System 1 is more influential than experience suggests (pdf 16).
- **Roadmap.** Five parts: two systems, statistical thinking, overconfidence, choice and rationality, two selves (pdf 16–18).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pdf 7–8)
- **Aim.** Kahneman pictures the "watercooler," where people gossip about others' judgments and choices. He wants to enrich the vocabulary used there (pdf 7).
- **Why gossip.** It is easier to spot others' mistakes than our own. Anticipating friends' and colleagues' judgments of our choices motivates self-criticism, more than New Year resolutions do (pdf 7).
- **Medical analogy.** A physician needs many labels, each binding an illness with its symptoms, causes, course and cures. Understanding judgment likewise needs a richer vocabulary than everyday language (pdf 7).
- **Bias.** Systematic errors are called biases, and they recur predictably in particular circumstances. Example: the confident, handsome speaker is judged more favorably than he deserves. This is the halo effect (pdf 7).
- **Silent mental work.** Most impressions and thoughts reach consciousness without our knowing how. Examples: seeing a lamp, sensing irritation in a voice, avoiding a road threat before noticing it (pdf 7–8).
- **Balance.** The focus on error no more denigrates intelligence than medical texts' focus on disease denies good health. Intuitive confidence is usually justified, "but not always." An observer is more likely than we are to detect our errors (pdf 8).
- **Goal.** Improve the ability to identify errors in others and eventually in ourselves. In at least some cases, an accurate diagnosis may suggest an intervention that limits damage (pdf 8).

*Intuition note.* Having a name for an error is like having a name for a disease. You can notice it earlier and discuss it with others.

### Origins (pdf 8–14)
- **Meeting.** In 1969, Kahneman invited Amos Tversky (32; Kahneman was 35) to a seminar at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem (pdf 8).
- **Starting question.** Are people good intuitive statisticians? Children are good intuitive grammarians. Amos reported a qualified yes from research at Michigan. The seminar concluded that a qualified no was better (pdf 8–9).
- **First study.** Both authors had not developed an intuitive sense of how reliable results from small samples are. They tended to trust findings based on inadequate evidence and to collect too few observations (pdf 9).
- **Survey of experts.** Amos surveyed experts at a Society of Mathematical Psychology meeting, including two textbook authors. They greatly exaggerated the likelihood that an original result would be replicated, even with a small sample. They also gave poor advice to a fictitious student about how many observations to collect. Kahneman concludes that even statisticians were not good intuitive statisticians (pdf 9).
- **Collaboration.** They worked together for fourteen years, enjoyed it, and did not reject each other's ideas out of hand. Amos was the more logical, theory-oriented thinker. Kahneman was more intuitive, rooted in the psychology of perception. Kahneman judges the work of those years the best either did (pdf 9).
- **Method.** Their research was a conversation. They invented questions and examined their intuitive answers: the first one that came to mind, even when known to be wrong. They believed that an intuition they shared would be shared by many others (pdf 9–10).
- **Resemblance.** They noticed that their guesses about toddlers' future professions followed resemblance to cultural stereotypes. This led to a theory of the role of resemblance in prediction (pdf 10).
- **Steve the librarian.** Steve is described as shy, withdrawn, helpful, meek, tidy and detail-loving. Is he more likely a librarian or a farmer? The resemblance to a librarian strikes everyone. But there are more than 20 male farmers per male librarian in the United States. So it is almost certain that more "meek and tidy" souls are on tractors than at library desks. Participants ignored these statistical facts and relied exclusively on resemblance. Kahneman calls resemblance a simplifying **heuristic** (rule of thumb) that causes predictable **biases** (pdf 10).
- **Availability heuristic.** Judging the size of a category by how easily examples come to mind. Example: estimating professors' divorce rate by recalling divorced professors (pdf 10–11).
- **Letter K.** Is K more likely first or third in an English word? Words starting with a letter are easier to recall than words with it third, true for every letter. So people were expected to exaggerate first-position frequency, even for K, L, N, R, V, which occur more often in third position (pdf 11).
- **Adultery example.** Kahneman doubted his impression that adultery is more common among politicians than among physicians or lawyers. Politicians' transgressions are more likely to be reported. His impression could be due entirely to journalists' choices and to availability (pdf 11).
- **Science paper.** In their fifth year they published "Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," explaining some 20 biases as manifestations of heuristics. It is reproduced at the end of the book (pdf 11).
- **Challenge to assumptions.** Social scientists in the 1970s broadly accepted two ideas: (1) people are generally rational, and their thinking is normally sound; (2) emotions such as fear, affection and hatred explain most departures from rationality. The article challenged both without discussing them directly. It documented systematic errors in normal people and traced them to the machinery of cognition, not to emotional corruption of thought (pdf 11–12).
- **Impact.** More than three hundred articles cited it in 2010. The ideas were used in medical diagnosis, legal judgment, intelligence analysis, philosophy, finance, statistics and military strategy (pdf 12).
- **Policy illustration.** Availability helps explain which issues are salient: people judge importance by ease of retrieval, largely set by media coverage. Examples given are the weeks of coverage after Michael Jackson's death and the little coverage of declining educational standards (pdf 12). Kahneman notes that his own choice of "little-covered" examples was guided by availability (pdf 12).
- **Why it spread.** The articles almost always included the full text of the questions. Readers could see their own thinking being tripped up. Philosophers and economists, having seen themselves fail, became more likely to question the dogmatic assumption that the mind is rational and logical. Conventional experiments alone would have let skeptics blame "fecklessness of undergraduates." Kahneman says luck played a large role and that this is a recurrent theme of the book (pdf 12–13).
- **Reception.** The focus on biases was criticized as unfairly negative. Some refined the ideas and others offered alternatives. By and large, the idea of systematic error is now generally accepted (pdf 13).
- **Decision making.** They then studied simple gambles. Example: would you accept a coin toss where you win \$130 on heads and lose \$100 on tails? They again found intuitive preferences that consistently violated the rules of rational choice in their own decisions. Five years after the Science article they published "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision Under Risk." By some counts it is more influential than the judgment work, and it is a foundation of behavioral economics (pdf 13).
- **Credit.** The Nobel Prize of 2002 was for the collaboration. Amos would have shared it had he not died, aged fifty-nine, in 1996 (pdf 14).

*Intuition note.* The early work called heuristics quite useful but sometimes the cause of severe, systematic errors. When a rule of thumb ignores relevant facts, such as how common farmers are, the result can be a predictable error. Because such errors recur, short questions like Steve's could demonstrate them.

### Where we are now (pdf 14–16)
- **Purpose.** The book is not an exposition of the early research. It presents a view of how the mind works from recent cognitive and social psychology, including both the strengths and the flaws of intuitive thought (pdf 14).
- **What was missing.** The early work addressed accurate intuition only by saying heuristics "are quite useful, but sometimes lead to severe and systematic errors." It did not ask whether all intuitive judgments under uncertainty come from these heuristics. It is now clear that they do not: experts' accurate intuitions are better explained by prolonged practice. Skill and heuristics are alternative sources of intuitive judgment (pdf 14).
- **Klein's firefighters.** The commander suddenly ordered everyone out without knowing why. The floor then collapsed. Afterwards he realized the fire was unusually quiet and his ears unusually hot. The fire's heart was in the basement (pdf 14).
- **Everyday expertise.** Other examples are the chess master and the physician. Expert intuition seems magical to us, but the author says it is not. Ordinary feats include detecting anger in a phone call's first word. They are no less marvelous, only more common (pdf 14–15).
- **Simon's account.** Herbert Simon studied chess masters. His account: a cue gives access to information stored in memory, and that information gives the answer. "Intuition is nothing more and nothing less than recognition." This is like a child saying "doggie!" Valid intuitions develop when experts learn to recognize familiar elements in new situations (pdf 15).
- **Failed intuition.** A chief investment officer put tens of millions into Ford stock because he liked cars at a show: "Boy, do they know how to make a car!" He had apparently not considered whether Ford stock was underpriced. Kahneman says it is reasonable to believe, from what is known about stock picking, that he did not know what he was doing (pdf 15).
- **Affect heuristic.** The early heuristics helped little here, but a broader conception now offers a good account. Emotion looms larger now. The executive's decision is an instance of the affect heuristic: judgments guided directly by feelings of liking and disliking, with little deliberation (pdf 15–16).
- **Substitution.** Intuition does its best. With relevant expertise, the answer that comes to mind is likely correct. With a hard question and no skilled solution, a quick answer may come, but to a different question. The executive answered an easier question (do I like Ford cars?) instead of the hard one (should I invest?) Kahneman calls this the essence of intuitive heuristics, usually done without noticing (pdf 16).
- **Fast and slow.** If no expert or heuristic answer comes, we often switch to slower, more deliberate, effortful thinking. Fast thinking includes expert and heuristic intuition and the automatic activities of perception and memory (pdf 16).
- **Two systems.** Kahneman uses System 1 (fast) and System 2 (slow) as a metaphor of two agents (details promised in the next chapter). System 1 is more influential than experience tells us and is the "secret author" of many choices and judgments (pdf 16).

*Intuition note.* Fast thinking includes automatic perception and memory, expert recognition, and heuristic answers to easier questions. When no expert or heuristic answer comes, we often switch to slower, effortful thinking. Still, System 1 is described as the secret author of many of our judgments and choices.

### What comes next (pdf 16–18)
- **Part 1.** The elements of the two-systems approach. Associative memory, the core of System 1, continually builds a coherent interpretation of the world (pdf 16–17).
- **Part 2.** Why is statistical thinking so hard? We think associatively, metaphorically and causally, but statistics requires thinking about many things at once, which System 1 is not designed to do (pdf 17).
- **Part 3.** Excessive confidence and the inability to acknowledge ignorance and uncertainty. We overestimate understanding and underestimate chance, and hindsight feeds illusory certainty. Kahneman credits Nassim Taleb's influence (pdf 17).
- **Part 4.** A conversation with economics about the assumption that agents are rational. It treats prospect theory (published 1979), the tendency to treat problems in isolation, and framing effects. Kahneman says these are readily explained by System 1 and challenge the rationality assumption of standard economics (pdf 17).
- **Part 5.** Two selves, the experiencing self and the remembering self, with different interests. Of two painful experiences, the longer is strictly worse. Memory formation (System 1) has rules that can be exploited so the worse episode leaves a better memory. People then choose by the remembering self and expose the experiencing self to unnecessary pain (pdf 17–18).
- **Conclusion.** It explores the three distinctions in reverse order: remembering vs. experiencing selves, classical vs. behavioral economics, System 1 vs. System 2. It returns to "educating gossip" and to what organizations might do (pdf 18).
- **Appendixes.** The review of judgment under uncertainty described earlier and the 1984 article on prospect theory and framing are reproduced. Kahneman says the Nobel committee cited them and that readers may be surprised by how simple they are (pdf 18).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Base rates against resemblance, in the Steve problem. The source gives only "more than 20 farmers per librarian"; the other numbers are invented.
- Take 100 librarians and 2,000 farmers (ratio 20:1).
- Suppose 40% of librarians fit "meek and tidy": 0.40 × 100 = 40 people.
- Suppose only 5% of farmers fit: 0.05 × 2,000 = 100 people.
- Among all who fit: 40 + 100 = 140. Chance of being a farmer = 100 / 140 ≈ 0.71.
- So even though the description fits librarians 8 times as often (40% vs 5%), a farmer is still more likely. Resemblance alone ignores the 20:1 base rate.

## Concepts
- [[bias]] (new) — systematic error that recurs predictably in particular circumstances (pdf 7)
- [[heuristic]] — simplifying rule of thumb used for a hard judgment; here the cause of predictable bias (pdf 10)
- [[representativeness / availability / anchoring]] — here shown as judging by resemblance (Steve) and by ease of memory search (availability); anchoring not mentioned (pdf 10–11)
- [[halo effect]] (new) — judging a confident, handsome speaker's comments more favorably than deserved (pdf 7)
- [[affect heuristic]] (new) — judging and deciding directly by feelings of liking and disliking, with little deliberation (pdf 15–16)
- [[attribute substitution]] (new) — answering an easier related question in place of a hard one, usually unnoticed (pdf 16)
- [[expert intuition as recognition]] (new) — a cue gives access to stored information, which gives the answer (Simon) (pdf 15)
- [[system 1 and system 2]] (new) — metaphor of fast intuitive and slow effortful thinking as two agents (pdf 16)
- [[prospect theory]] (new) — Kahneman and Tversky's theory of choice under risk, a foundation of behavioral economics (pdf 13)
- [[framing effect]] — decisions shaped by inconsequential features of how a problem is posed (pdf 17)
- [[experiencing vs remembering self]] (new) — two selves with different interests; memory formation, a System 1 feature, has rules that can be exploited (pdf 17)

## Questions this raises
- How does System 1 differ from System 2 in practice, and what makes the "two agents" metaphor useful?
- When can we trust intuition, given that confidence is usually, but not always, justified?
- Which environments let experts build valid intuitions, and which do not?
- Why do people violate rational choice rules in simple gambles, and how does prospect theory describe it?
