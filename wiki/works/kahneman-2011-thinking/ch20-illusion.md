# The Illusion of Validity

## L0 — One line
Subjective confidence is a feeling produced by a coherent story, so people stay sure of predictions (soldiers, stocks, politics) that the evidence shows to be little better than chance, or even worse.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people stay confident in judgments that they know, in general, to be poor? Kahneman's examples are army officer-candidate ratings, stock picking and expert political forecasting. In each, confidence is a feeling that evidence of failure does not dent. Examples: advisers' year-to-year rank correlation of about .01, and experts doing worse than equal-probability guesses. Skill shows up as persistence of individual differences. Where the world is unpredictable, prediction errors are inevitable and high confidence is not a sign of accuracy. Tests that predict an important outcome with modest validity (.20–.30) should still be used.

**Prerequisites:** correlation coefficient (−1 to 1, 0 = no link); base rate (how common something is overall); the System 1 / System 2 distinction; WYSIATI ("what you see is all there is").

## L2 — Key ideas
- Confidence reflects the coherence of the story and the ease of processing it; the amount and quality of evidence do not count for much (pdf 204, 207).
- In the army, Kahneman's team knew its forecasts were nearly useless, yet felt each individual forecast was valid: the "illusion of validity" (pdf 205–206).
- People are often reluctant to apply general facts (base rates) to particular cases, so knowing the rule did not lower confidence (pdf 206–207).
- Skill is diagnosed by consistency of individual differences across time. Mutual funds and a wealth-adviser firm show almost none; the adviser firm was rewarding luck as if it were skill (pdf 209–210).
- Many individual investors lose by trading, and on average the most active traders did worst (Odean, Barber) (pdf 208).
- The illusion is supported by real skill at analysing firms, by System 1 confidence, and by a professional culture (pdf 211–212).
- Hindsight makes the past look explainable, which breeds overconfidence about the future. Tetlock's experts did worse than equal-probability guesses; "foxes" did best, though still poorly (pdf 212–214).
- Errors are inevitable because the world is unpredictable. Short-term trends can be forecast, but little should be expected from long-term forecasts (pdf 214–215).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pdf 204).** System 1 jumps to conclusions and does not know the size of its jumps. Because of WYSIATI, only the evidence at hand counts. Because of confidence by coherence, confidence reflects how coherent the story is. Poor evidence can make a very good story. Kahneman calls the resulting confidence "preposterous" given how little we know, and also "essential" (pdf 204).

**The Illusion of Validity (pdf 204–207).**
- *Setting.* In his Israeli Army national service, Kahneman helped evaluate officer-training candidates, using British WWII methods (pdf 204).
- *Test.* In the "leaderless group challenge", eight strangers with no rank insignia had to carry a long log over a six-foot wall without the log touching the ground or the wall and without anyone touching the wall. Failure meant starting again (pdf 204).
- *Observers' impressions.* Observers noted who led, who was rebuffed, and who was stubborn, patient, a quitter and so on. They felt each man's "true nature" was revealed, and the impression felt as direct as the colour of the sky (pdf 205).
- *Forecasts.* When observations converged on a coherent story, the observers were fully confident and gave definite scores. Any other prediction seemed inconsistent with what they had seen (pdf 205).
- *Hidden doubt.* If challenged, they admitted "anything could happen". They knew with certainty that their forecasts were largely useless (pdf 205).
- *Feedback.* In feedback sessions every few months, they compared their ratings with commanders' opinions of the cadets. The story was always the same: predictive ability was negligible, "better than blind guesses, but not by much" (pdf 205).
- *No effect.* Another batch arrived the next day and they saw "true natures" as clearly as before. The dismal truth had no effect on how they evaluated candidates and very little on their confidence (pdf 206).
- *Naming.* Kahneman compared this to the Müller-Lyer illusion (lines known to be equal still look different) and coined "illusion of validity" (pdf 206).
- *Link to earlier themes.* Predicting from one hour of behaviour was substitution and the representativeness heuristic. The predictions were non-regressive. It was WYSIATI, since they had no way to represent their ignorance of the factors that would decide the outcome (pdf 206).
- *Base rates.* This resembles Nisbett and Borgida's students, who believed the statistics but did not apply the base rates to the individual they saw on video. People are often reluctant to infer the particular from the general (pdf 206–207).
- *Conclusion.* Confidence is a feeling reflecting coherence and cognitive ease, not a reasoned estimate of probability of being right. High confidence mainly shows that a coherent story exists, not necessarily that it is true (pdf 207).

*Intuition note:* A vivid story about one person feels like knowledge. A dull general statistic about how often such judgments fail does not compete with it, so the story tends to win.

**The Illusion of Stock-Picking Skill (pdf 207–211).**
- *Puzzle.* In 1984 Kahneman, his colleague Amos and Richard Thaler visited a Wall Street firm. Kahneman asked who buys a stock when you sell it. The host waved vaguely, expecting a buyer like himself (pdf 207). Most buyers and sellers have the same information and trade mainly because their opinions differ, each side thinking the price is wrong. Kahneman says that for most of them, this belief is an illusion (pdf 207).
- *Standard theory.* In Malkiel's account, the price already incorporates all available knowledge. If all assets are correctly priced, no one can expect to gain or lose by trading (pdf 207). Kahneman says this is "not quite right", because many individual investors lose consistently (pdf 208).
- *Odean's study.* 10,000 brokerage accounts, seven years, nearly 163,000 trades (pdf 208).
  - He looked at cases where an investor sold one stock and soon bought another, which shows the investor expected the bought stock to do better.
  - He compared the two stocks' returns over the following year.
  - On average the shares sold did better than those bought, by 3.2 percentage points per year, beyond trading costs (pdf 208).
  - This is an average. Some did much better and some much worse, but for the large majority doing nothing would have been better (pdf 208).
- *Barber and Odean.* The most active traders had the poorest results and the least active the highest. Men traded more than women, and women did better (pdf 208).
- *Mistakes.* Individuals sell winners and keep losers, but recent winners tend to do better in the short run. They also flock to stocks that are in the news. Professionals are more selective, which gives some justification for the label "smart money" (pdf 208–209).
- *Professionals.* Few stock pickers, if any, can beat the market consistently. The diagnostic for skill is consistency of individual differences. If differences in a year are entirely luck, rankings vary erratically and the year-to-year correlation is zero. With skill, rankings are more stable (pdf 209).
- *Funds.* Typically at least two of three mutual funds underperform the market in a given year. The year-to-year correlation is very small, barely above zero, so successful funds are mostly lucky (pdf 209).
- *Adviser data.* Kahneman got a spreadsheet of about 25 anonymous wealth advisers over 8 years. Each year's score largely determined the adviser's bonus (pdf 209–210).
  - He computed rank correlations for each pair of years (1–2, 1–3, … 7–8), giving 28 coefficients.
  - The average was .01, which Kahneman calls effectively zero, resembling a dice contest (pdf 210).
- *Reaction.* Executives expected "not very high" correlations, but none expected zero. They showed no sign of disbelieving the finding, but Kahneman had no doubt it was quickly swept under the rug (pdf 210). The advisers' own experience of careful judgment was more compelling than an "obscure statistical fact" (pdf 210–211). The illusion is ingrained in industry culture, and facts that threaten livelihood and self-esteem are not absorbed. One executive said he had done very well for the firm. Kahneman thought: if success was mostly chance, how much credit is he due? (pdf 211).

*Intuition note:* If the same people were truly better, the rankings would be more stable from year to year. A correlation near zero is what pure luck would produce: last year's rank would tell you nothing about this year's.

**What Supports the Illusions of Skill and Validity? (pdf 211–212).**
- Cognitive illusions can be more stubborn than visual ones. After learning about Müller-Lyer you still see the illusion but change your reported belief. The army team and the financial firm did not change feelings or actions (pdf 211).
- The most potent psychological cause, Kahneman says, is that stock pickers really do exercise high-level skills (analysing data, statements, management). But this skill is not sufficient for trading, where the key question is whether information is already in the price. Traders apparently lack the skill to answer this question, yet appear ignorant of their ignorance (pdf 211).
- Their confidence is a feeling, located in System 1 (pdf 212).
- A professional culture supports the faith: people can keep an unshakable faith in any proposition when sustained by like-minded believers (pdf 212).

**The Illusions of Pundits (pdf 212–214).**
- Everything makes sense in hindsight, and we feel that what makes sense now was predictable yesterday. Understanding the past breeds overconfidence about predicting the future. Kahneman credits Taleb for the point about narratives (pdf 212).
- History and luck: there was a fifty-fifty chance that the embryo that became Hitler was female. Compounding three such events (Hitler, Stalin, Mao) gives a probability of one-eighth of a twentieth century without any of them. Kahneman says it is impossible to argue that history would have been roughly the same without them (pdf 212).
- *Tetlock's study* (twenty years, book published 2005) (pdf 213):
  - 284 people who made a living commenting on political and economic trends gave more than 80,000 predictions.
  - For each case they rated three outcomes: status quo, more of something, or less (pdf 213).
  - The experts performed worse than if they had assigned equal probabilities to the three outcomes. In their best region, they were not significantly better than nonspecialists (pdf 213).
  - More knowledge brings only very slightly better forecasts, and those with the most knowledge are often less reliable, because knowledge enhances the illusion of skill. The more famous the forecaster, the more flamboyant the forecasts (pdf 213–214).
  - Experts resisted admitting error and offered excuses (wrong only in timing, an unforeseeable event, right for the wrong reasons) (pdf 214).
- *Hedgehogs vs foxes* (Tetlock, using Berlin's terms) (pdf 214):
  - Hedgehogs "know one big thing", have one theory, are confident and reluctant to admit error. Television likes them.
  - Foxes see many interacting forces, including blind luck. They scored best, though "still very poor". They are less often invited to TV debates.

*Intuition note:* Knowing more makes your story richer and your confidence higher, but not your accuracy. The fox admits the world is messy, which suits an unpredictable world.

**It Is Not the Experts' Fault—The World Is Difficult (pdf 214–215).**
- *Lessons.* Prediction errors are inevitable because the world is unpredictable. High subjective confidence is not to be trusted as an indicator of accuracy (low confidence could be more informative) (pdf 214).
- *What can be predicted.* Short-term trends can be forecast. Behaviour can be predicted with fair accuracy from previous behaviour. But officer-training and combat performance should not be expected to follow from obstacle-field behaviour, since both depend on situation-specific factors (pdf 214–215).
- *Limits.* A test that predicts an important outcome with validity .20 or .30 should be used, but expect no more. Expect little or nothing from stock pickers trying to beat the market. Expect not much from long-term pundit forecasts, though pundits may have insight into the near future. The line between predictable and unpredictable future is "yet to be drawn" (pdf 215).

**Speaking of Illusory Skill (pdf 215).** Five sample remarks apply the chapter's terms. One of them says that the question is whether the experts' world is predictable, not whether they are well trained (pdf 215).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four advisers A–D are ranked in two years (1 = best).
- Year 1: A1, B2, C3, D4.
- Year 2 (case I): A3, B1, C4, D2.
- Rank differences $d$: A −2, B 1, C −1, D 2. $\sum d^2 = 4+1+1+4 = 10$.
- Spearman rank correlation: $r = 1 - \frac{6\sum d^2}{n(n^2-1)} = 1 - \frac{60}{4\cdot 15} = 1 - 1 = 0$. Rankings look like luck.
- Year 2 (case II): A1, B3, C2, D4. $d$: 0, −1, 1, 0, so $\sum d^2 = 2$ and $r = 1 - \frac{12}{60} = 0.8$. This ranking is stable, which suggests skill.
- Kahneman's data were like case I: 8 years give $\binom{8}{2}=28$ year pairs (added step) with average correlation .01 (pdf 210). Spearman's formula is our choice; the text says only "correlation coefficients".

## Concepts
- [[illusion-of-validity]] (new) — feeling sure of specific predictions even when you know such predictions are barely better than guesses (pdf 206)
- [[illusion-of-skill]] (new) — belief in skill where outcomes are mostly luck, e.g. stock picking (pdf 207, 210)
- [[confidence-by-coherence]] (new) — confidence tracks the coherence of the story, not the quality or quantity of evidence (pdf 204)
- [[wysiati]] (new) — only the evidence at hand counts; ignorance is not represented (pdf 204, 206)
- [[base-rate-neglect]] (new) — general statistics are often not applied to the individual case (pdf 206–207, 210)

- [[hindsight-illusion]] (new) — the past seems explainable, so the future seems predictable (pdf 212)

## Questions this raises
- How can a team tell, before the data arrive, whether its domain allows skill at all?
- If confidence is uninformative, what would a better signal of forecast quality be (the text suggests in passing that low confidence could be more informative)?
- Where is the line between the "possibly predictable" near future and the unpredictable distant future?
- How can an organisation absorb statistical evidence that threatens its members' self-esteem and livelihood?
