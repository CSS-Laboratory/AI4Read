# The Illusion of Understanding

## L0 — One line
Tidy stories about the past make us feel we understand it, but they hide luck and the events that did not happen, so we overrate how predictable the world is.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do stories of the past feel like understanding? Kahneman argues that coherent narratives are easier to build from the little we know. They downplay luck, ignore the events that did not happen, and make outcomes look inevitable. Once we know an outcome, we misremember our earlier beliefs (hindsight bias) and judge decisions by their results (outcome bias). The halo effect turns cause and effect around in business stories. Because luck is large, success stories teach less than they seem to, and gaps between firms shrink by regression to the mean. This matters for judging decisions fairly and for distrusting tidy recipes for success.

**Prerequisites:** "correlation" as the share of factors two measures have in common; regression to the mean, WYSIATI (what you see is all there is), System 1 and the halo effect, all from earlier chapters.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Compelling stories are simple and concrete. They give talent, stupidity and intentions a larger role than luck, and focus on a few striking events rather than the countless events that failed to happen. Taleb calls such flawed stories of the past a narrative fallacy (pdf 195).
- A good explanation should have made the event predictable in advance. No story of Google's unlikely success meets this test, because no story can include the myriad of events that would have changed the outcome (the mind does not deal well with non-events). So luck mattered more in the event than in the telling, and the more luck, the less to learn (pdf 195–196).
- WYSIATI: coherent stories are easier to build from little information, and our comforting conviction that the world makes sense rests on our ability to ignore our ignorance (pdf 196).
- "Knew" is misused for the 2008 crisis, because people could not show then that it would happen, and Kahneman infers it was not knowable. The word implies the world is more knowable than it is. "Intuition" and "premonition" are likewise used only for past thoughts that proved true (pdf 196–197).
- Hindsight bias: people reconstruct earlier beliefs poorly, so they tend to overstate what they had predicted. Outcome bias follows: decisions are judged by results (pdf 197–198).
- Hindsight and outcome bias generally foster caution in decision makers who expect scrutiny. They can also give lucky reckless gamblers a halo of foresight (pdf 199).
- A generous CEO–firm correlation of about .30 gives the better CEO's firm the edge in only about 60% of pairs. We tend to expect more (pdf 200–201).
- Halo effect plus outcome bias make business-success books appealing. Successful firms' lead shrinks later because of regression to the mean (pdf 201–202).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the narrative fallacy (pdf 195–196)
- **Taleb's notion.** Flawed stories of the past shape our views of the world and our expectations for the future. Kahneman says narrative fallacies arise inevitably from our continuous attempt to make sense of the world (pdf 195).
- Compelling stories (author's claim) are simple and concrete. They give more role to talent, stupidity and intentions than to luck. They focus on a few striking events that happened, not on the countless events that did not (pdf 195).
- **Google example.** Two creative Stanford graduate students find a superior way to search the Internet. They get funding, and almost every choice they make turns out well. A year after founding, they were willing to sell for under $1 million, but the buyer found the price too high. Kahneman says mentioning this one lucky incident makes it easier to underestimate the many other ways luck mattered. Competitors appear blind and slow (pdf 195).
- The sense of having learned a general lesson is largely illusory. The ultimate test of an explanation is whether it would have made the event predictable in advance, and no story meets it because no story can include the myriad of events that would have caused a different outcome (pdf 195–196).
- Because the important events involve choices, we exaggerate skill and underestimate luck. Every critical decision turned out well, so the record suggests near-flawless prescience, although bad luck could have disrupted any step. The halo effect adds an aura of invincibility (pdf 196).
- **Rafter contrast.** A skilled rafter has run rapids hundreds of times and has learned to read the water. Few people get chances to learn to build a giant company. So luck played a more important role in the actual event than in the telling, and the more luck, the less there is to be learned (pdf 196).
- **WYSIATI.** We treat the limited information we have as all there is. It is paradoxically easier to build a coherent story when we know little, because fewer pieces must fit (pdf 196).

*Intuition note:* A story only contains what happened, but the outcome also depended on many things that did not happen. The mind handles such non-events badly, so a story leaves them out.

### The word "knew" (pdf 196–197)
- People say they "knew well before it happened that the 2008 financial crisis was inevitable." Kahneman objects to *knew*. In everyday language we use it only for what is true and can be shown true (pdf 196).
- Some people thought a crisis would come, but they could not conclusively show it at the time. Many intelligent, well-informed people did not expect a catastrophe, and Kahneman infers that the crisis was not knowable (pdf 196–197).
- The harm is not undeserved credit but the implication that the world is more knowable than it is (pdf 197).
- Core of the illusion: we believe we understand the past, which implies the future should be knowable. In fact we understand the past less than we believe. *Intuition* and *premonition* are likewise used only for past thoughts that proved true (pdf 197).

### The Social Costs of Hindsight (pdf 197–199)
- After a surprise (e.g., a one-sided football game between evenly matched teams), we revise our model of the world and our view of the past (pdf 197).
- The mind cannot reconstruct earlier beliefs well once they have changed. In attitude-change studies (e.g., on the death penalty), participants asked to recall their earlier opinion retrieve their current one instead. This is substitution (pdf 197–198).
- **Fischhoff and Beyth (before Nixon's 1972 trips to China and Russia).** Respondents assigned probabilities to fifteen possible outcomes. After the trips, they were asked to recall those probabilities. Events that occurred were recalled as having been given higher probability. Events that did not occur were recalled as always unlikely. People also overstated the accuracy of others' predictions. Similar results appeared for the O. J. Simpson trial and the Clinton impeachment (pdf 198).
- **Outcome bias.** Observers judge a decision by whether the outcome was good or bad, not by whether the process was sound. In a low-risk surgery with an unpredictable fatal accident, a jury will be prone to think the operation was risky (pdf 198).
- Agents (doctors, advisers, CEOs, diplomats) tend to be blamed for good decisions that turned out badly and under-credited for moves that look obvious only afterwards (pdf 198–199).
- **Duluth flood-monitor study.** Without hindsight information, 24% said the city should hire a full-time bridge monitor. Told that debris had caused a flood, 56% said it should have, although they were told not to let hindsight distort their judgment (pdf 199).
- The worse the consequence, the greater the hindsight bias. On 9/11, Bradlee said it seemed "elementary" to go straight to the president. Kahneman replies that on July 10, 2001 no one knew or could have known the tip would dominate history (pdf 199).
- Decision makers who expect hindsight scrutiny drift to bureaucratic procedure and reluctance to take risks. After malpractice suits grew, physicians ordered more tests, referred more cases and used conventional treatments. This protected them more than patients. Accountability is "a mixed blessing" (pdf 199).
- Hindsight also gives undeserved rewards to lucky reckless risk-takers, who are seen as having flair and foresight (pdf 199).

*Intuition note:* Once we know how things ended, "it was obvious" feels true, and we forget what we believed earlier. So we are prone to blame people whose good decisions turned out badly and to credit lucky gamblers with foresight.

### Recipes for Success (pdf 200–202)
- System 1 makes the world look tidier and more predictable than it is. This feeds the illusion that we can predict and control the future, and many business books serve this need (pdf 200).
- CEOs do influence firm outcomes, as systematic research shows, but the effects are much smaller than the business press suggests (pdf 200).
- **Correlation** is defined here as the extent to which two measures are determined by shared factors, ranging from 0 to 1. A "very generous" estimate for CEO quality and firm success is .30, meaning 30% overlap (pdf 200).
- **Pairs question.** Take many pairs of similar firms, one with a stronger CEO. Perfect correlation (1) would put the stronger CEO's firm ahead in 100% of pairs. If CEOs had no influence, it would be 50%. A correlation of .30 gives the stronger CEO's firm the lead in about 60% of pairs, a gain of 10 points over guessing (pdf 200–201).
- Expecting more than that signals overestimating the world's predictability. Still, improving the odds from 1:1 to 3:2 is a very significant advantage. From most business writers' view, a CEO with so little control would not be impressive, and it is hard to imagine crowds at airport bookstores buying a book about leaders who do only somewhat better than chance (pdf 201).
- **Rosenzweig's *The Halo Effect*:** success and failure stories consistently exaggerate the impact of leadership style and management practices, so their message is rarely useful (pdf 201).
- **Halo reversal.** Experts who know a company is thriving are likely to call its CEO flexible, methodical and decisive. A year later, if things go sour, the same executive is described as confused, rigid and authoritarian. We get causation backward: the CEO seems rigid because the firm is failing (pdf 201).
- **Built to Last** (Collins and Porras) analyses eighteen pairs of competing companies. Kahneman reads the basic message of this and similar books as: good practices can be identified, and good practices will be rewarded by good results. He says both messages are overstated (pdf 202).
- Comparing more and less successful firms is to a significant extent comparing luckier and less lucky firms. In the presence of randomness, regular patterns "can only be mirages" (pdf 202).
- Because luck plays a large role, the quality of leadership and management practices cannot be inferred reliably from observations of success (pdf 202).
- Even with perfect foreknowledge of a brilliant CEO, one still could not predict firm performance much better than a coin flip (pdf 202).
- **Later results (as reported).** The gap between the *Built to Last* outstanding firms and others shrank to almost nothing afterwards. The *In Search of Excellence* firms' profitability dropped sharply. In a twenty-year study of *Fortune*'s "Most Admired Companies," the worst-rated firms earned much higher stock returns than the most admired (pdf 202).
- Kahneman's explanation: the average gap must shrink because it was due in good part to luck. This is regression to the mean, not complacency of winners or extra effort by losers (pdf 202).
- These stories offer simple triumph-and-failure messages and ignore luck and regression, which maintains the illusion of understanding (pdf 202–203).

*Intuition note:* Part of a firm's lead over others is luck, so on average the lead shrinks later. A clean "secret of success" pattern in lucky winners is therefore suspect.

### Speaking of Hindsight (pdf 203)
Four sample sentences for use in conversation: "just hindsight"; learning too much from a "too tidy" success story is falling for a narrative fallacy; judging a firm as badly managed only because its stock fell is outcome bias, part hindsight and part halo; a stupid decision stays stupid even if it worked (pdf 203).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 100 firms with identical skill. Each year, a fair coin gives each firm luck of +1 (heads) or −1 (tails), and nothing else matters.
- Year 1: about 50 firms get +1. Writers call them "visionary".
- Year 2: of those 50, about 25 get +1 and 25 get −1. Their average is (25·(+1) + 25·(−1)) / 50 = 0.
- The year-1 gap (+1 for winners against −1 for the rest, a difference of 2) has fallen to 0 for the same winners. This extreme case has no skill at all, but it shows why gaps shrink with no change in management.

## Questions this raises

- How could a decision be evaluated fairly using only the information available when it was made?
- Can people reduce hindsight bias, for example by recording predictions with probabilities before outcomes are known? (The text only says people fail to recall them.)
