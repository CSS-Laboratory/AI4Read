# Apperly, I. A., & Butterfill, S. A. (2009). Do humans have two systems to track beliefs and belief-like states? *Psychological Review*, 116(4), 953–970. doi:10.1037/a0016923

## In one paragraph
Infants seem to pass false-belief tasks at 13–15 months, yet children fail standard tasks until about 4. Adults find belief reasoning effortful, but social life seems to need fast belief tracking. The authors resolve this with an analogy to number cognition. Humans have an efficient but inflexible system that tracks "belief-like states" (which the authors call registrations). It starts in infancy, may have animal analogues, and persists in adults. A second system is flexible but costly and develops later. It reasons about beliefs as such. The two systems can be told apart by "signature limits", which are arbitrary, fixed restrictions. For registrations these include no quantified contents and no level-2 perspective-taking. The account explains why quick social cognition need not be real belief reasoning.

## The path through the work
- [[apperly-2009-two-systems/main-p1]] — Borrowing from number cognition, the authors propose that infant and animal success on belief tasks may be best explained by an efficient but inflexible system, which in humans persists alongside a flexible but demanding one, and that "signature limits" can tell which system is at work.
- [[apperly-2009-two-systems/main-p2]] — Because reasoning about beliefs as such is cognitively costly, quick social cognition in infants, animals and adults may, the authors propose, rely on a limited, efficient system that tracks "belief-like states", which exists alongside a flexible but costly belief-reasoning system.
- [[apperly-2009-two-systems/main-p3]] — This part holds no main argument; it contains the reference list, the author note and nine footnotes (numbered 1–7, 9 and 10). The footnotes explain the false-belief task and qualify the authors' claims about infants, children and number.

## Big ideas across units
- **The number-cognition analogy.** Efficient infant systems with fixed limits persist beside flexible, language-dependent counting. The authors propose the same split for beliefs. Units: main-p1, main-p2; footnotes 5 and 10 in main-p3.
- **Signature limits as the diagnostic.** Restrictions that general resources (executive function, language, intelligence) cannot explain indicate an efficient system, and their nature suggests how the efficiency is achieved. Units: main-p1, main-p2.
- **The infant-vs-4-year-old puzzle and why both standard camps fail.** Neither "infants understand belief" nor "infants use behaviour rules" is secured by parsimony. Units: main-p1; footnotes 3, 4 and 9 in main-p3.
- **Belief reasoning as such is costly.** Reason-giving explanations are causally complex, abductive, normative and propositional. Adult belief reasoning depends on executive function and working memory. Units: main-p1, main-p2.
- **Efficient routes to tracking beliefs.** Automatization, behaviour rules or associations, and registrations are the options. Registrations are non-propositional proxies for belief, true or false, and imply specific limits. Units: main-p2 (main-p1 introduces belief-like states).
- **Converging evidence from animals and adults.** Chimpanzees and scrub-jays behave in socially sophisticated ways, but chimpanzees fail direct false-belief tests. Preliminary adult findings hint at fast perspective computation. Units: main-p1, main-p2.
- **Limits on content and psychological role.** Infant studies so far need only simple contents, such as object locations, and no level-2 perspective-taking. Units: main-p1, main-p2.
- **The two systems are not fully continuous.** The early system is not an "implicit homologue" of the later one, and the account differs from modular (Leslie's ToMM-SP) and other two-system views. Units: main-p1, main-p2.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** main-p1 and main-p2. main-p1 builds the analogy and the case for two systems. main-p2 depends on it, since it uses the signature-limits logic and the infant evidence from main-p1. It then gives the full account and compares it with existing theories.
- **Skippable on a first pass:** main-p3. It has no argument. Read footnote 2 if you need the false-belief task explained. Footnotes 4 and 9 qualify the infant claims.
- **Order:** read the units in order. The prerequisites are the false-belief task, looking-time methods, and executive function and working memory. Level-1 vs level-2 perspective-taking matters for main-p2.
