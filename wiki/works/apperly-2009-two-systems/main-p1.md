# Do humans have two systems to track beliefs and belief-like states? (part 1)

## L0 — One line
Borrowing from number cognition, the authors propose that belief reasoning uses an efficient but inflexible system (found in infants and animals) alongside a flexible but demanding one, and that "signature limits" can tell which system is at work.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Infants apparently pass false-belief tasks at 13–15 months, yet children fail standard tasks until about 4. Chimpanzees fail direct tests but behave in socially complex ways, and adults find belief reasoning effortful. The authors argue that neither "infants understand belief" nor "infants just use behaviour rules" is likely to be fully right. In number cognition, infants, adults and animals share an efficient system with arbitrary, fixed restrictions (e.g. a cap of about 3 items), called "signature limits", alongside flexible, language-dependent counting. The authors propose the same split for beliefs and say infants' belief tracking may show analogous limits in content and psychological role. This part builds the analogy and the prima facie case.

**Prerequisites:** the false-belief task; looking-time methods with infants; basic idea of a cognitive "system" with limited capacity.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Theory of mind faces competing demands: it must be fast and cheap for live interaction, and, in human adults, as flexible as any reasoning for explicit explanation (p. 3).
- The field is split between modular accounts (fast, fixed) and general-reasoning accounts (flexible, effortful). The authors say both have evidence and neither is likely fully correct (p. 4).
- Infant success (13–15 months) conflicts with failure until about 4 years. The "infants understand belief" camp and the "behaviour rules" camp both claim parsimony favours them, which suggests parsimony will not be decisive (pp. 5–7).
- In number cognition, infants, adults and animals share efficient systems with signature limits (about 3 items; ratio limits), while educated numerical skill is flexible and resource-hungry (pp. 8–12).
- Signature limits (arbitrary, fixed restrictions on what a system can do) are the diagnostic: they are relatively specific to the domain and not explained by number itself, language, general intelligence, information processing or executive limits. The 3–4 item limit may still reflect a specialised visual working memory (p. 11).
- For belief, the authors suggest infants may be limited in content (studies so far need only object locations, or simple features; none has yet suggested beliefs combining feature and location, beliefs needing quantifiers, or sensitivity to modes of presentation, which level-2 perspective-taking needs) or in psychological role (how beliefs are caused, how they interact with other states, and how they cause action) (pp. 16–17).
- Chimpanzees and scrub-jays show behaviour that is beneficial in virtue of what competitors see, or (the authors say, for scrub-jays) believe, but chimpanzees systematically fail direct false-belief tests and show no evidence of success on level-2 perspective-taking tasks. This is offered as converging evidence (pp. 18–19).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Abstract (p. 2)
- Four puzzles: infants apparently pass false-belief tasks at 13–15 months while children fail critical tests before 3–4 years; animals fail critical tests but show complex social behaviour; adult social fluency implies efficient belief processing, yet direct tests show belief reasoning is demanding (p. 2).
- Proposal: infant and animal success may be best explained by an efficient but inflexible capacity to track belief-like states. In humans it persists alongside later, flexible, demanding theory-of-mind abilities (p. 2).

### Introduction (pp. 3–5)
- Theory of mind = ascribing beliefs, desires, intentions to explain, predict and justify behaviour (p. 3). The authors say there is no consensus on its cognitive basis, even for belief ascription. Dominant accounts explain development or animal abilities, and say little about how everyday inferences are achieved (p. 3).
- Central contention: theory of mind is under competing demands. It must be fast and cheap enough for competition and cooperation, and flexible enough for jurisprudence, negotiation and self-awareness (p. 3).
- This is reflected in an old disagreement. **Modular view (e.g. Leslie):** belief ascription depends on modules that are fast, with structures fixed before or during infancy. **Alternative (e.g. Gopnik & Meltzoff):** it depends on flexible, effortful general reasoning plus knowledge learned in early childhood about beliefs (p. 4).
- **Authors:** both views have significant evidence and neither is likely fully correct. Following number cognition, the competing demands are solved by two systems (p. 4).
- Others have mentioned a two-system idea in general terms (Byrne, Csibra & Gergely, Leslie, Perner and others), but not developed it for belief reasoning (p. 4). The paper will examine the demands of belief reasoning, develop the hypothesis, evaluate it against infant, adult and animal evidence, and say what evidence could distinguish alternative two-system versions (pp. 4–5).

### The development of belief reasoning (pp. 5–7)
- Development is protracted, with language for mental states apparently playing a critical role. Standard false-belief tasks are passed around 4 years. Recognising that beliefs represent only a subset of their referents' features comes at 5–6 years (p. 5).
- Earlier hints: 3-year-olds fail to say where Sam will look for cheese moved while he slept, but look at the location where he believes it is when prompted ("I wonder where he's going to look") (pp. 5–6). Southgate et al. found similar looking in 2-year-olds with a non-verbal version (p. 6).
- **Onishi & Baillargeon (2005)**, violation-of-expectation (infants look longer at surprising events): a watermelon slice goes into a green box while the actor watches. The actor's view is blocked and the slice moves to a yellow box. The actor reappears and reaches. Infants looked longer when she reached into yellow than into green. The pattern was reversed when she had watched the move (p. 6). Related results were found at 13 months (Surian et al.) (p. 6).
- Two responses (p. 6–7). **Camp 1 (Onishi & Baillargeon; Leslie):** infants understand false belief, so there is no fundamental change at 4, and infant understanding is the "conceptual foundation" for later abilities. **Camp 2 (Perner & Ruffman):** infants need no understanding of belief; looking times could reflect rules such as "people look for objects where they last set eyes on them".
- Both sides claim parsimony favours them, which, the **authors** say, suggests parsimony will not be decisive. They think both sides are mistaken and propose widening the view to infants' limits, animals and adults, starting from number cognition (p. 7).

> **Intuition note.** The behaviour-rule camp holds that passing a task need not show what produced the pass. A system that tracks "where the person last saw it" and a system that tracks "what she believes" could, on that camp's view, give the same looking pattern on this task.

### Number cognition (pp. 7–12)
- Counting with a conventional symbol system plays a critical role in the protracted development of number reasoning (pp. 7–8).
- **Piaget:** infants were thought entirely incapable, and even counting children seemed to lack conservation of number. Improved methods showed success in younger children, but before the 1980s there was little evidence of numerical ability before 2–3 years (p. 8).
- Preferential-looking and habituation studies then showed precocious sensitivity (5–8 months is given for the first example): number of items, summing over short sequences, small versus large sets, tracking small sets after additions or subtractions (p. 8). So "entirely insensitive" is incorrect (p. 8).
- But infants also have stark limits (pp. 8–9):
  - Precise capacity limit of three items (four in one study). At 14 months, infants search about once for one item, twice for two, three times for three, but tend to search only once after seeing four (Feigenson & Carey) (p. 9).
  - Large numbers: 6-month-olds discriminate 8 vs 16 dots only when the ratio is large (e.g. 1:2), and perhaps only if both sets are above 3 (p. 9).
- Converging evidence (p. 9–10):
  - Other species show precise processing of very small numbers and approximate processing of large ones, with analogous limits. This suggests infant abilities are not distinctively human (p. 9).
  - Educated adults, when judging fast or when counting is disrupted, show the same 3–4 limit and ratio-limited comparison. The ratio threshold shrinks with age (pp. 9–10).
- **Disagreements** (pp. 10–11): (a) one analogue system versus separate precise and approximate mechanisms; (b) if separate, whether the precise one is shared with parallel individuation, visual working memory or object files; (c) how infant abilities relate to counting. Gelman & Gallistel say counting maps existing analogue number concepts into language. Others say counting principles must be learned, with conceptual development (pp. 10–11).
- **Four points of agreement used for the analogy** (pp. 11–12):
  1. Infants have one or more number systems, which persist in adults and have analogues in animals (p. 11).
  2. Their efficiency comes with distinctive limits. These are relatively specific to number and not explained by number per se, language, intelligence, information processing or executive capacity (the 3–4 limit may reflect a specialised visual working memory) (p. 11).
  3. These "signature limits" are key evidence for distinct systems and their similarity across infants, adults and animals (pp. 11–12).
  4. Infants and animals should not be said to understand number as educated people do. The limits are overcome only when children acquire a conventional number system, which takes years and relies on general resources (p. 12).
- **Model for belief:** efficiency (from one or more subsystems) plus flexibility (from demanding reasoning). Which system is in use is told by whether performance shows signature limits (p. 12).
- **Systems and concepts** (pp. 12–13): whether infants represent numbers as such is left open, and the analogy does not depend on it. The proposal has two parts. Part 1: two types of system for belief reasoning, one efficient, limited and inflexible, one flexible but demanding. Here "belief reasoning" just means dealing with tasks where belief matters, with no claim that beliefs are represented as such. Part 2 (later): how efficient belief reasoning works, which the authors will argue involves "belief-like" states. These resemble beliefs in guiding action but lack propositional content and differ in further respects (p. 13).

> **Intuition note.** The key idea from number is a method, not a conclusion about content. Arbitrary limits that do not track language or executive load are taken as key evidence for a separate, cheap system.

### What would show that there are two systems for processing beliefs? (pp. 14–19)

**Prima facie case (p. 14).** Adult belief reasoning can use all cognitively available facts to ascribe any belief the subject can entertain. Seen this way it is a "central process" and should be demanding of general resources. Yet it also guides fast activity such as competition and communication. The same tension, and the same two-type solution (efficient/inflexible plus flexible/demanding), appears in general reasoning, social cognition and number. The authors say it is "at least reasonable" to propose the same for beliefs (p. 14).

**Infants and children (pp. 14–18).**
- Adult-like belief reasoning develops over several years. It is tied to language and executive function, may be helped by training and siblings, and is altered to some degree by culture. The authors say these are exactly the traits expected of flexible, demanding reasoning (pp. 14–15).
- Infant success at 13 months therefore looks remarkable. Two readings (p. 15):
  - **Leslie; Surian et al.:** language and executive dependence is an experimental artefact, and infant tasks strip it away. Infant ability would then be limited only by experimenters' ingenuity and by the concepts infants can apply.
  - **Authors' alternative, from number:** later-developing abilities depend on language or executive function by their nature, while infants' abilities rest on a distinct, efficient set of processes. If so, the efficiency is likely bought with limited flexibility.
- The test is to look for arbitrary signature limits. A belief is an attitude to a content that plays a psychological role. In explicit adult reasoning the content is propositional (sentence-like). The role includes being caused and justified by perception, interacting with other beliefs and desires, and causing and justifying action. Infants may be limited in content, role, or both (p. 16).
- **Content.** Infant experiments so far only require tracking attitudes to object locations. Some may require attitudes to objects with features such as shape or colour. No study has yet suggested that infants track beliefs combining features and location ("The red ball is in the cupboard"), beliefs needing quantifiers ("There is no red ball in the cupboard"), or sensitivity to modes of presentation (needed for level-2 perspective-taking). If such limits were shown, the **authors** say, we could conclude the state has no propositional content (p. 16).
- **Role.** Infants and young children show some correct expectations about the causes of belief: they do not expect belief when a person is disengaged (e.g. facing a wall), merely from standing on an object, or from close proximity alone; some purposive interaction appears to be required (pp. 16–17). But children may struggle with level-1 perspective-taking before 24 months (disputed), and little is known about infants' grasp of inference or testimony as causes of belief (p. 17). Possible limits: infants may treat beliefs as independent of each other, like perceptions, and may treat beliefs as fixing parameters of basic object-directed actions (where someone reaches or walks) rather than interacting with desires. If shown, the state would lack the causal and justificatory structure of adult belief reasoning (p. 17).
- **Authors' conclusion:** the evidence falls well short of establishing several criteria for saying infants ascribe beliefs as such. They do not downplay infant findings and think current evidence probably underestimates infants' sophistication. Their contention is that infant belief reasoning may be subject to arbitrary limits, analogous to the 3-item and ratio limits, in content and psychological role (pp. 17–18).

> **Intuition note.** The proposal is testable. If infants were shown to be unable to track beliefs that combine a feature and a location, that need a quantifier, or that involve modes of presentation, or to treat beliefs as interacting with desires, that would be the kind of arbitrary limit the authors look for (pp. 16–17).

**Non-human animals (pp. 18–19; the section continues in the next part).**
- Animals, like infants, lack the linguistic, symbolic or executive capacities that seem needed for flexible number and belief reasoning. Several species show infant-like precise and approximate number abilities but not educated humans' flexibility, which supports parallel number systems (p. 18).
- **Chimpanzees** (most extensively studied). Despite controversy, the **authors** see consensus on two boundaries (pp. 18–19):
  1. They systematically fail direct false-belief tests, and there is no evidence of success at level-2 perspective-taking (p. 18).
  2. They do have some abilities: they follow gaze behind objects and around opaque barriers much as 18-month-olds do. They adapt food retrieval to what a dominant competitor can see or has seen, and can manipulate whether a competitor sees them (p. 19). Whether or not they represent perceptions or beliefs, their behaviour is beneficial by controlling what others see and believe (p. 19).
- **Scrub-jays** (p. 19). With a competitor present they cache far, dark and out of view, and re-cache more than when alone. These behaviours are absent for non-food items or with a partner. They prefer to recover, in private, caches a competitor observed. In company they prefer caches made in the presence of that same competitor. The **authors** regard these as belief reasoning, because they benefit the agent in virtue of facts about competitors' beliefs. They know of no evidence on the limits of scrub-jay belief reasoning (p. 19; the sentence continues into the next part).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show how a signature limit identifies a system. Imagine a hypothetical efficient tracker with two limits: it holds a belief only as "agent last saw object at location L", and it fails for any belief that needs a negation. We test three beliefs.

| Task belief | Needs only "last seen at L"? | Efficient tracker predicts |
|---|---|---|
| "The ball is in the green box" | yes | pass |
| "The red ball is in the cupboard" (feature + location) | no, feature added | fail |
| "There is no red ball in the cupboard" | no, quantifier | fail |

Score: 1 pass, 2 fails. A flexible reasoner with enough language and executive resources would pass all 3. A participant who passes only the first, and whose pattern does not change with extra memory load, shows a signature-limit profile. This parallels the number case. A 3-item tracker is right on 2 objects (search twice) and wrong on 4 (search once). The pattern of errors, not just the passes, reveals the system.

## Concepts
- [[two-systems-account]] — an efficient, limited system and a flexible, costly system; here argued by analogy with number (p. 4)
- [[signature-limits]] (new) — arbitrary, domain-specific limits (e.g. 3 items, ratio) used to identify an efficient system (p. 11)
- [[belief-like-state]] (new) — state that guides action like belief but lacks propositional content and more; this part only introduces the term (p. 13)
- [[theory-of-mind]] — ascribing beliefs, desires and intentions to explain, predict and justify behaviour (p. 3)
- [[modularity-tomm]] — belief ascription by modules, fast, structures fixed in infancy (Leslie's view as reported) (p. 4)
- [[theory-theory]] — roughly Gopnik & Meltzoff's alternative as described: general reasoning plus learned knowledge about beliefs (p. 4)
- [[violation-of-expectation-paradigm]] — looking longer at events that contradict expectations (p. 6)
- [[first-order-false-belief]] — a standard benchmark, passed around 4 years (p. 5)
- [[implicit-vs-explicit-tom]] — roughly the infant/animal versus adult contrast the paper builds on (pp. 2, 15)
- [[perspective-taking-level-1-level-2]] — level-2 needs sensitivity to modes of presentation (p. 16)
- [[propositional-attitude]] — a belief is an attitude to a content; adults' content is propositional (p. 16)

## Canonical instances
- [[onishi-baillargeon-watermelon-task]] — infants look longer when the actor acts against her belief (p. 6)
- [[garnham-cheese-task]] — 3-year-olds fail to say where Sam will look but look correctly when prompted (pp. 5–6)
- [[feigenson-carey-box-search]] — 14-month-olds search about as many times as items, up to three, but only once for four (p. 9)
- [[scrub-jay-caching]] — caching and recovering depend on competitors' observation (p. 19)
- [[chimp-competitor-visibility]] — chimpanzees adjust to what a rival sees or has seen, but fail false-belief tests (pp. 18–19)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] infant number system (3-item and ratio limits) ↔ belief tracking: efficient system with signature limits
- [structural-analogy] two-system proposal ↔ general reasoning and social cognition: efficient/inflexible versus flexible/demanding
- [metaphor] "central process" belief reasoning ↔ Fodor-style general cognition: flexible but resource-demanding

## Questions this raises
- What exactly would count as a "signature limit" in belief tracking, and how would it be tested in infants?
- If infants' states lack propositional content, what do they represent? (the authors promise an answer later)
- Do scrub-jays or chimpanzees show the same limits as infants?
- Do adults under time pressure show belief tracking with the same limits as infants?
