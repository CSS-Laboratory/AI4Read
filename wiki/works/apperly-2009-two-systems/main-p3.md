# Two systems for beliefs and belief-like states — back matter (references, author note, footnotes)

## L0 — One line
This part holds no main argument; it contains the reference list, the author note and nine footnotes (numbered 1–7, 9 and 10). The footnotes explain the false-belief task and qualify the authors' claims about infants, children and number.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Pages 47–70 are the back matter of Apperly & Butterfill (2009). They have a 20-page reference list (pp. 47–66), an author note (p. 67) and footnotes (pp. 68–70). The footnotes carry the content. They describe one typical false-belief task, with Sally and Anne (n. 2), note that some authors posit more than two systems (n. 1), and mention early-childhood evidence from intentional activities (n. 3). They also state an assumption about studies of early false-belief understanding (n. 4), note that 5–6-year-olds still have problems with related belief understanding (n. 6), and raise a puzzle about early success on location-transfer tasks (n. 9). The two number-related footnotes (nn. 5, 10) add remarks on number abilities.

**Prerequisites:** what a false-belief task is (explained in footnote 2); the paper's earlier sections, which are not in this part.

### Footnotes (pp. 68–70)
The attached note markers are in the main text, which is not in this part. Each footnote is therefore reported as it stands.

**Fn 1 (p. 68).**
- "Some of these authors" discuss more than two systems.
- The authors agree it is plausible that more than one system for fast, efficient theory-of-mind processing may exist.
- For clarity they refer throughout to the "two systems" view.
- Which authors are meant is not stated in these pages.

**Fn 2 (p. 68). One typical false-belief task.**
1. Sally puts her marble in a basket and goes outside to play.
2. In her absence, Anne moves the marble from the basket to a box. Sally now has a false belief about the marble's location.
3. Children are asked where Sally thinks the marble is, or where she will first look. Answering requires inferring Sally's false belief (Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith, 1985).

**Intuition note:** The test questions require the child to infer Sally's false belief, so the answer concerns where Sally thinks the marble is, not where it really is.

**Fn 3 (p. 68).**
- There is "modest evidence" that 2- and 3-year-olds use information about false belief in intentional activities.
- Cited examples are word learning (Carpenter, Call & Tomasello, 2002; Happé & Loth, 2002) and deception-related behaviours (Polak & Harris, 1999; Newton, Reddy & Bull, 2000; Reddy & Morris, 2004).
- The authors say it is possible that these findings should be interpreted in the light of tasks where children of this age intelligently exploit facts about which objects individuals are acquainted with. The examples are interpreting requests (Moll & Tomasello, 2007), their own communicative gestures (Liszkowski et al., 2006) and use of testimony (Koenig et al., 2004).
- How this evidence relates to the evidence from eye movements and looking times is an "open question".

**Fn 4 (p. 69).**
- The "aforementioned studies" (mentioned in the main text, not in this part) all show precocious false-belief understanding at very different ages.
- The authors assume this variation reflects differences in the sensitivity of methods, not differences in the cognitive processes tested.

**Fn 5 (p. 69).**
- Somewhat flexible number abilities have been shown in chimpanzees (e.g. Boysen & Berntson, 1989) and an African grey parrot (e.g. Pepperberg, 2006).
- Both received substantial training in symbolic representation of numerical quantity.

**Fn 6 (p. 69).**
- 5- and 6-year-olds are old enough to pass false-belief tasks but still have problems understanding:
  - how beliefs are acquired (Carpendale & Chandler, 1996; Robinson & Apperly, 2001);
  - how beliefs interact with desires (Leslie, German & Polizzi, 2004; Leslie & Polizzi, 1998);
  - the emotional consequences of false beliefs (Harris et al., 1989; Ruffman & Keenan, 1996).

**Intuition note:** Passing the classic task is not the same as having full belief understanding. Several related aspects of belief understanding still cause problems at ages 5–6, according to the cited studies.

**Fn 7 (p. 69).** The authors thank Josef Perner for suggesting "this possibility". What the possibility is, is not stated in these pages.

**Fn 9 (p. 69).** The numbering skips 8.
- Understanding "registration", as explained in the main text, appears also to let one pass standard unexpected-location-transfer false-belief tasks.
- Children do pass such tasks when gaze, not verbal response, is the measure (Clements & Perner, 1994; Southgate et al., 2007).
- So this is not an objection to the authors' view but "a genuine puzzle".
- One possibility is that an early-developing system for tracking registrations guides eye movements, while a later-developing system guides explicit judgements about beliefs. They point to their section "How could the two systems be related?" (not in this part).

**Intuition note:** The footnote shows a loose end the authors admit. The early system may explain the gaze results, but they present this only as a possibility.

**Fn 10 (p. 70).**
- The study discussed (not named in this footnote) examined children, not adults, so that participants could count (and understand symbolic number stimuli) but had no formal arithmetic training. They could not solve the estimation problems with symbolic arithmetic.
- The findings are also consistent with the view that only children map symbolic numbers to non-symbolic representations. The authors think it "more likely" that adults can do this too.

## Toy example (ours, not from the source)
Footnote 2's task, run as a three-row table of what is true and what Sally believes:

| Step | Marble really in | Sally believes it is in |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Sally puts marble in basket, leaves | basket | basket |
| 2. Anne moves it while Sally is away | box | basket (she did not see the move) |
| 3. Test: "Where will Sally look first?" | box | basket |

- Answer by reality: "box". Answer by Sally's belief: "basket".
- At step 3 belief and reality differ (2 locations, 1 mismatch). Only a response that tracks Sally's belief gives "basket".
- Footnote 9 says gaze and verbal answers can come apart in children. The same table could be scored once by eye movements and once by spoken answer.

## Concepts
- [[first-order false belief]] — understanding that a person holds a belief that differs from reality; tested by the Sally task (p. 68)
- [[unexpected transfer task]] — an object is moved while the protagonist is absent; the child says where they will look (pp. 68, 69)
- [[implicit vs explicit ToM]] — here: eye-movement/looking evidence versus explicit judgements, possibly produced by two systems (nn. 3, 9; pp. 68, 69)
- [[two-systems account]] — the "two systems" view; some authors posit more than two (fn 1; p. 68)
- [[registration]] (new) — a notion used in the main text; footnote 9 says understanding it appears to suffice for location-transfer tasks. Not defined in this part (p. 69)

## Canonical instances
- [[sally-anne-marble-task]] (new) — one typical location-transfer false-belief task (p. 68)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Early gaze success versus later verbal success on false-belief tasks (fn 9) ↔ dual-process reasoning: a fast system and a slow system giving different outputs on one problem.
- [metaphor] Footnote 10's choice of children who can count but lack arithmetic training ↔ experimental design: picking a population to rule out an alternative strategy.

## Questions this raises
