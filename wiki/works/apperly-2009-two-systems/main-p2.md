# Apperly & Butterfill (2009), part 2: adults, paths to efficiency, relations between the systems, comparisons, conclusion

## L0 — One line
Because reasoning about beliefs as such is cognitively costly, quick social cognition in infants, animals and adults may, the authors propose, rely on a limited, efficient system that tracks "belief-like states", which exists alongside a flexible but costly belief-reasoning system.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part asks how belief reasoning can be both efficient and flexible. The authors argue that belief reasoning as such is costly. Fast tracking of beliefs therefore needs another route: automatization, behaviour rules or associations, or "registrations" (belief-like states, relations to objects and places). Each route has signature limits (restrictions that general resources cannot explain); for registrations these include no quantified contents and no level-2 perspective-taking. They propose two systems: an efficient, inflexible one from infancy and a flexible, costly one that develops later. Adults have both. They argue that existing modular and two-system accounts do not explain how efficiency and flexibility coexist. Number cognition is the guiding analogy.

**Prerequisites:** the false-belief task and infant looking-time evidence (from part 1); level-1 vs level-2 perspective-taking; the number-cognition analogy (an efficient infant system with limits, plus a later symbolic system); basic "signature limit" logic.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Adult belief reasoning, as directly studied, is effortful. It depends on executive function and working memory, and egocentric bias (judging others' views by one's own) is common (pp. 20–21). Communication, however, seems to need fast belief tracking (p. 23).
- Preliminary studies, mostly unpublished, raise the possibility that adults compute an avatar's visual perspective quickly and without strategic control. This would hint at an efficient system in adults (pp. 21–22).
- At least part of the cost of belief reasoning arises because reason-giving explanations (explanations citing beliefs and desires as reasons) are causally complex, abductive (inference to the best explanation), normative (showing an action to be reasonable) and propositional (ascribing contents that can involve quantification and modality). An efficient route must avoid these four features, so it cannot be reasoning about beliefs as such (pp. 25–26).
- Innate "modules" and reasoning about facts do not, by themselves, explain efficiency (pp. 26–28). Automatization, behaviour associations or rules, and registrations might (pp. 29–36).
- Registrations are non-propositional, non-psychological proxies for beliefs, true and false. They imply signature limits: no quantifiers, limited property combinations, no mode of presentation, level-1 but not level-2 perspective-taking (pp. 33–35).
- Limits ("signature limits") that a lack of general resources or content cannot explain indicate an efficient process, and the nature of the limits can indicate how efficiency is achieved (p. 36).
- On the authors' account, the two systems cannot be fully continuous. The early system could not be an "implicit homologue" of the later one (pp. 38–39).
- The account differs from Leslie's ToMM-SP and from other two-system accounts in motivation, scope and predictions (pp. 39–44).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of the infant/animal section (p. 20)
- The authors predict that scrub jays' abilities will be limited in ways analogous to chimpanzees' and infants' (p. 20).
- Controversy: do chimpanzees or scrub jays track gaze only behaviourally, or do they represent what is seen? The authors set this aside here (p. 20).
- Common ground: these animals somehow act in ways that benefit in part because of facts about beliefs. Infants and animals show similar, limited abilities despite very limited language and executive function (p. 20).

### Theory of mind in adults (pp. 20–24)
**Framing.** Adults keep infant-type number mechanisms alongside learned counting. The question is whether adults have analogous parallel systems for beliefs (p. 20). The authors "take it as established" that adults reason flexibly about beliefs (p. 20).

**Evidence that flexible belief reasoning is demanding (pp. 20–21).**
- Direct investigation found it not automatic and found a role for strategic control (Apperly et al., 2006; p. 21).
- Adults' performance on belief problems is related to processing speed and executive function (p. 21).
- It may be disrupted by a concurrent working-memory or language task, or by brain injury affecting executive function (p. 21).
- Egocentric bias in interpreting speakers and predicting others' beliefs is common, and overcoming it is demanding (p. 21).
- In these respects belief reasoning resembles late-learned symbolic number abilities: flexible but resource-limited (p. 21).

**Preliminary evidence of efficient reasoning (unpublished; pp. 21–22).** Samson, Apperly, Braithwaite & Andrews (under submission) used a level-1 perspective task with a computer avatar. The participant judged how many objects they or the avatar could see.
- Participants were slower judging the avatar's view when they themselves saw more objects (p. 22).
- Reverse effect: they were slower judging their own view when the avatar saw fewer objects than when it saw the same number (p. 22).
- In a second experiment, participants judged only their own view for a block of 52 trials. The interference was not eliminated (p. 22).
- The authors infer that the avatar's perspective was computed anyway. This raises the possibility that the process is fast, efficient and resistant to strategic control (p. 22).
- Preliminary evidence from Kovacs & Mehler (2007) and Wang et al. (under submission) suggests similar interference for recent, not current, visual experience, i.e. "belief-like" states (p. 22).
- Predicted signature limit: since neither infants nor chimpanzees appear capable of level-2 perspective-taking, adults might not automatically compute an avatar's perspective in a level-2 task (pp. 22–23).

*Intuition note:* If irrelevant information still slows you down, that hints it was computed without being asked for. The authors raise the possibility that the avatar effect reflects a fast, uncontrolled process in adults, but they call the evidence early.

**Indirect argument from communication (p. 23).**
- Communication seems to depend on tracking what others know and think, and it is quick and seemingly effortless (Grice; Sperber & Wilson; p. 23).
- This conflicts with the lab findings. Keysar, Linn & Barr (2003) found adults' interpretations of simple instructions insensitive to perspective and belief differences that were manifest (p. 23).
- The authors' conclusion: if theory of mind is needed for everyday communication, adults need a fast, efficient system. They expect it to be inflexible, so there will be signature limits on how communication can be guided (p. 23).

**Summary (pp. 23–24).**
- Positive evidence in infants and animals covers only a small set of abilities, with some similar limits across groups (p. 24).
- The authors propose that adults' efficient capacity is at least partly underwritten by processes identical or similar to infants' (and perhaps animals'). Evidence will come from signature limits (p. 24).

### Different paths to efficiency give rise to distinct signature limitations (pp. 24–36)
Several authors have proposed two-system accounts, but the critical questions have not been adequately addressed (pp. 24–25).

**Why is belief reasoning costly? (p. 25).** Example: "She reached for the salt container because she saw the white grains, and—believing them to be sugar—intended to sweeten her pie." Such reason-giving explanations (Davidson) have four features, all liable to be costly:
1. Complex causal structure: perception → belief, with desire → intention → action. Some causes are remote in time and space.
2. They are abductive: inference to the best explanation, with no principled limit on what is relevant.
3. They have a normative dimension: a true explanation shows the action to be reasonable given beliefs and desires, except in special circumstances.
4. They ascribe states with propositional content, including quantification and modality.

The authors say that if anything demands working memory, inhibition and strategic control, it is this kind of reasoning (p. 25). An efficient route must avoid all four features. It therefore cannot be reasoning about beliefs as such (p. 26).

*Intuition note:* Part of the cost lies in what a belief explanation requires: weighing many interacting causes and open-ended content. A cheap system must get its predictions without doing that.

**Accounts that do not explain efficient belief reasoning (pp. 26–28).**
- *Innate competence or "module"* (Fodor, Leslie, Baillargeon and others). These accounts are driven by the problem of acquisition. Infants' limits are attributed to limited conceptual content, general processing or executive function, which grow later (p. 26). But the same added resources that give adults flexibility also make them inefficient at belief reasoning, and they are added to the same "one-system". Innateness therefore does not in itself explain efficiency (p. 27). The authors say this is not an argument against innateness. Calling a capacity modular, tacit or automatic presupposes that the efficiency problem can be solved rather than solving it (p. 27).
- *Reasoning about factual rather than mental states* (Perner; Csibra & Gergely). Example: "James ran towards the bus because it was about to depart" (p. 27). If beliefs and desires are largely shared, this gives the same predictions in a useful range of cases (pp. 27–28). The authors accept it may sometimes happen, but see three problems:
  - It cannot handle interpersonal differences in perception or belief (p. 28).
  - It still requires abduction over multiple propositionally individuated states under normative constraints (p. 28). Identifying facts may be cheaper than identifying beliefs (p. 28).
  - It is not necessary for efficiency, because efficient infant processes already compute over unobservables, e.g. intended phonic gestures in speech perception and causal relations (p. 28).
  - Verdict: possibly a developmental stepping-stone, but not a plausible route to efficiency by itself (p. 28).

**Accounts that might explain efficient belief reasoning (pp. 29–36).**

*Automatization* (Suddendorf & Whiten; Povinelli & Giambrone) (p. 29).
- Belief reasoning is first demanding and becomes efficient through practice. The analogy is symbolic addition: adults compute 10 from "4+6" even when it interferes (p. 29).
- Predicted example: a poker expert. Alternative: in fencing, belief inferences may become direct mappings from the opponent's behaviour to one's own plans (p. 29; footnote 7 not in these pages).
- Limits: it requires a prior non-automatic belief-reasoning capacity, and it is restricted to well-practised cases. It cannot explain novel cases, nor infants and animals, unless they already have non-automatic belief reasoning (pp. 29–30).

*Behaviour associations and behaviour rules* (Baldwin & Baird; Byrne; Povinelli; Perner & Ruffman) (p. 30).
- Regularities between observed behaviours (e.g. head orienting to an object is often followed by approach) could give predictions without belief reasoning. They are learned, perhaps like transitional probabilities in syllable sequences, or captured by innate rules (p. 30).
- The assumptions are "doubly conjectural": it is unknown whether suitable regularities exist and whether subjects can detect them (p. 30).
- Supporting facts: adults can learn regularities in activity ordering and apply them to novel cases (Baldwin et al., 2008). Infants segment activity into goal-matching units (Saylor et al., 2007) (p. 31).
- The authors suggest parsing behavioural regularities probably plays a key role, analogous to parsing phonology. By the language analogy it is essential but falls as far short of belief reasoning as babbling falls short of conversation (p. 31).
- Signature limits of associations. Unless innate, they depend on learning history, so they should cover commonly observed behaviours (head turning and approach) and not conceptually similar rarer ones (head turning and moving away). They should also be equally easy for regularities that make no sense in mental-state terms (p. 31).
- Innate behaviour rules remove the learning-history limit (pp. 31–32). Another limit holds for both: they link one behaviour to another but generate no content for "what is in the head" of the agent (p. 32). Following Penn & Povinelli (2007), any interference between the subject's own perspective and the target agent's perspective content falls outside what they can explain (p. 32).
- The three preliminary studies (Kovacs & Mehler; Samson et al.; Wang et al.) are of this kind. This does not undermine behaviour rules or associations as efficient solutions to some problems. It suggests they may be insufficient for all instances of efficient theory of mind (p. 32).

*Specialised processing of belief-like states* (pp. 33–36). Similar intermediate schemes have been suggested by Gomez, O'Neil, Doherty, Whiten and Call. The authors sketch one to show its plausibility and generate predictions (p. 33).
- **Field:** a region of space centred on an individual.
- **Encountering:** a relation between an individual, an object and a location. It obtains when the object is in the individual's field. Its conditions must be specified without anything psychological, yet can approximate perceiving. For example, occluded objects are not encountered unless noisy or moving on a natural trajectory that is not entirely occluded (p. 33).
- **Registration:** like encountering, but it continues after the object leaves the field. One registers an object at a location if one encountered it there and has not since encountered it elsewhere (p. 34).
  - Registrations resemble beliefs in having correctness conditions that may fail (p. 34).
  - They connect to action in two ways. As an enabling condition, registering an object and location enables acting on it later if it has not moved. As determiner of where one directs action when acting on the object, this requires the notion of an unsuccessful action, and it allows prediction from incorrect registrations. This can pass some false-belief tasks, e.g. Onishi & Baillargeon (p. 34; footnote 9 not in these pages).
- Registrations serve as proxies for true and false beliefs in a limited range of situations (p. 34).
- **Two restrictions** for efficiency: registrations are relations to objects and properties, not propositions. They set parameters for action independently of each other and of psychological states, or interact only in codifiable ways. Beliefs, by contrast, interact as complexly as reasons do (pp. 34–35).
- **Signature limits** (p. 35):
  - No tracking of beliefs with quantifiers ("no absences, then").
  - No indefinitely complex property combinations ("perhaps large melons and yellow melons but probably not large yellow melons").
  - No distinction between what is represented and how (mode of presentation). Including the agent's location allows a weaker type of perspective.
  - Hence level-1 but not level-2 perspective-taking (e.g. whether an agent sees an object from the back or the side).
- "Belief-like states": they resemble beliefs because they keep actions in step with information no longer immediately accessible (p. 35).

*Intuition note:* A registration is like a note "object X was last met at place P" with no thoughts about it. That is enough to predict where a competitor searches, but it cannot say "there is nothing in the box".

**Testing it (pp. 35–36).**
- In number, infants' precise numerosity is limited to 3 or 4 items. The limit is arbitrary: it is not predicted by the nature of numbers, nor explained by learning environment or by immature working memory or executive control. It is a fact about the cognitive system, whether domain-specific or a limited-capacity object-attention or visual-working-memory system (pp. 35–36).
- Likewise, reasoning about registration would be limited in ways arbitrary with respect to belief and inexplicable by learning history or general processing capacity (p. 36).
- Predicted: the infant capacity would be seen in adults with the same signature limits, and similar (not necessarily identical) abilities in at least some non-human animals. Convergence across these three sources would be strong evidence for specialised systems (p. 36).

**Summary (p. 36).** The three routes (automatization, behavioural associations, belief-like states) are not mutually incompatible, and more than one may be used. Each has different signature limits. Limits unexplained by lack of general resources or conceptual content indicate an efficient process, and their nature suggests how efficiency is achieved (p. 36).

### How could the two systems be related? (pp. 36–39)
- *Number analogy.* Adults' symbolic and non-symbolic number systems are separate enough to show double dissociations after brain injury (Lemer et al., 2003). They are not isolated: Gilmore et al. (2007) found 5- and 6-year-olds judged approximate numerical ratios similarly with dots or numerals. Gilmore et al. suggest the children did this by mapping symbolic representations onto their approximate representations of quantity. Apperly and Butterfill take this to suggest that the systems can work in concert (p. 37).
- *Possible architectures.* At one extreme, there is no direct information flow between the systems. The early system might constrain fast online processes such as communication and guide young children's attention to cases where perspectives diverge. Explicit judgements would infer content anew (pp. 37–38). At the other extreme, the later system depends on the early one, or they work in concert as in number (p. 38).
- *Evidence is scarce.* The same children show implicit success (anticipatory looking, e.g. Southgate et al., 2007, at 24 months) and explicit failure (Clements & Perner, 1994). Ruffman et al. (2001) found children confident in their wrong explicit predictions, as shown by betting, while their looking was correct. This is consistent with an early system guiding eye movements and a later one guiding explicit judgements, and with no direct information flow (p. 38).
- *Predicted unlikely relations.* The efficient system cannot come for free, so it restricts its inputs and the belief-like states it ascribes. Late-developing reasoning can use all available facts to ascribe any belief the subject can entertain. So the systems cannot be fully continuous (p. 38–39; contrasted with Csibra & Gergely, Russell, Surian et al.):
  - The early system is not an "implicit" homologue of the later one.
  - It cannot supply candidate belief contents for cases beyond its powers (contra Leslie et al., 2005).
  - Many beliefs of the later system could never be mapped back into the early one (p. 39).
- The authors note future work could prove incompatible with these predictions (p. 39).

*Intuition note:* If one system is fast because it is limited, it cannot hand the other system everything the other needs. The two probably do not form one smooth ladder.

### Comparing the current account with existing theories (pp. 39–44)
**Superficial resemblance: Leslie's ToMM-SP (pp. 39–41).**
- Leslie: an innate theory of mind module (ToMM) with sub-system 1 (from 6–8 months, goal-directed action) and sub-system 2 (from about 18 months, propositional-attitude representations). Baillargeon and colleagues extend sub-system 1 to represent the agent's belief if that belief is true (pp. 39–40).
- The authors say the resemblance is superficial. Both sub-systems are parts of one efficient module, and neither resembles their flexible, resource-demanding "System 2". This work supports the case for an efficient system but does not explain how belief reasoning can be both efficient and flexible (p. 40).
- *Selection processor (SP):* inhibitory, develops later, selects the appropriate belief content from alternatives computed by ToMM. Immaturity of SP explains failure until about 4 years. Continuing need for SP explains executive function in adults (pp. 40–41).
- **Objection (Doherty, 1999):** SP must itself reason about beliefs, making ToMM redundant. The authors say this matters less than whether the account explains efficiency plus flexibility (p. 41).
- **The authors' two reasons the explanation is illusory** (p. 41):
  1. ToMM supplies multiple alternative beliefs, and knowing that someone holds one of several beliefs is generally not enough to predict behaviour. ToMM alone is insufficient for belief reasoning.
  2. Merely representing beliefs as such is costly (propositional content, normative implications). So efficient systems cannot represent beliefs as such, and there is no reason to suppose ToMM could be efficient.

**Two systems, but not for belief reasoning (pp. 42–43).**
- Tager-Flusberg & Sullivan (2000): "social cognition" (including belief reasoning) vs "social perception" (faces, body postures; no epistemic states). Not in conflict with the authors' account, and not implied by it (p. 42).
- Gergely & Csibra (2003): infants process goal-directed actions by reasoning about how a goal is best achieved given physical constraints. The account concerns intentions, not beliefs, and explicitly denies infants ascribe mental content. The authors think simple mental content, at least belief-like states, may be necessary to explain the full range of efficient belief reasoning, so their account has more explanatory power (p. 42).
- Neuroscience (Brass et al.; Liepelt et al.): the "mirror network" (e.g. inferior frontal gyrus, active in doing and observing actions) vs the "mentalizing network" (medial prefrontal cortex, superior temporal sulcus, temporo-parietal junction). These claims concern action processing, not beliefs or belief-like states (p. 43).

**Underspecified two-system accounts (pp. 43–44).** Call & Tomasello, Doherty, Gomez, O'Neil, Penn et al., Perner and Whiten are cited. The authors claim three advantages (pp. 43–44):
- *Motivation:* they explain how the abilities can be cognitively efficient, not merely describe them.
- *Scope:* efficient processing of belief-like states is needed in adults too, not just infants and animals. The authors hypothesise that adults meet this need with a capacity present since infancy and perhaps shared with some animals (pp. 43–44).
- *Predictions:* efficiency costs distinctive limits on the complexity of belief-like states. Testing signature limits can show whether similar systems are used across infants, adults and animals, even with different tasks (p. 44).

### Conclusion (pp. 44–46)
- Three puzzles: infants pass false-belief tasks yet children first understand false belief at about four. Chimpanzees show complex social behaviour but contradictory lab success and failure on perspective-taking. Adults seem quick in social interaction yet direct investigation shows effortful belief reasoning (p. 44).
- The usual debate is polarised: mental concepts versus exclusively behavioural rules. Number cognition suggests an alternative (pp. 44–45).
- **The view** (pp. 45):
  - Infants and some animals solve some tasks with one or more efficient, limited, inflexible systems.
  - Limits are overcome years later when children acquire concepts such as belief and desire. This is gradual, appears related to language and executive function, and may be facilitated by explicit training.
  - This gives a new flexible but inefficient system. In adults both exist in parallel.
  - The efficient system plays a central role in guiding online interaction and communication. The flexible one enables top-down guidance (e.g. anticipating what a lecture audience might know) and explicit reasoning about causes and justifications of mental states.
- *Lessons from number* (pp. 45–46): it is not always helpful to ask whether children or animals possess a concept such as "number" or "belief". Unvariegated notions of concept possession may be inadequate. Also, when operating in complex domains with finite resources, "two systems are often the solution" (p. 46).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A registration tracker for an agent A, with two boxes (L1, L2) and a ball.
- **Case 1.** The ball is at L1 and in A's field, so A encounters it at L1. The ball is moved to L2 while A is outside the room. A has encountered the ball at L1 and has not since encountered it elsewhere, so A's registration is "ball at L1". Rule: A's search is directed to the registered location. Prediction: A goes to L1. This matches a false-belief answer without ascribing a belief.
- **Case 2.** The ball is at L1, then moved to L2 while A watches. A encounters it at L2, which overrides the L1 registration (not "encountered somewhere else since" is now false for L1). Prediction: A goes to L2.
- **Signature limit.** Suppose the box was empty and A saw it empty. There is no object to encounter, so there is nothing to register. The system cannot represent "A believes there is nothing in L1", which matches the authors' point that absences need quantifiers (p. 35).

## Concepts

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Registration (a persisting object-location relation guiding action) ↔ number cognition: a limited-capacity, object-based attention or visual-working-memory system for small numerosities (pp. 35–36) — both are limited systems that support useful judgements without full flexibility.
- [structural-analogy] Behaviour associations ↔ statistical learning of transitional probabilities in language — the text itself draws the analogy (p. 30).
- [structural-analogy] 3–4 item limit in infant enumeration ↔ signature limits of the belief-like system — an arbitrary capacity limit diagnoses a specialised system (pp. 35–36).
- [metaphor] Fast and slow two-system trade-offs ↔ other dual-process pictures — efficiency purchased at the cost of flexibility.

## Questions this raises
- Which signature limits can be tested directly in adults, e.g. do adults compute level-2 perspectives automatically?
- If the two systems are partly isolated, how do implicit and explicit measures relate in older children and adults?
- Could automatization, behaviour rules and registrations be told apart in a single task, or only through converging methods?
- How could a limit be shown to be "arbitrary" rather than due to learning history or general resources?
