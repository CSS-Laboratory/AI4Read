# Frugality versus Conspicuous Consumption

## L0 — One line
Two opposed, long-running narratives, frugality and conspicuous consumption, change how much people spend and so can deepen a depression or fuel a boom.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller asks how competing stories about displaying wealth affect spending and the economy. He argues that during the Great Depression a strong modesty and compassion narrative made big purchases such as new cars seem insensitive. This fed a loop of postponed buying and job losses, so the narrative probably prolonged the downturn. Later, poverty stopped being fashionable ("chic"), and he says the modest recovery after 1933 occurred partly because people spent more. Japan after 1990 had a similar frugality story. The "American Dream" narrative, which went viral slowly from 1931, now supports spending, especially on homes, and contributed to the housing boom before the 2007–9 crisis. He concludes that the causes and effects of the Depression go beyond the simple story of rounds of spending and interest rates.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an economic narrative spreading like an epidemic (earlier chapters); the multiplier idea of "rounds of expenditure"; basic Depression history.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Frugality and conspicuous-consumption narratives are perennial opponents. Their relative strength shifts over time and affects aggregate spending (p. 136).
- In the Depression, "new modesty" rested on the story that people suffered through no fault of their own. Showy spending then seemed callous, and the resulting self-imposed austerity helps explain the contraction (pp. 137, 140).
- Visible consumption such as cars was postponed. This started a feedback loop of lost jobs and more postponement. Ford's new-car sales fell 86% from 1929 to 1932 (pp. 144–145).
- Crazes (bicycles, blue jeans, cheap jigsaw puzzles) were cheap, but their craze character suggests narratives helped stop purchases of expensive goods (p. 149).
- Economic dynamics depend on subtle changes in narratives. When "poverty chic" (a fashion of claiming poverty) faded, Shiller says, spending rose, and the modest recovery after 1933 occurred partly for that reason (p. 149).
- Employers' morale concerns and moral stories affected hiring and layoffs, which may have lengthened unemployment for some (p. 147).
- The American Dream narrative, coined in 1931, spread slowly, gained contagion through celebrity (King), and became useful in advertising (pp. 151–154).
- It mutated into a homeownership story, used to justify spending and policy. It probably raised homeownership and added to the severity of the 2007–9 crisis (pp. 154–155).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (p. 136)
Frugality has ancient roots. Sumptuary laws (laws forbidding ostentation) existed in Greece, Rome, China and Japan. Stories against flaunting wealth are among the longest-running narratives. Opposing them are conspicuous-consumption narratives: success must be displayed. The two are at constant war: modesty is relatively strong in some periods, conspicuous consumption in others. Both affect spending and saving, hence the economy. Their effects may be ones economists would not anticipate (p. 136).

### Frugality and Compassion in the Great Depression (pp. 136–137)
- Frugality narratives were strong in the 1930s, with perceived involuntary unemployment and a reaction to the excess of the 1920s. The phrase *keep up with the Joneses* grew most rapidly in the 1930s (p. 136).
- The "new modesty" stayed high through World War II and into the 1950s, then declined (p. 137).
- It grew out of the narrative that people suffered through no fault of their own. Tragedy stories spread by word of mouth (p. 137).
- Even people with jobs reasonably postponed a new car, lavish parties and fashion. Shiller says this self-imposed austerity helps explain the severe early contraction and the fall in consumer purchases in World War II (p. 137).

### Depression-era narratives in their own words (pp. 137–143)
- **Chryold's letter** (Boston Globe column, March 1930) says she fears the "moral effect" of moving to a cheaper house on her family's "spirits." Shiller links this to Keynes's animal spirits. Managing spirits was a theme of the era, including President Hoover's optimistic talk (pp. 137–138).
- Shiller thinks it "highly likely" that such families postponed a new car. Children would see canceled vacations and movies rather than a missing car (p. 138).
- Concern about family morale was a new "epidemic" after 1929, peaking in 1931. Divorce was attributed to lost morale. A 1936 women's group called family morale the base of the American system (pp. 138–139).
- This narrative justified postponing unnecessary spending while keeping an air of normalcy, and so contributed to prolonging the depression. It also gave unaffected families a reason to avoid conspicuous consumption (p. 139).
- Allen (1931) and Weiman (1932) describe a changed national temper and a turn away from 1920s spending. Holtby (UK, 1931) asks, in questions, whether the period could free people from snobbery and "appearances" (p. 140). Hackett (1932) says the Joneses were considered callous toward human misery if they kept giving big parties (pp. 139–140).
- McCormick (1932) wrote that people are much nicer at the bottom of the market than at the top (p. 140).
- It was noted that crime did not rise despite unemployment. Shiller says "perhaps" neighborly sentiment softened the sense of failure (p. 141).
- Panhandling and apple selling spread. Selling apples made begging look more reputable. Stories of crimes by refused beggars created fear, which "surely discouraged" conspicuous consumption (p. 141).
- Roth's 1931 diary describes professionals living on borrowed money, with short tempers (p. 141).
- Shiller's conclusion: the fundamental change was collective sympathy, and by stopping conspicuous consumption people "inadvertently worsened" the Depression (pp. 141–142).
- In Germany, with higher unemployment, panhandling and youth crime rose before Hitler came to power. Shiller says this helps explain Hitler's appeal. After 1933 he imprisoned panhandlers and homeless people in camps (p. 142).
- Films played down luxury (Kingsley 1932). Shiller says movies offered "scripts for living," and people may consume less through "subconscious suggestibility" (p. 142).
- A 1932 sermon called public extravagance an offense that stirs resentment. Shiller notes the argument is moral, not self-interest (pp. 142–143).
- **"Poverty chic":** the Washington Post reported that "I cannot afford it" was said boastingly, because it implied one had lost lots of money in stocks, whether or not one had. People took pleasure in telling hardship stories. There was no shame because poverty was not their fault, and enjoying riches amid poverty was more immoral (p. 143).

*Intuition note:* Here the moral tone of talk, not only income, helped shape what felt acceptable to buy. Spending less could be a way to show sympathy.

### New modesty crazes (pp. 143–145)
- Bicycles became a craze, partly from the wish to postpone a car. Reasons for postponing were being unemployed or fearing it (p. 143).
- The 1931 film *Six Cylinder Love* shows a man's decision to buy an expensive car turning his wife and daughter into extravagant spenders and attracting sponging friends. Shiller treats it as part of a narrative constellation about extravagant purchases (p. 144).
- A 1932 Wall Street Journal piece reported that auto-industry evidence showed many people with money and need were denying themselves a new car out of fear of neighbors' criticism. It called this a new species of sales resistance (p. 144).
- Heffetz's "visibility index" ranks cars second of 31 consumption categories, after cigarettes. Shiller says that for someone who no longer wants to look rich, skipping a new car might be the best thing to do (p. 144).
- **Feedback loop** (Shiller's account): postponed purchases → job losses in auto and consumer industries → more postponement → another round of job loss, for several years. Ford's new-car sales fell 86% from 1929 to 1932 (pp. 144–145).

*Intuition note:* A fear of looking rich cut demand. Lower demand cut incomes, which cut demand again. Shiller's account suggests the narrative helped supply the first push; he gives no estimate of its size.

### The home and the labor market (pp. 145–147)
- Lucy Ambler's 1932 letter describes her laid-off husband and her shame. A "Globe Sister" (Catarina) asked her whether her dissatisfaction was not really with the present economic conditions rather than with her husband (pp. 145–146).
- Shiller asks us to imagine that talk about high-priced expenditures, along with the expenditures themselves, might be "verboten" for such couples (p. 146).
- Employers who hire the long-term unemployed may be seen as heroes, but they may also worry about weak workers. A 1936 relief administrator said many workers who had merely been "carried along" by employers before the Depression would never get back their old jobs (pp. 146–147).
- Bewley (interviews during a 1990s recession): managers were concerned about morale mainly because of its impact on productivity. Shiller concludes it "seems safe" that employers care about morale in hard times. They may use "nondifferentiation" wage policy, paying high and low performers the same, and keep weak staff for sentimental reasons (p. 147).
- Darker side: the worst Depression days gave employers an excuse to lay off weaker employees without stories of inhumanity. In better times they would rather not rehire them, which can mean long-term unemployment (p. 147).

### Modesty fashions: blue jeans and jigsaw puzzles (pp. 147–150)
- Blue denim, once workwear, grew fashionable in the 1930s. Earlier celebrities had worn denim, such as "Blue Jeans Bill" Williams (Indiana governor 1877–80). In the 1930s: Lady Levi's (1934), a logo (1936), Vogue's first jeans-clad cover model, and deliberately ripped jeans (pp. 147–148).
- Jeans' cultural associations changed over decades: poverty chic, cowboys, then Rosie the Riveter, youth rebellion and *Rebel Without a Cause* (1955). By then the sympathy-for-poverty link was losing force, and jeans "may have lost" status as an economic narrative (p. 148).
- Cheap cardboard jigsaw puzzles sold at newsstands. Shiller: bicycles, jeans and puzzles may be only rational responses to cheapness, but the craze nature suggests narratives help explain why people stopped buying expensive goods, and so the length and severity of the Depression (pp. 148–149).
- Perhaps people would not have cycled to work in the 1920s because it seemed odd, not because they were rich. Once such habits spread, they can weaken markets for costly goods and slow recovery. Shiller adds that if building a beautiful new house is considered bad taste and stirs profound resentment, those are good reasons not to build, helping to explain why housing construction "virtually stopped" (p. 149).
- **Poverty chic fades:** the Washington Post (1932) said claiming poverty now implies you never had anything. Shiller says people moved beyond poverty chic, perhaps because of changing narratives about what apparent poverty implied, and that economic dynamics depend on subtle changes in narratives. He concludes the modest recovery from 1933 happened "at least in part" because people spent more as poverty was no longer so chic. Causes and effects of the Depression extend beyond the simple story of rounds of expenditure and interest rates (pp. 149–150).

*Intuition note:* Shiller's claim is that demand depends on subtle changes in narratives, including what people think apparent poverty or spending says about them. He presents the 1933 recovery as partly explained by such a change, not wholly.

### After the Depression (p. 150)
- Declining modesty and compassion narratives may help explain many economic trends, Shiller says. Shiller says the modesty decline is "likely related" to the rise of the top 1% income share (Piketty, 2014) and to managers' declining loyalty to employees (Uchitelle, 2006). Trump's 2007 book supported a narrative downplaying modesty and compassion (p. 150).
- Japan after 1990 had a frugality narrative. In 1993 the Washington Post described consumers emulating the hermit monk Ryokan, and a book on "honest poverty" sold 350,000 copies. Shiller says the new virtue lasted through the "lost decades" (p. 150).

### "American Dream" and analogous narratives (pp. 151–154)
- James Truslow Adams coined *American Dream* in *The Epic of America* (1931). It was almost absent in ProQuest before 1931, apart from a bedspring brand. Shiller says, citing Figure 11.1, that it went viral, vastly outpacing similar terms like *American character*, in a long slow epidemic still growing; the figure itself plots only *American Dream* in books and news (pp. 151–152).
- Adams's definition is a land of opportunity "according to his ability or achievement," and "not a dream of motor cars and high wages merely" but of a social order where each person can reach their fullest stature (p. 151). Shiller says Adams seems to assert that the dream is in part a dream of material things, and that people want everyone to have the chance (p. 152). Other countries' "dreams" (China, France, Canada) were modeled on it (p. 151).
- Early discussion in the 1930s was mainly intellectual (O'Neil's 1933 play); Albee's 1960 play was more critical of consumerism. Some intellectuals used the term ironically, others thought it measured a real aspect of American character (p. 153).
- Martin Luther King, Jr.'s 1963 speech and the 1983 holiday kept it growing. Shiller says small, unpredictable events can become the backbone of a narrative that grows by contagion for decades (p. 153).
- Celebrity effect: since King's speech, under 0.1% of ProQuest hits mention Adams, but 3% mention King. Shiller says King, a celebrity, pushed Adams aside and gave the narrative the human interest it needed for enormous contagion; Adams was not enough of a celebrity to have his name attached to it (pp. 153–154).
- The narrative frames prosperity for everyone as not selfish. Shiller says it "turns upside down" Veblen's idea of conspicuous consumption undertaken solely to prove superiority. It is useful in ads: more than half the uses are in advertisements (p. 154).

### The mutating American Dream: homeownership (pp. 154–155)
- Early ads promoted books, plays and sermons. Later it became owning a home, implying patriotism. Over two-thirds of hits since 1931 also contain *house* or *home* (p. 154).
- Examples: Fannie Mae's 2003 slogan "As the American Dream Goes, So Do We"; the 2003 American Dream Downpayment Assistance Act; 265 bills and resolutions since 1973 using the phrase (p. 154). Bush's "Ownership Society" (2004) and his 2002 remark that owning a home is "realizing the American Dream" (p. 155).
- Controlled experiments show patriotic appeals may help marketing. Shiller says that by attaching the phrase to moral rectitude and patriotism, the epidemic "probably raised" the homeownership rate and stimulated business generally (p. 155).
- Mixed results: it probably boosted real estate, directly and via actual or expected government support. It also played a strong role in the pre-crisis housing boom and added to the severity of the 2007–9 crisis (p. 155).
- Today the narrative justifies conspicuous consumption and pretentious houses, in contrast to Depression frugality. It also offers a sense of moral rectitude, like the gold-standard narrative of the next chapter (p. 155).

*Intuition note:* The same spending can be framed as shameful (Depression) or as virtuous (American Dream). On Shiller's account the frame can change what people buy, and for houses it reached government policy and the housing boom.

## Toy example (ours, not Shiller's)
Suppose each lost dollar of spending cuts next-round spending by 0.5 (our assumption). A frugality narrative makes consumers postpone 10 of spending.
- Round 1: 10. Round 2: 0.5 × 10 = 5. Round 3: 2.5. Round 4: 1.25. First four rounds sum to 18.75.
- Total over all rounds: 10 / (1 − 0.5) = 20.

If "poverty chic" fades and the initial postponement falls to 4, the total is 4 / 0.5 = 8. The loop is the same, but the narrative changes its starting size and so the total drop (20 vs 8). Shiller gives no multiplier. This is only an illustration of his loop on p. 144 and of his claim that changing narratives change demand (p. 149).

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — a contagious story that changes economic decisions. Here: modesty, poverty chic and the American Dream (p. 136).
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — stories spread like epidemics. Shiller calls family-morale concern, apple sellers and the American Dream "epidemics" (p. 138, p. 141, p. 151).
- [[animal spirits]] — Keynes's idea that depressions are caused by declines in "animal spirits." Shiller links Chryold's talk of "spirits" to it (p. 138).
- [[frugality narrative]] (new) — stories that favor modesty and saving, and condemn showy spending (p. 136).
- [[conspicuous consumption narrative]] (new) — the story that success must be displayed. The Veblen idea is mentioned on p. 154 (p. 136).
- [[poverty chic]] (new) — Depression-era fashion of claiming or showing modest means. It later faded (p. 143, p. 149).
- [[consumption feedback loop]] (new) — postponed purchases → job losses → more postponement (p. 144).
- [[visibility index]] (new) — Heffetz's measure of how much others notice a spending category (p. 144).
- [[nondifferentiation wage policy]] (new) — paying high and low performers alike to protect morale (p. 147).
- [[American Dream narrative]] (new) — Adams's 1931 phrase. It spread slowly and mutated into homeownership (p. 151, p. 154).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Postponed-purchase feedback loop ↔ economics-finance: the multiplier of "rounds of expenditure." The narrative changes the first push and the strength of the loop.
- [structural-analogy] Visible purchases as social signals ↔ signaling: spending (or abstaining) reveals status or sympathy, and the meaning of the signal changed (poverty chic).
- [metaphor] American Dream's slow, celebrity-driven spread ↔ epidemiology: a long, slow epidemic whose "carrier" is a famous person.
- [structural-analogy] Housing as patriotic duty ↔ housing boom and bust: a narrative supports demand and expected government backstops.

## Questions this raises
- How could we separate the effect of the frugality narrative from falling incomes and job fears, given that cars are also cheaper to postpone when income is low?
- If narratives are contagious, what makes a story like poverty chic fade, and can policymakers predict the turning point?
- Does the homeownership version of the American Dream cause more borrowing, or merely accompany it?
- Why might hiring after a deep recession remain weak for workers laid off for "sympathetic" reasons?
