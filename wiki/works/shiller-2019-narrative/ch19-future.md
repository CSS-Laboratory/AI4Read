# Future Narratives, Future Research

## L0 — One line
Old economic narratives will probably return at unpredictable times, so economists should build long, consistent, digitized time series of what people say and combine them with formal contagion models.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller's closing chapter asks what comes next for narratives and for the study of them. Narratives are expected to recur irregularly, and new communication technology may change how fast they spread and fade. Economics has mostly ignored popular narratives. Shiller names several reasons: economists' only source is "hearsay", the topic is complex, and talk about narratives is politicized. His proposal is to collect narrative data every year for decades. Sources would be focused interviews, focus groups, archives of past focus groups, sermons, letters and diaries. Textual search, AI and formal epidemic models would be used to analyse them. The aim is a rigorous, nonpartisan, imperfect science of narratives.

**Prerequisites:** the book's narrative-epidemic idea (contagion and recovery rates in an SIR-type model); basic idea of time-series data and surveys.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Many narratives are likely to recur as variants, at irregular times, unlike hypothesized regular business "cycles"; old stories can be rediscovered in new contexts (p. 271).
- Communication technology and cultural habits (reading aloud, mentoring, influencer marketing) may change contagion and recovery rates, but no systematic quantitative study exists yet (pp. 273–275).
- In the chapter's epidemic models, a rise in the contagion parameter matched by a corresponding change in the recovery parameter may leave the epidemic's final size unchanged and only speed it up, so technology's effects are hard to predict (p. 275).
- Economists name leading indicators but rarely the narratives behind them, because, Shiller says, their only source about narratives is hearsay (p. 276).
- Policy should track narrative dynamics and spread counternarratives; Roosevelt's 1933 moral appeals are a speculative example (p. 278).
- Shiller argues that careful, polite and quantitative work should let narrative economics stay nonpartisan; textual analysis in economics is still small but expanding (p. 279).
- Shiller proposes five data sources, collected consistently over decades; incentives for this are weak (pp. 280–285).
- Hard problems remain: overlapping narratives, changing word meanings, and causation versus correlation (p. 286).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 271).**
- Epidemiology suggests variants of old epidemics will likely return, and Shiller expects many narratives to become epidemic again. Timing is unpredictable, and narratives do not recur at regular intervals (p. 271).
- Forgotten narratives may be rediscovered. A new context may raise an old narrative's contagion rate, sometimes after a long lag (p. 271).
- He uses long quotes to give readers a historical sense of past narratives that might have impact again if repeated in the same words. Like a joke or song, a narrative "has to be worded and delivered just right" to be effective (p. 271).
- There is no exact science of narratives' economic impact, but exact research methods can still contribute. He warns against the "seductive allure" of scientific analogies that give false precision (pp. 271–272).

**Altered Forms and Circumstances (pp. 272–273).**
- Perceived "economic strength" is driven by narratives, notably the other-people's-confidence narrative (ch. 10). That strength may be ephemeral (p. 272).
- In the Great Recession of 2007–9, confidence fell rapidly and a 1929-crash narrative (ch. 16) returned. A small mutation of narratives or circumstances could do this again (p. 272).
- The keep-up-with-the-Joneses narrative seems especially strong in the US at the time of writing. Shiller cites President Trump as modelling ostentatious living (p. 272).
- Basic-needs charity fell 29% in real terms from 2001 to 2014, per Indiana University's Lilly Family School of Philanthropy (p. 272). Shiller adds that these declines in modesty and compassion narratives extend to a lower willingness to help emerging countries (p. 272).
- Intelligent-machine narratives (chs. 13–14) are still much talked about but do not seem to have much economic impact at the moment. Adverse news on inequality or unemployment could revive scary versions (pp. 272–273).
- A ProQuest search found that articles containing both *robot* and *jobs* almost tripled between H2 2007 and H1 2009. The NBER dates the recession peak at December 2007 and its end at June 2009 (p. 273).

*Intuition note:* A narrative that seems dormant is not gone. A shock or new context can make a small variant spread quickly.

**New Technology Will Change Contagion Rates and Recovery Rates (pp. 273–275).**
- Books, printing, newspapers, telegraph, telephone, radio, television, the Internet and social media have all altered contagion. There is no systematic quantitative study of their impact (p. 273).
- Social media let like-minded extremists find each other. Contagion is not slowed by fact-checkers. Ideas can also be spread with central control that is poorly visible. Designers of platforms and search engines could alter contagion, and society increasingly demands that they do (p. 273).
- Technology is not the only, nor always the biggest, factor. Cultural factors also matter (p. 274).
  - Reading aloud in salons and family circles faded around 1900. The *Washington Post* in 1899 lamented this. Book and magazine clubs took off afterwards (p. 274).
  - Mentoring (Big Brothers from 1904, diversifying since about 1980) gives young people identity through the mentor's stories. Groups are especially effective for women and minorities (p. 274).
  - Influencer marketing (since 2015) and social media marketing (since 2009) should increase contagion rates for promoted stories (pp. 274–275).
- Technology might replace parts of decision-making itself: robo-advisers (first: Financial Engines, 1996, William Sharpe) and target date funds (interest from about 2007). For now people write the programs and make foundational decisions. Massive deference to machines "appears likely still to be far-off" (p. 275).
- Modelling these effects needs a better science of spread. In the models, a rise in the contagion parameter matched by a corresponding change in the recovery parameter may leave the ultimate size unchanged, and the epidemic just happens faster. We must integrate formal contagion models into economic models to begin to understand the impact of such technology (p. 275).

*Intuition note:* A faster-spreading story may also be forgotten faster. So "technology makes stories spread more" does not by itself tell us how many people a story will reach in the end.

**The Future of Research in Narrative Economics (pp. 275–277).**
- Without scientific methods the field is left to "prognostica­tors or prophets" (p. 276).
- Economic research has not emphasized the stories people tell. Press explanations of recessions as they begin mostly cite leading indicators (central bank policy, confidence indexes, unsold inventories), not ultimate causes (p. 276).
- Economists are typically silent on what changed those indicators. Shiller says it is usually changing narratives. There is no professional consensus on which narratives were most impactful (p. 276).
- Economists are reluctant to bring up popular narratives that seem important because their only source is hearsay, and they usually have no way of knowing whether similar narratives existed in past events; so in their analyses they do not mention changing narratives (p. 276).
- Counting words in digitized texts already helps, but there has not been enough organized research on the strength of competing narratives. AI can help, especially with unstructured data. The part III narratives are works in progress (p. 276).
- Needed: better data, integration into existing models, research on "peripheral" issues, collaboration with non-economists, and mathematical epidemiology as a link to the humanities (p. 277).

**A Place for Narrative Economics in Economic Theory (pp. 277–279).**
- Neglect (ch. 3) is likely partly due to complexity and time variation. Journalistic accounts of narratives often lack academic rigor, and their public standing may have been diminished by aggressive forecasts that proved wrong (p. 277).
- Economists long assumed consistent optimizers with rational expectations. Behavioral economics has moved the field closer to other social sciences (p. 277).
- "Nudge units" followed the UK Behavioral Insights Team (2010) and Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge* (2008). The OECD counts close to two hundred (pp. 277–278).
- Shiller advocates formalizing leaders' intuitive judgment. First step: understand narrative dynamics. Second: design policy that accounts for narrative epidemics, including counternarratives for more rational, public-spirited behavior. Even if slower than a more contagious destructive narrative, they can eventually be corrective (p. 278).
- Example: Roosevelt's inaugural address (4 March 1933) asked people to set aside fears and spend. His first fireside chat (12 March) asked them not to withdraw more money than needed (p. 278). Shiller *speculates* the moral standard made it work, since the chats roughly coincided with upturns. There is no way to quantify exactly how salient the narratives of the time were (p. 278).
- Judgment and discourse about narratives tend to be politicized and emotion-ridden, so scholars find it hard to research them without being accused of taking sides. Because many economists try to remain nonpartisan, they tend to rely on quantitative rather than qualitative observations (pp. 278–279).
- Some may doubt that nonpartisan discussion is possible. Shiller replies that if we are careful and polite it should be possible, and that if we maintain quantitative rigor we can make narrative epidemics part of economic science (p. 279). He also says economists can now collect data on narratives' essential elements of meaning, not just words, and model their transmission (p. 279).
- Textual search is a small but expanding area: fewer than 100 NBER working papers use the phrase "textual analysis". Examples: party affiliation, political polarization, news and price movements. Further sources: diaries, sermons, letters, psychiatrists' notes, social media (p. 279).

**Collecting Better Information about Changing Narratives Should Start Now (pp. 279–286).**
- Economists must make more serious efforts to collect time-series data on narratives, going beyond passive collection of others' words toward experiments that reveal meaning and purpose (pp. 279–280).
- Vast digitized data exist but are tiny relative to all human communication. Consistent multi-decade collection is needed for intertemporal comparison. There is little immediate payoff, and most collection serves marketing or election prediction. It must combine consistency with creativity, perhaps with psychoanalysts and philosophers (p. 280).
- First step: improve search engines. They do not say how they estimate total hits. Quoted phrases sometimes return more hits than unquoted ones; a Google spokesman said quotation marks may make Google "dig deeper". Shiller wants evidence that this does not compromise count accuracy. Google Ngrams could do more for accurate comparison through time (pp. 280–281).
- Collect data at least yearly, uninterrupted for decades, in many countries and languages (p. 281). Five sources:
  1. **Regular focused interviews.** Respondents are invited to tell stories about economic decisions. Precedents: Blinder (price setting), Bewley (wage setting). Merton and Kendall gave the method a foundation in 1946. Past researchers usually ran these interviews as one-time events and did not try to collect long time series. Had such data been collected, they could have been added to major datasets (e.g. the Panel Study of Income Dynamics), and the stimulus list would need to be augmented over time (pp. 281–282).
  2. **Regular focus groups.** Similar people are grouped so they talk naturally. This simulates the interpersonal contagion behind narrative epidemics. Stimulus words might be *stock market* or *bank*. Videos might later be analysed by facial-recognition algorithms (pp. 282–283). Only 0.04% of economics and 0.02% of finance articles in 2010–2019 mention *focus group* (p. 283). Shiller cites apparent inconsistencies, illustrating a chapter 8 proposition that narratives' impact may change through time: the outbreak of WWI caused the US stock market to collapse while WWII's outbreak caused it to soar; the 1920 Red-scare bombings were associated with decline, 9/11 with ample spending. Timely, well-led focus groups might have given a better understanding of why people behaved as they did (pp. 283–284).
  3. **A historical database of past focus groups.** Roper Center archive (since 1947) tabulates answers to single questions of changing wording. It does not record respondents' own words. Shiller would pool past focus-group results, organized as searchable time series, to plot epidemic curves (p. 284).
  4. **Sermon databases.** Existing ones seem made for sermon preparation, but sermons carry moral values relevant to economic decisions (pp. 284–285).
  5. **Digitized letters and diaries.** Existing ones do not seem to be based on random samples (p. 285).
- Academic incentives ("publish or perish") make these collections unlikely soon. Survey organizations should be funded to run them consistently (p. 285).
- Shiller's own surveys: investors since 1989 (with parallel ones in Japan and China); US homebuyers since 1993 with Case and Thompson. Both include open-ended questions. Other organizations started similar efforts and stopped (pp. 285–286).

*Intuition note:* Comparing narrative strength across time needs data collected consistently for decades. One-time studies cannot reveal how answers and stories changed through history.

**Tracking and Quantifying Narratives (pp. 286–287).**
- Difficulties: conflicting, overlapping narratives. No narrative reaches everyone, even in the simplest epidemic model. Spread may be largely random. Word meanings and a story's real meaning change over time (p. 286).
- Causation versus correlation: how do we distinguish narratives associated with behavior merely because they report it from narratives that create changes in it? (p. 286)
- Literary theorists who list basic stories face similar problems (ch. 2) (p. 286).
- Semantic technology is advancing (machine translation, Siri). Meanwhile researchers can use multiple assistants with explicit instructions to classify narratives by their emotional driving force (p. 287).
- Textual analysis may help move beyond 1930s-style income-consumption feedback and Keynesian multipliers, which Shiller says are still influential. Shiller says it will also help us better understand deliberate manipulations and deceptions and help formulate economic policies that take narratives into account (p. 287).
- Shiller hopes the book shows real progress is possible without sacrificing sound scholarship (p. 287).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates the epidemic point on p. 275. Take a standard SIR form (the chapter gives no equations) with population fractions $S$ (not yet heard), $I$ (spreading), contagion rate $\beta$ and recovery rate $\gamma$: $dS/dt=-\beta SI$, $dI/dt=\beta SI-\gamma I$.

Start at $S=0.99$, $I=0.01$.
- Case A: $\beta=0.4,\ \gamma=0.2$. $dS/dt=-0.4\times0.99\times0.01=-0.00396$. $dI/dt=0.00396-0.2\times0.01=0.00196$.
- Case B (both doubled): $\beta=0.8,\ \gamma=0.4$. $dS/dt=-0.00792$. $dI/dt=0.00792-0.004=0.00392$.

Every rate in B is exactly twice that in A. So the path of $(S,I)$ is the same and only the clock runs twice as fast. The ratio $\beta/\gamma=2$ is unchanged, so the final share reached is the same in both cases.

## Concepts
- [[narrative-contagion-sir]] — narratives spread and fade like epidemics, with contagion and recovery parameters; matched changes may only change speed (p. 275)
- [[economic-narrative]] — the stories people tell one another and themselves about their economic lives; Shiller says economic research has not emphasized them and so misses possibly valid explanations of major changes (p. 276)

- [[counternarrative]] (new) — a story policymakers spread to offset a destructive narrative; may be slower but can eventually correct (p. 278)
- [[narrative-time-series-program]] (new) — Shiller's plan for consistent, long-run, multi-source collection of narrative data (pp. 280–281)
- [[focus-group-as-contagion-lab]] (new) — group interviews that simulate interpersonal contagion of narratives (p. 282)
- [[nudge-unit]] (new) — think tank or government unit applying behavioral economics to nudge people away from irrational behavior without coercion; close to 200 worldwide per the OECD (pp. 277–278)
- [[bounded-rationality-heuristics]] — after the behavioral economics revolution, economists no longer routinely assume people always behave rationally (p. 277)
- [[rational-expectations]] — the assumption of consistent optimizers with all available information, which Shiller says omits some clearly important phenomena (p. 277)

## Canonical instances

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Narrative epidemic with contagion and recovery parameters ↔ epidemiology: SIR model — the chapter says its models behave this way (p. 275).
- [structural-analogy] Focus groups simulating interpersonal contagion ↔ agent-based modeling: heterogeneous interacting agents — both look at how local interaction produces aggregate patterns.
- [structural-analogy] Nudge units and counternarratives ↔ rational vs bounded rationality: both treat beliefs and framing as policy tools.
- [metaphor] "Timing unpredictable, not cyclical" ↔ business cycle theory: Shiller contrasts narratives with hypothesized regular cycles (p. 271).

## Questions this raises
- How could one test whether a narrative causes behavior rather than merely reporting it (p. 286)?
- Can consistent multi-decade data collection be funded when academic incentives reward short-term publication (p. 285)?
- Would a counternarrative's slower spread really still correct a faster destructive one (p. 278)?
- How would one measure whether platform design changes contagion rates in practice (p. 273)?
