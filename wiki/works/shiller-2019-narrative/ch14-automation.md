# Automation and Artificial Intelligence Replace Almost All Jobs

## L0 — One line
The "machines will take all jobs" story keeps returning in mutated forms, and Shiller argues that it may itself cause or deepen recessions by cutting spending and confidence.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why does the fear of technological unemployment keep coming back? Shiller traces it after World War II through waves of the words *automation* and *artificial intelligence* (1950s–60s, 1980s, mid-1990s, 2010s). Each wave claims a new "turning point" and gets fresh concreteness from a gadget, film or book. Shiller suggests the fear may have deepened recessions (1957–58, 1980s) and could do so again, because fearful people spend and invest less. He says no one can predict what machines will really do. The narratives themselves can still drive booms, busts and policy ideas such as a robot tax or universal basic income.

**Prerequisites:** the book's idea of an economic narrative spreading like an epidemic; the previous chapter's story of labor-saving machines in the 1920s–30s.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The automation/AI narrative has recurred at least four times since World War II, each time with a new variant (p. 196).
- Fear of depression after 1945 most likely reflected memories of old Depression narratives rather than a clear forecast. It faded as optimistic spending stories took over, which became self-fulfilling (pp. 197–198).
- The narrative repeatedly mutated: from replacing muscle to replacing brains, and from "labor-saving" to "machines running machines" (p. 199).
- The twin recessions of 1957–58 and 1960–61 may have been caused by reduced spending tied to public automation fears. The 1980s twin recessions may also have been worsened by a "new automation" story (pp. 201, 204).
- New concrete technology and entertainment refresh the story: robot films, Siri, Watson, driverless cars, machine learning (pp. 203–208).
- Fear of obsolescence may motivate other narratives, such as the dot-com boom and the Steve Jobs story (pp. 206, 208–209).
- Policy ideas also recur (single land tax, robot tax, universal basic income), and the narratives may feed instability in spending and markets (pp. 209–211).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: recurring waves (pp. 196–198).**
- Technological unemployment as a problem for the indefinite future did not vanish after WWII. It mutated, often under the terms *automation* and *artificial intelligence* (p. 196).
- Figure 14.1 shows the percentage of news articles containing these words, 1900–2019. Shiller reads at least four AI narratives, peaking in the 1960s, 1980s, 1990s and 2010s, and the 2010s one "looks to be heading even higher" (pp. 196–197).
- Each time the narrative suggested that the world was only just now reaching a frightening major turning point when the machines take over. The robots of *R.U.R.* (previous chapter) had no story of how their intelligence was achieved. Automation and AI gained epidemic force when ideas became concrete (p. 196).
- Shiller says fears of automation were likely associated with fears of an impending depression (p. 196). A year-end 1945 Fortune/Roper survey asked whether a widespread depression within about ten years was likely. Results: 48.9% depression, 40.9% probably avoid it, 10.2% don't know (p. 197).
- Shiller says the answers *most likely* reflected memories of the Great Depression and post-WWI narratives, rather than a clear forecast. No depression came. Shiller notes that the angry narratives of the recent depressions, such as profiteering, had faded and did not restart, and that the idea that prices should fall to 1913 levels no longer seemed realistic (p. 197).
- Postwar spending narratives (the "Victory Vacation Year" 1946, travel promoted as a way to spend war-bond savings, slides and home movies, the baby boom) offset Depression-era frugality. Optimism became a self-fulfilling prophecy (a term Merton coined in 1948) (p. 198).
- Shiller then asks why people expected depression anyway and why 1950s–60s recessions interrupted optimism. He says the answer must lie "in good part" in a Great Depression narrative that still had intermittent power: the same technological unemployment narrative in mutated form (p. 199).

*Intuition note:* In 1945 about half of Americans "expected" a depression, and Shiller says this most likely reflected old Depression memories rather than a clear forecast. Optimistic stories about spending after 1948 then became a self-fulfilling prophecy that helped promote high activity.

**The Automation Recession Narrative (pp. 199–202).**
- Singularity first meant infinite terms in equations (from general relativity, the black hole). It later meant the time when machines are smarter than people in all dimensions (p. 199).
- The mutation shifted attention from muscles replaced by machines to brains replaced by AI, which refreshed the narrative (p. 199).
- *Automation* suggests nobody near the process, "machines running machines." Around 1955 the word launched into an epidemic, and data-processing began to run whole business operations (pp. 199–200).
- Stories included a 1956 first "automation strike" and a 1956 refrigerator plant: 27 miles of conveyors, 2,613 machines and tools, two refrigerators a minute, no human hands (p. 200). Automation was also seen as foretelling the end of unions (p. 200).
- Samuel Lubell (1959) reported that in spring 1958 few people talked of automation as a cause of unemployment. By 1959 he wrote that every third or fourth worker one interviews is likely to cite a case of workers displaced by machinery (pp. 200–201).
- Shiller says the twin recessions of 1957–58 and 1960–61 "may have been caused" by reduced spending due to automation fears. The first was dubbed "the automation recession" (p. 201).
- *Desk Set* (1957): an IBM computer, Emerac, threatens a reference librarian's job. Emerac mistakenly sends out pink slips to everyone, and the mistake is later corrected (p. 201).
- Further episodes: the 1962 cybernation report, Meany's 1963 demand for a 35-hour week, Johnson's 1964 commission whose report came in 1966 when the scare was mostly over, and a 1965 *Wall Street Journal* article titled "Automation Alarm Is Proving False" (p. 202).

*Intuition note:* The same worry moved from "machines replace muscle" to "machines replace brains," which Shiller says refreshed the narrative. The word *automation* launched into an epidemic around 1955, and the scare seemed to dissipate rather quickly by 1965.

**Star Wars Stories (pp. 202–205).**
- Spikes of *automation* and *robot* in the early 1980s. One possible explanation is Atari and Apple's success making technical progress seem faster. The Robot Store (1983) sold humanoid robots, predicted 10–20% of US households would own one within two years, and flopped (p. 203).
- Around the same time came very successful robot films and shows, consistent with the spike: *Star Wars* (1977–83), *The Transformers* (1984–87), *Blade Runner* (1982), *The Terminator* (1984). Earlier robot films came before and after the word *robot* (coined by Čapek, viral from 1922) (p. 203). A similar film spike preceded the 1957–64 scare (p. 204).
- Shiller thinks most robot films were silly and probably had little economic impact, except possibly adding emotional color to fears of an automated future (p. 203).
- In the twin recessions of 1980 and 1981–82, unemployment reached double digits. Pollack (1982) described "new automation" of offices affecting secretaries and managers. Shiller says this viral narrative "may well be the real reason" the recessions were so damaging (p. 204).
- A third spike came around 1995. A 1995 article reported that a growing minority of economists and many technologists thought this time differs: smart devices can invade almost every job category involving computing, communicating or simple deduction, and performance roughly doubles every 18 months or so (p. 205). Voice: that article's own claim, reported by Shiller.
- This twist did not immediately produce a recession. Business-opportunity narratives dominated the 1990s, and automation talk trailed off in the 2000s (p. 205).

**The Dot-Com or Millennium Boom (p. 206).**
- The Internet (public around 1994) and the millennium launched a narrative of computing power. Stock prices rose more than twentyfold over 1974–2000, against a mere threefold rise after 2007–9 (p. 206).
- Fears of replacement were not stressed in dot-com talk. Shiller says people speak more of opportunity than of feeling inadequate, but it appears that such feelings "may have driven" people to join as tech stockholders (p. 206).

**Fears of the Singularity after 2007–9 (pp. 206–208).**
- Per Google Trends, the latest wave began around 2016 (p. 206).
- Siri (2011) and IBM's Watson winning *Jeopardy* (2011) looked to many like the long-awaited singularity. Earlier DARPA-funded SRI work (2003–08) did not go viral (pp. 206–207).
- Driverless cars are said to be able to replace many jobs, and they handle things once thought unprogrammable (p. 207).
- Machine-learning searches more than quadrupled between 2004 and 2019. AlphaZero learns chess without human ideas. Harari (cited) speaks of a "growing fear of irrelevance" and a "new useless class" (p. 208).
- Shiller says such existential fears *have the potential* to affect confidence and the economy if they become a sizable epidemic (p. 208).

**Of Jobs and Steve Jobs (pp. 208–209).**
- Isaacson's *Steve Jobs* (2011) sold 379,000 copies in its first week. Shiller attributes part of its success to timing, weeks after Jobs's death (p. 208).
- Jobs appears indispensable and unprogrammable, so his story appeals to people worried about obsolescence. Shiller calls it a "fantasy" for people who do not quite fit conventional society (pp. 208–209).

**Economic Consequences (pp. 209–211).**
- Shiller says the narratives "certainly affected" willingness to spend, invest, start businesses and speculate. Recession hardship is mistaken for machine effects, producing pessimism as a self-fulfilling prophecy (p. 209).
- Henry George proposed a single land tax with a public benefit, which Shiller calls essentially a universal basic income. This is analogous to a robot tax, whose idea is that taxing robot users funds help for the unemployed (p. 209). George's proposal assumed that the sole purpose of the new machines was to work the land, which Shiller says might be the case in a purely agricultural economy (p. 209).
- Other basic-income proposals: Rhys-Williams (1943), Theobald (1963), and BIEN (1986). The jobless-future story has helped sustain support for a progressive income tax and an earned income tax credit but has produced no universal basic income in any country (p. 210).
- Baker (1932) argued that fears were wrong until now, and he urged raising the purchasing power of the 95% of families with tiny incomes (p. 210).
- Searches for *universal basic income* rose from 2012. Attention to inequality (Piketty) and the "digital divide" has grown (pp. 210–211).
- Conclusion: no one can predict machines' effects, but the narratives can drive amplified booms, recessions and policy. They may show up in real estate and stock markets (next chapters) (p. 211).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Spending baseline: 100 units. Suppose a share $s$ of households fear job loss and cut spending by 10%.
- Round 1: $s = 20\%$. Spending = $0.8 \times 100 + 0.2 \times 90 = 80 + 18 = 98$. Spending falls by 2%.
- Round 2: layoff stories raise $s$ to 30%. Spending = $0.7 \times 100 + 0.3 \times 90 = 70 + 27 = 97$.
- Each round the fear is "confirmed" by weak demand, even though no machine changed. This mimics the book's idea that recession hardship is mistaken for machine effects (p. 209).

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — contagious story that changes economic decisions; here the automation/AI story (p. 196)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — stories spread and fade like epidemics; "epidemic" used for word waves (p. 199)
- [[narrative mutation]] (new) — the same basic story returns in new forms and words, refreshing it (p. 199)
- [[technological unemployment]] (new) — the fear that machines will cause lasting job loss (p. 196)
- [[singularity]] (new) — time when machines are smarter than people in all dimensions (p. 199)
- [[self-fulfilling prophecy]] (new) — an expectation that helps produce its own outcome (p. 198)
- [[universal basic income]] (new) — payments to all, proposed repeatedly as a response to job loss (p. 209)
