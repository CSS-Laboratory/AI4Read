# The Bitcoin Narratives

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that Bitcoin's price and fame owe much to a contagious story (anarchism, mystery, inequality, "the future") rather than to public understanding of its technology: people are interested in it largely because others are.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller introduces narrative economics: contagious popular stories can change economic decisions. He uses Bitcoin as a first example. The question is why its value went from $0 to over $300 billion although few buyers understand the technology. His answer: the story is well crafted for contagion. It combines anarchist feeling, a mystery hero, fear of inequality and of computers, hope of joining the "future", and a cosmopolitan identity. People are interested in Bitcoin because others are. This matters because such stories may lead to erratic price swings, and in Bitcoin's case they came with real entrepreneurial activity. The chapter is an intuitive first look; the epidemiology of narratives is explored in chapter 3.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an asset price; what a bubble is; basic idea of an epidemic (contagion rate).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An economic narrative is a contagious story with the potential to change economic decisions. Shiller adds it to the usual list of economic drivers (p. 3).
- Bitcoin has no value unless people think it has value, as its proponents readily admit. The underlying mathematics does not say why people would believe that (p. 4).
- Critics call Bitcoin a speculative bubble. Advocates point to gold, which is valued mainly because others value it; Shiller adds Garber's point that bubbles can last long (pp. 4–5).
- Shiller says that, at the very least, the technical theory is not central to the narrative. In his own experience, enthusiasts he asks typically respond with a blank stare (p. 5).
- Several themes make the story contagious: anarchism with its counternarrative, the Satoshi mystery, inequality, fear of computers, and "be part of the future" (pp. 6–10).
- Media counts show far more coverage of Bitcoin than of its technical components. Shiller infers that the story's contagiousness explains the gap (pp. 9–10).
- Shiller presents Bitcoin as a "membership token" in the world economy: it has no nationality, and a wallet makes the owner a "citizen of the world" in some sense. Shiller sums up: people are interested in Bitcoin precisely because many others are (p. 11).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction (pp. 3–4).** The book proposes a new theory of economic change. It adds contagious popular stories, spread by word of mouth, news and social media, to the usual economic factors (p. 3). Such thinking affects decisions on investing, saving and spending, and on education and jobs (p. 3). *Narrative economics* is the study of the viral spread of narratives that affect economic behavior. Shiller says it can improve our ability to anticipate economic events and help structure institutions and policy (p. 3). Bitcoin is the first of thousands of private cryptocurrencies. Shiller judges it the most remarkable by speculative enthusiasm and market price, not by actual use in commerce (p. 3).

*Definition.* An **economic narrative** is a contagious story that has the potential to change economic decisions: hiring, waiting, taking risks, starting a venture, investing in a volatile asset (p. 3). Economic narratives are usually not the most prominent narratives. They are identified by their potential to change behavior (p. 3). Bitcoin is called a successful one: highly contagious, with substantial economic changes and real entrepreneurial zeal. It also stimulated business confidence, at least for a time (pp. 3–4).

**Of Bitcoin and Bubbles (pp. 4–5).**
- The Bitcoin story mixes inspired cosmopolitan young people versus uninspired bureaucrats, riches, inequality, advanced technology and impenetrable jargon (p. 4). It spread as a cascade of surprises (p. 4).
- Total value once exceeded US $300 billion. Proponents admit Bitcoin has no value unless people think it has (p. 4).
- Timeline: a 2008 paper signed Satoshi Nakamoto; launch in 2009 (p. 4).
- Cryptocurrencies are computer-managed public ledger entries that can function as money if people value and use them (p. 4). The mathematical theory behind them does not say what makes people value them, or believe others will (p. 4).
- **Detractors' view:** a speculative bubble. Warren Buffett: "It's a gambling device" (p. 4). They compare it with the 1630s Dutch tulip mania, when one bulb was worth about as much as a house (p. 4).
- **Advocates' reply:** the label is the "ultimate insult" (p. 5). Gold is valued mainly because people perceive that others value it. Shiller adds Garber (2000), who points out that bubbles can last long: tulips are still valued, though less extremely. The same might happen to Bitcoin (p. 5).
- Volatility: a Wall Street Journal headline reported a 40% price rise in forty hours on no clear news. Shiller reads such volatility as evidence of the epidemic quality of narratives, which may lead to erratic price jostling (p. 5).
- Shiller makes no attempt to explain the technology, except to note it results from decades of research. Few people who trade Bitcoins understand it. Enthusiasts he asked about the Merkle tree or the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm typically give blank stares. So theory is not central to the narrative, apart from the sense that smart people invented it (p. 5).

*Intuition note.* If a thing is valued because others value it, the story about it can matter more than its inner workings. This fits Shiller's choice not to explain the technology (p. 5), although he does not give this as his stated reason.

**Bitcoin and Anarchism (pp. 6–7).**
- Anarchism opposes any government. A Google Ngrams search shows the movement beginning around 1880 and growing slowly. The term is decades older (Proudhon described anarchism in 1840). Shiller says it took about forty years to reach epidemic proportions and has shown immense staying power (p. 6).
- Proudhon's passage about being governed appeals to people frustrated by authority (p. 6). Bitcoin.org carried a 2016 passage by Sterlin Lujan calling Bitcoin a "catalyst for peaceful anarchy" and a "monetary weapon" against authority (p. 6).
- Most enthusiasts might not speak so extremely, but Shiller says this passage seems to capture a central element of their narrative (p. 6).
- Bitcoin is a contagious *counternarrative* to the older view of anarchists as bomb-throwing lunatics. Shiller says it is contagious because it exemplifies the impressive inventions that a free, anarchist society would eventually develop (pp. 6–7).
- The "hacker ethic" (Ross 1991; Himanen 2001), Wikipedia and Linux show cooperation outside government, profit and lawyers (p. 7).
- Shiller's verdict: among viral economic narratives "Bitcoin stands supreme". It is "well crafted for contagion, effectively capturing the anarchist spirit", and that is why most of us have heard of it. It is part bubble story, part mystery story. Laypeople can join it and build identity around it, and it generates stories of riches (p. 7).

**Bitcoin as a Human-Interest Narrative (pp. 7–8).** The story motivates the cosmopolitan class, those who aspire to join it, and those who identify with technology (p. 7). Its celebrity hero is Satoshi Nakamoto, whom no one will testify to having seen. Bitcoin.org says only that Satoshi left the project in late 2010 "without revealing much about himself" (pp. 7–8). People love mysteries. Repeated publicity about "detectives" who may have found Nakamoto made the contagion rate higher than it would otherwise have been (p. 8).

**Bitcoin and the Fear of Inequality (pp. 8–9).**
- Rising inequality leaves many people feeling helpless. Bitcoin prices first took off around the 2011 Occupy Wall Street protests. Shiller calls it "no coincidence" that the narrative stresses individual empowerment and freedom from government (p. 8).
- A second theme is computers taking control: Alexa, Siri, Tmall Genie, and driverless vehicles that may bring mass unemployment. This is the latest form of the labor-saving-machinery (Luddite) narrative, taken up in chapter 13 (pp. 8–9).
- The fear is gradual and existential, a fear of not being needed. Shiller suggests you may find that your employer seems increasingly indifferent, offers no raises and does not hire others like you (p. 9).
- Students' doubts about their education create an anxiety that indirectly feeds the contagion of cryptocurrencies. These seem at least superficially to offer hope of mastering computers (p. 9).

**Bitcoin and the Future (pp. 9–10).**
- ProQuest counts: one article on "elliptic curve digital signature algorithm", five on "digital signature algorithm", 26 on RSA (dating from 1977), and over 15,000 on Bitcoin (pp. 9–10).
- Shiller: the difference "must result" from the contagiousness of the larger story. The technical phrase sounds like exam material, while Bitcoin is about the "future" (p. 10).
- People often buy to be part of something exciting and new. Shiller cites a narrative that the people in charge of new technology will be the winners, and says being on the finance side, where Bitcoin sits, is attractive because stories show financiers taking control. Bitcoin enthusiasts may think experimenting with it will give insight into staying in control (p. 10).
- One need not understand Bitcoin to buy it. Vending machines sell it (p. 10).
- The "Be a part of the future" narrative, with news of exciting price moves, gives Bitcoin value. The price fluctuations "thrive on and produce contagious narratives" (p. 10).

**Bitcoin as a Membership Token (p. 11).** Many of the world's most successful people see themselves as part of a cosmopolitan culture. Bitcoin has no nationality, which gives it a democratic and international appeal, and its narrative carries the idea that no government can control or stop it. Paper money suggests an obsolete nationalism. Having a Bitcoin wallet makes the owner a "citizen of the world" and in some sense psychologically independent of traditional affiliations (p. 11).

*Conclusion.* People are interested in Bitcoin precisely because so many others are, and in new stories about it because they expect others to be interested too (p. 11). Its success is not really surprising given the principles of narratives found by thinkers on mind, history and feedback models. These are the subject of chapter 2 (p. 11).

*Intuition note.* Each theme links the coin to a worry or hope people already have. Shiller says such links raise the contagion rate (p. 8, for the mystery theme) and that price fluctuations and contagious narratives feed each other (p. 10).

## Toy example (ours, not Shiller's)
A town has 1,000 people. In week 0, 10 know a story. Assume each informed person tells exactly 1 uninformed person per week. The informed count doubles: 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640 (weeks 0–6). In week 7 the doubling would give $2 \times 640 = 1280$, but only $1000 - 640 = 360$ people remain, so the count is capped at 1,000.
Now suppose a buyer's willingness to pay rises with the number who know the story. Then the weekly rise in price is largest around weeks 6–7 (the informed count grows by 320, then 360), with no change in the asset itself. This shows how attention, not technology, can drive a price. It is only an illustration of contagion. Shiller treats the epidemiology of narratives in chapter 3.

## Concepts
- [[economic-narrative]] — a contagious story that may change economic decisions (p. 3)
- [[narrative-contagion-sir]] — here only the epidemic analogy: stories spread, with a "contagion rate"; the detailed treatment is deferred to chapter 3 (pp. 3, 8)
- [[speculative-bubble]] (new) — price high because of public excitement, per critics' view; tulip mania as the model case (p. 4)
- [[self-referential-value]] (new) — an asset valued because others value it (gold, Bitcoin) (pp. 4–5, 11)
- [[counternarrative]] (new) — a story that answers an older story, e.g. Bitcoin versus antianarchist stories (pp. 6–7)
- [[human-interest-narrative]] (new) — a story with a celebrity hero and mystery that raises contagion (pp. 7–8)

## Questions this raises
- How can one measure the contagion rate of a narrative, beyond counting articles as ProQuest does?
- Does a high price follow from the story, or does the price rise itself feed the story (p. 10)?
- Do the same tools explain narratives that spread in a more modest way than Bitcoin's?
- Is the media-count gap (15,000 vs 26 articles) evidence of contagiousness or just of newsworthiness?
