# Stock Market Bubbles

## L0 — One line
The 1929 "crash" narrative (human folly, suicide, moral judgment, celebrity legend) went viral, still lingers in public thinking, and probably serves to amplify end-of-boom drops.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller asks why the 1929 crash became a lasting story, when it was one episode in a decline lasting almost three years. He points to several contributing factors: record-setting drops, a new ticker technology that drew crowds, and stories of suicide and ruin. Moral readings of the 1920s may have played a role, and a celebrity legend (the shoeshine boy) attached to the story. Stories around the 1987 crash recalled and amplified the 1929 story, but no strong suicide or depression narrative developed for 1987. He concludes that the crash story probably amplifies end-of-boom drops in stock prices and in confidence. It was not contagious in 2019 but might return in mutated form.

**Prerequisites:** percentage changes and stock indexes; the idea of a "narrative epidemic" (stories spreading like infections) from earlier chapters.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The word "crash" was not commonly attached to the stock market before 1929. After 1929 it became a name for a view that growth depends heavily on the stock market, so indexes are treated as oracles (p. 229).
- The 1929 crash is better seen as a long decline than a two-day event: the closing S&P Composite (a US stock index) fell 86% from Sept 1929 to June 1932 (p. 230).
- Warnings existed before the peak but were controversial and inconclusive, and many people ignored them (pp. 231–232).
- Newspaper "stock market crash" epidemics peaked in 1929 and 1987. Newspapers focus on records, and 1987 was the only record one-day drop after 1929 (p. 233).
- Suicide and "ruin" stories helped magnify 1929 as a flashbulb memory (a vivid memory of a sudden event), though whether suicides really rose is questioned. A 50% margin requirement (a limit on borrowing to buy stock) in 1987, not in 1929, meant fewer people were ruined (pp. 233–235).
- Moral stories (sermons, "Roaring Twenties") helped frame the crash as judgment (pp. 235–236).
- The shoeshine-boy legend is a celebrity story that Shiller could not find in the ProQuest News & Newspapers database for the 1920s–30s (p. 237).
- The legacy narrative probably amplifies end-of-boom drops, and old stories are "scripts" for booms and crashes (p. 238).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction (p. 228).** Stock-bubble narratives are about excitement, risk taking and wealthier people, and are driven by social comparison. They connect to the confidence/panic narratives of chapter 10. The stock market differs from the whole economy, so these narratives form a distinct constellation (a cluster of related stories) with its own path of contagion (p. 228).

**A Narrative Is Born (pp. 228–229).**
- "Crash" became tied to the drops of Oct 28 and 29, 1929, and to the Great Depression. It implies a preceding period of crazy optimism and perhaps reckless behaviour (p. 228).
- The Trans-Lux Movie Ticker (first in the news 1925) showed trades on a large screen in brokerages, clubs and bars. Crowds could gather, which encouraged contagion of stock stories. A 1928 Associated Press account likened the thrill to watching one's racehorse win (pp. 228–229).
- "Crash" was not commonly used for the stock market before 1929. "Boom and crash" earlier was used most often for cannons, storm waves or Wagner's music, but after 1929 it went viral and usually described the stock market (p. 229). The new use of "crash" became a name for a view that economic growth depends heavily on the overall stock market, so that indexes are taken as oracles. Shiller says the persistence of the narrative helps explain public fascination with stock indexes and the widespread belief that the market is a fundamental indicator of the economy's vitality (p. 229).

*Intuition note:* Crowds gathering at the ticker encouraged stock stories to spread. After 1929 the word "crash" also became a name for a view of how the economy works.

**Crash: The Breaking Point between Speculative Excess and Hopelessness (pp. 230–233).**
- No important news arrived on Oct 28, 1929 besides the crash itself. Equally baffling is the 1920s rise: more than fivefold between Dec 1920 and Sept 1929. By June 1932 the market was below its Dec 1920 level. Earnings per share also rose, but the puzzle is why the market responded so heavily to them (p. 230).
- Oct 28–29 brought the S&P Composite down only 21%, and half of that was reversed on Oct 30. The closing index fell 86% from its Sept 7, 1929 peak to its June 1, 1932 trough. The relentless daily and monthly decline is more noteworthy than the October drops (p. 230).
- Records make good news. **Kempton (1955):** the storm of 1929 "conditioned the real history of the 1930s" and left the American dream "flawed and cracked" (pp. 230–231).
- **Noyes, NYT, Oct 1928:** stock talk dominated conversation, as in 1901, 1920 and 1925. This time, unlike before, conservative financiers openly predicted that an inflated market was "riding for a fall" (p. 231).
- In the year before the peak, actual volatility was low. Implied volatility (reflecting interest rates and the initial margin brokers demanded) was exceptionally high, suggesting brokers feared a big decline (p. 231).
- Still, the evidence was controversial and inconclusive. A high price-earnings ratio can predict a higher risk of declines. It is not like a storm warning, which most people heed. Many did not heed it in 1929 (pp. 231–232).
- Figure 16.1 (p. 232): the crash narrative seems to have begun weakly in 1926 (not taken seriously), shot up in 1929, and persists, more in books than in newspapers. Newspapers show two fast epidemics, each peaking within a year, so contagion was very strong in the short run (p. 232).
  - The first came with the record 12.8% Dow one-day drop on Oct 28, 1929, and a further drop the next day.
  - The second started with the 22.6% drop on Oct 19, 1987. That is almost double the Oct 28, 1929 percentage, though short of the 1929 two-day drop (p. 233).
- No other movement since 1929 has been widely called a crash. Shiller's explanation: newspapers focus on records, presumably because readers do, and 1987 was the only record one-day drop after 1929. Stories before Oct 19, 1987 recalled and amplified the 1929 story (p. 233).
- 1987 looks like a flashbulb memory (a vivid, sudden event, not easily forgotten; chapter 7). After decades its story no longer seems to fit any lively narrative constellation, and hence it is no longer virulent (p. 233).

*Intuition note:* In Shiller's account, the 1987 story spread because a record drop recalled the existing 1929 story, and it later lost its virulence because it no longer seems to fit a lively set of related stories.

**The 1929 Suicide Narrative (pp. 233–235).**
- Stories of businesspeople's suicides partly magnified the 1929 flashbulb memory, which may have been stronger than the 1987 one (p. 233).
- Whether the crash really caused suicides is questioned. **Galbraith (1955)** argued there were not many more suicides afterwards. Yet there were many such stories: twenty-eight in ProQuest News & Newspapers in November 1929 alone (p. 233).
- The affect heuristic (chapter 6) predicts such narratives make people temporarily more fearful about everything (p. 233).
- A Louisville Courier-Journal piece urged "Don't Shoot Yourself!": the crash had ruined men "only financially", and they still had the brains that made the money (p. 234).
- Terkel's 1970 interviews show suicide and 1929 coming up often, with embellishments and exaggerations. Interviewees Robertson, Andersen and Walther describe frenzy, suicides, ruin and "jumping out of windows" (pp. 234–235).
- The 1987 epidemic looks far stronger than 1929 in Figure 16.1, but much of its strength comes from memories of 1929. Suicides were attributed to 1987 too, but no strong narrative or reinforcing story of depression followed. A 50% margin requirement in 1987, not in 1929, meant many fewer people were "wiped out" (p. 235).

**Moral Narratives about 1929 (pp. 235–236).**
- Ideas about morality may have played a role. Some critics viewed the 1920s chicanery, selfishness and sexual liberation negatively but could not make a case against this putative immorality until the crash (p. 235).
- Sermons on Nov 3, 1929 blamed moral and spiritual transgressions. "Roaring Twenties" was rarely used in the 1920s and became common in the 1930s. Moralists likened Oct 28 to a lightning bolt from heaven (pp. 235–236).
- **Kempton:** the "myth of the twenties" (individual expression) was judged selfish by the "myth of the thirties"; the twenties were not so simple, with mixed values (p. 236).
- The crash became a dividing line between the 1920s and the morally superior but depressed 1930s. The idea of crash as divine punishment remains (p. 236).

**Celebrities and the Shoeshine Boy Narrative (pp. 236–237).**
- In the legend, Rockefeller, Baruch or Joseph Kennedy sold stocks before the 1929 peak after a shoeshine boy gave him stock advice. **Chudley (Business Insider, 2017)** tells it for Kennedy, who sold and shorted (pp. 236–237).
- Shiller found no evidence of it in ProQuest for the 1920s–30s. The earliest mention he found is in Baruch's 1957 memoirs, though even there the story is not exactly an epiphany at the moment the boy spoke (p. 237).
- Variants name bootblacks, barbers or policemen. A 1915 Minneapolis Morning Tribune piece argued the rising market was not about to turn because it did not hear of chambermaids and bootblacks cleaning up fortunes; such romances "usually mark the approach of the culmination of the advance." It does not seem to have the moral force of the shoeshine narrative, and it is not tied to a catastrophe or a celebrity (p. 237).

**Relevance Today (p. 238).**
- The feeling lingers that another crash might come. This probably amplifies end-of-boom drops and drops in confidence. Awareness that some people think this way might lead to expectations that others will react the same way (p. 238).
- In 2019 the story was not contagious but might return with a mutation or a change in the economic environment (p. 238).
- Policymakers might take a lesson that, during economic inflections, there is real analytical value in looking beyond headlines and statistics. "Stories and legends from the past are scripts for the next boom or crash" (p. 238).

*Intuition note:* An old story can act as a script for the next boom or crash. If people know that some others think in terms of the story, they might expect those others to react in an amplifying way.

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Population 1,000. At week 0, 10 people have the crash story. Each week every holder tells 2 random people, and each hearing is new with probability (share of the population who have not yet heard). Holders tell only in the week after they learn it.
- Week 1 new: 10 × 2 × (990/1000) = 19.8.
- Week 2: not yet heard = 1000 − 10 − 19.8 = 970.2, so new = 19.8 × 2 × 0.9702 ≈ 38.4.
- Week 3: not yet heard = 970.2 − 38.4 = 931.8, so new = 38.4 × 2 × 0.9318 ≈ 71.6.

New tellings grow about 2×, 1.9×, 1.9× per week, a fast early burst. A record event would act like a larger starting number of holders.

## Concepts
- [[economic-narrative]] — a story that spreads and bears on economic events; here the stock crash story, called a "continuing economic narrative" (p. 238)
- [[narrative-contagion-sir]] — stories spread and fade like epidemics; Fig. 16.1 shows two short newspaper epidemics (p. 232)
- [[flashbulb-memory]] (new) — vivid memory of a sudden event, not easily forgotten (p. 233)
- [[affect-heuristic]] (new) — a psychological principle that predicts stories of death make people temporarily more fearful about everything (p. 233)
- [[narrative-constellation]] (new) — a cluster of related stories; Shiller says the 1987 crash story is no longer virulent because it no longer seems to fit a lively one (pp. 228, 233)

- [[trans-lux-ticker]] — technology that gathered crowds around stock news (p. 229)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Newspaper "crash" epidemics ↔ epidemiology: short, sharp outbreaks that fade (p. 232).
- [structural-analogy] 1929 margin wipe-outs vs 1987's 50% margin requirement ↔ debt-deflation: leverage turns price falls into forced ruin (p. 235).
- [metaphor] Crash story as "script" ↔ conventional expectations: past stories guide what people expect others to do.
