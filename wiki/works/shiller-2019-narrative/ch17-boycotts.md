# Boycotts, Profiteers, and Evil Business

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that contagious anger narratives about business ("boycott", "profiteer", "fair wage") can lead people to see spending as a moral matter, and so can help bring on, or at least contribute to, depressions and recessions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people sometimes stop buying, and why do they stop at some times and not others? Shiller says the answer lies partly in narratives of anger at business. When prices jump or wages are cut, stories of "profiteers" and "evil business" spread. They make waiting for lower prices feel moral as well as sensible. He traces this through the boycott idea (1880), the 1920–21 depression, the Great Depression, the late 1940s, and the 1973–75 recession. He says the link between deflation and depression is not strong or consistent, because the anger depends on whether the narrative goes viral. The tone of future narratives about business and labor unions matters for the economy.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an economic narrative spreading like an epidemic (earlier chapters); inflation, deflation and the CPI; the basic idea of a recession.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The boycott story went viral from 1880, partly because it was controversial and seemed moral rather than self-interested; its moral authority faded when the public grew fed up, in the 1890s depression (pp. 240–241).
- "Profiteer" was a vivid, emotional word. It was very frequent around World War I and peaked in the 1920–21 depression; it suggested more need for collective action such as a boycott (pp. 241–243, 246).
- A "return to normalcy" narrative, with 1913 as the benchmark price level, made delaying purchases seem both moral and practical (pp. 244–246).
- Shiller suggests the 1920–21 depression may be seen as a consumer-boycott-induced "buyers' strike", and says an excess profits tax contributed to it (p. 249).
- Wage cuts met more acquiescence in 1920–21 than in the 1930s, partly because union propaganda was less viral (p. 251).
- "Fair wage" mentions follow a profiteer-like pattern, while Akerlof–Yellen presented their theory as if it applies equally at all times; Shiller says attention to fair wages appears to be heightened by changing narratives (p. 250).
- In the Great Depression, expecting prices to fall delayed purchases. Shiller suggests women's shopping talk may have mattered, and "Buy Now" campaigns tried to counter the "prices will fall" narrative (pp. 253–255).
- Boycott talk, buyers' strikes and an emotion-driven embargo recur (1949, 1973–75) (pp. 255–257).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 239).** Anger at business varies over time. Narratives blame business for rising prices, and the anger may last after inflation stops if prices still seem too high. Wage cuts can also inflame it. Anger may produce organized boycotts or unorganized postponement of spending. People then see buying in moral terms, and moral anger may combine with self-interested waiting for lower prices (p. 239).

**The Boycott Narrative (pp. 239–240).** "Boycott" entered most major languages from 1880. Charles Boycott was its most celebrated victim, not its inventor. He was land manager for an absentee landlord in Ireland. After a bad crop in 1880 he offered a 10% rent cut. Tenants demanded 25%. He resisted, and a tenants' organization appealed to the wider community (pp. 239–240).
- **Boycott's own account (letter to the *Times*, Oct 1880):** mobs, workers ordered off his farm, and shopkeepers warned to stop supplies, with his ruin openly the Land League's aim (p. 240).
- **Why Shiller thinks it went viral:** it was controversial. It offended human sensibilities but touched the prominent questions of inequality and concentrated wealth and power. Asking the whole community for moral support looked like a new and superior tactic. The community gained nothing directly, so the act seemed moral, not self-interested (p. 240).
- The boycott narrative centers on anger against businesspeople and carries a sense of conspiracy. It tends to recur when there is broad social opprobrium (disapproval). It matters economically because it affects willingness to spend and to compromise (p. 240).

*Intuition note:* A story may spread more easily when it makes an action look principled. A buyer who joins a boycott can tell himself he is doing right and not just saving money.

**The Boycott Narrative Goes Viral (p. 241).** Historian Leo Wolman (1916) said the boycott emerged "almost without warning" in 1880 and was unionism's most effective weapon for 10–15 years. By the middle of the 1890s depression the public was fed up, and moral authority disappears when most people become suspicious and annoyed. The American Federation of Labor acted to restrict boycott endorsements between 1893 and 1908. Boycotts recur, but each lasts only while its narrative stays strong (p. 241).

**Profiteer stories and World War I (pp. 241–243).**
- "Profiteer" was coined in 1912 (Oxford English Dictionary). It was very frequent around WWI and just after, and peaked in the 1920–21 depression (pp. 241–242; Fig. 17.1, p. 243).
- It plays on the older "privateer" (a state-backed pirate ship). Such vivid images helped contagion. Related phrases were "excess profits" and "boycotts" (p. 242).
- **Example (New York Tribune, 1918):** one man on a streetcar says the war has been "a godsend" to his plant. A woman hits both men with her umbrella. Shiller calls this a highly contagious narrative with a powerful visual image (p. 242).
- The 1920–21 depression was the sharpest (fastest decline and recovery) in US history since modern statistics began. It was called the "post-war depression". After WWII "postwar" changed meaning, so the depression lost a unique name. James Grant (2014) called it "The Forgotten Depression" (p. 242).
- It remained a powerful narrative in the 1930s and was part of the script for the Great Depression. Both depressions produced public outrage toward business (p. 243).

**The Return to "Normalcy" (pp. 243–245).** Postwar inflation totaled 100%, and by 1920 a deflation narrative had developed. It was contagious in association with the profiteer narrative. Thousands of articles reported individual prices back at 1913–14 levels. Readers found it natural that prices would return to old levels. That might eventually encourage buying a house or car, but only after prices fell fully (pp. 243–244).
- Harding popularized "normalcy" (he did not invent it). Shiller compares this to Trump's "bigly" and "yuge": words lent concreteness to the narrative. For Harding, the word reflected a tendency to conflate the depression conditions of 1920 with the still-vivid trauma of the war (p. 244).
- **Harding, inaugural address (Mar 1921):** prices "must reflect the receding fever of war activities". He said old wage levels might never return, and that "we must strive for normalcy to reach stability" (pp. 244–245).

**To Buy or Not to Buy (pp. 245–246).** In the 1920s, waiting for lower prices seemed moral and practical to most consumers. But postponing purchases helped bring on a depression. Shiller reports one 1920 observer: goods "do not move" because people "will not buy" (p. 245).
- Populist anger grew. A writer quoted by Shiller (no date given) described the principle behind the protests: if people buy only necessities, supply and demand will bring conditions back to normal. Thrift became a new virtue (p. 245).
- **Why 1913:** the Bureau of Labor Statistics published a CPI precursor in 1919, with data from 1913, the last full year of peace. It showed a dramatic rise since 1913, so 1913 became the benchmark. Commissioner Meeker (Jan 1920): the prices people complained about in 1913 had come to be regarded as ideal, though the text notes the ideal was mistaken (pp. 245–246).
- **Numbers:** CPI 9.8 in 1913; 20.9 by 1920 (more than double); 17.3 by mid-1921. It would have to fall much further to reach 9.8 (p. 246).
- **Claim (Shiller):** in extreme cases of deflation, embellished narratives might go viral, and only in that case would buying fall significantly. Consumers then see "some vengeful reward" in waiting. So the deflation–depression relationship across countries and long periods is not strong or consistent (p. 246).

*Intuition note:* The same price fall can cause a slump or not. On Shiller's account, what seems to matter is whether a story says that waiting is both smart and right. That is why he sees no strong, consistent link between deflation and depression.

**Profiteering and Fair Wage Narratives (pp. 246–250).**
- None of the older war-profit words (harpy, racketeer, bloodsucker, etc.) seems to have had the same connotations as "profiteer". It suggested wartime fortune-building at the expense of war heroes, and a big operation with government connections. That suggested a need for collective action such as a serious boycott. An added benefit of boycotts, from a US perspective then, was their lack of any link with communism (p. 246).
- It appeared in sermons against high food prices and in stories of lawyers who falsely told soldiers' families they needed an attorney, then charged 20% of benefits (pp. 246–247).
- **Public vs. economists:** economists understood why wartime inflation lasted to 1920 (indebted governments avoided raising taxes or interest rates), but the public did not. The public saw a battle between good and evil (p. 247).
- **Hazlitt (1920):** everyone denounces everyone else's "profiteering" in a ring from butcher to coal man (p. 247).
- **Fisher in Germany (1922):** a shop-owner feared being thought a profiteer and said she had still made a profit because she had bought the shirt for less. Fisher argued there was nothing moral or special about prewar prices or the 1913 dollar (pp. 247–248).
- **US case:** 28% consumer price inflation over the 19 months from the armistice to June 1920. The John A. Roberts Corporation was fined $55,000 for profiteering. A dress was bought for $16.75 and sold for $35. A scarf was bought for $6.50 and sold for $25. Shiller says the massive inflation created an illusion of high profits (p. 248).
- **An economist's explanation:** in swiftly rising prices a business could grow rich just by holding goods on the shelf. Profiteering is "a symptom of the disease, not the disease itself". Shiller thinks this probably convinced only a few. The concern receded only after prices began to fall, and not exactly in step, because the anger had its own dynamics (p. 248).
- **Policy and spending:** US inflation ended by June 1920 and prices dropped rapidly, though never to 1913 levels. A 60% excess profits tax on profits above the 1911–13 level was imposed in 1917 and revoked only in October 1921. Shiller says it contributed to the depression by encouraging firms to postpone profits. People also held off buying because of anger and the chance to gain from waiting (p. 249).
- **Shiller's relabeling:** perhaps "the 1920–21 consumer-boycott-induced depression" or "buyers' strike". Senator Capper (Jan 1920): "Profiteers are more dangerous than Reds" (p. 249).
- **Fair wages:** anger was sometimes fueled by wage cuts. Firms said they could not pay more when product prices fell. Shiller says a rational person should have seen cuts as sometimes necessary, but that explanation was not contagious. Unions had no incentive to explain it and found it in their interest to keep a story of evil management alive (p. 249).
- "Fair wage" usage follows a pattern very like "profiteer", though Shiller describes its growth as different (see Reading notes). Books peak near 1920–21, newspapers in the 1930s (p. 250).
- **Akerlof–Yellen (1990):** workers who feel underpaid may slow work in revenge. They presented it as if it applies equally at all times, but attention to fair wages appears to be heightened by changing narratives (p. 250).

**Narratives That Suddenly Ended the 1920–21 Recession (pp. 250–251).** The abrupt end has no obvious explanation. Presumably new narratives, poorly observable today, reduced expected price falls and anger. Good 1920 harvests were taken as a sign of good times. Banker Walker (late 1920) said prices might not fall to 1913 levels for a generation, because of war debt. By April 1921 there were claims of less profiteering. Many farmers were reportedly back to 1913-level prices for much of their produce. Business and wealth were less evil, and spending revived (jewelry, school lunches, automobiles). Shiller suggests narratives, not active government stimulus, may explain the recovery (pp. 250–251).

**Contrasting 1920–21 with the 1930s (pp. 251–252).**
- Labor historians find labor more acquiescent to wage cuts in 1920–21. Unions were fewer and weaker, so union propaganda was less viral. Grant attributes the quick end of the depression to wage flexibility (p. 251).
- In the 1930s, employers' wage-cut reasons were narrated as greed and lies. Clergy were criticized for preaching economics. The 1933 National Industrial Recovery Act regulated against wage cuts. Roosevelt's policy made cuts harder even after the 1935 court ruling. Shiller says firms would have found cuts difficult even without regulation (p. 252).
- "Normalcy" was less prominent in the 1930s. The earlier depression was seen as transition after war and influenza. The 1930s were seen through other narratives: confidence, frugality, technological unemployment and the 1929 crash (p. 252).

**Boycotts and Profiteers in the Great Depression (pp. 252–254).**
- References to 1920–21 began at the 1929 crash. The last big crisis has a special place in memory, which enhances contagion (p. 252).
- Many expected prices to fall as in 1920–21 and delayed purchases. In 1932 the narrative persisted that prices would fall to 1913–14 levels. That would have meant a further 20% fall beyond the 1933 bottom (p. 253).
- A month after the crash, news noted weak retail sales in luxury items at Christmas; buying was normal only because of price cutting (p. 253).
- Hoover and economists expected a short contraction in 1930. The public generally did not believe Hoover (p. 253).
- **Hackett (1932):** a housewife treats falling prices as an investor treats a falling stock market, waiting before buying more than necessities. If all twenty million housewives wait, recovery is "indefinitely delayed" (p. 253).
- **Shiller's reading:** women must have been talking like speculators, telling stories of shopping successes and mistakes. Even if average expected inflation were non-negative, a higher contagion rate for price-decline stories could cut spending significantly (p. 254).
- **Shiller's conjecture:** economists have neglected women's testimony, and the critical decision to postpone purchases "may have come more from women". Mrs. Foster (1932) said women spend 85% of incomes and could set an example of spending (p. 254).
- Boycotts of German, Japanese and Jewish-associated goods occurred, and Germans boycotted Western goods. Shiller says these "must have had" economic effects (p. 254).

**The "Buy Now" Campaign (p. 255).** In 1930 the Washington, DC, Chamber of Commerce launched "Buy Now for Prosperity", seeking the participation of clergymen. In 1933 Roosevelt launched his own campaign, and a "Buy in August" campaign used seasonality to imply prices would rise. Shiller reads these as attempts to counter the "prices will fall" narrative (p. 255).

**Later Boycott Narratives (pp. 255–257).**
- **1945–49:** after WWII, authorities remembered the 1920–21 narrative and kept wartime price controls for a while; a brief sharp recession (Apr–Oct 1945) was linked to demobilization. After controls were lifted, prices rose about 30% by 1949 versus 1945. There was talk of a buyers' strike and a 1949 recession like 1920's, with buyers waiting for lower prices (pp. 255–256).
- **1973–75:** the recession is widely attributed to the Arab oil embargo (from Oct 1973), "the selling counterpart of the boycott". It was principle- or emotion-driven and lasted long after the war ended. Only one of eleven OPEC countries (Iraq) was among the five Arab countries in the war (p. 256).
- Oil prices quadrupled, with anger, rationing (odd–even gasoline days), and homes kept dark in protest. US inflation was 12% in July 1974, a record excluding the world-war periods. The firefighting metaphor (an observer's July 1974 comparison of fighting inflation to fighting a forest fire, needing courage, team play and shared sacrifice) has moral overtones that might have caused people to curtail spending (p. 256).
- The April 1973 meat boycott reportedly cost 20,000 industry jobs. There was a one-day "Don't Buy Anything" boycott in August 1973, and a meat and grain boycott in January 1974. The boycott sentiment reportedly cut purchases of many goods and services, leading to, or at least contributing to, the recession (p. 257).
- **2007–9:** thousands of boycotts were reported but did not appear to reach the economic significance seen in earlier episodes. Shiller expects angry-boycott narratives to reappear. How emerging businesses and labor unions are seen, as good or evil, matters greatly for the future economy (p. 257).

## Toy example (ours)
Suppose 100 households each spend 10 per month on postponable goods, and a household that believes prices will soon fall back to normal buys nothing that month.
- If 10% hold the story: 90 households × 10 = 900.
- If 50% hold it: 50 × 10 = 500.
- Spending falls (900 − 500)/900 = 44%, with no change in income or prices. Only the share holding the story changed.
- The benchmark can be wildly out of reach. From Shiller's CPI numbers, falling from 17.3 to 9.8 means a drop of 7.5, which is 7.5/17.3 ≈ 43%. Waiting for "normal" can mean waiting for a very large fall.

## Concepts
- [[economic-narrative]] — a contagious story that changes economic decisions (here: anger and moral stories about business) (p. 239)
- [[narrative-contagion-sir]] — stories spread like epidemics; words like "profiteer" show short epidemic curves (p. 243)
- [[boycott-narrative]] (new) — anger-driven story that community refusal to deal is a moral tool; lasts only while the story is strong (pp. 240–241)
- [[profiteer-narrative]] (new) — story that businesses gouge consumers, especially in war and inflation; an economist quoted by Shiller calls it a symptom of an underlying problem, not a vicious habit (pp. 241–242, 248)
- [[return-to-normalcy-narrative]] (new) — belief that prices will fall to prewar (1913) levels, justifying delay of purchases (pp. 243–246)
- [[fair-wage-effort-hypothesis]] (new) — Akerlof–Yellen: workers slow work if paid unfairly; Shiller says attention to fair wages appears to be heightened by changing narratives (p. 250)
- [[buyers-strike-postponed-spending]] (new) — consumers delay purchases for moral and self-interested reasons, which can deepen depressions (p. 249)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Narrative-driven spending drop ↔ epidemiology: story prevalence acts like infection share, lowering aggregate spending — Shiller's contagion framing.
- [structural-analogy] Housewives all waiting for lower prices (Hackett) ↔ coordination game / multiple equilibria: each waits if others wait, so slumps can be self-reinforcing.
- [structural-analogy] Wage-cut anger ↔ incomplete contracts and fairness norms: unspecified fairness expectations constrain what firms can do.
- [metaphor] Profiteer as "symptom, not disease" ↔ price as signal: high prices carry information, but the angry public reads them as moral failure.

## Questions this raises
- How could we measure narrative prevalence directly, beyond word counts like Fig. 17.1, to test the claim that deflation matters only when stories go viral?
- Is the 1920–21 recovery really due to changed narratives, or to harvests, tax changes and wage flexibility that Shiller also mentions?
- Could Shiller's conjecture about women's shopping talk be tested with surviving diaries, press or surveys?
- Do online boycott campaigns today spread and fade in the same way as those described here?
