# Real Estate Booms and Busts

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that stories treating homes as speculative investments in scarce land, spread by media, indexes, promotions and social comparison, helped drive the 1997–2005 US housing boom, alongside supply constraints.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do housing booms happen, and what role do stories play? Shiller traces real estate narratives from land speculation to Florida's 1920s boom, homeownership campaigns, flipping and the 2000s boom. Before price indexes, newspapers found it relatively hard to report home prices regularly. Social comparison and the Internet made housing a status story. Supply constraints explain some city differences, but the 1997–2005 rise in real US prices far exceeded the rise in real rents. Shiller's surveys show housing talk and investment motives peaking in the boom and falling after; he reads this as probably the end of an epidemic. The chapter matters because the housing boom was a major factor in the 2007–9 crisis.

**Prerequisites:** real vs nominal prices (inflation-adjusted); price indexes; the idea of a speculative bubble; chapter 3's epidemic-style contagion (only referred to here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Real estate narratives are perennial but mutate: from farms, to empty city lots, to homes in metropolitan areas. All stem from a story about scarce land (p. 212).
- Homes were long seen as depreciating structures priced by construction cost, which gave bubbles little chance. Seeing a home as an investment in land, not a depreciating structure, kept the potential for bubbles alive (pp. 215, 221).
- Regular published price numbers (median price; the Case-Shiller index, a US home-price index) let media tell recurring home-price stories (pp. 215–216).
- Social comparison (people comparing themselves with others of similar rank) and the Internet turned house size and value into a status story, encouraging buyers to "stretch" (pp. 217–218).
- Promotion campaigns, scandals and laws (Ponzi's swamp-land sales; state "blue sky laws" against selling worthless speculative schemes) changed how land and homes were advertised (pp. 219–221).
- Supply constraints (limited land available for building) help explain differences across cities, but not the whole national boom (pp. 221–223).
- Flipping (buying and quickly reselling) went from condo conversions (apartments sold as owned units), to IPOs (first public sales of a company's shares), to houses, spreading a speculative home-as-investment story (pp. 223–224).
- Survey and media counts show talk and investment motives rising and falling with prices, which Shiller's likely interpretation treats as the end of an epidemic (pp. 226–227).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 212).** Shiller says the 2007–9 Great Recession was "fueled by stories" inflating the value of housing. Real estate confidence differs from economy-wide confidence (ch. 10): people see real estate as a personal asset and the economy as the product of myriad forces. Yet real estate is also a "socially informed asset", whose value depends on comparison with neighbors.

**Speculation and Land Bubbles (p. 213).** Before the twentieth century, popular narratives celebrated speculation in farmland or vacant city lots, not homes or stocks. An account written in 1840, after the collapse of a US land bubble that had started in 1837, tells of D., who sold his farm for prairie land and "lithographed cities". These turned out to be bogs. Shiller calls this a perennial narrative of a foolish speculator buying unseen land, later reused with a swamp in 1920s Florida (p. 213).

**The Florida Land Boom of the 1920s (pp. 213–215).**
- A ProQuest search finds virtually no speculative use of "home price" until the second half of the twentieth century. Earlier the phrase meant the domestic-market price, or a rich person's spending, e.g. Senator Sawyer's 1889 mansion, with no sense of appreciation (pp. 213–214).
- "Price per acre" peaked around 1900, referring to farmland as speculation. The Florida boom drew many hits but almost no "home price". The story stressed that cars made Florida land accessible to northerners (p. 214).
- By 1926 the boom was a national scandal. Promoters sold undeveloped home-size parcels sight unseen, which made such sales disreputable (p. 214).
- Land is a small part of a home's value. Davis and Heathcote estimate 36% on average for 1976–2006. Shiller presumes it was lower when the population was more rural (p. 214).
- Homes were traditionally seen as depreciating structures, with prices dominated by construction costs. A 1956 NBER study concluded that a construction cost index (wages and materials, no land) conformed closely to the long-term price index, corrected for depreciation. Shiller says that view leaves housing bubbles "little chance of getting started" (p. 215).

*Intuition note:* If people think of a house mostly as a wearing-out building, Shiller says housing bubbles have little chance of getting started. If they think of it as an investment in land, the potential for bubbles persists.

**Enter News, Numbers, and Narratives (pp. 215–217).**
- Newspapers found readers liked stories about prices in congested inner cities, where land is expensive. These may have spread the idea that distant properties shared the trend (p. 215).
- Indexes helped. Median existing-home prices were first mentioned in ProQuest News & Newspapers in 1957. The National Association of Realtors median was published from 1974. The Case-Shiller index appeared from 1991 (p. 216).
- Before indexes, news could not easily report regular home-price movements. A house is rarely resold within a day, and monthly medians were erratic. Repeat-sales indexes let monthly aggregate changes be inferred. They also led to a futures market at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, which mostly dried up after 2007–9 (p. 216).
- Common accounts assume investors extrapolate recent returns, forming a feedback loop. Shiller adds that narratives matter. The price rise seems driven less by future expectations than by the spread of talk that draws attention to the booming asset (p. 217).

**House Lust and Social Comparison (pp. 217–218).** Shiller draws on McGinn's 2007 book *House Lust* (voice: McGinn, reported by Shiller).
- McGinn believed boom-time conversation reflected a lust for status and maybe power, which sometimes drives ruinous actions (p. 217).
- He names several effects. The "high-five effect" is the vicarious thrill of cheering a winner (p. 217). "Our House Is Our Retirement Plan" implies one should stretch to buy the biggest house one can afford (p. 218). "It's So Easy to Peek in the Window" is caused by the Internet and social media; websites such as Zillow and Trulia (both founded 2006) let you find out any house's value free (p. 218).
- Shiller adds Festinger's "social comparison process": people compare themselves with others of similar rank and pay less attention to those far above or below. They stretch because they know others are stretching. In the early 2000s no other comparable success measure could be looked up online (p. 218).

*Intuition note:* Asking about pay is considered vulgar, but websites like Zillow let you look up what a house is worth. In Shiller's account, this gave people a comparable success measure to look at.

**The History of Homeownership Promotion (pp. 219–220).**
- The "Own Your Own Home" campaign (1914, Hill Ferguson, National Association of Real Estate Boards) followed the savings and loan and British building society movements, as an attempt to help people build savings (p. 219).
- It tried to change the presumption that borrowing is disreputable. A 1919 ad said not to let a mortgage scare you (p. 219).
- Retirement was virtually never mentioned in home ads until the 1920s, and the idea did not take off until the 1940s. Savings were a safety measure against illness or misfortune (p. 219).
- The movements were a moderate success, and homeownership rose (pp. 219–220). Better Homes in America began in 1922 (p. 220).
- Realtor campaigns continued: before the crisis, "Now is a good time to buy or sell a home". Afterwards, "Home Ownership Matters". They said homeowners tend to be successful and patriotic, giving a clearer rationale for buying (p. 220).
- Marketers help people past guilt about showing off. A 1900 ad, "A Princely Spot Is Orangewood", sold plots near Phoenix. In response to complaints, US states passed "blue sky laws" over 1911–33 against selling speculative schemes with no more basis than "blue sky" (p. 220).

**Mr. Ponzi and His Other Scheme (pp. 220–221).**
- Charles Ponzi, said to have invented the Ponzi scheme in 1920, was released in 1926. He soon returned to jail for violating Florida's blue-sky law, ; during the Florida boom he sold small parcels of land without disclosing that they were under water in a swamp (pp. 220–221).
- His name is famous for the scheme, but not attached to the swamp narrative (p. 221).
- After the scandals, laws treated land sales as securities sales. People came to think that investing in undeveloped land on future use was irresponsible. Advertising turned to going businesses and owner-occupied homes (p. 221).
- Because people still thought of homes as investments in land rather than depreciating structures, bubble potential persisted. Real estate stayed the simplest speculation, since many people own a home and need not learn much (p. 221).

**City Land and Stories (pp. 221–223).**
- Changing narratives do not explain some major swings in particular cities. Evidence suggests supply constraints can explain booms in some cities and not others (p. 221).
- If a growing city still has buildable land, new houses are built and immigrant demand may barely move prices. If land has run out, buyers outbid each other, prices rise, and wealthier people end up living there (p. 222).
- Saiz used satellite data on available land. Cities boxed in by water or steep terrain tend to have higher prices (p. 222).
- Existing owners have an incentive to block new building, particularly affordable housing (p. 222).
- Beyond these, Shiller says there is evidence that changing narratives play a role (p. 222).
- Numbers (Case-Shiller index; real, CPI-corrected). Real US home prices rose 75% from 1997 to 2005, while real CPI rent rose only 8%. This far exceeded what increased unmet demand for housing services could explain (pp. 222–223).
- Prices then fell and mortgage defaults surged, which spread crisis to the financial sector and the world. By 2012 real prices were only 12% above 1997. From 2012 to 2018 they rose 35% while real rents rose 13% (p. 223).

*Intuition note:* Shiller treats rents as a guide to demand for housing services. He says the price rise far exceeded anything increased unmet demand could explain, and points instead to a more speculative narrative.

**The Rise of Flipping (pp. 223–224).**
- Shiller says interest rates, tax rates or income are "not very helpful" for explaining the boom. He points instead to a shift toward seeing homes as speculative investments in land, a narrative lenders welcomed (p. 223).
- In the 1970s–80s, "flipper" meant someone who sells a speculative investment within a year. It spread during a condominium conversion boom. High inflation raised the tax advantage of owning, since mortgage interest was deductible and rent was not (p. 223).
- Developers converted apartments and offered renters discounted purchase contracts. Many renters flipped these to speculators, who flipped them again. Many admired flippers as entrepreneurs (p. 224).
- By the 1990s, flippers were IPO buyers who resold quickly. Ritter's 1991 article found that the initial price pop was followed by weak performance over later years, so buying at the offering and flipping looked optimal (p. 224).
- In the early 2000s, flippers were people who fixed up and quickly sold homes, again admired. Most people did not flip, but may have imagined "long-term flipping" by buying a primary residence as a long-term investment (p. 224).

**Mansions and Modesty (pp. 224–225).** In October 2012 the *Wall Street Journal* launched "Mansion", the year US prices started rising sharply again. It featured lavish homes with gushing admiration, while also reporting anxieties about ostentation. A 2017 article, "Tech CEOs: Lie Low or Live Large?", covered CEOs' dilemma. Occupy Wall Street's "We Are the 99%" slogan was in the background (pp. 224–225).

**The Donald Trump Narrative and Urban Investors (pp. 225–226).**
- Offsetting modesty, the Trump narrative says it pays to show wealth. Shiller says it is co-epidemic with the conspicuous consumption narrative (ch. 11) and contributed to the upturn in US prices after 2012 (p. 225).
- Figure 15.1 shows Google searches for "housing bubble" soaring in 2005, before the crisis, with news coverage partly delayed. Shiller says the curve resembles an Ebola epidemic curve (ch. 3) (p. 226).
- Trump University was founded in 2005, offering to turn anyone into a successful investor. The *Economist* ran a bubble cover story on 18 June 2005. Trump University closed in 2010 amid cries of fraud (p. 226).

**The Housing Market Today (p. 227).**
- Shiller's annual survey of recent homebuyers (four US cities, Yale School of Management): buyers who called investment "a major consideration" were 49% in 2004, 32% in 2010 and 42% in 2016 (p. 227).
- Those saying housing was discussed "Frequently" were 43% in 2005 and 28% in 2012. Shiller's "likely interpretation": the contagion rate of housing narratives fell, and the price decline could be viewed as the end of an epidemic (p. 227).
- ProQuest finds 246 stories with "housing bubble" in March–May 2005. One quoted Greenspan seeing "a little froth" and an "unsustainable underlying pattern", compared with his December 1996 "irrational exuberance" speech. There were 169 stories with Greenspan and froth in 2005–2007 (p. 227).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A house rents for \$1,000 per year and sells for \$15,000, so price/rent = 15,000 / 1,000 = 15. Suppose real rent rises 8% and real price rises 75%, echoing the chapter's 1997–2005 gap.
- New rent = 1,000 × 1.08 = \$1,080.
- New price = 15,000 × 1.75 = \$26,250.
- New ratio = 26,250 / 1,080 ≈ 24.3.

Housing services cost only 8% more, yet buyers pay 62% more per dollar of rent (24.3 / 15 ≈ 1.62). Shiller reads such a gap as evidence that demand for housing services alone cannot explain the boom (pp. 222–223). The toy does not show that narratives caused the gap.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — contagious story that changes economic decisions; here, home-as-investment-in-scarce-land (p. 212)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — talk may spread and fade like an epidemic (Shiller's likely interpretation); here "housing bubble" searches, and survey talk falling to 28% (pp. 226–227)
- [[social comparison]] (new) — people compare themselves with similar others, so house size becomes a status signal (p. 218)
- [[supply constraints in housing]] (new) — limited buildable land raises prices in some cities (pp. 221–222)
- [[flipping narrative]] (new) — admired quick resale of condo contracts, IPOs, houses, spreading speculative framing (pp. 223–224)
- [[homeownership promotion]] (new) — advertising campaigns promoting buying homes as saving and as respectable (pp. 219–220)
- [[extrapolative feedback loop]] (new) — investors expect recent gains to continue, pushing prices up; Shiller adds that talk also matters (p. 217)

## Canonical instances
- [[Florida land boom 1920s]] — sight-unseen swamp parcels became a national scandal (p. 214)
- [[US land bubble 1837]] — D.'s story as a perennial speculator narrative (p. 213)
- [[Own Your Own Home campaign]] — 1914 campaign to help people build savings through homeownership and to change the view that borrowing is disreputable (p. 219)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] housing talk rising and falling ↔ epidemiology: SIR-style contagion curves — the chapter compares the "housing bubble" search curve to an Ebola curve.
- [structural-analogy] extrapolating recent gains ↔ adaptive expectations: forecasts follow past price changes — both describe feedback loops.
- [metaphor] social comparison on Zillow ↔ social-network status competition: public numbers become a scoreboard.
