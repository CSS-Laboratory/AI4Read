# The Gold Standard versus Bimetallism

## L0 — One line
The 1890s fight over gold versus silver money shows, in Shiller's account, a monetary narrative going viral with the help of debtors' self-interest, class resentment, moral and religious imagery, and a mysterious idea.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why did stories about the gold standard and bimetallism (money backed by gold and silver) grip Americans in the 1890s, and what does this teach about narratives? Shiller argues the silver debate spread like an epidemic. It mixed debtors' hope of halving their debts, resentment of eastern elites, moral and religious imagery, and a mysterious monetary idea, which Shiller finds similar to Bitcoin's appeal today. Stories also seem to have helped trigger the 1893 bank runs. The uncertainty they produced led some businessmen to hold back from contracts and investment. By the 1930s the narrative had changed, and the public accepted the end of the gold standard in 1971. Money narratives live on and will likely mutate.

**Prerequisites:** what a currency peg and deflation are; debtor versus creditor; the idea of a narrative "epidemic" from earlier chapters.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The US went onto gold with the Coinage Act of 1873. Persistent deflation followed, which hurt debtors and fed the "Crime of 1873" story and the Silverite movement, which backed bimetallism, a system using both gold and silver (pp. 157–158).
- Silver at 16:1 (16 ounces of silver per ounce of gold) when the market ratio was about 30:1 would have let debtors cut debts roughly in half. Gold supporters therefore saw themselves as upholding truth and honesty, while the other side stressed injustice (pp. 158, 166, 171).
- "Gold standard" appears in text mainly in two decades, the 1890s and 1930s, the decades of the two biggest US depressions as measured by unemployment. Shiller calls these two epidemics (p. 158, Fig. 12.1, p. 159).
- Stories shaped beliefs through class and region, a best-selling book (*Coin's Financial School*), and Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech (pp. 160–161, 167–168).
- Narratives helped trigger the 1893 bank runs. Depositors seem to have followed a familiar script (pulling money from local banks) even though the rumor concerned Treasury redemption (pp. 164–165).
- Conflicting stories left people unsure about the value of money and contracts. Some businessmen refused to commit resources (pp. 168–169).
- Most Americans were fundamentally confused about bimetallism and the gold standard. Shiller gives debt relief, revenge for 1873, its "cool idea" quality and perceived injustice as reasons for bimetallism's contagion (pp. 170–171).
- By the 1930s the vocabulary had shifted from "debasement" to "devaluation". The public accepted the 1971 end of gold with few dislocations (pp. 172–173).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 156–157)
- Gold standard means fixing the currency to a set amount of gold. The government promises to redeem currency in gold, and the reverse, on demand (p. 156). The world abandoned it in 1971. Since then money is *fiat* (not backed by anything) (p. 156).
- Central banks still hold about 33,000 tonnes of gold, worth about $1.4 trillion (World Gold Council) (p. 156). Asked why the Fed holds gold, Bernanke answered "it's tradition" (p. 157).
- Shiller reads this as apparently pointing to narratives: central banks seem worried about public upset if they sold the gold (p. 157).
- Gold-and-money narratives have a peculiar emotional tone and a "mystique", like cryptocurrency narratives. Gold's history is tied to prolonged deflation, and the debates were acrimonious (p. 157).

### The Crime of 1873 and the Emotional Divide (pp. 157–161)
- The Coinage Act of 1873 effectively put the US on gold. Before it there was an implicit bimetallic standard, with a silver-to-gold ratio of 16:1 set in 1834 (p. 157).
- Persistent deflation followed for two decades. Some called the Act "a crime". Deflation lowered crop prices and raised the real value of debts, so it hurt indebted farmers. Buyers of big items also regretted not waiting (pp. 157–158).
- Under gold, a one-dollar contract meant delivering $1/20.67$ ounce of gold. Under bimetallism at 16:1 the debtor could deliver that gold *or* 16 times as many ounces of silver (p. 158).
- By the 1890s the market ratio was about 30:1. Debtors could therefore cut debts roughly in half by paying in silver. Shiller says the result would in effect have been a default on about half the value of all dollar-denominated debts. Gold supporters saw themselves as upholding truth and honesty (p. 158).
- **Figure 12.1** (p. 159): the frequency of "gold standard" in books and news is high mainly in two decades, the 1890s and 1930s; the caption speaks of "two separate epidemics" (the news series spikes sharply near 1895 and 1932; the books series is highest in the 1930s). The 1890s and 1930s were the decades of the two biggest US depressions by unemployment (p. 158). Shiller says we "ought to consider" how the narratives relate to severe depression. Both episodes involved talk of "debauching" the gold standard and of tradition and honesty (pp. 158–159). The word *devaluation* did not exist in the 1890s (p. 159).
- An April 1895 *Atlanta Constitution* report described people in Iowa as "crazy on the silver question", with near-unanimous sentiment for free silver coinage (pp. 159–160).
- Belief had geographic and class dimensions. Easterners and intellectuals favored gold, and westerners and farmers favored silver. Some easterners used epithets such as "Western lunatics" and "anarchists". One observer said such epithets have no effect other than irritation and anger; Shiller adds that this observer underestimated the power of geographically local idea epidemics (p. 160).
- The 1894 London International Bimetallism Conference said the long gold-standard deflation had depressed agriculture widely. It said the US had the strongest popular support for bimetallism (p. 161).
- *Coin's Financial School* (1894, W. H. Harvey) is a fictional dialogue in which a young "little financier" outargues a real professor. It is reputed to have sold a million copies. Shiller sees in it a key contagion element: a good story of a clever youth beating snooty experts (p. 161).

*Intuition note:* Debtors had a clear self-interest in silver, and easterners' contempt was resented. Shiller says other narratives played on this resentment and that the debate took on strong emotional significance, so the idea seems to have spread through feeling as well as argument.

### Bimetallism and Bitcoin (pp. 161–162)
- Shiller says the enthusiasm for bimetallism "seems similar" to the excitement for Bitcoin (p. 161). He says "maybe" part of Bitcoin's appeal is that understanding it takes effort and talent. He notes its air of mystery (as with conventional money, since few understand how paper money gets its value), the Satoshi detective-story mystery, and the idea that savvy young people understand it while old fogies never will, which "appeals to many" (p. 162).
- Shiller says it is no coincidence that Harvey made Coin a young man: in the 1890s the monetary standard offered some of the same mystery as Bitcoin. Young people then wondered what money is and why it has value, and they might have asked why they almost never saw a gold coin (p. 162).

### Silverites and Gold Bugs (pp. 162–164)
- Shiller says the Silverites anticipated Trump's 2016 supporters in sympathies and in intellectuals' contempt for them. A 1896 *Washington Post* report described Seattle as strongly nationalist (pp. 162–163).
- Silverites held that bimetallism was the only route to prosperity and that the depression would last forever under gold. Shiller calls this misguided, since gold had lasted decades without permanent depression. He adds that the idea itself worked against prosperity, because it discouraged spending and investing (p. 163).
- **McKinley (1896):** "sound money" would restore confidence and general prosperity (p. 163).
- "Gold bugs" (gold's fiery supporters) became a term that, rare in 1874, took off on what appears to be a hump-shaped infective curve peaking in 1896 (pp. 163–164). The post-election joke "Have you seen the General?" ("General who?" "General prosperity") faded in 1897 as the economy improved (p. 164).

### Narratives Trigger the 1893 Bank Runs (pp. 164–166)
- The depression began in spring 1893 with bank runs. One trigger was a rumor from April 17 that subtreasury offices would redeem notes only in silver worth about half as much. It had no basis beyond falling Treasury reserves, which newspapers highlighted because they fell below the round $100 million (p. 164).
- Yet the run was on commercial banks. **Noyes (1898):** panic is unreasoning. Frightened depositors withdrew the very legal tenders they feared would depreciate (p. 164).
- Noyes's explanation, as Shiller reports it: paper money was what people used, and going to the local bank was what people did in stories of past distress. They were following a script (pp. 164–165).
- Shiller concludes that the initial panic seems to have resulted from the contagion of bank-failure stories. This is not enough to explain the depression lasting to 1899 (p. 165).
- Many easterners held an "almost religious" attachment to gold. One basis was that contracts assumed gold, so changing it could amount to reneging (p. 165). Gold also had spiritual symbolism: wedding rings, the Bible, saints' nimbus, Whitman (p. 165).
- **Senators Jones and Stewart (1874):** gold is "the articulation of commerce" and the universal standard of value (pp. 165–166). Shiller says such statements oversimplify history. The "standard model" arose in the UK in the eighteenth century and was fully adopted in the US only in 1879. Gold-standard talk grew in a nice epidemic curve from 1874 (p. 166).

### Cross of Gold (pp. 166–171)
- **Howard (1895):** the opponents' narrative stressed unjust inequality, with plutocrats versus "toilers", and called gold's moral value a canard. This fed stories of grasping managers (p. 166).
- The counter-narrative portrayed Silverites as a dangerous, stupid mass (Davis, Merrill, 1896) (p. 167).
- **Bryan's speech (July 1896):** he joined gold to Christian morality ("crown of thorns… cross of gold"). He stretched out his arms as on a cross, and the reaction was near hysteria (pp. 167–168).
- Shiller thinks the lines were likely powerful because workers linked their suffering to Jesus's. The words were largely McCall's (January 1896). Bryan had attended McCall's talk and gauged the audience's reaction. His celebrity status gave the "authoritative stamp" the story needed to go viral (p. 168).
- Conflicting narratives left people unusually uncertain about money and near-term business. **Sloss (1896):** a builder would not sign a $50,000 house contract until after the election (pp. 168–169).
- Economists widely thought bimetallism might double the price level, since 30:1 was the market ratio. By Gresham's Law ("bad money drives out good"), silver would drive out gold (p. 169). So a $50,000 house would in effect sell for $100,000. Buyer and builder would then struggle to agree, because the politics were uncertain (p. 169).
- **Chicago Daily Tribune (1893):** free coinage would cost wage-earners and depositors nearly half their purchasing power (p. 169).
- Shiller asks how Bryan could come close to election and to committing that "fraud and robbery". He answers that Bryan's popularity came from a sequence of narratives about bimetallism that went viral because they seemed to justify it, at least to some voters (p. 170).
- Shiller argues that most Americans were confused. Gold and silver dollars circulated as equals, and silver certificates read "one silver dollar" (p. 170). The Treasury would give gold for silver *dollars* or certificates, but not for metallic silver. Otherwise anyone could buy silver, swap it for gold, buy more silver, and repeat, getting rich. Silver dollars were limited by the government (p. 170).
- Why the vehemence? Debtors saw halving their burden as salvation, and bimetallism was framed as revenge for 1873. It was also a subtle "capital idea" that few grasped, like Bitcoin's cryptography. Shiller adds that it might compensate for perceived injustice, "the source of much anger"; the cool idea and the injustice "together gave bimetallism intense contagion" (p. 171).

*Intuition note:* The idea was confusing yet exciting, and it offered relief plus justice. Meanwhile the fear of a major devaluation of the money supply made some businessmen delay contracts.

### The Yellow Brick Road (pp. 171–172)
- *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz* (May 1900) is a children's book. Some read it as a parable: yellow brick road = gold standard, silver slippers = Free Silver, Wizard = McKinley, Lion = Bryan, "Oz" = ounce (p. 171).
- It became a best seller and spawned many versions. The film changed the slippers to ruby for color film (p. 172). Shiller says the Oz constellation (a cluster of related stories) "might be" a barely recognizable vestige of the viral gold-silver narrative (p. 172).

### The End of the Gold Standard (pp. 172–173)
- Eichengreen and Temin describe the 1890s "mentality of the gold standard" and its "rhetoric of morality and rectitude" (p. 172).
- By the 1930s, with Keynes's help, the narrative shifted because of catastrophic unemployment. A November 1933 article by Sullivan, written around the time of the dollar's devaluation from $1/20.67$ to $1/35$ ounce of gold and the suspension of convertibility, explained the new narrative (p. 172).
- Per Sullivan (quoting Talleyrand), statesmen invent new terms for odious institutions. The word *devaluation* replaced the negative *debasement* and *inflation* (pp. 172–173).
- The UK left gold in 1931 and the US finally did in 1971 under Nixon. The public accepted it with few dislocations (p. 173). Trump's 2017 advocacy met a largely neutral reaction. Shiller says it "seems likely" that the future will bring new "mutations" of money narratives (p. 173).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A farmer owes \$1,000. Use the chapter's numbers: \$1 = 1/20.67 oz gold, legal ratio 16:1, market ratio about 30:1.
1. Gold standard: 1000 / 20.67 ≈ 48.38 oz of gold.
2. Bimetallism, paying in silver: 16 × 48.38 ≈ 774.1 oz of silver.
3. Market value of that silver in gold terms: 774.1 / 30 ≈ 25.80 oz of gold.
4. Share of the gold-value debt actually repaid: 25.80 / 48.38 ≈ 0.53 (equivalently 16/30 ≈ 0.53).
So the debt shrinks by roughly 47%, consistent with Shiller's "roughly in half". This gap explains why the story was attractive to debtors and alarming to creditors.

## Concepts
- [[economic-narrative]] — a contagious story that changes economic decisions; here gold and silver stories carry moral and class meaning (p. 157).
- [[narrative-contagion-sir]] — stories spread like epidemics; Shiller calls the "gold standard" frequency two epidemics and says "gold bug" followed what appears to be a hump-shaped infective curve (pp. 158–159, 163).
- [[gold-standard]] (new) — currency fixed to a set amount of gold and redeemable on demand (p. 156).
- [[bimetallism]] (new) — two metals back the currency, so debtors choose which to pay in; 16:1 here (p. 158).
- [[fiat-money]] (new) — money not backed by anything (p. 156).
- [[gresham-law]] (new) — "bad money drives out good"; silver would drive out gold (p. 169).
- [[narrative-constellation]] (new) — a cluster of related stories feeding each other (pp. 166, 172).
- [[bank-run-equilibrium]] — a withdrawal panic; here a rumor was one trigger, and depositors seem to have followed scripts from past distress (pp. 164–165).
- [[debt-deflation]] — Shiller only notes that deflation raised debts' real value and hurt debtors (p. 157; canon link; the term is not used in this unit).

## Questions this raises
- How can one tell whether narratives *caused* the 1890s depression, as opposed to merely accompanying it? Shiller only says the topic ought to be considered.
- Why did "devaluation" succeed as a term in the 1930s, and could wording change other economic debates?
- Does confusion about money systems make narratives spread faster, as with Bitcoin?
- How much did debtors' self-interest, rather than mystery or morality, drive the contagion?
