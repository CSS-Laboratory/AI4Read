# Diverse Evidence on the Virality of Economic Narratives

## L0 — One line
Shiller collects evidence from brain science, history, marketing and psychology that stories spread by contagion, and that which stories spread can depend on arbitrary details.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Does narrative contagion really affect the economy? Shiller offers diverse, suggestive evidence. The human brain seems built for stories, and fear circuitry may shape risky decisions. Contagious stories are old. Marketers sometimes engineer contagion (how fast a story spreads) through book jackets, logos and news. Storytellers may guess what others will like, as in Keynes's beauty contest. Emotional and "irrational" mental shortcuts (framing, the affect heuristic) are tied to economic narratives and judgments. (Framing: a retold story sets a reference point. Affect heuristic: strong feelings spill over to unrelated judgments.) He calls the evidence "tantalizing". It matters because it suggests that popular stories may help explain major economic events (causality is left to the next chapter), and that predicting which story will matter is hard.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an epidemic spreading by contact; basic idea of stock-market speculation; no math needed.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Brain-stimulation results suggest brain structure appears to embody a propensity to tell stories; a brain-imaging study found people tend to share content that engages self-related thoughts; and dramatic stories raised two hormones (oxytocin and cortisol) in listeners (p. 53–55).
- Fear of financial risk may use old fear circuitry shared with rats, so past stories may drive decisions when no logical answer exists (p. 56–58).
- Narratives have "gone viral" for millennia. The 1765 "there is no money" complaint was described by a letter-writer, Alexander Windmill, as an epidemic complaint in the American colonies (p. 58–59).
- Marketers sometimes engineer contagion (book jackets, logos, news, symbols). Shiller says popularity is rarely good evidence of a story's quality (p. 60–63).
- Book jackets are tied to a "phishing equilibrium", where, in a competitive market with manipulating rivals and normal profits, no firm can choose not to manipulate too (p. 61).
- Keynes's beauty contest (guessing what others think others think) applies to deciding which stories to spread (p. 63–64).
- Which stories go viral may be essentially random, like sexual-selection runaways in biology (a trait spreading because mates favour it, even if useless) (p. 64–65).
- Framing (a retold story sets a reference point), the representativeness heuristic (judging by resemblance to an idealised story) and the affect heuristic (strong feelings spill over to unrelated matters) are tied to narratives; a survey suggests a local earthquake may raise crash-risk estimates (p. 66–67).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening claim.** Evidence for narrative contagion's economic impact comes from brain structures, fear processing, the history of news media, book jackets, logos and beauty contests (p. 53).

**The Impulse to Convey Stories (p. 53–55).**
- Penfield (1958) electrically stimulated exposed brains of awake patients. This is possible because the brain has no pain receptors (p. 53). Stimulating one spot made a patient repeatedly hear the same music, starting at the same point (p. 53). Another spot produced a dream-like story told in order (p. 54).
- Shiller reads this as showing that brain structure seems to embody the propensity to make music and tell stories as event sequences that trigger emotions (p. 54).
- Falk's lab used fMRI (brain imaging) on people deciding whether to share health news. People tended to share content that enhances self-related thoughts and that involves forecasts about others' mental states (p. 54).
- Zak found that narratives with a "dramatic arc" raise oxytocin and cortisol in listeners, compared with "flat" narratives (p. 54).
- *Intuition note:* These studies suggest storytelling is partly biological. In Zak's experiment, a story with a dramatic arc changed hormone levels in the listener. The text does not say here that this makes such stories spread more.

**Neurological Responses to Stories Evoking Fear (p. 55–58).**
- Words like *panic* and *complacency* may sound like hype. But they describe mental states supported by brain structures, so we should ask whether financial panics share neurology with other panics (p. 55).
- Example: In 2017 the FDIC worried that US banks, reaching for yield, were lengthening the maturity of their investments. A sudden rise in interest rates could make banks pay more to keep depositors than they earn (p. 55).
- No expert reliably forecasts rates years ahead. Bankers have only fading memories of past narratives of rate rises and depositor runs. These feel less relevant after ten years of low rates, but no one can say how much less (p. 55–56).
- Shiller says it "may be best" to see such behaviour as driven by primitive neurological patterns (p. 56).
- Dogs and rodents today share some of these fear-management brain structures, which Shiller takes as evidence of common Mesozoic origins; fear is a normal emotion for all mammals (p. 56). Fear extinction (the fading of fear after danger passes) takes time (p. 56). Pavlov (1927): dogs conditioned to a metronome click with acid had the reaction extinguished when the acid was withheld (p. 56).
- In rats, lateral amygdala neurons fire more during fear acquisition and less during extinction. Not all reduce firing, so some residual fear stays (p. 56). Neuroscientists cited say there is much evidence suggesting that an amygdala–vmPFC–hippocampus circuit underlies extinction and is preserved across evolution (p. 56).
- In humans, thickness of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex correlates with success in fear extinction. PTSD represents failed extinction (p. 57). Shiller says it seems safe to say that evolution of this circuitry is not complete (p. 57).
- A PTSD-like state may afflict a whole population. Miłosz (1951) described a "naked fear" under Stalinism. If everyone in a city fears daily, this gives off "a collective aura" (p. 57).
- Shiller's inference (agreeing with Miłosz): it is reasonable to suggest that fear of job loss is less intense than fear of deportation, and that fear at any level relies on the same brain circuitry. In decisions with no logical answer, such as a risky investment, the mind may hand the decision to circuitry like a rat's. Memories of one's own and others' experience, passed on as narratives, may then decide (p. 57–58).
- Fear may decline by gradual extinction and be reversed by a dramatic new development or mutation in the narrative. Nuclear-weapon narratives seem possibly intense enough to renew fear but apparently have not. Which narrative will have economic impact is hard to predict, like box-office hits (p. 58).
- *Intuition note:* When no calculation can answer a question, old stories and old fears may fill the gap. Fear fades slowly, and a new twist may revive it.

**Narratives Have Been "Going Viral" for Millennia (p. 58–60).**
- In ancient Rome, news and stories circulated at the Forum and via the *praeco* (announcer). *Rumor* was the Latin word for contagious narrative (p. 58).
- Hume (1742): when causes create a passion in a people, many individuals may escape it, but the multitude will "certainly be seized" by it (p. 58).
- 1765, New-London Gazette: Alexander Windmill (apparently a pseudonym) described the sentence "there is no money" as an epidemic complaint repeated in conversation during a depression after the French and Indian War (p. 58–59). He calculated, perhaps with some exaggeration, fifty million repetitions a day in the American colonies. He assumed a million people saying it every twenty minutes through most daylight hours (p. 59).
- Later contagion terms: Mackay (1841), Le Bon (1895), collective consciousness (Durkheim, 1897), collective memory (Halbwachs, 1925), memes (Dawkins, 1976) (p. 59–60).

**Of Book Jackets and Company Logos (p. 60–63).**
- Viral and nonviral narratives may differ in aspects unrelated to our enthusiasm, such as links to other topics. Contagion is often natural but sometimes engineered by marketers (p. 60).
- The modern book jacket was invented around the 1920s. It looks like the publisher's work, so the author does not seem boastful. Early public resistance was real (Mann, 1921) (p. 60). Jackets still spread because readers decide whether to keep them, and displayed jackets reach passersby and visitors (p. 61).
- **Phishing equilibrium** (Akerlof and Shiller): in a competitive market where profits are competed down to normal levels, no single firm can refuse manipulation without risking bankruptcy. A certain level of dishonesty in narrative results (p. 61). Shiller says such equilibria may not be all that bad, noting that an art of book jackets has developed, some with significant value (p. 61).
- News is chosen by the media for viral impact. Example: the 2017 US eclipse was covered relentlessly and as "once in a lifetime", though, as Shiller notes, another US total eclipse would occur in 2024. Shiller judges it not genuinely newsworthy (p. 61–62).
- Logos (the word dates from the 1930s): Lacoste (launched 1933) benefited from founder René Lacoste's tennis fame. The fame is mostly forgotten but the logo persists (p. 62). Shiller suggests that logo marketing "perhaps" works because it increases contagion and familiarity (p. 62).
- Symbols and rituals can lower the narrative's forgetting rate. Experts do not fully understand why but see them linked with success (p. 62).
- Phools wrongly take popularity as evidence of quality. Contagion often results from arbitrary details such as meeting frequency and links to other contagious stories (p. 62–63).
- *Intuition note:* A story can spread because of contact points and links to other stories, not necessarily because it is true or important. Marketers sometimes engineer those points.

**Beauty Contests and Tail Feathers (p. 63–65).**
- Theory of mind is the tendency to model what is in others' minds. Shiller says the contagion of specific narratives may be related to storytellers' impressions of what others will think: people like to hear stories they can retell to others who will like them, so storytellers like to tell such stories (p. 63). Keynes (1936) used the beauty-contest metaphor to explain speculative markets; investors look at what others say and do, and are usually not looking at real evidence about the firm (p. 63).
- Keynes's contest (1936): readers pick the six prettiest of 100 faces. The winner's list is closest to the most popular faces overall (p. 63–64). Picking by one's own taste is not optimal. Picking what others find prettiest is better. Better still is picking what others think others find prettiest, and so on (p. 64). Investors need not follow this chain even if all are rational and know it (p. 64).
- Shiller's extension: when choosing to tell a story, we base the choice on how we think others will react, so the metaphor also applies to story contagion. He says we will likely spread a story, boom-time or despair, if we think others will like it enough to spread it further (p. 64).
- Stories that go viral are "essentially random", like mutations. Sexual selection suggests that winning mutations may be as random as the original ones. Shiller adds that something like this randomness may affect economic narratives going viral (p. 64).
- Prum (2017): sexual selection makes fluctuations like speculative bubbles. The peacock's heavy tail is favoured by females, in a *Fisherian runaway* (p. 65). Shiller says that in both evolutionary biology and narrative economics an ornament or display can become popular for no more reason than that it randomly began to be popular (p. 65).

**Irrational Impulses Inform Economic Narratives (p. 65–66).**
- Bruner: facts do not "stare anybody in the face". Our factual worlds are carpentered, not stumbled upon (p. 65). Narratives mix fact, emotion and human interest (p. 65).
- Abnormal narrative processing (*dysnarrativia*) can teach us about normal processing. Young and Saver list arrested narration, undernarration, denarration and confabulation, each linked to injury in a specific brain part (p. 65–66).
- Schizophrenia's voice-hearing is correlated with volume deficits in specific brain areas, and autism-related narrative disruption is related to brain anomalies (p. 66).

**Framing, the Representativeness Heuristic, and the Affect Heuristic (p. 66–67).**
- A retold story can set a reference point that influences decisions (framing). Representativeness heuristic (Kahneman–Tversky, 1973): expectations follow an idealised story rather than estimated probabilities. For example, we may judge a crisis by resemblance to a remembered one (p. 66).
- Katona felt interviewees invented expectation numbers to please him. Shiller *argues* they were thinking of stories about people and prices, and might answer an inflation question with a moralising story about politicians or unions (p. 66–67).
- Affect heuristic: strong emotions such as fear extend to unrelated events. The "risk as feelings" hypothesis posits a primitive system with its own risk heuristic (p. 67).
- Shiller's survey (with Akerlof, Goetzmann, Kim; since 1989): investors overstate crash risk, influenced by news, especially front-page stories. Respondents gave significantly higher crash probabilities if an earthquake occurred within thirty miles and thirty days (p. 67). Shiller hypothesises local earthquakes start local narratives with negative emotional valence (p. 67).
- Analogous evidence: the World Cup affects economic confidence, shark attacks affect local votes, and background music affects consumers and wine choices (p. 67).
- Trolling appears contagious: a group primed with nasty examples posted more similar comments (p. 67).

**Going Forward (p. 68).** Shiller calls the evidence from neuroscience and related observations "tantalizing" and says it suggests some entirely different explanations of the severity of major economic events. Part II considers organising principles, starting with causality from narratives to economic activity. Part III presents nine perennial narrative constellations (p. 68).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Model: each week, a share $c$ of current story-holders adds one new holder, and a share $f$ forget it. Net weekly growth is $c-f$ (ignoring saturation). Start with 100 holders.
- Baseline $c=0.3$, $f=0.2$: growth 0.1. After 3 weeks: $100\times1.1^3=133.1$.
- A jacket-like device raises contacts, $c=0.5$: growth 0.3. $100\times1.3^3=219.7$.
- A symbol-like device lowers forgetting, $f=0.1$ (with $c=0.3$): growth 0.2. $100\times1.2^3=172.8$.

The story's content is identical in all three cases, but the contact and memory details change its spread. This is the chapter's point about arbitrary details.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — contagious story that may change economic decisions; Shiller seeks evidence it does (p. 53)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — stories spread by contact and fade by forgetting; contagion and forgetting rates can be influenced (p. 60, 62)
- [[beauty contest / higher-order beliefs]] — choosing by what others think others think; here extended to story-telling (p. 63–64)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — framing, representativeness and affect heuristics shape judgments (p. 66–67)
- [[phishing equilibrium]] (new) — competitive market in which rivals manipulate customers and profits are competed to normal levels, so no one firm can choose not to manipulate (p. 61)
- [[fear extinction]] (new) — gradual fading of learned fear after danger passes; may be reversed by new narrative developments (p. 56, 58)
- [[theory of mind]] (new) — modelling others' mental states; may feed story-spreading choices (p. 63)
- [[affect heuristic]] (new) — strong emotion spills over to unrelated judgments, such as risk (p. 67)
- [[Fisherian runaway]] (new) — mate choice may give an evolutionary advantage to a useless trait; analogy for narrative popularity that starts randomly (p. 65)

## Questions this raises
- How can the direction of causality from narratives to economic activity be established (the next chapter's topic, p. 68)?
- How much of the evidence (rat fear circuits, earthquake survey) transfers to bankers' or investors' decisions?
- If viral stories are "essentially random" (p. 64), can anything be predicted about which will matter?
- Is a phishing equilibrium in news and marketing harmless or costly?
