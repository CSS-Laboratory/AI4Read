# The Laffer Curve and Rubik's Cube Go Viral

## L0 — One line
Shiller suggests that the Laffer curve may have gone viral partly because of a vivid story, visual imagery and authorities behind it, as part of a larger "constellation" of tax-cut narratives.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Whether a narrative goes viral is very hard to predict. Shiller uses the Laffer curve as a case study. It is a dull diagram that became famous after Wanniski's 1978 "napkin" story. He suggests that the napkin image might have raised the early contagion rate and may have lowered the forgetting rate. Rubik's Cube spread at the same time, helped by an authority (Scientific American). The Laffer curve was one of many linked stories (Swedish 100% tax rates, Proposition 13, Ayn Rand, Reagan's quips). Shiller thinks such stories likely helped create a public mandate for tax cuts. One lesson he draws is that striking visual images help a story be remembered; he also shows stories spreading in groups.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an epidemic model (contagion rate = how fast a story spreads; forgetting rate = how fast people drop it); the basic income-tax vs. tax-revenue trade-off. The Laffer curve is an inverted-U diagram of tax revenue against tax rate; a "constellation" is a group of related stories; "supply-side economics" is the view that lower taxes and less regulation raise growth.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Contagion and recovery rates of narratives are hard to predict; film insiders say the same about movies (p. 41).
- The Laffer curve went viral mainly through Wanniski's 1978 napkin anecdote, not in 1974 when Laffer reportedly introduced it (p. 44).
- Vivid, unusual visual details may help memory and so raise a story's chance of going viral (pp. 45–47).
- Rubik's Cube and "maximize shareholder value" spread at the same time as the Laffer curve, each helped by an authority or a persuasive phrase (pp. 47–48).
- The Laffer curve was one part of the "supply-side economics" constellation. Shiller discusses Swedish 100% tax stories as part of it, and Proposition 13 and Ayn Rand's novels as related background stories (pp. 48–50).
- Rhetorical form and a celebrity speaker helped Reagan's tax quip spread, with the form aiding its unaltered transmission (p. 51).
- Shiller thinks such narratives likely helped touch off a public mandate for tax cutting, and he suggests they encouraged entrepreneurship (pp. 51–52).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pp. 41–42).** Predicting contagion and recovery rates is among the toughest tasks in narrative study, because mental and social processes cannot be observed precisely (p. 41).
- **Valenti (film industry):** nobody can say what a movie will do before it opens (p. 41).
- **Goldman:** "Nobody knows anything" (p. 41).
- One-hit wonders show how hard it is to go viral. Hits from past years never seem to become real hits again, at least not without significant modification (pp. 41–42).
- Economics has its own one-hit wonder, the Laffer curve (p. 42).

**The Laffer Curve and the Infamous Napkin (pp. 42–45).**
- Art Laffer used the diagram at a 1974 dinner to justify cutting taxes without cutting expenditures (p. 42).
- Figure 5.1 shows how often the words "Laffer curve" appear in ProQuest newspapers and in Google Ngrams books. There are two epidemic-like curves: one rising until the early 1980s, one after 2000. The second became tied to "modern monetary theory" (p. 42; fig. p. 43).
- Contagion was linked to tax cuts for high earners and to Reagan (elected 1980) and Thatcher (1979). Both campaigned on tax cuts (p. 42).
- French newspapers show "la courbe de Laffer" also went viral, but less than in the US and UK. It may not have played a role in Mitterrand's election (pp. 42–43).
- **The narrative's punch line:**
  - Zero tax rate gives zero revenue. A 100% rate gives zero revenue because no one will work. Rates in between give positive revenue (p. 43).
  - The curve is an inverted U, so there are "always" two tax rates for a given revenue. Shiller calls this a surprise (p. 43).
  - To fund the government, the lower rate is better (pp. 43–44).
- The idea that taxes reduce incentives was old (Adam Smith). Mellon's "trickle-down" economics under Coolidge helped cut taxes in the 1920s, but the narrative then lost momentum (p. 44).
- The story did not go viral in 1974. Its contagion is explained by Wanniski's 1978 book *The Way the World Works*. Wanniski told of a steak dinner with Laffer, Cheney and Rumsfeld at which Laffer drew the curve on a napkin (p. 44).
- A napkin was later found among Wanniski's papers and is now in the National Museum of American History. The curator calls it "the real deal" (p. 44).
- **Laffer's reply:** Shiller says Laffer disowned the napkin story. In Laffer's own words, his "only question" about Wanniski's version was that the restaurant used cloth napkins; he adds "that's my story and I'm sticking to it". Shiller says his honesty could not stop a story that was too good to be stopped (pp. 44–45).

*Intuition note:* The idea that taxes reduce incentives was old (p. 44). Shiller suggests that the story's packaging (a fancy restaurant, powerful Washington people, a napkin drawing) helped it go viral (p. 46).

**Visual Aids Go Viral (pp. 45–47).**
- Laffer said he could hardly remember the event after Wanniski's story. Wanniski, a journalist, sensed a good story (p. 45).
- Scientific evidence says unusual visual stimuli aid memory. A graph on a napkin might have raised the initial contagion rate above the forgetting rate (p. 45). It may also have lowered the speed of forgetting, helping the epidemic reach a large share of the population (p. 46).
- The curve suggested we were on the inefficient, declining side. Wanniski said so without data, and economists protested that the US was not there (p. 45).
- The curve may give real guidance for corporate profit taxes. A small country that cuts its rate below others' may attract headquarters and gain revenue (p. 45).
- An objective analysis lacks a punchy story, so it could not have stifled the epidemic (pp. 45–46).
- The story may also have spread because of its urgency and epiphany: a professor drew on a napkin to show officials his idea (p. 46).
- **Support for the memory claim:**
  - Cicero, quoting Simonides: things communicated through the senses are most strongly fixed in the mind; sight is the most acute sense; and things received by hearing or understanding are most easily retained if they are also recommended to the imagination by the "mental eye" (p. 46).
  - Journals find that bizarre mental images at least in some circumstances aid memory. Lorayne advises imagining "a gigantic key growing in a flowerpot" (pp. 46–47).
  - Neuroscience: long-term memory involves many brain regions, including visual-image processing (p. 47).

*Intuition note:* In epidemic terms, a vivid detail may slow forgetting, so a story stays in more heads for longer. A story that is remembered longer has more chances to be retold.

**Rubik's Cube, Corporate Raiders, and Other Parallel Epidemics (pp. 47–48).**
- Rubik's Cube was invented in 1974 by Ernő Rubik. Its narrative claimed it embodied interesting mathematical principles (p. 47).
- Hofstadter's March 1981 *Scientific American* cover story linked it to quantum mechanics and quarks. People forget the details but remember that the cube is "somehow impressive" (p. 47).
- The cube was bigger than the Laffer curve in ProQuest and smaller in Google Ngrams. Both show hump-shaped paths (p. 47).
- "Leveraged buyouts" and "corporate raiders" went viral in the 1980s, often in admiring stories (p. 47).
- "Maximize shareholder value" was not used until the 1970s and grew until the twenty-first century (p. 47).
- Shiller reads each word as adding appeal: maximize suggests science, shareholder recalls whose money started the firm, value sounds idealistic. The phrase puts a nice spin on questionable raider practices (p. 48). He says the narratives that exploited the term are "most certainly eco­nom­ically significant" (p. 48).

**The Laffer Curve, Supply-Side Economics, and Narrative Constellations (pp. 48–50).**
- The Reagan administration cut the top federal income tax bracket from 70% to 28%, the top corporate rate from 46% to 34%, and the top capital gains rate from 28% to 20% in 1981. Capital gains returned to 28% in 1987 (p. 48).
- Shiller's conditional claim: if the Laffer epidemic had even a minor effect on these changes, it must have had a tremendous impact on output and prices (p. 48).
- The curve was one part of "supply-side economics" (growth through less regulation and lower taxes), which went viral at the same time. It was a powerful narrative for three reasons (p. 48):
  - it had visual imagery (the napkin);
  - it had authorities behind it (as the cube had *Scientific American*);
  - it suggested that politicians who raised taxes were fools.
- **Swedish story:**
  - Under Olof Palme, the effective income tax rate on high incomes reportedly went over 100%, and working more left people with less (pp. 48–49).
  - The *Boston Globe* (1976) reported that dentists worked under 30 hours and Bergman left the country (p. 49).
  - Astrid Lindgren's 1976 fairy tale *Pomperipossa in the World of Money* mutated the story further. The "Pomperipossa Effect" may have contributed to the downfall of Palme's government (p. 49).
- Similar 100%+ stories spread, even in the US. They were about government incompetence, but supported the impression that tax rates were too high (p. 49).
- **Evidence for the constellation:** "highest tax bracket" appeared in only 33 ProQuest stories in the 1950s, when the top bracket was 84–92%. It appeared in 520 in the 1980s, while the bracket fell from 70% to 28% (p. 49).
- Attention to the top brackets drew attention to the poorest. "Welfare mother" rose from zero in 1960 to a peak in the early 1970s (pp. 49–50).
- Proposition 13 led to a 1978 California constitutional amendment limiting property tax increases. The "taxpayer revolt" swept the US but came and went in a few years around 1978 (p. 50).
- In the background, free-market narratives grew. *The Fountainhead* (1943) grew slowly and *Atlas Shrugged* (1957) went viral. Rand's influence has kept growing after her 1982 death, unlike the brief taxpayer revolt. Shiller says "it seems" the novels were "a slower but ultimately larger epidemic" (p. 50).
- "Stimulate the economy" emerged in the late 1950s and grew rapidly from 1978 to 1980. It suggested that tax cuts for higher earners might free the "supposedly superior" people to contribute (pp. 50–51).

*Intuition note:* Stories can feed on one another. Shiller says the 100%-plus tax stories were about government incompetence, yet together they supported a general impression that tax rates had gone too high (p. 49).

**Celebrities, Quips, and Politics (pp. 51–52).**
- Shiller says other narratives were "surely influential" in the Reagan and Thatcher elections, for example Reagan's 1986 quip: "If it moves, tax it…" (p. 51).
- Walter Trohan wrote a version in 1967. The quip needed a celebrity, and Reagan was that celebrity (p. 51).
- Reagan's version has parallel if-then lines, each ending in a two-word command. Shiller thinks the form aided unaltered transmission, probably because it suggests everyone complains about taxes (p. 51).
- Shiller concludes it seems likely that the Laffer curve and other supply-side stories touched off an intense public mandate for tax cutting (p. 51).
- He also suggests the tax-cutting constellation propelled entrepreneurship (p. 52). Reagan's 1987 Soviet car-delivery joke (the buyer asks "morning or afternoon?" because the plumber is coming) is his example (p. 52).
- Rubik's Cube was "just a toy". Reagan's jokes made economically powerful narratives that encouraged risk taking and brought profound legal changes (p. 52).
- Narratives are stored in the human brain. The next chapter uses neuroscience to examine this repository (p. 52).

## Toy example
*(Ours; a simplified model, not Shiller's.)* Let a story have 1,000 tellers. Each period, tellers grow by a factor $1 + c - f$, where $c$ is the contagion rate and $f$ is the forgetting rate.
- Plain version: $c = 0.10$, $f = 0.12$. Period 1: $1000 \times 0.98 = 980$. Period 2: $980 \times 0.98 = 960.4$. The story fades.
- Napkin version: a vivid detail lowers forgetting to $f = 0.08$. Period 1: $1000 \times 1.02 = 1020$. Period 2: $1020 \times 1.02 = 1040.4$. The story grows.

A small change in $f$ moves the story across the threshold, which is the pattern Shiller suggests on pp. 45–46.

Laffer-pair check (ours): revenue $=100\,t(1-t)$. At $t=0.2$: $100 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 16$. At $t=0.8$: $100 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = 16$. Two rates give the same revenue, and the lower is cheaper for taxpayers.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — a contagious story that affects economic decisions; here the Laffer curve and the tax-cut stories (p. 42)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — Shiller suggests a narrative can take off when its contagion rate is above its forgetting rate (p. 45); Figure 5.1 shows epidemic-like curves (pp. 41, 42–43, 45)
- [[narrative constellation]] (new) — a group of related stories that feed on each other; here supply-side economics (pp. 48–49)
- [[visual imagery as memory aid]] (new) — vivid, unusual images aid memory and may lower the forgetting rate (pp. 45–47)
- [[celebrity and rhetorical form in transmission]] (new) — a famous speaker and parallel structure help a quip spread unaltered (p. 51)

## Canonical instances
- [[laffer-curve-napkin]] — a dull diagram became famous through an anecdote (pp. 42–45)
- [[rubiks-cube-fad]] — a toy epidemic helped by an authority (p. 47)
- [[maximize-shareholder-value]] — a phrase that gives questionable raider practices a nice spin (pp. 47–48)
- [[pomperipossa-effect]] — the Swedish tax-over-100% story (p. 49)
- [[proposition-13-taxpayer-revolt]] — a short-lived epidemic (p. 50)
- [[reagan-if-it-moves-tax-it]] — a quip made contagious by celebrity and form (p. 51)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Contagion vs. forgetting rates of stories ↔ epidemiology: infection vs. recovery rates — the same threshold logic, but the rates are unobservable here.
- [metaphor] Narrative constellation ↔ network effects: linked stories reinforce each other.
- [structural-analogy] Laffer curve's "two rates for one revenue" ↔ inverted-U relationships elsewhere in economics, where two input values can give the same output value.

## Questions this raises
- If contagion and forgetting rates cannot be observed, can the narrative be tested before it spreads?
- How can one separate the effect of the Laffer story from other causes of the 1980s tax cuts?
- Do economists' objections (the US was not on the declining side) ever slow a popular story?
- Why did some epidemics (taxpayer revolt) fade fast while others (Ayn Rand) grew slowly but larger?
