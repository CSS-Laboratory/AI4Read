# Shiller, R. J. (2019). *Narrative Economics: How Stories Go Viral and Drive Major Economic Events*. Princeton University Press. ISBN 9780691182292.

## In one paragraph
Shiller argues that economics should study how popular stories spread, because they help drive economic events that standard models miss. Stories spread like epidemics: they rise when contagion beats forgetting, then fade. Big events are usually driven by many linked stories, not one. Which stories spread can depend on vivid details, celebrities and random small beginnings. Old stories return in mutated forms. Shiller treats causality as running both ways: stories help cause booms, busts and recessions, and events change stories. He tests this on cases including Bitcoin, the Laffer curve, the gold standard, fear of machines, housing and the 1929 crash. He closes by proposing long, consistent, digitized records of narratives, analysed with epidemic models. The evidence is suggestive, not formal proof.

## The path through the work
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/front-preface]] — Shiller argues that economics should study how popular stories spread like epidemics and change economic behavior, because leaving them out keeps economists blind to a real mechanism of change and a key input to forecasting.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch01-bitcoin]] — Shiller argues that Bitcoin's price and fame owe much to a contagious story (anarchism, mystery, inequality, "the future") rather than to public understanding of its technology: people are interested in it largely because others are.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch02-adventure]] — Shiller argues that economics has lagged most other disciplines in attending to narratives, and that economists can and should learn about narrative from epidemiology, history, other social sciences, literary studies and neuroscience.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch03-contagion]] — Economic narratives spread and fade like disease epidemics, and big economic events usually cannot be explained by one simple story but need a list of many narratives (constellations and confluences).
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch04-do]] — Which narratives go viral depends partly on a human element interacting with economic circumstances and partly on apparently random small beginnings that can get amplified, so history is less orderly than later stories make it look.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch05-laffer]] — Shiller suggests that the Laffer curve may have gone viral partly because of a vivid story, visual imagery and authorities behind it, as part of a larger "constellation" of tax-cut narratives.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch06-diverse]] — Shiller collects evidence from brain science, history, marketing and psychology that stories spread by contagion, and that which stories spread can depend on arbitrary details.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch07-causality]] — Shiller argues that causality runs both ways: new contagious narratives, often random in origin, help cause economic events, and economic events change narratives.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch08-seven]] — Shiller proposes seven general features of economic narratives (speed and size, low visibility, constellations, mutation, truth not enough to stop false stories, repetition, human attachment) as a framework for spotting and studying them.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch09-recurrence]] — Perennial economic narratives come back after long gaps because they mutate, as flu viruses do, and this part of the book will study nine of them, organized by theme (pp. 107–113).
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch10-panic]] — Shiller argues that stories about public confidence (financial panic, then business confidence, then consumer confidence) have influenced economic fluctuations and spread in epidemic-like waves, and that the Great Depression narrative remains a powerful one.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch11-frugality]] — Two opposed, long-running narratives, frugality and conspicuous consumption, change how much people spend and so can deepen a depression or fuel a boom.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch12-gold]] — The 1890s fight over gold versus silver money shows, in Shiller's account, a monetary narrative going viral with the help of debtors' self-interest, class resentment, moral and religious imagery, and a mysterious idea.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch13-labor]] — Shiller argues that the fear of machines destroying jobs is a very old narrative that returns in epidemic waves, each time in a mutated form, and that it peaked in the 1930s, when (he infers) it worsened and prolonged the Great Depression.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch14-automation]] — The "machines will take all jobs" story keeps returning in mutated forms, and Shiller argues that it may itself cause or deepen recessions by cutting spending and confidence.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch15-real]] — Shiller argues that stories treating homes as speculative investments in scarce land, spread by media, indexes, promotions and social comparison, helped drive the 1997–2005 US housing boom, alongside supply constraints.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch16-stock]] — The 1929 "crash" narrative (human folly, suicide, moral judgment, celebrity legend) went viral, still lingers in public thinking, and probably serves to amplify end-of-boom drops.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch17-boycotts]] — Shiller argues that contagious anger narratives about business ("boycott", "profiteer", "fair wage") can lead people to see spending as a moral matter, and so can help bring on, or at least contribute to, depressions and recessions.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch18-wage]] — Shiller argues that the mid-century wage-price spiral story, with its moral outrage at greedy unions and business, likely provides the best explanation for the epochal changes in trend of inflation and interest rates.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/ch19-future]] — Old economic narratives will probably return at unpredictable times, so economists should build long, consistent, digitized time series of what people say and combine them with formal contagion models.
- [[shiller-2019-narrative/app-applying]] — Shiller argues that the Kermack-McKendrick SIR epidemic model, with modifications, is a workable starting framework for how economic narratives spread, peak and fade.

## Big ideas across units
- **Stories spread like epidemics.** A story spreads when contagion exceeds recovery, where recovery means forgetting. The result is a hump-shaped curve, and the SIR model gives it a formal form. Units: front-preface, ch01-bitcoin, ch03-contagion, ch08-seven, ch10-panic, ch19-future, app-applying.
- **Virality is partly arbitrary.** Vivid images, celebrities, rhetorical form, marketing and random small beginnings can matter more than a story's quality. Units: ch04-do, ch05-laffer, ch06-diverse, ch08-seven, ch11-frugality.
- **Causality runs both ways.** Narratives are treated as causes, possibly exogenous, and not only as by-products of economic news. Units: front-preface, ch07-causality, ch09-recurrence, ch10-panic, ch13-labor, ch14-automation.
- **Constellations beat single stories.** Big events need a list of linked or co-occurring narratives. Units: ch03-contagion, ch05-laffer, ch07-causality, ch08-seven, ch10-panic, ch12-gold, ch18-wage.
- **Old stories mutate and return.** Perennial narratives come back in new forms, as flu viruses do. Units: ch08-seven, ch09-recurrence, ch12-gold, ch13-labor, ch14-automation, ch15-real, ch18-wage, ch19-future.
- **Narratives shape spending, confidence and policy.** Stories of panic, modesty, anger at profiteers or fear of machines can lead people to postpone buying, investing and hiring. Units: ch10-panic, ch11-frugality, ch13-labor, ch14-automation, ch17-boycotts, ch18-wage.
- **Moral, emotional and identity framing helps stories spread.** Examples are human-interest figures, class resentment, moral judgment, anger at greed and patriotism. Units: ch01-bitcoin, ch08-seven, ch11-frugality, ch12-gold, ch16-stock, ch17-boycotts, ch18-wage.
- **Booms and crashes are narrative-laden.** Bitcoin, gold and silver, housing and the 1929 crash all show asset-market stories at work. Units: ch01-bitcoin, ch12-gold, ch15-real, ch16-stock.
- **Narratives can be measured.** Word-frequency plots serve as evidence in the cases. Shiller then proposes systematic data collection and epidemic models. Units: front-preface, ch10-panic, ch12-gold, ch19-future, app-applying.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** front-preface, ch01-bitcoin, ch03-contagion, ch08-seven and ch19-future give the thesis, the epidemic logic, the general propositions and the research agenda. Ch07-causality supports the causal claim that the rest of the book relies on.
- **Dependencies:** ch03 adapts the epidemiology that ch02 introduces, and ch01 defers its epidemiology to ch03. Ch08's propositions frame the historical cases that follow. Ch09 opens the perennial-narrative chapters and lists their themes. Ch07 picks up the causality question that ch06 leaves open. App-applying gives the formal SIR model behind ch03 and the models in ch19, and it needs differential equations and exponentials.
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch02-adventure, a survey of other disciplines with no math. Ch06-diverse can also wait, since Shiller himself calls its evidence "tantalizing". The appendix can be left out if you do not need the model equations.
- **Case chapters (ch10–ch18):** these can be read selectively once ch08 and ch09 are in hand. Ch11, ch13 and ch17 all use Depression-era background, and ch10 sets up the Depression narrative. Ch14 follows directly from ch13. Ch16 and ch15 take up asset booms and crashes. Ch12 links back to the Bitcoin theme of ch01. Ch05-laffer and ch04-do are short, self-contained cases of virality.
