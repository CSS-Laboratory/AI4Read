# Labor-Saving Machines Replace Many Jobs

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that the fear of machines destroying jobs is a very old narrative that returns in epidemic waves, each time in a mutated form, and that it peaked in the 1930s, when (he infers) it worsened and prolonged the Great Depression.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks why fear of "labor-saving machinery" and "technological unemployment" repeatedly goes viral, and what it does to the economy. Shiller follows the narrative from the ancient world through the Luddites (1811), the Swing Riots (1830), and the depressions of the 1870s and 1890s. He then covers the 1920s–30s, when stories about robots, technocracy (rule by technicians) and underconsumption (too little buying power) spread. Waves are linked to new machines, new words, news events and high unemployment. In the 1930s he infers that the narrative must have made people hesitate to spend, invest and hire. It faded after about 1935, and "electronic brains" took over after World War II.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an economic narrative spreading like an epidemic (earlier chapters); aggregate demand; the Great Depression as background.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The job-replacement narrative is old: Homer, Aristotle and Hero of Alexandria already imagined self-moving machines (pp. 174–175). Perennial narratives return in modified form (p. 174).
- Waves are triggered by "mutations" (new machines, new words) and are especially contagious in depressions (pp. 175–176).
- Both the 1870s and 1890s depressions had labor-saving-machinery stories blamed for unemployment (pp. 176–180). In the 1870s a counternarrative (a competing story: such processes raise the number of jobs) appeared, but Shiller says the discussion suggests it was not persuasive (p. 178).
- The word robot (1921) and the phrase *technological unemployment* (first 1917, epidemic from 1928) gave the story vivid labels (pp. 181–183).
- The 1928 official unemployment rate and Slichter's remark helped start the 1928 epidemic, before the 1929 crash (pp. 184–185).
- Underconsumption (people cannot buy all that machines produce) and purchasing-power stories linked machines to depressed demand and, in Shiller's account, shaped politics and behavior (Roosevelt, Senator Glass) (pp. 187–191).
- Technocracy (a society run by technicians) went viral (p. 193), and Shiller infers that it must have caused hesitation to spend, invest and hire (p. 194).
- The chicken-and-egg problem between real innovation and media interest gets the answer Shiller calls likely: "a little of both" (p. 187).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 174)
Fears that machines (water, wind, horse, steam, or better use of human power) replace workers and cause mass unemployment have a very long history. Shiller says these perennial narratives are reappearing in modified form in the twenty-first century and could damage confidence, as they did before (p. 174). He tracks epidemics of the terms *labor-saving machinery* and *technological unemployment* (Figure 13.1, Google Ngrams of books, 1800–2008, unsmoothed) (pp. 174–175).

### From Ancient Times to the Swing Riots (pp. 174–176)
- **Ancient roots.** The *Iliad* describes the self-navigating "automatic" tripod of Hephaestus. Aristotle (c. 350 BCE) wrote that if instruments worked on their own, "chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves." Hero of Alexandria wrote *Automata* (first century BCE). Water mills existed by the first century BCE. Shiller concludes that the idea, with fears of unemployment, predates the Common Era (pp. 174–175).
- **Eighteenth century.** Newspapers show great interest in technology but not much alarm about jobs. The term *industrial revolution* does not come up in Shiller's search of them (p. 175).
- **Nineteenth century.** Concern takes center stage, and the narrative is particularly contagious in depressions (pp. 175–176).
- **Luddites, 1811 (UK).** A mythical leader, Ludd. The mutation that made the old narrative virulent was a new power loom eliminating weavers' jobs (p. 176).
- **Swing Riots, 1830 (Britain).** A response to farm-job losses from the new mechanical thresher; imaginary "Captain Swing"; machinery destroyed. Shiller notes the change was frightening because farm life was ancient and new jobs probably meant moving to crowded cities. People used terms like *labor-saving devices/machines/processes*, not *technological unemployment* (p. 176).

*Intuition note.* The story stays the same, but each wave attaches to a new machine and new words. That is what "mutation" means here (pp. 175–176).

### Depression Narratives of the 1870s (pp. 176–179)
- **1873–79 depression.** Shiller says concern that labor-saving inventions were at least partly to blame took center stage, "likely worsening the depression." He grants the usual US explanation (speculation and the 1873 banking panic) but suggests the narrative may help to explain why it went global. Farmers adopted labor-saving machinery faster, and more workers destroyed machines while hired farm laborers threatened violence (pp. 176–177).
- **1876 Centennial Exhibition (Philadelphia).** It showed mostly modern machinery (a 1400-horsepower Corliss engine; agricultural machines) rather than history. Shiller says it raised fears about jobs (p. 177).
- **Philadelphia Inquirer 1876 (quoted voice).** Unemployment degrades and ruins; one man now does the work of a hundred people of fifty years earlier; steam from seven tons of coal makes 33,000 miles of cotton thread in ten hours, equal to 70,000 women's hand labor; consumption does not keep pace with machine production, so markets become glutted (pp. 177–178).
- **1879 Senate committee.** Senator Hoar set it up to report on how far labor-saving processes had displaced manual labor (p. 178).
- **Counternarrative (Daily American editorial, 1879).** Labor-saving processes tend to elevate the laboring classes, even if with hardship. Shiller says the overall discussion suggests such arguments "were not persuasive" (p. 178).
- **Henry George, *Progress and Poverty* (1879).** The book held that the technological advances were creating inequality and more poverty. In its own words: if labor-saving inventions reached perfection, wages and interest would be nothing and rent would take everything, leaving workers at landowners' mercy (pp. 178–179).

### Labor-Saving Inventions and the Depression of the 1890s (pp. 179–181)
- **LA Times 1894 (quoted voice).** Machinery and increased output have quite a lot to do with the depression. Hat-making labor productivity multiplied nearly nine times (people cannot wear nine times as many hats); flour labor down 80%, yet people can eat no more flour (p. 179).
- **SF Chronicle 1894, "The Great Problem."** The rich richer, the poor poorer; unsolved, it warns of a cataclysm (p. 180).
- **Dumbwaiter (1895).** A push-button system, one button per floor (p. 180).
- **Department stores.** A 1897 Chicago Daily Tribune letter calls stores "labor-saving machines" that could let one-third of the distribution workers do as well. Shiller notes Marshall Field's stores and Chicago's "Loop" railway as an efficient-retailing innovation that may have prompted the letter writer (p. 180).
- **Trusts.** In 1899 John C. Chase called the trust "a labor saving machine," apparently meaning it adopts such machines to dispense with labor (p. 181).

### Machines, Robots, and Future Technological Unemployment (pp. 181–182)
- **Forster, "The Machine Stops" (1909).** A future of buttons for everything; the machine malfunctions and destroys a world too dependent on it (p. 181).
- **Čapek, *R.U.R.* (1921).** Coined *robot* (Czech for worker), replacing *labor-saving invention* and *automaton*. English translation in New York, October 1922. The story is about the robots revolting. Shiller notes it reached only a small base, so the epidemic was gradual, and that the idea of a robot may have needed more development by creative people. Few newspapers mentioned robots in the 1920s (pp. 181–182).

### Before 1930: Increasingly Vivid Narratives (pp. 182–186)
- **1929 traffic "robot."** The LA Times described an automatic traffic light in Medford, Massachusetts, that replaced policemen (pp. 182–183).
- **Vocabulary.** *Technological unemployment* first appeared in 1917 and began its epidemic upswing in 1928, peaking in 1933. The term *power age* followed a parallel curve (p. 183). Shiller says that in the 1870s about half the US labor force was in farming and by 1880 a fifth, and that today it is under 2% (p. 183).
- **Prosperous start.** The epidemic began in 1928, a time of prosperity. In public talk, unemployment was blamed entirely on technological unemployment and not connected to any weakness in the US economy (p. 183). Philip Snowden, in the New York Times, called America the leader in labor-saving devices and the problem "to free the human being from slavery to the iron man" (p. 184).
- **Efficiency experts.** Time-and-motion studies treated workers like machines, and efficiency became associated with technological unemployment (p. 184).
- **Trigger events.** In March 1928 Senator Wagner asked the Labor Department for a study. It gave the first official US unemployment figures: 1,874,030 unemployed and 23,348,602 wage earners, "implying" 7.4%. This high rate amid abundance made people ask what caused it (p. 184). In April 1928 the Baltimore Sun reported Slichter: "at present the most serious" cause is technological unemployment; jobs are eliminated "faster than we are creating them." Shiller says the words plus the new statistics created a contagion, and the agricultural depression began to look like a model for an industrial one (pp. 184–185).
- **Stuart Chase, *Men and Machines* (May 1929).** Chase argued (his voice) that if purchasing power has reached its limit while mechanization accelerates, "only unemployment can result", and he called this a madhouse economy. Shiller stresses that the narrative started going viral before the crash; real stocks rose a further 20% in the five months after publication (p. 185).
- **National Business Show (21–26 October 1929, New York).** It showed office machines; a report said the office of the future would be a factory with "the robot" as principal worker (pp. 185–186).

*Intuition note.* Shiller's point is about timing: the fear was spreading in 1928–29, before the crash. So the crash was not what started it (p. 185).

### The 1930s: A New Form of Luddism Prevails (pp. 186–187)
- By 1930 the crash itself was often attributed to a surplus of goods people could not afford (p. 186).
- **Bix's theory (cited).** In most of the 1920s, popular innovations (Model T, radio, home electrification) did not obviously replace jobs. Union alarms were not contagious, since people had heard few job-replacement stories (pp. 186–187). By the 1930s the news carried job-replacing stories (dial phones, steel mills, linotype, sound movies, tractors, and so on), and unemployment reached an estimated 25% in 1933 (p. 187).
- **Causality.** Did stories follow the pace of innovation, or did media interest follow public concern? Shiller's "likely answer" is "a little of both" (p. 187).

### Underconsumption, Overproduction, and the Purchasing Power Theory of Wages (pp. 187–192)
- **Theory.** Unlike *technological unemployment*, the labor-saving-machines narrative was strongly tied to the idea that people could not consume all machine output, so chronic unemployment follows (p. 187). Its origins go back to the mercantilists (1600s). The popular terms appear around the 1870s; George called it an "absurdity" (p. 188). Underconsumption narratives appeared five times as often in the 1930s as in any other decade. Shiller calls it then mostly a popular theory, not an academic one (p. 188).
- **Purchasing-power theory of wages.** It said excessive competition had pushed wages so low that workers could not buy output, so forcing employers to raise wages would cure the Depression. Shiller notes the reality that deflation necessitates wage cuts. Cassel (1935) called these "charlatan teachings" (p. 188).
- **Roosevelt, 1932.** He told an "Alice in Wonderland" story casting Hoover as the Jabberwock, mocking claims that more production creates more buying, with foreign sales financed by loans and blocked by the Hawley-Smoot tariff. Shiller says the speech proposed investor protections, not a solution to underconsumption. The 1933 National Industrial Recovery Act tried to enforce fair wages; the outcome comes in chapter 17 (pp. 188–189).
- **Weakness of the theory.** Shiller says academic economists never seriously embraced it and it was never soundly explained. Often it was presented as an adjunct to technological unemployment, but some accounts mention no technology. Davis (1934, Agricultural Adjustment Administration; quoted voice) said the concentration of business, farm marketing and labor bargaining had reduced the area of the free market and increased the power of those controlling them. Shiller reads this as Davis seeing concentration as amplifying technological unemployment (pp. 189–190).
- **Social costs.** About a million workers of Mexican origin were deported ("repatriated") to free up jobs, with little public protest (p. 190).
- **Dial telephone.** Patented 1892, but during the Depression the focus turned to operators' lost jobs. In 1930 the Senate installed dial phones, and three weeks later Senator Carter Glass moved to remove them, objecting to becoming "one of the employes of the telephone company without compensation." The resolution passed. Shiller says it is hard to imagine without high unemployment, and that the story fed the atmosphere of fear (pp. 190–191).
- **Robots as cause.** LA Times 1931 (quoted voice): each man replaced is a lost consumer. Al Smith (1931): a man unsure of his job hoards, not spends. Einstein (1933): the depression is traced, for the most part, to technical progress cutting purchasing power. The LA Times said robots equaled "80 million hand-workers" against a 40 million male labor force (pp. 191–192).

*Intuition note.* The underconsumption story runs: machine replaces worker, worker loses income, demand falls. Shiller adds that even a man who has not yet lost his job will consume less because of the prospect of losing it, and he cites Al Smith (1931) for this. He reports the machine-to-demand chain as a popular explanation, and notes that academic economists never seriously embraced underconsumption (pp. 189, 191–192).

### A Word Is Born: Technocracy (pp. 192–194)
- By 1932 the US stock market had lost over 80% of its 1929 value. Shiller says a big part of the answer to why was a viral narrative that modern industry could produce more than people would ever want, leaving a persistent surplus (p. 192).
- **Technocracy / technocrat.** Older words, used in the 1920s; Veblen had imagined a "soviet of technicians" (pp. 192–193). A Columbia group led by Howard Scott called itself "Technocracy"; by 1933 Scott was as famous as movie stars (p. 193). It proposed "electric dollars" representing energy. *The A B C of Technocracy* (1933, pseudonym Frank Arkright) stated a billion horsepower of capacity, one horsepower equal to ten men's labor, and two eight-hour days a week of running time for those laborers. Shiller says the book gave credence to the idea that rising Depression unemployment was the start of an alarming new permanent condition. He compares the story to Bitcoin, pseudonym included (p. 193).
- **Effect.** Shiller says the idea of a world owned by technicians was frightening to most people and "must have resulted in a hesitation to spend, invest, and hire," which he says worsened and prolonged the Great Depression. The New York Times (1933) reported business contracts held up by fear (p. 194). Shiller says the technological unemployment narrative appears to have saturated the population by sometime in the 1930s, after which people no longer needed the phrase (p. 194).

### The Narrative Turns to World War II (pp. 194–195)
- Technological unemployment faded after 1935 but kept some influence before the war (p. 194). A Nazi Party official promised in 1933 to make replacing men with machines illegal in Germany (p. 195). Chaplin's *Modern Times* (1936), with the malfunctioning feeding machine, marked a narrative still remembered. In the war, robot soldiers and "robot bombs" sounded far-fetched or ineffective; the news was of human heroism (p. 195).
- After the war the narrative needed a new twist: "electronic brains" (computers), peaking around 1960, taken up in the next chapter (p. 195).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A simple epidemic model for one narrative. Population 1,000; 10 "infected" (retelling the story), 990 susceptible. Each step: new infections = 0.4 × S × I / 1000; recoveries = 0.2 × I.
- Step 1: new = 0.4 × 990 × 10 / 1000 = 3.96; recover = 2. I = 10 + 3.96 − 2 = 11.96; S = 986.04.
- Step 2: new = 0.4 × 986.04 × 11.96 / 1000 ≈ 4.72; recover = 0.2 × 11.96 = 2.39. I ≈ 11.96 + 4.72 − 2.39 = 14.29; S ≈ 981.32.
- Step 3: new ≈ 0.4 × 981.32 × 14.29 / 1000 ≈ 5.61; recover ≈ 2.86. I ≈ 17.04.
- (added step) I rises while 0.4 × S/1000 > 0.2, i.e. while S > 500. A "mutation" that raises 0.4 to, say, 0.8 would make I grow again whenever S > 250 (since 0.8 × S/1000 > 0.2 then). This mirrors the chapter's waves, though the chapter itself gives no model.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — a contagious story that changes economic decisions; here, machines replace jobs (p. 174)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — narratives spread, mutate and fade like epidemics; Ngram curves are read as epidemic curves (pp. 175, 183)
- [[technological-unemployment-narrative]] (new) — belief that labor-saving machines cause job loss; also "labor-saving machinery," "robots" (pp. 174, 183)
- [[underconsumption-theory]] (new) — popular idea that people cannot buy all machine output, so chronic unemployment results (p. 187)
- [[purchasing-power-theory-of-wages]] (new) — popular 1930s claim that forced higher wages would cure the Depression (p. 188)
- [[counternarrative]] (new) — competing story, e.g., labor-saving processes increase jobs (p. 178)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] narrative epidemic with mutation ↔ epidemiology: SIR contagion with new strains renewing spread — the chapter uses epidemic curves and "mutation" as analogy.
- [structural-analogy] fear of job loss cutting spending (Al Smith's hoarding, p. 192) ↔ macroeconomics: precautionary saving reduces aggregate demand.
- [metaphor] Technocracy's pseudonymous book ↔ finance: the Bitcoin story and Satoshi Nakamoto, as Shiller notes (p. 193).

## Questions this raises
- How can one tell whether media coverage drives public fear or follows it (the "chicken or egg" problem, p. 187)?
- Why were some waves (1920s) harmless while others (1930s) were contagious? Is Bix's explanation testable?
- How much did the narrative cause the Depression, compared with other causes, given that Shiller says only "likely" and "must have"?
- What new mutations bring the narrative back in the twenty-first century (p. 174)?
