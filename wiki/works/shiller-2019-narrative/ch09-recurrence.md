# Recurrence and Mutation

## L0 — One line
Perennial economic narratives come back after long gaps because they mutate, as flu viruses do, and this part of the book will study nine of them, organized by theme (pp. 107–113).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This chapter opens the book's part on "perennial" narratives. It asks how old economic stories can return and still spread. Shiller's answer borrows from epidemiology. Narratives mutate (new celebrity, images, punch line, or new associations), and creative people are probably often involved in designing the return, by linking an old story to a current epidemic. Mutated narratives can recur across countries and decades. Shiller then lists the biggest US downturns since 1854, which he treats as narratives themselves. He says no final proof of causality is possible, but narratives likely helped bring them about. Finally he lists nine narrative themes for the remaining chapters, ordered by theme, not by date.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an epidemic spreading through a population (from earlier chapters); basic knowledge of recessions and depressions.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Disease epidemics can recur after a mutation overcomes acquired immunity or raises contagiousness. Shiller uses this as a model for narrative recurrence (p. 108).
- The 1918 flu and the Great Depression are paired as cases of unusually intense, surprising "virulence." In the Depression the "disease" was carried by narratives (p. 108).
- A mutated narrative tends to have different features from the original, such as celebrity, images and punch line, although the gold-standard and bimetallism narratives share some deep similarity with the Bitcoin narrative. Shiller says that attaching a popular celebrity's name to a new money story "may be necessary for contagion" (pp. 108–109).
- A mutation can tie a story to economic decisions, or twist it into a different lesson. The labor-saving-machines story produced fear in the 1930s and a sense of opportunity in the 1990s (p. 109).
- Recurrence can be random accident, but Shiller thinks it more likely that creative people were involved in some element of the design, linking an old, proven story to a live epidemic. Recurrent narratives tend to be international, tend to be stronger in some countries, and are more similar in countries sharing a language or borders (pp. 109–110).
- Distant history is still useful. The "Heads I win, tails you lose" story has circulated since 1847, perhaps earlier (p. 110).
- Shiller calls past recessions and depressions narratives in themselves, active in producing later events. Thought in a downturn tends to emphasize the last large downturn and the record-holder, the Great Depression (p. 112).
- Shiller admits no final proof of causality, but says the narratives' cumulative influence is "beyond circumstantial" (p. 112).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 107–108)
- Earlier chapters covered the elements of narrative economics. This part covers nine "narrative constellations" (a term Shiller does not define here) that are perennial: they "won't completely go away" and appear in mutated forms (p. 107).
- Themes named: machines replacing workers, a return to the gold standard, special value of real estate and stocks, and evil businesses or labor unions (p. 107).
- Such narratives affect behavior by changing the popular understanding of the economy, perceptions of reality, ideas of what is meaningful and moral, or scripts for individual behavior (p. 107).
- Epidemiological analogy: an epidemic (flu, measles, mumps) can recur after a mutation changes its contagiousness. Epidemics tend to recur after a mutation overcomes acquired immunity, though sometimes the mutation results from environmental change that increases contagion (p. 108).
- The 1918 flu cost more lives than World War I. It mirrors the 1930s Great Depression, except that narratives carried the "disease." In both, virulence was especially intense and surprising (p. 108).

*Intuition note:* A disease can return when the virus changes enough that earlier infection no longer protects people. Shiller draws a parallel: a narrative also mutates (for example, a new celebrity's name) and can then spread again, and he says such variation may be necessary for contagion.

### How economic narratives mutate (pp. 108–109)
- When a narrative reappears, in another country or decades later, the mutated version tends to differ from the original in its celebrity, visual images and punch line (p. 108).
- Example: the gold standard and bimetallism narratives (ch. 12) have some deep similarity to the Bitcoin narrative, with William Jennings Bryan in place of Satoshi Nakamoto. Shiller says the next new money narrative will have yet another celebrity's name. A creator of a successful future electronic currency will best craft a contagious story, for example by attaching a popular celebrity. This variation "may be necessary for contagion" (pp. 108–109).
- A second kind of mutation comes when an event changes the narrative's associations, for example by showing it to be or not to be politically correct. People hesitate to repeat stories that would link them to a scandalous event (p. 109).
- A mutation (or a change in the narrative's surroundings) may tie the story better to economic decisions, and so make it an economic narrative (p. 109).
- A mutation may also raise contagion but twist the story so that it is no longer the same economic narrative. It may then carry a different moral (p. 109).
- Example: the labor-saving-machines narrative (ch. 13) created fear in the 1930s Depression. It mutated (ch. 14) to create a sense of opportunity in the dot-com boom of the 1990s (p. 109).
- Consequence for researchers: key words may turn up in searches over a much longer span than the period of their specific economic meaning (p. 109).
- A narrative can matter even when its timing does not match the event. It may plant a latent fear (technology will replace my job), and this may change behavior years later, when another narrative or news makes the feared replacement seem imminent (p. 109).

*Intuition note:* Searching for a phrase over time can mislead, because the same words may carry different meanings in different eras. A story can also sit quietly as a fear and affect behavior long after it first spread.

### How economic narratives recur (pp. 109–110)
- The mutations behind recurrence can be random accidents. More likely, creative people (marketing experts, politicians, phishers, social media enthusiasts) were involved in some element of the design (pp. 109–110).
- Such people know that old narratives proved their potential by going viral. These narratives are no longer contagious. The celebrity attached to the original may be forgotten or discredited, and the narrative may have been co-epidemic with another lost narrative. So the creators try to link the old story to some extant epidemic (p. 110).
- Recurrent narratives tend to have an international scope, partly because the news media learned long ago to watch foreign news, because what is viral in one country can often be made contagious in another (p. 110).
- At any time, narrative epidemics tend to be stronger in some countries than in others. They are more similar in countries that share a language or borders (p. 110).
- Most examples are American: Shiller knows the US best, and the NBER has chronicled US expansions and contractions back to 1854 (p. 110).
- **Critics' view (as Shiller reports it):** institutional change in the US has been so profound that distant history teaches little. **Shiller replies:** events and reactions 50, 100 and 150 years ago are surprisingly similar to today's (p. 110).
- Example: the "Heads I win, tails you lose" coin-toss story has circulated since 1847 (perhaps earlier). It was then sometimes attached to the Whig Party, Zachary Taylor or Richard Cobden. The stories were not told exactly as today, but the themes are surprisingly similar (p. 110).

### Big economic events, big narrative lessons (pp. 111–112)
NBER-defined biggest US events since 1854, to which later chapters return (p. 111):
- 1857–59 depression, followed by secession (1860–61) and the Civil War (1861–65), the most lethal US war (p. 111).
- 1873–79 depression that led to Henry George's *Progress and Poverty* (1879), the best-selling US economics book of all time. It accused the unrestrained free market of worsening inequality (p. 111).
- 1890s depression: two contractions (1893–94, 1895–97). Unemployment always exceeded 8% from 1893 to 1899. The period coincided with the Spanish-American War and the Philippine War (p. 111).
- Three short contractions 1907–14, beginning with the Panic of 1907. It ended only with advances by J. P. Morgan and other bankers and led to the Federal Reserve System. World War I followed (p. 111).
- 1920–21: brief but extreme depression with the sharpest US deflation ever (p. 111).
- Great Depression: US 1930–41, unemployment uniformly above 8%. Contractions 1929–33 and 1937–38. It took its name from Lionel Robbins's 1934 book. World War II followed immediately (pp. 111–112).
- 1973–75: severe recession linked to a Middle East war and oil embargo. Otto Eckstein called it the "Great Recession" in a 1978 book (p. 112).
- 1980–82: severe recession with two contractions, also linked to a Middle East war. At the time it too was called the "Great Recession" (p. 112).
- 2007–09: also named the "Great Recession"; this time the name "really went viral and has stuck" (p. 112).

Shiller's claims about these events (p. 112):
- The recessions and depressions are narratives in themselves and are active in producing later events. Thought in any downturn tends to emphasize the last large downturn and the record-holder, the Great Depression.
- Economic historians usually list contemporaneous events (bank failures, strikes, government acts, gold discoveries, crop failures, stock market events). Shiller instead looks at the narratives that likely helped bring on the downturns or worsen them.
- He says no final proof of causality is possible, because the events are very complicated and many narratives are involved. But the cumulative influence of narratives is "beyond circumstantial."

*Intuition note:* Thought in a downturn tends to focus on the last large downturn and the record-holder, so past crises feed into how a new one is understood. Shiller says there can be no final proof of causality, but he calls the cumulative influence of narratives "beyond circumstantial".

### The nine perennial narratives (p. 113)
1. Panic versus confidence
2. Frugality versus conspicuous consumption
3. Gold standard versus bimetallism
4. Labor-saving machines replace many jobs
5. Automation and AI replace almost all jobs
6. Real estate booms and busts
7. Stock market bubbles
8. Boycotts, profiteers, and evil business
9. The wage-price spiral and evil labor unions

- Most readers will recognize the recent forms but not the older ones (p. 113).
- Some chapters present opposing pairs, which suggest opposite actions and moral judgments. One constellation may work toward extinguishing the other, or may reinforce it through the controversy it creates (p. 113).
- The chapters are organized by theme, not chronology, because the themes matter beyond their moment. The aim is to extract common themes that help us recognize and anticipate the effects of future narratives (p. 113).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not Shiller's.)* Describe a story by four features: core theme, celebrity, image, punch line.
- Story A (1890s): theme "new money will fix things," celebrity Bryan, image "cross of gold," punch line "silver too."
- Story B (today): same theme, celebrity Nakamoto, image "digital coin," punch line "no banks."
- Shared features: 1 of 4, so overlap = 1/4 = 25%.
- In Shiller's picture, a mutated narrative tends to differ from the original in surface features (celebrity, images, punch line) while keeping some deep similarity, as with Bryan and Nakamoto. The 25% is only our illustration, not a measure Shiller uses.
