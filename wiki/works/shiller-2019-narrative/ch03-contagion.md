# Contagion, Constellations, and Confluence

## L0 — One line
Economic narratives spread and fade like disease epidemics, and big economic events usually cannot be explained by one simple story but need a list of many narratives (constellations and confluences).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller asks whether the logic of disease epidemics can explain how economic stories rise and fall. His answer: an epidemic needs a contagion rate above the recovery rate. In narratives, "recovery" means loss of interest or forgetting. Attention to bimetallism, Bitcoin and four economic models shows hump-shaped curves like an Ebola curve. Large narratives are often constellations of smaller stories. A confluence is a set of narratives not viewed as associated with one another but with similar economic effects at one time. Shiller proposes that economic fluctuations are substantially driven by contagion of oversimplified narratives. Big events usually cannot be explained by one simple story.

**Prerequisites:** rates of change; the idea of a time series; basic Keynesian income-spending rounds (explained in the text).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An epidemic starts only if the contagion rate exceeds recovery (plus death) rate; it falls when recovery outweighs contagion, giving a hump (pp. 18, 21).
- Contagion can fall without action, because the susceptible pool shrinks (p. 20); a small difference in contagiousness can decide whether an epidemic occurs (p. 21).
- Super-spreaders (a small share of carriers who infect many people) can raise the average contagion rate, so we cannot always accurately judge a narrative's contagiousness from our own fascination (p. 20).
- In narrative epidemics, "recovery" is loss of interest or forgetting (p. 21); bimetallism and Bitcoin attention curves resemble an epidemic curve (pp. 22–23).
- Epidemics can be slow (AIDS) or fast; timescale depends on parameters, and narrative and disease epidemics can feed each other (pp. 23–24).
- Economic theories (IS-LM, etc.) also show hump-shaped popularity, with a lag before visibility; celebrity stories may carry them (pp. 24–26).
- Keynesian multiplier rounds are a mechanical contagion of income, but narratives need to be added (pp. 27–28).
- Constellations are linked small narratives; confluences are narratives not viewed as associated with one another that have similar effects. Explaining big events needs a list, not one story (pp. 28–29).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Disease epidemics (pp. 18–21).**
- Example: Ebola in West Africa, 2013–2015. Figure 3.1 shows weekly new cases in Lofa County, Liberia, rising then falling (pp. 18–19).
- Rising phase: the contagion rate exceeds recovery plus death rate. Falling phase: recovery or death outweighs new infection, so the number infected declines (p. 18).
- Contagion fell partly through the efforts of Médecins Sans Frontières, over a hundred NGOs and individuals. Health-care workers were 21–32 times more likely to be infected; 815 confirmed and probable cases by 2015 (p. 19).
- Quarantine dates back at least to 1377 (Venice: 30 days by sea, then 40 days by land) (pp. 19–20).
- A second mechanism: the susceptible pool shrinks as people become immune or die. This works even with no intervention and is modelled in the appendix (p. 20).
- To end an epidemic, the contagion rate need only fall below the recovery rate, not to zero. New cases continue, but total sick falls to zero (p. 20).
- Rates are averages. Super-spreaders (e.g., Mary Mallon, at least 122 infected) can lift the average. For narratives, most of us may not be contagious enough for long enough to cause an epidemic without super-spreaders; marketing with accelerated analytics may enable them. So we cannot always accurately judge a narrative's contagiousness from our own fascination (p. 20).
- Onset and decline of epidemics tend to be mysterious. Causes (weather, fewer encounters) may be small and hard to document (pp. 20–21).

**Applying the model to narratives (p. 21).**
- Contagion happens through talk, social media, and news outlets copying each other (p. 21).
- "Recovery plus deaths" becomes recovery, loss of interest, or forgetting (p. 21).
- Threshold: the contagion rate must exceed the recovery rate for an epidemic to get started. Town #1 vs #2 example: an inconspicuous factor can put one above the threshold. Likewise minor story details can make one narrative an epidemic and not another (p. 21).

*Intuition note:* A story can take off only if it is passed on faster than people lose interest in it. Small differences in how contagious two stories are can mean an epidemic for one and none for the other.

**Contagion of the Bitcoin narrative (pp. 22–24).**
- Figure 3.2 plots the percentage of articles per year containing *bimetallism* or *Bitcoin*, 1850–2019 (ProQuest). It measures public attention, not price (p. 22).
- Each word marks a constellation of stories, including human-interest stories. Both are radical monetary ideas with alleged miraculous benefits (p. 22).
- Both curves resemble the epidemic curve. They look spiky because the axis covers over a century. They unfolded over years, not weeks. Bimetallism had a small secondary epidemic in the 1930s. Bitcoin has had no definitive end yet (pp. 22–23).
- Co-epidemics: in the 2018 Congo Ebola outbreak, researchers linked high contagion to narratives. Over 80% had heard misinformation; over 25% believed each statement. Shiller says the two epidemics fed on each other (p. 23).
- SIR model (Kermack–McKendrick, 1927; appendix): compartments susceptible (S), infected and spreading (I), recovered or dead (R). Shiller says we can think of Figures 3.1–3.2 as evidence on I. Such models tend to predict hump-shaped paths even without intervention, because the unexposed share declines and brings contagion below recovery (p. 23).
- Timing depends on parameters. Ebola could have lasted years with a lower initial contagion rate, provided contagion stayed above recovery. AIDS has been a slow epidemic, even slower than bimetallism and Bitcoin. Described as not very contagious, it can still grow because its recovery rate is smaller: an infected person can infect others for many years (p. 24).

*Intuition note:* Hump shapes arise naturally: growth stops when fewer people remain to be "infected" or more lose interest. The same shape appears over weeks, years or decades.

**Contagion of economic models (pp. 24–28).**
- Michel and coauthors (2011) found that mentions of famous people in books tend to rise then fall over decades (p. 24).
- Figure 3.3 (Google Ngrams): IS-LM (Hicks 1937), multiplier-accelerator (Samuelson 1939), overlapping generations (Samuelson 1958), real business cycle (Kydland and Prescott 1982). All hump-shaped. Content is irrelevant here; none is proven completely right or wrong (pp. 24–25, 27).
- For three models, the epidemic became visible over a decade after introduction. Shiller compares this to a disease growing unnoticed until a threshold. Spread goes through seminars and conferences, then textbooks, then media. Late on, the model may seem stale or be forgotten (p. 25).
- Spread was probably elementary and human. Warren Young suspects IS-LM's resemblance to the supply-and-demand diagram helped (p. 25).
- Keynes's celebrity and colourful life (Bloomsbury, Lopokova, the 1919 best seller) possibly made stories about him "donkeys" carrying IS-LM; Hicks was less colourful (pp. 25–26).
- Elements of narratives may survive in later narratives but tend to lose their punch and identity (p. 26).
- **Key proposition:** economic fluctuations are substantially driven by contagion of oversimplified, easily transmitted narrative variants. Not everyone is infected; those who miss an epidemic may deny it. Popular theories recede unless renewed (p. 26).
- Keynes's *General Theory* put forth the idea of a perfectly mechanical contagion, without using that phrase (Shiller's wording): a stimulus raises some incomes, they spend much of it, creating others' income, in multiple rounds. Samuelson's multiplier-accelerator adds investment dynamics, giving hump-shaped income responses. Shiller views this as an epidemic model of sorts, with income as the contagious element, but says it is not enough to think solely in terms of mechanical rounds of expenditure; we must think of rounds of expansion of narratives (pp. 27–28).

**Constellations and confluences (pp. 28–30).**
- Constellation: large narratives are often composed of many smaller ones sharing a theme. The full theme is visible only in the whole set (p. 28).
- Analogy: star constellations are chance alignments that humans read as a pattern (Cygnus, renamed Northern Cross by Christians) (p. 28).
- Narratives cluster partly because their credibility rests on other current narratives. Tellers need not refute them; assumed facts may be untestable. Shiller says that, with some accuracy, most people put on a show of knowledgeability and conceal ignorance; hence narratives contrary to prevailing thought may have lower contagion rates and not become epidemics (pp. 28–29).
- A narrative reaching only 20% of people could tip a country into recession if they postpone big purchases (p. 29).
- Confluence: narratives not seen as associated but with similar economic effects at one time, which may explain an exceptionally large event. Example: *Irrational Exuberance* (2000) listed twelve factors around 2000 (web, triumph of capitalism, business success stories, Republican dominance, retiring baby boomers, business media, optimistic analysts, retirement plans, mutual funds, falling inflation, trade volume, gambling culture) (p. 29).
- Big events usually cannot be tied to one constellation. Explaining them requires a list that is not itself a simple story (p. 29).
- Part III covers prominent narratives; constellations cannot yet be linked precisely to severe events. Why narratives go viral is 'something of a mystery', taken up in the next chapter (pp. 29–30).

## Toy example (ours)
Two stories each start with 100 tellers. Each week, a teller passes the story on to $c$ new people, and a share $r = 0.5$ of tellers lose interest.
- Story A: $c = 0.8$. Net weekly change $= 0.8 - 0.5 = +0.3$. Week 1: $100 \times 1.3 = 130$; week 2: $169$. It grows.
- Story B: $c = 0.4$. Net $= 0.4 - 0.5 = -0.1$. Week 1: $100 \times 0.9 = 90$; week 2: $81$. It fades.
What differs is which side of the threshold (contagion vs recovery) each story falls on, as in Shiller's Town #1 and #2. Constant rates give only growth or decay; a hump needs contagion to fall later (shrinking susceptible pool).
