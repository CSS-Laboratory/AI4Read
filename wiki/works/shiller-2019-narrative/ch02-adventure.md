# An Adventure in Consilience

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that economics has lagged most other disciplines in attending to narratives, and that economists can and should learn about narrative from epidemiology, history, other social sciences, literary studies and neuroscience.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks why economists should look outside economics to study narratives. Shiller says economics and finance use the word "narrative" less than other disciplines. They also tend to use it casually. He surveys how other fields treat stories: epidemiology models spread, history studies emotional stimuli, social sciences read values from personal stories, literary studies look for basic plots, and neuroscience studies metaphor. His conclusion is that collaboration across fields, above all with epidemiologists, could greatly improve economics. This matters because he calls epidemiology central to the book, and the next chapter adapts its models.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school ideas of academic disciplines and of epidemics spreading; no math in this unit.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Consilience** means the unity of knowledge across disciplines, especially science and the humanities. Shiller says the economy needs this because it consists of people who act on stories with emotions attached (p. 12).
- Academic disciplines tend to become insular, and the impulse to specialize often leads to overspecialization. By Shiller's account, economics has lagged most other fields in attending to narratives (pp. 12–13).
- When economists use the word "narrative", they mostly mean a conventional view they will criticize. They rarely document its popularity or its effect on behavior (p. 13).
- Epidemiology has about a hundred years of mathematical models of disease spread. It is central to the book, and the appendix surveys the models (p. 14).
- Historians such as MacMullen and North argue that understanding history and institutions requires the stories and emotions in people's minds (p. 14).
- Other social sciences find that people reveal values through personal stories, for example the founding stories of firms (p. 15).
- Some literary theorists find recurring basic plots. Structuralists reject such lists but hold that the mind organizes meaning (pp. 15–16).
- Contagious narratives often act as metaphors, and the brain seems wired to respond to stories that lead to thinking in analogies (p. 17).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pp. 12–13).**
- Consilience: the word was coined by William Whewell in 1840 and popularized by E. O. Wilson in 1994. It means the unity of knowledge across academic disciplines, especially between sciences and humanities (p. 12).
- The economy is made of conscious people who view their actions through stories with emotions and ideas. So narrative economics needs concepts from most university departments (p. 12).
- Researchers feel pushed to specialize until they can reasonably judge that they have all relevant knowledge on a narrowly defined subject. Shiller accepts this to some extent but says it often goes too far into overspecialization (p. 12).
- Economists seldom focus on the narratives that accompanied major events (p. 12).
- **Figure 2.1** shows the share of articles containing "narrative", by discipline. It covers anthropology, economics, finance, history, political science, psychology and sociology, for all years and for 2010–2019. Every field rises in recent years. Economics and finance are relative laggards (p. 13).
- Read from the chart (p. 13), only approximately: economics is about 3% (all years) and about 6% (2010–2019). Finance is about 1% and 2–3%. History is about 17% and 28–29%.
- Further criticisms of economists (p. 13):
  - Most economists appear not to be interested in using large text databases to study narratives.
  - They use the word casually, to refer to a conventional view they will criticize.
  - They rarely document a narrative's popularity, convey its human-interest stories, or consider how popularity affects behavior.
  - The word tends to appear in offbeat or popularizing economics journals.
- Shiller's conditional claim: to the extent an incipient theory of narrative economics holds promise for helping to anticipate major economic events, economists can and should learn from other fields. The chapter is meant as an exercise in consilience (p. 13).

*Intuition note:* Figure 2.1 is a crude proxy for attention to narrative. It counts articles that use the word, not how well they study narratives. Shiller reads it as evidence that economics is behind its neighbors.

**Epidemiology and Narrative (p. 14).**
- Medical schools have modeled disease spread mathematically for about a hundred years. The field is well developed and offers many models for different circumstances (p. 14).
- Shiller calls the field "central to this book". The appendix surveys the models and their possible applications to economic narratives (p. 14).

**History and Narrative (p. 14).**
- **MacMullen (2003):** deep historical understanding requires inferring what was on the minds of the people who made history, that is, their narratives. He prefers a word meaning "stimulus to some emotional response" (p. 14).
- MacMullen *asserts* that the American Civil War cannot be understood without vividly told stories. His example is the 1837 report of a mob shooting the abolitionist editor E. P. Lovejoy in Alton, Illinois. Shiller says it whipped antislavery feeling in the North into a fury that lasted for years (p. 14).
- Next sentence in the text, in the narrator's voice and not explicitly attributed to MacMullen: debate on how far the war was about slavery cannot be conclusive unless the emotional power of narratives is considered (p. 14).
- Douglass North (2005) *echoes* this. He stresses human intentionality, "essentially in the form of narratives", in the development of economic institutions (p. 14).

**Sociology, anthropology, psychology, marketing, psychoanalysis, religious studies (p. 15).**
- Over the last half-century, many schools have studied popular narratives under names such as narrative psychology, storytelling sociology and word-of-mouth marketing (p. 15).
- Common theme: asked about their objectives or philosophy of life, most people have little to say. They brighten when telling personal stories, and these reveal their values (p. 15).
- Prison example: interviewed inmates tend to respond well when asked to tell stories about other inmates. These stories tend to convey a sense of an "altered morality", not amorality (p. 15).
- O'Barr and Conley interviewed investment managers. Employees tended to tell a founding story of the firm, which tends to center on one man (rarely a woman) of exceptional foresight or courage. It is akin to tribal creation myths, and the narrative tends to revert to it to justify the firm's present stories (p. 15).

**Literary studies (pp. 15–16).**
- Some literary theorists, inspired partly by Jung's archetypes and Klein's phantasies, find that basic story structures repeat while names and circumstances change. This suggests the brain may have built-in receptors for certain stories (pp. 15–16).
- Lists of basic stories (p. 16):
  - Cawelti: "formula stories".
  - Propp: 31 "functions" present in all folk stories.
  - Tobias: 20 master plots in all fiction.
  - Booker: 7 basic plots (e.g. "rags to riches", "the quest").
- **Klages's report:** structuralist theory sees such lists as "overly reductive and dehumanizing". Yet structuralists believe the mechanisms that organize units and rules into meaningful systems come from the human mind itself (p. 16).
- **Brooks (1992):** narratology should ask how narratives work on readers to create models of understanding. Well-structured narratives fulfill a "passion for meaning", and their study leads to psychoanalysis (p. 16).
- Morson and Schapiro (2017) argue that better appreciation of great novels would help improve the modeling of economic life (p. 16).

**Neuroscience, neurolinguistics (pp. 16–17).**
- Narratives are sequences of words, so linguistics is relevant. Words carry direct meanings, connotations and metaphorical uses (p. 16).
- Modern neurolinguistics probes the brain structures and organization that support narratives (p. 17).
- Contagious narratives often act as metaphors. They suggest an idea or mechanism not mentioned in the story, and the story becomes a name for it (p. 17).
- The brain tends to organize around metaphors. Example: war metaphors such as an argument being "shot down" or "indefensible"; the brain notices the link to war narratives, though not always consciously (p. 17).
- Example: the word "crash" reminds most people of the 1929 crash and its aftermath (p. 17).
- **Lakoff and Johnson (2003):** metaphors mold thought and affect conclusions; they are not only colorful style. **Vartanian (2012):** analogy and metaphor "reliably activate" consistent brain regions in fMRI. Shiller infers that the brain seems wired for stories that lead to analogies (p. 17).

*Intuition note:* A one-word story such as "crash" can carry a whole history with it, and, as Shiller reports Lakoff and Johnson, such metaphors do more than decorate speech: they mold thought and affect conclusions (p. 17).

**Conclusion (p. 17).** Collaboration between economists and other experts holds the *promise* of revolutionizing economics. Epidemiologists' models are described as forecasting epidemic paths and showing how to counteract them. The next chapter adapts them, and this "marriage" is the book's first example of consilience (p. 17).
