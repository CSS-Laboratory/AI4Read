# Causality and Constellations

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that causality runs both ways: new contagious narratives, often random in origin, help cause economic events, and economic events change narratives.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller asks what is cause and what is consequence when stories and the economy move together. Economists usually treat narratives as a by-product of real economic news. Shiller disagrees. He argues that new narratives start in the minds of a few people and are often random, so they can be treated as causative innovations and possibly as exogenous. He supports this with economic history, controlled experiments from other fields, flashbulb memories, the recovery after 11 September 2001, and the long history of fake news. He also notes that archives may show only part of the "constellation" (the set) of narratives. Because poorly informed people drive aggregate decisions, he concludes that attention-getting narratives must drive those decisions.

**Prerequisites:** correlation vs causation; "exogenous" (coming from outside the system); the epidemic-style spread of narratives from earlier chapters.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Causality between narratives and the economy is hard to prove, but Shiller reports the general conclusion that it can be inferred even without controlled experiments, for example from quasi-controlled historical events (pp. 72–73).
- New narratives start with one person or a few. They can be treated as causative innovations and possibly as exogenous, and they interact with events in both directions (pp. 71–73).
- Self-fulfilling prophecies ("sunspots": extraneous noise that matters because people believe it will) do not, Shiller adds, come out of nowhere: they typically come from many narrative mutations, a few of which become epidemics (p. 74).
- A narrative becomes an economic narrative when it describes others taking action, i.e. it supplies scripts. Aggregate metrics alone can miss these motives (pp. 74–75).
- Controlled experiments in marketing, journalism, education, health, philanthropy and law show narratives changing behaviour (pp. 77–78).
- Flashbulb memories are selected partly for social reasons, so brief stories can have lasting effects. Shiller reads the post-9/11 recovery partly through this lens (pp. 80–83).
- Fake news is old and persistent. Shiller concludes that fake narratives affect economic outcomes rather than merely reflecting fundamentals (pp. 84–85).
- Archives may show only part of the narrative "constellation". Poorly informed people drive aggregate decisions, so attention-getting stories must drive them (p. 86).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 71–72)
- The book aims to help people anticipate depressions, recessions and secular (long-term) stagnation by including narratives in their thinking. The key problem is telling cause from consequence (p. 71).
- Economists attend to causality but usually give no causal role to the invention of new narratives. Shiller argues that causality goes both ways (p. 71).
- Few things are purely outside the economy. Narratives can still be seen as causative innovations, because each starts in one mind or a small collaboration. Mokyr calls such a person a "cultural entrepreneur" and traces the idea to Hume (1742): what depends on a few persons is largely ascribed to chance or unknown causes (p. 71).
- The inventors of contagious narratives are usually not famous, and we often never learn who they were (p. 72).

### Direction of Causality (pp. 72–74)
- Examples of the problem: did 1920s stock enthusiasm cause higher prices, or did higher earnings cause the enthusiasm? Did Bitcoin enthusiasm after 2009 raise its price, or was the price a logical reaction to news (p. 72)?
- Economists cannot usually run controlled experiments on whole economies. A controlled experiment (random trial) would give a drug to one group and a placebo to a control group. They look instead for natural experiments (p. 72).
- Farnam (1912) said history lets economists infer causality because random shocks occur, such as crazy government policies. Experiments are "tried for him without expense" (pp. 72–73).
- Friedman and Schwartz (1963) gave three "quasi-controlled experiments" for causation from monetary policy to the economy: the gold discoveries of 1897–1914 and the periods during and after the two world wars (p. 73).
- Whether these were truly exogenous can be debated. Since 1963, the general conclusion is that causality can be inferred without controlled experiments (p. 73).
- Shiller's addition: new narratives might be treated as exogenous, giving more quasi-controlled experiments. The gold discoveries and wars likely were exogenous because they were made possible by narrative innovations, such as gold-rush stories or fake news about foreign conspiracy (p. 73).
- Shiller says we must be wary of many (but not all) economists' supposition that causality always runs from events to narratives. Merton coined "self-fulfilling prophecy" in 1948 (p. 73).
- Sunspots: Jevons (1878) proposed that fluctuations were driven by variation in the sun's rays, of which sunspots are "a mere sign". He thought he discerned a correlation between sunspot counts and economic events. Causality could not run from the economy to the sun, but later research did not support the theory (pp. 73–74).
- Cass and Shell (1983) explained that sunspots may affect the economy if people mystically believe they should. Economists now use "sunspots" for any extraneous noise that matters because people believe it will. Farmer is a leader in this field (p. 74).
- Shiller's addition: these prophecies typically come from millions of narrative mutations, a few contagious enough to become epidemics. This process can be observed and modelled (p. 74).

*Intuition note:* Sunspots show the logic Shiller wants. If a cause is clearly outside the economy, the direction of causation is clear. Stories might be treated as playing the "outside" role even though people, not the sun, create them.

### Random Events, Birthdays, and Anniversaries (pp. 74–77)
- Most people have vague fears from narratives that do not change their actions. A narrative becomes an economic narrative when it tells of others taking action, such as investing and getting rich. Economic narratives tend to involve scripts: action sequences people may follow just because they heard others do so (p. 74).
- Looking only at aggregates (GDP, wages, interest and tax rates) risks missing motives. Shiller compares this to studying a religious awakening through the cost of printing tracts. Data on narratives are spotty. He cites Muller's "tyranny of metrics" (pp. 74–75).
- Decision-makers rarely explain their decisions, and hesitation to spend may reflect no identifiable thought at all (p. 75).
- Contagious stories are largely creative, not just logical reactions to events. Corrections last many days, during which media reinterpret news to inform and to amuse (p. 75).
- The process is in many ways random, like a microbe mutation. On 15 October 1929, two weeks before the crash, Irving Fisher said the market had reached a "permanently high plateau". Newspapers picked up the phrase. Shiller says it probably affected the duration of the debacle. The words came from outside the economy, so they are exogenous (pp. 75–76).
- Anniversaries can revive old narratives if tweaked, probably renamed, and reattached to a human-interest story. The media recall the 1987 crash on major anniversaries and will until a bigger one-day crash replaces it (p. 76).
- Bitcoin: by 2013 the narrative was fading and the price fell from over US $1000 to just over $200. New "mutations" kept it alive, notably the initial coin offering (ICO). ICOs were backed in effect as shares of corporations, and each coin had its own story. There were over 900 ICOs in 2017, and almost half failed within a year (p. 76).
- **Economists' common view (as Shiller describes it):** narratives are silliness following changes in real news about deep forces. These forces are presumed to be caused exclusively by scientific advances, resource discoveries or exhaustion, demographics, or economic research (p. 76).
- Shiller says this mode of thinking misses what may be the essential elements that cause change. Narratives are contagious, suggest scripts, repeat their messages and thrive on human interest (p. 77).

### Controlled Experiments from Outside Economics (pp. 77–79)
- Marketing: Escalas compared analytical self-referencing with narrative self-referencing and narrative transportation (a story in which "I", not "you", is used). Narrative transportation was more effective, especially when the analytical case is weak (p. 77).
- Journalism: Machill and coauthors told one group a plain TV report on air pollution and another a story with a protagonist (a baker with health problems) against antagonists. The story version was retained better (p. 77).
- Education: McQuiggan and coauthors had eighth-graders play a story game (Alyx on Crystal Island). The experimental group had an advantage over controls in "self-efficacy, presence, interest, and perception of control" (p. 78).
- Health: Slater and coauthors found didactic nutrition evidence ineffective. Narrative messages worked better when the audience identified with the people shown (p. 78).
- Philanthropy: Weber and coauthors (2006) found narrative organ-donation messages more effective than statistical ones (p. 78).
- Law: Bell and Loftus (1985) found that an irrelevant vivid detail (guacamole on a white shag carpet) helped obtain a conviction from the experimental jury (p. 78).
- Shiller's conclusion: economics can learn from other social sciences. Since experiments on whole economies are not readily available, it is all the more important to understand the building blocks of narratives. Stories are one key block (pp. 78–79).

### The Importance of Stories in Driving Human Activity (pp. 79–80)
- Emotion matters in narratives and shows in stories. Historical novels and films, though outside mainstream history, help us grasp feelings in history. Cronon (2013) says it is "a little hard to say" whether a history silent about conversation and stream-of-consciousness distorts the past more than a fiction that tries to represent them responsibly (p. 79).
- The dominant popular metaphor is the economy as a sick or healthy person, with "confidence" as the thermometer (p. 79).
- Sternberg's *Love Is a Story* describes relationships as made possible by a narrative. Likewise, the economy's story has dimensions beyond health: moral (loyalty vs opportunism, trust vs distrust, queue-jumping vs waiting) and affective (security vs insecurity, inner vs public direction) (p. 80).

### Flashbulb Memory (pp. 80–83)
- Memories tend to focus on a few salient, random images. A brief narrative may cause lasting behavioural change. People sometimes recall a sudden change of mental stance. In the extreme the memory forms so suddenly that it is a flashbulb memory (p. 80).
- Flashbulb status is connected to the emotions attached to the event and also to social psychological factors. Memories involving shared identity or rehearsed with others are more likely to become flashbulb memories. Shiller says they are therefore selected in a way that gives them a better chance of being involved in forming contagious narratives (p. 81).
- Examples: the narrative of the first shots at Fort Sumter in 1861 was vividly remembered decades later; a former first sergeant described in detail, 35 years after the event, what he was doing when first told he must lead his men on a mission that might get them killed. People recalled hearing of Pearl Harbor 40 years later (pp. 81–82).
- Such memories hold mostly meaningless details of the surrounding events. A random day decades ago would not be remembered (p. 82).
- 11 September 2001: vivid memories are, Shiller says, testimony to the attack's causal impact on economic actions. The NBER dated a recession from March 2001. Fears arose that it would be prolonged because people would stay at home (p. 82).
- Instead the recession ended in November 2001, one of the shortest in US history (p. 83).
- Shiller's explanation: a narrative took hold, with national leaders asking people to do symbolic things to uphold confidence. President Bush, two weeks after the attack, urged people to go back to work, fly and visit Disney World. He encouraged team spirit and gave a script of courage (p. 83).
- Most economists did not recognize the flashbulb quality of the attacks, which encouraged a contagious constellation of narratives and may have profoundly affected the US economy (p. 83).

*Intuition note:* Memories that involve a shared identity or are rehearsed with others are more likely to become flashbulb memories. That gives a few emotional stories a better chance of joining contagious narratives, and Shiller infers a causal role for the 9/11 attacks from the vividness of the memories. He hedges only the last step: the attacks encouraged a constellation of narratives that "may have" profoundly affected the economy.

### The Ubiquity of Fake News (pp. 84–85)
- Storytellers often use fiction for vividness. Fake news is not new, and people spread titillating stories without deciding whether they are false (p. 84).
- Fake news works partly because reality monitoring is imperfect. The brain judges sources by their links to other memories and may forget that it deemed a story unreliable (p. 84).
- Shiller uses fake wrestling as an example. Obvious fakery, such as the villain's cheating, is accepted as if true. Barthes notes that spectators rarely bet on outcomes; the excesses make it "a spectacle and not a sport" (pp. 84–85). Shiller infers that many people enjoy believing the story and do not care about its factuality (p. 85).
- The history is long: "fake wrestling" appears in print in 1890. Rome's *prolusio* preceded real gladiatorial combat (p. 85).
- Fact-checking sites debunk fake news, but most people do not read them, and fake news has damaged their credibility (p. 85).
- Shiller concludes that fake news "seems to be part of the normal human condition". The fakery is too creative to be caused by fundamental economic forces. "Instead, the opposite is true": fake narratives affect economic outcomes (p. 85).

### Evidence on Causation from Constellations of Narratives (p. 86)
- In studying narratives from archives we may be able to view only some of the superficial narratives, like trying to discern constellations on a partly cloudy night. Narratives come and go over years, while fluctuations such as panics can be sudden. The seeds may have been planted months or years earlier (p. 86).
- The mass of people whose consumption and investment decisions drive fluctuations are not well informed. Hence "it must be the case" that attention-getting narratives drive those decisions, often helped by celebrities or trusted figures (p. 86).
- Once we recognize that newly mutated stories within narrative constellations can cause current events, we have made substantial progress. Understanding how they act is hard, which leads to the next chapter (p. 86).

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text.* Let $N_t$ be narrative intensity and $g_t$ the price growth in %. Two-way causality: $g_t = 0.5\,N_{t-1}$ (narrative → economy) and $N_t = 0.6\,N_{t-1} + 0.4\,g_t$ (economy → narrative). Start with an exogenous story shock, $N_0 = 10$.

| t | $g_t$ | $N_t$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5·10 = 5 | 0.6·10 + 0.4·5 = 8 |
| 2 | 0.5·8 = 4 | 0.6·8 + 0.4·4 = 6.4 |
| 3 | 0.5·6.4 = 3.2 | 0.6·6.4 + 0.4·3.2 = 5.12 |

Without the shock ($N_0 = 0$), all values stay 0. The two series move together, so correlation alone cannot give the direction. Only the exogenous timing of the shock shows that the story came first. That is the logic of a quasi-controlled experiment.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — a story in which others act, giving scripts people may follow (p. 74)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — millions of mutations, a few contagious enough to become epidemics (p. 74)
- [[exogenous-shock]] (new) — a change not caused by the economy; Shiller treats some narratives this way (pp. 73, 76)
- [[two-way-causality]] (new) — narratives cause events and events change narratives (p. 71)
- [[quasi-controlled-experiment]] (new) — a historical event treated as a random shock to infer causation (p. 73)
- [[self-fulfilling-prophecy-sunspots]] (new) — extraneous noise matters because people believe it will (pp. 73–74)
- [[script]] (new) — an action sequence one may copy after hearing of others doing it (p. 74)
- [[narrative-transportation]] (new) — a story in which one imagines being another person, using "I" (p. 77)
- [[flashbulb-memory]] (new) — a sudden, vivid long-term memory, linked to emotion; more likely with shared identity or rehearsal with others (pp. 80–81)
- [[narrative-constellation]] (new) — the set of narratives behind one cultural change; archives may show only part (p. 86)
- [[cultural-entrepreneur]] (new) — Mokyr's term for the individual originator of a narrative (p. 71)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Sunspot self-fulfilling prophecies ↔ coordination game / multiple equilibria: beliefs not tied to fundamentals can select the outcome.
- [structural-analogy] Narrative mutation and contagion ↔ epidemiology: rare random mutants become epidemics when conditions favour them.
- [structural-analogy] Quasi-controlled experiments ↔ natural experiments in causal inference: an event treated as random substitutes for randomization.
- [metaphor] Constellations seen on a cloudy night ↔ incomplete archival data: we see only some stars.

## Questions this raises
- If narratives are only partly observable, how can we test whether a narrative was exogenous?
- Does the claim that "the opposite is true" for fake news follow from fakery being creative, or does it need further evidence?
- Could the post-9/11 recovery have other explanations, such as policy responses, that this chapter does not discuss?
- How would one measure "contagious enough in the current environment" before an epidemic occurs?
