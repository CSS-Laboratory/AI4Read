# The Wage-Price Spiral and Evil Labor Unions

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that the mid-century wage-price spiral story, with its moral outrage at greedy unions and business, likely provides the best explanation for the epochal changes in trend of inflation and interest rates.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how a story about wages chasing prices spread, and what it did. Shiller shows that "wage-price spiral" and "cost-push inflation" rose together, both especially strong sometime between 1950 and 1990. Vivid anecdotes about union demands and Jimmy Hoffa made the story contagious. Moral language gave central banks authority to tighten policy. Public views of inflation differed from economists' views, and the targets of blame differ across eras and countries. The narrative faded as independent central banks and inflation targeting (a central-bank aim of controlling inflation) arrived. It might return in a mutated form. Many narratives coexist, so no single index captures them.

**Prerequisites:** inflation and interest rates; the idea of a narrative "epidemic" from earlier chapters; reading a time-series plot.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Narrative:** unions demand higher wages, firms pass the cost into prices, and labor then uses the higher prices to justify new demands. The story is linked to cost-push inflation (price rises driven by the cost of labor and inputs) and contrasted with demand-pull inflation (price rises blamed on consumers demanding more goods than can be produced) (p. 258).
- **Epidemics:** the two phrases were roughly parallel in book frequency, and strong between 1950 and 1990 (p. 258–259).
- **Vivid stories:** the railroad "miles instead of hours" story and the Hoffa story spread the narrative and discredited unions (p. 259–260).
- **Real variables:** world inflation peaked in 1980, about when cost-push peaked in the figure, and long-term interest rates were high during the epidemic and have fallen since. Shiller says the narrative's dynamics likely provide the best explanation of these changes in trend (p. 260).
- **Moral framing:** greed was blamed on both labor and business. This gave central banks moral authority to tighten, even in 1957 when inflation was moderate (p. 261–262).
- **Public vs economists:** the public believed in a "wage lag" (wages forever trailing prices), while economists for the most part did not think inflation was such a big deal (p. 263–264).
- **Blame varies by era:** wartime anger targeted profiteers, and Germans in the 1920s blamed the US gold dollar (p. 265–266).
- **Outlook:** the narrative does not seem poised to return, but a mutated version could appear if inflation creeps up. Forecasting needs attention to many narratives (p. 266–267).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the narrative and its epidemic
- **The story (narrative content).** Labor, led by strong unions, demands higher wages. Management accommodates by raising final prices, without losing profit. Labor uses the higher prices to justify more demands, and the process repeats into out-of-control inflation (p. 258).
- Blame falls on labor and management, and some may blame the monetary authority, which tolerates the inflation (p. 258).
- It is linked to *cost-push inflation* (inflation driven by the cost of labor and inputs). It contrasts with *demand-pull inflation* (inflation caused by consumers demanding more than can be produced) (p. 258).
- **Figure 18.1** (Google Ngrams, books 1900–2008, no smoothing): the two phrases are roughly parallel and both are especially strong sometime between 1950 and 1990 (p. 258–259). In the image, cost-push peaks around 1980.
- **Moral values.** Shiller says the epidemics reflected changes in moral values, indicating deep concerns about being cheated and a sense of fundamental corruption in society (p. 258).
  - **The narratives claimed:** unions deceitfully claimed to represent all labor but represented only insiders. Politicians and central banks selfishly perpetuated inflation, which hurt workers not represented by strong unions (p. 258).
  - Gallup support for US unions fell from 72% (1936) to 48% (2009) (p. 259).
- **Anecdote 1, railroads (1950).** The NYT reported a demand to lower the passenger-train pay base from 150 miles or 7.5 hours to 100 miles or 5 hours. Railroads countered with 200 miles. Faster diesel trains meant a day's pay for 2.5–3 hours of work, and the mileage rate would rise 50% (p. 259). The story had conductors sitting as passengers after 2.5 hours. Shiller calls it outrageous and highly contagious (p. 260).
- **Anecdote 2, Hoffa.** Hoffa took over the Teamsters in 1957 despite corruption charges and led as "absolute dictator." He was investigated in a probe led by Robert F. Kennedy and convicted of bribery and fraud. He was in prison 1967–71 and vanished in 1975. Rumors gave vivid mental images of his end. Searches made news until 2013. Shiller says this fed the contagion that discredited unions (p. 260).
- **Real-world link.** The World Bank's global inflation rate peaked in 1980, approximately at the cost-push peak, and has mostly declined since. Long-term interest rates were high, reflecting inflation expectations, and have fallen since. Shiller says the narrative's dynamics "likely" best explain these trend changes (p. 260).
- **End of the narrative:** the independent central bank (free of political pressure that organized labor exploits) and inflation targeting (placing inflation control on higher moral ground) (p. 261).
- **Moralizing.** The spiral may look mechanical, but many believed greed on both sides caused it (p. 261).
  - **Eisenhower (1957):** the national interest must take precedence over temporary advantages that particular groups may secure at the expense of all the people; business should avoid unnecessary price increases, and wage negotiations should take account of the public's right to share in the benefits of technology (p. 261).
  - Inflation in 1957 rose from below zero (1956) to a 3.7% peak, far below 23.6% in 1920, yet stirred emotion (p. 261).
  - **LA Times editorial (1957):** it blamed the "greed" of big business and labor leaders and warned of a crash (p. 261–262).
- This moral authority let the Fed "step hard on the brakes", risking recession. The discount rate peaked in October 1957. **Sproul** complained that the Fed sometimes had to validate "public folly and private greed" by supporting higher costs and prices (p. 262).

*Intuition note:* A story that makes inflation look like a moral crime changes what officials may do. It also changes what people expect. Here the same narrative is tied to the inflation path and to the policy response.

### Inflation in a Constellation of Injustice and Immorality Narratives
- Gallup asked about the "most important problem" from 1935. In 1973–81, generally more than 50% answered inflation or the high cost of living (p. 262).
- **Friedman (1973):** inflation was a world-wide disaster, as serious as the Great Depression (p. 262).
- **Columnist Harris (1975, his voice):** culprits are hard to pin down. If labor gets "too much", why do families struggle? If grocers profiteer, why are they glum? Each blames another (p. 263).
- **Shiller's reading:** there were now multiple sources of evil, including labor, unlike the 1920s focus on business (p. 263).
- **Shiller's 1997 survey** (US, Germany, Brazil; public and university economists) found differences across countries, age groups, and especially between economists and public (p. 263).
  - Economists mostly did not see inflation as a big deal. US consumers disagreed on causes but were angry (p. 263).
  - Most common cause given: "greed", then "people borrow or lend too much". Targets of anger: government, manufacturers, store owners and many others, even "themselves" (p. 264).
  - The public believed in a *wage lag hypothesis*: wages forever lag prices, so inflation permanently lowers living standards. The spiral gave a geometric image of status spiraling down (p. 264).
- **The 1957–58 recession** was not a buyers' strike like the Great Depression. Luxury sales stayed strong, and anger was not aimed at the rich. Postponable everyday purchases suffered more (p. 264).
- The public sensed that no feasible policy could stop the spiral. The recessions of 1949, 1953 and 1957 lowered inflation only temporarily. The lingering Great Depression narrative suggested that it was perhaps too great a risk to try to control inflation by starting a bigger recession. The popular model implied that the nation should assume inflation will get worse and worse (p. 264).

### Angry at Inflation
- Out-of-control consumer price inflation has always induced anger, Shiller says, but anger narratives reflect the different circumstances of each inflationary period, so the target varies (p. 265).
- In wartime, governments may print money. Narratives blaming government may not be contagious then, and people more likely blame others, such as businesspeople safe at home (p. 265).
- The profiteer epidemic (ch. 17) led to excess profits taxes in both world wars (p. 265).
- In the US Civil War, anger at war profiteers was not aimed at tycoons causing inflation. A 1863 sermon attacked speculators as "famished wolves" who robbed the treasury and the dying (p. 265–266).
- **German hyperinflation (1917–23):** prices rose on the order of a trillionfold, not due to war. Fisher found Germans blamed not their own money-printing government but a rising US gold dollar, supposedly cornered by the US (p. 266).
- **Outlook (Shiller):** union strength and support may be resurging and inflation is tame, so the narrative does not seem poised to return. A mutation could appear if inflation creeps up, since the public watches consumer prices closely. It could add a moral dimension and perceived evil (p. 266).

### Perennial Narratives: A Summing Up
- Part III's nine constellations show the great complexity of the narrative landscape. No simple index, such as the Consumer Confidence Index, summarizes the economy's "strength" (p. 266–267).
- Shiller uses a biological analogy of many receptors and signaling molecules. Forecasting requires attention to many narratives and to new data (discussed in part IV) (p. 267).

*Intuition note:* Economic mood is not one dial. Several stories run at once, so forecasting needs close attention to many different narratives.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A simple three-round "spiral" with the story's logic: wages and prices both start at index 100. Each round, labor wins +5%, and firms raise prices +5% to keep profit.
- Round 1: $100 \times 1.05 = 105$ for wages, prices $= 105$.
- Round 2: $105 \times 1.05 = 110.25$, both.
- Round 3: $110.25 \times 1.05 = 115.7625 \approx 115.76$, both.
After three rounds both indices are up about 15.76%, and the ratio wage/price is $115.76/115.76 = 1$. Prices rise 15.76% here by construction, and wages keep pace with prices by assumption, so the toy neither supports nor refutes the "wage lag" belief or Shiller's claims.

## Concepts
- [[economic-narrative]] — a contagious story that changes economic decisions; here the wage-price spiral (p. 258)
