# Panic versus Confidence

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that stories about public confidence (financial panic, then business confidence, then consumer confidence) have influenced economic fluctuations and spread in epidemic-like waves, and that the Great Depression narrative remains a powerful one.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Which stories about confidence have moved economies, and how do they spread? Shiller traces three narrative classes: financial panic, business confidence and consumer confidence. He links them to crowd psychology (ideas about how crowds behave), suggestibility (being easily influenced by others), autosuggestion (telling oneself to succeed), weather-forecast analogies and leaders' efforts to talk confidence up. He suggests that talk of fear and optimism may backfire: the public came to assume officials were falsely optimistic, and calling the problem fear may worsen it. The Great Depression narrative has outlasted the event and may shape how 2007–9 is read. The evidence is mainly Ngram plots (word counts in digitised books) and quotations, not formal tests.

**Prerequisites:** bank runs and depositor panic; basic Great Depression history; reading a word-frequency plot (the chapter itself calls rising and falling narratives "epidemics").

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Three confidence narratives arrived in sequence (financial panic, business confidence, consumer confidence), and the author says each has a growth path spanning lifetimes (pp. 114–116).
- Nineteenth-century views help explain why people spoke of panics and never of consumer confidence: most people did not save, and panics were mostly seen as stories about wealthy people (pp. 116–117).
- The Panic of 1907 narrative centred on celebrity J. P. Morgan; the Federal Reserve was created after that panic and modelled on his consortium (pp. 117–118).
- Crowd psychology, suggestibility and autosuggestion rose in epidemic-like curves between roughly 1900 and the 1930s. The idea of suggestibility is opposed to "economic man", the rational optimizer, and may have led people to expect herd-like behaviour (pp. 119–122).
- Weather forecasting supplied an analogy for forecasting and measuring confidence. To the extent the public believes forecasts, there may be an element of self-fulfilling prophecy. Leading indicators (statistics meant to signal future economic swings) might possibly do the same (pp. 123–125).
- Robey saw Coolidge's and Mellon's optimistic statements as an effort to keep confidence up, and the investing public read the Fed's 1929 warning between the lines. In the 1930s the public came to assume leaders were falsely optimistic, and the idea that fear itself was the danger took strong hold of the public imagination (pp. 125–129).
- Kiplinger's 1930 list of causes differs from today's confidence-and-bank-failure story. Economists' theories tell only part of the story, and storytellers such as Groucho Marx had much more public influence than economists (pp. 129–133).
- Figure 10.4 suggests far more attention to the Great Depression in 2009 than in the 1930s, and the narrative may have intertwined with bank-run stories in 2007–9 (pp. 134–135).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 114–116)
- Since the early nineteenth century, narratives about confidence in banks, business, other people and the economy have influenced economic fluctuations. Economically, the most important stories are those about *other people's* confidence and about efforts to promote public confidence (p. 114).
- Raymond Moley (one of FDR's "Brain Trust" experts) described a Depression as much like a run on a bank, a crisis of confidence, and told a story: an Irishman said, "If my money's here, I don't want it. If it's not here, I want it." (p. 114)
- Confidence in banks includes confidence in the other customers, that they will not all try to withdraw at once (p. 114).
- Three classes of confidence narrative (pp. 114–115):
  1. **Financial panic:** psychologically based stories about banking crises.
  2. **Business confidence:** slow activity attributed not so much to financial crises as to general pessimism and unwillingness to expand business or to hire.
  3. **Consumer confidence:** slow sales attributed to individual consumers' fears, whose sudden lack of spending can bring about a recession.
- Figure 10.1 (Google Ngrams, 1800–2008) plots the succession: financial panic first, then business confidence, then consumer confidence. All these slow-moving narratives have growth paths that span lifetimes (pp. 115–116).
- As narratives spread about business losses and decreased consumer confidence, increasing self-censorship of narratives may, and sometimes does, encourage panic. Because people are aware that others self-censor, they increasingly try to read between the lines of public pronouncements to determine the "truth" (p. 115).
- Public interest in the idea that financial events relate to psychology began in the early nineteenth century, continued after the panic of 1857 and grew over the decades. The phrase *financial panic* peaks on Google Ngrams in 1910, three years after the Panic of 1907. The panic epidemic was part of a narrative constellation (a cluster of related stories) that grew with it, and individual panics ebbed and flowed inside it. A particularly strong narrative of 1907 involved a celebrity, J. P. Morgan, which made it last for decades. It stands out in Figure 10.1 as the highest point for public attention to financial panics (p. 115).
- Figure 10.2 shows the major US panics individually. The panic of 1857 was mostly forgotten within a few years and later returned as part of a narrative constellation about other panics. During 1857, news reports covered objective events (bankruptcies, runs, suspensions) but also rumours and emotions (p. 115).

*Intuition note:* Confidence stories rise and fall in epidemic-like waves, and an old event can be revived as part of a larger constellation of stories. Much of the chapter's evidence comes from this kind of word counting.

### Why 19th-century stories looked different (pp. 116–117)
- People and newspapers spoke of "panics" rather than "depressions" in the modern sense, and never spoke of consumer confidence. Panic stories were mostly viewed as stories about wealthy, pretentious people with bank accounts, who perhaps deserved some of the disruption (p. 116).
- In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries most people did not save at all. The Keynesian marginal propensity to consume (share of extra income spent) was close to 100%: most people, except those with high incomes, spent their entire income. To narrative spinners of those centuries, there would have been no point in surveying ordinary people about consumer confidence (p. 116).
- Most people then had no concept of retirement or sending children to college. Poverty was tended to be seen as moral degradation, not as related to the strength of the economy, and authorities were seen as responsible for instilling moral virtue. The idea that the poor should be taught to save grew gradually, through savings-bank propaganda. Contemporary thought was far from the idea that a depression might be caused by ordinary people trying to save too much (pp. 116–117).

### Morgan and the Fed (pp. 117–118)
- After the Panic of 1907 the US passed the Aldrich-Vreeland Act (1908), which created national currency associations as precursors to a central bank, and the Federal Reserve Act (1913), which founded the central bank. The Fed's purpose was to provide a cure for business panics (p. 117).
- With no central bank in 1907, Morgan used his own money and prevailed on other bankers to contribute to a bailout. This was a powerful story, and Morgan emerged as a central, model-worthy hero (p. 117).
- Shiller writes that the recovery of confidence was "in substantial measure" confidence in one man. He states that the Federal Reserve System was modelled after Morgan's 1907 consortium, that the new central bank was technically owned by bankers though created by the federal government, and that every Fed chair since fits the narrative as a Morgan "avatar" (p. 118).
- After 1930 the narrative mutated. Deficiencies of business and later consumer confidence were associated more with despair than with sudden fear. "Depression" had also come to mean melancholy, so its use for economic contraction reflected a new psychologically based narrative (p. 118).

### Measuring confidence (pp. 118–119)
- During the 1930s Gallup became the first social scientist to survey business and consumer confidence with scientific polling. Katona created the Michigan Index of Consumer Sentiment in 1952; the Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index followed in 1966 (pp. 118–119).
- Their questions do not ask about banking panics or investor stampedes, reflecting the changed narrative about business. But the change is not total, and panic narratives can be rekindled, as with Northern Rock in the UK in 2007, the first banking panic there since 1866 (p. 119).

### Crowd psychology goes viral (pp. 119–120)
- Mackay's *Memoirs of Extraordinary Popular Delusions* (1841) began to draw public attention to crowd psychology, and Le Bon popularised the term in *The Crowd* (1895). Crowd psychology grew in an epidemic-like path, peaking in the early 1930s. Its growing references appear to parallel the booming 1920s stock market (p. 119).
- **Suggestibility** is the idea that individual human behaviour is subconsciously imitative of and reactive to others. It and autosuggestion (suggesting to oneself) follow a fairly standard epidemic curve, peaking around 1920 (Fig. 10.3). The author says the concepts likely played a role in the 1920s exuberance and the 1930s depression (p. 119).
- Suggestibility is described as diametrically opposed to the "economic man" who is a rational optimizer. Its wide familiarity by 1920 indicates that people may have felt others are easily influenced and were therefore more likely to act expecting a highly unstable world. The narrative would lead them to expect herd-like behaviour and perhaps contribute to it. If you think others are an impressionable herd, you may be more likely to try to get ahead of them (p. 120).
- Method caution: look at what people *thought* these concepts explained. "These were their concepts much more than ours." (p. 120)

### Suggestion and the autosuggestion movement (pp. 121–122)
- Sidis (*The Psychology of Suggestion*, 1898) defined suggestibility with examples: copying a friend who rolls up a newspaper, and an absent-minded friend who follows the author in drinking from a glass (p. 121).
- Autosuggestion drew widespread interest from 1921. Coué, the most influential proponent, went on a US book tour in 1922. The key idea: most of us are not successful because we do not believe we can succeed, so one must repeatedly suggest to oneself that one will succeed. Coué advised reciting "Every day in every way I get better and better." Napoleon Hill (1925, 1937) added to the self-empowerment narrative by stressing the subconscious mind and a positive, wealth-building attitude (pp. 121–122).
- It was a mutation of an earlier hypnosis narrative. Marden (1920) wrote that people fall short of their possibilities because they do not think enough of themselves. His example: a hypnotist could convince the strongman Sandow he could not rise from a chair; one must believe one can before one can (p. 122).
- The movement began to fade after 1924 but appears to have had aftereffects. Shiller says Riefenstahl's *Triumph of the Will* (1935) appears to borrow from autosuggestion, and that Hitler's appeal was based in part on inspiring Germany out of its depression. At the time it was widely believed that the Depression resulted from a loss of confidence and that Germans needed a leader to restore it (p. 122).

*Intuition note:* If people believe minds are suggestible, they may expect unstable crowds and act accordingly. Interest in the unseen force of confidence was already strong in the 1920s.

### Forecasting weather, forecasting confidence (pp. 123–125)
- Scientific weather forecasting advanced after two inventions of the 1840s, the telegraph and the barograph. In a Crimean War story (1854), scientists concluded two storms were one and a forecast saved the British and French fleets. By the 1890s newspapers published daily forecasts (p. 123).
- Analogy: changes in public confidence seem analogous to shifting winds or air pressure ("in the air"). It seems natural for people to think that if meteorologists can forecast winds, economists should be able to forecast recessions (p. 123).
- To the extent the public believes economic forecasts, there may be an element of self-fulfilling prophecy: people hear that a recession is imminent and postpone activities. Conversely, because past recessions have always ended, people may come to expect any given one to end. In the author's analogy, storm warnings lasting six months might lead people to cancel activities for six months. With recession forecasts, people might see others cut spending after the warning and take it as evidence of a storm of lost confidence (pp. 123–124).
- The idea that fluctuations repeat follows an older scientific tradition. Halley noted comets at intervals of 75.3 years, predicted a return in 1758 and was proven right (p. 124).
- The earliest ProQuest mention of the business cycle (depression of 1858) appeared beside a reference to weather, noting a business cycle of the same length as a decade-long weather cycle; the writer left it open whether this was coincidence, providence, or not a fact at all. The idea of a fixed-interval cycle has become less popular with economists, but the narrative that recessions and confidence drops are somewhat periodic and forecastable remains entrenched in popular thinking (pp. 124–125).
- Weather forecasting also inspired the idea of statistically documented leading indicators. Mitchell and Burns (1938) pioneered the approach, which encourages people to go into precautionary mode after a stock decline, thus possibly creating the very recession that was forecast. Searches show a long slow epidemic for the term from the 1930s on (p. 125).

### Confidence as a barometer (pp. 125–129)
- We should be able to measure confidence as we measure air pressure. Unlike air pressure, confidence might be subject to influence, "in which case", the text says, good patriots are morally obligated to support public confidence. Coolidge took it upon himself to boost belief in the economy and stock market, and there was great controversy over his reassurances (the "Coolidge-Mellon bull tips") (p. 125).
- Robey (1928) identified a pattern: practically every time the stock market declined significantly or the public decried high borrowing to buy stocks, Coolidge or Mellon made a very optimistic statement or denied any problem. Robey doubted there was any rational basis for it and interpreted it as an effort to maintain confidence (p. 125).
- Shiller says the bull tips may have been part of attempts to mollify influentials who feared any disturbance of investor confidence. A 1928 WSJ anecdote: an executive bullish on his firm's stock would not sell any, because employee-shareholders would hear of it and it might disturb them or hint that they should get out (p. 126).
- In February 1929 the Fed warned it would not support banks lending into a rising market, adding a qualifier that it assumed no right to judge the merits of speculation, but the investing public read between the lines and reacted intensely. On August 9, 1929 the New York Fed raised its rediscount rate. The "battle" between Wall Street and the Fed probably added to the contagion of stories about the 1929 crash and led to a widespread impression that people in the know were sensing overspeculation (p. 126).
- After the crash, disillusionment with prognostications intensified. In 1930 one observer noted a strong tendency among business writers to put out nothing but optimistic statements. In 1931 Noyes (NYT financial editor) said men of affairs signing New Year prophecies will seek a hopeful side and exclude disagreeable offsets (pp. 126–127).
- At the same time no one wanted to be accused of shouting fire in a crowded theater. The narrative goes back to an 1884 NYT report. It did not catch on right away; a 1919 Supreme Court opinion by Holmes tied it to a celebrity, and it picked up a little in the 1930s and then went viral (p. 127).
- Throughout the 1930s the idea took root that the Depression resulted from "reckless talk" by opinion leaders oblivious to its psychological impact. In reality, Shiller says, prominent people seem to have been very aware of the effects, which led to another narrative: the public assumed a general bias toward false optimism and corrected for it. It is easy to see how expectations may have become much more volatile (pp. 127–128).
- Many people saw the Depression as a stampede or panic. Fisher (1930) wrote of the danger of panicky fear and cited "My only fear is the fear of fear" as the words of a courageous man. Mullen (1931) wrote that the only thing we need to fear is fear itself. In 1933 FDR said: "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself". Because he was a celebrity, Roosevelt went viral as the originator, taking credit for an idea that had already been repeated many times. ProQuest shows the phrase used more in the first decade of the 2000s than in the 1930s (p. 128).
- Viral narratives are not easily controlled and may have unintended effects. Emphasising the need for courage may create some patriotic resolve, but such exhortations make it doubtful that others will truly cast aside their fear. Thus identifying the problem as fear may only worsen it (p. 128).
- Poorhouse and unpaid-doctor narratives, even if exaggerated, reduced willingness to spend on anything but the barest necessities, e.g. neglected dental work (p. 129).
- Days after his 1933 inauguration, during a national bank run that had shut all banks, FDR gave a radio "fireside chat" asking people not to continue their demands on banks and not to be stampeded by rumours. The public honoured the request, the run ended and money flowed into the reopened banks (p. 129).
- This narrative constellation still influences us: it has not been powerful enough to prevent recessions but remains in our consciousness and may reassert itself. We follow the closing stock price and the monthly confidence indexes, not because economists urge us to, but because we are still subject to old narratives suggesting that confidence can break as suddenly as a shout of fire in a crowded theater (p. 129).

*Intuition note:* Optimism talk may raise confidence, but the public may correct for it once it assumes leaders talk this way. Stressing fear as the enemy may make others doubt that fear has really been cast aside, which may only worsen the problem.

### Narratives focused on mass unemployment (pp. 129–131)
- Kiplinger (1930) listed loosely stated causes of unemployment: machines, overloaded industrial centres, women entering male jobs, immigration, and business depression. He said there is a large element of truth in each, particularly machines, but that no one of them supplies an answer, nor even all five (p. 130).
- Of the five, only business depression would come to mind in today's popular narrative, which most would relate to loss of confidence. As the Depression wore on, more and more people began to think of it as driven by lost confidence (p. 130).
- Shiller supposes each cause matched a popular 1930 narrative, and some or many of these narratives probably had a long-term orientation, implying the Depression would go on forever (p. 130).
- The Dust Bowl, *The Grapes of Wrath*, Lange's photographs and other images (breadlines, apple sellers, Hoovervilles) became part of the Depression constellation (pp. 130–131).
- The 1930s were a turning point in measurement: before then no statistics reliably measured unemployment, and by the 1930s statistics began to focus on the unemployment rate. The monthly announcement may have encouraged thinking we are at risk of a repeat. A significant increase in the Ngram term "unemployment rate" did not occur until after 1960. The lack of 1930s coverage may reflect the public's unfamiliarity with the quantitative representation; people did not yet clearly differentiate involuntary unemployment from laziness and pauperism, whereas today's narratives focus on blameless unemployment (p. 131).

### A different narrative of the Great Depression develops (pp. 132–135)
- People today tend to identify the causes of the Depression as fear and loss of confidence related to bank failures; bank failures (and shadow-bank failures) were key narratives in 2007–9. Kiplinger's 1930 list did not mention bank failures, most of which happened after 1930 (p. 132).
- Some modern theories that explain the Depression's length and depth without relying directly on these narratives seem plausible. Cole and Ohanian (2004) argue that the 1933 National Industrial Recovery Act prolonged the Depression: it made cartels easier and wage cuts harder, and although the Supreme Court struck it down in 1935 they argue the administration kept the codes in effect. They also argue high unemployment persisted because the employed became "insiders" and the laid-off "outsiders", citing Lindbeck and Snower's argument that insiders ask for higher wages rather than for outsiders to be rehired (p. 132).
- Eichengreen and Temin argue that the Depression's length and pain were related to an unthinking commitment to the gold standard despite more downwardly rigid wages; they have shown that countries that left gold earlier recovered better (p. 132). Friedman and Schwartz blamed the Fed's control of the money supply; Eichengreen and Temin argued that declines in the money supply were mostly caused by the economy, not the Fed. Temin also observed that Friedman and Schwartz indicated no substantial correspondence between bank runs and measures of economic activity (pp. 132–133).
- Shiller says these economists tell only part of the story. Groucho Marx's autobiography recounts taking market tips from everybody (an elevator man, a Wall Streeter, his theatrical producer, someone on a golf course), and he views the experience as great folly. Shiller says ideas about the craziness of the Roaring Twenties and the Depression became legendary through such storytellers, who had much more public influence than economists (p. 133).
- Figure 10.4 suggests far more attention to "Great Depression" in 2009 than during the Depression itself, although people had not yet named it that and called it "hard times". "Breadline" grew rapidly from 1929 to 1934 and has decayed fairly steadily since. Google Trends confirms the 2009 interest, less dramatically (p. 134).
- 2007–9: after the Depression, bank runs were thought cured. Northern Rock (2007) brought back old narratives of panicked depositors, and, Shiller says, led to international skittishness, the Washington Mutual run a year later and the Reserve Prime Fund run days after. These led to an unconventional one-year US guarantee of money market funds. Apparently, governments were aware they could not allow old bank-run stories to feed public anxiety (pp. 134–135).
- In the heart of the recession the Depression narrative may have intertwined with bank-run narratives to create a popular perception: a Roaring Twenties-like euphoric period has ended, banks and stock market are collapsing as in 1929 and the economy might collapse again (p. 135).
- The Depression and its causes (euphoria, then loss of confidence) remain a powerful narrative, constantly on people's minds. Far less remembered are Depression-era narratives of modesty, compassion and simple living, mostly in remission and replaced by success narratives justifying conspicuous consumption (next chapter) (p. 135).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* A bank has 100 depositors with 1 unit each but holds only 50 units in cash. Each depositor chooses *wait* or *withdraw*. Withdrawers are paid in a random queue order until cash runs out. The bank survives if at most 50 people withdraw and fails otherwise. Waiters get 1.1 if the bank survives and 0 if it fails.
- **Everyone else waits (99 wait):** at most one person withdraws, so the bank survives. Waiting pays 1.1; withdrawing pays 1. So waiting is best.
- **Everyone else withdraws (99 withdraw):** the bank fails. Withdrawing pays 1 with probability 50/100, so 0.5 expected. Waiting pays 0. So withdrawing is best.
Both situations are stable, and only beliefs about *others'* actions differ. That is the idea behind Moley's "crisis of confidence": confidence in other depositors, not only in bank assets, can select the outcome. (Canon link; the bank-run equilibrium is not stated formally in this unit.)

- [[bank run equilibrium]] — Moley's depression-as-run story; panic as lost confidence that other depositors will not all withdraw (p. 114; canon link; equilibrium not stated in this unit)

## Questions this raises
- Can confidence-narrative effects be separated from real shocks, given that Ngram counts are only correlations?
- When does official optimism reassure, and when does it make the public doubt it?
- Do consumer-confidence indexes (Michigan, from 1952; Conference Board, 1966) predict spending better than the narratives they replaced?
- How do rival theories (gold standard, NIRA, insider–outsider, monetary) relate to the confidence story?
