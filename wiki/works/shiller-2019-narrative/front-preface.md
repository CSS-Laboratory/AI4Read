# Preface: What Is Narrative Economics?

## L0 — One line
Shiller argues that economics should study how popular stories spread like epidemics and change economic behavior, because leaving them out keeps economists blind to a real mechanism of change and a key input to forecasting.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The preface asks why economists miss big events such as the Great Depression and the 2007–9 crisis. Shiller suggests that mainstream theory leaves out narrative contagion: stories that spread by word of mouth and shift spending, investing and policy. He defines narrative economics as the study of that contagion and of efforts to make stories more contagious. He supports the case with forecasting failures, with Koopmans, Boulding and Keynes, and with examples such as Reagan and 1920s stock tales. He notes that in disease forecasting, understanding epidemics beats purely statistical methods, and he argues economics should use that insight. He ends by outlining the book's four parts and an appendix on epidemic theory.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school history; a rough idea of an epidemic curve (people infected, then recovering) helps.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Shiller was struck by Allen's account of 1920s stories of easy wealth. He says economists never took Allen's book seriously and the idea of narrative contagion never entered their mathematical models (p. ix–x).
- "Going viral" is an epidemic metaphor. Attention goes to the rising part of the infectives curve (the number of people currently "infected" by a story over time), but forgetting (the falling part) will likely be as important a cause of changes in behavior (p. x).
- Narrative economics has two focuses: word-of-mouth contagion of stories, and efforts to create or amplify contagious stories. Shiller studies other people's narratives, not a new one of his own (p. xi).
- Contagion is strongest when people feel a personal tie to someone in the story, such as a celebrity or stock figure. Professional narratives (ideas shared among intellectuals, such as the random walk theory that prices incorporate all information) can be distorted into popular ones (p. xii–xiii).
- Economists' one-year forecasts have been, on the whole, worthless for half a century, and the record of the International Monetary Fund (IMF), as reported in a study Shiller cites, is poor (p. xiv).
- Koopmans (1947) asked for theories grounded in knowledge of human motives. Shiller says, as Koopmans pointed out, traditional approaches fail to examine public beliefs, that is, narrative (p. xv).
- Boulding says economics should be considered a "moral" science and that it "creates the world it is investigating". Shiller adds that we often want to warn rather than forecast, so as to prevent disaster (p. xvi).
- Keynes's 1919 warning about Germany is offered as an example of narrative-based forecasting (p. xvii–xviii).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: Allen's *Only Yesterday* (pp. ix–xi).**
- As an undergraduate, Shiller read Allen's 1931 best seller on the 1920s boom, the 1929 crash and the start of the Depression. It struck him that this period's "rapid-fire contagious narratives" somehow contributed to the changing spirit of the times (p. ix).
- Allen reports dinner-table tales of sudden fortunes, and of people speculating without knowing the company's business. One example: buyers of Seaboard Air Line thought it was an aviation stock, but it was a railroad (p. ix).
- Shiller's view: the opposing narrative, that get-rich-quick schemes are folly, was apparently not very contagious (p. x).
- Shiller says economists never took Allen seriously and narrative contagion never entered their mathematical models. He calls this contagion "the heart of narrative economics" (p. x).
- ProQuest shows that "go viral" appeared in newspapers only around 2009. "Viral marketing" goes back to 1991 as a company name in Nagpur, India. Google Ngrams shows a similar path (p. x).
- Going viral and "trending now" describe the rising part of the infectives curve. The later falling part (forgetting) gets less attention, but for economic narratives it will likely be as important a cause of behavior change (p. x).
- Allen did not formalize contagion of narratives (p. xi).
- Shiller argues we must put narrative contagion into economic theory, or we stay blind to a real mechanism of change and a key input to forecasting. Shiller says the extensive medical literature on forecasting disease epidemics shows that understanding epidemics and their relation to contagion factors can help forecast better than purely statistical methods (p. xi).

*Intuition note:* Stories behave like infections. They spread, peak and fade, and Shiller thinks the fading will likely matter as much as the spreading. On his view, a model that ignores this misses a mechanism of economic change.

**Narrative Economics: What's in a Phrase? (pp. xi–xiii).**
- The term appears in Palgrave's 1894 dictionary, apparently meaning a research method that presents one's own narrative of history. Shiller instead studies others' popular narratives that went viral (p. xi).
- Two elements: (1) word-of-mouth contagion of ideas as stories; (2) efforts to generate new contagious stories or make stories more contagious (p. xi).
- Definition (Oxford English Dictionary): "a story or representation used to give an explanatory or justificatory account of a society, period, etc." Shiller adds that a story may be a song, joke, theory, explanation or plan with emotional resonance, easily conveyed in casual conversation (p. xi).
- Shiller suggests we can think of history as a succession of rare big events in which a story goes viral, often (but not always) with an attractive celebrity attached (pp. xi–xii).
- Example: late-20th-century narratives called free markets "efficient", which led to a public reaction against regulation. Reagan, narrator of *General Electric Theater* (1953–62), entered politics for free markets and won in 1980. Shiller says he used his celebrity to launch a "massive free-markets revolution" with good and ill effects (p. xii).
- Contagion is strongest when people feel a personal tie to someone in or at the root of the story. Shiller's example is the narrative of Trump as tough dealmaker and self-made billionaire, which he says led to Trump's 2016 election. He adds that in many cases a celebrity's name is merely added to an older, weaker story, such as the self-made man (p. xii).
- Other narratives: house prices only go up (house flippers on TV); gold is the safest investment (war and depression stories) (p. xii).
- Narratives are major vectors of rapid change in culture, zeitgeist and economic behavior. They may merge with fads, and marketers amplify them (p. xiii).
- **Professional narratives** circulate among intellectuals. The random walk theory holds that prices incorporate all information, so beating the market is futile. It has an element of truth, though a literature now finds imperfections it does not predict (p. xiii).
- The public may distort such narratives. Example: that buy-and-hold in the domestic stock market is the best investment, which conflicts with the professional canon (p. xiii).
- Past conversations cannot be recorded, so we rely on indirect sources. Contemporary narratives can be traced through social media and Ngrams (p. xiii).

**Better Forecasts of Major Future Events (pp. xiii–xv).**
- Shiller says most contemporary economists tend to think public narratives are "not our field". Other departments find economic theory hard to enter, leaving a gap (p. xiii).
- No economist credibly forecast the worldwide Depression. Only a handful predicted the 2005 US housing peak or the 2007–9 crisis. Late-1920s views were extreme in both directions, and there seems to have been no public forecast of what actually happened (p. xiv).
- Economists forecast a couple of quarters ahead accurately. For half a century their one-year forecasts have been, overall, worthless. Their predictions of negative quarterly US GDP growth a year ahead had no relation to actual outcomes (p. xiv).
- Fathom Consulting: of 469 recessions in 194 countries since 1988, the IMF's *World Economic Outlook* forecast only 17 in the preceding year. It predicted recessions that did not occur 47 times (p. xiv).
- People plan years ahead (college, thirty-year mortgages), so weather forecasting is a weak comparison. Shiller suggests economic theory may need fundamental improvement (p. xiv).
- Economists rarely cite what a businessperson or journalist thinks. Shiller says understanding a complex economy requires many conflicting popular narratives, valid or fallacious (p. xv).
- Koopmans (1947, "Measurement without Theory") criticized looking only at statistical properties of time series for leading indicators. He wanted theory based on knowledge of consumers' motives and habits and firms' profit aims (p. xv).
- Shiller says, "as Koopmans pointed out", traditional approaches fail to examine public beliefs, i.e. narrative. His thesis is that economists can best advance by developing "the art of narrative economics" and combining it with science (p. xv).

*Intuition note:* Koopmans argued that time-series statistics alone are not enough and that theory needs knowledge of human motives. Shiller reads this as a call to examine public beliefs, that is, narrative.

**The Moral Imperative of Anticipating Economic Events (pp. xv–xvii).**
- Forecasting aims to intervene now to improve outcomes. Often, Shiller says, we do not want to forecast but to warn: we want actions that prevent the disaster from happening (p. xvi).
- **Boulding (1969):** economics should be considered a "moral" science, concerned with human thought and ideals. He attacked the doctrine that tastes are given and cannot be studied. He said economics "creates the world it is investigating" (p. xvi).
- Shiller says newspaper accounts of central banks seem to treat the exact amount and timing of rate changes as central, not the accompanying words (p. xvi).
- **Kristol's view (1977, not Shiller's):** business confidence is not psychological but economic. Prospects for profitable investment govern it, "That and nothing else", and what officials do matters, not what they say. He mentioned Keynes's "animal spirits" as something Keynes may have believed governs investment, and dismissed that view (p. xvi).
- **Shiller replies:** Kristol names no forces that act independently of stories. He hints at politicization, and many economists have learned that flattering businesspeople pays. Shiller adds that describing the economy as driven only by abstract forces suggests a moral vacuum (pp. xvi–xvii).

**John Maynard Keynes: Narrative Economist (pp. xvii–xviii).**
- In *Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), Keynes predicted that Germany would become deeply embittered by the Versailles reparations. Others made similar predictions (Jane Addams), but he tied his to economic reality: Germany could not pay (p. xvii).
- Shiller says this exemplifies narrative economics because Keynes focused on how Germans would interpret the treaty given their economic conditions. It was a forecast of a coming war and a "final civil war" (p. xvii).
- World War II began twenty years later and cost sixty-two million lives. Shiller says Keynes's words ("vengeance", "despairing convulsions of revolution") suggest narratives with moral underpinnings, not "pure economics" (p. xviii).

**From Irrational Exuberance to Narrative Economics (pp. xviii–xix).**
- The book builds on decades of work by Shiller and colleagues, notably Akerlof, on his 2017 AEA presidential address, and on the 2018 Marshall Lectures. It links these ideas to epidemiology, with "thought viruses" behind many changes in economic activity (p. xviii).
- Social media speed up the spread, but the book also covers slower eras of telegraph, telephone and newspapers (p. xviii).
- **Structure.** Part I: basic concepts, plus the Bitcoin narrative (epidemic from 2009) and the Laffer curve narrative (viral mostly in the 1970s–80s). Part II: propositions, e.g. perennial narratives may mutate and revive. Part III: nine perennial narratives (confidence, frugality, job insecurity among them). Part IV: the future and research. An appendix links narratives to medical epidemic theory (pp. xviii–xix).

## Toy example (ours, not Shiller's)
A rumor starts in a town of 30 people. Rule: whoever hears it tells 2 unaware people in the next week, then stops (forgets).
- Week 0: 1 person hears (1 heard in total).
- Week 1: 1 teller × 2 = 2 new; total 3.
- Week 2: 2 tellers × 2 = 4 new; total 7.
- Week 3: 4 × 2 = 8 new; total 15.
- Week 4: 8 × 2 = 16, but only 30 − 15 = 15 are unaware, so 15 new; total 30.
- Week 5: 15 tellers, nobody unaware, 0 new. Week 6: 0 tellers.

Active tellers in weeks 1 to 6: 1, 2, 4, 8, 15, 0. The "going viral" phase doubles each week, and then the story dies. A forecaster who looks only at the early weeks would extrapolate growth and miss the fall.

## Concepts
- [[economic-narrative]] — a contagious story, song, joke, theory or plan with emotional resonance that can be told in casual talk and that shapes economic behavior (p. xi)
- [[narrative-contagion-sir]] — stories spread person to person like an epidemic, rising and then falling as people forget (pp. x–xi)
- [[infectives-curve]] (new) — the count of currently "infected" people over time; rising = going viral, falling = forgetting (p. x)
- [[narrative-economics]] (new) — the study of narrative contagion and of efforts to make stories more contagious, and of their effect on economic events (p. xi)
- [[professional-vs-popular-narratives]] (new) — expert-community ideas that may be distorted when they become popular (p. xiii)
- [[animal-spirits]] — the idea that the propensity to invest is governed by high or low "animal spirits" among businessmen. Here Kristol attributes it to Keynes and rejects it (p. xvi)
- [[efficient-market-hypothesis]] — here appears as the random-walk narrative: prices incorporate all information, so beating the market is futile (p. xiii)

## Questions this raises
- How can the strength or timing of a narrative's spread be measured, given that old conversations cannot be recorded (p. xiii)?
- Can epidemic-style forecasts really beat statistical ones for economic variables, as the medical literature on disease forecasting suggests (p. xi)?
- Why did the opposing 1920s narrative not spread (p. x), and what makes some stories more contagious?
- How would Kristol's "profitable prospects and nothing else" be tested against a narrative explanation (p. xvi)?
