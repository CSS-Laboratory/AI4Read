# Seven Propositions of Narrative Economics

## L0 — One line
Shiller proposes seven general features of economic narratives (speed and size, low visibility, constellations, mutation, truth not enough to stop false stories, repetition, human attachment) as a framework for spotting and studying them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shiller asks what general features economic narratives have, so that historically important ones can be understood and new ones identified as they develop. He offers seven propositions: epidemics vary in speed and size; important narratives may be rarely discussed; constellations of narratives matter more than single ones; a narrative's impact may change through time; truth is not enough to stop false narratives; opportunities for repetition drive contagion; human interest, identity and patriotism help narratives spread. The propositions are a framework for the historical cases in part III. They matter because popular narratives can have economic consequences, and important ones may be rarely discussed and hard to link to action.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a narrative "epidemic" with contagion and recovery (forgetting) rates from earlier chapters; basic factorial notation.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Narrative epidemics differ in contagion and forgetting rates, so some are fast, some slow, some big, some small. A fast start does not show long-run importance (pp. 88–89).
- Economically important narratives may be a tiny share of everyday talk. Krock's 1932 trip found banal talk, and people rarely discuss big decisions such as saving (pp. 89–91).
- Narratives reinforce one another in constellations. The link to action is rarely spoken and may be nonverbal (pp. 92–93).
- A narrative's effect depends on its current form and context. The 1914 and 1939 war starts produced very different stock-market reactions (pp. 93–95).
- A story's contagion is unaffected by its truth. False stories spread faster in one Twitter study, and corrections may spread slower (pp. 95–97).
- Contagion depends on frequent chances to slip a story into conversation. "Happy Birthday to You" spread in part because birthdays recur (pp. 97–100).
- Narratives usually rely on human-interest stories, and a recognised face lowers the likely rate of forgetting. The preference for compatriots helps explain why the foreign origin of ideas is often not acknowledged (pp. 100–103).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 87).** Shiller wants basic propositions to understand past narratives and spot new ones. Using Bitcoin, he says an economic narrative may remind people of forgotten facts, explain how the economy works, and shape how they see the purpose of an action. It may also make an action a learning experience, or a way to take part in the story (p. 87).

**Proposition 1: Epidemics can be fast or slow, big or small (pp. 88–89).**
- There is no standard course for a narrative epidemic. Rapid growth does not mean long-run significance. Medical-epidemiology models in the book's appendix allow parameters giving fast-big, fast-small, slow-big and slow-small epidemics (p. 88).
- A narrative can last for decades, longer than the data series economists use. So we must not rush to judge its impact. Treating it like a days-long Facebook meme could hide a long boom caused by a slow epidemic (p. 88).
- Best-seller lists cover short intervals (the New York Times list covers the current week). The short time frame explains why the Bible and Koran never appear. Hardly any books on decades-old lists are familiar; most were flash-in-the-pan short-term epidemics (p. 88).
- Contagion rates differ. A national emergency such as the start of a war might be an example of very high contagion. A patriotic story told only at suitable occasions might be an example of a successful narrative with very low contagion, which can still develop slowly into a huge epidemic if the forgetting rate is low enough (pp. 88–89).
- Recovery (forgetting) rates differ. They are often high for isolated narratives and low for narratives with constant reminders. Seeing beggars recalls depression narratives (p. 89).
- Appendix model, as Shiller reports it: a high contagion parameter with a low recovery rate means almost everyone eventually hears the story, sometimes very fast. A low contagion parameter with an even lower recovery rate can still reach most people, but slowly (p. 89).
- Example: after the 19 October 1987 crash, Shiller surveyed a random sample of US high-income individuals. 97% heard of it on the day. The average time was 1:56 p.m. Eastern. Most heard by word of mouth, not newspapers or evening TV (p. 89).

*Intuition note:* Speed and size depend on two knobs: how easily a story is passed on and how fast it is forgotten. A story can be a quick flash or a slow giant. Rapid early growth does not by itself show long-run importance.

**Proposition 2: Important narratives may be a very small share of popular talk (pp. 89–91).**
- Do not assume that the most important narratives are the ones constantly talked about. People always talk, so some narrative is always spreading. We must not be distracted by small talk that is not useful in explaining economic change (pp. 89–90).
- Arthur Krock (New York Times, 1932) travelled 10,000 miles, visited twenty cities in a month, and listened to hundreds of people. He was a little surprised that almost all talk was banal: little about books or plays, no new joke, no enthusiasm for any candidate (p. 90).
- Shiller reads this as a warning. Narratives that are contagious only in certain venues, or only at certain times, may still matter. Economic theories are not casual topics even though the media discuss them (p. 90).
- A Cleveland taxi driver spoke of men eating from garbage cans and asked when prosperity would return. Shiller reads this as a desperation narrative with a mental image and disgust. He thinks the driver probably hoped for some narrative offering clues about the future, not a quantitative answer (p. 91).
- Conversations rarely touch big decisions such as how much to save. Shiller says "maybe" the Depression-era decision was influenced by hardship narratives and by worried experts. Individual vague narratives might not determine behaviour, but a constellation may (p. 91).

**Proposition 3: Constellations have more impact than any one narrative (pp. 92–93).**
- A constellation is a group of narratives with different origins that seem grouped around one basic idea. They reinforce one another's contagion. Shiller avoids "grand narrative" and "metanarrative" because they suggest more organisation than simple story contagion warrants (p. 92).
- Some stories lose names and become "They say that…", which signals a constellation of authoritative voices. A narrative can borrow contagion from other contagious ones (p. 92).
- Cryptocurrency example: nearly two thousand coins competed with Bitcoin as of 2018. Among Bitcoin stories, singer Lily Allen turned down a 2009 Bitcoin-paid gig. Had she accepted and held, she would have been a billionaire by 2017. The story works by evoking regret (p. 92).
- Constellations are hard to delimit. Most narratives are never written down and sit in the background. In a car-purchase talk with a spouse, you may not tell the stories that make you feel secure. So the narrative–action link is hard to establish, and the final link may be nonverbal (p. 93).

*Intuition note:* A single vague story might not decide behaviour, but a constellation of related stories may. Because such stories are rarely spoken at decision time, the link to action is hard to establish.

**Proposition 4: Impact may change through time (pp. 93–95).**
- Impact depends on the narrative's current mutation (changed version) and related narratives. Digital word-flags may not mean the same thing over time, so narratives must be read in context (p. 93).
- 1987 crash: its memory need not make markets vulnerable if people think today differs. In 1987, along with other factors, narratives about portfolio insurance led to a predisposition to consider selling that was peculiar to that time (p. 93).
- 1914: after Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia (28 July), prices fell steeply. The NYSE closed until 12 December. Silber (2014) describes stories and rumours: a "European gold rush" of gold shipped to Europe; talk of the Panic of 1907; a baseless rumour that Russians were hoarding gold (pp. 93–94).
- 1939: after the UK declared war on Germany (3 September), the S&P Composite gained 9.6% in one day. Newspapers were surprised. Apparently a narrative that WWI had proved profitable for investors who held on played a role. The human stories of the two wars may be similar, but the narratives about successful investors differed (p. 94).
- Names matter. Synonyms never have exactly the same meaning, and a renamed narrative may attach to a different constellation (p. 94).

**Proposition 5: Truth is not enough to stop false narratives (pp. 95–97).**
- After the 2007–9 crisis, near-zero rates were read as a harbinger of a "lost decade", as in Japan in the 1990s. This was one observation with no statistical significance, yet it revived Great Depression narratives and "secular stagnation" fears (p. 95).
- Van Evera (1984), as Shiller reports: WWI started at least partly because a false narrative, the "Cult of the Offensive" (the country that attacks first will generally have the advantage), went viral. It led to an arms race and a war disastrous for both sides. Norman Angell's *The Great Illusion* (1911) refuted it, but the refutation did not spread as fast as the illusion (p. 95).
- By analogy, economic activity is not always based on up-to-date information. It can follow whatever narratives are going viral (p. 95).
- In simple, story-free facts (highway names), society stays on target. But the WHO (2003) found only about 50% adherence to chronic-illness treatment in developed countries. Advice shades into speculation and fiction. Shiller concludes that a story's contagion rate is unaffected by its underlying truth (p. 96).
- Vosoughi et al. (*Science*, 2018): stories vetted by six fact-checking sites (95–98% agreement). Across 126,000 rumours spread by three million people, false stories had six times the retweet rate of true ones. Shiller notes that the result may be specific to the study's time, when mistrust of conventional media was higher than usual. The authors read it as people being "more likely to share novel information" (pp. 96–97).
- Shiller's twist: a correction may be less contagious than the falsehood, so the false narrative may affect activity long after correction (p. 97).

*Intuition note:* Spreading and being true are separate things. The authors link spreading to novelty. So a correction may be less contagious than the falsehood and may act too late.

**Proposition 6: Contagion builds on opportunities for repetition (pp. 97–100).**
- Contagion depends on how often a story can be slipped into conversation, since changing subject is usually impolite. Novel ideas and concepts may add openings. Shiller says stock-market narratives probably became more contagious once the public watched stock indexes (1920s–30s), and the same happened with housing narratives after home-price indexes (after the 1970s). Media kept revisiting the indexes (p. 97).
- "Happy Birthday to You" is Shiller's example. Attached to it is a story: the ritual of cake, candles, wish and applause (pp. 97–98). It spread worldwide in many translations. It is contagious in part because birthdays constantly recur, not because it is anyone's favourite song. No edict or campaign drove it. Digital counts show English growth like an epidemic in the 1920s–30s, a pause around WWII, then renewed growth (p. 98).
- Warner/Chappell Music claimed a 1935 copyright but lost it in 2016, when the song was shown to be strikingly similar to the 1893 "Good Morning to All" (same melody, similar words) (p. 98).
- The mutation joined the growing ritual of the birthday party (popular from about the 1890s). That association raised contagion. Yearly recurrence reinforced memory and reduced the recovery rate. Inserting the person's name added human interest (p. 99).
- Why couldn't the original authors foresee the hit? The combinatorics. The song has sixteen words. Changing half of them gives $16!/8! = 518{,}918{,}400$ ways to replace them. With one hundred simple candidate words for the eight replaced words, there are $100^8$ (ten quadrillion) times 518,918,400 variants (p. 99).
- So the invention was likely a random, unlikely event. It was unappreciated at first, and its author is forgotten. It later led to a constellation of narratives in movies, TV and social media (pp. 99–100).

**Proposition 7: Narratives thrive on attachment (pp. 100–103).**
- Human-interest stories are attractive. A recognised face or voice engages our models of people and lowers forgetting. But such stories may not suffice on their own. Those who aspire to create viral narratives must choose their celebrities carefully, because narratives work best when the audience recognises and identifies with the celebrity (p. 100).
- George Washington cherry-tree story: it first appeared in print soon after his 1799 death, in Weems's book, as told by "an aged lady". Google counts: 188,000 hits for "I can't tell a lie" with "Washington", over a third as many as for the phrase alone. With "Lincoln" it is 102,000. Shiller says it "must be" the patriotic appeal. The story belongs to a constellation about honesty, a tradition that has "likely" helped the US economy by creating trust and limiting bribery (pp. 100–101).
- Different versions of a narrative swap in celebrities suited to the audience. Existing stories about a celebrity can enhance contagion. Constellations around celebrities are self-reinforcing, and the celebrities may reach superhuman status. Washington's face is on every one-dollar bill and quarter (pp. 101–102).
- Sometimes pithy quotes coined by ordinary people become contagious only after a famous name is attached. "From each according to his ability…" is attributed to Marx since the mid-twentieth century. But Louis Blanc stressed it in 1851, and a variation is in the Bible. The true-source story will not go viral because it is not contagious. A celebrity's discredit can end a narrative's contagion regardless of its truth (p. 102).
- Patriotism: people prefer individuals from their own country or group. So the spread of ideas is often not acknowledged, since acknowledging it requires admitting foreign origin and practically nobody has an incentive to present an idea as foreign. This creates an illusion that important ideas arose with a compatriot. Shiller also mentions party, regional and religious loyalty (p. 102).
- Patriotism also includes the feeling that only at home do important things happen. Example: CBS's "Your World in 90 Seconds" covers almost only US news, though the US has about 5% of world population (pp. 102–103).

**Summary (p. 103).** The seven propositions are restated in the list (p. 103). The summary adds: "Truth matters, but only if it is in-your-face obvious." Part III will use them as a framework for historical narratives (p. 103).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Early-phase growth of "tellers" of a story. Assume each teller passes the story to $c$ new tellers per period and $r$ of the tellers stop (forget). Then $I_{t+1}=I_t(1+c-r)$. We ignore saturation, so this holds only while the story is still small. Start with $I_0=10$.
- Story A: $c=0.5$, $r=0.1$, factor 1.4. $I_1=14$, $I_2=19.6$, $I_3=27.44$, $I_4\approx38.4$, $I_5\approx53.8$.
- Story B: $c=0.2$, $r=0.1$, factor 1.1. $I_1=11$, $I_2=12.1$, $I_3=13.31$, $I_4\approx14.6$, $I_5\approx16.1$.
- Story C: $c=0.2$, $r=0.2$, factor 1.0. It stays at 10 forever.

A is "fast", B is "slow", and C never takes off. Early fast growth alone does not show how big a story will finally become; this toy ignores saturation and fading, so it cannot say that. A reminder-rich story lowers $r$ and moves a C-type story towards B.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — contagious story that reminds people of facts, explains how the economy works, and affects how they see the justification or purpose of economic actions (p. 87)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — stories spread and fade; speed and size depend on contagion and recovery parameters (p. 88)
- [[forgetting-rate]] (new) — the recovery rate. It is low for narratives with constant reminders or a recognised face (pp. 89, 100)
- [[narrative-constellation]] (new) — narratives of different origins grouped by one idea that reinforce each other's contagion (p. 92)
- [[narrative-mutation]] (new) — a changed version of a narrative, with possibly different contagion and meaning (pp. 93, 99)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — only loosely related: economic activity is not always based on up-to-date information and is sometimes based on whatever narratives are going viral (p. 95)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Contagion and forgetting rates ↔ epidemiology: infection and recovery parameters set speed and size, as in the appendix models mentioned on p. 88.
- [structural-analogy] Narratives unspoken at decision time ↔ dispersed / tacit knowledge: influence without explicit articulation (p. 93).
- [structural-analogy] Impact changing as context changes ↔ Lucas critique: a relation observed in one setting may not hold in another (pp. 93–94).
- [metaphor] Constellations reinforcing contagion ↔ network effects: each related story raises the value of the others.

## Questions this raises
- How could one measure a "constellation" when most narratives are unwritten and rarely voiced (p. 93)?
- If correction is less contagious than falsehood, what design of correction could spread faster (p. 97)?
- The 1939 explanation is hedged ("apparently"). What evidence could test it (p. 94)?
- Can word-flag counts be made reliable given that meaning changes over time (p. 93)?
