# Why Do Some Narratives Go Viral?

## L0 — One line
Which narratives go viral depends partly on a human element interacting with economic circumstances and partly on apparently random small beginnings that can get amplified, so history is less orderly than later stories make it look.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do a few economic narratives spread widely while most fail? Shiller says the cause is hard to state or quantify. Scholars say narratives are central to human thinking and motivation. Narratives are universal, tied to norms, scripts and an interest in conspiracies. Examples are the sit-in, *Uncle Tom's Cabin* and the Rollaboard suitcase. An online music-market experiment suggests that when people see others' choices, random early choices can be amplified into unequal hits. Shiller also says a network of minds sometimes acts almost like a random number generator. His lesson: major events happen because of seemingly irrelevant narrative mutations, so history is not the logically ordered sequence later narratives present.

**Prerequisites:** the epidemic analogy (contagion rate, forgetting rate); basic idea of social norms and imitation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Virality is hard to explain or quantify. It mixes a human element with economic circumstances and some apparent randomness (p. 31).
- Scholars have argued that narratives are central to human thinking; Sartre, dreams and social movements are the examples given (pp. 31–32).
- The sit-in word spread like an epidemic, with a hump-shaped curve peaking around 1970; *Uncle Tom's Cabin* helped shape sympathy leading to the Civil War (pp. 32–33).
- Narrative is a human universal, and politeness norms help it spread. Stories transmit fear without direct observation (pp. 33–35).
- Popular narratives often carry an "us versus them" theme, and in extreme cases focus on imagined conspiracies. A narrative promotes a point of view; a chronicle merely lists events (pp. 35–37).
- Narratives may be seen as scripts, also called social norms. Imitating others is rational in one sense, but traditional theory does not model that kind of rationality (p. 37).
- The wheeled-suitcase history suggests a good idea can fail until it is packaged glamorously (pp. 38–39).
- Shiller takes the music-market experiment as confirmation that random small beginnings can lead to big epidemics (pp. 39–40).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 31).** Shiller says it is hard to state or quantify why few narratives go viral. The answer is a human element interacting with economic circumstances. Beyond simple regularities, a network of minds sometimes acts almost like a random number generator. The apparent randomness has to do with randomness in the mutation of stories into more contagious forms, and with moments of individual lives and attention that can cause a sudden climax of public attention (p. 31).

**Spontaneity of narratives (pp. 31–33).**
- Early twentieth-century scholars began to see narratives as central to thinking and motivation. Sartre (1938): a person sees everything through stories and tries to live as if recounting his life (p. 31).
- Dreams have characters, settings and event structure. Neuroscientists link them to a storytelling instinct and to spontaneous confabulation (inventing false memories or stories) seen in some damaged brains (p. 32).
- Sociologist Polletta: 1960s students called sit-ins unplanned, "like a fever." The story began with four students at Greensboro on 1 February 1960. It spread by word of mouth and news media, and within weeks across the US. Activists tried to spread it but did not tightly control the movement (p. 32).
- The word "sit-in" followed a hump-shaped epidemic curve; Google Ngrams show growth until 1970. "Teach-in" had a similar but less intense curve that faded earlier (p. 33).
- *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) sold over a million copies. A hit song, "Eliza's Flight", appeared in 1852, and many "Tom shows", typically including the Eliza scene, likely infected far more people than the printed book did. Shiller says these narratives played an unmistakable role in the North's decision to invade the South; the Civil War began in 1861 (p. 33).

*Intuition note:* Stories can spread like diseases, through people talking and through media. In the sit-in case, activists tried to spread the story but did not tightly control it.

**Universality (pp. 33–35).**
- Anthropologist Brown lists narrative as a human universal. Narrative is uniquely human, and some have proposed names like *Homo narrans*. Shiller says *Homo sapiens* is more flattering but not necessarily more accurate (p. 34).
- Plato (*Republic*) has Socrates worry that horrible stories make guardians too excitable. Cicero says nature forms people to be storytellers (p. 34).
- Other species have culture but not narrative transmission. Monkeys and birds learn fear by observing attacks or others' fear, which is imperfect. Human stories can spread fear without observing the stimulus. A strong enough story can produce a fight-or-flight response (pp. 34–35).
- Politeness norms also universally ease the transmission of narratives. People seek feedback to confirm or disconfirm their thoughts. It seems that the human mind strives for an enduring understanding by forming events into a narrative embedded in social interaction (p. 35).
- Music is also universal. Successful instrumental music typically merges into program music with a suggested story (p. 35).

**Conspiracy theories (pp. 35–36).** Popular narratives often have an "us versus them" Manichaean tone (good versus evil). Hofstadter says conspiracy narratives show "almost touching concern with factuality" (p. 36). Alertness to conspiracies is rational, since real ones exist. Still, the mind seems to have a built-in interest in them. This seems related to reciprocity and vengeance, which matter for economic behavior such as bargaining and punishing unfairness at a cost (p. 36).

**Story and narrative (pp. 36–37).** Per Merriam-Webster, a narrative reflects and promotes a point of view or set of values, so it is a particular form of story. Specific events are often little more than "bits of color" that make a concept more contagious (p. 36). Kasparov: even chess players see games as stories. White: the demand for closure in history is a demand for moral meaning (p. 37). Economists have tended to write as if a benevolent dictator could implement a plan; no such planner exists, and people, who may be selfish or altruistic, are influenced by stories (p. 37).

**Scripts and rolling suitcases (pp. 37–39).**
- Schank and Abelson: narratives may be seen as scripts, also called social norms. Example: the "prudent person rule" makes fiduciaries mimic a prudent person (p. 37).
- When in doubt, people may adopt roles they have heard of. In one sense it is rational to copy apparently successful people, since they may have unobserved reasons. Traditional theory does not model this and sees imitation as reflexive. Following scripts often looks "quite stupid" (p. 37).
- Ideas not packaged as scripts are often missed. Wheeled suitcases: Sadow (1972, leather strap, flopped over) had limited acceptance. Plath's Rollaboard (rigid collapsible handle) became popular in the 1990s. A former patent examiner told Shiller of an 1887 wheeled trunk patent that looks like much the same idea. John Allan May tried to sell a wheeled suitcase from 1932, told 125 groups and possibly 1,500 individuals, and was laughed at (p. 38).
- Shiller's "best guess": with Plath's invention, glamour overcame the sense that wheels looked ridiculous. 1991 ads tied it to pilots. Passengers saw flight crews using Rollaboards, and 1993 ads cited aircrews. "Maybe that is all it took" (p. 39).

*Intuition note:* People often fail to notice ideas that are not part of a script or not packaged well enough; Shiller's best guess is that glamour helped the Rollaboard spread.

**Experimental evidence (pp. 39–40).** Salganik and colleagues built an artificial online music market. Unknown bands' songs could be listened to, rated and downloaded. Subjects never communicated with one another except that they could observe songs' popularity. They were randomly assigned to an independent condition (no view of others' choices) or a shared condition (eight separate worlds, seeing downloads in their own world only). In the extreme shared version, songs were ranked by downloads; each customer saw earlier customers' choices (pp. 39–40). Findings: each world developed its own hits, only imperfectly correlated across worlds. Inequality of success was uniformly higher than in the independent world. Shiller says it seems logical to conclude that something about the random initial choices got amplified. He thinks the effect is likely even stronger in the real world because marketers play up audience size. He says the research may be taken as experimental confirmation that random small beginnings can lead to big epidemics (p. 40).

**Lesson (p. 40).** History is not the logically ordered sequence that later narratives present. Major things happen because of seemingly irrelevant mutations with slightly higher contagion rates, slightly lower forgetting rates, or first-mover effects. These can feed back into bigger narrative constellations, which the next chapter examines via the Laffer curve (p. 40).

*Intuition note:* If people copy what others already chose, random early choices can be amplified into big hits. So "why did this succeed?" may have no deep answer.

## Toy example
(Ours.) Two songs, A and B, are equally good. Ten people choose in order. Each picks the song with more downloads so far, with ties broken by a coin flip. The first coin flip gives A, so A = 1, B = 0. Every later person sees A ahead and picks A, so the final tally is A = 10, B = 0. Rerun with the first flip giving B and the result is B = 10, A = 0. Under independent choice, each person flips a coin and the expected result is about 5–5. Copying turns a tiny random start into a big, unequal win, as in Salganik's shared worlds. This is extreme; in the experiment, hits differed across worlds only imperfectly.

## Concepts
- [[economic narrative]] — per the Merriam-Webster definition Shiller quotes, a way of presenting a situation that reflects and promotes a particular point of view or set of values (p. 36)
- [[narrative contagion (SIR)]] — stories spread like epidemics, with hump-shaped curves (pp. 32–33)
- [[bounded rationality / heuristics]] — imitation and script-following not modeled by traditional theory (canon link; p. 37 describes the idea, not the term)
- [[narrative script / social norm]] (new) — narratives as scripts guiding behavior, e.g. prudent person rule (p. 37)
- [[first-mover effect in narratives]] (new) — an early lead gives one narrative a head start (p. 40)

## Canonical instances
- [[sit-in-1960]] — word and story spread as an epidemic (pp. 32–33)
- [[uncle-toms-cabin]] — novel and "Tom shows" built sympathy before the Civil War (p. 33)
- [[rollaboard-suitcase]] — a good idea with limited early acceptance that became popular in the 1990s; Shiller's best guess is that glamour helped (pp. 38–39)
- [[salganik-music-market]] — shared-world experiment that Shiller takes as confirming random amplification (pp. 39–40)

## Questions this raises
- Can contagion and forgetting rates of narratives be measured, given Shiller says the causes are hard to quantify?
- If outcomes are partly random, how can an economist forecast which narratives matter?
- Is imitation of apparent success "rational"? How would a model represent "When in doubt, imitate"?
