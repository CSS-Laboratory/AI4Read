# Arrow, K. J. (1951). *Social Choice and Individual Values*. Cowles Commission Monograph No. 12. New York: John Wiley & Sons; London: Chapman & Hall. xi + 99 pp. (2nd ed. 1963; 3rd ed. Yale University Press 2012.)

## In one paragraph
Arrow asks whether a procedure can turn individual rankings of social states into one rational social ranking while meeting a few reasonable conditions. He starts from the paradox of voting, in which majority rule can cycle. He assumes individuals have only ordinal preferences, with no utility comparison between persons. He then lists conditions for a "social welfare function": a wide domain of individual orderings, positive association, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-imposition and non-dictatorship. His General Possibility Theorem shows that with at least three alternatives and unrestricted individual orderings, any function that gives a social ordering and satisfies Conditions 2 and 3 is imposed or dictatorial. The compensation principle and the "individualistic" assumptions do not escape this result. The remaining hope is to restrict individual orderings, for example by single-peakedness. The book turned "what does society want?" into a formal question that can be proved.

## The path through the work
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch01-i]] — Arrow asks whether it is formally possible to build a procedure from known individual tastes to social decisions that meets natural conditions and is rational, a problem illustrated by the paradox of voting, while setting aside game aspects, changing tastes and non-rational individuals.
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch02-ii]] — Arrow sets the formal basis of his book: each person has only an ordinal "preferred-or-indifferent" relation $R$ (connected and transitive) over social states, with no cardinal or interpersonal utility, and choices follow from it.
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch03-iii]] — Arrow defines a social welfare function as a rule that states a corresponding social ordering for each set of individual orderings, and imposes five "apparently reasonable" conditions on it.
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch04-iv]] — Arrow argues that the compensation principle with compensation actually paid only restates unanimity comparison, and that the version with compensation merely possible (Kaldor, Scitovsky) does not give a consistent social ordering meeting his conditions.
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch05-v]] — With at least three alternatives that individuals may rank in any way, no social welfare function that gives a social ordering and satisfies Conditions 2 and 3 can avoid being imposed or dictatorial.
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch06-vi]] — Requiring each person's preferences to depend only on his own commodity bundle (and to favour more of everything) does not remove the impossibility of a satisfactory social welfare function when there is more than one commodity.
- [[arrow-1951-socialchoice/ch07-vii]] — A workable social welfare function needs some similarity in individuals' attitudes toward social alternatives. Full unanimity is not needed: Black's single-peakedness suffices for majority rule when the number of voters is odd, though Arrow says it is not obviously applicable (p. 89).

## Big ideas across units
- **Collective rationality means ordering.** Society should be able to rank alternatives as a rational individual does. Majority voting can fail this by cycling, and the paradox of voting returns as the core of the impossibility result. Units: ch01-i, ch02-ii, ch05-v, ch07-vii.
- **Ordinal preferences only, with no interpersonal comparison.** Utility is only an indicator of behaviour. Arrow rejects interpersonal comparison as meaningless, and Conditions 1 and 3 together exclude it. Units: ch01-i, ch02-ii, ch03-iii, ch05-v.
- **Reasonable conditions on aggregation.** A social welfare function is defined by conditions on how individual orderings map to a social one. These include independence of irrelevant alternatives, positive association, non-imposition and non-dictatorship, and together they express citizens' sovereignty and rationality. Units: ch03-iii, ch04-iv, ch05-v.
- **Impossibility theorem.** Under unrestricted domain, any rule satisfying Conditions 2 and 3 is imposed or dictatorial. The proof uses decisive sets and a smallest decisive set. Units: ch03-iii (statement cited), ch05-v (proof), ch06-vi (extension).
- **Welfare-economics shortcuts fail.** Unanimity (Pareto-type) comparison gives only a partial ordering. The Kaldor and Scitovsky compensation tests are arbitrary or intransitive. Studying "maximal states" is of limited use. Units: ch04-iv, ch06-vi.
- **Individualism alone does not rescue possibility.** Restricting each person's taste to their own commodities fixes only some pairwise comparisons, so the impossibility stays. Bergson's scheme is mathematically the same problem. Units: ch04-iv, ch06-vi.
- **Escape routes: restrict the domain or demand similarity.** Arrow relaxes the wide-domain requirement. Black's single-peaked preferences make majority rule work for an odd number of voters. He also considers the general will, consensus on ends, and agreement on the decision process, and finds no easy general solution. Units: ch05-v, ch07-vii.
- **Voting and the market as one category.** Both are collective social choice, so the result is read as applying to voting schemes of any complexity and to the market mechanism. Units: ch01-i, ch05-v, ch07-vii.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch01-i (the problem), ch02-ii (the formal basis), ch03-iii (the conditions), and ch05-v (the theorem and proof). These match the catalog's focus on Chs. I, II and V.
- **Dependencies:** ch03-iii uses the definitions of R, P, I and the choice function from ch02-ii. ch05-v needs Conditions 1–5 from ch03-iii. ch06-vi restates Theorem 2 from ch05-v and uses the quasi-ordering and maximal-state ideas from ch04-iv. ch07-vii builds on ch03-iii, ch05-v and ch06-vi.
- **Can be skipped or skimmed on a first pass:** ch04-iv (compensation principle) is a detailed application to welfare economics. Its main conclusion is that the Kaldor and Scitovsky forms do not give a consistent social ordering. The technical parts of ch06-vi, such as Lemma 4 and Szpilrajn's theorem, can also wait.
- **Read after the theorem:** ch07-vii is the constructive follow-up. It shows which restrictions (single-peakedness) restore possibility and why Arrow doubts that they apply widely.
