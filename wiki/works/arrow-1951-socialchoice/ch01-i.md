# Introduction: types of social choice and limits of the analysis

## L0 — One line
Arrow asks whether it is formally possible to build a procedure from known individual tastes to social decisions that meets natural conditions and is rational, a problem illustrated by the paradox of voting, while setting aside game aspects, changing tastes and non-rational individuals.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Social choices are made by voting, by the market, by a dictator, or by convention. In their ideal forms, dictatorship and convention involve no clash of wills. Voting and the market combine many people's tastes, so Arrow asks whether such combining can be "rational" in the way one individual can. In his example of three voters and three options, majority voting gives a cycle, so it fails the rationality condition there. He links this to welfare economics and to the dispute over comparing utilities between persons. The study is formal only. It ignores play motives, strategic misrepresentation of tastes, and the effect of the decision process on tastes. It assumes rational individuals.

**Prerequisites:** preference ordering (ranking), majority rule, basic utility idea from consumer theory.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Four methods of social choice: voting, the market, dictatorship, and convention (a code); voting and the market, by contrast, amalgamate (combine) the tastes of many individuals (pp. 1–2).
- Central question: can a procedure from known individual tastes to social decisions meet natural conditions and still be rational? The study is purely formal (p. 2).
- Paradox of voting: with three voters and three alternatives, pairwise majorities can form a cycle, so in that example majority rule fails collective rationality, i.e. the community cannot order the alternatives once for all and pick the highest available one (pp. 2–3).
- If rationality is identified with maximization, then finding a social maximum (a best social state derived from individual desires) is the central problem of welfare economics, the field that judges social states (p. 3).
- Any rule for aggregating individual utilities implies a value judgment (a judgment about what is better). The economist should test whether the judgments are mutually consistent (pp. 4–5).
- Voting and the market are treated as special cases of one category, collective social choice (p. 5).
- Excluded: game aspects (play, strategic misrepresentation) and tastes shaped by the decision process. Assumed: all individuals are rational (pp. 6–8).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. The types of social choice (pp. 1–6)
**Four methods.** In a capitalist democracy, voting typically makes "political" decisions and the market typically makes "economic" ones (p. 1). In the emerging democracies with mixed economies (Great Britain, France, Scandinavia) the same two modes prevail, with more scope for voting (p. 1). Elsewhere, and in smaller units, decisions are sometimes made by single individuals or small groups, and sometimes (more and more rarely) by traditional rules such as a religious code (p. 1). Footnote 1 notes that this classification follows Knight's custom, authority and consensus, with consensus split into voting and market (p. 1).

**Dictatorship and convention vs. voting and market.** In ideal dictatorship only one will is involved. In an ideal society ruled by convention there is a divine or assumed common will. So neither involves a conflict of wills (p. 2). Voting and the market amalgamate many tastes (p. 2). Dictatorship and convention can be rational in the sense that an individual can be. Arrow asks whether such consistency can be attributed to collective choice where many wills are involved (p. 2). Footnote 2 assumes the dictator, like the economic man, can always decide and decides the same way when faced with the same alternatives. This ability is called a symptom of an integrated personality, and the analogous problem for voting or the market is that of an integrated society. The formal existence of aggregation methods is called a necessary condition for such an integrated society; whether it is sufficient, or even an important part of the sufficient condition, is called dubious (fn. 2, p. 2).

**Scope.** The study covers only formal aspects: is it formally possible to build a procedure from known individual tastes to social decision-making that meets certain natural conditions (p. 2)?

**Paradox of voting.** A community has three voters and three alternatives (his examples: disarmament, cold war, hot war) (p. 2). Rational community behaviour is defined by analogy with consumer theory: it orders the alternatives once for all and picks the highest-ranked available one (p. 2). Natural rule: $x$ is preferred to $y$ if a majority prefers $x$ to $y$ (pp. 2–3). Let $A,B,C$ be alternatives and 1, 2, 3 the individuals (p. 3):

| Individual | Ranking (best to worst) |
|---|---|
| 1 | $A, B, C$ |
| 2 | $B, C, A$ |
| 3 | $C, A, B$ |

A majority prefers $A$ to $B$ (individuals 1, 3) and a majority prefers $B$ to $C$ (1, 2). So the community prefers $A$ to $B$ and $B$ to $C$. Rationality then requires $A$ preferred to $C$. But a majority (2, 3) prefers $C$ to $A$ (p. 3). So the rule fails the rationality condition as ordinarily understood (p. 3). Arrow asks whether other aggregation methods exist that are rational and satisfactory in other ways (p. 3). Footnotes: this pairwise method is that used in deliberative bodies, and the paradox seems to have been first pointed out by E. J. Nanson, 1882 (fn. 3, p. 3).

*Intuition note:* Each voter has a clean ranking, yet the majority's ranking goes round in a circle. A collective "ordering" needs more than a majority vote on each pair.

**Link to welfare economics.** If rationality means maximization, then finding a social maximum from individual desires is the central problem of welfare economics (p. 3). Arrow does not review its history. Debates he mentions:
- If interpersonal comparison of utility has meaning, social states could be ordered by the sum of utilities (Bentham, accepted by Edgeworth and Marshall). Even then, the form of the social utility function is open: sum, product, product of logarithms, or sum of pairwise products. So Bergson notes value judgments enter even here (p. 4).
- If interpersonal comparison is denied, the case is "clearly much worse". Robbins attacked the idea that economists could make policy recommendations without passing into ethics (p. 4).
- Kaldor, followed by Hicks, argue there is an economic sense in which one state is better than another even without interpersonal comparison. The compensation principle is examined in Chapter IV (p. 4).

**Two levels of argument.** Arrow says the controversy confuses two levels. Any way of making social choices from individual utilities implies a value judgment (Bergson). But given such judgments about the mode of aggregation, the economist should investigate the mechanisms satisfying them. He should check whether they violate other value judgments, and in particular whether the value judgments are consistent with each other, i.e. whether any mechanism of social choice can in fact satisfy them all (pp. 4–5). If majority choice is itself a value judgment, then in the voting paradox that judgment is self-contradictory in that situation (p. 5).

*Intuition note:* Arrow separates two levels. First come the basic value judgments about how to aggregate. Then the economist can ask whether any mechanism satisfies those judgments all together and whether they conflict with each other (pp. 4–5).

**Consistency and interpersonal comparison.** Bergson thinks an ordering of social states from individuals' indifference maps is possible, and Samuelson agrees. Lange assumes interpersonal measurability, and Lerner assumes meaningful interpersonal comparison of intensities (p. 5).

**Voting and market as one category.** In what follows, the voting/market distinction is disregarded; both are special cases of collective social choice (p. 5). Arrow lists earlier analogies between economic and political choice:
- Zassenhaus: a planned economy with influence replacing the market. Arrow finds the model very general and unclear for a socialist democracy (pp. 5–6).
- Bowen: voting as the demand for collective consumption. Income distribution and costs are taken as given (p. 6).
- Knight: both are collective choice among a limited range of alternatives. Knight also stresses that there is likely to be a greater tendency toward inequality under voting than under the market. Arrow calls these differences largely socio-psychological, not formal (p. 6).
- Black: papers stressing the similarity of market and electoral problems. Arrow says they are treated in Chapter VII, Section 2 (p. 6).
- Technical literature on election (a separate point, not an analogy): virtually every scheme proposed for single-member constituencies has been shown to have certain arbitrary features. Choosing candidates is presumably a device for choosing policies (p. 6).

### 2. Some limitations of the analysis (pp. 6–8)
The study excludes the "game aspects", which have two meanings (pp. 6–7):
1. **Enjoyment of the decision process as play.** The desire to play and win is real and should be considered in the mechanics of social choice, but is beyond the study's scope (p. 7).
2. **Strategic misrepresentation** (von Neumann–Morgenstern). Once a machinery exists, individuals will find it profitable, from a rational point of view, to misrepresent their tastes by their actions. Either misrepresentation is somehow directly profitable, or, more usually, some other individual is made so much better off that he could compensate the first (p. 7). Example: under plurality voting, a supporter of a minor-party candidate often votes for the lesser evil among the major candidates rather than "throw away his vote" (p. 7). Even where a procedure aggregating tastes into a consistent social preference pattern can be built, there still remains the problem of devising rules of the game so that individuals actually express their true tastes when acting rationally. This is akin to fair-division games, e.g. one player divides and the other chooses (p. 7).

**Tastes are data.** Individual values are taken as given and not altered by the decision process (pp. 7–8). This is standard in economic theory and the classical liberal creed, though Veblen, J. M. Clark and Knight assert it is unreal (p. 8). Reason: if values can be affected by the method, it becomes much harder to say what it means for one method to be preferable (p. 8).

**Rationality.** All individuals are assumed rational. The precise meaning comes in the next chapter (p. 8).

## Toy example (ours)
Three committee members rank projects $X, Y, Z$: member 1: $X>Y>Z$; member 2: $Y>Z>X$; member 3: $Z>X>Y$.
- $X$ vs $Y$: members 1 and 3 prefer $X$ → 2 votes to 1.
- $Y$ vs $Z$: members 1 and 2 prefer $Y$ → 2 to 1.
- $Z$ vs $X$: members 2 and 3 prefer $Z$ → 2 to 1.

So majority prefers $X>Y$, $Y>Z$, yet $Z>X$: a cycle. No project beats both others, so "choose the best" is undefined, although each member's ranking is consistent.

## Concepts
- [[paradox-of-voting-cycle]] (new) — Arrow's sense: pairwise majority preferences can be cyclic, so rational collective ordering fails (p. 3). Not the canon's turnout sense.
- [[cycling-intransitivity]] — majority prefers A to B, B to C, C to A (p. 3).
- [[social-choice-function-social-welfare-function]] — procedure from individual tastes to a social choice pattern, here discussed as welfare maximization (pp. 2–4).
- [[collective-rationality]] (new) — community orders alternatives once for all and picks the highest available one (p. 2).
- [[strategic-sophisticated-voting]] — misrepresenting tastes by action when profitable; here the "game aspect" excluded (p. 7).
- [[interpersonal-comparison-of-utility]] (new) — whether utilities of different people can be compared; disputed (pp. 4–5).
- [[institutions]] — voting, market, dictatorship and convention as methods of social choice (p. 1).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Collective rationality as a consistent ordering ↔ consumer theory: one agent's stable preference ordering, with choice from the available set — Arrow builds the analogy himself (p. 2).
- [structural-analogy] Excluded game aspects (strategic misrepresentation of tastes under a given rule) ↔ game theory: players choosing actions that hide their true preferences — Arrow cites von Neumann–Morgenstern here (p. 7).
- [metaphor] Fair-division game (one divides, the other chooses; p. 7) ↔ game theory: rules designed so that rational play secures a fair share.

## Questions this raises
- Which "natural conditions" must an aggregation procedure meet, and can any procedure meet them all?
- How far can value judgments about aggregation be tested for mutual consistency without interpersonal comparisons?
- If majority rule fails in this case, do other rules (e.g. compensation, utility sums) avoid cycles?
- How much does ignoring strategic behaviour limit the result?
