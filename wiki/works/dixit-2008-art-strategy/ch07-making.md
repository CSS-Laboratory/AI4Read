# Making Strategies Credible (and Epilogue: A Nobel History)

## L0 — One line
A threat or promise will not improve your outcome unless others believe it, and since carrying it out is often against your interest, the authors offer devices that change the payoffs, limit your own exit options, or use other people to make it believable.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Threats and promises will not improve your outcome unless others believe them, yet carrying them out is often against your interest. The authors sort ways of making moves credible into eight devices under three principles. Change the payoffs: contracts and reputation. Limit your ability to back out: cut communication, burn bridges, leave the outcome to chance. Combine both ideas: move in small steps. Use others: teams and mandated agents (negotiators with limited authority). The mirror-image tactics undermine an opponent's moves, but sometimes you should help the opponent be credible. A textbook case shows that renting can beat selling. An epilogue lists game-theory ideas honored by Nobel Prizes.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a strategic move (commitment, threat, promise) from the previous chapter; the prisoners' dilemma; looking forward and reasoning backward.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- In most situations, words alone should not be trusted; they must be backed up by actions to affect others' beliefs. The authors treat the "how" of credibility as an art, and success is usually a matter of degree (pp. 195–196).
- Eight devices rest on three principles: change payoffs (contracts, reputation); limit the ability to back out (cut communication, burn bridges, leave it to chance); small steps combine the first two principles; use others (teamwork, mandated agents) (pp. 196–197).
- Contracts need an enforcer with an independent interest, because the parties can jointly agree to drop it (renegotiate). Contracts alone cannot solve credibility. A strong reputation may make a formal contract unnecessary (pp. 198, 202).
- Irreversibility is a source of commitment, but it has costs: the Doomsday Machine (the film's automatic retaliation device) can leave no choice but to retaliate after an accident, and cutting communication makes monitoring hard (pp. 204–208).
- Brinkmanship is a "controlled loss of control": you fix the size of the risk, not the outcome (p. 209).
- Small steps make cooperation credible, but trust can unravel from the last step backward unless there is no clear final step (p. 210).
- You do not always want to undermine an opponent's credibility. In positive-sum games (where both can gain), enabling it can help both. Otherwise, the authors give counter-tactics for six of the devices (pp. 212–215).
- Case study: used-textbook buy-backs do not fix the publishers' commitment problem. Renting or licensing is offered as a way around it (pp. 216–217).
- The epilogue links the book's ideas to Nobel-honored work on equilibrium, repeated games, common knowledge and information (pp. 219–221).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: "In God We Trust?" (pp. 194–196)
- **Genesis.** God threatens death for eating the apple. The serpent suggests God is bluffing. Carrying out the threat would destroy his creation, so God punishes much less severely, and "the snake was right after all" (pp. 194–195).
- **Harry Potter.** Harry promises Griphook the sword, but plans to use it first. Griphook gets it only by taking it. Even Harry has a credibility problem (p. 195).
- **The problem.** We want others to act because of our threat or promise, but often it is not in our interest to carry it out. How do we change the game so it becomes credible? (p. 195)
- **Science vs. art.** The previous chapter covered the "what" of strategic moves, which is more amenable to science. This chapter covers the "how", which is more of an art, one that can be conveyed only partially and by suggestion. Success in art is usually a matter of degree (pp. 195–196).

### The eightfold path to credibility (pp. 196–212)
- **Words need actions.** A verbal contract is not worth much (Goldwyn: "A verbal contract isn't worth the paper it's written on," p. 196). Gutman's cash in *The Maltese Falcon* and Hobbes's line make the same point (p. 196).
- **Three principles, eight devices** (pp. 196–197):
  1. *Change the payoffs* so that following through is in your interest, turning a threat into a warning and a promise into an assurance. Devices: (1) contracts, (2) reputation. Both make breaking the commitment costlier than keeping it.
  2. *Limit your ability to back out.* Devices: (3) cut off communication, (4) burn bridges, (5) leave the outcome beyond your control or to chance.
  Combining principles 1 and 2 (the source says the two can be combined, p. 197): (6) move in small steps. A large commitment is broken into small ones, so the gain from cheating on one small piece can be outweighed by losing the rest.
  3. *Use others.* Devices: (7) teamwork, (8) mandated agents.

**Intuition note.** Credibility is manufactured by changing what you want at the moment of follow-through, or what you are able to do then, or who decides. The eight devices are variations on these three routes.

#### 1. Contracts (pp. 197–202)
- A penalty clause can make the remodeler's promise credible (p. 197).
- **The renegotiation flaw.** A dieter offers $500 to anyone who catches him eating fattening food. Nick Russo offered $25,000 to charity (p. 198).
  - The contract is flawed because nothing stops renegotiation. Since he will never violate it, no one will ever collect, so the enforcers gain by releasing him for, say, a round of drinks (p. 198).
  - For a contract to work, the party who enforces or collects must have an independent incentive to do so. His family, who want him skinnier, might not be tempted by a free drink (p. 198).
- **Business contracts work better.** A producer who wants the supply, not the penalty, will not renegotiate even if the supplier argues the penalty will never be paid (p. 198).
- **Making renegotiation costly.** In Schelling's Denver cocaine-rehabilitation example, a letter is published if addicts fail urine tests. The holder of the contract loses his job if he rewrites it (p. 199).
- **The ABC dieting show.** Dieters who failed to lose 15 pounds in two months would have bikini photos shown. One woman who lost 13 pounds was forgiven. The show was repeated with the Bluefish baseball staff, and a second woman just missed. Her claim of psychological damage implied a lawsuit threat, and ABC and the team backed down, so future participants are unlikely to find the device credible. What mattered was whether dieters believed the photos would be shown (p. 199).
- **Third-party enforcers** have no personal stake in the outcome. Their incentive comes from other sources (p. 199).
  - *stickK.com (Commitment Store)*: a bond (e.g. $250) goes to a charity if you fail, or you wager with a friend in a pari-mutuel option. The company gains nothing from your failure, has a reputation to keep, and might be sued for breach (pp. 199–200).
  - *Courts*: judges and jurors gain nothing directly. Jurors are motivated by civic socialization and fear of punishment for breaking their oath. Judges are motivated by professional pride and by promotion, which suffers if they are often overruled (p. 200).
  - *When courts are unreliable*: Lex Mercatoria, private judges at medieval trade fairs, and organized crime (p. 200).
- **Gambetta and the Sicilian Mafia.** A rancher says he and a butcher each want to cheat the other, so they need "Peppe" and both pay him a percentage. They did not use formal law because their deals avoided taxes (p. 201). Peppe (p. 201):
  - stores information about traders' past behavior, like a credit-rating agency;
  - punishes cheats, often violently;
  - may collude with a counterparty, and is kept honest only by his long-run reputation.
- **Other tribunals.** They get credibility from reputation and may develop expertise, even alongside reliable courts. Bernstein's study of New York diamond traders found the panel posts the name and photograph of a miscreant who defies it, which effectively drives him out of business. He also faces ostracism from a tight social and religious network (p. 201).
- **Conclusion.** None of these is proof against renegotiation: a third party acts only if one party brings the matter to it, and if both main parties are tempted to renegotiate they can do so at their joint will. Contracts alone cannot overcome the credibility problem. Success can be enhanced by adding parties with independent enforcement interests or a strong reputation at stake. If the reputation effect is strong enough, a formal contract may be unnecessary, as with the honorable hit man Sparafucile in *Rigoletto* (p. 202).

**Intuition note.** A contract is only as strong as the enforcer's reason to enforce it. If everyone with the power to enforce would gain by letting you off, the contract will not be enforced.

#### 2. Reputation (pp. 202–203)
- In a once-in-a-lifetime game, reputation may be unimportant and of little commitment value. When you play several games, future rivals will hear of your past actions, so you have an incentive to build a reputation (p. 202).
- Gambetta: only a record of acts of toughness, including murder, works. Dark glasses and a Sicilian accent do not separate the truly tough from others (p. 203).
- Public declarations put reputation on the line. Kennedy's inaugural address and his Berlin and Cuban missile crisis statements are examples (p. 203).
- Failure can be costly. George H. W. Bush's "Read my lips: no new taxes" was followed by a tax rise. The authors say this contributed importantly to his defeat in 1992 (p. 203).

#### 3. Cutting off communication (pp. 204–206)
- It succeeds because it can make an action truly irreversible. Examples: a last will (Cecil Rhodes's will needed an act of Parliament to change), mailed letters, and certified delivery (p. 204).
- **Dr. Strangelove.** General Ripper uses Plan R to send a wing of bombers to attack. His irreversibility moves (pp. 204–205):
  - He seals the base, cuts communications, and impounds radios.
  - He sends the go-code only when the planes are at their fail-safe points.
  - He keeps the recall code secret and kills himself rather than risk revealing it.
  - He phones the Pentagon and hangs up.
- **It failed.** The president ordered an army unit to attack Ripper's base and told the Soviets about the planes. The base was imperfectly sealed, and Mandrake guessed the recall code. Still, one plane got through (p. 205).
- **Lesson.** Reality is almost always between "100 percent effective" and "not at all." Something unexpected, an "unknown unknown," may nullify your efforts (p. 205).
- **Drawback.** If you are incommunicado, it may be difficult, if not impossible, to check that the rival complies, so you must hire others, as wills are carried out by trustees (p. 206).

#### 4. Burning bridges (pp. 206–207)
- Examples of burning or denying retreat: Xenophon, William the Conqueror's ships (1066), Cortés's ships, and Captain Ramius's letter in *The Hunt for Red October* (p. 206).
- Sun Tzu advised the reverse against an opponent: leave an escape route to reduce his resolve. The Trojans tried to burn the Greek ships; had they succeeded, that would simply have made the Greeks all the more determined (p. 206).
- **Polaroid.** It refused to diversify out of instant photography and so was committed to fight. Kodak entered in 1976. In 1990 Polaroid won a $909.4 million judgment, and Kodak withdrew (p. 207).
- **Building bridges.** In December 1989 East Germany's leader Krenz wanted to promise reform without a specific package. Dismantling parts of the Berlin Wall forced the government to reform or risk an exodus, since people could still leave. That made the promise credible (p. 207).

#### 5. Leaving the outcome beyond your control or to chance (pp. 207–209)
- **The Doomsday Machine.** It triggers itself automatically if anyone tries to untrigger it. Because the response is automatic, the deterrent threat is credible (pp. 207–208).
- **The cost.** There might be a small accident or unauthorized attack, after which the Soviets would not want to carry out their threat but would have no choice. To limit the consequences of errors, you want a threat just strong enough to deter (p. 208).
- **Brinkmanship.** If the action is indivisible, create a risk rather than a certainty of disaster (p. 208).
  - *Russian roulette*: with one bullet in six chambers, the shooter controls the size of the risk beforehand (1 in 6) but not the outcome. If he finds an empty chamber and pulls the trigger again, the risk rises to 1 in 5.
  - So brinkmanship is a "controlled loss of control" (p. 209).
  - The threatener hopes the opponent has lower risk tolerance and concedes before the explosion.
- **Advice.** Practice on low-stakes cases (kids) before high-stakes ones (spouse) (p. 209).

**Intuition note.** Handing the decision to a machine or to chance removes your option to back down, which is exactly why the threat is believed. The price is that you also cannot stop it when you would want to.

#### 6. Moving in steps (pp. 209–210)
- Break a large promise into pieces so each requires only a little trust (p. 209).
- **Cocaine example.** One $1 million purchase vs. 1,000 sequential $1,000 transactions. A double-cross may pay for $1 million, but $1,000 is too small to end a profitable ongoing relationship (p. 209).
- **Contractors** are paid daily or weekly on progress, so each side risks at most one day's or week's worth (pp. 209–210).
- **Last-step warning.** Strategic thinkers look forward and reason backward and worry about the last step. If you expect cheating in the last round, you break off one round earlier, and the problem unravels backward. So there should be no clear final step. The authors say that while continued business remains possible, cheating never pays. Hence be especially cautious about quality at going-out-of-business sales (p. 210).

#### 7. Teamwork (pp. 210–211)
- AA and diet centers use peer pressure. The AA approach changes payoffs by setting up a social institution in which pride and self-respect are lost when commitments are broken (p. 210).
- **Soldiers.** One soldier who hangs back slightly improves his survival chance without much hurting the attack. If all did so, the attack would turn into a retreat. Honor, loyalty, and the "million-dollar wound" counter this, as do penalties for desertion (pp. 210–211).
- **Roman army.** Falling behind was a capital offense. The neighbor was ordered to kill the deserter, and failing to do so was also a capital offense (p. 211).
- **Honor codes** work the same way. Failing to report observed cheating is itself a violation. Criminal law likewise penalizes failing to report a crime (p. 211).

#### 8. Mandated negotiating agents (pp. 211–212)
- If a worker says he cannot accept less than a 5 percent raise, why should the employer believe he will not back down to 4 percent? A union leader who needs members' ratification, or who would be removed for compromising, may be less flexible (pp. 211–212).
- Agents also help when you share bonds with the other side and might concede too much for the relationship. Sports players and authors use agents partly for this reason (p. 212).
- **Voluntary vs. imposed.** Someone who ties himself to the tracks may get less sympathy than someone tied there against his will (p. 212).
- **Machines.** A machine is a second type of mandated agent: very few people haggle successfully with vending machines. Many store clerks and government bureaucrats are required to follow rules mechanically, which makes the store's or government's policy credible; employees can say bending the rule is "above their grade" (p. 212).

### Undermining your opponent's credibility (pp. 212–215)
- **Not always desirable.** Thinking that you should always undermine the other player is a relic of zero-sum thinking. In positive-sum games, if the other's strategic move yields an outcome better for both, you gain by enhancing its credibility (p. 212).
  - *Prisoners' dilemma*: help the other make a credible promise to reciprocate cooperation (p. 212).
  - *Catalog merchants* Rainbow's End and B. B. Lean: meet-the-competition or beat-the-competition clauses remove each firm's temptation to cut prices, and each should want the other to be able to use them (pp. 212–213).
- **When the other's move hurts you**, make commitments yourself or prevent the other's move from being credible (p. 213). The counter-tactics, device by device:
  - *Contracts*: Mr. Russo's two selves, before and after the dessert trolley (BCE/ACE). The ACE self proposes a renegotiation that benefits everyone present, and BCE is no longer there to refuse. With all parties present, you must propose a deal good for everyone. In a repeated prisoners' dilemma you can plead that a cheat was an innocent error. You cannot hope to pull this trick too often, though children seem to get away with promising it will not happen again (p. 213).
  - *Reputation*: a reputation is valuable only to the extent that it is publicized, so secrecy can make it ineffective. The student can promise a professor that no one will know about an extension. A retailer can promise not to tell rivals about a lower price (p. 214).
  - *Communication*: a threat is pointless if the other cannot receive it, like threats to a child crying too loudly to hear (p. 214).
  - *Burning bridges*: follow Sun Tzu and leave an outlet free so the enemy believes there is a road to safety. This denies him a credible commitment to fight to the death (p. 214).
  - *Small steps (salami tactics)*: defy the opponent in slices so small that invoking the threat against any one slice is not in his interest. Schelling's child edges into the water. Small nations defy superpowers in tiny steps (pp. 214–215).
  - *Mandated agents*: refuse to deal with the agent and ask for the principal. Whether to try depends on the likelihood of success, how much you want the item, and the humiliation if you fail (p. 215).
- Devices may not work 100 percent of the time, and a situation may require more than one (p. 215).

**Intuition note.** Whether you should help or hinder the other side's commitment depends on whether their move is good or bad for you. The counter-tactics mirror six of the devices: contracts, reputation, communication, burning bridges, small steps and mandated agents.

### Case study: a textbook example of credibility (pp. 215–217)
- **Facts.** The US college textbook market is $7 billion. Texts often cost more than $150, and students might buy about eight a year (p. 216).
- **Proposal.** Congress wants bookstores to guarantee buy-back of used texts. This looks like it halves the student's cost: sell a $150 book back for $75 (p. 216).
- **Publisher's view.** If a text is resold twice, the publisher makes one sale instead of three. To earn $30 per student the publisher needs $90 on the first sale. This pushes the list price up to $150. After selling, it has an incentive to issue a new edition fast to kill the used stock (p. 216).
- **Commitment world.** If the publisher promised no revision and students promised no resale, it could sell three books at $50 each for the same money. Allowing for extra printing costs, the price is $60. Students pay $60 and keep the book, instead of a net $75 after resale (p. 216).
- **Losers under the present system.** Students who buy in the last year of an edition pay the full $150 (pp. 216–217). Students anticipate the coming revision, and many do not buy; about 20 percent of students do not buy required texts (p. 217).
- **Bookstores.** On a $150 text resold twice, a store makes $30 on the original sale and $37.50 on each of two resales (buying back at half-price, reselling at three-quarters of list). They would make much less selling a new $60 book three times (p. 217).
- **Forcing buy-backs** would lead stores to pay less, since they would expect to be stuck with obsolete texts (p. 217).
- **Solution.** A promise by students not to resell would have to be made credible, and banning used sales is not practical. Instead (p. 217):
  - rent or lease, with a deposit refunded when the book goes back to the publisher;
  - sell a license for each student in the class (university pays and bills students), so profit comes via the license and the book can be sold near production cost.
- **General lesson.** When there is a commitment problem, one way around it is to rent rather than sell, because there is then no used stockpile (p. 217).
- Two more credibility cases are in chapter 14 (pp. 217–218).

### Epilogue to Part II: A Nobel history (pp. 219–221)
- **Nash and Von Neumann.** Von Neumann pioneered game theory, first emphasizing zero-sum games and cooperative forms. Nash's breakthrough allowed general games combining conflict and cooperation (p. 219).
- **Harsanyi** (1994, with Nash) extended Nash equilibrium to games where players are unsure of others' preferences (p. 219).
- **Aumann** (2005): in repeated games, "almost anything can be a Nash equilibrium" when the game is repeated often enough, so multiplicity grows (p. 219).
- **Selten** refined Nash equilibrium by assuming a small chance that a player makes a mistake when moving. This forces strategies to be optimal even if the game takes an unexpected turn. It is much like look forward, reason backward, applied to simultaneous moves (p. 219).
- **Common knowledge** (Aumann): both know it, each knows the other knows it, and so on to infinity. Lack of common knowledge is the more common case. Concealing, revealing and interpreting information each need their own strategies (pp. 219–220).
- **Information economics and other Nobels** (p. 220):
  - *Schelling* (2005): commitment and strategic moves.
  - *1996*: Mirrlees and Vickrey, on designing a game to get truthful revelation of private information. Mirrlees worked on income tax and Vickrey on auctions. *The Economist* summarized: "How do you deal with someone who knows more than you do?"
  - *2001*: Akerlof (used-car market failure under private information), Spence (signaling and screening), Stiglitz (applications to insurance, credit, labor and other markets).
  - *2007*: Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson, on designing mechanisms and incentives so that others reveal information. A principal who cannot monitor an agent can devise incentive payments (p. 221).
- Later chapters will present these ideas, including Akerlof's lemons, Spence's signaling, the Vickrey auction and Myerson's revenue-equivalence theorem (p. 221).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A supplier promises a producer a shipment.
- Delivering costs the supplier 10 more than not delivering, so the supplier's gain from cheating is +10. The producer values delivery at 20.
- **Contract:** a penalty of 15 is paid by the supplier to the producer if there is no delivery. Cheating pays $10 - 15 = -5 < 0$, so the supplier delivers.
- **Renegotiation attempt:** the supplier says "you'll never collect 15, so cancel it." The producer compares: with the penalty, he gets 20 (delivery); without it, the supplier cheats and he gets 0. He refuses. The contract holds because he cares about the delivery (20 > 15), not the penalty.
- **Dieter contrast:** suppose enforcers get nothing if the dieter complies (0), and a free drink is worth 1 to each. Releasing him gives each $1 > 0$, so they accept and the contract dissolves. The difference: the enforcers have no independent interest in compliance.

## Concepts
- [[credible commitment]] — limiting or reshaping your own options and payoffs so others believe a move will be carried out (pp. 195–197)
- [[reputation]] (new) — a record of past follow-through that makes future moves credible, valuable only if publicized (pp. 202, 214)
- [[renegotiation]] (new) — parties to a contract jointly agreeing to drop it, which undermines enforcement (pp. 198, 202)
- [[burning bridges]] (new) — removing your own retreat to make fighting or following through your only option (p. 206)
- [[brinkmanship]] (new) — creating a risk, not a certainty, of mutual disaster that you do not fully control (pp. 208–209)
- [[salami tactics]] (new) — defusing a threat by defying it in slices too small to trigger retaliation (p. 214)
- [[mandated agent]] (new) — a negotiator whose authority to compromise is limited (by mandate, position or prestige), making the principal's position credible (pp. 211–212)
- [[shadow of the future]] — small steps and no clear final step make continued business worth more than the gain from a cheat (pp. 209–210)
- [[backward induction]] — look forward and reason backward; shows why trust unravels from a known last step (p. 210)
- [[subgame-perfect equilibrium]] — in the epilogue, Selten's idea of strategies optimal even if play takes an unexpected turn (p. 219)
- [[mutual vs. common knowledge]] — the epilogue's endless chain in which each player knows the other knows it, and so on (p. 220)
- [[zero-sum game & minimax]] — the early focus on pure conflict (p. 219)
- [[Nash equilibrium]] — the concept extended by Harsanyi and refined by Selten (p. 219)
- [[Bayesian game / types]] — Harsanyi's extension to games with uncertainty about others' preferences (p. 219)
- [[repeated game & folk theorem]] — Aumann's result that almost anything can be an equilibrium in a much-repeated game (p. 219)
- [[signaling]] — Spence's strategy for coping with information asymmetry (p. 220)
- [[mechanism design]] — designing a game or contract so that others reveal information or act as desired (pp. 220–221)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Moving in small steps ↔ repeated games: continued business outweighs a one-shot cheat, as in the shadow of the future (pp. 209–210).
- [structural-analogy] Unraveling from a known last step ↔ backward induction in finitely repeated games (p. 210).
- [structural-analogy] Textbook publisher unable to promise no new editions ↔ a durable-goods seller who cannot commit not to flood the market (inference; the text discusses only textbooks) (pp. 216–217).
- [metaphor] Mr. Russo's BCE and ACE selves ↔ dynamic inconsistency of a decision-maker over time (p. 213).

## Questions this raises
- When is a reputation strong enough to replace a formal contract, and what keeps the reputation-holder honest?
- If the last step must be uncertain to avoid unraveling, how can real relationships make the end genuinely unknown?
- When does helping an opponent's commitment pay (positive-sum games) and when does it hurt, and how can you tell in advance?
- How should rented or licensed goods be priced so that lack of a resale market does not simply raise the rental price?
