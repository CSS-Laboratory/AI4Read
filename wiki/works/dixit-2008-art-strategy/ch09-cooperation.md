# Cooperation and Coordination

## L0 — One line
When people choose separately and each choice affects the others, the equilibrium can be bad for the group as a whole; a coordinated rule, price or push can help where more individual effort cannot (though in the dentist case the market outcome is the best one).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks why uncoordinated choices so often give a poor outcome for society as a whole (more losers than winners), and what can be done. Its stories are exam cramming, congested bridges, QWERTY, speeding, neighborhood tipping, law-firm voting, and political platforms. One main answer is that one person's choice spills over onto others without a price. Games can have a congested middle equilibrium, two extreme "bandwagon" equilibria that history can lock in, or no equilibrium at all. Remedies are cartels, tolls, short intense campaigns that reach a critical mass, and package deals. The dentist case shows that when the spillover is only a price change, the market can be right.

**Prerequisites:** equilibrium as "nobody gains by switching"; prisoners' dilemma; reading a simple line chart; percentages; majority voting.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Relative-performance games.** When success depends on rank (exam curves, college football), one player's improvement worsens the others' rank. The game is not zero-sum (a game where not everyone can gain at once), because everyone can gain by cutting inputs. Escape needs an enforceable collective agreement, a cartel (an agreement among the players to limit competition); an outside enforcer is even better (pp. 254–256).
- **Congestion without prices.** Each driver ignores the delay he imposes on others. Selfish route choice gives 4,000 drivers on the bridge, but the best number is 2,000. A toll equal to the value of the time saved restores the optimum, unless collecting it costs too much (pp. 256–259).
- **Bandwagon effects and lock-in.** When more followers make an option more attractive, both extremes can be equilibria. History picks which one, and an inferior standard can persist, called lock-in (QWERTY, gasoline cars, light-water reactors) (pp. 259–265).
- **Critical mass.** It is enough to convert part of the population; the better equilibrium then sustains itself. This is why short, intense enforcement can beat long mild enforcement (pp. 262, 267).
- **Tipping.** Neighborhood segregation can arise from tolerant households. The mixed equilibrium is unstable, and Oak Park's policies remove the incentives that feed the tipping (pp. 268–271).
- **Piecemeal steps can be collectively worse.** Voting ignores intensity of preference, so a series of 9:1 votes can reach an outcome all dislike. Look ahead and consider reforms as a package (pp. 271–273).
- **Competition for the middle.** Parties converge on the median voter (the voter with half the electorate on each side); with three parties, or three taxi-seekers, there may be no equilibrium (pp. 274–276).
- **The market is not always wrong.** For dentists, the spillover is a price change, so, under the case's simplifications, the market allocation is best for society; the dental association may see it differently (pp. 279–280).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### For Whom the Bell Curve Tolls (pp. 254–256)
- **Ivy League football.** Each college over-invested in athletics, but the average record stayed 50:50, since every winner needs a loser. The colleges agreed to limit spring training to one day. Games were no less exciting, and everyone except some alumni was better off (p. 254).
- **Exams on a bell curve.** Relative standing matters more than absolute knowledge. If everyone studies more, relative standings and grades stay largely unchanged. A one-day limit would give the same grades with less effort (pp. 254–255).
- **Not zero-sum.** In a zero-sum game it is impossible to make everyone better off. Here it is possible, because the gain comes from reducing inputs (p. 255).
- **Diagnosis.** Studying is like a factory polluting: it makes life harder for others and there is no market in studying time. The result is a rat race and a multiperson prisoners' dilemma. Escape needs an enforceable collective agreement, a cartel (p. 255).
- **Enforcement.** High-school students cannot easily detect cheating (secret studying). In some small towns, cruising Main Street makes absentees visible, and punishment is social ostracism (p. 255).
- **Outside enforcer.** The 1968 TV ban on cigarette ads was fought by the companies, which expected to be hurt. It helped them all by ending costly defensive advertising and improved their profits (pp. 255–256).

*Intuition note.* If my effort only helps me against you, and yours helps you against me, we both pay and neither gains. We need a rule that binds everyone at once.

### The Route Less Traveled (pp. 256–259)
- **Setup.** The Bay Bridge takes 20 minutes with no traffic. Each additional 2,000 cars per hour adds 10 minutes for everyone on it (30 min at 2,000 cars, 40 at 4,000). BART takes about 40 minutes whatever the number of riders. There are 10,000 commuters (p. 256).
- **Equilibrium.** Each commuter minimizes his own time. The result is 4,000 drivers (40%) and 6,000 train riders (60%), with 40 minutes for all. Check: with 2,000 drivers (30 min), train riders would switch. With 8,000 drivers (60 min), drivers would switch. At 4,000 nobody gains by switching (p. 256).
- **Chart.** A rising line is bridge time and a flat line at 40 minutes is train time. The lines meet at E, at 4,000 drivers (p. 257).
- **Better pattern.** With 2,000 drivers, each saves 10 minutes. The 2,000 who moved to the train still spend 40 minutes, and so do the other 6,000. The total saving is 20,000 person-minutes (p. 257).
- **Why.** Each extra driver raises everyone else's time but pays no price for that, and only counts his own time (pp. 257–258).
- **The best pattern.** At 3,000 cars the time is 35 minutes, a saving of 5 minutes each, 15,000 in all. At 1,000 cars the time is 25 minutes, a saving of 15 each, again 15,000 in all. So 2,000 cars, with 20,000 minutes saved, is best (p. 258).
- **Ways to reach it.**
  - Central planning: issue 2,000 licenses, perhaps rotated monthly (p. 258).
  - A toll: if an hour is worth $12, a $2 extra cost equals 10 minutes. A toll $2 above the BART fare gives 2,000 cars. Drivers spend 30 minutes plus $2 and riders spend 40 minutes, so no one wants to switch. Toll revenue is $4,000 (plus 2,000 BART fares) (p. 258).
  - A private bridge owner who maximizes the value of time saved reaches the same pattern. The invisible hand works only when "commuting time" is priced (p. 258).
- **Caveat.** Collecting tolls can cost more than the time saved, and booths may cause the congestion. Then it may be best to tolerate the original inefficiency (p. 259).

*Intuition note.* Each driver counts only his own delay, but he also slows everyone else. A price that makes him pay for that harm brings private and group interests together.

### Catch-22? QWERTY and lock-in (pp. 259–265)
- **Conventions.** In earlier examples (where to meet in New York) any shared convention would do. Sometimes one convention is much better, yet a better one may not get adopted, and a change can be especially hard once one is established (p. 259).
- **QWERTY story.**
  - Scholes helped design the layout in 1873. It spread the most frequently used letters apart to reduce jamming on manual machines. It became the de facto standard by about 1904 (p. 259).
  - DSK cuts finger travel by over 50% and typing time by 5–10%, but nearly all keyboards are QWERTY, so everyone learns it. The text calls this a vicious circle (p. 259).
  - Navy claim: retraining on DSK paid for itself in ten days. Liebowitz and Margolis call that study into question; the text adds that it appears an interested party (Dvorak) was involved. A 1956 GSA study found a month of four hours a day was needed just to catch up to old QWERTY speed (p. 260).
  - Beginners must learn QWERTY because a remapped keyboard is not practical for non-touch-typists, which cuts the gains from DSK (p. 260).
- **Bandwagon chart.** The horizontal axis is the fraction of typists using QWERTY; the vertical axis is the chance that a new typist learns QWERTY. As drawn, at 85% QWERTY the chance is 95% (p. 260). The curve favors DSK: a majority of new typists learn DSK if QWERTY has less than a 70% share (p. 261).
- **Outcomes.** Equilibrium means the fraction is constant over time. The text states that stochastic approximation theory proves convergence (p. 261).
  - If QWERTY's share exceeds 72%, it grows until 98%, where new typists' choices equal the population share (p. 261).
  - If it falls below 72%, DSK takes over and QWERTY dies out (p. 262).
  - The mathematics says only that we end at one of these two outcomes. Starting from scratch, DSK would probably win, "But we are not. History matters." (p. 262)
- **Escape.** Coordinated action, such as manufacturers agreeing or the federal government training its staff, could switch the equilibrium. Only a critical mass needs to convert (p. 262).
- **Other cases.**
  - Gasoline cars: Arthur's account says gasoline looked patently inferior in 1890, then won a 1895 race, and steam lost out after the 1914 withdrawal of horse troughs. The authors ask how good steam would have been with 75 years of research; they say we may never know, but some engineers believe steam was the better bet (p. 263).
  - Nuclear reactors: Canada's heavy-water reactors generate power for 25% less than comparable US light-water reactors. Heavy-water and gas-cooled designs have a lower meltdown risk (pp. 263–264).
  - Cowen's study: Rickover chose light-water in 1949 because it was compact and furthest advanced. The *Nautilus* followed in 1954. GE and Westinghouse became the natural choice for civilian plants. An interim choice gained a head start down the learning curve (p. 264).
- **Lesson.** The historical reasons (key jamming, disease, submarine space) are irrelevant today. Game theory says to recognize early the potential for lock-in and spend time on what will be best for the future (p. 264).

*Intuition note.* When a choice is worth more the more others make it, an early lead can become permanent even if the leader is not the best option.

### Faster Than a Speeding Ticket (pp. 265–268)
- **Logic.** If nobody obeys the limit, you have two reasons to break it: some experts say driving with the flow is safer, and the police can catch only a small share of speeders (p. 265). As more drivers obey, both reasons vanish (p. 265).
- **Chart.** The horizontal axis is the percentage who obey. Line A is the benefit of obeying and line B the benefit of breaking the law. B is higher at the far left and A is higher at the far right. There are three equilibria, and only the extreme ones can arise from social dynamics (p. 265).
- **Contrast with commuting.** On the bridge, more followers make an option less attractive, so dynamics converge to the middle. With speeding, more company makes it more attractive, so they head to the extremes (p. 266).
- **Speed limit.** Raising it from 55 to 65 lowers the value of breaking it, since high speeds are riskier and fuel costs rise. It may be 20% more expensive to drive at 65 than at 55, and it could easily be 40% more at 75 than at 65 (p. 266).
- **Policy.** The limit need not be set so high that everyone is happy to obey. A short phase of very strict enforcement can create momentum to full compliance, and enforcement can then be cut back. Short intense enforcement can be much more effective than the same effort spread thinly over a longer time (pp. 266–267).
- **Fuel economy.** CAFE was raised in 2007 from 27.5 to 35 mpg for cars, phased in 2011–2020. The authors argue people keep SUVs partly because light cars are less safe among heavy ones. Cars are 20% heavier than two decades ago. Higher CAFE standards could work as a coordination device that shifts enough people to light cars (p. 267).
- **Friedman's argument** (from *Capitalism and Freedom*): people may be willing to give to poverty relief if everyone else does but not without that assurance. In large impersonal communities private charity is less likely to supply it. The authors use this to show that collective action is not only a left-wing view (pp. 267–268).

### Why Did They Leave? (pp. 268–271)
- **Puzzle.** Few neighborhoods are integrated, although most urban Americans regard mixed neighborhoods as desirable. Schelling's idea: segregation can be the equilibrium of a game even when all households have some tolerance (p. 268).
- **Preferences.** Very few whites insist on 99% or 95% white, but most would feel out of place at 1% or 5% white. The majority would be happy somewhere in between (p. 268).
- **Chart.** The vertical axis is the probability that a new entrant is white; the horizontal axis is the current white share. Equilibrium means the neighborhood's mix equals the mix of entrants. There are three equilibria: all white, all black, and a mix (p. 269).
- **Tipping.** Starting at 70:30, if one black family is replaced by a white one, the next entrant is white with probability above 70%. Say the mix shifts to 75:25; the pressure continues, and as the chart is drawn this goes on until the neighborhood is all white. The reverse chain reaction happens if a white family leaves. So the middle is not a stable equilibrium. From the extremes there is no tendency to return (p. 270).
- **Cause.** A departing family harms others but pays no fine. A departure tax would violate the freedom to live where one chooses. So policy should reduce the incentive for others to follow (p. 270).
- **Oak Park.** Two tools (p. 271):
  - Banning "For Sale" signs hides the news that would be read as bad; panics are avoided, or delayed if justified.
  - Insurance guarantees home values against a change in racial mix. It removes the economic fear that accelerates tipping, and if it works it costs taxpayers nothing.
- **Gentrification** is described as tipping to an all-rich equilibrium (p. 271).

*Intuition note.* In the unstable middle, a small push in either direction is amplified because each move makes the next move of the same kind more likely.

### It Can Be Lonely at the Top (pp. 271–274)
- **Story.** Justin-Case, a mythical law firm, had ten junior associates rated 1 to 10, each told only his own rating. They voted by majority on a partnership cutoff, starting at 1 (pp. 271–272).
- **Votes.** Each proposal to raise the cutoff by one passed 9:1. Those above gain a better average partner quality. Those below want their failure to be less informative, since outsiders cannot infer their exact ability. The sole dissenter is the associate right at the cutoff, who would lose partnership (p. 272).
- **Ending.** The cutoff reached 10, and then 11, so nobody made partner. Everyone was back in the old system, which they considered worse than promotion for all (pp. 272–273).
- **Moral 1.** Piecemeal steps can each look attractive to most voters and end worse for everyone, because voting ignores intensity. Each associate has nine small wins and one big loss that outweighs them (p. 273).
  - Individuals cannot stop the slope alone. The group must look ahead and reason back, and consider reforms only as a package. Similar problems tend to plague tax and tariff reform bills, which get killed by a series of amendments (p. 273).
  - Congress in 1989 tried a 50% pay raise. After public protest, each member had a private incentive to vote against it, provided it would still pass. Too many did, and each defection increased the incentive for the next, so the proposal died (pp. 273–274).
- **Moral 2.** If you are going to fail, fail at a difficult task, since failure lowers others' expectations of you. Examples are applying to Harvard instead of the local college or asking the most popular student to the prom (p. 274).
  - Psychologists see people take actions that raise their chance of failure so as to avoid facing their ability. "There is no invisible hand" in games against yourself (p. 274).

### Politicians and Apple Cider (pp. 274–276)
- **Setup.** Voters are spread uniformly on 0 (radical left) to 100 (arch-conservative). The incumbent picks a position, then the challenger responds (p. 274).
- **Logic.** If the incumbent picks 48, the challenger picks 49. Voters at 48 and below choose the incumbent, and the other voters, just over 51%, choose the challenger. If the incumbent is above 50, the challenger locates between it and 50. By looking ahead and reasoning backward, the incumbent chooses the middle (p. 274).
- **Median.** With non-uniform voters, the incumbent chooses the point with half of the voters on each side. The median is not the average, which weights distance. The challenger imitates, and each gets 50% if only issues count. The voters get "an echo rather than a choice" (p. 275).
- **Hotelling (1929).** His examples included cities that grow too large, churches too alike, and homogeneous cider (p. 275).
- **Three parties.** If parties take turns revising and have no ideological baggage, an outside party edges toward its neighbor and squeezes the middle party, which then jumps outside. There is no equilibrium. In practice, ideological baggage and loyalty prevent such rapid switches (p. 275).
- **Taxis.** Three people waiting for a taxi in Manhattan: the middle person is squeezed out and, if unwilling to be usurped, will move, so there may not be an equilibrium until the taxi comes. Uncoordinated decisions may have no determinate outcome (pp. 275–276).

### A Recapitulation (pp. 276–277)
- Types of failure (p. 276): the multiperson prisoners' dilemma (everyone makes the same, wrong choice); proportions that are wrong because of unpriced spillovers; two extreme equilibria needing conventions or penalties; slippery slopes; excessive homogeneity; mutually reinforcing expectations; no equilibrium.
- **Two fundamental problems with free markets.** (1) History matters, so lock-in may need policy to encourage diversity before a standard is set, or to coordinate a switch (examples: metric system, daylight saving time) (pp. 276–277). A short intense campaign can get a critical mass for behavioral standards (taxes, speeding, party arrival times). A mild long campaign would not (p. 277). (2) Much of life is outside markets (courtesy, clean air). Pricing may fix this, as with the bridge. Sometimes pricing changes the good: donated blood is typically superior to purchased blood (p. 277).

### Case Study: Allocating Dentists (pp. 277–280)
- **Setup.** 100,000 new dentists choose between city and rural practice. They are equally attracted and choose by income. Rural practice is like the train: roughly constant income and value as numbers grow. City practice is like the bridge: income falls as numbers rise (p. 278).
- **Chart.** A falling city line crosses a flat rural line at E. Left of E, city incomes are higher and more dentists choose the city. Right of E, the reverse. At E, choices replicate year to year (p. 279).
- **Discussion.** Unlike commuters, the market outcome at E is the best for society as a whole. An extra city dentist lowers other dentists' incomes, which is a cost to them, but the lower price benefits consumers. The two side effects exactly cancel. The difference from commuting is that nobody benefits from extra commuting time. Each dentist should pursue the highest-paying practice (pp. 279–280).
- **Dentists' own view.** The American Dental Association may weigh the dentists' income loss more than the consumers' gain. From its perspective there are too many city dentists, and the profession would gain from a fund subsidizing dental students who commit to rural practice (p. 280).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A road and a rail line, 100 commuters. Rail takes 30 minutes whatever the number. Road time is $10 + n/2$ minutes when $n$ drive.

- **Equilibrium:** road time = rail time, so $10 + n/2 = 30$, so $n = 40$. Total time = $40 \times 30 + 60 \times 30 = 3000$ person-minutes.
- **Total time for any $n$:** $n(10 + n/2) + (100-n)\cdot 30 = 3000 - 20n + n^2/2$.
- **Check:** $n=10$ gives $3000-200+50 = 2850$. $n=20$ gives $3000-400+200 = 2800$. $n=30$ gives $3000-600+450 = 2850$. $n=40$ gives $3000-800+800 = 3000$. The minimum is at $n = 20$, which saves 200 person-minutes. This is half the equilibrium number of drivers, as on the Bay Bridge.
- **Toll:** at $n=20$ road time is $10+10 = 20$. A toll worth 10 minutes makes the road cost 30 in effective terms, equal to the rail, so no one wants to switch.

## Concepts
- [[strategic-game]] — a situation where each person's payoff depends on everyone's choices; used for commuters, typists, drivers (p. 256)
- [[nash-equilibrium]] — a pattern where no one gains by switching; here, constant shares over time (pp. 256, 261)
- [[zero-sum-game-minimax]] — contrast case: in a zero-sum game it is not possible to make everyone better off; relative-rank games are not zero-sum, since everyone can gain by cutting inputs (p. 255)
- [[prisoners-dilemma]] — here extended to many players: everyone makes the same, wrong choice (pp. 255, 276)
- [[convention]] — a shared practice; several can be equilibria, and a better one may not win (pp. 259–260)
- [[backward-induction]] — "looking ahead and reasoning backward": used by the incumbent politician; advised for the associates as a group (pp. 273–274)
- [[externality-spillover]] (new) — one person's choice affects others without a price; a source of several of the failures in this chapter (pp. 255, 257–258, 276)
- [[congestion-toll]] (new) — a price on users equal to the harm they cause others (p. 258)
- [[bandwagon-effect]] (new) — an option becomes more attractive as more people use it; can give two stable extremes (pp. 260–261, 266)
- [[critical-mass]] (new) — the share that must switch for a better equilibrium to sustain itself (pp. 262, 267)
- [[lock-in-path-dependence]] (new) — early history makes an inferior standard persist; "history matters" (pp. 262, 264)
- [[tipping]] (new) — Schelling's term: a small disturbance of an unstable middle mix pushes it to an extreme (p. 270)
- [[median-voter]] (new) — with positions chosen sequentially, parties locate where half the voters are on each side (p. 275)
- [[cartel]] (new) — an enforceable collective agreement to limit competition (p. 255)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Bay Bridge congestion ↔ economics: negative externality fixed by a Pigouvian-style price — the toll equals the harm to others (the term is not used in this unit)
- [structural-analogy] QWERTY bandwagon chart with two stable extremes and one unstable middle ↔ dynamical systems: bistable system with a threshold
- [metaphor] Tipping at 70:30 ↔ physics: a ball balanced on a hilltop that rolls to either side after a small push
- [structural-analogy] Justin-Case sequence of majority votes ↔ social choice: agenda-dependent majority outcomes where intensity of preference is ignored
- [structural-analogy] Median-voter convergence ↔ industrial economics: firms locating next to each other (Hotelling's examples of cities and churches)

## Questions this raises
- The text gives 70% (where a majority of new typists favor DSK) and 72% (the dividing point for the dynamics). How are these two numbers related, and does the exact value matter, or only that an unstable middle point exists?
- When the toll costs more to collect than it saves, how should one compare it with licenses, rotation, or doing nothing?
- Why does the dentist case give a different answer from the bridge case, and how can one tell in advance whether a spillover is a "real" cost or a transfer?
- How large must a critical mass be, and how do planners know when a short intense campaign has reached it?
