# Dixit, A. K., & Nalebuff, B. J. (2008). *The Art of Strategy: A Game Theorist's Guide to Success in Business and Life*. W. W. Norton, 512 pp. (Successor to their *Thinking Strategically*, Norton, 1991, which the Yale ECON 159 syllabus lists.)

## In one paragraph
The book teaches strategic thinking through stories rather than formulas. A situation is strategic when other active decision makers react to what you do. So you should put yourself in their shoes, look ahead and reason backward, and use equilibrium as a starting point. It shows why sensible individual choices can leave everyone worse off, as in the prisoners' dilemma. It then shows how players escape through repetition and punishment, how they change the game with credible commitments, threats and promises, and how they mix their moves to stay unpredictable. Later chapters apply these ideas to information and signaling, coordination, auctions, bargaining, voting and incentives. They end with case puzzles and worked solutions. It matters because it gives strategic intuition before the formal apparatus.

## The path through the work
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch01-ten]] — Strategic situations are those where other active decision makers react to your choices, so good play starts with putting yourself in their shoes and anticipating their aims and reactions.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch02-games]] — In sequential-move games (players move in turn), work out the other players' future responses and use them to choose your best current move: "look forward and reason backward".
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch03-prisoners]] — In a prisoners' dilemma each player's dominant strategy is to defect, so self-interest leaves everyone worse off than mutual cooperation; cooperation can be sustained by credible rewards or, more usually, by punishment of cheating that is detected, clear, certain and not excessive.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch04-beautiful]] — A Nash equilibrium is a set of choices in which each player's choice is a best response to correct beliefs about the others; it is the place to start analysing simultaneous-move games, but when there are several equilibria players also need a focal point or some other selection method, such as a convention, to reach one.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch05-choice]] — In a zero-sum game, if letting the opponent see your choice in advance would hurt you, you should choose at random among your plain options, in proportions that leave the opponent indifferent.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch06-strategic]] — A strategic move (commitment, threat or promise) changes the game, often by fixing in advance how you will act, so that the other players respond in a way that is better for you.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch07-making]] — A threat or promise will not improve your outcome unless others believe it, and since carrying it out is often against your interest, the authors offer devices that change the payoffs, limit your own exit options, or use other people to make it believable.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch08-interpreting]] — When some players know more than others, their words alone cannot be trusted when interests differ, so players use actions whose cost differs by type (signals and screens) to reveal, conceal or elicit private information.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch09-cooperation]] — When people choose separately and each choice affects the others, the equilibrium can be bad for the group as a whole; a coordinated rule, price or push can help where more individual effort cannot (though in the dentist case the market outcome is the best one).
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch10-auctions]] — In sealed-bid and Dutch auctions you should bid as if you have already won; bidders also adapt to the rules, so many rule changes do not change what the seller gets.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch11-bargaining]] — Reasoning backward predicts a quick split that favours the side with the better fallback, the proposer, or the more patient party; strikes begin because the two sides lack a common view of where the talks will end.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch12-voting]] — With three or more options, voters often gain by not voting their true favourite, so outcomes depend on the voting rules and the order of votes as much as on preferences, and no rule removes this problem.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch13-incentives]] — When you cannot observe someone's effort, pay them a base amount plus a reward tied to an observable outcome, sized so that working hard is in their own interest while the job still looks attractive.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]] — Eighteen short puzzles apply the book's strategic tools: looking forward and reasoning backward, reading what other players' actions reveal, and using commitment and incentives.
- [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch15-workouts]] — Ten short worked solutions apply backward reasoning, best responses, credible commitment, dominant strategies and incentive design to concrete cases (flags, pricing, nuclear threats, auctions, preemption, commissions, royalties).

## Big ideas across units
- **Look forward, reason backward.** Anticipate others' future responses and work back from the end of the game. It starts in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch01-ten]] and is formalised as Rule 1 in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch02-games]]. It underlies strategic moves in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch06-strategic]], small-step credibility in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch07-making]], bargaining in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch11-bargaining]], and the puzzles and solutions in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]] and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch15-workouts]].
- **Commitment, credibility and the value of fewer options.** Cutting off your own options or fixing a response rule can help you. It is introduced in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch01-ten]] and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch02-games]]. [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch06-strategic]] gives the theory of threats and promises, and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch07-making]] the devices for making them believable. It returns in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch11-bargaining]] (strikes, brinkmanship) and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]].
- **Individually sensible choices can be collectively bad.** This runs from the prisoners' dilemma in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch01-ten]] and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch03-prisoners]] (and its resolution through punishment in repeated play) to congestion, lock-in and tipping in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch09-cooperation]]. Voting in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch12-voting]] and the two-tier takeover bid in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]] show related effects.
- **Equilibrium as a starting point, with multiple and bad equilibria.** [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch04-beautiful]] introduces Nash equilibrium, focal points and conventions. The stag hunt appears in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch03-prisoners]]. History-driven equilibria appear in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch09-cooperation]], and persistent bad conventions in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]].
- **Unpredictability and mixing.** Any exploitable pattern should be avoided. This is raised in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch01-ten]], developed in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch05-choice]] (Rule 5, minimax, randomized enforcement), and carried into information in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch08-interpreting]] (mixing makes actions reveal information only partly).
- **Actions reveal information.** Willingness to trade or bid is informative ([[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch01-ten]], [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]]). [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch08-interpreting]] covers signaling and screening, and the winner's curse and "bid as if you've won" appear in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch10-auctions]]. Bargainers prove their patience in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch11-bargaining]].
- **Designing rules and incentives.** Rules can make truth-telling or cooperation each player's best choice. Examples are screening with incentive-compatibility constraints ([[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch08-interpreting]]), the Vickrey auction ([[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch10-auctions]]), voting rules ([[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch12-voting]]), and pay schemes against moral hazard ([[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch13-incentives]]). Further examples are in [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch14-case]] and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch15-workouts]].
- **Real behaviour versus the selfish-calculator model.** Fairness, altruism, punishment of cheaters, learning and limited calculation all matter. See [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch02-games]] (ultimatum experiments), [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch03-prisoners]], [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch04-beautiful]] (mixed evidence on Nash), [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch05-choice]] (field versus lab), [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch10-auctions]] (eBay sniping) and [[dixit-2008-art-strategy/ch13-incentives]] (crowding out of intrinsic motivation).

## How to read it
- **Essential core:** ch01 gives the mindset (and contains the Introduction). ch02 (backward reasoning), ch03 (prisoners' dilemma) and ch04 (Nash equilibrium) are the basis for most of what follows. ch06 and ch07 form a pair: ch07 depends directly on the strategic-move ideas of ch06.
- **Dependencies stated in the units:**
  - ch03 uses ch02's backward reasoning and the idea of a payoff table.
  - ch04 uses dominant strategies from ch03.
  - ch05 uses Nash equilibrium from ch04.
  - ch06 uses chs. 2–4 (trees, dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, prisoners' dilemma, chicken).
  - ch08 uses mixed strategies from earlier chapters.
  - ch09 uses equilibrium and the prisoners' dilemma.
  - ch10 uses dominant strategies.
  - ch11 uses the ultimatum game of ch2.
  - ch12 uses backward reasoning and dominant strategies.
  - ch13 assumes the participation constraint and information asymmetry from the screening chapter (ch08).
  - ch14 draws on backward reasoning, dominant strategies, simultaneous versus sequential moves and Vickrey auction rules.
- **Application chapters:** ch09–ch13 are applications (coordination, auctions, bargaining, voting, incentives) that can be read in a different order after the core. The material only fixes the ch08 → ch13 dependency.
- **Optional on a first pass (reader's judgement):** the epilogues (Part I summary in ch04, the Nobel history in ch07) and the worked solutions in ch15. The ch15 digest notes that the problem statements, tables and trees are not in the pages it covers, so it is best used alongside the original problems. ch14 works best after the chapters whose tools it applies.
