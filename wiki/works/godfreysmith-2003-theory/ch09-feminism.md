# Feminism and Science Studies

## L0 — One line
Late-twentieth-century critics argued that science is political; Godfrey-Smith finds Lloyd's feminist history interesting, the arguments for the radical epistemologies unconvincing, and sees a gap in Science Studies' account of how theories connect to the world.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how late-twentieth-century critics came to treat science as political, and what feminist work and Science Studies contribute. Feminist work includes history of ideas (Lloyd on reason and maleness), case studies (primatology) and epistemology (Harding's categories; Longino's contextual empiricism). Godfrey-Smith finds Lloyd's historical story interesting but its epistemological consequences hard to work out, and finds the arguments for the radical options unconvincing. Science Studies, a mixed interdisciplinary field, became a battleground in the Science Wars and the Sokal hoax. He ends by noting a gap: after describing the social structure of science, we still need to understand how it connects to the natural world.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn, Feyerabend and the sociology of science (chs. 7–8); the idea of empiricism; basic evolutionary reasoning about reproductive success.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Science's image shifted from progressive and anti-authoritarian to a force that maintains the status quo; critics said it is "political" (pp. 136–37).
- Feminism's equal-opportunity claims about women in science raise policy questions, not philosophy-of-science questions; the philosophical work has three strands: history, case studies, epistemology (p. 137).
- Genevieve Lloyd: ideas of reason were historically tied to maleness and of nature to femaleness (Bacon's "marriage" model). Godfrey-Smith finds it compelling as history but its present epistemological consequences are hard to work out (pp. 138–39).
- Primatology: the view of passive females gave way, from the 1970s, to a view of active females. This roughly coincided with women entering the field; Hrdy is cautious about the causal role of gender (p. 140).
- Harding's categories (spontaneous empiricism, philosophical empiricism, radical epistemology) organise feminist epistemology; the author thinks the arguments for the radical options are unconvincing (pp. 141–42).
- Longino's contextual empiricism: rationality belongs to communities that choose from a diverse pool of views through critical, non-coercive dialogue. This is in the tradition of Mill and Feyerabend (p. 142).
- Whether gender gives a distinctive perspective that matters to science is an open, risky question (the McClintock case) (pp. 143–44).
- Science Studies mixed many fields and welcomed postmodernism; this led to the Science Wars and the Sokal hoax (pp. 144–47).
- Science Studies tends to reject explaining theory success by accuracy or explanatory power, which leaves a gap in its account of science (p. 147).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 9.1 "Science Is Political" (pp. 136–37)
- Author's generalisation (he calls generalisations risky): through much of the last three hundred years science was seen in the West as progressive and anti-authoritarian, most vividly in the Enlightenment. There were exceptions, e.g. romanticism and some Marxist thought (p. 136).
- In the late twentieth century, with the cold war as a crucial cause, many on the progressive side came to see science as maintaining the status quo, especially political inequalities. The anti-authoritarian image came to be seen largely as just "good PR", and it was argued that science is full of hidden features that include some people and exclude others (p. 136).
- Many thought that showing the links between science and power would reveal science as political, not neutral and specially authoritative. This bears on education, medicine and social policy (pp. 136–37).
- The main manifestation is feminist critique and feminist philosophy of science. The second part of the chapter treats Science Studies (p. 137).

### 9.2 The Man of Reason (pp. 137–39)
- Feminist thinking about science is diverse. It is perhaps unified by the idea that science and theorising about knowledge have helped keep women in a "second-class" position. Proposed reforms range from including more women, through encouraging a female "voice", to dethroning science (p. 137).
- Distinction: women's historical exclusion from science is an equality-of-opportunity matter. It raises policy questions (e.g. affirmative action) but not issues in philosophy of science (p. 137).
- Philosophical feminist work has three overlapping strands: (1) history of ideas and science; (2) analysis of specific fields and theories (social science, biology, medicine); (3) feminist epistemology, including the social structure of science when it bears on epistemology (pp. 137–38).
- **Genevieve Lloyd, *The Man of Reason* (1984).** She argues that early ideas about reason and knowledge were greatly affected by views about maleness and femaleness. Reasonableness became associated with maleness; femininity was associated with receptivity, intuition, empathy and emotion, in opposition to reason (p. 138). The author finds her story in the history of ideas very interesting, sometimes compelling, but says it is harder to work out what it means for epistemology (p. 138).
- Key source, per Lloyd: the old association of femaleness with nature (the earth is fertile and female). The mind–nature relation was modelled on male–female, and so were relations within the mind (perception/thought, reason/emotion) (p. 138).
- Best example, Bacon: knowledge is control of nature ("Knowledge is power"), and nature is female. The model is a marriage between knower (man) and nature (woman). A good husband is respectful but firm and in charge. Bacon: "Nature betrays her secrets more fully when in the grip and under the pressure of art" than in her "natural liberty". Useful knowledge is the product of the "nuptual couch" (pp. 138–39). Some other feminists are tougher on Bacon than Lloyd (Harding 1986) (p. 139).
- Author's assessment: it is likely these associations affected women's lives and early modern science. The harder question is what follows for us now. Metaphors survive (a phenomenon "yields" to analysis, which to his ears resonates with sexual conquest), but their present effect on science or society is hard to judge (p. 139).
- **Keller's view:** the inherited picture of science affects women entering science; a woman scientist must choose between "inauthenticity" and "subversion". Identifying with a scientist modelled on the patriarchal husband costs a woman inauthenticity, just as joining men in misogynist jokes does (p. 139).

*Intuition note:* Lloyd argues that Western ideas of reason were shaped by views about maleness and femaleness. The author finds this a compelling story in the history of ideas; what it implies for epistemology and science today is, he says, a harder question.

### 9.3 The Case of Primatology (pp. 139–40)
- The case is often seen as one where researchers' gender affected ideas and where science benefited from more women (p. 139).
- Early picture: passive females, with social and sexual life controlled, sometimes cruelly, by males. It was linked to evolutionary theory: in many animals, not all, male reproductive success varies much more than female. One male can impregnate many females ("sperm are cheap"); female success is limited by the costs of pregnancy (p. 140).
- This asymmetry matters evolutionarily, but was often used in simplistic explanations that ignored modifying factors. Early primatology took it to support the view that male sexual behaviour was finely honed by selection and female behaviour was not (p. 140).
- **Hrdy (2002):** from the 1970s, careful observation showed a more active and complex female role: varied sexual contact, and subtle manipulation of males, partly aimed at male behaviour toward offspring. The basic idea about male variance still stands, but its interaction with female strategies is now better understood (p. 140).
- This shift roughly coincided with an influx of women. Per Hrdy and others the author spoke to, primatologists fairly routinely accept that this had a significant role, more so in the US than in Britain. Hrdy is cautious; she suggests women tended to empathise with female primates and watched their behaviour more closely (p. 140).

*Intuition note:* Hrdy suggests that women researchers tended to empathise with female primates and watched their behaviour more closely. The author reports that the idea that women's growing presence mattered is fairly routinely accepted in primatology, and notes that Hrdy herself is cautious.

### 9.4 Feminist Epistemology (pp. 141–44)
- The field includes criticism of how science handles evidence, criticism of the social structure of science where it affects epistemology, and the most ambitious claim, that reason, evidence and truth are covertly sexist. It also makes reform proposals (p. 141).
- **Harding's categories** (author modifies them) (p. 141):
  - *Spontaneous feminist empiricism*: earliest and least controversial; uses a feminist viewpoint to criticise bias without challenging science's traditional ideals, methods and norms.
  - *Philosophical feminist empiricism* (Longino is probably most influential): revises traditional ideas but stays faithful to basic empiricist themes; relativism is avoided.
  - *Radical feminist epistemology*, with two approaches. (a) *Feminist postmodernism* tends to embrace relativism: genders, ethnic groups and classes see the world fundamentally differently, and a single "true" description is a harmful illusion. (b) *Standpoint epistemology* is not relativist: some facts are visible only from the standpoint of the oppressed or marginalised, who really have better access to them (pp. 141–42).
- **Author's verdict:** the arguments for the more radical options, especially standpoint theory, have not been convincing. Longino argues that any value is unlikely to be a general superiority: if some facts are more visible to marginalised people, other facts will surely be more visible to the privileged. The marginalised experience is more plausibly a special input into discussion, giving a "pool" of ideas (p. 142).
- **Longino's contextual empiricism:** the social group is the basic unit for distinguishing rationality from irrationality. Science is rational to the extent that it chooses theories from a diverse pool reflecting different viewpoints, through critical dialogue that reaches consensus without coercion. Diverse participants help produce a diverse pool. Epistemology then distinguishes good community-level procedures from bad ones (p. 142).
- Author: this follows an old tradition. Feyerabend (ch. 7) defended diversity, extending an argument from Mill (per Elisabeth Lloyd 1997); for Mill diversity feeds a "marketplace of ideas" (p. 142).
- The appeal of diversity is clear; the role of gender in it is a separate question, as Longino accepts. Class and ethnicity may matter as much or more. Many feminists nonetheless expect some gender "patterning" (pp. 142–43).
- Author's caution: women and men will have different perspectives on some facts (physical experience; early education, at least for the near future). Claims beyond this risk simplistic generalisation. Whether women's and men's experience and viewpoint differ systematically in a way likely to matter to scientific disputes is "a much harder question" (p. 143).
- Primatology is one case where a gender-linked bias is taken seriously. A stronger claim is that women scientists have a distinctive way of thinking, partly from their situation in male-dominated fields. **Keller on McClintock:** McClintock discovered "jumping genes", at first thought strange but correct. She was an outsider and had a "feeling for the organism" that gave her a different style (1983). Keller is cautious: she does not want to argue for a "sharp differentiation" but does seem to think there will be some systematic differences (p. 143).
- **Objections (author reports):** many would deny that this trait is gendered, since it may be found in many good biologists. Feminists, including Keller, fear stereotyping (pp. 143–44).
- A further possibility is that women bring less competitive, more cooperative interaction; many feminists now avoid such simple generalisations (Miner and Longino 1987). Ch. 10 returns to this (p. 144).

*Intuition note:* For Longino, rationality is judged at the level of the community: does its way of choosing theories draw on a diverse pool of viewpoints and reach consensus through critical dialogue without coercion? She presents this as a revised empiricism; the author discusses it conditionally ("If this is the right way to incorporate feminist ideas into epistemology…").

### 9.5 Science Studies, the Science Wars, and the Sokal Hoax (pp. 144–48)
- Fields contributing to the understanding of science expanded (sociology, feminism) and boundaries blurred. In the 1980s some workers created "Science Studies", drawing on history, sociology, philosophy, anthropology, classics, economics, literary theory, feminist theory, semiotics, cultural studies, critical theory, and later technology studies (p. 144).
- Result: neither a breakthrough nor a disaster. Cross-fertilisation is possible, but disciplinary boundaries will probably not vanish. Styles range from sober historical research to "wild flights of fancy" (pp. 144–45).
- **Postmodernism** (as relevant here): part of a tradition opposing the idea that language represents, or "stands for", objects and situations. Sometimes postmodernists seem to say that we now live in a time when symbols no longer represent: behind each symbol is another symbol, so, on this view, it is no longer useful to apply concepts like accuracy, reference and truth. At other times it seems an obscure argument for extreme relativism or skepticism (p. 145).
- Science Studies welcomed such ideas, which affected its image. A backlash followed, partly from scientists alarmed at the picture presented, partly from commentators criticising academia and education generally. This is the "Science Wars". Some attacks came from conservatives who feared the loss of Western values and charged that the humanities had been ruined and now science was attacked via relativism (pp. 145–46).
- **The Sokal hoax (1994–96):** physicist Alan Sokal submitted a parody paper to *Social Text*, "Transgressing the Boundaries: Toward a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity", using postmodern jargon. He expected acceptance to show the field had lost standards. *Social Text* published it (1996b); Sokal revealed the hoax in *Lingua Franca* (1996a). One thing that made it effective was that he spoke as a left-winger who thought the Left had been led away from science by trendy French theory (p. 146).
- Many English-speaking philosophers felt vindicated; for the most part they had not accepted postmodernism, Derrida had never been embraced by the philosophical establishment, and they thought their journals "hoax-proof" (the author does not know if tested). Shimony's 1996 presidential address to the Philosophy of Science Association reasserted Enlightenment values and called Sokal "a hero of the enlightenment" (pp. 146–47).
- Fine and Kitcher feared polarisation: philosophers ignoring neighbours, sociologists seeing philosophers as conservative (p. 147).
- **Aftermath (author):** Science Studies was not seriously damaged. Less tolerance now for jargon-laden writing, which is good. The feared widening of gaps between fields has not been realised (p. 147).
- **General tendency of Science Studies:** hostile to explaining scientific change by relations between theories and the structure of the world. It especially rejects explaining a theory's popularity by its real accuracy or explanatory power, and judging the past by present estimation of worth. Kuhn and ch. 8 sociology left this mark. This leaves a gap: after describing the social structure of science, we must understand how it connects to the natural world. This is a theme for later chapters (pp. 147–48).

*Intuition note:* The author says Science Studies' account of science has a gap: after describing the social structure of science, we still need to understand how it connects to the natural world.

## Toy example
(Our own illustration of Longino's "diverse pool" idea; not from the text and not empirical.)
Four facts exist: a, b, c, d. Each researcher notices only facts connected to their background.
- Homogeneous group of 3: each notices {a, b}. Pool = {a, b}, size 2.
- Diverse group of 3: Researcher 1 notices {a, b}, researcher 2 {b, c}, researcher 3 {c, d}. Pool = {a, b, c, d}, size 4.
- Facts raised in dialogue: homogeneous 2 of 4 (50%); diverse 4 of 4 (100%).

The diverse group has more material to criticise and weigh. Longino's claim, as reported, is that rational choice needs this kind of pool plus non-coercive critique. The toy says nothing about whether gender is the relevant source of diversity; the author calls that a separate, harder question (p. 143).

## Questions this raises
- If marginalised and privileged viewpoints each see some facts better, what procedure decides whose input counts in a dispute?
- How could one test whether women's presence, rather than other factors, caused the primatology shift?
- Can the "good community procedures" in Longino's account be specified without appeal to how theories relate to the world?
- Do cooperative and competitive styles in science differ by gender (ch. 10 returns to this)?
