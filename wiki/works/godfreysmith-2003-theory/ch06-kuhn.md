# Kuhn and Revolutions

## L0 — One line
Godfrey-Smith presents Kuhn's view that science gets its power from alternating orderly "normal science" and disorderly revolutions (breakdown, then a new framework), and he criticises parts of it, above all the claim that the world itself changes with paradigms.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn's picture: science alternates between normal science (orderly work inside one shared framework, a paradigm) and revolutions (breakdown, then a new paradigm). Godfrey-Smith asks how revolutions occur and how the later paradigm relates to the earlier. Kuhn's answers: revolutions usually follow a crisis (a period of disorder after confident work), and rival paradigms are incommensurable, meaning hard to compare by one common standard. Godfrey-Smith finds the crisis claim contested, linguistic incommensurability overstated, incommensurability of standards real, and Kuhn's claim that the world itself changes the worst material in the book. He thinks Kuhn's mechanism too tightly structured, yet the social-mechanism approach it began has flourished.

**Prerequisites:** chapter 5 ideas (paradigm, normal science, anomaly); the idea that a theory's terms get meaning from the surrounding theory; a rough idea of what "relativism" means.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Two modes of change.** Normal science is orderly and responsive to evidence but closes debate on fundamentals. Revolutions reopen fundamentals, and orderly assessment breaks down. Godfrey-Smith reads Kuhn as holding that science's special features come from the interaction of the two (pp. 88, 90).
- **How a revolution happens.** Usually a crisis plus a *candidate paradigm* (a newly proposed paradigm that seems to solve problems behind the crisis). Kuhn compares the switch to conversion or a *gestalt switch* (a sudden change in how something is seen as a whole). Historically, if genetics around 1900 was a revolution, a preceding crisis is hard to find (p. 89).
- **Shared criteria are vague.** Kuhn later accepted some criteria common to all paradigms. In his view, when stated broadly enough to be common to all science, they are too vague to settle hard cases, and they must often be traded off against each other. Sharper standards live inside paradigms and may change in revolutions (pp. 89–90).
- **Incommensurability has two parts.** Language (terms used slightly differently) is, in the author's view, overstated. Standards (what counts as good evidence or a good theory) is real and interesting (pp. 91–94).
- **Relativism: "it's complicated".** Kuhn explains apparent progress in two ways, one consistent with relativism and one apparently conflicting with it. He was not a relativist about science versus nonscientific approaches (pp. 94–96).
- **"Chapter X".** Godfrey-Smith rejects the claim that the world itself depends on paradigms: perception can be shaped by belief and still connect us to one real world (pp. 96–98).
- **Accumulation of knowledge.** Kuhn's pessimism about growth of knowledge of the world's structure seems badly overstated, since chemistry and molecular biology show continuing growth (p. 98).
- **Assessment and analogies.** The mechanism is too tightly structured, but it began a flourishing approach. It resembles Smith's *invisible hand* (individual-level traits yielding good outcomes for the whole) and *punctuated equilibrium* (long stasis broken by rapid change) (pp. 99–101).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 6.1 Considerable Upset (pp. 87–88)
- Kuhn's discussions of revolutions are the most famous and controversial parts of *Structure*. The author gives them a chapter but thinks normal science is just as important, perhaps of more enduring significance (p. 87).
- Kuhn argued that some periods of change involve a fundamentally different kind of process from normal science. Revolutionary periods see a breakdown of order and a questioning of the rules, followed by rebuilding that can create fundamentally new conceptual structures (p. 87).
- Kuhn often said revolutions have a "function" in science as a whole. The author says science results from the interaction of the orderly process and the periodic breakdowns, "and of nothing less" (p. 88).
- Inside normal science one can tell good work from bad and see progress. Across a revolutionary divide it may be unclear whether there is progress or even how to compare the work. People on different sides may be "speaking different languages". In his climax Kuhn says workers in different paradigms live in different worlds (p. 88).

### 6.2 Revolutions and Their Aftermath (pp. 88–91)
Two issues: how revolutions occur, and how what comes before relates to what comes after (p. 88).

**How they occur.**
- In Kuhn's story, large-scale change usually requires both a crisis and a new candidate paradigm. A crisis alone does not make scientists regard a paradigm as "falsified". There are no pure falsifications: rejecting one paradigm accompanies accepting another. A new idea that merely looks better does not suffice either, because without a crisis scientists lack motivation to consider radical change (p. 88).
- Kuhn's claims are qualified: they describe central, characteristic patterns, not every case (p. 89).
- *Historical doubts.* Was there a crisis before Copernicus or Darwin? "Maybe." If the appearance of genetics around 1900 was a revolution, it is very hard to find a crisis in earlier work on inheritance. Kuhn might call it a move from pre-paradigm science to normal science, but that could not be said of most biology then. The molecular revolution seems even less crisis-induced (p. 89).
- In the 1970 "Postscript" Kuhn qualified the role of crisis but still held crises are the "usual prelude" to revolutions, which is also controversial. The author suggests the emphasis sometimes seems driven more by Kuhn's hypothesised mechanism than by historical data. In that mechanism only a crisis can loosen a paradigm's grip (p. 89).
- *The spark.* The new paradigm, in the narrow sense of an inspiring achievement, usually appears to solve one or more problems that prompted the crisis. Kuhn did not think this could be described by an explicit philosophical theory of evidence and testing. He likened it to "conversion" or a gestalt switch, and he argued revolutions are capricious and disorderly, affected by idiosyncratic personal factors and accidents of history (p. 89).

**Why disorderly: standards of assessment.**
- Some principles by which evidence is assessed are liable to be destabilised by a crisis and can change in a revolution. Kuhn did not say traditional ideas about theory and evidence are completely misguided. Later he said some core ways of assessing theories are common to all paradigms: predictive accuracy, consistency with well-established theories in neighbouring fields, unifying disparate phenomena, and fruitfulness in new ideas. These provide "the shared basis for theory choice" (1977c, 322). Some commentators think these later essays change rather than clarify *Structure* (pp. 89–90).
- Kuhn thought that, stated broadly enough to be common to all science, these principles are so vague they cannot settle hard cases. The goals must often be traded off against each other. Within a paradigm, sharper versions operate, but they are more like habits and values from shared training than explicit principles. Kuhn came to see some variation in how they are acquired as a strength. These sharper standards are liable to change in revolutions (p. 90).
- Summary: change within normal science is orderly and responsive to evidence, but it works by closing debate on fundamental ideas. Revolutionary change challenges fundamentals, but orderly assessment breaks down. Displays of problem-solving power matter, and so do gestalt switches and leaps of faith (p. 90).

**Descriptive versus normative.** Kuhn's language suggests revolutions are bound to happen and have a positive role: science is a "supremely efficient instrument" (1996, 169). Some interpreters treat this as inessential. The author thinks it central: science is a social mechanism combining sustained cooperative work with a capacity to partially break down and reconstitute itself. When a paradigm runs out of steam, nothing in the community could reliably supply directions for orderly movement to a new paradigm. The goals of science are best served at such times by a disorderly process in which basic ideas are put back on the table. The author calls this strange but says he thinks it was Kuhn's picture (pp. 90–91).

*Intuition note.* On the author's reading, Kuhn thought that when a paradigm runs out of steam, nothing within the community could reliably give science directions for orderly movement to a new paradigm. So at such times the goals of science are best served by a disorderly process in which even basic ideas are put back on the table (pp. 90–91).

### 6.3 Incommensurability, Relativism, and Progress (pp. 91–96)
- **Non-cumulative.** Kuhn said revolutions are non-cumulative: there is no steady buildup of a commodity like truth. A revolution always gains some things and loses some. Do we usually gain more than we lose? In at least the middle chapters Kuhn seems to think there is no unbiased way to answer (1996, 109, 110). It will feel like gain, or there would have been no revolution, but that does not give an unbiased comparison (p. 91).
- **Meaning of "incommensurable".** Literally, not comparable by a common standard or measure. Carefully expressed: rival paradigms can be compared well enough to see they are incompatible rivals. Those inside a paradigm can say why theirs is superior, citing what it can and cannot explain. These comparisons are compelling only to those inside the paradigm from which the superiority claim is made. From "above", the two parties often seem to talk past each other (p. 91).
- **Two aspects.** (1) Communication: key terms are used differently, "slightly different languages". (2) Standards: even when communication is possible, people in different paradigms use different standards of evidence and argument and do not agree on what a good theory should do (pp. 91–92).

**Language.**
- Kuhn's claims rest on holism about meaning: a term gets its meaning from its place in the whole theoretical structure. "Mass" or "species" thus differ slightly between rival theories (p. 92).
- **Critics (Fodor and LePore 1992):** a holist cannot make sense of differences of degree, since one cannot say whether terms in two different networks have a "similar" role. They accuse holists of bluffing to hide the radical nature of their views (p. 92).
- The author says neither holists nor anyone else has had much success in developing a good theory of meaning for scientific language. He raises a different criticism: if meaning incommensurability were real, history should show the usual signs of failed communication. In his impression (he is not a historian), historians have not found many such cases in crucial debates across paradigms. Scientists are often adept at "scientific bilingualism" and improvise bridges like pidgin languages (Galison 1997). Deliberate misrepresentation for rhetorical points is not failed comprehension (p. 92).

**Standards.**
- The author calls this form much more important. Kuhn: paradigms tend to bring their own standards, and detailed standards are often internal to paradigms and liable to change in revolutions. "Paradigms provide scientists not only with a map but also with some of the directions essential for map-making" (1996, 109) (pp. 92–93).
- *Example 1: Newton's gravity.* Newton gave a mathematical description (the inverse square law) but no mechanism. His view that gravity acts instantaneously at a distance seemed very hard to supplement with a mechanism. Should a theory give a causal mechanism, or can gravity be an "innate" power of matter following a law? This was argued about in the early eighteenth century. Kuhn: there is no general answer. This kind of principle is present in one paradigm and absent in another (p. 93).
- *Example 2: Biometricians versus Mendelians.* In the late nineteenth century the Biometricians formulated a mathematical law of inheritance, with no mechanism and no easy way to add one. Mendel's mid-nineteenth-century work was rediscovered in 1900, launching genetics. For about six years the two camps debated. The Biometricians held that a mathematical law was the right goal. William Bateson (Mendelian) held that understanding the mechanism was the goal. The Mendelians won in the short term. Later the approaches were married, so modern biology has both math and mechanism (p. 93).
- Author's verdict: Kuhn's claims about linguistic incommensurability were overstated, but incommensurability of standards is a real and interesting problem (p. 94).

**Relativism.**
- Kuhn's discussion of incommensurability is the main reason his view is called "relativist". He was shocked by this reading (p. 94).
- Relativism, roughly: the truth or justification of a claim, or the applicability of a rule or standard, depends on one's situation or point of view (individual, society, group). It may be general or restricted to a domain (p. 94).
- Disagreement alone does not imply relativism, since some people might be wrong. "Depends on context" need not be relativism: a single rule set can say "If you are in circumstances X, you should do Y" (p. 94).
- Here the concern is relativism about standards of reasoning, evidence and justification, with the paradigm's users as the point of view. Is Kuhn a relativist about them? "It's complicated"; the author doubts everything Kuhn said fits together consistently (p. 94).
- Kuhn argued (author's report) that current paradigms are not closer than earlier ones to an "ideal" paradigm. Science is not heading to a final paradigm superior to all others, and we cannot say that there is, in principle, an ideal paradigm whose methodological principles are entitled to govern all science. That seems to take us close to a relativist view about non-shared standards (pp. 94–95).
- But in the final pages Kuhn said our present paradigms have more problem-solving power than earlier ones. He gave two intertwined explanations of apparent progress (p. 95):
  1. *"Eye of the beholder".* Each field has one paradigm at a time, the victors naturally view their victory as progress, and science is insulated from outside criticism. This deflationary account is consistent with relativism.
  2. *Real progress.* Science has a special efficiency, giving real progress across revolutions, measured in problem-solving power: the number and precision of solutions tend to grow (1996, 170). This seems to conflict with a relativist reading.
- The author finds the second hard to reconcile with Kuhn's earlier claims that revolutions bring losses as well as gains and that standards of problem importance change. He is skeptical that Kuhn's measurement is compatible with the rest of the book. If later paradigms have more problem-solving power, we seem entitled to regard them as superior, which moves away from relativism. Kuhn aimed at an intermediate position (1996, 205–6) (p. 95).
- *Science versus nonscientific approaches.* Reading Kuhn as a relativist here is "straightforwardly a mistake". He thought science is a uniquely efficient way of studying the world and superior to nonscientific procedures, and the author says that is perhaps the most important issue (pp. 95–96).
- *Theory-ladenness of observation.* Kuhn argued that observation is not a neutral source of information for choosing between theories, because what people see is influenced by their paradigm. This challenges empiricism and is deferred to chapter 10 (p. 96).

*Intuition note.* Both sides in an argument across paradigms can clearly see that the theories conflict. Each side's case for its own superiority is compelling only to those inside its own paradigm (p. 91).

### 6.4 The X-Rated "Chapter X" (pp. 96–98)
- Chapter X contains Kuhn's most radical claims: in some sense the world changes with paradigms, and reality is paradigm-relative. After a revolution "scientists work in a different world" (1996, 135) (p. 96).
- **Two attitudes among commentators.** (a) Kuhn exposes the idea of a single stable world as dependent on a failed view of science and outdated psychology, so changing our view of science requires changing our metaphysics. (b) This side of Kuhn is a mess: ideas, standards and perhaps experience change, but the world does not (p. 96).
- The author is in camp (b) and calls Chapter X the worst material in the book (p. 96).
- It is not always clear how radical Kuhn means to be. Some reasonable claims exist: scientists change behaviour and practices, so some bits of the world change in ordinary ways, and revolutions yield new technologies. These effects are limited by the causal powers of human action (breeding, genetic engineering, damming and polluting rivers). Kuhn's cases show he did not mean these: changed ideas about stars, planets and comets led astronomers to "living in a different world" (1996, 117) (p. 97).
- **The author's main objection.** Kuhn seems to think that believing in a single world independent of paradigms commits us to naive ideas about perception and belief. It does not: perception may be radically affected by beliefs and expectations and still connect us to one real world (p. 97).
- **The Lavoisier passage (1996, 118), paraphrased.** At the very least Lavoisier saw nature differently after discovering oxygen. Absent recourse to a "hypothetical" fixed nature that he "saw differently", the principle of economy urges us to say he worked in a different world. The author asks whether that is economical. Appeals to economy are often suspicious and weak in philosophy of science, and this one seems to have the accounting wrong (p. 97).
- A fixed world is "hypothetical" only in a special skeptical sense. For understanding science as a social activity, as Kuhn does, there is nothing hypothetical about science taking place in a single structured world that interacts with scientists through perception and action (pp. 97–98).

**Accumulation of knowledge.**
- Kuhn opposed the idea that science's history accumulates more and more knowledge of how the world really works. He sometimes allowed accumulated useful results, maybe problem-solving power, but not growing knowledge of the world's structure (p. 98).
- Like Popper and others, Kuhn seems to have been hugely influenced by the fall of the Newtonian picture around the start of the twentieth century. The author says he was surely too focused on theoretical physics. In chemistry and molecular biology it is much more reasonable to see continuing growth (with some hiccups), for example in knowledge of the structures of sugars, fats and proteins. There is no evidence these results will be replaced rather than extended. Theoretical physics may be a special case, not the model for all science (McMullin 1984). So Kuhn's pessimism seems badly overstated (p. 98).

### 6.5 Final Thoughts on Kuhn (pp. 98–101)
- Kuhn attributed science's success to a delicate balance in a complex, fragile mechanism: the ordered cooperation and single-mindedness of normal science plus the ability to break down and reconstitute in revolutions. Periodic disorder is as essential as the orderly behaviour (p. 99).
- **Criticisms of the mechanism as a description.** Two already mentioned: single paradigms rarely have the dominance Kuhn describes, and large-scale changes can occur without crises. Parts are especially hard to apply to biology, which Kuhn did not much discuss. The author's own view is that the account is in several ways too tightly structured and too specific; the real story is more mixed. Still, it was the first prominent attempt at a new kind of theory that looks at the social structure of science and the mechanisms of change, and this approach has flourished (p. 99).
- **Broad versus narrow construals of science (from chapter 1).** Some philosophies treat science as differing from everyday problem-solving only in detail and degree. Kuhn's does not: it stresses the differences. Science is organised behaviour with a specific social structure and seems to thrive only in certain societies. So it is a rather fragile cultural achievement, and subtle changes in education, incentives and politics could destroy its special mechanisms (p. 99).
- **Invisible hand.** Adam Smith argued ([1776] 1976) that individual selfishness in economic behaviour leads to good outcomes for society; the market distributes goods efficiently. There is an apparent mismatch between individual-level and whole-level characteristics. It is only apparent and disappears when we consider many individuals interacting. Similarly in Kuhn: narrow-mindedness and dogmatism in individuals lead to openness at the level of science as a whole. Anomaly and crisis stress normal scientists so much that wholesale openness to novelty arises in revolutions. The next chapter's critic suspected Kuhn of trying to excuse and encourage the most narrow-minded and unimaginative trends in science (pp. 99–100).
- **Punctuated equilibrium.** Gould, Eldredge and others argued in the 1970s that much biological evolution follows this pattern (Eldredge and Gould 1972). A lineage shows long periods of relative stasis, with low-level tinkering but little change to fundamental structures. Occasional much more rapid periods ("rapid" means thousands rather than millions of years) bring new fundamental structures. The rapid periods are disorderly and unpredictable compared with the simplest natural selection in large populations. In stasis the genetic system resists substantial change ("homeostasis"). The author calls the analogy with Kuhn striking (p. 100).
- The theory was controversial, partly because Gould sometimes presented it in radical forms (1980). The homeostasis idea is tendentious, and the idea that ordinary natural selection does not operate normally in rapid periods is very unorthodox. Over the years it has been moderated and, in its moderate form, has entered mainstream biology's description of at least some patterns in evolution (Futuyma 1998) (pp. 100–101).
- Gould's "Eternal Metaphors in Paleontology" (1977) argued that theorising about the history of life brings up the same basic ideas about change again and again, mixed into new combinations. The Kuhn–Gould analogy shows a similar convergence on a story about change. There is also a similar opposition between the punctuated story (Kuhn, Gould) and a more uniform, one-process view (Popper, some rival views in evolutionary biology). Gould acknowledged Kuhn's influence on him in the 1960s–70s (Gould 2002, 967). Kuhn himself was interested in (different) analogies with evolutionary change (1996, 171–72) (p. 101).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text. It models only the "standards" side of incommensurability.)* Two theories of inheritance are scored 0–10 on two criteria, mechanism (M) and mathematical precision (P):
- Theory A: M = 1, P = 9.
- Theory B: M = 8, P = 4.

Paradigm 1 weights the criteria 0.2 on M and 0.8 on P:
- A = 0.2·1 + 0.8·9 = 0.2 + 7.2 = 7.4.
- B = 0.2·8 + 0.8·4 = 1.6 + 3.2 = 4.8.
- Paradigm 1 ranks A first.

Paradigm 2 weights 0.8 on M and 0.2 on P:
- A = 0.8·1 + 0.2·9 = 0.8 + 1.8 = 2.6.
- B = 0.8·8 + 0.2·4 = 6.4 + 0.8 = 7.2.
- Paradigm 2 ranks B first.

The scores are the same, but the verdicts differ, so each side's ranking is convincing given its own weights. Both sides can still see that A and B differ. The limit of the model: Kuhn says the sharper standards are habits and values, not explicit numbers (p. 90).

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — shared framework and the orderly puzzle-solving within it; chapter 5 terms assumed (p. 87).
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — Kuhn's cycle; here a crisis is disorder after confident work, usually needed with a candidate paradigm (pp. 88–89).
- [[incommensurability]] — literally "no common measure", carefully put: rivals are comparable enough to be seen as rivals, but such superiority arguments are compelling only to those inside the paradigm making the claim. It has language and standards aspects (p. 91).
- [[theory-choice criteria (shared vs paradigm-specific)]] (new) — broad shared criteria (accuracy, consistency, unification, fruitfulness) versus sharper standards inside paradigms (pp. 89–90).
- [[gestalt switch]] (new) — Kuhn's comparison for paradigm change: a sudden change in how something is seen (p. 89).
- [[holism about meaning]] (new) — a term's meaning comes from its place in the whole theory (p. 92).
- [[relativism]] (new) — truth, justification or standards depend on one's situation or point of view (p. 94).
- [[problem-solving power]] (new) — number and precision of solutions to a field's problems; Kuhn's measure of real progress (p. 95).
- [[theory-ladenness of observation]] (new) — what people see is influenced by their paradigm; discussed in chapter 10 (p. 96).
- [[invisible-hand structure]] (new) — individual-level traits (selfishness; narrow-mindedness) combine into good outcomes for the whole (pp. 99–100).
- [[punctuated equilibrium]] (new) — long stasis broken by rapid, disorderly change; compared with Kuhn's pattern (p. 100).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Kuhn's dogmatic individuals → open science ↔ economics: Smith's invisible hand — a mismatch between individual and whole that disappears at the group level.
- [structural-analogy] Kuhn's normal science plus revolution ↔ evolutionary biology: punctuated equilibrium — long stability, then rapid change.
- [structural-analogy] Paradigm-specific standards (toy example) ↔ decision theory: weighted multi-criteria scoring — the same data, different weights, different ranking.

## Questions this raises
- If revolutions often occur without a crisis, which parts of Kuhn's mechanism survive?
- How could one measure "problem-solving power" across paradigms that disagree about which problems matter?
- Can a holist about meaning make sense of "slight" differences in meaning?
- Does the "intermediate position" between relativism and progress hold together?
