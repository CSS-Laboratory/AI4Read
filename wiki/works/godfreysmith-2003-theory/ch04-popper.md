# Popper: Conjecture and Refutation

## L0 — One line
Popper held that science is bold conjecture plus attempted refutation and that confirmation is a myth; Godfrey-Smith judges the theory flawed but keeps its core idea that scientific theories must be exposed to the risk of observational failure.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What separates science from non-science, and how should theories be tested and chosen? Popper's answer: a hypothesis is scientific if some possible observation could refute it. Science alternates bold conjectures with attempts to refute them. Observation, he said, can never confirm a theory, not even slightly. Godfrey-Smith finds this flawed. Testing is holistic, so blame can fall on auxiliary assumptions. Accepting an observation report is a "decision". Probabilistic theories forbid no particular observation. Popper has no good answer to why a well-tested theory should be preferred for building a bridge. The lasting insight is riskiness: scientific handling of ideas exposes them to observation. This also gives a more modest demarcation.

**Prerequisites:** basic deductive logic ("All F are G" is refuted by one F that is not G); idea of a hypothesis and an observational prediction; holism about testing (from chapter 2, only recalled here); the problem of induction (chapter 3, only recalled here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Demarcation by falsifiability.** Popper says a hypothesis is scientific iff some possible observation could refute it. A theory compatible with every observation takes no risk (p. 58).
- **Confirmation is a myth.** Popper held that tests can only refute, and that passing tests gives no reason, even slight, to think a theory true. Most philosophers reject this (pp. 58–60).
- **Science as a cycle.** Bold conjecture, then attempted refutation, then a new conjecture. Ad hoc patching is discouraged. Popper intended this as both description and ideal (p. 61).
- **Holism undermines clean falsification.** Testing needs auxiliary assumptions, so logic never forces rejection of the theory. Popper's reply is to treat the extra assumptions as testable conjectures and to expect a good scientist not to deflect blame; Godfrey-Smith says this shifts the focus from theories to behaviour. Accepting an observation report is also a "decision" (pp. 64–66).
- **Probabilistic theories** are falsifiable only "in practice", by a decision about what counts as too improbable. This damages Popper's picture of deductive refutation (pp. 66–67).
- **Bridge-building problem.** Godfrey-Smith holds that Popper has no good answer to why a well-tested theory is more rational to use than an untested one. "Corroboration" is only backward-looking (pp. 67–69).
- **Popper's lasting contribution**, in Godfrey-Smith's view, is the idea of riskiness: science tries to handle ideas so that they are exposed to falsification or modification by observation (p. 70).
- **A better demarcation** concerns ways of handling ideas, not whole theories. Marxism, Freudianism and evolution can each be handled more or less scientifically (pp. 71–74).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 4.1 Popper's unique place (p. 57)
Popper is the only philosopher in the book regarded as a hero by many scientists. His ideas have been used inside scientific debates, for example in biological classification and ecology (p. 57). His appeal comes from a few simple, striking ideas and a noble, heroic vision of science. Godfrey-Smith agrees with many criticisms of Popper and sees no way for Popper to escape their force (p. 57).

### 4.2 Popper's theory of science (pp. 57–61)
**Background.** Popper started in Vienna between the wars. He was not in the Vienna Circle but had contact with the logical positivists, with much disagreement. He counts as an "empiricist" in the book's broad sense (p. 57). Unlike the positivists, he was initially not much interested in a general theory of language and meaning. His first aim was to tell science from "pseudo-science", and he did not call pseudo-science meaningless (p. 58). His examples: Einstein's work as genuine science; Freudian psychology and Marxist views of society and history as pseudo-science (p. 58).

**Demarcation and falsificationism.** Popper called the problem of distinguishing science from non-science the "problem of demarcation". His solution, falsificationism, says: *a hypothesis is scientific if and only if it has the potential to be refuted by some possible observation* (p. 58). A scientific hypothesis must take a risk. A theory compatible with every possible observation is not scientific. Popper thought a Marxist or Freudian can fit any outcome into the theory, so these theories face no risk (p. 58).

**No confirmation.** Popper also claimed that all scientific testing is an attempt to refute theories by observation. For him, confirming or establishing a theory by agreement with observations is never possible (p. 58). For Popper, an observational test can only show a theory false, and the truth of a theory is never supported by evidence, "not even a little bit", however many predictions succeed (p. 59). He was a severe critic of logical empiricist "inductive logic" and, like Hume, an inductive skeptic, skeptical of all support except deductive logic (p. 59). Godfrey-Smith notes that most philosophers of science think this is very bad news for science. Popper argued that induction is a myth but science does not need it, and most philosophers think this defence failed. Some admiring scientists do not realize Popper held this (p. 59).

**Fallibilism vs. Popper.** *Fallibilism* (term from Peirce) is the view that we can never be completely certain about factual matters. Most philosophers accept it, and so did Popper. The harder question is whether we can reasonably raise our confidence in a theory when it passes tests. Popper said no; the logical empiricists and most others say yes (p. 59).

**Testing procedure.** We deduce an observational prediction from a theory and check it. If it fails, the theory is refuted. For Popper, if it succeeds, we may say only that the theory has not yet been falsified, not that it is true, probable, or more likely than before (pp. 59–60). We then try new predictions. For Popper, a theory that survives repeated attempts has only survived them, and our confidence in its truth never grows. Ideally we never stop trying to falsify it. Limited time and resources are a practical constraint only. We should keep a tentative attitude to all theories (p. 60).

**Universal vs. existential statements.** For a law "All F's are G", one observed F that is not G falsifies it, by deductive logic. It is never possible to assemble enough observations to demonstrate it conclusively when the cases are huge or infinite in number (p. 60). Cases with few F's that could all be checked were treated as unimportant by Popper and the logical empiricists. A logical empiricist might reply that "Some F's are G" has the opposite feature: easy to verify, hard or impossible to falsify. Popper claimed, and the logical empiricists tended to agree, that real scientific theories rarely take this form (p. 60).

**The Holy Grail analogy.** Popper believed science searches for true descriptions of the world, yet confirmation is impossible. Godfrey-Smith's analogy: a knight faces infinitely or enormously many grails, only one holy. All glow, but only the Holy Grail glows forever. The knight carries one at a time and rejects any that stops glowing. He can never know that the grail he now carries is the holy one (pp. 60–61). Likewise, a theory not yet falsified might be true, but we would never know or have reason to be more confident (p. 61).

*Intuition note.* Falsification is asymmetric. One counterexample settles "All F's are G", but where the F's are huge or infinite in number no pile of successes settles it. Popper put great emphasis on that asymmetry.

### 4.3 Popper on scientific change (pp. 61–63)
**The cycle.** Science changes by a two-step cycle repeated endlessly. Stage 1 is *conjecture*: a hypothesis offered to describe and explain some part of the world. A good conjecture is bold and takes risks by making novel predictions. Stage 2 is *attempted refutation*: critical testing. After refutation, a new conjecture follows (p. 61).

**Ad hoc moves.** Conjectures may be related to earlier ones and refined over many rounds. A scientist should not respond to a falsification with a new conjecture designed just to avoid the revealed problem and going no further, called an *ad hoc* move. Scientists should increase the breadth and precision of theories, that is, their boldness (p. 61).

**Description and ideal.** Popper intended the cycle both as a description of the general pattern in science and as a description of good behaviour. Some scientists become too wedded to hypotheses, but he thought much actual behaviour fits the pattern, especially great scientists such as Einstein (pp. 61–62). A great scientist combines imaginative, risky idea-generation with a hard-headed willingness to test them rigorously. Godfrey-Smith's image: a cowboy with a Stradivarius in his saddlebags (p. 62).

**Individuals and groups.** The pattern seems applicable to individuals and groups, including a division of labour where A conjectures and B refutes. Popper would be suspicious of the case where A is fixated on his conjecture and B on proving A wrong to advance his own (p. 62). Godfrey-Smith asks whether an open-minded community could be built from closed-minded individuals. One problem he raises is that the tests might then change nobody's beliefs. The community's flexibility might perhaps come from new graduate students, which would be a rather slow way for science to change (p. 62). Popper stressed community standards, but he seems to picture the good scientist as performing both roles, tentative even about his own theories (p. 63).

**Darwin analogy.** The cycle resembles Darwin's variation and natural selection: undirected variations are "tested" by their effects on survival and reproduction, and helpful heritable ones become common (p. 63). Popper once thought Darwinism not scientific, then retracted this. The analogy should not be taken too seriously, because populations do not "search" the way scientists do, and there are other differences (p. 63).

### 4.4 Objections to Popper on falsification (pp. 63–67)
**Godfrey-Smith's reframing.** He thinks Popper's demarcation question probably has no answer in Popper's form, since we should not expect to label every theory "scientific" or "not". A similar question is meaningful: can we describe a distinctive scientific strategy for handling ideas? (p. 63). Popper's claim that theories should take risks is good, but his picture of risk-taking is too simple (p. 64).

**Holism.** For Popper, theories are generalizations that forbid particular observations. "All iron expands when heated" forbids seeing iron contract. But we must doubt whether the sample was really iron, and the measurements of contraction and temperature (p. 64). This is *holism about testing* (chapter 2). Linking theory to observation requires many auxiliary assumptions. Examples given: purity of iron samples; the chemical techniques needed to test Chargaff's rules (the amounts of C and G are equal, and A and T are equal, in DNA). A surprising result can always be blamed on an auxiliary assumption, or even on a misdescribed observation, as is not uncommon with reports of miracles or UFO abductions (p. 64). So the problem threatens Popper's whole theory of science, not only demarcation (p. 64).

**Popper's response.** Popper was aware of it. He treated the auxiliary assumptions as conjectures that might be false and could be tested separately. He conceded that logic can never force a scientist to give up a theory, since other assumptions can always be blamed. A good scientist will not deflect blame but will expose the theory to tests (pp. 64–65). Godfrey-Smith's assessment: Popper moved from a characteristic of *theories* to a characteristic of *behaviour*. That retracts his original aim, but may be a step forward (see 4.6) (p. 65).

**Observation reports as "decisions".** Popper accepted that observation reports are not certain. Accepting one is a freely made "decision". Challenging it means more testing of the same conjecture-refutation form, which rests on further decisions (p. 65). Popper insisted that deciding on single observations differs from deciding directly on theories. Godfrey-Smith asks what the difference is, and why we could not decide to keep a theory and reject the conflicting reports. He is not recommending this, only saying Popper gave no good reason against it. His own reason is that observation is generally reliable, which needs a scientific theory of perception (chapter 10). Popper does not argue from the reliability of perception (p. 65).

**Effect on demarcation.** Any hypothesis system can be kept despite apparent falsification if people so decide. Godfrey-Smith's answer to whether Popper still separates science from pseudo-science is "yes and no". Yes: scientific theories can be made immune, and non-scientific ones rejected, by decisions. No: a scientific theory is falsifiable through a decision about an observation report. A pseudo-scientific theory (Popper says) clashes with no possible observation, so rejecting it needs a different kind of decision. That is a significant difference, but Popper has not shown why the scientific way is more rational (p. 66).

**Probabilistic theories.** Take a fair-coin hypothesis. Observing 100 heads in 100 tosses is very unlikely under it but not impossible. Any finite run of heads is possible. By Popper's criterion, a hypothesis that forbids no observation takes no risk, so such theories seem unfalsifiable and unscientific (p. 66). Popper accepted that logically they are unscientific. That would mock the role of probability in science, so he said scientists can *decide* that an extremely improbable observation is in practice ruled out. Each field must decide how low a probability counts as prohibited. So probabilistic theories are falsifiable only in a special "in practice" sense (p. 66). Godfrey-Smith agrees scientists do reject theories on such observations and use statistical methods to decide how improbable is too improbable. But in this move Popper has badly damaged his picture, in which accepted observations decisively refute by deductive logic. Now falsification occurs without a deductive link between observation and theory (pp. 66–67).

*Intuition note.* Popper wanted refutation to be pure logic. Auxiliary assumptions mean logic alone cannot force rejection of a theory, and both the acceptance of observation reports and the treatment of very improbable events rest on decisions. Godfrey-Smith concludes that this damages Popper's picture of refutation as deductive logic.

### 4.5 Objections to Popper on confirmation (pp. 67–70)
**Setting aside 4.4.** Popper thought rational theory choice could be described by falsification alone, so rejecting induction and confirmation was no problem. Godfrey-Smith assumes falsification works decisively and asks whether this succeeds. His answer is no (p. 67).

**Bridge-building.** Engineers will tend to use theories that have survived testing, and the empiricist holds this policy is rational. The task is to explain why (p. 67). Popper can say to prefer an unfalsified theory over a falsified one. But compare (1) a theory tested many times and never failed with (2) a brand new untested theory. Neither is falsified. Ordinarily we choose (1). Why would building with (2) be irrational for Popper? (pp. 67–68). Popper recognized and struggled with this; perhaps it has been the most common objection to Popper from other empiricists (e.g., Salmon 1981). In Godfrey-Smith's judgement Popper is not able to give a very good reply (p. 68).

**Corroboration.** Popper said a theory that has survived many falsification attempts is *corroborated*, and it is rational to choose corroborated theories. He gave a technical definition and held that the amount can be measured. Godfrey-Smith skips the technicalities (p. 68). The dilemma: if corroboration is just a new name for confirmation, Popper gives up a main difference from the logical empiricists. If it is so different that it is no guide to truth, why choose corroborated theories? (p. 68).

**Transcript vs. letter.** An academic transcript reports past performance and makes no explicit predictions. A letter of recommendation also makes claims about future performance. Confirmation (logical empiricist sense) is like a letter. Popper's corroboration is only like a transcript, because Popper thought no good reasons show that past performance guides the future. So corroboration is purely backward-looking, and no reason can be given for building with a corroborated rather than an unfalsified uncorroborated theory (p. 68).

**What Popper should say.** Godfrey-Smith thinks the best option is to stay with inductive skepticism: we do not know whether a design that worked will work again, and we will not know a new design is bad until we try it. There may be practical reasons for the familiar design (p. 69). Popper liked to say no policy is more rational than using the established design, and a decision must be made. Salmon replied that this does not help. If confirmation does not exist, no policy is more rational than choosing the untested design either. It is a tie (p. 69). Most people find this unsatisfactory. Still, theories of induction and confirmation have proved extremely hard to produce, and Popper's work shows what a theory of science might look like without them (p. 69).

**Hypothetico-deductive method.** Godfrey-Smith says Popper comes close to giving a recipe for science, though Popper insists there is no recipe for generating interesting conjectures (p. 69). Textbooks present the "hypothetico-deductive method" (a method, not the confirmation theory of chapter 3). Some versions combine Popper's view of testing with a less skeptical view of confirmation: conjecture, deduce predictions, and if they come out right the theory is "supported", otherwise rejected (p. 69). The pattern is Popper's, but "support" is not a Popperian idea. Godfrey-Smith thinks such discussions generally assume that more successful predictions give more reason to believe the theory (p. 70). Other versions start by collecting observations to generate a conjecture. Popper disagreed, arguing that fact-gathering can only take place guided by a conjecture; Godfrey-Smith calls this "a fairly minor point" (p. 70).

**Strong inference.** Introduced by chemist John Platt (1964). It is roughly Popperian testing plus an assumption Popper rejected: that all possible theories in an area can be listed and tested one by one, finding the truth by elimination ("Sherlock Holmes" method). For Popper this is impossible. There are infinitely many competing theories, so eliminating ten or a hundred leaves the same infinity. Popper thought we can only choose one, test it, and move on (p. 70). Objections to Popper's theory of change (4.3) are deferred to later chapters (p. 70).

**Lasting contribution.** Godfrey-Smith's verdict: Popper's single most important and enduring contribution is using riskiness to describe how theories contact observation. Theories can seem to have contact with observation but only "pseudo-contact", because they face no risk. This is an advance for empiricism, though Popper's analysis of how exposure works "does not work too well" (pp. 70–71).

*Intuition note.* Popper's rule "choose the well-tested theory" seems to need past success to be some guide to the future. Popper thought no good reasons can be given for believing that past performance is a reliable guide. On Godfrey-Smith's account, this leaves him no good answer to why we should prefer the well-tested theory.

### 4.6 Further comments on the demarcation problem (pp. 71–74)
**Handling of ideas.** Risk-taking is better used to distinguish scientific from unscientific *ways of handling ideas*, and we should not expect a sharp line (p. 71). The scientific way connects an idea with others, embedding it in a larger structure so it is exposed to observation. This exposure is not just simple falsification. Many ways of exposure can modify and assess an idea. Keeping a hypothesis apart from observational risk is unscientific handling (p. 71).

**Big ideas have both versions.** Asking whether Marxism or Freudianism *themselves* are scientific is a mistake. Each has scientific and unscientific versions. Handling Marxism scientifically means working out what difference its principles would make to observable things. This does not require one observation that would definitively refute it. An auxiliary assumption may always be at fault, and there is no simple recipe for such decisions (p. 71).

**Marxism.** Marxism holds that history is driven by class struggle, guided by changes in economic organization, producing a predictable sequence of political changes leading to socialism. Freudianism holds that normal child development includes conflicts between unconscious parts of the mind, much to do with sexual feelings toward parents (p. 71). Godfrey-Smith's view: over the twentieth century Marxism was handled scientifically enough to be disconfirmed. Much happened with little to do with class struggle (e.g., the growing political role of religious and cultural solidarity), and capitalist societies adapted to economic tensions in ways Marxism does not predict. It remains possible to hold the principles, but fewer people do. Many think it still has useful economic insights (p. 72).

**Freudianism.** Godfrey-Smith's view: it persists not through empirical success but through its striking character and a subculture in psychotherapy and literary theory that guards it. Those groups handle it very unscientifically. Most scientifically oriented psychology departments do not take it seriously (p. 72).

**Evolution and the Precambrian rabbit.** Evolution can also be handled scientifically or not. A common one-liner on what would refute it is "a Precambrian rabbit". Futuyma's textbook says "incontrovertibly mammalian fossils in incontrovertibly pre-cambrian rocks" would "refute or cast serious doubt on evolution" (p. 72). The Precambrian ended around 540 million years ago. A well-preserved rabbit fossil in 600-million-year-old rocks would conflict with evidence that only sponges and a few other invertebrates then existed and that mammals appeared over 300 million years later (p. 72). First we would suspect the finding (dating? hoax? cf. Piltdown man, 1908). This is another aspect of holism, the challenging of theory-laden observation reports (chapter 10). Suppose all agree it is a Precambrian rabbit (pp. 72–73).

It would not instantly falsify all evolutionary theory, because evolutionary theory is a diverse package: abstract models of what mechanisms can do (tested by mathematics and simulation) and claims about life's actual history. Small-scale evolution observed in the lab in bacteria and fruit flies would be unaffected (p. 73). But it would show serious errors somewhere in the package. These would at least include errors about the overall history of life, the processes through which a rabbit-like organism could evolve, and the "family tree" of species. The challenge would be to separate and reassess each idea, which could in principle even discard basic beliefs such as humans evolving from nonhumans (p. 73).

**Molecular test.** In the past twenty years molecular biology has exposed evolutionary theory to a huge, sustained test. DNA similarity is a good indicator of closeness of relationship, so the pre-molecular family tree could be compared with DNA data. Had DNA suggested humans split from chimps hundreds of millions of years ago and are close to squid, this would have been almost as bad as the rabbit (p. 73). In fact the data suggest humans and chimps diverged about 4.6–5 million years ago and chimps or bonobos are our nearest living relatives. Before this, it was unclear whether humans were closer to chimps or gorillas. The result: no huge surprises, many new facts, and many adjustments (p. 74).

*Intuition note.* The question is not "is this theory scientific?" but "is it being handled so that observation can hurt it?" The same big idea can pass or fail depending on how it is handled.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Hypothesis H: "All metal rods in this box expand when heated." The box holds 1,000 rods; we may test only a few.
- Test 1, 2, 3: rods expand. For Popper, H is "not yet falsified". No confidence rises, and 3 of 1,000 checked proves nothing.
- Test 4: a rod seems to contract. Logically "All F are G" is refuted if this is a metal rod that contracted. This needs auxiliary assumptions A1 "it is metal" and A2 "the thermometer is accurate". Refutation is deductive only if A1 and A2 are accepted. If A2 is doubted, H survives. Popper's reply: accepting A1 and A2 is a "decision", and a good scientist tests A2 separately rather than deflecting blame.
- Probabilistic variant: H′ says each toss of a coin gives heads with probability 1/2. Ten heads in a row has probability $(1/2)^{10} = 1/1024 \approx 0.00098$. If a field decides "below 0.001 counts as prohibited", H′ is falsified "in practice". Logic alone does not decide this threshold.

## Concepts
- [[demarcation problem]] — Popper's name for the problem of separating science from non-science, incl. pseudo-science (p. 58)
- [[falsifiability]] — a hypothesis is scientific iff some possible observation could refute it (p. 58)
- [[corroboration]] — Popper's measure of survival of falsification attempts; here read as purely backward-looking, like a transcript (p. 68)
- [[confirmation holism (Quine–Duhem thesis)]] — tests need auxiliary assumptions, so blame can fall away from the theory; the author calls it "holism about testing" (p. 64)
- [[fallibilism]] (new) — we can never be completely certain about factual matters; accepted by most philosophers and by Popper (p. 59)
- [[inductive skepticism]] (new) — Popper's view that no support except deductive logic exists; confirmation is a myth (p. 59)
- [[conjecture-refutation cycle]] (new) — Popper's two-step model of scientific change; bold conjecture, then critical testing (p. 61)
- [[ad hoc move]] (new) — patching a theory after falsification just to avoid the problem, going no further (p. 61)
- [[hypothetico-deductive method]] (new) — textbook method: conjecture, deduce predictions, test; often adds the non-Popperian idea of "support" (p. 69)
- [[strong inference]] (new) — Popperian testing plus eliminating a complete list of rivals; Popper rejects the list (p. 70)
