# Lakatos, Laudan, Feyerabend, and Frameworks

## L0 — One line
After Kuhn, Lakatos and Laudan pictured science as competition between large paradigm-like units, Feyerabend defended "anything goes", and the chapter ends by asking whether scientific change has one process or two.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn's picture of one paradigm per field drew criticism. Lakatos (research programs) and Laudan (research traditions) replaced it with several rival large-scale units competing within a field. Laudan's version is the better one: it separates accepting a theory from pursuing it, and it gives usable rules. Feyerabend argued that rules of method stifle creativity. His Galileo argument is a serious challenge to empiricist methods, but his proliferation-without-selection recipe ignores the practical use of science. The chapter closes with the "one-process versus two-process" question: do changes within a framework differ in kind from changes of framework? Popper and Quine say no. Kuhn, Carnap, Lakatos and Laudan say yes.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's paradigm, normal science and revolution; Popper's conjecture and refutation; Quine's holism and Carnap's analytic/synthetic distinction (from earlier chapters).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A Lakatosian research program has a hard core (never changed) and a protective belt (modifiable). It is progressive if changes extend it to new cases, and degenerating if they only patch existing problems (p. 104–105).
- Lakatos gives no rule for when to abandon a degenerating program. Feyerabend called this the Achilles' heel and said the account of rational theory choice is then "completely empty"; the author agrees there is a mismatch between Lakatos's rhetoric and reality (p. 105–106).
- Competing programs fit some fields (psychology) better than Kuhn's single paradigm. Mixed pictures also occur, such as the neutral theory budding off from the synthetic theory (p. 106–107).
- Laudan's research traditions are looser than programs: the core can shift, and a theory can move between traditions. His acceptance/pursuit distinction yields sharper rules: pursue the fastest-progressing tradition, accept the one with most problem-solving power (p. 108–109).
- Neither Lakatos nor Laudan seemed to ask how workers should be distributed across programs. Kuhn noted that science spreads risk (p. 110).
- Feyerabend's epistemological anarchism ("anything goes") is supported by several arguments; perhaps the most central is from history: Galileo's case seems to show that empiricist rules would have favoured the Aristotelians. He overextends it, since a rule that "may" fail is not thereby discredited (p. 111, 113–115).
- His marketplace of ideas lacks a mechanism for rejecting ideas. Applied science (the AIDS example) needs selection as well as proliferation (p. 116–117).
- One-process theories (Popper, Quine) and two-process theories (Kuhn, Carnap, Lakatos, Laudan) differ on whether changes within frameworks and of frameworks differ in kind, though the author warns against a simple split into two camps. The question ties back to confirmation and testing (p. 117–120).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 7.1 After Structure (p. 102–103)
The chapter covers philosophers who developed their views in response to Kuhn, then "pauses for a breath" to look at general patterns (p. 102).
- Lakatos's main idea is the research program. It is like a paradigm, but a field is expected to hold more than one program at a time. Large-scale change is competition between programs (p. 102).
- Kuhn's claim that a field usually has one paradigm was criticized from the start. Lakatos was the first to develop a parallel-and-competing picture. His version has problems, and Laudan later built a superior one (p. 102).
- Feyerabend defended "epistemological anarchism": "anything goes" replaces rules of method. Feyerabend's most important work (1975) was written, he said, as a kind of letter to Lakatos, who died in 1974 before replying (p. 103).

### 7.2 Lakatos and Research Programs (p. 103–108)
**Background and attitudes.**
- Lakatos worked with Popper and often said his ideas were implicit in Popper. The author says there is some truth in this but that Lakatos's ideas are better taken in their own terms (p. 103).
- Lakatos was dismayed by Kuhn. He read Kuhn as presenting change as irrational "mob psychology" (1970, 178). The author's reading of Kuhn is that science is a well-structured machine in which even revolutions have a positive role (p. 103).
- Lakatos accepted the force of Kuhn's historical cases. His project was to rescue the rationality of science (p. 103).
- **Lakatos's view on history:** write "rational reconstructions" that make scientists' decisions look as rational as possible, and note departures from what happened in footnotes. The author finds this "spectacularly strange" and is surprised it is not criticized more (Hacking 1983 is an exception) (p. 103–104).

**Structure of a program.**
- A research program is roughly analogous to a broad-sense paradigm. Competition between programs is, for Lakatos, what we find in science, and it is essential to rationality and progress (p. 104).
- A program is a historical entity: a sequence of related theories, each developed in response to problems with earlier ones. As for Kuhn, living for a while despite anomalies (unsolved empirical problems) is common and justifiable, and programs are sometimes abandoned (p. 104).
- So there are two kinds of change: (1) within a program, (2) among the collection of programs in a field (p. 104).
- Two components: the **hard core** (basic ideas essential to the program) and the **protective belt** (less fundamental ideas that apply the core to phenomena). Testable theories combine ideas from both (p. 104).
- Examples:
  - Newtonian program (18th century). Core: the three laws of motion and the gravitational law. Belt: changing ideas about matter, the universe's structure, and mathematical tools (p. 104–105).
  - Darwinian program (19th century). Core: species are linked by descent in a family tree (or very few trees), and change is mostly accumulation of tiny variations favoured by natural selection, with other causes secondary. Belt: shifting ideas about which species are related, inheritance, variation, competition, selection, and distribution of organisms (p. 105).

**Rules within a program.**
1. Change only the protective belt, never the hard core (p. 105).
2. Changes to the belt should be progressive (borrowing from Popper). A progressive program extends to more cases or treats present cases more precisely; it is increasing its predictive power. A program is degenerating if changes only cover existing problems and do not extend it to new cases (p. 105).
- Lakatos assumed all programs face anomalies. A degenerating program is falling behind, or barely keeping up, with them. He thought one could in principle measure how quickly a program progresses (p. 105).

**Choice between programs.**
- One might expect the rule "choose the most progressive program". Lakatos did not say that. He held it is acceptable to protect a degenerating program for a while, even after a rival has overtaken it (Lakatos 1971), since programs sometimes recover (p. 105–106).
- How long to wait? "Lakatos does not say" (p. 106).
- **Feyerabend's objection:** this is the Achilles' heel. Without a rule for when to switch, the account of rational theory choice is "completely empty" (p. 106).
- Lakatos did say that staying with a degenerating program is high-risk. The author grants that people can reasonably differ in attitude to risk, but says Lakatos did not follow this up. The author says the appearance of methodological strictness in his rhetoric is much undermined, agrees with Feyerabend that rhetoric and reality mismatch, and suspects ("sometimes looks as if") that the point was to describe episodes retrospectively as rational (p. 106).
- Contrast: Kuhn trusts the implicit shared values of paradigms. Lakatos wants methodological rules, or at least a story that science is guided by them (p. 106).

**Usefulness.**
- Competing programs fit some fields better than Kuhn's view. Psychology is an obvious example, and evolutionary psychology looks like a program in Lakatos's sense (p. 106).
- Mixtures occur. Looked at very broadly, evolutionary biology might contain something close to one paradigm, the "synthetic theory" (Darwinism plus genetics), but at a lower level we seem to find competing programs. The neutral theory of molecular evolution tries to understand most variation and change at the molecular level in terms of random processes rather than selection. It is compatible with the synthetic theory's central claims but conflicts with some standard applications of it (p. 107).
- In this and other cases, a program is "budded off" from the mainstream and explored for a few decades; then, if its limits are reached, it may be moderated and folded back into the mainstream. That seems to be happening to the neutral theory (p. 107).
- So fields may have dominant paradigms, competing programs, or general paradigms with budding programs. "Research program" is sometimes also used for non-competing approaches within a field (p. 107).

**Popper versus Kuhn once more.**
- Popper in places accepts that theories should be protected initially (Popper 1963, 49; 1970, 55). But facing Kuhn (1970), he did not blur the difference. He said normal science exists but is much rarer than Kuhn claimed, and he regarded it as a bad thing not to be encouraged (p. 107–108).

*Intuition note:* Lakatos treats a science as teams defending a fixed core while adjusting side-assumptions. He judges teams by whether the adjustments win new ground. His weak spot is that he does not say how long a team that is losing ground should keep waiting before giving up.

### 7.3 Laudan and Research Traditions (p. 108–110)
- In *Progress and Its Problems* (1977), Laudan also read Kuhn as presenting science as "basically a political and propagandistic affair" (1977, 4). The author calls this reading inaccurate. Laudan still wanted coexisting, competing paradigm-like units, which he called **research traditions** (p. 108).
- Differences from Lakatos (p. 108):
  - Lakatos: theories in a program are tightly linked by logic, each with a broader domain than its predecessor, and the hard core never changes.
  - Laudan: theories in a tradition are more loosely related; ideas can move in and out of the core; a later theory covering less territory is not bad, since sometimes a retreat is necessary; theories can leave one tradition and join another.
  - Example: Sadi Carnot's early thermodynamics arose in a tradition seeing heat as a fluid ("caloric") and was later taken over by a rival tradition seeing heat as motion of matter (p. 108).
- **Acceptance versus pursuit.** Earlier philosophies in the book tended to treat a scientist's attitude to a theory as belief or something like it, in degrees. Laudan distinguishes two attitudes (p. 108–109):
  - *Acceptance*: close to belief; treating the theory as true.
  - *Pursuit*: deciding to work with and explore an idea for reasons other than confidence that it is likely true. It can be reasonable to pursue an idea one definitely does not accept, for example because the payoff if true would be huge, or because one is best equipped to explore it (p. 109).
- **Laudan's rules** (p. 109):
  - It is always rational to pursue the tradition with the highest current *rate* of progress in problem-solving (1977, 111).
  - Acceptability is measured by present overall *level* of problem-solving power, not by rate of change. We should accept (perhaps cautiously) the theories with the highest level.
  - So a scientist might accept a mainstream tradition but work on a marginal one with a spectacular rate of progress, and this would be rational for Laudan.
- Any rule can mislead in some case, for example a tradition with a low rate now that is about to take off. This worried Lakatos. Laudan's distinction helps, but he does not try to cover every case or every kind of bad luck. The author notes it is unclear what principles a philosophy of science should seek. Some think seeking procedural rules is a mistake, but Laudan gave an "impressive" normative theory (p. 109).
- **A gap in both theories.** Neither asked whether the right choice depends on how many people already work in a program. Perhaps science is better served by hedging bets. The question has two forms: (a) individual choice, whether a program is overcrowded; (b) which distribution of people across programs is best for science. Lakatos and Laudan may have avoided (a) because it brings in selfish goals (p. 110).
- Kuhn, especially after *Structure*, saw that science's strength lies partly in distributing risk through different scientists' choices, especially during crises. This is ironic because he thought ongoing competition between paradigms was not usual. The topic returns in chapter 11 (p. 110).

*Intuition note:* Laudan separates "what I believe" from "what I work on". One can rationally work on a long-shot idea that is improving fast while still believing the mainstream view.

### 7.4 Anything Goes (p. 110–113)
- Feyerabend was influenced early by Popper but by the early 1960s was moving to his famous views, and he and Kuhn influenced each other (p. 110).
- In *Against Method* (1975) he argued for **epistemological anarchism**: opposition to all systems of rules in science. Great scientists are opportunistic and creative; rules of method straitjacket creativity; great scientists have always been willing to break even the most basic methodological rules. The only rule safe for imagination and progress is "anything goes" (p. 111).
- He used ideas about language and observation: rival theories are often linguistically incommensurable, and observations are contaminated by theoretical assumptions and so are no neutral test. The author says these arguments rest on speculative ideas about language and are not very convincing. The more interesting arguments concern history, and freedom and human well-being (p. 111).
- **Warning from Feyerabend:** the reader should not read the book's arguments as expressing his "deep convictions"; they merely show how easy it is to lead people by the nose in a rational way (1975, 32). The anarchist is an "undercover agent" using reason to destabilize it. The author's view: Feyerabend's deepest conviction was that science is an aspect of human creativity, to be assessed in those terms (p. 111).
- **Comparisons:** Michael Williams likens Feyerabend to the old skeptical tradition (Sextus Empiricus, Montaigne), which counterposes ideas without regarding any as established. The author calls this a useful comparison but only part of the story, and suggests Oscar Wilde as the other part: behind the paradoxes lies the message that aesthetic assessment matters most and free creativity must not be obstructed (p. 111–112).
- **Feyerabend on Kuhn** ("Consolations for the Specialist", 1970; p. 112):
  - Most philosophers saw disorder in Kuhn's picture. Feyerabend saw an incitement to become orderly and mechanical: Kuhn glorified the routine of normal science and its rigid education, and encouraged professionalization, narrow-mindedness and exclusion of unorthodox ideas.
  - He saw the blurring of descriptive and normative in Kuhn as a deliberate rhetorical device. He said this mind-set also breeds lack of concern for the moral consequences of science.
  - He also argued Kuhn was factually wrong: paradigms almost never exert the control Kuhn described, since imaginative individuals always try new ideas.
- The author says Feyerabend was not an "enemy of science" but of some kinds. In the 17th century, according to Feyerabend, science was an ally of freedom against the Church, and he admired Galileo. For Feyerabend, science has since become an enemy: scientists are turning into "human ants" (1975, 188), and society must be freed from the domineering scientific establishment, as once from the One True Religion (p. 112–113).

### 7.5 An Argument from History That Haunts Philosophy (p. 113–115)
**The argument.**
- *Against Method* spends much space on Galileo against the Aristotelians. Galileo defended the literal truth of Copernicus's claim that the earth goes round the sun, and faced arguments from everyday experience. Example: a ball dropped from a tower lands at its foot although, on Copernicus's view, the tower moves a long way while the ball falls (p. 113).
- **Feyerabend's claim:** if empiricism has any teeth, it entails that seventeenth-century people had excellent reasons to resist Galileo (p. 113).
- Galileo's reply (*Dialogue*, 1632): the ball has a mixed motion, falling while also moving in a huge circle with the tower. Everyday perception cannot distinguish "both move in a circle" from "neither does". He marvelled at those who did "violence to their own senses" in favour of reason (p. 113).
- **Feyerabend:** Galileo had to create a different observational description in which apparent motion fits Copernicus. Science must often break the constraints of an outdated worldview built into basic observation. It often challenges rather than follows observation (p. 113–114).
- The author's reconstruction of the challenge: do any principles of method or measures of confirmation avoid failing this test? Would traditional, especially empiricist, accounts have told us to stick with the Aristotelians and not bet on Galileo? This is the argument that "haunts" philosophy of science (p. 114).

**Where Feyerabend overreaches** (author's criticism).
- Feyerabend: "it is advisable to let one's inclinations go against reason in any circumstances, for science may profit from it" (1975, 156). The author calls the claim "obviously crazy": a rule that may go wrong is not thereby discredited (p. 114).
- **Train analogy.** The policy of catching scheduled trains may go wrong (a crash; or a different train might bring you the love of your life). Nobody takes these mere possibilities to discredit the rule. Applying rules of rational behaviour presupposes judgments of which outcomes are probable or typical, sometimes with exact numbers, often informally. Nothing is guaranteed, yet some policies can be justified (the Bayesian theory in chapter 14 rests on this). The same holds for science: "may" is not enough (p. 114–115).
- It is not clear that well-developed theories get the Galileo case wrong. Laudan's rules would probably steer well: they would tell us to pursue the Galilean program because of its rate of increase in problem-solving power, and in time it would be rational to accept it (p. 115).

*Intuition note:* Feyerabend's historical case asks whether a method would have backed the right side in a famous episode. The author thinks the case is a real test that should not be ignored, but the mere possibility that a rule may go wrong does not justify dropping rules, and it is not clear that well-developed theories actually fail it.

### 7.6 Pluralism and the Ramblings of Madmen (p. 115–117)
- Feyerabend (1970) proposed two principles, while saying "rules are made to be broken": the **principle of tenacity** (hold onto attractive theories despite initial problems so they can develop) and the **principle of proliferation** (invent new theories). If only tenacity were followed, nothing would change (p. 115).
- Against Kuhn's view that proliferation should wait for a crisis, Feyerabend says to propose new ideas all the time. The ideal is a population developing theories while inventing new ones, with pedestrian work not interfering with imaginative work (p. 115).
- He claimed to follow J. S. Mill's *On Liberty*: diversity of ideas creates a "marketplace of ideas" where the best prevail. Some of Feyerabend's descriptions of unusual sources of ideas, such as "the ramblings of madmen" (1975, 68), were intended as inputs into this marketplace (p. 116).
- **Feyerabend's point:** we can often only see the limits of our perspective by stepping outside it, at least temporarily. Even observable "established facts" may be laden with prejudice and outdated ideas, and alternative theories, even badly flawed ones, can supply an external vantage point (p. 116).
- **Author's criticism:** the picture has no mechanism for rejecting and eliminating ideas, so the range of ideas would only grow, apart from some dropped for boredom. What should we use when a bridge must be built? Not the most "creative" ones, surely. Feyerabend gave no satisfactory answer (p. 116).
- If we want only a lively imaginative community, the recipe suits, and science would resemble art. If science must guide practical problems, selection is needed too. Proliferation is part of science, and selection among alternatives is another part (p. 116).
- **Example:** Mbeki's South African government showed interest in radical ideas on AIDS causation (HIV unimportant or harmless). Mbeki has sometimes said he is simply interested in open-minded questioning and the exploration of diverse possibilities. The author calls this reply "rightly criticized as disingenuous": in a public-health setting, selection is of paramount importance, and we must act on the well-supported view that a virus transmitted through body fluids causes AIDS (p. 116–117).
- **Williams** says Feyerabend's "general spirit" is now conventional wisdom. The author gives an almost opposite summary: a few pieces have become conventional, but the "general spirit" includes the clearly false principle that science's social role resembles art's. Imagination is one side of science, not the only side (p. 117).

### 7.7 Taking Stock: Frameworks and Two-Process Theories of Science (p. 117–121)
**The distinction.** Should scientific (or conceptual) change be seen as (1) changes within the boundaries of a general framework and (2) changes of the frameworks themselves, as two different kinds of change (a **two-process** theory)? The alternative (one alternative) is a **one-process** unified account (p. 117).

**Contrasts.**
- *Popper vs. Kuhn:* For Popper, change is always conjecture and refutation, at every level. For Kuhn, change within and between paradigms are fundamentally different. A broad-sense paradigm is a framework: within it, principles come from the framework, so moves between frameworks are more problematic and often disorderly (p. 117–118).
- *Carnap vs. Quine:* Later Carnap used "linguistic frameworks". Their fundamental principles would appear as analytic sentences if stated. Moves within a framework test synthetic claims. Switching between frameworks is not sensitive to particular facts but to pragmatic assessment of overall usefulness. Carnap's frameworks are "thinner" than Kuhn's, being basic linguistic and logical rules, not scientific principles (p. 118).
- Quine ("Two Dogmas") argued against the two-layer view. All changes to the belief system involve holistic tinkering with the web of belief: as few changes as possible, keeping the worldview simple. There is no within/between distinction (p. 118).

**How to decide?**
- Many were persuaded by Quine's holism, which rests on general considerations, not the history of episodes. His strongest argument is usually seen as the claim that the within/between distinction cannot be marked in a way that is scientific and does not beg the question (p. 118).
- Kuhn distinguished normal from revolutionary change in actual cases and may have reached insights through this. **Friedman (2001)** argues Kuhn was right and Quine wrong. The text adds that Quine's claim seems to rest on an overly stringent conception of what is needed to recognize two kinds of change (p. 118–119).
- **Ricketts (1982)** on how Quine might resist: suppose a scientist pins basic principles on the laboratory door and says they change by a special process. If testing is holistic, the actual processes of belief modification are all of one kind: pragmatic adjustment to avoid tension and unexpected observations. There is no sense in which moves within are "guided by facts" and moves to the framework "merely pragmatic" (p. 119).
- The author's view: the issue leads back to testing and confirmation, but two-process ideas have been useful on specific cases, and Quine and allies would concede that usefulness is the ultimate arbiter here (p. 119).

**Kant and relativism.** Kant first developed an abstract framework guiding empirical investigation; for him it is fixed and universal among normal humans and cannot be escaped. Many twentieth-century philosophers kept the idea of "conceptual schemes" but held that they can change and vary across cultures. Some of the radical ideas in the next chapter can be seen as combining Kantian schemes with relativism (p. 119–120).

**Mixed cases.**
- *Feyerabend:* recognized the psychological power of frameworks but held that imaginative people can resist them. *Popper* rejected frameworks as constraining thought, calling this "the myth of the framework" (p. 120).
- *Galison (1997):* in physics, fundamental change in experimental traditions tends not to coincide with fundamental change in theory, because of their partial autonomy (instrumentation is another tradition). Disruptions are more local than on Kuhn's model, transitions are easier to manage, and the "seams" do not line up, which strengthens the whole (p. 120).
- Therefore no simple split between the one-process camp (Popper, Quine, and Feyerabend "in a sense") and the two-process camp (Carnap, Kuhn, Lakatos, Laudan, Friedman). Kant's universal framework and Laudan's traditions are very different kinds of things (p. 120).
- Left aside: whether people modify frameworks or jump between them (maybe no difference). Two-process views have been a recurring motif for a hundred years (p. 121).

*Intuition note:* The question is whether "adjusting a detail" and "changing the whole outlook" are different activities or the same activity at different sizes. Quine says the same; Kuhn says different. The dispute leads back to how testing and confirmation work.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two research traditions, A and B, in one field. Count "problems solved" as a crude measure of problem-solving power. This is our simplification, since Laudan gives no such count.

| Tradition | Problems solved now | Gained last 5 years | Rate per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 10 | 10 ÷ 5 = 2 |
| B | 40 | 30 | 30 ÷ 5 = 6 |

- Laudan's acceptance rule uses the level: 50 > 40, so accept A (cautiously).
- His pursuit rule uses the rate: 6 > 2, so it is rational to pursue B.
- A scientist may accept A and work on B, which is rational by Laudan's rules.
- Lakatos's view lacks such a rule. If a program were degenerating, he gives no number for how long to wait.
- Distribution (the gap noted on p. 110): if all 100 scientists pursue B, A gets none, and nothing in either theory says this is a problem.

## Concepts
- [[research programme (hard core, protective belt)]] — Lakatos's large-scale unit: a sequence of theories with an unchanged core and a modifiable belt, competing with rivals; progressive or degenerating (p. 104–105). Progress here means extending to new cases or greater precision (p. 105).
- [[progressive vs degenerating program]] (new) — progressive: expands to new cases or precision; degenerating: changes only cover existing problems (p. 105).
- [[research-tradition]] (new) — Laudan's looser unit: theories loosely related, core can shift, theories can move between traditions (p. 108).
- [[acceptance-vs-pursuit]] (new) — acceptance is treating a theory as true; pursuit is working on it for other reasons (p. 109).
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — in this chapter, a Kuhnian field usually has one paradigm; Lakatos and Feyerabend's criticisms of it (p. 102, 112).
- [[incommensurability]] — Feyerabend, like Kuhn, held that rival theories are often linguistically incommensurable (p. 111).
- [[epistemological-anarchism]] (new) — opposition to all rules of method in science; "anything goes" (p. 111).
- [[tenacity-and-proliferation]] (new) — Feyerabend's two principles: keep developing attractive theories, and keep inventing new ones (p. 115).
- [[one-process vs two-process theories]] (new) — whether change within a framework differs in kind from change of framework (p. 117).
- [[linguistic-framework]] (new) — Carnap's thin framework of basic linguistic and logical rules; switching is judged pragmatically (p. 118).
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — Carnap: framework principles appear as analytic sentences; moves inside test synthetic claims (p. 118).
- [[web of belief]] and [[confirmation holism (Quine–Duhem thesis)]] — Quine: all change is holistic tinkering with the whole network (p. 118–119).
- [[falsifiability]] — only implicit here: Popper's conjecture-and-refutation cycle as the one process (p. 117–118).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Laudan's pursuit vs. acceptance ↔ decision theory: choosing which option to explore differs from belief about which is true.
- [structural-analogy] Feyerabend's "marketplace of ideas" plus missing selection ↔ evolution: variation without selection yields accumulating variants and no adaptation.
- [structural-analogy] Distribution of workers across research programs ↔ portfolio diversification: hedging bets across uncertain options.
- [metaphor] Feyerabend's train argument ↔ Bayesian reasoning: a rule is justified by probable outcomes, not guaranteed ones (the author links this to chapter 14, p. 115).

## Questions this raises
- Can "progress rate" and "problem-solving power" be measured well enough to apply Laudan's rules, and how do we count problems of unequal importance?
- Would Laudan's rules really have told us to pursue Galileo? What counted as problem-solving then, and who judged it?
- What is the best distribution of scientists across rival programs, and is it set by individual choices or by a collective mechanism?
- Is there a non-question-begging way to separate changes within a framework from changes of framework, a possibility Quine denies and Friedman defends?
