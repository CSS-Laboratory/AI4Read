# Godfrey-Smith, P. (2003). *Theory and Reality: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Science*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press (Science and Its Conceptual Foundations). 272 pp.

## In one paragraph
The book is a chronological tour of about a century of argument about what science is and how it works. It starts with logical empiricism, which tried to explain science through logic and experience alone. It then follows the problems of confirmation and the replies of Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, Laudan and Feyerabend. Next come the sociological and feminist challenges. Godfrey-Smith takes sides throughout. In the later chapters he builds his own picture. It is a naturalistic, reformed empiricism in which science is a strategy of exposing even big ideas to experience. The strategy is carried out by a social structure that works as an "engine of self-correction". This picture is compatible with scientific realism about hidden structure. The book matters because it gives one coherent view in which testing, social organization, explanation and realism all fit together.

## The path through the work
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch01-introduction]] — Before surveying a century of theories of science, the author sets out the scope of "science", the kinds of theory one can give of it, and three recurring answers (empiricism, mathematics, social structure) that are probably pieces of one answer.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch02-logic]] — Logical positivism and logical empiricism tried to rebuild philosophy of science on logic, a theory of meaning and experience alone, and the view eventually declined. The author thinks an important mistake was refusing to let science describe hidden structure.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch03-induction]] — The logical empiricists tried to build a purely formal logic of confirmation, but the ravens problem and Goodman's grue riddle suggest that whether evidence confirms a hypothesis depends on background assumptions, on how the data were gathered, and on which categories we use.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch04-popper]] — Popper held that science is bold conjecture plus attempted refutation and that confirmation is a myth; Godfrey-Smith judges the theory flawed but keeps its core idea that scientific theories must be exposed to the risk of observational failure.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch05-kuhn]] — In Godfrey-Smith's reading, Kuhn held that science works well because normal science closes off debate about fundamentals and keeps scientists committed to one paradigm, until a critical mass of anomalies produces a crisis.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch06-kuhn]] — Godfrey-Smith presents Kuhn's view that science gets its power from alternating orderly "normal science" and disorderly revolutions (breakdown, then a new framework), and he criticises parts of it, above all the claim that the world itself changes with paradigms.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch07-lakatos]] — After Kuhn, Lakatos and Laudan pictured science as competition between large paradigm-like units, Feyerabend defended "anything goes", and the chapter ends by asking whether scientific change has one process or two.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch08-challenge]] — Late-twentieth-century sociology of science moved from describing science's social structure (Merton) to explaining scientific belief by social factors (strong program, Latour), and Godfrey-Smith accepts the social insight but rejects the slide to "facts are made, not found."
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch09-feminism]] — Late-twentieth-century critics argued that science is political; Godfrey-Smith finds Lloyd's feminist history interesting, the arguments for the radical epistemologies unconvincing, and sees a gap in Science Studies' account of how theories connect to the world.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch10-naturalistic]] — Godfrey-Smith defends a naturalism in which philosophy keeps its own (often normative) questions but may use scientific results to answer them, and he shows this at work by using psychology to make progress on the "theory-ladenness of observation" debate, which he says becomes much easier to settle from a naturalistic standpoint.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch11-naturalism]] — Naturalistic philosophers (Hull, Kitcher, Strevens) explain how science works by asking how its reward system makes selfish individual behaviour serve the goals of science as a whole, though Godfrey-Smith thinks this harmony may be overstated.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch12-scientific]] — Scientific realism is best stated as a claim about the aims of science (accurate representation of reality, including unobservable parts), not as a claim that our current theories are true, and the author argues against its main rivals.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch13-explanation]] — The covering law theory of explanation fails chiefly because explanation has a direction, and Godfrey-Smith argues that no single relation (law, cause, unification) is "the" explanatory relation, since standards of good explanation vary with scientific context.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch14-bayesianism]] — Bayesianism treats evidence as something that raises a subjective probability via Bayes's theorem, but Godfrey-Smith holds that a full theory of evidence also needs eliminative inference and a focus on the reliability of testing procedures.
- [[godfreysmith-2003-theory/ch15-empiricism]] — Godfrey-Smith argues that the three "isms" can be combined if empiricism is reformed inside a naturalistic picture, in which science is a strategy of exposing big ideas to experience, carried out by a socially organized "engine of self-correction".

## Big ideas across units
- **Empiricism: its rise, failure and reform.** Empiricism is introduced as one of three recurring answers, then developed in its logical form and its decline. It is finally reformed inside naturalism. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch10, ch15.
- **What makes something evidence?** Formal confirmation fails (ravens, grue). Popper tries to do without confirmation. Bayesianism and procedural naturalism offer later answers, and background assumptions and the reliability of procedures keep coming back. Units: ch03, ch04, ch14.
- **Testing is holistic.** Assumptions always sit between a theory and an observation. This undermines verifiability and clean falsification, and it feeds the debates over theory-ladenness and over frameworks. Units: ch02, ch03, ch04, ch07, ch10.
- **Demarcation and riskiness.** The question of what separates science from other inquiry starts as an open problem. Popper's falsifiability answers it, and the author reworks it as a matter of how ideas are handled. Units: ch01, ch04.
- **Frameworks, paradigms and revolutions.** Kuhn's normal science and revolutions lead to Lakatos's programs, Laudan's traditions and Feyerabend's anarchism. Explanation is later said to vary with context, following Kuhn. Units: ch05, ch06, ch07, ch13.
- **The social structure of science.** This is a central theme from the start. It runs through Merton, the strong program, Latour, feminist epistemology, Hull, Kitcher and Strevens, and the final "engine of self-correction". Units: ch01, ch08, ch09, ch11, ch15.
- **Realism about hidden structure.** The author thinks empiricism erred in refusing to let science describe hidden structure. He defends scientific realism against constructivism and constructive empiricism, and he reconciles it with empiricism. Units: ch02, ch06, ch08, ch12, ch15.
- **Naturalism as the author's method.** Philosophy may use scientific results to answer philosophical questions. This shapes the treatment of observation, group-level science and the final synthesis. Units: ch10, ch11, ch14, ch15.

## How to read it
- **Essential (from the material).** Ch01 frames the questions and the three answers. Ch02 and ch03 give the logical-empiricist background and the confirmation puzzles. Ch04 gives Popper. Ch05 and ch06 give Kuhn. Ch12 gives realism and ch15 gives the synthesis. These match the catalog's focus (chs. 2–7, 11–14), apart from ch15.
- **Dependencies.**
  - Ch04 recalls ch2 (holism) and ch3 (induction).
  - Ch05 recalls Popper, and ch06 builds on ch05.
  - Ch07 needs Kuhn and Popper, plus Quine and Carnap from earlier chapters.
  - Ch08 needs Kuhn and logical positivism, and ch09 needs chs. 7–8.
  - Ch10 draws on the logical empiricists, the Popper–Kuhn–Feyerabend debates and holism.
  - Ch11 recalls Kuhn (ch06) and Merton (ch08).
  - Ch12 draws on ch06 and ch08.
  - Ch13 draws on ch12 and Kuhn.
  - Ch14 needs the ravens and grue puzzles from ch03.
  - Ch15 gathers earlier themes: underdetermination, Kuhn, theory-ladenness and realism.
- **Candidates to skim on a first pass (my judgement, not stated in the digests).** These are ch09 (feminism and Science Studies, outside the catalog's focus) and the optional Humean section at the end of ch13. Ch08 and ch10 are also outside the catalog focus, but ch11 and ch15 draw on them.
