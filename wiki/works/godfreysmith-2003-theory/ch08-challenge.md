# The Challenge from Sociology of Science

## L0 — One line
Late-twentieth-century sociology of science moved from describing science's social structure (Merton) to explaining scientific belief by social factors (strong program, Latour), and Godfrey-Smith accepts the social insight but rejects the slide to "facts are made, not found."

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can sociology replace philosophy of science? Godfrey-Smith traces the field. Merton's "old" sociology described science's norms and reward system. The strong program (Barnes, Bloor) then claimed that scientific belief needs the same kind of explanation as any other belief, and it tended toward relativism. Shapin and Schaffer, and Latour and Woolgar, added "manufactured" or "constructed" facts. Godfrey-Smith values the questions they raise, such as how appeals to "nature" work in disputes. But he finds false dichotomies and a missing distinction between how people settle on what is real and what is real. The resulting picture seems to leave out science's responsiveness to the world.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's paradigms and incommensurability (earlier chapters); logical positivism; the idea of a causal versus other explanation; Wittgenstein only as sketched here.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Merton's "old" sociology described science's norms (universalism, communism, disinterestedness, organized skepticism: basic values said to govern scientific communities) and a reward system based on recognition for priority (pp. 122–123).
- Godfrey-Smith criticises Merton: disinterestedness seems to vanish once rewards are analysed, and Kuhn suggests organized skepticism needs a balance with trust (pp. 124–125). He still likes Merton's pattern of explaining social structure from individual incentives (p. 125).
- The strong program's symmetry principle says beliefs we think true and false get the same kind of explanation. Science is one local community among others (p. 126).
- The strong program's "interests" explanations were unclear about what kind of link was claimed. The program tends toward relativism (no single set of standards is entitled to govern justification) and faced the problem of reflexivity (what about the field's own theories, if all beliefs get the same kind of social explanation?) (pp. 127–128).
- Shapin and Schaffer present Boyle as building a new language game (a pattern of linguistic habits that makes sense within a way of life) and a new form of life (a set of basic practices, behaviours and values), both ideas borrowed from Wittgenstein. Godfrey-Smith faults their "manufacture of facts" talk and their claim that "ourselves and not reality" are responsible for knowledge (pp. 130–132).
- Latour and Woolgar studied a lab as an input–output "machine" and treated facts as built by hiding human work. Latour's "actor-network" approach (study the fine structure of dispute and negotiation in science) treats "society" and "nature" as products of settled controversies (pp. 132–133).
- Godfrey-Smith values Latour's questions about how "truth" and "nature" are used in disputes. He objects that Latour refuses to separate how people decide what is real from what is real (p. 134).
- Overall worry: the sociologists' picture seems to leave no place for belief responding to the world's real structure (pp. 134–135).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 8.1 Beyond Philosophy?
Ideas about science were in flux late in the twentieth century, including in fields bordering philosophy (p. 122). Sociology of science sometimes set itself up as a "successor discipline" to philosophy of science (Bloor 1983) (p. 122).

### 8.2 Robert Merton and the "Old" Sociology of Science
- Merton founded the field in the mid-twentieth century. His work is "basically mainstream sociology applied to" science's structure and history (p. 122).
- **Four norms** (1940s): *universalism* (a person's attributes and background are irrelevant to the value of their ideas); *communism* (common ownership of ideas and results); *disinterestedness* (scientists act for the common enterprise, not personal gain); *organized skepticism* (community-wide challenging and testing instead of trust). Humility is sometimes added but matters less (pp. 122–123).
- **Reward system** (paper first presented 1957): the basic currency is recognition, especially for being first. Merton claimed this is the only property right recognised in science, since published ideas become common property. The best case is having an idea named after you, e.g. Darwinism, Planck's constant, Boyle's law (p. 123).
- **Evidence for it:** priority disputes are common and often acrimonious: Galileo; Newton against Hooke and against Leibniz; Gallo against Montagnier over HIV. Darwin and Wallace are the famous exception. The moral fervour, even from onlookers, suggests a community standard (p. 123).
- **Function and deviance:** the system mostly encourages original thinking, but it can misfire. Merton held that fraud is very rare, plagiarism somewhat less rare, and libel and slander very common. Fraud is rare because of internal policing, from ambition and from organized skepticism (pp. 123–124). The typical slander is the accusation of plagiarism, much more common than actual theft (p. 124).
- **Scarcity of recognition:** few scientists can win it, which Merton linked to milder deviance such as the mania to publish (p. 124).
- **Godfrey-Smith's critique (author's view):**
  - Merton's analysis is "not quite right" here. Standing in a small community of colleagues can motivate, as Kuhn's normal science recognised. University administrations seeking a productivity measure also play a role (p. 124).
  - He cannot see what remains of disinterestedness once the reward analysis is given. What we seem to have is "a special kind of ambition and self-interest" (p. 124).
  - Kuhn argued that too much willingness to revise basic beliefs makes chaos. Science, Kuhn claimed, needs a delicate balance between skepticism and trust. Godfrey-Smith sees possible problems for organized skepticism here, though there is definitely something right in the idea (pp. 124–125).
- Storer's image: norms are the "motor," the reward system is the "electricity." Godfrey-Smith finds the relation between the two unclear, and notes that Merton himself noted that rewards can be in tension with norms (p. 124).
- **Keeper:** Merton gives a good pattern. Describe individual rewards and incentives, then show how they generate higher-level social features of science. The book returns to this in chapter 11 (p. 125).

*Intuition note:* Merton asks what makes scientists behave as they do and answers with incentives, mainly the wish to be first. Godfrey-Smith keeps the method but doubts the "disinterested" label.

### 8.3 The Rise of the Strong Program
- **Old versus new (a standard contrast, with "some truth"):** the older work described science's social structure and did not explain particular beliefs sociologically. The newer work tries to explain why scientists believe and act as they do and how thinking changes (p. 125). Godfrey-Smith adds that the newer work was also very interested in general norms, especially norms of reasonableness, and that at least some of Merton's work assumed a view of theories close to logical empiricism (networks of predictive generalizations) (p. 125).
- The newer work also embraced an "anti-positivist package": Kuhn, holism about testing, incommensurability, new ideas about observation, and speculative views of scientific language. Many historians and feminist critics accepted the same package (p. 125). Some sociologists thought sociology would replace a "dried up" philosophy of science, and the logical positivists became the "bad guys" (p. 125).
- **Strong program:** developed in 1970s Edinburgh, led "to some extent" by Barnes and Bloor (p. 126).
  - *Symmetry principle:* all belief and behaviour get the same kinds of explanation. We should not explain beliefs we think true differently from those we think false. Our own assessment should not affect the explanation (p. 126).
  - Applied to science: scientists are not pure thinkers attending only to data and logic. Scientific beliefs come from the same general forces as other beliefs. Every community has local, often unstated, norms for supporting claims and handling disagreement. Scientific disdain for other communities' beliefs is itself a local norm (p. 126).
  - Example: the belief that genes are made of DNA and a tribal belief that drought is due to a deity's ill will are both set by local norms of argument and justification. The norms differ but the general principles are the same. The Real World should not get a special role in explaining scientific belief that it lacks in other cases (p. 126).
- **"Interests" explanations:** the strong program also tried to explain some scientific beliefs by scientists' political interests and place in society (p. 126).
  - Example: MacKenzie (1981) linked key ideas in modern statistics to nineteenth-century English thinking about human evolution and eugenics. He argued these ideas matched the interests of the ambitious, reformist English middle classes (p. 127).
  - Sociologists say no simple determination is meant and sometimes use "reflect." Godfrey-Smith asks whether the benefit to a group is meant to *explain* an idea's popularity, and if so whether causally. He calls this a source of "obscurity," though some explanation is intended and causal analysis in social systems is hard (p. 127).
  - Kuhn was critical of this work. *Structure* analysed "internal" politics (textbooks, problem priorities) and treated insulation from external politics as a strength (p. 127).
- **Relativism:** definitions vary. Here it concerns standards of rationality, evidence and justification: no single set of standards is entitled to govern justification, and applicability depends on one's situation (pp. 127–128). The strong program does tend to be relativist in this sense: it holds that science has no authority beyond local norms, and that its norms can be justified only from inside. According to the strong program, saying "Science really is the best way of learning about the world" merely expresses our local norms, and no one can stand outside all local norms and say that one system really adapts us best (p. 128).
- **Reflexivity:** if all beliefs have the same kind of social explanation and no norms are "really" superior, what about sociology's own theories? Sociologists mostly accepted that their theories are justified only by local norms. Godfrey-Smith: this is "OK" but led to "endless methodological obsessing and navel-gazing" (p. 128).
- The program was varied and was partly pushed aside in the 1980s (p. 128).

*Intuition note:* The symmetry principle says to explain the DNA belief and the drought belief with the same toolkit. Taken together with relativism, the strong program's view is that no appeal to a belief's being "really right" should enter the explanation.

### 8.4 Leviathan and Latour
**Shapin and Schaffer, *Leviathan and the Air Pump* (1985).**
- It is sociologically informed history. It does not advocate the strong program but is often seen as a sophisticated development of it (pp. 128–129).
- It concerns the rise of experimental science in seventeenth-century England, through a dispute between Boyle and Hobbes. It was not "science versus religion" but a battle over specific issues and the proper form of scientific work. Boyle prevailed (p. 129).
- Boyle's new way of bringing experience to bear on theory was institutionalised in the Royal Society (1660). It became an important model, especially in England, though national "styles" differed (p. 129).
- Boyle separated public, cooperative investigation of "matters of fact" from speculative causal hypotheses (to be cautious) and from theology and metaphysics (to be excluded). This marked out a controllable area for dispute, and Shapin and Schaffer say Boyle saw his group as a model for order in a society scarred by civil war (pp. 129–130).
- Boyle also set up new ways of talking, e.g. about "vacuum." Aristotle's physics said vacuums cannot exist, and Boyle's pump could apparently evacuate all or almost all the air from a glass container. Shapin and Schaffer argue he reconstrued questions so that they could be tackled with his apparatus. Critics complained the pump could not settle their questions. Questions such as whether a perfectly pure vacuum could exist would have produced endless dispute (p. 130).
- **Wittgenstein's later ideas** (author's sketch): an "anti-theory" holding that philosophical problems arise from pathologies of language. Two ideas are popular in sociology (p. 130–131):
  - *Form of life:* a set of basic practices, behaviours and values. Actions make sense within it, but it cannot be justified externally (p. 130). It is unclear what unit it is for Wittgenstein, and sociologists adapted it to the groups they study (p. 131).
  - *Language game:* a pattern of linguistic habits that contributes to a form of life. Wittgenstein opposed the view that words are attached to their own meanings (such as mental images) which determine use. Socially maintained patterns of use are all there is to meaning (p. 131).
  - Shapin and Schaffer: Boyle's treatment of "vacuum" set up a new language game within the new form of life of experimental science (p. 131).
- **Contrast with positivism:** positivists claimed a theory of meaning shows that meaningful language only describes patterns in experience. Shapin and Schaffer say Boyle set up a new way of using language. So there is perhaps more of a connection to Bridgman's operationalism, which urged reforming language so each term connects to empirical testing (p. 131).
- **Problem:** "manufacture of facts" is not meant as deception. They want us to accept that facts in general are "made rather than found," a view Godfrey-Smith finds reminiscent of Kuhn's claim (Structure, ch. X) that the world changes during a scientific revolution (p. 131). Godfrey-Smith: denying that the scientist is a passive receiver does not require this talk, which often leads to trouble. Their closing line, "It is ourselves and not reality that is responsible for what we know" (1985, 344), is a false dichotomy. Both are responsible, through an interaction. Even that is imperfect, since human knowledge is part of reality. We need both a theory of human thought, language and social interaction and a theory of how these connect to the outside world (pp. 131–132).

**Latour and Woolgar, *Laboratory Life* (1979).**
- Latour, a French sociologist, spent a couple of years at the Salk Institute's molecular biology lab, knowing little of the science. The lab's work earned a Nobel Prize (the structure of a growth-regulating hormone) (p. 132).
- The account ignored the state of hormone knowledge, how methods discriminate chemical structures, and the fit with biology. It treated the lab as a machine turning chemicals, animals and blank paper into printed articles (fig. 8.1) (p. 132–133).
- Latour saw this processing as aimed at building structures of "support" around claims until they are taken as facts. A key step is hiding the human work, so that a fact looks given by nature (p. 132).
- Effect: the book was a huge success. The strong program came to seem crude, because it may have replaced the picture of nature just stamping itself on the community's minds with an equally crude picture of social "interests" doing so. Godfrey-Smith calls this not a very fair reading, a caricature like the sociologists' caricature of philosophy (pp. 132–133).
- **Actor-network theory** (Latour): study the fine structure of dispute and negotiation. Do not assume social "interests" or "nature" at the start. Both are *products*, not causes, of settled controversies (Latour 1987). Empiricism saw science as "data-driven," the strong program as "interest-driven," Latour sees the work itself as the driver (p. 133).
- Latour: explanations of why one side won should never cite nature itself, since both sides claim the facts. The winner's version becomes immune to challenge, which is described as facts being created (pp. 133–134).
- **Godfrey-Smith's assessment:**
  - Good strategy: ask what social role appeals to "truth," "nature" and "the facts" play before, during and after disputes (p. 134).
  - But studying how people decide what is real does not show that settling disputes *determines* what is real. Latour refuses to make this distinction when presenting his work. To some extent this seems to Godfrey-Smith due to Latour's interest in unorthodox philosophical positions, but sometimes just to a dashing, provocative writing style (p. 134).
  - Bloor (1999) attacked Latour and urged a return to the strong program without careless talk of "construction of reality." Godfrey-Smith doubts the strong program is the way of the future, but agrees with Bloor that recognising social structure does not require "strange inversions" of thought and reality (p. 134).

**Closing picture.** Radical and cautious sociology alike tended to suggest that science is controlled entirely by collective choices and interests (negotiation, hierarchy, power). Godfrey-Smith says there seems to be no place for belief responding to the world's real structure. Sociologists often grant that the world imposes some constraints, but they treat observations as so open to reinterpretation and negotiation that they cannot *guide* belief or theory change (pp. 134–135).

*Intuition note:* One main complaint is not that science is social, which Godfrey-Smith accepts. It is that some authors (such as Shapin and Schaffer, and Latour) seem to move from the claim that social processes settle disputes to the claim that reality is made by those processes (pp. 131, 134).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Use the symmetry principle as a template with three slots: (1) which local norm of justification was used, (2) who counts as an authority, (3) how disagreement was handled. Fill it for two communities.
- Community A (lab, belief "genes are DNA"): norm = published experiments; authority = senior researchers; disagreement = replication and peer critique.
- Community B (village, belief "drought = deity's ill will"): norm = elders' interpretation of signs; authority = elders; disagreement = ritual consultation.
Both rows fill all 3 of 3 slots, and the rows never include "the belief is true" as a slot. That is symmetry: the same kind of explanation for both, whatever we think of the belief. Godfrey-Smith's worry (p. 134) is about where such sociology ends up: a picture with no place for belief responding to the world's real structure.

## Questions this raises
- Does the symmetry principle require ignoring the world completely, or only not using truth as an explanation? The chapter states the principle (p. 126) but does not discuss this question.
- What kind of explanation is "reflects the interests of"? Causal, functional or something else (p. 127)?
- How should one treat the reflexivity problem without endless navel-gazing (p. 128)?
- What account of knowledge combines social processes with responsiveness to the world, as proposed on p. 132?
