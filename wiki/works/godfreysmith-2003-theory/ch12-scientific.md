# Scientific Realism

## L0 — One line
Scientific realism is best stated as a claim about the aims of science (accurate representation of reality, including unobservable parts), not as a claim that our current theories are true, and the author argues against its main rivals.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how scientific theories connect to reality. Godfrey-Smith defines scientific realism as two claims: a "naturalized" common-sense realism, and the claim that an actual and reasonable aim of science is accurate representation, including of unobservables. This is neutral about how often science succeeds, so optimists and pessimists can both be realists. He then discusses three families of opposition: traditional empiricism, metaphysical constructivism (Kuhn, Latour, Goodman), and van Fraassen's constructive empiricism, which he attacks with a graded line from observable to detectable. He ends by arguing that models, not only true sentences, represent the world.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's paradigm-change claim (ch. 6) and Latour (ch. 8) as background; the idea of induction; the observable/unobservable contrast.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The author's "Common-sense Realism" says there is a common reality whose structure does not depend on what people think, except where thoughts and symbols are part of reality or causally affect it (p. 174).
- He "naturalizes" it (makes it responsive to science) by also allowing dependence of reality on thought in ways science might uncover, a move prompted by puzzles in quantum mechanics (pp. 175–176).
- His Scientific Realism has two clauses: naturalized common-sense realism, and an actual and reasonable aim of science to represent reality accurately, including unobservables (p. 176).
- The formulation does not endorse any current theory. It allows optimistic and pessimistic versions, but not extreme pessimism (pp. 175–177).
- The author finds the "success implies truth" argument (realism is the only view that does not make science's success a miracle) unimpressive as a defence of realism, since success can occur even when a theory is wrong about the things it posits (pp. 178–179). The pessimistic induction (past posits such as phlogiston turned out not to exist, so current ones may too) does not establish extreme pessimism (pp. 177–178).
- Metaphysical constructivism (Kuhn, Latour, Goodman) holds that the world is in some sense created by scientific theorizing. The author rejects it. He tentatively explains why such strange-sounding claims arise in sociology of science: opposition to a "Bad View", that reality stamps itself on a passive mind (pp. 181–183).
- Van Fraassen's constructive empiricism says science should aim only at empirical adequacy (accurately describing the observable parts of the world). The author argues against it: he sees no sharp boundary between observable, detectable (observable, or very reliably inferable from the observable) and inferable-with-moderate-reliability (pp. 184–186).
- Science uses different representational vehicles (sentences, models). A good model probably has an abstract similarity to its target system, so analyses based only on truth and reference may be too narrow (pp. 186–189).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 12.1 Strange Debates (p. 173)
- Paragraph 1 (realist): science describes our world, which contains electrons, elements and genes. The world of 1000 a.d. had them too, although nobody knew it.
- Paragraph 2 (anti-realist): "electron" is a product of a specific history. It would have meant nothing in 1000 a.d. So the existence of electrons depends on our conceptualization.
- For some people paragraph 1 is obvious. For others paragraph 2 shows it is a naive mistake. The author says this argument has run for fifty years and reaches deep into philosophy (p. 173).
- Link back: Kuhn's "the world changes" (ch. 6) and Latour's view that nature is the "product" of the settlement of scientific controversy (ch. 8). The author criticised these and now gives a fuller account (p. 173).

**Intuition note:** The puzzle is whether "electrons existed in 1000 a.d." is a claim about the world or about our concepts. The chapter gives a more detailed account of how theory and reality are connected.

### 12.2 Approaching Scientific Realism (p. 174)
- The author defends a version of scientific realism: science aims at describing the real structure of the world. Whether it succeeds is "more complicated" (p. 174).
- "Realism" is used in many ways. The usual starting point is reality's independence from thought and language (Devitt 1997). This needs care. Thoughts and words are real parts of the world and have causal roles, as in every bridge or light bulb (p. 174).
- **Common-sense Realism:** We all inhabit a common reality with a structure that exists independently of what people think and say about it, except insofar as reality is comprised of, or is causally affected by, thoughts, theories, and other symbols (p. 174).
- Realists accept that we have different views and perspectives, but we live in the same world (p. 174).

### 12.3 A Statement of Scientific Realism (pp. 174–179)
- **Rejected option (Devitt's way):** state realism as the claim that the world really is the way our best-established theories describe it. The author sides with van Fraassen. On that formulation, realism would be false if current theories turned out false (p. 175).
- **Devitt's view:** no worry, provided we avoid realism about speculative frontier ideas. **Others:** this ignores the historical record. The author does not settle this. He wants a claim about science as a whole, compatible with various attitudes to current theories (p. 175).
- **Problem:** must scientific realists also be common-sense realists? Quantum mechanics, where measurement partly determines a system's state, is the vivid case. Some interpretations make trouble for common-sense realism. The author hopes (quietly) they are mistaken, but that is not the point (p. 175).
  - If scientific realism assumes common-sense realism, we hold an unreflective picture whatever science says.
  - If we sever them, a general claim about science describing the real world becomes hard to state (p. 175).
- **Solution:** widen the allowed thought–world relations beyond causal ones. This is a naturalistic modification (p. 176).
- **Common-sense Realism Naturalized:** as before, but also "except insofar as reality is dependent on thoughts, theories, and other symbols in ways that might be uncovered by science" (p. 176).
- **Scientific Realism** (p. 176):
  1. Common-sense realism naturalized.
  2. One actual and reasonable aim of science is to give us accurate descriptions (and other representations) of what reality is like, including accurate representations of unobservable aspects.
- Comments (p. 176):
  - "One aim" does not mean the only aim. Some theories or programs may serve other purposes.
  - "Actual" means at least a good proportion of real scientific work has this goal. "Reasonable" means scientists are not deluded or irrational in making this their goal, and can reasonably hope to succeed at least some of the time.
  - Nothing here endorses any current theory or says how often science succeeds.
- **Optimistic vs pessimistic realists** (p. 177). The optimist is confident that science is uncovering the basic structure of the world. The pessimist thinks it is very hard for us to reach the right theories, that evidence often misleads, and that we grow confident too quickly.
- **Limit:** if it were just about impossible to reach the right theories, it is hard to see how trying could be a reasonable aim. So extreme pessimism is not compatible with this realism. Popper gets close to the limit without reaching it (p. 177).
- **Kuhn:** besides the "changing worlds" claims, he at times seems a pessimistic realist. On the author's reading, Kuhn seems to think the world is so complicated that our theories always run into trouble in the end, independently of paradigms: nature resists being forced into "boxes" and all paradigms are doomed to fail eventually. The author finds this view more coherent and interesting (p. 177).
- **Pessimistic induction from the history of science** (p. 177). Fundamental ideas, especially in physics, have changed often, so we should expect current views to be wrong. The author prefers this name to "pessimistic meta-induction", since it is an induction about explanatory inferences, not about inductions. Pessimists list dead entities like phlogiston and caloric (Laudan 1981). Optimists reply with entities once doubtful and now accepted, such as atoms, germs and genes (pp. 177–178).
- These debates threaten this realism only if they threaten to establish extreme pessimism; the author says they do not support it (p. 178). The right confidence is not settled by track record alone. Methods of hypothesizing and testing may have improved, but history gives interesting data (p. 178).
- **Differences between fields** (p. 178):
  - McMullin (1984): basic physics should not be the model for all of science. Its entities are the most inaccessible, and it has powerful mathematical formalisms that are hard to interpret.
  - McMullin and Worrall (1989): confidence in basic physics should be confidence that low-level structural features are captured by models and equations. This is a special kind of confidence.
  - Molecular biology: entities are far from the lowest levels, we have varied access to them, formalisms are not hard to interpret, and history suggests steady accumulation of knowledge. So the right attitude differs from that toward theoretical physics.
- **Success-to-truth ("no miracles") argument** (Smart 1968; Putnam 1978): some realists claim that realism is the only philosophy of science that does not make science's success a miracle. The author finds it unimpressive as a defence (pp. 178–179).
  - The world does affect success and failure of theories.
  - But success, especially short or medium term, can come in other ways: errors that compensate, or wrong posits with the right structure in crucial places.
  - Laudan's example: Carnot thought heat was a fluid, yet worked out basic thermodynamics accurately. Fluid flow resembled the transfer of kinetic energy between molecules closely enough that the mistake did not matter much.
  - Conclusion: success does not point directly or unambiguously to truth (p. 179).
- Realism so stated is a "fairly definite yes-or-no choice" (see 12.7). The level of optimism has no simple answer. It varies by field, kind of theory, kind of success and kind of optimism. Simple slogans should not be trusted (p. 179).
- "Accurate representation" is broad. Truth applies easily only to language. Science also uses mathematical and other models and diagrams. So the term includes true descriptions, pictures that resemble reality as intended, and models with the right structural similarity (p. 179).

**Intuition note:** The author makes realism a thesis about what science aims at and may reasonably hope for. Then historical failures threaten it only if they threaten to establish that success is just about impossible. How confident to be in a given theory is a separate, field-by-field question.

### 12.4 Challenges from Traditional Empiricism (pp. 180–181)
- Survey (p. 180). Mostly opposed: logical positivism, Kuhn (vague, mostly opposed), many sociologists including Latour, Goodman, van Fraassen, Laudan. Feyerabend is hard to assess. Popper is in favour. Many naturalists (Fodor, Hull, Kitcher) are realists, but not all.
- Opposition falls into three families. The first is traditional empiricism. The author says he defends both scientific realism and a kind of empiricism, though the alliance is not always easy (p. 180).
- Traditional empiricists often worry for reasons to do with knowledge. If senses are our only source of factual knowledge, how could we know a world beyond sensation? It seems strange, though not absurd, to say a real world exists but cannot be known at all (p. 180).
- **Logical positivists:** questions about the "reality of the external world" are meaningless, so the debate is a waste of time. Some were also **phenomenalists**: all meaningful sentences translate into sentences about sensations. Then claims about external objects only describe sensation patterns (pp. 180–181).
- More holistic empiricist views of language have a similar result even without translation. Language and thought cannot "reach" beyond the senses. The author believes much twentieth-century empiricism held a version of this, though some commentators objected (p. 181).
- Recently the tension is debated as **underdetermination**: empiricists argue that there will always be alternative theories compatible with all actual, maybe all possible, evidence, so we lack empirical grounds to choose one. The author's formulation tolerates much pessimism, but underdetermination matters in its own right (see sections 15.2 and 15.3) (p. 181).

### 12.5 Metaphysical Constructivism (pp. 181–183)
- **Definition:** views (Kuhn, Latour, Goodman) holding that the world is in some sense created by scientific theorizing. Kuhn: the world changes with paradigms. Latour: nature is the product of settled controversies. Goodman (1978): new languages and theories create new "worlds". On these views, a theory cannot describe the world as it is independent of thought (p. 181).
- Literal reading looks strange. Might they be metaphors? Kuhn sometimes expressed skeptical realism. But Goodman and others, when asked, insist the claims are not metaphorical (Goodman 1996, 145) (pp. 181–182).
- **Kantian background** (p. 182):
  - The noumenal world is the world as it is in itself. We must believe in it but can know nothing about it.
  - The phenomenal world is the world as it appears. It is knowable but partly our creation.
  - Hoyningen-Huene reads Kuhn as Kantian. **Devitt's analysis:** constructivist antirealism combines the Kantian picture with relativism, in which different communities create different phenomenal worlds. This relativism is not Kant's, since for Kant all humans share one conceptual framework.
  - **Author's verdict:** adding a "phenomenal world" between us and reality achieves nothing.
- "Social constructivism" is also used for moderate claims, that we construct theories and classifications. These do not oppose scientific realism, because a realist insists that beyond ideas there is also the rest of reality (p. 182).
- **Author's "meta-sociology"**, offered tentatively as one explanation among various reasons (p. 183). Work in sociology of science is often organized around opposing a "Bad View": reality stamps itself on a passive mind with "unmediated compulsory force" (Shapin 1982). This view is seen as false and politically harmful. Positivism is described as reactionary for it. The reaction is a simple reversal, "theories construct reality". Woolgar embraces an "inversion". Bloor 1999 is an exception. Moderates are criticised from within the field. Result: one error is exchanged for another (p. 183).

**Intuition note:** The author does not deny that we construct ideas. He denies that constructing ideas constructs the rest of reality. He tentatively suggests one explanation of the stronger claim: an over-reaction to an unattractive view of passive minds.

### 12.6 Van Fraassen's View (pp. 183–186)
- Van Fraassen (1980) is a moderate and careful opponent. He is empiricist but uses no linguistic or psychological theory. He denies realism's claim about the aims of science, which is why the author's formulation was influenced by his (p. 183).
- **Instrumentalism** (in the sense the author thinks more appropriate): theories are devices for dealing with experience. Do not ask whether electrons "really, really exist". If a theory predicts well, what more is needed? Some scientists, especially in physics, express this view. It is linked to one strand of pragmatism (Rorty 1982) (pp. 183–184).
- **Constructive empiricism:** van Fraassen's own name. The author reserves "constructive" for section 12.5 views, and counts van Fraassen's view as a version of instrumentalism, though names matter little (p. 184).
- **Empirically adequate** theories accurately describe the observable parts of the world. To **accept** a theory is (1) to believe provisionally that it is empirically adequate and (2) to use its concepts in further work. Point 1 covers all observable phenomena in its domain, including uninvestigated ones, so induction problems arise. Point 2 recognizes that scientists "live inside" theories while staying agnostic about truth (p. 184).
- **Do the views conflict?** Van Fraassen: "science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate" (1980, 12). Taken alone this is compatible with the author's "one aim" claim. A realist can agree that sometimes science aims only at empirical adequacy and that caution about unobservables can be reasonable. The conflict arises because van Fraassen holds that science should aim at no more than empirical adequacy (p. 185).
- **Objection 1: observability is a continuum** (Maxwell 1962). Trees are observed. Smallest particles are only inferred. In between: telescope, light microscope, X-ray, MRI, electron microscope. The realist says the line is vague and not suited to support general conclusions about science's aims (p. 185).
- Van Fraassen accepts that the line is vague, that the unobservable is not "unreal", and that we learn the boundary from science. He still says science concerns only empirical adequacy. The author says this cannot be defended (p. 185).
- **Objection 2: detectable.** Something is detectable if observable, or if its presence can be very reliably inferred from what is observable. Science itself says what is detectable. Example: chemical structures of sugars and DNA are detectable but not observable. Why should science not aim to represent them? Beliefs about detectable structures may be less reliable, so more caution is needed, which is no problem (pp. 185–186).
- Extend again: include things inferable from observation with moderate reliability. Again more care, no obstacle in principle (p. 186).
- **Conclusion:** there is no boundary between what science can reasonably aim to tell us and what it cannot. As we learn, we learn which parts we can get reliable information about. Science may try to describe everything we can hope to gain reliable information about. Sometimes, especially in theoretical physics, van Fraassen's attitude may be adopted, at least temporarily. But empirical adequacy is not the aim of science (p. 186).

**Intuition note:** The author argues that there is no boundary between what science can reasonably aim to tell us about and what it cannot. The lines he considers (observable, detectable, moderately inferable) mark degrees of reliability, which call for more or less caution, not a ban on aiming.

### 12.7 Representation, Models, and Truth (Optional Section) (pp. 186–189)
- **Background:** twentieth-century philosophy mostly treated theories as sets of sentences and used truth and reference. A theory is good if true. Electrons exist if "electron" refers (pp. 186–187). The author thinks this emphasis may be a mistake (p. 187).
- **Vehicles:**
  - Science uses sentences, but also models. Mathematical models are abstract structures meant to represent key features of real systems.
  - Truth, falsity and reference do not seem useful for how they represent. A good model has an abstract similarity to its target system (Giere 1988). The details are hard to work out (p. 187).
  - The role of models became an important topic (Suppe 1977), and some argued for using models to redescribe all theories. The author says that is a mistake, since science has a range of vehicles and we should not replace a language-based analysis with a model-based one (p. 187).
- **Darwin vs. recent work:** Darwin's *Origin* states hypotheses in ordinary language. Recent treatments of how selection changes populations are mathematical models. They are written down but need commentary saying which real phenomena they represent. Such models need not be analysed with the same concepts as ordinary-language hypotheses (p. 187).
- A model is a structure meant to represent another structure by abstract similarity. Sometimes the aim is to model the unfamiliar on the familiar (Bohr's early "solar system" atom), but not always. A mathematical model treats variables as functions of others, and so represents a network of dependence. Commentary then treats this as representing the dependence structure of a real system (pp. 187–188).
- **Flexibility** (p. 188): different users may read the same model differently. One uses it purely to predict. Another reads it as a detailed picture of the system's dependence structure. A third takes it to represent only some features.
- **Progress** (p. 188): old theories may look like failures when judged by truth and reference. Recast as models, they may have had some of the right structure by current lights. Worrall (1989): nineteenth-century ether theories had good structural features although the ether does not exist.
- **Truth itself** (pp. 188–189). Option one: calling a theory true asserts a special, valuable connection to the world, traditionally "correspondence". That term misleads because it suggests "picturing", which modern theories of truth do not propose. Option two (Horwich 1990; Bloor 1999 in sociology of science): "true" signals agreement or disagreement with others and does not describe connections between language and world.
- The author has been cautious about truth and used the broad "accurate representation". Some go further and deny that representation is a genuine relation between symbols and world. This is radical (a claim of postmodernists, for example). It is hard to say which theories of symbols keep the familiar idea of representation (p. 189).

**Intuition note:** For models, asking whether they are true and whether their terms refer may not be the useful analysis; the author suggests asking about similarity or structure instead (writer's gloss). On his account, an old theory recast as a model may turn out to have had some of the right structure.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show the author's graded boundary against a sharp one. The reliability numbers are invented for illustration.

Five items and how reliably we can infer their presence from observation:
- A tree (seen directly): 1.00
- A bacterium (light microscope): 0.97
- DNA's chemical structure (indirect): 0.90
- A subatomic particle (long inference): 0.70
- A speculative entity (weak inference): 0.30

Van Fraassen-style cut: "observable" means reliability 1.00. Aim at accuracy about 1 item (the tree).
Detectable cut (very reliable, say ≥ 0.90): 3 items (tree, bacterium, DNA).
Moderate cut (≥ 0.70): 4 items.
Each cut grows the target set by one or two items, and nothing in the numbers marks 1.00 as special. The realist (pp. 185–186) says the right response is graded caution, not a ban: be more careful at 0.70 than at 0.97, but still aim at accuracy.

## Questions this raises
- If success does not point unambiguously to truth, what evidence does support belief in unobservable entities?
- When is a model's "right structure" enough to count as accurate representation, and who judges the similarity?
- Does the author's "yes-or-no" definition of realism hide disagreements about confidence that matter in practice?
- Is the "detectable" notion safe from circularity, given that science itself says what is detectable?
