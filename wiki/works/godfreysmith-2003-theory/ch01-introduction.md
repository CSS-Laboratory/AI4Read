# Introduction (Godfrey-Smith, *Theory and Reality*, ch. 1)

## L0 — One line
Before surveying a century of theories of science, the author sets out the scope of "science", the kinds of theory one can give of it, and three recurring answers (empiricism, mathematics, social structure) that are probably pieces of one answer.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This chapter sets up a survey of about a hundred years of argument about what science is, how it works, and what makes it different from other inquiry. It explains why the boundary of "science" cannot be fixed in advance, separates epistemological from metaphysical and descriptive from normative questions, and introduces three recurring answers: empiricism, mathematics, and social structure. Godfrey-Smith treats them as possible pieces of one answer, with empiricism and social structure most important. A history sketch of the Scientific Revolution (c. 1550–1700) supplies the people and ideas used later. The chapter matters because it frames the questions and the author's own stance for the whole book.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond general school history; the terms epistemology, metaphysics, descriptive/normative are defined below.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The book is a roughly chronological "grand tour" of about a century of argument about science. The author will take sides and, late in the book, start trying to assemble a positive picture (pp. 1, 7).
- What counts as "science" cannot be settled in advance. Physics and molecular biology are clear cases, basketball is not, and anthropology and archaeology are gray. The word is also rhetorically loaded (pp. 2–4).
- Whether "science" is read broadly or narrowly, the author wants two things: a general account of how humans gain knowledge, and an account of what sets the Scientific Revolution's descendants apart (pp. 4–5).
- Most issues in the book are epistemological (about knowledge, evidence and rationality) rather than metaphysical (about the nature of reality). A good habit is to ask constantly whether a claim about science is descriptive (says what is the case), normative (says what should be the case), or both (pp. 5–6).
- "Objective" is ambiguous (unbiased vs. mind-independent), so the author often avoids it. A single "scientific method" recipe is doubted. A logical theory of science (theories as abstract structures of sentences) is judged too abstract (pp. 6–7).
- Three answers recur: empiricism (experience is the only source of real knowledge about the world), mathematics, social structure. They may be rivals, but are more likely pieces of one answer (pp. 8, 13).
- Empiricism may look like an empty platitude, but the history of medicine suggests otherwise. The Snow and Semmelweiss cases show the attractiveness of simple empiricism, the Pettenkofer case shows that a direct test is no guarantee of success, and critics call it too simple a picture of thought (pp. 8–10).
- Not all great science makes much use of mathematics (Darwin). Social structure (trust, cooperation, credit) may be what makes science distinctive (pp. 11–13).
- A sketch of the Scientific Revolution (c. 1550–1700) gives the landmarks used later: Scholastic worldview (Christianity plus Aristotle), Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, mechanism (the world as tiny particles interacting by contact), Newton (pp. 13–18).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1.1 Setting Out (pp. 1–2)
- The book surveys roughly a hundred years of argument about what science is, how it works, and what distinguishes it from other ways of investigating the world. It uses philosophy, and also ideas from historians, sociologists and psychologists (p. 1).
- Ideas are presented in roughly historical order, with exceptions flagged. Reasons given: the history of ideas about science is interesting itself. Also, the field has recently been in a state of "fermentation and uncertainty", and tracing the path helps one understand the options (p. 1).
- The author will "take sides", marking probable wrong turns and ideas on the right track. Toward the end he will try to put the pieces together into a picture of how science works (p. 1).
- The debates matter outside philosophy: they affected other academic fields, education, medicine and the place of science in society (p. 2).
- Late-twentieth-century "Science Wars": some thought history, philosophy and sociology of science showed that science does not deserve its dominating role in Western cultures, and that myths about its trustworthiness and superiority were undermined. Others disagreed (p. 2).
- The wars have cooled, but disagreement on the nature and status of scientific knowledge remains. It usually does not influence day-to-day science, but sometimes does (p. 2).

### 1.2 The Scope of the Theory (pp. 2–5)
- To explain science we must know what we are explaining, but this cannot be settled in advance, because disagreements about what counts as science connect to all the other issues in the book (p. 2).
- **Consensus cases:** physics, and molecular biology (probably the most rapidly developing science of the last fifty years or so). Even these have hints of controversy: some say theoretical physics is becoming an esoteric model-building exercise with little contact with the real world (Horgan 1996), and molecular biology's business ties make it less exemplary for some (pp. 2–3).
- Testing hypotheses as physicists and molecular biologists do is science. Playing basketball is not. Between these, "disagreement reigns" (p. 3).
- Economics and psychology were once controversial but have settled into scientific status, at least in the United States and similar countries. Anthropology and archaeology are still in the debated border region. At Stanford, the anthropology department split partly over whether anthropology is fully scientific or "interpretive", closer to the humanities (p. 3).
- The gray area is unsurprising, because "science" is a loaded, rhetorically powerful word. Some people call a field "scientific" to suggest rigor and trustworthy results. Less commonly, someone may call an investigation scientific to say something negative (e.g. that it is dehumanizing). "Scientistic" is more often used when a negative impression is wanted (p. 3).
- **History of the word.** "Scientia" referred to results of logical demonstrations revealing general and necessary truths, closer to mathematics and geometry. Around the seventeenth century, what we now call science was more usually called "natural philosophy" or "natural history". Later "science" came to mean work linked to observation and experiment, and the tie to conclusive proof receded. The current senses of "science" and "scientist" are nineteenth-century (pp. 3–4).
- So no agreed list of what is included can be given in chapter 1. "For now we will have to let the gray area remain gray" (p. 4).
- **Broad vs. narrow views** (p. 4):
  - Broad: "science" is any work that assesses ideas and solves problems guided by observational evidence. It is found in all human cultures, though the word is Western.
  - Narrow: science is a cultural phenomenon localized in space and time. Only the sixteenth/seventeenth-century Scientific Revolution in Europe gave science in the full sense. Earlier we find only roots, in ancient Greece, the Arab world and the Scholastic tradition. Key figures include Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Descartes, Boyle and Newton.
  - The narrow view need not call non-scientific cultures ignorant or stupid. It distinguishes science from the investigation that goes with farming, architecture and technology, and asks how one approach developed by a few Europeans had such large consequences (p. 4).
- Whatever the use of the word, the author wants to develop both (p. 5):
  1. a general understanding of how humans gain knowledge of the world;
  2. an understanding of what makes work descended from the Scientific Revolution different from other investigation.
  The book moves back and forth between these two questions (p. 5).
- **Caution:** one of the hazards of philosophy is the temptation to produce theories too broad and sweeping. We should ask how confident we can be that all work called "science", even in the narrow sense, has much in common (p. 5).

*Intuition note.* The author does not start from a fixed definition of science, because the definition is itself part of the dispute. He accepts clear cases at the centre and lets the edges stay blurry for now.

### 1.3 What Kind of Theory? (pp. 5–8)
- **Epistemology** = questions about knowledge, evidence and rationality. **Metaphysics** = general questions about the nature of reality, "a more controversial part of philosophy". Philosophy of science overlaps with both. Most issues in this book are epistemological (e.g. how observational evidence can justify a theory; whether science can describe the world "as it really is"). Some metaphysical and philosophy-of-language issues also appear (p. 5).
- **Possible forms of a theory of science** (pp. 5–6):
  - an understanding of scientific thinking;
  - a *logical theory* of science: the abstract structure of theories and the relations between theory and evidence (favoured by many twentieth-century philosophers);
  - a *methodology*: rules or procedures scientists do or should follow;
  - a general theory of *scientific change* (favoured by recent philosophers influenced by history).
- **Descriptive vs. normative.** A descriptive theory says what actually goes on, without value judgments. A normative theory makes value judgments about what should go on. Some theories of science are meant as descriptive only, but most in this book have a normative element, officially or unofficially. Good habit: ask "Is this claim intended to be descriptive or normative, or both?" (p. 6).
- **"Objective"** is slippery (p. 6). Sense 1: absence of bias, impartiality. Sense 2: existence independent of our minds, as in asking whether there is a reality that exists regardless of how people conceptualize it and whether theories can describe it. Because of this, the author often avoids the word, addresses the questions in other language, and returns to "objectivity" in the final chapter (p. 6).
- **"Scientific method."** Bacon and Descartes tried to specify how scientists should proceed. In the twentieth century many became skeptical of a recipe: science is too creative and unpredictable, especially for Newton, Darwin and Einstein. Textbooks seem recently to have become more cautious about including a section on "the scientific method" (pp. 6–7).
- **Logical structure.** The philosopher treats a theory as an abstract structure, something like a set of interrelated sentences, and describes logical relations among its sentences, between theory and observational evidence, and between theories in related fields. These philosophers favour mathematical logic and rigor (p. 7).
- Historians and sociologists were often frustrated, suspecting the philosophers wanted to avoid contact with real science, perhaps to protect myths of perfect rationality or to play games with imaginary theories in artificial languages. Early chapters cover this work. The author argues it was often interesting, but his sympathy lies with those who want more contact with actual scientific work (p. 7).
- **The author's own direction** (a gradual development): describe the *scientific strategy* for investigating the world, and then the sort of connection to the world we are likely to achieve by following it. Initially this may "sound vague or impossible", but he hopes to show it makes good sense (p. 7).
- **Neighbouring fields.** Some people in history, sociology and psychology expected philosophy of science to be replaced by fields like sociology. That did not happen. They kept doing philosophy themselves, running into questions about truth, justification and the connection between theories and reality (pp. 7–8).
- The book's viewpoint is that philosophy of science benefits from input from other fields. The argument for this is postponed to chapter 10 (p. 8).

*Intuition note.* A claim about science can be a report ("scientists do this") or an instruction ("scientists should do this"). The author's advice is to keep asking, for each claim about science, which of the two it is meant to be.

### 1.4 Three Answers, or Pieces of an Answer (pp. 8–13)
- Three ideas recur. They can be read as rivals, as alternative starting points, or as pieces of a single more complicated answer (p. 8).

**Empiricism (pp. 8–10)**
- Slogan: "The only source of real knowledge about the world is experience." It is a view about all knowledge, not just scientific knowledge (p. 8).
- The empiricist tradition tends to see differences between science and everyday thinking as differences of detail and degree. It generally (not always) construes science broadly, starts from a general theory of thought, and is pro-science (p. 8).
- **Empiricism and Science:** scientific and everyday thinking have the same basic pattern. In each, experience is the only source of real knowledge about the world. Science is especially successful because it is organized, systematic and especially responsive to experience (p. 8).
- Consequence: "the scientific method", insofar as it exists, is found in everyday contexts too. On this view, the Scientific Revolution found no fundamentally new approach; instead a few brave and brilliant people freed Europe from darkness and dogmatism so that culture could "come to its senses" (p. 9).
- **Is it trivial?** Some readers may think the principles are empty platitudes ("what else could be [the source]?"). The author suggests the history of medicine says otherwise (p. 9):
  - *Semmelweiss* (cited via Hempel 1966): mid-nineteenth-century Vienna hospital; simple empirical tests showed that if doctors washed their hands before delivering babies, infection risk in mothers fell hugely. He was opposed and eventually driven out (p. 9).
  - *John Snow and cholera* (author describes in detail). Cholera spreads via drinking water, in poor, crowded places without sanitation. Rival theories blamed miasmas (foul gases from ground and swamps). This was before bacteria were known to cause disease. Snow hypothesized that water spread cholera, mapped the 1854 London epidemic, and found it seemed centred on a public pump in Broad Street. With great difficulty he got the handle removed, and the outbreak "immediately went away". It was central to the rise of the emphasis on clean water and sanitation (pp. 9–10).
- **Caution: Pettenkofer.** Koch and Pasteur developed the germ theory, and Koch isolated the cholera bacteria early. Pettenkofer, unconvinced, drank water mixed with the alleged germs, suffered no ill effects, and wrote to Koch that he had disproved the theory. Possible explanations: high stomach acid, or dead germs in the sample. Pettenkofer was lucky, and Koch was right. The lesson: direct empirical tests are no guarantee of success (p. 10).
- **Second line of criticism:** empiricism is false because it assumes an absurdly simple picture of thought, belief and justification, in which experiences "pour into the mind" and turn into knowledge. Refining it to be psychologically realistic is very difficult. Empiricists do not deny that elaborate reasoning is needed. They insist experience is somehow fundamental. Critics call this a hangover from an outdated picture. The debate recurs in the book (p. 10).

*Intuition note.* Empiricism sounds obvious, but the Snow and Semmelweiss stories show that "go and look" was resisted by those who "knew better". The Pettenkofer story shows that a direct test is no guarantee of success: his test seemed to refute Koch, and the author puts this down to luck (perhaps high stomach acid, or dead germs in the sample).

**Mathematics (pp. 10–12)**
- Galileo (1623) is quoted saying the universe is a book written in the language of mathematics, whose characters are triangles, circles and other geometric figures, without which one wanders in a "dark labyrinth" (pp. 10–11).
- **Mathematics and Science:** science is different, and especially successful, because it tries to understand the natural world using mathematical tools (p. 11).
- Relation to empiricism: emphasis on mathematics has often been used *against* empiricism, either because mathematics is thought to show another route to knowledge besides experience, or because empiricism is called trivial and unable to say what differentiates science. But the author says it can sensibly be combined with empiricism (p. 11).
- Galileo praised Copernicus for making "reason conquer sense", which might suggest opposition. The author calls this a *false opposition*: Copernicus was dealing with apparent conflicts between aspects of experience, given background beliefs, and Galileo relied heavily on observation (telescope). The author suggests we might argue that mathematics used as a tool within an empiricist outlook is what makes science special (p. 11).
- Mathematics will be a significant but not central theme, partly because of the history of the debates, partly because mathematics is "not quite as essential" as Galileo thought. Darwin's *Origin* ([1859] 1964) makes no real use of it, and most (not all) nineteenth-century biological leaps had little role for it. Mathematical parts of biology, including modern formulations of Darwin's theory, are more recent. So not all science, nor all the greatest science, uses much mathematics (pp. 11–12).

**Social structure (pp. 12–13)**
- **Social Structure and Science:** science is different, and especially successful, because of its unique social structure. This idea needed historians and sociologists to bring it to philosophers' attention (p. 12).
- **Shapin's critique** (Shapin 1994): mainstream empiricism often operates within the fantasy that each individual can test hypotheses personally. This is a fantasy for everyday knowledge and more so for science. Almost every move a scientist makes depends on networks of cooperation and trust. If each insisted on testing everything, science would stay rudimentary. Snow looks like a "lone ranger" at the pump, but even he must have depended on the testimony of others about the epidemic before and after his intervention (p. 12).
- Experience is everywhere. The hard part is working out which experience is relevant to testing hypotheses and who can be trusted. Shapin argues much of the Scientific Revolution was about new ways of policing, controlling and coordinating groups in research (p. 12).
- **Philosophers** (Hull 1988; Kitcher 1993) develop social accounts meant to fit with a form of empiricism. They stress a balance of cooperation and competition. Credit-seeking and status competition are not recent. The Royal Society (1660) and similar societies allocated credit efficiently, rewarding the right people without hindering the free spread of ideas, and created communities that could trust each other as co-workers and data sources. The empiricist can argue this made communities uniquely responsive to experience (p. 13).
- **Summary:** each idea has sometimes been taken as the exclusive starting point, but more likely they are pieces of a fuller answer. Empiricism and social structure are especially important and will recur. A future challenge is to integrate empiricism with social organization, which requires "significant changes" to traditional empiricist ideas (p. 13).

### 1.5 Historical Interlude: A Sketch of the Scientific Revolution (pp. 13–18)
- The Scientific Revolution is roughly 1550–1700. Some historians think the label is a mistake because it suggests sharp boundaries (Shapin 1996). The author uses it in the traditional way (p. 13).
- **Background:** Protestant challenge to the Catholic Church; the Renaissance's partial opening of culture; population recovering from the Black Death; more commerce; strained hierarchies; many unorthodox ideas around (pp. 13–14).
- **Scholastic worldview** (Christianity plus Aristotle, named after the universities or "Schools"): Earth a sphere at the centre, with moon, sun, planets and stars revolving around it. Ptolemy (c. 150 AD) developed a detailed model of the motions (p. 14).
  - Aristotle's physics distinguished "natural" from "violent" motion, within a general theory of change modelled on biological development (acorn to oak), with many events explained by purpose (p. 14).
  - Earthly things are mixtures of four elements (earth, air, fire, water) with natural tendencies. Earthy things fall toward the universe's centre, and fire rises. Projectile motion needs another kind of explanation. Heavenly objects are made of an unchanging fifth element whose natural motion is circular (p. 14).
  - Some versions had a "mechanism" (the author uses the term loosely): for example, each orbiting body might be positioned on a crystalline sphere. Ptolemy's model is harder to read this way, and he is sometimes thought to want a prediction tool (interpreters differ) (p. 14).
- **Copernicus (1473–1543)**, 1543: first detailed theory that Earth orbits the sun. The Earth has two motions: daily rotation and yearly orbit. The placement of bodies matches modern astronomy. But he insisted, following Aristotle and Ptolemy, that heavenly motions are circular, so both systems used compounds of circles and were about equally complicated. Writers differ on whether Copernicus predicted more accurately. His theory explained some phenomena far better, e.g. the planets' "retrograde motion" (p. 15).
- There seemed to be compelling arguments against taking it as literally true, e.g. why a dropped object falls at the foot of a tower if Earth has moved meanwhile. Osiander's added preface urged treating the theory as a calculating tool. This became a statement of **instrumentalism**: theories are only predictive tools, not attempts to describe nature's hidden structure (p. 15).
- **Galileo (1564–1642)** argued for the literal truth of Copernicanism. He improved telescopes (did not invent them) and found phenomena contradicting Aristotle. He used mathematics plus experiment to begin a new science of motion. He was forced by the Inquisition to recant and spent his last years under house arrest. Bruno was burned at the stake in 1600 (p. 15).
- **Kepler (1571–1630)**, a mystical thinker seeking mathematical harmony, abandoned circles. Planets move in ellipses around the sun, giving massive simplification and better predictive accuracy (pp. 15–16).
- **Anatomy:** Vesalius (1543) began freeing anatomy from ancient authority (especially Galen) toward an empirical path. Harvey (1628) established the circulation of blood and the heart as a pump (p. 16).
- **Mechanism** (mid-seventeenth century): the world is made of tiny "corpuscles" interacting only by local contact, and good explanations should be only in terms of mechanical interactions. The universe works like a clock. Descartes (1596–1650) thought an immaterial soul and a traditional God must be added. Most mechanical philosophers kept a role for a Christian God, though dropping souls or God was sometimes considered (p. 16).
- **Boyle (1627–91)** embedded mechanism in a publicized research program of systematic experiment, avoiding unempirical speculation. Scientific societies arose in London, Paris and Florence, to organize research and break the universities' institutional monopoly (p. 16).
- **Newton (1642–1727)**, *Principia* (1687): a unified mathematical treatment of motion on earth and in the heavens. He showed Kepler's ellipses to be the inevitable outcome of gravity between bodies, and improved Galileo's ideas. For centuries he was seen as having essentially completed those parts of physics. His physics was in some ways the culmination of mechanism, in some ways "post-mechanical", since gravity was hard to interpret mechanically (p. 17).
- **Result by 1700:** Copernicanism plus a form of mechanism replaced Scholastic views, and experiment plus mathematical analysis triumphed as method, though people disagreed about the nature of the combination (p. 17).
- **Afterwards** (p. 17): the Chemical Revolution (mid–late eighteenth century; Lavoisier on oxygen and combustion; Dalton, Mendeleyev and the periodic table in the nineteenth century); Linnaeus's classification (eighteenth century); nineteenth-century cell theory, Darwin, germ theory, Mendel and the basis of genetics. The French Enlightenment hoped to use science and reason against ignorance, superstition and oppressive institutions. The American and French Revolutions drew on empiricism, mechanism, Newton's inspiration and a wish to understand society on the model of physical science (pp. 17–18).

### Further Reading (p. 18)
- Recommends Hempel (1966) as for many years the standard introductory textbook and a clear statement of mainstream twentieth-century empiricism and Chalmers (1999) as a clear book with a different view from Hempel's and from the one defended here. Also lists reference works and several books on the Scientific Revolution and the history of medicine (p. 18).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* It illustrates the point of §1.4: social structure and trust can multiply what empiricists' individual tests can reach.

Suppose 10 researchers can each personally run 4 tests in a year, and each test settles one distinct claim.
- **Lone testers:** each person knows only their own results. Knowledge per person = 4 claims, so 4 × 1 = 4 claims known by any one person.
- **Trusting community:** each person also accepts the reports of the other 9. Total claims settled = 10 × 4 = 40. Each person now relies on 4 own + 36 reported = 40 claims.
- **But trust has a risk.** If 2 of the 10 reporters are unreliable, then 2 × 4 = 8 of the 40 reports are unreliable. That is 8/40 = 20%. So the community needs ways to decide *who* can be trusted, which is the problem Shapin stresses (p. 12).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Shapin's point that science depends on trust in others ↔ analytic-philosophy: testimony, reductionism vs. anti-reductionism — both ask whether trusting others' reports needs independent backing.
- [structural-analogy] Osiander's instrumentalism vs. Galileo's literal Copernicanism ↔ scientific realism vs. constructive empiricism — both ask whether theories aim at truth about unobservables or only prediction.
- [metaphor] The "logical structure of science" approach (theories as sets of sentences) ↔ analysis in analytic philosophy — both break a complex thing into formal parts.

## Questions this raises
- If "science" is a rhetorically loaded word, can any definition avoid taking sides in the Science Wars?
- Can empiricism be revised to allow for the role of trust and testimony, and how much of it survives? (The author says "significant changes" are needed, p. 13.)
- If a direct test (Pettenkofer) can mislead, what makes a test reliable?
- Is "the scientific strategy" (p. 7) different from a recipe, and how?
