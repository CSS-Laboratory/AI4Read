# Explanation (Godfrey-Smith, ch. 13)

## L0 — One line
The covering law theory of explanation fails chiefly because explanation has a direction, and Godfrey-Smith argues that no single relation (law, cause, unification) is "the" explanatory relation, since standards of good explanation vary with scientific context.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What is it for a scientific theory to explain something, not merely describe it? Godfrey-Smith first presents the covering law (D-N) theory: to explain is to derive the explanandum (what is explained) from premises that include a law. The flagpole-and-shadow case (the asymmetry problem) refutes it as a general account. Causal and unification theories then replace it, but he argues that both are right some of the time and neither is right always. Following Kuhn, he defends contextualism: fields and periods set their own standards of explanation. Explanation is internal to science, unlike on van Fraassen's view. An optional section covers Humean views (laws and causes as mere patterns). Lesson: do not look for one analysis.

**Prerequisites:** logical argument and deduction; the idea of a law of nature as a regularity; the realism/empiricism contrast (ch. 12); Kuhn's paradigms; Hume's suspicion of causal "connections".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Science seems to tell us why things happen, not only what happens. Some strong empiricist views are suspicious of this, and the question is closely connected to the analysis of evidence (pp. 190–191).
- The covering law (D-N) theory says explaining is showing that the explanandum can be derived from premises that include a law. On this view explanation and prediction differ only in what we know (pp. 192–193).
- The asymmetry problem (flagpole and shadow) is the "killer" objection. Law-based arguments can often run both ways, but explanations (special cases aside) run one way (pp. 193–194).
- The flagpole case points to causation as the key to explanation. Causation is itself controversial, especially among empiricists (pp. 194–195).
- Unification is the rival proposal (Friedman, Kitcher). It is also an "unofficial" theory inside logical empiricism, and it makes sense of the "Aha!" reaction (pp. 195–196).
- Causal and unification theories are not forced competitors, and even "pluralism" about them leaves out the key point. The author's contextualist view, following Kuhn, is that standards of explanation vary by field and period. This is not "anything goes" (pp. 197–198).
- In practice "explain" is used in diverse ways: technical measures, a rhetorical use, and a low-key use for describing mechanisms. The author's view differs from van Fraassen's, and he prefers "explanatory inference" to "inference to the best explanation" (pp. 198–199).
- Humeans treat laws and causation as patterns in events, not as extra "cement" or governing connections. Cartwright's work and a closer look at biology have also changed how philosophers treat laws (pp. 200–201).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 13.1 Knowing Why
- Chapter 12 defended a realism on which one aim of science is describing the real structure of the world. It is also common to think that science tells us why things happen. So we ask what it is for a theory to explain, as opposed to merely describe (p. 190).
- Philosophers and scientists have sometimes distrusted explanation as a goal. This is especially so in strong empiricism, which sees science as a system of rules for predicting experience (p. 190).
- Two problems: (1) analysing evidence for a theory; (2) analysing how already-chosen theories explain. The two can be separated in principle, but they are connected. Scientists often prefer theories because they explain puzzling phenomena well (p. 190).
- *Explanatory inference* (defined in ch. 3) is inference from data to a hypothesis about a structure or process that would explain the data. The author says it seems much more common in science than traditional inductive inference (generalizing from cases). So explanation and evidence are closely related (pp. 190–191).
- The author says the philosophy of science has approached explanation mistakenly, so some of the chapter's views are "unorthodox" (p. 191).

### 13.2 The Rise and Fall of the Covering Law Theory
- Logical positivists were wary of explanation because they sometimes associated it with deep metaphysical insight. They made peace with it by construing it in a low-key way, which gave the *covering law theory*. It was the dominant theory for a good part of the twentieth century and is now "dead" (p. 191).
- Hempel and Oppenheim (1948) developed it. Terms: the **explanandum** is what is being explained. The **explanans** is what does the explaining. If we ask "why X?" and answer "because Y", X is the explanandum and Y the explanans (p. 191).
- Core claim: to explain something is to show how to derive it in a logical argument. The explanandum is the conclusion. The premises are the explanans and must include at least one law of nature that makes a real contribution to the argument. For a good explanation in the fullest sense the premises must also be true (pp. 191–192).
- It covers particular events (the 1929 stock market crash) and patterns (Newton explaining Kepler's laws from mechanics plus assumptions about the solar system). The arguments need not be deductively valid. Non-deductive ("inductive") arguments are allowed if the premises include a law and give high probability to the conclusion (p. 192).
- To explain is to show that the explanandum is to be expected, given the laws. Hence explanation and prediction are nearly the same. Prediction is made before the event; in explanation the event has already happened and we show it could have been predicted (p. 192).
- A "law of nature" here was meant to be a basic regularity or pattern in the flow of events, not something grandiose (p. 192).
- "D-N" (deductive-nomological; *nomos* = law) strictly names only the deductive cases of covering law explanation (p. 193).
- **The asymmetry problem.** A flagpole casts a shadow. We can deduce the shadow's length from the pole's height, the sun's position, optics and trigonometry. The argument can be made deductively valid (p. 193).
- The same laws let us deduce the pole's height from the shadow's length, an equally good argument logically. But explaining the pole's length by the shadow is not fine, unless this is a very unusual self-regulating flagpole (p. 193). The covering law theory says both directions explain. Bromberger (1966) gave a version of the objection (p. 193).
- So explanations have directionality. Not all good arguments containing laws are good explanations. The theory sees explanation as very similar to prediction, differing only in what you know and don't know; the author says this is a mistake (p. 193).
- Symptoms: if only disease D produces symptom S, we can infer D from S, but we cannot explain D by S. Also, S can be explained by D even if S is not always to be expected given D (the unreliable symptoms of syphilis). This is a separate problem (pp. 193–194).
- **Hempel's reply:** he anticipated the problem but dismissed it, accepting that if his theory allows explanations to run in two directions where it seems they run in only one, both must really be OK. The author says this is reasonable in some physics cases where the direction is unclear, but "hopeless" for flagpole and shadow (p. 194).
- Conclusion: the asymmetry problem is "the killer", and it points toward a better account (p. 194).

*Intuition note.* A good argument can go from effect to cause as well as from cause to effect, but in cases like these an explanation goes only one way (special cases aside). A theory that treats "explaining" as "showing something is to be expected" misses this direction.

### 13.3 Causation, Unification, and More
- **Causal proposal.** The shadow is caused by sunlight interacting with the pole. That is the direction of causation, and also of explanation. This suggests that to explain something is to describe what caused it (the dinosaur extinction example); the author says this conclusion seems compelling but has not been universally accepted (p. 194).
- The proposal raises the question "what is causation?". Many philosophers, especially in the empiricist tradition after Hume, distrust causation as a hidden, unobservable connection. Scientific realism eased this somewhat (p. 195).
- Late in the twentieth century the main proposal was that explaining is giving information about how something was caused. Salmon and Suppes used probability theory for this. It also applies to patterns: why does inbreeding increase birth defects? Because of a general causal process, namely an increased chance that two copies of a recessive gene meet in one individual (p. 195).
- What information is needed? Railton's idealized "complete" explanation contains the whole causal history in total detail. In practice only the pieces relevant in the context of the request are needed (p. 195).
- **Unification proposal** (Friedman 1974; Kitcher 1981, 1989). Explanation connects diverse facts by subsuming them under a few basic patterns and principles. It was an "unofficial" theory within much of logical empiricism, set against the "official" covering law theory (e.g. Feigl 1943). Connecting events to a general law is already a kind of unification (pp. 195–196).
- Scientists try to reduce the number of fundamental things and to develop general explanatory schemata. The "Aha!" reaction often comes from seeing an odd phenomenon as a case of something more general (p. 196).
- Kitcher's historical argument: Darwin's theory and Newton's later work on matter were compelling early on despite few specific new predictions, because they promised to explain so much by unifying phenomena under few principles (p. 196).
- Kitcher distrusted causation. He held that causal talk about the flagpole asymmetry is a loose summary of more basic asymmetries involving unification (Kitcher 1989) (p. 196).
- **Author's response to the rivalry:** we need not choose. Much of the time explaining is describing causal mechanisms or history, but not all the time. Some explanatory relations between patterns or principles are hard to put in causal language, and often involve unification. Various relations can be explanatory (p. 196).
- Salmon came to accept that unification is part of the story, sometimes seeing the two as two sides of one coin and sometimes as alternative projects. Kitcher (personal communication, 2002) now thinks avoiding causation was probably a mistake (p. 197).
- **The author's "unorthodox" move.** "Pluralism" is a step in the right direction, but the whole issue has been approached wrongly. It is a mistake to assume that explanation needs analysis by one special relation, or by a definite short list of them (p. 197).
- Instead, the idea of explanation works differently in different parts of science, and in the same part at different times. What is sought under "explanation" is not constant. Ordinary pluralism ignores that fields set definite criteria, and standards good in field A need not suffice in field B. The author's label is **contextualism**: standards for good explanation depend partly on scientific context (p. 197).
- **Kuhn (1977a)** argued that theories or paradigms tend to bring their own standards of good explanation, and standards for what counts as "causal" also depend on paradigms. So explanation and causation are, at least to some extent, internal to fields and historical periods (p. 197).
- A philosopher might reply that different views about causation do not mean there is no fact of the matter. The author grants this "for now", but says the reply has little force for explanation: if two fields call different relations "explanation", neither need be making a factual error (p. 198).
- Kuhn's example: does Newton's gravity explain falling objects, given that it gave a mathematical relationship but no intuitive mechanism? Some said no. Over time it became part of Newtonianism that the right kind of mathematical law counts as an explanation. Kuhn's view is that the idea of explanation evolves with our ideas about science and the universe (p. 198).
- The covering law theory fails as a general account, but some explanations are at least close to Hempel's model. The mistake is applying it to all cases (p. 198).
- **Limits.** This does not mean anything can count as an explanation. Traditions have reasons, and views about explanation depend on views of what the world contains. Some conceptions embed factual errors. Example: if good explanations must always rest on God's will and there is no God, that conception is mistaken. Some philosophers might make the same argument about causal conceptions, but many treatments of explanation will not be ruled out by factual error (p. 198).
- **Actual use of "explain".** It is diverse: technical senses (mathematical measures of "the amount of variation explained"), fields with no technical standard, a rhetorical use ("your theory accommodates this result but doesn't really explain it", often meaning an unnatural-looking derivation, where unification often seems important), and a low-key use. On realism much of science describes how things work (photosynthesis, DNA replication), and calling these descriptions explanations adds nothing extra (pp. 198–199).
- **Van Fraassen (1980).** He also denies a single special explanatory relation and offers a "pragmatic", context-varying account. But he denies that explanation is "inside" science: scientific reasoning does not assess explanatory power. For him explanation is what people do when they use theories to answer questions external to science. The author holds that explanation is thoroughly internal to science, "but variously so" (p. 199).
- **Explanatory inference vs. inference to the best explanation.** The latter name suggests a single measure of "explanatory goodness". The author avoids it. Explanatory inference, as he uses it, is devising and comparing hypotheses about hidden structures that might be responsible for data, and explanation is "pretty diverse". Ch. 14 returns to this (p. 199).
- **Summary:** the covering law theory is dead as a general account. Causation, unification and derivation from laws are often involved. But fields have different concepts and standards of explanation (pp. 199–200).

*Intuition note.* The author's point is that "explanation" is not one thing with one definition. Its standards are set inside each field and can change, as with Newton's mathematical law becoming an accepted explanation. But the world still constrains them, so a standard built on a false factual assumption can be wrong.

### 13.4 Laws and Causes (Optional Section)
- Laws and causation both seem to pick out a special connection between things. Causation is sometimes called, half jokingly, "the cement of the universe" (Mackie 1980; the phrase was first used by Hume) (p. 200).
- Recent skeptics generally do not say "there is no causation". They say "yes, there is, but it is no more than this…". **Humeanism** analyses laws and causation as patterns in the arrangement of things, not an extra connection. Present-day Humeans do not share Hume's kind of empiricism, but they avoid believing in unobservable "cement" (p. 200).
- A Humean treats laws as regularities or basic patterns. This leaves behind the connotation that laws direct, guide or govern. The traditional "governing" view sees laws as responsible for the patterns, not identical with them (Dretske 1977; Armstrong 1983). The Humean regards this as a seduction to avoid. The author says the logical empiricist attitude to laws was basically Humean (p. 200).
- Causation is debated likewise: a special kind of regular pattern, versus a connection responsible for patterns. On the second view the connection need not be mysterious; maybe ordinary science can describe it (Dowe 1992; Menzies 1996) (pp. 200–201).
- Cartwright (1983) argued that "laws of physics" usually do not describe real systems, only highly idealized fictional ones (p. 201).
- Philosophers are also no longer obsessed with laws as *the* goal of theorizing. They once thought genuine science must contain laws. But most biology has little use for laws and is no less scientific (p. 201).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Flagpole of height $h = 10$ m, sun at elevation $\theta = 45^\circ$. Optics and trigonometry give shadow length $s = h/\tan\theta$.
- Forward: $s = 10/\tan 45^\circ = 10/1 = 10$ m. A valid derivation, and a good explanation, since the pole causes the shadow.
- Reverse: $h = s\cdot\tan\theta = 10 \times 1 = 10$ m. It is equally valid logically, with a law and true premises. But "the pole is 10 m because the shadow is 10 m" is not an explanation.
The covering law theory counts both derivations as explanations. The difference lies only in the direction of causation, which is the point of the asymmetry problem.

## Concepts
- [[deductive-nomological explanation]] — explaining by deriving the explanandum from premises including a law; also called the covering law theory (pp. 191–193)
- [[explanandum-explanans]] (new) — the thing explained vs. the premises doing the explaining (p. 191)
- [[asymmetry problem]] (new) — law-based arguments run both ways but explanations run one way, so the covering law theory over-generates (p. 193)
- [[causal theory of explanation]] (new) — to explain is to give (relevant) information about what caused the event or pattern (pp. 194–195)
- [[unification theory of explanation]] (new) — explanation connects diverse facts under a few basic patterns and schemata (p. 196)
- [[explanatory contextualism]] (new) — standards for good explanation depend partly on scientific context (field, period, paradigm) (p. 197)
- [[explanatory inference]] (new) — inference from data to hypotheses about hidden structures that might explain it, with no single measure of "explanatory goodness" (p. 199)
- [[humeanism about laws and causation]] (new) — laws and causes are patterns in events, not extra connecting "cement" (p. 200)
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — Kuhn: paradigms bring their own standards of explanation and of causal relations (p. 197)
- [[scientific realism vs. constructive empiricism]] — van Fraassen's "pragmatic" view puts explanation outside science, against the author's internal view (p. 199)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Explanatory asymmetry (cause → effect only) ↔ causal inference: correlation or deducibility is symmetric, but causal direction is not.
- [structural-analogy] Unification as explanation ↔ model simplicity: explaining many facts with few principles resembles compressing data with a few parameters.
- [metaphor] Contextualism about explanation ↔ sociology of science: field-specific standards of "good explanation".

## Questions this raises
- If standards of explanation are field-relative, what stops a field adopting weak standards? The author mentions the limit of factual error and the good reasons traditions generally have (p. 198).
- If the covering law theory is dead because of direction, can the causal theory itself say what makes causal information relevant in a context (p. 195)?
- How should explanatory inference be assessed in ch. 14 if there is no single measure of explanatory goodness (p. 199)?
- Is the Humean view of laws compatible with laws being explanatory, given that "governing" is rejected (p. 200)?
