# Naturalistic Philosophy in Theory and Practice

## L0 — One line
Godfrey-Smith defends a naturalism in which philosophy keeps its own (often normative) questions but may use scientific results to answer them, and he shows this at work by using psychology to make progress on the "theory-ladenness of observation" debate, which he says becomes much easier to settle from a naturalistic standpoint.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
After logical empiricism broke down, what should philosophy of science be? Godfrey-Smith answers: naturalism. Philosophy starts from our best current science and does not try to justify it from outside. Unlike Quine's 1969 version, it keeps distinctively philosophical questions, such as normative ones about good and bad belief-forming methods. The worked example is the claim that observation is "theory-laden" and so cannot test theories neutrally. Some versions of the claim are harmless. For perception itself, Fodor's psychology-based reply suggests that the theories that do shape perception are low-level assumptions, not high-level scientific theories. The author sketches a naturalistic empiricism built on this.

**Prerequisites:** the logical empiricists and the Popper–Kuhn–Feyerabend debates (earlier chapters); the idea of holism in testing (ch. 2); basic empiricism.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Naturalism is best summarised as: philosophy may use scientific results to answer philosophical questions, even in philosophy of science. It opposes foundationalism, the demand for an outside justification of science (pp. 149–150).
- Quine's 1969 naturalism would absorb epistemology into psychology. The author rejects this and defends a version where philosophical questions remain distinct and science is a resource for philosophy, not a replacement (p. 151).
- Normative questions (questions involving value judgments, such as how we should handle evidence) are an important example of what stays philosophical. Normative naturalists have often judged them instrumentally, that is, by whether a method is a good way of achieving the agent's goal. Dewey compares good reasoning with good farming (pp. 152–153).
- Other lasting tasks: comparing the everyday and scientific pictures of the world, and checking how different sciences fit together (pp. 153–154).
- "Theory-ladenness" arguments are a mixture. Some are harmless, some reduce to holism about testing (the problem, introduced in ch. 2, of how assumptions affect an observation's relevance to a theory), some trouble only logical positivism (with its ideal of a purely observational language, sharply separate from theory) (pp. 156–158).
- Theory-ladenness of language does not by itself block observation from testing a theory. The rabbit-fossil example illustrates this (pp. 157–158).
- Fodor argues that perception seems influenced only by low-level assumptions, not high-level scientific theories, and links this to a modular view (perception uses automatic, innate processing units). On this view scientific theories do not bias how things seem. The author stresses the kind of evidence, not the truth of modularity (pp. 158–160).
- Observation raises two separate questions, reliability and neutrality. Both are empirical and linked in complicated ways (p. 160).
- The result is an evolutionary, naturalistic empiricism in which naturalism is "primary" (pp. 161–162).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 10.1 What Is Naturalism? (pp. 149–150)
- Logical empiricists held that philosophy of science concerns above all the logic of science. By the mid-1970s this view had broken down, and other fields tried to take over territory (p. 149).
- Slogan: "philosophy should be continuous with science." The author finds it hard to interpret. Naturalists reject a sharp separation of philosophy from other fields but disagree on the connection (p. 149).
- A more useful summary: philosophy can use results from the sciences to answer philosophical questions, even in philosophy of science (pp. 149–150).
- **Objection (from other positions):** using science to study science is vicious circularity. One must stand outside science to assess it (p. 150).
- **Foundationalism** here: the idea that philosophy of science should be done from an external, more secure standpoint, making no assumptions about particular scientific ideas. Naturalism opposes it (p. 150).
- Naturalists think the project of giving general philosophical foundations for science is always doomed. They also think science does not need such foundations. A description of knowledge must draw on science and will be no more certain than the science it uses (p. 150).
- Beyond this sketch there is much disagreement. The label is attractive, so it risks dissolving into platitudes (Sober: "no artificial ingredients"). The author nonetheless says he will often suggest that naturalism is our best hope for the core problems (p. 150).

*Intuition note:* The circularity worry assumes we need a safe starting point. Naturalists say the search for such a foundation is doomed and that science does not need it. We start from our best science, accept it may change, and still do philosophy.

### 10.2 Quine, Dewey, and Others (pp. 150–155)
- **Origins.** Quine's "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969) is often called the birth of modern naturalism, but modern naturalism did not come entirely from Quine. Dewey was a naturalist from about 1925. The author says Dewey is superior in some areas but was neglected, and Quine had the most influence (pp. 150–151).
- **Quine's two claims (1969).** (a) Attack on the idea of giving "foundations" for scientific knowledge, now central to naturalism. (b) A more radical claim: epistemology is so tied to scientific psychology that it should be absorbed into psychology. Only questions best answered by psychology matter (p. 151).
- **The author's reply.** He opposes (b). In his version, philosophical questions exist and differ from scientists' questions. Science contributes answers but does not replace the questions. In Quine's 1969 version, science is the only source of both questions and answers. For the author, science is "a resource for philosophy, not … a replacement" (p. 151).
- **Normative questions** (those involving value judgments) are an important example of questions not directly addressed within science. Psychology might describe how beliefs form but apparently would not tell us which mechanisms are good or bad, how to handle evidence, or how to tell a good argument from a bad one. Their answers may depend on psychological facts, but answering them stays a task for philosophy (p. 152).
- "Normative naturalism" is the name for views that keep this normative side (term coined by Laudan 1987). Toward the end of his career Quine modified his view, moving closer to it (Quine 1990) (p. 152).
- **Basis for the values.** Normative naturalism faces the problem of locating values in a world of facts. A common reply is instrumental: an action is *instrumentally rational* if it is a good way of achieving the agent's goal, whatever that goal is. Whether A is likely to lead to B is a factual matter, so whether A is instrumentally rational is factual (p. 152).
- That instrumental rationality is one kind of rationality is uncontroversial. That it is the only kind is controversial. Some normative naturalists hold it is the only kind relevant to epistemology. Assessing ultimate goals is then rejected or not addressed (pp. 152–153).
- **Dewey (Logic, 1938).** "Good" and "bad" reasoning are meant as we mean "good" and "bad" farming. Some techniques are better at achieving farmers' usual goals. Consequences are factual and learned from experience. Present methods may be improved. There is no philosophical problem with such value judgments, and the same holds in epistemology (p. 153).
- **Other lasting philosophical questions.**
  - Comparing the everyday picture of the world with the scientific one. This needs concise summaries of both. In philosophy of mind, the question is how the everyday picture of thoughts, beliefs and desires compares with psychology and neurobiology (p. 153).
  - Relations between sciences: do their fragments fit together or conflict? The philosopher patrols boundaries and takes a "synoptic" view. Criticism of particular scientific ideas is possible but comes from the overall scientific picture (pp. 153–154).
- **Summary of the author's naturalism.** Begin from our best current scientific picture of humans and their place in the universe, without giving an outside justification for using it. That science is uncertain and may change. The questions need not come from science and are often traditional ones about belief, justification and knowledge (p. 154).
- He does not say all philosophy must be naturalistic. Philosophy also acts as an "incubator" for speculative ideas, and benefits from its loose organisation. But to the extent that we can expect to solve big problems in fields like epistemology, naturalism is "probably the right approach" (p. 154).
- **Agenda from ch. 1:** (1) how humans gain knowledge of the world; (2) what makes science descended from the Scientific Revolution different from other investigations (p. 154). In more detail (pp. 154–155):
  - Does a naturalistic investigation of observation support the familiar idea that observation and experiment make science responsive to the world's real structure? Some sociology of science (including Latour, on one reading) gives "responsiveness" no role. In principle there could be an institution that looked like science, with experiments as "PR" and theories changing by negotiation between factions. How do we know ours is not one? The author separates an in-principle question (does human thought and perception allow responsive belief?) from an in-practice one (do actual communities achieve it?).
  - Suppose science is shown to be responsive: what contact with the world does it achieve? Is the traditional concept of truth coherent and useful here, and does it help us understand scientific progress at all? (Kuhn believed it does not.) What can a naturalist make of "inductive logic"? Which features of science should be strengthened, and which are self-defeating?
- Recent philosophy draws on more sources of ideas. Some see chaos and lost discipline. The author, like others, sees progress (p. 155).

*Intuition note:* The author's naturalism is a division of labour. Science supplies facts about minds and the world. Philosophy still asks what we *should* believe and how the pictures fit together.

### 10.3 The Theory-Ladenness of Observation (pp. 155–162)
- **The debate.** Can observational evidence be an unbiased, neutral source when choosing between theories, or is it "contaminated" by theoretical assumptions? This matters especially to empiricists. Radical theorists of science used theory-ladenness against mainstream empiricism (pp. 155–156). It becomes much easier to settle from a naturalistic standpoint (p. 156).
- "Observation" here means all sensory contact with the world. Empiricists have generally treated it as theory-neutral, which is often the basis for its claim to be an "objective" way to settle disagreements (p. 156).
- **The arguments (Hanson, Kuhn, Feyerabend).** Observational judgments are affected by the observer's theoretical beliefs, so observation cannot test theories or paradigms without bias, so traditional empiricism is false. The author calls the arguments "a mixture": they concern observation reports, observation as psychology, or resulting beliefs. Some trouble only logical positivism, others all views except radical skepticism or extreme relativism (p. 156).
- **Milder arguments (the author starts with the more innocuous ones).**
  - Theory tells scientists where and what to look for. True, but no sensible empiricist denied it. It does not affect testing unless scientists refuse to look where unfriendly observations might be found (p. 156).
  - It is claimed that scientists must use theoretical assumptions to decide which observations to take seriously, since some are errors and are discarded ("filtered"). Theoretical beliefs affect this filtering, so bias is possible, and the author says these problems are real. It is the holism about testing of ch. 2 (pp. 156–157).
  - Partial suggestions: the assumptions affecting relevance can be tested separately. Perhaps in crucial tests scientists should be more reluctant to discard observations. It is hard to know which pieces of common sense help, are trivial, or are "flat wrong" (p. 157).
- **Language arguments.** To be relevant to science an experience must be put into words. The vocabulary is influenced by the scientist's theoretical framework, so no part of language is totally "theory-free" (p. 157).
  - Some versions threaten only the logical positivist ideal of a purely observational language. Showing that observation language is "theoretical" in some sense does not end the matter, and "everything depends on" which theories affect it and how (p. 157).
  - Low-level "theories" may never affect real testing. Example: the assumption that objects keep their shape when unobserved (p. 157).
  - Suppose observation reports are affected by the very theories being tested. Feyerabend tried to show this for seventeenth-century descriptions of motion. This "looks like trouble", but even then it may or may not matter. A theory can supply the concepts of a report without affecting its capacity to test that theory. Not every result in theory T's concepts favours T (p. 157).
  - Example: "I saw rabbit fossils in Precambrian rocks" is heavily laden with biological and geological theory. Even if one says it is not an observation report, it would still be a massive shock to evolutionary theory (pp. 157–158).
  - Even on a simple falsificationist view, which the author says is inadequate, theory-derived concepts do not stop nature saying NO to a conjecture. So theory's influence on observational vocabulary does not, on its own, prevent observation from being an unbiased test (p. 158).
- **Perceptual experiences themselves (the most important aspect).** **Kuhn and others:** experiences are influenced by beliefs including theories, so at no stage of observation do theories play no role. They used mid-twentieth-century psychology that replaced a "passive" view of perception with an active, intelligent one. The same retinal stimulation can be caused by many objects, so the visual system must use theoretical assumptions to choose (Gregory 1970) (p. 158).
- **Fodor's reply (1984, "Observation Reconsidered").** The author largely follows it. Again everything hinges on which theories affect observation and how (p. 158).
  - The Müller-Lyer illusion (fig. 10.1, p. 159) has two equal lines, but the lower one tends to look longer. Psychology explains it by unconscious background assumptions in visual processing. This has been taken as showing theory-ladenness (pp. 158–159).
  - Fodor notes that some background knowledge has no effect. The illusion persists despite knowing it is an illusion or knowing the theory of illusions. Perception seems to be influenced by some theories and not others. The influential ones are low-level assumptions about the physical layout of the world, such as three-dimensional space and the effect of distance on apparent size (p. 159).
  - Fodor links this to a research programme positing **modules** (Fodor 1983): automatic, innate pieces of mental machinery that work unconsciously and use a fixed subset of background knowledge. Their output goes to "central" mechanisms with access to all a person's theories. So later responses to observation may be affected by any theory, but the module's output, how things *seem*, is not biased by which scientific theory the person accepts (p. 159).
  - This does not solve the whole problem. Even if observation itself is unbiased, what a person does with it returns to holism about testing (pp. 159–160).
- **Why this supports naturalism.** Observation is a natural phenomenon studied by psychology and psychophysics. Naturalistic philosophers can use these results to see how observation works in science (p. 160).
- **Two sets of questions** (p. 160):
  1. How reliable is observation as a way of forming true beliefs, and when is it unreliable?
  2. Is observation neutral between competing theories? Can people with different theoretical commitments agree on what was observed, giving an intersubjective basis for theory choice?
- These are distinct but linked. Reliable shared senses can be expected to produce consensus. Agreement without reliability is possible: we might all be deluded in the same way. Some philosophers have thought colour vision is like this (p. 160).
- For question 2, Fodor's kind of evidence is relevant. The important thing is "what kind of scientific evidence is relevant", not whether the modular theory is ultimately right. We need to know what perceptual mechanisms are like, whether they are similar across normal humans, and what role high-level scientific beliefs play (p. 160). This evidence does not itself settle reliability, which psychology and psychophysics can investigate systematically (p. 161).
- **Reliability without true theories.** Perception might use low-level theories that make it reliable although those theories are not true. The example is a built-in Euclidean geometry, not strictly true of our universe. Such habits did not stop Einstein revising the belief that space is Euclidean (p. 161).

*Intuition note:* Whether "theory-laden" observation is a problem depends on which theories affect observation, and how. Psychology supplies relevant evidence: if only low-level assumptions shape perception, then scientific theories do not bias how things seem to us.

- **Sketch of a naturalistic empiricism** (a recurring theme of the rest of the book) (p. 161).
  - First understand the actual role of observation in the sociological patterns of science and in settling controversies. Then add scientific results about observation. Then ask what contact with the world science has, given observation's role and the channel it provides (p. 161).
  - Observation is physical contact between mind and world, a product of evolution. Its reliability is due to our evolutionary history and the contingent fit between our structure and our surroundings. We need science because much of the world is inaccessible to ordinary observation. Science exposes theoretical ideas to observation by construing and embedding them in conceptual frameworks so that even very general hypotheses can be exposed (p. 161).
  - This is a kind of empiricism the author is optimistic about, one in which naturalism is "in a sense, primary": its advantages are not established by philosophy alone (pp. 161–162).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Two observers, A and B, look at the two lines of the Müller-Lyer figure. Each line is 10 cm long.
- Module stage: for both observers the module outputs "lower line looks longer", say about 11 cm versus 10 cm in apparent size. A knows it is an illusion and B does not, but this knowledge does not enter the module, so the output is the same.
- Central stage: A has measured the lines with a ruler (10 cm and 10 cm) and concludes "the lines are equal, 10 − 10 = 0 cm difference". B concludes "the lower is longer".
- Difference in what they believe: 0 cm (A) versus about 1 cm (B). The difference arises *after* the module, in central processing (the stage the text links to holism about testing). It is not a difference in how things seem. This matches Fodor's point that the seeming is unbiased by the observer's theories while later responses are not (p. 159).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Instrumental rationality ↔ decision theory: choosing means for given goals without judging the goals — as described here, the means–end relation is factual (p. 152).
- [metaphor] Fodor's perceptual module ↔ encapsulated software: a fixed-input component feeding a central processor (p. 159).
- [structural-analogy] Filtering of data by theory ↔ data cleaning and outlier removal in empirical research: assumptions decide which data count (p. 156).

## Questions this raises
- If normative judgments in epistemology are only instrumental, who or what fixes epistemic goals, and is that circular? (pp. 152–153)
- How exactly can the effect of observation on theory change be separated from the effect of the theories used to filter observations (holism)? (p. 157)
- If perception could be reliable via false low-level theories, how should we assess reliability of observation reports in practice? (p. 161)
- Does the evidence for modularity hold up for experts' trained perception (e.g., in microscopes or instrument reading)? The text does not say.
