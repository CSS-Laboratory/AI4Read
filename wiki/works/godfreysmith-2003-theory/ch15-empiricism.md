# Empiricism, Naturalism, and Scientific Realism?

## L0 — One line
Godfrey-Smith argues that the three "isms" can be combined if empiricism is reformed inside a naturalistic picture, in which science is a strategy of exposing big ideas to experience, carried out by a socially organized "engine of self-correction".

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can empiricism, naturalism and scientific realism be combined, or do they make a "muddy paste"? Godfrey-Smith says the main conflict is between empiricism and realism. He argues it eases if empiricism is placed inside a naturalistic picture. Humans are organisms in causal contact with the world, and the reliability of perception and of scientific inference can itself be studied. Science is then a strategy: testing even the biggest ideas against experience. It is carried out by a social structure of competition and cooperation, criticism and trust. He answers a critic who says this buries empiricism rather than reforming it, and he lists open problems.

**Prerequisites:** the book's earlier themes (underdetermination, Kuhn, theory-ladenness, scientific realism); the idea of epistemology (the theory of knowledge). No math.

## L2 — Key ideas
- A reviewer predicted that combining empiricism, naturalism and realism would give a "muddy paste". The chapter is a reply to that prediction (p. 219).
- The big tension is empiricism vs. realism. It comes from picturing the mind behind a "veil of ideas" and from underdetermination of theory by evidence (the claim that many rival theories fit all the evidence) (p. 220).
- Naturalism helps avoid the veil picture. Humans are biological organisms in causal interaction with the world, so there are not two domains, one accessible and one mysterious (p. 222).
- Perception also faces "underdetermination", yet it can be studied scientifically and found reliable. The same question can be asked of scientific inference, using history of science (pp. 222–223).
- Science is a strategy, a kind of choice: expose even the biggest ideas to experience. Narrowly, "science" also names a social organization that carries out the strategy (pp. 224–225).
- That organization works along two dimensions: work at one time (competition/cooperation) and work across times (cumulative work). It gives an "engine of self-correction" (p. 225).
- Challenge: the complex social story may replace empiricism rather than reform it. Reply: this loses the wood for the trees, because the strategy is still a modified empiricism (pp. 227–230).
- "Objectivity" is a poor concept here. Scientific belief comes from interaction between our psychology, our social organization and the world (p. 229).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 15.1 A Muddy Paste? (p. 219)
- The chapter ties together three ideas: empiricism, naturalism and scientific realism. Each has been defended. The harder question is whether they fit together as a package. Declaring oneself an "empiricist naturalist realist" is not enough (p. 219).
- **Anonymous reviewer's view:** the three can each be defended individually but do not go well together. For example, a good case for realism needs opposition to some central empiricist ideas. The reviewer predicted a "muddy paste". The author will try anyway and leaves the verdict to readers (p. 219).

### 15.2 The Apparent Tensions (pp. 220–221)
- Definitions as used here. *Empiricism*: traditionally, our source of knowledge about the world is experience. *Naturalism*: philosophical problems, including epistemological ones, can only be resolved within a scientific picture of ourselves and our place in the universe. *Scientific realism*: science can reasonably aim to describe the real structure of the world, including unobservable structure (p. 220).
- Much of the problem comes from empiricism. "Experience is the only source" is vague, and attempts to sharpen it often give troublesome views (p. 220).
- **Empiricism vs. realism (the big one).** Traditional empiricism was drawn to a "veil of ideas" picture: the mind is shut in behind its sensations. Extreme versions deny it even makes sense to think about what lies beyond experience. Milder versions find it hard to see how experience can support hypotheses about hidden structure. This tempts people to say science concerns only patterns in the observable (p. 220).
- This is now often debated as "underdetermination of theory by evidence". **Empiricists argue** that many alternative theories always fit all the evidence, so we have no empirical grounds, and hence no grounds at all, to pick one as how the world really is (p. 220).
- **Empiricism vs. naturalism, first source.** Empiricism has often been foundationalist: begin from our ideas and experience, then work outward. Naturalism says this "starting within the circle of our ideas" is a bad mistake (p. 220).
- **Second source.** Sociologists of science (and others) have held that empiricism is a collection of myths. They argue that in real science experience does not act as a neutral "arbiter" of theoretical disputes. Theory-ladenness arguments (section 10.3) are used for this. The author says he tried to defuse most of them, but they remain influential (p. 221).
- **Naturalism vs. realism.** Less problem. It is hard to be a naturalist without taking science seriously as a description of the world, which suggests naturalism requires a form of realism. Still, sociologists who see themselves as the truly naturalistic students of science have sometimes argued that the myths to drop include myths about science's contact with reality (p. 221).
- Conclusion: the big tension is empiricism vs. realism. The way out is via naturalistic ideas (p. 221).

*Intuition note:* Much of the empiricism–realism clash comes from a picture. If we only ever touch our own experiences, the hidden world looks unreachable. The chapter's reply targets that picture and reforms empiricism rather than dropping it.

### 15.3 Empiricism Reformed (pp. 221–227)
**Step 1: epistemology in general (pp. 221–223).**
- Many philosophers now agree the "veil of ideas" picture is misleading, but relatives of it are easy to fall back into. One is a two-domain picture: an accessible, familiar domain (experience, or the observable) and an inaccessible, "theoretical" one (p. 221).
- The author's naturalism is influenced by Dewey (1929). Humans are biological organisms embedded in a physical world they evolved to deal with. Trying to know the world is one aspect of causal interaction with it, and much of that interaction is practical (p. 222).
- Perceptual mechanisms are tools for coordinating our dealings with the world. From the inside we cannot establish with certainty what lies behind a sensory input. But seen "sideways-on" (the viewpoint of biology and psychology), we can establish regular principles for how perception responds to distant objects and helps us navigate (p. 222).
- This applies to all organisms with perception, not only humans or scientists. So we should not posit two domains. We are systems embedded in a world of objects at many distances, with varied kinds of contact. Access via thought and theory is "a complicated kind of causal interaction", and it keeps expanding with technology. Things once reached by speculative inference can later be observed, scanned or assayed (p. 222).
- **Underdetermination revisited.** Perception faces a similar issue: in principle, alternative layouts of objects could produce the same stimuli (light, sound). Psychologists themselves speak of "underdetermination" here. Yet we make reliable judgments, and we can know we do, by studying our perceptual mechanisms scientifically. We can learn what kind of reliability we have (pp. 222–223).
- The same approach can be applied to scientific inference and modeling. Ask what reliability different reasoning and model-building strategies actually achieve (p. 223).
- Structures move from being inaccessible (only speculative models) to routine study. Example: the genetic composition of an organism, via DNA sequencing (p. 223).
- So we can go back to older models and ask how well we did, and which approaches steered us to good or bad models. Example question: do preferences for simple theories lead to good choices? This needs the history of science, which records cases where approaches succeeded or failed (p. 223).

*Intuition note:* The author answers "how can we know the unobservable?" by pointing to perception. It also infers distant things from limited stimuli, and we can still test how reliable it is. Reliability is something to be measured, not ruled out in advance.

**Step 2: empiricism about science (pp. 223–227).**
- People share basic biology but differ greatly in how they pursue understanding. They differ especially in how they assess big explanatory theories, as opposed to everyday matters. Shared biology may make us fairly empirical about food and getting home. The author says this does not carry over to theories about our place in the universe (p. 223).
- Recalling chapter 10: science may be seen as a *strategy*, namely subjecting even the biggest theoretical ideas and disputes to testing by observation. It is not dictated by language, rules of thought or biology. It is more like a choice, by an individual or a culture. It means construing and embedding ideas so that exposure to experience is sought even for the most general hypotheses. This view of science is "a kind of empiricism" (pp. 223–224).
- **Two-part story**, related to the broad vs. narrow uses of "science" (chapter 1). (a) The *strategy*: assess big ideas by exposing them to experience. (b) A particular *socially organized way* of carrying it out, developed in and after the Scientific Revolution. "Science" can be used, narrowly, for (b) (p. 224).
- An individual could carry out the strategy alone, internalizing the dialogue between imaginative and critical voices, like the "old fantasy of the lone empiricist". This is far from the usual way. To see what distinguishes the tradition of work deriving from the Scientific Revolution from other approaches, we must study its social structure (p. 224).
- The social structure has two dimensions (pp. 224–225):
  - *Work at one time:* a reward system and internal culture producing an efficient mix of competition and cooperation, and a useful division of labor across approaches (chapter 11).
  - *Work across times:* science is cumulative, with each generation building on earlier ones ("standing on shoulders", Newton). This needs trustworthy transmission of ideas and a reward system for carrying on earlier work.
- With such a structure, the imaginative/critical dialogue becomes real, and there are social mechanisms that reliably produce checking. In Kim Sterelny's phrase, this is an "engine of self-correction". Dogmatic individuals can play a potentially useful role provided the community as a whole is flexible and open-minded (p. 225).
- Two "balances" appear in figures discussed earlier. Competition/cooperation (Merton, Hull, Kitcher; chapters 8 and 11). Criticism/trust (a main theme of Kuhn; also part of Shapin's work). **Shapin** would resist calling it a "balance", because the word implies the relation is good, and he takes no stand on that. **Kuhn** did think science's relation of criticism and trust is uniquely effective (p. 225).
- **Suspicion of "balance":** it makes the relations sound like precious, fragile achievements. How do we know we couldn't do better? Feyerabend thought recent science had lost its balance between imaginative and pedestrian work (chapter 7). A feminist criticism of Hull's claim of balance was discussed in chapters 9 and 11 (pp. 225–226).
- **Historical questions.** Did the two dimensions arise in crucial transitions or gradually? Together or separately? (p. 226)
  - Cumulative structure is old in some fields and newer in others, and can be gained and lost partially. Toulmin and Goodfield (1962) describe schools that sustained work and then faded, replaced by lone workers often "reinventing the wheel". Gradually, field by field, this gave way to cumulative work (p. 226).
  - For work at a time, the mid-seventeenth century may be crucial. Shapin and Schaffer (1985) stress Boyle and the Royal Society creating a culture of controlled criticism and a network of trust. They do not endorse the result, but many philosophers would. If this culture is crucial, the earlier "adventurers" such as Galileo matter slightly less (p. 226).
  - **Alchemy:** precursor to chemistry, based on detailed recipes plus strange theories (rocks growing in the earth; reactions tied to planetary astrology). It was empirical in being results-oriented but organized by secrecy and restricted communication, partly from semi-mysticism and partly from hopes of gold. Boyle contrasted his open culture with alchemical secrecy and with Scholastic emptiness and dogmatism (pp. 226–227).
- **Diversity.** Chapter 1 raised the possibility that "science" is too varied for a detailed theory (the "disunity of science" discussion). The later chapters took diversity seriously with "mixed" or "pluralist" views: some fields might fit Kuhn's normal science, others might fit Laudan or Lakatos better (chapter 7); a contextualist view of explanation (chapter 13). Diversity need not mean relativism. The account here is still fairly general, and others might propose less general views (p. 227).

*Intuition note:* The chapter splits "science" into a general strategy (test big ideas against experience) and a community machine (reward, trust, cumulative work) that makes the testing reliable. Empiricism supplies the first part. The second part is what the sociological and historical chapters described.

### 15.4 A Last Challenge (pp. 227–230)
- **The challenge:** is calling this a vindication of empiricism misleading? Has the book shown empiricism right all along, or rejected all its central ideas? (p. 227)
- **Critic (via Kuhn):** science is a more complicated machine than traditional empiricism imagined. Empiricist ideas are not just vague but wrong, and cannot describe the balances of social organization. The author's criticisms of Kuhn's details do not seem likely to help with this basic challenge, and we seem likely to end up with an equally complicated view (pp. 227–228).
- **Author's reply, in the empiricist's voice:** "there is a lot of complexity, but the basic ideas of empiricism capture the most fundamental features". This is the reply he proposes, while noting the objections (p. 228).
- **Critic's point on "sources":** asking whether knowledge has one source or several is like asking how many pipes feed a tank. "Epistemology is not plumbing." On this view the discussion of structure, frameworks and rewards should replace the simple empiricist ideas, as a different and better story (p. 228).
- **Objectivity.** The author avoided this term (chapter 1) as ambiguous and misleading. It is used in two senses: (1) impartiality, a loose family of contrasts between belief shaped by caprice, prejudice or point of view and belief that avoids such influences; (2) existing independently of what people think (colors? moral values?), linked to chapter 12 on realism (pp. 228–229).
- Some discussions merge the senses: science is objective if belief change is controlled by contact with real things. The book "sort of says yes", but the questions are bad ones. "Real objects or social factors?", "products of the world or of human creativity?", "are we responsible or is the world?" are false dichotomies (p. 229).
- **Author's view:** scientific belief results from interaction among our psychological capacities, our social organization and the world's structure. The world does not "stamp" beliefs on us, but science is responsive to the world via observation (p. 229).
- **Critic's suspicion:** empiricism is kept because it is simple and rhetorically useful. Against religious fundamentalism, the empiricist can say science shapes belief by observation, judges ideas by how they stand up to testing, and is open-minded, anti-authoritarian and flexible. A complex story would make science's distinguishing features non-obvious. The author notes: "simple answers are often false" (pp. 229–230).
- **Verdict:** he recognizes the force of the argument that empiricism was buried rather than reformed, but judges it erroneous. Modern science has a general strategy and a complex social structure that carries it out, and the first part is "a modified, naturalistic form of empiricism" (p. 230).

*Intuition note:* The dispute is about which feature to treat as the core of science. The author says the simple strategy is the core and the social machinery is how it is carried out, so complexity does not defeat the empiricist label.

### 15.5 The Future (pp. 230–231)
- Three issues worth working on (p. 230):
  1. The role of frameworks and paradigms in theory change. Should we follow Kuhn and Carnap in a sharp distinction between two "tiers" of conceptual change, or is that a beguiling image that causes problems?
  2. The reward system and the relation between individual- and community-level goals. Past treatments generalized and assumed scientists share similar motivations. Input from sociology of science could give differences between fields and subcultures.
  3. Representation. The author used a broad concept of "representation" for the theory–reality relation, avoided making reference and truth central, and stressed models. There is not yet a good philosophical theory of representation, and "a big cloud of uncertainty" remains.
- **Progress claimed** (pp. 230–231): the idea that all science describes patterns in experience has been (mostly) abandoned; sophisticated realism has been developed; the field is less dominated by language and gives more attention to models; theories of testing and evidence are in far better shape than fifty years ago; studying the reward structure in relation to epistemology is a crucial advance.

## Toy example
*(Our own, with invented numbers.)* The chapter's main move: "underdetermination" exists even in perception, yet reliability can be measured, and the same can be done for scientific modeling strategies.

1. A viewer sees a patch of light that could come from a real cup or a cup-shaped photo. The stimulus alone does not settle which.
2. Researchers run 200 trials with known layouts, and the viewer is right in 190. Measured reliability = 190 / 200 = 0.95.
3. Now a modeling strategy, "prefer the simpler model". Take 20 past cases where the structure later became directly observable, so the old models can be checked. The simpler model was right in 14. Success rate = 14 / 20 = 0.70. In the other 6 cases (20 − 14 = 6) the more complex model was right, so 6 / 20 = 0.30 (assuming exactly one of the two models was right in each case).
4. Lesson: logical underdetermination does not stop us from measuring how well a method works. In the author's terms, "how well did we do?" is an empirical question that draws on history of science (p. 223).

## Concepts

- [[scientific realism vs. constructive empiricism]] — realism, defined here as science aiming to describe real structure including the unobservable (p. 220).
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — Kuhn-style paradigm-dominated work, which may fit some fields and not others (p. 227).
- [[research programme (hard core, protective belt)]] — the views of Laudan and Lakatos, mentioned as possibly fitting some fields better (p. 227).
- [[naturalism]] (new) — philosophical problems, including epistemological ones, are tackled within a scientific picture of ourselves (p. 220).
- [[veil-of-ideas]] (new) — picture of the mind confined to its sensations, trying to reach a hypothetical outer world (p. 220).
- [[empiricism-reformed]] (new) — empiricism recast within naturalism: science is a strategy of exposing big ideas to experience (pp. 222–224).
- [[scientific-strategy]] (new) — testing even the biggest theoretical ideas by observation. It is a choice, not dictated by language or biology (p. 224).
- [[social-organization-of-science]] (new) — reward system and culture giving competition/cooperation at a time and cumulative work across times (pp. 224–225).
- [[engine-of-self-correction]] (new) — social mechanisms that reliably check and scrutinize ideas (p. 225).
- [[objectivity]] (new) — a loose family of senses: impartiality, or independent existence. The author finds it unhelpful and criticizes its false dichotomies (pp. 228–229).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Perception as inference under underdetermination ↔ scientific theory choice: both infer hidden causes from limited data, and reliability can be assessed empirically.
- [structural-analogy] Engine of self-correction ↔ computational social science: error-correcting feedback in a community of agents with incentives, which could be modeled.
- [metaphor] "Epistemology is not plumbing" ↔ network or systems models of knowledge: there is no single "source", only interacting channels.

## Questions this raises
- If the reliability of perception is itself established by science, is that circular? The text does not discuss this.
- How could one measure whether a "balance" between criticism and trust is good rather than merely stable, given Shapin's reluctance and the feminist and Feyerabend worries (pp. 225–226)?
- Can the simple empiricist label survive if different fields need different pluralist accounts (p. 227)?
- What would a good theory of "representation" look like, if reference and truth are not central (p. 230)?
