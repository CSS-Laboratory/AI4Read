# Induction and Confirmation

## L0 — One line
The logical empiricists tried to build a purely formal logic of confirmation, but the ravens problem and Goodman's grue riddle suggest that whether evidence confirms a hypothesis depends on background assumptions, on how the data were gathered, and on which categories we use.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What makes an observation evidence for a scientific theory? The logical empiricists wanted a logical theory of confirmation, treated as a relation between sentences. Godfrey-Smith separates induction, projection and explanatory inference, and shows that simple hypothetico-deductivism fails. He then presents two puzzles. The ravens problem (Hempel): a white shoe seems to confirm "all ravens are black". Goodman's grue riddle ("grue" is a made-up colour word tied to a date): two inductive arguments with the same form, one good and one bad. Goodman concludes that confirmation cannot be purely formal. The discussion suggests that it may depend on background knowledge, on whether the observation is a genuine test, and on the choice of terms or kinds.

**Prerequisites:** deductive validity and logical form; the idea of a generalization ("all F's are G"); the Hume-style worry about the future resembling the past; basic idea of a function fitted to data points.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Confirmation is the question of what links an observation to a theory so that it counts as evidence. The logical empiricists treated it as an abstract relation between sentences; the author says it has become fairly clear that this approach is doomed (p. 39).
- Hume's inductive scepticism: appealing to past success of induction begs the question, so there is no rational basis for expecting the future to resemble the past (pp. 39–40).
- The author uses "induction" narrowly, for inference from observed cases to a generalization. Projection goes from observed cases to the next case. Explanatory inference goes to a hypothesis about a structure or process. He suggests it may be wise not to rank either induction or explanatory inference as more fundamental (pp. 42–44).
- Simple hypothetico-deductivism ("a hypothesis is confirmed when its consequences are true") is hopeless in its simple form, as the T-or-S and T&S cases show (p. 45).
- Ravens problem: two plausible principles (Hempel's instance rule and equivalence) imply that a white shoe confirms "all ravens are black". Hempel accepts this; Good's reply and the order-of-observation reply suggest confirmation may depend on background knowledge and on whether there was a genuine test (pp. 46–49).
- The Wason selection task (a card-choosing test from psychology) has the same structure as the ravens problem. People tend to ignore the card that is a real test and choose one that is not (pp. 49–50).
- Goodman's grue argument ("grue" means: first observed before 2010 A.D. and green, or not first observed before 2010 A.D. and blue): arguments 2 and 3 have the same form but differ in quality, so no purely formal theory of induction exists. Deductive logic is unaffected (pp. 51–53).
- Each proposed fix for grue faces difficulties in the text: a ban on time-reference (Goodman says this is language-relative), Goodman's own appeal to terms with a history of normal use, natural kinds (kinds unified by real similarity), and simplicity. The problem links to curve-fitting and to categories in science (pp. 53–55).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 3.1 The Mother of All Problems (pp. 39–40)
- The central problem is how observations confirm a scientific theory, i.e. what connection makes an observation evidence for a theory (p. 39). The author calls it in some ways the fundamental problem of philosophy of science of the last hundred years. It does not vanish when logical empiricism is abandoned (p. 39).
- The logical empiricists aimed at a logical theory of evidence: confirmation as an abstract relation between sentences. The author says it has become fairly clear that this approach is doomed (p. 39).
- Related is the problem of induction: why expect observed patterns to continue, and what justifies generalizing from observed to unobserved cases (p. 39).
- **Hume's argument:** supposing the future totally unlike the past involves no contradiction. **Objection:** past reliance on experience has turned out well. **Hume's reply:** this begs the question, since it uses past successes to justify trust in the future, which is the issue at stake (p. 40).
- Hume concluded that we have no reason to expect the past to resemble the future (inductive scepticism). He did not suggest we stop using induction, which is psychologically natural, but held it has no rational basis (p. 40). Confirmation is not the same problem as induction, but is closely related (p. 40).

*Intuition note:* Hume's point is about circularity. Using "induction worked before" to defend induction already assumes that past success predicts future success.

### 3.2 Induction, Deduction, Confirmation, and Explanatory Inference (pp. 40–45)
- The logical empiricists sought to show how evidence supports a theory. They did not try to show theories could be proved (p. 40). They wanted one theory of confirmation covering simple cases (white swans) and cases like Copernicus or Darwin, where nobody generalized from many observed instances (p. 40). The theory was not a recipe for confirming theories. It was an account of the relations between theory-sentences and observation-sentences (p. 40).
- **Possible defence (the author's suggestion on their behalf):** scientific procedures might rest on assumptions described by the theory of confirmation. Perhaps scientists do things that cannot be justified if confirmation does not exist (pp. 40–41).
- **Deductive logic** transmits truth with certainty. In a deductively valid argument, true premises guarantee a true conclusion. Valid arguments with false premises may have a false conclusion, though it also might not (p. 41).
- **Why much scientific inference is non-deductive (logical empiricist reasoning):** all evidence comes from observation of particular cases, while science aims at generalizations with infinite range. No finite set of observations conclusively establishes such a generalization, so inference to it is non-deductive. One counterinstance, in contrast, suffices to refute it (p. 41).
- **Terminology.** Carnap used "induction" broadly for all good non-deductive argument. The author instead restricts "induction" to inference from particular observations to generalizations. Example: "All the many swans observed so far have been white", so all swans are white. "Enumerative" or "simple" induction is the traditional form. Mathematical induction is really deduction (p. 42).
- **Projection:** from observed cases to a prediction about the next case, e.g. the next swan will be white (p. 42).
- **Explanatory inference** (Peirce: "abductive"; also "inference to the best explanation"): inference to a hypothesis about a structure or process that would explain the data. Example: unusually high iridium levels in 65-million-year-old layers support the Alvarez hypothesis of a huge meteor impact, since iridium is more concentrated in meteors than near the earth's surface. The author sets aside the further claim that the impact caused the dinosaur extinction (pp. 42–43).
- **Which is fundamental?** Reichenbach claimed all non-deductive inference can be reconstructed from induction-like inference plus deduction. Carnap did not claim this but seemed to view induction as a model for the other kinds. Most logical empiricist work focused on induction (p. 43). Harman (1965) argued the reverse: inductions are justified only when they are explanatory inferences in disguise (p. 43).
- Explanatory inference seems far more common in actual science (p. 43). But traditional-looking inductions exist: Chargaff's rules (1947, from eight kinds of organisms), that C ≈ G and T ≈ A in DNA samples, rested on induction. The DNA structure now explains why they hold (pp. 43–44). The author suggests it may be a good idea to refuse to rank one kind as more fundamental, and says the argument from simplicity for a single kind is unconvincing (p. 44).
- **Two logical empiricist strategies:** Hempel: make inductive logic resemble deductive logic. Carnap: apply probability theory to artificial languages. Carnap's models needed more and more special assumptions and came to seem irrelevant to real science. The idea of using probability remains popular and is taken up in chapter 14 (p. 44).
- **Hypothetico-deductivism (simple version):** a hypothesis is confirmed when its logical consequences turn out true (p. 45). Glymour: hopeless in the simple form, yet fits many historical episodes (p. 45). Problems: (i) a hypothesis has testable consequences only together with other assumptions; (ii) any theory $T$ implies $T \lor S$ for any $S$. Observing $S$ (observational) establishes $T \lor S$, which would then confirm $T$, "obviously absurd"; (iii) if $T$ implies $E$, then $T \& S$ implies $E$, so $E$ confirms $T \& S$ for arbitrary $S$ (p. 45).
- The author asks whether the fault lies in the original idea, in its logical summary, or in basic logic. The logical empiricists kept the logic and modified the ideas about evidence. Their use of simplified artificial cases led some to see "logic-chopping" (pp. 45–46).

*Intuition note:* Simple logic says too much is confirmed. If "true consequence confirms" is read as pure logic, irrelevant add-ons ($S$) get confirmed for free.

### 3.3 The Ravens Problem (pp. 46–50)
- **A simple suggestion that fails:** each observed raven leaves one fewer way for the hypothesis to fail, so its probability rises. This fails because (i) for infinitely many instances the number of ways to fail does not shrink, and (ii) even for finitely many cases it is weak support and gives no help with projection to the next raven (p. 46).
- **Hempel's two principles:** (a) any observation of an F that is G supports "All F's are G"; (b) evidence confirming $H$ also confirms anything logically equivalent to $H$. Logical equivalence: it is impossible for one to be true and the other false (pp. 46–47).
- **The problem:** "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All nonblack things are not ravens". A white shoe fits the second, so by (b) it confirms "All ravens are black". Goodman's phrase: "indoor ornithology". Hempel discovered the problem himself, and there is no agreed solution (p. 47).
- **Hempel's response: accept it.** The white shoe confirms, presumably only by a tiny amount. "All F's are G" is a statement about everything: if something is an F it is G. On this reply, the shoe also confirms "all ravens are green" and "all aardvarks are blue". Most others are not comfortable with this (p. 47).
- **First reply (Good, 1967):** whether a black raven or white shoe confirms depends on other knowledge. Suppose we know either (1) all ravens are black and ravens are extremely rare, or (2) most ravens are black, a few are white, and ravens are common. Then casually seeing a black raven supports (2), which says not all ravens are black, because under (1) we would hardly see ravens (p. 47).
- Link to holism of testing (chapter 2): relevance of an observation depends on other assumptions. A biologist would not ask whether thousands of black ravens make "all ravens are black" likely, since genetics and bird coloration lead us to expect variation such as albinism (p. 48).
- **Second reply (order of observation):**
  - "I have a raven behind my back; see its colour?" You should say yes, because a white raven refutes your theory.
  - "I have a black object behind my back; see whether it's a raven?" You need not care. You hold that all ravens are black, not that all black things are ravens.
  - The same black raven is relevant in the first case and irrelevant in the second (p. 48).
  - For the shoe: "I have a white object; see what it is?" Yes, since a raven would refute you, and a shoe gives some support. "I have a shoe; see what colour?" You need not care, and there is no support (p. 48).
  - Proposal: confirmation occurs only when the observation arose in a genuine test, one that could have disconfirmed the hypothesis (pp. 48–49).
- **Hempel's view:** he wanted a relation between hypothesis and observation alone, regardless of extra information or order of observation. The author suggests no such relation exists. Hempel thought some observations are "automatically" relevant to hypotheses. The author agrees this holds for deductive refutation (a nonblack raven is bad news however it was found), but not for confirmation (p. 49). The order point does not fully solve the problem (why does order matter? what if both properties are observed at once?). The author returns to it in chapter 14 and argues confirmation must take account of how data were generated (p. 49).
- **Wason selection task** (Fig. 3.1): four cards with half masked. Card A shows a circle on the left, B a blank left, C a circle on the right, D a blank right. Question: which masks must be removed to test "if circle on left, then circle on right"? Many people answer "only A" or "A and C"; the correct answer is A and D (pp. 49–50). The structure matches the ravens: D is like "white object behind back" (a real test), C is like "black object behind back" (not a test, though it fits the hypothesis). The author suggests this may show why confirmation is hard to analyze. Not every observation that fits a hypothesis is a useful test (p. 50).

*Intuition note:* Evidence is not just "a case that fits". The case counts if it was found in a way that could have shown the hypothesis false.

### 3.4 Goodman's "New Riddle of Induction" (pp. 50–55)
- **Goodman's aim** (Goodman 1955): to show there cannot be a purely formal theory of confirmation. He does not think confirmation is impossible or induction a myth (p. 50).
- **Formality in deduction:** the Socrates argument is valid because of its form, "All F's are G; a is an F; so a is G", whatever the terms, provided they pick out definite properties and keep their meaning throughout (p. 51). The logical empiricists wanted this feature in a theory of induction (p. 51).
- **Argument 2:** All the many emeralds observed, in diverse circumstances, before 2010 A.D. have been green; so all emeralds are green. This looks like a good inductive argument. The author notes emeralds are green by definition in mineralogy and calls this an unfortunate example choice (pp. 51–52).
- **Argument 3:** the same, with "grue" in place of "green". Definition: an object is grue iff it was first observed before 2010 A.D. and is green, or was not first observed before 2010 A.D. and is blue (p. 52).
- Clarifications: grue objects are not strange, and an object need not change colour to be grue. Anything green and first observed before 2010 is grue, so all emeralds seen so far are grue. Argument 3 would lead us to expect future emeralds to be blue, and it conflicts with argument 2 (p. 52).
- **Result:** arguments 2 and 3 share the form "All the many E's observed, in diverse circumstances, prior to 2010 A.D., have been G; so all E's are G". One is good and the other bad, so goodness of an induction cannot be just its form, and there can be no purely formal theory of induction and confirmation. "Grue" causes no problem in deductive arguments (pp. 52–53).
- **The new riddle:** what exactly is wrong with argument 3? (p. 53)
- **Obvious fix:** restrict the terms allowed in inductions. "Green" is allowed and "grue" is not. Goodman says it is hard to spell out. If the fault is that "grue" is defined using a specific time, Goodman replies this is language-relative. Define bleen: first observed before 2010 and blue, or not first observed before 2010 and green. In a language where grue and bleen are basic, "green" is: first observed before 2010 and grue, or not first observed before 2010 and bleen. So which term "contains a reference to time" depends on the starting language (p. 53).
- **Goodman's own solution:** a good induction must use terms with a history of normal use in our community. Most philosophers disliked this, since it makes the value of inductions depend on which language we happen to use (pp. 53–54).
- **Natural kinds:** many instead focus on properties. Greenness might be a natural, objective feature and grueness not, so a good induction must use terms we have reason to think pick out natural kinds (kinds unified by real similarity). That leads to hard questions about properties and kinds (p. 54).
- **Links to science.**
  - *Curve-fitting* (Fig. 3.2): three data points lie almost on a straight line, suggesting a prediction for $x = 4$. Infinitely many functions fit the points as well or better but predict differently at $x = 4$. Choosing a strange one resembles preferring grue to green (p. 54).
  - *Simplicity* may guide choice, but is the green induction clearly simpler? Goodman: simplicity is language-relative. Also, simplicity is hard to justify when we want to learn what the world is like, because simpler theories are easier to use but there is no obvious reason the world should be simple (pp. 54–55).
  - *Categories:* the issue arises in economics and psychology. Examples: whether economies with very high inflation form a natural kind, and whether DSM IV disorders like "schizophrenia" are natural kinds. The periodic table seems to pick out real natural kinds, but the author asks whether this can be hoped for in all sciences (p. 55).
- **Closing:** these problems are simple but resistant to solution. The author returns to them in chapter 14, and the next chapter treats a philosophy motivated by these frustrations (pp. 55–56).

*Intuition note:* On Goodman's argument, the pattern "all observed so far were X" seems to support "all are X" for some X (green) but not for others (grue), although the two arguments have the same form. So a purely formal theory cannot say which Xs are fit for projecting into the future.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A small world has four hidden objects. For each we know one fact and the other fact is hidden:

| Object | Known | Hidden |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | is a raven | colour |
| 2 | is not a raven | colour |
| 3 | is black | species |
| 4 | is not black | species |

Hypothesis $H$: all ravens are black. Which unmaskings could refute $H$?
- Object 1: if the hidden colour is nonblack, we get a nonblack raven, so $H$ is refuted. It can test.
- Object 2: a non-raven cannot violate $H$ whatever its colour. It cannot test.
- Object 3: black things cannot violate $H$ whatever the species. It cannot test.
- Object 4: if the hidden species is raven, we get a nonblack raven, so $H$ is refuted. It can test.

So only objects 1 and 4 are genuine tests, as in the selection task (cards A and D) and the "behind the back" cases (pp. 48–50). If object 4 turns out to be a white shoe, the text's second reply says this gives some support, since a refutation was possible. If object 3 is shown to be a black raven, it gives no support, because the check could not have refuted $H$ (p. 48).

## Questions this raises
- Why should the order in which properties are learned, or the procedure that generated the data, matter for confirmation (promised in chapter 14)?
- If "green" counts as natural and "grue" not, what makes a kind natural, and does the answer differ across sciences?
- Can probability theory handle the ravens and grue problems without special assumptions, given Carnap's difficulties?
- If one counterinstance refutes a generalization, can science do without confirmation (the next chapter's theme)?
