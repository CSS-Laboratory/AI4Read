# Naturalism and the Social Structure of Science

## L0 — One line
Naturalistic philosophers (Hull, Kitcher, Strevens) explain how science works by asking how its reward system makes selfish individual behaviour serve the goals of science as a whole, though Godfrey-Smith thinks this harmony may be overstated.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Traditional empiricism ignored the social side of science. This chapter asks how individual motives and the community's goals relate. Hull says scientists want their work *used*. This desire produces citation, selective checking and the seriousness of fraud. Kitcher models how a reward system could spread workers sensibly across rival research programs. Strevens improves on that model. Godfrey-Smith concludes that science exploits experience at the level of social groups, in a public dialogue between speculative and critical voices. He suggests that Hull's claim of harmony between individual and group benefit may be overstated, citing feminist critiques and the Skinner/Tolman case, and he notes that science's internal culture may change.

**Prerequisites:** expected payoff (probability × reward); decreasing marginal returns; Kuhn's and Merton's accounts of science (chs. 6, 8, as recalled here).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Hull: the main professional motive is recognition, above all having one's work used and cited. Merton stresses being first. (p. 164)
- The desire for use generates citation networks, selective replication, and the special gravity of fraud. Checking is a by-product of self-interest, not goodwill. (pp. 164–165)
- Hull gives a *mechanism* for Popper's dialogue between imagination and criticism. Roles are spread across people, so there is no need for each scientist to be cautious and skeptical about their own work. (p. 165)
- The Hull–Kuhn–Smith structure is "invisible hand"-like, but Hull's hand is not very hidden. Hull's evolutionary analogy for science is, in Godfrey-Smith's view, interesting but has so far yielded few new insights. (pp. 165–167)
- Kitcher: a "ruler of science" would often hedge bets across rival programs. Equal sharing of a fixed prize among successful workers leads selfish individuals to something similar. (pp. 167–168)
- Strevens argues that Kitcher's scheme invites free riding, and that rewards proportional to contribution are better and more realistic. (p. 169)
- In the author's naturalistic sketch, science pursues its empirical strategy at the group level: a literal dialogue, with some dogmatic members tolerated if the group stays responsive to evidence. (p. 170)
- Hull's harmony thesis may be overstated: feminist worries, and the Skinner/Tolman case. The reward culture may also be changing. (pp. 170–171)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 11.1 Science as a Process
**Hull's starting point.** Naturalistic philosophy tries to avoid empiricism's neglect of social structure. Hull's *Science as a Process* (1988) draws on decades among systematic biologists. Scientists are curious like other people. The social structure of science puts that curiosity to work through empirical testing. What is special is an unusually good fit between individual motives and the goals of science as a whole (p. 163). Hull and Merton hold that science runs on a combination of cooperation and competition, and neither is fundamental. Its special features come from their interaction via the reward system (p. 163).

**Recognition as use.** Hull: the main professional motive is recognition, and especially *use*. Scientists want others to use their work and give credit. Merton stresses being first to have an idea. Hull counts as a real reward the use of a later, more convenient version of an idea (p. 164).

**What follows from the desire for use** (Hull's argument, p. 164):
- Everyone inherits ideas and methods, so no one can contribute without using others' work. To use others' work as support, one must cite it. So the desire to be used requires using and citing. Scientists trade credit for support. This is a special kind of self-interest, not mainly goodwill.
- Replication is stressed in traditional accounts, but no one has time to do much of it. Checking often falls to those who need to rely on a result, or whose own work it discredits or makes less important.
- Fraud is worse than theft because all who used fraudulent work find their own work on the topic deemed unreliable. Theft or plagiarism harms only the victim.

**Harmony.** Hull argues the system sometimes malfunctions, but the general result is harmony between individual behaviour and science's goals. Examples: teamwork, close scrutiny of hypotheses, discouraged shoddy work, free sharing of publishable ideas, and serious treatment of low-status work (p. 165).

**Contrast with Popper.** Popper and others picture science as a dialogue between an imaginative and a critical voice. Hull offers a mechanism for it. Roles are distributed across people, so there is no need for each scientist to be cautious about their own work (p. 165).

**Invisible hand.** Earlier (ch. 6), Kuhn's account was likened to Adam Smith's argument. In both, selfish or dogmatic individuals produce a good whole. Hull says his hand is not really invisible, because the link between individual and group is not so unexpected (p. 165).

**Where does the desire come from?** Hull assumes curiosity and recognition are basic motives. Godfrey-Smith suggests (his own view) that the specific desire for credit-as-use is unusual. Basic credit-seeking is probably *shaped* by science's culture. There is probably also *selection*, since those who dislike the system may not finish graduate school (pp. 165–166). Hull and Merton both look at possible roles for broader cultural features (p. 166). The author gives these points:
- European societies were comfortable with individual credit.
- The French Academy's early communal handling of credit failed, so it went individualistic.
- Oldenburg's Royal Society used rapid publication in the Proceedings to allocate credit, plus anonymous refereeing. This is basically today's system.

**Evolutionary analogy.** Hull treats scientific change as variation and selection. Ideas replicate like genes, at different rates depending on their manifestation in brains and public systems (books, journals, computers). Some ideas outcompete others (p. 166). Toulmin, Campbell and Dennett tried similar analogies, and Popper's view has one (p. 166). Godfrey-Smith: the analogy has not yielded much new insight, as with other cultural-evolution attempts. Biological populations have special features that make evolutionary theory useful; other systems, which lack these features, can be described in evolutionary terms with a bit of "shoehorning," but we do not seem to gain much. The analogy can still be interesting without founding a new theory of science (pp. 166–167).

*Intuition note.* For Hull, cooperation in science is not primarily goodwill but a special kind of self-interest. Wanting to be used pushes people to cite and to check what they rely on, and it helps explain why fraud is taken so seriously. The good behaviour comes largely from the incentive structure.

### 11.2 Kitcher and the Division of Scientific Labor
**Gap in Lakatos and Laudan.** They described competing research programs and gave rules for individual rational choice. The author says we can also ask what distribution of workers across rival programs is best *for the community*; Kitcher takes up this question (p. 167).

**The ruler's problem.** Suppose you allocate resources "from above." Two approaches address one problem. Program 1 looks more promising, but no one knows which will work. Either one succeeds while the other fails, or both fail. How do you maximize the chance of solving the problem? In many situations the best choice is not everything to one program. Some "bet-hedging" will often be advisable, even when one program is obviously more promising (p. 167). The answer depends on how much more promising one program is and on how each program responds to extra resources. Kitcher represents this mathematically (p. 167).

**Decreasing marginal return.** In the simple case, each added worker raises success chances less and less. After a point extra workers are better used on the alternative. Unless the total pool is small and the difference in promise large, the best distribution puts some workers on each program (pp. 167–168).

**Individual choice and reward systems.** Which reward system will tend to make individual choices produce the distribution the ruler would want? Kitcher's options (p. 168):
1. A fixed reward to everyone on the eventually successful program. This would send everyone to the more promising program ("all its eggs in one basket").
2. Reward for choices that maximize the community's chance of success. This would work in principle but is unrealistic.
3. Only workers on the successful program are rewarded, and the "pie" is divided equally among them. An individual's reward depends on how many others chose that program.

**Why option 3 works (Kitcher's argument).** A crowded program offers a small share. The less promising program, if it succeeds, has fewer sharers. So an individual maximizing expected payoff will often choose it. Selfish choices then yield a good community outcome. Kitcher suggests this is, with simplifications, close to real science; the pie is prestige, not cash (p. 168). The structure is an "invisible hand" one: the outcome may be something individuals are uninterested in or unaware of (pp. 168–169).

**Strevens's correction (2003).** Kitcher was too optimistic. Equal sharing gives a fairly good but often not the best distribution. Sometimes you do best to join the more promising program even though you add little to its chance, because others have already made success likely. Your efforts would have been more productive on the alternative. So free riding is encouraged (p. 169). Strevens's scheme gives only the successful program's workers a payoff. It is *unequal* and proportional to each worker's contribution to the program. Early joiners who made a big difference get more than late joiners who made little. Strevens argues this is both better for the community and closer to actual science (p. 169).

**Summary.** Hull, Kitcher, Strevens and others study how individual incentive relates to community success. Hull most bluntly: science has hit on an effective way of coordinating individual energies (p. 169). The model presented is only the simplest part of a complicated one (p. 169).

*Intuition note.* If a prize is split among everyone who backs the winner, crowded options pay less each. Self-interest then spreads people out. Strevens adds that splitting equally lets latecomers coast on others' work, so paying by contribution is better.

### 11.3 Social Structure and Empiricism
**Science as a social strategy.** Continuing the sketch of naturalistic empiricism from ch. 10, science exploits humans' contact with the world through experience to assess hypotheses. A lone individual can follow this strategy only to a very limited extent, and the result lacks science's distinctive features. Science's power is cumulative and coordinated: each generation builds on the last, and organizes itself through collaboration and public discussion. The speculative/critical dialogue can then be literal, not internalized in one mind. The group can include members wedded to their own ideas, provided the group remains flexible and responsive to evidence (p. 170).

**Needed supports.** A suitable reward system and external supports are needed. Obvious ones: scientists can make a living, and society allows open-ended questioning. More subtle: Merton's and Hull's work suggests a specific internal culture and a delicate competition/cooperation balance (p. 170). Open questions (the author's): could a slightly different reward system work as well or better? Is intense, egoistic competition needed? Fans of competitive societies will be inclined to say yes; those preferring communitarian or socialist ones may say no (p. 170).

**Feminist point.** Some feminists hold that science's competitive, individualist culture suits men's temperaments more than women's, hampering many women. If this is real, and if women bring a different style of thought and science benefits from such diversity, then (with "a lot of 'ifs'") the competitive culture will tend to produce subtle uniformity and reduce the frequency of a valuable input (p. 170).

**Skinner and Tolman.** Hull discusses sociological work on temperament and leadership of successful scientists. Aggressive faith in one's ideas can be useful in at least some fields. A survey-based study of famous US psychologists contrasted two behaviorists (p. 171):
- Skinner was dogmatic and pushy, with an almost absurdly strict version of behaviorism.
- Tolman was modest, open-minded and considerate, with a more flexible version.
- Skinner had much more influence. Temperament's role cannot be known for sure, but the data are suggestive. Hull found similar results in a smaller study.

Godfrey-Smith *conjectures* (as would many psychologists) that psychology would have been far better with Tolman dominant; some Tolman ideas are being revived. He *suspects* Hull would reply that the feminist and Skinner–Tolman problems are a small price for the present balance (p. 171).

**Change may be coming.** The ideas of Merton, Kuhn, Hull and Kitcher might describe science from the seventeenth to the twentieth century, but change may well be in the air (Ziman 2000). Big financial rewards are becoming more visible, especially in biotechnology. Kuhn warned that insulation from external political and economic pushes was a key source of strength. We do not know how fragile science's social structure is (p. 171).

**Closing.** Naturalists hope combining philosophical analysis with input from other disciplines will yield a complete picture of how science works and what connection it gives us to the world. Sociology of science and Science Studies have often mishandled that connection; this is the topic of ch. 12 (p. 172).

*Intuition note.* Individual advantage and community benefit can come apart, as Skinner's success suggests. The author flags this as suggestive, not settled.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Simplification: success probabilities are fixed (A = 0.6, B = 0.3), unlike Kitcher's decreasing-returns functions. The prize is 1 unit of prestige, split equally among workers on the successful program. Expected payoff of a worker on a program with $n$ workers is $p/n$. There are 6 workers.

- Try 4 on A, 2 on B. Each A worker gets $0.6/4 = 0.15$. Each B worker gets $0.3/2 = 0.15$.
- If an A worker switches to B, they get $0.3/3 = 0.10 < 0.15$.
- If a B worker switches to A, they get $0.6/5 = 0.12 < 0.15$.
- So nobody gains by switching. The self-interested split is 4/2, not 6/0: with 6 on A, a lone B worker would expect $0.3/1 = 0.3 > 0.6/6 = 0.1$.

Selfish choices spread workers across both programs, as in Kitcher's argument (p. 168).
