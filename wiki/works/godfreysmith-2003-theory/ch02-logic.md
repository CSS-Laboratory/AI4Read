# Logic Plus Empiricism

## L0 — One line
Logical positivism and logical empiricism tried to rebuild philosophy of science on logic, a theory of meaning and experience alone, and the view eventually declined. The author thinks an important mistake was refusing to let science describe hidden structure.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What did logical positivism and its later form, logical empiricism, claim about language and science, and why did they decline? Godfrey-Smith traces empiricism from Locke to the Vienna Circle. The positivists used the analytic–synthetic distinction and the verifiability theory of meaning to dismiss much traditional philosophy, and they treated logic as philosophy's main tool. Quine's holism, failures in inductive logic, and pressure from history, psychology and realism eroded the view. The author judges that empiricism erred in denying that science describes hidden structure. The unit explains where many later debates in philosophy of science come from.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a valid argument; "and" in logic (A&B is false if A is false); the everyday notion of testing a hypothesis.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Empiricism has tended to lapse into skepticism and sometimes into phenomenalism, partly because of the "veil of ideas" picture of the mind (pp. 19–20).
- The logical positivists (Vienna Circle) were a scientific, Enlightenment-minded movement defined partly by what they opposed: Hegelian idealism, Heidegger-style obscurity, and nationalism (pp. 22–25).
- Their language theory had two pillars: the analytic–synthetic distinction (logic and mathematics are analytic) and the verifiability theory of meaning (pp. 25–27).
- Their view of science: its aim is to track and predict patterns in experience, never with certainty. Logic, deductive and inductive, is the main philosophical tool. They sharply separated logic from history and psychology (pp. 28–30).
- Verifiability was hard to formulate (the "and" counterexample), and Quine's holism threatened both the verifiability link and the analytic–synthetic distinction (pp. 30–33).
- Logical empiricism replaced sentence-by-sentence verifiability with a holistic theory of meaning but kept the observational/theoretical split and the focus on logic (pp. 34–35).
- Empiricists would not accept that theoretical terms describe hidden reality (Hempel's "dilemma"). The author argues this was a mistake: science has "depths" (pp. 35–37).
- Decline came from several factors: breakdown of the language view, holism, failed inductive logic, a new role for history and psychology, and later scientific realism (p. 37).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 2.1 The Empiricist Tradition (pp. 19–22)
- The unit covers a revolutionary early-twentieth-century empiricism. The early form is "logical positivism"; the later, moderate form is usually "logical empiricism." Terminology varies, and "logical empiricism" is sometimes used for the whole movement (p. 19).
- Empiricism is often summarised (chapter 1) as the claim that the only source of knowledge is experience. Its classical stage (Locke, Berkeley, Hume) rested on a "sensationalist" theory of mind. Sensations (patches of colour, sounds) are all the mind has access to, and thought tracks patterns in them. The author says this theory of mind is not implied by the basic empiricist idea (p. 19).
- Empiricism tends to lapse into **skepticism** (the claim that we cannot know anything about the world) in two forms. *External world skepticism* asks how we can know anything about the real world behind the flow of sensations. *Inductive skepticism* (made vivid by Hume) asks why patterns in past experience should hold in the future (pp. 19–20).
- Empiricists often gave up on the first problem. **Phenomenalism** holds that our concept of the world is just a concept of a patterned collection of sensations. Mill defined matter as "a Permanent Possibility of Sensation"; Mach drew the world as seen through his own left eye (Fig. 2.1, p. 21). Hume gave up on both kinds of skepticism, which the author calls unusual (p. 20).
- The author calls phenomenalism strange. Empiricists drifted into it partly through a picture of the mind confined behind a "veil of ideas." He and many others think this picture is a mistake, but it is hard to build an empiricism free of its influence (p. 20).
- Historical background: rationalists (Descartes, Leibniz) held that pure reasoning can yield knowledge independent of experience, with mathematics as the model. Empiricists (Locke, Hume) held that experience is our only way of finding out what the world is like. Kant took an intermediate position: thinking involves an interaction between experience and preexisting mental structures. Space, time and causation cannot be derived from experience, since one needs them to learn from experience. He held that mathematics gives real knowledge of the world without needing experience to justify it (pp. 20–21).
- **Author's view:** empiricists must avoid overly simple pictures of how experience affects belief. The mind does not passively receive facts, and its active role must be recognised, while staying true to basic empiricist principles (p. 21).
- In twentieth-century discussions of science, "rationalist" was often a broad label for confidence in human reason, and the views so called were often forms of empiricism (p. 21).
- Sweeping generalisation: the empiricist tradition has tended to be pro-science, worldly rather than religious, and politically moderate or liberal (Hume, Mill, Russell). Religion has the most counterexamples (Berkeley was a bishop; van Fraassen is religious). The logical positivists fit the pattern (p. 22).

> **Intuition note.** Empiricism is often summarised as the claim that experience is the only source of knowledge. If experience is pictured as private sensations behind a "veil," the world beyond them becomes hard to reach, which, the author suggests, partly explains why empiricists have drifted towards skepticism or phenomenalism (p. 20). He adds that it is hard to set up an empiricism free of this picture.

### 2.2 The Vienna Circle (pp. 22–25)
- Logical positivism was a European empiricism after World War I, built by scientifically oriented people who disliked much contemporary philosophy: the Vienna Circle, founded by Schlick and Neurath, with Carnap as a central figure (p. 22).
- "Positivism" comes from Comte. Carnap suggested in the 1930s the name "logical empiricism." This does not mean later stages were "more empiricist"; the author says the opposite is true. The author uses "logical positivism" for the intense early version, and "logical empiricism" for the moderate later one. Ideas changed most markedly after World War II (p. 22).
- The positivists drew inspiration from Einstein and from developments in logic, mathematics and philosophy of language. They hoped to settle philosophy's problems "once and for all": some solved, others rejected as meaningless. Their language views were influenced by early Wittgenstein, who was not an empiricist. Some say they adapted him, others that they misinterpreted him (pp. 22–23).
- What they opposed: post-Kant systems they found pretentious, obscure, dogmatic and politically harmful. Hegel's "absolute idealism" held that reality as a whole has a spiritual or rational character; history is a world spirit becoming conscious of itself, and the state matters more than individuals. These ideas were taken to support nationalism. Reichenbach opened *The Rise of Scientific Philosophy* with an obscure Hegel passage and said the fault lies with Hegel, since whatever factual meaning the passage might convey is "smothered with misused language" (pp. 23–24).
- Heidegger was another rival. The author borrows Sheehan's summary: mortality defines us, and finite meaning is all the reality we get. The author simplifies this to the idea that life rests on practical coping rather than knowledge. Heidegger mixed descriptions of lived experience with metaphysical speculation about "Nothing." Carnap gave humorous logical dissections of this. Recent work (Friedman 2000) shows that Carnap and Heidegger understood each other better than once supposed (p. 24).
- The author says it was not a simple pitched battle: many philosophies jostled, including a "back to Kant" movement (p. 24).
- Positivism was a plea for Enlightenment values against mysticism, romanticism and nationalism. The positivists were internationalists who liked the idea of a universal precise language. Neurath and others were democratic socialists, interested in Bauhaus-type movements (pp. 24–25).
- The Circle flourished from the mid-1920s to the mid-1930s. Ayer's *Language, Truth, and Logic* (1936) brought the ideas to England, where philosophy abandoned absolute idealism and returned to empiricism (p. 25).
- The Nazis persecuted the positivists, to varying degrees. Heidegger joined the Nazi party in 1933. Many positivists fled, especially to the United States (Carnap, Reichenbach, Hempel, Feigl). Schlick stayed and was murdered by a former student in 1936. In the U.S. their voice was moderated, partly by criticisms from sympathisers and, the author says, no doubt partly by the different climate (p. 25).

### 2.3 Central Ideas of Logical Positivism (pp. 25–30)
**Two ideas in the theory of language (p. 25).**

*Analytic–synthetic distinction.* An **analytic** sentence is true or false simply in virtue of its meaning, whatever the world is like. A **synthetic** sentence is true or false in virtue of both its meaning and how the world is. Example: "All bachelors are unmarried" is analytic; "All bachelors are bald" is synthetic (false). Analytic truths are "empty," with no factual content. Their necessity is only because they are empty. Kant introduced the terminology (pp. 25–26).

- The positivists claimed that all of logic and mathematics is analytic. This let them treat mathematical knowledge within empiricism. Mathematical propositions record conventional decisions to use symbols in a particular way. Synthetic claims about the world can still be expressed in mathematical language (e.g. there are nine planets). A surprising proof, once broken into small steps, was held to be trivial at each step (p. 26).
- The *a priori* (known independently of experience): the positivists held that the only things seeming to be knowable a priori are analytic and so empty of factual content (p. 26).
- Geometry example: Kant, impressed by Newtonian physics, claimed Euclid's geometry is synthetic and a priori. In the 19th century non-Euclidean geometries were developed as mathematical exercises. Einstein's physics then showed that a non-Euclidean geometry is true of our world. The positivists split geometry into two parts: a pure, analytic part that describes possible geometrical systems, and a synthetic part that says which system applies to our world (p. 26).

*Verifiability theory of meaning.* It applies only to non-analytic sentences, and to the kind of meaning involved in saying something about the world (p. 27).
- Formulations: the meaning of a sentence is its method of verification; knowing the meaning is knowing how to verify it; a sentence with no possible method of verification has no meaning (p. 27).
- "Verification" meant verification by observation, broadly construed. The author says "testability" would be a better word, since testing tries to find out whether something is true or false (p. 27).
- It concerns verifiability in principle, not in practice. Conclusive verification was not required. There only had to be the possibility of observational evidence counting for or against the claim (p. 27).
- Early on, the idea was that all factually meaningful sentences could be translated into sentences about sensations and their patterns. This translation program was dropped as too extreme, but the verifiability theory was kept (p. 27).
- Use as a "weapon": science and everyday talk are verifiable and meaningful. Poetry and expressions of emotion fail the test harmlessly. Parts of language that are supposed to say something about the world but fail include, for the positivists, most traditional philosophy, much of ethics, and theology (p. 27).

> **Intuition note.** Positivism ties meaning to possible observation. A factual claim that no observation could ever count for or against would, on this view, say nothing. This is the tool used to sort science from "blather."

**Picture of science (pp. 28–30).**
- Science was seen as a more sophisticated version of everyday reasoning and problem-solving (p. 28).
- *Observational vs theoretical language.* "Red" is observational and "electron" is theoretical. At sentence level, "The rod is glowing red" is observational and "Helium atoms each contain two electrons" is theoretical. Where to draw the line was disputed. Schlick: only terms referring to sensations are observational. Neurath: terms for many ordinary physical objects are observational, since only statements about physical objects allow public, "intersubjective" testing. Carnap came to think many ways of marking the distinction are acceptable, and this led him towards "tolerance" of alternative linguistic frameworks (p. 28).
- *Logic.* For the positivists, logic is the main tool of philosophy, and about the only useful thing philosophers can do is give logical analyses of language, mathematics and science. **Deductive logic** describes patterns of argument where, if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. **Inductive logic** was supposed to be a theory of arguments that support their conclusions without a guarantee. This was (and is) much more controversial (pp. 28–29).
- The positivists embraced that error is always possible, and did not think science reaches absolute certainty, despite some critics' claims (p. 29).
- Logical analysis of science was kept sharply distinct from the history or psychology of science. Reichenbach's "context of discovery" vs "context of justification" is, per the author, a misleading wording. The real distinction is between the study of the logical structure of science and the study of its historical and psychological aspects, not before vs after. The author notes that dismissing history and psychology came, in time, to be regarded as a big mistake (p. 29).
- Summary: the aim of science and everyday thought is to track and anticipate patterns in experience. Schlick: scientists seek "the rules which govern the connection of experiences, and by which alone they can be predicted." Predictions are rational but never guaranteed. Traditional philosophy, seeking another route to knowledge, lapses into meaninglessness (pp. 29–30).
- Interpretation is controversial. Some recent writers (Friedman 1999) argue for a weaker link to traditional empiricism, but in the book's sense of empiricism the link is strong (p. 30).
- *Operationalism* (Bridgman 1927): scientists should tie all theoretical terms closely to direct observational tests. It was a proposed tightening of scientific language rather than an analysis of how science already works (p. 30).
- The author calls the later image of the positivists as stodgy, conservative or antidemocratic very unfair given their actual politics, though their writing was often dry and technical. Neurath's metaphor: we are "like sailors who have to rebuild their ship on the open sea," replacing planks one by one while keeping the ship afloat (p. 30).

### 2.4 Problems and Changes (pp. 30–33)
- *Internal problems.* Formulating verifiability was hard. If "Metals expand when heated" is testable, so is "Metals expand when heated and the Absolute Spirit is perfect," because if the first part is false the whole conjunction is false (if A is false then A&B must be false). Patching this led to new problems (Hempel 1965) (p. 31). Inductive logic also ran into serious trouble (chapter 3) (p. 31).
- *Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1953)* argued for a holistic theory of testing and, through it, of meaning (p. 31).
- **Holism about testing:** a single hypothesis cannot be tested in isolation, only networks of claims and assumptions, because only the network makes definite predictions. Testing a claim needs assumptions about instruments, circumstances, records and other observers, so you test a long conjunction. If a test goes wrong, something in the conjunction is false, but the failure does not say where (p. 31).
- Example: testing "high air pressure goes with fair, stable weather." You observe high pressure with unstable weather. The hypothesis may be wrong, but the barometer may be unreliable, or the weather observations may be mistaken (pp. 31–32).
- **Author's assessment:** parts of this are convincing. Only a network tells us what to expect, and a failed prediction has a range of possible explanations. But we may often have good reasons to blame one place rather than another, and science seems to have effective ways of deciding. Giving a theory of these decisions is difficult. The mere fact that failed predictions have many possible explanations does not settle the holism debate (p. 32).
- Quine's metaphor: theories "face the tribunal of sense-experience . . . as a corporate body" (1953, 41). He said mainstream empiricism had a simplistic view of testing and must be replaced by a holistic empiricism (p. 32).
- *The puzzle:* the positivists already accepted this kind of holism. Feigl (1943): "No scientific assumption is testable in complete isolation." Carnap (1937) and Ayer said similar things. Quine credited Duhem ("Duhem–Quine thesis"). Yet many philosophers agreed with Quine that the positivists had erred (p. 32).
- **Author's tentative resolution** ("it might be fair to say"): the positivists officially accepted holism but did not appreciate its significance. Verifiability seems to attach observable outcomes to each sentence in isolation. Given the link between testing and meaning, holism about testing leads to holism about meaning, which causes problems for many positivist ideas (pp. 32–33).
- *Attack on analytic–synthetic.* Quine denied the distinction exists. Part of his argument was aimed at a version the positivists no longer held. **Quine:** analyticity treats some claims as immune to revision, but none is. **Author:** Carnap already allowed analytic statements to be revised, by dropping one linguistic and logical framework for another. Within a framework some statements are analytic and untestable, but frameworks can change (p. 33).
- The author finds more convincing Quine's argument that there is no scientific sense to a sharp distinction. All our beliefs form a single "web of belief" that meets experience only as a whole. An unexpected observation can prompt many possible changes. Even sentences that look analytic can be revised, and Quine noted that quantum physics had led some to suggest revising logic (p. 33).

> **Intuition note.** On the holist argument, testing one claim needs a bundle of other assumptions. When a prediction fails, something in the bundle is false, but the failed test itself does not say where. Quine's picture of a single web of belief led him to hold that no sentence, not even an apparently analytic one, is immune to revision (p. 33). The author finds the holist point about testing partly convincing but not decisive (p. 32).

### 2.5 Logical Empiricism (pp. 34–37)
- After World War II, the aim of destroying traditional philosophy became careful logical analysis of language and science. Socialist-political aims were dropped or muted. The analytic–synthetic distinction was not rejected but was questioned, under pressure from Quine (p. 34).
- Verifiability was replaced by a holistic empiricist theory of meaning. A theory is an abstract structure connecting many hypotheses, connected as a whole to the observable realm. A term such as "electron" gets its meaning from its place in the structure and the structure's link to observation (p. 34). Feigl's 1970 diagram (Fig. 2.2) shows postulates linked in stages, through primitive, defined and empirical concepts, to the "soil" of experience. Feigl applied it to a single theory, whereas Quine's holism covers a whole person's beliefs (p. 34, p. 35).
- The observational/theoretical distinction was roughly kept, but observation was now described in terms of observable physical objects, not private sensations (Carnap thought a sensation language might occasionally be useful). The role of logic and the separation from history and psychology were unchanged (p. 34).
- Example of their work: explanation. For Hempel, to explain something is to show how to infer it by a logical argument whose premises include at least one natural law (Hempel and Oppenheim 1948; Hempel 1965) (p. 35).
- *Do theoretical terms describe the unobservable?* Positivists said science only describes patterns in observables. Logical empiricists "agonized"; in their hearts the answer was yes, but it became harder to defend. In "The Theoretician's Dilemma" (1958), Hempel, as a fairly traditional empiricist, was attracted to the idea that these parts of language only help pick out patterns in observables; if they are any good, their "goodness" has to show up in how the theory handles observables, so there would be no justification for taking them to describe real objects beyond experience. But Hempel and the logical empiricists had to concede that this view does not make much sense of actual scientific work: scientists who use "electron" or "gene" as if doing more than tracking patterns (pp. 35–36).
- Why they resisted: they distrusted "layered" views of reality like Plato's, with a perfect realm behind appearances. The author replies that hidden structures posited in science are not "pure and perfect" or "more real," and what is unobservable now may become observable later. Still, much science posits entities truly hidden at the time. Carnap, Hahn and Neurath: "In science there are no 'depths'" (p. 36). Prediction became the permitted goal in place of the forbidden one of describing hidden structure (p. 37).
- **Author's verdict:** twentieth-century empiricism made an important mistake. We can make sense of science only by treating much of it as an attempt to describe hidden structures behind observable phenomena, a version of scientific realism (discussed later). There is no fixed two-layer split but a continuum of accessibility: genes are less hidden than electrons, and electrons less hidden than quarks. What is deep may later surface (p. 37).

> **Intuition note.** Empiricists feared that "hidden reality" talk would revive Plato. The author says one can accept hidden structure without a perfect second world, since "hidden" is a matter of degree and changes over time.

### 2.6 On the Fall of Logical Empiricism (p. 37)
- By the mid-1960s the view was under threat, and by the middle or late 1970s near extinction. Factors: breakdown of the underlying view of language; holist arguments; the frustrating history of inductive logic (chapter 3); a new role for history and psychology (chapters 5–7); and eventually scientific realism, which was possible only after logical empiricism had begun to decline (p. 37).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Holism in miniature. Suppose a prediction follows only from three claims jointly: H ("high pressure goes with fair weather"), B ("my barometer is reliable") and W ("my weather observations are accurate"). The prediction fails.

- Each claim is true (T) or false (F), so there are $2^3 = 8$ combinations.
- The prediction is guaranteed only when H, B and W are all true. That is the single combination TTT.
- A failure rules out TTT only. The other $8 - 1 = 7$ combinations remain possible: FTT, TFT, TTF, FFT, FTF, TFF, FFF.
- So the failure does not say which of H, B, W is false. H is false in 4 of the 7 (FTT, FFT, FTF, FFF): only H is wrong in FTT, and H together with other claims in the rest. H is true in the other 3 (TFT, TTF, TFF).
- If you then check the barometer independently and find it works (B true), the remaining combinations are FTT, TTF, FTF. Evidence beyond the failed test helps place the blame. This matches the author's point that holism alone does not settle where blame goes (p. 32).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Duhem–Quine holism (a failed prediction impugns a conjunction) ↔ logic: the conjunction A&B is false when one conjunct is false, but its falsity does not say which — same logical point, applied to testing.
- [structural-analogy] Verifiability theory of meaning ↔ Popper-style falsifiability (canon link; not stated in this unit): both tie scientific standing to possible observational outcomes.
- [structural-analogy] Quine's web of belief ↔ Neurath's ship: both picture revision as piecemeal, with no fixed foundation outside the system.
- [metaphor] Feigl's "soil" of experience anchoring a theory network ↔ model validation in data science: observable outputs anchor a model whose internals are never directly checked.

## Questions this raises
- If holism says failed tests do not locate the error, what "effective ways" does science use to place blame (p. 32)?
- Can the verifiability principle be stated so as to exclude traditional philosophy but include all of science (pp. 30–31)?
- If analytic statements can be revised by changing frameworks (Carnap), how real is the difference from Quine's view that none is immune (p. 33)?
- How can a theory describe "hidden" structure without reviving a Platonic two-layer picture of reality (pp. 36–37)?
