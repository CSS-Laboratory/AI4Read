# Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse (Hall, 1973)

## L0 — One line
Television messages are encoded into a meaningful discourse and then decoded; the two meanings need not match, and Hall argues that in societies like ours communication between broadcasting elites and audiences is necessarily "systematically distorted", so that beyond the denotative level, making it more effective is not a neutral technical task but a political one.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Hall asks how formal study of "televisual language" can be joined to societal structure and cultural policy. He treats the message as a coded discourse sitting between two moments, encoding and decoding. These are linked to production and reception structures, but the codes of the two sides may not be symmetrical. Using the TV Western, he argues that meaning, including the meaning of violence, depends on codes and context, not on counting content. Connotative meanings are structured in dominance, not closed. Hall offers four decoding positions: dominant, professional, negotiated, oppositional. Beyond the literal level, cultural policies for "better communication" are hard to call neutral; Hall says misreading the problem as technical is unconscious collusion with dominant interests.

**Prerequisites:** sign, signifier and signified; denotation (literal meaning) vs connotation (associated, ideological meaning); polysemy (one sign, more than one possible meaning); the idea of ideology; the effects-research tradition (violence, "selective perception"). Below, "message-form" means the coded, symbolic shape a message takes, and a "determinate moment" is a stage that cannot be skipped or ignored.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The whole communicative circuit matters, but the symbolic message-form is a determinate moment: an event must become a "story" to be communicated (pp. 1–2).
- Production, circulation and reception are linked moments of one process; encoded meaning (I) and decoded meaning (II) may differ, so "misunderstanding" depends on asymmetry of positions and codes (pp. 3–4).
- Hall hopes a semiotic approach will dispel the "lingering behaviourism" of media research: what audiences receive are messages about violence, not violence (pp. 5, 8).
- A violent act has no fixed meaning; it signifies within a coded field and is polysemic, with a preferred meaning but no guarantee of uptake (pp. 8–9).
- Visual signs are probably less misunderstood than words at the denotative level, but connotation links signs to ideology (pp. 11–13).
- Connotative meanings are more open than denotative ones but "structured in dominance"; polysemy must not be confused with pluralism (pp. 13–14).
- "Selective perception" is probably less random and private than assumed; Hall proposes a typology of four decoding positions (pp. 15–18).
- Misreading connotative "misunderstandings" as technical faults is, Hall says, a type of unconscious collusion with dominant interests (pp. 18–19).

### Aim and framework (pp. 1–2)
Hall says the Colloquy's two themes, formal "televisual language" and societal "cultural policies", seem to pull apart. He wants one framework in which the link between societal and symbolic structures is "absolutely pivotal" (p. 1). He organises it around the encoding/decoding moments. He argues that, in societies like ours, communication between broadcasting's production elites and audiences is necessarily "systematically distorted communication". This bears on policies meant to help audiences receive television "better" (p. 1).
His semiotic perspective is not a closed formal study of the discourse. It includes the social relations of communication and the "competences" of producers and receivers in using the language (p. 1).
Halloran asks for study of the whole mass communication process. Hall agrees but stresses what is distinctive here. Television production yields a message: sign-vehicles organised by codes (rule-systems linking signs to meanings) in syntagmatic chains (sequences of signs). Circulation and reception also occur in this symbolic form (pp. 1–2).
The raw historical event cannot in that form be transmitted by, say, a television newscast; it can only be signified within the aural-visual forms of televisual language. The event must become a "story" before it can become a communicative event, and then the formal sub-rules of language are "in dominance", without wiping out the historical event or its historical consequences (p. 2). Encoding and decoding are only "relatively autonomous" but are determinate moments. The message-form is a determinate moment, though at another level it comprises only the surface movements of the communications system and requires, at another stage, to be integrated into the essential relations of communication, of which it is only a part (p. 2).

### The circuit (pp. 2–4)
- **Production.** Broadcasting institutions, routines and technical infrastructure produce the programme. The circuit begins here in one sense. Production is framed by meanings: professional ideologies, institutional knowledge, assumptions about the audience (p. 3).
- **Not a closed system.** Production draws topics, agendas, personnel, images of the audience and "definitions of the situation" from the wider socio-cultural system. Citing Elliott, Hall says the audience is both source and receiver. Circulation and reception are therefore moments of production, fed back through "skewed and structured" feedbacks. Production is "predominant" as point of departure. Production and reception are not identical but are differentiated moments of one totality (p. 3).
- **Decoding.** The structure yields an encoded meaningful discourse. Before it can have an effect, satisfy a need or serve a use, it must be perceived as meaningful and decoded. The decoded meanings are what influence, entertain, instruct or persuade (p. 3). Effects and "gratifications" are themselves framed by structures of understanding and by social and economic structures (p. 4).
- **Diagram (p. 4).** Programme as meaningful discourse; encoding side with meaning structures I; decoding side with meaning structures II. Each side rests on frameworks of knowledge, structures of production and technical infrastructure.
- **Result.** Meanings I and II "may not be the same". Codes may not be perfectly symmetrical. The degree of understanding or misunderstanding depends on (a) the symmetry or asymmetry between encoder-producer and decoder-receiver positions and (b) the identity or non-identity of the codes. The lack of fit relates to structural differences between broadcasters and audiences. It also relates to an asymmetry at the transformation into and out of message-form (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* Sender and receiver each hold their own code. How well meaning survives depends on how similar the codes and social positions are. Hall treats "misunderstanding" as a feature of this relation, not simply a fault in the receiver.

### Example: the TV Western and violence (pp. 5–11)
**Semiotics against behaviourism (p. 5).** Hall hopes the semiotic paradigm will dispel the "lingering behaviourism" in media research. He cites Gerbner: violence on screen is not violence but messages about violence. Research has continued as if this distinction could not be grasped (p. 5).

**Genre as shared code (pp. 5–6).** The early simple-structure TV Western had a clear good/bad moral universe, clear narrative and a violent climax. Quantitatively it had many violent incidents. Researchers such as Himmelweit suggested that most children (boys earlier than girls) learned to read it like a "cowboys-and-Indians" game. It was therefore hypothesised that such stylised Westerns were less likely to trigger aggressive imitation than non-stylised programmes with high violence ratios (pp. 5–6). Hall says this implies tightly coded rules, so widely shared between producer and audience that decoding was likely to be highly symmetrical to encoding. This reciprocity of codes is what conventionalisation entails and what makes a genre possible (p. 6).

**Why does the convention arise? (pp. 6–7).** The Western arose from the historical opening of the American West, but the genre-codes selectively turned that history into a symbolic "West". Hall calls this naturalisation of history by codes one of the most important test-cases for a semiology grounded in history (p. 6). Its deep-structure elements include the frontier, the "virgin land" before law, independent men facing Nature or Evil, masculine prowess, morality shown in gesture and appearance, and women as subordinate or disposable (pp. 6–7). From this limited deep structure came many surface transformations. Hall lists the psychological, baroque (*Left Handed Gun*; title garbled in the source), end-of-the-West, comic, "spaghetti", Japanese and Hong Kong, parody (*Butch Cassidy*), return-of-violence (*The Wild Bunch*), domestic soap-opera (*The Virginian*) and Latin-American revolution Westerns (p. 7). In *Hud* the hero drives a Cadillac and the horse rides in a truck. Reversing a few lexical items yields an opposite meaning, not a break-up of the code (p. 7).

**Violence does not signify alone (pp. 7–9).** Hall says that if we refuse to isolate violence from its matrix in the genre's codes, the researchers' long preoccupation with it appears more and more arbitrary, because many other crucial meanings were transmitted while they counted bodies (p. 7). Hall does not deny that violence was present or that codes regulated its signification. A violent element, like any unit of a structured discourse, signifies only through the meaning of the whole and its relations of similarity and difference with other elements (p. 8). Burgelin (quoted) says a villain's vicious acts cannot be read from a bare count, whether ten or twenty. What matters is the meaning given by the juxtaposition with the single good act, and structural analysis offers a way to approach the problem of finding this rare or missing item that traditional content analysis does not (p. 8). Hall applies this:
- Many bad-violent acts (marked) and no redeeming act (unmarked) = unrepentant villain. He can be shot down without excuse, with a brief and "bad" death, provided the hero does not shoot him in the back or unawares and does not draw first (p. 8).
- Bad-violent acts (marked) plus a single good act (marked) = possible salvation: death-bed reconciliation, restitution, at least a "good" death (p. 8).

**Polysemy and preferred meaning (p. 9).** Hall states three points: (a) a violent act cannot signify in isolation; (b) it signifies only relative to other elements and the rules governing their combination; (c) its meaning is not fixed or single. It is "polysemic", but its structure delimits meanings and effects a "closure", so a preferred meaning is suggested. Possible meanings lie on a scale from dominant to subordinate. Hence, Hall says, there can be no law ensuring that the receiver takes the preferred meaning in just the way it was encoded (p. 9). Researchers had treated the other elements as inert supports for violence, but violence might be present to warrant the character (p. 9).

**The quick-draw item (pp. 9–10).** Take the hero who draws faster and shoots the villain dead. Asked what norm, proposition or cultural signification it signifies (Gerbner's term), one reading is that the hero draws faster and shoots better than his enemy and kills the villain with a single shot. Hall says this might be called a "behavioural" or "instrumental" interpretation (p. 9). Research suggests, however, that this behavioural message is stylised by genre conventions (a code-of-codes, or meta-code), whose intervention appears to neutralise one set of meanings and set another in motion. They displace the same denotative content from one reference-code to another (p. 10). Hall cites Berger and Luckmann (habitualisation frees the foreground for innovation), Turner (ritual redistributes focus between domains) and Freud (condensation and displacement). He then speculatively proposes a connotative reading: being a certain kind of man means mastering all contingencies through a practised professional "cool". On this reading the message is about conduct, or even professionalism, not about violence. He recalls Warshow's observation that the Western is about codes of conduct (p. 10). The table on p. 10 contrasts non-genre reading ("violence"; norm: shoot to kill without hesitation) with genre reading ("decorum"; norm: master contingencies by professional cool).

*Intuition note:* The same shot can be read as a lesson in how to kill or as a display of how a man behaves, depending on the codes the viewer holds. Counting incidents cannot tell which reading is in play.

**Conclusion (p. 11).** Attention to codes opens onto latent cultural content. Interplay of codes and content displaces meanings between frames and brings repressed content of a culture to the surface in "disguised" forms (p. 11). Hall cites Eco: "Semiology shows us the universe of ideologies arranged in codes and sub-codes within the universe of signs" (p. 11). In his own view, if the insights of semiotics are not to be lost in a new kind of formalism, it must increasingly be pushed in this direction (p. 11).

### Visual signs: denotation and connotation (pp. 11–14)
- **Iconic sign.** The televisual sign is visual with strong aural-verbal support, and iconic in Peirce's sense: it reproduces some properties of its referent. Its "naturalism" lies less in encoding than in the viewer's learned perceptions. Following Eco, iconic signs reproduce the conditions of perception in the receiver (p. 11).
- **Denotative competence.** Perceptual codes are widely shared by culture-members. So denotative visual signs probably give rise to fewer misunderstandings than linguistic ones. In societies like ours, linguistic competence is very unequally distributed between classes and segments of the population, predominantly by the family and the education system. "Visual competence" at the denotative level is more widely diffused (pp. 11–12).
- **Caveat.** It is not universal: cartoons, diagrams, unfamiliar conventions and some cutting and editing puzzle viewers (p. 12). Iconic signs may also support misreading because they are so "natural": viewers take the image for the thing (p. 12). Hall expects most viewers to have little difficulty identifying denotatively what the visual signs refer to. People seem to pick up visual codes early, without formal training (p. 12).
- **Connotation.** The visual sign is also connotative, pre-eminently in modern mass communication. Connotation is where the denoted sign meets the deep semantic structures of a culture and takes on an ideological dimension (p. 12). In advertising almost no communication is "purely denotative". Barthes' /sweater/ connotes warmth, then winter. In fashion it may connote haute couture or informal dress. In a romantic sub-code it may connote an autumn walk in the woods (pp. 12–13).
- **Open codes.** Connotative codes are structured but more open than denotative ones. They link to ideologies, history and ethnography. They refer to "maps of meaning" with power and interest "written in" (p. 13). Barthes (quoted): connoted signifiers are, "if you like", the "fragments of ideology" (p. 13).
- **Dominance without determination.** Denotation is bounded by closed codes; connotation, though bounded, stays open and polysemic. Hall insists that polysemy must not be confused with pluralism. Connotative codes are not equal. A dominant cultural order exists, neither univocal nor uncontested (p. 13). New or troubling events must be assigned to a connotational domain to "make sense". Hall says "dominant", not "determined", because more than one mapping is always possible. Yet a pattern of preferred readings exists, institutionally imprinted and itself institutionalised (p. 13).

### Misunderstanding and interpretive work (p. 14)
Preferred mappings embed the social order: everyday knowledge, the rank order of power and interest, legitimations and sanctions (p. 14). To clarify a denotative misunderstanding we need primarily to refer to the immanent world of the sign and its codes. Connotative ones must be resolved by reference, through the codes, to social life, history, economic and political power, and ultimately ideology (p. 14). Since mappings are "structured in dominance" but not closed, communication consists not in unproblematic assignment of items to prearranged codes but of performative rules ("logics-in-use") that seek to enforce or prefer one semantic domain over another. Formal semiology has too often neglected this, though it forms the deep structure of a great deal of broadcast time, especially in political and other sensitive areas (p. 14). Dominant meanings also involve the "work" of winning legitimacy for a decoding (e.g. the Chile coup) (p. 14). Terni says "reading" means not only the capacity to identify and decode signs but also a "subjective capacity" to relate them creatively. Hall's only quarrel is with "subjective": he thinks the televisual process takes "objective" (systemic) responsibility for the relations among signs, and so delimits the total environment of which viewers become aware (p. 14).

### Misunderstanding and selective perception (p. 15)
Television producers whose message fails to get across are frequently concerned to straighten out the kinks in the communicative chain. At the denotative level some misunderstanding surely exists, though its extent is unknown. Possible reasons: the viewer does not "speak the language", the logic or concepts are too alien, or the editing is too swift (p. 15). At another level, encoders also mean that the audience has made sense of the message differently from what was intended; Hall says what they really mean is that viewers are not operating within the dominant or preferred code. For encoders the ideal is perfectly transparent communication; what they must confront instead is systematically distorted communication (p. 15). Recent research explains discrepancies as individually "aberrant" readings due to "selective perception". Hall calls this the door through which a residual pluralism is kept. Though there will always be individual, private readings, his own tentative view is that selective perception is almost never as selective, random or privatized as the concept suggests; the patterns show more structuring and clustering than is normally assumed. Any new approach to audience studies via decoding would have to begin with a critique of selective-perception theory. He notes Eco's intermediate level of sub-cultural formations but prefers a different framework (p. 15).

### Four decoding positions (pp. 16–18)
Hall adapts Gramsci (hegemonic and corporate formations) and Parkin to propose four "ideal-type" positions (p. 16). Literal or denotative errors are a kind of noise in the channel. Connotative "misreadings" have a societal, not a communicative, basis. They signify structural conflicts and negotiations in economic, political and cultural life (p. 16).
1. **Dominant (hegemonic).** The viewer takes the connoted meaning "full and straight" and decodes it in the reference-code in which it was coded. This is the ideal-typical case of "perfectly transparent communication", or as close to it as Hall thinks we are likely to come for practical purposes (p. 16).
2. **Professional.** The meta-codes broadcasters use when transmitting a message already signified hegemonically. They are "relatively independent" (technico-practical criteria: visual quality, news values, "professionalism"). They operate within the hegemony and reproduce dominant definitions by bracketing the hegemonic quality (p. 16). Political elites give the hegemonic interpretation (Northern Ireland, the Chilean coup, the Industrial Relations Bill). Formats, personnel, images and staging are chosen by the professional code (pp. 16–17). Professionals link to elites through the institutional position of broadcasting as "ideological apparatus" and through the structure of access ("over-accessing" of elite personnel and definitions). Hall says it may even be said that professional codes reproduce hegemonic definitions by not overtly biasing their operations, so that reproduction is inadvertent and unconscious, "behind men's backs". Conflicts between dominant and professional significations still occur (p. 17).
3. **Negotiated.** Majority audiences probably understand adequately what was dominantly defined and professionally signified. Hegemonic definitions are global: they tie events to grand totalisations such as "the national interest". A hegemonic viewpoint (a) defines the mental horizon or universe of possible meanings of a society and (b) carries the stamp of legitimacy, appearing "natural" and "taken for granted" (p. 17). Negotiated decoding mixes adaptive and oppositional elements. It acknowledges the legitimacy of hegemonic definitions but makes its own ground-rules and "exceptions" at the situational level. It runs on "situated logics" arising from unequal positions in relation to power. It is shot through with contradictions (pp. 17–18). Example: a worker may accept, at the level of the national-interest economic debate, that everyone must take lower pay to combat inflation, yet still strike for better pay or oppose the Industrial Relations Bill at shop-floor level (p. 18). Hall suspects that the great majority of so-called misunderstandings arise from disjunctures between hegemonic-dominant encodings and negotiated-corporate decodings. These mismatches most provoke elites and professionals to see a "failure in communications" (p. 18).
4. **Oppositional.** The viewer understands both the literal and connotative inflection but decodes in a globally contrary way: he "detotalizes" the preferred message and "retotalizes" it in another framework, e.g. reading "national interest" as "class interest". Hall calls one of the most significant political moments (which also coincide with crisis points inside broadcasting organisations) the point when events normally decoded in a negotiated way begin to get oppositional readings (p. 18).

*Intuition note:* The four positions differ in how far the viewer accepts the dominant frame. Hall suspects that the great majority of so-called "misunderstandings" come from a mismatch between the elite's global frame and the viewer's situated frame (p. 18).

### Implications for cultural policy (pp. 18–19)
Beyond the strictly denotative level, it is extremely difficult to identify "improving communications" as a neutral educational goal. The educator or policy-maker performs one of his most partisan acts when he colludes with the re-signification of real conflicts and contradictions as if they were simply kinks in the communicative chain (p. 18). Denotative mistakes are not structurally significant; connotative ones are or can be (p. 18). Reading systematic distortions as technical faults misreads a deep-structure process as a surface phenomenon. The decision to intervene so as to make the hegemonic codes of dominant elites more effective and transparent for the majority audience is, Hall says, not technically neutral but political. Misreading a political choice as a technical one is, Hall says, a type of unconscious collusion with dominant interests, a form of collusion to which social science researchers are "all too prone". They may reproduce hegemony not by open submission but by "operating the 'professional bracket'" (p. 19).

## Toy example (ours, invented numbers)
A bulletin tells viewers that a wage freeze is needed in the national interest. Five viewers decode it.
- A agrees fully → dominant position.
- B agrees "nationally" but will still strike locally → negotiated.
- C hears "national interest" as "class interest" → oppositional.
- D does not know what "freeze" means → denotative error (noise).
- E agrees in general but exempts her own workplace → negotiated.

Tally: dominant 1, negotiated 2, oppositional 1, denotative error 1; 1+2+1+1 = 5.
Only 1/5 = 20% decode "full and straight". Four of five read the denotation correctly (4/5 = 80%). Yet 3 of 5 (60%) depart connotatively (B, C, E). In Hall's terms (p. 18) most of these departures are the negotiated type, a mismatch of levels. A policy that fixed only D's problem would leave the 60% untouched. The figures are made up and illustrate the typology, not any finding.

## Concepts
- [[encoding-decoding]] — a programme is encoded as a meaningful discourse and then decoded; meanings I and II may not be the same (pp. 3–4)
- [[preferred-reading]] — the closure a message's structure suggests among polysemic meanings; uptake is not guaranteed (p. 9)
- [[systematically-distorted-communication]] (new) — the label Hall uses (note 23 cites Habermas) for the lack of fit between broadcasting elites' encodings and audience decodings, which he links to asymmetries of position and code (pp. 1, 4, 15–16)
- [[denotation-connotation]] (new) — denotation: literal identification of the sign (bounded, "closed" codes); connotation: associative, ideological level (open, polysemic) (pp. 12–13)
- [[polysemy]] (new) — one sign can map into more than one connotative configuration; not the same as pluralism (p. 13)
- [[hegemonic-dominant-code]] (new) — decoding the connoted meaning "full and straight"; hegemony defines the mental horizon and appears natural (pp. 16–17)
- [[professional-code]] (new) — broadcasters' relatively independent technico-practical meta-codes that reproduce hegemonic definitions (p. 16)
- [[negotiated-code]] (new) — accepts the global hegemonic frame but makes exceptions at the situational level (pp. 17–18)
- [[oppositional-code]] (new) — understands the preferred meaning but retotalises it in a contrary framework (p. 18)
- [[selective-perception]] (new) — "aberrant individual reading" explanation that Hall thinks is less random than assumed (p. 15)
- [[iconic-sign]] (new) — sign reproducing some properties of its referent; naturalism lies in the viewer's learned perception (p. 11)
- [[feedback]] — "skewed and structured" return of audience and societal input into production (p. 3)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Hall's denotative errors as channel noise ↔ Shannon noise: Hall calls literal errors a kind of noise in the channel but treats connotative misreadings as a different matter with a societal, not communicative, basis (p. 16).
- [structural-analogy] Preferred reading and closure ↔ Framing (Entman): both stress selection among possible meanings that suggests an interpretation.
- [structural-analogy] Hall's critique of "selective perception" ↔ selective exposure: Hall argues departures are structured, not random.
- [metaphor] Encoding/decoding asymmetry ↔ transmission view: Hall turns the sender-to-receiver model into a relation of positions and codes.

## Questions this raises
- How could one test Hall's claim that selective perception shows "structuring and clustering" (p. 15)?
- Are the four positions a typology of people or of readings of single messages? A viewer may take different positions on different topics.
- If "professionalism" reproduces hegemony without bias (p. 17), what would non-collusive professional practice look like?
- How far does the claim that visual competence is more widely shared (p. 12) hold for modern editing and genre conventions?
