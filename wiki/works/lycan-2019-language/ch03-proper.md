# Proper Names: The Description Theory

## L0 — One line
Russell held that ordinary proper names are equivalent to definite descriptions, which would let him extend his solutions to the four reference puzzles to names; Searle loosened this to clusters of descriptions, and Kripke and others raise many objections to both versions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The four puzzles about reference (nonexistents, negative existentials, informative identities, failure of substitution) arise for proper names as well as for descriptions. Frege answered with "senses", but said little about what senses are. Russell's Name Claim says ordinary names are disguised definite descriptions (e.g. *the winner of the 1968 election*), so his Chapter 2 solutions would carry over to names. Two opening objections (no single description fits a name; speakers would talk past each other) lead Searle to a looser cluster theory. Kripke's further objections (modal, Gödel/Schmidt, Cicero/Tully, thin content, fiction) target both versions. The chapter shows why descriptivism about names is attractive and why it is contested.

**Prerequisites:** Russell's Theory of Descriptions (Ch. 2); the four puzzles; referential opacity; narrow vs wide scope; first-order quantifier notation.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The four puzzles arise first and just as sharply for names; so Russell's analysis of descriptions alone does not settle the matter if names are Millian, i.e. contribute only their referents (p. 32).
- Frege's solution: a name has a sense (a way of presenting its referent) as well as a referent. Inside "believes that", a name refers to its sense (pp. 32–33). Lycan suspects it sounds right because it is schematic (p. 34).
- Russell's Name Claim: everyday names are, at the level of logical form, equivalent to definite descriptions. The puzzle solutions then carry over. The claim is independent of the Theory of Descriptions (pp. 34–35).
- Direct support for the Name Claim: the "who?" question, the spot-check test, and how names are taught and learned (p. 35).
- Opening objections: in many cases no single determinate description is the one a speaker has in mind; and different descriptions in different mouths would leave speakers unable to disagree about one person (pp. 36–37).
- Searle's Cluster Theory ties a name to a loose, weighted cluster of descriptions; it is meant to meet both objections (p. 38).
- Kripke's objections 3–7 (modal, Gödel/Schmidt, belief reports, too little descriptive content, fiction) are aimed at Russell and Searle together (pp. 38–42).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and "Frege and the Puzzles" (pp. 31–34)
- **Millian name:** an expression that means only the thing it designates; the label view that J. S. Mill seemed to defend. One may think that, although descriptions are not singular terms, names still are (p. 32).
- Lycan's point: the four puzzles were not specific to descriptions; they arose first for names (p. 32).
- **Nonexistents.** (1) *James Moriarty is bald* is meaningful because the name has a *sense* even with no referent; the name "expresses" the sense but denotes nothing (p. 32).
- Sense, for Frege: a "way of presenting" the referent. It is abstract, not mental, but reflects a way of thinking of the referent. Frege sometimes states senses as descriptions (e.g. *the Stagirite teacher of Alexander*). A sense determines a unique referent; several senses can fix the same referent (p. 32).
- **Negative existentials.** (2) *Pegasus never existed* seems true and seems to be about Pegasus, but if it is true it cannot be about Pegasus. An impressionistic Frege-style solution: (2) can be taken to mean roughly that the sense of *Pegasus* (the conception of a winged horse ridden by Bellerophon) fails to find a referent. Lycan notes it is unclear this was Frege's view. It is also not straightforward, because a name expresses but does not denote its sense, so (2) is not literally about that sense (p. 32).
- **Frege's puzzle.** (3) *Mark Twain is Samuel Langhorne Clemens* should be trivially true if the names are Millian, yet it seems informative and contingent. On Frege's view the names present the referent in different ways (different senses), and what he calls cognitive significance goes with sense. Frege writes that if $a=b$, then "a = b" has the same truth-value as "a = a", but the sense of "b" may differ from that of "a", so the thought expressed may differ (p. 33). Lycan notes we are not told how "a = b" can be contingent (p. 33).
- **Substitutivity.** (4) *Albert believes that Samuel Langhorne Clemens was less than 5 feet tall*: substituting *Mark Twain* produces a falsehood, so the position after *believes that* is referentially opaque. Frege's move: since belief is cognitive, he supposed that truth-value depends on the senses of the embedded expressions. He suggests that *believes that* shifts the reference of the name to its own sense, and the two names have different senses (p. 33).
- Lycan's verdict: the solutions sound right, he suspects, because they are schematic. Frege says little about what sense is, which may have an air of labelling rather than solving. A more testable version comes in Ch. 10 (p. 34).
- *Intuition note:* For Frege a name carries a way of thinking about its referent, and this is what is meant to explain why co-referring names are not always interchangeable. The open question, in Lycan's view, is what such a sense is (pp. 32–34).

### Russell's Name Claim (pp. 34–36)
- **Name Claim:** everyday proper names are not genuine Millian names; at the level of logical form they are equivalent to definite descriptions. Russell says they "abbreviate" descriptions (p. 34).
- This is a second appearance/reality distinction, and a more surprising one, since names look conceptually simple (p. 34).
- If the Name Claim is true, replace each name by its description and use the Chapter 2 solutions. Russell thus analyzes Frege's senses instead of taking them as primitive (p. 34).
- The Name Claim is independent of the Theory of Descriptions. One can hold either without the other (pp. 34–35).
- Support: (a) explanatory power on the puzzles. (b) Direct argument: as with definite descriptions, a sentence containing a name seems intuitively to entail the clauses of the description analysis, and the clauses jointly entail the sentence. E.g. *Pegasus never existed* merely assures us there was no such winged horse; *Sherlock Holmes never existed* plausibly means there was never a legendary English detective at 221B Baker Street, and so on. Lycan says Russell gave at least one direct argument and that a second, (c), is easily extracted from his writings (p. 35). (c) The *who?* question (Lycan's extracted second argument, not given explicitly by Russell as far as Lycan knows): asked who Lili Boulanger is, a description is the proper answer, whereas a second name would not do unless already tied to a description (p. 35). (d) Spot-check test: asked who they mean right after using a name, people in general immediately and instinctively come up with a description (p. 35). (e) Searle (1958): teaching a name means giving descriptions; learning it means eliciting them (p. 35).
- Lycan: *abbreviate* is too strong. All Russell actually needs is the weaker thesis that names are somehow equivalent in meaning to descriptions, which Lycan calls the **Description Theory** of proper names (p. 36).
- *Intuition note:* The idea is that a name's meaning is the identifying information you would give if asked who it is. Lycan says these phenomena are very robust, so the Name Claim is not just a desperate lunge to solve the puzzles (p. 35).

### Opening objections (pp. 36–37)
- **Objection 1 (Searle).** If a name is equivalent to a description, there must be a particular one. Taking the conjunction of all predicates I believe true of Sellars would make (5) *Wilfrid Sellars was an honest man* entail (6) *There is at least one philosopher with whom I had a fairly violent argument in George Pappas' living room in 1979*, which it does not (p. 36).
- The spot-check answer does not show the description was already determinately in mind; what I say depends on what I think you want. Lycan's stronger thesis: in many cases there is no single determinate description the speaker has in mind, even tacitly (pp. 36–37).
- **Objection 2.** Different people know different things. Assuming the descriptions are those in speakers' minds, as the spot-check test reveals, the Name Claim implies that the same name has many different senses for different people, and even for one person at different times, so every name is multiply ambiguous (p. 37).
- Worse: if I think of Sellars as the author of *Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man* and you think of him as Pittsburgh's most famous philosopher, then my claim (that Sellars used to tie his shoes with one hand) becomes the generalization (7) and your denial the different generalization (8). They are logically compatible, so on Russell's view we do not contradict each other. Lycan says this seems quite wrong (p. 37).
- Footnote 3: appeal to the Chapter 2 semantic referent does not help, since (7) and (8) remain compatible. A later descriptivist could use descriptions used referentially and speaker-reference, giving a weaker notion of disagreement (p. 43).

### Searle's Cluster Theory (pp. 37–38)
- A name is associated with a vague cluster of descriptions. "This is N" asserts that a *sufficient but vague and unspecified number* (SBVAUN) of the "standard identifying statements" are true of the object. Searle adds that being N is having an SBVAUN of the relevant properties (p. 38).
- Searle says the vagueness is what distinguishes names from descriptions, and why we have names at all. Lycan notes that if the Name Claim were correct, a name's only function would be to save breath or ink. For Searle a name is a peg on which to hang descriptions (p. 38).
- Refinements Lycan suggests: a Searlean would naturally require that "sufficient" mean at least over half, since otherwise two obviously distinct individuals could both be the referent; and some way of weighting descriptions by importance is surely needed (p. 38).
- Objection 1 is mooted, since no single description is required. Objection 2 is blunted (Searle believes): different subclusters can still fix the same individual (p. 38). Footnote 4: the speakers' sentences still have different meanings, so non-disagreement may remain (p. 44).
- Kripke (building on Barcan Marcus) criticizes Russell's and Searle's views together (p. 38).
- *Intuition note:* Searle keeps the descriptive idea but lets the name be fixed by a "good enough" bundle of facts, not one exact description.

### Kripke's Critique (pp. 38–42)
- **Objection 3 (modal).** Suppose *Richard Nixon* is equivalent to *the winner of the 1968 election*. Could Nixon have lost? The answer seems unequivocally *yes*, assuming *could* expresses merely theoretical, logical or metaphysical possibility rather than something about our knowledge. According to the Description Theory the question means (9): is it possible that one and only one person won the 1968 election and whoever won it lost it? The answer to that is clearly *no* (pp. 38–39).
  - Against Searle: Nixon might have become a sandalmaker and satisfied none of the cluster descriptions. But it is not possible for a person to satisfy an SBVAUN of the cluster and satisfy none. On Searle's view that sandalmaker would not have been Nixon, which seems wrong (p. 39).
  - **Dummett's reply:** the objection assumes the description has narrow scope (a "secondary" occurrence under "it is possible that"). With wide scope the question matches (10): one and only one person won, and concerning whoever won, is it possible that that person lost? (p. 39). Using the indicative (11), *It is possible for Richard Nixon to have lost the 1968 election*, presuming Nixon is equivalent to *the winner of the 1968 election*, with $W$ = *won the 1968 election* and *lost* represented as *did not win*:

    Narrow scope (corresponds to (11) and is false):
    $$\text{Possible: } (\exists x)\big(Wx \;\&\; (y)(Wy \to y=x) \;\&\; (z)(Wz \to \sim Wz)\big)$$

    Wide scope (presumably true):
    $$(\exists x)\big(Wx \;\&\; (y)(Wy \to y=x) \;\&\; (z)(Wz \to \text{Possible: } \sim Wz)\big)$$

    (pp. 39–40). Colloquially: exactly one person won, and whoever won could have lost (p. 40).
  - Some philosophers "rigidify" descriptions ("the *actual* winner"); this is developed in the next chapter (p. 40). Footnote 5: Frege gave names no scope at all (p. 44).
- **Objection 4 (Gödel/Schmidt).** In Kripke's fiction, Schmidt proved the Incompleteness Theorem and Gödel stole and published it. It seems clear (Lycan, reporting Kripke's example) that even people who know nothing else about Gödel refer to Gödel rather than Schmidt when they use the name, so when they say *Gödel proved the Incompleteness Theorem* they speak falsely. If no one proved it (perhaps the proof simply arose by a random scattering of atoms), it is even more obviously true that their uses refer to Gödel, yet those uses are not backed by any Searlean cluster (p. 40).
- **Objection 5 (belief reports).** (12) *Some people are unaware that Cicero is Tully* is ostensibly true, but if the Name Claim is correct it is hard to interpret: Kripke says there is no single proposition denoted by the *that* clause that the community of normal English speakers expresses by *Cicero is Tully* (Kripke 1979b: 245), since the names are equivalent to different descriptions for different people. Expanding the clause with one speaker's shared description for both names gives (13), which is equivalent to (14), *Some people are unaware that one and only one person was a famous Roman orator who denounced Catiline and who figures in some famous examples of Quine's*. No doubt (14) is true, but Lycan says it surely does not express what (12) means (pp. 40–41). Lycan says it is unclear how Searle could handle this (p. 41).
- **Objection 6 (too little content).** Most people, Lycan says, associate *Cicero* only with an indefinite description such as *a famous Roman orator*, and *Richard Feynman* only with something like *a leading [then] contemporary theoretical physicist*, yet they refer successfully. Two names of one person could share the same indefinite backing, and no Russellian theory could then explain the failure of substitution in belief contexts (p. 41).
  - Donnellan's example: a half-awake child is told *This is our friend Tom*. Next morning he has only a vague memory that Tom is a nice man and no descriptive material associated with the name, yet Donnellan argues he refers to Tom (p. 41).
- **Objection 7 (fiction).** If the Name Claim is correct, any sentence with a fictional name in a primary (wide-scope) position comes out false: (15) *Sherlock Holmes lived at 221B Baker Street* is false, since (16), its supposed equivalent, is false (no such person existed). But, the objection runs, some fictional sentences, such as (15) itself and *Hamlet was a Dane*, are true or at any rate not false (p. 42).
  - Lycan says Russell would not have been much swayed, since he would call such sentences only *make-believe-true* or *true-in-fiction* rather than true. Lycan's two remarks: if (15) were literally true, then something would be true of the real Baker Street; and if writing in stories made sentences true, then "Holmes existed" would be true too. Some insist that fictional sentences are truth-valueless rather than false; Lycan says those sympathetic to this will want a Kripkean theory (Kripke 1972/1980; Donnellan 1974) (p. 42).
- Kripke has a further, more fundamental objection, which needs the apparatus of Ch. 4 (p. 42).

### Summary (pp. 42–43)
The puzzles apply to names; Frege's senses do not really explain; Russell's Name Claim extends the Theory of Descriptions; it faces at least two powerful objections; Searle's looser version avoids them; Kripke's further objections hit Searle as hard as Russell (pp. 42–43). Footnote 2 notes that Frege and Russell differ: for Frege a nondenoting singular term normally leaves its sentence without truth-value (p. 43).

## Toy example (ours)
Cluster theory with the *more than half* refinement (p. 38). Name "Zed". Cluster: (a) wrote *Book B*, (b) lives in Oslo, (c) plays the cello, (d) born in 1950, (e) teaches at University U. That is 5 descriptions, so more than half means at least 3 ($5/2 = 2.5$).
- Person P satisfies a, b, c, e (4 of 5). $4 > 2.5$, so P is the referent. If P is wrongly believed to have been born in 1951, the theory still gives P.
- Person Q satisfies c and d (2 of 5). $2 < 2.5$, so Q is not the referent.
- Strict Russell-style version (the one description "a and b and c and d and e"): P fails (d), so "Zed" would refer to no one. The cluster is more forgiving.
- Kripke-style test: suppose P could have become a sandalmaker satisfying 0 of 5. $0 < 2.5$, so the cluster theory says that man would not be Zed, the result that seems wrong to Lycan in Nixon's case (p. 39).

## Questions this raises
- What exactly would a Fregean sense have to be to explain these puzzles, if senses are not descriptions?
- If rigidified descriptions ("the actual winner") answer Objection 3, do they also help with the Gödel/Schmidt case?
- Does Searle's theory really block non-disagreement (fn. 4), if the speakers' sentences still differ in meaning?
- What is the "further and more fundamental" objection of Kripke that needs Ch. 4's apparatus?
