# Traditional Theories of Meaning

## L0 — One line
A theory of meaning must explain the "meaning facts"; the two traditional entity theories (meanings as mental ideas, or as abstract propositions) are tested against them, and the Proposition Theory survives better but is not unproblematic.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What is a meaning, and what must a theory of meaning explain? Lycan lists the "meaning facts": meaningfulness, synonymy, ambiguity, meaning containment and entailment, merely verbal disputes. He then tests two entity theories. The Ideational Theory (meanings are actual ideas in particular minds) faces four objections. The Proposition Theory (meanings are abstract propositions that sentences express) escapes them, though only barely on one, and describes the facts neatly. But it meets its own objections: propositions are weird and unfamiliar, may merely restate the data (Harman), and have no causal power. Lycan gives replies to each. Early forms of the weirdness and causal-power objections pushed 1950s philosophers toward "use" theories.

**Prerequisites:** the Referential Theory of Meaning (Ch. 1), the sentence/proposition contrast (Ch. 2), and what an abstract entity is.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A theory of meaning needs its own data, the "meaning facts": meaningfulness, synonymy, ambiguity, containment/entailment, merely verbal disputes (pp. 69–70).
- An entity theory takes meanings to be individual things. Ordinary talk of "a meaning", and even of counting meanings, lends it some support (p. 70).
- The Ideational Theory (meanings are actual mental states) is open to four objections: image/concept trouble, words without images, publicness of meaning, and never-thought sentences (pp. 71–72).
- The Proposition Theory depicts meaningfulness, synonymy, ambiguity and verbal disputes through one relation, "expressing". It treats propositions as people-independent and language-independent (eternal, "if you like"), and as the fundamental bearers of truth (pp. 72–74).
- On Moore's classic answer, understanding a sentence is grasping a proposition and knowing that the sentence expresses it. This lets people into the theory (p. 75).
- Objections to the Proposition Theory: weirdness, unfamiliarity, Harman's "no explanation" charge, and lack of causal power. Lycan gives a reply to each (pp. 75–77).
- Early forms of the weirdness and causal-power objections led ordinary-language philosophers to demand a theory tied to behaviour, hence "use" theories, which vary with the notion of "use" (p. 77).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and the meaning facts (pp. 69–70)
Since the Referential Theory is untenable (Ch. 1), meaning must be confronted directly (p. 69). Any theory needs data, "the meaning facts" (p. 69):
1. **Meaningfulness.** Some strings of marks or noises, especially whole sentences, are meaningful and others are not. Perhaps this is the basic question for the theory (p. 69).
2. **Synonymy.** Two distinct expressions can mean the same (p. 70).
3. **Ambiguity.** One expression can have more than one meaning, so expressions and meanings are not correlated one to one (p. 70).
4. **Containment.** One expression's meaning can be contained in another's: *female* and *deer* in "doe". The special case is entailment: *Harold is fat and Ben is stupid* entails *Ben is stupid*. There is also joint entailment, as in the "Grannie" example, where two sentences together entail a third and neither alone does (p. 70).
5. **Merely verbal disputes.** In some disputes the parties agree on what happened. They differ only on whether it counts as a "so-and-so", and onlookers say they are just talking past each other (p. 70).

**Reification** is talking of meanings as if they were individual things, such as shoes. Lycan says he at least half-heartedly tried to avoid it in this list, though he slipped into talking of "meanings" (p. 70). An **entity theory** officially takes meanings to be individual things. Ordinary talk gives it some support: "meaning" is used as a common noun, with quantifiers, and even with counting ("This word has four different meanings") (p. 70). Lycan says there are at least two kinds of entity that meanings might be taken to be: mental items (ideational theories) and abstract items (propositions).

### Ideational Theories (pp. 70–72)
**View.** The usual target is Locke. Here a string is meaningful when it expresses, or corresponds to, a content-bearing mental state of the speaker (idea, image, thought, belief). The states are *actual states of particular people at particular times* (p. 70).

**Handling the facts.**
- Synonymy: two expressions express the same idea.
- Ambiguity: more than one idea could be expressed.
- Verbal disputes: both parties have the same thought in confusingly different words (p. 71).

Such theories are unpopular. Four objections follow (pp. 71–72).
- **Obj. 1 (what is an "idea"?).** Mental images are too detailed: a dog image is of a dog of some particular shape or breed. An abstract "concept" is circular until "concept" is explained without using meaning. A concept such as *dog* is not true or false on its own, so it cannot be the meaning of a whole sentence. A whole thought might serve, but not every sentence expresses anyone's actual thought. An abstract Fregean "thought" belongs to a very different theory (p. 71).
- **Obj. 2 (words without images).** Words like "is", "and", "of" have no particular images or contents. If images are what is on offer, some words, such as "chiliagon" and "nonentity", could not psychologically have images. Even for "red" we may virtually never call the image to mind when understanding the word (p. 71).
- **Obj. 3 (publicity).** Meaning is public and social, but ideas are subjective and differ from person to person. A reply appeals to what all "dog" ideas share, but that is a *type* of idea (a universal), not an idea (pp. 71–72).
- **Obj. 4 (unuttered sentences).** Some perfectly meaningful sentences have never been uttered, and some of those never will be. Their contents have been thought by no one, so they correspond to no actual mental entity (p. 72).

*Intuition note.* Actual mental states are too private, too detailed and too few to be meanings. One possible reply is to talk of *possible* ideas, but these are no longer anyone's actual ideas, and Lycan says the ideational view then collapses into the Proposition Theory (p. 72).

### The Proposition Theory (pp. 72–75)
**Move from ideas to propositions.** The ideational theorist might appeal to merely possible ideas, contents someone might have had. That posits abstract thinkables unrelated to anyone's actual thoughts. The proposition theorist says "let's call such thinkables 'propositions'". So, if the ideational theorist makes this move, the ideational view collapses into the Proposition Theory (p. 72). Propositions are language-independent and, unlike ideas, people-independent: "entirely general and, if you like, eternal" (p. 72). Russell said little about their nature, and Moore was more forthcoming. Frege had an elegant Proposition Theory, but seems to have held that understanding what a proposition is amounts to understanding its role in the theory (p. 72).

**Core.** According to Russell and Moore, a meaningful string S stands in the **expressing** relation to an abstract proposition P. Gibberish g expresses none. The relation is "bloodless": propositions are abstract, changeless and powerless (p. 73). The facts become:
- Meaningful: S expresses some proposition.
- Synonymous: S1 and S2 express the same proposition. They may be in one language or in different languages (p. 73).
- Ambiguous: S is ambiguous iff it expresses at least two distinct propositions P1 and P2 (p. 73).
- Merely verbal dispute: the parties do not disagree over any proposition. They express the same proposition in forms of words that only look in conflict (p. 73).

**Further features.**
- Propositions are identified by "that" clauses ("the proposition that snow is white"). The "that" frees the reference from any particular expression (p. 73).
- They are objects of mental states, such as believing, doubting, hoping, fearing, seeing and hearing *that*. They could have them in any language, if we must think in language at all (p. 73).
- They are the fundamental bearers of truth and falsity. Argument: sentence (1) "The present Queen of England is bald" changes truth-value with time of utterance, because it expresses different propositions on different occasions. Propositions' truth-values are permanent (pp. 73–74).
- Most proposition theorists hold that propositions have internal structure. Only a sentence expresses a full proposition. A word like "snow" expresses a **concept**, an abstract constituent of propositions. Word-level facts are handled in the same way: synonymous words express the same concept, and ambiguous "snow" expresses different concepts (p. 74).

**Against the four ideational objections.** It avoids Obj. 1 (propositions and concepts are not mental), Obj. 3 (they are intersubjective) and Obj. 4 (shown above). It "only barely" evades Obj. 2: the theorist can insist that "is" and "chiliagon" express concepts. But without a characterisation independent of linguistic meaning this sounds empty or circular. Lycan notes that Chapter 10 presents a sophisticated version that can supply one (p. 74). Positing such entities to explain the data is compared to positing subatomic particles (pp. 74–75).

**Understanding.** Another meaning fact, which some consider more important: we understand sentences immediately and not gibberish. This brings people into the theory. The classic Moorean answer is that to understand S is to *grasp* (apprehend) some proposition P and to know that S expresses P (p. 75).

**Attractions.** The theory suits common sense, helps describe entailment, meaning inclusion, antonymy and redundancy, and lends itself to the mathematical treatments of possible-worlds semantics (Chs. 10–11) (p. 75).

*Intuition note.* The theory links each meaningful sentence to a proposition that it expresses. Many meaning facts then become simple facts about which sentences express which propositions. The propositions are abstract, which is where the objections below begin.

### Objections to the Proposition Theory and replies (pp. 75–77)
- **Obj. 1 (weird entities).** Propositions are nowhere in space, uncreated and indestructible, and they have no causal properties (p. 75).
  - *Lycan's reply:* Occam's Razor is premature. We could know propositions to be unnecessary only if a rival theory explained the facts as well without them, and none exists yet (pp. 75–76).
- **Obj. 2 (alien to experience).** Understanding words hardly seems to be a case of "grasping" a nonspatial eternal object (p. 76).
  - *Moore's reply:* hearing an understood sentence differs from hearing one you do not understand. There is "another act of consciousness—an apprehension of their meaning". What is apprehended, he says, is what he calls a proposition (p. 76).
  - *A different reply:* science also explains the familiar by the unfamiliar (p. 76).
- **Obj. 3 (Harman).** Harman: the theory does not in fact explain anything, but merely repeats the data in jargon. It sounds as if "expresses a proposition" is just a fancier way of saying "is meaningful", and the suspicion remains until an independent way of understanding proposition talk is shown. The text compares Molière's physician and "dormitive virtue" (pp. 76–77).
  - *Reply:* once the theory is elaborated, with "grasping" as well as "expressing", it has some predictive and so some explanatory power. Whether it is plausible is a separate question (p. 77).
- **Obj. 4 (no causal role).** Meaning affects behaviour, and legal decisions in capital cases sometimes turn on it, so meaning must have some causal power. Propositions lack it, so they look like unnecessary posits (p. 77).
  - *Reply:* behaviour is not the only thing to explain. The meaning facts are the primary data, and propositions help to explain those (p. 77).

**Aftermath.** From early forms of Objections 1 and 4, 1950s ordinary-language philosophers concluded that meaning must be explained in terms connected to behaviour, namely language use. "Use" has many modes, some irrelevant to meaning, so different conceptions of linguistic use give competing theories (p. 77).

*Intuition note.* Lycan's replies answer the objections in turn, though he leaves open whether the elaborated theory is plausible (p. 77). Early forms of the weirdness and causal-power objections are what the 1950s ordinary-language philosophers took as a moral toward "use" theories (p. 77).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Let the expressing relation be a list of pairs:
- *Snow is white* → P1; *La neige est blanche* → P1.
- *Ann went to the bank* → P2 and → P3 (river-bank, money-bank).
- *Snow white the* → nothing.

Checks, using Lycan's definitions:
- Meaningful: the first three sentences each express at least 1 proposition, and the last expresses 0, so it is gibberish.
- Synonymous: the first two share P1, so they are synonymous (p. 73).
- Ambiguous: the third expresses 2 distinct propositions (P2, P3), and 2 ≥ 2, so it is ambiguous (p. 73).

This only illustrates how the definitions work, and it does not show that the theory is true.
