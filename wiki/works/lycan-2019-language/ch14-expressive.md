# Expressive Language

## L0 — One line
Some language expresses feelings or attitudes, either primarily or by the way (exclamations, expressive words, irony, sarcasm, slurs), and it is not covered by speech-act theory or by purely Gricean conversational implicature.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Most philosophy of language targets fact-stating. This chapter asks how to explain language that expresses feelings or attitudes. Lycan covers four things. First, interjections and "expressives" such as "damn". Second, verbal irony, where the echoic and pretense theories largely agree and neither fits every case. Third, sarcasm, which Camp analyses as "meaning inversion". Fourth, slurs, where the "received view" (a slur denotes a group and conventionally implicates a negative attitude) faces five objections. The central lesson is that irony has one necessary feature, normative expressiveness (perhaps echoic allusion too), and no clear criterion. The received view of slurs faces objections about non-derogatory uses, thought and degrees of offensiveness.

**Prerequisites:** speech acts and performatives; Grice's conversational implicature (cancelable) and conventional implicature; use vs. mention; truth-conditional semantics (Davidson's T-sentences).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Traditional theories of meaning centre on fact-stating, yet many normal utterances are not propositional; expressive parts of sentences also need explaining (pp. 183–184).
- "Expressives" (Kaplan) are words with syntactic roles that only express attitudes, e.g. "goddam". Treating exclamations as elliptical performatives may fit some ("Thank you", perhaps "Damn") but not obviously all (pp. 184–185).
- Grice's claim that irony is plain implicature is hard to sustain: in some cases, e.g. (5) and (11), there is no obvious implicatum, and the ironic aspect seems only to express a normative attitude (p. 186).
- Echoic and pretense theories share three main tenets; neither literal echoing nor strong pretense is required for irony. One necessary feature is normative expressiveness; echoic allusion is perhaps a second (pp. 187–188).
- Sarcasm has syntactic marks (word order, negative polarity items, "like"), which Lycan calls fatal to the conversational implicature analysis; some sarcastic sentences can be heard only as sarcasm, so the message is noncancelable (p. 189).
- Camp: sarcasm involves "meaning inversion" of a pretended commitment on a normative scale, at one of four scopes (pp. 189–190).
- Received view of slurs: denotation plus conventional implicature of negativity. Objections cover non-derogatory uses, analytic-truth consequences, missing neutral counterparts, pejorative thought, and degrees of offensiveness (pp. 191–193).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and introduction (pp. 183–184)
Expressive language includes purely expressive utterances (as in Chapter 6) and expressive particles inside sentences, such as "goddam" (p. 183). It also includes whole sentences that may or may not be used literally but sometimes noncancelably express attitudes, chiefly in irony (especially sarcasm) and in pejoratives (p. 183). These phenomena are neither illocutionary nor merely conversational implicature (p. 183).

The Anglo-American tradition is geared to fact-stating (p. 183). Use theorists and Austin complained about this against Verification and Truth-Condition Theories, and the complaint could also apply to Grice's view (p. 183). Many normal utterances have no illocutionary force and are not propositional (p. 184). Even if moral judgments are truth-apt, some utterances are evincings or ventings (cheers, groans). More importantly, parts of grammatical sentences have expressive features that a theory of meaning must explain (p. 184).

### The Expressive per se (pp. 184–185)
- Many ventings are articulate words: "Ouch" is in dictionaries as an interjection, and "Ha, ha" is often ironic (p. 184).
- *Ellipsis idea.* "Thank you" surely abbreviates the pure performative "I [hereby] thank you". Lewis analysed "Hurrah for Porky" as "I cheer Porky". But a bare "Hooray!" does not seem to be a performative with hidden syntax (p. 184).
- "Damn!" is a better candidate for ellipsis. A linguist might show it has a tacit object ("Damn it") and possibly a higher subject "God" (syntactically refuted by Quang 1971), and possibly a hortatory subjunctive "Let/may God damn [whatever]". But that is not the normal use: sincere utterers need not believe in God or urge condemnation (p. 184).
- Lycan sees no reason to treat all cases alike. "Ouch" may just be an exclamation; "Hooray" and "Damn" might have hidden propositional structure, but that needs positive argument. Some cases may counterexample verificationist and truth-conditional theories (p. 184).
- Some items are *subsentential*. "That damn cat has pooped on your pillow" is fine. "My son has married damn a new-age guru" is ungrammatical, so "damn" is not merely an interjection there. "Goddam" does not mean "condemned to Hell by God"; it is "expressive only, despite its quite determinate syntactic role" (pp. 184–185).
- **Kaplan's "expressives":** words of a language with syntactic properties that only express attitudes or mental states and seem to add no content to propositional meaning (p. 185).

*Intuition note:* "Damn" in "that damn cat" changes how you feel about the cat, not what is claimed about it. A sentence can have grammar for such a word without having content for it.

### Irony and Sarcasm (pp. 185–188)
Grice held irony and sarcasm are simply conversational implicature. Lycan says several facts about sarcasm embarrass this view. He assumes irony is the wider category, and irony may not have a single correct analysis (p. 185).

Examples: (1) "Karbunkle's not the smartest kid on the block" (ironic understatement). (2) Mercutio's "'tis not so deep as a well… 'twill serve". (3) "He's a genius, you know", said where the man only overrates his intelligence. (4) "Where are the Thought Police when you need them?" Also (5)–(11), such as Russell's "Hegelians… will probably conclude that he [the King of France] wears a wig" and (8) "A revolution is a dinner party after all!" (pp. 185–186).

**Against Grice:** it is hard to reconstruct each as a Gricean inference "He has said that p…". In (5) and (11) there is no obvious implicatum. The ironic aspect seems only to express an attitude, and that attitude is normative or refers to a social norm (p. 186). Grice himself quietly admitted that the speaker only "made as if" to say p (p. 186; note 5, p. 195).

Wilson (2006: 1722) states a very general idea: irony draws attention to a discrepancy between the description of the world the speaker apparently puts forward and the way she wants to suggest things were. Many other acts do this too, so two more specific accounts have emerged (p. 186).

**Echoic theory** (Sperber and Wilson 1981; p. 186):
- Echoing means repeating someone's utterance, sometimes critically or mockingly.
- Verbal irony is echoic "allusion": echoing a usually imaginary thought or utterance tacitly attributed to someone else.
- Its point is to express a dissociative attitude and/or show the thought's falsity, risibility or inadequacy.
- Application: in (1) the speaker echoes someone who misjudges how stupid Karbunkle is. (2) responds to Romeo's "Courage, man; the hurt cannot be much". (3) echoes the subject's self-assessment (p. 186).

**Pretense theory** (Clark and Gerrig 1984; Recanati 2004; Currie 2006): the speaker only pretends to assert or ask what the sentence does, mocking one who might use it with its apparent force (p. 187). For (1), the speaker makes believe not to appreciate Karbunkle's stupidity (p. 187).

**How far do they differ?**
- They share three tenets: (i) Grice's model is wrong because an ironic utterance is not a genuine assertion, question etc.; (ii) irony expresses an attitude, not just extra information; (iii) the attitude is dissociative (p. 187).
- "Pretense" must be only Crimmins's "shallow" pretense, "talking as if". That sharpens the question of how the theories differ. Both echoing and pretending do sometimes figure in irony (p. 187).
- For pretense: Grice (1978) noted that irony cannot be prefaced by "To speak ironically", since announcing the pretense would spoil it, whereas "To echo [U]…" is fine. A pretense theorist may add that "echoing" a merely possible utterance is really "as if" echoing (p. 187).
- For echo: Wilson and Sperber argue pretense alone does not suffice. Example (12), "The first penguin to achieve a standing broad jump of 3 meters will be named Karbunkle", gets only "What?", because no one said anything about penguins. So pretense theorists must add the attributive feature ad hoc. Out-of-the-blue utterances can be ironic if attributable to a person, group or culture (pp. 187–188).
- Wilson (2006) says pretense talk is appropriate only with mimicry. Lycan thinks this too strong, since shallow pretense needs no mimicry (p. 188).
- Better point: in some examples, such as (9) and (11), there is genuine commitment to, if not outright assertion of, the truth of all or part of what is uttered. In (13), "the bank clerk helpfully shut the door in my face", only a small part is ironic. It may be replied that the speaker pretends to find the act helpful (p. 188).

**Lycan's stock-taking:** actual echoing is not required, and pretense in any strong sense seems not required. At best there must be echoic "allusion", which is not echoing and is pretense only in a very weak sense. An ironic utterance may be a genuine speech act with its literal force. So there is one necessary feature, normative expressiveness, "perhaps" echoic allusion (it is strained in (9)–(11)), and some typical features, but no clear criterion. Irony is very likely not a natural kind (p. 188).

*Intuition note:* Irony needs a target, some belief or attitude that you distance yourself from. Beyond that, what counts as irony is a loose bundle of typical features, not a strict definition.

### Sarcasm (pp. 188–190)
**Paradigm:** a speaker utters a declarative, meaning the opposite (not just the negation) of what it means, in an ironic tone, thereby expressing a normative attitude, as in (14) "You're a fine friend". Questions, imperatives and exclamations can be sarcastic, and the ironic tone is not required. There is no sharp line between sarcasm and irony; (4), (5), (6) and (8) may be borderline (p. 188).

**Syntactic marks, "fatal to the conversational implicature analysis"** (p. 189):
- (15) "A fine friend you are" differs from (14) only in word order but cannot be heard as sincere.
- Negative polarity items ("ever", "any") appear in non-negative sarcastic sentences, as in (16)–(18), which can be heard only as sarcasm, so the message is noncancelable.
- Camp (2012) notes the indicator "like": (19) "Like that's a good idea".

**Camp's analysis:**
- Sarcasm always involves "meaning inversion", where "meaning" is broad enough to include implicatures and illocutionary force (p. 189).
- Sarcastic utterances presuppose a normative scale, pretend to (or evoke) one commitment on it, and communicate an inversion of that pretended commitment (p. 189).
- Four scopes (p. 189):

| Scope | What is inverted |
|---|---|
| Propositional | the proposition a sincere assertion would carry, as in (14) |
| Lexical | one expression, as in (13) |
| "Like"-prefixed | the focal content of an embedded declarative, as in (19) |
| Illocutionary | the whole speech act, as in (20) "Could I entice you to eat another small slice?" |

- In (21), B says Aunt Louisa "never stays for more than a month". This is a genuine true assertion. B pretends to implicate that she is at the easy end of a guest-burden scale, and thereby implicates the contrary: her visits are burdensome (p. 190).
- In (22), "Thanks for holding the door", said after the door slammed, the scale is politeness. By pretending to thank, the speaker shows how low the action ranks (p. 190).
- Lycan sets Camp's appeal to pretense aside and leaves its assessment to the reader. He calls her analysis the most thorough and sophisticated he knows and "inversion" a good thing to mean by "sarcasm" (p. 190).

### Pejorative Language (pp. 190–193)
**Kinds** (the list is "far from exhaustive"; Lycan numbers two items "(iv)", so there are seven entries) (pp. 190–191):
- Vague negative adjectives ("goddam", "rotten", "lousy"); it seems adequate to classify them as Kaplan expressives.
- Vague words for disliked people ("jerk", "creep"). They are expressive but not expressives, since they have referential content (applying to persons).
- Words adding normative assessment to descriptive concepts ("assassinated", perhaps "murdered").
- Specific terms for types of people: "moron", "lunatic", "slut".
- Impolite terms for religions or ideologies.
- Derogatory terms for sexual orientation.
- Racial and ethnic slurs.

**Slurs:** "frog", "limey" and others denote types of people (ethnicity, race, nationality), but also express negative attitudes and/or unflattering beliefs about them, and are offensive (p. 191). This is controversial: some deny that slurs denote (rejected in note 8, p. 195), and slurs can be used without expressing negativity. So he states the **"received view"**: slurs denote and conventionally implicate negativity (p. 191).

The negativity may be mere feeling, or a vague belief, which would be a conventional implicature on a weak reading. Lycan does not decide, but slurs are conventional words, not ordinary words plus sneers (p. 191).

**Objection 1: non-derogatory uses.** If true, this would seem to refute the conventional implicature model, since conventional implicatures are noncancelable (p. 191). Three sorts:
1. *Mock insults* among friends. They work precisely because the words are derogatory (p. 191).
2. *Appropriation or reclamation* (Hom 2008; Anderson and Lepore 2013), as with "Black is beautiful". **Reply:** this changes the meaning, as if "but" were made to imply no contrast. A reclaimed slur is a former slur, derogatory no longer in the new dialect (pp. 191–192).
3. *Troublesome examples* (Hom, Hornsby, Richard): (23) "He is not a n—r", (24) "He called me a n—r" and (25) "Institutions that treat Chinese people as chinks are racist" (p. 192). Even if these only mention the word, merely voicing it is usually offensive, which Anderson and Lepore take to show slurs are taboo words, not just implicature generators. It remains to be explained why (23)–(25) do not carry the offensive implicatures, if they do not (p. 192).

**Objection 2 (Croom 2011):** the received view gives "African American" and the slur the same literal meaning. It then seems committed to racist claims literally expressing analytic truths knowable a priori. Lycan adds that T-sentences face a similar problem: "'Xiang is a chink' is true iff Xiang is a chink" and the Chinese version together entail "'Xiang is Chinese' is true iff Xiang is a chink" (p. 192). Proponents must accept this, arguing strict literal truth is not what matters for offensiveness. Or one might adopt the Conjunction Theory (pejorative content as a conjunct of what is said), but Saka (2007) criticises that (p. 192).

**Objection 3:** the received view implies every slur has a neutral counterpart. DiFranco (2015) argues compositional slurs such as "slant-eye" do not always have one. Ashwell (2016) argues that some slurs, such as "slut", have no non-normative counterpart (pp. 192–193).

**Objection 4 (anticipated by Williamson 2009, himself a received-view proponent):** implicature is about public communication, so it does not carry over to silent private thought, yet we think pejoratively (p. 193).
- **Williamson's reply:** pejorativeness is still essentially communicative, and there is communication in thought, "in silent communication with oneself" (p. 193).
- Lycan cannot tell whether Williamson posits a deeper flatly literal level of thought. He notes any expressive, such as a silent "Hooray!", can occur in thought. The issue deserves philosophy of mind discussion and recurs in the next chapter (p. 193).

**Objection 5:** slurs vary in offensiveness ("n—r" probably the worst, "limey" has lost its sting), and the received view does not explain this (p. 193).
- **Reply (Lycan):** the differences are consequential, socially so, and he expects legally so, but they are not themselves linguistic even though they are differences between words. What explains them is general social and especially historical facts (Saka 2007; Popa-Wyatt and Wyatt 2017, via discourse roles and power imbalances) (p. 193).
- **Rejoinder (Lycan, on behalf of use theorists):** the reply insists on a narrow sense of "linguistic", which a use theorist would and should reject. There is not so sharp a difference between meaning and use broadly speaking (p. 193).

*Intuition note:* The received view splits a slur into "what group" and "what attitude". Each objection tests that split. It struggles when the attitude seems to vary, vanish or be absent from the group-term, or when slur and neutral term seem to share their literal truth-conditions.

### Summary (p. 194)
Expressive language has two main categories beyond mere exclamations: irony and pejoratives. Each of the echoic and pretense theories "is onto something" but is too strong as it stands. Sarcasm is a specific form of irony, and the received view of slurs faces strong objections (p. 194).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
*Camp-style scale for (22).* Let politeness run 1 (rude) to 5 (very polite). Holding a door for someone ranks 5. Slamming it in someone's face ranks 1.
1. The speaker says "Thanks for holding the door", pretending to commit to rank 5 for the addressee's act.
2. The actual act ranks 1.
3. The communicated inversion places the act at 1. The distance is 5 − 1 = 4 points.
4. This fits Camp's idea that the speaker draws attention to how low the act ranks. The pretended rank is high, so the inversion goes to the low end.

*Deletion test for expressives.* Compare "That damn cat has pooped on your pillow" with "That cat has pooped on your pillow". Both are true under the same fact (the cat pooped), so "damn" seems to add no truth-conditional content. The first adds only an attitude toward the cat. The author says such words "do not seem to contribute content to propositional meaning" (p. 185).

## Concepts
- [[expressive-kaplan]] (new) — a word with syntactic role that only expresses attitude and adds no propositional content, e.g. "goddam" (p. 185)
- [[performative utterance]] — an utterance that does what it says; the ellipsis analysis treats "Thank you" as one (p. 184)
- [[conversational implicature]] — Grice's model; here shown inadequate for irony and sarcasm (pp. 185–186)
- [[conventional-implicature]] (new) — noncancelable implied content tied to a word's use; the received view's account of slurs (p. 191)
- [[verbal-irony]] (new) — expressing a dissociative, normative attitude toward a tacitly alluded pre-existing belief or attitude; a loose, probably non-natural kind (p. 188)
- [[echoic-theory-of-irony]] (new) — irony as echoic "allusion" to an attributed (often imaginary) thought (p. 186)
- [[pretense-theory-of-irony]] (new) — irony as pretending, in a shallow "talking as if" sense, to perform the speech act (p. 187)
- [[sarcasm-meaning-inversion]] (new) — Camp: sarcasm inverts a pretended commitment on a normative scale, at four scopes (p. 189)
- [[received-view-of-slurs]] (new) — slurs denote a group and conventionally implicate negativity (p. 191)
- [[use vs. mention]] — examples (23)–(25) may only mention a slur, yet voicing it is still offensive (p. 192)
- [[meaning as use]] — the use theorist's rejoinder: meaning and use are not sharply separable (p. 193)

## Questions this raises
- If irony is "very likely not a natural kind" (p. 188), what should a theory of meaning explain about it?
- Do the non-derogatory uses (23)–(25) involve cancelation, or something else, like taboo?
- Can pejorative thought be explained without silent communication? Lycan says the issue recurs next chapter (p. 193).
- Is the line between "linguistic" and "social/historical" facts about words defensible (Objection 5 and Rejoinder)?
