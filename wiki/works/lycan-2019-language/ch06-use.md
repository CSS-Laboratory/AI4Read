# "Use" Theories

## L0 — One line
"Use" theories hold that an expression's meaning is its role in rule-governed social practice, but they struggle to say what makes such a practice a language rather than a mere game.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can meaning be explained without abstract "propositions"? "Use" theories, inspired by Wittgenstein, say an expression's meaning is its role in rule-governed social behaviour, as a chess piece's identity is its role in chess. Sellars adds inferring as a social act, to handle complex sentences. Lycan raises six objections: Twin Earth, proper names, novel sentences, understanding without use, how language differs from ordinary games, and how a sentence can say that P. Brandom's inferentialism defines use by commitments and entitlements in giving and asking for reasons. It answers some objections. Lycan suggests it is closer in spirit to verification accounts than to Wittgenstein.

**Prerequisites:** the Proposition Theory (an earlier chapter); Russell/Strawson on descriptions (Ch. 2); Twin Earth and the Causal–Historical Theory (earlier chapters, only referred to here); the idea of entailment.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Wittgenstein and Austin reject treating sentences as abstract specimens. Utterances are behaviour in a social practice (p. 79).
- Language is learned as conventional social behaviour. Expressions are like game pieces, and meaning is constituted by tacit rules of correct use (pp. 80–81).
- Sellars' Inferential Theory (Lycan's name for it) handles entailment (one sentence following from another) as a rule-governed practice of inferring, backed by social sanctions (p. 82).
- Lycan's objections: Twin Earth, names, novel sentences, empty jargon use, and how language differs from ordinary games (pp. 82–84).
- Objection 5 asks what distinguishes language from ordinary rule-governed games that mean nothing. On Lycan's rejoinder, such games seem to meet the use theorist's explicit conditions, so those conditions are not sufficient for language (p. 84).
- Objection 6 adds that games do not license saying that P (p. 85).
- Brandom defines use as the commitments and entitlements of asserting (what a speaker must defend and may infer). Keeping track of them is called "scorekeeping". Lycan says this overcomes objections 5 and 6 and may evade 3 (p. 86).
- Brandom treats reference as a shadow of inferential practice, which Lycan says worsens objection 2 (the name problem). Lycan also suggests that, because defense and justification are central, inferentialism is closer in spirit to verification accounts (Ch. 8) than to Wittgenstein's original idea (p. 86).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and opening (pp. 79–80)
- **Russell's habit and the objection to it.** Russell wrote a sentence on the blackboard and studied (as he contended) the proposition it expressed. Wittgenstein and Austin said this picture is wrong (p. 79).
  - Language is behaviour, specifically social practice (p. 79).
  - Blackboard sentences and "propositions" are "fairly violent abstractions" from real utterings (p. 79). Footnote 1 gives three examples. Speech has no pauses between words. "Sentence" presupposes grammaticality. We silently correct semi-grammatical speech (pp. 87–88).
  - To utter something is first of all to do something. It is behaviour swept into a rule-governed social practice (p. 79).
- **Two obstacles (overview, p. 79).** Lycan says "use" theories of this kind face two main obstacles: explaining how language use differs from ordinary rule-governed activities such as chess that generate no meaning, and explaining how a sentence can mean that so-and-so. He says Brandom has recently offered a "use" theory that claims to meet both (p. 79).
- **Strawson link.** Strawson's objections to Russell come from the same perspective. Lycan says they are still intuitively compelling to many, which recommends the perspective (p. 80).

### "Use" in a roughly Wittgensteinian sense (pp. 80–82)
- **Caveat.** Lycan sketches "a Wittgensteinian view" and does not attribute a theory to Wittgenstein. Wittgenstein opposed systematic theorizing, and his followers object to "Wittgenstein's theory of…" (p. 80).
- **Method.** Start from understanding, which is what is learned when one learns a language (p. 80).
  - What is learned is a complicated social behaviour. It covers how to behave when others make certain noises, and what noises to make when circumstances are right (p. 80).
  - The rules are rarely articulated. Children pick them up without realizing it (p. 80).
- **Against other theories.** Entity theories treat meanings as static, inert things. Wittgenstein also scorned the view that meaning essentially involves reference. He did not deny that some referential relations exist (p. 80).
- **Game analogy.** Linguistic activity is governed by rules as playing a game is. Expressions are like game pieces. A knight is defined by its legal moves, so an expression's meaning is constituted by the tacit rules for its correct conversational use (pp. 80–81).
- **Simple cases.** Expressions such as "Hello", "Thanks", "Bless you" and "You're on" do not seem to mean what they do by standing for things or expressing propositions. They are conventional devices (greeting, thanking and so on) with appropriate occasions and responses. "Meaning" here is the function they perform in current social practice. On the Wittgensteinian view, this is the home of all meaning, although most expressions have vastly more complicated social roles (p. 81).
- **Language-game** is Wittgenstein's term: the greeting game, the wedding game, the arithmetic game (p. 81).
- **Builders' language** (1953: 2). Four words, "block", "pillar", "slab" and "beam", are used by a builder and an assistant. The builder says "slab" and the assistant brings a stone of that shape (p. 81).
  - **Objector:** "slab" refers to such blocks and means the proposition that the assistant is to bring one (p. 81).
  - **Wittgensteinian reply:** this misses the point. In this little game the function is to trigger a useful action, not to refer or express an eternal proposition. So there is no reason to posit meanings as entities (p. 81).
- **Extrapolation problem.** Long sentences, such as "The present Queen of England is bald", have no easily identifiable social role beyond asserting that content. An extra mechanism is needed. The positivists used verification (Ch. 8). Sellars used inferring as a social act (pp. 81–82).
- **Sellars' rules** (p. 82):
  - language-entry rules govern what to say in response to nonlinguistic events such as observations;
  - language-exit rules govern what to do in response to utterances;
  - language–language rules govern what to say as the product of inference. These are the most important.
  - Lycan calls this the **Inferential Theory of Meaning**.
- **Entailment.** Meaningfulness, synonymy and ambiguity are not a problem. Entailment looks harder, but it can be reconstrued as a rule-governed practice of inferring (p. 82).
  - On this theory, "Harold is fat and Ben is stupid" entails "Ben is stupid" because asserting the first while denying the second draws severe social sanctions (and failing to act as if the second is true at least raises eyebrows) (p. 82).
  - According to "use" theories, this practice itself makes the inference valid. No independent truth-preserving guarantee does (p. 82).

*Intuition note.* On this view, the meaning of "slab" is a place in a routine: say it and the assistant acts. Entailment is likewise treated as a routine: if you assert one sentence, others expect you not to deny what follows from it, and to behave as if it is true.

### Objections and some replies (pp. 82–86)
Lycan says the Inferential Theory avoids every objection raised against the Referential, Ideational and Proposition theories. It is also naturalistic. Still, Lycan says there are some formidable problems (p. 82).

1. **Twin Earth.** The objection says the language-games on Earth and Twin Earth are exactly parallel, yet the words differ in meaning. A statement and its twin may differ in truth value, so meaning is not exhausted by role in a language-game (p. 82).
   - *Reply:* classify games more finely. We deal with H₂O and our twins with XYZ, so the rules differ. This was Sellars' original intention. His entry and exit rules were stated in terms of things in the environment (pp. 82–83).
2. **Proper names.** Try stating a rule of use for "William G. Lycan" that every competent speaker obeys without exception. Lycan says the only candidate rules that occur to him push the use theorist into a description theory of names. He adds that Wittgenstein found descriptivism congenial, but had not read Kripke (p. 83). No reply is given here.
3. **Novel sentences.** We understand long, new sentences at first hearing. Conventions have never been directed at those sentences, unlike chess pieces, "Slab" or "Hello" (p. 83).
   - The Wittgensteinian must grant Frege's Principle: we understand novel sentences compositionally, from the words and how they are combined (p. 83).
   - So meaning is not just conventional norms directed upon the sentence. It also depends largely on internal structure (p. 83). No direct reply here.
4. **Use without understanding.** The objection asks whether one could know an expression's use and follow it mechanically without understanding it. Lycan's example is an undergraduate who understood nothing of a phenomenology course but earned (or "earned") an A. Use perfect, or at least graded A; meaning nil (p. 83). No reply here.
5. **Games vs. language-games.** Chess moves and tennis shots lack the meaning of linguistic expressions, except if used as a code (p. 84).
   - **Example:** a community that puts "words" together by arbitrary rules, such as threes with equal syllable counts or rhyming pairs of growing length. A newcomer could learn every rule and still not know what, if anything, was said. In this simple case, at least, nothing is being said. Lycan finds it hard to see how adding complexity would help (p. 84).
   - **First reply:** someone might argue that, with rich enough rules tied to surroundings, reference and predication could be recovered. Suppose every third player shouts "Here, waiter" when the waiter enters and gets a martini. Then "waiter" seems to refer to the waiter (p. 84).
   - **Rejoinder:** perhaps the utterances then mean something, but only because they refer, not just because of their deployment (p. 84). Lycan then stipulates no reference to things outside the game; it seems even more obvious that the game is not even the beginning of a language. So the use theorist's explicit conditions are not sufficient for something's being a language (p. 84).
   - **Second reply (Waismann, 1965a):** he hints that genuine language-games are "integrat[ed] … into life", so that a language-game cannot be encapsulated (p. 85).
   - **Rejoinder:** shaggy-dog jokes are encapsulated language-games. Integration is usually thought of as referring, which the Wittgensteinian rejects. Lycan finds it hard to see how the Wittgensteinian can spell out integration with other practices in a way that (a) explains how moves take on propositional content, but (b) does not secretly introduce referring (p. 85).
   - Lycan uses "propositional content" in a weak sense: whatever property of a sentence is somehow expressed by a "that" clause, without taking this to be an expression relation to an abstract proposition (p. 85).
6. **Saying that P.** One clear sense in which a practice is an actual language is that one can say that P in it, and saying things is surely essential to language. Chess or the parlor game licenses no indirect discourse. Nobody has said, asked or suggested anything (p. 85).

*Intuition note.* Objections 5 and 6 are the core worry. Rules for use can be written down for games that mean nothing. So rules alone seem not to explain what makes sounds into saying something.

### Inferentialism (pp. 85–87)
- Lycan notes that one might be tempted to concede to the Referential Theory. He instead turns to Brandom (1994), the most recent version of Sellars' view (pp. 85–86).
- **Brandom's use.** A normative conception: a sentence's use is the set of commitments and entitlements tied to uttering it publicly (p. 86).
  - The paradigm is asserting. One commits to defending the assertion against challenges, typically by inferring it from a less challengeable sentence (p. 86).
  - Asserting licenses others to repeat it, infer from it and take on commitments. It also gives the speaker entitlement to further inferences (p. 86).
  - "Giving and asking for reasons" is rule-governed, and score is kept. Scorekeeping (as in Lewis 1979) means tracking commitments and entitlements, not antagonism (p. 86).
  - The reasons rightly offered for S, and the norms for using S to defend other sentences, constitute S's meaning (p. 86).
- **Reference** is just a shadow cast by inferential practices on whole sentences. It is not a fit subject for independent theorizing, and the Causal–Historical Theory is entirely beside the point; this would please Wittgenstein. Lycan adds that this exacerbates objection 2 (p. 86).
- **Lycan's remarks on how Brandom fares against the objections** (p. 86). He says it overcomes some of them:
  - 5: chess moves and "Slab" are not things one supports with evidential reasons, and they are not inferences.
  - 6: Lycan says it is not a problem, because Sellars gave an elegant inferentialist account of "that" clauses.
  - 3: subsentential expressions have meaning only derivatively, but Brandom recognizes a weak compositionality, so he "may evade" it.
  - He also treats names, descriptions, indexicals, quantification and anaphora by their contributions to commitment/entitlement potentials.
- **Lycan's remark.** The centrality of defense, support, justification and acceptance suggests that inferentialist theories are closer in spirit to verification accounts than to Wittgenstein's original idea (Ch. 8) (p. 86).
- **Other approaches.** Lycan mentions a somewhat different use theory (Alston; Barker) based on Austin's "illocutionary force"; that concept is introduced only in Ch. 12. Grice's theory comes next (p. 87).

### Notes worth keeping
- Footnote 2: Lycan says *Investigations* §43 is often misquoted. It says meaning can be defined as use only for a large class of cases, though not for all. Lycan says Wittgenstein did not hold that meaning is use, period (p. 88).
- Horwich (1998) (footnote 4): each word has a "basic use regularity", and "accepting" a sentence is supposed to be a psychological notion, not a form of social behaviour. Lycan says that, unless he has missed it, nothing explains how the "schema" in Horwich's account of compositionality has a use regularity (p. 88).

## Toy example (ours)
Language-game A has two rules: R1, builder says "slab" → assistant brings a slab; R2, "beam" → assistant brings a beam.
- Builder says "slab", "beam", "slab". Assistant brings slab, beam, beam.
- Moves 1 and 2 follow the rules. Move 3 breaks R1. That is 2 of 3 correct, with 1 sanctionable error.
- On the use view, the meaning of "slab" is its rule R1. Nothing about referring appears in the table.

Game B is a parlor game with two rules: R1′, a player says "xx" → pass the bowl; R2′, "yy" → pass the pepper.
- Its rule table has exactly the same form as A's: 2 rules, each pairing an utterance with an action.
- Nothing in the table distinguishes A from B. Yet in B nobody says anything (objection 5, p. 84). So rule-table form alone is not enough.

## Concepts
- [[meaning as use]] — an expression's meaning is its characteristic function in rule-governed social behaviour. This is "a roughly Wittgensteinian" view, not a doctrine Wittgenstein held universally (pp. 80–81, 88).
- [[language-game]] — a rule-governed social practice in which words are moves, such as greeting or wedding talk (p. 81).
- [[inferential-theory-of-meaning]] (new) — Lycan's name for a view built on Sellars' entry, exit and language–language rules, with inferring as a social act (p. 82).
- [[inferentialism-brandom]] (new) — a sentence's use is the commitments and entitlements of asserting it, tracked by scorekeeping in giving and asking for reasons; the reasons and norms for using it constitute its meaning (p. 86).
- [[entity-theory-of-meaning]] (new) — view treating meanings as static, inert things, which Wittgenstein and Austin attacked (p. 80).
- [[compositionality]] (new) — Frege's Principle: we understand novel sentences from their words and how they are combined (p. 83).
- [[propositional-content]] (new) — Lycan's weak sense: whatever property is somehow expressed by a "that" clause, without taking it to be a relation to an abstract proposition (p. 85).
- [[semantic externalism]] — Twin Earth meaning differences are used against use theories. Sellars' environment-involving rules are offered as a reply (pp. 82–83).
- [[causal-historical theory of reference]] — according to Lycan, beside the point for Brandom (p. 86).

## Canonical instances
- [[builders-language-slab]] — a primitive language-game whose point is to trigger action, not to refer (p. 81).
- [[chess-pieces-as-meaning]] — game pieces are defined by their rules, as expressions are (p. 81).
- [[harold-ben-entailment]] — entailment as social sanction against asserting one and denying the other (p. 82).
- [[twin-earth-language-games]] — objection 1 (p. 82).
- [[parlor-game-community]] — rule-governed mock language with no saying (p. 84).
- [[waiter-martini-game]] — reference recovered from rich game rules (p. 84).
- [[spy-chess-code]] — chess moves with meaning only by stipulated code (p. 84).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Scorekeeping of commitments (Brandom, citing Lewis 1979) ↔ convention: both are shared public record-keeping that sustains coordinated expectations.
- [structural-analogy] Wittgenstein's rule-governed language-games ↔ convention (Lewis): regularities kept up by mutual expectation, though the unit does not make this link.
- [structural-analogy] Inferentialism's focus on justification ↔ verification accounts (Ch. 8): Lycan suggests the two are close in spirit (p. 86).
- [structural-analogy] Use theory via "illocutionary force" ↔ speech act theory (Ch. 12): same social-action stance (p. 87).
- [structural-analogy] Objection 1 ↔ semantic externalism: identical behaviour, different meanings.

## Questions this raises
- If Sellars' rules mention water, not just behaviour, is the theory still a theory of use as behaviour?
- Can inferentialism say what makes a game of giving and asking for reasons a language without bringing in reference?
- No reply to objection 2 is given where it is raised, and Brandom's rejection of reference is said to make it worse. Could a use theorist give a rule for names without descriptivism?
- If Lycan is right that Brandom's theory is closer in spirit to verification accounts, how far is it still a "use" theory?
