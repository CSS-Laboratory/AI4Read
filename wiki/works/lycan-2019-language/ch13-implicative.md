# Implicative Relations

## L0 — One line
Utterances can imply things that their sentences do not entail, and Lycan distinguishes several kinds (conversational, presuppositional, conventional, indirect-force); some of these differ in how they are grasped, whether they can be cancelled (denied without contradiction), and what happens when the implied thing is false.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Besides entailment, utterances carry other implications. Lycan surveys four. (1) Conveyed meanings and invited inferences, which Grice explains as conversational implicature (a message conveyed without being said), worked out from norms of cooperative talk. (2) Presupposition, where failure supposedly leaves a sentence without truth-value. (3) Conventional implicature, carried by particular words such as "but". (4) Indirect force, where a sentence performs a speech act other than the one its mood suggests. He reports objections to Grice (especially Davis's) and the rival Relevance Theory. He finds clear cases of semantic presupposition hard to come by. He thinks Searle's Gricean account of indirect force leaves data unexplained.

**Prerequisites:** entailment, truth-value, Russell's and Strawson's debate about "the present King of France", speech acts and felicity conditions, deictic (context-dependent) terms.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A speaker can say that P and convey Q, without effort for either party; a competent speaker must grasp this (pp. 163–4).
- Grice: a hearer reasons from the Cooperative Principle (a rule to contribute what the talk requires) and its maxims to what the speaker implicates. Such implicatures can be worked out and are cancelable: the speaker can deny them without contradiction (pp. 165, 167).
- Lycan argues that Strawson's inference from the fact that no one would reply 'That's false' does not follow, because that reply would be misleading by Strength (p. 167).
- Three objections to Grice's theory: speed of reasoning, Davis's "positive stage" gap, and the need for pragmatics before a full proposition exists (p. 168).
- Relevance theorists reject specific maxims in favour of a general cognitive drive for efficiency. They stress processes behind what is explicitly conveyed ("explicature"): saturation (filling a slot in logical form, e.g. "Henry is ready" — for what?), free enrichment (adding unmandated explicit content; a disputed category), and ad hoc concept construction (broadening or narrowing a word's sense in context) (pp. 169–172).
- Semantic presupposition (the implied sentence is necessary for both the sentence and its negation, and this cannot be cancelled) has, Lycan says, no uncontroversial examples; he treats some candidates as conversational implicature and one, (25), as conventional implicature, and leaves (23) and (24) harder (pp. 172–174).
- Conventional implicature is carried by word choice, is not worked out and is not cancelable. Lycan suggests a good way to investigate the kinds of implication is to ask what penalty follows when the implied sentence is false (pp. 174–175).
- Indirect force is not fully handled by Searle's Gricean strategy; surface-grammatical marks of indirect force balk it, since there is nothing to work out (pp. 176–177).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and the idea of implication (pp. 162–163)
Sentences entail other sentences, but they also linguistically imply things they do not strictly entail. Hearers pick up such implications either by assuming that speakers are cooperative or by noticing that they are deliberately uncooperative, and drawing inferences from either (p. 162). Davidson spoke of capturing the "felt implications" of sentences, meaning entailments. Grice (1975) taught us that implication comes in kinds (p. 163). Lycan surveys four.

### Conveyed Meanings and Invited Inferences (pp. 163–164)
**Conveyed meaning.** A speaker utters a sentence meaning P but obviously intends mainly to convey Q (p. 163). Example: "There's the door" said to an obstreperous visitor. It does not mean "You are to leave now", and the speaker has not come out and said so (p. 163).
- This differs from the pathological speaker-meaning mismatches of Chapter 7; it is a normal phenomenon (p. 163).
- Asked if Smedley is a good philosopher, I say (1a) "Smedley summarizes texts pretty accurately and has very nice handwriting" or (1b) "Smedley is very good at ping pong". I convey that he is not very good at philosophy. Whether the literal content is true is immaterial. Hearers grasp this without noticing (p. 163).
- A foreigner who took such utterances literally would miss something important about the language (pp. 163–4).

**Invited inference** (Geis and Zwicky, 1971) (p. 164):
- (2) "If you mow my lawn, I'll give you ten dollars" is literally one-way. I could add "if you don't, I'll give you ten dollars anyway". On hearing (2) alone, the hearer fills in that if the lawn is not mown there will be no ten dollars: the mere "if" is heard as "if and only if".
- (3) "Martha watched the Education School burning and smiled with pleasure" is heard as causal. It says only that two things happened.
- (4a) "John and Marsha fell in love and they got married" versus (4b) with the order reversed. The temporal order is invited, not entailed.

*Intuition note:* Hearers routinely get more out of a sentence than its literal content. Those extra messages are what the rest of the chapter tries to classify and explain.

### Conversational Implicature (pp. 164–169)
**Cooperative Principle (CP)** (Grice 1975, original p. 26, as quoted by Lycan on p. 165): make your conversational contribution as required, at the stage it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the exchange (p. 165). Its corollaries are the maxims. Lycan's numbering, with short descriptions (p. 165):
- M1 Strength: be as informative as required.
- M2: not more informative than required.
- M3 Truthfulness: do not say what you believe false.
- M4 Evidence: do not say what you lack adequate evidence for.
- M5 Relevance.
- M6: avoid ambiguity.
- M7: be brief.

Their function is to expedite the giving and receiving of information (p. 165).

**Grice's template** for the hearer's reasoning (p. 165, quoting p. 31 of Grice). In outline:
1. The speaker has said that p.
2. There is no reason to think he is not observing the maxims (or at least CP).
3. He could not be doing so unless he thought that q.
4. He knows (and knows that I know that he knows) that I can see that the supposition that he thinks q is required.
5. He has done nothing to stop me thinking q.
6. So he intends, or at least allows, me to think q: he has implicated that q.

"Implicate" is Grice's technical term for this indirect communication (p. 165).

**Applications** (pp. 165–6):
- "There's the door": the door seems irrelevant to what the visitor has in mind. By M5 the door must be relevant to something the speaker has in mind; after a few more steps (he has deliberately shown he wants me to know where the door is, and why?) the visitor concludes that the speaker must want him to go out. This reasoning is subconscious and fast. Grice himself did not offer it as a literal psychological model (p. 165).
- Context may help: the visitor may realize that he has been fairly offensive, that no drink has been offered, that the speaker is not smiling, and that it is 6:45 p.m. Background assumed to be shared is Stalnaker's "common ground" (p. 166).
- (1a)/(1b): by Strength and Evidence I imply I cannot say anything stronger. Since (we may suppose) I am well placed to judge, this deliberately invites the hearer to conclude that there is nothing good to say (p. 166).
- Hearers assume any utterance after a question is an answer ("We had spaghetti last night" as a reason for lateness). Lycan adds that skilled speakers, such as advertisers and politicians, can use implicature to convey falsehoods without uttering false sentences (p. 166).
- (2): if I would pay anyway, mentioning the lawn would violate Relevance and the rule against prolixity (p. 166).
- (3) and (4a) are harder. For (3) the inference probably combines Relevance with knowledge of burning and of Martha's likely attitude. (4a) may involve narrative assumptions (p. 166).

**Flouting** a maxim, that is, violating it blatantly, also generates implicature (p. 166).
- (5) A reviewer writes that "Ms X produced a series of sounds that corresponded quite closely to the score of…". Grice's gloss: presumably to emphasize a striking difference from what is usually called singing (pp. 166–7).
- A more common type of example is a sentence that is too obviously false; Grice cites sarcasm there (p. 167).
- Metaphor: Grice suggests metaphor typically flouts M3. Grice: examples like "You are the cream in my coffee" characteristically involve categorical falsity, so the contradictory of what is made as if to be said is a truism, which the speaker cannot be trying to get across. The most likely supposition is that the speaker attributes to the hearer some feature in respect of which the hearer resembles (more or less fancifully) cream in coffee (p. 167). Lycan defers assessment to Chapter 15.

**Two characteristic features** (p. 167):
1. An implicature is something worked out, or workable-out, by reasoning of this kind. If no such reasoning is available, the implication is of another kind.
2. It is cancelable. Example: "Smedley is very good at ping pong. But don't get me wrong—he's a terrific philosopher too."

Notes 2–3 add Grice's third feature, "detachability" (which Lycan says faces obvious objections), and the particularized/generalized distinction, whose basis Lycan calls highly controversial (pp. 178–9).

**Use against Strawson** (p. 167). Strawson's first objection to Russell (Chapter 2) is that no one would answer "The present King of France is bald" with "That's false". He infers that the sentence was not false.
- **Lycan's reply:** that does not follow. We would not say "That's false" because it would be misleading by Strength. A much stronger and more informative reply is available: "there is no king of France."
- Even if Strawson's thesis is correct, this argument does not show it (p. 167).
- Lycan links this to Austin's complaint about moving too quickly from "sounds funny" to "false" (p. 167).

**Three objections to Grice's theory.** The basic idea of implicature is almost universally accepted; Grice's theory of it is less so (p. 168).
1. Suspicion of the complex, instantaneous, unconscious reasoning. **Lycan's reply:** we do much quick subconscious reasoning in many areas of life (p. 168).
2. **Davis (1998)** (also Harnish, and Sperber and Wilson), which Lycan calls the more serious one. Gricean reasoning has a negative stage and a positive stage.
   - Negative stage: the hearer detects that speaker meaning diverges from sentence meaning.
   - Positive stage: the hearer works out what the speaker does mean.
   - Davis objects that Grice gives little help with the positive stage. Lycan illustrates: why is causal relevance the obvious candidate for (3)? What signals the resemblance reading of the cream-in-coffee metaphor? Nothing in Grice's theory predicts or hints at the answer (p. 168).
   - Philosophers missed this because we already know the implicatum. The cure is to pretend we do not; this is not easy (p. 168).
3. **Atlas, Levinson:** Grice's reasoning starts from a complete proposition ("He has said that p"). But getting that proposition from the sentence already needs Grice-like reasoning, not just assigning referents to deictic terms.
   - Levinson's "intrusive" constructions build literal content from parts' generalized implicatures. Examples: (6) "If you watch an education school burning and smile with pleasure, you're a borderline sociopath"; (7) "John and Marsha didn't fall in love and get married; they got married and fell in love" (pp. 168–9).

**Explicature** (Carston, 1988; Recanati, 1989) was first proposed as a new kind of implication, intermediate between implicature and entailment. The explicatum is cancelable, but if left uncanceled it is counted as said (p. 169).
- Example (8): "She put down the letter, shed a single tear, and walked slowly but steadily out to the cliff's edge; then she jumped." Her jumping off the cliff is not entailed, since one could add "not off the cliff, mind you". Carston and Recanati hold that if the speaker leaves it uncanceled in a conversationally reasonable time, he is counted as having said it (p. 169).
- **Lycan:** tenable arguments exist on both sides. Perhaps the rule is a discourse convention, but there are tacit conditions on why an implication goes uncanceled. Later Relevance Theory dropped this timing feature and all concern with what is "said", but kept the notion of explicature (p. 169).

*Intuition note:* Grice explains how hearers get from what is said to what is meant by assuming the speaker is being cooperative. The objections ask whether such reasoning can start from a ready-made proposition, and how it finds the positive answer.

### Relevance Theory (pp. 169–172)
Sperber and Wilson (1986) and Carston (2002) began by developing Grice but became rivals (p. 169).
- **Core claim (of the relevance theorists):** they reject the idea that there are particular Gricean maxims. They hold that implicatures come from all-purpose cognition aimed at efficiency of information transfer; Wilson and Sperber (2004: 608) say expectations of relevance arise because the search for relevance is basic to human cognition (p. 169).
- **Relevance** is a favorable balance of positive cognitive effects over processing time and effort. A positive cognitive effect is, roughly, an improvement in the hearer's representation of the world, such as a true and useful belief (p. 169).
- On this view every utterance carries a presumption of its own optimal relevance: it is relevant enough to be worth the hearer's effort, and the most relevant one compatible with the communicator's abilities and preferences (p. 170). This is the rival to CP.

**Arguments** that the theories differ (p. 170):
- We constantly say loose or exaggerated things, known by both sides not to be strictly true ("record" for tapes and CDs). These do not normally initiate Gricean reasoning about flouting Truthfulness. Wilson and Sperber suggest that expectations of truthfulness are only a rough byproduct of expectations of relevance.
- Relevance theorists argue that, psychologically at least, Grice's picture (full literal content first, then reasoning) cannot be right: a sentence never encodes a complete proposition. It must be disambiguated and its deictic elements assigned referents, including computing Harman's function α. These steps are also guided by the drive for relevance. Levinson's intrusive constructions show that implicatures can embed and affect literal content. So disambiguation, reference assignment and implicature-finding go on at once, with trade-offs (p. 170).

**Further processes behind "explicature",** now understood, more vaguely, as what is explicitly conveyed rather than merely implicated or implied however strongly (p. 170):
- **Saturation** (Recanati): filling a slot in logical form. Examples: "This way is shorter" (than what?), "I am too old" (for what?), "Henry is ready", "Sandy has had enough". It is linguistically mandated (Carston, 2004). **Lycan:** it is not a new idea; if context-sensitivity is everywhere (contra Cappelen and Lepore), saturation is just the work of α. Relevance theorists rightly emphasize, he says, that computing α now draws widely on contextual assumptions and considerations of cognitive improvement (p. 170).
- **Free enrichment** (p. 171). Like saturation, it adds explicitly conveyed material not merely implied. It differs in three ways:
  1. it is not linguistically mandated, since there is no slot in logical form;
  2. it outruns what was already a complete proposition;
  3. it is cancelable.

  Examples (Carston): (9) "She has a brain" [high-functioning]; (10) "It's going to take time for these wounds to heal" [considerable]; (11) "You're not going to die" [from this cut]; (12) "I've had a shower" [today]; (13) "The baby has a temperature" [high]; (14) "I'm parked out back" [my car is]; (15) "No one goes there anymore" [no one of worth] (p. 171).
- **For free enrichment** (p. 171):
  - the added material is triggered by a particular term, like saturation;
  - the literal content is often ludicrously weak ((10), (13)) or prodigiously false ((11), (14)) without being metaphor;
  - if these are explicatures, Levinson's constructions are not so intrusive, because the embedded contents are explicit.
- **Against it** (p. 171):
  - it is hard to accept an implication that is not linguistically mandated yet is mandatory, explicit and not just clearly implied;
  - every proffered example can be and has been disputed and claimed to be of a more familiar type; Lycan says some are just saturations marked in logical form (Stanley; Stanley and Szabó; Taylor), others are ordinary implicatures, and some are actual entailments.
  - On the entailment option, "and" may simply mean "and then" or "and as a result", which would account for (6) and (7). Grice's "cancelation" would then be disambiguation.
  - Lycan notes (p. 171) that literal-minded philosophers will deny the material is explicit and hear it as ordinary implicature, but adds that linguists are more likely to be right on this sort of issue.
- **Ad hoc concept construction** ("loosening and tightening"): a concept is broadened, narrowed or both in context (pp. 171–2). Examples (p. 172):
  - (16) "She was upset, but she wasn't upset."
  - (17) "a rectangular bit of lawn" [trapezoidal would count]
  - (18) "I haven't been to the bank" [a place to withdraw cash on demand]
  - (19) "Nurse Ratched is not a human being" [not a sane, compassionate person]
  - (20) "Her hair is red" [not fire-engine red]

*Intuition note:* Relevance Theory says hearers do not first decode a full literal sentence and then consult maxims. Working out what is meant, including the referents and the exact sense of each word, is guided throughout by one drive: get useful information cheaply.

### Presupposition and Conventional Implicature (pp. 172–175)
**Semantic presupposition** (p. 172). Strawson (1950) said that "The present King of France is bald" does not entail that there is a king but merely presupposes it. On his view, when there is no king, the sentence and its negation lack truth-value rather than being false.
- Formal version: if S1 entails S2 and S2 is false, S1 is false and its negation is true. If S1 presupposes S2 and S2 is false, S1 and its negation lack truth-value.
- It resembles entailment in being noncancelable: both S1 and its negation necessitate S2 (p. 172).
- Note 9: Strawson himself held that sentences do not have truth-values at all, not that they have a third value (p. 179).
- **Lycan:** there are no uncontroversial examples (p. 172).

**Candidate pairs** (pp. 172–3): (21a) "It was Grannie who robbed the diaper service" / (21b) "Someone robbed it"; (22) "Have you stopped beating your spouse?" / "You have beaten your spouse"; (23) "Rocky realized that his fly was open" / "was open"; (24) "Fred, who was fat, could not run" / "Fred was fat"; (25) "She was poor but she was honest" / "Being poor inhibits [or contrasts with] being honest". The claim is that each a-sentence and its negation necessitate the b-sentence.

**Lycan's assessment** (p. 173):
- (21): the negation (21′) "It was not Grannie… ; no one robbed it" is peculiar but not self-contradictory; if so, it does not necessitate (21b) in the strong sense needed. The negation does conversationally implicate (21b) via Strength, but that is cancelable, as necessitation is not.
- (22): if you are married and have never beaten your spouse, "No" is correct. One can stop only what one was doing. But "No" misleads by Strength; the non-misleading answer cancels: "No, because I never have."
- (23): (23′) "Rocky did not realize… because his fly wasn't open" does not seem contradictory, but there is not so obvious a Gricean explanation of the negation's implicating (23b).
- (24): perhaps the best alleged example. (24′) "It's false that Fred, who was fat, could not run, because Fred wasn't fat" sounds contradictory or anomalous. Yet **Lycan** hears (24) as false if Fred was not fat, since the speaker called him fat. That does not explain what is wrong with (24′).
- If the Direct Reference Theory of names is correct, perhaps Strawson is right that sentences with nonreferring names lack truth-value. Then sentences with proper names semantically presuppose referents. This is where the problems of Apparent Reference to Nonexistents and Negative Existentials arose (pp. 173–4).
- "Pragmatic presupposition" has not been defined, and no one type of pragmatic implication has been meant by it to the exclusion of other types (p. 174).

**Conventional implicature** (Grice 1975) is Grice's third classification for (25) (p. 174).
- (25′) "It's false that she was poor but she was honest; being poor does not inhibit being honest" is not contradictory, but awkward. The implication is noncancelable.
- If she was poor and honest, the speaker said two true things, yet if poverty does not contrast with honesty something is still wrong ("Wilt is 7′2″ but he's tall"). "But" is like "and" except for a special connotation, probably its raison d'être.
- Two differences from conversational implicature: it is grasped immediately, not worked out; and it is not cancelable (p. 174). It is normally carried by tendentious choice of particular words.
- Grice's example: "He is an Englishman; he is therefore brave" (p. 174). **Lycan** doubts it fits, because he thinks "therefore" carries the implication in its semantic meaning (note 11, p. 179).
- Other cases: "too", "either", and personal pronouns ("Who is this pebble?" is unacceptable) (p. 174).
- Lycan treats the term as a lexical phenomenon; he says this is a choice, and other usages (e.g., Potts) differ (note 12, p. 179).

**Penalty catalogue** (pp. 174–5). Lycan suggests that a good way to investigate these kinds of implication is to ask what follows when the implied sentence S2 is false:
| Relation of S1 to S2 | If S2 is false |
|---|---|
| S1 entails S2 | S1 is false |
| S1 semantically presupposes S2 | S1 lacks truth-value |
| S1 conversationally implicates S2 | S1 may be true, but the utterance is misleading |
| S1 conventionally implicates S2 | S1 is misworded, even if not false |
| speech act has S2 as felicity condition | the act is infelicitous in an illocutionary way |

The last row is "illocutionary implication". Example: promising to return your champagne glasses noncancelably implies I intend to return them (p. 175).

*Intuition note:* The kinds of implication differ in what goes wrong when the implied thing is false. Lycan suggests that this is a good way to investigate them.

### Indirect Force (pp. 175–177)
Declaratives normally make statements, interrogatives seek information, and imperatives issue directives. The fit is imperfect (p. 175).
- (26) "I want you to go to the Broccoli Festival with me": declarative, normally a request or order.
- (27) "Can you pass the salt?": interrogative, normally a request.
- (28) "Believe me when I say I'll never again mix…" and (29) "Tell me how you saved Gal Gadot…": imperatives, normally a statement and a question.
- (30) "I want you to tell me what has become of my children three": declarative, normally a question.

The deeper problem is that each also has a literal reading matching its mood. Those readings are uncommon and hard to hear. The puzzle is why the nonliteral uses are the normal uses (p. 175).

**Searle's conservative approach** (Searle 1975): indirect force is predicted using known speech-act principles plus Gricean mechanisms, with no new apparatus (p. 175).
- It starts from generalizations such as: S can make an indirect directive by asking whether or stating that a preparatory condition about H's ability holds; or by stating, but not by asking, that the sincerity condition holds (p. 176).
- For (27), the hearer infers in Gricean fashion that the speaker means something other than the literal question. The hearer then notes two things: an allusion to a preparatory condition for a request, and that the request is one the speaker probably wants fulfilled (p. 176).

**Problems** (pp. 176–7):
- It inherits the two objections to Grice: doubts about the posited reasoning and Davis's positive-stage complaint. Davis's problem is worse here, since the hearer must pick out an unexpected force as well as a content. What cues mark (27) as a request? The ability condition is a felicity condition for many acts. Narrowing to directives, then excluding orders by power relations and tone, leaves room for error at each step (p. 176).
- Searle suggests a convention is also at work. But "Can you…?" requests are idiomatic without being idioms like "kick the bucket", since a literal answer is possible ("I can, yes, but…"). If there is conventional force, it needs explaining (pp. 176–7).
- Morgan (1978) offers a quasi-conventional mechanism; Bach and Harnish (1979) a less convention-like "standardization". Both treat indirect force as "short-circuited" implicature (p. 177).

**Syntactic marks** (Gordon and Lakoff, 1975) (p. 177):
- (31) "Why paint your house purple?" must be discouragement.
- (32) "Why don't you be nice to your brother for a change?" must be a reproach.
- (33) "Would you get me a glass of water?" and (34) "I would like a dry martini, please" must be requests.
- (35) "Here, I need that wrench" must be a request or something stronger.
- **Lycan:** Searle's approach cannot predict these data and does not even apply, since there is nothing to work out; they "wear their indirect forces on their sleeves". No theory has been uncontroversially successful. Bach and Harnish dispute some data, such as the grammaticality of (33).

*Intuition note:* If indirect force comes from reasoning, the reasoning is hard to specify. If it is built into the words, it is not reasoning. The data themselves are partly disputed, and Lycan says no theory has been uncontroversially successful.

### Summary and notes (pp. 177–180)
The chapter's Summary repeats the above. Notes 8 and 13 report Millikan (2017): generalized implicatures, and "quasi-conventional" phrases like "Can you…?", are just ossified or fully conventional literal meanings, so nothing is left to explain pragmatically (p. 179).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Apply the chapter's two tests, cancelability and penalty when false, to three sentences.

1. **"Ann has two sisters"** and "Ann has a sister". Try cancelling: "Ann has two sisters, but she has no sister." This is contradictory, so it is entailment. If Ann has no sister, the first sentence is false.
2. **"Some of the cookies are gone."** By Strength (M1) the hearer reasons: a stronger statement ("all are gone") was available, so presumably it is false. This yields "not all". Cancelling: "Some are gone — in fact, all of them" is fine, so it is conversational implicature. If all are gone, the sentence may still be true, but the speaker misled.
3. **"Tom is a chef but he is a poor cook."** A contrast is felt at once, with no reasoning. Cancelling: "…but I don't mean any contrast" is bad. So it is conventional implicature, carried by "but". If there were in fact no contrast (say, being a chef did not lead one to expect good cooking), the sentence would be misworded, not false.

Result: in these cases, cancelability separates (1) from (2), and "worked out or immediate" separates (2) from (3). The penalty also differs: false, misleading, misworded.

## Concepts
- [[conversational implicature]] — what is conveyed via the hearer's reasoning from CP and maxims; worked out and cancelable (p. 165, 167)
- [[cooperative principle & maxims]] — CP plus corollary maxims; Lycan lists seven, numbered by him (p. 165)
- [[conveyed-meaning]] (new) — what a speaker obviously means beyond what the sentence says, without pathology (p. 163)
- [[invited-inference]] (new) — a nonentailed inference hearers readily draw, e.g., "if" → "iff" (p. 164)
- [[cancelability]] (new) — a speaker can forestall an implicature without contradiction (p. 167)
- [[flouting-a-maxim]] (new) — blatant violation of a maxim that generates implicature (p. 166)
- [[common-ground]] (new) — background information mutually assumed in a conversation (Stalnaker) (p. 166)
- [[relevance-theory]] (new) — implicatures arise from a general cognitive drive for relevance, not specific maxims (p. 169)
- [[explicature]] (new) — in later Relevance Theory, what is explicitly conveyed rather than merely implicated; in later use understood more vaguely than at first (pp. 169–170)
- [[saturation]] (new) — filling a linguistically mandated slot in logical form (p. 170)
- [[free-enrichment]] (new) — cancelable, non-mandated explicit enrichment of a complete proposition (p. 171)
- [[ad-hoc-concept-construction]] (new) — contextual broadening or narrowing of a concept (pp. 171–2)
- [[semantic-presupposition]] (new) — if the presupposed S2 is false, S1 and its negation lack truth-value; noncancelable (p. 172)
- [[conventional-implicature]] (new) — noncancelable, immediately grasped implication carried by word choice (p. 174; Lycan's lexical usage, note 12)
- [[indirect-force]] (new) — a sentence's normal speech-act force differing from that of its mood (p. 175)
- [[speech act]], [[felicity conditions]] — used for "illocutionary implication" (p. 175)

- [structural-analogy] Relevance Theory's effect-versus-effort balance ↔ cost-benefit models of cognition: relevance is a ratio of gain to cost.
- [metaphor] Short-circuited implicature ↔ convention: a pattern becomes automatic through repeated use, as in Millikan's "ossified" meanings.

## Questions this raises
- Can the positive stage of Gricean reasoning (Davis) be specified without already knowing the answer?

- If semantic presupposition has no clear examples, does "presupposition" do any work beyond implicature?
- How can indirect force be both worked out and marked on the surface of sentences?
