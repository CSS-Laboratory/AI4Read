# Verificationism

## L0 — One line
The Verification Theory says a sentence's meaning is the set of experiences that would tend to show it true, but Lycan argues that, by Duhem's and Quine's point, an individual sentence has no distinctive set of such experiences of its own.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether meaning can be explained by how we would find out whether a sentence is true. The logical positivists said yes: a sentence is meaningful if and only if some possible experiences would tend to show it true, and its meaning (its verification condition) is that set of experiences. The worst of Lycan's seven objections, following Duhem and Quine, is that an individual sentence has such a condition only relative to a mass of background assumptions. Quine concludes that individual sentences have no meanings. He also rejects "analytic" sentences (true solely by the meanings of their words). Lycan finds a key premise of each of two Quinean arguments for this conclusion unjustified or obscure.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a theory of meaning (sentence meaning as a proposition); basic logic (conditionals, "true by definition"); the notion of empirical evidence.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Verificationism is an epistemic theory: a sentence is meaningful iff its truth would make a difference to future experience, and its meaning is its verification condition (pp. 103–105).
- It treats analytic sentences as a special case: they have no empirical content but have meaning of a sort, because their truth is guaranteed by their words' meanings (p. 105).
- Objections 1–2: the theory covers only fact-stating language, and it seems we must already understand a sentence before we can say how to verify it (pp. 105–106).
- Objections 3–6: bad or at least highly controversial metaphysics (solipsism, instrumentalism, behaviorism), self-application, Erwin's punch-card machine, and the shaky observational/theoretical distinction (pp. 107–109).
- Objection 7 ("the Big One"): an individual sentence has a verification condition only relative to a background of default auxiliary assumptions (p. 110).
- Quine's holism leaves no sentence immune to revision, so he concludes there are no analytic sentences; Lycan notes a repercussion (no two sentences precisely synonymous), though a thoroughgoing Quinean should not accept that argument (p. 111; note 7, p. 113).
- Quine's two arguments that sentences have no meanings each rest on a premise Lycan finds unjustified or obscure (pp. 111–112).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and "The Theory and Its Motivation" (pp. 103–105)
- **Verification Theory:** a sentence is meaningful iff its being true would make some difference to the course of future experience. An experientially unverifiable "sentence" is meaningless (p. 103).
- A sentence's particular meaning is its **verification condition**: the set of possible experiences that would tend to show it true. Its **falsification condition** is the set that would tend to show it false (pp. 103, 104).
- The theory flourished in the 1930s–40s. It drove logical positivism, which many moral philosophers, poets and theologians saw as attacking their foundations (p. 103).
- Slogan: "a difference must make a difference." A bit of language must matter to future experience. The positivists posed it as a challenge: if you cannot say how a sentence's truth would affect experience, in what sense is it meaningful for you? (pp. 103–104).
- Positivists suspected that many utterances passing for meaningful in the works of the "Great Dead Philosophers" were not even meaningful. They used the principle as a criterion of meaningfulness (p. 104).
- Classic alleged failures: "Everything has just doubled in size"; the universe began 5 minutes ago with ostensible memories; the evil-demon deceiver (p. 104).
- The theory also has a specific form, anticipated by Peirce. A sentence's meaning is its verification condition. This is a practical test of what a sentence means, unlike the "naive Proposition Theory," which does not say how to link a sentence to a proposition (p. 104).
- It is an epistemic account: a sentence's meaning is its epistemology, i.e. what its proper evidence base would be (pp. 104–105).
- Sentences true by definition (e.g. "No bachelor is married") have no empirical content. They have meaning of a sort because their truth is guaranteed by the meanings of their words. They are called **analytic** (p. 105).

*Intuition note:* If nothing you could ever experience would differ between "S is true" and "S is false," the positivists say S says nothing. Meaning is then what a sentence tells you to expect.

### Some Objections (p. 105)
- The positivists never found a formulation that fitted exactly the strings they wanted; every precise version was too strong or too weak (Hempel 1950) (p. 105).
- To test formulations they had to appeal to clear cases of both kinds: meaningful strings and meaningless ones. That assumes already that there are grammatical strings of meaningful words that are literally meaningless, a "very bold claim" (p. 105).
- These are not principled objections, but they suggest two that are.

### Objection 1: only fact-stating language (pp. 105–106)
- Wittgenstein would and did complain that the theory is another monolithic attempt to capture the "essence" of language, and that all such attempts fail (p. 105).
- **Lycan's more specific objection:** the theory covers only descriptive language. We also ask, command, write poems, joke. An adequate theory should cover all meaningful uses; it is hard to see how this one could (p. 105).
- **Positivist reply:** they meant only "cognitive" meaning, roughly being a statement of fact (pp. 105–106).
- **Lycan:** that restriction is damaging for a general theory of linguistic meaning. It would not help with the later objections anyway (p. 106).

### Objection 2: understanding comes first (p. 106)
- To ask whether a sentence is verifiable, and what would verify it, we must already know what it says. To verify a virus claim, we must already know what viruses are (p. 106).
- If we know what it says, it is already meaningful. So verifiability is conceptually posterior to meaning, the opposite of what the theory says (p. 106).
- Related point: the sentences the positivists rejected (doubling in size, 5-minute universe) are not meaningless in the way gibberish or word salad is (p. 106).
- **Possible verificationist reply (Lycan's suggestion):** such strings give an *illusion* of understanding. We can parse them and understand each word, but it does not follow that they mean anything as wholes (pp. 106–107).

### Objection 3: bad metaphysics (p. 107)
- Verifying experiences are described in an "observation language." If it uses only subjective sense impressions, every meaningful statement is about my own impressions. If solipsism is false, I cannot meaningfully say so (p. 107).
- With Hempel's looser "directly observable characteristics," meaning still collapses into observational evidence without remainder (p. 107).
- Result for science: **instrumentalism**, where statements about electrons, memory traces or galaxies are abbreviations for complex statements about laboratory data. Lycan finds this "grotesque," though the positivists thought it importantly true (p. 107).
- Result for mind: a very strong behaviorism, since my only evidence about your thoughts is your behavior (p. 107).
- **Lycan's point:** these views may even be true, but a theory of meaning should not settle them in one step. Metaphysics should not be settled by a theory of language (p. 107).

### Objection 4: self-application (p. 108)
- Is the Verification Principle itself empirically verifiable? (p. 108)
- **If not verifiable:** it is meaningless (one positivist embraced this, "a ladder to be kicked away"), or analytic, or a stipulation. Hempel called it a "proposal." Stipulation is not of much help to someone seeking a correct theory of meaning (p. 108).
- Taken as a correct definition of the prior meaning of "meaning," Lycan says we do not know what specifically semantic evidence would bear it out. It does not seem analytic: Lycan doubts that anyone who understands "meaning" and "verify" knows that meaning is verification condition (p. 108).
- **If verifiable:** this presupposes that we recognize meanings independently of assigning verification conditions. It is also unclear what the empirical data would be (street surveys, dictionaries, intuitions). The principle's own meaning would also coincide with its own verification condition, a "nasty tangle" that Lycan is unsure is vicious (p. 108).
- Lycan: the self-application problem is "a real one, not just a superficial trick question" (p. 108).

### Objection 5: Erwin's punch-card machine (pp. 108–109)
- Suppose a machine, so far always right, lights "TRUE" or "FALSE" for any coded declarative sentence (pp. 108–109).
- For any string S: code S, feed the card, the machine lights "TRUE." These experiences would raise S's probability drastically, even if S is gibberish (p. 109).
- Erwin concludes: every string is verifiable, so the theory is trivialized, and it assigns S the wrong meaning, since S's verification condition would concern punch cards (p. 109).
- Lycan: he thinks something is wrong with that argument, but finds it hard to say exactly what (p. 109).

### Objection 6: observation language (p. 109)
- Any version needs a firm observational/theoretical distinction (p. 109).
- "Direct observation" is vexed: technology- and project-relative (glasses, magnifying glass, microscope, electron microscope?) (p. 109).
- Observations are theory-laden at least to a degree: theories under test partly determine what counts as an observation and how a datum is described (p. 109).
- Lycan only mentions these knotty issues; they set up a deeper objection (p. 109).

### Objection 7: "The Big One" (p. 110)
- **Claim (Duhem; Quine 1953, 1960):** no individual sentence has a distinctive verification condition, except relative to a mass of background theory (p. 110).
- **Duhem:** no experiment has ever singlehandedly verified or falsified a hypothesis. Auxiliary assumptions are always needed to link hypothesis to apparatus. Refutation happens only because other, disputable assumptions are held fixed. Astronomers using telescopes assume virtually all of optical theory (p. 110).
- **Everyday case:** "There is a chair at the head of the table." A first try: if you walk in by this door, you will have an experience as of a chair there. This depends on eyes open, working senses, lights on, and more, with no foreseeable end. Further hedges raise further questions (camouflage, Martians, "Q-radiation") (p. 110).
- **Conclusion:** the "verification condition" of an empirical statement presupposes a massive background of default auxiliary assumptions. They are usually reasonable, but almost any may fail. Intrinsically the sentence has no determinate verification condition (p. 110).
- This embarrasses a theory identifying meaning with verification condition (p. 110).

*Intuition note:* A test never checks one sentence alone. It checks the sentence together with everything needed to connect it to an observation. So "what would show S true?" has no answer independent of those assumptions.

### Two Quinean Issues (pp. 111–112)
**1. Against analyticity (p. 111).**
- Lycan says some of Quine's arguments are unconvincing and gives only his sense of the underlying idea (p. 111).
- Quine keeps the positivists' epistemological bent: meaning, if anything, is a function of evidential support. But his epistemology is **holistic**: support for a sentence is a matter of its evidential relations to many others (p. 111).
- When belief revision is needed, we have a wide choice of what to give up (Duhem). No belief is immune to revision. Even "Either P or not P" might be abandoned given weird quantum phenomena (p. 111).
- An analytic sentence would by definition be unresponsive to the world and so immune to revision. Therefore there are none (p. 111).
- Repercussion: if S1 and S2 were precisely synonymous, the conditional from S1 to S2 should be analytic. So with no analytic sentences, no two English sentences are precisely synonymous, not even the pair saying Bambi's mother was a doe or a female deer (p. 111).
- Note 7 says a thoroughgoing Quinean should not accept this argument and hints at the previous paragraph (p. 113).

**2. Against meaning (pp. 111–112).**
- Quine urges eliminativism about meaning, in his doctrine of the **indeterminacy of translation** (p. 111).
- **Argument A (Quine 1969):** (1) Individual sentences have no verification conditions. (2) If a sentence had any meaning, it would be a verification condition. So (3) sentences have no meanings (pp. 111–112).
  - Lycan: this "saves the village by destroying it." The problem is premise 2. If sentences lack verification conditions, why keep verificationism when other theories exist? (p. 112)
- **Argument B (field linguist):** the total evidence available to a linguist fails to determine one translation manual. Many incompatible manuals fit it. The underdetermination is radical: not even all physical fact vindicates one manual over its rivals. So no translation is uniquely correct. If sentences had meanings, correct translations would preserve them. So sentences lack meanings (p. 112).
  - Lycan: the premise that all physical fact fails to rule in one manual lacks a clear defense (p. 112).

### Summary (p. 112)
The chapter's summary repeats the theory, the "medium-sized" objections, Duhem–Quine as the worst, Quine's attack on analyticity, and his inference that sentences have no meanings (p. 112).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Duhem's point in miniature, in the chair case. Let H = there is a chair at the head of the table. Take three auxiliaries: A1 = eyes open, A2 = lights on, A3 = no camouflage. The conjunction H∧A1∧A2∧A3 predicts the experience E. Suppose E fails.
- Logic gives only ¬(H∧A1∧A2∧A3). It does not say which conjunct is false.
- Count truth-assignments of the four sentences: 2⁴ = 16. Exactly 1 makes all four true (excluded), leaving 15.
- Assignments with H false: 2³ = 8. Assignments with H true and not all of A1–A3 true: 8 − 1 = 7. Check: 8 + 7 = 15.
- So 7 of 15 consistent options keep H. The failed experience does not by itself refute H. "The" verification condition of H worked only because we assumed A1–A3.

## Concepts
- [[verification-principle]] (new) — a sentence is meaningful iff some possible experiences would tend to show it true (p. 104)
- [[verification-condition]] (new) — the set of possible experiences that would tend to show a sentence true; identified with its meaning (p. 104)
- [[epistemic-theory-of-meaning]] (new) — meaning located in ways of finding out; a sentence's meaning is its evidence base (p. 104)
- [[cognitive-meaning]] (new) — the positivists' restricted sense: roughly, being a statement of fact (pp. 105–106)
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — analytic = true solely through meanings of component terms; verificationists allow it, Quine denies it (pp. 105, 111)
- [[observation-language]] (new) — the uniform vocabulary for describing verifying experiences; sense impressions or "directly observable characteristics" (p. 107)
- [[instrumentalism]] (new) — scientific statements about unobservables abbreviate statements about lab data; a consequence, per Lycan (p. 107)
- [[confirmation holism (Quine–Duhem thesis)]] — hypotheses are tested only together with auxiliary assumptions; no individual sentence has a distinctive verification condition except relative to background theory (p. 110)
- [[indeterminacy of translation]] — evidence fits many incompatible translation manuals; Quine infers no meaning facts (pp. 111–112)
