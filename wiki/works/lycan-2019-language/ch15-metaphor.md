# Metaphor (Lycan, ch. 15)

## L0 — One line
Metaphor is far more common than philosophers usually admit, and several theories give rival answers to what "metaphorical meaning" is and how hearers grasp it: a purely causal effect, a hidden simile, speaker-meaning, a make-believe frame, a stretched word-concept, or analogical word meaning.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Metaphor is common, so philosophy of language must say what "metaphorical meaning" is and how hearers grasp it so easily. Davidson says metaphor works only causally and has no special meaning. Simile theories say metaphors abbreviate comparisons (*X is like Y*), taken literally or figuratively. Searle treats metaphor as speaker-meaning (what a speaker means beyond the words), found by Gricean reasoning. Walton notes that some metaphors call to mind a possible game of pretend. Relevance theorists call metaphor an extreme case of stretching a word's concept to fit context. A last family builds on words' splitting into many related meanings by analogy. Lycan raises objections to most of these; he believes a Davidson–Searle hybrid is possible.

**Prerequisites:** Gricean speaker-meaning and indirect speech acts (earlier chapters); the literal/non-literal distinction; what a simile is; the idea of ambiguity.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Figurative language is not an exception: Lycan says virtually every sentence has figurative elements, and the dead/fresh metaphor line is a matter of degree (p. 197). He concludes that, it seems, intellectual honesty requires philosophers to confront it (p. 197).
- Two questions drive the chapter: what is metaphorical meaning, and how is it conveyed? (pp. 197–198)
- Davidson's causal theory denies any metaphorical meaning. Lycan presses objections (some from Goodman and Moran): the theory leaves paraphrase, inference, misinterpretation, metaphorical truth, and the new dictionary entries of dead metaphors hard to explain (pp. 199–200).
- The Naive Simile Theory makes metaphors abbreviated literal similes. It faces three objections: the tension becomes shallow, similarity is vacuous, and the respects of similarity are themselves metaphorical (pp. 200–201).
- Fogelin's Figurative Simile Theory uses asymmetric similarity based on salient features. It avoids those three objections but meets three new ones: false stereotypes, proliferating ambiguity, and convoluted metaphors (pp. 201–204).
- Searle's Pragmatic Theory gives a three-step Gricean procedure. Lycan judges that Searle wins the rounds on rules, open-endedness and unparaphrasability, but that Davidson's point about non-propositional uptake is a telling point against Searle (pp. 204–208).
- Walton notes that some metaphors are best understood through a possible make-believe game. This explains extended metaphors and non-reversibility, but the model does not fruitfully apply to all metaphors, especially the more interesting ones (pp. 208–209).
- Relevance theory treats metaphor as an extreme case of loosening and tightening of concepts. Lycan worries that it does not extend to whole-sentence metaphors, and it seems to imply that all metaphors are paraphrasable (pp. 209–210).
- Infinite polysemy, a thesis defended by several authors: words can take unlimited related new meanings by analogy. For Ross and Kittay, metaphorical meaning is a separate, second-order operation on these (pp. 210–212).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and "A Philosophical Bias" (pp. 196–197)
- Overview (p. 196): Lycan says metaphor is far more prevalent than philosophers generally admit. Most theorists see metaphor as bringing out similarities. Davidson says this is purely causal. The Naive Simile Theory says metaphors abbreviate literal comparisons; Lycan says both views are easily seen to be inadequate. The Figurative Simile Theory avoids the three most obvious objections to the Naive theory, but not all the tough ones. Searle uses Gricean apparatus; Walton points to make-believe; relevance theorists see an extreme case of ad hoc concepts; a further theory rests on analogical differentiation of word meanings.
- Lycan says philosophers tend to think literal speech is the default and metaphor an occasional aberration, made mainly by poets and poets manqué. He calls this bias only a bias (p. 197).
- He claims that virtually every sentence has importantly metaphorical or figurative elements. "Level" is almost invariably metaphor unless it is about a horizontal physical layer (though he allows that "higher/lower level" may now be literal) (p. 197).
- **Dead metaphors** are phrases that began as novel metaphors and now mean literally what they once meant metaphorically, such as a river's "mouth" (p. 197). Everyone concedes these exist, so the letter of Lycan's claim is widely granted (p. 197).
- Lakoff and Johnson (1980): the novel/dead distinction is one of smooth degree, not kind. Fresh metaphors become current and then only very gradually die (p. 197).
- Lycan's own aside asks how many expressions in the previous paragraph were used metaphorically rather than literally, assuming the distinction is not one of degree; he leaves it unanswered (p. 197).
- *Intuition note (ours, from Lycan's point on p. 197):* If almost all speech has figurative parts, a theory of meaning that covers only literal speech leaves out much of ordinary language.

### The Issues (pp. 197–198)
- Taxonomy varies: "metaphor" is sometimes nearly synonymous with "figurative" and sometimes names one narrow figure. Lycan does not make fine distinctions (p. 197).
- The two linguistic questions: what is "metaphorical meaning," broadly construed, and by what mechanism is it conveyed, that is, how do hearers grasp it when all they hear is a sentence with a different literal meaning? Lycan sets aside why people use metaphor, its power, and its role in various walks of life (pp. 197–198).
- Examples: (1) *Simon is a rock*; (2) *Juliet is the sun*; (3) *Now is the winter of our discontent / Made glorious summer by this sun of York*; (4) *When the blood burns, how prodigal the soul / Lends the tongue vows* (p. 198).
- Beardsley (1967) names two features working in tandem. There is conceptual "tension" (humans differ categorially from rocks or suns). Yet the sentence is intelligible, perhaps even exceptionally informative, and may express an important truth (p. 198).
- Other theorists state the first feature more strongly: a metaphorical sentence read literally is incoherent, absurd, or at best transparently and wildly false. Lycan adds that we shall see below that this is not always so (p. 198).

### Davidson's Causal Theory (pp. 198–200)
- Logical positivists disdained figurative language, presumably because of verificationism. On their view there is no linguistic metaphorical meaning, only emotive or affective significance (p. 198).
- **Davidson (1978)** also rejects metaphorical meaning and linguistic mechanisms for conveying it. Unlike the positivists, he thinks sentences like (1)–(4) have meanings, but only literal ones: "Metaphors mean what the words, in their most literal interpretation, mean, and nothing more" (p. 199).
- His positive account is causal: a metaphor makes us attend to some likeness, and a simile tells us in part what a metaphor merely nudges us into noting (p. 199). Lycan says Davidson seems to be saying there is no logic or linguistic mechanism for this; a pill or a bump on the head (Davidson's phrase) could do as well (p. 199).
- **Lycan's objections:**
  - Davidson's view implies that the only difference between (1)–(4) and a word salad is psychological effect. But we can often paraphrase metaphors more literally, draw inferences from them, and sometimes take ourselves to have learned new empirical facts. Moran adds embedding in conditional antecedents (p. 199).
  - If Davidson is right, one can never misinterpret a metaphor. On the Causal Theory, an eavesdropper who hears (2) as "she is stupid and smells horrible" would not be wrong, only causally different (p. 199).
  - Goodman: if metaphorical utterances have only literal meaning, they will normally be false and only occasionally and accidentally true. Given how prevalent nonliteral usage is, if metaphorical utterances are rarely true, then utterances in general are rarely true (p. 199).
  - Moran: when a metaphor dies, the expression gets a new literal meaning and an additional dictionary entry. This would be inexplicable, or at least arbitrary and odd, if the metaphor had no meaning before (p. 200).
  - Other views reject metaphorical sentence meaning yet give more plausible accounts of communication. Lycan concludes there is no reason to accept the purely causal theory (p. 200).
- *Intuition note:* Davidson says a metaphor works like a nudge to notice a likeness. Lycan replies that a mere nudge leaves it hard to explain why metaphors can be paraphrased, misinterpreted, true, or later listed in dictionaries.

### The Naive Simile Theory (pp. 200–201)
- Philosophers since Aristotle have noticed that metaphors and similes both express or invite comparisons. On the Naive Simile Theory a metaphor derives from the corresponding simile by ellipsis (leaving out words): (1) is short for *Simon is like a rock*, and (2) for *Juliet resembles the sun* (p. 200).
- (3) is trickier: it needs a contextually fixed *x, a Yorkist who resembles the sun* and a paraphrase of "made glorious summer." Lycan says probably all this could be worked out; (4) is still tougher (p. 200).
- The theory accommodates both of Beardsley's features. Statements of likeness are obviously intelligible, and the tension comes from the move from likeness to actual ascription (p. 200).
- **Objection 1 (Beardsley):** on this theory the metaphor is synonymous with the simile, so the tension is the merest surface appearance. But there is no particular tension in *Juliet is like the sun*; a metaphor seems to have a more substantive inherent tension (p. 200).
- **Objection 2 (Searle):** "Similarity is a vacuous predicate: any two things are similar in some respect or other" (p. 201). Salient, well-known solar properties (a gas ball, 93 million miles away) are not what Romeo ascribes, and the theory gives no hint why. So it offers no mechanism for conveying metaphorical significance (p. 201).
- **Objection 3 (Searle):** the respects of similarity are themselves often metaphorical. In *Sally is a block of ice*, "hard" and "cold" are metaphorical, so Sally is only like something hard and cold. If she is unyielding, unemotional and unresponsive, many other things are too: bonfires are, yet neither *Sally is like a bonfire* nor *Sally is a bonfire* is metaphorically compatible with the original. The naive simile theorist would need a further literal similarity between cold things and unemotional things, with no evidence for it. Searle conjectures that people simply associate coldness with lack of emotion (p. 201).
- This suggests Fogelin's modification: metaphors abbreviate similes themselves taken figuratively (p. 201).

### The Figurative Simile Theory (pp. 201–204)
- Similes are often, perhaps usually, figures of speech. Lycan offers (not Fogelin's own) a symmetry test: literal similarity is symmetric, but nobody would propose *the sun is like Juliet*. So these similes are nonliteral, and nonliteral similes best paraphrase metaphors (p. 201).
- **The three objections are easily sidestepped** (p. 202):
  - Against 1: there is already conceptual tension in the figurative simile.
  - Against 2: the figurative simile already carries particular respects of similarity.
  - Against 3: no literal similarities are required.
- **The price:** the explanatory work moves to the figuratively interpreted similes, which then need explaining in turn. The two original questions arise again for them (p. 202).
- **Salience and asymmetry (Fogelin 1988):** "A is similar to B if and only if A has a sufficient number of B's salient features" (p. 202). A chipmunk is like a rat except for cuteness. A rat is not like a chipmunk, because cuteness is highly salient in chipmunks and rats are not cute (p. 202).
- **Literal vs figurative:** the standard of salience in a way reverses. Fogelin says it is literally true that Churchill looked like a bulldog, literally false that he was like one, but figuratively true. In the figurative case we compare him to a bulldog while trimming the feature space in terms of the subject's salient features (p. 202). Lycan believes the idea is that the hearer charitably assumes the similarity holds and ignores the bulldog features that make the literal claim false. He is not sure what the matching features would be (p. 202).
- **Standards of similarity:** on this view a sentence can have a metaphorical meaning in context without any expression changing its meaning. The standard of similarity is like an indexical, but can take more than one value within one context. So a sentence can be metaphorically true and literally false on one occasion, like "Muffie is small" for an undersized moose (p. 203). Lycan calls this a nice advantage (p. 203).
- **Three further difficulties** (Lycan: "at least three"):
  1. A statement may remain accepted as metaphorically true even when the simile proves false. Searle's *Richard is a gorilla*: primatologists say gorillas are shy, rather sensitive and very intelligent. Lycan adds that he knows of no evidence that pigs are particularly greedy or fat (p. 203). Fogelin can reply that in figurative similes "gorilla" and "pig" are used figuratively. But Lycan objects that trimming the feature space presupposes, or at least strongly suggests, that the shared features are literally possessed by both, so on Fogelin's theory a metaphor still bottoms out in literal sharing of genuine properties. In Searle's examples it is far from obvious what those properties would be (p. 203; Lycan thinks Fogelin's reply weak, n. 9, p. 214).
  2. Many sentences admit either literal or metaphorical readings (*Adolf is a butcher*; *The worm has turned*). Even an anomalous sentence can often be imagined literally true: Davidson's "You are pigs" would have been literally true if addressed by Odysseus to his men in Circe's palace. Lycan thinks there has very likely never been a sentence with no metaphorical understanding. So any simile theorist will have to call even a sentence usually heard literally (*Ernest is lost*) semantically ambiguous, and Lycan finds such a proliferation of ambiguities implausible (pp. 203–204).
  3. Some metaphors are too convoluted to parse as similes. In (4), blood cannot literally burn; "the soul" is probably used metaphorically (and even if not, souls cannot lend anything to tongues); "tongues" is not used to mean tongues; vows cannot be lent. Lycan's first-pass simile paraphrase, full of placeholders, leaves us not much wiser, and it needs refinement. No wonder simile theorists stick to simple subject–predicate examples (p. 204).
- *Intuition note:* A figurative simile can say what the metaphor means without needing a literal match. Lycan worries that this may only put off the puzzle: we still have to say how a figurative simile means anything (p. 202).

### The Pragmatic Theory (pp. 204–208)
- Unlike the causal view, the Naive Simile Theory supplied a notion of "metaphorical meaning" (a shallow one), and Fogelin's version supplies a more robust one (p. 204).
- Davidson argued globally against metaphorical meaning. Lycan discusses two of his five or six arguments, the ones he takes to be most pertinent against the other theories (p. 204).
  - **(a)** There are no instructions for devising metaphors and no test for metaphor that does not call for taste (p. 204).
  - **(b)** Metaphors are open-ended; some (cummings) cannot be paraphrased; and our uptake is not, in general, propositional: "Seeing as is not seeing that" (p. 204). Fogelin argues that his theory explains the open-endedness and unparaphrasability facts too. Davidson adds that if a sentence had a metaphorical meaning, we would expect some paraphrase to express it fairly accurately (pp. 204–205).
- Lycan: Davidson's attack can be exaggerated by his own rhetoric. His critical arguments concentrate on meaning change and ambiguity, and he is careful to deny metaphorical truth only of sentences (p. 205). This leaves open a middle way (p. 205).
- **Searle (1979b)** joins Davidson in deflating "metaphorical meaning" and rejecting an ambiguity view, but takes seriously that metaphor is genuine linguistic communication, with a cognitive mechanism computing something worth calling metaphorical meaning (p. 205). Lycan calls it the Pragmatic Theory: metaphor as a species of Gricean (or relevance-theoretic) communication, parallel to Searle's account of indirect speech acts. The hearer reasons that the speaker means something other than what the sentence literally means, then works out what (p. 205). Searle himself says the problem of explaining metaphor is a special case of the general problem of how speaker meaning and sentence meaning come apart (p. 205).
- **Three steps** (p. 205):
  1. Decide whether to look for a non-literal interpretation.
  2. Use principles or strategies to generate a range of possible speaker meanings.
  3. Use further strategies to pick the likely ones. Lycan notes that if these cannot narrow the list to one or two, that would explain the frequent open-endedness of metaphor.
- **Step 1:** the Gricean strategy is to look for another meaning when the literal one would be obviously defective. (1)–(4) fit this. Searle says the defect can also be excessive plain truth, as in *A revolution is not a dinner party*. Other cues: kind of discourse (Romantic poets) and explicit flagging (Kittay) (p. 206).
- **Step 2:** the main strategy is to look for similarities. Searle gives eight principles, e.g. a property that is salient or well known (principle 2), or one only often imputed to the thing (principle 3, as in the gorilla and pig cases) (p. 206).
- **Step 3:** Searle mentions just one strategy: consider which candidates are likely or possible features of the subject (Juliet cannot be a giant ball of gas). Lycan adds that hearers also know what speakers are likely to express (p. 206).
- **Contrasts:** Searle contends that in indirect speech acts proper the speaker also means what she says. Lycan adds that Searle does not address ordinary implicature but might say the same. The difference from irony seems to be just that metaphor works by similarity or comparison, irony by choosing the opposite (p. 206).
- **Davidson and Searle:** Lycan says they agree more than they disagree. Both deny special metaphorical meanings of expressions and use existing apparatus (Fogelin groups them as "fecund falsehood" theories). Lycan does not see how Davidson could dispute that there is metaphorical speaker-meaning. Davidson does argue that what some metaphors convey is not propositional. The biggest disagreement is over rules and mechanisms (pp. 206–207).
- **Rebuttals (Lycan's verdicts):**
  - Rules: Searle supplies many plausible principles. He would very likely concede the point about taste, since he does not claim completeness or determinate results. Lycan says Searle wins this round on points (p. 207).
  - Open-endedness: Searle's account predicts it. Unparaphrasability: Searle grants that metaphor is often used because no handy literal expression exists, but argues that any linguistic meaning could in principle be formulated. Lycan thinks Searle wins this round also (p. 207).
- **Non-propositionality:** Searle's account is propositional to the core (speaker-meaning is meaning that so-and-so). If Davidson is right that what we notice is not, in general, propositional, then by Searle's own principle it is not a linguistic meaning of any kind, not even a speaker-meaning (p. 207).
  - Against Davidson: Lycan says "in general" makes the claim fairly ambitious, indeed false. Many poetic metaphors may be non-propositional, but everyday metaphors are often perfectly paraphrasable in context. Hans finds his flat a mess and says "You pig!", meaning fairly precisely that his roommate is a filthy slob (had the food been eaten, perhaps a glutton). Lycan thinks Davidson overstated his case by overlooking speaker-meaning (p. 207).
  - Against Searle: writers of fresh literary metaphors may have no speaker-meaning or propositional intent at all. Metaphor sometimes has a quasi-perceptual character and in some cases affects one's perceptual set. Lycan calls this a telling point against Searle (pp. 207–208).
- Lycan: each view has at least one advantage over the other. He believes a hybrid of the Causal and Pragmatic theories is possible and leaves it as an exercise (p. 208).
- **Three further objections to Searle** (p. 208):
  1. Cooper and Moran: if metaphorical meaning is just speaker-meaning, it is confined to the speaker's intentions, yet in fresh metaphor the interpretation may outrun what the speaker had in mind.
  2. Richards, and Lakoff and Turner: metaphor is not essentially linguistic, since we often think in metaphor. Lycan says this general matter needs much more investigation.
  3. Ross and Kittay: analogical phenomena indisputably involve meaning and meaning shift, and are addressed by neither Davidson nor Searle.

### Pretense (pp. 208–209)
- **Walton (1993)** argues that some metaphors are best understood by reference to games of make-believe; he does not offer this as a general theory, and it does not appeal to pretended speech acts. A metaphorical statement in context calls to mind a possible game of make-believe in which the thing characterized serves as a prop. No actual game or participation is needed; the point is to "frame" the thing in an illuminating way (p. 208).
- Example: the *Argument is war* family of metaphors suggests a game in which what people say in an argument generates fictional truths about acts of war. Participation is not necessary; recognizing the merely possible game is enough (pp. 208–209).
- **Explanatory points** (p. 209): the model works particularly well for usefully extended metaphors, and
  1. it explains why, in paradigm cases, metaphor seems to bring two realms together: the fictional truths generated by taking the thing as a prop;
  2. the framing effect applies to metaphorical expression generally, not only to interpreting metaphorical assertions;
  3. understanding need not rest on seeing similarities, since the make-believe may work in part by convention;
  4. it explains non-reversibility: an argument as a prop could make a cannon shot fictionally true, but a real cannon shot would not make an objection fictionally true.
- **Limits:** Walton grants that the model does not fruitfully apply to all metaphors, especially the more interesting ones; he cites (2) and a musical passage described as a "rainbow." Lycan finds it hard to see how it applies to calling someone a pig (p. 209).

### The Relevance View (pp. 209–210)
- **Sperber and Wilson (S&W, 2008)** reject the dominant idea that metaphor is a special, exceptional use of language. They do not accept the Gricean view that utterances are taken as strictly literal unless there is reason to suspect otherwise, since strict literality is very rare (p. 209). They see metaphors as "a range of cases at one end of a continuum" that includes literal, loose and hyperbolic interpretations, with no mechanism specific to metaphors (p. 209).
- Metaphors are extreme cases of ad hoc concept construction (loosening and tightening). In (5), *My chiropractor is a magician*, the hearer builds *magician\**, broadened to include people without supernatural powers and narrowed to people who concern themselves with back pain. The inference is no different in kind from that for less exotic ad hoc concepts (p. 210).
- To the protest that metaphor has a distinctive rhetorical or poetic nonliteralness, Lycan says that is exactly what S&W deny. They add that degree of metaphoricalness and poetic effect are mutually independent; haiku is literal but poetic (p. 210).
- **Worry (White 1996):** the argument focuses on particular words and may not extend to metaphors whose locus is whole sentences. (4) may be such an example, as may cummings and Auden (*The hour-glass whispers to the lion's paw*). The latter two may also be unparaphrasable as Davidson pointed out, yet S&W's account, like Searle's, seems to imply that all metaphors are paraphrasable (p. 210).
- *Intuition note:* Relevance theory says a metaphor is just a very stretched use of a word, like loose talk. Lycan worries that this may not fit metaphors that live at sentence level.

### Metaphor as Analogical (pp. 210–212)
- **Infinite polysemy thesis** (Weinreich, Lyons, Cohen, Davidson 1986, Ross, Kittay, and tacitly relevance theorists): it concerns lexical meaning, and says virtually any word may take on any number of novel, distinct meanings without limit in suitable sentence environments, and hearers grasp them on the spot (pp. 210–211).
- These meanings are generated from existing ones by intricate but fairly tractable mechanisms of analogy used automatically. Very few differences are brute ambiguity like "bank" or "die"; the meanings are systematically interrelated (p. 211).
- **Examples:** "dropped" (a stitch, a hem-line, a friend, her eyes); "picked/fixed a date"; "charged" (gun, jury, battery); Lycan's "dead" list; prepositions "in," "on," "of"; possessives (p. 211).
- **Diagnostic:** Lycan grants that someone might deny that all these uses differ in meaning. But asking whether such a sentence can have more than one truth-value depending on disambiguation, the answer is obviously yes. Amelia Bedelia, deaf to such variations, powders the furniture, sketches the curtains and dresses the chicken in trousers; Lycan says "dust," "draw" and "dress" each have different senses, however related (pp. 211–212).
- **Analogy and metaphor:** generated analogical meanings are (per se) rarely if ever metaphorical. For Ross and Kittay, metaphorical meaning results from a separate operation on analogical meanings: "a move from one system to another" (Kittay, p. 212). Lycan does not expound their theories (p. 212).

### Summary and Questions (pp. 212–213)
- The summary repeats the chapter's positions in the order above. Lycan sets seven discussion questions on the theories (p. 213).

## Toy example (ours)
Searle's three steps applied to *You pig!* said to a roommate. The stereotype list is ours, loosely based on Lycan's pig stereotypes (messiness, filth, greed, obesity, crassness; p. 203).
- Step 1: literally, a person is not a pig. The utterance is defective, so look for a speaker meaning.
- Step 2: candidates from the stereotype, five in all: *filthy, greedy, fat, crass, four-legged*.
- Step 3, context A (Hans finds a filthy flat; p. 207). *Four-legged* cannot be true of a person: 5 − 1 = 4. The scene gives evidence only for *filthy*, so the other 3 drop: 4 − 3 = 1. One candidate remains, so the meaning is determinate.
- Context B (flat clean, food gone): the evidence supports only *greedy*, so again 1 candidate.
- Context C (a messy, overweight guest at a buffet): *filthy, greedy, fat* all fit, so 3 candidates. The speaker meaning stays open-ended, as Lycan says Searle's account predicts (p. 207).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Searle's three-step metaphor interpretation ↔ Searle's account of indirect speech acts: both start from a defective literal reading, then use Gricean reasoning (p. 205).
- [structural-analogy] Fogelin's contextually determined standard of similarity ↔ indexicals and gradable adjectives ("small"): one sentence is true on one standard and false on another (p. 203).
- [structural-analogy] Walton's possible make-believe game ↔ pretense theories of irony and reference (Chs. 4, 14): each uses pretense, but Walton's has no pretended speech acts (p. 208).
- [structural-analogy] Relevance theory's *magician\** ↔ ad hoc concept construction in the literal use of words: the same inference process at different distances from literal (p. 210).
- [metaphor] Davidson's bump on the head ↔ causal explanation of belief change without reasons: the effect is not mediated by meaning (p. 199).

## Questions this raises
- Can a hybrid of Davidson's and Searle's views handle both everyday paraphrasable metaphors and fresh non-propositional ones? (p. 208)
- If metaphor is thought and not only speech (Richards; Lakoff and Turner), what happens to theories built on speakers and hearers? (p. 208)
- How are Ross's and Kittay's analogical meanings related to second-order metaphorical meaning? (p. 212)
- Does Fogelin's trimming of the feature space always require literally shared properties? (p. 203)
