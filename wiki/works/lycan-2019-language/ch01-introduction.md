# Introduction: Meaning and Reference

## L0 — One line
The attractive idea that words mean what they stand for (the Referential Theory) faces several objections, and it looks as though meaning must involve more than naming.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Some strings of marks or noises are meaningful sentences with meaningful parts. Ordinary speakers grasp even brand-new sentences at once. A theory of meaning must explain what makes strings meaningful, what makes each mean what it does, and how speakers manage this. The commonsense answer is the Referential Theory: expressions mean what they stand for. Lycan gives three objections. Many words name nothing, or are not even nouns. A list of names says nothing, and adding relation words starts Bradley's regress (an endless chain of new linking entities). Coreferring terms (terms for the same thing) can differ in meaning. So it seems reference is not the whole story, though it remains worth studying.

**Prerequisites:** basic grammar (noun, predicate, subject, verb); the idea of a proper name.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Understanding a novel sentence like (1) needs no conscious effort. Speakers cannot have seen it before, so this ability needs explaining (pp. 1–2).
- The starting data: some strings are meaningful sentences; their parts are meaningful; each sentence means something in particular; speakers understand and produce them effortlessly (p. 2).
- The Referential Theory says an expression means what it stands for. On this view, understanding a sentence is knowing what its words refer to (p. 3).
- Objection 1: not every word names an actual object. Examples are empty names (names of nonexistent things, such as Pegasus), "nobody", predicates like "fat" (which names no individual, and naming an abstract quality leads to regress), nouns like "sake", and words like "of" and "and" (pp. 3–4).
- If "fat" names a property and the linking "is" must then name a relation too, Bradley's infinite regress follows (p. 4).
- Objection 2: a sentence is not a list of names. A list says nothing, and a linking verb brings the regress back (p. 5).
- Objection 3: coreferring terms (terms with the same referent) are often not synonymous, which seems to show that meaning is more than reference (p. 5).
- Reference still matters, and it introduces concepts needed to assess theories of meaning (p. 5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview (p. 1)
- A theory of meaning should explain what it is for a string to be meaningful, what makes it mean what it does, and how humans produce and understand utterances effortlessly (p. 1).
- Lycan's verdict on the Referential Theory has two parts. Comparatively few words stand for things. If all words were like proper names, serving just to pick out individual things, we would not be able to form grammatical sentences (p. 1).

### Meaning and Understanding (pp. 1–3)
- Sentence (1) says Hitler visited the US in 1931, had an affair in Keokuk, and invented the electric can opener. It is false, yet the reader understands it perfectly and effortlessly (p. 1).
- Lycan is certain that nobody wrote or uttered (1) before him, and he doubts that anyone has produced even a remotely similar sentence. So the reader did not understand (1) by having seen it or something like it (p. 2).
- "Because you speak English" (an answer Lycan imagines the reader giving, true so far as it goes) only pushes the mystery back. Speaking English includes understanding novel sentences as complex as (1) (p. 2).
- A clue: (1) is made of words you understand individually, and you know something about how they are combined. Lycan calls this important but, for now, only suggestive (p. 2).
- Four strings are compared. (2) is random letters and (4) is real words in a jumble. (1) and (3) are meaningful. (2) and (4) are gibberish. (4) has meaningful words, but they are not linked into a sentence and together mean nothing (p. 2).
- **Data to explain** (p. 2):
  - Some strings are meaningful sentences.
  - Their parts are meaningful.
  - Each sentence means something in particular.
  - Competent speakers understand and produce many sentences effortlessly and almost instantly.
- **Three questions** (p. 3): In virtue of what is a string meaningful? In virtue of what does it mean what it does? How can humans understand and produce meaningful speech?

*Intuition note:* Two puzzles arise together: what makes strings meaningful and mean what they do, and how speakers grasp even brand-new sentences so easily. A theory of meaning should answer both.

### The Referential Theory (p. 3)
- **The view:** expressions have their meanings because they stand for things, and what they mean is what they stand for. Words are like labels that represent, designate, name, denote or refer (p. 3).
- Examples the theory offers: "Adolf Hitler" denotes Hitler. "dog", "chien" and "Hund" refer to dogs. "The cat sat on the mat" represents a cat's sitting on a mat, presumably because its parts designate the cat, the mat and the relation of sitting on. Sentences "mirror" the states of affairs they describe (p. 3).
- Word–thing links are mostly arbitrary and conventional (p. 3).
- Understanding a sentence would be knowing what its component words refer to (p. 3).
- Lycan grants that the view is natural and that reference is the cleanest word–world relation. He says it soon meets serious objections (p. 3).

### Objection 1: not every word names an actual object (pp. 3–4)
- **(a) Empty names and quantifier pronouns.** "Pegasus" denotes nothing, since there is no winged horse. In (5) "I saw nobody", it would be a tired joke to take "nobody" as a name and reply "You must have very good eyesight, then". Carroll's "Who did you pass on the road?" plays on this, and cummings' poem works by using "anyone" as a name (pp. 3–4).
- **(b) Predicates.** In (6) "Ralph is fat", "fat" does not name Ralph. It describes him (p. 4).
  - Suggestion: "fat" names an abstract quality, fatness, and "is fat" means "has fatness" (p. 4).
  - If subject–predicate meaning is built by joining the name of a property to the name of an individual with the copula "is", then "is"/"has" needs its own abstract entity, the having relation. That makes (6) mean "Ralph bears the having relation to fatness", which needs a third entity for "bears", and so on without end. This is **Bradley's regress** (F. H. Bradley, pp. 17–18 of his 1930 work, as cited) (p. 4).
- **(c) Nouns that name nothing.** Quine's examples are "sake", "behalf" and "dint". They are nouns but, intuitively, name neither individual things nor kinds of things. They seem to mean something only by occurring in longer constructions (p. 4).
- **(d) Other parts of speech.** "very", "of", "and", "the", "a", "yes", "hey" and "alas" do not seem to refer to anything, yet are meaningful (p. 4).
- **Aside:** not everyone is convinced the theory is refuted even here. Montague (1960) set out to build a technical theory that assigns highly abstract referents even to such words, so that they would mean, at least in part, by referring to what they supposedly refer to. Lycan returns to this in Chapter 10 (p. 4).

*Intuition note:* Naming is only one thing words do. Once we try to make every word a name, we invent odd entities. For example, "fat" would need a thing, and then the link between thing and person would need another thing.

### Objection 2: a sentence is not a list of names (p. 5)
- On the theory, a sentence would be a list of names. (7) "Fred Martha Irving Phyllis" asserts nothing, even if some of the named items are abstract (p. 5).
- One might suppose that (8) "Ralph fatness", a name of an individual plus a name of a quality, would mean that Ralph is fat (early in his career Russell suggested that a list of names of the right sorts of things in the right order forms the collective name of a state of affairs). But (8) is ungrammatical (p. 5).
- To get subject–predicate meaning one must insert a verb, as in (9) "Ralph (has/exemplifies) fatness". That relaunches Bradley's regress (p. 5).

### Objection 3: more to meaning than reference (p. 5)
- Lycan says that specific phenomena, discussed in the next two chapters, seem to show there is more to meaning than reference (p. 5).
- Coreferring terms are often not synonymous: they share a referent but differ in meaning, e.g. "Jorge Mario Bergoglio" and "the Pope" (p. 5).
- Lycan: it looks as though we should conclude that there must be at least one way of being a meaningful expression other than naming, possibly even for some expressions that do name things. Better theories exist, each with its own difficulties (Part II) (p. 5).
- Reference is still studied next, in the following three chapters. It matters in its own right, and it introduces concepts needed to assess theories of meaning (p. 5).

### Summary (pp. 5–6)
Lycan's summary restates two of the data (meaningful sentences, meaningful parts) and the amazing speed of understanding novel sentences. It adds that the Referential Theory faces several compelling objections (pp. 5–6).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Try to give every word of "Ralph is very fat" a referent.
1. "Ralph" → the person Ralph. 1 of 4 words done.
2. "fat" → the property fatness. 2 of 4 done.
3. "is" → the having relation. 3 of 4 done.
4. "very" → nothing plausible. 3 of 4 words have a candidate referent (Objection 1d, p. 4).
5. Check the regress on "Ralph has fatness". The items are Ralph, the having relation and fatness, which is 3 items. They still need something to bind them, so add the relation "bears". Now there are 4 items.
6. The same need arises again, so the next stage has 5 items. Stage $k$ has $k+2$ items and still needs one more linker, so the process never ends (p. 4, as described).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Bradley's regress ↔ regress arguments elsewhere in philosophy: each added link needs a further link, so the gap never closes (p. 4)
- [metaphor] Words as labels ↔ lookup tables: each word maps to one item. Objections 1–2 show what such a table cannot do.

## Questions this raises
- If "Bergoglio" and "the Pope" have the same referent but different meanings, what is the extra ingredient of meaning?
- How do finitely many known words yield understanding of endlessly many new sentences? What does "something about how they are strung together" amount to?
- Could Montague-style abstract referents for "and" and "of" rescue the theory, or do they change what "refer" means?
- Can a referential theorist say how predicates like "fat" work without Bradley's regress?
