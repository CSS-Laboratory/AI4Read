# Proper Names: Direct Reference and the Causal–Historical Theory

## L0 — One line
Proper names are normally rigid, which counts against description theories; on Kripke's picture, what ties a name to its bearer is a causal–historical chain rather than a description in the speaker's head, though that picture needs repair.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke argued that ordinary proper names normally keep the same referent across possible worlds, while Russellian descriptions do not. So names are not equivalent to such descriptions. Direct Reference (DR) theorists go further and say a name contributes only its bearer. But the four puzzles then return, leaving a near-paradox. A separate question is what makes a name refer to its bearer. Kripke's causal–historical picture answers: a chain of communication back to the naming. Five objections show the picture needs refinement. Extended to natural-kind terms such as "water", and combined with Putnam's Twin Earth cases, the picture suggests, if the extension is right, that meanings are not fixed by what is in speakers' heads alone.

**Prerequisites:** Russell's description theory and the four puzzles (Ch. 3); Millian names; the idea of a "possible world" as a way things might have gone.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A possible world is a way things might have gone. A flaccid designator changes referent across worlds; a rigid designator keeps it in every world where the item exists (pp. 46–47).
- Kripke: names are normally rigid and Russellian descriptions are flaccid, so names are not equivalent to such descriptions. This is a generalisation about normal uses, not a universal thesis (pp. 47–48).
- A description tied to a name may only fix its reference, not give its meaning. This is Kripke's reply to Russell's spot-check and Searle's teaching arguments: it blocks their use as arguments for the Name Claim, but does not refute the Name Claim itself (pp. 48–49).
- Rigid does not imply Millian. DR theory claims the stronger Millian view, and it faces the four puzzles. Lycan finds the sketched DR explanations of why we hear belief sentences opaquely not very plausible (pp. 49–52).
- Pretense theories (Walton, Crimmins, Evans) say speakers of negative existentials and identity statements do not straightforwardly assert what they seem to (pp. 52–55).
- Semantics of names and the theory of referring vary independently. Kripke's causal–historical picture is a theory of referring (pp. 55–56).
- Naming-after, Santa Claus, and five objections show the picture needs refinement. As Lycan reports it, the majority position seems to be that some descriptive conditions are still needed (pp. 57–61).
- Kripke and Putnam extend rigidity and the causal–historical view to natural-kind terms. If that extension is right, Twin Earth examples seem to show that meaning is not in the head (pp. 61–63).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview (p. 45)
Lycan previews the chapter. Kripke used possible worlds against description theories. DR is highly plausible in light of Kripke's arguments, but the four puzzles haunt it. A separate question is what makes a name designate its bearer. Kripke's causal–historical picture answers it and needs refinement. Kripke and Putnam extend the picture to natural-kind terms. If that move is basically correct, Twin Earth examples seem to show that meaning is not determined solely by what is in speakers' and hearers' heads (p. 45).

### Possible Worlds (pp. 46–47)
- The actual world is the whole universe. Some things are false but might have been true. The world could have been different (p. 46).
- A **possible world** is one of the many ways things might have gone. Lycan takes this as a metaphor or heuristic and hopes the controversial metaphysical issues will not much affect Kripke's use of it (p. 46).
- A sentence with a fixed meaning can vary in truth-value across worlds. Example: *May is Prime Minister* is true actually, but false in countless worlds, for instance where May did not succeed Cameron, never went into politics, or never existed (p. 46).
- A singular term can also vary its referent. *The present British Prime Minister* designates May actually, but in some other worlds someone else or no one (pp. 46–47).
- **Flaccid designator:** a singular term that designates different things in different worlds. **Rigid designator (Kripke):** a term that denotes the same item in every world, at least every world in which that item exists (p. 47).
- Note 2 explains the qualification. Without it, rigid terms would have to denote in every world, so their referents would have to exist in every world. Ordinary things do not. Kripke is inclined to think the natural numbers do, and if so the numerals for them presumably designate the same things in every world (p. 64).

*Intuition note:* Hold the words fixed and imagine other ways the world could be. A description picks out whoever (if anyone) fits it in each world. A rigid term picks out the same item each time.

### Rigidity and Proper Names (pp. 47–49)
- **Kripke's further objection (1972/1980: 74ff.):** Russellian descriptions are flaccid, but proper names (usually) do not vary across worlds. In a world where Aristotle does something else, our name "Aristotle" denotes him there. So names are not equivalent to Russellian descriptions. A description used referentially (Donnellan) may go rigid (p. 47).
- **Qualifications:** "usually" and "normally" matter. Kripke generalises about normal uses only. Flaccid names exist, e.g. "Jack the Ripper" and "Chummy" (used as a synonym for *whoever committed the crime*). Lycan adds that probably any name has occasional flaccid uses, such as Frege's *Trieste is no Vienna* (and Bentsen's remark to Quayle), but these are not standard uses (p. 47).
- **Kripke's test:** plug a term into the frame "N might not have been N."
  - With *the President of the United States in 1970* the result is true on its most natural reading. So the description is flaccid (pp. 47–48).
  - With *Nixon* the result is at best very strange. It might mean that Nixon might not have existed. He could have lacked the name, but that is not failing to be Nixon himself. He could have lacked the stereotypical properties, but that is the unusual flaccid use (p. 48).
- Kripke argues that when we use "Nixon" and then describe hypothetical worlds, we are still talking about the same person. Lycan's illustration: *Might Nixon have joined the Black Panthers?* concerns Nixon himself as a Panther, not a world where whoever is President is a Panther (p. 48).
- **Reply to Russell's spot check and Searle's teaching argument:** both assumed that an associated description gives the name's meaning. Kripke distinguishes a weaker relation: the description might only determine the referent on an occasion. *The first woman ever to win the Prix de Rome* can indicate who "Lili Boulanger" refers to without being synonymous with it. A firm psychological association between name and description does not entail equivalence in meaning. Telling a child *Theresa May is the British Prime Minister* does not show that the name simply means that (p. 48).
- Lycan notes this undermines the spot-check argument for the Name Claim, not the Name Claim itself (pp. 48–49).

*Intuition note:* A description can serve as a pointer that picks out the person. That does not by itself make it the name's meaning. Kripke's modal test suggests that names normally behave differently from descriptions when we imagine other worlds.

### Direct Reference (pp. 49–52)
**Rigid vs Millian.**
- A **Millian name** makes no propositional contribution but its bearer. *Jason is fat* then consists of Jason himself and the property of being fat (p. 49).
- Millian implies rigid; rigid does not imply Millian. If arithmetical truths are all necessary, *the positive square root of nine* is rigid. It is not Millian, since it uses conceptual content to secure its reference (p. 49).
- Kripke argued for rigidity, not for Millianism, and does not believe names are Millian (p. 49).
- The **Direct Reference (DR) Theory** is the Millian conception. Marcus (1960, 1961) was the first in the twentieth century, cited by Kripke as having directly inspired his work; later DR theorists include Kaplan (1975) and Salmon (1986). They extend DR to some other singular terms, notably personal and demonstrative pronouns. A problem is that speakers understand *I* without knowing who is referred to. Lycan defers this to Ch. 11 (p. 49).
- Note 4 adds that Kaplan's "dthat" makes a description denote its actual satisfier rigidly (pp. 64–65).

**DR and Substitutivity (pp. 50–51).**
- Take (1) *Albert believes that Samuel Langhorne Clemens was less than 5 feet tall.* It goes false when "Mark Twain" is substituted. DR theorists use two theses. The **positive** thesis says the substitution preserves truth, so (2) *Albert believes that Mark Twain was less than 5 feet tall* is true. At the very least, belief sentences have transparent readings (p. 50).
- On the DR line, (2) seems false because we usually take the complement clause to reproduce how the subject would speak or think. DR theorists point out that such suggestions are not always true, perhaps not ever true (p. 50).
- (3) *Columbus believed that Castro's island was China* (attributed to Chisholm) can be asserted with no presumption that Columbus represented Cuba that way (p. 50).
- The Le Chat case: suppose you and I know that Jacques is the notorious thief called "Le Chat". We read that police believe Le Chat dropped the anchovies. We say *The police think Jacques dropped the anchovies* (p. 50).
- (4) *Some people doubt that Tully is Tully* can be heard, perhaps as a minority interpretation, as doubting of Cicero that he was Tully (p. 51).
- So transparent positions exist. But the **negative** thesis is also needed: names have no non-Millian readings, even in belief contexts. Most of us hold that every belief sentence also has an opaque reading, where substitution can change truth-value. Columbus in one sense believed that Castro's island was China and in another did not (p. 51).
- Lycan: it is hard to deny that opaque readings are more readily heard. DR theorists must explain this as an illusion, so that sentences like (1)–(4) cannot literally mean what we usually take them to mean. Explanations have been sketched (Salmon, Soames, Wettstein, Marcus). In Lycan's opinion none has struck him as very plausible, though Soames (2002) is perhaps the most promising (p. 51).

**Other puzzles (pp. 51–52).**
- **Frege's Puzzle:** on DR, *Clemens is Mark Twain* can mean only that the common referent is itself. Yet it is virtually never understood that way, and one can doubt it without doubting anyone's self-identity. The DR theorist must explain away these judgements (p. 51).
- **Nonexistents and negative existentials** are, in Lycan's view, if anything worse. If a name's meaning is simply to refer to its bearer, what of all the perfectly meaningful names that have no bearers? Salmon (1998), Soames (2002) and Braun (2005) make DR attempts to meet these two problems (pp. 51–52).

**The trilemma (p. 52).**
- Kripkean reasons (Ch. 3) say names cannot abbreviate flaccid descriptions or have substantive senses, so names intuitively seem Millian. But the original puzzles seem to leave DR pretty well refuted. It appears we must choose among three views: names are Millian, or abbreviate descriptions outright, or have a looser Searle-style sense. None is acceptable (p. 52).
- Proposed ways between the horns: Plantinga (1978) and Ackerman (1979) use rigidified descriptions such as *the actual winner of the 1968 election*, so names are rigid but not Millian. Devitt (1989, 1996) radically revises Frege's notion of sense. Lycan mentions his own weakened DR without detail (p. 52).

*Intuition note:* Rigid is weaker than Millian. Kripke's argument establishes rigidity, not the stronger Millian claim. DR's stronger Millian claim then has to handle all four puzzles.

### Pretense Theories (pp. 52–55)
- Kripke (1972/2012) suggested that writing fiction involves pretending that the name really refers. So it is presumably part of the story's pretense that the name has the ordinary semantic function of names (p. 52).
- Kripke observed that the main problem is that this view cannot account for negative existentials. Lycan adds that it cannot explain anything about names, including bearerless ones, outside deliberate fiction, and that real entities in fiction cause trouble (Kroon 1994) (p. 52).
- **Evans (1982)** extends the idea to negative existentials, broadening pretense to cover shared illusions or mistaken testimony (pp. 52–53).
- **Walton (1990):** statements made within make-believe can double as real-world assertions. *The guests at the Last Supper are gradually fading* may be really true of the painting. He also allows "unofficial" games, and the notions of betraying and disavowing a fiction (p. 53).
- **Walton on negative existentials:** (5) *Gregor Samsa does not exist* is a disavowal.
  - The speaker pretends to attempt to refer and disavows that kind of attempt. What is asserted is that attempting to refer in this manner fails (pp. 53–54).
  - Walton extends the analysis to names outside fiction. There the speaker need not pretend, since the pretense is not essential to what is asserted (pp. 53–54).
  - Lycan's example is (6) *Methuselah does not exist* (the exoplanet, supposed for argument's sake not to exist). On Walton's view, what makes attempts the same kind is that it is fictional, in an implied unofficial game, that they successfully refer to the same thing (p. 54).
  - Lycan finds this hard going. The maverick seems simply to disagree about a matter of fact. Lycan imagines Walton replying that the problem is the additional belief allegedly expressed by (6) (p. 54).
- **Crimmins (1998) on identity (pp. 54–55):**
  - He takes his cue from Russell's remark that with *Scott is the author of Waverley* we are half-tempted to think there are two people. He also notes that even trained philosophers say things like "when two things are identical, the one thing has the same properties as the other" (p. 54).
  - So in making an identity statement the speaker talks as if there were two things bearing a "promiscuous" sameness relation. Crimmins calls this "shallow" pretense. Using Frege's modes of presentation, the pretense licenses representing distinctions among modes of presentation as distinctions among objects as presented (p. 55).
  - The real assertion is true because the pretended one is pretend-true. That holds because the two modes of presentation attach to one individual. The sentence itself is trivially and necessarily true and does not say anything about modes of presentation (p. 55).
- Lycan: pretense theories offer a tortuous way out. An easier route might be speaker-meaning versus literal meaning (Chs. 2, 7). The problematic sentences mean what DR says, while speakers may mean something more descriptive. He leaves the assessment to the reader (p. 55).

### Semantics vs the theory of referring (p. 55)
- Semantics asks what names contribute to sentence meaning. A philosophical **theory of referring** asks what relation ties an utterance of a name to its referent (p. 55).
- The two vary independently. Russell and Searle blurred them: for each, the description gives the name's meaning and also determines who the bearer is. Being a DR theorist, by itself, tells us nothing about what attaches a name to its referent. The same goes for Kripke's weaker rigidity thesis: so far he has been talking semantics only (p. 55).

### The Causal–Historical Theory (pp. 56–57)
- Most of Kripke's objections to the Name Claim translate into objections to the Description Theory of referring. That theory predicts the wrong referent (the Gödel/Schmidt case, Ch. 3 objection 5) or no referent (objections 1 and 6) (p. 56).
- **Kripke's picture (1972/1980: 91):** a baby is born and the parents name him. The name spreads from link to link as if by a chain. A speaker at the far end may be referring to Feynman even without remembering from whom he heard the name. The speaker knows Feynman was a famous physicist but cannot identify him uniquely (p. 56).
- Lycan's gloss: my use of "Feynman" is the latest link in a chain of reference-borrowings. The first link is the infant's being given the name. I need not be in any cognitive state of Russell's or Searle's sort, nor have any interesting true belief about Feynman. All that is required is that a chain of communication in fact goes back to him (p. 56).
- A new user first learns a name from a predecessor only by sharing a psychologically salient backing of identifying descriptions. But there is no reason to think that backing fixes the name's sense. It is needed only to fix reference (p. 56).
- Taken at face value, this view makes the right predictions about Donnellan's Tom. Reference succeeds because of the appropriate causal connection (p. 56).
- **Jonah (Kripke 1972/1980: 66–7):** distinguish complete myths from substantially false accounts of real people. Suppose nothing the Bible says of Jonah was true. Then either the character was made up, or the story is grounded in a real person. In the several subcases where a real person is the source, today the Bible seems to be saying false things about him (pp. 56–57). Note 10 mentions the Robin Hood case, which Kaplan once took to favour the causal–historical view over Searle's (p. 65).
- **Ambiguous names:** description theories treat them as abbreviating different descriptions. The causal–historical theory says a name is ambiguous because more than one person has been given it. What disambiguates a use is its causal–historical grounding, i.e. whose naming ceremony started its etiology (p. 57).
- Kripke emphasises that he offers only a picture. Lycan says the only way to make a picture into a theory is to take it overliterally and see how it needs refining. Kripke does that but leaves the refinement to others (p. 57).

*Intuition note:* On this picture, what makes my "Feynman" refer to him is the chain of passing the name on. It is not what I can say about him. The descriptions shared when the name is passed on are needed only to fix reference, to help point at the right person.

### Problems for the Causal–Historical Theory (pp. 57–61)
**Two issues Kripke raised (pp. 57–58).**
- **Naming after:** Lycan's friend John Lewis named his dog "Napoleon" after the emperor. This is a causal link of the wrong kind. Kripke requires that the receiver of the name "intend when he learns it to use it with the same reference as the man from whom he heard it" (1972/1980: 96). Lewis did not meet this requirement (pp. 57–58).
- **Santa Claus and Dracula:** the chain for "Santa Claus" may trace back to a historical saint, probably a real person, and the use of "Dracula" goes back to Vlad. But we use these names for the fictional characters, not for the real people. So how do they differ from "Jonah"? Why do we not say there was a real Santa Claus whose mythology is garishly false? Kripke only raised the problem. Lycan says probably the most obvious feature is that these names carry very powerful stereotypes and have ossified into fictional descriptions, unlike "Jonah" (p. 58).
- Devitt (1981a) gives a fairly well-developed theory. The objections below would apply to any version of the view as described (p. 58).

**Objection 1: empty names.** How can the theory accommodate names that have no actual bearers? Lycan's best bet (Devitt 1981a; Donnellan 1974): empty names are introduced at a time, through fiction or error, and chains spread from there. The chain's first link is the naming event itself, not the nonexistent bearer (pp. 58–59). Note 11 says this helps with names for future individuals and for abstract objects. It also notes that the naming event might then seem to be the referent (p. 65).

**Objection 2: unnoticed change of reference (Evans 1973).** "Madagascar" originally named part of the mainland, and a misunderstanding of Marco Polo's changed it to the island. In the switched-babies case, the man called "Jack" is called that because a woman named another baby. We do not want "Jack" to refer to the other baby. Reply (Devitt 1981a: 150): **multiple grounding.** Perceptual encounters can also ground chains. The structure is mangrove-like, and a large preponderance of groundings in the island or the man can override the original ceremony. Lycan says this is vague, perhaps objectionably so (p. 59).

**Objection 3: misidentified object of naming.** Lycan names a shelter cat "Liz", but unbeknownst to him it was the wrong cat; the right cat was later brought home. The imposter was named in the ceremony. Yet his own cat is surely the bearer. Multiple grounding does not seem to help. Lycan says what matters is which cat he had in mind and believed he was naming. Devitt's "abilities to designate" are mental states of a sophisticated type. If so, repair will need a significant foray into the philosophy of mind (pp. 59–60).

**Objection 4: categorial mistakes (qua-problem).** Evans cites Chambers on King Arthur's son Anir, whom legend has perhaps confused with a burial place. The theory would make *Anir must be a green and lovely spot* say that a human is a green spot. Similar cases: person versus institution ("Emerson Hall" for the philosophy department); a shadow mistaken for a person. Devitt and Sterelny (1987) call this the qua-problem and concede that those grounding a name must intend to refer to something of the appropriate category. Lycan calls this a mild concession to descriptivism (p. 60).

**Objection 5: tangled confusions (Evans; Rosenberg 1994).** Rosenberg points out that only obvious descriptive material determines the intuitively correct referent. Heidi lectures on the Vienna Circle. Helmut swaps the names "Schlick" and "Neurath" when telling Reinhold. He intends to use the names as Heidi did but fails. A chain of borrowings leads back to the positivists, so Kripke's picture predicts that *Neurath was murdered* is about Neurath. Intuitively Helmut is talking about Schlick (pp. 60–61). Note 13 says Rosenberg argues Devitt's theory is also subject to this (p. 65).

**Verdict (p. 61).** The majority position, Lycan says, seems to be that Kripke initially overreacted. On that view, causal–historical chains of some kind are required and descriptions do less work than Russell or Searle thought, but (as critics including Kripke himself maintain) there still are some descriptive conditions. The trick is to move back toward descriptivism without going as far as even Searle's weak descriptivism. "Causal descriptivism" (Kroon 1987) accepts the causal–historical proposal as getting the cases right but makes it a descriptive condition; Kroon defends this mainly by extending the naming-after objection (p. 61).

### Natural-Kind Terms and "Twin Earth" (pp. 61–63)
- **Extension (Kripke 1972/1980; Putnam 1975a):** rigid designation and the causal–historical theory are extended to natural-kind terms like "gold", "water" and "tiger". These are not singular terms, but Kripke and Putnam argued they are more like names than adjectives. On their view they are semantically rigid, each referring to the same natural kind in every world in which that kind has membership, and some version of the causal–historical theory characterises their referring use (p. 61).
- **Opposed view:** descriptive stereotypes (water as a clear, odourless, tasteless potable liquid that falls as rain). Kripke and Putnam gave modal arguments. There could have been water without rain, lakes or streams, and water might have had an odour or taste. Tigers might have been born tame, and we might even find out that no tiger has ever had stripes (pp. 61–62).
- **What makes something water or a tiger (Kripke and Putnam):** its scientific nature, H₂O or a distinctive genetic code. On their view water is H₂O in every possible world, though in some worlds H₂O has an odour or taste. What varies from world to world are the elements of the stereotype (p. 62).
- **Objection and reply:** it was a substantive empirical discovery that water is H₂O, so it seems possible that it not be. Kripke and Putnam reply that this "possibility" is only scientific ignorance, not genuine metaphysical possibility (p. 62).
- **Consequence, if the view is correct:** as Putnam puts it, meaning "ain't in the head". Twin Earth is a planet within the same world, a near-duplicate of Earth with molecular duplicates of each of us. The one difference is that its "water" is XYZ, a superficially water-like substance ("fool's water") (p. 62).
- Twin Earthlings call XYZ "water", but in Twin English the word means XYZ, not H₂O (p. 62).
- **Argument:** May and Twin Theresa utter word-for-word identical sentences about getting food and "water" to victims. May's means food and H₂O; Twin Theresa's means food and XYZ. They are physical duplicates, so, given Putnam's background assumptions, utterance meanings are not determined by total brain or bodily states (pp. 62–63).
- Again given those assumptions, Putnam's example shows more: meanings are not determined even by the whole community's pattern of usage, since English and Twin English speakers are alike physically and in the public use of the words (p. 63).
- Note 15: a high proportion of the human body is water, so Twin Earthlings could hardly be our molecular duplicates. Lycan suggests ignoring this or using another natural kind (p. 66). Note 16: Burge (1979) argues that what a speaker means depends in part on community usage. Lycan says this would be less surprising but does sustain Putnam's claim that meaning is not in the head (p. 66).

*Intuition note:* If a kind-term refers by being causally linked to the real stuff, two identical speakers can mean different things when linked to different stuff. This is conditional on the Kripke–Putnam view being correct. It is contested by Searle, Rosenberg and Segal (note 14, p. 64).

### Summary (pp. 63–64)
Lycan's summary: Kripke argued that names normally function as rigid designators. DR theorists take the Millian view, but the four puzzles seem to make it untenable, leaving something of a paradox. Pretense theories reinterpret negative existentials and identity statements. Kripke's causal–historical picture is a theory of referring, refined by Devitt and others. It was extended to natural-kind terms, and if it is correct, Twin Earth examples seem to show that meanings are not entirely determined by the contents of speakers' and hearers' heads (pp. 63–64).

## Toy example (ours)
Three worlds. W1 (actual): May is PM and Cameron is not. W2: Cameron is PM, May exists. W3: nobody is PM, May does not exist.
- Description *the PM*: denotes May in W1, Cameron in W2, nobody in W3. It is flaccid.
- Name "May" (used rigidly, as in our world): denotes May in W1, May in W2, and nothing in W3, because she does not exist there. Rigid means the same item in every world where it exists, so this is allowed.
- Test frame "N might not have been N" with the description: *The PM might not have been the PM* is true, because in W2 the PM is a different person from W1's.
- The same frame with the name: no world where May exists gives us a different person, so the sentence is false on its natural reading. At most it is true on the reading "May might not have existed", which is the W3 case.
- Reference chain: Ann names baby Bo (link 0). Cy learns of Bo from Ann, Di learns from Cy. Di's use of "Bo" refers to Bo although Di has no uniquely identifying description of Bo (only something like "Cy's friend's baby"). This is the causal–historical picture. Kripke adds that each hearer must intend to use the name with the same reference as the speaker.

## Concepts
- [[rigid-designator]] — a term denoting the same item in every world where that item exists; Kripke's test is the frame *N might not have been N* (p. 47)
- [[flaccid-designator]] (new) — a singular term that denotes different things in different worlds, such as Russellian descriptions (p. 47)
- [[possible-world]] (new) — a way things might have gone; used here as a heuristic picture (p. 46)
- [[millian-name]] (new) — a name that contributes only its bearer to what a sentence says. Millian implies rigid, not conversely (p. 49)
- [[direct-reference-theory]] (new) — the Millian view of names, extended to some pronouns and demonstratives, with a positive and a negative thesis (pp. 49–51)
- [[reference-fixing-vs-meaning]] (new) — a description may only identify a name's referent without giving its meaning (p. 48)
- [[transparent-vs-opaque-reading]] (new) — in a belief sentence a term either just refers to its bearer or reproduces the subject's way of representing it (pp. 50–51)
- [[pretense-theory]] (new) — the view that speakers of some name-sentences pretend or talk as if, instead of straightforwardly asserting (pp. 52–55)
- [[causal-historical-theory-of-reference]] — a use of a name refers via a chain of borrowings back to the naming; Kripke requires the receiver to intend to use the name with the source's reference (pp. 56–58)
- [[multiple-grounding]] (new) — Devitt: a name's chain can be grounded in several encounters with the bearer, not only the naming ceremony (p. 59)
- [[qua-problem]] (new) — the problem of categorial mistakes about a name's bearer; Devitt and Sterelny concede that grounders must intend to refer to something of the appropriate category (p. 60)
- [[causal-descriptivism]] (new) — Kroon: take the causal–historical proposal and make it a descriptive condition (p. 61)
- [[natural-kind-term]] (new) — a common noun like "water" or "tiger" that, per Kripke and Putnam, is semantically rigid and refers to a kind with a scientific nature (pp. 61–62)
- [[semantic-externalism]] — if the Kripke–Putnam view is correct, meaning depends partly on the external world, so Twin Earth duplicates can mean different things (pp. 62–63)
- [[freges-puzzle]] — on DR, *Clemens is Mark Twain* can mean only that the referent is itself, yet it is virtually never understood that way (p. 51)
