# Psychological Theories: Grice's Program

## L0 — One line
Grice tried to reduce sentence meaning to speaker-meaning, and speaker-meaning to speakers' intentions and other mental states. Lycan judges the second step defensible, but the first step seems to succeed only by conceding too much to rival theories of sentence meaning.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can linguistic meaning be reduced to psychology? Grice proposed two stages. Stage two analyses speaker-meaning (what a speaker means on an occasion) through layered intentions. Stage one builds sentence meaning out of speaker-meaning. Lycan says stage two has survived many objections in complicated form. Stage one meets four obstacles: sentence meaning constrains what speakers can mean, most sentences are never uttered, novel sentences are understood at once, and sentences are often used non-literally. Grice answers with "abstract resultant procedures". Lycan thinks this gives away most of the program, because it reintroduces meanings of parts and syntax as a base.

**Prerequisites:** the difference between a sentence type and its tokens; belief and intention as mental states; compositionality (a whole's meaning built from its parts).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Grice's program is a reduction of linguistic meaning to psychology, in two stages: sentence meaning → speaker-meaning → intentions and other mental states (p. 91).
- Speaker-meaning differs from sentence meaning in cases of context-dependence, mistaken usage and sarcasm (p. 91).
- The analysis of speaker-meaning has three intention clauses (G1–G3). They need not be conscious (p. 92).
- Four objections are meant to show the analysis is too demanding (no audience; no intent to produce belief) or too weak (gibberish; the captured soldier). Griceans revise it, and a complicated version remains tenable (pp. 93–96).
- Four obstacles face stage one: sentence meaning constrains speaker-meaning, unuttered sentences, novel sentences, and non-literal usage (pp. 96–98).
- Grice's reply defines meaning for unstructured utterances, then for groups, then builds sentence meaning from "abstract resultant procedures" (p. 99).
- Lycan's verdict: he thinks that appeal gives away most of the program, since meanings of parts plus syntax now do the work (p. 100).
- Lycan's own suggestion: a Gricean theory of referring (speaker-reference, speaker-extension) could perhaps ground word meaning. On the face of it, it competes with the Description and Causal–Historical theories (p. 100).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview and Grice's Basic Idea (pp. 90–92)
- Grice holds that an expression has meaning because it expresses a concrete idea or intention of its user. "Express" here has a literal, psychological sense, not the relation between sentences and propositions (pp. 90–91).
- Sentences are types of marks and noises. Their tokens are produced by people for a purpose, usually to communicate (p. 90).
- Grice (1957, 1969) grounded sentence meaning in the mental. Lycan calls this the reduction of linguistic meaning to psychology (p. 91).
- The linchpin is *speaker-meaning* (Grice: "utterer's meaning"). It differs from sentence meaning, and this is Grice's departure from the classic ideational theories (p. 91). Three illustrations:
  1. Strawson's "This is a fine red one." Its meaning is not fully determinate. One speaker may mean a pear, another a fire engine (p. 91).
  2. A speaker wrongly thinks "jejune" means callow or puerile. He says that the "Piccolomini" Mass is jejune. The sentence means *meager and unsatisfying*, which he would judge false, but he *meant* callow and puerile (p. 91).
  3. Sarcasm: "That was a brilliant idea" meant as "very stupid" (p. 91).
- The program has two stages (p. 91):
  - Stage 1: sentence meaning → speaker-meaning. This is plausible, as Wittgenstein stressed. Sentences have meaning because of what speakers use them to do, and, it seems, conventional meaning rests on communicative practices (p. 91).
  - Stage 2: speaker-meaning → a complex of psychological states centred on a type of intention (p. 91).
- Lycan notes that Grice put most of his energy into stage 2 (p. 92). He calls the explication of speaker-meaning in mental terms even more plausible than stage 1 (p. 92).

### Speaker-Meaning (pp. 92–96)
Lycan's paraphrase for declaratives, of "By uttering x, S meant that P" (p. 92):
- (G1) S uttered x intending that A (the hearer) form the belief that P.
- (G2) S further intended that A recognize S's original intention (the one in G1).
- (G3) S still further intended that A form the belief that P at least partly on the basis of recognizing that original intention.

Example: in the "Piccolomini" case, I meant the Mass is callow and puerile because I intended you to form that belief at least partly by recognizing that I intended it (p. 92). The intentions may be tacit, not conscious (p. 92).

*Intuition note.* On this analysis, to mean something is to try to get someone to believe it, at least partly through their seeing that you are trying. Lycan notes that ordinary intentions are often only tacit, so the speaker need not be aware of them.

Lycan reviews objections; the analysis has been revised nearly constantly since 1969 (p. 93).

**Objection 1: no audience (p. 93).** Soliloquy, and Ziff's George saying "Claudius murdered my father" in a soliloquy, in conversation, and in delirious fever. George means the same each time, yet Grice requires an audience-directed intention. Grice (1969) replies with hypothetical audiences: S intends that, were anyone present with normal perception, that person would form the belief. Lycan: perhaps S must intend this, since even self-talk assumes the words would make sense to someone. A possible counterexample is a desert-island solitary who never formed the concept of an audience. Lycan calls this highly controversial, since many philosophers deny such a language is possible (p. 93).

**Objection 2: no intent to produce belief via recognition (pp. 93–95).** G3, even G2, may be too strong. G1 may also fail where the speaker knows the audience already has the belief.
- *Conclusion of argument:* a prover means the conclusion but intends the audience to believe it on the argument's merit, not on recognizing the intention (pp. 93–94).
- *Examinee:* a pupil answering "Waterloo, 1815" does not intend to induce the belief in the examiner (p. 94).
- Schiffer: the speaker is her/his own audience. This might do, but not for Examinee (p. 94).
- Grice's two revisions (p. 94):
  1. G1 demands intending *activated* belief, which may account, though not very naturally, for Examinee.
  2. G1 is weakened: the audience is intended to believe only that the speaker believes P.
- Lycan finds revision 2 reasonable, as it deals briskly with Examinee. But, as Grice grants and Schiffer emphasizes, neither revision helps Conclusion of argument. Communication does not always succeed by the audience taking the speaker's word (p. 94). Lycan's own example: Grice has communicated his theory to us without intending us to accept it on his say-so, so the weakened G1 being satisfied does not help (p. 94).
- Lycan: the own-audience move still faces counterexamples. These are a second proof of a theorem while the first is on the blackboard, and two philosophers joyfully chanting "Names only name!". Each already has the activated belief and knows the other has it, yet they surely mean it (pp. 94–95).
- Lycan drops the objection here. A footnote offers a possible fix: talk not just of activation but of reinforcement (p. 95; n. 6, p. 101).

Objections 1–2 say the analysis is too demanding. Objections 3–4 say it is not demanding enough (p. 95).

**Objection 3: gibberish (p. 95).** George, asked by an army officer what he would say if asked to identify himself, utters "Ugh blugh blugh ugh blugh." He means to show contempt and means the officer to recognize it through recognizing that intention. Grice's conditions are met, yet George did not mean anything in a linguistic sense. Lycan adds that a wider sense of "communication" seems still to be captured (p. 95). Footnote 7 links this to Urmson's thumbscrew example and Grice's "Redefinition I". Lycan has never seen how that redefinition rules such cases out (p. 101).

**Objection 4: the captured soldier (p. 95).** An American soldier, captured by Italians, barks "Kennst du das Land wo die Zitronen blühen?", the only German he knows. He intends them to believe he is a German officer, to recognize that intention, and to form the belief partly because of it. Yet he does not seem to mean that he is a German officer (Searle 1965). Grice's response: the audience must be intended to believe there is a "mode of correlation" between features of the utterance and the intended belief type. Schiffer uses "mutual knowledge*". Lycan declines to pursue these (p. 96).

**Assessment.** Griceans such as Schiffer and Avramides have modified the account to handle all these cases and more. So a complicated version remains tenable. It is generally agreed that speaker-meaning is somehow a matter of speakers' intentions and other mental states (p. 96).

*Intuition note.* Each counterexample shows a way the three clauses can be satisfied without meaning, or meaning can exist without them. The fixes make the analysis more complicated, but Lycan says a complicated version remains tenable.

### Sentence Meaning (pp. 96–100)
Grice's (1968) construction is elaborate. Lycan first states obstacles, then outlines Grice's way round them (p. 96). The natural start: a sentence means that P only in the sense that English speakers, when they utter it, always or at least normally speaker-mean that P (p. 96).

**Obstacle 1: speaker-meaning is too cheap and sentence meaning constrains it (pp. 96–97).**
- Ziff's cases: George, with electrodes in his head, says "Glyting elly beleg", believing it synonymous with "I feel fine". A man cries "Gleeg gleeg gleeg!" intending to make people believe it is snowing in Tibet (pp. 96–97).
- Lycan does not read these as counterexamples to the analysis of speaker-meaning. He thinks George did mean he felt fine, and the madman meant it was snowing in Tibet (p. 97).
- His point: *if* Grice's analysis of speaker-meaning is right, then in a suitably disordered state any speaker might mean anything by any noises. Then stage 1 gets no formal constraint on what speakers mean by a sentence, only statistics (p. 97).
- In real life this is not so, for two reasons. (a) Most people are not deranged. (b) Sentences have the meanings they have. Unless the speaker is oddly mistaken about a word's meaning or some more elaborate stage-setting is in place (sarcasm is one possibility), one cannot mean "It's warm here" by "It's cold here" (Wittgenstein's example). A sentence's prior meaning partly controls what a speaker can mean by it (p. 97).
- Reason (b) embarrasses stage 1, because sentence meaning should not have to constrain speaker-meaning if it is analysed away into it. Lycan concedes that "should not" may be too strong: there is no flat-out circularity, and a special construct out of speaker-meaning might constrain speaker-meaning in general. The Gricean must still explain why the constraint is so robust (p. 97).

**Obstacle 2: unuttered sentences (p. 97).** Most meaningful sentences are never uttered, so nobody has meant anything by them (Platts 1979); so their meanings can hardly be fixed by what speakers normally mean by them. Appealing to what speakers *would* have meant is tempting but not much use. Most such sentences would never have been uttered, and our only handle on what would have been meant is what we already know the sentence to mean (p. 97).

**Obstacle 3: novel sentences (p. 98).** A novel sentence can be understood at once, yet there is no pre-established fact of what speakers normally mean by it. Its first use may be also the last and may be nonliteral (Lycan's long example sentence about a philosophy corporation's president). Blackburn (1984) notes that a sentence may be uttered with almost any intention. His alternative ties meaning to a social regard-licence, independent of utterers' intentions. Lycan calls this not Gricean, since it severs sentence meaning from communicative intentions (p. 98).

**Obstacle 4: non-literal use (p. 98).** Figurative use and indirect speech acts are common. If "normally" is used to define a sentence's meaning, Grice must say what it means independently of standard meaning. Private codes: the Japanese "East wind, rain" signal for Pearl Harbor has, so far as Lycan knows, been used only as an attack signal. Everyday cases include "Where's the fire?", a polite question about the time, the idiom of burying the hatchet, and "Business is business". Metaphor is, for Grice, a species of conversational implicature (p. 98).

*Intuition note.* Stage 1 needs facts about what speakers mean by each sentence. But many sentences are never used, are brand-new, or are used non-literally. And what speakers can mean seems limited by what the sentence already means.

### Grice's reduction of sentence meaning (pp. 99–100)
Lycan sketches it, and how Grice would have approached what the text calls "objections 1–4" (read here as the obstacles; see Reading notes) (p. 99).
1. Start with meaning in one person's *idiolect* (that person's own speech). Distinguish *structured* utterances (with meaningful parts, such as declarative sentences, compositional) from *unstructured* ones ("Ouch", a beckoning gesture). Grice uses "utterance" broadly, including nonverbal acts (p. 99).
2. Unstructured $x$ means that P in S's idiolect iff (roughly) S has in her/his repertoire the procedure: utter $x$ if, for some audience A, S intends A to believe that S believes that P. Grice argues that the final clause is a harmless simplification of "S speaker-means that P" (p. 99).
3. For a group G, $x$ (unstructured) means that P for G iff (a) many members of G have the procedure of uttering $x$ if, for some A, they want A to believe that they believe that P; and (b) retaining that procedure is, for them, conditional on assuming that at least some other members of G have it (p. 99).
4. Lycan thinks (a) plus (b) is what is supposed to overcome obstacle 1, and says this seems exactly right (p. 99).
5. For structured sentences, Grice uses "resultant procedures". Lycan's reading (the article is dense): the speaker has a complex, abstract procedure made up of the concrete procedures for the parts. Sentence meaning is then a function of the utterance meanings of the ultimate parts, and the group analysis explicates those (p. 99).
6. Few abstract procedures ever actually occur. That helps with obstacles 2–3, since unuttered and novel sentences at least arguably correspond to hypothetical speaker-meanings. It may also help with obstacle 4, since a literal meaning never actually matched may still match a hypothetical resultant speaker-meaning (p. 100).

### Lycan's assessment (p. 100)
- Lycan thinks this appeal gives away most of the program. Suppose Grice could supply "expression meaning" for words; then, with syntax (Chapter 9), sentence meanings could be built from it, which need not be a brute positing of propositions (p. 100).
- Chapter 2's *speaker-reference* contrasts with semantic reference in Gricean fashion. Lycan suggests that perhaps we could define *speaker-extension* for names and predicates, and borrowing the repertoire/procedure language. This two-tier reduction would still face problems, but not obstacles 2–4 (p. 100).
- It suggests a research program: can a name's semantic denotation be analysed through speaker-reference? On the face of it, that competes with the Description Theory and the Causal–Historical Theory (p. 100).
- But this composite view concedes that another, reference-based theory of sentence meaning is correct. It adds only, though significantly, a new theory of referring (p. 100).

*Intuition note.* Once word meanings plus syntax do the work, the intention-based story is no longer the base of sentence meaning. It would become a theory of how words refer or apply, a new type of theory of referring.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Checking the group analysis (p. 99) on an unstructured utterance. Take a group of 5: Ana, Ben, Cy, Di, Ed. Let $x$ = a double knock on a door, and P = "someone is here".
- Ana, Ben, Cy and Di each have the procedure: knock twice if they want a hearer to believe they believe someone is here. That is 4 of 5.
- Ed has no such procedure.
- Condition (a), "many members": 4 of 5 is 80%. The text gives no cut-off for "many", so we assume 4 of 5 counts.
- Condition (b): each of the four keeps the procedure only because they assume at least one other member has it. Remove the other three and Ana would drop it. This holds for all four.
- Result: the double knock means "someone is here" for this group. Ed's lack does not change this.

Now suppose only Ana has the procedure. Condition (a) fails (1 of 5), so the knock does not mean this for the group, whatever happens with (b). It may still be speaker-meaning for Ana.

## Concepts
- [[speaker (non-natural) meaning]] — what S means by uttering x: intending A to form a belief via recognition of that intention (G1–G3; later weakened). Lycan calls it "speaker-meaning" (p. 92)
- [[explication]] — Grice's reduction of sentence meaning to speaker-meaning and then to mental states, used as analysis (p. 91)
- [[analysis]] — here, an analysis of "By uttering x, S meant that P" in terms of intentions (p. 92)
- [[conversational implicature]] — mentioned only: Grice treats metaphor as a species of it (p. 98)
- [[convention]] — mentioned only: Schiffer's alternative uses Lewis's theory of conventions (n. 9, p. 102)
- [[reductive-program]] (new) — the aim of reducing linguistic meaning to psychology (p. 91)
- [[idiolect]] (new) — one person's own distinctive speech; Grice defines meaning here first (p. 99)
- [[structured-vs-unstructured-utterance]] (new) — with or without meaningful parts that contribute compositionally (p. 99)
- [[resultant-procedure]] (new) — abstract complex procedure built from concrete procedures for a sentence's parts (p. 99)
- [[speaker-reference]] (new) — Chapter 2's notion of speaker-intended referent as against semantic reference; Lycan extends it to speaker-extension (p. 100)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Group-level meaning as a repertoire of procedures kept up by expecting others to keep them ↔ philosophy of language: convention (Lewis; the text mentions Lewis only in n. 9) — both rest meaning on mutual reliance within a community.
- [structural-analogy] Stage 1 (sentence meaning from use) ↔ Wittgenstein's meaning as use (the text cites Wittgenstein for the plausibility of stage 1).
- [structural-analogy] Abstract resultant procedures built from parts ↔ compositionality and syntax (Chapter 9) — meaning of a whole fixed by meanings of parts and structure.
- [metaphor] Speaker-meaning "comes cheap" without constraints ↔ underdetermination in translation: behaviour alone fixes little — only a loose resemblance.

## Questions this raises
- Can "normally" in "what speakers normally mean" be defined without using the sentence's standard meaning?
- If sentence meaning constrains speaker-meaning (p. 97), is the reduction circular, or only explanatorily incomplete?
- Can speaker-reference ground the denotation of names better than description or causal-historical theories?
- Does Blackburn's regard-licence idea (p. 98) avoid the obstacles better than Grice's?
