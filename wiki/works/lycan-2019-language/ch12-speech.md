# Speech Acts and Illocutionary Force

## L0 — One line
Saying something is often doing something (promising, warning, stating), so an utterance has an illocutionary force as well as a content, and it seems that theories that count only truth-conditional content leave out part of meaning.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Austin noticed "performative" utterances (such as *I apologize*), which perform a social act rather than describe anything. This challenges verificationism and the Truth-Condition Theory. Austin's own sharp contrast between performatives and constatives (ordinary fact-stating sentences) then turned into a distinction between force and content, found in nearly every utterance. Speech acts follow constitutive and regulative rules, and they can go wrong in many ways besides falsity. Explicit performatives such as *I state that…* raise Cohen's unsolved problem about their truth conditions. Alston and Barker tried to build a "use" theory of meaning on illocutionary acts.

**Prerequisites:** truth conditions and T-sentences; verificationism; Wittgenstein's "use" idea; Grice's speaker-meaning (the text treats these as already met in earlier chapters).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Performative utterances (*I promise…*, *I double*) are declarative in form but do something instead of describing; T-sentences for them seem to leave out what matters (pp. 151–152).
- Austin's "hereby" test separates performatives from constatives, but very simple cases (*Hooray!*) cannot pass it, and explicit statings like (8) pass it while also being fact-stating (pp. 152–153).
- The performative/constative contrast becomes a distinction between illocutionary force and locutionary content within one utterance; perlocutionary effects are a third feature (pp. 153–154).
- Lycan believes force is a real kind of meaning, and that denying it is meaning is a verbal scuffle (p. 154).
- Rules are of two kinds: constitutive rules, which must be obeyed if the act is to be accomplished at all, and regulative rules, whose violation makes the act defective (infelicitous) (pp. 150, 155).
- Infelicity has many forms. Falsity is only one, and the "true-false fetish" mistakes other defects for falsity (p. 156).
- Cohen's problem: the "tempting" view of explicit performatives' truth conditions cannot be sustained, and the "liberal" view has bad consequences. A two-truth-value proposal is attractive, but no proposed way out is entirely satisfactory (pp. 156–158, 161).
- Alston's illocutionary act-potential theory faces objections. Barker's Gricean version in effect answers them (pp. 158–159).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Overview (p. 150)
Austin's performatives perform a conventional social act without stating or describing anything. Speech acts are governed by constitutive and regulative rules. Austin came to see no principled line between performatives and ordinary declaratives. Cohen raised a truth-condition problem for explicit performative prefaces. Alston and Barker offered illocutionary "use" theories of meaning (p. 150).

### Performatives (pp. 150–152)
- Lycan lists examples (1)–(7): *I promise to pay you for the diapers*; *I pronounce you husband and wife*; *I christen this ship the Ludwig Wittgenstein*; *I apologize*; *I double* (bridge); *Raise you five* (poker); *Nay* (a vote). Except possibly the last two, they are declaratives, so the verificationist must address them and give verification conditions (pp. 150–151).
- T-sentences are tried. The one for (1) (true iff I promise to pay you) is rejected: "No, not really". For *I double*, "true iff I double" is possibly acceptable on the occasion, but it seems to leave out something more important than this slightly degenerate truth condition. As Austin might have put it, in saying *I double* I am not describing myself as doubling; I am actually doing it, and nothing more. No one could acceptably rejoin "That's false, you don't double." The later report *He doubled* is true. When originally said as a move in the game, it does not seem like a candidate for truth or falsity. The T-sentence for *Nay* is not even grammatical (p. 151).
- This gives a further objection to verificationism and the Truth-Condition Theory (a Wittgensteinian objection combined with the first objection to the Truth-Condition Theory). A Wittgensteinian might assimilate (4), (5), (7) to the builders' *Slab!* language and to *Hello*. Even for (1)–(3) and (6), it seems that, although declarative in mood, none is in the business of stating a fact (p. 151).
- In his 1961 article Austin called sentences like (1)–(7) **performatives** and contrasted them with **constatives**: the usual descriptive, fact-stating, true-or-false sentences. In uttering a performative one is not, or at least not ostensibly, describing anything. Uttering (1) is making the promise; uttering (3) in the proper context is christening. Austin called such social actions speech acts and so gave birth to speech-act theory (pp. 151–152).
- Lycan says that, whatever the outcome for a theory of meaning, we must study "doing things with words" (Austin's title phrase) or leave out an important range of linguistic phenomena. He adds two further reasons: speech-act theory is the most effective cure for the tendency to think only declaratives matter, and ignorance of it has caused mistakes in other areas of philosophy (p. 152).

*Intuition note:* *I double* is a move in a real language-game (bridge). When said as a move, it does not seem to be a candidate for truth or falsity. This is the point of the Wittgensteinian objection (p. 151).

### Illocution, Locution, and Perlocution (pp. 152–154)
- **"Hereby" criterion** (Austin 1961): an utterance is counted as performative if one could fairly put *hereby* before the main verb. The word emphasizes that the act is constituted by the speaker's very utterance. It works well for (1)–(6), though *I hereby double* would be stilted (p. 152).
- It marks off performatives from constatives: *The cat is hereby on the mat* is nonsensical or at least false, because saying so does nothing to make it so (p. 152).
- Some clear nonconstatives are too simple to pass. *Hereby nay* is ungrammatical, but *Nay* is plausibly a laconic form of *I vote nay*, which does meet the condition. *Hooray!*, *Shame!*, *Damn!* are harder to hear that way. Lewis (1970) proposed to understand *Hurrah for Porky* as *I cheer Porky*. Perhaps *Shame!* means *I hereby castigate you*. Lycan says these hypotheses are not obviously correct (p. 152).
- Sentence (8), *I state that I have never traveled to a Communist country*, passes the test, so it should be counted as performative (an act of stating). But it is also indelibly fact-stating: the speaker's statement is true or false. If it is uttered under oath and the speaker has traveled to a Communist country, the speaker can be indicted for perjury. So (8) seems to be either both performative and constative, or neither. Further cases: (9) judge, (10) report, (11) advise, (12) warn. Even (1) has a paraphrase, *I promise that I will pay you*, which at least asserts that I will pay (p. 153).
- Such examples made Austin realize that a single utterance can have both a performative and a constative aspect. Virtually every utterance does, even without an explicit preface. Testifying merely *I have never traveled…* still performs an act of stating, in addition to expressing the propositional content. Austin called the performative feature **illocutionary force** and contrasted it with **locutionary** (propositional) content (p. 153).
- The same declarative can have different forces in different contexts: the Rottweiler sentence could be a warning, a threat, a mere observation, or a soothing reassurance. Children gibe at a complaint with "Is that a threat or a promise?" (p. 153).
- Nondeclaratives have more obviously distinctive forces; the point of the interrogative and imperative moods seems to be to indicate ranges of force. (13) can be paraphrased as *I (hereby) ask you whether…*. (14), *Go to the Music Library…*, could have the force of a command, order, request or suggestion, depending on intentions, purposes and power relations (pp. 153–154).
- So Austin's original distinction turned into a distinction between force and content as aspects of a single utterance. Austin (1962) elaborated a massive catalogue of illocutionary forces (admitting, announcing, thanking, vowing…) (p. 154).
- **Perlocutionary effect** is a third feature. *Frighten* and *convince* are like performative verbs but fail the "hereby" test, because they describe the act by its actual effects on the hearer, which are not guaranteed or constituted by the utterance itself. Austin calls these perlocutionary acts; other examples are alarming, amusing, persuading, insulting (p. 154).
- Verification and truth-condition theories identify a sentence's meaning with its locutionary content alone. But if you do not understand distinctions of force, an important aspect of language has not been mastered, so it seems these theorists have left something out (p. 154).
- **Lycan's view:** the possible reply "force is pragmatic, not meaning" is, he believes, just a schoolyard scuffle over the word *meaning*. There are already kinds of meaning besides locutionary meaning (speaker-meaning, for example). Force is another kind, and each is real and indispensable to language use (p. 154).

*Intuition note:* "Content" is what is said about the world. "Force" is what you are doing with it. "Perlocution" is what happens in the hearer. Only the perlocutionary effect is explicitly said to be not guaranteed by the utterance itself; force varies with context and intentions (pp. 153–154).

### Infelicities and Constitutive Rules (pp. 155–156)
- Speech acts are conventional, governed by usually unwritten rules. Searle divides them into **regulative rules**, which regulate antecedently existing behaviour (etiquette: *Never chew with your mouth open*), and **constitutive rules**, which "create or define new forms of behavior" (chess: *Bishops move only diagonally*) (p. 155).
- Lycan adds a more demanding notion: a **strongly constitutive rule** is one whose violation aborts the purported act. If I violate one while trying to perform act A, I have simply failed to perform A (p. 155).
- Examples: breaking a bottle on the USS North Carolina while uttering (3) does not christen her, since I lack standing, and she was already christened. An unlicensed officiant, or an underage partner, means the wedding does not succeed. Raising someone five requires that I am playing poker and that five is within the betting limit (p. 155).
- If I violate only a regulative rule (no constitutive rule), the act is performed but defectively, "infelicitously". A wedding that succeeds but with a couple lying in their vows is defective. Uttering (1) insincerely is an infelicitous promise. Shouting (1) across a room and not being heard is an infelicity of a different sort (p. 155).
- Borderline case: (4) said in a flauntedly unrepentant, sneering tone. Is that a grievously infelicitous apology, or no apology at all? (p. 155).
- Austin emphasized the multifariousness of infelicity: an ill-advised move, insincerity, lack of authority, rudeness, going unheard, the wrong audience, pomposity, a false presupposition (e.g. apologizing for something I did not do) (p. 156).
- For acts of stating, falsity is one common defect: a regulative rule says that what is said should be true (p. 156).
- Austin complains of the true-false fetish, the misguided idea that truth-value is all that matters in speech. We often mistake other infelicities for falsity and assume a defective sentence is not true. Falsity is one form of infelicity among many (p. 156).

*Intuition note:* A strongly constitutive rule decides whether the act exists at all; a regulative rule decides whether an act that was performed is defective. Lycan says there are borderline cases between the two (p. 155).

### Cohen's Problem (pp. 156–158)
- **The problem:** Cohen (1964) asked about the truth conditions of sentences like (8)–(12). What is (8)'s truth condition? (p. 156).
- **Tempting view:** read the truth condition out from under the performative preface. What an utterer of (8) states is that he has never traveled, not that he is stating something. *I did state it* would be no defence against perjury. (9)–(12) surely are not automatically true because I did judge, report, advise, warn. (Lewis 1970 took exactly that bold position, Lycan remarks.) The "I state that" is just a preface that makes the force explicit. A further argument: explicit performatives seem to be verbose equivalents of simple statements, warnings or orders (pp. 156–157).
- **Cohen's objection** (p. 157):
  - Suppose Lucy overhears Eleanor utter (12) and says *Eleanor has warned Franklin that…*. She refers to the same individuals and predicates the same relation; only the tense changes. Surely *warn* in (12) means warn.
  - So the preface is not just a tag or flag for force. Grammatical mood is basically such a flag, but *I warn you that* has internal structure, and its parts have their own meanings and referents.
- **It gets worse:** prefaces can contain long adverbial modifiers. (15a) *I admit freely…*; (15b) *with reluctance* (modifying *admit*, not *had several…*); (15c) *gladly and with the greatest pleasure*; (15d) *Because I am concerned to tell the whole truth, I admit…*; (15e) a theological clause. On the tempting view the only locutionary content would be the complement clause, but that grows less plausible down the list. (15d)'s preface contains a clause the speaker asserts in passing. (15e)'s contains several controversial assertions, and the theology seems to be part of what is said (p. 157).
- It seems the tempting view cannot be sustained. What then becomes tempting is the **liberal** view: the locutionary contents include the prefaces (p. 157).
- **Why the liberal view is bad:** (8)–(12) would be automatically true whenever uttered with no relevant constitutive rule violated. No perjury charge would stick if a witness testified only in explicit performatives. Semantically, (8)–(12) would not even entail their complement clauses (p. 157).
- **Two-truth-value hypothesis** (Cresswell 1973; Bach and Harnish 1979, who joined the liberal view): besides the main act, the utterer also describes himself as performing it. In (16), *I order you to attack and capture the University of Chicago*, the main act (ordering) involves no truth-value, but the self-description is true in a degenerate sense. For (8)–(12), whose main acts are truth-liable, there would be a primary content (that I have never traveled) and a self-descriptive content that is nearly always automatically true (that I do so state). Lycan finds the hypothesis attractive, because (15a–e) suggest neither the tempting nor the liberal truth-value is expendable (p. 158).
- To make it more palatable, the two truth-values can be said to attach to different things: the statement made (false if I have traveled) and the sentence uttered (true, taken liberally). A perjury charge would be for a false statement, not a false sentence (p. 158).
- (15d) and (15e) would need elaboration, since the utterer of (15e) seems to have made two or three further assertions. The earlier examples are borderline: would the utterer of (15a) assert that the admission was given freely? Lycan says a full theory of speech acts would have to sort out such subtleties at length (p. 158).
- The Summary says Cohen's problem has not been solved, and Further Reading says none of the proposed ways out is entirely satisfactory (pp. 159, 161).

*Intuition note:* *I state that p* seems to carry two things: that p, and that I am stating p. Giving each its own truth-value would let us say the statement is false while the sentence is, in a degenerate sense, true (p. 158).

### Illocutionary Theories of Meaning (pp. 158–159)
- **Alston (1963):** tried to work Austin's pragmatics into a theory of locutionary meaning itself. A sentence's meaning is its illocutionary act-potential, the range of illocutionary acts it could be used to perform. If you can use it in every illocutionary way it affords, you know its meaning, and that is all there is to sentence meaning. This would qualify as a "use" theory, though superficially far from Wittgenstein's (p. 158).
- Objection 1: potential-act descriptions such as *assert that gorillas are vegetarians* already presuppose propositional content and exploit the meanings of their complement clauses, so the view did nothing to illuminate locutionary meaning (pp. 158–159).
- Objection 2 (Maureen Coyle): *Mother will eat the oyster*, *Will mother eat the oyster?* and *Mother, eat the oyster!* share locutionary content but differ violently in illocutionary act-potential (p. 159).
- **Barker (2004)** in effect heads off both objections. Against the first, he understands illocutionary acts Gricean-style, through speakers' intentions and beliefs. Oversimplified: to assert that P is to utter a sentence intending to get the hearer to believe that P. The contents come from the mental attitudes that partly constitute the acts, not from complement-clause meanings. Against the second, he partly traces the commonalities to shared underlying propositional attitudes, and partly argues on sophisticated syntactic grounds that locutionary content cannot be separated from illocutionary force in the first place (p. 159).
- Lycan notes that this last point is also a further objection to the standard truth-conditional theory, which assumes content is determined independently of force (p. 159).

## Toy example (ours)
Take the sentence *I warn you that the ice is thin.*
- **Content:** that the ice is thin. **Force:** warning. **Perlocutionary effect, if any:** the hearer is frightened.
- Case A: said to a skater, ice thin, speaker sincere. The act succeeds and is felicitous, and the content is true.
- Case B: ice thick, speaker honestly mistaken. The warning is performed but its content is false: falsity is one defect.
- Case C: the speaker says it only to tease and does not mean it. The act is performed but insincere (a regulative-rule violation in the chapter's terms), so it is infelicitous.
- Case D: said by an actor on stage. Our own reading, not the chapter's: no real warning is performed, since a condition for the act is missing.
- On the two-truth-value view, in Case B the primary content (the ice is thin) is false, while the self-descriptive content (that I am warning) is true in the degenerate sense.
- The perlocutionary effect (fear) is separate: the hearer may ignore the warning, and it is still a warning.

## Concepts
- [[speech act]] — an utterance considered as a conventional social act, e.g. promising or christening (p. 152)
- [[performative utterance]] — an utterance that performs an act instead of (ostensibly) describing; Austin's "hereby" test marks it (pp. 151–152)
- constative (new) — an ordinary descriptive, true-or-false sentence, contrasted with a performative (p. 151)
- hereby criterion (new) — an utterance is counted as performative if "hereby" can fairly be put before its main verb (p. 152)
- [[locutionary / illocutionary / perlocutionary act]] — content / the act done in saying / the effect on the hearer (pp. 153–154)
- constitutive vs. regulative rules (new) — rules that create or define a form of behaviour vs. rules that regulate pre-existing behaviour (p. 155)
- strongly constitutive rule (new) — Lycan's term: a rule whose violation means the act is not performed (p. 155)
- [[felicity conditions]] — closest concept: Austin's "infelicity" is the umbrella for the many ways an act can go wrong; falsity is one (pp. 155–156; n. 5, p. 160)
- true-false fetish (new) — the mistaken idea that truth-value is all that matters in speech (p. 156)
- Cohen's problem (new) — what the truth conditions of explicit performatives like (8)–(12) are (p. 156)
- two-truth-value hypothesis (new) — the utterance has a primary content and a self-descriptive content (p. 158)
- illocutionary act-potential (new) — Alston's term: the range of illocutionary acts a sentence can perform; he identifies this with meaning (p. 158)
- [[meaning as use]] — here realised by Alston's and Barker's illocutionary theories (pp. 158–159)

## Canonical instances
- *I double* — a move in bridge; when said as a move it does not seem a candidate for truth or falsity (p. 151)
- Sentence (8), *I state that I have never traveled…* — seems to be both performative and fact-stating, or neither; the perjury case (p. 153)
- Rottweiler utterance — one declarative with the possible forces of warning, threat, observation, reassurance (p. 153)
- Christening the USS North Carolina — a constitutive-rule violation (p. 155)
- Chicago wedding with an unlicensed officiant — a failed act (p. 155)
- (15a–e) admissions with growing prefaces — the argument against the "force label" view (p. 157)
- Mother and the oyster — same content, different act-potentials (p. 159)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Constitutive rules of speech acts ↔ games: the rules of chess or football define the game, as Lycan says (p. 155).
- [structural-analogy] Locutionary content ↔ Frege's sense and reference: Austin only gestured toward them (p. 160, n. 1).
- [metaphor] "Hereby" performatives ↔ commits and ledger entries in a database: the utterance itself creates the new state.
- [structural-analogy] Barker's intention-based acts ↔ Grice's speaker-meaning: the act is characterised by intended hearer attitudes (p. 159).

## Questions this raises
- Are all speech acts as purely conventional as *I double*? (Note 2, p. 160, says some force is more a matter of speaker intentions.)
- Where should the line between strongly constitutive and merely regulative rules fall, as in the jeering apology (4)?
- Could the two-truth-value view handle (15e), where the preface itself asserts theology?
- If force and content cannot be separated (Barker), what remains of the truth-conditional programme?
