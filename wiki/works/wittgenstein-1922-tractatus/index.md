# Wittgenstein, L. (1922). *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (trans. C. K. Ogden with F. P. Ramsey; intro. B. Russell). London: Kegan Paul. First published in German as "Logisch-philosophische Abhandlung", *Annalen der Naturphilosophie*, 1921.

## In one paragraph
The *Tractatus* argues that the world is the totality of facts, built from simple objects. A meaningful proposition is a logical picture of a possible state of affairs. It can picture a fact because it shares the fact's logical form. Every proposition is a truth-function of elementary propositions, and in the end all of them can be generated by one operation, joint negation. The propositions of logic are tautologies and say nothing. The form that pictures share with reality cannot be said, only shown. The same holds for ethics, the sense of the world and the mystical. Philosophy is therefore not a theory but an activity of clarification. Wittgenstein's own propositions are a ladder to be thrown away, and "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent". The book fixes the limits of language and the task of philosophy.

## The path through the work
- [[wittgenstein-1922-tractatus/ch00-introduction]] — Russell presents Wittgenstein's view that a proposition pictures a fact by sharing its logical structure, a structure that can be shown but not said, and he hesitates because Wittgenstein himself says much about what cannot be said.
- [[wittgenstein-1922-tractatus/ch01-propositions]] — The world is the totality of facts, what is the case is the existence of atomic facts (combinations of simple objects), and a picture is a fact that must share a form of representation with what it pictures.
- [[wittgenstein-1922-tractatus/ch03-proposition]] — A thought is a logical picture of facts, and a proposition is a sign that is itself a fact: its sense is expressed by the way its elements are combined, not by a mere list of words.
- [[wittgenstein-1922-tractatus/ch04-proposition]] — A proposition is a logical picture of a possible state of affairs, fixed by its truth-conditions. Logical form shows itself in propositions and cannot be said, and philosophy is an activity of clarifying, not a theory.
- [[wittgenstein-1922-tractatus/ch05-proposition]] — Every proposition is the result of repeated truth-operations (in the end, the single operation N, the negation of all values of a variable) on elementary propositions, so there are no "logical objects", and the limits of my language mean the limits of my world.
- [[wittgenstein-1922-tractatus/ch06-propositions]] — Every proposition is built by repeated application of one operation to elementary propositions; logic, mathematics and ethics are not further propositions about the world, and what cannot be said shows itself, and of it one must be silent.

## Big ideas across units
- **Propositions as pictures.** A picture is a fact that shares a form with what it pictures. The idea runs from Russell's summary through the ontology of Props. 1–2, the thought as logical picture in Prop. 3 and the proposition as a model of reality in Prop. 4, and it is still assumed at the end. Units: ch00, ch01, ch03, ch04, ch06.
- **Saying versus showing.** Logical form, a picture's form of representation, and the formal properties of language and world cannot be said and show themselves. The same holds for the solipsist's insight, for ethics and for the mystical. Units: ch00, ch01, ch04, ch05, ch06.
- **A world of facts built from simple objects.** The world is facts, not things. Atomic facts are independent of each other, and simple objects form the fixed substance that makes picturing possible. Names stand for objects, and only in a proposition does a name have meaning. Units: ch00, ch01, ch03, ch04.
- **Everything is a truth-function of elementary propositions.** Complex propositions are built from elementary ones, which are concatenations of names. In the end one operation, joint negation N, generates them all, and this gives the general form of proposition. Units: ch00, ch04, ch05, ch06.
- **Logic says nothing.** Tautology and contradiction are limiting cases without sense. Logical propositions show formal properties, so inference needs no laws and logic has no surprises. Units: ch00, ch04, ch05, ch06.
- **Pseudo-concepts and pseudo-propositions.** "Object", "number" and the identity sign are not real concepts or relations, and mathematical equations are pseudo-propositions. Units: ch00, ch04, ch05, ch06.
- **Philosophy as clarification, not theory.** Philosophical "propositions" are senseless rather than false, because they misunderstand the logic of language. Logical syntax and the theory of types are meant to prevent this, and the book ends by calling its own propositions a ladder to discard. Units: ch00, ch03, ch04, ch06.
- **The limits of language and world.** Nothing can be said about the world as a whole. The limits of my language mean the limits of my world, the subject is a limit of the world, and value lies outside the world. Units: ch00, ch05, ch06.

## How to read it
- **Essential first:** ch01 (the ontology and the picture idea). ch04 (what a proposition is, and saying versus showing) and ch06 (the conclusions about logic, ethics and the ladder) follow from it.
- **Orientation:** ch00 is Russell's summary and covers the whole argument in advance. It is a good way in, but it is also Russell's reading, and it ends with his doubts about Wittgenstein's position.
- **Dependencies:**
  - ch03 builds on the picture idea of ch01 (2–2.18).
  - ch04 builds on the ideas of objects, atomic facts and logical space from ch01.
  - ch05 assumes elementary propositions as concatenations of names from ch04.
  - ch06 assumes elementary propositions, truth-functions and the operation N from ch04 and ch05.
- **Can be skimmed first time:** the technical parts, namely the count of truth-possibilities in ch04, the notation for operation series and logical probability in ch05, and the theory of types and substitution details in ch03. The arguments of ch05 and ch06 can be followed without working through these. Most units want basic propositional logic (truth tables); ch01 needs none.
- **Reading advice from the catalog:** read the seven main numbered propositions and their first-level comments, and follow the numbering down only where needed.
