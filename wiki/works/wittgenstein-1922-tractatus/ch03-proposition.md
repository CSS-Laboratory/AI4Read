# Proposition 3: the thought as logical picture; the proposition

## L0 — One line
A thought is a logical picture of facts, and a proposition is a sign that is itself a fact: its sense is expressed by the way its elements are combined, not by a mere list of words.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks how thought and language can represent the world. Wittgenstein answers that a thought is a logical picture of facts. A proposition is a perceptible sign, itself a fact, projected onto a possible state of affairs. Simple signs (names) stand for objects, but only whole propositions have sense. Fundamental confusions, of which philosophy is full, easily arise when one sign works in different ways, so we need a notation that obeys logical syntax (logical grammar). In this syntax the meaning of a sign ought never to play a role. He uses this to argue that no proposition can speak about itself, and that Russell's paradox then vanishes.

**Prerequisites:** the picture idea from 2–2.18 (a picture shares logical form with what it pictures); a state of affairs (a configuration of objects, 3.21); functions and variables; Russell's paradox (a class or function applied to itself).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A picture is true or false by agreeing with reality. This cannot be seen from the picture alone, so no picture is a priori true (a priori true: its possibility alone would guarantee its truth, 3.04) (2.224–2.225, 3.04, pp. 30–31).
- The thought is the logical picture of facts. What is thinkable is possible, and we cannot think anything unlogical (3, 3.02, 3.03, pp. 30–31).
- The propositional sign (the perceptible sign that expresses a thought) is a fact, not a mixture of words. Its arrangement expresses the sense (3.14, 3.1431, p. 32).
- Names mean objects. Objects can only be named, and a proposition says how things are, not what they are (3.203, 3.221, pp. 32–33).
- Complexes are given by descriptions, and there is exactly one complete analysis of a proposition (3.24–3.25, p. 33).
- Only in a proposition does a name have meaning. An expression is presented by a propositional variable (a variable whose values are propositions), and the values are fixed by describing those propositions (3.3–3.317, pp. 34–35).
- Signs are arbitrary. The sign is the perceptible part of the symbol, and to recognize the symbol in the sign we must consider its significant use. Logical syntax (the rules of logical grammar) ought not to mention the meaning of signs (3.32–3.33, pp. 35–36).
- No proposition or function can be its own argument, which is "the whole theory of types". Russell's paradox then vanishes (3.332–3.333, pp. 36–37).
- A particular method of symbolizing may be unimportant, but it is always important that it is a possible method, and the possibility of every single thing reveals something about the nature of the world. Every correct symbolism must be translatable into every other (3.3421–3.343, p. 37).

## L3 — Walkthrough
Voices: claims are the author's, except views he mentions. One is the old saying that God could create everything except what is contrary to logic, which he corrects (3.031). Another is Frege's calling the proposition a compounded name, which he says ordinary printing made possible (3.143). He also comments on the symbolism of Frege and Russell and on Russell's theory of types (3.318, 3.325, 3.331). The unit uses decimal numbering, so I cite (number, p. page).

**2.181–2.225 (end of the picture section).** If the form of representation is logical form, the picture is a logical picture. Every picture is also a logical picture, but not every picture is spatial (2.181–2.182, p. 30). The logical picture can depict the world (2.19, p. 30). A picture has logical form in common with what it pictures. It represents a possibility of existence and non-existence of atomic facts (2.2–2.201, p. 30). It represents a possible state of affairs in logical space and contains that possibility (2.202–2.203, p. 30). It represents what it represents through its form, independently of truth (2.22, p. 30). What it represents is its sense. Truth or falsity consists in agreement or disagreement of sense with reality (2.221–2.222, p. 30). To find out whether it is true we must compare it with reality, and the picture alone cannot tell us (2.223–2.224, p. 30). So no picture is a priori true (2.225, p. 30).

*Intuition note:* A picture shows how things would be if it were true. Whether they are so must be checked outside the picture.

**3–3.05 The thought.** The thought is the logical picture of facts. An atomic fact is thinkable means that we can imagine it (3, 3.001, p. 30). The totality of true thoughts is a picture of the world (3.01, p. 30). A thought contains the possibility of the state of affairs it thinks, so what is thinkable is also possible (3.02, p. 30). We cannot think anything unlogical, since we would have to think unlogically (3.03, p. 31). Of an unlogical world we could not say how it would look (3.031, p. 31). Language contradicting logic is as impossible as giving coordinates of a figure contradicting the laws of space (3.032, p. 31). We could present spatially a fact that contradicts physics, but not one that contradicts geometry (3.0321, p. 31). An a priori true thought would be one whose possibility guaranteed its truth. We could know a priori that a thought is true only if its truth could be recognized from the thought itself, without an object of comparison (3.04–3.05, p. 31).

**3.1–3.13 Proposition and propositional sign.** The proposition expresses the thought perceptibly. Its sensible sign (sound, written mark) is used as a projection of the possible state of affairs, and the method of projection is thinking the sense (3.1–3.11, p. 31). The propositional sign is the sign expressing the thought. The proposition is the propositional sign in its projective relation to the world (3.12, p. 31). The proposition contains what belongs to the projection, but not what is projected. So its sense is not yet contained, only the possibility of expressing it. It contains the form of its sense, not its content (3.13, p. 31).

**3.14–3.144 The sign is a fact.** The propositional sign consists in its elements, the words, being combined in a definite way. The propositional sign is a fact (3.14, p. 32). The proposition is not a mixture of words, just as a musical theme is not a mixture of tones. It is articulate (3.141, p. 32). Only facts can express a sense, and a class of names cannot (3.142, p. 32). Ordinary printing hides this, which is why Frege could call the proposition a compounded name (3.143, p. 32). Imagine the sign made of tables, chairs and books: their mutual spatial position then expresses the sense (3.1431, p. 32). So we must not say that "aRb" says "a stands in relation R to b". We must say that "a" standing in a certain relation to "b" says that aRb (3.1432, p. 32). States of affairs can be described but not named. Names resemble points; propositions resemble arrows, and they have sense (3.144, p. 32).

*Intuition note:* A sentence works by its parts standing in a certain arrangement, not by a list of words. The arrangement is what expresses the sense.

**3.2–3.263 Names, analysis, elucidation.** Elements of the propositional sign correspond to the objects of the thought. These elements are simple signs, and the proposition is then completely analysed (3.2–3.201, p. 32). Simple signs are names. A name means the object, and the object is its meaning (3.202–3.203, p. 32). The configuration of simple signs corresponds to the configuration of objects in the state of affairs (3.21, p. 32). Objects can only be named, not asserted. A proposition says how a thing is, not what it is (3.22–3.221, p. 33). The postulate of simple signs is the postulate of determinate sense (3.23, p. 33). A complex is given only by a description, which is right or wrong. If the complex does not exist, the proposition about it becomes simply false, not nonsense. That an element signifies a complex can be seen from an indeterminateness in the propositions containing it (3.24, p. 33). The proposition is articulate (3.251, p. 33). There is one and only one complete analysis of a proposition (3.25, p. 33). A name cannot be analysed further by definition, since it is a primitive sign (3.26, p. 33). A defined sign signifies via the signs that define it, and a primitive sign and a defined sign cannot signify in the same way (3.261, p. 33). What the sign does not express is shown by its application (3.262, p. 33). Primitive signs are explained by elucidations, which are propositions containing them. They can be understood only if the meanings of these signs are already known (3.263, p. 33).

**3.3–3.318 Expressions and variables.** Only the proposition has sense, and only in the context of a proposition does a name have meaning (3.3, p. 34). An expression (symbol) is any part of a proposition that characterizes its sense. Expressions are everything essential for the sense of a proposition that propositions can have in common with one another (3.31, p. 34). It presupposes the forms of all propositions in which it can occur (3.311, p. 34). It is represented by the general form of those propositions, in which it is constant and everything else variable (3.312, p. 34). So an expression is presented by a variable whose values are the propositions containing it. This is the propositional variable (3.313, p. 34). An expression has meaning only in a proposition, and every variable, even the variable name, can be conceived as a propositional variable (3.314, p. 34). If we turn into variables all the signs whose meaning was fixed arbitrarily, a class of propositions always remains. It is then no longer dependent on any agreement and depends only on the nature of the proposition and corresponds to a logical form, a logical prototype (3.315, p. 34). The values of the variable are determined by describing the propositions. This describes symbols only and asserts nothing about what is symbolized (3.316–3.317, p. 35). He conceives the proposition, like Frege and Russell, as a function of the expressions it contains (3.318, p. 35).

**3.32–3.328 Sign, symbol, logical syntax.** The sign is the part of the symbol perceptible by the senses. Two different symbols can share a sign and then signify differently (3.32–3.321, p. 35). Symbolizing two objects with the same sign but by different methods of symbolizing can never indicate a common characteristic of the objects, since the sign is arbitrary (3.322, p. 35). Everyday language lets one word signify in different ways. The word "is" appears as the copula, as the sign of equality and as the expression of existence. In "Green is green" the first word is a proper name and the last an adjective, so the two words have not merely different meanings but are different symbols (3.323, p. 35). Thus the most fundamental confusions easily arise, and the whole of philosophy is full of them (3.324, p. 36). To avoid these errors we must employ a symbolism that never applies the same sign in different symbols and never applies in the same way signs that signify differently. Such a symbolism obeys logical grammar, or logical syntax. The logical symbolism of Frege and Russell is such a language, but it does not exclude all errors (3.325, p. 36). To recognize the symbol in the sign we consider the significant use. A sign fixes a logical form only together with its logical-syntactic application (3.326–3.327, p. 36). A sign that is not necessary is meaningless, and this is the meaning of Occam's razor. If everything in the symbolism works as though a sign had meaning, then it has meaning (3.328, p. 36).

**3.33–3.334 Types.** In logical syntax the meaning of a sign ought never to play a role; it must admit of being established without mention of meaning. It ought to presuppose only the description of expressions (3.33, p. 36). Russell's error is that he has to speak of the meaning of signs when drawing up his rules (3.331, p. 36). No proposition can say anything about itself, because the propositional sign cannot contain itself, and this is "the whole theory of types" (3.332, p. 36). Likewise a function cannot be its own argument, because the functional sign already contains the prototype of its own argument and cannot contain itself. Suppose $F(fx)$ could be its own argument, giving $F(F(fx))$. Then the outer and inner $F$ have different meanings, since the inner has the form $\varphi(fx)$ and the outer $\psi(\varphi(fx))$. Only the letter $F$ is common, and it signifies nothing by itself (3.333, pp. 36–37). This is clear if we write

$$(\exists\varphi):F(\varphi u)\,.\,\varphi u = Fu$$

in place of $F(F(u))$. Russell's paradox then vanishes (3.333, p. 37). The rules of logical syntax must follow of themselves if we know how each sign signifies (3.334, p. 37).

*Intuition note:* In $F(F(u))$ the same letter does two different jobs. Written out, the two jobs have different forms; this is how Wittgenstein says Russell's paradox vanishes.

**3.34–3.343 Essential and accidental; translation.** Accidental features come from the particular way of producing the sign. Essential features alone enable the proposition to express its sense (3.34, p. 37). The essential is what all propositions that can express the same sense have in common (3.341, p. 37). One could say that the real name is what all symbols signifying an object have in common. It would then follow, step by step, that no sort of composition is essential for a name (3.3411, p. 37). In notation something is arbitrary, but it is not arbitrary that if we fix something arbitrarily, something else must hold (3.342, p. 37). A particular method of symbolizing may be unimportant, but it is always important that it is a possible method. The possibility of every single thing reveals something about the nature of the world (3.3421, p. 37). Definitions are rules for translating one language into another. Every correct symbolism must be translatable into every other, and this is what all have in common (3.343, p. 37).

## Toy example
*(Ours.)* Let a book stand for Ann and a cup for Ben, with the rule: the object on the left is taller than the object on the right. A book at position 1 and a cup at position 2 then say that Ann is taller than Ben. Swap them and the sign says that Ben is taller than Ann. The sign is the arrangement, a fact, not the list of the two objects (compare 3.1431, 3.142).

Propositional variable: take "Ann loves x", with names Bob, Cy, Di as possible values. If these are the only propositions of this kind we consider, the variable's values are exactly the 3 propositions "Ann loves Bob", "Ann loves Cy", "Ann loves Di". The class is fixed by describing them, and "Ann loves" is the constant part. If we also made "Ann" and "loves" into variables, only the form "a R b" would remain (3.313–3.317).

## Concepts

- [[logical-picture]] (new) — a picture whose form of representation is logical form, and every picture is also one (2.181–2.182, p. 30)
- [[propositional-sign]] (new) — the perceptible sign expressing a thought; it is a fact, not a list of words (3.12, 3.14, pp. 31–32)
- [[name-object-meaning]] (new) — a simple sign (name) means an object, and only in a proposition does a name have meaning (3.203, 3.3, pp. 32, 34)
- [[analysis]] — one and only one complete analysis of a proposition, ending in simple signs (3.201, 3.25, pp. 32–33)
- [[elucidation]] (new) — propositions explaining primitive signs; they are understood only if their meanings are already known (3.263, p. 33)
- [[propositional-variable]] (new) — a variable whose values are the propositions containing a given expression (3.313, p. 34)
- [[logical form vs. grammatical form]] — in everyday language one word may belong to different symbols, so a symbolism obeying logical syntax is needed (3.323–3.325, pp. 35–36)
- [[logical-syntax]] (new) — the rules of logical grammar for a symbolism that does not apply one sign in different symbols; they must be establishable without mention of the meaning of signs (3.325, 3.33, p. 36)
- [[theory-of-types]] (new) — no proposition can say anything about itself, and no function can be its own argument (3.332–3.333, pp. 36–37)

## Questions this raises

- If logical syntax may not mention meaning (3.33), how do we recognize a symbol in a sign (3.326)?
- Is the claim of exactly one complete analysis (3.25) compatible with the translatability of all symbolisms (3.343)?
- How does the remark that what is thinkable is also possible (3.02) relate to the point that we cannot say how an unlogical world would look (3.031)?
