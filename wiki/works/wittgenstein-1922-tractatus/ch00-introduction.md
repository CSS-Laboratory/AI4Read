# Russell's Introduction to the *Tractatus*

## L0 — One line
Russell presents Wittgenstein's view that a proposition pictures a fact by sharing its logical structure, a structure that can be shown but not said, and he hesitates because Wittgenstein himself says much about what cannot be said.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Russell explains the problem of the *Tractatus*: what a logically perfect language (no nonsense, no ambiguity) would require. In Wittgenstein's view a sentence pictures a fact by sharing its logical form, the pattern of its parts. Non-atomic propositions (those that do not assert an atomic fact) are built from atomic ones by one operation, joint negation (*none of these is true*). Hence philosophy gives no correct propositions, only clarification, and logic, ethics and the world as a whole cannot be said, only shown. Russell hesitates, since Wittgenstein says much about the unsayable; he tentatively suggests a hierarchy of languages, and still calls the book one no serious philosopher can neglect.

**Prerequisites:** propositional logic (and, or, not, truth tables); quantifiers ∀, ∃; the idea of a propositional function.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Wittgenstein's concern is the logical question: what must one fact (a sentence) be like to symbolise another? The conditions are sense rather than nonsense, and uniqueness of meaning. (p. 7–8)
- For Wittgenstein, a sentence and the fact it asserts must share structure; he contends that this common structure cannot itself be said, only shown. (p. 8)
- In Wittgenstein's view a proposition is a picture of a fact. Russell reports that from this it results that nothing correct can be said in philosophy: philosophy is an activity of elucidation, not a theory. (p. 9–10)
- Wittgenstein holds that complex facts rest on atomic facts (facts with no facts as parts), whose constituents, fully analysed, are simples (objects). Simples are a logical necessity demanded by theory, not something we can isolate. (p. 10–11)
- Wittgenstein maintains that every proposition is a truth-function (a proposition whose truth depends only on the truth of the propositions it contains) of atomic propositions. Joint negation (*not-p and not-q*) is enough to build all truth-functions, and he extends it to general propositions. (p. 11–13)
- Consequences Russell reports for Wittgenstein: propositions of logic are tautologies (e.g. *p or not p*), there is no causal nexus (necessary link between events), identity is dropped, *object* is a pseudo-concept (to say that something is an object says nothing), and nothing can be said about the world as a whole. (p. 14–16)
- For "A believes p", Russell says what is logically basic is a relation between two facts, reducible to a relation of constituents. (p. 16–17)
- Russell's hesitation: Wittgenstein manages to say much about what cannot be said. Russell tentatively suggests a hierarchy of languages as a possible escape, while admitting objections he cannot answer. (p. 18–19)

## L3 — Walkthrough
The Introduction has no numbered sections. The headings below are ours and follow Russell's order.

### A. Aim and scope (p. 7)
Russell says the book may or may not give "the ultimate truth", but it is an important event. It starts from the principles of Symbolism (the theory of signs and what they stand for). It argues that traditional philosophy arises from ignorance of those principles and from misuse of language. The order is: logical structure of propositions and inference, theory of knowledge, principles of physics, ethics, the Mystical (p. 7).

Russell separates four problems about language (p. 7):
1. What happens in the mind when we mean something. This belongs to psychology.
2. The relation between thoughts, words or sentences and what they mean. This belongs to epistemology.
3. Using sentences to convey truth rather than falsehood. This belongs to the special sciences.
4. What relation must one fact (such as a sentence) have to another to be able to symbolise it? This is a logical question, and it is Wittgenstein's.

In practice, language is always more or less vague, so what we assert is never quite precise. Logic therefore has two tasks: (1) conditions for sense rather than nonsense in combinations of symbols; (2) conditions for uniqueness of meaning (pp. 7–8).

### B. The logically perfect language (p. 8)
- A logically perfect language has syntax that prevents nonsense and single symbols with unique meanings. Russell says the claim is not that any language is logically perfect, nor that we can now build one. Language fulfils its function of meaning only in proportion as it approaches this ideal (p. 8).
- Its essential business is to assert or deny facts. Once the syntax is given, a sentence's meaning is fixed when its words' meanings are known (p. 8).
- **Perhaps the most fundamental thesis of Wittgenstein's theory (Russell's judgement):** sentence and fact must, however the language is constructed, have structure in common. Wittgenstein holds that this cannot be said in language and can only be *shown*. Whatever we said about it would again need the same structure (p. 8).
- A name is a simple symbol, with no parts that are symbols. The ideal language has one name per simple and never one name for two simples. Complexes get complex symbols built from the symbols of their parts. Russell admits that speaking of "a complex" already breaks the rules of philosophical grammar (p. 8).
- Russell reports that most philosophical propositions are "not false but senseless" (they come from not understanding the logic of our language). An example is whether the Good is more or less identical than the Beautiful (4.003) (p. 8).
- A complex in the world is a fact. A *Sachverhalt* is a fact not compounded of other facts. A *Tatsache* may consist of several facts. *Socrates is wise* is both. *Socrates is wise and Plato is his pupil* is a Tatsache only (p. 8).

> *Intuition note.* A sentence can say something about the world only because it has the same shape as the situation. The shape cannot be described from outside, because describing it would use that same shape again. That is why Wittgenstein says it is "shown".

### C. Pictures and the limits of philosophy (pp. 9–10)
- Linguistic expression is compared to geometrical projection. One figure can be projected in many ways, each like a different language. Its projective properties stay the same, and these correspond to what proposition and fact share (p. 9).
- Elementary case: a statement about two men (treated as simples) needs two names. A relation between them needs a relation between the two names. In *Plato loves Socrates*, the word *loves* between the two names establishes a relation between them, and this lets the sentence assert a relation between the men. The text cites 3.1432: we must not say that the complex sign *aRb* says that a stands in a certain relation R to b; we must say that *a* standing in a certain relation to *b* says that aRb (p. 9).
- Theory of Symbolism begins with 2.1, "We make to ourselves pictures of facts." The picture is itself a fact, and its elements correspond to objects. Picture and pictured must share something, the *form of representation* (2.161, 2.17) (p. 9).
- We speak of a *logical picture* when we wish to imply no more than identity of logical form. A logical picture of a fact is a *Gedanke* (thought). A picture can be true or false, but in both cases it shares the logical form with the fact (p. 9).
- 4.014: gramophone record, musical thought, score and sound waves all share logical structure (p. 10).
- Logical constants are not represented by signs. They are present in the proposition as in the fact. The shared "logical manifold" cannot be represented (p. 10).
- **Consequence (Russell reporting Wittgenstein):** everything properly philosophical belongs to what can only be shown. Nothing correct can be said in philosophy. Every philosophical proposition is bad grammar, and the best that philosophical discussion can do is lead people to see that such discussion is a mistake. Philosophy is not one of the natural sciences, and is not a theory but an activity; a philosophical work consists of elucidations. Its aim is the logical clarification of thoughts (4.111–4.112) (p. 10).
- Russell notes the proviso: the statements used to lead the reader to the theory are ones the theory itself condemns as meaningless (p. 10).

> *Intuition note.* What a sentence and a fact have in common cannot itself be represented, since whatever represented it would need the same structure. So, on this view, philosophy cannot add doctrines; its result is to make propositions clear.

### D. Facts, atomic facts and simples (pp. 10–11)
- The world consists of facts. Russell says facts cannot strictly be defined, but they are what make propositions true or false (p. 10).
- An atomic fact (= Sachverhalt) has no facts as parts but does have constituents. "Socrates is wise" contains Socrates and wise. Fully analysed (in theory, not in practice), the constituents are *simples* or *objects* (pp. 10–11).
- Wittgenstein does not claim that we can isolate simples or know them empirically. They are "a logical necessity demanded by theory, like an electron" (p. 11).
- His ground for holding that there must be simples: every complex presupposes a fact. Even infinite complexity would still leave objects and atomic facts (4.2211) (p. 11).
- Naming a complex has meaning only because a proposition about the relatedness of its constituents is true. So naming complexes presupposes propositions, and propositions presuppose naming simples. Naming simples is "what is logically first in logic" (p. 11).
- The world is fully described by all atomic facts together with the fact that these are all. Naming objects is not enough. From that total every true proposition can in theory be inferred (p. 11).
- Atomic propositions are logically independent: none implies or contradicts another. Logical inference thus concerns *molecular* (non-atomic) propositions (p. 11).

### E. Truth-functions and the general form of proposition (pp. 11–14)
**Definition (Russell's gloss).** A truth-function of $p$ (or of $p,q,r,\dots$) is a proposition containing them whose truth or falsehood depends only on theirs (p. 11).

**Apparent exceptions.** "A believes $p$" looks like a non-truth-function, because from A's believing $p$ we cannot infer that $p$ is true. Other apparent exceptions: *$p$ is very complex*, *$p$ is about Socrates*. Wittgenstein holds, for reasons given later, that they are only apparent and that every function of a proposition is really a truth-function. So defining truth-functions in general would define all propositions from atomic ones (pp. 11–12).

**Sheffer's result (as reported by Russell).** All truth-functions of given propositions can be built from either *not-$p$ or not-$q$* or *not-$p$ and not-$q$*. Wittgenstein uses the latter (p. 12). Write $\mathrm{N}(p,q)$ for "not-$p$ and not-$q$" (our notation, added step). Russell's steps:
1. "Not-$p$ and not-$p$" is equivalent to "not-$p$". This defines negation: $\neg p = \mathrm{N}(p,p)$ (added step: notation).
2. "$p$ or $q$" is the negation of "not-$p$ and not-$q$". So $p\lor q=\neg\,\mathrm{N}(p,q)$ (added step: notation) (p. 12).
3. Further truth-functions are developed at the beginning of *Principia Mathematica* (p. 12).

**Generality.** For a propositional function $fx$ (a function whose values are propositions, e.g. "$x$ is human"), apply joint denial to all its values at once. That gives *$fx$ is false for all values of $x$*. Its negation is $(\exists x).fx$. Starting from not-$fx$ instead gives *$fx$ is true for all values of $x$*, written $(x).fx$ (p. 12). Russell says Wittgenstein's method differs from previous methods in that generality enters only in specifying the set of propositions. After that, truth-functions are built exactly as for a finite list $p,q,r,\dots$ (p. 12).

**The symbol** $(\bar p,\bar\xi,N(\bar\xi))$ (p. 13):
- $\bar p$ = all atomic propositions.
- $\bar\xi$ = any set of propositions.
- $N(\bar\xi)$ = the negation of all the propositions making up $\bar\xi$.

The symbol means whatever can be obtained by:
1. taking any selection of atomic propositions and negating them all;
2. taking any selection from the set so obtained, together with any of the originals;
3. continuing indefinitely.

Wittgenstein calls this the general truth-function and the general form of proposition. Russell says it is somewhat less complicated than it sounds: one uniform process makes all non-atomic propositions from atomic ones. It rests on three foundations (p. 13):
- (a) Sheffer's proof about simultaneous negation.
- (b) Wittgenstein's derivation of general propositions from conjunctions and disjunctions.
- (c) The assertion that a proposition occurs in another only as argument of a truth-function.

**Results (Russell's report).** The totality of propositions is defined. Propositions are all that follows from the totality of atomic ones (together with the fact that it is the totality). A proposition is always a truth-function of atomic propositions. If $p$ follows from $q$, the meaning of $p$ is contained in that of $q$, so nothing can be deduced from an atomic proposition. He maintains that all propositions of logic are tautologies, e.g. *$p$ or not $p$* (pp. 13–14).

> *Intuition note.* One operation, *none of these is true* (our gloss on joint denial), applied again and again to the atomic propositions, is meant to yield every other proposition. Russell says this gives an amazing simplification of the theory of inference: if one proposition follows from another, its meaning is contained in the meaning of the other.

### F. Causality, names, identity, "object" (p. 14–15)
- **Causality.** Since nothing can be deduced from an atomic proposition, there cannot, in Wittgenstein's logic, be a causal nexus (a necessary link between events). He says future events cannot be inferred from present ones, and that superstition is belief in the causal nexus. That the sun will rise tomorrow is a hypothesis, because no compulsion makes one thing happen because another does (p. 14).
- **Names.** In Wittgenstein's theoretical logical language only simples get names, one name per thing. According to him, we cannot describe the totality of nameable things. That would require a property every thing has by logical necessity (p. 14).
- **Identity.** It has been sought to find such a property in self-identity, but Wittgenstein subjects identity to a destructive criticism from which Russell sees no escape. He rejects defining identity through the identity of indiscernibles ($x=y$ if every property of $x$ is one of $y$), because that principle appears not to be logically necessary. It is logically possible for two things to share all properties, and if this never happens that is an accident of the world, which logic must not admit. Wittgenstein banishes identity and adopts the convention that different letters mean different things. Identity between a name and a description, or between two descriptions, is easy to provide for in his system. Examples: *Socrates is the philosopher who drank the hemlock* and *The even prime is the next number after 1* (p. 14).
- **"Object" is a pseudo-concept.** Russell thinks Wittgenstein is right that any other method of speaking of the totality of things is equally fallacious, which amounts to saying that *object* is a pseudo-concept. *$x$ is an object* says nothing, so one cannot say *there are more than three objects in the world*. One can say *there are more than three objects which are human*, because the word *object* can then be replaced by a variable satisfying *$x$ is human*. When we try to say *there are more than three objects* with no property given, replacing *object* by a variable becomes impossible and the proposition is seen to be meaningless (pp. 14–15).

### G. The world as a whole; solipsism (pp. 15–16)
- Russell says this is one instance of Wittgenstein's fundamental thesis: it is impossible to say anything about the world as a whole, and whatever can be said is about bounded portions of the world. The view may have been first suggested by notation, which Russell says is much in its favour, though once suggested it has much else to recommend it. Russell does not profess to know whether it is ultimately true: in the Introduction he is concerned to expound it, not to pronounce on it (p. 15).
- According to this view, we could speak of the whole only from outside the world. The analogy is the field of vision. It has no visual boundary for us, because nothing lies outside it. Likewise our logical world has no logical boundary (p. 15).
- Logic, he says, fills the world. So logic cannot say *there is this and this in the world, but not that*, because that would apparently presuppose excluding certain possibilities, which would need a view from beyond the boundary. What we cannot think we cannot think, so we also cannot say what we cannot think (pp. 15–16).
- **Solipsism** (the view that only my mind exists): he says what it intends is quite correct, but this cannot be said, only shown. The world is *my* world because the limits of the one language I understand indicate the limits of my world. The metaphysical subject is a boundary of the world, not part of it (p. 16).

### H. "A believes $p$" (pp. 16–17)
- Wittgenstein (5.54, 5.542): propositions occur in propositions only as bases of truth-operations. *A believes that $p$*, *A thinks $p$*, *A says $p$* have the form *'$p$' says $p$*. This is a co-ordination of facts via co-ordination of their objects, not of a fact with an object (p. 16).
- Russell says the opposed theory is his own 1906–7 articles. The problem is the logical form of belief, and it covers all *propositional attitudes* (doubting, desiring, etc.) (p. 16).
- **Russell's analysis (his own voice).** "A doubts $p$" suggests a relation between a person and a proposition. That cannot be ultimate, since persons are fictions, and so are propositions except as facts in their own right. As such a fact, a proposition may be spoken words, an image or incipient movements. What matters to logic is the element common to all these. Psychology also studies intention and association, which the logician sets aside (pp. 16–17).
- Belief then reduces to a relation between two facts: the believer's words and the fact making them true or false. This relation is analysable, because the meaning of the sentence is a function of its words' meanings. Russell says it would perhaps help to say that the proposition occurs "as a fact, not as a proposition", though this must not be taken too literally. So it does not occur as in a truth-function (p. 17).

### I. Further topics and Russell's criticisms (pp. 17–19)
- **Number.** Russell thinks Wittgenstein's theory stands in need of greater technical development; his theory of number (6.02), as it stands, can deal only with finite numbers. No logic is adequate until it handles transfinite numbers. Russell sees nothing that prevents Wittgenstein from filling the gap (pp. 17–18).
- **The mystical.** Russell says Wittgenstein's attitude grows out of his doctrine that a logical proposition is a picture of the fact, with a structure in common. This structure cannot be put into words, since it is a structure of the words as well as of the facts. So everything involved in the expressiveness of language is inexpressible in a perfectly precise sense; according to Wittgenstein this inexpressible contains the whole of logic and philosophy (p. 18).
- **Method.** Wittgenstein's ideal teaching confines itself to scientific propositions, and proves to the learner that any philosophical assertion is meaningless (p. 18).
- **Russell's hesitation (his voice).** What causes Russell's hesitation is that Wittgenstein manages to say a good deal about what cannot be said, which suggests to the sceptical reader that possibly there is a loophole through a hierarchy of languages or some other exit. Ethics is placed in the inexpressible, yet he conveys ethical opinions. His defence is that the mystical can be shown. Russell says it may be that this defence is adequate, but for his part it leaves him with a certain sense of intellectual discomfort (p. 18).
- **Generality.** Russell calls this a purely logical problem in which the difficulties are peculiarly acute. The theory of generality considers all propositions of the form $fx$, and this belongs to the part of logic that can be expressed. But the totality of values of $x$ is the totality of things in the world, which involves conceiving the world as a whole; for Wittgenstein the feeling of the world as a bounded whole is the mystical, so the totality of values of $x$ is mystical (6.45). Russell says this is expressly argued when Wittgenstein denies that we can make propositions about how many things there are in the world, for example that there are more than three (pp. 18–19).
- **Hierarchy of languages (Russell's suggestion).** These difficulties suggest to Russell the possibility that every language has, as Wittgenstein says, a structure about which nothing can be said in it, but that another language may deal with that structure, having a new structure itself, and that this hierarchy may have no limit (p. 19). **Wittgenstein would reply** that his theory applies unchanged to the totality of such languages. Russell says the only retort would be to deny that any such totality exists; the totality would then be a fiction, a mere delusion, and the supposed sphere of the mystical would be abolished. He admits the hypothesis is very difficult and that he can see objections he does not know how to answer, yet does not see how any easier hypothesis can escape Wittgenstein's conclusions. Even if it proved tenable, it would leave untouched a very large part of the theory, though possibly not the part Wittgenstein would stress most (p. 19).
- **Verdict.** Russell says he is unable to be sure of a theory's rightness merely because he cannot see any point on which it is wrong. Still, to have built a theory of logic that is not at any point obviously wrong is, he says, a work of extraordinary difficulty and importance. In his opinion this merit belongs to the book, which no serious philosopher can afford to neglect (p. 19).

## Toy example (ours)
Build "not" and "or" from joint denial $\mathrm N(a,b)$ = "not-$a$ and not-$b$", true exactly when all its arguments are false (here, both $a$ and $b$).
- Negation: $\mathrm N(p,p)$. If $p=T$, then $\mathrm N(T,T)=F$. If $p=F$, then $\mathrm N(F,F)=T$. So it equals not-$p$.
- Disjunction: $p\lor q=\mathrm N(\mathrm N(p,q),\mathrm N(p,q))$.
  - $p=T,q=F$: $\mathrm N(T,F)=F$, then $\mathrm N(F,F)=T$. So $p\lor q$ is true, which is correct.
  - $p=F,q=F$: $\mathrm N(F,F)=T$, then $\mathrm N(T,T)=F$. So $p\lor q$ is false, which is correct.
- Generality over a domain $\{a,b,c\}$ with $fa=T$, $fb=F$, $fc=F$:
  - Jointly denying all values: $\mathrm N(T,F,F)=F$. This is "$fx$ false for all $x$", and it is false. Its negation, $(\exists x).fx$, is true.
  - Jointly denying the negated values: $\mathrm N(F,T,T)=F$. This is $(x).fx$, which is false because $fb=F$.

## Concepts
- [[picture theory of meaning]] — a picture is a fact whose elements correspond to the objects in the reality; a logical proposition is such a picture and shares logical form with the fact it pictures (pp. 9, 18)
- [[logical form vs. grammatical form]] — philosophical problems arise from not understanding the logic of our language (here "bad grammar", "senseless") (p. 8, p. 10)
- [[saying-showing]] (new) — the structure shared by sentence and fact cannot be said, only shown (p. 8)
- [[logically-perfect-language]] (new) — an ideal language with nonsense-blocking syntax and uniquely meaningful symbols; Russell says no language is claimed to be perfect, but language fulfils its function only as it approaches this ideal (p. 8)
- [[atomic-fact-and-simple]] (new) — a fact with no facts as parts, made of simples; simples are a logical requirement (pp. 10–11)
- [[truth-function]] (new) — a proposition whose truth depends only on the truth of its arguments (p. 11)
- [[joint-negation-operation]] (new) — "not-$p$ and not-$q$" (Sheffer), from which all truth-functions are built, extended to sets by $N(\bar\xi)$ (pp. 12–13)
- [[general-propositional-form]] (new) — $(\bar p,\bar\xi,N(\bar\xi))$, a process generating all propositions from atomic ones (p. 13)
- [[object-as-pseudo-concept]] (new) — *$x$ is an object* says nothing; objects can be spoken of only under a property (pp. 14–15)
- [[propositional-attitudes]] (new) — believing, doubting, desiring, etc., covered by the problem of the logical form of belief; Russell analyses belief as a relation between facts (pp. 16–17)
- [[hierarchy-of-languages]] (new) — a possibility Russell suggests, tentatively, as an escape: each language's structure is treated in a further language, with possibly no limit (p. 19)

## Canonical instances
- [[plato-loves-socrates]] — the word "loves" relates the two names, so the sentence asserts a relation between the men (p. 9)
- [[gramophone-record]] — record, score, sound waves and thought share one logical structure (p. 10)
- [[field-of-vision]] — an analogy for a world without a boundary visible from inside (p. 15)
- [[sun-will-rise]] — an example of a hypothesis, not an inference, in the absence of a causal nexus (p. 14)
- [[a-believes-p]] — an apparent non-truth-function, analysed as a relation of facts (pp. 16–17)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Joint denial as the single primitive of truth-functions ↔ Boolean logic: one two-input operation (NOR) generates all others — Russell reports Sheffer's result (p. 12).
- [structural-analogy] Sentence and fact sharing logical form ↔ structure-preserving mapping (isomorphism) between model and target — the shared form does the representing (pp. 9–10).
- [structural-analogy] Russell's hierarchy of languages ↔ object-language/metalanguage distinction: statements about a language's structure are made in another language (p. 19).
- [metaphor] Projection in geometry ↔ translation between languages: invariants survive any projection (p. 9).

## Questions this raises
- Can the saying/showing distinction be applied to Wittgenstein's own sentences without making them meaningless?
- Does Russell's hierarchy of languages really avoid the world as a whole, or does it only postpone it?
- How can complex propositions be truth-functions of atomic propositions that are logically independent, given the apparent exceptions Russell promises to treat later?
- Does the reduction of belief to a relation between two facts leave out the psychological part of meaning (p. 17)?
