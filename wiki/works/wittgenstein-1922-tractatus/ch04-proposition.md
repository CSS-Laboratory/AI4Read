# Proposition 4: the thought as significant proposition

## L0 — One line
A proposition is a logical picture of a possible state of affairs, fixed by its truth-conditions. Logical form shows itself in propositions and cannot be said, and philosophy is an activity of clarifying, not a theory.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks what a proposition is and what philosophy can do with it. The answer: a proposition pictures reality by sharing its logical structure. It is an expression of agreement and disagreement with the truth-possibilities (the ways the atomic facts could exist or not) of elementary propositions (the simplest propositions), which are concatenations, or chains, of names. Logical form cannot itself be pictured; it can only be shown. Philosophical "propositions" about form, objects or numbers are senseless, and philosophy is an activity of clarification. Tautology (true in every truth-possibility) and contradiction (false in every one) are limiting cases: they say nothing about the world, yet they are part of the symbolism.

**Prerequisites:** propositional logic (truth tables, ∼, ∨, tautology); binomial coefficients; the Tractatus's earlier ideas of objects, atomic facts and logical space.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The thought is the significant proposition, and the totality of propositions is the language. Colloquial language disguises logical form (§§4, 4.001, 4.002, pp. 38–39).
- Most philosophical propositions are senseless, not false. They arise from misunderstanding the logic of language (§4.003, p. 39).
- A proposition is a picture or model of reality. It shows its sense and says that things stand so (§§4.01, 4.022, pp. 39–40).
- If one does not see that propositions have a sense independent of the facts, one can easily believe that true and false are two equal relations between signs and things. The sign "∼" corresponds to nothing in reality (§§4.061–4.0621, p. 43).
- Philosophy is not a natural science. It is an activity of logical clarification. It should limit the thinkable and thereby the unthinkable, the latter from within through the thinkable (§§4.111–4.114, pp. 44–45).
- What mirrors itself in language cannot be said. Formal properties, relations and concepts show themselves, so "object", "number" etc. are pseudo-concepts expressed by variables (§§4.12–4.128, pp. 45–49).
- Elementary propositions are concatenations of names. Every proposition is an expression of agreement and disagreement with their truth-possibilities; for n elementary propositions there are $L_n$ such possibilities (§§4.22, 4.4, 4.42, pp. 49–51).
- Tautology and contradiction are without sense but not senseless. They are the limiting cases of combining signs (§§4.46–4.466, pp. 53–54).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**3.344–3.5 (end of Prop. 3, p. 38).** What signifies in a symbol is what it shares with all symbols that can replace it under the rules of logical syntax (§3.344). What is common to all notations for the truth-functions is, for example, that they can all be replaced by ∼p and p ∨ q (§3.3441). The sign of a complex is not arbitrarily resolved in the analysis, so that its resolution would differ in every propositional structure (§3.3442). A proposition determines a place in logical space. The existence of this place is guaranteed by the existence of the constituent parts alone, by the existence of the significant proposition (§3.4). The propositional sign and the logical co-ordinates are the logical place, and the geometrical and the logical place agree in that each is the possibility of an existence (§§3.41, 3.411). Yet the whole logical space must already be given by it, otherwise denial, the logical sum, the logical product etc. would always introduce new elements in co-ordination (§3.42). The applied, thought, propositional sign is the thought (§3.5).

**4–4.0031 (pp. 38–39).** The thought is the significant proposition (§4). The totality of propositions is the language (§4.001). Humans can build languages expressing every sense without knowing how each word means (§4.002). Colloquial language is part of the human organism and not less complicated than it. It is humanly impossible to gather the logic of language from it immediately. Language disguises the thought: from the external form of the clothes one cannot infer the form of the thought they clothe, because the clothes are built for another purpose than to let the form of the body be recognized (§4.002, p. 39). Most propositions and questions written on philosophical matters are not false but senseless. Questions of this kind therefore cannot be answered, but only their senselessness stated. Most of them result from our not understanding the logic of our language. They are of the same kind as the question whether the Good is more or less identical than the Beautiful, so the deepest problems are really no problems (§4.003). All philosophy is "Critique of language", though not in Mauthner's sense. Russell's merit is to have shown that apparent logical form need not be real form (§4.0031).
*Intuition note:* The outer form of everyday language is like clothing that hides the form of the body (the thought). Wittgenstein traces most philosophical questions to our not understanding the logic of our language, and credits Russell with showing that the apparent logical form of a proposition need not be its real form.

**4.01–4.016 (pp. 39–40).** The proposition is a picture, a model of reality as we think it is (§4.01). It does not look like one, but neither do a musical score or phonetic spelling, and these prove to be pictures (§4.011). A proposition of the form aRb is perceived as a picture (§4.012). Apparent irregularities, like ♯ and ♭ in a score, also picture, only in another way (§4.013). Gramophone record, musical thought, score and sound waves all stand to one another in the pictorial internal relation that holds between language and the world; the logical structure is common to all of them (§4.014). The rule that lets one read the symphony from the score is a law of projection, a rule of translation between languages (§4.0141, p. 40). The possibility of all similes, of all the imagery of our language, rests on the logic of representation (§4.015). Hieroglyphs picture facts, and the alphabet keeps the essence (§4.016).

**4.02–4.031 (pp. 40–42).** We understand a propositional sign without having its sense explained (§§4.02, 4.021). The proposition shows its sense. It shows how things stand if it is true, and it says that they so stand (§4.022). One needs only to say Yes or No to a proposition to make it agree with reality; reality must therefore be completely described by the proposition. A proposition is the description of a fact (§4.023, p. 41). As the description of an object describes it by its external properties, propositions describe reality by its internal properties. The proposition constructs a world with the help of a logical scaffolding, so one can see in it the logical features reality has if it is true. One can draw conclusions from a false proposition. To understand a proposition is to know what is the case if it is true, so one can understand it without knowing whether it is true; one understands it if one understands its constituent parts (§4.024). Translation translates only constituent parts, not whole propositions (§4.025). The meanings of simple signs (words) must be explained to us if we are to understand them, and by means of propositions we explain ourselves (§4.026). A proposition must communicate a new sense with old words (§§4.027, 4.03). It asserts only in so far as it is a picture (§4.03). In the proposition a state of affairs is, "as it were, put together for the sake of experiment" (§4.031, p. 41). One can say that the proposition represents such and such a state of affairs instead of saying that it has such and such a sense (§4.031, p. 42).

**4.0311–4.0412 (p. 42).** Names stand for things and are connected, so the proposition presents the atomic fact like a living picture (§4.0311). The possibility of propositions rests on the principle of representing objects by signs. Wittgenstein's fundamental thought is that the logical constants do not represent and that the logic of the facts cannot be represented (§4.0312). A proposition pictures only in so far as it is articulated; even *ambulo* is composite (§4.032). Proposition and state of affairs must have the same number of distinguishable elements, the same logical (mathematical) multiplicity (§4.04). This multiplicity cannot itself be represented (§4.041). Attempted generality notations lack the necessary multiplicity and so fail:
- "Gen. fx" does not show what is generalized.
- $f(x_g)$ does not show the scope.
- "$(G,G).F(G,G)$" does not fix the identity of the variables (§4.0411).

The idealist "spatial spectacles" explanation fails for the same reason (§4.0412).

**4.05–4.0641 (pp. 42–44).** Reality is compared with the proposition (§4.05). Propositions can be true or false only by being pictures of reality (§4.06). **Diagnosed error (a belief Wittgenstein describes, not his own view):** if one does not observe that propositions have a sense independent of the facts, one can easily believe that true and false are two relations between signs and things signified with equal rights. One could then say, for example, that p signifies in the true way what ∼p signifies in the false way (§4.061). Wittgenstein asks whether we cannot make ourselves understood by means of false propositions as hitherto with true ones, so long as we know they are meant to be false, and answers No (§4.062). A proposition is true if what we assert by it is the case; so if by "p" we mean ∼p and what we mean is the case, then "p" in the new conception is true and not false (§4.062). Because p and ∼p can say the same thing, "∼" corresponds to nothing in reality. Negation is no characteristic of sense ($\sim\sim p = p$), and p and ∼p have opposite senses but one and the same reality corresponding to them (§4.0621, p. 43).

*Black-spot simile (§4.063).* A black spot on white paper is described by saying of each point whether it is black or white. Black corresponds to a positive fact, white to a negative one. To say a point is black or white, I must first know the conditions for calling it so. Likewise, to say "p" is true I must have determined the conditions for calling "p" true, and thereby the sense. The simile breaks down because a point can be indicated without knowing what white and black are, whereas a proposition without sense corresponds to nothing. So the verb of the proposition is not "is true" (against Frege), but that which is true must already contain the verb (§4.063, pp. 43–44).

Every proposition must already have a sense. Assertion and denial cannot give it, since what is asserted is the sense itself (§4.064). The denying proposition fixes a logical place by saying that it lies outside the place of the denied proposition. That a denial can be denied again shows that what is denied is already a proposition (§4.0641).
*Intuition note:* Truth and falsity are not extra labels that give a sentence meaning. The sentence has its sense first, as a picture, and then reality agrees or disagrees with it.

**4.1–4.116 (pp. 44–45).** A proposition presents the existence and non-existence of atomic facts (§4.1). The totality of true propositions is the total natural science (§4.11). Philosophy is not a natural science. The word philosophy must mean something that stands above or below, but not beside, the natural sciences (§4.111). Its object is logical clarification of thoughts. It is an activity, not a theory, and a philosophical work consists essentially of elucidations. Its result is not a set of "philosophical propositions" but the making clear of propositions; philosophy should clarify and sharply bound thoughts that are otherwise murky and blurred (§4.112). Psychology is no nearer to philosophy than other sciences (§4.1121). The theory of knowledge is the philosophy of psychology. Wittgenstein asks whether his study of sign-language does not correspond to the study of thought processes that philosophers held essential to the philosophy of logic (the "Only …" that follows implies yes: it does correspond). He continues that they got entangled for the most part in unessential psychological investigations, and that there is an analogous danger for his method (§4.1121, pp. 44–45). Darwin's theory has no more to do with philosophy than any other hypothesis (§4.1122). Philosophy limits the disputable sphere of natural science (§4.113). It should limit the thinkable and thereby the unthinkable; it should limit the unthinkable from within through the thinkable (§4.114). It will mean the unspeakable by clearly displaying the speakable (§4.115). Whatever can be said can be said clearly (§4.116).

**4.12–4.1213 (p. 45).** Propositions can represent the whole of reality but cannot represent logical form, the thing they must share with reality. To do so one would have to stand outside logic, that is, outside the world (§4.12). Logical form mirrors itself in propositions, and propositions show it (§4.121). Examples: fa shows that a occurs in its sense. fa and ga show that they are about the same object. Contradiction and consequence are shown by structure (§4.1211). "What can be shown cannot be said" (§4.1212).
*Intuition note:* A proposition shares a logical form with reality, but it cannot also depict that form; to do so it would have to stand outside logic. The form shows itself in the proposition's structure instead.

**4.122–4.128 (pp. 46–49).** In a certain sense one can speak of formal properties of objects and atomic facts and of formal relations. Wittgenstein also calls a property of the structure of a fact an internal property, and a relation of structures an internal relation. The holding of such internal properties and relations cannot be asserted by propositions but shows itself in them (§4.122). A property is internal if it is unthinkable that its object lacks it. Example: two blues stand eo ipso in the relation brighter/darker (§4.123). The existence of an internal property of a possible state of affairs is not expressed by a proposition but expresses itself in the proposition presenting that state of affairs. It would be as senseless to ascribe a formal property to a proposition as to deny it the formal property (§4.124). Likewise an internal relation between possible states of affairs expresses itself by an internal relation between the propositions (§4.125). Whether all relations are internal or external "disappears" (§4.1251). Series ordered by internal relations are called formal series; the series of numbers is ordered by an internal, not an external, relation. So is the series of propositions aRb, (∃x):aRx.xRb, … (§4.1252, p. 47).

Formal concepts, such as object, number and function, are not presented by functions. Their expression is a propositional variable, in which only the characteristic feature is constant. The variable signifies the concept and its values the objects falling under it (§§4.126–4.127). Every variable is the sign of a formal concept (§4.1271). Hence "object" is a pseudo-concept, rightly expressed by the variable name x, for example in (∃x,y)… Used otherwise, as a proper concept word, it yields senseless pseudo-propositions: one cannot say that there are objects (as one says there are books), nor that there are 100 objects, and statements such as that 1 is a number are senseless. The same holds for Complex, Fact, Function and Number. They signify formal concepts and are presented in logical symbolism by variables, not by functions or classes (as Frege and Russell thought) (§4.1272, p. 48). A formal concept is given with an object that falls under it, so both cannot be introduced as primitive ideas (§4.12721). The general term of a formal series can be expressed only by a variable. Frege and Russell overlooked this, so Wittgenstein says their way of expressing the general proposition that b is a successor of a is false and contains a vicious circle. One fixes the series by giving its first term and the general form of the operation (§4.1273, pp. 48–49). The question whether a formal concept exists is senseless (§4.1274). Logical forms are anumerical, so there are no pre-eminent numbers in logic and no philosophical monism or dualism (§4.128, p. 49).

**4.2–4.28 (pp. 49–50).** The sense of a proposition is its agreement and disagreement with the possibilities of existence and non-existence of atomic facts (§4.2). The simplest proposition, the elementary proposition, asserts the existence of an atomic fact (§4.21). That no elementary proposition can contradict it is a sign of an elementary proposition (§4.211). It consists of names in concatenation (§4.22). In the analysis of propositions we must come to elementary propositions, which consist of names in immediate combination; the question is raised how the propositional connexion comes to be. Even if the world is infinitely complex there must be objects and atomic facts (§§4.221, 4.2211). A name occurs only in the context of an elementary proposition (§4.23). Names are written x, y, z, and elementary propositions as fx, φ(x,y), or p, q, r (§4.24). Wittgenstein stipulates: if I use two signs with one and the same meaning, I express this by putting "=" between them; "a = b" means that the sign "a" is replaceable by the sign "b"; a definition is a symbolic rule (§4.241). Such expressions assert nothing about the meaning of the signs (§4.242). Wittgenstein asks whether we can understand two names, or a proposition containing them, without knowing whether they signify the same thing or different things. He then gives an example: if I know the meaning of an English word and a synonymous German word, it is impossible for me not to know that they are synonymous. Expressions like a = a, or expressions deduced from these, are neither elementary propositions nor otherwise significant signs; this is said to be shown later (§4.243, p. 50). If the elementary proposition is true the atomic fact exists, and if it is false the atomic fact does not exist (§4.25). The specification of all true elementary propositions describes the world completely (§4.26). For n atomic facts, the possibilities of existence and non-existence number
$$K_n=\sum_{\nu=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\nu}\tag{4.27}$$
and the same number of truth/falsehood possibilities corresponds for n elementary propositions (§§4.27–4.28).

**4.3–4.431 (pp. 50–52).** Truth-possibilities are presented by T/F schemata with $K_n$ rows (added step: the row count follows from §4.28). The text prints schemata for 3, 2 and 1 propositions (§4.31, p. 51). A proposition is the expression of agreement and disagreement with these truth-possibilities, which are its conditions of truth and falsehood (§§4.4, 4.41). It seems probable even at first sight that introducing elementary propositions is fundamental for understanding the other kinds of propositions; indeed the comprehension of general propositions depends palpably on that of elementary ones (§4.411). The number of ways a proposition can agree or disagree with the truth-possibilities of n elementary propositions is
$$L_n=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{K_n}\binom{K_n}{\kappa}\tag{4.42}$$
Agreement is marked by T and absence of T means disagreement (§4.43). The expression of this agreement and disagreement expresses the truth-conditions of the proposition, and the proposition is the expression of its truth-conditions (§4.431). Frege quite rightly put truth-conditions at the beginning, as explaining the signs of his logical symbolism, but his explanation of the truth-concept is false. If the true and the false were real objects (the arguments in ∼p), the sense of ∼p would by no means be determined by Frege's determination (§4.431, p. 52).

**4.44–4.45 (p. 52).** The sign from coordinating T with the truth-possibilities is a propositional sign. No object corresponds to a complex of T's and F's, so there are no logical objects (§§4.44, 4.441). Frege's assertion sign ⊢ is logically meaningless. A proposition cannot assert of itself that it is true (§4.442). If the sequence of the truth-possibilities is fixed once for all by a rule of combination, then the last column by itself expresses the truth-conditions. Written as a row for p, q it becomes "(TT–T)(p, q)", or more plainly "(TTFT)(p, q)"; the dash fills the TF place, which has no T (§4.442, p. 52). For n elementary propositions there are $L_n$ groups of truth-conditions, which can be ordered in a series (§4.45, pp. 52–53).

**4.46–4.466 (pp. 53–54).** The two extreme groups are tautology (true for all truth-possibilities) and contradiction (false for all) (§4.46). The proposition shows what it says, while tautology and contradiction show that they say nothing. The tautology has no truth-conditions, being unconditionally true, and the contradiction is true on no condition; both are without sense. For example, I know nothing about the weather when I know that it rains or does not rain (§4.461). They are not senseless but part of the symbolism, as 0 is in arithmetic (§4.4611). They are not pictures, since one allows every state of affairs and the other none (§4.462). The truth-conditions fix the range left to the facts (§4.463). Tautology leaves the whole of logical space to reality and contradiction fills it, so neither determines reality (§4.463, p. 54). Tautology is certain, propositions possible, contradiction impossible (§4.464). The logical product of a tautology and a proposition says the same as the proposition, so the product is identical with it (§4.465). To a definite logical combination of signs corresponds a definite logical combination of their meanings, and propositions true for every state of affairs cannot be combinations of signs at all (§4.466). Tautology and contradiction are the limiting cases of the combinations of symbols, namely their dissolution. Their signs are combined with one another, but these relations are meaningless and unessential to the symbol (§§4.466, 4.4661).

**4.5 (p. 54, breaks off).** It now appears possible to give the most general form of proposition. In describing the most general form of proposition only what is essential to it may be described, otherwise it would not be the most general form (§4.5).

## Toy example (ours)
Take n = 2 elementary propositions p, q.
- $K_2=\binom20+\binom21+\binom22=1+2+1=4$ truth-possibilities (rows TT, FT, TF, FF in the author's order).
- $L_2=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{4}\binom4\kappa=1+4+6+4+1=16$. (Added step: by the binomial theorem this equals $2^{K_2}=2^4=16$.)
- Truth-conditions as columns: "p and q" is T only on row TT, so it is (TFFF)(p,q). "p or q" is (TTTF). Tautology is (TTTT) and contradiction is (FFFF).
- (Added step, reading $\kappa$ as the number of truth-possibilities with which the proposition agrees; the text does not say this.) Of the 16 groups, exactly $\binom44=1$ (agreement with all four rows) is the tautology and $\binom40=1$ (agreement with none) is the contradiction.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] truth-conditions as columns of T/F, $L_n=2^{2^n}$ groups (our computation: $K_n=2^n$, $L_n=2^{K_n}$) ↔ propositional logic: Boolean functions of n variables — each proposition is a Boolean function.
- [structural-analogy] shown vs. said ↔ type theory/metalanguage: statements about the form of a system cannot be made inside it.
- [metaphor] proposition as restricting a body's movement in space (§4.463) ↔ information theory: a message as narrowing the set of possibilities.

## Questions this raises
- If statements about logical form are senseless, what status do the Tractatus's own sentences have?
- How can a general form of proposition (§4.5) be given if only what is essential may be described?
- How do truth-functional operations generate all propositions from elementary ones?
- Can the claim that no elementary proposition can contradict an elementary proposition (§4.211) be reconciled with ordinary cases?
