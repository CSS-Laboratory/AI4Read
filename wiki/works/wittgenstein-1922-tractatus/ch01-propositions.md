# Tractatus 1–2.18: the world, atomic facts, objects, pictures

## L0 — One line
The world is the totality of facts, what is the case is the existence of atomic facts (combinations of simple objects), and a picture is a fact that must share a form of representation with what it pictures.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
In numbered remarks, the passage states what the world consists of. The world is the totality of facts, not of things. Atomic facts are combinations of objects. Objects are simple and form the fixed substance of the world. Each object carries the possibility of its occurrence in atomic facts, and that possibility is its form. Configurations of objects change. Atomic facts are independent of one another. From 2.1 on he introduces pictures. A picture is a fact whose elements stand for objects. It can represent a reality only if it has a form in common with it.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a possible versus an actual state of the world; the everyday difference between a thing and a fact; no formal logic needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The world is the totality of facts, not of things. The facts also fix what is not the case (p. 25, 1–1.12).
- A fact is the existence of atomic facts. An atomic fact is a combination of objects (p. 25, 2–2.01).
- An object cannot be thought apart from the possibility of occurring in atomic facts. That possibility is its form (pp. 25–26, 2.011–2.0141).
- Objects are simple and form the world's substance. Without substance, whether a proposition had sense would depend on whether another proposition were true, and no picture of the world could be formed (pp. 26–27, 2.02–2.0212).
- Substance fixes form, not material properties. Configuration of objects is what varies (p. 27, 2.0231, 2.0271).
- Atomic facts are mutually independent (p. 28, 2.061–2.062).
- A picture is itself a fact. Its elements correspond to objects, and its structure represents that the things are so combined (p. 28, 2.13–2.15).
- A picture must share a form of representation with what it pictures. It cannot represent that form; it shows it forth (p. 29, 2.16–2.172).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Author's note on numbering.** Decimal numbers show a proposition's logical importance. Proposition n.1, n.2 … comment on n, and n.m1, n.m2 … comment on n.m (p. 25, footnote).

**1–1.21 The world and facts.**
- The world is everything that is the case. It is the totality of facts, not of things (p. 25, 1, 1.1).
- The facts determine the world, and so does their being all the facts. The totality of facts fixes both what is the case and all that is not the case (p. 25, 1.11–1.12).
- The facts in logical space are the world. The world divides into facts (p. 25, 1.13, 1.2).
- Any one fact can be the case or not be the case while everything else stays the same (p. 25, 1.21).

*Intuition note.* The world is not a heap of things but what is the case. It is fixed by the facts together with their being all the facts (1.11), and that totality also fixes what is not the case.

**2–2.0141 Atomic facts and the form of objects.**
- What is the case is the existence of atomic facts. An atomic fact is a combination of objects, also called entities or things (p. 25, 2, 2.01).
- It is essential to a thing that it can be a constituent of an atomic fact (p. 25, 2.011).
- In logic nothing is accidental. If a thing can occur in an atomic fact, that possibility must already lie in the thing (p. 25, 2.012). It would, so to speak, look like an accident if a fitting state of affairs were added later to a thing that could exist alone (p. 25, 2.0121).
- Parenthetical remark: a logical entity cannot be merely possible. Logic treats every possibility, and all possibilities are its facts (p. 26, 2.0121).
- We cannot think an object apart from the possibility of its connexion with other things, just as we cannot think spatial objects apart from space (p. 26, 2.0121).
- A thing is independent in that it can occur in all possible circumstances. This independence is a form of connexion with the atomic fact, so a form of dependence. (Words cannot occur in two different ways, alone and in the proposition.) (p. 26, 2.0122).
- If I know an object, I also know all its possibilities of occurring in atomic facts. Every such possibility must lie in the nature of the object, and a new possibility cannot subsequently be found. To know an object I must know not its external but all its internal qualities (p. 26, 2.0123, 2.01231).
- If all objects are given, all possible atomic facts are given (p. 26, 2.0124).
- Every thing is, as it were, in a space of possible atomic facts. I can think of this space as empty, but not of the thing without the space (p. 26, 2.013).
- A spatial object must lie in infinite space (a point in space is a place for an argument). Examples: a visual speck need not be red but must have a colour, having, so to speak, a colour space round it. A tone must have a pitch, and a touch object a hardness (p. 26, 2.0131).
- Objects contain the possibility of all states of affairs. The possibility of occurring in atomic facts is the form of the object (p. 26, 2.014–2.0141).

*Intuition note.* An object comes with a built-in range of ways it can combine. Like a speck that must have some colour, it cannot be thought of without that range.

**2.02–2.0271 Simple objects and substance.**
- The object is simple. Every statement about complexes can be analysed into a statement about their parts and propositions that completely describe the complexes (pp. 26–27, 2.02, 2.0201).
- Objects form the substance of the world, so they cannot be compound (p. 27, 2.021).
- If the world had no substance, whether a proposition had sense would depend on whether another proposition were true. Then no picture of the world, true or false, could be formed (p. 27, 2.0211–2.0212).
- Any imagined world must have a form in common with the real one. This fixed form consists of the objects (p. 27, 2.022–2.023).
- Substance determines only a form, not material properties. Those are first presented by propositions, formed by the configuration of objects. Roughly, objects are colourless (p. 27, 2.0231–2.0232).
- Two objects of the same logical form differ, apart from external properties, only in being different (p. 27, 2.0233). If a thing has a unique property, a description can single it out. If several things share all their properties, it is impossible to point to any one of them. A thing not distinguished by anything cannot be distinguished by me, for otherwise it would be distinguished (p. 27, 2.02331).
- Substance is what exists independently of what is the case. It is form and content. Space, time and colour are forms of objects (p. 27, 2.024–2.0251).
- Only if there are objects can the world have a fixed form. The fixed, the existent and the object are one. The configuration is the changing and variable (p. 27, 2.026–2.0271).

*Intuition note.* Objects are what stays the same however things turn out. What varies is the configuration of the objects (2.0271).

**2.0272–2.063 Atomic facts, structure, reality.**
- The configuration of objects forms the atomic fact. Objects hang one in another like chain links, combined in a definite way (p. 28, 2.0272–2.031).
- That way is the structure of the atomic fact. Form is the possibility of structure. The structure of a fact consists of the structures of atomic facts (p. 28, 2.032–2.034).
- The totality of existent atomic facts is the world, and it also determines which atomic facts do not exist (p. 28, 2.04–2.05).
- Existence and non-existence of atomic facts is reality. The first is a positive fact, the second a negative fact. Total reality is the world (p. 28, 2.06–2.063).
- Atomic facts are independent. From the existence or non-existence of one we cannot infer that of another (p. 28, 2.061–2.062).

**2.1–2.18 Pictures.**
- We make pictures of facts. A picture presents facts in logical space and is a model of reality (p. 28, 2.1–2.12).
- Elements of the picture correspond to, and stand for, objects. A picture consists in its elements being combined in a definite way. A picture is a fact (p. 28, 2.13–2.141).
- That the elements are so combined represents that the things are so combined (p. 28, 2.15).
- The connexion of the elements is the picture's structure. The possibility of this structure is its form of representation. This is the possibility that things are combined as the elements are (pp. 28–29, 2.15–2.151).
- The picture is thus linked with reality; it reaches up to it. It is like a scale applied to reality. Only the outermost points of the dividing lines touch the object measured (p. 29, 2.1511–2.15121).
- According to this view the representing relation, which makes it a picture, also belongs to the picture. It consists of co-ordinations between the elements and the things, which are, as it were, the "feelers" of the elements with which the picture touches reality (p. 29, 2.1513–2.1515).
- A picture and what it pictures must have something identical, and this is the form of representation. The picture can represent every reality whose form it has (p. 29, 2.16–2.171).
- The picture cannot represent its form of representation; it shows it forth. It represents its object from without and therefore rightly or falsely. It cannot place itself outside its form (p. 29, 2.172–2.174).

*Intuition note.* A picture is made of the same kind of stuff as what it depicts, a fact with a structure. What they share, the form of representation, cannot be represented by the picture; the picture shows it forth (2.172).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take three objects $a, b, c$. Suppose the only possible atomic facts are $F_1$: "$a$ left of $b$" and $F_2$: "$b$ left of $c$".
- Independence (2.061): each of $F_1, F_2$ may exist or not, so there are $2\times 2 = 4$ possible worlds: {both}, {$F_1$ only}, {$F_2$ only}, {neither}.
- Suppose {$F_1$ only} is actual. This set of existing atomic facts is the world (2.04). It also fixes that $F_2$ does not exist (2.05).
- Picture: write the letters "A B" on a page, with "A" left of "B". The two letters are elements standing for $a$ and $b$. The fact that "A" is left of "B" represents that $a$ is left of $b$ (2.15). It is true here. It would be false if $F_1$ did not exist.
- What page and world share is the possibility of left-right order. The page can use this order but cannot say it (2.172).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] atomic facts as independent yes/no states ↔ probability: independent binary variables whose combinations give possible worlds. This is my analogy only; the text gives no probability.
- [structural-analogy] picture as a model sharing structure with reality ↔ computational social science: a model matches its target in structure. 2.12 calls the picture a "model of reality".

## Questions this raises
- Which things count as simple objects? The text gives only colour, pitch, hardness and spatial points as illustrations of forms.
- If a picture cannot represent its own form, how can that form be said at all (2.172)?
- How do propositions relate to pictures of atomic facts, given the claim that propositions first present material properties (2.0231)?
- Is the independence of atomic facts compatible with the claim that facts at once fix what is and is not the case (1.12, 2.05)?
