# Proposition 5: Propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions

## L0 — One line
Every proposition is the result of repeated truth-operations (in the end, the single operation N, the negation of all values of a variable) on elementary propositions, so there are no "logical objects", and the limits of my language mean the limits of my world.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The unit asks how complex propositions relate to elementary ones, and what logic is. Wittgenstein answers that all propositions are built from elementary propositions by one operation, joint negation, applied repeatedly. He also argues that inference needs no laws of inference and that the propositions of logic say nothing. Logical constants such as "not" and "or" name no objects, and identity is not a relation. Logic cannot anticipate which elementary propositions there are, since the application of logic decides that. The limits of my language mean the limits of my world, and logic fills the world, so that the limits of the world are also its limits. The subject is a limit of the world, not part of it.

**Prerequisites:** propositional logic (truth tables, ∨, ⊃, ∼, quantifiers ∃ and ∀); the Tractatus idea that elementary propositions are concatenations of names (from Prop. 4, not restated here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions, and their truth-grounds can be tabulated (p. 55–56). Entailment is containment of truth-grounds, so it is visible in the signs (p. 56–57).
- Inference needs no laws of inference. No inference leads from one state of affairs to an entirely different one, and no causal nexus justifies such an inference (p. 57–58).
- Tautology and contradiction are the limiting cases. A tautology says nothing, and probability is a ratio of truth-grounds (p. 58–59).
- Operations differ from functions. A series is generated by successive application of an operation, written $[a, x, O'x]$ (p. 60–61).
- All propositions come from elementary ones by truth-operations, so there are no logical objects or constants (p. 62–63).
- Every truth-function results from the operation $N(\bar\xi)$, joint negation. Generality and identity are reworked in this light, and the identity sign is dropped (p. 66–70).
- The application of logic decides what elementary propositions there are, and logic cannot anticipate that. If they cannot be given a priori, trying to give them leads to obvious nonsense (p. 73–74).
- The limits of my language mean the limits of my world. Solipsism is correct in what it means but cannot be said. The subject is a limit of the world (p. 74).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 5–5.02: truth-arguments, argument vs. index
- Propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions. An elementary proposition is a truth-function of itself (p. 55). Elementary propositions are the *truth-arguments* (the inputs) of propositions (5.01, p. 55).
- Argument and index are easily confused. In Russell's $+_c$, the $c$ is an index showing that the whole sign is the addition sign for cardinal numbers, a way of symbolizing that rests on arbitrary agreement. (In the name Julius Cæsar, "Julius" is an index; an index is always part of a description of the object whose name it is attached to.) (5.02, p. 55).
- In $\sim p$, by contrast, $p$ is an argument. The sense of $\sim p$ cannot be understood unless the sense of $p$ is already understood (p. 55).
- Wittgenstein says this confusion is, if he is not mistaken, at the root of Frege's theory of propositions and functions. For Frege the propositions of logic were names and their arguments were the names' indices (p. 55).

### 5.1–5.156: truth-grounds, following, probability
- Truth-functions can be ordered in a series. Wittgenstein says this is the foundation of probability theory (5.1, p. 55).
- 5.101 says the truth-functions of any number of elementary propositions can be written in a schema; it displays all 16 for two propositions. Examples: $(TTTT)(p,q)$ is tautology; $(FTTT)$ is $\sim(p\cdot q)$; $(TTTF)$ is $p\vee q$; $(FFFT)$ is $\sim p\cdot\sim q$ ("neither", also $p\mid q$); $(TFFF)$ is $p\cdot q$; $(FFFF)$ is contradiction (p. 56).
- The truth-possibilities of the arguments that verify a proposition are its **truth-grounds** (p. 56).
- **Following (5.11–5.121).** The truth of a proposition follows from the truth of others if the truth-grounds common to those others are all truth-grounds of it (5.11). In particular, $p$ follows from $q$ if all truth-grounds of $q$ are truth-grounds of $p$ (5.12, 5.121). Then the sense of $p$ is contained in that of $q$ (5.122, p. 56).
- Wittgenstein remarks that if a god creates a world in which certain propositions are true, he thereby also creates a world in which all propositions consequent on them are true (5.123, p. 56). A proposition asserts every proposition that follows from it. So $p\cdot q$ asserts both $p$ and $q$ (5.124–5.1241, p. 56).
- Two propositions are opposed if no significant proposition asserts both. A proposition that contradicts another denies it (p. 57).
- **Inference is visible in structure (5.13–5.132).** That one proposition follows from others is perceived from their structure. The relations are internal. They exist as soon as the propositions exist, and we need not first state them in a proposition (5.131, p. 57).
- Concluding from $p\vee q$ and $\sim p$ to $q$ hides the link in ordinary notation. Writing $p\vee q$ as $p\mid q\,.\,|\,.\,p\mid q$ and $\sim p$ as $p\mid p$ makes the connection obvious (with $p\mid q$ = neither $p$ nor $q$) (5.1311, p. 57). The fact that we can infer $fa$ from $(x)\,.\,fx$ shows that generality is present also in the symbol $(x)\,.\,fx$ (p. 57).
- The method of inference is to be understood from the two propositions alone, and only they themselves can justify the inference. Laws of inference, as in Frege and Russell, are senseless and superfluous (5.132, p. 57). All inference is a priori (5.133, p. 57).
- **No outer inference (5.134–5.136).** From an elementary proposition no other can be inferred. No inference leads from the existence of one state of affairs to that of another, entirely different one. No causal nexus justifies such an inference (p. 57). Future events cannot be inferred from present ones, and "Superstition is the belief in the causal nexus" (5.1361, p. 57).
- The freedom of the will is that future actions cannot be known now. We could know them only if causality were an inner necessity like logical deduction (5.1362, p. 58). The connexion of knowledge and what is known is that of logical necessity, and saying that A knows that $p$ is the case is senseless if $p$ is a tautology (5.1362, p. 58). If a proposition's being obvious to us does not entail that it is true, obviousness is no justification for our belief in its truth (5.1363, p. 58).
- **Strength, tautology, contradiction (5.14–5.143).** If a proposition follows from another, the latter says more than the former (5.14). If $p$ follows from $q$ and $q$ from $p$, they are one proposition (5.141). A tautology follows from all propositions and says nothing (5.142, p. 58).
- Contradiction is something shared by propositions that no proposition has in common with another; tautology is what is shared by all propositions that have nothing in common with one another. Contradiction vanishes, so to speak, outside all propositions, tautology inside them. Contradiction is their external limit, tautology their "substanceless centre" (5.143, p. 58).
- **Probability (5.15–5.156).**
  - Let $T_r$ be the number of truth-grounds of $r$. Let $T_{rs}$ be the number of those truth-grounds of $s$ that are also truth-grounds of $r$. Then the ratio $T_{rs}:T_r$ measures the probability $r$ gives to $s$ (5.15, p. 58). In the schema it is counted by the T's in matching columns (5.151, p. 58).
  - Propositions with no truth-arguments in common are independent. Independent propositions, e.g. two elementary propositions, give each other probability $\tfrac12$. If $p$ follows from $q$, then $q$ gives $p$ probability 1. Certainty is a limiting case of probability (5.152, p. 59).
  - There is no special object peculiar to probability propositions (5.1511, p. 58). A proposition is in itself neither probable nor improbable. An event occurs or does not (5.153, p. 59).
  - In the urn example, with equal numbers of white and black balls and draws replaced, experiment shows the drawn numbers approach each other. So this is not a mathematical fact (5.154, p. 59).
  - "Equally probable" means that all circumstances known to me, including hypothetically assumed natural laws, give neither event more probability than the other. What experiment verifies is that the two events are independent of circumstances I do not know closely (p. 59).
  - Probability is a generalization, needed only in default of certainty. A probability proposition is, as it were, an extract from other propositions (5.156, p. 59).
- *Intuition note.* Write each proposition as a row of T's and F's, one per truth-possibility. That $p$ follows from $q$ then just means that every T of $q$ is also a T of $p$ (for several premises, take the T's they share), so entailment can be read off the signs. Probability is the share of $r$'s T's that are also T's of $s$.

### 5.2–5.254: operations
- Structures of propositions stand in internal relations. We show them by presenting a proposition as the result of an operation on other propositions, its *bases* (5.2–5.21, p. 60). The operation expresses the relation between the structures of its result and its bases (5.22, p. 60).
- Truth-functions of elementary propositions are results of **truth-operations** (5.234, p. 60). The sense of a truth-function of $p$ is a function of the sense of $p$. Denial, logical addition and logical multiplication are operations (5.2341, p. 60).
- An operation shows itself in a variable. It characterizes not a form but only the difference between forms (5.24–5.241, p. 60). The same operation makes $q$ from $p$ and $r$ from $q$. This is expressed only by treating $p, q, r$ as variables (5.242, p. 60).
- An operation asserts nothing. Only its result does, and that depends on the bases (5.25, p. 61). Operation and function must not be confused. A function cannot be its own argument, but the result of an operation can be its own basis (5.251, p. 61).
- Only so is progress from term to term in a formal series possible (from type to type in the hierarchy of Russell and Whitehead). Wittgenstein says they did not admit this progress but used it anyway (5.252, p. 61).
- Successive application: $O'O'O'a$ is the threefold application of $O'\xi$ to $a$ (5.2521). The general term of the series $a, O'a, O'O'a,\dots$ is written $[a,\,x,\,O'x]$ (5.2522). Its parts are the series' beginning, an arbitrary term $x$, and the term immediately following $x$ (p. 61).
- "Successive application" is equivalent to the concept "and so on" (5.2523). Operations can cancel one another and vanish, e.g. $\sim\sim p = p$ (5.253–5.254, p. 61).
- *Intuition note.* An operation is a recipe, not a thing in the proposition. Applying "not" twice returns the start, so the recipe leaves no trace.

### 5.3–5.32: all propositions are truth-operation results
- All propositions are results of truth-operations on elementary propositions. Applying a truth-operation to truth-functions yields a new truth-function. The result of truth-operations on results of truth-operations is also the result of *one* truth-operation on elementary propositions (5.3, p. 61–62).
- The schemata of 4.31 stay significant when $p, q, r$ are not elementary (5.31, p. 62). All truth-functions come from a *finite* number of successive truth-operations (5.32, p. 62).

### 5.4–5.476: no logical constants
- It becomes clear that there are no "logical objects" or "logical constants" in the sense of Frege and Russell (5.4, p. 62). Results of truth-operations that give the same truth-function of elementary propositions are identical (5.41, p. 62).
- $\vee,\supset$ are not relations like right and left. The mutual definability of Frege's and Russell's "primitive signs" shows they are not primitive and signify no relations (5.42, p. 62).
- From a fact $p$ infinitely many others follow ($\sim\sim p,\sim\sim\sim\sim p,\dots$). He says the first is hardly to be believed and the second no less wonderful: infinitely many propositions of logic (of mathematics) follow from half a dozen primitive propositions. But all propositions of logic say the same thing, namely nothing (5.43, p. 62).
- **5.44.** Truth-functions are not material functions. *Wittgenstein asks:* does $\sim\sim p$ deny $\sim p$, or affirm $p$, or both? *He replies:* $\sim\sim p$ does not treat of denial as an object. The possibility of denial is already prejudged in affirmation. If there were an object $\sim$, then $\sim\sim p$ would say something other than $p$ (p. 63).
- 5.441 gives the same disappearance for quantifiers: $\sim(\exists x)\,.\,\sim fx$ says the same as $(x)\,.\,fx$ (p. 63). If a proposition is given, the results of all truth-operations on it are given with it (5.442, p. 63).
- **Primitive signs (5.45–5.4611).** If logic has primitive ideas, these must be independent of one another. If a primitive idea is introduced, it must be introduced in all contexts where it occurs, e.g. denial in $\sim p$, $\sim(p\vee q)$, $(\exists x)\,.\,\sim fx$ (5.451, p. 63). The introduction of a new expedient in the symbolism of logic must always be an event full of consequences; no new symbol may be introduced in brackets or in the margin with an innocent face. Wittgenstein says the words in the definitions of Principia Mathematica were not justified and are "not allowed" (5.452, p. 63–64).
- All numbers in logic must be capable of justification; rather, it must become plain that there are no numbers in logic, and there are no pre-eminent numbers (5.453). In logic there is no side by side and there can be no classification, so there cannot be a more general and a more special (5.454). The solution of logical problems must be simple, since they set the standard of simplicity (5.4541, p. 64).
- When the logical signs are rightly introduced, the sense of all their combinations comes with them. The proper primitive sign is the most general form of their combinations, not $p\vee q$ or $(\exists x)\,.\,fx$ (5.46, p. 64).
- $\vee,\supset$ need brackets, unlike real relations. This shows they are not the real primitive signs. "Logical operation signs are punctuations" (5.461–5.4611, p. 64).
- **General form.** It is clear, says Wittgenstein, that everything that can be said beforehand about the form of all propositions can be said on one occasion. All logical operations are already contained in the elementary proposition, since $fa$ says the same as $(\exists x)\,.\,fx\,.\,x=a$ (5.47, p. 65). Where there is composition there is argument and function, and where these are, all logical constants already are. One could say that the one logical constant is what all propositions, according to their nature, have in common; that is the general form of proposition (5.47, p. 65).
- The general form of proposition is the essence of proposition (5.471). Giving it gives the essence of all description, hence of the world (5.4711, p. 65). Describing the most general propositional form is describing the one and only general primitive sign in logic (5.472, p. 65).
- **Logic takes care of itself (5.473–5.4733).** A possible sign must also be able to signify, and everything possible in logic is permitted. "Socrates is identical" means nothing because there is no property called identical; the proposition is senseless because we have made no arbitrary determination, not because the symbol is in itself impermissible (5.473, p. 65). Later: it says nothing because no meaning has been given to "identical" as an adjective (5.4733, p. 66). In a certain sense we cannot make mistakes in logic (5.473, p. 65).
- Self-evidence, he says, can be discarded in logic only by language itself preventing every logical mistake. Logic is a priori in that we cannot think illogically (5.4731, p. 65). Occam's razor says that unnecessary elements of a symbolism mean nothing (5.47321, p. 65). *Frege says* every legitimately constructed proposition must have a sense. *Wittgenstein says* every possible proposition is legitimately constructed, and if it has no sense this can only be because we have given no meaning to some of its constituent parts, even if we believe we have (5.4733, p. 65–66).
- The number of necessary fundamental operations depends only on our notation (5.474). The task is to construct a system of signs of definite mathematical multiplicity, and to express a rule, not to list primitive ideas (5.475–5.476, p. 66).

### 5.5–5.52: the operation N, negation, generality
- **Definition.** Every truth-function is the result of successive application of the operation $(-----T)(\xi,\dots)$ to elementary propositions. It denies all propositions in the right-hand bracket, and he calls it their negation (5.5, p. 66). A bracketed expression with order indifferent is written $(\bar\xi)$, where $\bar\xi$ stands for all its values (5.501, p. 66). Its values may be given by:
  1. direct enumeration,
  2. a function $fx$ whose values for all $x$ are the propositions,
  3. a formal law constructing the propositions, in which case the terms form a formal series (5.501, p. 66–67).
- Wittgenstein writes $N(\bar\xi)$ for the negation of all values of the propositional variable $\xi$ (5.502, p. 67). With one value, $N(\bar\xi)=\sim p$. With two, $N(\bar\xi)=\sim p\cdot\sim q$ (5.51, p. 67).
- Wittgenstein asks how the all-embracing logic that mirrors the world can use such special catches and manipulations. He answers: only because all these are connected into an infinitely fine network, the great mirror (5.511, p. 67).
- **5.512.** *Question:* how can the stroke $\sim$ bring a false $p$ in the true $\sim p$ into agreement with reality? *Answer:* what denies in $\sim p$ is not $\sim$ but what all signs that deny $p$ have in common. That is the common rule constructing $\sim p,\ \sim\sim\sim p,\ \sim p\vee\sim p,\ \sim p\cdot\sim p,\dots$ (p. 67).
- We could say that what is common to all symbols asserting both $p$ and $q$ is $p\cdot q$, and for symbols asserting either $p$ or $q$ it is $p\vee q$. Two propositions are opposed when they have nothing in common. Every proposition has only one negative, since only one proposition lies altogether outside it (5.513, p. 67). So $q:p\vee\sim p$ says the same as $q$, and $p\vee\sim p$ says nothing (p. 67).
- In a fixed notation there are rules for building all propositions that deny $p$, all that assert $p$, and so on. These rules are equivalent to the symbols and mirror their sense (5.514, p. 67–68).
- It must be recognized in our symbols that what is connected by $\vee,\ \cdot$ must be propositions. This is the case, he says, because $p$ and $q$ presuppose $\vee,\sim$ (5.515, p. 68). *Wittgenstein asks* whether the sign of the negative proposition must be built from the sign of the positive, or whether the negative proposition could be expressed by a negative fact. *He replies* that even then the negative proposition is indirectly constructed with the positive. Each presupposes the other (5.5151, p. 68).
- **Generality.** If the values of $\xi$ are all values of $fx$, then $N(\bar\xi)=\sim(\exists x)\,.\,fx$ (5.52, p. 68). Wittgenstein separates the concept *all* from the truth-function. Frege and Russell tied generality to the logical product or sum, which makes $(\exists x)\,.\,fx$ and $(x)\,.\,fx$ hard to understand (5.521, p. 68).
- The generality symbol refers to a logical prototype, makes constants prominent, and occurs as an argument (5.522–5.523, p. 68). If objects are given, all objects are given. If elementary propositions are given, all of them are (5.524, p. 68).
- Rendering $(\exists x)\,.\,fx$ as "$fx$ is possible", as Russell does, is incorrect. Certainty, possibility and impossibility are shown by an expression being a tautology, a significant proposition or a contradiction (5.525, p. 69).
- The world can be described completely by completely generalized propositions, without from the outset co-ordinating any name with a definite object. To reach the customary way of speaking, we need simply add after an expression: there is one and only one $x$ which…, and this $x$ is $a$ (5.526, p. 69). Such a proposition is composite, since in $(\exists x,\varphi)\,.\,\varphi x$ we mention $\varphi$ and $x$ separately (5.5261, p. 69).
- The truth or falsehood of every proposition alters something in the world's general structure. The range left to that structure by the totality of elementary propositions is what the completely general propositions delimit (5.5262, p. 69).
- *Intuition note.* One operation, the joint denial of all the propositions in a bracket, is enough to build every truth-function (5.5). So $\sim$, $\vee$ and the generality symbols need no separate objects behind them (5.4, 5.52). They are ways of building propositions.

### 5.53–5.5352: identity
- Identity of object is expressed by identity of sign, and difference of objects by difference of signs, not by an identity sign (5.53, p. 69).
- Identity is not a relation between objects. $(x):fx\supset x=a$ says that only $a$ satisfies $f$, not that only things having a certain relation to $a$ do (5.5301, p. 69). Saying that only $a$ has that relation to $a$ would need the identity sign itself (p. 69).
- Russell's definition of $=$ fails because it cannot say that two objects share all properties. This proposition is significant even if never true (5.5302, p. 70). Roughly, saying of two things that they are identical is nonsense. Saying of one thing that it is identical with itself says nothing (5.5303, p. 70).
- Replacements (5.531–5.5321, p. 70):
  - $f(a,b)\cdot a=b$ becomes $f(a,a)$.
  - $f(a,b)\cdot\sim a=b$ becomes $f(a,b)$.
  - $(\exists x,y)\,.\,f(x,y)\,.\,x=y$ becomes $(\exists x)\,.\,f(x,x)$.
  - $(\exists x,y)\,.\,f(x,y)\,.\,\sim x=y$ becomes $(\exists x,y)\,.\,f(x,y)$.
  - "Only one $x$ satisfies $f$" becomes $(\exists x)\,.\,fx:\sim(\exists x,y)\,.\,fx\,.\,fy$.
- So the identity sign is not an essential constituent of logical notation (5.533). Apparent propositions such as $a=a$ and $(x)\,.\,x=x$ cannot be written in correct notation (5.534, p. 70).
- Problems about such pseudo-propositions disappear, including those from Russell's Axiom of Infinity. What the axiom is meant to say would be expressed in language by there being an infinite number of names with different meanings (5.535, p. 70).
- Russell rendered the nonsense $p$ is a proposition in symbols as $p\supset p$, and put it as a hypothesis before certain propositions to show that their argument places could only be occupied by propositions. For a non-proposition as argument the hypothesis becomes meaningless, not false (5.5351, p. 70–71). *Wittgenstein asks*, about the proposal to express that there are no things as $\sim(\exists x)\,.\,x=x$, whether it would not be true if there were things that were not identical with themselves (5.5352, p. 71).

### 5.54–5.5423: propositions in propositions, the subject
- In the general form, propositions occur in propositions only as bases of truth-operations (5.54, p. 71). Psychological forms such as "A thinks $p$" seem to relate $p$ to an object $A$, and Wittgenstein says modern epistemology (Russell, Moore) conceives them so (5.541, p. 71).
- He says they are really of the form "'$p$' says $p$". This co-ordinates facts by co-ordinating their objects, not a fact and an object (5.542, p. 71).
- So there is no soul or subject as in "contemporary superficial psychology". A composite soul would not be a soul (5.5421, p. 71). A correct account of "A judges $p$" must show that judging nonsense is impossible, which Russell's theory does not (5.5422, p. 71).
- Perceiving a complex is perceiving that its constituents are combined in such a way. This perhaps explains why a figure can be seen as a cube in two ways, for we see two different facts (5.5423, p. 71–72).

### 5.55–5.5571: elementary propositions and logic's limits
- We must answer a priori the question of all possible forms of elementary propositions (5.55, p. 72). An elementary proposition consists of names. Since we cannot give the number of names with different meanings, we cannot give the composition of the elementary proposition (p. 72).
- Every question that logic can decide can be decided without further trouble. If we get into a situation where we need to answer such a problem by looking at the world, this shows we are on a fundamentally wrong track (5.551, p. 72). The experience needed to understand logic is that something *is*, which is no experience. Logic precedes the "How", not the "What" (5.552, p. 72).
- *Wittgenstein asks* how logic could be applied if this were not so (5.5521, p. 72). He reports Russell's claim that there are simple relations between different numbers of things, and asks: between which numbers, and how would experience decide it? There is no pre-eminent number (5.553, p. 72). Any enumeration of special forms would be arbitrary (5.554, p. 73).
- 5.5541 says it should be possible to decide a priori whether one could get into a situation needing a sign of a 27-termed relation. 5.5542 asks whether we may ask this at all, whether a sign form can be set out without knowing if anything can correspond to it, and whether it makes sense to ask what must be in order that something can be the case (p. 73).
- We have a concept of the elementary proposition apart from its special form. Where symbols are built by a system, the system is what matters logically (5.555, p. 73). There cannot be a hierarchy of forms of elementary propositions. Only what we ourselves construct can we foresee (5.556, p. 73).
- Empirical reality is limited by the totality of objects, and the boundary reappears in the totality of elementary propositions. The hierarchies are and must be independent of reality (5.5561, p. 73).
- All propositions of our colloquial language are, just as they are, logically completely in order (5.5563, p. 73).
- **Application.** The application of logic decides what elementary propositions there are, and logic cannot anticipate that. Logic may not collide with its application but must touch it, so they may not overlap (5.557, p. 73–74). If elementary propositions cannot be given a priori, trying to give them must lead to obvious nonsense (5.5571, p. 74).
- *Intuition note.* Logic supplies the rules for combining elementary propositions, not a list of them. Which ones there are is decided in the application of logic, which logic cannot anticipate.

### 5.6–5.632: limits of language and world
- "The limits of my language mean the limits of my world." (5.6, p. 74). Logic fills the world, and the limits of the world are also its limits. We cannot therefore say in logic that this is in the world and that is not. That would apparently presuppose excluding certain possibilities, which cannot be the case, since otherwise logic would have to get outside the world's limits, that is, consider them from the other side too. "We cannot therefore say what we cannot think" (5.61, p. 74).
- This is the key to how far solipsism is true. What solipsism *means* is correct, but it cannot be said; it shows itself. That the world is my world shows itself in the limits of the language that only I understand meaning the limits of my world (5.62, p. 74).
- The world and life are one (5.621). "I am my world. (The microcosm.)" (5.63, p. 74).
- The thinking, presenting subject does not exist (5.631). In a book "The world as I found it", the subject alone could not be mentioned. This would show that in an important sense there is no subject (p. 74). The subject does not belong to the world but is a limit of the world (5.632, p. 74).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Show that $p\vee q$ and $p\cdot q$ arise from $N$ alone, and compute a probability by 5.15.

Column order (deduced from the 5.101 schema): $(p,q)$ = (T,T), (F,T), (T,F), (F,F). Check: $p$ is $(TFTF)$ and $q$ is $(TTFF)$.
- $p = T\,F\,T\,F$; $q = T\,T\,F\,F$.
- $N(p,q)$ is true only where both are false: $F\,F\,F\,T$ = "neither" (5.51; schema $(FFFT)$).
- $N(N(p,q))$ flips that: $T\,T\,T\,F$ = $p\vee q$, matching $(TTTF)$ (5.5 allows successive application).
- $N(p)=F\,T\,F\,T$ and $N(q)=F\,F\,T\,T$. Then $N(N(p),N(q))$ is true only where both are false: $T\,F\,F\,F$ = $p\cdot q$, matching $(TFFF)$.
- Probability by 5.15: let $r=p\vee q$, so $T_r=3$ (columns 1, 2, 3). Let $s=p$, with T's in columns 1 and 3. Both are T's of $r$, so $T_{rs}=2$. Hence $r$ gives $s$ the probability $2:3$.
- Check against 5.152: $r=p$ has $T_r=2$ (columns 1, 3). $s=q$ has T's in columns 1, 2, of which only column 1 is shared, so $T_{rs}=1$. The ratio is $1:2$, as 5.152 says for independent propositions.

## Concepts
- [[truth-function]] (new) — a proposition built from elementary propositions (its truth-arguments), whose truth-grounds can be tabulated as in 5.101; Wittgenstein says all propositions are truth-functions of elementary ones (p. 55–56).
- [[truth-ground]] (new) — a truth-possibility of the arguments that verifies the proposition; following is containment of truth-grounds (p. 56).
- [[truth-operation]] (new) — an operation producing a truth-function from elementary propositions as bases. It asserts nothing; only its result does (p. 60–61).
- [[joint-negation-N]] (new) — $N(\bar\xi)$, the negation of all values of $\xi$, from which every truth-function is built (p. 66–67).
- [[internal-relation]] (new) — a relation between propositional forms that exists with the propositions themselves and shows in their structure (p. 57, 60).
- [[tautology-contradiction-limits]] (new) — contradiction is the external limit of propositions, tautology their "substanceless centre". A tautology says nothing (p. 58).
- [[no-logical-constants]] (new) — $\sim,\vee$, etc. are not names of objects or relations. One could say that the one logical constant is the general form of proposition (p. 62–65).
- [[logical-probability]] (new) — the ratio $T_{rs}:T_r$ of shared truth-grounds, not a property of a proposition in itself (p. 58–59).
- [[identity-sign-eliminated]] (new) — identity is expressed by sign identity, so $=$ is not essential to logical notation (p. 69–70).
- [[saying-vs-showing]] (new) — what solipsism means cannot be said, but it shows itself (p. 74).
- [[limits-of-language]] (new) — the limits of my language mean the limits of my world (p. 74).

## Canonical instances
- [[sheffer-stroke-inference]] — $p\vee q,\ \sim p\ \therefore\ q$ rewritten with $\mid$ so the link shows (p. 57).
- [[urn-example]] — equal white and black balls, with replacement: probability as a statement about known circumstances (p. 59).
- [[socrates-is-identical]] — a sign-sequence that is senseless because "identical" has no meaning as an adjective (p. 65–66).
- [[russell-axiom-of-infinity]] — what it is meant to say would be expressed in language by there being infinitely many names with different meanings (p. 70).
- [[world-as-i-found-it]] — a hypothetical book that could not mention the subject (p. 74).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Joint negation $N$ generating all truth-functions ↔ logic: the NOR (Sheffer-type) functional completeness result — the same truth-table construction.
- [structural-analogy] Operation $O'$ applied successively, $[a,x,O'x]$ ↔ mathematics: recursive definition of a sequence from a starting term and a successor rule.
- [structural-analogy] Ratio of truth-grounds as probability ↔ probability theory: counting favourable cases among equally weighted possibilities.
- [metaphor] "The subject is a limit of the world" ↔ philosophy of mind: the eye is not part of the visual field.

## Questions this raises
- If every proposition is a truth-function of elementary ones, how does the "infinitely fine network" of 5.511 fit with the finite number of truth-operations in 5.32? What is the role of a "formal law" in 5.501?
- If probability is only a ratio of truth-grounds, how does that fit 5.154's remark that what experiment can verify is independence from circumstances one does not know closely?
- If elementary propositions cannot be given a priori (5.5571), what could count as discovering them in application?
- How does the claim that logic cannot say what is in the world (5.61) relate to the statement that solipsism is correct but unsayable (5.62)?
