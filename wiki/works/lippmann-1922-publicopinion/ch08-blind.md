# Blind Spots and Their Value

## L0 — One line
A stereotype saves effort and focuses energy because it hides some facts, but those blind spots can move to the centre of events and frustrate the people who rely on it.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how stereotypes work in history and politics. Stereotypes are the world we expect, not necessarily the world we want. What affects behaviour is the version of an idea that ordinary people carry, not the version a thinker wrote. Using the American idea of "progress" and the Great War, he shows that a stereotype works while it fits the facts. At some point it parts company with them. Then its blind spots (the facts it leaves out) may cause harm unless critics, leaders and a tolerant public respond. Several doctrines each have a point of "blind automatism", where effort is assumed to stop and things happen by themselves. Blind spots also tend to spare us hesitation and bewilderment.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype as a fixed mental picture (earlier chapters of the book).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A stereotype is the world we expect, not necessarily an ideal. When events match it, we get a sense of familiarity and feel we are moving with the movement of events (§1, para. 1).
- History is moved by the current versions of ideas held in individual minds, not by the systems that geniuses formulate (§1, paras. 2–3).
- A world-conquering idea must correspond, even roughly, to something. In England in 1820–1850, Progress was backed by striking material evidence from the mechanical revolution (§1, paras. 5–7).
- The American progress stereotype brought real gains: productive work, satisfaction, and a quantitative habit of thought. It also hid slums, overcrowding, and the need for war aims (§2, paras. 2–5).
- Because the images are simpler and more fixed than events, there is always a point where stereotype and facts part. Blind spots then move from the edge of vision to the centre (§2, para. 6).
- Doctrines swing in cycles. A shattered stereotype is likely to take with it what it wisely noticed (§3, paras. 1–2).
- Progress, laissez-faire (trusting free energies to bring harmony), collectivism (as held in the Marxian mind) and strong government each contain a spot of "blind automatism": a point where effort is assumed to stop and things happen by themselves (§3, para. 3).
- Blind spots tend to keep away distracting images that might cause hesitation, so the stereotype saves time and defends our position (§3, para. 4).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1 (stereotypes versus ideals; current versions)
- Lippmann says "stereotype" rather than "ideal". Ideal suggests something good to be copied. Our repertory also holds ideal swindlers, jingoes, agitators and enemies. The stereotyped world is the kind of world we expect (§1, para. 1).
- Events that match give a sense of familiarity. Examples: Athenians who want no qualms need their slave to be a slave "by nature". A golfer who claimed 95 but scored 110 tells friends he was not himself that day; Lippmann glosses this as not knowing the duffer who lost fifteen strokes (§1, para. 1).
- Without a few people in each generation who systematise stereotypes (Laws of Political Economy and so on), most of us would use a haphazard, shifting assortment. Writers on culture, tradition and "group mind" usually mean these perfected systems (§1, para. 2).
- Studying them is necessary, but the historian, politician and publicity man cannot stop there. What operates in history is "shifting imitations, replicas, counterfeits, analogies, and distortions" in individual minds (§1, para. 2).
- So Marxism is whatever the warring sects who claim faith believe. Christian history cannot be deduced from the gospels, nor American political history from the Constitution. One must go to the versions as understood and administered. There is some reciprocal influence, but current versions are what affect behaviour (§1, para. 3).
- Footnote: actual culture is hard to study. Busy people record beliefs only incidentally, and the student rarely knows how typical his data are. Perhaps the best he can do is follow Bryce: move among all sorts of people and seek unbiased persons skilled at sizing up. Much guesswork is involved, and scholars, who enjoy precision, often confine themselves to the neater formulations of other scholars (§1, para. 3, footnote).
- **A critic (Times Literary Supplement, per the footnote), quoted:** The critic ventures to prophesy that Relativity will follow Evolution. A technical theory becomes a general, vague term, is misunderstood, and spreads through inaccurate popular expositions. Some applications will be justified, some absurd, many truisms. The technical theory then returns to scientists (§1, para. 4). Footnote: Einstein said in 1921 that people tended to overestimate his theory's influence and to underestimate its certainty (§1, para. 4, footnote).
- Lippmann's addition: such a "world-conquering career" needs an idea to correspond, however imprecisely, to something (§1, para. 5).
- **Bury's view:** a speculative idea hardly penetrates the general mind until it has a concrete embodiment or striking material evidence. For Progress in England in 1820–1850 Bury says both conditions were fulfilled. Lippmann adds that the most striking evidence was the mechanical revolution (Bury lists steam navigation, gas lighting, the first railway). For the average householder such miracles formed the pattern of his belief in the perfectibility of the human race (§1, para. 5).
- Tennyson, on the first Liverpool–Manchester train (1830), thought the wheels ran in grooves. He wrote "ringing grooves of change" (§1, para. 6). A notion more or less applicable to one journey was generalised into a pattern of the universe "for ever" (§1, para. 7).
- That pattern gave evolution an optimistic turn. Bury says evolution is neutral between pessimism and optimism, but it promised continual change and the visible conquests of nature were striking. So the popular mind blended them, in Darwin and then Spencer, into "progress towards perfection" (§1, para. 7).

*Intuition note:* Ideas that travel are changed on the way. What people act on is the distorted, popular copy. Such an idea is unlikely to spread widely unless it matches something concrete or strikingly visible.

### 2 (the American progress stereotype and the war)
- The "progress" stereotype was composed fundamentally of mechanical inventions and has remained mechanical on the whole. In America, more than anywhere else, it has suffused the whole moral code. The fundamental stereotype is that small becomes big, slow becomes fast, poor becomes rich: "whatever is shall be more so" (§2, para. 1).
- Not every American holds it (Henry Adams did not; William Allen White does not). Those who preach it in the success magazines do (§2, para. 2).
- Its merits: an impersonal criterion, an earthly criterion, and a habit of thinking quantitatively. Its faults: it confuses excellence with size, happiness with speed, and human nature with contraption. Lippmann calls the love of the superlative "in essence and possibility a noble passion" (§2, para. 2).
- It fitted an extraordinary range of facts. It turned pugnacity, acquisitiveness and lust of power into productive work, and gave satisfaction in work, mating and play. Its near-perfect success meant that any challenge to it is called "un-American" (§2, para. 3).
- Yet it is partial. Americans saw expanding cities but not slums, census growth but not overcrowding. They also missed the drift from the land, unassimilated immigration, resource waste, and industrial relations. They were unprepared for the end of isolation and for the war (§2, para. 4).
- In the war, the stereotype produced "something like a physical miracle". Example: two million troops were transported and supplied overseas. Footnote (Mitchell): total goods production did not greatly rise over 1916, but war production did (§2, para. 5).
- But among those most affected by the stereotype there was no place for considering the fruits of victory, so aims were ignored or treated as automatic. For them victory meant only annihilation in the field. At Paris the pattern did not fit. In peace one can keep replacing big with bigger, but after absolute victory one cannot go on to a "more absolute" one. Without a new pattern the end was an anticlimax (§2, para. 5).
- There is always a point where stereotype and facts part, because images are simpler and more fixed than events. Blind spots then come from the edge of vision to the centre. Unless there are courageous critics, leaders who understand the change, and a people tolerant by habit, the stereotype may frustrate effort. Examples: those who cried for a Carthaginian peace in 1919 and deplored Versailles in 1921 (§2, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* A stereotype works like a spotlight. It is bright where it points and dark elsewhere. When events move into the dark part, the same focus that helped may now mislead.

### 3 (cycles of doctrine; blind automatism)
- Held uncritically, a stereotype censors much. When it is shattered, "likely as not" what it wisely noted is lost too. Shaw now sees Free Trade, laissez-faire and Darwinism as excuses for "doing the other fellow down" (§3, para. 1).
- **Lippmann's counterfactual:** a hundred years ago Shaw would not have seen it so. He would have seen the foolish doing the strong down, and government purposes obstructing invention and enterprise (§3, para. 1).
- In theory Shaw has turned right around against laissez-faire, though he is still none too eager for any guiding government he knows. Most advanced prewar thinking made the same turn. Since the war showed guiding governments with censors, propagandists and spies, Roebuck Ramsden and Natural Liberty have been readmitted to serious thinking (§3, para. 2).
- Common to each cycle: a point where effort ceases and things happen "of their own accord". The progressive stereotype, powerful to incite work, almost completely obliterates the attempt to decide what work and why. Laissez-faire assumes spontaneous movement to pre-established harmony. Collectivism seems, in the Marxian mind, to assume determinism toward efficient, wise officials. Strong government relies at last on the notion that all that matters to the governed will be known by the governors. Each has "a spot of blind automatism" (§3, para. 3).
- That spot hides a fact that would check the movement. If each believer faced it (what to do with time saved; human nature; how to secure officials; doubt of one's own inspiration), "more Hamlet and less Henry the Fifth" would result (§3, para. 4).
- Blind spots keep away distracting images and emotions that might cause hesitation. So the stereotype saves time, defends our position, and tends to preserve us from trying to see the world steadily and as a whole (§3, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Not seeing everything can spare us hesitation and so support confident action. The price is that those inside the stereotype tend not to notice what would check them.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town council uses the stereotype "bigger is better" and tracks only population. Population grows 20% a year; housing units grow by a fixed 20 per year.

| Year | Population | Housing units | Persons per unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1000 | 400 | 1000/400 = 2.50 |
| 1 | 1000×1.2 = 1200 | 420 | 1200/420 ≈ 2.86 |
| 2 | 1200×1.2 = 1440 | 440 | 1440/440 ≈ 3.27 |

Population rises 20% a year, which the council cheers. Crowding rises from 2.50 to about 3.27. It sits outside the stereotype, like the slums and overcrowding Americans did not see (§2, para. 4). If it keeps rising, it moves from the edge of vision to the centre.

## Concepts

## Questions this raises
