# The Transfer of Interest

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that a common public will can form when a vague, emotionally charged symbol is found to which many people with different private opinions can attach the same feeling, so the differences stay hidden.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how millions of people, each with private and shifting pictures of an unseen world, produce one "public opinion." He thinks the facts can be explained more convincingly without a mysterious national "oversoul." His answer is that emotion can, within limits, be transferred from one stimulus to another. A leader who finds a symbol (e.g. "Americanism") that each faction can read in its own way may be able to unite them, usually at the cost of blurring the real conflicts. He illustrates this with Hughes's 1916 speech, Wilson's Fourteen Points and Hamilton's role in American union. Such unity often rests on vagueness, and such symbols can fail or "burst."

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a pseudo-environment (the mental picture people act on) and of stereotypes; the basic idea of a conditioned response.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Vote counts do not show the mix of motives behind them. The only factor left after the contradictions cancel is a "least common denominator" (§1, paras. 3–5).
- Lippmann thinks unity can be explained more convincingly without an "oversoul" (§1, para. 6; §2, para. 1).
- In Hughes's 1916 speech, a leader tries to unify different factions by binding phrases, blurred words and silence on the points that cannot be compromised (§2, paras. 4–14).
- Vagueness at a crucial point in public debate is almost always a symptom of cross-purposes (§2, para. 15).
- In the starving-child case the emotion is first-hand, while the stimulus and the response are second-hand. Within limits, emotion can be moved to a new stimulus, such as a symbol (§3, paras. 3–5).
- Lippmann calls such phrases symbolic: they stand for a "truce or junction" between ideas. A leader or interest that masters the current symbols is master of the current situation, within limits (§3, para. 6).
- The Fourteen Points were a deliberate attempt to crystallize a common opinion across governments and peoples. In Lippmann's reading, at least some of the wording (e.g. point eight) was chosen with care to serve many interests at once (§4, paras. 1, 7–12).
- Going up a "hierarchy of symbols" lets you include more factions but loses meaning, and even the emotion grows thinner. At settlement everybody expected everything, and the treaty makers chose to realize the expectations of those with most power at home (§5, paras. 2–3).
- In Lippmann's American example, symbols were genuine where they were fed by actual experience; federation became a true symbol for those whose interests led them across state lines (§6, paras. 1–4).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §1 (unnumbered opening): the problem
- **Question.** People differ in contact points, stereotyped expectations and enlisted interest. How then does any common will, "National Purpose" or "Public Opinion" emerge from such private imagery? (§1, para. 1)
- **Harvey and 1917.** Ambassador Harvey stated confidently the motives of Americans in 1917, which differed from Wilson's. Lippmann says nobody can know what went on in thirty or forty million minds. The war was won by many efforts, driven in unknown proportions by Wilson's motives, Harvey's and hybrids of the two. A soldier who thought it a war to end war did think that (§1, para. 2).
- **Harvey and 1920.** Sixteen million voted Republican and nine million Democratic. Harvey said the vote was for or against the League of Nations. Lippmann replies that saying the League was the issue did not make it the issue. Many Southern Democrats voted to maintain the Southern social system, and pro- and anti-League Southerners voted the same ticket (§1, para. 3).
- **Republicans.** Republican voters ranged from irreconcilables to League advocates. They were also mixed up with motives such as business, taxes and wheat prices. With only two ways to express a hundred feelings, there is no certain way of knowing the decisive combination (§1, para. 4).
- **Result.** What remained after contradictions cancelled was the least common denominator: Democrats out, Republicans in. That sufficed to alter policy for four years. The reasons are unrecorded and keep changing, so Harding faces different opinions from those that elected him. Wilson, apparently elected on the cry that he kept the country out of war, led it into war within five months (§1, para. 5).
- **Explanations offered.** Some, following LeBon, stress the erratic working of opinion. Others infer a "collective soul" or "oversoul" because settled aims emerge from drift (§1, para. 6).

*Intuition note.* A vote is a coarse signal of many different wishes. The "will of the people" can be a thin residue, such as "change," that need not predict what the winners will actually do.

### §2: Hughes's speech, 31 July 1916
- Lippmann thinks the facts can be explained more convincingly without an oversoul, because campaigns routinely get differently minded people to vote alike. Hughes had to form a majority from groups split on Taft vs. Roosevelt, pro-German vs. pro-Allies, war vs. neutrality and Mexican intervention vs. non-intervention. Lippmann is interested only in the method, not the morality (§2, paras. 1–3).
- **Binders.** "Lincoln" and the phrase for the party of Lincoln transfer piety to the candidate and recall a common history, while the 1912 schism goes unmentioned (§2, para. 4).
- **Safe opening.** Hughes attacked the spoils system in diplomatic appointments, and "Deserving Democrats" was a discrediting phrase that he evoked at once. In Lippmann's account the opening avoided both 1912 and the 1916 conflicts (§2, para. 5).
- **Mexico.** Huerta: Hughes said Huerta was in fact the head of the Mexican government, then said recognition should follow "correct principles." Everybody believes in correct principles and believes he possesses them, so the issue blurs. Hughes described Wilson's policy as "intervention," a stretched word that covered both Wilson's acts and the interventionists' aims (§2, paras. 6–8).
- Detailed history (Tampico, Vera Cruz, Villa, Columbus, Carrizal) aroused no contrary passions. On the issue, "friendship" served non-interventionists and "new policy... firmness" served interventionists (§2, paras. 9–10).
- **Europe.** "American rights on land and sea" covered the pro-Allies (coerce Germany) and the pro-Germans (British sea power), who read it oppositely. The Lusitania, like the 1912 schism, was an obstacle to harmony. Hughes said he was confident there would have been no destruction of American lives by the sinking, and Lippmann says what cannot be compromised must be obliterated. Hughes was silent on the future of relations with Europe (§2, paras. 11–14).
- **Conclusion.** Public opinion built from divergent opinions is clouded, with a "neutral tint." Where superficial harmony is the aim and conflict the fact, obscurantism is the usual result. Almost always vagueness at a crucial point is a symptom of cross-purposes. Lippmann adds that Hughes did not invent this technic and did not use it with the utmost success (§2, para. 15).

*Intuition note.* Hughes did not resolve conflicts. He chose words each camp could read as its own and stayed silent where they could not be bridged.

### §3: why vague ideas unite deep feelings
- Our grip on the unseen environment is slight, though feeling may be intense. A report can arouse as much emotion as reality, and sometimes more, because the feeling can be set off by more than one stimulus. Pictures fade and are replaced by names or symbols, while the emotion continues (§3, paras. 1–2).
- Even careful thinkers use a shorthand of names, signs and samples. If they forget they simplified, they slip into verbalism (talking about names regardless of objects) (§3, para. 2).
- **Conditioned response.** An emotion attaches to many things, e.g. fear first to a danger, then to the idea of it, then to something similar. In one respect, human culture is an elaboration of stimuli and responses around a fairly fixed emotional core (§3, para. 3).
- **Starving child example.** People differ in susceptibility to distant ideas. The idea of a starving Russian child evokes a wish to feed the child, but the person can only give money to an organization or to "Mr. Hoover." So cognition and conation (the urge to act) are indirect. Only the emotion in the person is first-hand (§3, para. 4).
- **Transfer.** Within varying limits, emotion is transferable in both stimulus and response. If one man dislikes the League, another hates Wilson and a third fears labor, you may be able to unite them with a symbol such as Americanism, if it is the antithesis of what they all hate. Each reads it differently: isolation, rejecting a certain politician, resisting revolution. It means nothing particular but can attach to almost anything (§3, para. 5).
- **Symbols.** Declaring for Americanism, Progressivism and similar words aims to amalgamate factions that would split over a specific program. Feeling then flows toward conformity rather than scrutiny. Such phrases stand for "a sort of truce or junction between ideas," like a railroad center. Whoever masters the current symbols masters the current situation. Limits exist: in 1917 "Holy Russia" burst under suffering and defeat (§3, para. 6).

*Intuition note.* The feeling is real and personal, but within limits what triggers it can be swapped. A leader who wants to unite people looks for a trigger that works for many of them at once.

### §4: the Fourteen Points
- They were an attempt to knit together the "imponderables" of the war across allies, enemies and neutrals. Modern communication made this experiment possible (§4, para. 1).
- **Situation, end of 1917.** The Russian offensive failed (July) and the Bolshevik revolution followed (November). The French had a severe defeat in Champagne, and England had submarine losses, Flanders and Cambrai. War weariness was widespread (§4, para. 2). Morale, the organization of perception by official propaganda, was breaking down, and people searched for new attachments (§4, para. 3).
- Brest-Litovsk showed that negotiation seemed possible. Censorship hid most doubt, but Lord Lansdowne's speech drew a response. The old war symbols had lost their power to unify (§4, para. 4). Central Europe saw similar cross-cutting divisions (§4, para. 5).
- Allied engagements, including secret treaties published by the Bolsheviks, ran against the popular idea of the war. A footnote notes that Wilson said he had not heard of the treaties until Paris. Lippmann finds this perplexing, since the text of the points could not have been formulated without knowing them (§4, para. 6).
- **Design.** Joint Allied action, the ideal response, proved impossible at the October conference. By December Mr. George and Wilson were each moved to respond independently, and Wilson chose a statement of peace terms under fourteen heads. Numbering gave a business-like impression, and "peace terms" rather than "war aims" offered an alternative to Brest-Litovsk (§4, para. 7). The terms had to unify the Allies and satisfy each nation without making it a "catspaw," and they also had to be the terms of a possible peace, so that, if the German center and left were ripe for agitation, they would have a text to use against the governing class. Lippmann says no one man may have had all these considerations in mind, but all the men concerned had some of them in mind (§4, paras. 8–10).
- **Points.** The first five and the fourteenth might be described as popular generalizations everyone then professed to believe. Point three was aimed at the Paris Economic Conference. Point six answered Russian suspicion, and point seven (Belgium) was unqualified (§4, paras. 10–11).
- **Point eight (Alsace-Lorraine)**, in Lippmann's reading (§4, para. 11):
  - "Righted," not "restored," left room for a plebiscite, since it was not certain that all the French would fight on indefinitely for reannexation if offered a plebiscite, and even less certain that the English and Italians would.
  - "Prussia" reminded South Germans that the territory was not theirs.
  - "1871" was the date of the grievance as remembered. French officialdom planned to bring the Saar in under the name "Alsace-Lorraine," meaning that of 1814–15. By insisting on 1871 the President was, in Lippmann's reading, defining the boundary, adverting to the secret treaty and casting it aside.
- **Point nine.** "Clearly recognizable lines of nationality" was what the Treaty of London lines were not (§4, para. 12).

### §5: unanimity without agreement
- Enthusiasm did not mean agreement on a program. The phrases stood for opposing ideas but evoked a common emotion, which helped rally the western peoples for the desperate ten months of war still ahead (§5, para. 1).
- The points concerned an invisible future, so conflicts of interpretation stayed hidden. Harmonization is a hierarchy of symbols. Higher up you include more factions and may keep the emotional link while losing the intellectual one, but even the emotion grows thinner. In the balloon image you see far and wide but "see very little." Private meanings are read in, so "whatever you want badly is the Rights of Humanity." Wilson's phrases were understood in endlessly different ways, and no negotiated public document existed to correct the confusion. At settlement "everybody expected everything," and the European treaty authors chose to realize the expectations of those of their countrymen with the most power at home (§5, para. 2).
- The treaty makers came down to the Rights of France, Britain and Italy. They kept symbols with roots in constituents' imagination, such as France, and did not risk anything for Europe. The distinction between an omnibus like Europe and a symbol like France is not sharp. The scope of unifying ideas has swelled and shrunk through history. Lippmann says it is "probably true" that real integration has increased (§5, para. 3).

*Intuition note.* The higher the symbol, the more people it holds and the less it says. Hence, at settlement, everybody expected everything and only some expectations were realized.

### §6: American union before 1789
- In the decade before 1789 most men, it seems, felt their state was real and the confederation unreal. State symbols were fed by experience from childhood, work and residence (§6, para. 1).
- Their experience, not their needs, set the limit. Needs (common defense, an economic regime) matched the larger environment. The confederation was an "omnibus," not a symbol, and the harmony an omnibus creates among divergent groups is transient (§6, para. 2).
- Some classes' personal interests crossed state lines, and for them federation became a real symbol. Hamilton, born in the West Indies, had no attachment to one state. He traded the capital's site for two votes (White and Lee) to get the assumption of state debts. For White and Lee, the symbol of their province was the highest political entity they served (§6, para. 3).
- "In the crystallizing of a common will, there is always an Alexander Hamilton at work." (§6, para. 4)

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town of 100 voters has three groups. A (40 people) dislike the League, B (30) hate the president, C (30) fear labor.
- A specific program such as "reject the League" is supported by A only: 40 of 100, which is below a majority of 51.
- A symbol, "Americanism," is read by A as isolation, by B as rejecting that politician and by C as resisting revolution. Each group accepts it, so 40 + 30 + 30 = 100 of 100 support the candidate.
- The unity is real as emotion but empty as program. Once policy is chosen, e.g. "isolation," B and C get nothing specific from the symbol. This is one way such a symbol can come under strain.

## Questions this raises
- If vague symbols hide conflicts, how can a public debate still reach real decisions?
- When does a symbol "burst," and can one predict it?
- Can specific language ever build a common will, or only vague language?
- Does the least-common-denominator reading apply to all elections, or only to some?
