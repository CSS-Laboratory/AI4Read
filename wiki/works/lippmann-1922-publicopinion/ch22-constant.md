# The Constant Reader

## L0 — One line
Readers tend to judge a newspaper by how it treats the part of the news they feel involved in, so papers hold readers with features beyond general news; and Lippmann argues that tracing the press's faults to "Big Business" alone is a logical error.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks what holds a newspaper's readers and why reporting has not become a respected profession. His answer: no contract binds readers. Each reader tends to test the paper against his own experience and cannot check most other news. So papers add local, personal and entertainment features to keep a steady public. Reporting is poorly paid and has not developed strong professional traditions. Lippmann rejects Upton Sinclair's view that capitalist ownership is the deeper cause, because the anti-capitalist press is also unreliable and Sinclair ignores it. He believes the matter leads to the question of what news is.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype (a ready-made picture in the head); basic newspaper economics (advertising versus circulation).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Readers are bound to a paper by no bond; a paid subscription is, Lippmann believes, not a great factor for a big daily. Their loyalty depends on how they feel, or on habit. Everything turns on this constancy (§1, paras. 1–2).
- Each reader tends to judge a paper, if at all, by its handling of events he feels involved in. Lippmann asks what better criterion the breakfast-table reader has (§1, para. 2).
- Few readers can test the accuracy of distant news. General readers have no legal standing over misleading news (§1, paras. 3–4).
- News a reader cannot test is judged by other standards than accuracy, for example fit with stereotypes and interest (§1, para. 5).
- Papers hold readers with local "diary" content, society gossip and other features. Political and social news alone does not sustain circulation (§2, paras. 1–6).
- The editor's income depends on advertisers, who pay for an effective group of readers. General news quality is not in itself sufficient to make very large numbers of readers discriminate among dailies (§2, para. 7).
- Straight reporting is poorly paid and has not developed corporate traditions in sufficient measure. Lippmann holds that this economics does not go "to the root" (§2, para. 8; §3, para. 1).
- Lippmann argues that Sinclair's "Brass Check" diagnosis (that those who write and print the papers sell the truth to Big Business) commits a logical error: it ignores the anti-capitalist press. He believes the matter leads to the question of what news is (§3, paras. 2–6).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §1 The reader's constancy
- Lippmann says a newspaper's buyer is bound by no bond. A paid subscription is, he believes, not a great factor for a metropolitan daily. The reader alone judges his loyalty daily (§1, para. 1).
- Everything turns on constancy, yet no tradition reminds the reader of it. His loyalty depends on feeling or habit, and often on obscure elements (§1, para. 2).
- The most important element: each person tends to judge a paper, if at all, by its treatment of news in which he feels involved. Examples are his business, church or party (§1, para. 2).
- If the paper satisfies him on what he thinks he knows, it is "fairly certain" to escape violent criticism from him (§1, para. 2).
- Lippmann's rhetorical question: what better criterion does the breakfast-table reader have than agreement with his own opinion? The implied answer is that he has none. So most men tend to hold the paper most strictly accountable as "special pleaders" on matters of their own experience, not as general readers (§1, para. 2).
- Only the interested party can usually test a report. If news is local and there is competition, the editor will probably hear from the man who feels misrepresented. This corrective weakens as the subject recedes into the distance. The only people who can correct what they think is a false picture of themselves printed in another city are members of groups well enough organized to hire publicity men (§1, para. 3).
- Law gives a general reader no standing if he thinks he is misled. Only an aggrieved party can sue for libel or slander, and must prove material injury. Lippmann reads this as embodying the tradition that general news is not of common concern, except for matter called immoral or seditious (§1, para. 4).
- Footnote: this is not a plea for censorship. Lippmann says competent, preferably non-official tribunals to sift charges of untruthfulness might be a good thing (§1, para. 4).
- News read without a personal criterion is judged by "some other standard" than accuracy. To the reader it is indistinguishable from fiction. He accepts it if it fits his stereotypes, and keeps reading if it interests him (§1, para. 5).
- Footnote: Lippmann notes that Sinclair shows no indignation at socialist papers, even those as unfair to employers as certain papers he cites are to radicals (§1, para. 5).

*Intuition note:* A reader can usually test only the news he already knows. So he tends to trust or distrust the whole paper mostly on that small sample. For news he cannot test, he judges by other standards than accuracy, such as fit with his stereotypes and interest.

### §2 What holds readers, and the economics of the press
- Some papers, even in big cities, assume readers want to read about themselves: weddings, funerals, lodge meetings, birthdays (§2, para. 1).
- **Greeley's formula** (letter of 3 April 1860 to "Friend Fletcher", about to start a country paper): the subject of deepest interest to an average person is himself, next his neighbours. Chronicle anything of interest to a dozen families, such as a new church, a farm sale or a mammoth beet (§2, paras. 2–3).
- Lippmann takes from James Melvin Lee the phrase "the printed diary of the home town" for this role. He says every newspaper must in some measure fill it. Large cities have small local dailies on Greeley's pattern, perhaps twice as many as general dailies in Manhattan and the Bronx (§2, para. 4).
- Some people find their own lives dull and, like Hedda Gabler, want a thrilling one. For them some papers carry the lives of imaginary people, with whose "gorgeous vices" they can safely identify. Hearst's interest in high society caters to people who never hope to join it. In great cities, Lippmann says, "the printed diary of the home town" tends to be the printed diary of a smart set (§2, para. 5).
- City dailies carry distant news to citizens, but political and social news does not primarily hold circulation. Interest in it is intermittent. The readers these features hold together are, so far as big news is concerned, not able to be critical, and in big news competition in any one community is not very serious. Press services standardize the main events, and scoops are rare. There is apparently not a very great reading public for such massive reporting as that of the *New York Times* (§2, para. 6).
- So most papers go beyond general news to differentiate themselves: scandal, crime, sports, pictures, advice columns, women's pages, comics, partisanship. They do so, Lippmann says, not because publishers and editors are interested in everything but news, but because they must hold the alleged host of passionately interested readers whom some critics of the press suppose to be "clamoring for the truth" (§2, para. 6).
- **Editor's position:** his enterprise rests on "indirect taxation" levied by advertisers on readers. Advertisers pay for his skill in holding an effective group of customers. These customers judge by private experience and stereotypes, having no independent knowledge of most news. If their judgment is not unfavourable, the editor is at least within range of a circulation that pays. But general news quality, especially on public affairs, is not in itself sufficient to make very large numbers of readers discriminate among dailies (§2, para. 7).
- **Reporters:** reporting, the theoretical foundation, is the worst-paid and least-regarded branch. Able men enter it by necessity or for experience, meaning to leave. Rewards go to specialty work, signed editorial-quality correspondence, executives and men with a distinctive knack. Lippmann says this is due, no doubt, to what economists call the "rent of ability", and that this principle operates with "peculiar violence" in journalism, so that newsgathering does not attract as many trained and able men as its public importance would seem to demand (§2, para. 8).
- Lippmann thinks the chief reason straight reporting has not developed such corporate traditions in sufficient measure is that able men take it up intending to leave it as soon as possible. Such traditions give pride of craft, raise admission standards, punish breaches and help men insist on their status (§2, para. 8).

*Intuition note:* The paper must keep a steady crowd of readers, because advertisers pay for that crowd. General news alone does not hold the crowd well enough, so papers add features that do. Lippmann says this left-handed relation between papers and public information is reflected in the low pay of reporters.

### §3 Sinclair's "Brass Check"
- Lippmann says economics does not go to the root. Stopping there would be a "false determinism". The intrinsic power of the reporter appears so great, and so many very able men pass through the work, that Lippmann concludes there must be some deeper reason why, comparatively speaking, so little serious effort has gone into raising reporting to the level of medicine, engineering or law (§3, para. 1).
- **Sinclair's view** (Lippmann notes that Belloc makes practically the same analysis for English newspapers): the "Brass Check" is in the pay envelope of those who write and print the papers. It is the price of selling the "fair body of truth" to Big Business (§3, paras. 2–3).
- Lippmann reads this as saying that a body of known truth and well-founded hopes is prostituted by a more or less conscious conspiracy of rich owners. His test: if the theory is correct, the fair body of truth would be inviolate in a press not connected with Big Business. If such a press failed to contain it, something would be wrong with Sinclair's theory (§3, para. 4).
- Such a press exists, yet Sinclair does not tell readers to subscribe to the nearest radical paper. He proposes a subsidized "National News" with a board "of all creeds or causes". Lippmann asks why, and answers: Sinclair "cannot convince anybody, not even himself" that the anti-capitalist press is the remedy for the capitalist press (§3, para. 5).
- Sinclair ignores the anti-capitalist press in both theory and proposal. Lippmann calls collecting unfairness from one set of papers, ignoring another set, and blaming their one supposed common trait a "gross logical error". Lippmann says that to blame "capitalism" you are compelled to prove that the faults do not exist except where capitalism controls (§3, para. 5).
- Lippmann says one would have supposed that the inability to take any non-capitalist paper as a model of truthfulness and competence would make Sinclair and those who agree with him look more critically at their assumptions. They would then have asked themselves, for example: where is the fair body of truth that Big Business prostitutes but anti-Big Business does not seem to obtain? That question, Lippmann believes, leads to the question of what news is (§3, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Lippmann's point is about method. A cause is not shown by looking only where the fault is expected. If papers outside Big Business are also unfair, Sinclair's theory is in trouble, since he would have to show the faults exist only where capitalism controls.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A baker reads a paper with 10 stories. Two concern baking, which he knows. Eight concern distant places.
- Paper A gets both baking stories right: he rates it 2/2 accurate and trusts it. Only 2 of 10 stories (20%) were tested; 8 (80%) were not.
- Paper B gets one baking story wrong: he rates it 1/2 and may drop it, whatever its other 8 stories say.
- Even if Paper A's other 8 stories were all false, his trust would not change, since he cannot check them. The reader's test is indifferent to most of the paper's content, which is Lippmann's point.

## Questions this raises
- If general news alone does not hold readers, what then is news, the question Lippmann's last sentence points to?
- How would a tribunal, preferably non-official, judge fairness in news without becoming censorship?
- Does the own-experience test differ for local and distant news, as the passage on competition suggests?
- Is Lippmann's test of Sinclair's theory fair, given that Sinclair's theory claims a link to Big Business specifically?
