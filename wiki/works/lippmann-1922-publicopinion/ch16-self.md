# The Self-Centered Man

## L0 — One line
Democratic theory inherited the old premise that governing is a natural endowment, so that spontaneous politics works only within the ruler's direct and certain knowledge, but modern record, measurement and continuous reporting now make it feasible to bring the unseen environment into judgment (§3).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why is there so little literature on where public opinions come from, and why did democratic theory leave their sources unexamined? Lippmann answers that early democrats, like Aristotle, took the art of government to be a natural endowment, and held that spontaneous politics works only within the ruler's direct and certain knowledge. Their ideal of universal dignity clashed with that "science", so they treated the voice of the people as a mystery. He argues that measurement, record, comparative analysis and continuous reporting now make it feasible to bring the unseen environment into judgment, though the reporting is often done badly. This matters because better opinion depends on self-criticism: we must watch how our own opinions are put together.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school civics (democracy, electorate, popular sovereignty); a rough idea of Aristotle as an ancient political thinker.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Public opinion is treated as the prime mover of democracy, yet the sources and processes that form it are little studied; the literature covers government machinery (§1, para. 1).
- Lippmann holds that a still more momentous question than how to turn individual opinion into public action is how to validate our private versions of the political scene (§1, para. 2).
- Opinion based on exact record and analysis (realistic opinion) takes time, money, labor and patience, and it frustrates what makes casual opinion feel interesting or dramatic. Unless a conviction grows in the community that prejudice and intuition are not enough, it will not find enough support; that conviction grows as self-criticism increases (§1, para. 2).
- Almost every political theory has an inscrutable element that goes unexamined in its heyday; for the democrats it was popular sovereignty. They were asserting human dignity in a bitter struggle, and admitting human fallibility would have opened a breach in their defenses because aristocrats exploited it (§1, para. 4; §2, paras. 1–2).
- Democrats and aristocrats shared Aristotle's premise that governing is a natural endowment. On either side, it was no part of political science to ask how knowledge of the world reaches the ruler (§2, paras. 3–4).
- Spontaneous politics works only within the ruler's direct knowledge. The democrats' hope that all men in a very large environment could govern therefore conflicted with their own science (§3, paras. 1–2).
- What changed is the invention of measurement, record, evidence rules and psychological correction, so that continuous reporting of an unseen environment (the part of the world beyond direct knowledge) is feasible, though the beginnings are crude and often badly done (§3, paras. 4–5).
- For the political thinkers who counted, speculation revolved around the self-centered man who had to see the whole world through a few pictures in his head (§3, para. 6).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### § 1 — A neglected question
- Public opinion is supposed to be the prime mover in democracies, yet the literature is relatively thin. Good books exist on government and parties, that is, on the machinery that in theory registers opinions after they are formed. Relatively little exists on the sources and processes from which opinions arise (§1, para. 1).
- American political writers have been most interested either in making government express the common will or in preventing it from subverting government's purposes; by their traditions they wished either to tame opinion or to obey it (§1, para. 1).
- **Hart's view (quoted):** the most momentous question of government is how to transmit individual opinion into public action (§1, para. 1, footnote).
- **Lippmann replies:** a more momentous question is how to validate our private versions of the political scene (§1, para. 2).
- Lippmann sees a prospect of radical improvement by developing principles already in operation, as he says he will try to show later. That development depends on how well we learn to use knowledge of how opinions are put together to watch over our own as they form (§1, para. 2).
- Casual opinion is the product of partial contact, tradition and personal interests. It "cannot in the nature of things take kindly" to a method of political thought based on exact record, measurement, analysis and comparison (§1, para. 2).
- The mental qualities that decide what seems interesting, important, familiar, personal and dramatic are the ones that realistic opinion, in the first instance, frustrates (§1, para. 2).
- Realistic opinion takes time, money, labor, conscious effort, patience and equanimity. Unless a conviction grows in the community at large that prejudice and intuition are not enough, working it out will not find enough support. That conviction grows as self-criticism increases (§1, para. 2).
- Judging by the oldest and most powerful democracies, they have made a mystery of opinion. Skilled organizers of opinion understood the mystery well enough to create majorities on election day, yet political science regarded them as "low fellows" or "problems", not as holders of the most effective knowledge on how to create and operate opinion. The tendency of students, orators and editors was to look on opinion as men in other societies looked on uncanny forces (§1, para. 3).
- Almost every political theory has an inscrutable element left unexamined in its heyday: Fate, a divine monarchy, a class of the better born. The more obvious angels, demons and kings have gone out of democratic thinking, but the need to believe in reserve powers of guidance persists. Eighteenth-century democrats, in the doctrine of popular sovereignty, found the answer to their need of an infallible origin for the new social order (§1, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Lippmann sees better opinion as depending on how well we use knowledge of how opinions are put together, including our own. Little has been written on this, and democracies made a mystery of opinion. Realistic opinion is hard work and works against the habits of mind that make casual opinion feel interesting and dramatic.

### § 2 — Why the mystery was kept
- The early democrats were practical politicians in a bitter struggle. What drove them was the assertion of human dignity, not whether John Smith had sound views on a public question (§2, para. 1).
- Every analyst of human fallibility seemed to degrade that dignity. Aristocrats exploited these observations, so admitting any truth in them would have opened "a breach in the defenses" (§2, paras. 1–2).
- As Aristotle insisted the slave was a slave by nature, democrats had to insist the free man was a legislator and administrator by nature. They could not stop to say that a soul might not yet have, or might never have, this technical equipment and yet keep an inalienable right not to be used as an unwilling instrument (§2, para. 2).
- So they insisted that reasoned righteousness welled up spontaneously. All of them hoped so, many believed it, though the cleverest, like Jefferson, had private reservations. One thing was certain: if public opinion did not come forth spontaneously, nobody in that age believed it would come forth at all (§2, para. 3).
- Their science had the same major premise as Aristotle's: the art of government is a natural endowment. Men differed radically on who is so endowed, but agreed that the greatest question was to find those in whom political wisdom is innate (§2, para. 3).
  - Royalists said kings.
  - **Hamilton (paraphrased):** strong minds exist in every walk of life, but the representative body, with too few exceptions to influence the spirit of government, will be composed of landholders, merchants and men of the learned professions (§2, para. 3).
  - Jefferson thought God deposited the political faculties in farmers and planters, and sometimes spoke as if they were found in all the people (§2, para. 3).
- In deciding who was fit to govern, knowledge of the world was taken for granted: the aristocrat believed those who dealt with large affairs had the instinct; the democrats held that all men had it and could therefore deal with large affairs. In either case it was no part of political science to think out how knowledge of the world could be brought to the ruler. Those for the people did not try to work out how to keep the voter informed; a good heart, a reasoning mind and balanced judgment, ripening with age, were what counted. Lippmann: "Men took in their facts as they took in their breath" (§2, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Both sides argued about who is born wise. In neither case did political science ask how knowledge of the world reaches the ruler. In their struggle, the democrats felt they had to defend human dignity by claiming natural ability.

### § 3 — The limit of direct knowledge, and what changed
- Effortless facts are limited to the customs and obvious character of one's own place. The outer world had to be conceived, and men did not conceive it instinctively or absorb trustworthy knowledge of it just by living. Spontaneous politics was therefore possible only in an environment confined within the ruler's direct and certain knowledge. Lippmann says there is no escaping this conclusion wherever government is founded on the natural range of men's faculties (§3, para. 1).
- **Aristotle (quoted):** if citizens are to judge and distribute offices by merit they must know each other's characters; otherwise elections and lawsuits "will go wrong" (§3, para. 1).
- Class government could claim that its small ruling class knew each other. Democrats faced a huge ruling class, the male electorate (§3, para. 2).
- Their science said politics is an instinct that works in a limited environment. Their hopes said all men in a very large environment could govern. The way out, "without much discussion", was to assume the voice of the people is the voice of God (§3, para. 2).
- They could not explain how a Bostonian could conceive a Virginian's views, or Congressmen have opinions about China or Mexico. Then few men could bring an unseen environment into their judgment. There were some advances since Aristotle (a few newspapers, books, perhaps better roads, better ships) but no great advance, so eighteenth-century political assumptions had essentially to be those of the preceding two thousand years. The pioneer democrats lacked the material to resolve the conflict between the known range of attention and their faith in human dignity (§3, para. 3).
- The assumptions antedated the modern press, press services, photography and film. More significantly they antedated measurement and record, quantitative and comparative analysis, canons of evidence, and psychological correction of a witness's prejudice (§3, para. 4).
- **Lippmann's caution:** he does not say our records are satisfactory, our analysis unbiased or our measurements sound. He says the key inventions for bringing the unseen world into the field of judgment have been made; they had not been made in Aristotle's time and were not yet important enough to be visible to political theory in the age of Rousseau, Montesquieu or Jefferson (§3, para. 4).
- Lippmann thinks a later chapter will show that even the English Guild Socialists took their deeper premises from the older system (§3, para. 4).
- That older system, whenever competent and honest, had to assume that no man could have more than a very partial experience of public affairs. In the sense that he can give only a little time to them, that assumption is still true and of the utmost consequence. But ancient theory was compelled to assume also that the available attention would have to be confined to matters close at hand (§3, para. 5).
- Lippmann says it would have been visionary then to suppose that a time would come when distant, complicated events could be reported, analyzed and presented so that an amateur could make a really valuable choice. That time is now in sight. There is no longer any doubt that continuous reporting of an unseen environment is feasible. It is often done badly, but the fact that it is done at all shows it can be done, and the fact that we begin to know how badly it is done shows it can be done better (§3, para. 5).
- With varying degrees of skill and honesty, distant complexities are already reported every day: by engineers and accountants for businessmen, secretaries and civil servants for officials, intelligence officers for the General Staff, and some journalists for some readers. Lippmann calls these crude beginnings but radical. He calls them as radical as the change in the scale of human life that lets Mr. Lloyd George discuss Welsh coal mining after breakfast in London and the fate of the Arabs before dinner in Paris (§3, para. 5).
- This possibility "breaks the spell" on political ideas. Thinkers who built without seeing that range of attention is the main premise "built on sand". For the thinkers who counted, speculation revolved around the self-centered man seeing the world through a few pictures in his head (§3, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Lippmann presents the old assumptions as ones that competent, honest theory had to make, given the tools of the time. His claim is that a time is now in sight when an amateur could make a really valuable choice about distant, complicated events, because continuous reporting is feasible, though crude and often badly done.

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* Aristotle's maxim says citizens must know each other's characters. Count the acquaintance pairs this needs: $n(n-1)/2$.
- $n=5$: $5\cdot4/2 = 10$ pairs.
- $n=50$: $50\cdot49/2 = 1225$ pairs.
- $n=1000$: $1000\cdot999/2 = 499{,}500$ pairs.

The need grows far faster than the electorate. The old premise therefore works in a small group, and in a large electorate knowledge must come by reports, not by acquaintance.

## Questions this raises
- If realistic opinion needs effort and self-criticism, what motivates ordinary people to supply them?
- Who checks the reporters of the unseen environment, and how do we tell good reporting from bad?
- Lippmann says still-valid limits of time remain. How much can an amateur judge, given little time?
- Why were opinion organizers seen as "problems", though they knew how opinion is made?
