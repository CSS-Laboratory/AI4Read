# Stereotypes (Lippmann, *Public Opinion*, ch. 6)

## L0 — One line
We see only a small part of the world, and for the most part we define first and then see: we pick out what our culture has already defined and tend to perceive it in a stereotyped (ready-made, standard) form.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how what people bring to events shapes their reports. Our opinions reach beyond what we can observe, and even eyewitnesses seem to add something of their own. In untrained observation we notice signs, name the type, and then fill in the picture from stored images. These ready-made pictures of types, supplied by culture, are stereotypes. They save effort, and dropping all of them would impoverish life, since some regularities are real. What matters is their character and how gullibly we use them. Art, fiction, photographs and film help supply them. Lippmann also warns against explaining national or group differences by inborn "minds" before tradition, schooling and unequal experience have been ruled out.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school level; percentages.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Opinions cover more space and time than anyone can observe, so they are pieced together from reports and imagination (§1, ¶1).
- A report is a joint product of knower and known. The observer is always selective and usually creative (§1, ¶2).
- For the most part we define first and then see: culture supplies the definitions, and we tend to perceive what we picked out in the form our culture gave it (§1, ¶5).
- Göttingen test: forty presumably trained observers wrote reports of a staged brawl. Lippmann says a majority saw a scene that had not taken place, and reads this as their brawl stereotypes filling in the scene (§1, ¶¶7–10).
- If we cannot fully understand people's acts until we know what they think they know, we must appraise both their information and the minds that filtered it, since accepted types intercept information on its way to consciousness. Americanization, superficially at least, is a swap of European for American stereotypes (§2, ¶1).
- Stereotypes save effort in a hurried, distant modern life. Dropping all of them would impoverish life; what matters is their character and how gullibly we use them, and a philosophy that sees each mind as catching only phases of the world leads us to hold them lightly (§3).
- Film and photographs give ready-made, seemingly real images, and Lippmann says film imagery is evoked by the words people read in newspapers (§4, ¶¶1–2).
- Talk of "group minds" invites serious confusion unless instinctive equipment is separated from learned stereotypes. Until differences in nurture (upbringing and training) are discounted, judgment about differences of nature must be withheld (§5, ¶¶1–2).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Seeing is partly making
- **Limits of observation.** Each person lives in a small circle and sees at best "a phase and an aspect" of any wide-reaching public event. This holds for insiders who draft treaties as much as for those they govern. Opinions therefore cover more than we observe and are pieced together from others' reports and imagination (§1, ¶1).
- **Even eyewitnesses.** Experience seems to show that the eyewitness brings something to the scene which he later takes away from it, and that oftener than not his account is a transfiguration of the event. A footnote summarises a reviewer of Locard's book. Witness credibility varies by witness, event and type of perception. Touch, smell and taste have low evidential value. Words not actually heard will be supplied by the witness, in perfect sincerity, to fit his theory of the conversation. Visual judgments of distance and number are error-prone, and the untrained observer's sense of time is highly variable (§1, ¶2, footnote).
- **Lippmann's conclusion:** few facts in consciousness *seem* merely given, and most *seem* partly made. A report is the joint product of knower and known. The observer's role is "always selective and usually creative", and the facts we see depend on where we stand and on the habits of our eyes (§1, ¶2).
- **Unfamiliar scenes (James's phrase; Dewey's examples).** An unfamiliar scene is like the baby's world, and Dewey says a really new thing strikes an adult in the same way: as "one great, blooming, buzzing confusion". Dewey's examples are foreign speech, a crowded street for the countryman, and a factory for the newcomer. Strangers of another race proverbially look alike to the visiting stranger, and an outsider sees only gross differences in a flock of sheep that the shepherd individualises. For Dewey, giving things meaning means adding definiteness and stability to what is vague (§1, ¶3).
- **Definitions depend on who supplies them.** Dewey's example is "metal". The experienced layman's definition would probably include smoothness, weight, hammerability and resistance to decay. The chemist is likely to ignore these and define metal as an element that combines with oxygen to form a base (§1, ¶4).
- **Main claim.** For the most part, Lippmann says, we do not first see and then define: "we define first and then see." We pick out what our culture has already defined and tend to perceive it in the form stereotyped for us (§1, ¶5).
- **Clemenceau at Paris (Lippmann's questions and claim).** Lippmann asks what one would find in Clemenceau's mind, and whether he saw the Germans of 1919 or the German type as learned since 1871. He says Clemenceau saw the type and took to heart the reports that fitted it, and, "it seems", only those. A blustering junker counted as an authentic German. A labour leader confessing the empire's guilt did not (§1, ¶5).
- **Göttingen experiment** (reported via von Gennep and van Langenhove). During a psychology congress, before presumably trained observers, a staged scene lasted about twenty seconds. A clown rushed in, chased by a man with a revolver, who fired after the clown fell. The President asked for immediate written reports and forty came in (§1, ¶¶6–9).
  - Errors on principal facts: 1 report under 20%; 14 at 20–40%; 12 at 40–50%; 13 over 50%.
  - In 24 accounts 10% of the details were pure inventions; the share was exceeded in 10 accounts and lower in 6.
  - The source's verdict: ten reports false (a quarter), 24 half legendary, 6 near exact evidence.
- **Lippmann's reading.** More than a majority of trained observers saw a scene that had not taken place. They saw "their stereotype of such a brawl". In 34 of 40, stereotypes preempted at least one-tenth of the scene (§1, ¶10).
- **Art supplies shapes (Berenson, extended by Lippmann).** Berenson says things would scarcely have outlines clear enough for us to recall at will "but for the stereotyped shapes art has lent them". Lippmann adds that the shapes also come from moral codes, social philosophies and political agitation, and says that if "politics", "business" or "society" replace "art" in Berenson's passage, the sentences are no less true. Berenson's example is the Renaissance canon of the human figure, which people "had perforce" to see in that way (§1, ¶¶11–12).

*Intuition note:* We do not receive the world like a camera. We recognise a type, such as a brawl, a German or a sunset, and the stored picture of that type fills the gaps. Lippmann reads the Göttingen reports this way, even though the observers were presumably trained.

### 2. How stereotypes govern vision
- If we cannot fully understand people's acts until we know what they think they know, then to do justice we must appraise both their information and the minds that filtered it, since accepted types intercept information on the way to consciousness (§2, ¶1).
- **Americanization** is, superficially at least, replacing European stereotypes with American ones. If the "inoculation" succeeds, the change of mind is a change of vision (§2, ¶1).
- **Voice: a "kindly gentlewoman"** (quoted from Bierstadt). She asks what can be hoped for the Americanism of a man with a London tailor, or whose breath smells of garlic. Lippmann uses her to show how much weight stereotypes carry (§2, ¶1).
- **The Melting Pot pageant** (told by Lippmann's friend). Foreign-born workers in native costume were led by "Uncle Sam" up into a wooden pot and out the other side in American clothes, singing the Star-Spangled Banner. To the promoters, and probably most of the actors, it seemed they had expressed the most intimate difficulty to friendly association between older and newer Americans; Lippmann adds that the contradiction of their stereotypes interfered with full recognition of common humanity. People who change their names want to change the attitude of strangers (§2, ¶¶2–3).
- **A slight connection is enough.** A reporter who expects radicals to have odd hair will see a bobbed and bearded audience when only two heads are bobbed and four faces bearded, because to a hurried observer a slight connection is enough (§2, ¶4).
- **The landscape example.** A man who has rarely looked at a landscape except to judge it for building lots, but has seen landscapes in parlour paintings, recognises a rosy sunset as a landscape. Two days later he will probably recall chiefly a parlour landscape. In turn, painters and Japanese viewers will have seen and remembered more of the forms they had learned, unless they are the very rare people who find fresh sight for mankind (§2, ¶¶4–5).
- **Summary of the mechanism.** In untrained observation we pick recognisable signs from the environment. The signs stand for ideas, and we fill the ideas out from our stock of images. We notice that a thing is "man" or "sunset" and then see chiefly what our mind already holds (§2, ¶5).

*Intuition note:* A few cues are enough to call up a whole stored picture. What we later remember is then often the picture rather than the scene.

### 3. Economy, and what matters about stereotypes
- Seeing everything freshly and in detail is exhausting and, among busy affairs, practically impossible. Among friends and close associates there is no substitute for individual understanding. We admire those who know us rather than our classification, because classification serves some purpose not necessarily ours (§3, ¶1).
- Modern life is hurried, and distance separates people in vital contact, such as employer and employee or official and voter. We notice one trait (agitator, plutocrat, Harvard man) and fill in the rest from the stereotype (§3, ¶2).
- Lippmann calls the influences that create and maintain the repertory of stereotypes the most subtle and pervasive of all. We are told about the world before we see it, and, unless education has made us acutely aware, these preconceptions govern the whole process of perception deeply. They make the slightly familiar seem very familiar and the somewhat strange sharply alien. Small signs, from a true index to a vague analogy, call them up (§3, ¶3).
- Because there are uniformities in the environment that are accurate enough, and attention must be economised, abandoning all stereotypes for a wholly innocent approach "would impoverish human life". Without practical uniformities there would be no economy and only error in the habit of accepting foresight for sight (§3, ¶3).
- **What matters** is the character of stereotypes and the gullibility with which we use them. These depend on our philosophy of life (§3, ¶4).
  - If we assume the world is codified by a code we possess, we are likely to report a world run by our code.
  - If our philosophy tells us that each person's intelligence catches at best only phases and aspects of the world, as in a coarse net, we tend to know our stereotypes are only stereotypes, to hold them lightly and modify them gladly. We also tend to see when, where and why our ideas arrived.
  - "All useful history is antiseptic in this fashion": it enables us to know which fairy tale, school book, tradition or novel planted a preconception in this mind or that.

*Intuition note:* Lippmann does not argue for abolishing stereotypes, since he says dropping all of them would impoverish life. He points instead to awareness: we tend to hold them lightly when we know they are stereotypes and realise where they came from.

### 4. Censors, pictures and the cinema
- Would-be censors of art do not underestimate the influence of fiction on perception, but they generally misunderstand it. Like Plato on the poets, they feel vaguely that types acquired through fiction tend to be imposed on reality (§4, ¶1).
- Lippmann says there can be little doubt that the moving picture is building up imagery that is then evoked by the words people read in newspapers, and that nothing in the race's experience has aided visualisation comparably. Earlier sources were limited: Giotto's frescoes for the Florentine, temples for the Athenian. He adds, with "perhaps", that sparse portraiture in the East may have reduced the faculty of practical decision. In the West, secular description has grown enormously (§4, ¶1).
- Photographs now hold the authority the printed word once had. They seem utterly real and to come "without human meddling". On screen, observing, describing, reporting and imagining are done for you (§4, ¶2).
- **Example: *Birth of a Nation*.** Lippmann says a hazy notion of the Ku Klux Klan takes vivid shape. The shape may be historically wrong and morally pernicious, but he doubts that anyone who has seen the film and knows no more about the Klan than Mr. Griffiths will hear the name again without seeing "those white horsemen" (§4, ¶2).

*Intuition note:* A film is a stereotype-maker that needs almost no effort from the viewer. Its images may later be called up by words.

### 5. "Group minds" versus stereotypes
- Speaking of "the French mind" or "the bolshevik mind" makes us liable to serious confusion unless we separate instinctive equipment from the stereotypes, patterns and formulae that build the mental world. Failure to make this distinction accounts for a great deal of loose talk about collective minds, national souls and race psychology (§5, ¶1).
- A stereotype transmitted by each generation to the next may seem almost biological. Lippmann cites Wallas that in some respects we may indeed have become "biologically parasitic upon our social heritage". Yet there is "not the least scientific evidence" that people are born with their country's political habits. Where political habits are alike in a nation, look first to nursery, school and church. Until you have thoroughly failed to see tradition being handed on by parents, teachers, priests and uncles, Lippmann calls it a solecism of the worst order to ascribe political differences to the germ plasm (§5, ¶1).
- Lippmann says it is possible to generalise tentatively, with decent humility, about comparative differences within the same category of education and experience, yet even that is tricky, since no two experiences are exactly alike (the older son never has the younger's experience). Until differences in nurture are discounted, judgment about differences of nature must be withheld. His analogy is comparing yields of soils in Labrador and Iowa without knowing how they were treated (§5, ¶2).

*Intuition note:* Before blaming "nature", check what people were taught. Lippmann's claim is about withholding judgment until nurture is discounted, not a proof that no inborn differences exist.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Clemenceau-style filtering. A statesman receives 10 reports from a country. 6 describe a blustering official (fits his "type"); 4 describe a conciliatory labour leader (does not fit).
- Actual share of bluster: 6/10 = 60%.
- If he keeps only reports that fit his type, he keeps 6 of 6 = 100%, and his picture is "everyone there blusters".
- The 4 conciliatory reports are dismissed as "not authentic".
- The share of bluster in what he keeps (100%) is higher than in what arrived (60%), with no new facts. The shift comes from the filter, not the world.

## Questions this raises
- How can we test whether a belief is a stereotype or an accurate generalisation, given that Lippmann says stereotypes can track real uniformities?
- Can the Göttingen result be replicated, and how do its percentages depend on how errors were counted?
- If film and photographs feel more real than text, does the "remedy" of historical awareness work for images?
- How would we separate nurture from nature in practice, as Lippmann demands in §5?
