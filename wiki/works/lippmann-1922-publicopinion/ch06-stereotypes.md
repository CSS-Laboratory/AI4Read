# Stereotypes (Lippmann, *Public Opinion*, Ch. 6)

## L0 — One line
We do not first see and then define the world; we mostly define first and then see, so culturally supplied "stereotypes" shape what we perceive, remember and report (§1).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks why people's pictures of events differ from the events. His answer: nobody can observe more than a small part of the world, and even eyewitnesses add something of their own. We pick out what our culture has already defined for us and fill the rest in from stored images, which he calls stereotypes. A staged brawl at a psychology congress shows this: 40 trained observers wrote reports, and most contained large errors. Stereotypes save effort and are often roughly right. What matters is their character and how gullibly we use them. Art, cinema and schooling supply them. Talk of "group minds" confuses learned stereotypes with inborn nature. (§1–§5)

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Opinions reach beyond direct observation.** Each person sees only a small circle and "a phase and an aspect" of any big event, even insiders. Opinions must be pieced together from reports and imagination. (§1)
- **A report is a joint product of observer and observed.** The observer's role is "always selective and usually creative", so many "facts" in consciousness are partly made. (§1)
- **"We define first and then see."** In the blooming, buzzing confusion of the world we pick out what our culture has defined and perceive it in the stereotyped form. Clemenceau accepted only reports that fitted his "German type". (§1)
- **Göttingen experiment.** Forty trained observers saw a staged brawl. Only one report had under 20% errors on the main facts, and 13 had over 50%. Lippmann's reading is that they saw their stereotype of a brawl. (§1)
- **Stereotypes intercept information before it reaches consciousness.** To understand people's acts we must judge both their information and the minds that filtered it. Americanization, the Melting Pot pageant and the "landscape" example show a change of stereotypes as a change of vision. (§2)
- **Stereotypes are economical, not simply bad.** Fresh, detailed seeing of everything is exhausting and impossible in hurried modern life. Some practical uniformities exist, so dropping all stereotypes would "impoverish human life". Intimate relations still need individualized understanding. (§3)
- **What matters is their character and our gullibility.** These depend on our philosophy of life. If we know we see only "phases and aspects", we hold stereotypes lightly. History that traces where our ideas came from works as an "antiseptic". (§3)
- **Media supply the images.** Censors vaguely feel that types acquired through fiction tend to be imposed on reality, although they generally misunderstand this influence. The cinema, whose photographs seem to come "without human meddling", gives imagery that later words in newspapers evoke. *Birth of a Nation* is the example. (§4)
- **Do not confuse stereotypes with inborn "group mind".** Shared political habits should first be explained by nursery, school and church. We cannot judge differences of nature until we discount differences of nurture. (§5)

### §1 — The limits of observation and the claim "define first, then see"

**Starting point.** Each of us lives on a small part of the earth's surface, moves in a small circle, and knows few people well. Of any public event with wide effects we see "at best only a phase and an aspect". This holds for the insiders who draft treaties and laws as much as for those who receive them. (§1)

**Consequence.** Our opinions cover a bigger space, a longer time and more things than we can observe directly. They must be pieced together "out of what others have reported and what we can imagine". (§1)

**Even the eyewitness is not naïve.** (§1)
- A footnote gathers research on witness credibility. It cites Locard and a review of his book in the *Times Literary Supplement* of 18 Aug 1921.
- Credibility varies by class of witness, class of event and type of perception.
  - Touch, smell and taste have low evidential value.
  - Hearing is "defective and arbitrary" about the source and direction of sound.
  - In listening to talk, witnesses supply unheard words in good faith and arrange the sounds to fit their own theory of what the talk was about.
  - Vision also errs in identification, recognition, judging distance and estimating numbers, for example the size of a crowd.
  - The untrained sense of time is very variable.
- Memory tricks and the "incessant creative quality of the imagination" add to these weaknesses.
- Münsterberg's *On the Witness Stand* is mentioned as a popular book on the subject.

**Main claim.** The eyewitness brings something to the scene and takes it away again. What he thinks is an account of the event is often a "transfiguration" of it. Lippmann's formulation:
- Few facts in consciousness are "merely given"; most are "partly made".
- A report is "the joint product of the knower and known".
- The observer's role is "always selective and usually creative".
- "The facts we see depend on where we are placed, and the habits of our eyes." (§1)

> **Intuition note.** Seeing is not a camera. What you report is your input plus what you already carried in. Different habits of the eye give different "facts".

**The unfamiliar as confusion.** (§1)
- William James is quoted: a new scene is like the baby's world, "one great, blooming, buzzing confusion".
- Dewey says any new thing strikes an adult this way, as far as it is really new and strange.
- Dewey's examples:
  - a foreign language heard as babbling;
  - the countryman in a crowded street;
  - the landlubber at sea;
  - the ignoramus watching experts play a complicated game;
  - the inexperienced man in a factory;
  - strangers of another race, who "proverbially look alike" to an outsider;
  - a flock of sheep, where an outsider sees only gross differences while the shepherd sees each one.
- Dewey's conclusion: acquiring meaning means introducing (1) definiteness and distinction and (2) consistency or stability of meaning into what is vague and wavering.

**Who introduces the definiteness matters.** (§1)
- Dewey's example is the word *metal*.
  - The experienced layman would define it by smoothness, hardness, glossiness, brilliancy and heavy weight for its size, and by serviceable properties: it can be hammered and pulled without breaking, softened by heat and hardened by cold, it keeps its shape, and it resists pressure and decay.
  - The chemist would ignore these aesthetic and utilitarian qualities. He would define a metal as "any chemical element that enters into combination with oxygen so as to form a base".

**The central sentence.** "For the most part we do not first see, and then define, we define first and then see." In the confusion of the world we pick out what our culture has defined for us and tend to perceive it in the form stereotyped for us. (§1)

**Clemenceau at Paris.** (§1)
- Lippmann asks how many of the men at the Paris peace conference could see the Europe about them rather than "their commitments about Europe".
- He asks whether Clemenceau's mind held images of the Europe of 1919 or "a great sediment of stereotyped ideas" built up in a long, pugnacious life.
- Clemenceau saw "the German type" as he had learned to see it since 1871. Of the reports from Germany he took to heart only those that fitted the type.
- Examples: a blustering junker was an authentic German. A labor leader confessing the empire's guilt was not.

> **Intuition note.** The stereotype works as a filter on evidence. Reports that fit are "authentic"; reports that do not fit are discarded. The stereotype then looks confirmed.

**The Göttingen experiment.** (§1) The source is a footnote citing von Gennep and van Langenhove.
- Setting: a Congress of Psychology; presumably trained observers.
  - A clown ran into the hall, chased by a man with a revolver. They fought in the middle of the room, the clown fell, the other fired, and both rushed out.
  - It lasted about twenty seconds.
  - It was staged and photographed in advance.
- The President asked everyone to write a report at once, because a judicial inquiry was said to be sure. Forty reports came in.
- Error on the principal facts:

| Share of mistakes | Number of reports |
|---|---|
| under 20% | 1 |
| 20%–40% | 14 |
| 40%–50% | 12 |
| over 50% | 13 |

- Invented details: in 24 accounts 10% of the details were pure inventions. The share was exceeded in 10 accounts and lower in 6. "Briefly a quarter of the accounts were false."
- The cited classification: the 10 false reports are "tales and legends"; 24 are half legendary; 6 approximate exact evidence.
- Lippmann's reading:
  - More than a majority of forty trained observers saw a scene that had not taken place.
  - It is easier to say what happened than to invent what did not, so what they saw was "their stereotype of such a brawl".
  - Life-long stored images of brawls "flickered before their eyes". In one man they displaced under 20% of the scene, in thirteen men over half.
  - In 34 of 40 observers the stereotypes pre-empted at least one-tenth of the scene.

> **Intuition note.** Even trained, motivated observers, writing minutes after the event, fill gaps with a ready-made script. Training does not remove the filter.

**Berenson on art.** (§1)
- Berenson, an art critic, says things would scarcely have clear, recallable outlines for us "but for the stereotyped shapes art has lent them".
- Lippmann widens this. The shapes come not only from painting, sculpture and literature but also from moral codes, social philosophies and political agitations.
  - Replace "art" with "politics", "business" or "society" in Berenson's passage and it stays true.
- Berenson's passage:
  - Unless years of study have taught us to see "with our own eyes", we mould what we look at into the forms of the one art we know.
  - That is our standard of artistic reality. Shapes we cannot match in our "paltry stock of hackneyed forms and tints" make us blame the painter for failing to show things "as we know they certainly are".
- Berenson adds that:
  - we dislike a painter who "does not visualize objects exactly as we do";
  - medieval art is hard to appreciate because "our manner of visualizing forms has changed in a thousand ways".
- A footnote on Dante's Virgil:
  - We dress Virgil as a Roman with a "classical profile".
  - Dante's image of him was probably as medieval as his whole idea of the poet. Fourteenth-century illustrators show him as a medieval scholar in cap and gown.
- Berenson on the human figure: Donatello and Masaccio created a new canon of the figure and features, which the Humanists sanctioned. It presented the ruling classes with the type of person most likely to "win the day". No one had the power to break through this standard of vision, so people "had perforce to see things in that way and in no other".

### §2 — Stereotypes filter information; illustrations

**Main point.** To understand the acts of others we must know what they think they know. So we must judge both the information available to them and "the minds through which they have filtered it". Accepted types, current patterns and standard versions "intercept information on its way to consciousness". (§2)

**Americanization.** (§2)
- It is, superficially at least, replacing European stereotypes with American ones.
  - A peasant who saw his landlord as lord of the manor and his employer as the local magnate is taught to see them by American standards.
  - When the "inoculation" succeeds this is a change of mind and so a change of vision: "His eye sees differently."
- A gentlewoman quoted (via Bierstadt, *New Republic*, 1 June 1921) says clothes, food and breath affect one's "Americanism".
  - Her examples are a London tailor, sauerkraut and Limburger cheese, and garlic on the breath.
  - Lippmann uses her to show how strongly stereotypes can govern acceptance of the "brotherhood of man".

**The Melting Pot pageant.** (§2) A friend of Lippmann's saw it on the Fourth of July in an automobile town with many foreign-born workers.
- A huge wooden and canvas pot stood at second base of a baseball park, with steps up to the rim on two sides.
- Workers of all the foreign nationalities in the factories entered in native costume, singing national songs, dancing folk dances and carrying the banners of Europe.
- The master of ceremonies was the grade-school principal dressed as Uncle Sam. He led them up the steps and into the pot, and called them out on the other side.
- They came out in derby hats and standard American clothes (stiff collar, polka-dot tie), each "undoubtedly" with an Eversharp pencil, singing the *Star-Spangled Banner*.
- Lippmann's reading:
  - To the promoters it expressed "the most intimate difficulty" to friendly association between older and newer Americans.
  - The contradiction of their stereotypes blocked recognition of "common humanity".
  - People who change their names know this: they want to change themselves and strangers' attitude toward them.

**Some real link, but a hurried observer needs only a slight one.** (§2)
- Example: two bobbed heads and four beards in a radical audience make it a "bobbed and bearded audience" for a reporter who already expects it.
- The connection between our vision and the facts exists, "but it is often a strange connection".

**The landscape example.** (§2)
- A man has looked at land only to judge its building-lot potential, but he has seen landscapes hanging in the parlor. From them he learned that a landscape is a rosy sunset or a country road with a church steeple and silver moon.
- In the country he sees no landscape for hours. Then the sun sets, rosy, and he calls it beautiful.
- Two days later he will most likely remember chiefly a parlor landscape.
- Lippmann's comments:
  - He did see a sunset (unless drunk, dreaming or insane), but he saw and remembered more of what the oil painting taught him than an impressionist painter or a cultivated Japanese would have taken away.
  - Painter and Japanese in turn see more of the form they learned, except for the "very rare people who find fresh sight for mankind".
  - In untrained observation we pick recognizable signs from the environment. The signs stand for ideas, and "these ideas we fill out with our stock of images".
  - We notice that the thing is "man or sunset" and then see chiefly what our mind is already full of on those subjects.

> **Intuition note.** Perception is mostly recognition of a category, followed by filling in from memory. The "filling in" is also what we remember later.

### §3 — The economy of stereotypes, and how to hold them

**Economy.** (§3)
- Trying to see everything "freshly and in detail" rather than as types is exhausting and, among busy affairs, practically impossible.
- With friends, close associates or competitors there is no shortcut: individualized understanding is needed.
- The people we love and admire most have minds "peopled thickly with persons rather than with types". They know us rather than our classification.
- Intuitively, all classification serves some purpose not necessarily our own. No association has "final dignity" unless each takes the other as an end in himself. Any contact lacking the axiom of personal inviolability of both is tainted.

**Modern life.** (§3)
- It is hurried and multifarious, and physical distance separates people in vital contact (employer and employee, official and voter). There is no time or opportunity for intimate acquaintance.
- So we notice one trait of a well-known type and fill in the rest from stereotypes: "He is an agitator… an agitator is this sort of person, and so he is this sort of person."
- Lippmann's list of labels: intellectual, plutocrat, foreigner, "South European", from Back Bay, Harvard Man (versus Yale Man), regular fellow, West Pointer, old army sergeant, Greenwich Villager, international banker, from Main Street.

**The repertory of stereotypes.** (§3)
- The subtlest and most pervasive influences are those that create and maintain the repertory.
- "We are told about the world before we see it. We imagine most things before we experience them."
- Such preconceptions, unless education has made us acutely aware, govern the whole process of perception. They:
  - mark objects as familiar or strange, exaggerating the difference, so the slightly familiar looks very familiar and the somewhat strange sharply alien;
  - are aroused by small signs, from a true index to a vague analogy;
  - once aroused, flood fresh vision with older images and project into the world what memory has resurrected.

**Why not abandon them?** (§3) If the environment had no practical uniformities, the habit of "accepting foresight for sight" would give no economy and only error. But uniformities exist that are accurate enough, and the need to economize attention is inevitable. So abandoning all stereotypes for a wholly innocent approach "would impoverish human life".

> **Intuition note.** Stereotypes are a trade-off: cheap in attention and often roughly right, but they cause error exactly where the individual differs from the type.

**What matters.** (§3)
- Not whether we have stereotypes, but their character and "the gullibility with which we employ them". These depend on our philosophy of life.
  - If we assume the world is codified according to a code we possess, our reports describe a world run by our code.
  - If we think each person is a small part of the world whose intelligence catches only phases and aspects "in a coarse net of ideas", we know our stereotypes are only stereotypes. We hold them lightly and modify them gladly.
  - We also tend to see when, where and how our ideas started and why we accepted them.
- "All useful history is antiseptic in this fashion": it shows which fairy tale, school book, tradition, novel, play, picture or phrase planted a given preconception in a given mind.

### §4 — Censors, the cinema and photographs

**Censors.** (§4)
- Those who wish to censor art do not underestimate its influence, though they generally misunderstand it and are "absurdly bent" on stopping others from finding anything they have not sanctioned.
- Like Plato on the poets, they feel that types acquired through fiction tend to be imposed on reality.

**The moving picture.** (§4)
- It is steadily building up imagery that is then evoked by the words people read in newspapers. No aid to visualization in the race's history compares with the cinema.
- Earlier eras had few pictured objects. A Florentine saw saints in church frescoes standardized by Giotto, and an Athenian saw gods in temples.
- In the East, where the spirit of the second commandment was widely accepted, portraiture of concrete things was even more meager, and "perhaps" for that reason the faculty of practical decision was reduced. (Lippmann's tentative suggestion.)
- In the West, over the last few centuries, secular description grew enormously: word picture, narrative, illustrated narrative, the moving picture and, "perhaps", the talking picture.

**Photographs.** (§4)
- They have today the authority over imagination that the printed word had yesterday and the spoken word before. They seem utterly real and seem to come "directly to us without human meddling".
- Words or an inert picture need an effort of memory before a mental picture exists. On the screen, observing, describing, reporting and imagining are already done for you, "without more trouble than is needed to stay awake".
- Example: your hazy notion of the Ku Klux Klan takes vivid shape when you see *Birth of a Nation* (the source writes "Mr. Griffiths"). The shape may be historically wrong and morally pernicious, but it is a shape. Lippmann doubts that anyone who has seen it and knows no more about the Klan than the film-maker will hear the name again without seeing "those white horsemen".

> **Intuition note.** The more effortless and "unmediated" a medium seems, the more power its images have to become our stereotypes.

### §5 — Stereotypes versus "group minds" and inborn character

**Distinction.** (§5) When we speak of "the French mind, the militarist mind, the bolshevik mind", we confuse things unless we separate the instinctive equipment from the stereotypes, patterns and formulae that build the mental world to which native character adapts. Failure to do so causes "oceans of loose talk about collective minds, national souls, and race psychology".

**Caveats and the argument.** (§5)
- A stereotype may be transmitted so consistently from parent to child that it looks almost biological.
- Lippmann cites Graham Wallas (*Our Social Heritage*): in some respects we may be "biologically parasitic upon our social heritage".
- But there is "not the least scientific evidence" that people are born with the political habits of their country.
- Where political habits are alike in a nation, look first to the nursery, school and church, not to "Group Minds and National Souls".
- Until you have thoroughly failed to see tradition handed on by parents, teachers, priests and uncles, ascribing political differences to the "germ plasm" is "a solecism of the worst order".

**Comparing groups.** (§5)
- One can generalize tentatively and humbly about differences within the same category of education and experience, but even this is tricky. Almost no two experiences are exactly alike, not even for two children in one household: the older son never has the experience of being the younger.
- So "until we are able to discount the difference in nurture, we must withhold judgment about differences of nature."
- His analogy: judging the productivity of two soils from their yield without knowing which is in Labrador and which in Iowa, or whether each has been cultivated, enriched, exhausted or left wild.

> **Intuition note.** Before explaining group differences by "nature", rule out that people were taught different pictures of the world.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)

**Clemenceau-style filtering, by hand.** Suppose 10 reports arrive from a country. The reader's stereotype says "people there are bluster-and-boast types".

- Of the 10 reports, 6 describe a blusterer and 4 describe a conciliatory person.
- The true share of blusterers in the sample is 6/10 = 60%.
- The reader accepts only reports that fit the type. He accepts the 6 fitting reports and discards the 4 others, so he sees 6 blusterers out of 6 accepted reports: 6/6 = 100%.
- Gap between belief and sample: 100% − 60% = 40 percentage points.
- Next year the stereotype is "confirmed" again, because the 4 conciliatory reports are always labelled "not authentic". The stereotype is never tested.

What this shows: a filter on evidence can turn a majority (60%) into a unanimity (100%) without any lie, and without the believer noticing. It parallels the junker and the labor leader in §1.

**Göttingen arithmetic check.** 1 + 14 + 12 + 13 = 40 reports; reports with at least 40% error: 12 + 13 = 25, and 25/40 = 62.5%, which is a majority (§1). Also 24 + 10 = 34 reports with at least 10% invented details, matching "thirty-four out of the forty" (§1).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] "We define first and then see" ↔ cognitive psychology: schema-driven perception, where prior knowledge fills in missing input — both say perception combines input with stored structure.
- [structural-analogy] Clemenceau accepting only fitting reports ↔ communication theory: selective exposure and confirmation of existing views — both are a filter that keeps incoming information in line with prior belief.
- [structural-analogy] Stereotypes as templates organizing experience ↔ communication theory: Goffman's frame ("what is going on here?") and Entman's framing — both treat interpretation as shaped by a prior pattern.
- [structural-analogy] The cinema's effortless, accumulating imagery (§4) ↔ communication theory: cultivation — both claim repeated, consistent media images shape beliefs about social reality.
- [structural-analogy] Opinions pieced together from reports plus imagination, with stereotypes carried "in our heads" ↔ communication theory: pseudo-environment — Lippmann's own idea, close to this chapter's logic, but the chapter does not use the term.
- [metaphor] History as "antiseptic" (§3) ↔ media literacy: tracing the source of a belief to loosen its hold.

## Questions this raises
- If stereotypes are partly accurate and economical, how can we tell when a particular stereotype has become "gullible" use rather than useful use?
- The Göttingen experiment was staged and the observers were told a "judicial inquiry" was likely. Would repeated or group reporting change the error rates or the invented details?
- Lippmann says cinema photographs seem to come "without human meddling". How does that perceived directness make images more or less open to challenge than words?
- Can "antiseptic history" (§3) actually reduce stereotype use, or does it only change which stereotypes we hold?
