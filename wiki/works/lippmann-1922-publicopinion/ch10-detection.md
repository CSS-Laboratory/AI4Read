# The Detection of Stereotypes (Lippmann, Public Opinion, ch. 10)

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that stereotypes distort our pictures of space, time, numbers, causes and values, and he points to signs that help us spot these distortions.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How can we tell when a stereotype is replacing the facts? Lippmann points to warning signs. Contradictory absolute principles are often a good clue. Ignoring practical distances is another. Using the wrong time-scale, or picking dates to suit our mood, is a third. Generalising from a casual sample is a fourth. Assuming that things that arrive together, or feel alike, are causally linked is a fifth. Craving absolutes ("forever", "one hundred percent") is a sixth. Many examples come from the war and politics. Public opinion is built from such stereotyped pictures, so these signs can help us criticise it. His contrast case is the Sheffield random-sample survey, which shows how costly honest sampling is.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype as a picture in the head (earlier chapters); basic notions of random sampling and of correlation versus cause.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Absolute principles invoked one after another, with contradictions between them, are often a good clue to false absolutism. Treated as eternal, such principles censor objections, isolate the issue and arouse inappropriate emotion (§1).
- Stereotypes can blind us to space. The 1918 demand for an "Eastern Front" ignored 5,000 miles of poor railway (§2). Practical space means available transport, not geometric distance (§3).
- Time is misjudged in the same way: the long will, the amending clause, the 99-year contract. Wisdom includes choosing the time-scale that fits the matter (§4–§6).
- A telescopic time-scale is not "more true" than a microscopic one; the right choice depends on the practical purpose (§5). Lippmann says arguments from history and "race" often rest on a start date chosen to fit the view or mood (§6).
- The future is called the most illusive time of all: the temptation is to skip steps, or to slide from hope to probability. James's "faith ladder" shows the slide (§7–§8).
- Our usual generalisations about groups are drawn from casual samples. The Sheffield survey shows what a fair sample requires (§8).
- The more untrained the mind, the more readily it reads co-occurrence or shared feeling as cause and effect. Emotion binds feared things into one system (§9).
- Opinion generally culminates in a system of all evil and another of all good, and real space, time and numbers are lost in the stereotype (§10).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §1 Ruritania and false absolutism
- **Opening.** Diplomats who had to talk out loud to the warring peoples learned to use a large repertory of stereotypes, since even heroic soldiers would not face death gladly for all the ideas said to be essential to civilization (§1).
- **Fictional case (Lippmann's invention).** In one nation the war party, which controlled the foreign office, the high command and most of the press, had claims on the territory of several neighbours. The cultivated classes called these claims the Greater Ruritania (§1). The idea aroused no enthusiasm abroad (§1). So the statesmen divided the claim into sectors and gave each sector the principle that some ally found hard to resist, because that ally hoped to use the same principle for its own claims (§1).
- **The sectors and their principles.**
  - A mountain region of alien peasants was claimed as a natural geographical frontier.
  - A Ruritanian-inhabited region was claimed on the principle that no people should live under alien rule.
  - A commercial city, not inhabited by Ruritanians but part of Ruritania until the 18th century, was claimed by Historic Right.
  - An alien-owned mineral deposit was claimed as reparation for damage.
  - A territory 97% alien, belonging to another nation's natural frontier, was claimed for cultural superiority and the defence of civilization.
  - A port with no tie to Ruritania was claimed for national defence (§1).
- **Lippmann's conclusion.** He is certain Europe could not be resettled consistently on any one such principle. The very use of principles so pretentious and absolute meant, he says, that the spirit of accommodation did not prevail and so the substance of peace was not there (§1). Once one treats factories, mines or mountains as perfect examples of some eternal principle, one is no longer arguing but fighting: the principle censors out objections, cuts the issue off from its context and starts an emotion that suits the principle but not the docks and real estate. Each defence then needs a further defence, buffers upon buffers, until fighting seems less dangerous than talking (§1).
- **Clue.** The principles "blanketed each other", and the series of contradictions showed that each sector got the stereotype that would obliterate the facts interfering with the claim. Lippmann says contradiction of this sort is often a good clue (§1).

*Intuition note:* When an argument switches its high principle from one case to the next and the principles contradict each other, that is often a good clue that the principle is serving the claim and hiding the facts.

### §2 Space: the "Eastern Front" of 1918
- In spring 1918, after Russia withdrew, large numbers of people demanded that an Eastern Front be re-established, with the idle Japanese army taking Russia's place (§2).
- Between Vladivostok and the battle line lay 5,000 miles and one broken-down railway. Military authorities said that landing on the rim of Siberia had as little to do with reaching the Germans as climbing from cellar to roof of the Woolworth building had to do with reaching the moon. The enthusiasts, in effect, carried the army on a "magic carpet" (§2).
- **Diagnosis.** The stereotype was "war on two fronts", with Germany held between France and Russia. It had been the starting image of strategists for a generation or two, and nearly four years of battle-maps deepened it. Facts that conflicted with it, such as the distance, could not come "vividly into consciousness" (§2).
- **Americans versus French.** Lippmann says the Americans dealt with the facts more realistically. Part of the reason was that they had no prewar preconception of a continental war. Part was that their own stereotype, the western front, shut out other theatres (§2).
- The American stereotype won no hearing against the French view, because the Americans believed in their powers while the French (before Cantigny and the Second Marne) had grave doubts. Confidence gave the American picture the vividness James lists as marks of what we call "real" (§2).
- Facts that did not fit were either censored or stretched. A phantom German army in eastern Siberia was "supposed" to exist between March and June 1918. It was made of some prisoners seen, more prisoners thought about, and chiefly the delusion that the 5,000 miles did not exist (§2).

### §3 What true space is
- A straight map line from Bombay to Hong Kong tells nothing about the voyage. One must know ships, schedules, speed, accommodation and cost. In practical life space is "available transportation" (§3).
- **Motoring example.** A man says it is 3 miles. That is true on foot, but by car there is a six-mile detour, so it is 9 miles, six of them ruts and puddles. The pedestrian and the aviator describe the space they cover, not the space I must cover (§3).
- Boundary lines drawn under formulas such as self-determination have run through a factory, a village street, a church nave, a cottage between kitchen and bedroom, or between pasture and water. On the colored ethnic map such a line was ethnically just, that is, just in the world of that map (§3).

*Intuition note:* A distance is real for a given traveller and a given means of travel. A map line made for one purpose can be useless for another.

### §4 Time: will, constitution, specious present
- **The will.** The first William James (per his great-grandson Henry James) left a long will of restraints and instructions for his children. The courts upset it. The law against perpetuities recognises limits to imposing one's "moral stencil" on the future, yet allows it for a limited time (§4).
- **Constitutions.** An amending clause is a good index of how far the authors trusted their opinions to reach later generations. Hard-to-amend constitutions carry rules that, given humility, should be ordinary statutes (§4).
- **Presumptions about time.** To one person an institution that has existed for his whole conscious life is part of the permanent furniture of the universe; to another it is ephemeral. Geological, biological and social time differ, and social time is the most complex (§4). Part of wisdom is picking the time-conception that fits the matter. The wrong choice runs from the dreamer who ignores the present to the philistine who sees only it (§4).
- **Specious present.** James says we have no direct "realizing" sense of long durations (§4). Titchener, cited via Warren, puts the longest duration felt directly, the "specious present", at about six seconds. The "ideational present" adds memory images to carry days, months and years into the present (§4).
- **Vividness.** In the ideational present vividness is proportionate to the number of discriminations perceived. A bored vacation drags while it lasts but seems short in memory. Great activity passes fast but is long in memory (§4).
- **Von Baer's thought-experiment (via James).** If we noted 10,000 distinct events per second instead of about 10, and life held the same number of impressions, life might be 1,000 times shorter, and we would live less than a month and know nothing of the change of seasons. Reversed, a being with 1/1000 of our sensations would live 1,000 times longer. Seasons would pass like quarters of an hour, and plants would seem to appear instantly (§4).

### §5 Wells and the choice of time-scale
- Wells, in his *Outline of History*, scales the time from Columbus to now as three inches. On that scale the Altamira painters are 55 feet away, the Neanderthalers 550 feet, and the last dinosaurs a mile or so. More or less precise chronology does not begin until after 1000 B.C. (§5).
- Wells wanted to change moral perspective by changing time perspective. Lippmann replies that no telescopic scale (astronomical, geological, biological) that minimises the present is "more true" than a microscopic one. Lippmann calls Strunsky right in saying that if Wells has in mind his subtitle, the probable future of mankind, he may ask for any number of centuries, but if he is thinking of salvaging Western civilization he must think in decades (§5). Lippmann: "It all depends" on the practical purpose, and sometimes the perspective should be lengthened, sometimes shortened (§5).
- **Misuses of time-scale.** Lippmann's four cases are:
  - saying it does not matter if 15,000,000 Chinese die of famine, because in two generations the birthrate will make up the loss;
  - pauperising a healthy young man out of sentiment for an immediate difficulty;
  - buying off an aggressive empire for immediate peace;
  - demanding a "showdown" with a neighbour.

  He calls the last two cases victims of a specious present (§5).

### §6 Social problems and popular history
- **Timber.** A sound policy replants what each species and age is cut. Cutting less is waste and cutting more is exploitation. In an emergency the overcut is a charge on the future (§6).
- **Coal.** Coal forms on geological time and is limited. The computation of reserves, rates of use and alternative fuels must be squared with an ideal standard of how long to protect posterity. His example is 100 years, with hypothetical figures (§6).
- **Trolley contract.** A company wants a 99-year monopoly, a term that to its men means "forever". If surface cars may be obsolete in 20 years, the contract condemns a later generation to inferior transport. A subsidy now would be better. The officials lack a realising sense of 99 years (§6).
- **Irish and English.** The Famine of 1847 and similar events are ancient wrongs to the average Englishman but almost contemporary to a patriotic Irishman. Such "foreshortenings" separate peoples (§6).
- **Historic Rights.** In the Alsace-Lorraine debate the answer depends on the start date: Ancient Gaul, Henry I, 1273, 1648, 1688. A person arguing from history is "fairly certain" to pick dates that support the present aim (§6).
- **Races.** During the war the difference between "Teutons" and "Anglo-Saxons" and French was held to be eternal. A generation earlier, historians stressed the common Teutonic origin. Lippmann's rule: if we like a people we trace branches to the trunk, and if we dislike them we call each branch a separate trunk (§6).
- **Family trees.** A "founder" is chosen as the ancestor from whom it is desirable to start (or the earliest on record), and descent is traced through males unless the female line is remarkable (§6).

*Intuition note:* "History" often means choosing the start date that makes the present claim look inevitable. The same facts give different answers from different start dates.

### §7 The future
- The temptation is to jump over necessary steps and, as hope or doubt prompts, to exaggerate or minimise the time needed (§7).
- **Industrial management.** Management covers functions that need no training, some training, or a lifetime. A discriminating programme would match responsibility to training. A sudden "dictatorship of the proletariat" tries to skip preparation time. Resistance to sharing responsibility denies that capacity changes over time. Rotation in office and contempt for the expert assume that what takes years to learn need not be learned (§7).
- **"Backward peoples".** Article XIX of the League Covenant ties mandates to the stage of development (§7). Lippmann says that how the mandatories and the mandated conceive that time will deeply influence their relations; he judges that in the case of Cuba the American and Cuban estimates virtually coincided, and that more often they have not coincided. Where rulers see backwardness as hopeless or profitable, the tie "festers". He claims that backwardness is far more often conceived as an "intrinsic and eternal" inferiority. He applies this to the "race wars" and the failure to see that time would erode slave morality (§7). (These are Lippmann's 1922 judgments.)
- We find it hard not to picture the future as obeying our present purposes (§7).

### §8 Numbers, samples and the faith ladder
- We must "summarize and generalize" and treat samples as typical. Fair sampling is hard and belongs to statistics. Lippmann says his own mathematics is poor (§8).
- **Sheffield survey.** Social workers wanted an accurate picture of the mental equipment of Sheffield's workers. Assuming the test is fair and the census classifies the class, about 104,000 men and 107,000 women should have been questioned, which is impractical (§8).
- Professor Bowley advised not fewer than 408 men and 408 women. This would give a deviation from the average of not more than 1 in 22, so at least 816 people were needed (§8). The social workers judged that picking them through clubs, missions or personal contacts would give entirely worthless results, since such people would represent only their own coteries. The workers were instead reached by a "neutral", "accidental" or "random" method, at immense cost (§8).
- **Result.** Of about 200,000 workers, about one quarter were "well equipped", approaching three-quarters "inadequately equipped", and about one-fifteenth "mal-equipped" (§8).
- **Contrast.** Our usual national characterisations ("volatile Irish", "logical French") are generalisations from samples chosen by a method that Lippmann calls statistically wholly unsound. The employer judges labour by his most troublesome or most docile worker. Lippmann says the casual mind picks a sample that supports or defies its prejudices and makes it represent a whole class (§8).
- **"Working class".** Lippmann says a phrase like the working class covers only some of the truth for a part of the time. Farm hands, clerks, servants and others slip out of the net. Appeals to "Labor" fix on two or three million trade unionists and tacitly credit the other seventeen or eighteen million with their views (§8). The stereotype of Labor as Emancipator selects the evidence that supports it; alongside the real movements of working men there exists a fiction of the Labor Movement, which deals with the future (§8).
- **Faith ladder (James).** Men slide up seven steps: not absurd; might have been true; may be true now; fit to be true; ought to be true; must be true; shall be true, at any rate true for the speaker (§8). James added that acting so may sometimes help make it true. Lippmann adds that no one would have insisted more than James that, so far as we know how, we must avoid substituting the goal for the starting point (§8).
- If we think something ought to be true, we can almost always find an instance or a believer. The first six people we meet may have read the same newspaper. We cannot send a questionnaire to 816 random people every time we estimate a probability, but with any large mass of facts the presumption is against our having picked true samples if we act on a casual impression (§8).

*Intuition note:* A sample picked through contacts we already have mostly reflects our own circle, whose members may agree with us for shallow reasons (the same newspaper). The Sheffield workers used a neutral, accidental or random method instead.

### §9 Cause and effect
- Few big issues have obvious causes, even for scholars. The commonest form of reasoning, he says, is the intuitive *post hoc ergo propter hoc* ("after this, therefore because of this") (§9).
- The more untrained the mind, the more readily it links two things noticed together. Seen mostly through stereotypes, sequence or parallelism is easily taken for causation (§9).
- This is most likely when the ideas arouse the same feeling. A strong feeling attached to one idea is likely to pull in any other idea that feels about the same. Painful things tend to collect into one system of cause and effect, and likewise pleasant things (§9).
- **Examples.** Increase Mather, 1675, said smallpox began at an alehouse to show God's displeasure at drunkenness. In 1919 a professor of celestial mechanics, discussing the Einstein theory, wrote that it may well be that Bolshevist uprisings are visible signs of a deep, world-wide mental disturbance, and that the same spirit of unrest has invaded science (§9).
- Lippmann says that things we hate may have no more real connection than smallpox and alehouses, or Relativity and Bolshevism, yet they are bound together in the same emotion. In a mind like this "Anything can be related to anything else, provided it feels like it", and the mind cannot tell how preposterous it is (§9).

### §10 The system of all good and all evil
- Lippmann says it generally all culminates in a system of all evil and another of all good, and then our love of the absolute shows itself. We dislike qualifying words such as *rather, perhaps, if, but, not quite, almost, temporarily, partly*, yet nearly every opinion about public affairs needs one; in our free moments everything tends to behave absolutely, one hundred percent, everywhere, forever (§10).
- In wartime, Lippmann says, it is not enough to say that our victory will help democracy more than the enemy's; one must insist that it will end war forever. After the war the relativity of the result fades, and "the pendulum swings" between omnipotence and impotence (§10).
- **Conclusion.** Real space, time, numbers, connections and weights are lost. The perspective, background and dimensions of action are clipped and frozen in the stereotype (§10).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show why a convenient sample misleads, as in §8.
- A town has 1,000 workers. 280 are "well equipped" by some test, so the true share is 280 / 1,000 = 0.28.
- A mission club has 100 members, of whom 80 are well equipped (an assumed bias). The other 900 workers include 200 well equipped. Check: 80 + 200 = 280.
- A surveyor who asks only the club gets 80 / 100 = 0.80, nearly three times the truth.
- A random sample of 50 workers would be expected to contain 0.28 × 50 = 14 well-equipped people. This echoes Lippmann's point: a sample of the right size still needs a neutral selection method (§8).

## Concepts
- [[stereotype-lippmann]] — a fixed picture that selects evidence and blocks inconvenient facts. In this chapter it is shown at work on space, time, numbers and causes (§2, §8).
- [[false-absolutism]] (new) — invoking an absolute principle or "one hundred percent" claim that censors context and objections (§1, §10).
- [[practical-space-and-time]] (new) — space measured by available transport, time by the scale that fits the practical purpose; a telescopic scale is not "more true" than a microscopic one (§3, §5).
- [[specious-present]] (new) — the roughly six-second span felt directly; longer time is built from memory and conception (§4).
- [[casual-sample]] (new) — a sample picked by convenience or prejudice, then treated as typical of a whole class (§8).
- [[faith-ladder]] (new) — James's seven-step slide from "not absurd" to "shall be true" (§8).
- [[post-hoc-reasoning]] (new) — treating sequence or shared feeling as cause and effect (§9).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Casual versus random sampling ↔ survey statistics: selection bias and representativeness — Lippmann's contrast is the idea that selection method decides validity.
- [structural-analogy] *Post hoc* reasoning ↔ causal inference: correlation versus cause — same warning, informal vocabulary.
- [structural-analogy] Choosing the start date to fit the claim ↔ framing: selecting and emphasising aspects of reality — both are selective emphasis in service of a conclusion.
- [metaphor] Specious present ↔ the time horizons of agenda setting: what is salient now crowds out the long run.

## Questions this raises
- If no time-scale is "more true", how can we decide which one a given policy question deserves?
- How large and how random must a sample be for ordinary people to trust a group generalisation? Bowley's 816 was for a specific precision.
- Does naming a stereotype as such weaken it, or does emotion keep it alive anyway?
- Is the "faith ladder" harmful, or sometimes useful, as James hints when he says acting may help make something true?
