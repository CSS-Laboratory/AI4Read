# News, Truth, and a Conclusion

## L0 — One line
News signals events and truth is a different thing; as social truth is organised today the press cannot supply the knowledge democratic theory demands, and Lippmann says the remedy lies deeper than the press, in social organisation built on analysis and record.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks what the press can be expected to do. His preferred answer: news signals events, while truth brings hidden facts to light and relates them. The two coincide only where social conditions take recognisable, measurable shape. Elsewhere the journalist's version of the truth is only his version. If newspapers must let every adult form an opinion on every topic, he says they fail and will keep failing. On the whole, news quality indexes how well society is organised. The remedy lies deeper: social organisation built on analysis and record, and dropping the theory of the "omnicompetent citizen" (a reader able to judge every issue).

**Prerequisites:** the idea of stereotypes as simplified pictures of the world; basic ideas of representative government and of popular sovereignty (rule by the people's opinion).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Lippmann's preferred hypothesis is that news and truth are not the same thing. News signals an event. Truth brings hidden facts to light and relates them in a picture of reality that people can act on (para. 2).
- The two coincide only where social conditions are measurable. Only in that sector are charges of perversion or suppression more than partisan judgment (para. 2).
- Beyond that area no discipline or fixed rules guide the journalist. The more he understands his weaknesses, the more he admits that his opinion is partly built from his own stereotypes, code and interests (para. 3).
- He lacks the sustaining conviction of a method like the one that freed physical science from theological control. The control employers and readers exercise over him is one opinion controlling another, not truth controlled by prejudice (para. 4).
- The press can fight to extend "reportable truth", but it can normally record only what institutions have recorded for it (para. 5).
- The press has come to be regarded as an organ of direct democracy, and the theory unconsciously treats the reader as omnicompetent. Lippmann calls the resulting "Court of Public Opinion" unworkable (paras. 7–8).
- On the whole, the quality of news indexes social organisation. At best the press serves institutions; at worst a few use it to exploit disorganisation (para. 9).
- Remedy: social organisation based on a system of record and analysis, decentralised decisions coordinated by comparable record, and abandoning the omnicompetent citizen (para. 10).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: which hypothesis? (para. 1).** Studies of the press will depend on the hypothesis held. If we assume with Sinclair and most of his opponents that news and truth are the same, Lippmann believes we arrive nowhere. We would prove that the newspaper lied, that Sinclair's account lied, and so on. We would vent our feelings "into air" (para. 1).

**News versus truth (para. 2).** The hypothesis that seems to Lippmann most fertile is that news and truth are not the same and must be clearly distinguished. A footnote says that when he wrote *Liberty and the News* he did not understand the distinction clearly enough to state it (para. 2).
- Function of news: to signalize an event. Function of truth: to bring hidden facts to light, relate them, and make a picture of reality on which men can act (para. 2).
- Only where social conditions take recognisable, measurable shape do the body of truth and the body of news coincide. This is a comparatively small part of the whole field of human interest (para. 2).
- In this sector, and only in this sector, the tests are exact enough to make charges of perversion or suppression more than partisan judgment (para. 2).
- Example: a paper that states six times that Lenin is dead, when its only information is a report from a source repeatedly shown unreliable, has no excuse. The news is that Helsingfors says Lenin is dead. Editors are most responsible for judging the reliability of the source. For stories about what the Russian people want, no such test exists (para. 2).

*Intuition note:* Lippmann ties the meaning of a charge such as "perversion" to the existence of an exact test. Where such a test exists, the charge can be checked. Where none exists, he says it is no more than a partisan judgment.

**The character of the profession (para. 3).** Lippmann thinks the absence of exact tests accounts for the character of the profession, as no other explanation does. The body of exact knowledge is very small and needs no outstanding ability or training. The rest is the journalist's discretion (para. 3).
- Example: the County Clerk's record that John Smith went bankrupt is fixed. The story of why he failed (his frailties, the economic conditions) can be told in a hundred different ways (para. 3).
- Medicine, engineering and even law have a discipline with authority; applied psychology has none to direct the journalist. No canons direct his mind or coerce the reader's or the publisher's (para. 3).
- His version of the truth is only his version. He cannot demonstrate it, just as Sinclair Lewis cannot demonstrate that he told the whole truth about *Main Street* (para. 3).
- The more he understands his weaknesses, the more ready he is to admit that, where there is no objective test, his opinion is in some vital measure built from his own stereotypes, code and interest. He sees the world through subjective lenses, like Shelley's dome of coloured glass that stains the white radiance of eternity (para. 3).

**Why he lacks the physicist's freedom (para. 4).** This knowledge tempers his assurance. He may have moral courage, but he lacks the sustaining conviction of a certain technic (method) that finally freed the physical sciences from theological control. The physicist's proofs were so clear that he broke away from all control; the journalist has no such support in conscience or in fact (para. 4).
- The control by employers and readers is, in Lippmann's words, not control of truth by prejudice but of one opinion by another that is not demonstrably less true (para. 4).
- Example: Judge Gary asserts that unions will destroy American institutions; Gompers asserts that they are agencies of the rights of man. The choice has in large measure to be governed by "the will to believe" (para. 4).

**What the press can and cannot do (para. 5).** The reporter cannot perform the task of deflating such controversies to the point where they can be reported as news. It is possible and necessary for journalists to bring home to people how uncertain the truth behind their opinions is. By criticism and agitation they can prod social science toward more usable formulations and statesmen toward more visible institutions. The press can fight for the extension of reportable truth (para. 5).
- As social truth is organised today, the press is not constituted to furnish from one edition to the next the knowledge the democratic theory of public opinion demands (para. 5).
- This is not due to the Brass Check, as the quality of news in radical papers shows, but to a society whose governing forces are imperfectly recorded (para. 5).
- The theory that the press can itself record those forces is false. It can normally record only what institutions have recorded for it; the rest is argument and opinion (para. 5).

**The standard is wrong (paras. 6–8).**
- If the press is not so universally wicked or conspiring as Sinclair would have us believe, it is far more frail than democratic theory has yet admitted. It is too frail to carry the whole burden of popular sovereignty or to supply spontaneously the truth democrats hoped was inborn (para. 6).
- Expecting this uses a misleading standard. We misunderstand the limited nature of news and the complexity of society. We overestimate our endurance and public spirit, and we suppose an appetite for uninteresting truths that honest analysis of our tastes does not find (para. 6).
- If newspapers are charged with translating all public life so that every adult can reach an opinion on every moot topic, they fail, are bound to fail, and will keep failing. It is not possible to assume that a world run by division of labour and distribution of authority can be governed by universal opinions held by the whole population (para. 7).
- Unconsciously the theory sets up the single reader as theoretically omnicompetent. It asks the press to create, in thirty minutes a day, a mystical force called Public Opinion that will take up the slack in public institutions (para. 7).
- The press has often, mistakenly, pretended it could do this. At great moral cost to itself it has encouraged a democracy to expect newspapers to supply the machinery of information that governmental organs do not supply themselves (para. 7).
- Institutions, having failed to furnish themselves with instruments of knowledge, have become a bundle of "problems" that the population, reading the press as a whole, is supposed to solve (para. 7).
- The press has come to be regarded as an organ of direct democracy, like initiative, referendum and recall. The "Court of Public Opinion" is to lay down the law for everything all the time. Lippmann says this is not workable and, given the nature of news, not even thinkable (para. 8).
- News is precise in proportion to the precision with which the event is recorded. An event that cannot be named, measured, given shape and made specific either does not become news or is subject to the accidents and prejudices of observation (para. 8).

*Intuition note:* In Lippmann's account, news is precise in proportion to how precisely the event is recorded. Under today's organisation of social truth, the knowledge democratic theory demands runs into the fact that society's governing forces are imperfectly recorded.

**Conclusion: news as index of organisation (para. 9).** On the whole, the quality of news about modern society is an index of its social organisation. The better the institutions, the more interests are formally represented, the more issues are disentangled and the more objective criteria are introduced, the more perfectly an affair can be presented as news. At best the press is a servant and guardian of institutions. At worst it is a means by which a few exploit social disorganisation. To the degree that institutions fail to function, the unscrupulous journalist can "fish in troubled waters" and the conscientious one must gamble with uncertainties (para. 9).

**The searchlight and the remedy (paras. 10–11).**
- The press is no substitute for institutions. It is like a searchlight beam that moves restlessly about, bringing one episode and then another out of darkness. Men cannot do the work of the world by this light alone or govern society by episodes, incidents and eruptions (para. 10).
- Only when people work by a steady light of their own does the press, turned upon them, reveal a situation intelligible enough for a popular decision (para. 10).
- The trouble lies deeper than the press, and so does the remedy. It lies in social organisation based on a system of analysis and record, and in its corollaries: abandoning the theory of the omnicompetent citizen, decentralising decisions, and coordinating them by comparable record and analysis (para. 10).
- If at the centres of management there is a running audit that makes work intelligible to those who do and supervise it, issues are not mere collisions of the blind. The news is then uncovered for the press by a system of intelligence that is also a check upon the press (para. 10).
- The troubles of the press, of representative government and of industry (capitalist, cooperative or communist) go back to a common source: the failure of self-governing people to rise above casual experience and prejudice by building a machinery of knowledge (para. 11).
- Compelled to act without a reliable picture of the world, governments, schools, newspapers and churches make small headway against violent prejudice, apathy, and the preference for the curious trivial over the dull important (para. 11).
- Lippmann calls this the primary defect of popular government and believes its other defects can be traced to it (para. 11).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A local paper prints a story on a factory closing with 10 statements. Suppose 3 can be checked against a record (the company's filing says the plant closed; payroll shows 120 jobs lost; the closing date is in a notice). The other 7 are interpretation (why it failed; what workers feel; who is to blame).
- Statements with a record test: 3 of 10 = 30%. Only for these can "the paper lied" be more than a partisan judgment.
- Statements without a test: 7 of 10 = 70%. Two reporters could differ without either being demonstrably false.
- Suppose a public audit of the firm's accounts turned 2 of the 7 into recorded facts. Testable statements would then be 3 + 2 = 5 of 10 = 50%.

This mirrors Lippmann's claim that news can be presented more perfectly as institutions record more (paras. 2, 9).

## Questions this raises
- Which social facts have become recorded enough to count as reportable truth since Lippmann wrote, and who controls those records?
- If citizens are not omnicompetent, how are decisions legitimately delegated and checked (decentralisation, running audit)?
- Is Lippmann's split between measurable and unmeasurable areas sharp, or a matter of degree?
- Could a system of intelligence that checks the press become a new source of control?
