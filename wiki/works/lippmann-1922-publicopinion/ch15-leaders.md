# Leaders and the Rank and File

## L0 — One line
Leaders hold their followers through shared symbols and tend to prefer policies whose costs the followers do not directly see; persuasion has become a self-conscious art, and the old democratic belief that knowledge rises spontaneously from the people can no longer be believed.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how leaders and followers actually relate. Symbols merge many different feelings into one target. This gives unity in a crisis, but it also allows exploitation. Over the longer run, leaders must watch morale and the "incidence" of policy: who feels its costs, and when. Leaders are likely to have a free hand when followers are remote from the action or costs are hidden or deferred. Masses do not invent programs; leaders vocalize existing feeling and then attach a program to it. Officials also control information, so they are in some degree censors and propagandists. Psychological research and modern communication have made persuasion a self-conscious art, and this undermines the democratic dogma that knowledge rises spontaneously from the people.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of stereotypes and the unseen "pseudo-environment" from earlier chapters; basic politics (taxes, elections, war).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Symbols do for the rank and file what privileges do within the hierarchy: they conserve unity by merging differences at a common emotional target (§ 1, para. 1).
- The symbol is both a mechanism of solidarity and of exploitation: it lets people work for a common end, but because the few who are strategically placed choose the concrete objectives, it also lets a few exploit the many (§ 1, para. 3).
- In emergencies, unity may matter more than being right. Yet Captain Wright, quoted by Lippmann, argues that secrecy and "camouflage" also do harm, above all to the generals themselves (§ 1, paras. 4–6).
- Lippmann says a symbol "immobilizes personality" but makes the mass mobile, and Lippmann says that in an uncertain, eruptive situation it is usually necessary to secure unity and flexibility "without real consent", which the symbol does (§ 1, para. 7).
- Leaders are likely to have a free hand when the action is remote, results are hidden or postponed, or obligations are indirect. This fits foreign affairs, long-term debts and attacks on visible targets such as the "greedy landlord" (§ 2, paras. 3–5).
- Masses do not generate programs. The leader first voices existing feeling, then connects his program to it by analogy and association (§ 3, paras. 1–4).
- Officials control access to facts, so every leader is in some degree a propagandist (§ 3, para. 9).
- Persuasion has become a self-conscious art, and the old democratic dogma can no longer be believed (§ 4, para. 3).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Symbols and unity (§ 1)
- No successful leader is too busy to cultivate the symbols that organize his following. Symbols are focal points where differences merge, from totem pole to flag. Many leaders cherish them while being unbelievers themselves (§ 1, para. 1).
- Emotion is discharged at a common target, and the idiosyncrasy of real ideas is blotted out. The leader therefore hates what he calls "destructive criticism" (free spirits call it the elimination of buncombe). **Bagehot** is cited: "our royalty is to be reverenced, and if you begin to poke about it you cannot reverence it" (§ 1, para. 1).
- Lippmann's view: poking about with clear definitions serves all high purposes except the easy conservation of a common will. Poking about, as every responsible leader suspects, tends to break the transfer of emotion from the individual to the institutional symbol, and the first result, as he rightly says, is a chaos of individualism and warring sects. The disintegration of a symbol (Holy Russia, the Iron Diaz) is always the beginning of a long upheaval (§ 1, para. 1).
- Great symbols take over (by transference) the loyalties of an old, stereotyped society to landscape, faces and memories. This core is nationality. Lesser symbols of debate are always referred back to these "proto-symbols" and, if possible, associated with them. Example: a municipal subway fare becomes a contest between "the People" and "the Interests", then "the People" is inserted in the symbol "American", so that in the heat of a campaign an eight-cent fare becomes "unAmerican"; the Revolutionary fathers, Lincoln and the dead in France are invoked against it (§ 1, para. 2).
- Because it siphons emotion out of distinct ideas, the symbol enables common work. It also lets a few "fatten on many", deflect criticism and seduce men into facing agony for objects they do not understand (§ 1, para. 3).
- Symbols are not "altogether instruments of the devil". They are the tempter in science and contemplation, but in action they may be beneficent and sometimes necessary. The necessity is often imagined. Lippmann adds that when quick results are imperative, manipulating masses through symbols may be the only quick way to get a critical thing done, and that in times such as war two conflicting opinions, though one happens to be right, can be more perilous than one wrong opinion (§ 1, para. 4).
- Example, March 1918: Foch and Sir Henry Wilson foresaw disaster to "Cough's" army from scattered reserves. They kept their view within a small circle. An excited newspaper debate, Lippmann says, would have dissolved the armies before Foch could win it. What mattered most was unbroken expectation about the source of command (§ 1, para. 5).
- The reverse danger is a conspiracy of silence. **Captain Wright's view (quoted):** chiefs are painted as Napoleons, are hard to displace, and come to believe the illusion. General Staffs are then freed from control, and "the nation lives, or rather dies, for them" (§ 1, para. 6).
- Wright nevertheless approves Foch's silence. Lippmann calls this a paradox: democratic theory was conceived for tranquillity, not emergencies. Where masses must cooperate in an uncertain and eruptive environment, it is usually necessary to secure unity and flexibility "without real consent", and the symbol does that. It obscures personal intention and immobilizes personality, yet it sharpens group intention and renders the mass mobile. It is the instrument by which, in the short run, the mass escapes "the inertia of indecision" or of headlong movement (§ 1, para. 7).

*Intuition note:* A flag lets millions who disagree act as one, because everyone attaches their own feeling to it. That is useful in a crisis, but because a few choose the concrete objectives, the same symbol also lets them deflect criticism and exploit the many.

### 2. Morale and the incidence of policy (§ 2)
- Over the longer run, give and take grows between leaders and led. Morale is good when each man's whole strength is evoked by the command. So a leader must consider his decision not only on "the merits" but also by its effect on any part of his following whose continued support he requires (§ 2, para. 1).
- War example: "out of every nine men who went to France five became casualties" (Wright's figures). Endurance far exceeded expectation, but a limit existed. Commands did not publish candid loss totals. In France the casualty lists were never published; in England, America and Germany, publication of the losses of a big battle was spread over long periods. The Somme cost nearly 500,000 casualties; the 1917 Arras and Flanders offensives cost 650,000 British casualties (footnote). Veterans know more than the public and judge commanders by their own suffering. A mutiny can break out over a comparatively minor but cumulative blunder, like Nivelle's 1917 offensive; revolutions and mutinies generally follow a small sample of a big series of evils (§ 2, para. 2).
- **Incidence of policy** (Lippmann does not define the term; "who feels the effects, and when" is our reading of his use) determines the leader–follower relation. The leader is likely to have a free hand if the followers are remote from the action, results are hidden or postponed, obligations are indirect, and above all if assent is an exercise of some pleasurable emotion. Programs that do not at once impinge on followers' private habits are the most popular, like prohibition among teetotalers (§ 2, para. 3).
- Foreign affairs: the incidence of policy is for a very long time confined to an "unseen environment", where nothing is felt to be wholly real. Because before a war nobody has to fight or pay, governments go along without much reference to their people. Example: Americans do not settle whether the liberated country is Czechoslavia or Jugoslovakia (§ 2, paras. 3–4).
- In local affairs costs are more easily visible, so all but the most exceptional leaders prefer policies with indirect costs: they dislike direct taxes, like long-term debts, and like voters to believe "the foreigner will pay". Labor leaders prefer money-wage rises to price cuts. Popular interest in millionaires' profits (visible, minor) exceeds interest in industrial waste (huge, elusive). In a housing shortage a legislature smites landlords and investigates builders rather than building houses (§ 2, para. 5).
- But the working out of policy follows its own logic, not opinion. Higher freight rates may not save the railroads if the final price exceeds what consumers will pay (§ 2, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* People accept a policy more easily when its bill is far away or invisible. But the economy responds to real costs, not to earlier approval.

### 3. How leaders and masses interact (§ 3)
- Leaders often pretend they have merely uncovered a program that existed in the minds of their public. Lippmann says that when they believe it they are usually deceiving themselves: not because a multitude is necessarily inferior, but because thought is a function of an organism and a mass is not an organism (§ 3, para. 1).
- The mass is exposed to suggestion: it reads news "with an aura of suggestion". Lippmann says the "ostensible leader" often finds that the real leader is a powerful newspaper proprietor. He adds, "I think", that if all suggestion could be removed, as in a laboratory, one would find something like this: responses that could theoretically be charted in a "polygon of error", with a central group and variants at both ends. In that case, putting vague feelings into words would make them more definite (§ 3, para. 2).
- Barring the effect of suggestion, nothing in mass feeling would fatally determine a particular policy. The feeling only demands that policy be connected, by analogy and association if not logic, with the original feeling (§ 3, para. 3).
- Launch sequence (as in Mark Antony's speech): first the leader vocalizes prevalent opinion and identifies with the audience. If trusted, he is then expected to present a plan, which will not be found in the slogans. When incidence is remote, trusted men using accepted symbols can go far without explaining substance (§ 3, para. 4).
- Wise leaders seek a certain measure of consent, provided they think publicity will not strengthen opposition too much and debate will not delay action too long. They take the subordinates of the hierarchy, if not the whole mass, into their confidence. Yet when the facts are very complicated there is always some illusion in such consultation, since not all the contingencies can be as vivid to the whole public as to the experienced. Lippmann adds that no one can ask for more, and only theorists do (§ 3, para. 5).
- If the established powers are sensitive and well-informed, visibly trying to meet popular feeling and actually removing some causes of dissatisfaction (however slowly, provided they are seen to proceed), they have little to fear: it takes "stupendous and persistent blundering", plus almost infinite tactlessness, to start a revolution from below. Demagogy stops at relieving tension by expressing the feeling; the statesman knows such relief is temporary, and so will not rouse feeling he cannot sluice into a program that deals with the facts (§ 3, para. 6).
- Leaders hate to resign, so they mend fences: scapegoats, minor grievances redressed, jobs rearranged, small favors. Favors reach a limited number of people. The anonymous multitude gets propaganda (§ 3, paras. 7–8).
- Established leaders are believed to have better sources of information, and they have a very great deal of control over access to the facts. Since withholding information requires some notion of what the public should know, every leader is in some degree a propagandist (§ 3, para. 9).

*Intuition note:* Followers supply a mood, and the leader supplies the plan. He wins trust by speaking their feelings first.

### 4. The manufacture of consent (§ 4)
- Lippmann thinks no one denies that the manufacture of consent is capable of great refinement. It is an old art, supposed to have died out with democracy, but it has improved because it now rests on analysis, not rule of thumb. He says the practice of democracy has turned a corner, in a revolution "infinitely more significant than any shifting of economic power" (§ 4, paras. 1–2).
- Persuasion has become "a self-conscious art and a regular organ of popular government". He says none of us begins to understand the consequences, but the knowledge of how to create consent will alter every political calculation and modify every political premise, and that under the impact of propaganda the old constants of our thinking have become variables (§ 4, para. 3).
- Conclusion: one can no longer believe the dogma that knowledge for managing human affairs comes up spontaneously from the human heart. Acting on that theory exposes us to self-deception and to forms of persuasion we cannot verify. Lippmann says "it has been demonstrated" that we cannot rely on intuition, conscience or the accidents of casual opinion if we are to deal with the world beyond our reach (§ 4, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* If opinions can be shaped by skilled persuaders, then, on Lippmann's argument, we cannot simply trust spontaneous public opinion to supply the knowledge needed for the world beyond our reach.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A mayor can raise $20 per household per year for a new bridge, with 1,000 households, in two ways. Total: 1,000 × $20 = $20,000 either way.
- A: a direct tax bill of $20 each. Cost is visible and the incidence is immediate.
- B: a 1-cent charge on each of about 2,000 small purchases a year. Per household: 2,000 × $0.01 = $20. The cost is the same but scattered over trivial items.
Lippmann's remark that leaders prefer policies with indirect costs suggests they would favour B. The totals are equal, and only the visibility differs.

## Concepts
- [[symbol-as-focal-point]] (new) — a shared emblem where individual differences merge and emotion is discharged at one target (§ 1, para. 1)
- [[incidence-of-policy]] (new) — who feels a policy's effects and when; remote, hidden or deferred incidence makes it likely that leaders have a free hand (§ 2, para. 3); the gloss is ours, not a definition by Lippmann
- [[morale]] (new) — the rank and file's state of mind: good when each gives full strength on command (§ 2, para. 1)
- [[manufacture-of-consent]] (new) — deliberate, analysis-based creation of agreement, now a regular organ of popular government (§ 4, paras. 1–3)
- [[stereotype-lippmann]] — great symbols take over the loyalties of an "ancient and stereotyped society" (§ 1, para. 2), and the mass hears reports "already stereotyped to a certain pattern of behavior" (§ 3, para. 2). The chapter uses the idea but does not define it.

## Questions this raises
- If persuasion now rests on analysis, what replaces "spontaneous" public knowledge as the basis of democracy?
- How can one tell necessary secrecy (Foch) from camouflage (Wright's General Staffs)?
- Does the incidence rule hold when costs become visible quickly, for example through modern media?
