# Censorship and Privacy

## L0 — One line
Propaganda needs a barrier between the public and the event, so those who control access to facts can decide what picture the public acts on.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how the pictures in people's heads can be shaped by others. His evidence is the French High Command's wording of its Verdun communiqués (official war reports) in 1916. Officers chose what to say, what to omit and how often to repeat it, to steady public feeling. They once even invented a battle. Lippmann calls this propaganda and says propaganda in the strict sense is impossible without limits on access to the real event. Military censorship is the simplest limit, but not the most important. Everyday privacy customs also separate the public from facts. A reader can partly protect himself by asking who actually saw the facts behind an opinion.

**Prerequisites:** the idea that people act on mental pictures of events; basic knowledge of the First World War.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Wartime communiqués were drafted in editorial conferences, with word-by-word argument over their effect on public mood (§1, para. 1–3).
- Authorities showed the public only some facts, framed to steady morale (§1, para. 4). The staff knew the real situation in the first case (§1, para. 5).
- In the Douaumont case, headquarters did not know the truth and published an imagined battle. The press then built an "encirclement" story on it (§1, para. 5–8).
- Repeated, selective casualty figures built a picture of endless German dead. The picture produced its inference almost automatically, not by argument (§2, para. 2, 5).
- Lippmann calls this propaganda. A patriotic purpose does not change that (§2, para. 5).
- Armies are hard to control fully; naval and diplomatic censorship is usually more complete (§2, para. 6).
- Propaganda in the strict sense needs a barrier between public and event (§3, para. 1).
- Privacy norms are elastic and historically changing. They act as barriers too, so ask how you got your facts (§3, para. 2–3).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. The Verdun communiqués
**The editing conference.** At the worst hour of Verdun, Joffre and his staff argued over the words of the next day's press report. The source is Pierrefeu, who edited the communiqués (§1, para. 1).
- On 23 Feb 1916 the Prime Minister's office asked, through Berthelot, for a stronger report that stressed the size of the German attack. The aim was to prepare the public for the worst (§1, para. 2).
- Pierrefeu's draft was judged too moderate. Pellé's own draft seemed too alarming (§1, para. 2).
- Pierrefeu left out von Deimling's captured order of the day. Including it would break the public's accustomed formula and turn the report into "pleading". He suggested a separate note (§1, para. 2).
- Castelnau chose the simpler text, stressed the first phrase and inserted "as had been anticipated" to reassure. He agreed that the order should go out as a separate note. Joffre approved (§1, para. 3).

**Lippmann's reading.** Within hours the 200–300 words would paint a picture for readers everywhere. Civilians had to keep hope yet accept possible defeat without panic (§1, para. 4).
- In fact the General Staff was not fully prepared: supporting trenches, alternative roads and barbed wire were lacking (§1, para. 4).
- Lippmann's judgment: a full story might have caused "faction and counter-faction" about the officers' competence. So only certain facts were presented, and only in a steadying way (§1, para. 4).
- Here the men who arranged the pseudo-environment (the picture offered to the public) knew what the real situation was (§1, para. 5).

**Douaumont (26 Feb).** The Germans announced they had taken Fort Douaumont. Chantilly did not know how the fort had been taken, yet the French had to say something while the German report went around the world (§1, para. 5).
- Headquarters imagined an attack plan which, as the staff itself explained, had a thousand to one chance of being true (§1, para. 5).
- The communiqué spoke of a bitter struggle, heavy enemy losses, and French troops who had retaken and gone beyond the position (§1, para. 6).
- Footnote: the New York Times translation exaggerates the French "pertes tres elevees" (§1, para. 6).
- Actual events: during a troop relief the fort was forgotten. A battery commander and a few men remained. Germans entered through an open door and took them prisoner. There was no battle and no losses. French troops were beyond the fort on either side, but the fort was in German hands (§1, para. 7).
- The text did not say the fort was surrounded, but readers concluded it. Military writers expected a German surrender. Later they asked why the starving garrison had not surrendered. The press bureau had to ask them to drop the encirclement theme (§1, para. 8).

*Intuition note:* Officials chose which facts the public could see. In the second case they did not even know the facts, yet still had to publish a picture. The public then reasoned from that picture.

### 2. Attrition and propaganda
- As the battle dragged out, the communiqué editors set out to neutralize German persistence by continual insistence on German losses (§2, para. 1).
- Until late 1917 the Allied orthodox view was that the war would be decided by "attrition", the wearing down of the enemy by killing. Strategy and diplomacy were said not to count (§2, para. 1).
- The communiqués ascribed heavy losses to the Germans almost daily, with some appearance of justice ("heaps of corpses", "hecatombs"). Major Cointet's method produced figures that rose by about 100,000 every fortnight (300,000, 400,000, 500,000). Formulae were repeated until some were overworked (§2, para. 2).
- Pierrefeu says the repetition impressed neutrals and Germany itself. Denials from German wireless (Nauen) failed to undo it (§2, para. 2).
- The thesis given to the censors was that German manpower was declining. Within weeks the Allies would face the Germans with ten million against seven million (§2, para. 3).
- Pierrefeu says the command had converted itself to this belief: through what he calls an extraordinary aberration of mind, only the enemy's attrition was seen, and its own forces appeared not to be subject to attrition. General Nivelle shared these ideas. Pierrefeu adds: "We saw the result in 1917" (§2, para. 4).

**Lippmann's analysis.** This is propaganda. A group that can prevent independent access to the event arranges the news to suit its purpose. A patriotic purpose does not affect the argument (§2, para. 5).
- The inference (attrition is going our way) is not drawn as an argument. It follows almost automatically from the picture of endless German dead, with French dead left out (§2, para. 5).
- The picture was meant to offset German advances. It also tended to make the public accept a defensive strategy and give up the idea of great movements and surrenders (§2, para. 5).

**Means of control.** The General Staff selects correspondents, controls their movements, censors their messages and operates the wires. The government adds cables, passports, mails, customs, blockades, legal power over publishers and meetings, and the secret service (§2, para. 6).
- The control is imperfect for armies. The enemy's communiqué reaches neutrals, and soldiers' talk spreads when they are on leave. A footnote says that before St. Mihiel and the Argonne-Meuse offensive "everybody in France" told everybody the secret (§2, para. 6).
- Naval and diplomatic censorship is almost always more complete, because fewer people know and their acts are easier to supervise (§2, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Repeating casualty figures while leaving out the French dead built a picture that carried a conclusion nobody argued for.

### 3. Barriers and privacy
- Propaganda in the strict sense is impossible without some censorship. Access to the real environment must be limited before anyone can build a pseudo-environment that he thinks wise or desirable (§3, para. 1).
- People with direct access may misconceive what they see. But no one else can decide how they misconceive it unless he decides where they look and at what (§3, para. 1).
- Military censorship is the simplest barrier but not the most important. It is known to exist, so it is partly agreed to and discounted (§3, para. 1).
- Secrecy standards differ with time and subject. The line between "public interest" secrecy and "none of the public's business" fades gradually. Privacy is elastic (§3, para. 2).
- Examples: a fortune is private; a land sale is public but its price may not be. Salaries are more private than wages. Privileged conversations include lawyer–client and doctor–patient. Corporate affairs and theology were once private or public in the opposite way to today. Infectious disease was once private (§3, para. 2).
- Privacy notions sometimes conflict, for example when the Bolsheviks published the secret treaties (§3, para. 2).
- Whether the reasons for privacy are good or bad, the barriers exist. Ask who saw, heard or counted the thing, how much he was allowed to see, and who told whom. You can rarely answer, but the questions remind you of the distance between your opinion and the event. "The reminder is itself a protection" (§3, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* Not only wartime censors but ordinary secrecy habits stand between us and events. Asking who actually saw the facts can remind you how far your opinion often is from the event.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A club newsletter editor knows the team's results over 10 matches: 4 wins, 6 losses, 20 goals scored and 25 conceded. The editor prints only that the team scored 20 goals in 10 matches, 2 per match. Members form the picture of a strong attack and conclude the team is doing well. The omitted figure is 25 conceded, which gives a net of 20 − 25 = −5. No argument was made. The members see only the 20 goals, and they never see the 25. This is the mechanism of the Cointet figures: show one number, leave out the other, and let the picture draw the inference. The member who asks who counted, and what was left out, gets the protection Lippmann describes.

## Concepts
- [[pseudo-environment]] — the picture of events that people actually act on, built from what they are shown (§1, para. 5; §3, para. 1)
- [[propaganda]] (new) — a group controlling access to an event arranges its news to suit a purpose; Lippmann says that the purpose being patriotic in this case does not affect the argument (§2, para. 5)
- [[censorship-as-barrier]] (new) — limits on access to the real event; a necessary condition of propaganda in the strict sense (§3, para. 1)
- [[privacy-norms]] (new) — shifting social standards of what may be concealed; barriers exist whether the reasons for privacy are good or bad (§3, paras 2–3)
- [[source-chain-questioning]] (new) — asking who actually saw the facts behind an opinion, and how much he was allowed to see (§3, para. 3)

## Canonical instances
- [[verdun-communique-editing]] (new) — a general staff edits wording to steady public mood; not all facts shown (§1, para. 1–5)
- [[fort-douaumont-communique]] (new) — an imagined battle published because the truth was unknown; the press adds "encirclement" (§1, para. 5–8)
- [[cointet-casualty-figures]] (new) — repeated German casualty figures produce a picture of attrition success (§2, para. 2, 5)

## Questions this raises
- If military censorship is "discounted" because it is known, which barriers are not discounted, and why?
- Does the argument still hold if the staff itself does not know the facts, as at Douaumont?
- How can a reader judge which privacy barriers are justified?
- Can the source-chain questions be answered in practice for news today?
