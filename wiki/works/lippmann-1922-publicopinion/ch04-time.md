# Time and Attention

## L0 — One line
Lippmann uses rough survey and newspaper-content figures only to make more concrete how much effort goes each day into informing people about public affairs, mainly through newspapers, and he adds that daily direct exposure to the "unseen environment" is small.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How much attention do people give each day to informing themselves about public affairs? Lippmann combines three older studies. Two questionnaires (Chicago businessmen, New York students) and one content analysis of newspaper space (Wilcox) are compared. Most respondents say they read newspapers about a quarter of an hour a day, and most say they prefer public-affairs content. The space newspapers once gave to such matter seems roughly similar to those stated tastes. Lippmann says the figures are rough and allow no "elaborate deductions"; they only help make our notions of the daily effort somewhat more concrete. Even counting all media at the most favourable estimate, daily direct exposure to the "unseen environment" (his phrase, not defined here) is small.

**Prerequisites:** percentages and simple proportions; the idea of a questionnaire survey and of self-report bias.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Three estimates from different times, places and methods agree tolerably well on daily attention to public affairs (para. 1).
- About 70–75% of respondents said they read newspapers about 15 minutes a day. Lippmann reads this only as "rather low" attention, not as a literal figure (paras. 2–3).
- Self-reports may be biased (a possible bias about time spent; gendered answers that Lippmann suspects follow "ideals"), but the number of papers read gives a less subjective check (paras. 3–5, 7).
- Stated interests: roughly two thirds to 71% favoured public-affairs content (paras. 6, 8).
- Wilcox's analysis of about 9,000 columns, with advertising excluded, gives 76.5% of edited space for the items that "may be supposed" to deal with public affairs (paras. 9–12).
- Lippmann says this "would seem to show" that business men's and students' tastes still correspond more or less to editors' judgments. If accurate replies could be had from more typical groups, he would expect a smaller share of time and space for public affairs, but more than a quarter hour on the paper and a greater net amount of space (para. 13).
- Conclusion: even with all other media added, at the most favourable estimate, daily direct exposure to the unseen environment is small (para. 14).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: the evidence (paras. 1–3).** Only rough estimates of daily attention to public affairs are possible. Three studies are cited: Wilcox (1900), Scott (c. 1916), and Hotchkiss and Franken (1920) (para. 1, footnote).
- Hotchkiss and Franken sent a questionnaire to 1,761 New York college students and got answers from nearly all. Scott sent one to 4,000 prominent Chicago business and professional men and got 2,300 replies (para. 2).
- 70–75% of those who replied thought they spent 15 minutes a day on newspapers. In Chicago, 4% guessed less and 25% more. Among New Yorkers, a little over 8% guessed less and 17.5% more (para. 2).
- Caveats: few people judge 15 minutes accurately. Respondents may be biased against seeming to spend too much time on newspapers, and perhaps want to seem fast readers. The figures justify only this: over three quarters of those in the selected groups rate their attention to printed news of the outer world as rather low (para. 3).

**A less subjective check (paras. 4–5).** Scott asked how many papers his Chicagoans read daily: 1 paper, 14%; 2, 46%; 3, 21%; 4, 10%; 5, 3%; 6, 2%; all papers (eight then), 3% (para. 4).
- Two- and three-paper readers make up 67%, close to the 71% who rated themselves at 15 minutes. The readers of 4–8 papers coincide roughly with the 25% who rated themselves above 15 minutes (para. 5). (added step: 10+3+2+3 = 18%, so "roughly" is loose.)

*Intuition note:* Lippmann says the time guesses are not to be taken literally. He checks them against a less subjective measure, the number of papers read, and finds them "fairly well confirmed" (para. 4).

**Section 2: what people attend to (paras. 6–8).**
- Students named the "five features which interest you most". Just under 20% chose general news, just under 15% editorials, just under 12% politics, a little over 8% finance, a little over 6% foreign news, 3.5% local, nearly 3% business and 0.25% labor. Ignoring a scattering of other answers, about 67.5% picked news and opinion on public affairs (para. 6).
- Girls professed more interest than boys in general, foreign and local news, politics, editorials, the theatre, music, art, stories, cartoons, advertisements and "ethical tone"; boys in finance, sports, the business page, "accuracy" and "brevity". Lippmann says these differences correspond a little too closely with the ideals of the "cultured and moral" versus "manly and decisive" for him not to suspect the utter objectivity of the replies (para. 7).
- Scott's businessmen were asked why they preferred one paper to another. Nearly 71% named local news (17.8%), political (15.8%), financial (11.3%), foreign (9.5%), general (7.2%) or editorials (9%). About 30% chose on other grounds, from just under 7% for ethical tone down to one twentieth of 1% for humor (para. 8).

**Space actually given (paras. 9–12).** Lippmann says no space data were collected for the papers the two groups read at the time of the questionnaires. Lippmann uses Wilcox's analysis from over twenty years earlier of 110 papers in 14 large cities, covering over 9,000 columns (para. 9).
- Averages: news 55.3 (war 17.9; general 21.8 = foreign 1.2, politics 6.4, crime 3.1, misc. 11.1; special 15.6 = business 8.2, sport 5.1, society 2.3); illustrations 3.1; literature 2.4; opinion 7.1 (editorials 3.9, letters 3.2); advertisements 32.1 (para. 10).
- For a fair comparison he drops advertisements and recomputes, which he thinks justifiable for his purposes. His reasons: advertisements were a tiny part of stated preferences, and the press prints the ads it can get, while the rest of the paper is designed to readers' taste. Footnote: objectionable ads and rare crowded-out ads are exceptions (para. 11).
- Revised shares: war 26.4, general news 32.0 (foreign 1.8, political 9.4, crime 4.6, misc. 16.3), business 12.1, sport 7.5, society 3.3, illustrations 4.6, literature 3.5, opinion 10.5 (editorials 5.8, letters 4.7) (para. 11).
- Adding the items "which may be supposed to deal with public affairs" (war, foreign, political, misc., business and opinion) gives 76.5% of edited space in 1900. This is set beside Chicago's 70.6% of stated reasons (1916) and the students' 67.5% (1920) (para. 12). (added step: 26.4+1.8+9.4+16.3+12.1+10.5 = 76.5.)

*Intuition note:* In Lippmann's view, removing ads makes "what editors chose to print" comparable with "what readers say they want". The three percentages (76.5, 70.6, 67.5) are in the same range.

**Inference and conclusion (paras. 14–15).** Lippmann says this "would seem to show" that the tastes of business men and college students in big cities still correspond more or less to the averaged judgments of big-city editors twenty years before (para. 13).
- Since 1900, features have "undoubtedly" grown relative to news, and circulation and paper size have grown. So, he says, if accurate replies could be had today from more typical groups, you would expect a smaller percentage of time and space for public affairs. You would also expect the average man to spend more than a quarter hour on his paper, and a greater net amount of space for public affairs even though its share is lower (para. 13).
- No elaborate deductions are to be drawn; the figures "help merely" to make our notions of the effort of acquiring the data of our opinions somewhat more concrete. Newspapers are the principal source, supplemented by magazines, forums, the chautauqua, church, political gatherings, union meetings, women's clubs and film news serials. Even at the most favourable estimate, the daily time of direct exposure to the unseen environment is small (para. 14).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A paper has 50 columns: 20 ads and 30 edited. Of the edited columns, 12 deal with public affairs.
- Raw share: 12/50 = 24%.
- Share of edited space, ads excluded (Lippmann's method): 12/30 = 40%.
- Check the method on Wilcox's number: general news 21.8 of 67.9 non-ad space is 21.8/67.9 ≈ 32.1%, close to his 32.0+.

## Concepts
- [[attention-budget]] (new) — the amount of attention people give each day to informing themselves about public affairs, which Lippmann finds small (paras. 1, 3, 14).
- [[self-report-bias]] (new) — respondents may understate time or shape answers to fit ideals of how they should appear (paras. 3, 7).
- [[newspaper-space-analysis]] (new) — classifying printed columns by subject to see what editors supply (para. 9).
- [[unseen-environment]] (new; Lippmann's phrase here) — the part of the world from which we are exposed to information only indirectly; Lippmann does not define it in this chapter (para. 14). (canon link: [[Pseudo-environment]]; not stated in this unit.)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [metaphor] Daily attention as a scarce budget ↔ economics: attention as a limited resource allocated among uses — the text only counts minutes and shares.
- [structural-analogy] Comparing supply (space) with demand (stated tastes) ↔ media content analysis versus audience research — two independent measures cross-checked.

## Questions this raises
- How reliable are the three samples (students, prominent businessmen, big-city papers) for the "average man"?
- If readers' tastes follow editors' choices, which one drives the other? Lippmann does not say here.
- What follows from small attention time for the quality of public opinion? This unit only sets up the premise.
