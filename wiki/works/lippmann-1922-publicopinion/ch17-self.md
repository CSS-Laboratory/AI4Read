# The Self-Contained Community

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that the American democratic ideal was built for a small, isolated, uniform community where everyone sees the same facts, and that problems arise when its picture is applied to a wide, unseen environment.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Self-centred groups would struggle for existence if they rubbed against each other. Machiavelli described this plainly, while many other thinkers assumed a pre-set harmony. Democratic thinkers wanted self-rule and peace without force. They convinced themselves the ideal was at home in small farming townships, and Jefferson shaped the American image from them. That model needs isolation, security, simple affairs and shared morals. Its authors could imagine no other basis for spontaneous self-government. Once the township picture became a universal stereotype, people used it to view a complex world. Lacking instruments for reporting the real environment, they fell back on inherited pictures, fixed principles and legal deduction. Lippmann presents this as a limit of democratic theory in its formative period.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype (a simplified inherited picture) and a rough sense of who Hobbes, Machiavelli and Jefferson were, as the chapter explains them.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Lippmann says it has always been evident that self-centred groups would struggle if they rubbed against each other; Hobbes's "gladiators" passage contains at least this much truth (§1).
- Many schools escape conflict by assuming a pre-established harmony of roles. Machiavelli instead took conflict for granted and described how it works (§2, paras 1–2).
- Lippmann reads Machiavelli's "by the eye" as meaning that judgments are subjective; he says Machiavelli knew that people who see public relations privately are in perpetual strife (§2, paras 3–5).
- Democratic philosophers wanted self-rule and non-imposed peace. They located the ideal in remote rural townships, and Jefferson shaped the American image from it (§3).
- Lippmann says spontaneous democracy needs an enclosed, simple, secure environment; this lies behind the democrat's fear of foreign contact, trade, manufacture and crisis (§4).
- From the enclosed-community premise came the "omnicompetent citizen" and the assumption of enough public spirit. Important affairs then became those everyone cares about (§5, paras 1–2).
- Citizens pictured the wider world from inherited stereotypes and legal-moral principles, because they had few instruments for reporting it (§5, paras 3–5).
- In the township setting, facts, sources and morals could be assumed shared. Disagreement could then arise only in applying accepted standards to accepted facts, and reasoning errors would be quickly exposed in free discussion (§5, para. 5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1
Lippmann says groups of self-centred people would struggle for existence if they rubbed against each other. He says there is at least this much truth in Hobbes's famous passage. **Hobbes (quoted):** kings and sovereigns, because of their independence, are at all times in continual jealousies and in the "state and posture of gladiators," weapons pointing, eyes fixed on each other (§1).

### 2
- **Harmony schools.** One great branch of thought imagined a just pattern of relations. Each person has defined functions and rights. If he fills his role, it does not matter whether his opinions are right. Lippmann lists caste systems, Plato, Aristotle, the feudal ideal, Dante's Paradise, bureaucratic socialism, laissez-faire, syndicalism, guild socialism, anarchism and Lansing's international law (§2, para. 1).
- All assume a pre-established harmony (inspired, imposed or innate). The more authoritarian imagine a conductor; the anarchistic are inclined to think a "more divine concord" would be heard if each player improvised (§2, para. 1).
- **Conflict-minded school.** Other philosophers took conflict for granted and asked how their side could win. They seem more realistic because they generalize universal experience (§2, para. 2).
- Machiavelli is the classic case. Lippmann says he "truly described the technic of existence for the self-contained state" (§2, para. 2). His bad name comes chiefly, Lippmann says, from his attention to the Medici, from imagining himself the Prince, and from turning description into praise (§2, para. 2).
- **Machiavelli (quoted, *Prince* Ch. XVIII):** a prince must appear merciful, faithful, humane, upright and religious. Men judge generally more by the eye than by the hand. Everyone sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are, and one judges by the result (§2, para. 3).
- **Lippmann's reading:** this is the cynicism of a man who saw truly without knowing quite why. Lippmann takes "by the eye" as Machiavelli's way of saying that judgments are subjective. Machiavelli could not imagine people who had learned to correct their vision (§2, para. 4).
- Lippmann says Machiavelli found a world of people whose vision could rarely be corrected; since they see public relations in a private way, they are in perpetual strife. They see their own personal, class, dynastic or municipal version, take it as right, and meet others who are similarly self-centred. Then their existence is endangered, or at least what they, for private reasons, regard as their existence and take to be a danger; the end then justifies the means (§2, para. 5).

*Intuition note:* If people see only their own corner of a larger world and cannot correct their view, clashes follow when they meet. Lippmann takes Machiavelli's "by the eye" as his way of pointing to this subjectivity.

### 3
- Consciously or otherwise, democratic philosophers knew political knowledge was limited, self-government would have to be limited, and self-contained states stood like gladiators. They also knew men want to decide their own fate and find a peace not imposed by force. How to reconcile the wish and the fact? (§3, para. 1; the question is Lippmann's rhetorical one.)
- Greek and Italian city-states showed corruption, intrigue and war (Madison, quoted in a footnote, calls democracies "spectacles of turbulence"). Their own cities showed faction. So they looked, guided somewhat perhaps by Rousseau, to remote, unspoiled country villages, and saw enough to convince themselves that the ideal was at home there (§3, para. 2).
- Jefferson most of all shaped the American image of democracy. De Tocqueville (quoted) says the doctrine of the sovereignty of the people had been nurtured in the townships before it took possession of the state. Lippmann says it took possession of the minds of those who formulated the stereotypes of democracy (§3, paras 2–3).
- **Jefferson (quoted):** those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God, if ever He had a chosen people (§3, para. 3). Lippmann notes that Jefferson cherished almost exclusively small landowning farmers (§3, para. 3). He adds that if you wore glasses that obliterated the slaves, you could see the image of what democracy was to be (§3, para. 2).
- **Lippmann's judgment:** Jefferson was right that independent farmers come nearer to spontaneous democracy than any other society. But to preserve the ideal you must fence these communities off. So Jefferson disapproved of manufacture, foreign commerce, a navy and intangible property, and in theory of any government not centred in the small self-governing group. **A critic (quoted):** this would let us live in "eternal rusticity," "apathized and vulgar" (§3, para. 4).

### 4
- The ideal (ideal environment plus selected class) did not conflict with the political science of its time, but it did conflict with the realities. Stated absolutely, partly through exuberance and partly for campaign purposes, its special conditions were soon forgotten and it became the "political gospel." It supplied the stereotypes through which Americans of all parties have looked at politics (§4, para. 1).
- It was fixed because no one then could conceive public opinions that were not spontaneous and subjective. Democratic theory has never been able to conceive itself in a wide, unpredictable environment: "The mirror is concave." Democrats see every contact outside the self-contained group as a threat to democracy as originally conceived, and Lippmann calls that fear "wise." Spontaneous democracy needs simple affairs within every man's direct and certain knowledge (§4, para. 2).
- Lippmann says the democrat understood what an analysis of public opinion seems to demonstrate: decisions about an unseen environment are manifestly settled at haphazard (words from an Aristotle footnote). So he tries to minimize that environment. He fears foreign trade, distrusts manufactures and big cities, and wants protection if he must have manufactures. He founds Utopian communities in the wilderness (§4, para. 3).
- His slogans (Self-Government, Self-Determination, Independence) carry no notion of consent or community beyond the group's frontiers. In foreign policy, democracies have generally had to choose between "splendid isolation" and a diplomacy that violated their ideals. Lippmann says the most successful democracies (Switzerland, Denmark, Australia, New Zealand and America until recently) have had no foreign policy in the European sense. He says even the Monroe Doctrine arose from the desire to add to the two oceans a glacis (a protective buffer) of states republican enough to have no foreign policy (§4, para. 3).
- Lippmann says danger is a great, perhaps an indispensable, condition of autocracy (a footnote cites Fisher Ames wanting a formidable neighbour on the outside). Security was seen as necessary if democracy was to work. Insecurity means surprises, uncontrolled outsiders and quick decisions. Every democrat feels that dangerous crises are incompatible with democracy, because the inertia of masses means that for quick action a very few must decide and the rest follow rather blindly. Hence, Lippmann says, democratic wars are fought for pacifist aims and believed to defend civilization (§4, para. 4).
- The enclosure was not cowardice. The democrats saw a "dazzling possibility": every human rising to full stature. With what they knew of government, they could conceive autonomous individuals only in an enclosed, simple society (§4, para. 5).

*Intuition note:* Spontaneous self-rule by everyone, as Lippmann describes it, requires that affairs lie within every man's direct and certain knowledge. So the theory needs small, simple, safe surroundings, and democrats see contact outside them as a threat.

### 5
- From the premise came the dogma that one man is as competent as the next. Lippmann says the doctrine of the omnicompetent citizen is true for most practical purposes in the rural township, where villagers rotate through offices as jacks of all trades. Trouble began when the stereotype was applied universally, so that people looked at a complicated civilization and saw an enclosed village (§5, para. 1).
- The citizen was also assumed public-spirited and unflaggingly interested. Being public-spirited enough for the township turned easily into being enough for any purpose, since quantitative thinking does not suit a stereotype. Then only affairs in which everybody was interested seemed important (§5, para. 2).
- People formed pictures of the outside world from unchallenged pictures in their heads, supplied by parents and teachers and little corrected by experience. The sources were a few feeble newspapers, pamphlets, speeches, religious training and rumour. Opinions based on objective report were few compared with those based on casual fancy (§5, para. 3).
- Self-sufficiency became a spiritual ideal. Isolation, pioneer loneliness, democratic theory, Protestantism and limited political science led men to draw political wisdom from their own consciences. Lippmann says this explains the turn to legalism, with laws deduced from absolute principles. He notes foreign observers' surprise at the contrast between American practical energy and "static theorism." Community opinion about the outer world consisted chiefly of a few stereotyped images arranged by legal and moral codes and animated by local feeling (§5, para. 4).
- Summary of the logic: democratic theory, lacking instruments for reporting its environment, fell back on the voter's accumulated wisdom. In the self-contained community the facts were before everyone, sources were obvious and equally accessible, and a homogeneous moral code could be assumed (§5, para. 5).
- Therefore disagreement could arise only in applying accepted standards to accepted facts. Since reasoning was standardized, errors would be quickly exposed in free discussion, so "truth could be obtained by liberty within these limits." Training stressed drawing deductions from a premise rather than finding the premise (§5, para. 5).

*Intuition note:* On the township premise, free debate could find truth because everyone started from the same facts and values. Lippmann says the sources were obvious and the moral code homogeneous, so the theory saw no need to guarantee them.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A village has 3 farmers, A, B and C. All see the same broken bridge, and all agree on one rule: repair if the total labour cost is less than the total benefit, counted in days.
- Facts everyone sees: repair costs 6 days of work. Each farmer saves 3 days a year by using the bridge.
- Total benefit: 3 × 3 = 9 days. 9 > 6, so repair.
- If B first says "benefit is 1 day each" (3 < 6), A can check the same fact and correct B. The disagreement would be about applying the shared rule to the shared facts, so discussion can fix it. This mirrors Lippmann's village case.

Now add an unseen event. Only A has heard that a distant bridge-building tax is coming, which would cut the benefit to 1 day each (3 days total). A and B now argue from different facts. Debate does not settle it, because the cause lies outside the shared view. This is the situation the township model did not provide for.

## Questions this raises
- If the township premise is false for large societies, what "instruments of knowledge" can supply the missing facts (§5, para. 5)?
- Is Lippmann's claim that democratic wars are fought for pacifist aims a general pattern or an observation about a few cases (§4, para. 4)?
- Does the "omnicompetent citizen" fail only because of scale, or also because of assumed interest (§5, paras 1–2)?
- How would shared facts be guaranteed when "the sources of information" are no longer obvious (§5, para. 5)?
