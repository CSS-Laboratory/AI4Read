# Self-Interest Reconsidered

## L0 — One line
"Self-interest" explains opinion only once we know which of a person's many selves, and which learned idea of its interest, is engaged, so economic position, though it limits opinion, cannot by itself say which opinion follows.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks why the common claim that self-interest shapes opinion explains so little. His answer: people have many selves, shaped by situation. Both "self" and "interest" are conceived through learned ideas, not given by instinct. He tests this on economic determinism (Marxism). Economic position, he argues, limits opinion but cannot say which opinion follows. He then argues from James and McDougall that instincts are mostly learned in their objects and means. The theory of fixed class interest and the hedonist calculus are both called false determinism. Human ends and means stay open to education and change. The chapter matters for the study of opinion formation: who is addressed, and which self answers, shapes the response.

**Prerequisites:** the idea that people act on mental pictures of the world (earlier chapters); Marx's basic claim that class position shapes ideas; "instinct" as inborn behaviour.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The same story is re-enacted differently by each hearer, and the more mixed the audience, the more the responses vary (§1).
- A person has many selves, called out by situation. Which self is engaged strongly affects the opinion formed (§2).
- "Self-interest determines opinion" is so true as to be "mere tautology" and is not illuminating until we know which self selects and directs the interest (§2, para. 4).
- Preparing characters for all the situations men may meet, such as war and economic life, is one function of moral education. It depends on an honest exploration of the environment, because in a falsely conceived world we misbehave (§3, paras. 4–5).
- The naive doctrine of self-interest ignores cognition: self and interest are both conceived, mostly by convention (§4, para. 1).
- Lippmann reads Madison as claiming only that different property situations lead to different opinions, so one can tentatively infer a probable difference, not which opinions (§4, para. 3).
- Lippmann concludes economic determinism fails as a master key, citing Marx's and Lenin's predictions and the socialists' own agitation (§4, paras. 4–8).
- Instincts are fixed only in a "central part". Their objects and means are learned, so craving and action are not fatally fixed (§5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Stories change as they travel
- The same story is not the same to all hearers. Each enters it at a different point, re-enacts it, and adds his own feelings (§1, para. 1).
- In almost every story that catches our attention we become a character and act the role in our own "pantomime". An artist of compelling skill sometimes forces us to enter very unlike lives, but that is rare (§1, para. 1).
- Print checks individual fancy somewhat. Rumour has little or no check, so each gossip embroiders it (§1, para. 2).
- Larger audiences share fewer common words, so the common factors become more abstract. Varied people then give the story their own character (§1, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* A vague message is easy to fill with your own meaning. The wider the audience, the vaguer the shared message must be, and the more varied the responses.

### 2. Many selves
- A person's reading of public affairs depends on his many characteristics: sex, age, career, moods and others (§2, para. 1). He treats news as a "mimic enlargement" of his private life, not his private problems as samples of the larger world (§2, para. 1).
- That private life need not be the one he would describe: outwardly happy people may be brutal in public, and haters of their families may love "mankind" (§2, para. 2).
- Character is not fixed. It varies with time, memory and circumstance. The legend of the solitary Englishman who dresses for dinner in the South Seas bears witness to a fear of losing an acquired character (§2, para. 3).
- Examples of the stakes: the Japanese demand to settle in California may be conceived as wanting to grow fruit or to marry the white man's daughter. A territorial dispute may be conceived as a real-estate deal or as "a rape". The feeling is "tepid" in one case and "red hot" in the other (§2, para. 4).
- **Lippmann's conclusion:** it is "so true as to be mere tautology" that "self-interest" determines opinion, but the statement is not illuminating until we know which self out of many selects and directs the interest so conceived (§2, para. 4).
- Religious teaching saw "higher" and "lower" selves. A modern man would probably note many, less sharply separated, and would call the theologians' grouping arbitrary and external, though still an authentic clue to the variety of human nature (§2, para. 5). We see the same body but often a different man depending on whom he faces and on his circumstances (§2, para. 6).
- Consistency varies from Dr. Jekyll's split soul to single-minded Don Quixote. We distrust unrelated selves and find rigid ones arid (§2, para. 7).
- Characters take form from a man's conception of his situation, and he imitates the one he thinks fits. This gives the comedy of the deacon at a poker game (§2, para. 7).

*Intuition note:* "What does he want?" has no one answer. It depends on which role the person thinks he is playing, and the same situation described differently calls out a different role.

### 3. How characters are formed
- Lippmann says the analysis of character is "perhaps" still as doubtful as in Hippocrates' day (the humors). Recent theories (Cannon, Adler, Kempf) "appear to follow much the same scent", and no one would be likely to claim settled conclusions on nature versus nurture (§3, para. 1).
- Modern psychologists treat adult behaviour as an equation: manifest wishes over layers of repressed wishes, opposed by environmental resistance, equals behaviour (Kempf, footnote) (§3, para. 2).
- **Lippmann's own suggestion:** he has not seen it formulated. Repression may be fixed relative to the various selves, not the whole person. There are things one will not do as a patriot that one will do otherwise (§3, para. 2).
- Social situations can surface buried impulses, as psychoanalysis can. We learn about ourselves when the unexpected occurs (§3, para. 2).
- War example: murderous hate is controlled in civil life. When war breaks out, the selves attuned to love of country come first, and the impulse to kill gradually becomes central and then unmanageable. It spreads to old hatreds by analogy that, once feelings have cooled, we recognize as "the most far-fetched", and regaining self-control takes time after peace (§3, para. 3).
- Civilians have no ideal code of conduct for war, unlike the soldier (Croly: modern war is "inherent in the political structure of modern society, but outlawed by its ideals"). Only their higher selves have a code and patterns, and when they must act in what the higher regards as a lower character, profound disturbance results (§3, para. 4).
- Lippmann says preparing characters for all the situations men may meet is one function of moral education, which therefore depends on the sincerity and knowledge with which the environment is explored; in a falsely conceived world we misbehave. The moralist must choose: either give patterns for every phase of life or guarantee that pupils never meet the situations he disapproves of. He says the prevailing moral culture "so generally refuses" to do this, and each generation will go unprepared unless taught to conceive the personality it will have to be (§3, para. 5).

*Intuition note:* Our characters are tuned to situations we have prepared for. If morality ignores war or business, people meet them with no fitting character and behave badly.

### 4. Economic determinism criticised
- The naive doctrine of self-interest omits cognition. It forgets that ideas of things and of self are acquired, not instinctive (§4, para. 1).
- **Madison (Federalist 10)** says landed, manufacturing and moneyed interests divide civilised nations (§4, para. 2). In context Madison was arguing for the federal constitution and its tendency to break and control the violence of faction, which he traced to "the nature of man" in many forms: opinions on religion and government, attachment to leaders, and even "frivolous and fanciful" distinctions. The most common and durable source he names is the unequal distribution of property (§4, para. 2).
- **Lippmann reads Madison:** property differences cause *different* opinions, not particular ones. From differing economic situations one can tentatively infer probable difference of opinion, not what opinions will be (§4, para. 3).
- **Lippmann's argument from socialist practice:** orthodox socialists call the next stage inevitable yet agitate to produce class consciousness. So the theory rests on a hypothesis about human nature, not prophetic insight (§4, para. 4).
- Economic position does matter: income changes contact and opportunity, and machine workers tend to interpret experience differently from handicraftsmen or traders (Veblen). If that were all the materialistic conception of politics asserted, it would be an immensely valuable hypothesis, though the interpreter would often have to abandon it and always be on guard (§4, para. 5).
- But it is rarely obvious which of a man's many relations produces an opinion. This is the Smith (landlord, importer, shareholder, employer) and Jones (weaver) problem, and without special inquiry you cannot tell; the economic determinist cannot tell (§4, para. 5).
- Ownership predicts, "in ninety-nine cases out of a hundred", resistance to losing ownership. It does not predict long or short views, or competitive or cooperative ones (§4, para. 6).
- **Lippmann's claim about Marx and Lenin:** Marx made a good guess about the trusts and then "went wholly wrong". The first socialist experiment came from the collapse of a pre-capitalist system in the East. Lippmann explains this by Marxians assuming that position produces a clear conception of interest, which did not arise even in Marx and Lenin (§4, para. 7).
- **Lippmann's footnote:** Lenin in 1917 did not follow the Marxian formula but tried "will, force, and education". A later note says Lenin abandoned communism for now and said Russia must create capitalism first. Lippmann calls this "at least consistent with Marxist dogma" but says it shows "how little determinism" there is in the opinions of a determinist (§4, para. 7).
- **Conclusion:** economic interests are made of variable concepts of interest, so there is no single class interest. A class interest can be conceived "largely or narrowly, selfishly or unselfishly" (§4, para. 8).
- So the Marxian remedy fails. Property held in common might not be conceived as a whole, and a group that failed to "see communism in a communist manner" would divide again (§4, para. 8).
- Lippmann says that for every case where one man must lose for another to gain, there are "endless cases" where men simply imagine the conflict because they are uneducated. Under socialism partial access to the facts would still create conflict, so a socialist state will not be able to dispense with education, morality or liberal science. He adds that the Russian communists' zeal in propagating their faith would be odd if economic determinism alone determined opinion (§4, para. 9).

*Intuition note:* Position gives you the materials for an opinion, not the opinion. People still have to interpret their position, and interpretations differ.

### 5. False determinism and instinct
- The socialist theory and the hedonistic calculus are both "false determinism". Both assume that unlearned dispositions fatally yet intelligently produce one type of behaviour (class interest, or pleasure and pain) (§5, para. 1).
- Both rest on a naive view of instinct. James defines it as acting to produce ends without foresight and without previous education, though he radically qualified this view (§5, para. 1).
- Lippmann doubts such instinct figures in social life. James: "every instinctive act in an animal with memory must cease to be 'blind' after being once repeated" (§5, para. 2).
- McDougall: dispositions can be excited by ideas of objects, not only native excitants. Only the "central part of the disposition" stays common. Lippmann glosses this: man has an instinct of fear, but what he will fear and how he will try to escape is determined not from birth but by experience (§5, paras. 2–3).
- Appetites, loves, hates and fears attach freely to objects, and each generation inherits the results of the last one's conditioning, which gives the variety of human nature (§5, para. 4).
- So there is no prima facie case that a craving or behaviour is fatally constituted. Psychology and anthropology support this (§5, para. 5).
- **Conclusion:** men pursue their interest, but how is not fatally determined. Within whatever limits of time the planet supports human life, man can set no term on the creative energies of men and can issue no "doom of automatism" (§5, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* What people want and how they get it are learned, so they might be learned differently. This leaves room for education.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 100 factory owners and a proposed wage-raising law. Suppose 100 of 100 oppose losing their factory, so position predicts that opinion perfectly. On the wage law, owners differ by the idea of interest each holds: 40 see wages as cost and oppose; 35 see wages as customers' buying power and support; 25 see wages as a fairness issue and support. Check: 40 + 35 + 25 = 100. The rule "owners oppose" is right for 40 of 100, wrong for 35 + 25 = 60. The same position gives different opinions depending on which conception of interest is engaged (compare §4, para. 6).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Many selves called out by situation ↔ framing: the way a situation is defined changes the response — both make the interpretation, not the facts, the driver.
- [structural-analogy] Larger audiences share only abstract common factors, so responses diverge ↔ encoding/decoding: meaning is made again at reception.
- [metaphor] Rumour "grows wings and horns" ↔ cascades in social networks: content changes as it is passed on.

## Questions this raises
- If "which self is engaged" matters, who or what decides which self is called out: the situation, the speaker, or the media description?
- How could one test Lippmann's claim that position only limits opinions? What would count as "probable difference" (§4, para. 3)?
- Does Lippmann's claim that moral education should prepare people for every situation fit his treatment of the war example, or does it overreach?
- Is his reading of McDougall and James, that social life contains little blind instinct, supported by anything beyond the quotes (§5, para. 2)?
