# Stereotypes as Defense

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that stereotypes do more than save effort: they may be a defense of our position in society, which is a further reason we so often hold to them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people cling to stereotypes when a more disinterested (unbiased) view is possible? Lippmann answers that a system of stereotypes may be the core of our personal tradition and a defense of our social position. It is charged with feeling, because it projects our sense of our own value and rights onto the world. Aristotle's defense of slavery shows a "perfect stereotype": it shapes perception before reason works. Experience that fits a stereotype strengthens it. Experience that contradicts it is either explained away or, in an open mind, absorbed. German tales of Belgian snipers show a legend feeding old hatreds and provoking a defensive reaction.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype as a fixed mental picture of people or things (assumed as background); no math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Besides saving effort, stereotypes may be the core of our personal tradition and a defense of our social position (para. 1).
- They are not neutral: they guarantee self-respect and are "highly charged" with feeling, and any disturbance of them seems like an attack on the foundations of our universe (para. 3).
- Aristotle's "slaves by nature" claim is Lippmann's model of a stereotype. It blocks the doubt that would test each case of slavery (paras. 5–6).
- The hallmark of the perfect stereotype is that it precedes reason and imposes a character on sense data (para. 7).
- To some extent, outside stimuli blend with the stereotype, like red seen through blue glasses. Fitting experience reinforces the stereotype (para. 8).
- When experience contradicts the stereotype, a person who is no longer plastic, or who has a powerful interest at stake, dismisses the contradiction. If he is curious and open-minded, it modifies the picture (para. 9).
- The German tales of Belgian snipers show a stereotype-driven legend that tapped old hatreds. Catholic resistance to the tales was defensive (paras. 12–14).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §1 Stereotypes as defense (paras. 1–3)
- Economy of effort is not the only reason we keep our stereotypes. Lippmann says they **may** be the core of our personal tradition and the defenses of our position in society (para. 1).
- They form an ordered, more or less consistent picture of a *possible* world, not necessarily a complete one. Our habits, tastes, capacities, comforts and hopes have adjusted to it. In it we "feel at home", and it fits "as snugly as an old shoe" (para. 2).
- Hence any disturbance of the stereotypes seems like an attack on "the foundations of the universe". Lippmann says it is an attack on the foundations of *our* universe, and in big matters we do not readily separate our universe from the universe (para. 3).
- Examples he gives: a world where those we honor are unworthy and those we despise are noble is "nerve-racking". If maxims such as the meek inheriting the earth and the first being last were true, they would shake the self-respect of those who live as if they were untrue (para. 3).
- His conclusion: a pattern of stereotypes is not neutral, and not merely a shortcut for ordering "the great blooming, buzzing confusion of reality". It is the guarantee of our self-respect and the projection of our sense of our own value, position and rights. It is therefore charged with feeling and is "the fortress of our tradition" (para. 3).

*Intuition note:* A stereotype is both a map and a flag. Questioning the map feels like questioning who we are and where we stand. That may help explain why stereotypes are charged with feeling.

### §2 Aristotle, and what happens when experience meets a stereotype (paras. 4–9)
- **Setting (para. 4).** Aristotle defended slavery in the fourth century B.C., against growing skepticism. Citing Zimmern (Greek Commonwealth), Lippmann says Athenian slaves were largely indistinguishable from free citizens. The Old Oligarch is quoted: if citizens could legally beat slaves, an Athenian might be mistaken for a slave and beaten, since the Athenian people are not better clothed or better looking. Lippmann draws the inference that this absence of visible difference would tend to dissolve the institution. Aristotle's task (Politics, Bk. 1) was to teach Greeks a way of seeing their slaves that was compatible with slavery continuing (para. 4).
- **The argument (para. 5).** Aristotle: a man is a slave by nature if he is fitted to be the chattel of another and so is one. Lippmann reads this as saying only that whoever happens to be a slave is by nature intended to be one. He judges it logically worthless, and "not a proposition at all": it is part of a stereotype. Aristotle adds that slaves perceive reason but cannot use it, and that nature makes slaves' bodies robust for labor and free men's bodies erect and fit for civil life (para. 5).
- **Diagnosis (para. 6).** Aristotle erected a barrier between himself and the facts. By declaring that those who are slaves are intended to be slaves, he excluded the question whether *these particular men* are the ones nature intended. That question would have tainted each case of slavery with doubt. Since being a slave was not evidence that a man was destined to be one, no certain test would have remained. Each slaveholder was then to see his slaves as natural slaves and treat their servile work, competence and muscles as confirmation (para. 6).
- **Hallmark (para. 7).** The perfect stereotype precedes the use of reason. It is a form of perception that imposes a character on sense data before they reach the intelligence. Lippmann compares it to the lavender window-panes on Beacon Street and to a door-keeper at a costume ball. He says nothing is so obdurate to education or criticism. It "stamps itself upon the evidence in the very act of securing the evidence" (para. 7).
- **Travellers (para. 7).** Travellers' accounts often tell what the traveller carried abroad. One who carried his appetite and his comforts reports meals, bathrooms and tipping. A more serious traveller may bury his head in Baedeker and return with an orderly impression of Europe, rated one star or two (para. 7).
- **Blending (para. 8).** In some measure, stimuli, especially printed or spoken words, evoke some part of a stereotype system. Sensation and preconception then occupy consciousness together, like red seen through blue glasses giving green (para. 8).
- **If experience fits (para. 8).** The stereotype is reinforced. Lippmann's example is a man who "knows in advance" that the Japanese are cunning and happens to meet two dishonest Japanese (para. 8).
- **If experience contradicts (para. 9).** Two outcomes:
  1. If the man is no longer plastic, or a powerful interest makes revision inconvenient, he dismisses the case as an exception that proves the rule, discredits the witness, finds a flaw, and forgets it.
  2. If he is curious and open-minded, the novelty enters the picture and modifies it.
  - A third, extreme case: a striking incident plus general discomfort with his scheme may make him distrust all accepted views. He may then expect things not to be what they are supposed to be. In the extreme case, especially if he is literary, he may develop a passion for inverting the moral canon, making Judas, Benedict Arnold or Caesar Borgia the hero (para. 9).

*Intuition note:* In Lippmann's account, the stereotype shapes what is seen before reason weighs it. When experience fits, the stereotype is reinforced. When it contradicts, the two main outcomes are to defend the picture or to change it.

### §3 The German tales of Belgian snipers (paras. 10–14)
- Lippmann uses the tales to show the stereotype's role. The puzzle (a question he poses): why did an organization of German Catholic priests, Pax, begin as early as 16 August 1914 to refute slanders that were valuable to German morale? (He names "the Jesuit order" in the question.) Lippmann notes that atrocity stories and German readiness to believe them are not remarkable (para. 10). A later passage (para. 14) says the Catholic resistance was defensive, aimed at fictions that aroused animosity against all Catholics.
- **Van Langenhove's account (para. 11, Belgian sociologist).** Rumors spread early in the invasion and were printed in the press. They said Belgians, urged on by clergy, attacked by surprise, signalled positions, and mutilated wounded soldiers, and that priests preached such acts. The highest state powers endorsed them. Anger turned against the Catholic clergy generally. Protestants let old religious hatred revive, "a new Kulturkampf", and Catholics acted against this hostile attitude (para. 11).
- **Lippmann's speculative explanation (para. 12).** There *may* have been some sniping. It would be no less extraordinary, he says, if an army never under fire did not regard every bullet as unauthorized. Sensitive soldiers *can be imagined* convincing themselves that their victims were terrible people. The legend *may* then have reached censors and propagandists, who saw its value, whether or not they believed it. Since the legend came from "their heroes", the German civilians were not only entitled to believe it but unpatriotic if they did not (para. 12).
- **Why it spread (para. 13).** In the fog of war there was "no check and no control". The legend tapped an old hatred: for most patriotic Protestant Germans, especially of the upper classes, Bismarck's victories included a long quarrel with Roman Catholics. By association, Belgian priests came to stand for priests in general, and hatred of Belgians became a vent for all their hatreds. Lippmann compares this to some Americans who made a compound, "synthetic" enemy out of the enemy abroad and all their opponents at home, the Hun in Germany and the "Hun within the Gate" (para. 13).
- **Catholic resistance (para. 14).** It was defensive, aimed at fictions that stirred animosity against all Catholics, not only Belgian ones. Van Langenhove says the Informations Pax had only an ecclesiastical bearing and "confined their attention almost exclusively" to acts attributed to priests. Lippmann ends by wondering a little, without claiming an answer: what did this revelation of Bismarck's empire's attitude do to German Catholics, and is there any obscure link to Erzberger, the Catholic Centre Party leader, signing the armistice? (para. 14)

*Intuition note:* In Lippmann's account, the legend tapped an old hatred, and in the fog of war nothing checked it. The Catholics were the people it threatened, and their resistance was defensive.

## Toy example (ours, not Lippmann's)
A man holds the stereotype that clerks at shop X are rude. Over 10 visits, 6 clerks are rude and 4 are polite.
- **Rigid observer** (para. 9, first outcome): the 4 polite clerks are dismissed as exceptions that prove the rule. He counts only the 6. His evidence: 6 of 6 = 100% rude. The stereotype survives unchanged.
- **Open observer** (para. 9, second outcome): he counts all 10. His evidence: 6 of 10 = 60% rude. His picture is modified to: often rude, not always.
Same events, different records, because the stereotype acted when the evidence was taken in. The numbers are ours, to show the mechanism.

## Questions this raises
- Lippmann says the stereotype's grip depends on whether a person is "plastic" or has strong interests. What else decides whether contradiction leads to rejection or revision?
- Lippmann's account of the sniper legend is speculative ("may", "one can imagine"). What evidence would test his account?
- If stereotypes also guard self-respect, can corrective information work without addressing the feeling attached to them?
- Is the Aristotle case a stereotype that serves power, or one held in good faith? Does Lippmann say?
