# The Entering Wedge

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that experts who prepare facts for men of action, working inside organizations, are the true pioneers of a new social science, and he thinks they mark the beginning of experimental method, from which both action and science would benefit.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why is fact-based reform so unpopular, and where might a remedy begin? Lippmann says expert fact-gathering (research bureaus, audits, budgeting) breaks down the drama and stereotypes (simplified mental templates) that make opinion interesting, so it is dull or painful. Yet the Great Society (the huge modern society) was built by technical knowledge, and the more enlightened directing minds have gradually called in experts to make parts of it intelligible. As things go now, social scientists stand outside and explain decisions afterwards. If they instead prepare the facts before decisions, they gain a better position. He thinks that exchange of technic and results among expert staffs shows the beginning of experimental method, as comparable experiences begin to approach genuine experiment. Many existing organizations are already this "wedge", though not all are disinterested.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of stereotypes and the pseudo-environment from earlier chapters; the idea of a controlled experiment.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Fact-based reform is unattractive because it reverses how interesting opinions are built: it breaks drama and stereotypes and gives impersonal facts (§1).
- Every complicated community has sought special men. The Great Society was made by measurement and quantitative analysis, so lawyers' deductive thinking was not enough (§2, para. 1).
- The growth of experts was "blind natural selection", driven by executives' overload, and "not so much" a spontaneous creative evolution (§2, para. 2).
- Social scientists were accepted later than physical scientists mainly because they have few victories to show (the chief reason given); related points are that, unless they study the historic past, they cannot prove theories before offering them, and that they face a dilemma between library and action (§3, paras. 1–3).
- Social science's apologetic tone is explained by its opportunities, not by "capitalism"; dignity will come with a method (§3, para. 4).
- The key change is strategic position: the expert prepares facts before the decision, not explanations after it (§3, para. 6).
- Practical experts are, Lippmann claims, the true pioneers of a new social science. He thinks ("I think") that exchanging results among expert staffs shows the beginning of experimental method, as comparable experiences begin to approach genuine experiment (§4, para. 2).
- The "wedge" already includes bureaus, libraries, lobbies and associations; not all are disinterested, but all begin to demonstrate the need for expertness between citizen and environment (§4, para. 3).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Why the remedy is unloved
- The remedy is expert fact-finding: governmental research, industrial audits, budgeting. Its pioneers had to fight for a hearing (§1).
- Interesting public opinion is built from a casual fact, a large screen of stereotypes (simplified mental templates), and a dramatic identification. The bureaus reverse this: they break the drama and the stereotypes and offer an unfamiliar, impersonal picture of facts (§1).
- This is painful or dull. Those for whom it is painful (the trading politician, the partisan with much to conceal) often exploit the public's dullness to remove their own pain (§1).

*Intuition note:* A story with a hero is easy to follow. A table of costs gives no one to cheer for. So, Lippmann suggests, fact-based reform has to fight hard for a hearing.

### 2. How experts came in
- Every complicated community has sought special men (augurs, priests, elders). American democracy, despite a theory of universal competence, used lawyers (§2, para. 1).
- Lippmann's claim: the Great Society grew by technical knowledge, made by engineers using exact measurement. It could not be governed by men who think deductively about rights and wrongs. It could be controlled only by the technic that created it (§2, para. 1).
- So the more enlightened directing minds gradually called in statisticians, accountants, auditors, engineers, scientific managers and others. The ideal is an executive at a flat-top desk, with one typed sheet prepared for approval or rejection (§2, para. 1).
- Lippmann says this development was the work "not so much" of a spontaneous creative evolution as of "blind natural selection". Executives could not cope, so they demanded memoranda, marked letters, summaries, graphs, and engineers to choose machines. He shed burdens like a man struggling with a load who removes hat, coat, collar (§2, para. 2).

### 3. The social scientist's position
- Social scientists were called in more slowly than chemists or physicists, mainly because they have few victories to show (§3, para. 1).
- Reason given: unless dealing with the historic past, they cannot prove theories before offering them. A physical scientist's error costs others nothing; a social scientist's advice, if wrong, may have incalculable consequences. He is "far more responsible, and far less certain" (§3, para. 1).
- Dilemma of thought and action: in the library he relies on a casual, meagre printed record; in the world he needs a long apprenticeship before reaching where decisions are made. He cannot dip in and out; there are no privileged listeners (§3, para. 2).
- The man of affairs, observing that the social scientist knows only from the outside and recognizing that verification is possible only in the "real" world, has developed a rather low opinion of social scientists who do not share his views (§3, para. 2).
- In his heart of hearts the social scientist shares this estimate of himself and only half believes in his own work. His data are uncertain and his means of verification lacking; if he is really critical and saturated in the scientific spirit, he cannot be doctrinaire (§3, para. 3).
- Lippmann says that if so much social science is apologetic rather than constructive, the explanation lies in its opportunities, not in "capitalism". Physical scientists won freedom through a method whose conclusions could not be suppressed. Social scientists will gain dignity when they have a method, by turning the directors' need for instruments of analysis into opportunity (§3, para. 4). (The sentence is garbled in the source; see Reading notes.)
- Current data are unrelated material recorded spasmodically, quite often as accidents of administration (reports, debates, census, tariffs). It must be pieced together like the Piltdown skull. The scholar has practically no supervision of how the data are collected (§3, para. 5).
- The author's image: medical research without hospitals or animal experiment, based on patients' stories, nurses' differing diagnoses and tax statistics on druggists' profits (§3, para. 5).
- Categories come from officials who were administering a law or wanted to justify, persuade, claim or prove. Scholarly suspicion about discounting information is a virtue, but a very thin one when it is merely a corrective for social science's unwholesome position (§3, paras. 5–6).
- Main shift: the employed expert, as mediator among representatives and "mirror and measure of administration", prepares the facts for men of action. He stands in front of the decision, not behind it (§3, para. 6).
- Current sequence: man of affairs decides, then the scientist explains afterwards (ex post facto, "academic in the bad sense"). Preferred sequence: the disinterested expert first formulates the facts, later compares decision and facts (§3, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* Explaining a decision after the fact is guesswork about unclear evidence. Preparing the facts beforehand lets the expert compare the decision, which he understands, with the facts he organized, and make what wisdom he can from it.

### 4. The wedge
- Physical science's change came slowly, then fast. Once inventors were treated as cranks. As industry came to depend on invisible laws, scientists moved into offices and laboratories (§4, para. 1).
- Pure science developed faster than applied, helped by contact with practical decision (§4, para. 1).
- Even so, decision-makers had only commonsense. They administered a world complicated by scientists, so they now call in statisticians, accountants and other experts (§4, para. 1).
- Lippmann's claim: these practical students are the true pioneers of a new social science, "in mesh with the driving wheels" (words in quotation marks; the footnote cites Perry's 1920 address). Both will benefit, he says: action gains clarified beliefs, beliefs gain continuing test in action (§4, para. 2).
- Conditional argument: if it is conceded that all large forms of human association must, because of sheer practical difficulty, contain men who will come to see the need for expert reporting of their particular environment, then the imagination has a premise to work on. In the exchange of technic and result, Lippmann sees (he says "I think") the beginning of experimental method (§4, para. 2).
- When each school district, budget, health department, factory and tariff schedule is knowledge for others, the number of comparable experiences "begins to approach the dimensions of genuine experiment". His figures: 48 states, 2,400 cities, 277,000 schoolhouses, 270,000 manufacturing establishments, 27,000 mines and quarries, a wealth of experience "if only it were recorded and available". He adds that trial and error carries so slight a risk that any reasonable hypothesis might get a fair test (§4, para. 2).
- The wedge is driven by some executives and statesmen, municipal research bureaus (hundreds, some half dead), legislative reference libraries, lobbies, voluntary organizations, trade associations, publications and foundations. Not all by any means are disinterested; "That is not the point." All of them do begin to demonstrate the need for interposing some form of expertness between citizen and environment (§4, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* Recorded results from many similar units can work like a large experiment, if they are comparable and shared. Lippmann is describing a beginning, not a finished science.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A board must decide whether a school is expensive. Experts record the same categories in three districts.
- District A: $400,000 for 1,000 pupils → 400,000 / 1,000 = $400 per pupil.
- District B: $360,000 for 800 pupils → 360,000 / 800 = $450.
- District C: $270,000 for 900 pupils → 270,000 / 900 = $300.

Before deciding, the board sees that A is between B and C. A stereotype ("our schools waste money") is replaced by an impersonal comparison. Because the records use the same categories, the units are comparable. This is the preparation-before-decision role.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Comparable records across many jurisdictions ↔ quasi-experimental comparison: many similar units give approximate experiments.
- [metaphor] Executive's summaries, graphs, memoranda ↔ information overload and filtering: reduces load but shapes what is seen.
- [structural-analogy] Expert-prepared facts before decision ↔ evidence-based policy: the same sequence of evidence before decision.

## Questions this raises
- If experts shape the facts, who checks their categories, given Lippmann admits not all are disinterested (§4, para. 3)?
- Can impersonal fact reports compete for attention with drama and stereotypes (§1)?
- Is the "experimental" comparison of units valid if records are not collected in comparable ways (§3, para. 5; §4, para. 2)?
- Does the expert gain influence that the public cannot review?
