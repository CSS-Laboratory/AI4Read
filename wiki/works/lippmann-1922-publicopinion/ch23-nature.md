# The Nature of News

## L0 — One line
In the first instance, news is not a mirror of social conditions but a report of events that have taken a recordable, overt shape, and so it is shaped by systems of record, press agents, editors' routines and readers' stereotypes.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks what news is and why it so often misses the underlying story. His answer: reporters cannot watch everything, so newspapers watch fixed places where events become overt (openly visible) and recorded. Where good records exist (stock prices, elections), news is usually precise. Where they do not (motives, working conditions, public opinion), news is debatable, and interested press agents (publicity men hired by groups) supply the shape. Editors, under time pressure, rely on stereotypes and routine, and a wide audience is reached only when readers can identify personally with a story. Strike coverage therefore tends to show the inconvenience more than the causes. The power to create opinion lies in a combination of these elements.

**Prerequisites:** The idea of a stereotype as a familiar, ready-made pattern through which people read events; basic knowledge of how newspapers work; no math.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- News usually needs an overt, definable event; ongoing conditions tend not to be news until some aspect of them has obtruded (§1 para. 3; §2 para. 2).
- Newspapers work through routine "watchers" at fixed places, mostly where affairs touch public authority (§1 para. 2; §2 para. 1).
- News is more certain where a machinery of record exists, and more debatable where it does not (§2 paras. 4–5).
- Weak records create the press agent, who acts as both censor and propagandist (§2 paras. 6–8).
- A press agent who wants free publicity has to start something, as the suffragists did (§3 para. 1).
- In strikes, the overt act triggers stereotypes and the reader's own feelings, so the real issues are often missing. Lippmann thinks much of what workers see as deliberate misrepresentation comes from the practical difficulty of uncovering such news and the emotional difficulty of making distant facts interesting (§3 paras. 5–8).
- Editors need standardization, and newspapers copy each other's models, as with war news and, similarly, Russian news (§4).
- A wide audience is reached through personal identification; the more involved readers become, the more they tend to resent contrary news, and papers usually find it easier to let a subject fade than to change position (§5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Reporters cannot see everything
- Reporters are few and cannot be everywhere. The wide coverage they achieve would be a miracle if it were not a standardized routine (§1 para. 1).
- Papers station watchers at places such as Police Headquarters, the Coroner's Office, the County Clerk's Office, City Hall, the White House and the Senate. In the majority of cases they belong to associations that employ the watchers (§1 para. 2).
- **Given's example (John Smith):** a broker is ignored by the papers for ten years. When he fails and his lawyer files an assignment at the County Clerk's office, a reporter reads the docket entry and knows his business status within minutes (§1 para. 2).
- Lippmann's gloss: the papers know only the bald recorded facts, not what Smith or a novelist would know. Before a series of events becomes news it must usually make itself noticeable in some more or less overt act, generally a crudely overt one (§1 para. 3).
- Rumours give no definite peg for a story, and publishing them would be libel. Something definite with unmistakable form must occur; it may be, for example, a bankruptcy, fire, riot, arrest, speech, vote or price change. Until some aspect is an accomplished fact, news "does not separate itself from the ocean of possible truth" (§1 para. 3).

*Intuition note:* A paper usually cannot report slow change inside a life or a firm. It tends to report the moment the change leaves a paper trail or a visible act.

### 2. Records, certainty, and the press agent
- A good journalist finds news oftener than a hack. Usually, though, it is the stereotyped shape an event takes at an obvious place that uncovers the run of the news. The most obvious place is where affairs touch public authority: births, deaths, contracts, lawsuits, epidemics (§2 para. 1).
- So news is "in the first instance" not a mirror of social conditions but the report of an aspect that has obtruded itself. The seed analogy: news may tell you when the sprout breaks through, or what someone says is happening underground. It does not tell you how the seed germinates. The more points at which a happening can be fixed, measured or named, the more points where news can occur (§2 para. 2).
- **Unscored baseball:** if scoring were banned and the umpire decided by his own sense of fairness, a report would be the umpire's decisions, the crowd's noise, and a vague account of men moving on an unmarked field. Newsgathering needs apparatus and rules for naming, scoring and recording (§2 para. 3).
- Photographing every play could take the last vestige of dispute out of the game. Moving pictures settled which blow of Dempsey's knocked out Carpentier (§2 para. 3).
- Where there is a good machinery of record, news works with great precision: stock tickers, election returns (when counting and tabulating are well done), vital records (except where there is concealment or neglect), imports and exports through the custom house. The machinery exists for only some aspects of industry and government, and in nothing like the same degree for internal and over-the-counter trade (§2 para. 4).
- Lippmann thinks ("I think") there is a very direct relation between certainty of news and system of record. The reformers' main complaints concern topics where the paper is like the umpire without a score: states of mind, motives, public opinion, foreign policies, much news about the future, wages, working conditions, unemployment, and similar topics (§2 para. 5).
- Such data are at best spasmodically recorded. They may be hidden, not kept, or not yet measurable. The news is then "bound to be debatable, when it is not wholly neglected." Unscored events are reported as conventional opinions or not at all, until someone protests, investigates or makes an issue of them (§2 para. 5).
- **Press agents.** This enormous discretion over facts is steadily convincing every organized group that, whether it wants publicity or wants to avoid it, the discretion cannot be left to the reporter. **Cobb:** New York papers once counted about twelve hundred accredited press agents; by 1919 many direct channels to news were closed and information is "first filtered" through publicity agents (§2 para. 6).
- Were reporting the simple recovery of obvious facts, the press agent would be little more than a clerk. But facts on big topics are subject to choice and opinion, so everyone wants to choose the facts. The agent saves the reporter trouble with a clear picture, but it is the one he wants the public to see. He is "censor and propagandist," responsible only to his employers, and to the whole truth only as it fits their view of their interests (§2 para. 7).
- Lippmann's conclusion: modern facts do not spontaneously take a knowable shape. Someone must give them one, and since reporters cannot and there is little disinterested organization of intelligence, interested parties do (§2 para. 8).

*Intuition note:* Where a measuring and recording system exists, news can be precise. Where it does not, someone has to give the facts a shape, and interested parties tend to supply it.

### 3. The press agent's stunt, and the strike
- The virtues of a cause are not news, unless they are so strange that they jut out of the routine of life, because it is not worth saying that nothing has happened when nobody expected anything. So a publicity man who wants free publicity has to start something: a stunt that ties his cause to an event already news. The suffragists did this and kept suffrage in the news (§3 para. 1).
- Suffragists had a concrete objective: the vote, a simple right. Labor disputes are probably the chief charge against newspapers. The right to strike is simple, but the causes and aims of a particular strike are subtle (§3 para. 2).
- The measure of bad conditions is a vague standard of living, hygiene, security and dignity. An editor may occasionally, prompted by a social worker for example, send reporters to investigate, but necessarily cannot do so often. Convincing reports need many columns, time, money and talent; the steel worker in Pittsburgh required a staff and several volumes. A daily paper cannot regularly do such surveys (§3 para. 3).
- Journalism is normally not a first-hand report of raw material but of material once it has been "stylized." Bad conditions might become news if the Board of Health reports a high death rate. Otherwise they wait until workers organize and make a demand. An easy settlement has low news value. A strike or lockout raises it; a stoppage hitting a service readers depend on, or a breach of order, raises it more (§3 para. 4).
- The underlying trouble appears through symptoms: demand, strike, disorder. These are incidents in a process that is complex for workers. Reporter and reader lack direct experience of it, so they wait for an overt act, which calls up their stereotypes about strikes (§3 para. 5).
- Strikers feel the foreman's temper, monotony, bad air, bad housing, and invest the slogans with those feelings. Reporter and reader see at first only a strike and catchwords and invest them with their own feelings, which may be: insecure jobs, shortages, higher prices, inconvenience. These too are realities, but workers are at a disadvantage, since news of their grievances or hopes is almost invariably uncovered by an overt attack on production (§3 para. 5).
- The chain: complex circumstances → overt act → stereotyped bulletin → meaning the reader injects from his own experience. That meaning is "eccentric" to the central trouble but automatically the most interesting. Entering the central issues means stepping into other lives (§3 para. 6).
- So the easiest reporting describes the strike as interference with the reader's life. Lippmann thinks that a great deal of what looks to workers and reformers like deliberate misrepresentation (he says he thinks it the crucial part) comes from the practical difficulty of uncovering news and the emotional difficulty of making distant facts interesting, unless, as Emerson says, we can perceive them as only "a new version of our familiar experience" (§3 para. 7).
- Issues are often absent from headlines and leading paragraphs. Pressures toward this routine are: the economy of noting only the stereotyped phase, the difficulty of finding journalists who see what they were not trained to see, lack of space for unconventional views, and the economic need to interest readers quickly. An editor therefore prefers "the indisputable fact": the strike and the reader's inconvenience (§3 para. 8).
- Subtler truths about industry are unreliable without exact records and quantitative analysis. News will tend "to make of moles mountains, and of mountains moles" (quoting Emerson, quoting Isocrates). Absent real law and order in industry, the bulk of news will, unless consciously and courageously corrected, work against those with no lawful, orderly way to assert themselves. It notes the trouble arising from the assertion, rather than the intangible reasons. Lippmann adds that trying disputes by appeal through the newspapers puts a burden on papers and readers which they cannot and ought not to carry (§3 para. 9).

*Intuition note:* The reader's inconvenience is real but is a side issue. Because it is easy to see and interesting, it can crowd out the cause of the dispute.

### 4. The editor and mutual influence
- The editor reads bulletins and rarely sees the events. He must woo at least a section of his readers every day against rivals and works to deadlines. Each bulletin needs a quick judgment: understood, related to others, played up or down. Without standardization, stereotypes, routine judgments and a fairly ruthless disregard of subtlety, he "would soon die of excitement." Page size, caption length, libel law and the buying public all constrain him (§4 para. 1).
- Newspapers influence each other most here. At the war's outbreak, rich dailies that could pay cable tolls led, and their presentation became a model. It came from the English press, because English correspondence was easier to buy and English papers easier for American journalists to read, and London was the news centre (§4 para. 2).
- Russian Revolution news: access was closed by censorship and language, and "the hardest thing to report is chaos." Formulation passed to censors and propagandists in Helsingfors, Stockholm, Geneva, Paris and London. For a long time they were unchecked and created stereotypes of hate and fear that for a long time crushed journalism's desire to go and see and tell (§4 para. 2).

### 5. Selection, identification, and opinion
- Each paper results from selections about what to print, where, how large, and with what emphasis. There are conventions, not objective standards. Two papers' headlines (one about Britain pledging aid against French aggression, the other about Mrs. Stillman's other love) differ by the editor's judgment of what will absorb readers' half hour (§5 para. 1).
- That goal is not the ethical perspective of religion. It is to provoke feeling and personal identification, as in drama. The reader needs a "familiar foothold," supplied by stereotypes. Calling plumbers a "combine" cues hostility; calling them a group of leading business men cues a favourable reaction (§5 para. 1).
- The power to create opinion lies in the combination of these elements. Editorials reinforce, and give a clue where the news is too confusing to identify with (§5 para. 2).
- **Bagehot:** it has been said that a middle-class Englishman, if he can be got to think about "snails in Sirius," will soon have an opinion, and that on ordinary topics a grocer has a full creed on foreign policy, with no doubt at all. Lippmann notes that the same grocer doubts his groceries. Resting in the negative implies lack of interest or a vivid sense of competing alternatives (§5 paras. 3–4).
- The general-news reader is interested and cannot rest in the negative, yet has poor means of checking. Unless independent checks exist, his very interest may make it difficult to reach a balanced view. The more involved he is, the more he tends to resent a different view or a disturbing bit of news. A paper that has evoked partisanship therefore cannot easily change position, and usually lets the subject taper off (§5 para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Interest is the price of attention, but it also makes readers committed. A paper that has excited its readers finds it hard to change position.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town has 10 events in one week; each is a hidden condition or an act:
- 3 events are acts at watched places (a bankruptcy filing at the clerk's, a fire reported to police, a council vote).
- 7 events are unrecorded conditions (low pay in a factory, a manager's bullying, rumours of risk).
- 2 reporters cover the 3 watched places.

The paper can report 3 of 10 events, or 30%, and all 3 are acts. The 7 conditions (70%) appear only if someone makes them overt. Say workers strike over the bullying; that is event 11, an overt act. The report says "strike; buses delayed" and omits "bullying." Result: 4 of 11 events reported, and none of the 7 conditions directly. This is Lippmann's picture.

## Concepts
- [[stereotype-lippmann]] — a familiar ready-made pattern that gives readers a foothold and cues their feelings (§5 para. 1)
- [[news-as-overt-act]] (new) — a series of events usually has to make itself noticeable in some definite, overt act before it becomes news (§1 para. 3)
- [[machinery-of-record]] (new) — systems of naming, scoring and recording; where they exist news works with great precision, and Lippmann thinks the certainty of news is closely related to them (§2 paras. 3–5)
- [[press-agent]] (new) — interested go-between who shapes facts for reporters, as "censor and propagandist" (§2 para. 7)
- [[personal-identification]] (new) — readers attend to news they can enter emotionally, as in drama (§5 para. 1)
