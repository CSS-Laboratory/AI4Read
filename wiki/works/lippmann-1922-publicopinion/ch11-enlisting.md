# The Enlisting of Interest

## L0 — One line
Distant public affairs interest more than a few people for long when they are turned into pictures that allow identification with a side; outsiders especially need a struggle with suspense.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how remote public affairs become interesting enough to move people. His answer is that the mind reworks impressions. It tends to personalize abstractions, turn them into physical metaphors, and take part in the action. Audiences need a clear side to identify with and a struggle with suspense. Fighting appears at every turn in political imagery. Popular taste wants a realistic start and a happy ending. High costs bind producers to stereotypes. Political ideologies and propaganda follow the same formula: a plausible account of present evil, then an unverifiable happy future. The chapter helps explain why politics is staged as a fight; a footnote links clear hero-marking to news.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype as a simplified mental picture (earlier chapters); no math.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The mind actively reworks pictures; it personalizes and dramatizes. This is the "human stereotype" (§1.1–1.2).
- Names are porous and empty, so people add human traits, then physical metaphors (John Bull, river, rock) (§1.3–1.4).
- Dull, unseen public affairs need an "artist" to translate them into pictures. Clear thinkers are almost always good visualizers, but intuitive people may grasp inner feeling better. Intuition is hard to communicate (§1.5–1.7).
- An idea is not fully one's own until one identifies with part of the picture (empathy). Popular works almost always mark the hero clearly (§2.1–2.2).
- The audience must have something to do. Sex and fighting are the strongest exercises, and a fight about sex has the widest appeal; politics is interesting when there is a fight, or an "issue" (§2.2–2.4).
- Patterson says suspense and struggle, not beauty, explain the popular film's pull. Lippmann says she would be right only if she made clear that the masterpieces lack easy identification or a popular theme (§2.5–2.6).
- Popular taste: realistic beginning, desirable ending. High costs bind producers to existing stereotypes. The new forms of successful pioneers become new stereotypes (§3.1–3.4).
- Ideologies and propaganda use the same formula, and once public fiction and private urgency are enmeshed, the original self and stereotype may be lost to sight (§4.1–4.2).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. From impression to allegory
- **Mind as creator.** The mind is not a film that records once. Pictures fade, combine and are reworked into a personal expression; we "distribute the emphasis and participate" (§1.1).
- **Personalizing.** We personalize quantities and dramatize relations. Social Movements, Public Opinion and the like are treated as persons. Persons like the Pope or Lenin become ideas. The "deepest" stereotype is the *human stereotype*, which imputes human nature to inanimate or collective things (§1.2).
- **Why.** There are too many impressions to hold vividly, so we use names. A name is "porous": old meanings slip out and new ones in. Names are also too abstract, so we see them through a personal stereotype (§1.3).
- **Metaphor.** Human qualities are vague, so they are visualized physically. England becomes John Bull. Migration becomes a river or a flood. Courage is a rock, purpose a road, oppression the rack (§1.4).
- **Popularizing.** Making public affairs human requires abstraction from the original, then animation of the abstraction. We cannot be much moved by what we do not see, and we see little of public affairs. They stay dull and unappetizing until someone with the makings of an artist turns them into pictures (§1.5).
- **Visualizers and intuitives.** Lippmann says "one may, I imagine, assert with Bergson" that practical intelligence is most closely adapted to spatial qualities. A "clear" thinker is almost always a good visualizer, but is often by that much external and insensitive. Intuitive people, whose intuition is "probably" musical or muscular perception, may grasp inner qualities better. Visualization may catch stimulus and result, but the intermediate and internal part is often caricatured by a visualizer (§1.6).
- **Limit of intuition.** Intuitions are private and largely incommunicable. Where action needs many people of one mind, it is "probably true" that in the first instance no idea is lucid for practical decision until it has visual or tactile value. A visual idea matters only once it touches some craving of our own (§1.7).

*Intuition note:* We cannot see most of public life, so we fill the gap with simple characters and images. A visual idea matters to us only once it touches some craving or stress of our own personality.

### 2. Identification and the fight
- **Identification.** Pictures, then words that recall pictures, convey ideas best. The idea is fully ours only when we identify with some aspect of the picture, which Vernon Lee calls empathy. It can be subtle and unconscious (§2.1).
- **Marked handles.** In popular representation the handles for identification are almost always marked (you know who the hero is at once), and a work without definite marking is unlikely to be easily popular. The audience must also have "something to do" (§2.2).
- **Two strongest exercises:** sexual passion and fighting. They blend, so a fight about sex has the widest appeal (§2.2).
- **In politics.** The sexual motif is hardly present in American political imagery, with listed exceptions (war, scandal, race conflict). The fighting motif appears everywhere. Politics is interesting when there is a fight, or "issue," so issues are found even where differences do not truly call for pugnacity. Patterson's film advice is quoted: add an antagonist, an obstacle or a problem (§2.3).
- **Distant spectators.** People directly involved can stay absorbed without an issue. Outsiders need struggle, suspense and victory (§2.4).
- **Patterson (quoted).** She says suspense separates museum masterpieces from the Rivoli and Rialto films: people stay over an hour at the film, under ten minutes at the museum. Lippmann says she would be "wholly right" had she made it clear that the masterpieces lack either an easy mode of identification or a theme popular for this generation (§2.5).
- **Conclusion.** So that a distant situation is not a "gray flicker" on the edge of attention, it should be capable of translation into pictures in which the opportunity for identification is recognizable; unless that happens, it will interest only a few for a little while. "We have to take sides" (§2.6).

*Intuition note:* People stay interested when they can pick a side and wait for the outcome. Contemplating the true, the good and the beautiful gives the audience nothing to do, so it does not by itself hold distant spectators.

### 3. Realism, romanticism and stereotypes
- **Taste.** Audiences want a drama that starts realistic enough to allow identification and ends romantic enough to be desirable but not inconceivable. The film audience rejects pure fantasy (no familiar foothold in the age of machines) and rejects relentless realism because it does not enjoy defeat in a struggle that has become its own (§3.1).
- **Fixed by stereotypes.** What will be accepted as true, good or desirable is not eternally fixed; it is fixed by stereotypes from earlier experience. If investment were not so exorbitant, men of imagination could use screen and magazine to enlarge and criticize the stock of images. Given the present costs, film makers must adhere to the stereotypes they find or pay the price of frustrating expectation. Stereotypes can be altered, but not in time to guarantee success for a film released six months from now (§3.2).
- **Pioneers.** Pioneering artists resent managers and editors. Lippmann says this is "not quite fair": they forget their own rewards and the fact that they seek audiences no earlier artist expected (§3.3).
- **Catching on.** Success (Sinclair Lewis, *Main Street*) comes from saying what many were obscurely trying to say. The new form is then copied until it becomes a stereotype. The next pioneer finds it hard to make the public see Main Street otherwise (§3.4).
- **Quarrel with the public.** Pioneers stay on their chosen plane and will not soften the result. Big audiences are more interested in themselves, as schools and tradition have revealed them. An intermediate class of artists mixes realism and romance and, as Patterson advises (quoted), gives "the triumphant resolution of a set of difficulties" (§3.5).

### 4. Ideology
- Political ideologies follow the same rules: a recognizable real evil (the German threat, class conflict) forms the "foothold of realism." Because ideology also describes an unseen future, it crosses the frontier of verification. In describing the present you are "more or less tied down to common experience"; in describing what nobody has experienced you are bound to let go (§4.1).
- Examples: Marxists are "hard as nails" about present brutalities and "mostly sunshine" about the day after the dictatorship. War propagandists found bestiality everywhere, then promised eternal peace after victory (§4.1).
- Lippmann says "plenty of this is quite cynically deliberate": the propagandist starts with a plausible analysis, then stops analyzing because tedium would kill interest, and offers a "passport to heaven" (§4.1).
- The formula works when public fiction meshes with private urgency. Once meshed, the original self and stereotype that made the junction may be lost to sight (§4.2).

*Intuition note:* Political movements often begin with a believable picture of present trouble, then offer a hopeful ending nobody can check. Once people are committed, they may forget what first drew them in.

## Toy example (ours, not Lippmann's)
Take two versions of a news item on a town budget, scored on three conditions drawn from the chapter: (1) a marked hero/identifiable person, (2) an antagonist or struggle, (3) an unresolved outcome (suspense). This is a simple checklist, not Lippmann's measure.
- Version A: "Council approves budget 7–0." Hero: no (0). Struggle: no (0). Suspense: no (0). Score 0 of 3.
- Version B: "Mayor Diaz fights council to save the library; vote Friday." Hero: Diaz (1). Struggle: council (1). Suspense: vote pending (1). Score 3 of 3.

Total: A = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0; B = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. On Lippmann's account B is the more likely to hold a distant reader, whether or not a real "issue" exists (§2.3, §2.6).

## Questions this raises
- Does the "fight" formula explain why news favors conflict, and what would a non-conflict format look like? (A footnote at §2.2 says the clear marking of the hero "bears heavily on the character of news" and refers to Part VII.)
- Can stereotypes be changed faster if production costs fall, as §3.2 conditionally suggests?
- How could one test that identification, not only suspense, drives attention (cf. Lippmann's qualification of Patterson)?
- Is the propagandist's "cynical deliberateness" typical, or do many believers hold the formula sincerely?
