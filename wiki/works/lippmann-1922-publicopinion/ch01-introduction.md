# The World Outside and the Pictures in Our Heads

## L0 — One line
People act on mental pictures of an environment they cannot see directly, so the study of public opinion should start from the relation between those pictures and the real world.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks why people so often misjudge public affairs. His answer: nobody knows the large political world directly. Each person responds to a "pseudo-environment", a picture made by himself or supplied by others. The behaviour is a response to the picture, but its consequences fall in the real world. Lippmann supports this with stories (the 1914 island, the Joffre cult, the Senate debate on the marines) and argues that psychoanalysis and explanations by fixed motives pass over how the environment is pictured. He then outlines the book: the limits on access to facts, stereotypes, the making of opinion, a critique of democratic theory, and a proposal for an independent expert organisation that makes unseen facts intelligible.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond general knowledge of World War I-era politics; names are explained as they appear.

## L2 — Key ideas
- People's knowledge of the larger environment is indirect. They treat whatever picture they believe as if it were the environment itself (§1).
- Great men are usually known to the public only through fictitious personalities. Heroes and devils are made by the same mechanism (§2).
- Behaviour responds to the pseudo-environment, but its consequences occur in the real environment. At the level of social life, adjustment to the environment happens through fictions (§3).
- "Fictions" are not lies. They are man-made representations of varying fidelity, and they are unavoidable because reality is too big and complex for direct acquaintance (§3).
- The analyst of public opinion must keep three things in view: the scene of action, the picture of it, and the response to the picture. The 1919 Senate debate shows this (§4).
- Groups in conflict live in the same world but think and feel in different ones. In each of many centres of authority, decisions rest on some view of the facts, and Lippmann asks which view and why (§5).
- Before turning to innate human differences, Lippmann advises attending first to differences in what people know. The imagined world fixes what people do, not what they achieve (§6).
- Lippmann argues that representative government cannot work successfully unless an independent expert organisation makes unseen facts intelligible to decision-makers (§7).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §1 The island, the armistice and old maps
- **The island (1914).** A few English, French and Germans lived on an island with no cable and a mail steamer every sixty days. In mid-September they learned from the captain that for over six weeks the English and French had been at war with the Germans. For six weeks they had acted as friends when they were in fact enemies (§1).
- **Europe was similar.** On the continent the interval of mistaken belief may have been only six days or six hours, but there was one. Until 25 July, men made and bought goods they could not ship or import, and planned careers. They relied on the picture in their heads (§1).
- **Armistice.** When news of the armistice came, the end of the war was celebrated. Yet in the five days before the real armistice, several thousand young men died (§1).
- **Hindsight.** Looking back, we see how indirectly we know our environment. Whatever we believe to be a true picture, we treat as the environment. We flatter ourselves that this is easy to see in other ages, but it is harder to remember for the beliefs we act on now (§1).
- **Examples.** Men started for the Indies and found America. They hanged old women as evil. A caliph burned the library at Alexandria (§1).
- **St. Ambrose and Cosmas.** Writing in 389, Ambrose said that discussing the earth's nature does not help hope of the life to come, since Scripture suffices. Lippmann likens this to Plato's prisoner who declines to turn his head (§1).
  - A century and a half later, the monk Cosmas wrote a *Christian Topography*, basing everything on Scripture as he read it. His universe is a flat parallelogram, twice as broad east-west as long north-south. A conical mountain stands in the north, and the sun and moon circle it. The sky is a roof on four walls, and angels live between earth and sky. The Antipodes are denied because St. Paul is said to place all men on the face of the earth, so none could live on the back (§1).
  - For Cosmas, nothing in this map was absurd. Only by remembering his absolute conviction, Lippmann says, can we begin to understand how he would have dreaded Magellan or Peary (§1).
- **Parallel for war and politics.** Almost the whole of each party believes absolutely in its picture of the opposition and takes as fact what it supposes to be the fact. Therefore, like Hamlet, it will stab Polonius behind the curtain, thinking him the king (§1).

*Intuition note:* People act on what they believe the world to be, not on direct knowledge of it. The cost of a wrong picture shows up when action meets reality; thought alone may go uncorrected for a long time.

### §2 Fictitious personalities
- Great men are usually known to the public only through a fictitious personality. The saying "no man is a hero to his valet" holds only a modicum of truth, because valets and secretaries are often immersed in the fiction themselves (§2).
- Royal personages have at least two distinct selves, the public and the private. The official biographer gives the first and the revealing memoir the second (§2).
  - The Charnwood *Lincoln* portrays an epic figure, not an actual person. Oliver's *Hamilton* is, in Lippmann's words, a majestic abstraction; Oliver himself calls it an essay on American union. The Repington and Margot Asquith diaries are self-portraiture. Their intimate detail shows how the authors like to think of themselves (§2).
- **Victoria (Strachey).** The public greeted the girl-queen with enthusiasm. She was contrasted with her uncles, "nasty old men" in Strachey's account, who had vanished as winter snow does (§2).
- **Joffre (Pierrefeu, an officer on Joffre's staff).** For two years the world paid him almost divine homage. Parcels, candy, wine, portraits and letters arrived from many countries. He was called Savior of the World and Father of his Country. Children wrote to him, as did the half-crazy and young girls offering to marry him (§2).
- Lippmann says this ideal Joffre was compounded of the victory, the despair of the war, personal sorrows and hope. By the same mechanism devils are made. Everything good was to come from Joffre, Foch, Wilson or Roosevelt, and everything evil from the Kaiser, Lenin and Trotsky. To many simple and frightened minds, every political reverse, strike, obstruction or mysterious fire had its cause in these figures (§2).

### §3 Symbols, communication and the pseudo-environment
- **Rare and normal cases.** Worldwide concentration on one symbol is rare. In normal public life, symbols compete, so each is less inclusive and carries less feeling. They are subject to checking, comparison and argument. Only in the middle phases of a war do whole populations achieve the *union sacrée* (national unity), when fear, pugnacity and hatred dominate. At almost all other times, and even in a deadlocked war, a wider range of feelings produces conflict, choice, hesitation and compromise (§3).
- **After the armistice.** The symbol of Allied Unity soon vanished. Each nation's symbolic picture of the others and of itself frayed, and the leaders (Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George) became merely the negotiators and administrators for a disillusioned world (§3).
- Lippmann says it is "no matter here" whether this is regretted or applauded. His first concern is to treat fictions and symbols as part of the machinery of human communication (§3).
- **Miss Sherwin of Gopher Prairie** (from Sinclair Lewis's *Main Street*) cannot imagine three million men. Professionals think in terms of two hundred divisions, but she has no access to order-of-battle maps. So she fastens on Joffre and the Kaiser as in a personal duel. Joffre's office had no maps for a photograph, so maps were put up and removed afterwards. Popular ideas require a general with maps (§3).
- **Principle.** "The only feeling that anyone can have about an event he does not experience is the feeling aroused by his mental image of that event." Hence, until we know what others think they know, we cannot truly understand their acts (§3).
- **Two cases of one mechanism.**
  - A girl from a Pennsylvania mining town grieved when a window-pane cracked, because it meant a relative had died. She mourned her father, who proved alive by telegram. She had built a fiction from one external fact, a remembered superstition and a turmoil of remorse, fear and love (§3).
  - An Attorney-General, frightened by a bomb, convinced himself from revolutionary literature that a revolution would come on 1 May 1920. "Abnormality in these instances is only a matter of degree" (§3).
- **The common pattern** is: a casual fact, creative imagination, the will to believe, and a counterfeit of reality to which there is violent response. Under certain conditions men respond as powerfully to fictions as to realities, and often help create the fictions (§3).
- **Pseudo-environment.** A pseudo-environment is inserted between man and environment. Behaviour is a response to it, but the consequences of acts operate in the real environment. If the behaviour is only thought or emotion, it may be long before the fictitious world breaks. When it leads to action on things or people, contradiction soon develops, and one feels "maladjustment". At the social level, adjustment takes place through the medium of fictions (§3).
- **Fictions are not lies.** Lippmann means "a representation of the environment which is in lesser or greater degree made by man himself". The range runs from hallucination to a scientist's self-conscious schematic model. A fiction is not misleading so long as its degree of fidelity can be taken into account (§3).
- **No alternative.** The alternative, direct exposure to sensation, is no real alternative. The real environment is too big, complex and fleeting for direct acquaintance, so we reconstruct it on a simpler model. "To traverse the world men must have maps of the world." The persistent difficulty is to get maps on which no one's need has sketched in a "coast of Bohemia" (§3).

*Intuition note:* Simplified pictures are necessary, not a defect. The risks Lippmann points to are a picture whose fidelity cannot be taken into account, and one into which someone's need has sketched features.

### §4 The triangle, and the Senate and the marines
- **Triangle.** The analyst must begin with a triangular relationship: the scene of action, the human picture of that scene, and the human response to that picture, which works out on the scene of action. Lippmann likens it to a play whose plot is acted out in the actors' real lives, and notes that films often show this double drama: two men quarrel about money, and a flashback shows that the real motive is an old jilting (§4).
- **The case.** On 29 September 1919 Senators read a *Washington Post* report that American marines had landed on the Dalmatian coast. The paper said it appeared established that the orders came from the British Admiralty via the War Council and that the American Navy Department's approval was not asked. It added that cables told of forces under Mr. Daniels's presumed control carrying on what amounted to naval warfare without his knowledge, and that under the League plan foreigners would be able to direct American forces in emergencies (§4).
- **The debate.**
  - Knox demands an investigation. Brandegee, half a minute later, asks what would happen if marines were killed. Knox replies that it would be war.
  - McCormick says the administration is prone to small unauthorised wars. New submits a resolution calling for the facts (§4).
  - Emotionally the Republican Senators want to believe the report, because they are fighting the League. Hitchcock defends the action as lawful under war powers. Both sides then assume the report is true, and draw conclusions according to partisanship. They do so in a debate on a resolution to investigate the report (§4).
  - Lippmann's comment: it is hard even for lawyers to hold back response until the evidence is in. "The fiction is taken for truth because the fiction is badly needed" (§4).
- **Outcome.** An official report found that the marines had landed at the Italian Government's request to protect Italians, and the commander was thanked. They had not fought the Italians, and acted by established international practice unrelated to the League (§4).
- The picture was furnished, probably with the aim of deceiving, by a man who cared nothing about the Adriatic but much about defeating the League. The Senate responded by sharpening its partisan differences (§4).

*Intuition note:* The response came before the evidence did, because the story fitted what the audience already wanted. The "scene" (Adriatic) and the "picture" (Washington) were different, and behaviour followed the picture.

### §5 Many worlds, many centres of decision
- Even allowing for deliberate fraud, political science must explain nations that each believe they are defending themselves, and classes that each believe they speak for the common interest. They are likely to be said to live in different worlds; more accurately, they live "in the same world, but they think and feel in different ones" (§5).
- Political adjustment in the Great Society takes place to these private, group, class, national and sectarian "artifacts". They determine a very large share of men's political behaviour (§5).
- Lippmann sketches the complexity. There are perhaps fifty sovereign parliaments with at least a hundred legislative bodies, plus hierarchies of provincial and municipal assemblies. Inside each are parties, hierarchies rooted in classes and cliques, and individual politicians (§5).
- From these come commands that set armies in motion, tax, censor and so on. For each decision, some view of the facts is taken as conclusive. Lippmann asks "What view of the facts, and why that one?" Lippmann adds that the formal political structure exists in a social environment of corporations, associations and groupings that, as often as not, make the decision the political body registers, and he asks on what those decisions are based (§5).
- **Chesterton's lamp post.** Modern society, he says, is intrinsically insecure because it rests on the notion that all men will do the same thing for different reasons, which founds it on a coincidence rather than a communion or convention. Four men may meet under one lamp post for four different reasons, but expecting this night after night is unwise. Lippmann replaces the four men with governments, parties, corporations, professions, voters, editors, judges, bankers and advertisers (§5).

### §6 Method: what men know, not innate nature
- Before dealing with innate differences between men, we should do well to fix attention on the large differences in what men know of the world. Lippmann does not doubt there are important biological differences. But it is "worse than shallow" to generalise about comparative behaviour until the environments to which behaviour responds are measurably similar (§6).
- **Pragmatic value.** He says this refines the old nature-versus-nurture dispute: the pseudo-environment is a hybrid of "human nature" and "conditions". We do not know how men would behave in response to the facts of the Great Society, only how they behave in response to what can fairly be called a very inadequate picture of it. No honest conclusion about man or society follows from such evidence (§6).
- **Clue to the inquiry.** Assume that what each man does is based on pictures made by himself or given to him, not on direct and certain knowledge. The way the world is imagined determines what men will do, but not what they will achieve (§6).
- **Marxian communists.** They stress "materialism", yet place their hope on forming a class-conscious group by propaganda. Lippmann asks what propaganda is if not an effort to alter the picture men respond to, and likewise treats class consciousness and national consciousness as ways of realising the world (§6).
- **Other theories.** Pleasure and pain, conscience, self-interest and the wish for security or prestige each leave open how men come to think one course will serve those ends. Lippmann says such names stand for some of the ways people act, and instinctive dispositions may exist, but no statement of an end, or description of the tendency to seek it, can explain the behaviour that results (§6).
- **Theorising as evidence.** That men theorise at all shows that pseudo-environments are a determining element. If the connection between reality and response were direct and immediate rather than indirect and inferred, there would be no indecision or failure (§6).
- **Psychoanalysis.** Lippmann describes Freudians as concerned with the maladjustment of individuals to other individuals and concrete circumstances. He says the psychoanalyst almost always assumes the environment is knowable, or at least bearable, to any unclouded intelligence. That assumption, he says, is itself the problem of public opinion. The psychoanalyst examines adjustment to an X called the environment. The social analyst examines the X, the pseudo-environment (§6).
- The social analyst is in debt to the new psychology, since the study of dreams, fantasy and rationalisation has shown how the pseudo-environment is put together. Yet he cannot take a "normal biological career" within the existing order, or one "freed" from conventions, as his criterion. Conservative critics assume the first and romantic ones the second. Lippmann calls both ideas merely public opinions; the psychoanalyst as physician may perhaps assume them, but the sociologist may not use products of public opinion as criteria for studying it (§6).

### §7 Definitions and plan of the book
- **The unseen world.** The political world is "out of reach, out of sight, out of mind". It has to be explored, reported and imagined. Man has invented ways to see, hear, weigh and count beyond his senses, and gradually builds a trustworthy picture of the world beyond reach (§7).
- **Definitions.**
  - *Public affairs* (roughly) are the features of the world outside concerning other people's behaviour, in so far as it crosses ours, depends on us or interests us.
  - *Public opinions* are the pictures in those people's heads of themselves, others, and their needs, purposes and relationships.
  - Pictures acted upon by groups, or by individuals acting for groups, are *Public Opinion* with capitals (§7).
- **Plan.** The book first asks why the picture inside misleads. It treats the factors limiting access to facts: artificial censorship, limited social contact, little time for public affairs, compression of events into short messages, the poor fit of a small vocabulary to a complex world, and the fear of facing facts that threaten routine (§7).
  - Next it examines how stored-up images, preconceptions and prejudices shape the trickle of messages. It then looks at how limited messages, formed into stereotypes, become identified with a person's interests, and how opinions crystallise into a "National Will" or "Group Mind". The first five parts are descriptive (§7).
  - Next comes an analysis of traditional democratic theory, arguing that democracy in its original form never seriously faced the problem of pictures not matching the world. It then examines the English Guild Socialists. Lippmann's conclusion is that they ignore the difficulties as the original democrats did. Both assume a mysterious knowledge in men's hearts of the world beyond their reach (§7).
- **Author's thesis (first person: "I argue").**
  - In politics or in industry, representative government cannot work successfully, whatever the basis of election, unless an independent expert organisation makes unseen facts intelligible to decision-makers (§7).
  - Lippmann attempts to argue that serious acceptance of the principle that personal representation must be supplemented by representation of unseen facts would alone permit satisfactory decentralisation, and would let us escape the fiction, which he calls intolerable and unworkable, that each of us must acquire a competent opinion on all public affairs (§7).
  - The problem of the press is confused, he says, because critics and apologists expect the press to realise that fiction. News and the economics of journalism seem to show that newspapers reflect, and so intensify, the defective organisation of opinion. His conclusion is that opinions must be organised for the press if they are to be sound, not by the press as today; he conceives this as first a task for a political science that formulates in advance of decisions (§7).

*Intuition note:* Because no one can see the whole environment, Lippmann argues that citizens should not be expected to master all public affairs; instead, unseen facts should be made intelligible by an independent expert organisation.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A farmer believes the market price is 10 per crate (his picture). The true price has fallen to 6 (the environment). 
- His behaviour follows the picture: he ships 100 crates, expecting 100 × 10 = 1000.
- The consequence falls in the real world: 100 × 6 = 600.
- The gap is 1000 − 600 = 400. Only now, when the act meets reality, does he feel "maladjustment" and correct the picture.
- Had he only *thought* about the price without shipping, it might have been a long time before any break appeared. This illustrates §3's point on thought versus action. The picture fixed his effort (100 crates), not his result (600).

## Questions this raises
- If everyone relies on pictures, how can one tell the fidelity of a picture without direct access to the environment?
- Lippmann says the imagined world determines what men do but not what they achieve. Can empirical work separate effort from outcome?
- What would an "independent, expert organisation" need to be trusted by all groups with differing worlds?
- Can the criticism of psychoanalysis be tested, or does it describe different aims?
