# Intelligence Work

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that permanent, independent fact-gathering bureaus, kept separate from decision-makers, can make unseen reality visible and so help self-government with its central difficulty (if his analysis is sound).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Voters cannot directly see much of the modern environment, and Lippmann asks how self-government can cope with that. His answer is "intelligence work": expert bureaus that record, measure and compare what is out of sight and report it neutrally. To keep experts from becoming a bureaucracy, the staff that investigates must be separate from the staff that decides or acts. They need secure funds, tenure and access to the facts. Comparable measures and exchange of data would allow useful competition and coordination. If his analysis of public opinion is sound, he argues, this is the "clue to betterment": it can reduce the gap between the environment people conceive and the effective one.

**Prerequisites:** the idea that people act on the environment as they conceive it, which may differ from the effective environment; basic US government (Congress, cabinet departments).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Democratic practice has outrun its theory. Many bureaus already represent interests voters cannot see (census, children's statistics) (§1, paras 1–2).
- Lippmann says a good Secretary wants experts to bring the distant reality to his desk, without a "policy" of their own (§1, para 4). An expert who takes a side and meddles in the decision is soon discounted (§2, para 2).
- Experts influence decisions by making the invisible visible. This confronts decision-makers with a new environment; of three possible responses, adjusting behaviour to the enlarged environment is, Lippmann believes, the most frequent (§2, paras 2–3).
- Experts tend to become a bureaucracy that censors facts and absorbs decisions, unless their function is correctly defined. The only institutional safeguard Lippmann names is separating investigating staff from executing staff (§2, paras 4–5).
- Independence turns mainly on funds, tenure and access to facts. Lippmann tentatively sketches a charter, trust fund and life tenure (§3, para 3; §4).
- Comparable standards of measurement let competition serve ideal ends. Lippmann says the difference between higher and lower motives is clarity of aim, not altruism versus selfishness (§5).
- A network of bureaus could simplify by revealing hidden complexity, but it might also become a dead weight, and no institution is fool-proof (§8, paras 1–2). Lippmann thinks enough has been done to show that unseen environments can be reported in a way neutral to prejudice. As discrepancies shrink, federalism would work more by consent than coercion (§8, paras 3–5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Unrecognised representation
- Democratic theory says adult electors decide out of a will in them. In practice, adaptations have grown up that represent interests normally out of sight, and theory ignores them (§1, para 1).
- Examples: courts guarding forgotten interests; the Census Bureau; the Geological Survey; the Department of Agriculture; the Children's Bureau (§1, para 2).
- Published comparative infant-mortality statistics are often followed by a lower baby death rate. Before publication babies had no place in officials' picture of the environment. The statistics made them "as visible as if the babies had elected an alderman" (§1, para 2).
- Even if foreign ambassadors are as candid as they can be, they are still "limited channels". So the State Department keeps embassies, consuls and (Lippmann assumes) secret agents, with reports passing through the Division of Far Eastern Affairs (§1, para 3).
- Secretaries who turn to their divisions for help want the experts to bring the Far East to the desk, so that inferences hold in the East, not only inside the report. If the Secretary is "worth his salt", he will not tolerate the suspicion that his experts have a "policy". He does not want their policy. He wants what different classes of people think and are likely to do. He may take his policy from the Pacific Coast, but he will take his view of Japan from Japan (§1, para 4).

*Intuition note:* Interests that voters do not see are not automatically represented by an election. Counting and reporting can make them visible to officials.

### 2. The expert's power and its danger
- The best diplomatic service is the one where assembling knowledge is most divorced from controlling policy. In many British embassies and the Foreign Office there were nearly always men who quite successfully discounted the "war mind", whereas one American ambassador reported only what would cheer up the folks at home (§2, para 1).
- The expert's power depends on separating himself from decision-makers. Once he cares too much about the outcome, he sees what he wishes to see, and so stops seeing what he is there to see. His constituency is intangibles: non-voters, mute and unborn people, relations between things. Voting is in the last analysis a test of strength, so intangibles cannot be used to form a political majority (§2, para 2).
- Yet the expert can disturb the line-up of forces by confronting those who hold force with a new environment (§2, para 2).
- Men cannot long act in a way that they know contradicts the environment as they conceive it. Faced with an obtrusive fact they can: (a) ignore it, crippling themselves; (b) take it into account but refuse to act, paying in discomfort; or (c) adjust their behaviour to the enlarged environment, which Lippmann believes is the most frequent case (§2, para 3).
- The expert is not ineffectual. The subtler the elements, the more "irresponsible power" he wields, and relevant facts will increasingly elude voters. Because experts are human, they will tend to censor facts and impose decisions, and so become a bureaucracy, unless their function is correctly defined (§2, para 4).
- Only institutional safeguard: separate the executing staff from the investigating staff as absolutely as possible. They should be recruited differently, paid if possible from separate funds, answer to different heads and have no personal stake in each other's success. In industry, Lippmann believes that in time we shall see that, to bring industry under social control, the machinery of record will have to be independent of boards and shareholders (§2, para 5).

*Intuition note:* In Lippmann's account, an expert's power depends on not caring which decision is made. Keeping investigators apart from decision-makers protects that position.

### 3. Starting where one can
- Reform does not start on cleared ground. Attacking the administrative tangle all along the line would engage every force of reaction, and a truly logical reorganisation would disturb more passions than there is time to quell. Any new scheme would still need officials, and the old ones may sabotage it if too ruthlessly treated. Provided the principle gets a foothold somewhere in every social agency it will make progress, and the way to begin is simply to begin (§3, para 1).
- Better: put agencies that "hold up a mirror" into existing machinery wherever there is an opening. When outsiders, chiefs and subordinates see the same facts, obstruction diminishes. A reformer's opinion that a bureau is inefficient is only an opinion. Recorded, compared work moves the argument to "another plane" (§3, para 2).
- Suppose each of the ten cabinet departments had a permanent intelligence section. Independence from Congressional committees and from the Secretary would turn on funds, tenure and access to facts (§3, para 3).

### 4. Funds, tenure, access
- **Funds:** the legislature must keep ultimate control, but the staff should be safe from "left-handed, joker and rider attack". Lippmann suggests (tentatively, "might, perhaps") a federal charter with a trust fund for a minimum staff and a sliding scale for growth. Congress could repeal the charter but would have to do so openly (§4, para 1).
- **Tenure:** for life, with liberal pension, sabbatical years, and dismissal only after trial by professional colleagues (§4, para 2).
- **Access:** the organic act should give a right to examine all papers and question any official or outsider. This continuous work differs from sensational inquiries. The bureau should have the right to propose accounting methods and, if the proposal is rejected or later violated, to appeal to Congress under its charter (§4, para 3).
- Each bureau would link Congress and the department: the "Congressional eye" and the department's answer to criticism. Visible operation might reduce minute legislation born of distrust (§4, para 4).

### 5. Coordination, competition, measurement
- Bureaus should adopt comparable standards and exchange records. Departments buying the same things could then use the same measure and be treated as competitors, without over-centralisation (§5, para 1).
- The value of competition depends on the value of its standards, so the question is what the competitors compete for. The most visible standard (money, power, popularity, applause, "conspicuous waste") usually sets the rules. Lippmann says that by and large there are no measures for efficiency, productivity or service, so there is less competition for these ideals (§5, paras 2–3).
- Higher and lower motives differ not as altruism versus selfishness but as easily understood aims versus obscure ones. "Serve your country" has meaning in war, where it becomes concrete acts, but is a platitude in peace (§5, para 3).
- So the more comparable elements and quantitative measures ("index numbers", used loosely) one invents for desired qualities, the more competition can serve ideal ends. This works between workers, shops, schools, departments, ships, states and cities (§5, para 4).

*Intuition note:* People tend to compete for aims that are easy to see and measure. In Lippmann's view, to turn competition toward service we must invent measures for it.

### 6. Exchange of material; a "national university"
- Departments ask for information another department may already hold. Example: the State Department needs facts on Mexican oil, probably scattered across five departments, and now gets them by reference book or by private secretaries phoning. It should instead ask its own bureau, which draws on a central clearing house (§6, para 1).
- Such a centre would meet problems of definition, terminology, statistics and logic. Except a few diplomatic and military secrets, its material could be open to scholars, and it could hold "the makings of a national university". Staff training and recruiting would go together (§6, para 2).

### 7. Other levels of government
- The principle applies to states, cities and counties through federations of bureaus. Legal frontiers often do not match effective environments (example: the New York metropolitan district). The first step is coordination "not of decision and action, but of information and research" (§7).

### 8. Risks and conclusion
- The network might become a dead weight: red tape, pedants, soft jobs. "There are no fool-proof institutions" (§8, para 1).
- If one could assume a circulation of men, data and criticism between departments, industry and universities, the risk of dry rot would be smaller. Bureaus would simplify by revealing a complexity now unmanageable, and could reduce personnel per unit of result (§8, para 2).
- If his analysis is sound, intelligence work is "the clue to betterment". His suggestions are only illustrations. Present records are few and instruments crude, but Lippmann thinks enough has been done to show that unseen environments can be reported effectively, and to divergent groups in a way neutral to their prejudice (§8, para 3).
- If the intelligence principle works, men could overcome what Lippmann calls the central difficulty of self-government: dealing with an unseen reality. As long as there was no way of establishing common versions of unseen events and common measures, the only image of democracy that would work, even in theory, was an isolated community whose political faculties were limited by the "range of their vision" (Aristotle's maxim, as Lippmann cites it) (§8, para 4).
- Lippmann says there is now a way out, a long one: fundamentally the same way that lets a Chicago citizen, with no better eyes or ears than an Athenian, see and hear over great distances. It is possible, and will become more possible with more labour, to reduce the discrepancies between the conceived and effective environments. As that is done, federalism will work more by consent, less by coercion. Federalism swings toward imperial centralisation or parochial anarchy wherever the union is not based on correct and commonly accepted ideas; these do not arise spontaneously but must be pieced together by analysis, with instruments invented and tested by research (§8, para 5).
- No electoral device, area change or property change "goes to the root". You cannot take more political wisdom out of human beings than there is in them. No reform, however sensational, is truly radical unless it consciously provides a way of overcoming the subjectivism of opinion based on limited individual experience (§8, para 6; text ends mid-sentence).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
The War Department builds 200 m of brick wall for $3,000. The Post Office builds 150 m for $1,800. Totals alone ($3,000 vs $1,800) show no comparison. With a shared measure, cost per metre:
- War Department: 3000 ÷ 200 = $15 per m.
- Post Office: 1800 ÷ 150 = $12 per m.
The $3 gap is 3 ÷ 12 = 25% of the Post Office's cost. Now each department can see the other's result and compete on cost. This is Lippmann's idea of using the same measure for the same things (§5, para 1).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Independent intelligence bureau ↔ auditing/measurement in organisations: record-keeping separated from the managed unit — Lippmann draws this parallel himself for industry (§2, para 5).
- [structural-analogy] Index numbers for competition ↔ metrics that shape behaviour: what is measurable becomes what is pursued (§5, paras 2–4).
- [metaphor] Agencies that hold up a mirror to institutions ↔ feedback: outputs shown back to those who produce them (§3, para 2).

## Questions this raises
- Can any body stay as neutral as Lippmann requires, if experts also "enjoy power" (§2, para 4)?
- If the most visible measure rules the game (§5, para 2), could poor index numbers make competition worse?
- Are comparable records enough to overcome subjectivism, when different groups may interpret the same facts differently (§8, para 3)?
- How would open access for scholars (§6, para 2) be balanced against secrets and misuse (footnote on trade associations)?
