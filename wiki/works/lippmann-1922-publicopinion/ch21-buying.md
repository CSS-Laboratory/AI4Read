# A New Image; The Buying Public

## L0 — One line
Democratic theory assumed that good public opinion would arise spontaneously, but for an invisible environment the machinery for gathering news is technical and expensive, and readers pay for it only in a concealed way (through the prices of advertised goods), so the press must respect the buying public that advertisers want to reach.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter XX asks what is wrong with democratic theory. Lippmann says it fixed on the source of power (voting) and assumed that the people's will is instinctively good. He proposes judging government by results and by objective records and audits. Chapter XXI asks who supplies citizens with information. Civil liberty protects free speech, but it does not create the means of finding out distant truths. Readers expect news free and pay for the press only in a concealed way, through the prices of advertised goods, so the press depends on advertisers. Advertisers buy a prosperous, urban circulation. The papers must respect this buying public. Loyal readers give a paper as much independence from advertisers as modern economics allows.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an "invisible environment" beyond personal experience; basic newspaper economics (price, circulation, advertising).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Where institutions and education do not report the environment well, common interests largely elude self-centered public opinion and can be managed only by a specialized class that is hard to hold to account (Ch. XX §1 ¶1).
- The "democratic fallacy" is attention to where power comes from, not to how it is used (Ch. XX §1 ¶4).
- Lippmann ties human dignity to standards of living and, to the degree such criteria can be made exact and objective, wants government judged by them, with acts recorded and audited (Ch. XX §1 ¶6–7).
- Democratic thought treated opinion-making as a matter of civil liberties and gave news-gathering very little attention (Ch. XXI §1 ¶1, ¶3–6).
- Civil liberty is vital, but it does not guarantee public opinion about an invisible environment (Ch. XXI §1 ¶5).
- Readers expect news free; they pay for the press only when the payment is concealed, through the prices of advertised goods (Ch. XXI §2 ¶1–4).
- Circulation is a means to an end: it is sold to advertisers, so papers must respect the buying public (Ch. XXI §3 ¶1–2).
- Roughly speaking, general news depends on about 175 city dailies (Given, 1914) and press associations, supported by prosperous buyers in large cities (Ch. XXI §3 ¶3–4).
- Constant readers give a publisher as much independence from advertisers as modern economics allows (Ch. XXI §4 ¶2).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter XX, §1 — A new image
- **Lesson.** Suppose no institutions or education report the environment so well that public realities stand out against self-centered opinion. Then common interests largely elude public opinion. They can be managed only by a specialized class whose interests reach beyond the locality. This class is "irresponsible": it uses information that is not common property. It can be held to account only after the accomplished fact. (Ch. XX §1 ¶1)
- **Democratic theory.** It does not admit that self-centered opinions are insufficient for good government, so theory and practice conflict. **Cole's view (as quoted):** man's dignity requires his will to be expressed "in any and every form of social action". Theory assumes expression of will is men's consuming passion and that they have the art of government by instinct. **Lippmann replies:** self-determination is only one interest among others, such as a good life, peace and relief from burdens. The original assumption was that expressing one's will would spontaneously bring a good life, because the instinct was innate. (Ch. XX §1 ¶2)
- **Emphasis on mechanism.** Democrats sought a perfect environment and a perfect voting system, an "El Dorado". Theory worked well enough in limited areas and brief periods that were isolated and rich in opportunity. When isolation ended and society became complex, democrats kept devising better voting units. Meanwhile men cared about order, rights, prosperity, sights and sounds, and not being bored. Self-government is not instinctive, so men desire it for its results. That is why the impulse is strongest as a protest against bad conditions. (Ch. XX §1 ¶3)
- **The democratic fallacy** is preoccupation with the origin of government rather than with its processes and results. River image: regulating the source does not completely control the river. The crucial interest is how power is exercised, and that cannot be controlled at the source. (Ch. XX §1 ¶4)
- Control wholly at the source makes the vital decisions invisible. On most questions no will exists, so those in power act on opinions hidden from the electorate. (Ch. XX §1 ¶5)
- **Dignity redefined.** Dignity should rest on the whole personality, i.e. on a standard of living where capacities are exercised. Government is then judged by minimum health, housing, necessities, education, freedom, pleasures and beauty. To the degree these criteria can be made exact and objective, political decision (the concern of comparatively few) is brought into relation with men's interests. (Ch. XX §1 ¶6)
- **Realistic prospect.** There is no conceivable prospect that the whole invisible environment becomes clear to all. Even if there were such a prospect, it is extremely doubtful whether many of us would wish to be bothered to form opinions on everything. The non-visionary prospect is that each acts more on a realistic picture within his own sphere, and that more experts keep these pictures realistic. Beyond our attention, control depends on standards of living and audits measuring officials and industrial directors. We can increase control by insisting that acts be plainly recorded and results objectively measured. Lippmann hedges: perhaps we can only progressively hope to insist on this, since the working out of such standards and audits has only begun. (Ch. XX §1 ¶7)

*Intuition note:* Regulating power at its source (the vote) does not control how power is used. Lippmann moves the test of government from expressing the people's will to the results of power, and to whether acts are recorded and results measured.

### Chapter XXI (Part VII: Newspapers), §1
- The idea that men must study the world in order to govern it has played a very minor part in political thought. Reporting machinery had made little progress from Aristotle to the founding of democratic premises. (Ch. XXI §1 ¶1)
- **Pioneer democrat (Lippmann's account):** he would have been puzzled if asked where the information behind the people's will was to come from. He almost always assumed the people's will exists at all times. The task was to work out the ballot and representative government; if properly worked out under the right conditions (such as the self-contained village or shop), the mechanism would somehow overcome the brevity and narrow range of attention. Lippmann says guild socialists are still fixed on finding the right voting unit. (Ch. XXI §1 ¶2)
- **Civil liberties.** Democrats treated opinion-making as a problem of civil liberties. **Milton (quoted):** "Who ever knew Truth put to the worse, in a free and open encounter?" Lippmann asks whether the encounter generates truth, "like fire by rubbing two sticks". He finds several different theories of the origin of truth behind the doctrine: the truest opinion wins in the competition of opinions (probably sound if the competition runs over a sufficiently long time, i.e. the verdict of history); the capacity to recognize truth is inherent in all men, and truth freely circulated will win acceptance. The doctrine also derives from experience that men are unlikely to discover truth if they cannot speak it, except under the eye of an uncomprehending policeman. (Ch. XXI §1 ¶3)
- He stresses the practical value of civil liberties. If liberties must be curtailed in wartime, suppressing thought is a risk that might prevent recovery from the war, if the hysterics who exploit the necessity were numerous enough to carry the taboos of war into peace. Fortunately the mass of men is too tolerant long to enjoy professional inquisitors. (Ch. XXI §1 ¶4)
- **Limit.** Civil liberty does not guarantee public opinion in the modern world. It assumes truth is spontaneous or that the means of securing truth exist once interference stops. For an invisible environment this is false. Truth about distant or complex matters is not self-evident, and the machinery for assembling information is technical and expensive. (Ch. XXI §1 ¶5)
- Until quite recently political science was taught as if newspapers did not exist; Lippmann says no American student of government or sociologist had written a book on news-gathering. The public shares the disdain. It admits the press is the chief contact with the unseen environment, yet assumes the press should supply spontaneously a true picture of the world. (Ch. XXI §1 ¶6)

*Intuition note:* Freedom to speak removes interference, but it does not supply the facts. Finding out about distant or complex matters needs technical and expensive machinery.

### §2 — Readers' economic prejudices
- Readers treat truth as "not earned, but inspired, revealed, supplied gratis". They expect truth from the paper, however unprofitable it is, and paid until recently the smallest coin. They make no contract, will stop paying or switch papers when it suits them. Lippmann approvingly repeats a remark by "somebody": "The newspaper editor has to be re-elected every day." (Ch. XXI §2 ¶1)
- The relationship is an anomaly: the product is sold below cost, and the community judges papers ethically like a church or school. But the taxpayer pays for the public school, and churches and private schools have subsidies or fees. Law, medicine and engineering are paid by consumers. A "free press", in readers' attitude, means papers virtually given away. (Ch. XXI §2 ¶2)
- Critics of the press voice the community's moral standards. The citizen pays for the telephone, railroad and entertainment, but not openly for news. (Ch. XXI §2 ¶3)
- He pays handsomely to advertise, and indirectly for others' advertisements, hidden in commodity prices. Paying the price of an ice cream soda openly for the world's news would be thought an outrage. "The public pays for the press, but only when the payment is concealed." (Ch. XXI §2 ¶4)

### §3 — Circulation and the key papers
- Circulation is a means to an end. It is an asset only when sold to the advertiser, paid through indirect taxation of the reader. **Ochs (quoted in footnote):** a paper may set ad rates so that net circulation receipts are a credit. (Ch. XXI §3 ¶1)
- The circulation sold may be "quality" or "mass", with no sharp line. Customers for most advertised goods are people with surplus over bare necessities. A paper reaching the fairly prosperous offers advertisers most. Except for certain lines of goods, an analytical advertising agent does not rate circulation among the poor as a great asset, unless it is enormous, as seems to be the case with certain of Mr. Hearst's properties. (Ch. XXI §3 ¶1)
- A paper that angers those it pays best to reach is a bad advertising medium. Lippmann says unreported scandals of dry-goods merchants are less common than critics suppose. The real problem is that readers who do not pay for news-gathering can be capitalized only as circulation sold to merchants. Such a press is bound to respect the point of view of the buying public. A paper can flout an advertiser or attack a bank, but if it alienates the buying public it loses "the one indispensable asset". (Ch. XXI §3 ¶2)
- **Given (1914):** of over 2,300 US dailies, about 175 were in cities over 100,000. These make up the press for "general news" and the press associations. The rural and special press, by and large, take general news from them. International news may depend on press associations and a few rich metropolitan dailies. (Ch. XXI §3 ¶3)
- Roughly, news-gathering is supported by prices paid for advertised goods by prosperous city dwellers. They depend on trade, merchandising, manufacturing and finance. Their purchasing power is concentrated, though perhaps less in total than that of farmers and workingmen. (Ch. XXI §3 ¶4)

### §4 — The double claim
- This public are both best customers and advertisers, so the newspapers' effect on it matters. It is "not unanimous". Except in times of danger, respectable opinion is divided enough to allow considerable differences of policy, which would be greater but for publishers' being members of these communities. (Ch. XXI §4 ¶1)
- Publishers run a speculative business with circulation "free love", not a marriage contract. They aim to turn casual buyers into constant readers. A paper that can really depend on its readers' loyalty is as independent as a paper can be, given the economics of modern journalism; readers who stay by it are a power greater than any single advertiser and great enough to break up a combination of advertisers. **Ochs (footnote):** more readers means less dependence on the advertiser. (Ch. XXI §4 ¶2)
- When a paper betrays its readers for an advertiser, Lippmann says you can be fairly certain either that the publisher sincerely shares the advertiser's views, or that he thinks, perhaps mistakenly, that he cannot count on readers' support if he openly resists dictation. The question is whether readers who do not pay cash will "pay for it in loyalty". (Ch. XXI §4 ¶2)

*Intuition note:* Readers pay little openly, so the money comes through advertisers who want certain readers. The paper is bound to respect those readers. The author names loyal readers as the counterweight.

## Toy example (ours, invented numbers)
A paper costs 3¢ per copy to produce, sells at 1¢, and has 10,000 copies; 6,000 buyers are prosperous. An advertiser pays 4¢ per prosperous reader.
- Costs: 10,000 × 3¢ = $300. Sales: 10,000 × 1¢ = $100. Gap: $200.
- Advertising: 6,000 × 4¢ = $240. Total revenue $340. Profit $40.
- Now the paper offends the prosperous readers and loses 3,000 of them: 7,000 copies, 3,000 prosperous. Costs 7,000 × 3¢ = $210; sales $70; advertising 3,000 × 4¢ = $120. Revenue $190. Loss $20.
Readers cover only a third of the cost, so the paper must keep its prosperous readers.

## Questions this raises
- If readers' loyalty is the check on advertisers, what makes readers loyal to a paper that reports truthfully?
- Can audits and objective standards replace opinion for matters that are hard to measure?
- If key papers supply general news to others, how far does the buying-public bias spread?
