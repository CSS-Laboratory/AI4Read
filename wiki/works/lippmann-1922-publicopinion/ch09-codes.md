# Codes and Their Enemies

## L0 — One line
Moral codes rest on stereotyped pictures of facts, so, Lippmann argues, public opinion is mainly a moralized version of the facts (in the present state of education), and opponents who see different facts look perverse to us.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how our moral codes shape what we see, and how we explain people who disagree with us. His answer: every moral code contains a pattern of stereotypes about human nature, society and history, and this pattern largely decides which facts we notice. In the modern world, disagreements about the facts assumed matter more than disagreements about ideals. Codes treated as myths cannot separate their truths from their errors; codes treated as hypotheses can. Because we trust our own version of the facts, we tend to explain opponents as wicked, bribed or conspiring, rather than as people who see another aspect of the same reality. Tolerance needs the habit of treating our views as partial and second-hand.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a stereotype as a simplifying mental picture (earlier chapters); what a moral code is (a set of rules of conduct).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- What we see combines what is there with what we expect; expertise widens the aspects we can see, but a little expertise can also exaggerate stereotyping (§1, paras. 1–3).
- Stereotypes carry feeling and judgment: we "see" a bad man instead of studying him, unless we deliberately keep prejudice in suspense, so the judgment comes before the evidence (§3, para. 1).
- A people with altogether neutral vision is unthinkable in any civilization worth thinking about. Prejudice can be detected, discounted and refined, and what matters is whether prejudices are friendly (§3, para. 1).
- Moral codes assume a picture of human nature, the world and history; in the modern world, differences in assumed facts are more serious than differences in ideals (§3, paras. 5–6).
- A myth puts truth and error on the same plane of credibility; a scientific code treats its picture as a testable hypothesis (§3, paras. 6–7).
- Lippmann's theory: in the present state of education, public opinion is primarily a moralized and codified version of the facts, not a moral judgment on neutral facts (§4, para. 3).
- Codes differ within one person; this is one reason why generalizing about human nature is dangerous (§4, paras. 1–2).
- We avoid explaining opponents by "they see different facts"; we invent villains and plots instead (§5, paras. 1–5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1 (unnumbered opening section)
- Familiar cues call up whole images: a hat or gait makes us mistake a stranger for a friend; a tinkle in sleep sounds like a bell (§1, para. 1). Lippmann is "inclined to think" that perception with almost no expectation is extremely rare and highly sophisticated (§1, para. 1). Usually, seeing is a combination of "what is there" and "what we expected to find" (§1, para. 1).
- Expertness means more aspects we are prepared to discover, plus the habit of discounting our expectations (§1, para. 2). Few can be expert on public questions, and experts are expert on few topics (§1, para. 3). Sometimes a little expertness may exaggerate the habit of squeezing everything into our stereotypes (§1, para. 3).
- If we are not very careful, we tend to visualize familiar things with images already in mind. The American view of Progress and Success contains a picture of human nature and society, and we read these qualities back into successful men (§1, para. 4).
- The older economists, finding society too complicated, built a simplified diagram, like a child's cow (§1, para. 5). It had a thrifty capitalist, an entrepreneur, freely contracting workmen, a landlord, and consumers using the pleasure-pain calculus. In the books, these people always cooperated harmoniously (§1, para. 5).
- Popularized, this fiction became the economic mythology of the day. Those who benefited most by success came to believe they were the kind of men they were supposed to be (§1, para. 6).

*Intuition note:* A simplified model made for thinking can turn into a belief about how things really happened. Then we read the model's qualities back into the stories of real people.

### 2
- The vanquished did not recognize the official portrait (§2, para. 1). Lippmann cites William James: no one "sees further into a generalization than his own knowledge of detail extends" (§2, para. 1). Captains of industry saw the trusts as monuments of success; defeated competitors saw monuments of failure. Both sides published statistics to prove their case (§2, para. 1).
- When a system of stereotypes is well fixed, attention goes to supporting facts and away from contradicting ones (§2, para. 2). Perhaps because they are attuned to find it, kindly people discover much reason for kindness and malicious people much malice (§2, para. 2).

### 3
- Stereotypes describe the unseen world and also judge it. They are loaded with preference and feeling (§3, para. 1). Unless we deliberately suspend prejudice, "we see a bad man" (§3, para. 1). Lippmann lists many national and group types seen this way, among them the "greedy Jew" and the "wily Oriental" (§3, para. 1). He presents these as examples of prejudiced seeing, not as true descriptions. Such judgment precedes the evidence and contains the conclusion that the evidence is, he says, "pretty certain to confirm" (§3, para. 1).
- A people without prejudices is, he says, so unthinkable in any civilization of which it is useful to think that no scheme of education could be based on it. Prejudice can be detected, discounted and refined. The quality of thinking depends on whether prejudices are friendly to other people and ideas (§3, para. 1).
- Morality, good taste and good form first standardize and then emphasize certain of these prejudices. Rationally, facts are neutral to our views of right and wrong, but in practice our canons greatly determine what we perceive and how (§3, para. 2).
- A moral code is a scheme of conduct for typical instances, aimed at some purpose (God's will, success, service of mankind) (§3, para. 3). Applying it to a real case is uncertain: told not to kill, if his children are attacked, may a man kill to stop a killing? The Ten Commandments are silent, so interpreters multiply. Even then he may misjudge the facts. Lippmann says such confusions infected most Germans in August 1914 (§3, para. 4).
- Far more serious in the modern world than differences of moral code are differences in the assumptions about facts to which the code is applied (§3, para. 5). Examples: the Golden Rule assumes uniform human nature; Shaw's reversal assumes it is not. "Competition is the life of trade" packs in a whole tome of assumptions about economic motives, industrial relations and the commercial system. The claim that America will never have a merchant marine unless it is privately owned assumes a proved connection between a certain kind of profit-making and incentive. The Bolshevik claim "every state is an apparatus of violence" is a historical judgment, not self-evident to a non-communist (§3, para. 5).
- At the core of every moral code are a picture of human nature, a map of the universe and a version of history (§3, para. 6). Codes under the influence of science treat these as hypotheses, so their holders are humble. Codes inherited unexamined treat them as completed myths, so their holders are dogmatic (§3, para. 6). In a myth, truth and error, fact and fable are "all on the same plane of credibility" (§3, para. 6).
- A myth is not necessarily false; it may be wholly or partly true, and one that has long affected conduct is, he says, almost certain to contain much that is profoundly and importantly true. It never contains the power to separate its truths from its errors. That power comes only from accepting that every opinion is "only somebody's opinion" and open to the test of evidence (§3, para. 7). Asked why evidence is the better test, Lippmann says there is no answer unless one uses the test to test itself (§3, para. 7).

*Intuition note:* Two people can share the same rules ("do not kill") yet act differently because they assume different facts. A myth has no power to separate its truths from its errors; a hypothesis is held as open to proof or contradiction, so it can be tested.

### 4
- Lippmann claims moral codes of every kind (personal to international) centre on stereotypes about psychology, sociology and history. The same view rarely persists through all our codes (§4, para. 1).
- Example: two business partners in a war. The soldier gets a dollar a day and nobody believes economic incentive would make him a better soldier. The contractor gets a handsome profit and few believe he would produce munitions without it (§4, para. 1). The patriotic code assumes one human nature, the commercial code another. Codes are probably founded on true expectations to this extent: a man who adopts a code tends to show the nature it demands (§4, para. 1).
- So generalizing about human nature is dangerous. A loving father can be a sour boss and a rapacious jingo. Even people with the same religious beliefs can go to war, because conduct follows the assumed view of facts (§4, para. 2).
- **Orthodox theory:** public opinion is a moral judgment on a group of facts. **Lippmann's theory:** in the present state of education, it is primarily a moralized and codified version of the facts (§4, para. 3). The stereotype pattern "largely determines" which facts we see and in what light. Hence a journal's news policy tends to support its editorial policy; a capitalist literally sees one set of facts and aspects of human nature, his socialist opponent another, and each regards the other as unreasonable or perverse, when the real difference is one of perception (§4, para. 3).
- **American editor:** "There are no classes in America." **Communist Manifesto:** history is the history of class struggles (§4, para. 3). Each pattern makes confirming facts vivid and others vague. The communist will also see with different emphasis what he and the editor both see (§4, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* People often fight over what the facts are, not over what is right. Each side feels the other is perverse, when the real difference is perception.

### 5
- If my moral system rests on my version of the facts, anyone who denies it seems perverse, and the last explanation we try is that he sees different facts, because that threatens our assurance (§5, para. 1). People admit two sides to a "question" but not to a "fact"; only long critical education changes this. We become truly tolerant of an opponent only when we are in the habit of recognizing our opinions as partial experience seen through stereotypes (§5, para. 1).
- When a pattern fits experience at a crucial point, people take it as "reality." Lippmann's map example: an airline drawn from New York to Boston hides the real detours, and works as long as I arrive. A success story as a straight path works while he succeeds (§5, para. 2). If we insist on our charts and he rejects them, we tend to regard him as a dangerous fool and he tends to regard us as liars and hypocrites; thus we gradually paint portraits of each other. Admitting that the opponent saw another aspect of the same reality "would shake the whole scheme" (§5, para. 2).
- Fiume: the Italians in Paris held that Fiume *was* Italian, fixing on the Italian majority inside the city boundaries. The Americans saw a central European port, with Jugoslavs in the suburbs and hinterland (§5, para. 3). Some Italians explained the Americans' "perversity" with a rumour that an American diplomat had a Jugoslav mistress (§5, para. 3).
- Slander is a common way to explain away opposition. Lippmann believes it is less prevalent in America than in Europe, but rare is the official about whom no scandal is repeated (§5, para. 4).
- Opposition becomes villains and conspiracies: profiteers, a capitalist plot, bought statesmen, agitators, spies. At the extreme, everything from strikes to short skirts becomes a sub-plot of a plot by Moscow, Rome, Free Masons, the Japanese or the "Elders of Zion" (§5, para. 5). Lippmann introduces this list with "if you go stark, staring mad looking for plots"; it is not offered as a set of true claims.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Four true facts about one merger: F1 the price fell from \$10 to \$9 (a 10% fall, since 1/10 = 0.10); F2 the firm hired 200 workers; F3 twelve small shops closed; F4 the firm bought three rivals.
- Pattern A ("progress, economy") makes F1 and F2 vivid: 2 of 4 facts noticed.
- Pattern B ("restraint of trade") makes F3 and F4 vivid: the other 2 of 4.
Each observer cites only true facts, so each thinks the other has ignored the "obvious." Under Lippmann's theory, the gap is one of perception, not honesty. Both then risk explaining the other as bought or malicious (§4, para. 3; §5, para. 1).

## Questions this raises
- If a code is mainly a version of the facts, how can two sides ever agree on what counts as evidence?
- Can "friendly" prejudices be taught, and how would we test whether they are friendlier?
- Does the claim that codes differ within one person (§4, para. 2) conflict with the idea of one stable stereotype system?
- How far can the habit of seeing opinions as partial reduce conspiracy explanations?
