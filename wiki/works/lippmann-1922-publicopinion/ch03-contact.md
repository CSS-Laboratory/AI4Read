# Contact and Opportunity

## L0 — One line
Beyond censorship and secrecy, the circulation of ideas is limited by travel, trade, cost and supply, and the "social set" (the group whose approval matters most to us) plays a big part in deciding which information and sources are admissible.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks why much of the public hears little of what happens. His answer has several parts. Reaching everybody is very hard: even the US wartime propaganda needed enormous effort. Access depends on transport, communication costs and income. It also depends on people's own habits of staying within their circle. The social set, a group of people whose approval matters to us, filters what is admissible; its leaders watch other sets, and a few people who move between sets link them. Opinion on large affairs often trickles down from a cosmopolitan (international) top set. Since contact and position limit what we see and may know, moral judgment is more common than constructive thought.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday ideas of social class, social circles and mass media.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Lippmann believes the huge wartime campaign very largely succeeded, while the war lasted, in creating something that might almost be called one public opinion; in peace nothing similar exists, so whole groups hear only vaguely about much that happens (§1.3–1.4).
- Transport, mail, cables, ports, tariffs and rates shape the circulation of ideas. Monopoly is one barrier; cost and limited supply are even greater ones (§1.5–1.6).
- Income sets how much communication is possible, but ideas decide how income is spent. Some limits are self-imposed, a lack of curiosity (§2.1–2.2).
- The social set is where ideas are sorted, accepted or rejected, and where admissible sources are fixed (§2.3–2.4).
- Social standing in the first generation, at least, follows the character of a man's work rather than income (§2.6).
- A set resembles a biological clan more than an economic class. Its leaders guard its pattern and watch other sets; a few boundary-crossers (people who move between sets) link sets (§3.1–3.3).
- Imitation tends to run from superior to inferior; the top, international set, where public and private affairs practically merge, holds great power over foreign affairs (§3.4–3.5).
- Position and contact limit what we can see and are allowed to know. Effective thinking needs judgments to be "liquidated" (set aside), which is hard to sustain (§4.2).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Limits on the circulation of ideas
Censorship and privacy intercept much information at its source. A much larger body of fact never reaches the whole public, or reaches it very slowly (§1.1).

**Wartime propaganda as a measure.** Lippmann uses George Creel's account as a rough estimate of the effort needed to reach "everybody" (§1.2). Creel's machinery included:
- more than 6,000 news releases;
- 75,000 Four Minute Men giving at least 755,190 speeches to over 300 million people in total;
- Boy Scouts delivering Wilson's addresses, and periodicals sent to 600,000 teachers;
- 200,000 lantern slides and 1,438 poster and advertising designs;
- chambers of commerce, churches, societies and schools as channels (§1.3).

Lippmann says this excludes the Liberty Loans, food propaganda, Red Cross and other campaigns, and Allied bureaus (§1.3). He thinks it was probably the largest, most intensive effort to carry a fairly uniform set of ideas to a nation. He believes it very largely succeeded, while the war lasted, in creating something that might almost be called one public opinion (§1.4). In peace nothing like it exists, so ghettoes, enclaves and classes hear only vaguely about much that happens. They live "in grooves" and meet few people of other sorts (§1.4–1.5).

**Channels.** Travel, trade, mails, wires, radio and railroads affect the supply and quality of information in an intricate way. They are in turn affected by technical, economic and political conditions. Examples are passport rules, rates, new ports and tariffs. Salem was reduced to a genteel provincial town by a change in shipbuilding. Faster transit is not necessarily all good; Lippmann says it would be difficult to say that France's Paris-centred railway system has been "an unmixed blessing" (§1.5). He calls the League of Nations' study of railroad transit and access to the sea one of the most constructive features of its program (§1.6). Monopoly of cables, ports, passes and similar points is a barrier to exchange of news and opinion. Cost and available supply are even greater barriers, even without monopoly (§1.6).

*Intuition note:* Reaching a whole nation is costly even for a government in wartime. In ordinary times, with nothing like that effort, some groups hear only vaguely, and what reaches people depends partly on roads, prices and who controls them.

### 2. Income, self-limitation and the social set
Income lets a person overcome almost every tangible obstacle. Individual and community income determine how much communication is possible. But ideas determine how income is spent (§2.1). Some people spend spare time and money on motoring, bridge and films, talking to the same people. Lippmann says they suffer from "anemia", a lack of appetite and curiosity, not from censorship or cost (§2.2). They move within a fixed radius of acquaintances "according to the law and the gospel of their social set". Men's circle of talk is wider than their set; women's is frequently almost identical to it (§2.3).

**Social set.** It is those who count as "people" in "people are saying", the people whose approval matters most to us. In it, ideas from reading, lectures and talk converge and are sorted, accepted, rejected and sanctioned. There it is decided which authorities and sources are admissible (§2.3–2.4). The usual mark of a set is the presumption that the children may intermarry. Contact between sets at the same level is easy. Between higher and lower sets there is hesitation and "a faint malaise". Individuals in the US do move somewhat freely between sets (§2.5).

**Standing and income.** Economic position is not measured by income. In the first generation, at least, character of work determines standing, and it may take a generation or two to fade. Banking, law and medicine are rated at a different social value from salesmanship or teaching, and those differently again from plumbing or dressmaking. Financial return does not necessarily coincide with these grades (§2.6).

*Intuition note:* Besides external barriers, people limit themselves. Their social circle decides which sources count as acceptable, so wide access does not guarantee wide knowledge.

### 3. Structure of social sets
A set when formed is not a mere economic class but more nearly resembles a biological clan, tied to love, marriage and children. Its opinions meet canons such as Family Tradition, Respectability, Propriety and Taste. This picture of the set includes an authorized version of the standing of the other sets. The knowledge becomes overt at marriage, war or upheaval. Lippmann links it to Trotter's "instinct of the herd" (§3.1).

**Leaders and links.** Social leaders, like the van der Luydens and Mrs. Manson Mingott in *The Age of Innocence*, are custodians and interpreters of the set's pattern. They must also know what other sets are doing, "a kind of ministry of foreign affairs" (§3.2). Vertically, society is bound together, so far as it is bound by social contact, by exceptional, often suspect people who move between sets (Beaufort and Olenska). These make personal channels through which Tarde's laws of imitation operate. Large sections have no such channels. They rely on "patented accounts" of society and films of high life (§3.3). Lippmann says the hierarchy is bound together by the social leaders, who at any one level form something like a set of their own. Such groups may develop a hierarchy of their own, but among the assimilated mass that considers itself the "nation" he says a variety of personal contacts lets standards circulate (§3.3).

**Radiant points.** Citing Ross, Lippmann says the superior is likely to be imitated by the inferior, the powerful by subordinates, the rich by the poor and the city by the country. The powerful, rich, urban top set is fundamentally international in the western hemisphere; London is in many ways its centre. In it the distinction between public and private affairs practically disappears (§3.4). It has only a fluctuating control over the US national government, but its power in foreign affairs is always very great, and in wartime its prestige is enormously enhanced. Its members have concrete personal contact with the outer world. In matters affecting the larger relationships of the Great Society, Dr. Kennicott of Gopher Prairie will often be found holding what he thinks is purely his own opinion, though it has trickled down from High Society, transmuted by provincial sets (§3.5).

*Intuition note:* Most members of a set live within it. A few boundary-crossers and a cosmopolitan top group connect sets, so opinions on big affairs that feel personal may be second-hand, as with Dr. Kennicott.

### 4. Conclusion
The inquiry need not give an account of the whole social tissue. It need only fix in mind how big the social set's part is in our spiritual contact with the world, how it tends to fix what is admissible, and how it determines how things shall be judged. Each set more or less determines for itself affairs within its competence, above all the detailed administration of judgment. The judgment itself is formed on patterns that may be inherited from the past or transmitted or imitated from other sets (§4.1). Unlike almost every other set, whose opinions are first-hand only about local affairs, the highest set has the big decisions of war and peace as intimate experiences within a circle of what, potentially at least, are personal acquaintances (§4.1). Since position and contact shape what can and may be known, moral judgment is much more common than constructive thought. In truly effective thinking, he says, the prime necessity is to liquidate judgments, regain an "innocent eye", disentangle feelings, and be curious and open-hearted. Lippmann adds that political opinion on the scale of the Great Society requires selfless equanimity rarely attainable by anyone for any length of time; we are concerned in public affairs but immersed in private ones, with limited time and attention and constant interruption (§4.2).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two sets, X and Y, each have 5 people. Inside a set, everyone passes a message to all others in it. A news item starts with one person in X.
- No link: it reaches the 5 members of X and 0 of Y. Reach = 5/10 = 50%.
- One boundary-crosser b in X also talks to one person y1 in Y. The item reaches 5 in X, then b tells y1, then y1 tells the other 4 in Y: 5 + 1 + 4 = 10 of 10.

One personal channel changes reach from 50% to 100%. Without it, only a broadcast aimed at Y directly (as Creel's campaign did) could reach Y.
