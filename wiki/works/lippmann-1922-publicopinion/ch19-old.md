# The Old Image in a New Form: Guild Socialism

## L0 — One line
Lippmann argues that guild socialism, by moving democracy's "natural unit" from the farm township to the workshop, still gives no better way than orthodox democracy to turn self-centred opinion into social judgment, because the wider environment is known only by inference.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Reformers swing between centralised empire and local autonomy; neither avoids force or the limits of opinion. Lippmann tests G. D. H. Cole's guild socialism (industry run by guilds), which Cole hopes will be free of coercion. He argues that Cole's "Commune" gets every coercive power of a modern government that he can think of. He then argues that the shop is a real, directly known unit only for shop-internal matters. The function of a whole industry or nation is a concept, to be imagined, taught and believed. "Functional" representation therefore does not remove the orthodox democrat's problem. Cole's scheme of functions assumes the agreement it is meant to produce. It applies his view that opinion on unseen matters is limited.

**Prerequisites:** representative democracy; Lippmann's earlier ideas of stereotype and the inferred (unseen) environment; the word "sovereign".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Reformers oscillate between a centralised "Roman peace" and local autonomy. Decentralised decisions yield a chaos of local opinions; centralised ones reflect a small capital-city set. Force is needed either way (§1).
- Lippmann calls coercion "the surd" (our gloss: in mathematics, an irrational root such as $\sqrt{2}$ with no exact tidy value; here, what theory finds hard to express) in almost all social theory. Individualists, socialists and pluralists do not accept it as part of their ideals, though their schemes depend on it (§1, para. 6).
- Cole's Commune is said to be non-sovereign, yet its listed powers include every coercive power of a modern government that Lippmann can think of (§2).
- The guildsmen swap the rural township for the workshop as the natural democratic unit. Lippmann says that, unless it is a very small factory, what Cole really has in mind is the shop, because only a perfectly familiar environment is a natural unit; the rest of the plant and industry is inferred (§3).
- A worker can have a real opinion on matters entirely within the shop. On external relations it faces access limits, stereotype and self-interest. Lippmann says that, if history is any guide, literal rule by shop "wills" would give either a perpetual logroll (trading of favours) or warring shops (§4).
- Lippmann argues that functional representation does not escape the orthodox democrat's problem. A function of an industry or nation is a concept, not an experience, so voting on it is as indefinite as orthodox voting (§5).
- Lippmann argues that defining "function" by expressed interest fails; that leaving it to trade unions would create strong vested interests and, he thinks, pressure for a stronger Commune; and that having the Commune define it removes the theory's premise. He says Cole assumes what the society was meant to deduce (§6).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §1 (opening, unnumbered): the swing between Rome and isolation
- **Lippmann:** reformers facing quarrelling self-centred groups chose either the "path to Rome" (imposed peace) or isolation and autonomy. Almost always they took the path they had least recently travelled (§1, para. 1).
- The difficulty is the same on both paths. Decentralised decisions soon end in a chaos of local opinions. Centralised ones rest on the opinions of a small social set at the capital. Force is needed in any case (§1, para. 2).
- Laissez-faire individualism was extreme devolution: whoever owns the property decides. Lippmann calls this "plural sovereignty with a vengeance" (§1, para. 3).
- Its excesses produced counter-currents. Trusts made a Roman peace inside industry and predatory imperialism outside. Legislatures were asked to regulate them, unions federated, and the political system grew stronger at Washington and weaker in the states (§1, para. 4).
- Most socialist schools, from Marxians to the New Nationalists, saw centralisation as the first stage towards absorption of business by the state. This never happened except for a few months in the war. Thought then swung back, not to atomic individualism but to a sort of "molecular" individualism of voluntary groups (§1, para. 5).
- Each theory promises that no one need follow Machiavelli. Yet each is founded by coercion, uses it to survive, and is discarded through it, without accepting it in its ideal. Individualists, socialists and "the new pluralist" expect or hope that aggression motives fade. "Coercion is the surd in almost all social theory, except the Machiavellian" (§1, para. 6). (Our gloss: a surd is an irrational root such as $\sqrt{2}$ with no exact tidy value. Lippmann says the temptation to ignore coercion is strong because it is absurd, inexpressible and unmanageable.)

*Intuition note:* Lippmann says the theories he lists were set up by force, use force to survive and fall by force, yet leave it out of their ideals. The temptation to ignore it is strong because force does not fit a tidy scheme.

### §2: Cole's Commune
- **Cole's view:** the present state is mainly an instrument of coercion. Guild society will have no sovereign power, only a coordinating Commune (§2, para. 1).
- **Lippmann lists Cole's powers for the Commune:** it settles price disputes, sometimes fixes prices, allocates natural resources, labour-power and income, and controls the issue of credit. It taxes and appoints judges. It confers coercive powers on guilds, declares war, controls armed forces and police, and makes laws regulating personal conduct and property (§2, para. 2).
- It is organised federally, with local and provincial communes under a National commune. Lippmann cannot think of any coercive power of a modern government that Cole forgot (§2, para. 3).
- **Cole:** the aim is a society of "free service". Lippmann notes that Cole admits force is needed for the transition, including direct action by trade unions (§2, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* Lippmann compares the Commune's listed powers with those of a modern government and can think of no coercive power left out. He implies that calling the Commune non-sovereign does not match its powers.

### §3: What keeps the Guild society non-coercive? The shop as natural unit
- Cole's first answer is the Marxian one: abolishing capitalist property removes the motive to aggression. Lippmann says Cole does not really believe it, because Cole cares intensely how the post-revolution society is governed (§3, para. 2).
- Guildsmen and communists both propose to establish equality by force. Guildsmen see that an equilibrium needs institutions to keep it. They put their faith in what they believe to be a new theory of democracy. Cole's stated object is to get the mechanism right and adjust it to the expression of men's social wills (§3, paras. 3–4).
- Like Jefferson, the new democrat looks for an environment where self-government can be real. Interest has moved from country to city, so Cole turns to the factory. Cole calls it the natural and fundamental unit of industrial democracy and says larger Guild organs must be based largely on factory representation (§3, para. 4).
- **Lippmann's reading:** Cole's "factory" is a work place where men are in personal contact, small enough to be known directly by all its workers. Unless it is a very small factory, Lippmann says, Cole is really thinking about the shop. "The only natural unit is a perfectly familiar environment." The rest of the plant and industry is an inferred environment (§3, para. 5).

### §4: The shop's external relations
- Lippmann says almost everybody will admit that self-government in a shop's purely internal affairs concerns matters that "can be taken in at a single view" (Aristotle, quoted by Lippmann). But wages, supplies, marketing and planning are not purely internal. Isolation is impossible: the shop democracy is thoroughly entangled in outside ("foreign") affairs (§4, para. 1).
- Management of external relations is "the test" of the theory. These relations run through representatives in a federal chain from shop to plant to industry to nation. **Cole**, as Lippmann presents him, claims the whole chain is inspired by self-government because it derives from the shop, and representatives carry out the workers' "actual will as understood by themselves" (§4, para. 2).
- **Lippmann:** applied literally, this principle would, if history is any guide, give either a perpetual logroll (trading of favours) or a chaos of warring shops. A worker can have a real opinion about matters entirely within the shop, but his "will" about his shop's place in the plant, industry and nation is open to the limits of access, stereotype and self-interest; if it is a generalisation from the individual shop's experience, it is "more likely to be wrong than right". Representatives would often find no such "actual will" to carry out (§4, para. 3).

*Intuition note:* Lippmann holds that a worker can form a real opinion about what he knows directly. On matters out of sight his opinion is limited by access, stereotype and self-interest. In his view, basing the vote on the shop does not remove that limit.

### §5: The reply from "function", and Lippmann's rejoinder
- **Guildsmen reply (as Lippmann imagines them):** you are entangled in an ancient fallacy, looking for somebody to represent a group of people. A representative can act only for "some particular function", and each person helps choose as many representatives as there are distinct essential groups of functions (§5, para. 1).
- **Lippmann:** the functional representatives must still coordinate and regulate; he assumes the shops conflict, since otherwise there would be no need. By what standard does each judge the others? A shop's views on its role in the industry are matters of "education or propaganda", not an automatic product of shop-consciousness (§5, paras. 2–3).
- The function of a vocation, a great industry, a district or a nation is, he says, "a concept, not an experience", which has to be imagined, invented, taught and believed. Once you admit that shops' views of a function need not coincide, Lippmann says, the functional representative must conceive a common interest. He represents your views about other people's views of your function. So, Lippmann says, in voting for him you vote as indefinitely as the orthodox democrat does (§5, para. 3).

### §6: Defining "function"
- The guildsmen assume society analysed into functions and harmoniously recombined, with agreement on purposes and on each group's role. Lippmann notes that the scheme of function assumed in the Catholic feudal society that gave the theory its name was not worked out by mortal man, and he finds it unclear how guildsmen think theirs will be worked out. Sometimes they seem to argue it will develop from trade union organisation, at other times that the communes will define it (§6, para. 1).
- **Cole's view:** a man should count (vote) as many times as there are functions in which he is interested. Lippmann separates two meanings of "interested": involved, or mind occupied. Lippmann's example: John Smith may have been tremendously interested in the Stillman divorce case and read every word of the news, while young Guy Stillman, whose legitimacy was at stake, probably did not trouble himself at all. Lippmann fears Cole leans towards the John Smith meaning (§6, para. 2).
- **Cole:** the uninstructed voter "waives his right to vote". Lippmann answers that there are two kinds of uninstructed voter: one who knows he does not know, who abstains, and one who does not know and does not care, who is brought to the polls by party machinery. With communes controlling taxes, wages, prices and credit, elections would be fought as passionately as now. So, Lippmann concludes, the way people show their interest will not delimit functions (§6, paras. 3–5).
- Two other routes to defining functions:
  - By trade unions. The struggle would harden unions into vested interests. Some, such as miners and railroad men, would be very strong, and some of their leaders at least would not readily give up that power. Lippmann thinks radicals would fairly soon be asking for communes strong enough to define the guilds' functions (§6, para. 5).
  - By the Commune. Lippmann argues that then the theory's premise disappears. The premise was that an obvious scheme of functions lets the shops relate voluntarily to society. If there is no settled scheme in every voter's head, Lippmann says, he has no better way under guild socialism than under orthodox democracy to turn a self-centred opinion into a social judgment (§6, para. 6).
- Lippmann argues that even a good scheme devised by Cole and his friends would be judged by the shop democracies, from which all power derives, by what they learn of it and imagine, so defining the scheme would be the main business of politics under guild socialism as elsewhere. Cole, he says, "has inserted in his premise what he wishes a guild society to deduce" (§6, para. 6).
- **Footnotes:** Lippmann uses Cole rather than Soviet Russia. He says all competent observers seem to agree that Russia in 1921 does not illustrate a communist state in working order; it is in revolution, so it shows what a revolution is like and very little about a communist society. He says the Russian communists have relied not on the spontaneous democracy of the Russian people but on the discipline, special interest and noblesse oblige of a specialised class, the loyal and indoctrinated party members. In the "transition", he believes, the cure for class government and the coercive state is "strictly homeopathic". He admires the Webbs' *Constitution* but has not convinced himself that it is not an intellectual "tour de force"; Cole seems to him more authentically in the spirit of the socialist movement and so a better witness (§6, footnotes).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three shops: A has 20 workers, B has 10, C has 10. The Commune must split a fund of 80 units. Each shop knows its own needs: A claims 50, B 30, C 30 (total 110 > 80).
- Each shop's "actual will" is clear only for its own claim. No shop's experience says how to rank the claims.
- Standard 1, by headcount (40 workers): A gets 80 × 20/40 = 40; B and C get 80 × 10/40 = 20 each.
- Standard 2, proportional to claims: A gets 80 × 50/110 = 36.36; B and C get 80 × 30/110 = 21.82 each. Check: 36.36 + 21.82 + 21.82 = 80.
- The two standards give different results. The choice between them is not part of any shop's direct knowledge. It is a "concept" to be argued, taught and believed (§5).

## Concepts
- [[guild-socialism]] (new) — Cole's scheme of industrial self-government through guilds, based on factory or shop units and coordinated by a Commune (§2–§3).
- [[coercion-as-surd]] (new) — Lippmann's claim that coercion is the surd in almost all social theory: the theories he lists depend on force but do not accept it in their ideals (§1).
- [[natural-unit-of-government]] (new) — a unit small enough that members know its affairs directly; Lippmann says the shop, not the plant, qualifies (§3).
- [[functional-representation]] (new) — representatives chosen for each function, not for a group of people; a person may vote several times (§5–§6).
- [[pseudo-environment]] — here called the "inferred environment": whatever lies beyond direct experience and must be imagined (§3, §5). This unit does not use the term "pseudo-environment".
- [[stereotype-lippmann]] — access, stereotype and self-interest limit opinion about matters out of sight (§4). Reuse the canon id "Stereotype (Lippmann)".

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Lippmann's shop as a "perfectly familiar environment" ↔ pseudo-environment: direct knowledge versus inferred picture, and its limit on democratic competence.
- [structural-analogy] Representatives needing a "common interest" beyond experience ↔ framing (Entman): common problem definitions must be built, not found.
- [metaphor] Function as something conceived, not experienced ↔ agenda setting: much public concern is about things known only through media.

## Questions this raises
- Does Lippmann's argument apply to any small-unit democracy, or only to Cole's version?
- If a scheme of functions must be taught and believed, who teaches it, and how does that relate to propaganda?
- Is the party discipline Lippmann notes in Russia a general solution to the problem of coordinating self-centred groups?
- Could deliberate education about the wider environment make a federal structure work better than Lippmann allows?
