# The Role of Force, Patronage and Privilege

## L0 — One line
The less material for a common consciousness separate communities have, the more a national government's hold on them depends on force, or on the milder alternative of patronage and privilege, rather than on a voluntary system based on shared information (§6).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how a large nation can cooperate when each locality judges events by its own local picture. He reads American history through this question. The Federalist leaders (Hamilton, Madison) built checks and balances, and Hamilton turned to the gentry. Jefferson turned the Constitution into a democratic "fiction", and Jackson's patronage created a new governing class. Congress, with no adequate way to learn national facts, trades local favours (pork, logrolling). Lippmann's conclusion: the other options are terror, or a highly developed system of information. The second is, he says, in its very earliest development. So the less common consciousness there is, the more cooperation rests on force, patronage and privilege.

**Prerequisites:** basic US history (Constitution, Federalists, Jefferson, Jackson); the idea of a stereotype as a set of images and phrases through which people describe politics (as used on these pages).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Hamilton diagnosed the Confederation's failure: states judged national measures by local interest and poor information (§1.1).
- The framers answered with checks and balances, a machine meant to neutralise local opinion and to deadlock local and class interests. They had no concept of common consent based on common information (§1.3–1.4).
- Jefferson's "revolution of 1800" was a revolution in the mind. Reading the Constitution through democratic stereotypes made a "great conservative fiction" (§3).
- Patronage under Jackson, unintended by democrats, created a governing class and the two-party system. It did for a mass electorate what Hamilton's finance did for the upper classes (§4.3–4.4).
- The stereotype of democracy governs visible government. The real adaptations stay invisible (§4.5).
- Lippmann thinks Congress is essentially a group of blind men: it has no systematic, adequate, authorised way to learn national facts. Local opinions summed up are not enough (§5.3).
- Without information, Congress oscillates between blind regularity (tempered by occasional insurgency) and logrolling (§5.5).
- Force, patronage/privilege, or developed information: these are the three bases of large-scale cooperation (§6).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. The federalists' problem
- **Hamilton (Federalist 15):** Under the confederation, the "concurrence of thirteen distinct sovereign wills" was needed for every important measure. State rulers judge by immediate interests, without knowledge of national circumstances and with local bias. Execution therefore fluctuates with the discretion of the poorly informed and prejudiced opinion of every part. Assemblies deliberating at a distance, at different times and under different impressions cannot long cooperate (§1.1).
- **Lippmann:** Ten years under a Congress that John Adams called merely a diplomatic assembly gave the leaders an instructive but afflicting lesson about self-centred communities entangled in one environment. At Philadelphia (1787) they reacted against the premise of eighteenth-century democracy (§1.2).
- As Lippmann uses it, "concave democracy" refers to communities managing their own affairs spontaneously in self-contained environments. The framers took *government* to be power to make national decisions and enforce them, and *democracy* to be localities and classes insisting on self-determination by immediate interests (§1.2).
- They could not conceive separate communities acting on the same version of the facts. Lippmann says we only begin to conceive this, for parts of the world with free news and a common language, and only for some aspects of life (§1.3).
- So they planned on the theory that men can be governed if special interests are balanced. **Madison:** "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition" (§1.3).
- Lippmann says they did not aim at perpetual deadlock, only at deadlocking local and class interests so these could not obstruct government. Checks and balances was "in one very important sense" their remedy for the problem of public opinion. They did not know how to manipulate a large electorate (§1.4). They saw no other way to replace the sword's violence with the magistrate's mild influence than a machine to neutralise local opinion. Burr's 1800 seizure of New York City with Tammany Hall taught Hamilton a lesson, but Hamilton was killed before he could take account of it (§1.4).

*Intuition note:* If people share no common picture of the facts, you cannot count on voluntary agreement. The framers designed institutions so that local interests cancel each other out.

### 2. Gentry and a candid constitution
- Lippmann thinks it an "inversion of the truth" to say Hamilton made the Union to protect class privilege. He used class privilege to make the Union. **Hamilton:** if we expect man to serve the public, "we must interest his passions in doing so" (§2.1).
- Hamilton needed men whose passions could be attached quickly to a national interest: the gentry, public creditors, manufacturers, shippers and traders. By a series of fiscal measures he attached the provincial notables to the new government (§2.1).
- There was little pretence. The convention met behind closed doors, and ratification came from probably no more than one-sixth of adult males. The Federalists argued for union, not democracy. The only democratic organ the constitution was intended to give the government was the House, on a suffrage limited by property, and it was believed so licentious that it was checked by the Senate, electoral college, veto and judicial interpretation (§2.2).
- This conservative reaction could not last. The men were a minority, their motives suspected, and the gentry were too weak after Washington retired (§2.3).

### 3. Jefferson's mental victory
- Jefferson's 1800 election changed no great policy but set a new tradition. He taught Americans to see the Constitution as a democratic instrument and stereotyped their political phrases (§3.1).
- The framers failed to see that a frankly undemocratic constitution would not be tolerated. Jefferson himself, so far as his constitutional opinions ran, was no readier than Hamilton to hand government to the "unrefined" will of the people. His ambiguity came partly from believing in both union and spontaneous democracies, which his age's political science could not reconcile (§3.2).
- Lippmann says the frank denials were "expunged from consciousness". The Constitution was rewritten "in spirit", chiefly through a new set of stereotypes, partly by amendment and practice (§3.2).
- It was a "great conservative fiction". Lippmann's "fair guess" is that, had everyone read it as its authors did, loyalty to the Constitution and to democracy would have seemed incompatible and it would have been violently overthrown. Jackson then carried out the revolution Jefferson prepared (§3.3).

*Intuition note:* A shared way of seeing can change how a set of rules works while its text stays largely the same. Lippmann's fair guess is that here the new picture kept the document from being overthrown.

### 4. Patronage
- The founders treated office as a kind of property. Democratic theory, with its "omnicompetent citizen", meant that once people saw the Constitution as a democratic instrument, permanence in office was certain to seem undemocratic. Jackson founded the practice of turning office into patronage (§4.1).
- Rotation in office was seen as a reform. It honoured the average man, broke a class monopoly and was an old remedy for corruption and bureaucracy. It applied the image of the self-contained village to a great territory (§4.2).
- The result was unintended. It founded a new governing class, doing for a large electorate what Hamilton's finance did for the upper classes. Lippmann says patronage weaned natural leaders from the self-centred community. It brought together provincial celebrities who, in the absence of a sense of common interest, would otherwise have torn the union apart (§4.3).
- The democrat could not have what he liked, because his theory came from an ideal environment and he lived in a real one. Patronage produced the two-party system. This was a substitute for the gentry and a discipline for governing the checks-and-balances deadlock, all "invisibly" (§4.4).
- Lippmann adds that the more deeply the democrat felt democracy's moral impulse, the less ready he was to see the truth of Hamilton's statement that communities deliberating at a distance cannot long cooperate. That truth postpones anything like full realisation of democracy in public affairs until the art of obtaining common consent has been radically improved (§4.4).
- Rotation was the ostensible theory, but the professional politician was permanent. The stereotype of democracy controlled the visible government. The real adaptations had to be invisible, even when everybody knew about them. Only laws, speeches, platforms and formal administration had to conform to the image (§4.5).

*Intuition note:* Official talk can follow an ideal picture while real practice follows other rules. Patronage helped hold the country together without being named in the official story.

### 5. Congress: the blind men
- A philosophical democrat would have pointed to representative government as the way separate communities cooperate. Instead Congress's prestige has declined while the Presidency has grown (§5.1).
- Lippmann does not accept that the custom of sending only local celebrities to Washington explains Congress's decline. The decline began when Congress was most eminent. He thinks it more probable that Congress lost the eminent as it lost influence (§5.2).
- His main reason (he writes "I think") is that Congress is "essentially a group of blind men in a vast, unknown world". With some exceptions, the only method recognised in the Constitution or in the theory of representative government for Congress to inform itself is exchanging opinions from the districts (§5.3).
- Congress has value as a "market-place". But even if districts sent their wisest men, summed local impressions are not a wide enough base for national policy, and no base at all for the control of foreign policy. The real effects of most laws are subtle and hidden and, he says, can be known only by "controlled reporting and objective analysis" (§5.3).
- Analogy: a factory head must read an accountant's cost sheets, not just talk to the foreman. Local pictures need instruments to calibrate them (§5.3).
- The President informs Congress only as he chooses. He cannot be heckled and he controls censorship. Congress relies on leaks, private tips and investigations (§5.4).
- Apart from a few sources (investigations, executive communications, private data, the press, expert commissions), the creation of congressional opinion is "incestuous". From this, national legislation is either prepared by a few insiders and pushed through by partisan force, or broken into local items (tariffs, navy yards, rivers and harbours, pensions) handed out to concave communities as tangible evidence of the benefits of national life (§5.5).
- Such communities see the marble building more readily than the cumulative cost of the pork barrel. Without information, a legislature oscillates between blind regularity and logrolling. Logrolling makes regularity palatable (§5.5).
- This is not the individual Congressman's fault, except when he is complacent about it. He cannot understand a fraction of his bills, so he specialises and takes somebody's word on the rest. Back home, local groups judge him through local spectacles (§5.6).

### 6. Conclusion
Patronage and pork "amalgamate and stabilize" special opinions and ambitions. Lippmann names two alternatives: government by terror and obedience (in decay), or government based on information and analysis so developed that "the knowledge of national circumstances" is evident to all (in earliest development). For any large association (League of Nations, industrial government, federal union), the existing material for a common consciousness decides how far cooperation depends on force or on patronage and privilege. Hamilton's skill was knowing how to calculate these principles (§6).

*Intuition note:* The less shared knowledge there is, the more cooperation rests on force or on patronage and privilege. In Lippmann's account, developed common information is the only other alternative.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three villages A, B, C must all approve a national road, as under the confederation. It costs 90, split 30 each. Value to each village: A 60, B 40, C 20 (total 120).
- Local net: A 60−30 = +30; B 40−30 = +10; C 20−30 = −10. C vetoes, so nothing happens despite national benefit 120 > cost 90.
- Add a "pork" project that C's people value at 15 but that has no real national benefit; A and B pay for it (7.5 each). C's view: −10 + 15 = +5, so C approves. A: +30 − 7.5 = +22.5; B: +10 − 7.5 = +2.5; both still approve.
- National total: 120 − 90 − 15 = +15 (the road is still worth building, but the favour is a pure cost, and C does not see it).
- This shows the chapter's mechanism: unanimity from local self-interest fails, and local favours can substitute for a shared picture of the facts.

## Concepts
- [[stereotype-lippmann]] — the set of images, ideas and phrases in which Americans describe politics to each other, e.g. Jefferson's democratic reading of the Constitution (§3.1)
- [[pseudo-environment]] — (the term itself is not used in this chapter) the ideal environment, the self-contained village or township, from which democratic theory was drawn, versus the real one in which the democrat lived (§4.2, §4.4)
- concave-community (new) — a self-centred community that judges everything by local interest and local sight (§1.2, §5.5)
- checks-and-balances-as-opinion-remedy (new) — machinery to neutralise local opinion and deadlock local and class interests where common information is absent (§1.4)
- patronage-and-privilege (new) — the milder alternative to force that attaches leaders and communities to the nation (§6)
- conservative-fiction (new) — the democratic reading of the Constitution, which Lippmann calls a "great conservative fiction" and guesses kept it from violent overthrow (§3.3)
- logrolling (new) — local favour-trading that makes party regularity palatable to constituents (§5.5)

## Questions this raises

- Is the "conservative fiction" a deception or a useful shared image, and does Lippmann judge it either way?
