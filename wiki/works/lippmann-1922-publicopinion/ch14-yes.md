# Yes or No

## L0 — One line
Symbols have no power of their own; trusted authorities plant them in minds, and because a mass can for practical purposes only say Yes or No, a few people in organized hierarchies must present the choices.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann asks how symbols take hold of people and how a mass of people can act at all. His answer: on most subjects people cannot check the facts, so they trust authorities, usually those who seem to run things. Every cooperating group has a hierarchy, the "machine." The leaders deal with concrete matters. Their followers are held together by privileges, not by shared conviction. A mass cannot construct, negotiate or administer. For practical purposes, its direct action ends in saying Yes or No to a choice that a few have presented. Different private reasons are joined by common symbolic phrases. Lippmann concludes that initiative and referendum (direct-vote reforms) do not remove the need for this machine.

**Prerequisites:** Lippmann's earlier point that the pictures in people's minds differ in subtle ways (§5, para. 4), and the term "stereotype" (a ready-made pattern of seeing, passed down within a hierarchy, §3).

## L2 — Key ideas
- A symbol has no power except what it gains by association in human minds. It works only if spoken by well-placed people at the right moment (§1, paras 1–2).
- In 1920, private citizens quoted in a newspaper gave reasons for opposite votes that Lippmann calls equally noble and almost reversible. The same noble phrases could serve either side (§1, paras 3–4).
- Where all facts are out of sight, a true report and a plausible error read, sound and feel alike. So, except on the few subjects we know well, we choose between trustworthy and untrustworthy reporters, not between true and false accounts (§2, paras 2–3).
- Complete independence is unthinkable. On all but a very few matters, the utmost independence we can exercise is to multiply the authorities we hear (§2, paras 5–6).
- Every institution that needs many people to cooperate has some hierarchy (in American politics, "the machine"). Leaders are in direct contact with some crucial part of their environment. Followers are held together by privileges, not by common conviction (§§2–4).
- Lippmann says it is not necessary to invent a collective intelligence to explain why group judgments are usually more coherent than the man in the street's. He calls the Oversoul (a supposed guiding mind over the whole group) a superfluous mystery once we look at the machine. A group thinking in concert can, as a group, do little more than assent or dissent (§§3–4).
- A mass can migrate, strike, boycott, applaud or hiss, and so occasionally resist or coerce, but cannot construct or administer. For practical purposes the limit of direct action is saying Yes or No on an issue presented to it (§5, paras 1–2).
- Common symbolic phrases carry individual emotion but drop most of the intention. Hierarchies tie them to a Yes or No, so varied reasons can add up to what Lippmann calls a common will (§5, para. 4).
- Lippmann says there is no such thing as "direct legislation." Even the flexible preferential ballot depends on the quality of the choices presented, and those are set by a few (§6, para. 1).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1. Symbols and their circulation
- Lippmann says it is tempting to treat symbols as having independent energy. Museums and folklore are full of dead emblems, so a symbol's power is only what it gets by association in the mind. He says that a patient, detailed study of how a symbol circulates would show a purely secular history (§1, para. 1).
- The Hughes speech, the Fourteen Points and Hamilton's project all used symbols, but by particular people at particular moments. The words must be spoken by strategically placed people at the opportune moment, or they are mere wind. Symbols must be "earmarked." Otherwise the choice among them would paralyse us, like the donkey between two bales of hay (§1, para. 2).
- Example: reasons for their vote, as private citizens gave them to a newspaper before the 1920 election (§1, para. 3).
  - **For Harding:** Wilmot (inventor) said that those who vote for Harding and Coolidge will be held by posterity to have signed a second Declaration of Independence. Clarence (salesman) said that Harding would avoid entangling alliances, and that Washington as a city would benefit from the change of party.
  - **For Cox:** Marie (stenographer) said it is our duty, pledged on the fields of France, to join the League and share the burden of enforcing peace. Spencer (statistician) said refusing would cost us our own respect and that of other nations.
- Lippmann's verdict: the two sets of phrases are "equally noble, equally true, and almost reversible." He asks whether Wilmot and Clarence would admit they meant to default on our duty or did not want peace (they would not), and says Marie and Spencer would have argued that the League was, as Wilson called it, a disentangling alliance and a Declaration of Independence for all the world, plus a Monroe Doctrine for the planet (§1, para. 4).

*Intuition note:* The same noble words can serve opposite sides. So the words alone do not seem to explain the vote. Lippmann points instead to who says them (§1, para. 2) and to the action the hierarchies attach them to (§5, para. 4).

### 2. How symbols take root: authority
- A symbol is planted in a mind by a person we recognise as authoritative; at first, symbols become congenial because congenial and important people introduce them. In infancy we depend on older beings, and certain beloved and authoritative persons are the "first bridge to the invisible world" (§2, paras 1–2).
- Where all the facts are out of sight, a true report and a plausible error read, sound and feel alike. Except on a few subjects where our own knowledge is great, we cannot choose between true and false accounts, so we choose between trustworthy and untrustworthy reporters (§2, paras 2–3).
- Choosing the most expert is easier than choosing truth, but still too hard. The experts are not certain who among them is the most expert, and an expert is likely as not too busy or impossible to reach. So we use people who are easy to identify: those at the head of affairs, such as parents, teachers and masterful friends (§2, para. 4).
- Lippmann says our dependence on trusted people is sometimes regarded as undignified, yet complete independence is "unthinkable." The nearest thing to an independent adult, a hermit, must have accepted a repertory of painfully acquired information before retreating (§2, para. 5).
- On all but a very few matters, the utmost independence we can exercise is to multiply the authorities we hear and stir up the experts. We can often judge who wins a debate, but we are "virtually defenseless" against a false premise (or neglected aspect) that none of the debaters challenges. Lippmann says democratic theory proceeds on the opposite assumption, for purposes of government, of an unlimited supply of self-sufficient individuals (§2, para. 6).
- The people on whom we depend for contact with the outer world are those who seem to run it, though they may run only a very small part. Running part of affairs does not make the nurse an authority on physics, nor Mr. Smith on the Constitution, nor prove Mr. Smoot the best man to consult on taxation. Yet the nurse may for a while decide what zoology the child learns, Mr. Smith will have much to say on what the Constitution means to his household, and Lippmann asks who shall define the limits of Senator Smoot's authority (§2, para. 7).
- Priests, lords, party leaders, bosses and their organized followings administer human affairs. In every institution that requires the cooperation of many persons some hierarchy exists, even if its ranks are vague or concealed. In American politics it is called a machine, or "the organization" (§2, para. 8).

*Intuition note:* Most of us cannot check most facts, so trust takes the place of checking. Whoever seems to be running things may get that trust, even outside his own field.

### 3. Leaders and followers
- Leaders are in direct contact with some crucial part of the environment. Their notion of that environment may be very limited, and their stereotypes often make them "absurd routineers," but they decide, give orders and bargain, and something definite happens (§3, para. 1).
- Followers are held by a system of privileges, not by common conviction. Abolishing privileges can break one machine. Lippmann believes a machine is "certain to reappear" in any coherent group, because privilege is entirely relative and uniformity is impossible. In his thought experiment, a communist group in which no one has anything the others lack would, he is convinced, still form an organization of insiders around the man who is going to make the vote-winning speech, from the mere pleasure of being his friend (§3, para. 2).
- It is not necessary to invent a collective intelligence to explain why group judgments are usually more coherent than the remarks of the man in the street. A group thinking in concert can do little more than assent or dissent. Hierarchies hand down traditions and stereotypes (§3, para. 3).

### 4. Democratic theory and the machine
- Lippmann says distance alone lends enchantment to the view that masses cooperate in any complex affair without a central machine managed by very few people. Citing Bryce (who speaks of affairs of state), he says very few people govern in any particular institution. Landslides replace one machine with another, and the democratic revolution set up two alternating machines. Nowhere does the machine disappear: there is an inner circle with concentric circles fading into the uninterested (§4, paras 1–2).
- Lippmann says democrats have never come to terms with this commonplace of group life and have invariably regarded it as perverse. There are two visions of democracy: one presupposes the self-sufficient individual; the other an Oversoul regulating everything. The Oversoul has some advantage because it at least recognizes that the mass makes decisions not spontaneously born in every member. But if we look at the machine, the Oversoul is a "superfluous mystery": the machine consists of people who can be named and described, and they perform the duties usually assigned to the Oversoul (§4, paras 3–4).

### 5. Why the machine exists: Yes or No
- The machine exists because no common idea emerges by itself from private notions, not because human nature is perverse. A mass can migrate, strike, boycott, applaud or hiss, but "nothing can be constructed, devised, negotiated, or administered" by mass action (§5, para. 1).
- A union may win by a strike, for example joint control, but cannot exercise its win except through an organization. A nation can clamour for war but must obey a general staff (§5, para. 1).
- The limit of direct action is, for all practical purposes, "the power to say Yes or No" on an issue put to the mass (§5, para. 2).
  - Only in the very simplest cases does an issue present itself in the same form, spontaneously and at about the same time, to all members of a public.
  - Even there, impromptu ringleaders emerge. Without them a crowd mills about or stands by, as fifty people did while a man committed suicide.
- Most of what we make of impressions from the invisible world is a kind of "pantomime played out in revery." There is rarely a practical issue, so no great habit of decision. News usually arrives with suggestions about how to feel, and if we feel implicated, the revery is uncomfortable until we can feel Yes or No (§5, para. 3).
- Many people can say Yes for different reasons. Public symbolic phrases "carry the individual emotion after evacuating most of the intention." Hierarchies link the phrases to a vote (§5, para. 4).
- Example: Smith opposed the League, Jones opposed Article X, Brown opposed Wilson. Each votes Republican, and a common will is expressed (§5, para. 4).
- Professional politicians learned this, Lippmann says, long before the democratic philosophers, and built the caucus, nominating convention and steering committee to formulate a definite choice. The Peace Conference shrank to the Council of Ten, then to the Big Three or Four, who wrote a treaty the minor allies, their constituents and the enemy could only take or leave (an example he calls rather brutal; more consultation is generally possible and desirable). The essential fact is that "a small number of heads present a choice to a large group" (§5, para. 5).

*Intuition note:* For practical purposes a crowd can answer a question but not write one. Someone must first present a concrete choice, and Lippmann says it is connected to individual opinion by the transfer of interest through symbols.

### 6. Initiative, referendum, direct primary
- Abuses of the steering committee led to proposals such as the initiative, referendum and direct primary. Lippmann says these merely postponed or obscured the need for a machine by complicating the elections, or, as H. G. Wells once said "with scrupulous accuracy," the selections. No amount of balloting can remove the need to create an issue, a measure or a candidate, on which voters can say Yes or No (§6, para. 1).
- Lippmann says "direct legislation" does not exist. The citizen votes Yes or No on abbreviated bills, even if a better amendment occurs to him. He adds that for certain kinds of issues he thinks there are distinct benefits (§6, para. 1).
- Mass decisions are necessarily simple, but the world they act on is complex. The preferential ballot is more flexible, but still depends on the quality of the choices offered, and those choices are presented by energetic coteries who circulate petitions and round up delegates (§6, para. 1).
- Footnote: Laski (quoted) says proportional representation, by leading as it seems to lead to the group system, may deprive electors of their choice of leaders. Lippmann agrees that the group system tends to make selection of the executive more indirect, but adds that it also tends to produce assemblies in which currents of opinion are more fully represented. Whether that is good or bad cannot be determined a priori. A more representative assembly needs a higher organization of political intelligence and habit than a rigid two-party house, so it is a more complex form and "may therefore work less well" (§6, footnote).
- Conclusion: "The Many can elect after the Few have nominated" (§6, para. 1).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Nine villagers vote on a proposal: "Approve the Park Plan? Yes/No." A two-person committee wrote the proposal.
- No votes: 3 object to cost, 2 to noise, 1 dislikes the mayor. 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 No.
- Yes votes: 3.
- Result: 6 of 9 = 67% say No, so "the village rejects the plan." But there are three different reasons, and the symbol "No to the Park Plan" hides them.
- Nobody could vote for "a cheaper, quieter plan" because it was not on the ballot. The committee's choice of question set the limit of the mass decision, which is Lippmann's point.

## Questions this raises
- Lippmann believes the machine is certain to reappear in any coherent group. What evidence could test that claim?
- If we can judge debates but not unchallenged premises, who finds the neglected premise?
- Do referendums with many options, or deliberation before voting, widen the Yes/No limit?
- Is the trust in "those who seem to run things" fair in a world with many specialist authorities?
