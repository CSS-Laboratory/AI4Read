# Speed, Words, and Clearness

## L0 — One line
Our access to the unseen environment is limited by compressed news, imprecise words, and poor conditions for attention; and because the world is vast and the messages are few, the biggest part of opinion must be constructed in the imagination (§2, §6).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Lippmann examines what limits our access to the world beyond our sight. News arrives as a few coded, compressed words, and words evoke different images in different minds. Many readers have little time to spare. The quality of attention, including the power to tell things apart ("lucidity"), is hurt by city noise, monotonous work and emotional conflict, and is pushed lower still when an appeal must reach a mass of poorly equipped people. These limits, together with the complexity of the facts, replace workable ideas with misleading fictions. They also leave us without adequate checks on people who deliberately mislead. Part III, on stereotypes, follows.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday experience of news and language. The chapter cites early psychology experiments (word association) in plain terms.

## L2 — Key ideas
- News about distant events can pass through telegraph coding, translation and cutting. Lippmann's examples show long speeches and statements reduced to short items (§1, §2).
- Language is an imperfect vehicle: the same word need not evoke the same idea in writer and reader (§2).
- Millions cannot read, or cannot understand what they read, and Lippmann assumes a good three-quarters of those who can have only some part of half an hour a day to spare. Necessarily, the ideas the words evoke form the biggest part of the original data of opinion (§2).
- Lucidity of mind is the power to separate superficial analogies and notice differences. It differs greatly between people; Lippmann's examples (infant, botanist, car owner) tie it to experience and interest (§3).
- Lippmann draws on Ferenczi's schematic stages of the child's sense of reality, which are magical. He adds that in the more advanced sections of the most advanced peoples, trial and error after repeated failure has led to a new principle (§3).
- Lippmann says laboratory studies indicate clearly that slight fatigue and distraction tend to "flatten" association (the reply a stimulus word calls up). He then asks what city life must do (§3, §4).
- Emotional "complexes" (ambition, economic interest, animosity, prejudice, class feeling) derange association. Since numbers constitute power, appeals to huge, unequal audiences depress the quality of attention further (§5).
- A broad appeal is aimed at widely shared susceptibilities, a narrow one at uncommon ones. The "psychic level" on which a stimulus acts determines whether the potential public is large or small (§5).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**§1 Coded news.** The unseen environment is reported chiefly in words, sent by wire or radio to editors. Because telegraphy is expensive and facilities are often limited, press dispatches are usually coded (Phillip's Code). A dispatch on German shipping may go over the wires as "Washn i. The Uni Stas rgds tq of Ger spg…". A Berlin item on Chancellor Wirth is culled from a long foreign-language speech, translated, coded and decoded. Lippmann is told a good operator can transcribe 15,000+ words in an eight-hour day (§1).

**§2 Words as imperfect carriers.**
- A few words must often stand for a whole succession of acts, thoughts, feelings and consequences. In the Korean Commission item, Lippmann reads the chain as follows: eyewitnesses (their accuracy unknown) report to the makers of "authentic reports," who report to a commission five thousand miles away. It prepares a statement, probably much too long for publication, from which a correspondent culls an item 3½ inches long (§2).
- Lippmann thinks it doubtful that even a supreme master of style could pack all the elements of truth that complete justice would demand into a hundred-word account of several months in Korea (§2).
- Words, like currency, are reused to evoke different images on different days. There is no certainty that a word calls up the same idea in reader and reporter (§2).
- In theory, unique names for every fact and relation, agreed by all, would allow communication without misunderstanding. The exact sciences approach this ideal, which is part of why scientific inquiry is the most effective world-wide cooperation (§2).
- Lippmann: men command fewer words than they have ideas to express, and language, as Jean Paul said (cited via White), is "a dictionary of faded metaphors" (§2).
- **Briand (reported, to the Chamber of Deputies):** Lloyd George's words, "badly understood and badly transmitted," "seemed to give the Pan-Germanists the idea that the time had come to start something" (§2).
- **Footnote, a neutral observer quoted in the Manchester Guardian:** words are tokens of value whose value changes. Nations that understate keep their "word-currency sound"; those that overstate suffer "inflation." English readers of the French press should convert, as a banker converts francs to pounds. The observer hopes the argument works both ways, since there is value behind English reticence too (§2, footnote).
- Of those watching a speaker or writer, millions can read hardly at all, and millions more can read the words but cannot understand them. Of those who can both read and understand, Lippmann assumes a good three-quarters have some part of half an hour a day to spare for the subject (§2).
- Hence the ideas that words evoke are the biggest part of the original data of opinion. The messages are few and the world is vast, so the biggest part of opinion must be constructed in the imagination (§2).
- Examples: for a resident of New York, "Mexico" likely evokes a composite of stock images, and for "Japan" Lippmann asks whether it is a vague horde of stock figures and things. In 1920 a group of New England college students defined "alien" as hostile, an enemy, a foreigner at war, and so on. Yet "alien" is an unusually exact legal term, far more exact than "sovereignty," "rights," "imperialism," or "socialism," about which we readily take sides (§2).

*Intuition note:* A word is a cue, not a container. Readers fill it with their own pictures, so the same headline can start different thoughts in different heads.

**§3 Lucidity of mind.**
- Lucidity is the power to dissociate superficial analogies, attend to differences and appreciate variety. It is relative (§3).
- Examples: an infant versus a botanist; a Union League Club member who sees no real difference between a Democrat, a Socialist, an anarchist and a burglar; a car owner who spots a carburetor difference from far away (§3).
- Hobby talk feels like a return to "the three dimensional world" (§3).
- **Ferenczi (as reported):** the child identifies partly similar things more easily than the adult does. Stages: crying ("magical hallucinatory omnipotence"), pointing ("magic gestures"), then talking ("magic thoughts and magic words"). Partial success tends to confirm a way of acting; failure tends to stimulate another. Each stage may persist for certain situations, overlaid and only visible at times (§3).
- Many individuals, parties and even nations rarely seem to go beyond the magical organization of experience. In the more advanced sections of the most advanced peoples, trial and error after repeated failure has led to a new principle: crops come from sunlight, moisture, seeds, not festivals or majorities (§3).
- A footnote says Ferenczi does not describe the maturer phase of realism based on science (§3).
- Allowing for the categories' schematic value, the critical quality is the power to discriminate among crude perceptions and vague analogies (§3).
- **Zurich association studies (conducted under Jung; Lippmann's reading):** these "indicate clearly" that slight mental fatigue, inner disturbance of attention, or external distraction tend to "flatten" the response. An example of the very "flat" type is the clang association (cat–hat), a reply to the sound and not to the sense of the stimulus word. One test showed a 9% rise in clang in the second hundred reactions (§3).

*Intuition note:* Telling things apart is a skill that experience and attention build and tiredness erodes. When it fails, one thing is treated as another.

**§4 City life.**
- Lippmann asks: if a metronome flattens discrimination, what do hours of factory noise, heat, typewriters and telephones do to political judgment formed from newspapers read in street-cars? (§4)
- He then says that, under modern industrialism, thought goes on "in a bath of noise." If its discriminations are often flat, here is "some small part of the reason." The "burden of thought" is a burden when conditions make it burdensome, and no burden when they are favorable (§4).
- The movement for a shorter day and better workplaces is, if the intellectual quality of life is to improve, only "the merest beginning." So long as many jobs are an endless, aimless routine, the worker's whole life will tend toward an automatism in which nothing is particularly distinguished from anything else unless announced "with a thunderclap" (§4).
- Rare calm places remind us why minds are caught up by headlines and catch-words (§4).

**§5 Inner disorder and mass audiences.**
- Lippmann says experiment shows that the speed, accuracy and intellectual quality of association are deranged by what "we are taught to call" emotional conflicts. Reaction times to neutral and "hot" words can vary from 5 to 32 fifths of a second, or fail entirely (Jung, Clark Lectures) (§5).
- Ambition, economic interest, animosity, racial prejudice and class feeling distort reading and behavior (§5).
- Since numbers are power, appeals reach illiterate, feeble-minded, neurotic and undernourished people. Lippmann says their mass is "very considerable," and that there is reason to think it much more considerable than we generally suppose (§5).
- A broad appeal addresses widely distributed susceptibilities; a narrow one, uncommon ones. Susceptibilities are like an alpine country: isolated peaks, separate plateaus, and deep continuous strata. A refined scholar may respond like anyone else when starving and afraid (§5).
- So large magazines prefer a pretty girl's face. The "psychic level" on which the stimulus acts determines whether the public is to be potentially a large or a small one (§5).

**§6 Summary.** The environment is refracted by censorship and privacy at the source, by barriers at the other end, scant attention, poor language, distraction, unconscious feeling, wear and monotony. With the obscurity and complexity of the facts, these thwart clear perception, substitute misleading fictions, and deprive us of checks on deliberate misleaders (§6).

## Toy example (ours, not Lippmann's)
A reporter hears "Mexico" and pictures {oil, revolution, peasants}. A reader pictures {sand, cactus, oil}.
- Union of images: {oil, revolution, peasants, sand, cactus} = 5.
- Shared images: {oil} = 1.
- Overlap = 1/5 = 0.2.

One word, one shared image in five. Both people feel they understood. This is only an illustration of §2, not Lippmann's measure.

## Concepts
- [[pseudo-environment]] — here the "unseen environment" is reported to us chiefly by words, so the biggest part of opinion must be constructed in the imagination (§1, §2); the term itself is not used in this chapter.
- [[words-as-currency]] (new) — words are tokens whose value shifts between users and over time (§2, footnote).
- [[lucidity-of-mind]] (new) — power to dissociate superficial analogies and see differences; relative (§3).
- [[psychic-level-of-appeal]] (new) — the level of susceptibility on which a stimulus acts; it determines whether the potential public is large or small (§5).
- [[conditions-of-attention]] (new) — noise, fatigue, monotony, emotion that flatten discrimination (§3–§5).

## Questions this raises
- If words cannot carry meaning reliably, what do readers fill the gaps with? (Part III suggests stereotypes.)
- How strong is the evidence that city noise degrades political judgment, beyond the laboratory analogy?
- Are some topics less vulnerable to "faded metaphor," as the exact sciences are said to be?
