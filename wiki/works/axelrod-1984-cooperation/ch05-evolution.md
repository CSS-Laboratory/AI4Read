# The Evolution of Cooperation in Biological Systems

## L0 — One line
Under the conditions the chapter gives, reciprocity (as in TIT FOR TAT) can get started, thrive and defend itself in a biological world, as analysed with the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and foresight is not necessary (p. 88, p. 100, p. 105).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Biology once assumed that cooperation helps the group. Recent theory finds selection acts mainly on individuals, so cheating threatens cooperation. The chapter applies the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma to evolution. Always-defect (ALL D) is stable however likely a new meeting is, so the start of cooperation needs explaining. The chapter offers two mechanisms: kinship and clusters of reciprocators. TIT FOR TAT is robust. If the chance of meeting again, w, is high enough, it is stable too. The model suggests why symbioses go with lasting association or recognisable partners. It is also applied to territoriality and ageing, with speculative suggestions on disease and Down's syndrome. It matters because cooperation needs no brain or planning.

**Prerequisites:** Prisoner's Dilemma payoffs (T, R, P, S); the idea of a strategy; TIT FOR TAT and ALL D from chapters 1–3; basic natural selection.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Biology moved away from group-benefit explanations toward individual selection. Cooperation is then a puzzle, since cheating is easy (pp. 89–91).
- If payoffs are fitness and pairings are random and never repeated, or if the number of meetings is known, defection is the only stable strategy. With an unknown end (probability w of meeting again), ALL D stays stable, but other strategies can be stable too (pp. 92–93).
- Strategies need no brain. Bacteria and even a virus can use conditional strategies, but memory and discrimination of individuals grow with neural complexity (pp. 93–95).
- The chapter splits the question into robustness (doing well among many other strategies), stability (resisting mutant invaders once established) and initial viability (getting a foothold in a mostly noncooperative world). TIT FOR TAT is robust (pp. 95–96).
- TIT FOR TAT is stable if and only if w is large enough (p. 96). Kinship and clustering are the two mechanisms offered for how it can get a foothold against ALL D (pp. 96–99).
- Proposition 7 of chapter 3 shows an asymmetry between reciprocity getting started in a cluster and the reverse happening once it is established: "the gear wheels of social evolution have a ratchet" (p. 99). Once established, TIT FOR TAT can also resist whole clusters of mutants (p. 100).
- Retaliation must be possible: a defector must not be lost among anonymous others, and w must be high enough (p. 100). Continuous contact, fixed meeting places, territories and face recognition all serve this (pp. 100–102).
- When w falls (illness, age), reciprocity should weaken. The authors apply this speculatively to microbes, disease and Down's syndrome (pp. 103–105).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The chapter has no numbered sections. Headings below are ours, in the author's order.)

### Opening: the biological problem (pp. 88–91)
- The chapter returns the favour of earlier chapters, which borrowed biology. Its main conclusion: foresight is not necessary for cooperation to evolve (p. 88).
- Before about 1960, cooperation was treated as needing no special explanation. The reason was a misreading that put most adaptation at the level of populations or species, so cooperation was always "adaptive". Reviews now find no sound basis for group-benefit selection. Selection at species or population level is weak, and Darwin's individualistic emphasis is more valid (p. 89).
- Two extensions of theory explain cooperation: **kinship theory** (a gene's-eye view; altruism can pay if players are closely related) and **reciprocity theory**. Nearly all clear altruism outside humans occurs where relatedness is high. Example: the honeybee worker's suicidal sting (pp. 89–90).
- Cooperation also occurs with low or no relatedness. Examples: lichen (fungus and alga), ants and ant-acacias, fig wasps and fig trees. These mainly illustrate reciprocity (p. 90).
- Open difficulties: how cooperation starts from an asocial state, and how it is kept stable. Emphasis on individualism highlights cheating. Some cases once assigned to kinship (cooperative breeding in birds, primate groups) involve players not closely enough related. So either they are part-kin altruism with part cheating, or more of the behaviour is stable reciprocity (pp. 90–91).
- Claimed contributions: (1) a probabilistic treatment of meeting again, which sheds light on ageing and territoriality; (2) attention to initial viability and robustness as well as final stability; (3) application at the microbial level, with speculative suggestions on chronic and acute disease phases and on Down's syndrome (p. 91).

### The Prisoner's Dilemma in evolution (pp. 92–93)
- Benefits are disproportionately available to cooperating groups. Each individual can do even better by exploiting the others' cooperation. The Prisoner's Dilemma formalises this (p. 92).
- If payoffs are fitness, and interactions are random pairings that are not repeated, any population with a mixture of heritable strategies evolves to all defectors. No single mutant can do better against that population. When players never meet again, defection is the only stable strategy (p. 92).
- With a known number of interactions, always defecting is still stable and still the only stable strategy. The argument is backward: defecting on the last move is optimal, hence on the next-to-last, and so on to the first (pp. 92–93).
- The chapter 1 model instead gives probability $w$ that the same two individuals meet again after the current interaction. It depends on lifespan, mobility and health. For any $w$, ALL D is stable: no mutant can invade it (p. 93).
- **Evolutionarily stable:** a population using the strategy cannot be invaded by a rare mutant with a different strategy. Several strategies may be stable. Proposition 1 of chapter 1: when $w$ is sufficiently great, no single strategy is best whatever the others do. Analysis is not hopeless, because stability, robustness and initial viability can still be analysed (p. 93).

*Intuition note:* With a known end, cooperation unravels from the last move. With an unknown end, "maybe we meet again" keeps the future alive. Even then, ALL D remains stable, so the start of cooperation needs explaining.

### Who can play? (pp. 93–95)
- An organism needs no brain. Bacteria (1) respond to their chemical environment, (2) can therefore respond differently to what others do, (3) can inherit such conditional strategies, and (4) affect the fitness of neighbours and are affected by them. A virus can use a conditional strategy (pp. 93–94).
- Limits: bacteria cannot remember or interpret a complex past, and probably cannot tell the origin of harm. A bacterium might make bacteriocin (toxin harmless to its own strain) conditional on others' toxins, but could not aim it at an offender (p. 94).
- Primates gain a more complex memory, richer information processing, better estimates of future interaction, and better discrimination between individuals. Discrimination lets one reward one individual's cooperation and punish another's defection (pp. 94–95).
- The model is less restricted than it looks. It can apply to a bacterial colony and its primate host. Payoffs need not be comparable across sides, provided each side satisfies the Prisoner's Dilemma inequalities. Simultaneous, discrete choices are for most purposes equivalent to continuous interaction. Treating choices as sequential would make little difference (p. 95).

### Three questions (p. 95)
1. **Robustness:** what strategy thrives among many other strategies?
2. **Stability:** when can an established strategy resist mutant invasion?
3. **Initial viability:** how can it gain a foothold in a mostly noncooperative world?

### Robustness and stability (p. 96)
- TIT FOR TAT won both rounds of the chapter 2 tournament and five of six major variants of round two. In the ecological analysis it kept doing well as weaker rules were displaced (p. 96).
- Chapter 3: TIT FOR TAT is evolutionarily stable if and only if the interaction has a sufficiently large probability of continuing. ALL D is stable whatever that probability is, so the origin of cooperation needs explaining (p. 96).

### Initial viability 1: kinship (pp. 96–98)
- Close relatedness permits true altruism, here meaning loss of fitness by one individual to benefit another. It can evolve when cost, benefit and relatedness give net gains to the altruism genes in the relatives (p. 96).
- Not defecting in a single-move Prisoner's Dilemma is altruism of a kind. Counting payoffs as **inclusive fitness** gives each player part interest in the partner's gain. This can often remove the inequalities T > R and P > S, so cooperation is then unconditionally favoured. Parent–offspring and sibling pairs are promising (p. 97).
- Once genes for cooperation exist, selection favours basing cooperation on cues. Relatedness is uncertain (promiscuous fatherhood, unclear group margins). Reciprocation is itself a cue to relatedness, so more selfish behaviour after a negative response is advantageous when relatedness is low or in doubt. Conditional behaviour is thereby acquired, and cooperation spreads to less related partners. When the probability of meeting again is high enough, reciprocity can thrive and be stable with no relatedness (p. 97).
- **Sea bass example** ("at least on first evidence"): each fish has both sexual organs. Pairs take turns as egg-layer and sperm-provider, and up to ten spawnings occur per day. Pairs tend to break up if roles are not shared evenly. Fischer suggested the testes condition evolved when the species was sparse and inbred, so relatedness may initially have promoted cooperation (p. 98).

### Initial viability 2: clustering (pp. 98–99)
- A small group using TIT FOR TAT has a proportion $p$ of its interactions with fellow members. If the cluster is a negligible share of the interactions of the others, ALL D individuals still score about $P$ per move. As shown in chapter 3, if $p$ and $w$ are large enough, the cluster is initially viable among overwhelmingly ALL D (p. 98).
- Clustering often goes with kinship and they reinforce each other, but clustering can work without kinship (p. 98).
- A cluster of TIT FOR TATs gives each member a non-trivial chance of meeting a reciprocator. The reverse (ALL D re-invading) is asymmetrical: proposition 7 of chapter 3 shows "the gear wheels of social evolution have a ratchet" (p. 99).

### The chronology (pp. 99–100)
- ALL D is the primeval stable state. Reciprocity gains a foothold by kinship between mutants, or by mutants arriving in a cluster. Once many strategies exist, TIT FOR TAT is very robust and gradually displaces others in ecological simulation. If $w$ is great enough it is stable, and especially secure because it resists invasion by whole clusters of mutants. So cooperation can start, thrive and defend itself (pp. 99–100).

*Intuition note:* (Our gloss:) A lone reciprocator among defectors gets exploited. A group of reciprocators mostly meets each other and earns cooperation payoffs. The chapter calls the reverse move asymmetric (the "ratchet") and says TIT FOR TAT, once established, resists whole clusters of mutants (pp. 99–100).

### Applications: conditions for retaliation (pp. 100–103)
- Two requirements: defectors must not be lost among anonymous others, and $w$ must be high enough (p. 100).
- **No recognition needed if contact is continuous**, as in most mutualisms: hermit crab and sea anemone, cicada and its microorganisms, tree and mycorrhizal fungi (p. 100).
- **Fixed meeting place:** cleaner mutualisms (small fish or crustaceans eating parasites of larger fish) occur in coastal and reef settings with fixed home ranges. They seem unknown in the free-mixing open sea (pp. 100–101).
- Mutualisms generally go with continued association (quasi-permanent pairing, inbred or asexual stocks). Free mixing and transitory pairing without recognition favour exploitation such as parasitism. Examples: ant colonies have many symbioses, while less permanent honeybee colonies have no known symbionts but many parasites. *Chlorohydra viridissima* passes algae through the egg and has a stable association. Two other hydra species lack egg transmission, and their algal infection is described as parasitism. Impermanence tends to destabilise symbiosis (p. 101).
- **Territoriality** reduces the discrimination needed: neighbours interact often, strangers rarely. Male territorial birds react more aggressively to an unfamiliar song than to a neighbour's (p. 102).
- **Face recognition** in humans widens the range of stable cooperation. Prosopagnosia (loss of face-naming with few other symptoms, from lesions in an identifiable brain region) suggests recognising faces uses a significant part of the brain's resources (p. 102).
- **Monitoring $w$:** when $w$ falls below the stability threshold, reciprocating no longer pays. Illness or ageing of a partner signals declining $w$, so both partners should become less cooperative, and there is an incentive to take a one-time gain by defecting (p. 103).

### Microbial and speculative applications (pp. 103–105)
- A symbiont that can still infect other hosts would be expected to shift from mutualism to parasitism when continued interaction with its host becomes less likely (host injured, threatened by another infection, or ageing). Offered evidence: normal gut bacteria contribute to sepsis when the gut is perforated, and *Candida albicans* can become invasive in sick or elderly persons (p. 103).
- **Cancer** (only insofar as it is due to latent viruses): cancers tend to start when chances of transmission to the next generation are falling rapidly. The Burkitt's lymphoma virus may have slow (chronic) or fast (acute or lymphoma) forms. Some evidence suggests malaria can trigger lymphoma, which may then compete with malaria for transmission. With single infection a slow course is expected. With double infection, rapid exploitation might begin at once or later in ageing (pp. 103–104).
- **Down's syndrome** (tentative): egg formation is asymmetrical, and unlucky chromosomes go to a polar body. A homologous chromosome that is "first to defect" gets into the egg nucleus. If both of a pair try, an extra chromosome may result. The authors argue P > S, because a chromosome sent to the polar body contributes zero fitness while carriers of extra chromosomes have generally extremely low fitness. Perceptible "defection" triggering others, and chromosomes behaving this way, are "pure speculation". If so, the model would explain the rise with age in abnormal chromosome counts in eggs but not sperm (pp. 104–105).
- Conclusion: Darwin's individualism is formalised in game theory, showing conditions under which reciprocity-based cooperation can evolve without foresight (p. 105).

## Toy example (ours; payoff numbers are assumed, not from this unit)
Use T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0 and w=0.9. The expected number of moves per pairing is 1/(1−w)=10. Later moves count w, w², … so the expected number of moves after the first is w/(1−w)=9.
- TIT FOR TAT vs TIT FOR TAT: 3×10 = 30.
- TIT FOR TAT vs ALL D: 0 on move 1, then 1 per later move: 0 + 1×9 = 9.
- ALL D vs ALL D: 1×10 = 10.

A native ALL D player meets almost only ALL D, so it scores about 10 (the cluster is a negligible share of its meetings).
A cluster member has proportion p = 0.2 of meetings inside the cluster: 0.2×30 + 0.8×9 = 6 + 7.2 = 13.2 > 10. The cluster is viable.
With p = 0 a lone TIT FOR TAT scores 9 < 10 and loses. So p matters, as the chapter says (p. 98).

## Concepts
- [[evolutionarily stable strategy]] — a strategy a population uses that no rare mutant with a different strategy can invade (p. 93)
- [[shadow of the future]] — here the probability $w$ that the same two individuals meet again; set by lifespan, mobility, health (p. 93)
- [[iterated-prisoners-dilemma]] (new) — repeated PD where players recognise past partners and may condition on history (p. 92)
- [[tit-for-tat]] (new) — the reciprocity strategy of chapter 2 (its definition is not given in these pages); robust, and stable if $w$ is large enough (p. 96)
- [[all-d]] (new) — unconditional defection; stable for every $w$ (p. 93)
- [[kinship-inclusive-fitness]] (new) — counting payoffs including relatives' gains, which can remove T > R and P > S (p. 97)
- [[clustering]] (new) — reciprocators interacting disproportionately with each other, which lets them invade ALL D (p. 98)
- [[robustness-stability-initial-viability]] (new) — the chapter's three-part test of an evolving strategy (p. 95)
- [[ratchet-effect]] (new) — cooperation is easier to start than to lose (proposition 7) (p. 99)
- [[mutualism]] (new) — close association of mutual benefit between species; needs lasting contact (p. 100)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Evolutionarily stable strategy ↔ decision-game-theory: Nash equilibrium — no rare deviant gains, but the biological version works through reproduction, not choice.
- [structural-analogy] $w$, probability of meeting again ↔ repeated games: discount factor/patience in the folk theorem — both make future payoffs matter.
- [structural-analogy] Clustering of TIT FOR TAT ↔ network reciprocity: local interaction helps cooperators.
- [metaphor] Ratchet ↔ path dependence: easy to enter, hard to leave.

## Questions this raises
- How large must $w$ be for TIT FOR TAT to be stable, and how do T, R, P, S change this threshold (chapter 3)?
- If kinship and clustering both help, how can one tell which started a real case of cooperation?
- Which of the disease and chromosome applications could actually be tested, given the authors call parts speculative?
- What happens when partners cannot recognise each other but need not stay in contact?
