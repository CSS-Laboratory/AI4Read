# The Live-and-Let-Live System in Trench Warfare in World War I

## L0 — One line
In the quiet sectors of the Western Front, small enemy units facing each other for long periods formed an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma, and reciprocity let restraint start, last, and then collapse when headquarters imposed raids it could monitor.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why did enemy soldiers in World War I trenches often restrain their fire? Axelrod reads the quiet sectors as an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma between small units facing each other for long periods. Cooperation started locally, was sustained by reciprocity with retaliation, and was destroyed when headquarters introduced raids (prepared attacks on enemy trenches) that it could monitor. He also notes that ethics and ritual emerged. The case suggests that friendship is hardly necessary for reciprocity-based cooperation to get started.

**Prerequisites:** the Prisoner's Dilemma (a game in which defecting is the best move in a single round, yet mutual cooperation beats mutual defection) and its payoffs T (defect against restraint), R (mutual restraint), P (mutual punishment) and S (restrain against defection); reciprocity (answering the other side's move in kind); the idea of repeating a game; TIT FOR TAT (cooperate first, then copy the other side's last move).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Axelrod sets four questions: how the system started, how it was sustained, why it broke down, and why it was characteristic of this war but of few other wars (p. 74).
- Locally, the quiet-sector situation fits a Prisoner's Dilemma, with T>R>P>S and R>(T+S)/2. Axelrod says Ashworth does not describe it this way (p. 75).
- At the national level, Axelrod says the war approximated a zero-sum game (one side's gain is the other's loss). Locally, mutual restraint was much preferred to mutual punishment (pp. 76–77).
- The same small units meeting for long periods turned the one-move game into an iterated one, where conditional strategies are possible (p. 77).
- Cooperation started through meals, Christmas fraternization, weather truces, and tacit restraint. Direct verbal truces were easily suppressed (pp. 77–79).
- Cooperation was sustained by provocable strategies, by demonstrations of ability to retaliate, and by briefing newcomers on tacit understandings. Headquarters, with few exceptions, could enforce orders it could directly monitor (pp. 79–82).
- Raids, which headquarters could monitor, destroyed the system. After a raid neither side knew what to expect, and retaliation was no longer damped (pp. 82–83).
- The strategies evolved by trial and error and imitation, with deliberate adaptation rather than blind mutation. Ethics and ritual emerged as two additions to the theory (pp. 83–87).
- The case shows that friendship is hardly necessary for reciprocity-based cooperation to get started (p. 87).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the puzzle and four questions (pp. 73–75)
- A British staff officer was astonished to see German soldiers walking about within rifle range. He concluded that "both sides apparently believed in the policy of 'live and let live'" (pp. 73–74).
- The system was endemic in trench warfare. It flourished despite senior officers' efforts to stop it, and despite the ease with which the high command could repress local efforts at a direct truce (p. 74).
- Axelrod wants to explain four things (p. 74):
  1. How the system got started.
  2. How it was sustained.
  3. Why it broke down toward the end of the war.
  4. Why it was characteristic of trench warfare in World War I, but of few other wars.
- A second goal is to use the case to elaborate the theory (p. 74).
- The evidence comes from Tony Ashworth (1980), a British sociologist. His work uses diaries, letters and reminiscences, with material from virtually every one of the fifty-seven British divisions. Axelrod relies on it for quotes and historical interpretation (pp. 74–75).

### The game (pp. 75–77)
- **Axelrod's reading:** the quiet sectors were an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma. Ashworth does not put it this way (p. 75).
- Players: the small units facing each other. Choices: shoot to kill, or deliberately shoot to avoid causing damage (p. 75).
- Reasoning (p. 75):
  - Weakening the enemy promotes survival if a major battle is ordered. So in the short run it is better to do damage now, whether or not the enemy shoots back.
  - This gives $P>S$ (mutual defection beats unilateral restraint) and $T>R$.
  - Mutual restraint beats mutual punishment, $R>P$, because both would suffer for little or no relative gain.
  - Together: $T>R>P>S$.
  - Both sides also prefer mutual restraint to random alternation of serious hostilities: $R>(T+S)/2$.
- The usual player is the battalion: about 1,000 men, half in the front line at any time, facing the enemy across 100–400 yards of no-man's-land (the empty ground between trenches) (pp. 75–76).
  - It organized, fed, paid and clothed its men, and arranged their leave. All of the officers and most of the other soldiers knew each other by sight (p. 76).
  - It was large enough to be "held accountable" for aggression from its sector, and small enough to control its men's behavior (p. 76).
  - A battalion could face parts of one to three enemy battalions, so there were hundreds of face-offs (p. 76).
- **A common soldier's view:** neither side meant to advance in that district. Shelling and bombing were answered with equal damage (Belton Cobb 1916; p. 76).
- **Axelrod on the high commands:** headquarters wanted an offensive spirit. The Allies pursued attrition: equal losses meant a net gain for them because Germany would be exhausted first (p. 76).
- **Axelrod's comment:** at the national level the war approximated a zero-sum game. But at the local level, mutual restraint was much preferred to mutual punishment (pp. 76–77).
- Locally the dilemma persisted: it was prudent at any moment to shoot to kill. What differed was that the same units faced each other for long periods in immobile sectors. This changed a one-move game, where defection is dominant, into an iterated game where conditional strategies (ones that depend on the other side's earlier moves) are possible (p. 77).
- Axelrod says the result accorded with the theory: both sides followed strategies that would not be first to defect but would be provoked by defection (p. 77).

*Intuition note:* In one meeting, shooting looks better whatever the enemy does. When the same two units meet again and again, each can reward restraint and punish shooting. That is why cooperation became possible here (p. 77).

### How cooperation got started (pp. 77–79)
- The first stage of the war (from August 1914) was mobile and bloody. As the lines stabilized, nonaggression emerged spontaneously in many places (p. 77).
- Early cases may have been tied to meals served at the same times on both sides. A November 1914 account describes quiet ration-fetching hours (pp. 77–78).
- By Christmas there was extensive fraternization. Later, direct truces were sometimes arranged by shouts or signals. One section treated 8–9 A.M. as "private business" (p. 78).
- Direct truces were easily suppressed. Soldiers were told to fight, not fraternize. Some were court-martialed and whole battalions punished. Such arrangements became rare (p. 78).
- Bad weather made major action nearly impossible. Ad hoc weather truces arose, and when weather improved, restraint sometimes simply continued (p. 78).
- More effective in the long run were ways to coordinate without words. Soldiers realized that if one side showed a kind of restraint, the other might reciprocate (pp. 78–79).
  - Similar needs let them see the other side probably was not following unconditional defection (p. 79).
  - **A soldier's reasoning (Hay 1916):** shelling the ration road invites the enemy to do the same to yours (p. 79).
- Once started, reciprocity could spread (p. 79):
  - to longer hours;
  - to other kinds of restraint;
  - by imitation, to neighboring sectors.

### How it was sustained (pp. 79–82)
- Sustaining strategies were provocable, meaning they would retaliate if the other side defected (p. 79).
- Soldiers demonstrated retaliatory ability. German snipers shot a hole in a cottage wall, and artillery fired a few accurate shots. This showed that restraint was not weakness and that defection would be self-defeating (pp. 79–80).
- Retaliation was often more than TIT FOR TAT, commonly two-for-one or three-for-one. One writer (Greenwell 1972) says that, on the Germans' system of retaliation, three come back for every rifle grenade his side fires (p. 80).
- **Axelrod's guess:** there was "probably" a damping process. The instigator might not escalate further, and not every shot hits, so the exchange tends to de-escalate (p. 80).
- **Rotation:** about every eight days a battalion swapped with one behind it. Outgoing units explained the tacit understandings to incoming ones, sometimes with a single remark. This let the new unit "pick up the game right where the other left it" (pp. 80–81).
- **Artillery:** it was less vulnerable to retaliation, so it had a smaller stake in the system. Infantry were therefore solicitous toward artillery observers. The ideal answer to "I hope you are not going to start trouble" was "Not unless you want" (p. 81).
- **High commands:** all wanted to stop tacit truces, fearing they sapped morale (p. 81).
  - With few exceptions, headquarters could enforce any orders they could directly monitor (observe directly). So they could order large battles (p. 81).
  - Between battles they could not monitor orders to keep up pressure. It was hard to tell who was shooting to kill and who was avoiding retaliation (p. 82).
  - Soldiers became expert at defeating monitoring, for example by sending home a piece of kept enemy wire as "proof" of a patrol (p. 82).

### How it broke down (pp. 82–83)
- What destroyed the system was a kind of aggression headquarters could monitor: the **raid**, a prepared attack on enemy trenches by 10–200 men (p. 82).
  - Success produced prisoners, and failure produced casualties as proof of the attempt. A raid could not be faked (p. 82).
  - No cooperation was possible in a raid, since neither live soldiers nor dead bodies could be exchanged (p. 82).
- Hundreds of small raids disrupted the system (p. 82):
  - Afterward neither side knew what to expect. The raider expected retaliation but not when, where or how; the raided side did not know whether more would follow.
  - Headquarters could order and monitor retaliatory raids. This prevented damping, and the process "echoed out of control."
- British High Command began raiding for other reasons: to show the French that it was doing its part, to restore an offensive spirit, and to promote attrition. Whether morale and casualty effects were realized "has been debated ever since" (pp. 82–83).
- **Axelrod's conclusion:** the raids' indirect effect was to destroy the conditions needed for tacit restraint, by stopping battalions from using their own reciprocity strategies (p. 83).

*Intuition note:* The raids removed the battalions' freedom to choose their own responses. Headquarters could now check and control the aggression, and a raid could not be shared out quietly. In Axelrod's account, the live-and-let-live system could not survive that (pp. 82–83).

### The mechanism of evolution (pp. 83–84)
- The cycle: cooperation got a foothold through local exploratory actions; it was sustained by long contact; it was undermined when units lost freedom of action (p. 83).
- Axelrod says the mechanisms "must have been" trial and error and imitation of neighboring units (pp. 83–84).
- It involved neither blind mutation nor survival of the fittest (p. 84):
  - Soldiers understood their situation and tried to make the most of it, in line with the **echo principle**: "To provide discomfort for the other is but a roundabout way of providing it for themselves."
  - They learned that restraint needs a demonstrated willingness to be provoked, so that cooperation rests on reciprocity.
  - Not survival of the fittest: although an ineffective strategy meant more casualties, replacements typically meant that the units themselves survived.

### Two additions to the theory (pp. 84–87)
- **Ethics** (pp. 84–85):
  - A Saxon soldier apologized after a salvo landed: "We are very sorry about that; we hope no one was hurt." Axelrod reads this as moral regret and concern, beyond an instrumental effort to avoid retaliation (p. 85).
  - Sustained cooperation tended to make the sides care about each other's welfare. **Axelrod's reading in Prisoner's Dilemma terms:** experience altered the payoffs, making mutual cooperation more valued (p. 85).
  - The converse held. When raids broke down cooperation, an ethic of revenge arose, and revenge evoked revenge. Both cooperation and defection were self-reinforcing (p. 85).
  - The abstract point: preferences affect behavior and outcomes, and behavior and outcomes also affect preferences (p. 85).
- **Ritual** (pp. 85–87):
  - Rituals were perfunctory small-arms fire and deliberately harmless artillery (pp. 85–86).
  - Shelling was so regular in targets, times and rounds that Colonel Jones could tell "to a minute" where the next shell would fall (p. 86). A British evening gun at seven was just as regular (p. 86).
  - This ritual fire sent a double message: aggression to the high command, peace to the enemy (p. 86).
  - Ashworth says these acts symbolized and strengthened fellow-feeling and the belief that the enemy was a fellow sufferer (p. 87). Axelrod says the rituals helped strengthen the moral sanctions that reinforced the system (p. 87).

### Conclusion (p. 87)
- The system shows that friendship is hardly necessary for reciprocity-based cooperation to start. Under suitable circumstances cooperation can develop even between antagonists (p. 87).
- The soldiers had a fairly clear understanding of the role of reciprocity. The next chapter uses biological examples to argue that such understanding is not really necessary for cooperation to emerge and prove stable (p. 87).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take payoffs per day for one battalion: T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0. Check: 5>3>1>0, and R=3 > (T+S)/2 = 2.5.
- One meeting: if the enemy restrains, shooting gives 5 against restraint's 3. If the enemy shoots, shooting gives 1 against 0. So shooting is better either way.
- Ten days against a TIT FOR TAT enemy (restrain on day 1, then copy your last move):
  - Restrain every day: 10 × 3 = 30.
  - Shoot every day: day 1 gives 5. Then the enemy shoots back, and the nine days after give 1 each: 5 + 9 × 1 = 14.
- Repetition makes restraint pay, which is the chapter's central point.
