# The Robustness of Reciprocity

## L0 — One line
Cooperation based on reciprocity can start in small clusters, thrive among many kinds of strategies, and protect itself once established, provided the future casts a large enough shadow.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The closing chapter reviews the book's evolutionary tools and asks what the results mean for real life. Its answer: reciprocal cooperation can begin in small clusters, do well in a varied world, and resist invasion once established. It needs no trust, no altruism, no foresight and no central authority. It does need recognition, memory, a large enough shadow of the future, and some clustering. TIT FOR TAT is robustly successful but not ideal; the author finds quick provocability valuable and, in many circumstances, a slightly limited response more stable. The author draws lessons for institutions, antitrust, arms control and international relations.

**Prerequisites:** the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (payoffs T, R, P, S, repeated play); the parameter $w$ (weight of the next meeting); the results of chapters 1–8 (stability, tournaments, clusters).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Evolution needs differential growth of the successful plus a source of variety. Both can be non-biological: imitation, learning, trial and error (pp. 169–170).
- Each tool answers a different question. Collective stability says what lasts once established; the tournament says what is likely to get established; the ecological and territorial analyses track how success changes over generations (pp. 170–172).
- Cooperation needs very little to be assumed about the players: no rationality, messages, trust, altruism or central authority (pp. 173–174). It does need recognition and memory, a large enough shadow of the future, and some clustering of discriminating strategies (pp. 174–175).
- TIT FOR TAT's robust success is due to being nice, provocable, forgiving and clear. It is not an ideal strategy and has weaknesses (pp. 176–177).
- Institutions: frequent, durable interaction promotes cooperation; to hinder collusion, reduce the shadow of the future. Rapid turnover of executives could lessen cooperation, so accountability should extend past one's term (pp. 178–184).
- Provocability should be quick, but limited so that it does not cause an endless echo of retaliation (pp. 184–187).
- Durability, not friendship or trust, is the foundation of cooperation. Foresight speeds up evolution, as the better scores in round two show (pp. 182, 187–189).
- Success comes from eliciting cooperation, not from beating others (pp. 189–190).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The evolutionary approach (pp. 169–170)
- Basic principle: what is successful is likely to appear more often. The mechanism may be natural selection, reelection in Congress, or avoiding bankruptcy in business. With intelligent players, others may convert to a successful strategy by imitation or by informed learning (pp. 169–170).
- Evolution also needs variety. In biology this comes from mutation and gene reshuffling. In social life it comes from trial in "trial and error". A new pattern may be a random variant of an old one, or deliberately built from experience and theory (p. 170).

### The tools (pp. 170–172)
- **Collective stability** asks where the process stops: which strategies cannot be invaded if everyone uses them. It showed that TIT FOR TAT is collectively stable if the shadow of the future is large enough. Always defecting is collectively stable under all possible conditions (p. 170).
- Strength: it considers all possible new strategies. Limit: it says what lasts once established, not what gets established first. Many strategies can be stable (pp. 170–171).
- **Tournament**: it captures the variety of things happening at once. Round one had entries from professional game theorists. Round two had entrants who knew round one's results, so ideas could be refinements or new conceptions (p. 171).
- **Ecological analysis**: each generation, strategies grow in proportion to their previous success. It introduces no new strategies. It follows the strategies already in the tournament over hundreds of generations. It tests whether early winners stay successful after poor performers drop out. Growth can mean better survival or more imitation (pp. 171–172).
- **Territorial analysis**: the 63 strategies of round two sit in a territory. Each location interacts with its four neighbours. A location with a more successful neighbour adopts the strategy of the most successful neighbour. Success is local (p. 172).
- All tools need the performance of any strategy against any other. Simple cases are solved algebraically (TIT FOR TAT against an always-defector). Complex cases are simulated, as in the tournament. Game length was varied to represent time discount and uncertain ending. Probabilistic strategies were averaged over repetitions (p. 172).

### The setting and main results (pp. 172–174)
- The setting captures the fundamental dilemma of cooperation. Each player can help the other, but helping is costly. Mutual gain exists when the other's help is worth more than the cost of one's own. Two difficulties: you must get the other to help although the other gains short-run by not helping, and you are tempted to take help without giving any (p. 173).
- Main results: cooperation can start from even a small cluster of reciprocators in a world where no one else cooperates. Its two key requisites are reciprocity and a shadow of the future large enough to make reciprocity stable. Once established, it can protect itself from uncooperative strategies (p. 173).
- Little is assumed. Players need not be rational. They need no messages or commitments ("deeds speak"). They need no trust or altruism, since successful strategies can elicit cooperation even from an egoist. No central authority is needed, because reciprocity can be self-policing (pp. 173–174).
- Needed assumptions:
  - Players can recognize someone met before and remember the history. The author says these demands are not as strong as they might seem: even bacteria could meet them with a strategy such as TIT FOR TAT that reacts only to recent behaviour (p. 174).
  - The shadow of the future must be large: a large enough chance of meeting again, and the next meeting not discounted too much. WWI trench example: the same small units faced each other for long periods, so a broken tacit understanding could be punished on the same unit (p. 174).
  - Successful strategies must be able to thrive, and there must be variation (mutation, imitation or designed ideas) (pp. 174–175).
  - To get started, some clustering is needed. In a world of unconditional defection, a lone cooperator cannot prosper unless others around will reciprocate. Small clusters of discriminating individuals can succeed if even a small proportion of their interactions is with each other. The strategies must cooperate first and must discriminate between responders and non-responders (p. 175).

> **Intuition note:** Cooperation can grow from a small group that meets each other often enough. It does not need friendship or foresight. It does need recognition and memory, a future that casts a large enough shadow, and some clustering (pp. 174–175, 187–188).

### TIT FOR TAT (pp. 175–177)
- The conditions say what is necessary, not which strategy is best. TIT FOR TAT cooperates first, then copies the other's previous move (p. 175).
- Evidence: it won round one (game theorists' entries) and round two (over sixty entries by people who knew round one). It won five of the six major variants of round two and was second in the sixth. In the ecological simulation of hundreds of hypothetical rounds it was again most successful. The author reads this as showing that it does well with good and bad rules alike (pp. 175–176).
- Reasons for success (p. 176):
  - Nice: never the first to defect, which avoids unnecessary trouble.
  - Provocable: retaliation discourages persistence in defection.
  - Forgiving: this helps restore mutual cooperation.
  - Clear: easy to recognize, and so easy to see that cooperating with it is best.
- It is not an ideal strategy:
  - Nice rules need a large enough shadow of the future (p. 176).
  - No strategy is ideal independent of what others use. In extreme environments, with too few reciprocators, even TIT FOR TAT does poorly (p. 176).
  - A single defection can set off an unending echo of alternating defections: not forgiving enough. It is also too forgiving toward totally unresponsive rules such as a random rule (pp. 176–177).
  - What can be said: it performs well where the other players use more or less sophisticated strategies designed to do well (p. 177).
- If nearly everyone uses a nice strategy, they can afford to be generous. A population of nice rules can protect itself against clusters of any other strategy as well as against single individuals (p. 177).
- Chronology: cooperation begins in small clusters, thrives with nice, provocable, somewhat forgiving rules, and is then protected from invasion. Overall cooperation tends to go up, not down: "a ratchet" (p. 177).

> **Intuition note:** TIT FOR TAT does well because it is easy to read and hard to exploit. The author does not claim it is perfect: it can get stuck in echoes and can be too gentle with rules that ignore it.

### Cooperation in real institutions (pp. 177–181)
- Congress: early members were known for deceit and treachery, yet patterns based on the norm of reciprocity emerged and proved stable (p. 177).
- Diamond markets: millions of dollars of goods are exchanged on a verbal pledge. Participants expect to deal again and again, so exploitation does not pay (pp. 177–178).
- Ron Luciano, an umpire, let trusted catchers call pitches on his bad days. **Luciano says:** no one took advantage. The author explains that the reliance could work because Luciano would have had many opportunities to retaliate if he suspected abuse (p. 178).
- Business: **a purchasing agent (Macaulay 1963) says** you phone the other man rather than read contract clauses at each other if you want to do business again. A packaging manufacturer found it had no legally binding contract in two-thirds of its orders. Fairness is guaranteed by the anticipation of future dealings, not by lawsuits (p. 179).
- Courts are invoked when that anticipation breaks down. **Macaulay, as reported:** perhaps the most common contract case fought to appellate courts is a wrongful termination of a dealer's franchise, after which there is no future between the parties (p. 179).
- Lloyd's of London: independent brokers pooled risks so often that they became a federated organization with a formal structure (p. 179).
- Design lesson: structure relationships to have frequent, durable interactions among specific individuals (p. 180).
- Antitrust: sometimes the aim is to hinder cooperation. The author says that, since reciprocity needs no formal agreement or meeting, antitrust should attend more to conditions that foster collusion than to searching for secret meetings (p. 180).
- Example: the government picks two firms for competitive airplane contracts. Specialization in Air Force or Navy planes means the same firms often meet in the final competition (Art 1968). The government should reduce specialization or compensate for it. Firms would then meet less often, later interactions would be worth less, and the shadow of the future would shrink. If the next interaction is far off enough, tacit collusion stops being stable (pp. 180–181).
- Arms control: it need not rely only on treaties and could evolve tacitly. The US and USSR know they will deal with each other for a long time. The leaders need not like each other, "neither did the soldiers in World War I" (p. 181).
- Driving a rival to bankruptcy is risky. The target may escalate before it is weakened. Example: Japan's attack at Pearl Harbor responded to US economic sanctions. Japan judged that attacking was better than waiting while sanctions accumulated (p. 181).

### Time perspective, monitoring, misperception (pp. 181–184)
- Putting the future in doubt removes the shadow of the future, and cooperation cannot then be sustained. Cooperation is ripe when interaction will likely last and players care enough about their future together (pp. 181–182).
- The foundation is durability, not trust. Players can reach cooperation by trial and error, imitation or blind selection. Whether they trust each other matters less than whether conditions are ripe (p. 182).
- Monitoring: players must be able to observe and respond to prior choices, otherwise defection cannot be punished. This need not be perfect (p. 182).
- Misperception test: round one was rerun with a 1 percent chance that each choice is misperceived. There was more defection. Surprise: TIT FOR TAT was still the best rule. A misunderstanding could start a long echo of alternating retaliation, but it could often end the echo with another misperception. Less forgiving rules got out of trouble less often (pp. 182–183).
- Turnover: executives are moved about every two years. They have an incentive to look good short-run and give two executives a mutual incentive to defect as either term ends. The result could be less cooperation (p. 183).
- Lame-duck politicians have the same problem: private goals tempt them (pp. 183–184).
- Solution: turnover is needed in democracy, so accountability must extend beyond the term. Parties are in a long-term relationship with voters and can be held responsible. Example: the Republican party was punished after Watergate (p. 184).
- Organizations should record the state in which a position was left to the successor. An executive who double-crossed a colleague before transferring should be judged accordingly (p. 184).

### Provocability and its limits (pp. 184–187)
- **Author's personal view:** he began by thinking one should be slow to anger. The tournament shows it is better to respond quickly (p. 184).
- Reason: waiting risks sending the wrong signal. The longer defections go unchallenged, the more the other concludes that defection pays, and the harder the pattern is to break. TIT FOR TAT gives the quickest feedback (pp. 184–185).
- Arms control: the sooner the US detects and answers Soviet probes, the better. Waiting for probes to accumulate risks a response so large that it provokes more trouble (p. 185).
- The speed of response depends on detection time. Quick detection means the next move comes soon, which raises the shadow of the future $w$. The author says only arms control agreements whose violations can be detected soon enough can be stable. Violations must be detected before they accumulate so far that the victim's provocability no longer deters the challenger (p. 185).
- Theory agrees: a collectively stable nice rule must be provocable by the very first defection (proposition 4, chapter 3). The response need not be immediate or certain, but must have a real probability of coming eventually, so that the other player does not end up with an incentive to defect (pp. 185–186).
- Danger: retaliation can lead to unending mutual defection, as in blood feuds between clans (Black-Michaud 1975). This is due to the echo effect: each side answers the other's last defection with a new one (p. 186).
- A central authority is often unavailable, and courts can be too costly for routine matters. The best then is a self-policing strategy: provocable, but with a response not so great as to cause an endless echo (p. 186).
- Example: the Warsaw Pact partly mobilizes. NATO should raise its alert, with troops moved from the US matching troops moved from the USSR. **Betts (1982) recommends** that this be automatic, so the Soviets see it as standard procedure that follows their mobilization. He also recommends it be limited, e.g. one US division for every three Soviet divisions (pp. 186–187).
- **Author's conclusion:** unlike TIT FOR TAT, which answers with exactly equal defection, a response slightly less than the provocation would often make cooperation more stable. Then escalation can stabilize and possibly reverse (p. 187).

> **Intuition note:** Answer wrongdoing early, so the other side learns that it does not pay. But answer a bit less than you were hit, or the two sides may keep retaliating forever.

### Friendship, foresight and learning (pp. 187–190)
- Friendship is not necessary; durability is. Major powers can be sure they will interact year after year, so next year casts a large shadow on this year's choices (pp. 187–188).
- Foresight is not necessary, but without it evolution can take very long. Humans use foresight to speed it up (p. 188).
- Evidence: round-one average score per move among the game theorists' rules was 2.10, only slightly better than halfway from $P=1$ to $R=3$. Round two scored 2.60, a little better than three-quarters of the way. The second-round players used round one's results, including the value of reciprocity (p. 188).
- Attempts to exploit the unsophisticated round-one entries all failed in round two, showing that the reciprocity of strategies like TIT FOR TAT is extraordinarily robust. The author hopes people can use the tournament as surrogate experience (pp. 188–189).
- Self-reinforcement: once the word gets out that reciprocity works, you avoid starting trouble and defect after another's defection. So does everyone. Appreciation of reciprocity gets stronger (p. 189).
- This is the ratchet effect from chapter 3: once reciprocity-based cooperation is established, it cannot be overcome even by a cluster of exploiters. It can take long if based on blind forces, or be quick if intelligent players appreciate it (p. 189).
- Lesson: TIT FOR TAT succeeds without doing better than anyone it interacts with. It elicits cooperation rather than defeating others. Many situations are not like football or chess with one winner. Mutual cooperation can be better for both sides (pp. 189–190).

### International relations (pp. 190–191)
- The author says the most important problems of humanity lie in international relations, where egoistic nations face each other in near anarchy. Many take the form of an iterated Prisoner's Dilemma: arms races, nuclear proliferation, crisis bargaining, military escalation. A realistic understanding must add factors the simple model omits (ideology, bureaucratic politics, commitments, coalitions, mediation, leadership) (p. 190).
- **Gilpin (1981) is cited:** all political theory addresses how humanity can understand and control blind forces of history (p. 190).
- Chapter 6 advice, for leaders too: don't be envious, don't be the first to defect, reciprocate both cooperation and defection, don't be too clever. The chapter 7 techniques for promoting cooperation may also help (pp. 190–191).
- Closing: trial and error is slow and painful, and we may lack time. Better understanding could let foresight speed up the evolution of cooperation (p. 191).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Assumed payoffs per move: $T=5, R=3, P=1, S=0$. Each pair plays 10 moves. Population: nearly all always-defect (ALLD). A small cluster plays TIT FOR TAT (TFT).
- TFT vs TFT: $10 \times 3 = 30$.
- TFT vs ALLD: TFT gets 0 on move 1, then 1 on each of 9 moves, so $0+9=9$.
- ALLD vs ALLD: $10 \times 1 = 10$.
- Let $p$ be the share of a TFT member's games played against other TFT members. Its total is $30p + 9(1-p) = 9+21p$. Since the cluster is small, an ALLD player almost always meets ALLD and gets 10.
- TFT beats ALLD when $9+21p > 10$, i.e. $p > 1/21 \approx 0.048$.
- So a few percent of interactions inside the cluster are enough here. This shows the chapter's point that clusters of discriminating, nice strategies can start cooperation, and that a lone cooperator ($p=0$, scoring 9) cannot.

- [[reciprocity]] — answering the other's cooperation with cooperation and defection with defection; self-policing (p. 173)
- [[evolutionarily-stable-strategy]] — the chapter's collective stability: a strategy that cannot be invaded if everyone uses it (p. 170); the link to ESS is ours, not stated in the chapter

- [[tit-for-tat]] (new) — cooperate first, then copy the other's last move; nice, provocable, forgiving, clear (pp. 175–176)

- [[clustering]] (new) — some interactions among nice, discriminating strategies; needed to start cooperation (p. 175)

## Questions this raises
- How large must the shadow of the future be for TIT FOR TAT to be collectively stable? (The chapter says only "large enough".)
- How much less than proportional should a response be to stop echoes without inviting exploitation?
- If misperception is more frequent than 1 percent, does TIT FOR TAT still win?
- Which real settings lack recognition or memory, so that the conditions fail?
