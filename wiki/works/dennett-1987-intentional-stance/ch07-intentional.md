# Intentional Systems in Cognitive Ethology: The “Panglossian Paradigm” Defended

## L0 — One line
Dennett argues that describing animals by beliefs and desires, and describing organisms as well designed, are the same kind of risky, rationality-assuming interpretive strategy. He holds that both are defensible because they work and generate empirical questions to put to nature.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Can ethologists (scientists of animal behaviour) describe animals as having beliefs and desires? How would they test such descriptions? Dennett says yes. The *intentional stance* means predicting behaviour by treating a system as a rational believer and wanter. It is risky but fruitful, and it is tested through its own rationality assumption. He illustrates with vervet monkey alarm calls. He then argues that *adaptationism* (explaining traits by asking what natural selection favoured them for) is the same kind of strategy. He defends it against Gould and Lewontin. His later “Reflections” add fieldwork lessons, replies to critics, and a thought experiment about telling natural from artificial selection.

**Prerequisites:** no equations. You need the idea of natural selection, the logic of substituting equals (“a rose by any other name”), and the basic aim of behaviorism (explaining behaviour without mental terms).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Opacity test and rationality.** Sentences with *believes that*, *wants that* and similar idioms are *referentially opaque*: swapping a co-referring term can change truth value. That test picks out the mentalistic vocabulary (words for mental states). Separately, and more controversially, Dennett holds that using these idioms presupposes rationality in the system (pp. 240–242).
- **Orders of intentionality.** A first-order system has beliefs and desires but none about beliefs and desires. A second-order system has such attitudes about attitudes, and so on upward. Dennett ranks competing readings of one vervet alarm call from fourth order down to zero order (tropism, a fixed automatic response) (pp. 243–246).
- **Rationality as a testing lever.** Because the stance assumes rationality, the competing readings can be tested against behaviour. Evidence can demote the animal to a lower-order reading or promote it to a higher-order one (pp. 247–249).
- **Holmes method.** Dennett holds that stance-guided ruses and traps give the researcher more investigative power than behaviorist methods. They must be run as controlled experiments, and failures often demote (pp. 251–254, 271).
- **Intentional profile as specification.** An intentional profile says what information a system must be able to use. It is a task description for the engineers of its internal machinery (pp. 255–256).
- **Free-floating rationales.** A design can have a rationale that no individual represents, such as the deceptive point of a broken-wing display. Dennett says evolution “chose” it for that reason (p. 259).
- **Adaptationism = Skinner-style controversy.** Dennett compares Gould and Lewontin’s complaints against adaptationism with Skinner’s against mentalism (explaining behaviour by mental states). Both strategies are risky and open to the charge of being too easy. He says they are stances, not classical theories, and that they often work (pp. 261–266).
- **Fieldwork lessons.** In Kenya the classical Holmes method proved of limited use. Vervet life seems to offer few occasions of private knowledge. Dennett thinks this helps explain why vervets have only a modest communication system (pp. 272–277).
- **Adaptationism as retrospective radical interpretation.** Dennett holds that proper adaptationist thinking just is adopting a special version of the intentional stance in evolutionary thinking. His thought experiment suggests, but does not prove, that there may be no foolproof marks of natural as against artificial selection (pp. 283–285).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The Problem (pp. 237–240)
- **Setting.** Dennett, a philosopher and outsider to ethology, says the new cognitive ethologists have dropped behaviorism and seek a vocabulary that describes their data powerfully. They also want a way of framing hypotheses that will *eventually* lead to information-processing models of the nervous system (p. 237). His plan has four parts. First, a quick analysis of the problem. Second, a proposal, which he calls intentional system theory. Third, its continuity with adaptationism. Fourth, a limited defence of adaptationism against Gould and Lewontin (pp. 237–238).
- **Quine’s radical translator.** Quine imagined an anthropologist-linguist in an alien community with no interpreters. Quine’s thesis of the indeterminacy of radical translation says it must always be possible in principle to produce nontrivially different translation manuals, equally well supported by all the evidence, for any language. Quine’s scruples are strictly behaviorist. Dennett asks what happens to the translator’s task if one gives up behaviorism and uses cognitivist vocabulary (p. 238).
- **Vervet data.** Seyfarth, Cheney and Marler (1980) report this. Vervets give different alarm calls to different predators. Playbacks with no predator present make them run into trees (leopard call), look up (eagle call) or look down (snake call). Adults call mainly to leopards, martial eagles and pythons. Infants overgeneralize, for example giving leopard alarms to various mammals. Classification improves with age (p. 238).
- **The questions.** The abstract is in almost pure Behaviorese. It is just informative enough to be tantalizing. It prompts questions the data do not answer: do the vervets *really* communicate or mean what they say (p. 239)? The cognitivist is tempted to use mentalistic language at face value. His main aim is to chart the cognitive *processes* behind the talents, not to satisfy curiosity about “IQ”. Dennett answers yes to whether everyday belief and desire talk can serve as a suitably rigorous abstract language. The conditions are that we are careful about what we are doing and saying when we use words like *believe* and *want*, and that we understand the assumptions and implications of the strategy we must adopt when we use them (p. 239).
- **Level-changing.** Adopting the intentional stance is one case of a familiar strategy: changing levels of description to gain predictive power or generality. The price is submerged detail, with a danger of trivialization on one side and easy falsification on the other. Calling something *food* ignores the chemistry of nutrition, and few would object (pp. 239–240).
- **Semantic information.** The intentional stance is riskier. It banks on a still imprecise concept of *semantic information*, as against Shannon–Weaver information. Shannon–Weaver theory measures the capacity of channels and storage vehicles and is mute about their contents. Semantic information is a real but abstract commodity. Ordinary belief and desire talk recounts its storage, transmission and transformation informally but, Dennett says, quite surefootedly (p. 240).

*Intuition note.* Dennett offers belief–desire language as a middle-level language. It can describe what an animal can do before anyone knows how its brain does it. The price is that the language rests on a concept (semantic information) that is not yet well defined.

### Intentional System Theory (pp. 240–242)
- **Intentionality** is aboutness: some states point to other things (p. 240). Dennett plucks two points from the literature.
- **Point 1: referential opacity.** The normal substitution rule says that replacing a term by another term with the same referent preserves truth. Example: if Bill is the oldest kid in class, (1) *Mary is sitting next to Bill* and (2) *Mary is sitting next to the oldest kid in class* stand or fall together (p. 241). Intentional idioms block this. Russell: (3) *George IV wondered whether Scott was the author of Waverley* is true. It seems unlikely that (4) *George IV wondered whether Scott was Scott* is. Likewise (5) *Burgess fears that the creature rustling in the bush is a python* does not license (6) *Burgess fears that Robert Seyfarth is a python*, even if the creature is Seyfarth (p. 241). In one sense we do accept such substitutions, but the idioms focus on conceiving, seeing or thinking of *as*. The Mad Strangler neighbour is a further example (p. 242).
- **The mark of mentalism.** The marked idioms include *believes that*, *knows that*, *expects*, *wants*, *recognizes* and *understands*. The mentalistic vocabulary shunned by behaviorists and celebrated by cognitivists is quite well picked out by the opacity test (p. 242).
- **Point 2: rationality.** Dennett calls this point somewhat controversial but widely shared. Using intentional idioms carries a presupposition of rationality in the system to which states are attributed (p. 242).

*Intuition note.* Saying that George IV wondered whether Scott was Scott is odd because he would not think of Scott under that description. Belief sentences are sensitive to how something is picked out. That is why they suit talk about how information is represented in a head (p. 242).

### Vervet Monkeys as Intentional Systems (pp. 242–250)
- **Definition.** To adopt the stance toward vervets is to try tentatively to characterize, predict and explain their behaviour with intentional idioms. An *intentional system* is a thing whose behaviour is predictable by attributing beliefs and desires (and rationality). The hypotheses about *which* beliefs and desires are testable in virtue of the rationality requirement (p. 242).
- **Orders** (p. 243). A first-order system has beliefs and desires with no intentional idioms inside them: (7) x believes that p; (8) y wants that q. A second-order system has attitudes about attitudes: (9) x wants y to believe that x is hungry; (10) x believes y expects x to jump left; (11) x fears that y will discover that x has a food cache. A third-order system can have states such as (12) x wants y to believe that x believes he is alone. Dennett suspects most people can keep track of only about five or six orders at best (Cargile 1970) (p. 243).
- **Why orders matter.** Higher orders intuitively mark more intelligence. Grice and others, notably Bennett, argue that genuine human-style communication needs at least three orders in speaker and audience (p. 243). The presence of specific linguistic tokens seems neither sufficient nor necessary for communication. Grice’s criteria require at minimum states like (13) Utterer *intends* Audience to *recognize* that Utterer *intends* Audience to produce response r (p. 244). The sociobiological literature on reciprocal altruism also speculates about higher-order states. Trivers speculates about a brain-power arms race, and Humphrey reaches similar conclusions by a different route. So there may be several routes to the view that higher-orderedness is a deep mark of intelligence, not just a reliable symptom (p. 244).
- **Caveat reported from critics (parenthetical).** Dennett says the orders are not a uniform scale. He reports that several critics told him the step to second order is the crucial move, since it brings in embedding. Beyond it, complexity plausibly reflects memory or attention limits, and chunking and artificial aids mean there seems to be no interesting difference between fourth and fifth order. He points to Cargile for the natural limits of iteration (pp. 244–245).
- **The test case.** Tom gives a leopard alarm in the presence of Sam. Dennett lists readings from romantic to killjoy (pp. 245–246):
  - *Fourth order:* Tom wants Sam to recognize that Tom wants Sam to believe there is a leopard (variants: a carnivore; a four-legged animal; a live animal bigger than a breadbox) (p. 245).
  - *Third order:* Tom wants Sam to believe that Tom wants Sam to run into the trees. Here the call is an imperative, not a declarative (p. 245).
  - *Second order:* Tom wants Sam to believe that there is a leopard (or that he should run into the trees). There is no recognition by Sam of Tom’s role. If Tom could do as well by growling like a leopard, it would be only second order (pp. 245–246).
  - *First order:* Tom wants to cause Sam to run, and has a noisemaking trick that does so. This is like saying “Boo!”. The perpetrator needs no conception of the victim’s mind (p. 246).
  - *Zero order:* Tom, like other vervets, is prone to leopard, eagle and snake anxiety, each with its own vocalization. The effects on others are just tropism, in both utterer and audience (p. 246).
- **Footnote 1.** Probe the stimulus-equivalence class by swapping in dogs, hyenas, stuffed or green leopards, firecrackers and so on. Whether such tests concern anxiety specificity or the *meaning* of one-word sentences depends on whether the tests for the other components of an nth-order attribution come out positive (p. 246 n. 1).
- **Parsimony.** Lloyd Morgan’s canon enjoins settling on the most killjoy hypothesis that accounts systematically for observed and observable behaviour. The behaviorist creed that low-level curves could be fitted to the data long blocked exploring higher-order readings. That a lowest-order story can in principle always be told is no longer interesting. It is like saying biologists could in principle ignore the concept of food. Today the question is what gains in perspicuity, prediction and generalization a risky higher-level hypothesis might bring (pp. 246–247).
- **An empirical question.** The stance does not replace empirical work with armchair work. It suggests which questions to put to nature. Evidence may demote an animal or promote it (p. 247). Lone male vervets travelling between bands silently take refuge in trees on seeing a leopard. That undercuts the killjoy hypothesis about anxiety yelps. Dennett adds that ad hoc “context switch” modifications can save any hypothesis (p. 247). At the other end, vervets apparently have so few different things to say that there is little prospect of real theoretical utility in a fancy fourth-order hypothesis. He adds that high-powered interpretations are needed only in contexts where one must rule out or in irony, metaphor, storytelling and illustration (“second-intention” uses). The evidence is not yet in, but one would have to be romantic indeed to have high expectations (p. 247).
- **The truce anecdote** (Seyfarth, in conversation; p. 248). A band was losing a territorial skirmish. A losing-side monkey issued a leopard alarm with no leopard present. All vervets took up the cry and fled to the trees. This was possibly (barring killjoy reinterpretation) an incident of great cleverness. If it was no lucky coincidence, it is devious. It is not a mere imperative, because a rational vervet should not expect rivals to honour his imperative. So either the call is *considered* informative, a warning and not a command, and credibility but not authority explains the effect. Or the utterer is more devious still, wanting rivals to think they are overhearing a command meant for his own side. These heights of sophistication are strictly implied by the higher-order interpretation with its inevitable rationality presupposition (p. 248).
- **Playbacks.** Dennett first took the success of hidden-speaker playbacks as a demoting datum, since Gricean monkeys should be perplexed by disembodied calls. But Seyfarth took great care. Vervets recognize individual callers. Sam’s call was played only when the audience could suppose Sam present and hidden (pp. 248–249). Dennett wonders whether such care was necessary. If sloppier scheduling worked just as well, that would be a very important demoting datum. If the monkeys are baffled except under the scrupulous conditions, that would strongly support the claim that Tom does believe the noisemaker is Sam. He adds that this would show vervets are not only capable of believing such things but must believe them for the observed reaction to occur (p. 249).
- **Attenuation and conditionals.** Dennett expects grounds for the verdict that vervets are believers only in some attenuated way compared with humans. The rationality assumption helps to look for signs of attenuation. Frame conditionals like (14): if x believed that p, and if x was rational, then since “p” implies “q”, x would (have to) believe that q. This yields a further belief attribution, which with a plausible desire gives a testable behavioural prediction (p. 249). Footnote 4 notes that (14) states the rationality assumption explicitly, while *if x really believed that p…* uses it silently (p. 249 n. 4).
- **Boy who cried wolf** (suggestion from Susan Carey, p. 250 n. 5). If a third- or fourth-order analysis is right, then by “framing” one vervet as a false alarmer via hidden speakers, others, being rational, should lower trust in him in various ways. Dennett asks whether a credibility gap can be created, and whether the possibly nasty results would be justified (p. 250).

*Intuition note.* The list of readings turns one vague question (does Tom mean it?) into several rival claims. Each predicts different behaviour in different situations. The stance is useful because it makes such predictions available.

### How to Use Anecdotal Evidence: The Sherlock Holmes Method (pp. 250–257)
- **The Catch-22.** Scientific canons rule out anecdotes. But they then virtually admit only oft-repeated, stereotyped behaviour, which reveals no particular intelligence, since it is more or less plausibly explained as instinct, tropism or conditioned response. Novel acts speak of intelligence but are anecdotal. Premack and Woodruff’s attempts to make chimps repeat intelligent behaviour supplied long training histories for behaviorists to cite (p. 250).
- **The way out.** Consider how we establish our own higher-order intentionality. Any single short stretch of human behaviour can get a not obviously ad hoc demoting explanation. But as anecdote piles on anecdote, the claim that it is all coincidence or undetected training becomes the more extravagant hypothesis (p. 251). The intentional stance is an engine for designing anecdotal circumstances (ruses, traps, litmus tests) and predicting outcomes (p. 251).
- **Examples.** Odysseus tests his swineherd. Cherniak’s case from “A Scandal in Bohemia”: Holmes has Watson throw a smoke bomb and yell “fire”. His opponent, Irene Adler, runs to the hidden photograph. Holmes learns its location and confirms an elaborate intentional profile. She wants the photograph, believes it is where she goes, believes the yeller believed there was a fire, and wants to retrieve it unnoticed (p. 251). Mystery writers’ variant: provoking the telltale move. A detective stages a “power failure” and the guilty party, who alone believes an incriminating cuff link is under the table, is found crawling beneath it (pp. 251–252). Footnote 6 uses Hamlet and the arras to show behaviour revealing intentional profiles (p. 252 n. 6).
- **Power over behaviorist methods.** Such stratagems provoke novel but interpretable behaviour under controlled, admissible conditions. Dennett says this gives a significant increase in investigative power over behaviorist methods (p. 252). Quine’s radical translator needs “query-and-assent” with natives. Ethologists cannot play that game, and a vestige of it appears in stimulus-substitution studies for vocalizations, typically to the exclusion of more manipulative experiments. Dennett says that without a goodly amount of such telling behaviour, no mountain of data on stimulus meaning will reveal that utterances are communicative acts rather than merely audible manifestations of peculiar sensitivities. He adds that Quine tacitly presupposes his translator has already satisfied himself of the natives’ communicative behaviour, presumably by the everyday Holmes method (pp. 252–253).
- **It cuts both ways** (pp. 253–254). Failure is often strongly demoting (p. 253). Footnote 7 says Dennett is not concluding that vervets, chimps or any nonhuman animals have already been shown to be higher-order systems (p. 253 n. 7). He uses Woodruff and Premack’s (1979) deception experiment as the case:
  - *Setup.* Sadie sees food placed in one of two closed boxes. A cooperative trainer shares food. A competitive trainer, if he discovers it, takes it all. The competitive trainer always wore sunglasses and a bandit’s mask. Training sessions give the behaviorist grist, and Dennett asks whether the mask then becomes a mere eliciting stimulus (p. 253).
  - *Result and doubts.* Sadie points to the wrong box for the competitive trainer only. Suspicions abound (p. 254). The footnote about the village idiot who always took the nickel warns against stopping too soon in interpreting a “lower” creature (p. 254 n. 8).
  - *Proposed test.* A deceiver must (being rational) start with the belief that the trainer does not know where the food is. So introduce transparent plastic boxes in another context, so the chimps come to know that anyone can see through them. Then run a one-trial novel test: food in a plastic box next to an opaque one, with the competitive trainer looking at the plastic box in Sadie’s view. If Sadie still points to the opaque box, she lacks a grasp of deception (p. 254). Improvements: offer an option that would look better to her only if the first were hopeless. Also ask whether she is puzzled that her trick keeps working and the trainer just sulks (p. 254).
- **Dennett’s predictions** (p. 255). Vervets, chimps, dolphins and other higher nonhuman animals will show mixed and confusing symptoms of higher-order intentionality. They will pass some higher-order tests and fail others. They will in some regards be alert to third-order sophistication while failing to grasp some apparently even simpler second-order points. No crisp, rigorous set of intentional hypotheses of any order will be clearly confirmed. He says the reason is that humans are not unproblematic exemplars of third- to fifth-order systems either. Our language use gives illusions of more definition in our mental representation than we have. He expects gaps and foggy places, as in interpretations of small children, because the systems are imperfectly rational (p. 255).
- **Why the results are still valuable.** An intentional profile is like engineers’ specs for an information-processing competence. It says what information must be receivable, usable, rememberable and transmittable, in terms of discriminations drawn and not of machinery. It sets a design task for the representation-system designer. Dennett notes the same division of labour in AI, where the intentional stance is what Newell calls the knowledge level (pp. 255–256). He adds that gaps and defects point to the shortcuts nature used and make the designer’s job easier (p. 256).
- **Bees and TALESPIN.** A fancy intentional story about bees removing dead sisters (knowing, deciding) would give the designer a huge job. In fact oleic acid from dead bees triggers a “remove it” subroutine. A live bee dabbed with it is dragged out (p. 256). AI shows similar prowess mixed with stupidity. The TALESPIN story program wrote “Gravity drowned” because it treated gravity as an unmentioned agent that is always pulling things down (pp. 256–257).
- **Whole creatures.** Dennett had earlier urged AI to model whole simple animals, even invented ones. He now tentatively thinks actual animals may be more fruitful and, surprisingly, more tractable. He suspects that cooperation between bee and spider people and AI people would be mutually enriching (p. 257).

*Intuition note.* The Holmes method does not make a single odd act count as evidence. The odd act counts when a controlled set-up makes it unlikely without the rational attitudes in question. Failures are informative too, as the Sadie design shows.

### A Broader Biological Perspective on the Intentional Stance (pp. 257–260)
- **Division of labour.** The stance describes cognitive competences in a black-box way that heavily constrains the machinery to go in the box. Field ethologists can frame no positive hypotheses about actual representational machinery; that is someone else’s specialty. Footnotes: some biologists have bridged the gap for relatively simple creatures, and the gap is narrower in nonmammals (p. 257 n. 11). The price is throwing behaviorist scruples to the winds, so Dennett turns to the broader context of biology (p. 258).
- **Distraction display.** Ground-nesting birds feign a broken wing to lure predators from the nest. If this is *really* deception, the bird needs a highly sophisticated representation. Dennett imagines a soliloquy in Dawkins’s style, in which the bird reasons about the predator’s desires and beliefs. He thinks it unlikely in the extreme that any bird is an intentional system of this intelligence. A more realistic soliloquy has the bird feel a tremendous urge to do the dance and wonder why, though even this is, he says, wildly romantic (p. 258). How sensitive the bird’s control system is to relevant variables is empirical. If birds display when a wounded bird or likely prey offers a better target, the behaviour is unmasked as very low order, like the bees’. If some birds show sophistication (distinguishing predators, or appreciating you cannot fool the same predator with the same trick again), the higher-order reading is promoted or even confirmed (pp. 258–259).
- **Free-floating rationales.** Suppose the killjoy reading is right. Would we discard “deception”? Dennett says yes and no. We would no longer credit the individual bird with the rationale, but the rationale won’t go away. Its raison d’être is its deceptive power, which is why it evolved. Who appreciated it, if not the bird or its ancestors? Dennett’s answer: nobody. Evolution by natural selection “chose” the design for this “reason”. He calls this the problem of *free-floating rationales*. If it is no coincidence that the smart behaviour occurred, the rationale passes from the individual to the evolving genotype (p. 259). Moths’ eye spots are a non-behavioural example, since no moth figured out camouflage paint. To say the rationale is *there* is to say there is a domain in which it is predictive and hence explanatory. In a community with bats but not birds as predators we would not expect eye spots (p. 259).
- **Figurative talk taken seriously.** Species have long been said to “learn” or “try out” strategies, and giraffes to stretch their necks. Maynard Smith’s evolutionarily stable strategies depended on taking seriously the resemblance between long-term evolutionary patterns described in intentional terms and short-term interactions of rational agents. This warranted applying the same normative–descriptive calculi, with impressive results (p. 260).

*Intuition note.* A feature can have a point (for example, that a trait works because it fools predators) even when no individual ever represented that point. Dennett says that in this case the explanation about *why the trait exists* belongs to the evolutionary history, not to the animal’s mind.

### The “Panglossian Paradigm” Defended (pp. 260–268)
- **Terms.** The strategy that unites intentional system theory with evolutionary theory is the deliberate adoption of *optimality models*. Both are aspects of adaptationism, described by Gould and Lewontin (1979) as a programme based on faith in the power of natural selection as an optimizing agent (p. 260). Gould and Lewontin call the use of optimality models the Panglossian paradigm, after Voltaire’s Dr. Pangloss, a caricature of Leibniz who could rationalize any calamity (p. 260).
- **Dennett’s reading of Gould and Lewontin.** He says their case has been widely misinterpreted, perhaps because their rhetoric is harsher than their explicit conclusions. Their closing call is for “pluralism” and leaves adaptationism a legitimate place. But their arguments are presented as attacks on its fundamental integrity. Critics of an earlier version of Dennett’s essay called his position adaptationism that Gould and Lewontin had shown to be bankrupt (pp. 260–261). Dennett’s reaction was déjà vu. He sees a replay of Skinner’s campaign against mentalism, though he says he does not suggest Skinner is their conscious inspiration (p. 261).
- **Parallels Dennett lists** (pp. 262–263):
  - *Too easy.* Lewontin says optimality arguments dispense with knowing anything concrete about genetics. Skinner says mentalistic way stations are too easily invented. Gould and Lewontin: new adaptive stories can always be postulated, or temporary ignorance pleaded. Footnote 14 gives E. O. Wilson’s similar remark about sociobiology (p. 262).
  - *Unfalsifiability and diversion.* Both complain of unfalsifiability. Both say easy stories divert attention from hard details: genetic drift, “material compensation”, phyletic inertia and architectural constraints for Gould and Lewontin; reinforcement histories for Skinner. Skinner complains that the world of the mind steals the show (p. 262).
  - *Abuses as targets.* Skinner mocked psychoanalytic “explanations” of what proved to have hormonal or mechanical causes. Gould and Lewontin cite Barash’s “anticuckoldry” account of mountain bluebird aggression (pp. 262–263).
  - *Utility versus origin.* They fault adaptationists for failing to distinguish current utility from reasons of origin. Dennett says this parallels a claim, common but not found by him explicitly in Skinner, that mentalistic interpretation confuses post hoc rationalization with real reasons (p. 263).
  - *Backsliding.* Skinner used mentalistic idioms as shorthand. Gould and Lewontin are subtler and stress the necessity of some adaptationist explanations, so they are sharply disanalogous to Skinner. But they do not seem to Dennett to acknowledge fully their reliance on adaptationist thinking (p. 263).
- **Gould’s engineering criterion** (pp. 263–264). In *Ever since Darwin*, Gould saves Darwinism from vacuous fitness by saying traits confer fitness by an engineer’s criterion of good design, not by their survival. Dennett: we can then rate designs as better or worse given assumptions about conditions and needs, and that, he says, is adaptationism. Is it Panglossian? The usual disclaimer is that nature is a satisficer (Simon), settling for good enough. Dennett replies with the old joke: the optimist says this is the best of all possible worlds, the pessimist sighs and agrees (p. 264).
- **Constraints and optimality.** What looks far from optimal under one set of constraints may be optimal under a larger set. The dismasted sailboat’s jury-rig may be the best the sailors could do, even allowing for their ignorance of aerodynamics. When must we stop adding conditions? He sees no principled limit, but does not think it a vicious regress, because it typically stabilizes after a few moves and the discoveries are potentially illuminating (p. 264). Satisficing itself can often be shown to be the optimal strategy once search costs are added as a constraint (see Nozick). Gould and Lewontin are right to suspect a “tautology machine” in adaptationism. But, Dennett thinks, they are committed to playing on the same stage (p. 264).
- **Skinner’s point conceded.** Skinner is equally right that mentalistic explanations are in principle unfalsifiable. If Joe fails to come to class, a thousand revisions preserve his rationality. Dennett grants that the dangers pointed out by Skinner and by Gould and Lewontin are real. Adaptationists and mentalists risk building edifices out of almost nothing. But it can be wise to take the risk, since the payoff is often high and the task of a more cautious theorist extraordinarily difficult (pp. 264–265).
- **Stances, not theories.** They are strategies for organizing data and generating questions to ask nature, so the charge of question-begging or irrefutability would be fatal if they were theories in the classical mold, but Dennett says making it misreads their point. Dennett cites Oster and Wilson: optimality models are provisional guides, not keys to deeper laws. The vacuity criticism would be truly telling if we always, or even very often, availed ourselves of the available slack. If we revised intentional profiles post hoc whenever people failed us, the practice would be exposed as a sham and would have died out long ago. Likewise if adaptationists were always or often forced to revise their constraint lists post hoc. But, Dennett says, in a nutshell *they work*, not always but gratifyingly often (p. 265).
- **The circle objection** (p. 266). The evidence that all important constraints are located is that the optimizing calculation predicts well. Is that circular? The claim rests on three steps: (1) the optimal design given those constraints is A; (2) Mother Nature optimizes; (3) A is the observed design. Dennett asks what other argument could ever be used to convince us that all relevant considerations in some feature’s ancestry were located. Dennett admits that here one *assumes* Pangloss in order to infer that the list of constraints is complete. Dawkins (1980) says evolutionarily stable strategy theory as a whole is a tool, not a testable true-or-false hypothesis. Maynard Smith: we test hypotheses about constraints, optimization criteria and heredity, not the general proposition that nature optimizes (p. 266).
- **Blindness has a mirror image.** Dobzhansky says the usefulness of a trait must be demonstrated, not taken for granted. Cain replies that uselessness cannot be taken for granted either, and that trivial characters investigated have proved adaptively significant. Cain slyly compares Dobzhansky’s attitude with Hooke’s curiosity (1665) about insect antennae. Hooke could not well imagine their use but felt sure they have some notable office assigned by Nature. Cain concludes that, apparently, the right attitude to enigmatic organs was fully understood by the middle of the seventeenth century, at least in England (pp. 266–267).
- **The “why” question.** Gould asks why cicada cycles are prime-numbered years. Dennett says one has not answered a “why” question while abstemiously setting out the history without an adaptationist gloss. Without it we won’t know why (p. 267). Footnote 17 adds Boden’s case for a “cognitive attitude” in biology (p. 267 n. 17).
- **The Skinnerian analogy.** A devout Skinnerian answered Dennett’s question why he said something by saying that he had been reinforced for saying it in the past. Dennett had asked for a rationale, not provenance. That there is no reason at all is possible, but its plausibility drops near zero as complexity and *apparent* meaningfulness rise (p. 267). When a supportable rationale is found, it is a mistake to insist that “the real reason” must make no allusion to it. A microphysical causal explanation is not in competition with a rationale-giving one. Dennett cites Lewin’s report of the 1980 Chicago conference as a sign that biologists have apparently not yet quite absorbed this. Lewin writes that land vertebrates have four legs because their fish ancestors did, and that the optimal-design answer ignores this (pp. 267–268).
- **Conclusion.** Evolutionists asking “why” seek the rationale for selection. The more complex the feature, the less likely there is *no* sustaining rationale. A nonadaptationist historical story does not *require* the falsehood of all adaptationist stories of the same feature. The complete answer will almost always allude, at least minimally, to better design. We should not try to answer whether this is the best of all possible worlds. But Pangloss’s assumption, especially of rationality in fellow cognizers, can be immensely fruitful if we keep from turning it into a dogma (p. 268).

*Intuition note.* Dennett’s claim is not that nature is perfect. It is that asking what would make a design good is part of how biologists work out what a trait is for. A wrong guess still tells them what was missing from the model (pp. 264–266, 279–280).

### Reflections: Interpreting Monkeys, Theorists, and Genes (pp. 269–285)
Dennett says four years of discussion and fieldwork expanded his perspective. He plans to tie off loose ends, report fieldwork on vervets, review the adaptationism controversy, and draw out implications (p. 269).

### Ancestry and Progeny (pp. 269–272)
- **Origins.** Dennett’s 1976 “Conditions of Personhood” argued that higher-order intentions mark a critical difference between us and other beasts. After discussing the issue with Premack in 1975 and commenting on his 1978 article, he found the March 1981 Dahlem conference on animal minds his first wide-ranging introduction to the field, and there he was invited, or challenged, to show how adopting the stance helps scientists design experiments. His impromptu applications produced testable hypotheses. Participants urged him to write an introduction for non-philosophers, which became the essay, originally in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (BBS) (pp. 269–270).
- **Commentary.** Dennett recommends the commentaries by Bennett, Menzel and Lewontin as the most vehement critics. Skinner and Rachlin give the behaviorist perspective. Dawkins, Eldredge, Ghiselin and Maynard Smith illuminate the evolutionary debate (p. 270).
- **Later developments.** Cheney and Seyfarth ran a good variation of the boy-who-cried-wolf experiment. Vervets have two acoustically very different calls with apparently the same meaning (a rival band approaching). Repeated playback of one individual’s call produces habituation. With two “synonymous” calls they obtained evidence of habituation *not to the sound but to the import* of the call, and speaker-relative habituation. So a vervet can lose credibility on a topic by being “framed” (p. 270). Ristau studied piping plover distraction displays and Marler audience sensitivity in chickens. Byrne and Whiten surveyed primate studies. Wimmer and Perner used a variation on the experiment Dennett had proposed for testing Premack’s chimp Sarah, in order to show the marked onset of beliefs about beliefs in young children (pp. 270–271).
- **A misuse corrected.** Dennett says some researchers mistook the Holmes method for a defence of casually obtained anecdotes. His position: a unique behaviour is useless as evidence for attributing an intentional state, however valuable as a hint, unless it can be *shown* to be an otherwise unlikely behaviour provoked by the conditions that would give a rational agent beliefs and desires making it rational. Showing this always requires controlled experiments. The method was a way of seeing which experiments needed to be done (p. 271).
- **Two senses of “intentionality”.** Ethologists tend to conflate the philosophers’ sense (aboutness, Brentano) with the everyday sense (acting on intentions). Dennett still thinks it important to keep them distinct. Otherwise it is all too easy to think one has eliminated appeals to intentionality by swapping talk of intentions and expectations for talk of information storage and goal structures (p. 271). He once proposed capitalizing the philosophers’ term, but abandoned this. Searle has reinvented the scheme, but Dennett says he utterly disbelieves in what Searle calls Intentionality (p. 272).

### Out of the Armchair (pp. 272–277)
- **Good news and bad news.** In June 1983 Dennett watched Seyfarth and Cheney in Kenya. The bad news: the classical Holmes method has very limited applicability to vervets and, by extrapolation, other “lower” animals. The good news: the stance generates plausible, indirectly testable hypotheses about why, and about the selection pressures that probably shaped vervet communication (p. 272).
- **The MIO grunt.** A monkey in a bush often gives a Moving Into the Open grunt before moving out. Others often repeat it, and spectrographic analysis has not yet revealed a clear difference. If no echo comes, the grunter stays five or ten minutes and repeats. If echoed, he often moves cautiously out (pp. 272–273).
- **Candidate translations** (pp. 273–274):
  - “I’m going” / “I read you”: vervets are tacitum, so what would be the use? (p. 273).
  - Permission request: this would be knocked out if higher-ranking vervets originated MIO before subordinates. They tend to move first, so it seems not a request (p. 273).
  - Command (“Follow me!”): Cheney finds it not very plausible. It goes without saying that low-ranking animals follow superiors. Cheney adds that even a “May I?” vocalization, which one might expect when approaching a dominant for grooming, has no audible sign (p. 273).
  - Shared fear (“I’m really scared”) was a further possibility (p. 273).
  - Coordination (asking whether the other is ready to go) and especially a “Coast clear?” exchange in which the answerer says it is clear and promises cover. The behaviour so far observed is compatible with the last, which would orient the monkeys to cooperative vigilance. Responders watch the leave-taker and look in the right directions (p. 274).
- **Testing the best candidate.** Dennett and Seyfarth considered whether a rival male might give a deliberately misleading MIO response. Evidence would be miniscule, since it would need Originator not seeing, Responder seeing a predator, and Responder seeing that Originator didn’t. They considered a trap with a stuffed python. On reflection the technical problems looked virtually insurmountable: how would they establish the “liar” had seen the “predator” and was not sincerely reporting (p. 274).
- **Dennett’s lesson on talking with the natives.** His repeated fantasy of a vervet suit, a robot or a puppet made him realize there is no substitute in radical translation for talking with the natives. You can test more hypotheses in half an hour of chitchat than in a month of unobtrusive observation. But you must become obtrusive, as when pointing and asking “Gavagai?”. In mystery-story traps the Holmes method often depends on imparting (mis)information verbally. Without words, manoeuvring subjects into the right frame of mind and knowing you have succeeded is arduous at best, and often next to impossible (p. 275).
- **Lab work.** It is often next to impossible in the field to establish that particular monkeys were shielded from particular information. Seyfarth and Cheney are running experiments with captive vervets in California, and others with chimps. The early results are tantalizing but equivocal. Dennett’s hunch is that isolation is such an unusual predicament for vervets that they will prove unprepared by evolution to exploit it (p. 275).
- **Main lesson.** Vervets live in a world where secrets are virtually impossible. They live in the open in close proximity, with no solitary projects of any scope. This contrasts with orangutans and chimps. So it is a rare occasion when a vervet learns something that it alone knows *and knows that it alone knows*. Knowing others’ ignorance, and the possibility of maintaining it, is critical. Dennett says that without such occasions in abundance there is little to impart. Without frequent opportunities to recognize that one knows something others don’t, devious reasons for or against imparting information cannot even exist. He says he can think of no way of describing this simplicity in the vervets’ Umwelt (their lived world) that does not use higher-order intentional idioms, explicitly or implicitly (pp. 275–276).
- **Language.** Vervets could not make use of most features of a human language for their lifestyle. Their communicative needs are few but intense, and opportunities limited. Dennett writes that, if they could not make use of a fancy human-like language, we can be quite sure evolution has not provided them with one. If it had, language would change their world. But without the original information-gradients needed to prime the evolutionary pump, such a language couldn’t get established (p. 276). So the MIO grunt is not crisply translated by any familiar human interchange. Like asking a dog whether it wants to go for a walk, it is a mixture of question, command, exclamation and harbinger. But fanciful translation hypotheses still help uncover informational roles. Thinking of MIO as “Coast clear?” directs attention to testable hypotheses about further relationships and dependencies (pp. 276–277).

*Intuition note.* Dennett says a stance-generated translation need not be exactly right. If the “Coast clear?” reading is even “sort of” right, it suggests what else must be true of the monkeys. Dennett links the vervets’ limited code to their living conditions: they have little to hide or tell.

### The Panglossian Paradigm Reconsidered (pp. 277–283)
- **Central theme.** Problems of interpretation in psychology and in biology are the same problems. Psychologists can’t do their work without the rationality assumption, nor biologists without optimality assumptions, though some in each field are tempted to deny and denounce them. The social sciences are overrun with adoptions of the intentional stance, some much more problem-ridden (p. 277). Dennett lists controversies such as *Verstehen*, false consciousness, methodological individualism, the proper role of idealization in economics, and the principle of charity. He calls all of them problems about justifying particular adoptions of the stance (pp. 277–278).
- **Reply to Ghiselin.** Ghiselin proposes rejecting teleology: ask “What has happened?” and not “What is good?”. Dennett calls this an illusion parallel to Skinner’s claim that asking about the history of reinforcement beats asking what a person believes or wants. One cannot answer historical questions with a “pure” non-interpretational investigation. Without answers to “why”, we cannot categorize what happened into the right parts. Using even *eye*, *leg* or *lung* commits one to assumptions about what is good, as *avoidance* or *recognition* commits the psychologist to assumptions about rationality (p. 278). We take on optimality assumptions not from naive belief in the best of all possible worlds but because we must be interpreters. Dennett quotes Maynard Smith’s commentary: the aim is to *understand* the selective forces that shaped behaviour, not to confirm or refute that animals always optimise (pp. 278–279). Footnote 1 criticizes Kitcher’s question of whether an optimality-backed description is more likely to be true because of that fact alone. Dennett says the question wrongly assumes attribution and justification could be separated (p. 279 n. 1).
- **Same method for “why”.** The folk psychologist asks why John declined the invitation, presuming a good reason in John’s eyes. The adaptationist asks why birds lay four eggs, presuming four is better than two or six, and explores energy, survival probability and food scarcity. Developmental constraints might make alternatives “unthinkable”, but even that discovery would be illuminated by the question (p. 279).
- **Stories ramify.** Gould and Lewontin say one can always contrive a story. Dennett grants that a plausible story is no proof, but says such stories ramify, like lies. Either they lead to falsified predictions elsewhere or they do not. If they ramify without recalcitrance, that tells biologists little. If a prediction fails, the adaptationist asks what was left out. Starting from a naive understanding of the organism’s problem, he works out how it ought to be designed, and finds experiments showing it is not so designed. The adaptationist then asks whether the results point to a more sophisticated understanding instead of concluding “second-rate design” (pp. 279–280).
- **Psychology’s version** (Kahneman and Tversky 1983; pp. 280–281). Subjects who had lost a ten-dollar theatre ticket: more than half said they would not buy another. Subjects who found they had lost a ten-dollar bill at the ticket window: only 12% said the loss would affect their decision to buy a ticket. The two cases are equivalent, in that either way you are ten dollars down if you go. The bias is rationally indefensible if only the costs and benefits mentioned are considered. But Kahneman and Tversky suggest that “bookkeeping costs” of separate mental accounts (losses versus costs of doing business) may give efficiency and lucidity of planning. These may far outweigh occasional losses. Dennett says this suggests something about the internal organization of our cognitive systems. Nothing is proved yet. But the discovery of such a systematic weakness suggests its elimination would be more costly than we might have imagined (pp. 280–281).
- **The “backward analogy” objection.** Dennett reports that several authors have suggested he has the analogy exactly backward. Dahlbom writes that romanticism is the fashion in science, with a trend away from atomism, empiricism, functionalism (adaptationism) and gradualism. He sees the Eldredge–Gould–Lewontin commotion as an instance, and asks why Dennett places Gould and Lewontin with Skinner rather than with Chomsky and Kuhn (p. 281). Amundson contrasts “environmentalist” theories (behaviorist learning theories and selectionist-adaptationist biology) with “structuralist” ones (nativist and cognitive psychology, and evolutionary theories stressing morphological and embryological constraints) (p. 281).
- **Dennett’s reply.** Dahlbom’s pivotal figure is Chomsky, whose extreme nativism is tantamount to denying there is learning, a point Dennett jokes about. Dennett says extreme structuralism shares with extreme behaviorism a premature willingness to stop asking “why” (pp. 281–282). He recalls his earlier advice to canvass nearby processes before distant ones. He then asks, as a question, to whom the structuralists in evolutionary biology can pass the buck for the *Baupläne* (basic body plans) relative to which adaptation only fine-tunes. One can say the biosphere is built that way, but those looking for Enlightenment will always ask one more time why. It is the puritanical distaste for teleological and functional thinking that unites, in this regard, Skinner and Ghiselin, and Lewontin and Chomsky. That leaves Kahneman, Tversky, Dawkins, Maynard Smith and other intentionalists on the other side of the fence (p. 282).
- **Allies and Dennett’s stance.** Kitcher (1985) says Gould and Lewontin distort their point by claiming unfalsifiability is an insuperable obstacle. He holds that successful adaptationist work already presupposes attention to rival possibilities. Mayr (1983) says little is wrong with the adaptationist programme as such, and that Aristotelian “why” questions are legitimate in the study of adaptations, provided one avoids an exclusively atomistic manner (pp. 282–283). Dennett sticks to his guns: (1) adaptationist thinking in biology is precisely as unavoidable, as wise, as fruitful and as risky as mentalist thinking in psychology and cognitive science generally; (2) *proper* adaptationist thinking just *is* adopting a special version of the intentional stance in evolutionary thinking, uncovering the free-floating rationales of designs in nature (p. 283).

### Adaptationism as Retrospective Radical Interpretation (pp. 283–285)
- **Orthodoxy and resistance.** Dennett says the view is less radical than it appears and has been implicit all along in the uncontested triumphs of Darwinian biology (Dawkins, *The Blind Watchmaker*). Resistance comes from various ideologies and phobias. In chapter 8 he says he will show a constellation of philosophers (Searle, Fodor, Dretske, Burge, Kripke) united by antipathy to some of its implications (p. 283).
- **The evidence problem.** Evolutionary explanations are essentially historical narratives (Mayr). Natural selection shows how every feature *can* result from a blind, non-teleological process. Some features (dachshunds’ short legs, tomatoes’ thick skins) result from artificial selection, where the breeders’ goal and design rationale were represented in their minds. Can such special cases be told apart in retrospective analysis? (pp. 283–284).
- **Thought experiment** (from Dennett’s BBS reply; p. 284). Imagine a world where actual hands supplemented the “hidden hand” of natural selection: farsighted, reason-representing bioengineers not limited to domesticated organisms. Would their products be distinguishable from those of agentless Darwinian winnowing, where all rationales are free-floating? They might be (organisms with service manuals attached), but they might not if the engineers concealed their interventions as best they could. Footnote 3 notes NovaGene’s “DNA branding” (p. 284 n. 3).
- **Telltale discontinuities?** Natural selection, lacking foresight, cannot see the wisdom of *reculer pour mieux sauter* (step back in order to jump forward better). Designs not approachable by gradual, stepwise redesign, with each step at least no worse for the gene, would seem to require a foresightful helping hand at some point in their ancestry. But such a saltation might be a mere lucky leap. Dennett asks at what point we dismiss cosmic accident as too improbable and settle for interventionist engineers (p. 285).
- **Conclusion drawn.** These questions *suggest*, but do not prove, that there may be no foolproof marks of natural as opposed to artificial selection. This would not embarrass orthodox evolutionary theory against creationists, he says, since it would be foolhardy to claim the power to read history so finely as to rule out absolutely the earlier presence of rational designers. It may be a wildly implausible fantasy but is a possibility after all (p. 285). Organisms we *know* result from foresighted redesign are known through direct knowledge of recent events. Dennett’s Martian biologists (given a hen, a Pekingese, a barn swallow and a cheetah) might note the hen’s lost broodiness. The text of this unit breaks off while discussing the Pekingese (p. 285).

*Intuition note.* Dennett uses the breeder thought experiment to ask whether a design whose rationale was once represented by someone can be told apart, by retrospective analysis, from a design whose rationales are all free floating. His questions suggest, without proving, that it may not always be possible (pp. 283–285).

## Toy example
*(Ours; not from the text.)* We test two readings of a caller who gives a leopard alarm when shown a leopard model. Reading Z (zero-order tropism): the caller always yelps. Reading A (audience-directed): the caller wants others to believe there is a leopard, so a rational caller who thinks nobody can hear should stay silent. We assume this is what rationality implies here. This mirrors Dennett’s lone-male observation (p. 247).

Run 10 trials alone and 10 trials with a group present (20 trials in all).
- Z predicts 10 calls alone + 10 with a group = 20 calls.
- A predicts 0 calls alone + 10 with a group = 10 calls.

Suppose we observe 2 calls alone and 9 with a group.
- Z’s misses: alone, 10 − 2 = 8 trials where the predicted call did not occur; group, 10 − 9 = 1. Total 9 mismatches of 20.
- A’s misses: alone, 2 unexpected calls; group, 1 missing call. Total 3 mismatches of 20.

A fits better. This is a demotion of Z, not a proof of A. Z’s defender could add a “context switch” (ad hoc modification, p. 247). The 3 leftover misses could mean attenuated rationality (p. 249). The result does not choose among second, third or fourth order (pp. 245–246).

## Concepts
- [[intentional stance]] — deciding to predict and explain a system’s behaviour with intentional idioms like believes and wants, which presupposes its rationality (p. 242)
- [[intentionality]] — aboutness: states that point to other things; Dennett separates this from the everyday sense of acting on intentions (pp. 240, 271)
- [[referential opacity]] — in intentional idioms, substituting a co-referring term can turn truth into falsehood; picks out mentalistic vocabulary (pp. 240–242)
- [[propositional attitude]] — attitudes like believes that p, wants that q, expressed with intentional idioms (p. 243)
- [[recursive mindreading / orders of intentionality]] — first-order systems lack attitudes about attitudes; higher orders embed them; Dennett suspects most people can keep track of only about five or six orders (pp. 243–246)
- [[rational-agent assumption / principle of efficiency]] — attributing intentional states presupposes rationality, used to derive testable predictions as in conditional (14) (pp. 242, 249)
- [[folk psychology]] — the everyday attribution of beliefs and desires, used in explaining behaviour, e.g. why John declined an invitation (p. 279)
- [[theory of mind / mentalizing / mindreading]] — attributing mental states; appears in Premack and Woodruff’s question whether chimps have a theory of mind (pp. 250, 269)
- [[second-order false belief]] — mentioned only as beliefs about beliefs; Wimmer and Perner used a variation of Dennett’s test to show their onset in young children (p. 271)
- [[radical-translation]] (new) — Quine’s imagined task of interpreting a wholly alien language; Dennett recasts it for animals (pp. 238, 275)
- [[sherlock-holmes-method]] (new) — designing ruses, traps and litmus tests to provoke interpretable behaviour; Dennett insists on controlled experiments (pp. 251, 271)
- [[semantic-information]] (new) — information as contents, as against Shannon–Weaver capacity (p. 240)
- [[free-floating-rationales]] (new) — reasons a design has that no individual represents; evolution “chose” it for them (p. 259)
- [[adaptationism]] (new) — strategy of explaining traits by asking what selection favoured; uses optimality models (p. 260)
- [[satisficing]] (new) — Simon’s idea that nature settles for good enough; Dennett says it can often be shown optimal when search costs are added (p. 264)
- [[lloyd-morgan-canon]] (new) — settle on the most killjoy hypothesis that accounts systematically for the behaviour (p. 246)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dennett’s rationality assumption for predicting action ↔ theory-of-mind modelling: inferring beliefs and desires by assuming rational action — both infer attitudes from behaviour plus rationality.
- [structural-analogy] Grice’s three orders (13) ↔ Rational Speech Act: nested speaker and listener reasoning — both embed attitudes about attitudes.
- [structural-analogy] Wimmer and Perner’s use of a variation of Dennett’s experiment (p. 271) ↔ false-belief tasks: probing beliefs about beliefs in children.
- [structural-analogy] Transparent versus opaque box test for Sadie (p. 254) ↔ knowledge-versus-ignorance attribution: does the agent track who can see what?
- [structural-analogy] Behaviorist demoting explanations ↔ Clever Hans effect: a simple cue story rivals a mind-reading story.
- [metaphor] Intentional profile as spec for a designer ↔ machine theory of mind: a behavioural-level description constraining implementation.

## Questions this raises
- If a unique behaviour counts as evidence only when shown to be provoked by conditions that would make it rational (p. 271), how much evidence is enough to promote a creature to a higher order?
- Dennett claims the intentional stance is not a theory in the classical mould (p. 265). What would count as it failing, if not repeated post hoc rescue (p. 265)?
- How does the claim that vervet life offers few occasions of private knowledge (pp. 275–276) fit with later lab tests of what individual animals know about others’ ignorance?
- Can free-floating rationales (p. 259) be distinguished from rationales that were once represented by breeders or engineers (pp. 284–285)?
