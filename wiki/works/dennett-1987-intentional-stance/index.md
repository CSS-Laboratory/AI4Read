# Dennett, D. C. (1987). *The Intentional Stance*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (A Bradford Book), xi + 388 pp.

## In one paragraph
Dennett argues that having beliefs and desires means being a system whose behaviour is reliably predicted by treating it as a rational agent. This is the "intentional stance", which he contrasts with the physical and design stances. Belief is a real but abstract pattern in behaviour, more like a center of gravity than an inner sentence. Folk psychology works because we are mostly rational, and he keeps it separate from the sub-personal science of how brains do the job. He doubts that propositions and a "language of thought" are the right units. He also holds that meaning is derived, in us as in artifacts, so there is no deeper fact about what a thought "really" means. The book is the root of rational-agent models of mind, and it bears on when it pays to treat a machine as if it believes.

## The path through the work
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch01-setting]] — Dennett starts his science of the mind from the third-person, materialist view, treats everyday "folk psychology" as a useful but fallible system like folk physics, and says the intentional stance explains why it works (pp. 5, 11).
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch02-true]] — Belief is an objective pattern in behaviour that shows up only when you treat a system as a rational agent (the intentional stance), and the success of that prediction is what makes a system a believer.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch03-three]] — Dennett suggests that folk belief–desire psychology might best be viewed as an abstract, idealizing, rationality-based calculus; the part worth keeping reduces, conceptually, to an intentional system theory that he keeps separate from the sub-personal science of how brains realize it.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch04-making]] — Dennett argues that even simple mistakes resist a complete, unqualified belief-and-desire description, and that human belief is like "froggy" belief all the way up, not a contrasting literal kind.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch05-beyond-p1]] — Dennett explores, without offering a finished theory, how to keep a useful concept of belief when the classical "proposition" is in trouble, and floats a theorist's fictional "notional world" as a tentative, exploratory candidate (pp. 118, 152–153, 173).
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch05-beyond-p2]] — Dennett argues that the philosophers' de re / de dicto split, as usually drawn, does not mark a special kind of belief, and that a realistic psychology should work with notional worlds and treat propositions only as rough, indirect measures of information.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch06-styles]] — Dennett argues that mental representation comes in several styles (explicit, implicit, tacit), that cognitive science has so far offered almost no empirical evidence that the mind's inner states are explicit, sentence-like items, and that belief-like states may not be sentences in a language of thought.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch07-intentional]] — Dennett argues that describing animals by beliefs and desires, and describing organisms as well designed, are the same kind of risky, rationality-assuming interpretive strategy. He holds that both are defensible because they work and generate empirical questions to put to nature.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch08-evolution]] — Dennett argues that if our minds are products of natural selection, our intentionality (the "aboutness" of our mental states) is *derived*, like a vending machine's, so there is no deeper fact fixing what our thoughts "really" mean; this rejects the doctrine of "original intentionality".
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch09-fast]] — Dennett sets beside Searle's claim that no program can suffice for a mind a different, coherent empirical claim, that perhaps only organic brains are fast enough to run the right program in real time, and argues that Searle's real concern is consciousness.
- [[dennett-1987-intentional-stance/ch10-mid]] — Dennett suggests that many of the big quarrels among Quine, Sellars, Chisholm, Putnam, Davidson, Bennett, Fodor, Stich and himself over the status of intentional attributions are amplified minor differences, because just about everyone accepts Brentano's irreducibility thesis (intentional talk cannot be reduced to physical talk) and agrees, with different emphases, that such attribution is not purely descriptive.

## Big ideas across units
- **The intentional stance as a prediction strategy.** Treating a system as a rational believer and wanter is one stance among others (physical, design). It is risky but fruitful, and it is tested through its own rationality assumption. It is introduced as the explanation of folk psychology's success and applied to animals and to evolution. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch07, ch10.
- **Belief as a real pattern, not an inner sentence.** Beliefs are abstracta (like centers of gravity) rather than posited inner states. This gives a "milder" realism, with instrumentalism about attributions and realism about brains. Units: ch02, ch03, ch04, ch10.
- **Rationality as the interpretive lever.** Attribute what a system *ought* to believe and want, then revise downward. This idealised norm is defended against Stich's "what would I believe?" method. It lets ethologists test rival readings of vervet calls, and it appears as the Normative versus Projective principles. Units: ch02, ch03, ch04, ch07, ch10.
- **Gaps and indeterminacy.** Imperfect rationality, errors and function-ascription can leave no fact of the matter. This follows Quine's indeterminacy and Brentano's irreducibility thesis. Units: ch02, ch04, ch08, ch10.
- **Doubt about the language of thought.** Dennett treats it as a plausible but unproven hypothesis. The empirical evidence is only at the semantic level. Explicit, implicit and tacit representation are separated, and connectionism is a possible alternative. Units: ch02, ch04, ch05-p1, ch06.
- **Propositions in trouble.** The Fregean demands cannot all be met, and the de re / de dicto split marks no real kind of belief. Dennett turns instead to theorist-invented notional worlds and to propositions as rough, dollar-like measures of information. Units: ch04, ch05-p1, ch05-p2.
- **Folk psychology versus sub-personal science.** Intentional system theory is a competence theory, neutral on implementation. How brains realise it is a separate, sub-personal question about syntactic engines approximating semantic ones. Units: ch01, ch03, ch06.
- **Derived intentionality all the way down.** Meaning is not original in humans, because natural selection is a designer without foresight. Free-floating rationales and function-reading replace a deeper fact about meaning, and the Chinese Room is recast as a worry about consciousness. Units: ch07, ch08, ch09.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch02 (the core argument) and ch03 (folk psychology as calculus; the split into intentional system theory and sub-personal psychology). These are the catalog's focus chapters. ch01 is a short orientation and sets the project and the folk-physics comparison.
- **Depends on ch02–ch03:** ch04 assumes the intentional stance, the design and physical stances, and "propositional attitude". ch06 and ch07 return to the language of thought and opacity. ch08 assumes the intentional stance and natural selection.
- **Technical block:** ch05-p1 and ch05-p2 are one chapter in two parts. ch05-p2 needs the Twin Earth and Fregean material from ch05-p1, and it also uses predicate logic. It can be skimmed on a first pass if you only want the main line, with its upshot taken as notional worlds plus propositions as rough measures.
- **Optional on a first pass:** ch09 (the Searle debate, assuming you know the Chinese Room) and ch10 (a survey comparing nine philosophers; terms are defined as they appear). ch10 works best as a recap after the other chapters. ch07 is useful for animal applications and for the link to adaptationism.
