# Making Sense of Ourselves

## L0 — One line
Dennett argues that even simple mistakes resist a complete, unqualified belief-and-desire description, and that human belief is like "froggy" belief all the way up, not a contrasting literal kind.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Stich objects that Dennett's rationality-based account of belief cannot describe ordinary errors, such as a lemonade seller giving the wrong change. Dennett replies that any total belief-story for such an error ends in a gap or a caveat. He holds that "rational" is a general-purpose, pre-theoretical term of cognitive approval. He thinks Stich's "what would I believe?" method still relies on an idealised standard. The reflections add that frogs and humans are both intentional systems, and that the contrast between literal and metaphorical belief misdescribes their differences. The apparent literal precision of human belief comes partly from our being "bathed in words". Realists and eliminativists both accept a contrast Dennett rejects.

**Prerequisites:** the intentional stance and design/physical stances (earlier chapters); "propositional attitude" (a mental state with a sentence-like content, e.g. believing *that p*); basic idea of logical consistency. Terms used above: an *intentional system* is one we predict by treating it as having beliefs and desires; *pre-theoretical* means used before, and independently of, any formal theory; *Realists* hold that humans really have beliefs with precisely specifiable contents; *eliminativists* hold that there are no beliefs.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Errors strain belief-description.** Intentional interpretation tries to make sense of acts. Dennett claims (as a challenge, not a proof) that the transition into a mistake does not fully make sense, so no total belief-set fits it without caveats such as "sort of" believes; he allows exceptions with a "peripheral" etiology (pp. 85–88, 103).
- **Cases of "irrationality" are contested.** Dennett says it is "at least not obvious" that there are cases of systematic irrationality. Because they are controversial, they fit his view (p. 87).
- **Folk-psychological episodes are not a neutral base.** "Slipped my mind", "noticed" and "overlooked" are often applied on general principles, not introspection. Dennett suggests we are largely "inveterate auto-psychologists" who confabulate and self-justify (pp. 88–91).
- **Content ascription to inner mechanisms needs the same holistic interpretation.** Dennett claims that Stich's "norm-free" content-ascription method depends on his own method and does not replace it (pp. 91–93).
- **Rationality is a pre-theoretical term of cognitive approval.** It is not deductive closure, perfect consistency, or "whatever evolution gave us". Formal theories criticise and refine it, but they are not its final benchmark (pp. 94–99).
- **Dennett argues that Stich's "what would I believe?" method does not escape the idealised standard.** Measuring against ourselves, he says, is measuring against an idealised standard. Dennett cannot show the two methods differ in results, but does not see why they could not (pp. 99–101).
- **No best complete story of the lemonade seller's mistake.** Dennett claims there is none. He compares the opposing assumption with the illusion that there must be a best translation manual, and says it seems to him easier to sustain the indeterminacy claim for cognitive errors than for Quine's translation case, which needs a "knife-edge" (pp. 103–105).
- **Dennett rejects a literal-versus-metaphorical contrast between human and frog belief.** He calls humans the most prodigious intentional systems on the planet. We are bathed in words as well as information. That may make our beliefs look as finely individuated as sentences. He says the idea that brain representations are sentence-like needs independent support (pp. 106–115).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The chapter has no numbered sections. I follow its order: the main reply to Stich (pp. 83–101), then the "Reflections" with three headed parts (pp. 103–115).

### Part I. Reply to Stich: errors (pp. 83–88)

**Stich's objection.** In Stich's paper, Dennett's refinement of folk belief via the intentional system would "leave us unable to say a great deal that we now wish to say about ourselves" (p. 83). Stich's examples are three simple cognitive failures: the forgetful astronaut, the lemonade-stand boy who gives wrong change, and the man who miscalculates his bank balance (p. 83). Dennett notes that these are not "familiar irrationality" or "inferential failings". Stich cites better cases elsewhere (Wason and Johnson-Laird; Nisbett and Ross) (pp. 83–84). Dennett grants that they are suboptimal behaviour his view is not supposed to handle (p. 84).

**Dennett's claim.** Errors, as malfunctions or outcomes of misdesign, cannot be *predicted* from the intentional stance. He adds that there will be an instability in merely *describing* them at the level of the agent's beliefs and desires (p. 84).

**The lemonade case, in detail (pp. 84–86).**
- Sign: "LEMONADE—12 cents a glass". I hand over a quarter and get lemonade, a dime and a penny (p. 84).
- Once the error is pointed out we expect surprise, blushing, a slap on the forehead, an apology and two cents more (p. 84).
- Each reaction is explained by attributed states. Surprise comes from the belief that he gave the right change. Blushing comes from the desire not to cheat. The slap comes from the beliefs that 25 − 12 = 13, that this is obvious, and that no one his age should err (p. 84).
- The particular error is unpredictable, perhaps only actuarially expected. After the mistake we can resume the intentional interpretation with the usual risk (pp. 84–85).

**"But exactly which mistake?"** Dennett lists what the boy plausibly believes (p. 85):
1. he has given the right change;
2. I gave him a quarter;
3. lemonade costs 12 cents;
4. a quarter is 25 cents;
5. a dime is 10 cents;
6. a penny is 1 cent;
7. he gave me a dime and a penny;
8. 25 − 12 = 13;
9. 10 + 1 = 11;
10. 11 ≠ 13.

Only (1) is false. How can he believe it while believing all the rest?
- It is not plausible that he *mis-inferred* (1) from the others (p. 85).
- He might infer (11) "he gave me 11 cents change" from (9) and (7). Inferring (1) from (11) would make sense only under the misapprehension (12) "11 cents is the right change from a quarter". We would expect that if he believed (13) "25 − 12 = 11" (p. 85).
- If the story simply gives him (13) instead of (8), we get a perfectly rational thinker with one false belief. That is not a case of miscalculation or irrationality. Dennett says Stich rightly does not want this case, since Dennett allows mere false belief with special stories about its acquisition (pp. 85–86).
- If we attribute (13) *and keep* (8), he believes 25 − 12 = 13, 25 − 12 = 11 and 13 ≠ 11. That is blatant, incredible irrationality. "Something has to give" (p. 86).
- Stich's proposal is that the boy is "not yet very good at doing sums in his head." Taken with the inconsistent attributions, this leaves brute irrationality. Taken to temper or withdraw the attributions, it agrees with Dennett: even the simplest errors need scare-quotes or caveats on the total attribution set (p. 86).

**The demand for an inference is obtuse.**
- Demanding an inference, even a mis-inference, to (1) demands a practice or tendency with a rationale. Mere succession in time, or regular causation, does not make an inference (p. 86).
- Inferences are passages of thought for which there is a reason, "but people don't make mistakes for reasons" (p. 86). Demanding reasons for mistakes breeds spurious belief structures like (11)–(13). Accepting reasonless belief is no better (p. 86).
- The belief (1) is not baseless. He would not have believed it if his hand were empty or full of quarters. It rests on a distorted or mistaken perception of what he hands over (pp. 86–87).
- **Conclusion:** the boy is "on top of the situation", but we must descend from beliefs and desires to another level of theory to describe his mistake. No belief-and-desire account will make sense completely. We must cope with "the sheer senselessness of the transition in any error" (p. 87). Dennett offers one example as a challenge, not a general proof (p. 87).

*Intuition note.* Try writing down everything the boy believes. Either he believes a contradiction, which is hard to credit, or he has one odd false belief, which is not a "mistake" story. The gap shows up at the transition into the error.

**Slips versus allegiance to bad procedures (pp. 87–88).**
- Such mistakes are slips in good procedures. The blushing acknowledgement partly confirms the working hypothesis that the boy is fundamentally rational. He corrects the error and does not defend it (p. 87).
- In Stich's putative cases of genuine irrationality, people persist and defend their practice, and have philosophical defenders (Cohen 1981). Dennett says it is "at least *not obvious*" that there are any cases of systematically irrational behaviour (p. 87).
- The cases are all controversial, "which is just what my view predicts". This does not mean we are always rational. It means that when we are not, the cases defy description in ordinary belief-desire terms (p. 87).
- Fall-backs are available: the agent "sort of" believes, or believes "for all practical purposes", or holds a falsehood that makes the act rational. They are themselves subject to the usual tests, so finding one does not confirm it. If no saving interpretation exists, no interpretation is settled on (pp. 87–88).

### Part II. Forgetting, noticing, and the folk-psychological "arena" (pp. 88–93)

**"Just forgot."**
- Saying the seller "just forgot" or "overlooked" some facts looks like a modest psychological hypothesis (information stored safely, address misplaced). It may be supported by a later detailed theory (Cherniak 1983, 1986; Thomason 1986). For now, Dennett says, we make it "simply on the basis of our abhorrence of the vacuum of contradiction" (p. 88).
- **Tennis example (first person).** I am reminded at breakfast of tennis with Paul. At 12:45 I am finishing dessert when Paul appears in tennis gear. I say "It completely slipped my mind!" (p. 88).
- Dennett's analysis: I say this not on introspective evidence. I may have thought of the date many times. I say it on *general principles*: if I end up having lunch as usual, the date must have slipped my mind at the last minute. I think a primary motive is to banish the possibility that I am starkly irrational, believing both that I play tennis and that I am free to lunch (pp. 88–89).
- There is no direct link between conscious thought and what we say has slipped our mind (p. 89). If I cannot recall an appointment but it keeps me from making a conflicting one, it has and has not slipped my mind. At the intersection I turn right to the tennis court without a conscious thought about the date, so it had not slipped my mind (p. 89).
- **Hot pan.** I say "Be careful of this pan, it is very hot" and burn myself on it. We would say "You didn't think what you were saying!", meaning that had I really believed it I couldn't have done what I did. I could likewise not think what I was thinking (p. 89).
- This is not the forgetting of a taxi number or the date of Hume's birth, where the information is gone for good. Saying "I completely forgot" does not mean I completely forgot it (pp. 89–90).

**"Notice", "overlook", "ignore", "conclude" (p. 90).**
- These look like introspective reports. Dennett argues "ignorings" and "overlookings" are reported only retrospectively, in self-justifying or self-critical moods. The chess player who won says "I ignored the queen-side pawns"; had he lost he would say "I simply overlooked" them (p. 90).
- "Did you notice Joe evading your questions?" I may answer yes with no conscious thought about it, because my reactions took appropriate account of it. Since I did the appropriate thing, "I must have noticed, mustn't I?" — this is how we reason when we apply the word (p. 90).
- You, the reader, concluded that I missed my tennis date from "polishing off dessert", probably without a conscious "he must have had lunch". The boy likewise probably did not consciously think the eleven cents was the right change (p. 90).
- **Imagined objector:** "if he didn't consciously think it, he unconsciously thought it", positing an unconscious controlling thought that explains or even *is* his belief (p. 90).

**Do not model academic psychology on the folk "illata" (pp. 90–91).**
- Tempting view: retreating from irrationality takes us to the folk arena of thinkings, concludings and forgettings, as concrete "clockable" episodes that psychologists can model and test (pp. 90–91).
- The examples suggest, "though they do not by any means prove", that we should not model serious psychology too closely on these putative *illata* (the folk theory's postulated entities) (p. 91).
- We postulate such processes to make sense of behaviour, especially our own. Evidence from psychopathology and normal foibles (Nisbett and Wilson 1977) makes it more plausible that each of us is "in most regards a sort of inveterate auto-psychologist", inventing interpretations in a mix of confabulation, retrospective self-justification and occasionally good theorising (p. 91). Some privilege may remain in small corners (p. 91).
- Confabulation under hypnosis, in Korsakoff's syndrome, in split brains and in agnosias suggests these displays are "a normal way unmasked" and not a skill learned after trauma (p. 91).
- So folk mental activities are not a neutral field to which we can resort when normative demands hit irrationality. Their counterparts in cognitive psychology, or their "realizations" in the brain, will not fare better (p. 91).

**Stich's norm-free vision versus Dennett's holism (pp. 91–93).**
- Stich envisions a naturalised, norm-free psychology that settles indeterminacies by real, causally potent states whose content is identified independently of ideal rationality (pp. 91–92).
- Dennett: interpreting inner, sub-personal machinery depends on the *whole person's* beliefs and desires, just as interpreting a public utterance depends on the utterer's. Stich's method "depends on mine and is not an alternative, independent method" (p. 92).
- **Jones example.** A mechanism in Jones reliably produces "It is raining" when it rains nearby and he is asked. Have we found the mechanism subserving his belief? Maybe. It depends on whether Jones believes it is raining when, and only when, the mechanism is on. It might be a senseless mechanism, like the "assent-inducing tumor" Dennett imagined elsewhere (p. 92).
- We need a standard: the system of *abstracta* that fixes belief and desire by a hermeneutical process telling "the best, most rational story". If Jones passes the right tests of understanding, we may find confirmation of our hypothesis. Where he falls short, we *thereby* diminish our grounds for ascribing belief content to the mechanism (p. 92).
- Whatever illata we find, we interpret them by holistic attribution. We may not find structures lining up belief-by-belief with our catalogue (p. 93).
- **Contrast.** Stich and Fodor would take such an outcome (all grant it is possible) as showing "there were no such things as beliefs after all". Dennett takes it, as very likely, to show that the brain's concrete representation systems are "not sentential in character" (chapter 5) (p. 93).
- Sentences in our heads need interpretation just like public ones. Thinking "Now is the time for violent revolution!" does not settle whether I thought that thought. It depends on what I believed and intended (p. 93). Even if "cerebroscopes" showed the boy internally tokening "this is the right change", that would not settle the interpretation, and "there is no unqualified catalogue of his intentional states and acts of the moment" (p. 93).
- **Conclusion:** the problems are really there in folk practice, though they lurk behind confabulations and excuses. The phenomena can be coherently described from the design or physical stance, which Stich and Dennett agree on. So Dennett finds no truths of folk theory he must forswear (p. 93).

### Part III. What is rationality? (pp. 94–99)

**Hard, soft, flexible lines (p. 94).** Stich's "hard line": the idealising rationality assumption is really found in folk practice. His "soft line": fiddle with the ideal to fit folk practice, which does not really invoke rationality. Dennett takes a "flexible line". Rationality is in folk practice, and it is not what some theorists think, so the idealisation needs fiddling (p. 94).

**Stich's dilemma (p. 94).** If rationality is logical consistency and deductive closure (plus game theory, probability), absurdities follow (Oscar the engineer). If it is "whatever evolution has provided", we get tautology or counterexamples. Dennett's reply: "What then do I say rationality is? I don't say." He admits ten years of hedging and says he will give reasons for resisting a declaration while still insisting on the role of the rationality assumption (p. 94).

**What rationality is not.**
- *Not deductive closure.* Deductive closure is the logically guaranteed resting point of the demand to believe obvious consequences of one's beliefs. Oscar shows what is wrong with letting entailment expand an agent's beliefs (pp. 94–95). Powers (1978) develops a theory of knowledge acquisition by deduction and must talk of "pseudo-believes". Stich has his own term, "sub-doxastic states" (p. 95).
- *Not perfect consistency.* Discovering a contradiction is rightly an alarm. Making root-and-branch elimination of inconsistency the cognizer's pre-eminent goal would swamp the system in bookkeeping and search (Cherniak 1986; Darmstadter 1971) (p. 95).
- **Self-objection:** who said anything about inconsistency of *beliefs*? When we discuss the wise design of cognitive structures we have left belief proper behind and discuss structurally identified features with more-or-less apt intentional labels (p. 95).

**Formal theories as tools.** Turning to evolutionary considerations, Stich suggests that established theories (deductive and inductive logic, decision theory, game theory) will be "of no help" in assessing what an organism "ought to believe". Dennett says this is not true. One who no longer treats them as the final benchmark can still use them to criticise and reformulate strategies, as one uses a dictionary or grammar book (with defeasible authority) to criticise spelling or grammar. One can also reject their advice (pp. 95–96).

**Evolution (p. 96).**
- Dennett does not define rationality as what evolution gave us. He claims that if an organism is a product of natural selection, we can assume most of its beliefs are true and most of its belief-forming strategies rational (p. 96).
- Stich objects that selection favours only selective advantage, and false beliefs sometimes pay. Dennett agrees there are rare describable circumstances, such as "better safe than sorry", but this does not rebut his claim (p. 96).
- A strategy that is "patently invalid" yet works most of the time is *irrational* only if one clings to the ideals of Intro Logic. Simon holds that it is rational to *satisfice*, jumping to possibly invalid conclusions when further calculation costs more than a wrong answer (pp. 96–97).

**Normative theory versus useful practice.** Stich says that, so long as we distinguish a normative theory of inference from a set of inferential practices that generally get the right (or selectively useful) answer in the right environment, the two need not, and generally do not, coincide (Stich's own pp. 53–54, quoted on p. 97). Dennett finds this puzzling, because there are normative theories for different purposes, including "generally getting the right answer". Deductive logic might say "sit tight and infer nothing". That is bad advice for a creature in a busy world and fine if avoiding falsehood at all costs is the goal. Rationality consists partly in a good sense of when to rely on what (p. 97). Few "rational animals" ever used formal techniques self-consciously (p. 97).

**Slippery concept.**
- A system whose normal, designed operation is impeccable but which suffers occasional malfunctions would be improperly called irrational. Whether a slow failsafe system or a fast one that is 90 percent malfunction-free is more rational depends on the application (p. 97).
- Dennett uses "rational" as a general-purpose term of cognitive approval, with only conditional, revisable allegiances to proposed methods. Dennett asks what Anderson and Belnap (1974) could be appealing to, if not an assumedly shared rationality for which it is an open question which formal system captures it (pp. 97–98).
- Cherniak (1983, p. 23) says memory compartmentalisation departs from rationality *prima facie* but is advisable overall (p. 98). Dennett's gloss: it is rational to be inconsistent sometimes. That is not the pseudo-paradox that it is rational sometimes to be irrational (p. 98).
- Rationality is "systematically pre-theoretical". One may decline to identify it with any formal system and still appeal to it, without shirking explicitness (p. 98).

**Factunorm Principle.** Appeals to rationality rely on shared intuitions about what makes sense. Wertheimer (1974, quoted p. 98): what and how we do think is evidence for the principles of how we ought to think, and we accept this whenever we try to determine what we ought to think (p. 98).

*Intuition note.* Dennett refuses to define rationality by one formal system. He treats it as a standard we already use, which logic and decision theory can sharpen or correct. That keeps the assumption useful without making it a rigid test that humans fail.

### Part IV. Stich's alternative: projecting ourselves (pp. 98–101)

- Dennett says it may seem he is "backing into" Stich's view: we attribute states by projecting ourselves, asking "what would I believe if I were in its place?" and not "what ought this creature believe?" (Dennett suggested the name *ideological solipsism*) (pp. 98–99).
- Stich says idealised rationality plays "no role at all". Dennett replies that measuring "against ourselves" *is* measuring against an idealised standard, since we take ourselves to approximate rationality. Stich himself says this explains why intentional description falters under egregious irrationality (p. 99).
- The two methods will typically give very close results. Is there room for them to diverge in special cases? (p. 99)
- **Cognitive dissonance.** Dennett floats a story on which Stich, aware of his own tendency to affirm the consequent, imputes it to others, and calls it an "unlikely story". A better story is that Stich has learned about cognitive dissonance and now expects people to favour a self-justifying belief over a better-supported uncomfortable one (p. 99). This is a fine example of an empirical discovery that can tune the intentional stance. But is it not an empirical question, Dennett asks, whether all people respond so? If Stich builds this (apparently) suboptimal tendency into his method of attribution, he forgoes discovering people immune to it (pp. 99–100).
- **Jones's addition.** After three months of building an ugly addition, Jones might come to believe it was a cheap way to learn carpentry, or that its boldness distinguishes his house. Which delusion he holds is determined by what he says and does, asking which beliefs and desires would make his acts rational. The delusion must be surrounded by plausible support, generated on the counterfactual assumption that it is rationally held. Even where attributed beliefs are suboptimal, "the test of rational coherence is the preponderant measure" (p. 100).
- Dennett cannot show the two methods yield different results, but does not see that they could not. He is "not clear enough about just what Stich is asserting" (p. 100).
- **Simulation worry.** An interesting idea in Stich's view is that we use "ourselves as analog computers": I put myself in your frame of mind and see what I think. But the state is make-believe belief. Imagining I am a suspension bridge gives results only as good as my knowledge of bridge engineering. So, he argues, knowledge organised into something rather like a theory is needed in both cases (pp. 100–101).
- Even if we did interpret by simulation and self-observation, this would not show the guiding question is "what would I believe?" and not "what ought he to believe?". A wary attributer might use empathy to *generate* candidates and then test them against his theory of the other. Even imagined self-attribution (three weeks to live) uses both simulation and reflection on what kind of person one is (p. 101).
- **Frog rejoinder.** Stich ends with rhetorical questions about what a frog *ought to* believe. Dennett grants they are answerable only problematically, but sees no embarrassment. His own rhetorical question: is the exact content of what a frog *does* believe any more determinable? (p. 101)

### Reflections: When Frogs (and Others) Make Mistakes (pp. 103–115)

#### The Lemonade Seller's Mistake (pp. 103–105)

- **Dennett's claim.** Unless the mistake has a "peripheral" etiology (he misperceived the coins, was misinformed about their value, or about arithmetic), there will be a "surd spot" in the intentional tale. On his view "there is no saying what someone actually believes whenever he makes a cognitive mistake" (p. 103).
- **Stich's reply.** Every mistake is some particular mistake, so there seems always to be a best complete belief story, even if we cannot find it. A complete story assigns truth or falsity to every relevant attribution, like a cross-examination (p. 103).
- **Dennett:** this is an illusion, close kin to the illusion, first pointed out by Quine, that there must be a best translation manual between any two languages. Quine could give no realistic example of indeterminacy because it needs a knife-edge: two manuals where *all* relevant information fails to favour either. "The actual world abhors knife-edges even more than vacuums" (p. 104).
- He thinks the claim is easier to sustain for cognitive errors (cf. Wheeler 1986). Details do mount up for one story of the mistake, but they shed no uncontroversial light on *what the person believed* (p. 104).
- **Imagined completion.** Suppose conclusive laboratory tests show that once "warmed up" he makes change semi-automatically, while thinking of other things. Suppose a change-making "module" is located in his brain and NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance imaging) shows it was operating, and that 350 ms before it finished, a girl's smile in a passing car caught his eye, and the penny-fetching submodule exited after one penny instead of three. Visual and tactile feedback was received but ignored, because of the attention shift (caused by my slurping) and the module's report of success (p. 104).
- Did he believe he gave a dime and a penny? He saw what he was doing, had no hallucination, and would have said "a dime and a penny" if asked. The only power such a belief lacks, we may suppose, is the power to *initiate* a correction (pp. 104–105).
- **Imagined dialogue (objector in quotes, Dennett's counter).** "He had the belief but didn't attend to it." "He also believed he gave thirteen cents, but didn't attend to that." Could he have attended to both and not noticed the contradiction? "No; if he really understands them he must recognize their incompatibility." Dennett asks: then isn't it just as constitutive of belief that one recognises when one is being contradicted? Beliefs must raise alarms when contradictory propositions are accepted, so, on that standard, he must not *really* believe both (p. 105).
- The debate leaves sub-personal psychology behind because the imagined mechanisms need interpretation just as outward behaviour does, under the same rules, hence the same conflict. The grounds for saying "I gave him a dime and a penny" is tacitly represented are strong, and so are the grounds that it is not strong enough for full belief. One can legislate for tidiness, but then the inner facts are only more behavioural grounds (p. 105). "The module told the rest of the system" is defeasible, like "Bill told him that p" (p. 105).
- This suggests we are looking in the wrong place (peripheral modules). Should we look centrally, at "fixation of belief"? The myth of truth written in cubbyholes of the "Belief Box" dies hard (next two chapters). It is less compelling for nonhuman objects of the intentional stance such as frogs (pp. 105–106).

*Intuition note.* Even with a full account of the inner machinery, deciding whether the seller "believed" he gave the right change reduces to the same interpretive judgment as before. The new facts act as more evidence, not as a final answer.

#### Frog Psychology (pp. 106–109)

- Stich asks what a frog *ought to* believe: that an insect is flying off to the right, that some food is there, or a conditional belief. Does it matter if fellow frogs have died from similar bugs? (p. 106)
- A sample intentional gloss: the frog sees your shadow, wants to get away, believes you are behind it, cannot see your net on the left, so thinks that is the escape route and jumps left (p. 106).
- **"See."** Do frogs, fleas and scallops "really see"? Frog vision is very impoverished compared with ours. Where to draw the line is largely a lexical problem (p. 106). Blind people with a head-mounted camera and a tingler array can identify letters. Their "point of view" shifts above their heads (Livingston 1978). Some philosophers worry about intrinsic properties of real seeing, but any supposition that frogs have or lack these is "sheer guesswork". Either we cannot know whether frogs see, or we go by information-gathering capacities, and then prosthetic vision is one sort of seeing on the same footing (pp. 106–107). The third-person view makes progress and the first-person view peters out (p. 107).
- **Belief and desire.** The gulf is greater here. No frog could believe whales are mammals or that Friday follows Thursday, or want a pizza. It is not just remoteness: frogs have webbed feet and catch insects with their tongues, yet (it seems) no frog could properly be said to believe those propositions (pp. 107–108). For even the best case (a predator is behind it), there seem to be no principles fixing the content precisely (e.g. predator *qua* predator or *qua* thing-to-avoid). Davidson (1975) and Dretske (1985) make similar points (p. 108).
- Yet treating frogs, birds, monkeys, dolphins, lobsters and honeybees from the intentional stance comes naturally and works well in its narrow range. "Try catching frogs without it." (p. 108)
- The vast difference in range suggests (a suggestion Dennett finds immensely persuasive) that belief talk about frogs is only a metaphorical extension of its proper use for human "true believers". He calls this probably the most powerful source of skepticism toward his position, which says there is nothing more to *our* having beliefs and desires than our being voluminously predictable from the intentional stance (p. 108).
- He is hit from two sides, by those who think only humans have beliefs and by those who think there are none. He says he can rebut both by showing where they part company with him (p. 108).

**Why frog psychology is "gratuitous" (pp. 108–109).**
- The frog's leap could be predicted from the design stance given a functional blueprint of the nervous system. From the physical stance, Laplace's demon (an ideal calculator of all physical interactions) could predict it without the concept of an efferent neuron (p. 109).
- So frog psychology is a "practically useful but theoretically gratuitous shortcut". Predictions from the lower stances have hegemony over intentional predictions. No unpredictable "emergent property" or "critical mass effect" blocks them. Only complexity stands (practically) in the way (p. 109).
- Laplace's demon would say the same of frog *biology*. Still, biological categories seem to have a "more robust reality": we can see the nervous system and measure neurons (p. 109).
- **The writers' view (p. 109–110).** Frog "desire to escape" is a *façon de parler* alluding to a dispositional trend or neurophysiological state. A mature neurophysiology may well speak of information but will be silent about beliefs and desires, because "strictly speaking, the frog has none."

**Dennett's response.** He endorses all this except the tone and the last line, since "strictly speaking" implies a mistaken contrast (p. 110).
- **Realists** (Fodor, Dretske) read it as: we humans really have beliefs and desires, and belief attributions to us are non-metaphorical and capable of precision. Dennett is unsure where individual Realists draw the line. Fodor (1986) is sure paramecia lack beliefs (p. 110).
- **Eliminativists** (Stich, the Churchlands) accept the contrast and the Realists' picture of real belief. Strictly speaking frogs have none, and neither do we (p. 110).
- Dennett agrees that if beliefs had to be what Realists think, there would be none, for frogs or us. He is not tempted by the contrast. We too are bathed in information, have astronomically complicated nervous systems, and many internal transactions exhibit "froggy under-specificity" (e.g. the content of the perceptual "belief" when a looming shadow makes you flinch) (p. 110).

#### The Illusions of Realism (pp. 110–115)

- **Realist picture.** Beyond metaphorical cases, we have genuine beliefs and desires and act on them. Explaining Mary's running upstairs by her belief that she left her purse on the bed is non-metaphorical. If imprecise, it is correctable, because there is a definite fact about which content she has (pp. 110–111).
- **Quine's Ralph.** Ralph has seen a man in a brown hat and suspects he is a spy. He has also seen a gray-haired man at the beach, unaware that they are the same man (Quine 1956, quoted p. 111). There is a world of difference between believing that the man in the brown hat is a spy and believing that "Ortcutt" is a spy. On the Realist view every nuance expressible in language can in principle distinguish beliefs (Davidson 1975: "Without speech we cannot make the fine distinctions between thoughts that are essential to the explanations we can sometimes confidently supply.") (p. 111). Large families of beliefs travel together (p. 111).
- On this Realist vision, beliefs and desires are propositional attitudes, as numerous and distinct as the propositions that complete them. Frogs, or at least paramecia, lack them, and "propositional attitude psychology" is psychology properly so called (pp. 111–112).
- **Dennett's diagnosis.** The wrong turning is accepting the contrast. "Belief and desire are like froggy belief and desire *all the way up*." We are only the most prodigious intentional systems on the planet (p. 112).
- **Two baths.** The illusion arises because we are bathed in *words* as well as in information. We assert, deny, request, command and promise, and we talk to ourselves in natural languages (p. 112). Whether there is also a language of thought is a distinct question, but one source of that hypothesis is failing to distinguish the two baths. Words, sentences in the head and public utterances are easily confused with beliefs. We are tempted to think that identifying such a verbal act identifies an underlying internal state exhibiting intentionality (pp. 112–113).

**Ralph again (pp. 113–114).**
- If Ralph were a fox terrier or a small child, his belief could not be about Ortcutt *qua* man named Ortcutt, nor that he is a spy. The concept of a spy is as dependent on a verbal society as Thursday or surname (p. 113).
- For an adult language-user, the total perceptual "belief" content resists precise specification as in the frog. Yet it may seem that we can distil a few critical propositions, because his action is to phone, jot a note or say something linking the man to spyhood (p. 113). Dennett then asks about the case where Ralph is only left in a dispositional state of wariness: can that state be catalogued by precise attitude attributions? Let us suppose it is determinate enough to control a voluminous verbal response under cross-examination, including "the man is a spy" (p. 113).
- **Introspection.** Asked what we believe, natural-language sentences arise in us as candidates for endorsement. Forster: "How can I tell what I think until I see what I say?" Dennett says there is no better way to tell what someone thinks than seeing what he says. But treating the clues as a published poem, or a released sequestered volume, may be like supposing a head cold is composed of internal sneezes, some of which escape. The crisp individuation may be only an artifact of the environmental demand for a particular act (Churchland 1981: a "one-dimensional projection" of a multi-dimensional state) (pp. 113–114).
- **Creeds and judging.** No one mistakes reciting a creed or saying a sentence to oneself with believing it. Churches and states care because saying is indirectly but powerfully connected with believing, and they hope to turn saying into wholehearted judging. Whether there is "insincere judgment" is a puzzle within folk psychology (p. 114).
- What explains action is the central state of belief, not the peripheral state of having related oneself to a public language product. Lulu's buying lasagna is not explained by her having written "lasagna" on a list. She needs beliefs and desires to interpret the list, even if memorised. The language-of-thought believers say these are more linguistic objects, in Mentalese (p. 114).

**Language of thought and frog belief (pp. 114–115).**
- The hypothesis does not deny natural-language sentences in our heads. It claims more sentences lie behind them in an unlearned language (pp. 114–115). Dennett says there are good reasons to want a systematic representational medium in the brain (next two chapters). But the idea that its elements gather content in sentence-like bunches "needs independent support". Without it, he asks, what reason is there to suppose human belief is all that different from frog belief? In both, behaviour is controlled by a complex internal state alluded to by folk attribution. If human beliefs seem more finely individuated, we may be attending to the *products of linguistic behaviours*, which are *ipso facto* as distinct as the language's discriminations (p. 115).
- **Fodor's example (1985, p. 87):** to know that John thinks Mary wept is to know it is highly probable he thinks somebody wept. Dennett: the use of "thinks" is symptomatic of the Realist confusion. As a doctrine about what people would do when provoked to occurrent "saying-to-oneself", it has some plausibility. As a doctrine about different, individuated internal states a theorist would find in the subject's "belief center", it has none (p. 115).
- If Ralph were pumped long enough, some declarations should be ignored as unreliable. He might not grasp "insidious" and "double agent", or be halfhearted or injudicious in expressing himself (p. 115). The unit's pages end here.

## Toy example (ours)
**Aim:** show, for this one case, how the belief-stories Dennett considers each end in a contradiction, a plain false belief, or a caveat. The numbers follow Dennett's lemonade case (p. 85); the three-way comparison is ours.

Facts: price 12, payment 25, correct change = 25 − 12 = 13. The boy returned 10 + 1 = 11 and believes "11 is the right change".

| Story | Attributed arithmetic beliefs | Check | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 25 − 12 = 13 and 10 + 1 = 11 and "11 is the right change" | (added step) taking both "13 is the right change" and "11 is the right change" commits him to 13 = 11, yet 13 ≠ 11 | Contradiction; he cannot be taken as reasoning sensibly |
| B | 25 − 12 = 11 (false), 10 + 1 = 11 | 11 = 11, consistent | Not a "slip": a rational thinker with one false belief (p. 86), so not a story of miscalculation |
| C | A with a caveat: he "sort of" believes 25 − 12 = 13 | The caveat weakens attribution (8) | Dennett's point: a scare-quote or caveat is needed (p. 86) |

Counting: in A, the number of beliefs held with 13 and 11 as "the right change" is 2 incompatible values for one quantity. In B there is 1 false belief and 0 incompatible pairs. In C the count is "not a full belief". None of these three stories gives 0 false beliefs, 0 contradictions and a full attribution of the mistake. That is the gap, for this case (Dennett offers it as a challenge, p. 87).

## Concepts
- [[intentional stance]] — predicting a system by treating it as having beliefs and desires that make sense; here pressed on errors and frogs (pp. 83–93, 106–112)
- [[physical / design stance]] — predicting frog behaviour from physics or from functional design; gives "hegemony" over intentional predictions in principle (p. 109)
- [[folk psychology]] — the everyday practice of belief/desire attribution, including "forgot", "noticed", "overlooked"; Dennett says it contains confabulation (pp. 88–91)
- [[propositional attitude]] — in the Realist picture, beliefs are as numerous and distinct as the propositions that complete them (p. 111)
- [[intentionality]] — the "internal state exhibiting intentionality" that we are tempted to find behind verbal acts; Dennett ties content to holistic interpretation (pp. 92–93, 112)
- [[referential opacity]] — Ralph believing the man in the brown hat is a spy, not that "Ortcutt" is, though same man; Dennett does not use the term here (p. 111)
- [[simulation theory]] — Stich's idea that we use ourselves as "analog computers"; Dennett argues it still needs theory-like knowledge (pp. 100–101) (canon link; Dennett writes "something like simulation" and does not use the label "simulation theory" here)
- [[rational-agent assumption / principle of efficiency]] — here the assumption that agents are rational, interpreted as "cognitive approval", not deductive closure or perfect consistency (pp. 94–98)
- [[rationality as general-purpose cognitive approval]] (new) — pre-theoretical, with only conditional allegiance to any formal system (p. 97)
- [[illata vs abstracta]] (new) — illata are postulated inner entities; abstracta are the interpretive system fixing belief and desire (pp. 91–92)
- [[interpretive indeterminacy of belief in error]] (new) — no unqualified catalogue of the agent's beliefs for a mistake (pp. 93, 103)
- [[confabulation]] (new) — inventing self-interpretation without support; Dennett says it may be a normal process (p. 91)
- [[eliminativism / realism about belief]] (new) — Stich and the Churchlands say no beliefs; Fodor and Dretske say humans really have them (pp. 110–111)
- [[language of thought]] (new) — hypothesis that sentences in an innate "Mentalese" lie behind belief; Dennett asks for independent support for sentence-like contents (pp. 114–115)
- [[Factunorm Principle]] (new) — how we do think is evidence for how we ought to think (Wertheimer, quoted) (p. 98)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's "best, most rational story" ↔ theory-of-mind modelling: inverse planning assumes the agent acts rationally and infers beliefs from actions — both infer mental states by assuming rationality; Dennett denies a fixed formal definition.
- [structural-analogy] Stich's "ourselves as analog computers" ↔ simulation theory: mindreading by running one's own mind offline; Dennett's rejoinder is that simulation still needs theory.
- [structural-analogy] The lemonade "knife-edge" ↔ underdetermination in statistical inference: data may fail to favour one model, but real data rarely tie exactly.
- [metaphor] "Bathed in words" versus "bathed in information" ↔ machine theory of mind and construct validity: fluent verbal output may suggest more finely individuated beliefs than the underlying system has.
- [structural-analogy] Confabulation ↔ implicit versus explicit ToM: verbal self-report may be a constructed interpretation, not read-out of a state.

## Questions this raises
- If both intentional description and physical description can be coherent, what test makes the intentional one *better* for a given system, say a frog versus a person?
- Can a "norm-free" psychology, as Stich wants, identify content without any interpretive standard? What would count as a result?
- If sentence-like individuation partly comes from our language, what would belief look like in creatures with no language, such as infants?
- Do the two methods ("what would I believe?" vs "what ought he believe?") ever give different predictions in an experiment?
