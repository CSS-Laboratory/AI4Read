# Beyond Belief (part 1/2): propositions, sentences in the head, and notional worlds

## L0 — One line
Dennett explores, without offering a finished theory, how to keep a useful concept of belief when the classical "proposition" is in trouble, and floats a theorist's fictional "notional world" as a tentative, exploratory candidate (pp. 118, 152–153, 173).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks how belief-talk can stay useful when the usual "propositional attitude" picture is in trouble. He sets out three Fregean demands on propositions. Twin Earth-style arguments (thought experiments about physical duplicates) are meant to show the demands cannot be met together; he supposes them sound without endorsing them. He then examines two retreats: sentences in the head, and a "notional world" invented by the theorist. The first ends with unsolved problems; the second he offers tentatively. It ends with the Shakey's Pizza Parlor puzzle about *de re* and *de dicto* beliefs. He suggests the puzzle may come from using one set of categories for two styles of cognitive operation. No finished theory is given.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a sentence that is true or false; reference vs meaning; type vs token; the basic idea of a possible world; very light set notation.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Beliefs are standardly called *propositional attitudes* (three slots: a person, an attitude type, a proposition). Dennett says there is no stable, received interpretation of this technical term, and no consensus on what a proposition is (pp. 117, 120–121).
- Three views of propositions (sentence-like, sets of possible worlds, arrangements of objects and properties) seem to Dennett to be united by three Fregean demands (Frege's notion of a "Thought" underlies the orthodox view). A proposition must be a *truth-value-bearer* (the thing that is true or false), must be composed of *intensions* (rules that fix what a term applies to), and must be "graspable" by the mind (p. 122). Dennett says the demands cannot be satisfied simultaneously, as the arguments of pp. 127–129 are meant to show (pp. 122–123, 127).
- Dennett defines "graspable" weakly: propositions are graspable iff attitude predicates are projectible (usable for reliable prediction), well-behaved predicates of psychological theory (p. 126). He *supposes* the Twin Earth and Kaplan arguments sound (thought experiments about physical duplicates and about indexical words), without endorsing them, and then asks what is left (pp. 127–129).
- The retreat to *sentential attitudes* (sentences of a "language of thought" in the head) is, he says, the most compelling answer. Dennett argues that it faces unsolved problems: where the syntax comes from, language-neutrality, and how to fix the explicit message of a brain element (pp. 130–151).
- He suspects that the conviction that every contribution of a brain element can be put into a sentence rests in part on conflating *determinateness* with *explicitness*. A subsystem's contribution could be determinate and contentful without being expressible as a sentence (p. 147; pp. 147–149).
- Alternative: *notional attitude psychology*. A theorist invents a fictional "notional world" to characterize a subject's *narrow* psychological state (the state fixed by the organism alone). It is hetero-phenomenological, meaning from the outside, not introspective (pp. 152–155).
- Comparing notional worlds does not make comparing beliefs routine. A language of thought would give no more leverage, for example with contradictory beliefs (pp. 162–164).
- In the Shakey's Pizza Parlor story (a man moved between two look-alike restaurants), the philosophical schools disagree about Tom's *de re* beliefs but, in that case, make no different predictions about his behaviour. Dennett suggests the problems come from applying one set of categories to two styles of cognitive operation (pp. 170–173).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the end of the preceding text and the chapter's aims (pp. 116–121)

**The Realist view (the position Dennett criticizes):** there has to be a fact, however hard to find, about exactly which proposition is the content of Ralph's belief (p. 116). Efforts to state principles for such content have produced a "cottage industry"; Loar (cited) finds it extraordinary that psychological states should be captured by a neat set of content-specifications (p. 116).

**Dennett:** he agrees with Stich and the Churchlands that this ingenuity recalls the late blooming of Ptolemaic epicycles (p. 116). For a frog, an intentional-stance description is always an idealization. Beyond the best approximation, there are no facts about what the frog "really believes" (p. 116). Some may still hope for a Realist theory of *human* propositional attitudes. He says the next two chapters, and the reflections that follow them, should dash that hope (p. 116).

**Chapter 5 begins (p. 117).** Beliefs have a less secure place in a scientific ontology than electrons or genes. A plausible reason to talk of them is to explain and predict behaviour. That may not be good enough, because belief-talk ties one into philosophical problems (p. 117). Aim: untangle or expose some of these problems and suggest versions or substitutes of belief that are worth salvaging (p. 117).

**Plan (pp. 117–118):**
1. Propositional Attitudes: several incompatible doctrines of propositions, developed as responses to Frege's demands, and attacks, with a common theme, on all versions of the standard doctrine.
2. Sentential Attitudes: the retreat to a "language of thought" and its serious unsolved problems.
3. Notional Attitudes: a position midway between propositional and sentential attitudes, using the theorist's fiction of a *notional world*.
4. "De re and de dicto Dismantled": announced as the final section, giving alternative diagnoses of the *de re/de dicto* intuitions. No heading for it is visible on the pages of this part (see Reading notes).

The essay is "exploratory and diagnostic," not a theory of belief (p. 118).

**Two ways to see the project (pp. 118–119).** One is as natural-language semantics (how "believes that" works in English). Philosophers who start there often move on to claims about how a *psychological* theory of belief must go. Dennett calls this drift natural, even defensible, so long as one accepts the added burdens of argument (p. 119). **Dennett:** if such conclusions are sound but no psychological theory will go as the conclusion says it must, the right further conclusion is "so much the worse for the concept of belief" (p. 119).

**How to pick out beliefs (pp. 119–120).** Books can be picked out by title, author, topic or location, and "the same book" can mean same edition, same text, or same text or good translation. By analogy, the privileged way to refer to a belief is by the *proposition believed*. Tom, Dick, Harry and monolingual Frenchmen can share the same belief although their belief *tokens* differ in many ways (pp. 119–120). The term *propositional attitude* is Russell's; the formulae have three degrees of freedom: person, attitude type, proposition (p. 120). Besides the philosophers' problems about propositions there are now psychologists' problems about instantiating belief-states. Dennett notes that non-philosophers in cognitive science are adopting these formulations in the innocent belief that a concept so popular among philosophers must be sound, agreed upon and well tested, and adds "If only it were so" (p. 120).

### Propositional Attitudes (pp. 121–130)

**If** a good way to characterize a person's psychological state is to give their propositional attitudes, **then** getting the *right* propositions is critical. That needs a view of what counts as one proposition or two (p. 121). The literature has three characterizations:
1. Propositions are *sentence-like entities* with a syntax and a type–token distinction (Harman) (p. 121).
2. Propositions are *sets of possible worlds*. Two sentences express the same proposition just in case they are true in exactly the same worlds; such propositions have no tokens in a head (Stalnaker; also Field, Lewis) (p. 121).
3. Propositions are *arrangements of objects and properties in the world*; the proposition that Tom is tall consists of Tom and tallness (Russell, Donnellan, Kaplan, Perry) (p. 121).

**Three Fregean demands (pp. 122–123).** What *seems* to Dennett to unite these views is a set of three classical demands, and he says the diversity of doctrine arises because the demands cannot be satisfied together (p. 122). A proposition (Frege's "Thought") must:
- **(a)** be a (final, constant, underived) **truth-value-bearer**: if p is true and q false, they are not the same proposition. This fits the common view of propositions as the medium of information transfer, though one can accept (a) without that view (Evans) (p. 122).
- **(b)** be composed of **intensions** (à la Carnap, *extension-determiners*). Different intensions can determine the same extension (the intension of "three squared" is not that of "the number of planets"). One intension cannot determine different extensions. Intensions determine extensions but are not thereby *methods* of finding them (p. 122). Condition (b) cuts two ways: what a proposition is *about* is defining, so if p is about a and q is not, they differ. And since extension does not fix intension, p and q both being about a and attributing F to a does not suffice for sameness, because they may refer to a in different ways (pp. 122–123). Frege unified (a) and (b) by letting a whole sentence have an extension, the True or the False (p. 123).
- **(c)** be "graspable" by the mind. Frege does not say what grasping is (p. 123).

**Churchland's measure-predicate analogy (pp. 123–124).** The catalogue of attitude predicates (believes that p, desires that p, ...) resembles the catalogue of measure predicates (has a length of n meters, ...). **Churchland (quoted):** the idea that believing is standing in a relation to an abstract entity has no more to recommend it than the idea that weighing 5 kg is standing in a relation to the number 5; contexts like *x weighs 5 kg* are better catalogued with adverbial contexts like *x weighs very little* (pp. 123–124).

**Dennett:** this does not dissolve the metaphysical problems. But it ties propositions' fate to that of numbers in physics, which disarms the suspicion of a *special* abstract-object problem in psychology (p. 124). It also separates two views often run together. One is that taking propositions seriously means giving them a causal role; that requires concrete tokens, hence view (1). The other is that a proposition's function is just to be the object named in the slot of the predicate, "measuring" a state. This second view does not say attitude predicates lack logical structure (p. 124). It leaves open that we may later find something in the creature mirroring the proposition's form (pp. 124–125). Footnote 1 (p. 125) quotes Field arguing that the measurement analogy leads to a language of thought; Dennett replies that this is a premature guess, not an implication of the view.

**A communication problem (pp. 125–126).** Debates about the "form" of propositions (e.g. infinitely long disjunctions) may be about brain structures or only about the logical form of abstract objects. In the second case an infinite disjunction poses no more problem than π does in physics (p. 125). Dennett therefore adopts the minimal Churchland reading as a neutral base (p. 126).

**Graspability, defined (p. 126).** Propositions are graspable **if and only if** predicates of propositional attitude are projectible, predictive, well-behaved predicates of psychological theory. Rationale: Frege's demand that the mind can grasp propositions amounts to requiring that they make a difference to a psychological state, and psychological states are those whose variation predicts behaviour (p. 126; fn. 2: having a big red nose is not itself a psychological state, but believing one has it can be). This weak graspability is still demanding enough to conflict with (a) and (b) (p. 126).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett strips "grasping" down to the attitude predicate working well in predicting behaviour. Even on this weak reading he says a conflict with conditions (a) and (b) arises (p. 126).

**The arguments against joint satisfiability (pp. 127–129).** Putnam, Fodor, Perry, Kaplan and Stich have argued that (a)–(c) cannot all hold (p. 127).
- **Twin Earth (Putnam's argument).** A near-duplicate planet has XYZ, indistinguishable by normal macro-properties, where Earth has H₂O. My Doppelgänger (atom-for-atom replica) and I are, for the argument's sake, psychological replicas. Yet my belief that water is H₂O is about water and true; his is about XYZ and false. We are psychological twins but not propositional-attitude twins, so propositions meeting (a) and (b) are not graspable. Putnam: either meaning is not in the head or meaning does not determine extension (p. 127).
- **Comparison with knowing (Stich).** "Knows" is not, or not purely, a psychological verb because knowing entails truth. Putnam's claim goes further: even with a pure psychological verb, any proposition-component meeting (a) and (b) makes the predicate impure (pp. 127–128).
- **Dennett's remarks.** The thought experiment rests on dubious doctrines of natural kinds and rigid designation. A variant avoids this: my wallet is in my coat pocket, my Doppelgänger's is not; each has a counterpart belief, mine true and his false; different propositions, same psychology (p. 128).
- **Kaplan's argument.** Frege says that to say today what was said yesterday with "today" one must say "yesterday"; the thought is the same. **Kaplan (quoted):** one can lose track of the days and not realize one is expressing the same thought, so it seems that thoughts are not appropriate bearers of cognitive significance (pp. 128–129).
- Other arguments exist (Perry's, discussed later). Footnote 3 gives Vendler's: **if** my one enduring belief that Angola is independent is counted as one belief, and its truth-value can change during a decade, then it cannot be a propositional attitude, though it is a constancy of psychological state (p. 129).

**Dennett's stance (p. 129).** He does not want to endorse any of these arguments here and now, and he urges resisting a fight to the death over Twin Earth. He proposes to give a little ground and see where that leads. Suppose they are sound. One claim extracted from Kaplan (which Putnam and Perry would accept, Dennett gathers): if my thought contains an indexical (a word like "now" or "today", whose reference depends on context), the proposition I am related to can depend, imperceptibly to me, on something like a Greenwich clock hand. **Dennett's response:** it is frankly incredible that my behaviour-predicting state depends on such remote features. He concedes that long-range effects of my acts (a bet, a sworn denial) may be better predicted from the proposition I in fact expressed than from the one I took myself to express. That, he says, only heightens the contrast between the variable accessibility of propositions and the built-in accessibility of something else (he asks: of what?) (pp. 129–130). Footnote 4 says one could sum up the arguments thus: propositions are not graspable because they can elude us (p. 129).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett does not claim these arguments are proven. He asks: if the arguments are taken to show that propositions are psychologically inert at least under certain special circumstances (p. 130), what object is more accessible, graspable and effective in the propositional role? The next section tries sentences in the head.

### Sentential Attitudes (pp. 130–151)

**The appeal (pp. 130–131).** The most compelling answer, judged by the number of its sympathizers, is sentences in the head, though Dennett expects it to prove unsatisfactory in the end (p. 130). Routes to it:
- If one figuratively "grasps" a proposition, one must literally grasp something concrete and proposition-like: a Mentalese sentence. For those who already take propositions to be sentence-like, this is a short retreat. It consists of giving up (a) and (b), and then something must replace them (p. 130).
- Belief states are made of symbols for cats and mats; the problem of tying symbols to the world is isolated and postponed as a problem of *reference* for inner sentences (Field's route) (pp. 130–131).
- To explain *opacity* (believing it would be nice to marry Jocasta differs in effects from believing it would be nice to marry the mother of Oedipus), the two states might differ in physical "syntax." Opacity of indirect discourse is then subsumed under the "super-opacity" of strict quotation of different Mentalese sentences (Fodor) (p. 131).
- Most relevant: indexicals. Quine's *eternal sentences* remove indexical effects; a psychologically perspicuous alternative would build indexicals in. Tokens of one sentence type (*I am tired*) express different propositions in different mouths (pp. 131–132).

**Kaplan's schema (p. 132).** Indexicality appears to be the linguistic counterpart of the subject-relativity of mental states. A sentence type means just one "thing", and all its tokens share it; it is no proposition. Kaplan calls this the sentence's *character*. Dennett quotes the picture:

$$\text{Character: Contexts} \to \text{Content}\qquad \text{Content: Circumstances} \to \text{Extensions}$$

or: Meaning + Context → Intension; Intension + Possible World → Extension (p. 132). No mastery of my language ensures that I can tell which proposition I have expressed, but my competence apparently gives me access to the character of what I said (pp. 132–133). Dennett then asks whether this could be generalized to the language of thought: could the *objects of belief* be the characters of Mentalese sentences (p. 133)?

**Perry's version (p. 133).** Perry speaks of *senses entertained* (Kaplan's characters) and *thoughts apprehended* (contents). **Perry (quoted by Dennett):** you and I entertaining the same sense (that a bear is about to attack me) both roll into a ball, though we apprehend different thoughts; apprehending the same thought, we behave differently. So senses are what individuate psychological states in explaining and predicting action. On the proposed two-stage account, the first stage (senses, modeled on characters) gives psychologically pure predicates with graspable objects; the second (thoughts, modeled on contents) is psychologically impure but takes us on to extensions (p. 133).

**Putnam's startling answer (p. 134).** Asked what features of an entity, taken alone, fix its propositional attitudes, Putnam says: nothing. This conflicts with Descartes, for whom which thoughts he was entertaining seemed fixed by his own mind (p. 134). Dennett lists at least four ways to resolve the conflict and follows the irenic one: accept Putnam's case against propositions and hold that what Descartes grasped are Perry's senses (p. 134). The question becomes the **organismic contribution** to the fixing of attitudes. Studying it in isolation is Putnam's *methodological solipsism*, the study of psychology in the narrow sense (the strategy Fodor 1980 recommends) (pp. 134–135).

**An enlarged schema (pp. 135, 137).** Dennett extends Kaplan's schema backwards:

$$(0)\quad \text{Syntactic features} + \text{linguistic conventions} \to \text{Character}$$
$$(1)\quad \text{Character} + \text{Context} \to \text{Content}$$
$$(2)\quad \text{Content} + \text{Circumstances} \to \text{Extension}$$

The language of thought is needed to fill the first gap, as the raw material (p. 135). Putnam's Doppelgänger case seems to fit: replicas share syntax, hence character; Earth vs Twin Earth context accounts for the different content (pp. 135–136). But what licenses the step from syntactic type identity to character type identity? A replica of *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* in a language called "Schmenglish" might not be an autobiography. The reason we may ignore this for Mentalese must have to do with a more intimate form–function relation, so the role of linguistic conventions in stage (0) must be played by something not conventional (p. 136; fn. 6 reports McCarthy's view that formal constraints on long texts limit rival readings). Further, a stage before (0) seems missing, because the "text" is not given in psychology:

$$(-1)\quad \text{physical features} + \text{design considerations (minus functional irrelevancies)} \to \text{syntactic features}$$

Typeface does not count as syntax unless it functions as such; Morse tokens can share syntax with printed English. So brain-writing tokens may differ physically yet share syntax (p. 137).

**Sententialism (p. 137).** Dennett names the strong position:

$$x \text{ believes what } y \text{ believes} \iff (\exists L)(\exists s)\big(L \text{ is a language of thought} \wedge s \in L \wedge \text{there is a token of } s \text{ in } x \wedge \text{there is a token of } s \text{ in } y\big)\tag{S}$$

with tokens in functionally relevant places (swallowing slips of paper does not count) (p. 137). So believers must share a language of thought to believe the same thing, and the *language neutrality* of propositions is given up. Dennett calls this apparently onerous unless it can be defended or trivialized (p. 138; fn. 7 notes Field's dismissal of cross-organism type-identity).

**Defending it (pp. 138–140).** It may be an empirical question whether people share a language of thought (perhaps dogs think in Doggish; perhaps one human brain-writing, Zeman). There might be several Mentalese languages (Mentallatin, Mentalgreek), in which case we could not share the same *psychological* states but might still share propositional attitudes. A different idiosyncratic Mentalese for each person would be a theoretical calamity (p. 138). Theory development tends to replace multilingual with monolingual hypotheses by moving to a higher functional level, where syntactic differences become differences beneath syntax. This blurs syntax and semantics (p. 139). Carried to a "Pickwickian extreme" (French and English as notational variants of one ur-language), it yields a very abstract monolingualism; anyone who thinks there *has* to be a single Mentalese must, Dennett says, be ignoring such principles (p. 139). Even if multilingualism holds, we need to point to similarities, as between *j'ai faim*, *I'm hungry* and *Ich habe Hunger*. That type-casting cannot be syntactic, nor can it be wholly semantic in the propositional sense, since indexicals express different propositions on different occasions. It needs an intermediate taxonomy by *roles* in a functionalistic theory (pp. 139–140; fn. 8). A syntax-neutral level is therefore valuable (p. 140).

**The brain as syntactic engine (pp. 140–143).** The sentential theorist would need to describe the nervous system purely syntactically and purify the "text" of physical scribbles. But what makes a feature syntactic is its capacity to make a semantic difference, so this cannot proceed innocent of semantic assumptions (pp. 140–141). Sensory *transducers* (turn physical energy into internal signals) and motor *effectors* (turn internal commands into movement) anchor interpretation. An idea that licenses speculation about the semantics of mental representation is that their semantic properties are at least partially determinable from their relations, however indirect, to transducers and effectors (p. 141). Dennett asks us temporarily to treat transducers as hidden-source "oracles" and effectors as obedient producers of unknown effects. This is the brain's-eye view: brains are *syntactic engines*, and the control functions of a nervous system must be explicable at that level or remain forever mysterious (p. 141).

The alternative, which Dennett calls most improbable, is that content could be an independent, detectable causal property. Coin example: one of two coins has spent exactly ten minutes on my desk. No coin machine can test directly for that, though it might test indirectly and unreliably if, say, the desk were radioactive. The brain's testing of semantic properties must likewise be indirect, driven by syntactic properties. We are "semantic engines" only at another level of interpretation (p. 142). This gives a diagnosis of Putnam, Kaplan and Perry: if meaning is only indirectly detectable by a system, it is not the property to use in building projectible predicates. What we want is a property related to meaning roughly as guilt-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt is to guilt, which Dennett says is a bit less reliable for predicting the jury (p. 142). They are, in effect, driving an ancient wedge (real vs apparent) into the realm of propositions believed. Dennett notes that the analogous theoretical move earlier gave philosophers sense data and qualia, a sort of inner copy; Mentalese sentences are in effect inner copies of propositions. He disclaims argument by guilt by association (pp. 142–143).

**Syntactic vs semantic psychology and believe\* (pp. 143–144).** Methodological solipsism plus a language of thought tempts one to split psychology into syntactic and semantic parts. The hope: *de-interpret* transducer messages (*it is F now, getting G-er and G-er intermittently H*) and effector commands, then give central states a *relative* semantic interpretation in terms of these uninterpreted predicates. This parallels Field's 1972 suggestion for Tarskian semantics: do the theory of reference for primitives separately and the rest first, temporizing with statements like *"snow" refers to whatever it refers to* (p. 143). **Field's proposal (quoted, 1978):**

$$X \text{ believes that } p \iff \exists S\,[\,X \text{ believes}^* S \wedge S \text{ means that } p\,]\tag{Field 1}$$

This splits belief into (a) what it is to believe\* a sentence (a relation between a person and a syntactically characterized object, given by dispositions to use the sentence) and (b) what it is for a sentence to mean that p. Only (b) is semantic (p. 144). With natural-language sentences this is safe enough. With Mentalese (sentence-analogues, needed for mute, animal and prelinguistic believers) the definition becomes highly problematic, though Field himself calls the shift a minor modification (p. 144).

**The Rep example (pp. 144–146).** Take a peripheral reporter element Rep near the retina. Perhaps its signal is the Mentalese for *There is now a small red spot in the middle of the visual field*, de-interpreted as *there is now an FGH at J of K*. But it might instead be *There's a tomato in front of me*, *At least ten retinal cells of sort F are in state G*, or *I am being appeared to redly*. These have different syntactic analyses. We can find an item's shape but not its syntactic form except through its powers of combination with other elements, hence ultimately its environmental import (p. 145). **Thought experiment (design a language of thought):** write belief sentences into boxes on a map of the system. The inscription *snow is white* stores information only if machinery links its box to all boxes containing *white*, to *snow* and *precipitation* boxes, and to the periphery. This is like taxonomic lattices or semantic nets in AI (pp. 145–146). Without the links the inscriptions are mere decoration; once the links exist the inscriptions are still only mnemonic labels (at best). The real "syntax", the structure on which function depends, is in the links, which Dennett uses as a wild card; no one yet knows how to specify them (p. 146). In computer languages, properly entered inscriptions *create* links. Any language of thought would have to share this feature if its postulation is to avoid fruitless epiphenomenalism (inert labels). Then form and function are tight, and stage (0)'s separation of syntactic features from linguistic conventions has no counterpart. Getting the text independently of its interpretation is not a real prospect, so Field's split as a research strategy is forlorn (p. 146). It might survive as a distinction of reason, visible only in retrospect in a mature psychology. Supposing it resembles the syntax/semantics distinction for natural language would be gratuitously strong sententialism (pp. 146–147).

**Determinateness vs explicitness (pp. 147–149).** Even with the best functional description of the system, there is no guarantee that Rep's functions can be aptly expressed by any claim that its message inserts sentence S as a premise. **Dennett suspects** that the conviction that such linguification must be possible rests in part on conflating *determinateness* with *explicitness* (p. 147). A contribution may be perfectly determinate and describable without being described as an explicit assertion in any language (pp. 147–148). He is not only pointing to noisy, non-contentful causal links (smelling sulphur may make someone think of baseball for no reason). The stronger claim is that there *could* be content-sensitive, informational, designed relations between subsystems that defy a sententialist interpretation (p. 148). **Example:** Pat says that Mike has a thing about redheads. It *could turn out* that Mike has a piece of cognitive machinery that comes into play whenever redheads are the topic, makes flattering hypotheses less likely to be entertained, and brings aggressive behaviour closer to implementation. Its role *could* elude the format *Mike believes that: $(x)(x \text{ is a redhead} \supset \dots)$* however many qualifiers are added. Its contribution could be determinate and contentful while any sentence expressing it is no more than a mnemonic label; in such a case we might say beliefs are *implicit* in the system. Which belief? That redheads are untrustworthy, that most are, or $(x)(x \text{ is a redhead} \supset \text{the probability is } 0.9 \text{ that } x \text{ is untrustworthy})$? The concern with sentence form is idle **when the sentence is only part of a stab at capturing the implicit content of some nonsentential machinery** (pp. 148–149).

**A possible reply and the overcoat (pp. 149–150).** It might still be argued that sentential states are required for the *compositionality* a finite brain needs; the only (arguably) universal finite-means representation systems we have are languages. **Dennett:** for all he can see, this may be so. If so, much psychology could be cognitive and nonsentential, with Mentalese needed only at a central interface. The earlier mistake may have been to suppose that the *transducer–effector overcoat* was thinner than it is (p. 149). Even taking that overcoat as context, Mentalese predicates would be only partly interpreted, not enough to separate my attitudes from my Doppelgänger's. Variation: hook a duplicate of my sentential system to a different overcoat. The same state (storing that all Fs are very GHs) subserves in me the belief that greyhounds are very swift, in the other the belief that palaces are very expensive houses (pp. 149–150). If the overcoat counts as external context the tokens share character; if the boundary is drawn further out they share only syntax, and the different overcoats play the role of stage (0)'s conventions (p. 150).

**A general schema (pp. 150–151).** Kaplan's schema is a special case of $C + E \to I$ (a character-like concept $C$ for narrow application, an embedding environment $E$, and a richer semantic or functional characterization $I$). In biology and engineering a spark-producer in context is seen to be a fuel-igniter (p. 150). The richer a stage's semantics, the more abstract or tolerant the syntax. Different physical sentences can share syntax, different syntax can share character, different characters can express one proposition, and different propositions can attribute the same property to an individual (the Dean of Admissions vs the tallest Dean). Transplanted to psychology: people believing the same proposition can be in different narrow states; people in the same narrow state can differ in syntactic state; people in the same syntactic state can differ physically; and redrawing the system–environment boundary can turn same into different (pp. 150–151; fn. 12 on Burge).

> **Intuition note.** In Dennett's box thought experiment, a sentence written in a box stores nothing unless links give it a role in the system. He says the structure on which function depends is in the links, and that nobody yet knows how to specify them (pp. 145–146).

### Notional Attitudes (pp. 151–166)

**Motivation (pp. 151–152).** Any sentential characterization is a syntactic type-casting and, Dennett says, would cut too fine. In Putnam's thought experiment we grant that physical replicahood is sufficient but not necessary for identity of organismic contribution; syntactic replicahood (at some level of abstraction) could also be granted sufficient, but narrow-psychological twinhood would not seem to require syntactic twinhood at any level (p. 151). Analogy: Turing machines computing the same function need not share a machine table unless levels of description are adjusted (pp. 151–152). The analogy is imperfect. Even if complete twinhood required syntactic twinhood, it would not follow that *partial* psychological similarity can always be described in one general syntactic system. Vain or paranoid people are psychologically similar, in ways largely captured by shared beliefs, yet not plausibly by a shared Mentalese. Nor is the similarity captured by propositional attitudes, because of indexical beliefs (*People admire me*) (p. 152). What we want is an intermediate position: **notional attitude psychology**. We *want* me and my Doppelgänger to have exactly the same notional attitudes, so that our propositional differences come entirely from environment, and we want people "of like mind" on some topics to share some notional attitudes (p. 152).

**The notional world (pp. 152–153).** A person's *notional world* is a sort of fictional world devised by a third-party theorist to characterize narrow-psychological states. My Doppelgänger and I (Earth and Twin Earth) have the same notional world; you and I live in the same real world but have different, overlapping notional worlds (p. 152). It is full of notional objects, which may or may not match real ones. The relations between real and notional things are puzzling, which is one reason for the methodological-solipsist retreat (p. 153).

**Brentano and Husserl (p. 153).** Dennett asks what notional objects are but the intentional objects of Brentano, and calls methodological solipsism apparently a version of Husserl's *epoché*, or bracketing. He then asks whether the alternative is Phenomenology, and answers: not quite. Phenomenologists get at their *own* notional world by a somewhat introspectionist reduction (*auto*-phenomenology). Dennett proposes *hetero*-phenomenology: determining the notional world of *another*, from the outside (p. 153).

**James, Fodor, and the "given" (pp. 153–155).** Fodor (following James) contrasts naturalistic and rational psychology. James praised the former, psychology in the wide sense, for taking mind in its environment. **Fodor's moral from Twin Earth:** naturalistic psychology casts its net too wide to be doable. The Phenomenologists apparently draw the same conclusion, and both turn to methodological solipsism (p. 154). Phenomenologists claim to find a *given* in experience. If Fodor claimed to find a Mentalese text *given* in the hardware, there would be as much reason to doubt it (p. 154). **Dennett agrees with James** that psychology cannot proceed without the semantic properties of internal states, and these cannot be found without looking at relations to the environment. But he says nothing requires that environment to be real; an idealized or imaginary one may do as well. One needs a Tarskian-style model, perhaps fictional, to get proto-semantics started (p. 154). A notional world is a model, not necessarily the true one, of one's internal representations. It consists of the things *represented*, not of representations *in* me, and so far, Dennett says, this is pure Brentano as he understands him. The theorist's description exists on paper; the inhabitants are the intentional objects of the subject (pp. 154–155).

**Constructing it: the frozen organism (pp. 155–157).** Suppose we get a frozen organism and a Laplacean snapshot of its internal structure, enough to say how it *would* respond to any new input. We are like people shown an alien gadget and asked what it is for (cherry pitter or sail mender?). If equally good candidates exist, the function may be undeterminable; the truth might then be an inert historical fact, like whether some gold in my teeth was once Julius Caesar's (p. 155). For an organism we can say what it is for: surviving, flourishing, reproducing. We can infer what it would do "if…" and hence what *ideal environment* it is best fitted to, meaning the environment it is best fitted for, not the best possible one. It might be downright nasty (p. 156). For simple organisms with no plasticity, rather different environments may fit equally well. As the capacity to learn and remember grows, the class of acceptable models shrinks. Memory traces are *designed* to be telltale, so we can read them by assuming the dispositions attached are in general appropriate. But we cannot tell information from misinformation, so the extrapolated world is ideal in the sense of *unreal*, not best (p. 156). A duplicate with a faked history would have the same notional world, because that world is the world apparent to the creature in its current total dispositional state (p. 157). For adaptive organisms like us, the exercise would end in a unique solution apart from virtually indistinguishable variations (e.g. a Boston not distinguishable from Twin Earth Boston) (p. 157).

**"The myth" (pp. 157–158).** Dennett calls this practically useless but theoretically important. It exposes the assumption that narrow psychological traits *supervene* on (are fixed by) physical traits, and it preserves the underdetermination of ultimate reference that was Putnam's moral. If there is a language of thought, this is how one would have to bootstrap to it, without bilingual help. If there is any third-person alternative to the Phenomenologists' introspectionist method, and hetero-phenomenology is possible at all, it will have to proceed by this method (pp. 157–158). Even the fullest description of a person's notional world would not specify a *unique* world, since differences beyond the person's ken would fit equally (p. 158). Footnote 13 (p. 158): the objects of fears and desires, and a dream house, are treated indirectly via the subject's *specifications*, which are ordinary denizens of the notional world.

**Formal picture (pp. 158–159).** Lewis (1978) treats "the" world of Sherlock Holmes as a set of possible worlds consistent with the Holmes texts (p. 158). Similarly the notional world might be viewed formally as the *set of possible worlds consistent with the maximal description* (p. 159). Then Putnam's Earth and Twin Earth are both members of the set that *is* the notional world I share with my Doppelgänger. The H₂O/XYZ difference is below our discrimination thresholds, provided neither of us is, or consults, a wily chemist (p. 159). Beliefs are then *about* things in the Pickwick-about sense (Goodman): not genuinely relational, since no Pickwick exists to be related to. Brentano calls such relations relationlike. An enabling assumption of notional attitude psychology is that the theorist can use Pickwick-aboutness and its kin as the semantic properties needed for the foundations of a theory of mental representation (p. 159).

**SHRDLU as example (pp. 159–161).** Winograd's SHRDLU is a simulated "robot" in a simulated blocks world. **Fodor (quoted):** the machine lives in a notional world, its beliefs all false, and it is in "the situation that Descartes dreads: it's a mere computer which dreams that it's a robot" (p. 160). **Critics:** SHRDLU, lacking real perception and action, has no beliefs. **Gentle reply (Dennett's description of the defense):** adding real cameras and arms would be costly and of little psychological interest. The blocks world is an admissible notional world (in the set of Tarski models for the core system) and an innocent bit of verisimilitude (p. 160). Dennett says this defense would be optimistic in SHRDLU's actual case: clothing it in robotics would not be trivial, and by keeping the world notional Winograd excused himself from many hard problems. Yet it is, he thinks, the assumed ideal line of defense of such research (p. 161). **Difference from Husserl:** SHRDLU's "introspections" have no privileged role. Its notional world is fixed from outside by public facts. The counterpart of the Cartesian claim is that even all public facts underdetermine propositional attitudes. This places hetero-phenomenology on the naturalistic side of the Jamesian division (p. 161). Imagined ideal environments are common in engineering (engines pulling a fictional horse) and help compare *functional* similarity independent of implementation (p. 161).

**Comparing notional worlds (pp. 162–163).** *Romeo and Juliet* and *West Side Story* are similar in what is represented, independent of means (script, film, opera) and not syntactically; what is represented must be taken to be elements of a notional world. Notional worlds are agent-centered, so to compare them one superimposes the centers. Two paranoids then appear similar, while a masochist and sadistic partner differ despite similar casts (p. 162; fn. 15). A rigorous decision procedure for comparison is dim, but so are the prospects for conditions of propositional-attitude identity: my belief that salt is sodium chloride and the chemist's cannot be crisply matched. A language of thought would not help, because sentences are already comparable (the chemist and I use the same words) and the problem of comparing beliefs remains even if our brains use the same Mentalese words (p. 163).

**Contradictory beliefs (pp. 163–164).** Bill allegedly believes both that Tom is to be trusted and that he is not. Finding both sentences in his brain would not settle which he believes: one may be vestigial, or intermittently consulted, or his behaviour may conflict. In none of the cases does the presence or absence of an explicit contradiction in Mentalese play more than a peripheral supporting role in calling Bill vacillating, forgetful, indecisive or irrational. Behaviour counts for more but does not settle it either (pp. 163–164). To call someone irrational is, in part, to say he ill fits his niche. In bad cases we may be unable to devise any notional world for him. An avowedly inconsistent description would not be of *a* notional world (a set of possible worlds cannot have contradictory properties). His notional world may be shattered into fragmentary, overlapping, competing worlds. Offering an inconsistent description is a surrender, like lapsing into direct quotation (*Nothing noths*) (p. 164).

**What recommends it? (pp. 164–165).** Notional-world hetero-phenomenology does not settle the disputes or sharpen the boundaries of everyday belief-thinking; it inherits the problems and recasts them (p. 164). Building the notional world of an actual creature from its physical constitution is a remote prospect. The other direction is more promising: start from a description of a notional world and design a "creature" with it. AI starts with representational *powers* and works toward *means* (p. 165). Kaplan's *vivid names*, Schiffer's *modes of presentation* and Searle's *aspects* are supposed to be definable in narrow psychology, so notional attitude psychology should in principle capture them. This is to be explored in the next section (p. 165; fn. 18).

**Further skepticism (pp. 165–166).** A world constituted by the subject's mind has been a leitmotif in philosophy since Descartes (idealism, phenomenology, verificationism, the coherence theory of truth; Goodman 1978, Putnam 1978). Its ubiquity is no proof of soundness; it may be an eternally tempting mistake (p. 165). It also clashes with a compelling intuition about reference. If notional attitudes are the counterpart of character, then:

$$\text{notional attitude} + \text{environment} \to \text{propositional attitude}$$

So a switched Doppelgänger and I would wake with attitudes about each other's planet. This is counterintuitive to many people (not all): after the switch, his thinking *I wonder if Susan has made the coffee yet* is surely not about *my* wife. That intuition is the essence of the *causal theory of reference* (Kripke, Evans, Donnellan), and the thought experiment isolates it (p. 166; fn. 19: the first-person pronoun is not affected by world-switching).

> **Intuition note.** Notional world theory splits a belief into what is fixed by the creature (the notional attitude) and what the setting adds (the proposition). Its schema implies that an unnoticed switch of environment changes what one's thoughts are about; Dennett reports that this is counterintuitive to many and that it clashes with the causal theory of reference (p. 166).

### Shakey's Pizza Parlor and *de re* / *de dicto* beliefs (pp. 166–173)

(No section heading is visible on these pages for this stretch. See Reading notes.)

**The story (pp. 167–168).** Two near-identical Shakey's Pizza Parlors stand some fifty miles apart (Costa Mesa and Westwood Village). Dick and Harry trick Tom: Dick drugs his beer in Costa Mesa, and they move him asleep to the Westwood Village parlor, where he wakes. Tom bets that his initials are carved on the leftmost stall door of the men's room. The exact wording of the written wager would decide who wins. A wager naming the Costa Mesa Shakey's makes Tom win. A wager naming the parlor in which they are currently seated makes Dick win. A sentence with a vacuous name or description would make the wager ill formed (or false, for a strict Russellian about definite descriptions). Tom wrote down the Westwood door (not under that description), lost, and admitted it. Which door had he had in mind? He could rightly say the Costa Mesa door (he vividly recalled carving it), but he also pictured the door a few feet away (p. 168).

**The philosophers' distinction (p. 168).** Dennett says everyone knows the distinction between belief *de re* and belief *de dicto* in his heart, but it is hard to characterize precisely and uncontroversially. He marks it by using "of" for *de re* and "that" for *de dicto*. Bill believes *that* the captain of the Soviet Ice Hockey team is a man, but not *of* the captain that he is, since Bill is unacquainted with him. He does believe *of* his own father that he is a man (pp. 168–169).

**Tom's *de re* beliefs (p. 169).** Tom's causal contact entitles him to *de re* beliefs about both parlors:

| | Costa Mesa Shakey's | Westwood Village Shakey's |
|---|---|---|
| True | bought a pizza in it tonight; dozed off in it; put his hat on a peg in it | is in it now; woke up in it; his hat is on a peg in it |
| False | is in it now; woke up in it; his hat is on a peg in it | bought a pizza in it tonight; dozed off in it; puts his hat on a peg in it |

So Tom has a dual list; every true *de re* belief has a false twin about a different object. Yet there is something unitary in his psychological state: we could say there is unity in his notional world and duality in the real world, so each notional attitude spawns a pair of propositional attitudes (p. 169).

**Awkward consequences (pp. 169–170).** Tom believes *of* his hat that it is on a peg behind his head. Can he believe *of that peg*, with which his only causal link has been gravitational attraction, that his hat is on it? The causal theorist must deny it. Tom believes *that* his hat is on a peg behind his head (true) and *that* the peg is wooden, but not *of that peg*. He believes truly that there is a leftmost stall door in the adjacent room and falsely that it has his initials; so he believes *that* the leftmost stall door has his initials but not *of* the leftmost stall door that it does (p. 170). Philosophers disagree: Kaplan (1968) would say the rapport with the peg is too casual; Kaplan (1978) would weaken the causal requirement; others accept the oddities as implications of a good theory pushed *in extremis* (p. 170).

**Dennett: no predictive difference in this case (pp. 170–171).** The schools do not differ in what they predict Tom will do (e.g. which sentences he bets on). Each retrospectively redescribes the losing bet. So in the imagined case, if not perhaps in other, more normal cases, the presence or absence of a particular *de re* belief plays no predictive, hence no explanatory, role. **He asks** whether we should then abandon the concept for one that characterizes the crucial variables in both normal and abnormal cases (p. 171). He then qualifies this.

**The qualification (p. 171).** The failure may come from focusing too narrowly on a contrived *local* indiscernibility. Outlandish cases (practical jokes, mirrors, gorilla suits, twins) produce only momentary or unstable effects of the desired mistaken identity. Our capacity to keep track of things through time is not well described by theories that atomize psychology into momentary states (fn. 21). Perhaps this follows: formal semantics needs an object fixed at a time and context for truth or reference, and overt linguistic behaviour supplies candidates (utterances). Moving the game inside and positing analogous mental objects does violence to the psychological situation, and anyone who imports formal-semantic categories into psychology is "bound to create a monster." It is not yet clear whether this threatens *all* versions of mental representation theory, all those that suppose syntactic objects in the head with a principled semantic interpretation (pp. 171–172).

**Robot Theater (p. 172).** On *Laugh-In*, robots played by Arte Johnson and Judy Carne try to act out domestic routines but their actions miss (grasping just short of the doorknob, pouring coffee beside the cup). Their notional worlds did not quite match the real world; half an inch of displacement before they started would, Dennett says, have made things go swimmingly. Their internal representation was updated by snapshots, not continuously. The skit's joke, in Dennett's description, is that *we are not at all like that.* Dennett then asks whether we are or are not like that, and reports the hope of cognitive science that we are like that, only much better (p. 172). Cognitive science can treat the *de re/de dicto* anomalies as experiments inducing pathology in the machinery, rich in clues to its design. That one must work hard to contrive such cases shows how good we are at updating. The process is practically continuous but has a perspicuous description as swift revision of an internal model. Verbal informing raises the chance of dislocations and of notional objects with no real counterparts; these problems are soluble within cognitive science (p. 173; fn. 22 on the frame problem, which is either the most difficult problem AI must, and can eventually, solve, or the *reductio ad absurdum* of mental representation theory).

**Closing paragraphs of this part (p. 173).** **This suggests** that the Shakey's puzzles come from applying a single set of categories to two or more very different styles of cognitive operation. In one, we have internal representations whose content guides behaviour. In the other, we have procedures for *keeping track* of things in the world, which let us consult the things themselves and minimize our *representations* of them. No one has yet disentangled the goals and assumptions of the various converging theoretical enterprises. Armed tentatively with notional worlds, which Dennett calls picturesque but not demonstrably sound, perhaps progress can come from considering the origins of the problematic distinctions in the theoretical problems that gave birth to them (p. 173).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett says the puzzle cases must be contrived with effort because we are good at updating our internal representations. He suggests they are better read as clues about how that machinery is built than as a test of a belief taxonomy (p. 173).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show "notional attitude + environment → propositional attitude" (the schema on p. 166) and why two people can share a narrow state yet differ in propositional attitude and truth.

Setup. Both Anna and Ben have the same internal state $N$: "the nearest hill is steep." Define "steep" as slope $\ge 30\%$.
- Anna's environment $E_A$: nearest hill $H_1$ with slope 40%.
- Ben's environment $E_B$: nearest hill $H_2$ with slope 10%.

Function $N + E \to$ proposition (about a particular hill):
- $N + E_A$ → "$H_1$ is steep." Test: $40 \ge 30$, so **true**.
- $N + E_B$ → "$H_2$ is steep." Test: $10 \ge 30$ is false, so **false**.

Anna and Ben have identical $N$ but different propositions and truth values. Same $N$ with the environments swapped gives Anna "$H_2$ is steep" (false) and Ben "$H_1$ is steep" (true). The difference comes entirely from $E$, as in the Twin Earth switch.

Set-of-worlds version of a notional world. Suppose three worlds: $w_1$ (hill 40%, people call it "steep"), $w_2$ (hill 35%, people call it "steep"), $w_3$ (hill 10%, people call it "gentle"). The description "the nearest hill is one people call steep" is consistent with $w_1, w_2$ but not $w_3$. The notional world is $\{w_1, w_2\}$, which has $2$ of $3$ worlds. It does not pick a unique world, as on p. 158.

## Concepts
- [[propositional-attitude]] — a mental state relating a person to a proposition; Dennett says the technical term has no stable interpretation (pp. 117, 120–121).
- [[intentional-stance]] — idealizing characterization of a system (e.g. a frog) by belief-talk; beyond the best approximation, no fact of what it "really believes" (p. 116).
- [[folk-psychology]] — ordinary belief-talk psychology; Dennett says the coming chapters give a more realistic sense of what academic psychology might make of it (p. 116).
- [[referential-opacity]] — Jocasta vs the mother of Oedipus: different psychological consequences despite identity (p. 131).
- [[intentionality]] — aboutness; here handled by the notional world, with Pickwick-aboutness and Brentano's intentional objects (pp. 153, 159).
- [[fregean-conditions-on-propositions]] (new) — (a) truth-value-bearer, (b) composed of intensions, (c) graspable by the mind; jointly unsatisfiable according to the cited authors (pp. 121–127).
- [[graspability-as-projectibility]] (new) — Dennett's weak reading: attitude predicates are projectible, predictive predicates of psychological theory (p. 126).
- [[narrow-psychology]] / [[methodological-solipsism]] (new) — studying the organismic contribution to attitudes in isolation from environment (pp. 134–135).
- [[language-of-thought]] / [[sententialism]] (new) — Mentalese sentences; sententialism: believers share beliefs iff they share tokens of sentences of a language of thought (p. 137).
- [[character-content-schema]] (new) — Kaplan's stages from syntax to extension, extended by Dennett to $C+E\to I$ (pp. 132, 135, 137, 150).
- [[determinateness-vs-explicitness]] (new) — a subsystem's contribution may be determinate without being expressible as a sentence (pp. 147–149).
- [[notional-world]] / [[notional-attitude]] (new) — a theorist's fictional world, perhaps a set of possible worlds, used to characterize narrow states; consists of things represented (pp. 152–155, 158–159).
- [[hetero-phenomenology]] (new) — determining another subject's notional world from the outside, in contrast with the introspective auto-phenomenology of the Brentano–Husserl tradition (p. 153).
- [[de-re-de-dicto]] (new) — the distinction Dennett marks with "of" and "that"; he says it is hard to characterize precisely (p. 168).

## Canonical instances
- [[twin-earth]] — physical replicas with different attitudes; Dennett supposes the argument sound without endorsing it (pp. 127–129).
- [[wallet-twin-variant]] — Dennett's variant of Twin Earth without natural kinds (p. 128).
- [[kaplan-today-yesterday]] — same Thought, possibly unrecognized as the same (pp. 128–129).

- [[shakeys-pizza-parlor]] — Tom's dual list of *de re* beliefs (pp. 167–170).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] notional attitude + environment → propositional attitude ↔ theory-of-mind: belief ascription as a function of an agent's internal state plus what the observer knows of the setting — both factor a belief into agent-side and world-side contributions.
- [structural-analogy] notional world as the set of worlds consistent with a description ↔ belief-state models: an agent's belief as a set of possible world-states consistent with its evidence.
- [metaphor] SHRDLU's notional blocks world ↔ machine theory of mind: evaluating a system in a simulated environment; the analogy is loose.
- [structural-analogy] "keeping track" of things vs internal representations (p. 173) ↔ perspective-taking: relying on the world as external memory instead of re-representing it.

## Questions this raises
- If *de re* beliefs play no predictive role in the Shakey's case, in which more normal cases might they matter (p. 171)?
- What would "determinate but not explicit" content look like in a real cognitive model (pp. 147–149)?
- How could one test whether people in different cultures share a language of thought or only a "syntax-neutral" role-structure (pp. 138–140)?
- Does hetero-phenomenology (building a notional world from outside) differ in practice from ordinary belief ascription (pp. 153–158)?
