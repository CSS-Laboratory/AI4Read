# Three Kinds of Intentional Psychology (and "Instrumentalism Reconsidered")

## L0 — One line
Dennett suggests that folk belief–desire psychology might best be viewed as an abstract, idealizing, rationality-based calculus; the part worth keeping reduces, conceptually, to an intentional system theory that he keeps separate from the sub-personal science of how brains realize it.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks what everyday belief–desire talk (folk psychology) is, and whether science could reduce it to neurophysiology. His suggestion: folk psychology might best be viewed as an abstract calculus for predicting behaviour by treating systems as if they were rational agents. He splits folk psychology into two theories. Intentional system theory says what a believer must be able to do and stays silent on implementation. Sub-personal cognitive psychology asks how brains manage it. Beliefs are *abstracta* (calculation-bound constructs), like centers of gravity, and need not be inner states. So he proposes conceptual, not microphysical, reduction. The Reflections restate his instrumentalism: realism about brains, centers-of-gravity status for beliefs. The stance predicts without knowledge of mechanisms.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a scientific theory and of reduction (e.g. chemistry to physics); belief and desire as everyday explanations of action; simple logical notation $(x)(Fx \equiv Gx)$; what a computer or Turing machine is, roughly.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Socratic questions (what do all believers-that-p share?) can get a conceptual or a causal answer. Dennett says the two answers need not conflict, and that a better view of Ryle, or one he ought to have held, deserves rehabilitation (pp. 43–45).
- Folk psychology is a learned craft, like our knowledge of grammar. On Dennett's proposal it works through a rationality assumption: a system believes and desires what it *ought* to, and does what it would be rational to do. He calls this a myth, but one that works very well because we are pretty rational (pp. 46–52).
- Dennett calls folk psychology idealized (it calculates what we ought to believe and do), abstract (not committed to inner states) and instrumentalistic (a calculating device, yet people really do have beliefs). Beliefs and desires are *abstracta* (calculation-bound), not *illata* (posited inner entities). The ordinary notion of belief sits somewhere midway between the two, which suggests to him that it is unappealing as a scientific concept (pp. 52–57).
- So he proposes a "divorce": pure intentional system theory (a competence theory, neutral on implementation) and sub-personal cognitive psychology (a performance theory about brains) (pp. 57–61).
- The brain can only respond to the form of its inputs (a syntactic engine), yet its job is to respond to their meaning (a semantic engine); it can only approximate this, by a bag of tricks (pp. 61–63). Semantic properties cannot be captured by micro-reduction, that is, from the physical parts alone (p. 65).
- Dennett proposes, as a prospect worth exploring, that the part of folk psychology worth caring about reduces conceptually to intentional system theory. Intentional systems are meant to legitimize mentalistic talk, as Turing machines do for computational talk. He finds type-type identity theory (each mental type matched to a physical type) and Turing-machine functionalism (each matched to a machine state) too strong; if mentalistic talk can be legitimized in this way, no reduction is needed (pp. 65–68).
- Dennett's instrumentalism is realism about brains plus centers-of-gravity status for beliefs; he says attributions can be true, but must be understood with a grain of salt (pp. 71–73).
- Competence models (what a system can do; Marr's computational level) predict well but need not mirror the mechanism's parts. Examples: the Newfie joke, the pocket calculator, chess opponents (pp. 74–81).

## L3 — Walkthrough

Dennett lists *leitmotifs* that return here and later: some of the most disputed questions about belief attribution are "empty questions" in Parfit's sense; a false contrast between our beliefs and those of lower animals creates the conviction that beliefs must have determinate content; the Twin Earth case is compared with exchanges of thermostats; the language-of-thought endorsement is only "attenuated and conditional" (p. 42).

### Folk Psychology as a Source of Theory (pp. 43–57)

**Definition versus theory (pp. 43–44).**
- Following Socrates, the question what two believers-that-cats-eat-fish must share is ambiguous or vague in intent. It can ask for something like a definition or something like a theory (p. 43).
- *Magnets:* first answer, they attract iron. Second answer, they share a microphysical property that explains this capacity. People knew what magnets were long before science told them (p. 43).
- A child typically learns "magnet" by learning the folk physics in which the term is embedded, not by an explicit definition (p. 43).
- *Valence:* first answer, disposed to combine in integral ratios. Second answer, a microphysical property explaining this. The theory of valences was in hand before its microphysical explanation (pp. 43–44).
- So what appears in Plato to be a contrast between definition and theory can be viewed as a special case of the contrast between one theoretical answer and another, more reductive one (p. 44).

**Ryle and Fodor (pp. 44–45).**
- *Fodor's charge:* Fodor (1975) contrasts "conceptual" and "causal" answers. He argues Ryle favours the conceptual answers and wrongly takes them to conflict with causal ones (p. 44).
- *Dennett's verdict:* there is justice in the charge, but a better view of Ryle (or at least one he ought to have held) deserves rehabilitation. If Ryle thought conceptual answers rule out causal answers, he was wrong. If he thought only that a microreductive psychology cannot supply the conceptual answers, he was on firmer ground (p. 44).
- Some concepts have an essential causal element. A genuine Churchill autograph must have been caused in the right way, which rules out photocopies and forgeries (p. 44).
- Some, including Fodor, hold that intelligent action is like this. Ryle can reply that, even if every intelligent act is caused, *how* it is caused is inessential. That could be true even if all intelligent behaviour in fact shared a causal pattern. That is, Ryle can plausibly claim that no causal account could capture the class of intelligent actions except *per accidens* (pp. 44–45).
- Footnote 1 says this paragraph corrects a misrepresentation of both Fodor's and Ryle's positions in Dennett's earlier review (p. 45).
- Ryle's logical behaviorism was tainted by an anti-scientific bias, but need not have been. Valence in chemistry was "logical chemical behaviorism": by definition, an element of valence $n$ is disposed to behave in certain ways, however physics later explains this. Chemistry has reduced to physics "in some sense" (p. 45).
- The parallel plan for psychology: answer the question what all believers-that-$p$ have in common conceptually first, then see whether the resulting theory can be "reduced" to something else, neurophysiology most likely (p. 45).
- The open issue is what *kind* of theoretical bonds to expect between psychological claims and those of the physical sciences (pp. 45–46).

*Intuition note.* A "what is X?" question can be answered by the role X plays in everyday theory, or by the physical stuff that fills the role. Both can be right at once. Dennett wants to do the first for belief before asking about the second.

**What folk psychology is (pp. 46–47).**
- "Belief" and "desire" are ordinary words, like "magnet", not technical terms like "valence". So we must first look at folk psychology (p. 46).
- We learn to *use* folk psychology as a vernacular social technology or craft, not as an explicit theory. Our knowledge is like our knowledge of the grammar of our native tongue (p. 46).
- Footnote 2: philosophers' use of "believe" as the general ordinary term is "a considerable distortion". We usually talk of what people *think* and *know* (p. 46).
- Roughly, beliefs are information-bearing states that arise from perceptions and, with appropriately related desires, lead to intelligent action. That much is "relatively uncontroversial" (p. 46).
- Many questions remain open: do animals have beliefs, what role does language play, are beliefs built of parts, do they enter causal relations, how reliable is introspection (pp. 46–47).
- There are two reasons for studying folk psychology (p. 47):
  - *Anthropological:* it is a phenomenon like a religion. The account should include whatever folk include, however misguided.
  - *Proto-scientific:* it seems largely true, so it is a candidate for incorporation into science. This quest should be critical and eliminate what is false or ill founded.
- A way to separate the essential from the gratuitous is to ask what must be kept to explain its predictive and explanatory success. It is open to us in the end to discard folk psychology, and with it the presumed theoretical entities, if it is a bad theory. We would then need a replacement that explains the predictive power of the residual folk craft (p. 47).

**Strengths and weaknesses (pp. 47–48).**
- We use folk psychology constantly, and it is as pervasive as our folk physics of middle-sized objects (pp. 47–48).
- *Weaknesses:* we often cannot make sense of behaviour even in retrospect, cannot predict reliably, and cannot settle disputes about attributions (p. 48).
- *Four strengths:* (i) it is extraordinarily reliable in large areas, e.g. highway driving; (ii) it has great generative power and efficiency, e.g. reading a film plot at a glance; (iii) small children acquire it with very limited experience; (iv) we use it knowing next to nothing about what happens in people's skulls (p. 48).
- Explanations citing beliefs and desires normally not only describe an action's provenance but also defend it as reasonable. They are reason-giving explanations, which make an ineliminable allusion to the agent's rationality (p. 48).

**The proposal: a rationalistic calculus (pp. 48–51).**
- Dennett suggests that folk psychology might best be viewed as a rationalistic calculus of interpretation and prediction: an idealizing, abstract, instrumentalistic method that has evolved because it works (pp. 48–49).
- We approach each other as *intentional systems* (Dennett 1971): entities predicted by attributing beliefs, desires and rational acumen by these rough principles (p. 49):
  1. A system's beliefs are those it *ought to have*, given its perceptual capacities, epistemic needs and biography. In general they are true and relevant. False beliefs need a special story about deceptive features of the environment.
  2. Its desires are those it *ought to have*, given its biological needs and the most practicable means of satisfying them (survival, procreation, food, security, etc.). "Abnormal" desires need special stories.
  3. Its behaviour consists of the acts it *would be rational* for an agent with those beliefs and desires to perform.
- *Ought to have* means would have if it were *ideally* ensconced in its environmental niche (p. 49).
- The crude version needs refinement. The relativity to perceptual capacities is shown by a colorblind being in an environment where green food is poisonous. Relativity to biography means it will have learned what it was given relevant evidence for, in a form compatible with its cognitive apparatus (p. 49).
- Evolution gives a "passable jury-rig", not a best of all possible worlds. So we should expect *design shortcuts* that yield false perceptual beliefs in abnormal circumstances (illusions). We should also expect *design bonuses*, where the cheap design also works outside the evolutionary environment: our eyes give true beliefs on Mars (p. 50).
- Dennett proposes continuing this thinking all the way in, to deliberation design and belief design. It imputes no occult powers, only those common sense already imputes to evolution and learning (p. 50).
- We treat each other as rational agents. This myth works very well because we are *pretty* rational. Together with home truths about needs, capacities and circumstances, this single assumption generates attributions and predictions in profusion (p. 50).
- Folk psychology can thus be viewed as a sort of logical behaviorism. To believe that $p$ is to be disposed to behave in certain ways under certain conditions, namely the ways it would be rational to behave given the person's other beliefs and desires (p. 50).
- *Circularity reply:* this looks circular, but an account of what it is for an element to have a given valence also refers to the valences of other elements. We get a system of interlocking attributions, which is saved from vacuity because it yields independently testable predictions (p. 50).

*Intuition note.* We cannot ascribe one belief alone. We ascribe a whole profile that makes rational sense together, and the test of the profile is whether its behavioural predictions come true.

**Could we do it, and do we? (pp. 50–52).**
- We *could* use the method: Dennett thinks the answer is obviously yes, which one can ascertain by reflection and thought experiment. This does not mean it always gives good results (pp. 50–51).
- Do we in fact use it? We use it self-consciously when perplexed, and then it often works. The ease of this gives "some support" for the claim that we are not switching methods but making explicit what we do tacitly (p. 51).
- Dennett thinks no other view of folk psychology can explain why we predict so well on slender evidence. It works to the extent that evolution designed us well, so we *approximate* the ideal (p. 51).
- But evolution does not guarantee that we will always do what is rational; Dennett says it guarantees that we won't. If we are designed by evolution, we are almost certainly nothing more than a bag of tricks patched together by a *satisficing* Nature (Simon's term). Nature's demands differ from a logic course's, so there was probably some evolutionary pressure toward "irrational" methods (p. 51).
- Footnote 3: true beliefs are generally more useful, but in special circumstances a few false beliefs may be better. Example: a beast $B$ that believes "I can't beat up $x$" about some beasts it could in fact beat. He adds that erring on the side of prudence is a well-recognized good strategy and "costs less" in discrimination tasks (p. 51).

**How rational are we? (p. 52).**
- Tversky and Kahneman (1974) and Nisbett and Ross (1978) suggest we are only minimally rational. Dennett says this jaundiced view is an illusion, because these psychologists deliberately design situations that provoke irrational responses (p. 52).
- A more optimistic impression comes from artificial intelligence (AI) research: even sophisticated AI programs make misunderstandings that small children avoid (p. 52).
- However rational we are, the *myth* of our rational agenthood structures our attributions and our own deliberation. Without the myth, the concepts of belief and desire would, he says, be uprooted (p. 52).
- Folk psychology is *idealized*: it predicts what we will believe, desire and do by determining what we *ought* to. Footnote 4: it tests action predictions directly, and belief/desire predictions indirectly through further action predictions. As usual, attributions are underdetermined by the data (the Duhemian thesis) (p. 52).
- It is *abstract*: the beliefs and desires it attributes are not, or need not be, presumed to be distinguishable intervening states of an internal causal system. The concept of belief is like that of a center of gravity. The calculations are like those with a parallelogram of forces, not with a blueprint of levers and cogs (p. 52).
- It is *instrumentalistic*, but in a way the most ardent realist should permit, he says. People really do have beliefs and desires, as they really have centers of gravity and the earth has an Equator (p. 53).
- Reichenbach distinguished *illata* (posited theoretical entities) from *abstracta* (calculation-bound entities or logical constructs). The beliefs and desires of folk psychology, but not all mental events and states, are *abstracta* (p. 53).

*Intuition note.* Dennett says people really have beliefs and desires in the way they really have centers of gravity: as calculation-bound *abstracta*, not as parts in the furniture of the physical world (pp. 53, 72).

**An opposing view and two steps away from conflict (pp. 53–54).**
- **Opposing view** (each tenet has been held by some philosopher, and at least most have been espoused by Fodor, p. 53): beliefs and desires are real, intervening internal states in causal interaction under covering laws. Folk psychology is a naturalistic, empirical, descriptive theory built by induction. To suppose two people share a belief is, on this view, to suppose them to be in some structurally similar internal condition, e.g. the same words of Mentalese in functionally relevant places (p. 53).
- *Step 1:* Dennett grants "a measure" of this. We do not derive all attributions from idealized rationality. We also use empirical generalizations learned inductively. Examples: children believe in Santa Claus; people tend toward the more self-serving interpretation unless depressed; people can be made to want things by linking them to glamorous people. These are not laws, even probabilistic ones, though some become science ("hot cognition", cognitive dissonance) (p. 54).
- We rely on our own parochial group of neighbors, which Dennett says explains difficulty understanding foreigners. This is a reasonable shortcut for satisficers, but he insists that the empirical lore is laid over a fundamental generative and normative framework (p. 54).
- *Step 2:* the issue is not what folk psychology truly is in the field, but what it is at its best. To refute Dennett, one must show that it *ought* to be committed to distinguishable, causally interacting *illata* (p. 54).
- Dennett could concede that folk psychology *is* so committed, but does not. He says the evidence is quite strong that our ordinary notion of belief has next to nothing of the concrete in it (p. 54).

**Jacques, Sherlock, Tom and Boris (pp. 54–55).**
- Jacques shoots his uncle in Trafalgar Square and is caught by Sherlock. Tom reads of it in the *Guardian* and Boris in *Pravda*. Their experiences differ greatly, yet all believe a Frenchman committed murder in Trafalgar Square (pp. 54–55).
- None need have said it, even to themselves, and it would mean different things to each. The shared property is visible only from the point of view of folk psychology (p. 55).
- If folk psychologists insist that in imputing this belief they are postulating a similarly structured object in each head, Dennett calls this a gratuitous bit of misplaced concreteness (p. 55).

**A mixed bag; Anaxagoras's seeds; core beliefs (pp. 55–56).**
- Folk psychology is a mixed bag, and there is no reason not to grant that it is much more complex, variegated (and in danger of incoherence) than Dennett's sketch. The ordinary notion of belief no doubt does place beliefs somewhere *midway* between *illata* and *abstracta*. This suggests to him that the folk concept of belief is unappealing as a scientific concept (p. 55).
- *Analogy:* Anaxagoras's seeds, where everything contains seeds of everything and bread arises when bread seeds form a majority. They are bad theoretical entities, a "bastardized cross" between properties and parts, prone to regress with problematic identity conditions. Beliefs are "rather like that" (p. 55).
- There seems no way to avoid saying we have infinitely many beliefs. Intuition gives no stable answer to whether the belief that 3 > 2 is the same as the belief that 2 < 3 (p. 55).
- The obvious response is that most beliefs are stored *implicitly*, by virtue of a small number of explicitly stored *core beliefs*. Core beliefs look like promising *illata*; virtual beliefs look like paradigmatic *abstracta* (pp. 55–56).
- Dennett suspects it is more complicated. There is no reason to suppose that the core elements (there must be some in any complex information-processing system) will explicitly represent, or be, a subset of our beliefs at all. All of your thousand paradigm beliefs could be virtual, and the explicit store might hold entirely unfamiliar information (e.g. memory addresses, procedures). It is folly to prejudge this empirical issue (p. 56).

**Causal explanation by belief (pp. 56–57).**
- Ordinary causal talk ("Her belief that John knew her secret caused her to blush") can be saved: whatever the core elements were, they played the direct causal role. Dennett says we should not tamper with the ordinary catalogue of beliefs, even if all turn out virtual, since it gives predictable, manipulable regularities (p. 56).
- What the four men share is probably only a virtual belief derived from very different explicit stores. Sharing it would still explain or predict common action on new information, e.g. the "jackpot question" (pp. 56–57).
- "Had Tom not believed that $p$ and wanted that $q$, he would not have done $A$." Is this causal? Dennett's analysis: Tom was in one of an indefinitely large number of structurally different states of type $B$. Each licenses attribution of belief that $p$ and desire that $q$ through its normal relations with many other states. This state, given background conditions, was causally sufficient to initiate the intention to do $A$, and without a type-$B$ state he would not have done $A$ (p. 57).
- Dennett calls this surely as unspecific and unhelpful as a causal explanation can get. It commits itself to there being some causal explanation or other in a very broad area (the intentional interpretation is held to be supervenient on Tom's bodily condition), but its true informativeness and utility in prediction lie in asserting that Tom, however his body is structured, has a particular set of these elusive intentional properties, beliefs and desires (p. 57).
- **The "divorce":** because the ordinary notion is pulled in two directions, split it into two theories (p. 57):
  - one strictly abstract, idealizing, holistic and instrumentalistic: *pure intentional system theory*;
  - one concrete and microtheoretical, about the actual realization of intentional systems: *sub-personal cognitive psychology*.
- Their differences and interrelations should show whether plausible reductions are in the offing (p. 57).

### Intentional System Theory as a Competence Theory (pp. 58–60)
- Intentional system theory is a close kin of decision theory and game theory, which are also abstract, normative and couched in intentional language. It borrows "belief" and "desire" but gives them a technical meaning (p. 58).
- It is a *holistic* logical behaviorism. It predicts and explains the actions of whole systems from belief–desire profiles and treats their realizations as black boxes. The subject of attribution is the whole system (person, animal, even a corporation or nation), not its parts (p. 58).
- Individual beliefs cannot be attributed in isolation. This is the clearest difference from Ryle, whose behaviorism took on the "impossible burden" of treating individual beliefs as individual dispositions (p. 58).
- The theory deals with the "production" of new beliefs and desires from old. This creates an *illusion* of naturalistic descriptions of internal processing, when the processing is in the manipulation of the theory. A naive student might likewise take a parallelogram of forces to picture a mechanical linkage (p. 58).
- Jeffrey's analogy with physics (pp. 58–59): kinematics (position, time, velocity, acceleration) gives a simplified, idealized level appropriate for, e.g., the *initial* design of a gearbox. For friction, bending and efficiency one must switch to dynamics, at the cost of increased complexity and diminished generality (p. 59).
- Belief can be studied at a highly abstract level, setting out normative demands on the design of a believer. Examples: what epistemic capabilities must a system have to survive in environment $A$? What must it already know to learn $B$? What intentions are needed to mean something by saying something? (p. 59).
- Intentional system theory gives *performance specifications* and is silent on implementation. This neutrality is "the most useful feature" of intentional characterizations (p. 59).
- *Evolutionary biology:* it is the intentionally characterized capacity (to acquire a belief, desire or intention) that has survival value, however realized. If a noxious insect appears, birds and bats that come to believe it is not good to eat have the advantage. Given their very different neural structure, this shared trait probably has no common description more concrete than the intentional one (p. 59).
- The intentional predicate is projectible and more general than species-specific counterparts, so it is preferable. Even if we knew the implementation, evolutionary biology would not want to "reduce" all intentional characterizations (p. 59).
- This generality is essential for saying anything defensible about intelligence in general, meaning, reference or representation. Dennett's Martian case: if they are smart enough to get here, they "most certainly" have beliefs and desires in the technical sense, whatever their internal structure and however folk intuitions rebel (p. 60).
- **Retort** (Fodor 1985, p. 79, cited by Dennett): there must be *some* explanation of the success of intentional prediction. Internal processes must mirror the intentional interpretation's complexity, or success would be a miracle (p. 60).
- **Dennett's reply:** He agrees: this is all quite true and important. The realization's complexity will resemble the interpretation's complexity, as with valence. But individuals are also products of individual learning histories, so there is no reason to suppose individual believers-that-$p$ regulate dispositions with *exactly* the same machinery, as atoms of valence 4 do. Studying implementation variation is the job of the third theory (p. 60).

*Intuition note.* Intentional system theory says *what* a system must be able to do, not *how* it does it. That is why it applies to birds, bats, Martians and machines alike, and also why it can say little about any one mechanism.

### Sub-personal Cognitive Psychology as a Performance Theory (pp. 61–65)

**The syntax–semantics problem (pp. 61–63).**
- The brain, as intentional system theory and evolutionary biology show, is a *semantic engine*: its task is to discover what inputs *mean* and "act accordingly". But as physiology shows, it is just a *syntactic engine*: it can only discriminate inputs by structural, temporal and physical features (p. 61).
- The syntax of a system does not determine its semantics, so no entity could get semantics from syntax alone. The brain cannot be designed to do an impossible task, but it can *approximate* it by exploiting fortuitous correspondences between structural regularities and semantic types (p. 61).
- *Example 1:* an animal needs to know when it has eaten but settles for a "friction-in-the-throat-followed-by-stretched-stomach detector". A mechanical switch normally co-occurs with real goal satisfaction. It is easily tricked but works in the normal environment (p. 61).
- *Example 2:* a machine to intercept English death threats. A perfect machine would need *death threat in English* defined as a feature of symbol strings, with no reason to expect a principled definition. A crude filter for patterns such as "...I will kill you..." or "...you...die...unless..." has some utility and can be refined. If you want to get semantics out of syntax, variations on this basic strategy are, Dennett says, your only hope (p. 62).
- Footnote 9: one might "cheat" by exploiting syntactic cues that strongly suggest semantics (e.g. the duck-looking symbol is eaten by the wolf-looking symbol). Dennett calls this hoping for hieroglyphics. It cannot be the brain's fundamental method of getting semantic classes out of raw syntax, because a brain that could be said to know what dogs look like would already have solved its problem (pp. 62–63).
- The most one can hope for, and the most natural selection can have produced, are systems that *seem* to discriminate meanings by discriminating tokens, of wildly disjunctive types, that co-vary reliably with meanings. Brains also follow self-improvement strategies during individual lifetimes (p. 63).

**The task and its *illata* (pp. 63–64).**
- Sub-personal cognitive psychology proposes and tests models of pattern recognition, concept learning, expectation, goal-directed behaviour, problem-solving and so on. They must give a simulacrum of content-sensitivity in ways demonstrably like people's brains, with the same powers and the same vulnerabilities to deception, overload and confusion (p. 63).
- Here are the good *illata*. Some may resemble folk psychology's entities (beliefs, desires, judgments, decisions) but many will not (e.g. Stich's sub-doxastic states). The only similarity one can be sure of is the *intentionality of their labels*: events with content, bearing information (p. 63).
- To keep the intentional labels, the theorist must keep looking outside the system: what normally produces the configuration, what effects the responses have, what benefit accrues to the whole system. Ignoring this abandons semantic interpretation, and hence psychology (pp. 63–64).
- Progress will blur the boundary between this theory and intentional system theory, as chemistry and physics have been knit together (p. 64).
- **Figure (p. 64):** the panel labelled Black Box Behaviorism shows a shaded (opaque) box with arrows in and out. The panel labelled Black World Glass Box Perspectivalism shows the inside visible and the surrounding world shaded. Ignoring the external world (Putnam's "psychology in the narrow sense" or methodological solipsism; Gunderson's lampoon) is at best abstract neurophysiology, pure internal syntax with no hope of a semantic interpretation (p. 64).
- Psychology "reduced" this way would not be psychology, since it could not explain the reliability with which intelligent organisms cope with their environments. It should work toward physiological foundations by showing how the brain implements the intentionally characterized performance specifications (p. 64).

**Macro-reduction (pp. 64–65).**
- **Friedman's suggestion** (quoted): contemporary psychology explains individual cognitive activity first and then micro-reduces social cognitive activity to it. Friedman proposes the opposite: find a theory of social activity first, then give a *macro* reduction of individual cognitive activity (pp. 64–65).
- Dennett "largely" agrees but says the macro level is broader than the social. Non-language-using animals must also be covered. The macro level is organism–environment interaction, development and evolution, with social interaction a proper part (p. 65).
- There is no way to capture semantic properties by micro-reduction. They are "super-relational": the relation a token must bear to have content is a relation to the whole life, and counterfactual life, of the organism it serves, its survival requirements and its evolutionary ancestry (p. 65). Footnote 11: counterfactuals enter because content depends partly on the normal or designed role of a vehicle, whether or not it plays that role (p. 65).

*Intuition note.* A brain state does not carry meaning in its physical shape alone. What it is *for* depends on the organism's history and environment, so looking only at neurons loses the meaning.

### The Prospects of Reduction (pp. 65–68)
- Of the three psychologies (folk psychology, intentional system theory, sub-personal cognitive psychology), what might reduce to what? The one-step micro-reduction of folk psychology to physiology will never be found, and should never be missed, even by staunch friends of materialism (pp. 65–66).
- Dennett's prospect: the part of folk psychology worth caring about reduces *conceptually* to intentional system theory (p. 66).
- **Type-type identity theory:** for every mentalistic predicate $M$ there is a predicate $P$ expressible in physical-science vocabulary such that

$$(x)\,(Mx \equiv Px) \tag{1}$$

  This is now widely agreed to be too strong. Believing that cats eat fish is intuitively a functional state that might be physically realized in many ways (p. 66).
- **Turing machine functionalism:**

$$(x)\,(Mx \equiv x \text{ realizes some Turing machine } k \text{ in logical state } A) \tag{2}$$

  This is still reductionist and still too strong. There is no more reason to think Jacques, Sherlock, Boris and Tom have the same program in any relaxed sense than to think their brains share a physico-chemical feature (p. 66).
- Dennett's weakened version:

$$(x)\,(x \text{ believes that } p \equiv x \text{ can be predictively attributed the belief that } p) \tag{3}$$

  It seems blatantly circular, but an informative answer needs only a systematic way of making the attributions (p. 67).
- *Parallel with Turing machines:* two realizations are in the same logical state when some system of description says both realize a given machine and, predictively, are in the same state of its machine table. One does not *reduce* Turing machine talk; one *legitimizes* it by giving rules of attribution and exhibiting predictive power. If mentalistic talk can be legitimized likewise, no reduction is needed, and that is the point of the intentional system concept (p. 67).
- Dennett admits his concept is woefully informal and unsystematic compared with Turing's, but says the domain it systematizes is itself something of a mess (p. 67).
- **Brentano's Thesis** (intentionality is the mark of the mental) is traditionally an *irreducibility* thesis. Given intentional systems, its first half (all mental phenomena are intentional) can be read as a *reductionist* thesis, parallel to Church's Thesis (pp. 67–68).
- Church's Thesis says every "effective" procedure is Turing-computable. It is not provable, since it relies on an informal notion, but it is generally accepted and reduces a fuzzy-but-useful notion to a crisply defined one (p. 68).
- Analogously, the claim that every mental phenomenon alluded to in folk psychology is *intentional-system-characterizable* would, if true, reduce the mental as ordinarily understood to a clearly defined domain. Like Church's Thesis it cannot be proven but could be made compelling by piecemeal progress on hard cases (p. 68).
- The final reductive task is almost the reverse of the usual one: to show how a system described in physiological terms could warrant interpretation as a realized intentional system (p. 68).
- Footnote 12: Block's (1978) arguments claim functionalist theories err by "chauvinism" (excluding Martians) or "liberalism" (including contraptions, human puppets). Dennett embraces the broadest liberalism, paying the price of a few recalcitrant intuitions (p. 68).

*Intuition note.* We do not translate "Turing machine" talk into physics; we simply justify it by showing it predicts. Dennett wants belief talk justified the same way, by the predictions it supports.

### Reflections: Instrumentalism Reconsidered (pp. 69–81)

**Four other themes (pp. 69–71).**
- The chapter was written before "True Believers" but follows it in expository order (p. 69).
1. The rationality presupposed by the intentional stance is treated in the next chapter (p. 69).
2. *Syntax and semantics.* **Searle** echoes Dennett's rhetorical question: the computer, he says, has a syntax but no semantics. Searle says Dennett's behaviorism makes him unable to accept mental contents literally construed (p. 69). Searle also says only machines with the same causal powers as brains could think (p. 70). **Dennett replies:** both agree a computer is a syntactic engine. Searle thinks the organic brain somehow escapes this limit, and Dennett calls those "conjured causal powers" the very "alchemy" his rhetorical question warned against. He pursues this in chapters 8 and 9 (pp. 69–70).
3. *Operationalism.* Searle (1980b) calls Dennett's reduction of the mental to intentional systems "operationalism". Dennett does not mind and avows sympathy with Turing's vision (p. 70).
4. *Core versus virtual beliefs.* Even if the brain must be organized into a modest set of core elements, no reason has been given to suppose these are beliefs rather than some as yet unnamed and unimagined neural data structures of very different properties. Dennett is a staunch realist about the core information-storing elements. He doubts, with the Churchlands, that they will be recognizable as the beliefs of folk psychology. He does not draw the Churchlands' moral (pp. 70–71).

**Instrumentalism (pp. 71–73).**
- **Fodor's definition** of a Realist about propositional attitudes: (a) there are mental states whose occurrences and interactions cause behaviour in ways that respect, at least approximately, the generalizations of common-sense belief/desire psychology; and (b) these states are also semantically evaluable (p. 71).
- Dennett is "a sort of realist" but not Fodor's capital-R Realist. He expects the actual internal states causing behaviour not to be functionally individuated, even approximately, as belief/desire psychology carves things (p. 71).
- **Fodor's "First Anti-Realist option"** (quoted): the great virtue of instrumentalism is that you get all the goodness and suffer none of the pain. You need not answer hard questions about what the attitudes *are* (p. 71).
- Dennett distinguishes classical instrumentalism, a complete rejection of realism about everything unobservable to the naked senses, from his position. He has not ducked these ontological questions. He keeps a contrast between realism about brains and their parts, states and processes, and "instrumentalism" about the belief-states that appear as *abstracta* when the intentional stance is adopted (pp. 71–72).
- **Friedman** (quoted): scientists distinguish aspects of theoretical structure meant literally from those serving a representational function. No one believes that phase space or Hilbert space is part of the furniture of the physical world (p. 72).
- Dennett now judges "selective instrumentalism" a *tactical error*, given the confusion. He should have said his "ism" is whatever serious realists adopt about centers of gravity. Beliefs are *abstracta*, not furniture of the physical world, and attributions are true only if we exempt them from a certain familiar standard of literality (p. 72).
- He rejects fictionalism (theoretical statements are useful falsehoods) and the view that they are neither true nor false, mere calculating instruments (p. 72).
- Attributions of belief under the intentional stance *can* be true. But one must also grant that these are true, understood "with a grain of salt": (1) the earth–moon gravitational attraction acts between the two centers of gravity; (2) hand calculators add, subtract, multiply and divide; (3) a Vax 11/780 is a universal Turing machine; (4) the robot SHAKEY "makes line drawings even when its CRT is turned off". All belief talk has the same status (*veritas cum grano salis*), which derives from the uses of the intentional stance (pp. 72–73).

**The stance as a limiting case of the design stance (pp. 73–74).**
- The intentional stance is a limiting case of the design stance. One assumes only that whatever the design is, it is optimal (p. 73).
- In design practice a frank homunculus (an intentional subsystem) bridges a gap of ignorance: the theorist does not yet know how to design the subsystem but knows what it is supposed to do, so pretends a demon is there that wants only to do that task and knows how. Then one designs the surrounding system assuming that component is perfect (p. 73).
- *Example:* in the HWIM speech-recognition system, the phonological analysis module was temporarily played by human phonologists reading spectrograms, with another human as control module. Once the rest was designed, a machine replaced them. During testing the phonologists tried to act as *stupid* homunculi, using only acoustics and phonemes (pp. 73–74).
- Until the replacement is made, one is committed to no genuinely explanatory design assumptions about that subsystem, yet can progress on the other subsystems and the whole (p. 74).
- *Example:* von Kempelen's eighteenth-century chess "automaton" hid a human midget chess master (a literal homunculus). The intentional stance's success as a predictor is so neutral about design that it does not distinguish this from Berliner's Hitech, a strong chess program. Both are intentional systems in good standing (p. 74).

**Competence versus performance; Marr (pp. 74–76).**
- Dennett calls intentional system theory almost literally a black box theory. That makes it behavioristic to Searle and Nagel but hardly in Skinner's sense. It is an attempt at what Chomsky calls a *competence model*, as against a performance model (p. 74).
- **Marr's three levels** (p. 74–75):
  - *computational* (misleadingly named): what function the system serves, i.e. what function it must "compute", specified formally as a rule-governed mapping from an element of a set of inputs $x$ to an element of a set of outputs $y$, neutral about implementation;
  - *algorithmic*: the computational processes, neutral about physical mechanisms;
  - *hardware*: the physical implementation.
- Marr claims we cannot properly address lower-level questions until the highest level is clear. Dennett takes this, like Chomsky's parallel point, as a *strategic maxim*, not an epistemological principle. Marr underestimates how much computational-level (or intentional stance) descriptions can mislead a theorist who forgets how idealized they are (p. 75).
- Dennett's reason for "instrumentalism": decomposing a competence model into parts, phases or steps need shed no light on the decomposition of the actual mechanism, even when the competence model is excellent (pp. 75–76). Footnote 1 quotes Bechtel (1985): Dennett seems to reduce instrumentalism versus realism to an empirical issue about how the cognitive system is structured; realism would be vindicated if intentional idioms map reasonably onto processing states (p. 76).

**Example: the Newfie joke (pp. 76–77).**
- The joke: a man finds his Newfie friend with both ears bandaged. The friend explains that he was ironing a shirt when the telephone rang; asked about the other ear, he says he had to call a doctor (p. 76).
- The text is "radically enthymematic": it never mentions answering the phone, or the iron's similarity to a receiver. The hearer fills these in with know-how. A long list of things the hearer needed to know could be made, and a reaction such as laughing is "almost proof positive" that one's intentional characterization includes these beliefs (p. 76).
- Yet we have little idea what a good processing theory of comprehension would look like. Readers may report visual imagery, but reports vary, and even someone with no imagery who laughs must have some unconscious information-sensitive process (pp. 76–77).
- The beliefs (e.g. people usually answer the phone when it rings) do not "come to mind" as verbal judgments. It is also implausible that each is consulted independently by a deductive mechanism (p. 77).
- We can still predict at this level: children raised on wash-and-wear clothes who do not know what ironing looks like will be baffled. But treating the list as axioms from which the joke's point is deduced gives only a particularly ill-favored performance model. The intentional characterization, for all its predictive and even explanatory power, sheds virtually no light on the underlying mechanisms (p. 77).

**Example: the hand calculator (pp. 78–79).**
- Pencil-and-paper arithmetic is an exemplary computational-level model of a pocket calculator. It predicts the output for any input, e.g. "26 × 329" (p. 78).
- Do the stages of my calculation mirror the calculator's stages? Some do and some do not; the fit can be accidental. There are indefinitely many ways to engineer a calculator that fits the competence model (p. 78).
- There is not just one arithmetical competence model. Arithmetic ignores finitude; the calculator truncates or rounds. Arithmetic says 10 divided by 3, times 3, is 10, and 20 divided by 3, times 3, is 20. Dennett's calculator gives 9.9999999 and 19.999999. This mismatch is a clue about the machinery (p. 78).
- One can use the competence model to generate hypotheses whose falsification refines it toward a performance model. Descending to the algorithmic level may lose familiar categories (perhaps nothing counts as the step where you put down the four and carry the five). This does not diminish the competence model's utility as a predictor or ideal (p. 78).
- Footnote 2: our own ability to do arithmetic without round-off error does not show that we are not finite mechanisms. Perhaps we are bigger and fancier ones (p. 79).
- Moving to greater realism is not always wise. That an object can be expected to approximate optimality may be a deeper and more valuable fact than any available from greater detail (p. 79).

**Chess and the objection from unpredictability (pp. 79–81).**
- **Objection** (Fodor 1981): chess is interesting precisely because the opponent's moves are *not* predictable from the intentional stance (p. 79).
- Dennett: this sets the standard of prediction too high. In endgames, "forced moves" are predictable from the intentional stance, and only from it, with virtually 100 percent reliability. The main doubt is whether the opponent will resign. They are forced only in the sense of being "dictated by reason" (p. 79).
- In the middle game one seldom predicts down to a unique move, but the thirty or forty legal moves can usually be cut to an unordered list of about half a dozen likely ones. This is a tremendous predictive advantage, gained in near-total ignorance of mechanism (pp. 79–80).
- Chess programs economize in the same way, concentrating on branches where the opponent plays its best response. A bold "blunder" can surprise such a program. Whether to redesign it for greater realism depends on the costs of maintaining that detail under the time clock. The risks are probably tolerable (p. 80).
- **Fodor's objection** (Fodor 1981b, as quoted on p. 80): assuming Black's rationality includes assuming Black notices a threat, whereas I hope the threat goes unmarked. **Dennett replies:** one may rationally predict Black's lapse *because* of what one knows about what Black is and is not likely to notice. Being approximately rational, Black is likely to notice obvious threats; if he is unlikely to notice a threat, it must be because the threat is somewhat distant in the search tree. So even when exploiting another agent's foibles, we use the rationality assumption (p. 80).
- Since the stance's power is not explained by any knowledge of mechanisms, Dennett resists the realism that infers belief-like and desire-like states in all such objects. Bechtel suggests his instrumentalism could be reconstructed as realism about abstract relational properties relating an organism or artifact to its environment; Millikan has a similar positive account. Dennett says this is a viable option, but chapter 5 will show the properties one would end up being a realist about are hardly worth the effort (p. 81).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Alice is at home and wants coffee. She can: (A) walk to Cafe A (5 min), (B) walk to Cafe B (20 min), (C) stay put, (D) make tea at home. We use a simple utility, value minus 0.2 per minute of walking. Dennett gives no numbers, so this is our illustration of principles (1)–(3), p. 49.

- *Attributed profile:* she believes both cafes are open; coffee is worth 10, tea 3.
  - A: 10 − 0.2×5 = 10 − 1 = 9.
  - B: 10 − 0.2×20 = 10 − 4 = 6.
  - C: 0.
  - D: 3 − 0 = 3.
  - Prediction: she walks to Cafe A (highest, 9).
- *Change one belief:* she saw a "closed" sign on Cafe A, so she believes A is closed. The false belief has a special story: a deceptive sign (p. 49).
  - A: 0 − 1 = −1.
  - B: 6; C: 0; D: 3.
  - Prediction: she walks to Cafe B.
- *Holism:* the prediction changes only because the belief profile changes as a whole (p. 58).
- *Implementation neutrality:* a lookup-table system ("sign on A says closed → go to B; else → go to A") and a utility-computing planner give the same outputs on these two cases. The intentional description cannot tell them apart (p. 59). Only further tests, like the calculator's 9.9999999 versus 10, could separate them (p. 78).

## Concepts
- [[folk-psychology]] — the everyday belief–desire craft, learned like grammar, not as an explicit theory (p. 46)
- [[intentional-stance]] — predicting by attributing beliefs, desires and rational acumen; here "intentional systems" and the stance's limiting-case relation to the design stance (pp. 49, 73)
- [[physical-design-stance]] — the intentional stance as a limiting case of the design stance: assume only that the design is optimal (p. 73)
- [[rational-agent-assumption]] — beliefs and desires are those a system ought to have; behaviour is what would be rational; a "myth" that works because we are pretty rational (pp. 49–50)
- [[propositional-attitude]] — Fodor's realism and Dennett's "sort of realist" view of beliefs and desires (p. 71)
- [[intentionality]] — Brentano's Thesis read via intentional systems as a "reductionist" thesis (p. 67); intentionality of the labels in sub-personal theories (p. 63)
- [[illata-vs-abstracta]] (new) — Reichenbach's contrast between posited entities and calculation-bound constructs; beliefs are *abstracta* (p. 53)
- [[intentional-system-theory]] (new) — an abstract, holistic, normative theory akin to decision and game theory; a competence theory, neutral on implementation (p. 58)
- [[sub-personal-cognitive-psychology]] (new) — performance theory of how brains realize intentional systems, a syntactic engine approximating a semantic one (pp. 61–63)
- [[competence-vs-performance-model]] (new) — what a system can do, versus how its mechanism does it; Marr's computational level versus algorithmic/hardware levels (pp. 74–75)
- [[instrumentalism-about-belief]] (new) — Dennett's "selective" instrumentalism: realism about brains, centers-of-gravity status for beliefs; not fictionalism; he later judges the label a tactical error (pp. 71–73)
- [[core-vs-virtual-beliefs]] (new) — explicit stored elements versus beliefs only implicitly stored; core elements may not be beliefs (pp. 55–56)
- [[conceptual-reduction-legitimization]] (new) — reducing folk psychology to intentional system theory by providing rules of attribution, not by identity with physical or functional types (pp. 66–67)
- [[logical-behaviorism]] (new) — Ryle's view and Dennett's "holistic" version: belief as disposition to rational behaviour given other attitudes (pp. 45, 50, 58)
- [[satisficing-evolution]] (new) — evolution as a "satisficing Nature", so we only approximate rationality (p. 51)
- [[semantic-vs-syntactic-engine]] (new) — brain as syntactic engine approximating a semantic engine (p. 61)
- [[homunculus-design-strategy]] (new) — inserting an intentional subsystem as placeholder in design (p. 73)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's intentional-stance prediction (utility-rational agent from beliefs/desires) ↔ theory-of-mind: inverse planning and the rational-agent assumption — both infer or predict from an assumption of rational action, though Dennett gives no Bayesian machinery here.
- [structural-analogy] Competence versus performance (Marr's computational level) ↔ AI evaluation: a model's behavioural success on a test does not fix the mechanism behind it; Dennett's calculator and Newfie cases make this point.
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's "folk psychology vs sub-personal psychology" split ↔ theory-theory and simulation debates: whether mindreading rests on an inner theory or other machinery is a sub-personal question, as distinct from the intentional description of what it achieves.
- [metaphor] Core versus virtual beliefs ↔ implicit vs explicit ToM: both use an implicit/explicit contrast, but Dennett's is about how information is stored, not about whether mindreading is conscious.

## Questions this raises
- If beliefs are *abstracta*, what makes one attribution profile *true* rather than merely useful, given that attributions are underdetermined (p. 52, fn 4)?
- When the intentional stance is used on non-human systems (Martians, programs), how do we tell sheer success from real "belief-like" states (pp. 60, 81)?
- Dennett contrasts explicitly stored core elements with virtual (implicit) beliefs. What would it take empirically to show that some core elements are beliefs (pp. 55–56, 70–71)?
- If semantic properties are "super-relational" (p. 65), can a purely behavioural test of a system's ToM reveal them, or only its competence?
