# Styles of Mental Representation

## L0 — One line
Dennett argues that mental representation comes in several styles (explicit, implicit, tacit), that cognitive science has so far offered almost no empirical evidence that the mind's inner states are explicit, sentence-like items, and that belief-like states may not be sentences in a language of thought.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Ryle attacked the idea that intelligence works by inner rule-consulting. Cognitive science rejected his attack and now often assumes a "language of thought" (inner sentence-like symbols). Dennett asks what "representation" can mean for a computer and for a brain. He separates explicit, implicit and tacit representation, and argues that Ryle's regress must end in tacit know-how, which might carry much more of the mind than current cognitive science supposes. In the later reflections he judges the language-of-thought idea neither vindicated nor entirely discredited, and sees connectionism (networks of simple units) as a possible alternative. He declines eliminativism (dropping belief talk) on tactical grounds. It matters because folk-psychological prediction may survive even if the inner machinery is not sentence-like.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of an infinite regress; what a computer program and a stored data item are; the intentional stance (predicting by attributing beliefs and desires); a rough idea of neural networks.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Ryle's attack on "intellectualist" inner rule-following was dismissed by cognitive science, partly because of the computer metaphor. Dennett thinks the metaphor, properly used, can free theorists from Ryle's worries, but is often abused by cognitive scientists (pp. 213–215).
- Claims that mental representations have a syntax (symbols, types and tokens, composition rules) rest, as far as Dennett can see, solely on a priori reasoning. The empirical evidence so far is, in Dennett's view, evidence at the semantic level only (pp. 215–216).
- Dennett proposes definitions: explicit = a physically structured token with an interpretation and a reading mechanism; implicit = logically implied by what is stored explicitly. A third notion, tacit, means know-how built into the system without being represented (pp. 216–218).
- Rote rule-following (as with the first bridge player) supplies, Dennett says, the best model for cognitive science because it separates storage from understanding. Explaining understanding by storage and manipulation requires storers stupider than the understander, or a Rylean regress follows (p. 220).
- A pocket calculator honours arithmetic truths without storing them, so the truths are potentially explicit but not implicit in it. A clock-switched system shows "transient tacit representation" (pp. 221–223).
- When a system has many tacit states that are variations on a theme, it becomes useful (virtually mandatory) to change state by editing and revising, and the system then begins to look somewhat like a language. Even then, the elements get their external semantics only from their global functional roles (pp. 223–225).
- In the Reflections, Dennett says the language-of-thought idea is neither vindicated nor entirely discredited. The frame problem (an AI problem central to modelling thinking) is a serious worry, connectionism (networks of simple units) is a possible alternative on which it is too early to give a verdict, and generativity (a virtually unlimited capacity to differentiate) is still the best argument for the language of thought (pp. 227–232).
- Dennett declines to follow either eliminativist branch (Stich's or Churchland's), on tactical rather than doctrinal grounds. The intentional stance has great, if flawed, predictive power, and Dennett expects a future sub-personal cognitive psychology (a theory between folk psychology and bare biology) that is "cognitive" but not "computational" (pp. 232–235).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The chapter has no numbered sections. The headings below are mine and follow the author's order. The chapter's main essay runs to p. 225. The "Reflections" begin on p. 227.

### 1. Ryle's attack and its rejection (pp. 213–215)
- **Ryle's target.** In *The Concept of Mind* (1949), Ryle attacked the "intellectualist myth". This is the idea that minds are composed mainly of episodes such as thinking private thoughts, consulting rules and recipes, applying general truths to cases, and deducing implications (p. 213).
- **Ryle's argument.** Ryle knew that such schoolroom activities exist. But he held that taking them as the model for all intelligent behaviour gets matters just backward (p. 213). Public practices such as deliberate consideration of maxims depend on agents already having full-blown mental talents. Explaining those talents by yet another inner process of calculation would start an infinite regress (p. 213).
- **Cognitive science rejected this.** It speaks openly of inner mental representations and operations on them. Dennett says it might with some justice be called intellectualist science. He cites Fodor's slogan that there is no computation without representation (p. 214).
- **Why the rejection was easy.**
  - Ryle was attacking a mishmash of views. Cognitive science is openly physicalist and so has no "ghosts in the machine". Most of its representations are presumed unconscious, so it needs no "privileged access". Dennett calls this "backstage" intellectualism (p. 214).
  - Ryle was also deeply suspicious of any claim for inner representations, because he thought all such postulates incoherent. Dennett adds that he thinks Ryle had good reason for this suspicion (p. 214).
  - Dennett takes the main contribution to the conviction that Ryle must be wrong on this point to be the growing influence of the computer metaphor. On the common understanding he reports, a computer is a mindless manipulator of internal explicit representations, with no regress, and since whatever is actual is possible, such manipulators are not the perpetual motion machines Ryle would have us think. Dennett adds that this common understanding has not yet received its proper defense (p. 215).
- **Dennett's position.** He holds that the metaphor, properly used, can liberate theorists from Ryle's worries. It is also often abused (p. 215). Dennett grants that computers somehow represent things with internal representations; this shows only that it is possible that brains do. To understand how brains might do it by analogy, we had best be clear about how computers represent. There are several styles of computer representation, and only some are plausible models for the brain (p. 215).

*Intuition note.* Ryle said inner rule-consulting leads to endless regress. Cognitive science answered that computers do it without regress. Dennett grants that computers represent somehow, but says we had best check which kind of representation the computer actually uses.

### 2. The syntax commitment lacks empirical support (pp. 215–216)
- Cognitive scientists usually commit to a view about "syntax". The representations have symbols, multiple tokens of types (types individuated syntactically, not semantically), formation rules like a grammar, and a meaning of the whole that is a function of the meanings of the parts (p. 215).
- Dennett says that, if this heavy commitment is made, it is solely on the basis of aprioristic reasoning, so far as he can see. There is, he says, almost no empirical evidence yet for any substantive hypothesis about this syntax (citing Stabler 1983) (p. 216).
- He does not deny that excellent evidence exists. His claim is that it has been at the semantic level only. It shows what information is relied upon by cognitive processes, not how the reliance is effected (p. 216).
- We cannot yet say whether information in the human system is represented "explicitly" or "implicitly". One reason is that these terms are used in many different ways (p. 216).

### 3. Definitions: explicit, implicit, potentially explicit (pp. 216–217)
- **Explicit.** (Dennett offers definitions of how the terms should be used, not of how they are used.) Information is represented explicitly in a system if and only if there actually exists, in the functionally relevant place, a physically structured object (a formula, string or tokening). Its elements must come from a system with a semantics or interpretation, and there must be a provision (some mechanism) for reading or parsing it (p. 216). This leaves room for non-linear systems such as "map-reading" or "diagram interpreters" (p. 216).
- **Implicit.** Information is represented implicitly if it is logically *implied* by something stored explicitly (p. 216).
- Implicit does not mean "potentially explicit". Example: all of Euclid's theorems are implicit in the axioms and definitions. A mechanical Euclid machine, with axioms stored, may churn out only some theorems. All are implicit, but only a proper subset are potentially explicit. The limit is about ordinary hardware limits (finite speed, short lives), not about "incompleteness" (pp. 216–217).
- **Is "merely implicit" explanatory?** Dennett asks whether an item that is merely implicit can ever be cited in a cognitive or intentional explanation of an event. He thinks there is a strong but tacit undercurrent of conviction that an item of information can play a role only by being rendered explicit, because (apparently) it must have a physical embodiment (p. 217).
  - He suspects that this is "almost backwards". Explicit representations considered in isolation are inert as information bearers. They become information bearers only when given roles in larger systems. Then the features that make them explicit play problematic roles at best (p. 217).
- **A reply and a footnote point.** Someone might say implicit representation is not representation. But, he says, if that is what "representation" is to mean, there are ways of holding or even sending information in a system that do not involve representing it. A spy can send a message indirectly by sending explicit premises from which it follows (footnote 1 refers to Borges's story). Implicit storage has no upper bound: more implicit information needs no more space (p. 217).

*Intuition note.* "Explicit" means a readable token exists in the right place. "Implicit" means it follows logically from such tokens. Dennett suspects it is almost backwards to think information must be explicit to play a role in explanation; he does not say that implicit information is explicit.

### 4. The tacit, and Ryle's regress (pp. 217–218)
- Implicit depends on explicit. But in the sense of "tacit" that Dennett will use, explicit depends on tacit (pp. 217–218).
- Ryle's point: explicit proving on blackboards depends on know-how. This know-how cannot be explained by explicit rules or recipes in the agent. To manipulate those, an inner agent with know-how would be needed, which leads to regress (p. 218).
- At the bottom there must be a system that merely has the know-how. If it can be said to represent it at all, it does so neither explicitly nor implicitly (as defined) but tacitly. The know-how is built into the system so that it need not be explicitly represented. What people often call "implicit" is what Dennett means by "tacit" (p. 218).
- **Where Ryle was right and wrong.** Ryle thought the regress must stop with systems that have merely tacit know-how. Dennett agrees with Ryle on this (p. 218). Ryle also thought it obvious that whole people are not composed of subsystems that explicitly represent anything. Dennett says he was wrong that this is obvious. Decades of cognitive psychology show it is not obvious. It might still be true, or much closer to the truth than current ideology supposes (p. 218).
- **Conscious versus explicit.** All these terms differ from "conscious" and "unconscious". What you consciously represent to yourself is at best indirect evidence of what is explicitly represented in you unconsciously (p. 218).
- **Chomsky's "hard line".** When Chomsky speaks of an explicit representation of grammar, he means an unconscious one. It is not merely tacit in the system's operation. It is also not merely implicit in something more basic that is explicit. The grammar itself is unconsciously but explicitly represented (p. 218).

### 5. Three bridge players (pp. 218–220)
The rule is "Third Hand High!" It tells the third player to play his highest card in the suit led. It is a tactical rule, not a rule of the game (p. 219).
- **Player 1 (explicit consulter).** He retrieves the rule from memory, checks that its conditions are met, and then plays. This is conscious, explicit rule-following. Dennett says Ryle's claim was that anyone who takes this as a model for all mentation (from shoe-tying to understanding language) is, shall we say, benighted. But at least some cognitive scientists, he says, think just that (p. 219).
- **Player 2 (intuitive).** He has never heard the rule. He plays just like player 1 in card-choosing behaviour. It does not follow that he does not unconsciously follow an explicit version. The hard-line hypothesis is that the backstage processing resembles the introspective account player 1 might give (p. 219).
- **Player 3 (combines both).** He knows the rule, sees its exceptions, and thinks about its rationale. He is a better player, but a much worse model for cognitive psychology. Dennett suspects Ryle had this player in mind when dismissing the idea. Player 3 lacks a very important property that player 1 has: stupidity (p. 220).
- **Why stupidity is useful.** Player 1 shows that one can store and act on something without understanding it. Rote memorization decouples memory from understanding, like a singer memorizing Russian lyrics without knowing their meaning. To explain understanding by storage and manipulation, the storers and manipulators must be stupider than the understander, or a Rylean regress follows (p. 220).
- **The regress and "size".** The storers must have some know-how. It might be merely tacit, or based on a further rule-following process of narrower horizons and greater stupidity. Dennett calls the in-principle possibility of ending this regress in finitely many steps a fundamental guiding insight of cognitive science (p. 220). But it must end in merely tacit know-how. Dennett asks whether Ryle might be right about the *size* of the largest merely tacit knowers (whole people), and whether virtually all backstage know-how could be merely tacit. He says this is hard to answer, in part because "tacit" has so far been given only an impressionistic, ostensive definition (p. 220).

*Intuition note.* Dennett says the rote rule-follower, who stores and applies a rule without understanding it, supplies the best model for cognitive science. The open question is how much of the mind could be built from such parts, down to merely tacit know-how.

### 6. The pocket-calculator benchmark (pp. 220–222)
- **The question.** Does a pocket calculator represent the "truths of arithmetic" explicitly, implicitly or tacitly? Inside the hardware there are no numerical propositions written in code. The only obvious explicit numerals are on the buttons and in the display (p. 221).
- **Hidden explicit representation of numbers.** Suppose it computes 6 × 7 by adding in binary 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 + 7, holding interim totals in an accumulator. Then the process differs from that for 7 × 6. The interim results for 6 × 7 are 14, 21, 28, 35 (as printed), and for 7 × 6 they are 12, 18, 24, 30, 36. This is explicit and systematic representation of *numbers*. But where are any true arithmetical *propositions* represented? (p. 221)
- **Dennett's answer.** The machine has the dispositional property of answering arithmetic questions correctly, without looking up any facts or rules stored in it. Engineers who knew arithmetic designed it to "honor" all those truths. The truths are *potentially explicit* in it, since it can produce explicit answers. But they are not *implicit* in it, since there are no explicit truths in it that imply them. The only explicit representations are the questions, answers and interim results (p. 221).
- **Comparison with Ryle.** Dennett takes Ryle's view to be that people have "peripheral" explicit representation (sotto voce rehearsings, and so on) and nothing deeper. Likewise the calculator has nothing deeper than its peripheral representations (pp. 221–222).

### 7. Design, automatization and transient tacit representation (pp. 222–223)
- **Design process.** Calculator designers begin with an explicit specification of truths and rules. They end with a device that obeys the rules without representing them explicitly (p. 222).
- **Human analogue?** Humans sometimes learn skills that are first governed (or so "introspection" strongly assures us) by explicit consultation of rehearsed rules, like the first bridge player. With practice the skill becomes "automatized". Examples are the tennis backhand and agreement in a second language (p. 222). Two possibilities:
  - Fodor's: automatization merely *hides* explicit recipe-following beneath conscious access. His example is a hidden shoe-tying recipe read by a hidden subsystem (p. 222).
  - The calculator-inspired one: practising is a process of partial self-design, which yields a "device" that obeys the rules without consulting any expression of them (p. 222).
- **Switching systems.** The second possibility may look probable only for static, hard-wired tasks. But systems can change mode. An automatic elevator can follow one set of rules from nine to five on weekdays and another at other times. All it needs is a clock-controlled switch between two control systems, each of which tacitly represents a set of rules. Dennett calls this **transient tacit representation**: all the rules are tacitly represented all the time, but only one set is tacitly represented *as being followed* (pp. 222–223).
  - Equivalently, one might say the system tacitly and permanently represents a rule to follow one rule system on weekdays from nine to five and another at other times. Its current state transiently and tacitly represents the time of the week. This is a vehicle, though not of explicit representation, for indexical propositions such as that it is now weekend time (p. 223).
- **Animals.** An aquatic-and-terrestrial animal might obey one rule set on land and another in water. Getting wet could be the trigger for the change of internal state (p. 223).
- **More elaborate switching.** If perceptual analysis machinery drives the system into its states, there is no apparent limit to the complexity of what the current state could tacitly represent. Examples: that one is now in grandmother's house, or that there is now a distinct danger of attack by a predator approaching from the north-northeast. The theorist's wording of such contents is not "translated by" any terms in the system, since the whole point of tacit representation is that it is tacit. States of such a system get their semantic properties directly and only from their globally defined functional roles (p. 223).

*Intuition note.* A system can "know" what time of week it is just by being in the right mode, with no sentence stored anywhere. What a state means is fixed by what the whole system does when in that state.

### 8. Economy, editing and language-likeness (pp. 223–225)
- As the number of possible states grows, designs must rely on economies of multiple use of resources. Where states are variations on a theme, it becomes useful (virtually mandatory) to change state by *editing and revising* part of the current subsystem rather than *discarding and replacing* a near-duplicate one (p. 224).
- This requires systematicity: the loci of substitution must have fixed ways of changing their functions as a function of the substituends' identity. The whole system thus begins to look somewhat like a language, with counterparts of the syntactic features listed in section 2 (p. 224).
- **Is it then truly explicit?** Dennett says there will be uses for that view. But the "syntactical" elements first have an entirely *internal* semantics, "referring" to memory addresses, internal operations and other states, not to outer-world things (p. 224). Footnote 3 adds: Fodor himself sees that the internal semantics of programming languages does not solve the problem of mental reference or intentionality. Dennett's claim is that we lack compelling reasons to think the syntactical elements of states with external semantics will admit any straightforward external interpretation (p. 224).
- Insofar as the states can be interpreted as having external semantic properties, they obtain them in the same way as merely tacitly representing states. Only the global role of a state fixes its informational (external) semantics (p. 224).
- **Extended usage.** It might be profitable to grant indirect external semantic properties to some elements. A belief state that snow is white *might* have identifiable "snow" and "white" components (p. 225).
- **Contrast with natural language.** From the meaning of "snow is white", "snow is cold" and "milk is white" we can say what "milk is cold" means. In "Mentalese" counterparts we could not tell what the "milk is cold" state means ("it may not mean anything at all") until we know its global role (p. 225).
- **Dennett's stated goal.** The essay's goal is simply to explore, and perhaps improve our view of, some open, empirically researchable territory. Ryle said a priori that we *couldn't* be mental-representation-manipulators. Fodor and others say a priori that we *must* be. Some details of the computer metaphor suggest what we *might* be, and may eventually shed some light on what we are (p. 225).

*Intuition note.* Even if inner states come to look like words and sentences, their meaning about the outside world is not read off their form. It comes from what the whole system does with them.

### Reflections: The Language of Thought Reconsidered (pp. 227–235)

### 9. An imagined triumph and why it did not happen (pp. 227–229)
- The language-of-thought idea (Fodor 1975) began as a fine stab in the dark, inspired by relatively aprioristic arguments. It is now "neither vindicated nor entirely discredited" (p. 227).
- **Imagined vindication, using Marr's three levels** (Dennett points back to pp. 74–75; those pages are not in this unit) (p. 227).
  - Computational level: folk-psychological calculation is specified as rigorously as arithmetic and works as a competence model (p. 227).
  - Algorithmic level: the finite, approximating states resemble those of the computational level without radical change of categories (p. 228).
  - Hardware: the mapping of algorithms onto neural hardware goes smoothly (p. 228).
  - Then, Dennett says, Fodor and the other Realists would be in heaven. It would be a reduction of propositional attitude psychology to "High Church Computationalism". Having an attitude would be, as Fodor put it, a *computational* relation to an internal representation (p. 228, citing Fodor 1975, p. 198).
- **Why it did not happen.**
  - Progress in cognitive science has been largely a matter of showing how peripheral sensory, perceptual and motor systems work *without* explicit-representation computations (p. 228).
  - For central processes (planning, problem solving, belief fixation), there has been almost no empirical advance. Fodor himself admits there are no computational formalisms for this and "no idea" how to develop them (quoted from Fodor 1983, p. 129) (pp. 228–229).
  - Sententialist models do not just fail to be testable. They seem to lead systematically to brittle, inefficient dead ends. At the heart lies the Frame Problem of AI. Dennett says a strong case can be made that it spells the doom of propositional attitudes as computational relations to internal representations (p. 229).

### 10. Connectionism as a possible alternative (pp. 229–232)
- The idea would be entirely discredited if a clear alternative had been formulated and shown to handle the tasks it was postulated to perform. Fodor's 1975 challenge defied the skeptics to find one. Connectionist models (McClelland and Rumelhart 1986) are seen by some enthusiasts as such an alternative. Dennett says it is too early for verdicts (p. 229).
- **Typical differences from High Church Computationalism** (pp. 229–230):
  1. "distributed" memory and processing; units play multiple, equivocal roles and disambiguation occurs only globally (no propositions at memory "addresses");
  2. no central control, but a partially anarchic system of somewhat competitive elements;
  3. no complex message-passing between units;
  4. reliance on statistical properties of ensembles;
  5. mindless, inefficient making and unmaking of partial pathways until the system settles, not necessarily on a predesignated "right" answer.
- **Still computational, but at a lower level.** Each unit's behaviour is a computed function of other units' behaviour. But what is computed is not a proposition about Chicago. It is a new value of a threshold-like parameter with no univocal external-world semantic role. This level is closer to neuroscience than to psychology (p. 230).
- **How anything gets to be "about Chicago".** There must be a higher level at which external semantics is attributed to global features of network activity. Dennett says that here the description lapses temporarily into metaphor and handwaving: the relations are statistical, emergent and holistic, not computational. The psychological-level "virtual machine" is not formally specifiable in psychological vocabulary as a high-level algorithm (p. 230). Unlike a normal machine language, there is no direct translation between high-level phenomena and low-level ones. So the hardware does add something: which content-relative effects occur depends on low-level features of operation history (pp. 230–231).
- **NETtalk** (Sejnowski 1986) starts with random dispositions to pronounce text and is "corrected" as it proceeds. It generalizes, recognizes exceptions and generalizes on those. Yet no rules are explicitly represented. They are tacitly represented in the emergent dispositional structure. Nothing like checking whether a rule applies or looking up exceptions occurs, which are the covert intellectualist activities Ryle disparaged (p. 231).
- **Grounds for skepticism** (p. 231):
  - existing networks use "unrealistic props", such as NETtalk's "teacher", and two-way traffic between nodes that neuroscience does not know;
  - worries about *scaling up*: models that work with dozens or hundreds of elements sometimes fail at realistic size.
- **Aeronautics analogy.** Early flying machines were built with sound mechanistic materials and nothing flew. Connectionists offer "synthetic wonder tissues", and it is not yet shown how to sew them into flying machines. Perhaps earlier design work will be adaptable to the new materials. Footnote 2 cites Touretzky and Hinton's (1985) construction of a production system from a Boltzmann machine (pp. 231–232).

### 11. Generativity and the fate of Realism (p. 232)
- **Generativity.** The best argument for the language of thought remains that the whole system, at least in humans, must be capable of virtually unlimited differentiation and of basing behaviour on it (in Dennett's paraphrase of Fodor's battle cry: no differentiation without representation). We have only one clear example of indefinitely extendable and articulate representation: natural language (p. 232).
- **Dennett's reply.** What represents the world in us will almost surely be interpretable in retrospect as if composed of terms and sentences. That would not in itself vindicate linguistic objects as the basic building blocks of cognition. The linguistic level may be an innocently emergent property of distributed activity, as a picture is discernible in a mosaic whose pieces are held together by principles that ignore the picture's element boundaries (p. 232).
- **Do Realism and the language of thought stand or fall together?** Fodor has always maintained they do, joined by Stich and the Churchlands (p. 232).
  - If the folk-psychological competence model is forever only an idealized competence model, with little relation to the mechanisms (as would be so if Connectionism triumphs), what should we say about folk categories? (p. 232)
  - **Stich's view** (as reported): he holds some hope for a "modified Panglossian prospect", in which mature psychology "cleaves reasonably closely" to the pattern presupposed by folk psychology. In pessimistic moments he anticipates the negative outcome. Churchland more cheerfully anticipates it. Both declare it would show there are no beliefs after all (pp. 232–233).
  - **What replaces folk psychology.** Stich, when optimistic that Fodor will prove almost right, would use a purely "syntactic" theory, keeping versions of folk categories but de-interpreting them. In pessimistic moments he would, with Churchland, keep content but drop the assumption that inner vehicles behave syntactically as Realists suppose. Dennett's summary: "Belief dis-contented versus content dis-believed" (p. 233).
- **Dennett's two-phenomena suggestion.** Both positions could be "vindicated" at once. Folk talk about beliefs confusedly alludes to two sorts of phenomena: the verbally infected, only derivatively contentful states of language users ("opinions"), and the deeper states one might call animal belief (frogs and dogs have beliefs but no opinions). Stich's syntactic theory can be more or less right for opinions. Churchland can be the methodological guru for the internal, information-sensitive, non-sentential states (p. 233).

### 12. Why Dennett declines eliminativism (pp. 233–235)
- **Self-refutation worry (Dennett's courtroom case).** He chooses neither camp. Until the world shares their view, what would an eliminativist tell a judge asking whether he *believes* he has seen the defendant? He would have to say he is "of the opinion" that there is no such thing as belief. Dennett notes this line of attack is familiar and no news to Stich and Churchland (pp. 233–234).
- **Tactical, not doctrinal.** He does not endorse any a priori argument that makes these categories immune to scientific discredit. The problem is tactical. It is not mere prudence. What matters is not losing sight of the tremendous (if flawed) predictive power of the intentional stance. The judge's method of seeking truth is, "beyond any doubt", the best way we know (p. 234). Dennett asks whether there is any reason to believe a serious rival method of truth-seeking will arise in a Golden Age of eliminative psychosyntax or eliminative neurobiology (p. 234).
- **Burden on Churchland.** He must explain the predictive and explanatory power of folk psychology and the social sciences. He must explain economic models that presuppose rational agents, and cognitive psychologists' experiments that rely on assumptions about subjects' beliefs and desires. That power is real and needs explanation. Dennett suggests Churchland might say that an instrumentalist calculus is available in the meantime (p. 234).
- **The imagined headlines.** Suppose sub-personal psychology differed dramatically between groups, e.g. a headline claiming scientists have proved most left-handers incapable of belief. Dennett says that is not what we would say. The reluctance is also a recognition of an empirical fact: reliable, robust patterns in which all behaviourally normal people participate, described in terms of belief and desire (pp. 234–235).
- **Example.** On July 20, 1969 the belief that a man had stepped on the moon spread around the world. In no two people was the effect of the news the same, and the causal paths were no doubt almost equally various, but the claim that they therefore had nothing importantly in common is, Dennett says, false, and obviously so. There are indefinitely many ways to distinguish those with the belief from those without it, and the methods would correlate highly. Science should not, or could not, turn its back on that (p. 235).
- **The Golden Age as Dennett sees it.** Much as Churchland does, with shifts of emphasis. As laid out in chapter 3 (not in this unit), there will be folk psychology, intentional system theory (its abstract idealization), and a well-confirmed *sub-personal cognitive psychology* between folk psychology and biology. The last will be "cognitive" (information-transformation among content-laden items, mental representations). Its style will not be "computational": the items will not look or behave like sentences manipulated in a language of thought (p. 235).

*Intuition note.* Dennett's reason for not dropping belief talk is tactical: the intentional stance predicts well. He expects a future sub-personal psychology to describe inner content-bearing states that do not look or behave like sentences.

## Toy example (ours)
Aim: show "tacit" versus "explicit" versus "implicit" with numbers.

*Machine T (tacit).* A heating controller has two hard-wired circuits. Circuit A turns the heater on below 20 °C. Circuit B turns it on below 16 °C. A clock switch hands control to A from 08:00 to 22:00, and to B otherwise.
- At 10:00 and 18 °C: A is in control. 18 < 20, so heat on.
- At 23:00 and 18 °C: B is in control. 18 < 16 is false, so heat off.
- Nowhere is a sentence such as "it is daytime" or "heat below 20" stored. The circuit that is in control is the only carrier of "it is day". This matches the elevator case: the current state transiently and tacitly represents the time (p. 223).

*Machine E (explicit).* A card in a slot reads "Heat if below 20 °C". A reader parses it. This is an explicit token with a reading mechanism (p. 216).

*Implicit.* Suppose the card also stores "Heat if below 20 °C" and "20 < 21". The consequence "Heat if below 20 °C, so heat if below 19 °C" is implied, so it is implicit. Whether the machine can ever derive it depends on its mechanism, so implicit is not the same as potentially explicit (added step; cf. the Euclid machine, p. 216).

*Lesson.* The T and E machines behave identically from outside at 18 °C and 10:00. Only T lacks any readable rule. Ryle's regress stops at T-style know-how.

## Concepts
- [[explicit-representation]] (new) — a physically structured token with an interpretation, plus a mechanism for reading it (p. 216)
- [[implicit-representation]] (new) — information logically implied by what is stored explicitly; not the same as "potentially explicit" (p. 216)
- [[tacit-representation]] (new) — know-how or information built into a system without being represented explicitly or implicitly; what many people loosely call "implicit" (p. 218)
- [[transient-tacit-representation]] (new) — several tacit rule sets exist permanently; the current system state tacitly represents which is being followed (p. 223)
- [[intellectualist-myth]] (new) — Ryle's name for the view that intelligence consists of inner rule-consulting and deduction (p. 213)
- [[rylean-regress]] (new) — the infinite regress from explaining know-how by inner consultation of explicit rules; ends in merely tacit know-how (p. 218)
- [[hard-line-hypothesis]] (new) — Chomsky-style view that grammar is unconsciously but explicitly represented; applied to the intuitive bridge player (pp. 218–219)
- [[language-of-thought]] (new) — Fodor's thesis that thinking is computation over sentence-like internal representations (pp. 214, 227)
- [[connectionism]] (new) — networks of simple interconnected units with distributed, non-propositional representation and no central control (pp. 229–230)
- [[frame-problem]] (new) — AI problem at the core of central-process modeling; Dennett says a strong case can be made that it spells the doom of propositional attitudes as computational relations to internal representations (p. 229)
- [[sub-personal-cognitive-psychology]] (new) — theory between folk psychology and biology, content-laden but not sentence-like (p. 235)
- [[folk-psychology]] — everyday belief–desire framework; here possibly only an idealized competence model (p. 232)
- [[intentional-stance]] — Dennett's predictive strategy; its great (if flawed) predictive power is Dennett's tactical reason against eliminativism (p. 234)
- [[propositional-attitude]] — belief-like states; Fodor's realism takes them to be computational relations to internal representations (p. 228)
- [[intentionality]] — contentfulness of inner states; Dennett holds that their external semantic properties are fixed only by the global functional role of the state (p. 224)
- [[physical-design-stance]] — the calculator was designed by engineers to honour arithmetic, without representing it (p. 221)

## Canonical instances
- [[third-hand-high-bridge-players]] — three bridge players show conscious, intuitive and sophisticated rule-following (pp. 218–220)
- [[pocket-calculator-6x7]] — truths of arithmetic potentially explicit but not implicit; interim results are explicit representation of numbers (p. 221)
- [[euclid-machine]] — theorems implicit in axioms but only some potentially explicit (p. 216)
- [[automatic-elevator]] — transient tacit representation by a clock switch (pp. 222–223)
- [[milk-is-cold-mentalese]] — in Dennett's Mentalese counterparts, one could not tell what a "milk is cold" state means until its global role is known (p. 225)
- [[nettalk]] — a network that learns pronunciation with no explicit rules (p. 231)
- [[moon-landing-belief-1969]] — robust belief patterns despite varied causal paths (p. 235)
- [[eliminativist-in-court]] — the self-refutation worry for the eliminativist (pp. 233–234)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] tacit, state-defined content ↔ machine theory of mind: distributed weights encode behaviour without addressable "propositions" — both locate content in global functional role, not in stored sentences.
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's two-phenomena split (opinions versus animal belief) ↔ implicit versus explicit ToM: a verbal-reportable system versus a non-verbal state tracker — loosely parallel division of labour.
- [metaphor] Rylean regress ↔ infinite regress in recursive mindreading ("I think you think…"): both need a stopping point in simpler capacities.
- [structural-analogy] moon-landing example (varied causal paths, common pattern) ↔ rational-agent assumption/BToM: belief–desire patterns predict behaviour even when the mechanisms vary.

## Questions this raises
- What would count as empirical evidence for or against a syntax of mental representation, as opposed to evidence about the information relied upon?
- How could one test whether a person's backstage skill is hidden explicit rule-following (Fodor's "How to Tie Your Shoes") or automatized self-design?
- If beliefs are only a "pattern" over varied inner mechanisms, how do we decide whether an animal or an artificial system has a belief rather than just a state?
- Could the generativity argument be met by emergent language-like behaviour in networks without a language of thought?
