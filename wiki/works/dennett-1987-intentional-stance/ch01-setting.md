# Setting Off on the Right Foot

## L0 — One line
Dennett starts his science of the mind from the third-person, materialist view, treats everyday "folk psychology" as a useful but fallible system like folk physics, and says the intentional stance explains why it works (pp. 5, 11).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks how a responsible, materialist science of the mind should begin. He argues that common sense is not enough, partly because its "obvious facts" conflict. He therefore makes a tactical choice: start from the objective, third-person view of the physical sciences. He answers Nagel's objections to this choice. He then compares folk psychology, the everyday vocabulary of belief and desire, with folk physics (our everyday expectations about how objects behave). Both usually work well in daily life. Academic physics often contradicts folk physics, and Dennett, with a few others, holds that folk psychology's concepts are likewise vulnerable in principle. He says the success of folk psychology is explained by the intentional stance, which later chapters develop.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday familiarity with "belief", "desire" and the idea of a scientific theory.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The book is about *how to talk about the mind*. It is a philosophical book, but not only for philosophers: readers from other disciplines are meant to benefit too (p. 1).
- Nobody can avoid philosophical preconceptions. The only choice is whether to examine them explicitly (p. 2).
- We must start from common sense, but relying on any one item is treacherous. Its intuitions often conflict, and some parts are probably cognitive illusions of our species (pp. 3–4).
- Sorting out common sense is not a systematic task for a purely foundational or axiomatic approach. One must work opportunistically, playing intuitions, findings and arguments against each other (p. 4).
- Dennett's starting point is a tactical choice: the objective, materialist, third-person world of the physical sciences. Nagel asserts it is a mistake, and Dennett says each side begs the question (assumes what is in dispute) against the other (pp. 5–6).
- Folk psychology is the family of "mentalistic" concepts. It seems wonderfully able to make sense of people, whose behaviour is hopelessly unpredictable by the methods used for landslides, germination and magnetism (p. 7).
- Folk physics and folk psychology are parallel: natural, effortless, perhaps partly innate, and in principle open to revision. Academic physics often contradicts folk physics (pp. 8–11).
- Folk science usually works in daily life, and our faith in it is not due just to loyalty to the world view we grew up with. Dennett's explanation for folk psychology's success is the intentional stance (p. 11).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening and aims (pp. 1–3)
- Dennett compares talking about the mind to talking about sex: for many people slightly embarrassing, undignified, maybe even disreputable. Many prefer to talk about the brain (p. 1).
- Many things need saying that the languages of neuroanatomy, neurophysiology or behaviourist psychology cannot say. Attempts to complete the biological and social sciences without mind-talk have shown their futility (p. 1).
- He sees a near-consensus among cognitive scientists and "liberated" neuroscientists. There can be, and "must be, somehow", a responsible materialist science of the mind as well as the brain. There is no consensus on how (p. 1).
- The book is philosophical, but it is written for readers in other disciplines too. Philosophers have much to say about mentalistic talk, but only a small portion of it could possibly be true or useful, and much of even the best is apt to be misinterpreted (pp. 1–2).
- Philosophy does not often give stable, reliable "results" the way science does at its best. It can give new ways of framing questions and seeing what matters (p. 2).
- Every theorist of the mind faces tactical problems about which questions to try to answer. The phenomena are baffling, the data on the brain are overwhelming, and persistent intuitions intrude. So theorists in every field risk setting off on the wrong foot because of some philosophical misconception about the phenomena, the options, the tasks or the conditions of success (p. 2).
- There is no avoiding philosophical preconceptions. Some of the most debilitating past misconceptions were gifts from academic philosophy that scientists misconstrued by exaggeration and oversimplification; Dennett says logical positivism and, more recently, Kuhnian incommensurability of paradigms come to mind. Still, philosophers think they can help, and a growing number of others have turned to them with cautious skepticism (p. 2).
- The book presents the foundations of his theory of mind, his account of the intentional stance. Dennett says there are few major theoretical innovations, but several in exposition and defence, especially in the reflections after each reprinted essay (pp. 2–3).
- The last chapter compares his view systematically with others, using their criticisms. The basic introduction is the next chapter, "True Believers", which he now considers a replacement for "Intentional Systems" (1971) as his flagship statement. In the rest of this chapter he steps back and comments on some unargued assumptions of the other essays (p. 3).

**Intuition note.** Dennett says theorists cannot avoid philosophical preconceptions. The only option is whether to examine them explicitly and carefully at some point, rather than carry them unexamined.

### Common Sense and the Third-Person Point of View (pp. 3–7)
- Common sense says many Earth creatures have minds, but it is not enough. It leaves too many pressing questions open and often yields persuasive intuitions that contradict each other (p. 3).
- Dennett's examples:
  - From one vantage point it is "obvious" that warm-blooded animals have minds like ours and insects are "mere automata".
  - From another, the gulf between us and the chimpanzee looks larger than that between a pigeon and a robot.
  - The idea that no automaton could be conscious is perennially popular, but can be made to look unimaginative and parochial (p. 3).
- Some of the apparently well-attested pathologies, such as blindness denial and hemi-neglect, are so counterintuitive that recounting them often provokes derisive dismissal. A literature professor firmly assured a student that Dennett must be making them up (pp. 3–4). Where many "obvious facts" compete, common sense is not enough (p. 4).
- No rules govern how theorists appeal to common sense. We must start from it if we hope to be understood or to understand ourselves, but relying on any one item is "treacherous" (p. 4).
- Even if some parts of common sense are immutable Truth, other parts are probably just cognitive illusions of our species, persuasive because of design shortcuts in our cognitive systems. His example: to a phototropic moth it may seem *a priori* true that heading toward the Light is always Right. Other parts are diluted, popularized old science (p. 4).
- Sorting common sense into true, false, misleading and unreliable is a good task for a philosopher. It is not a systematic task amenable to a purely foundational or axiomatic approach. One must "wade in opportunistically", playing intuitions, empirical findings, theories, arguments and thought experiments against each other (p. 4).
- Some useful skirmishes are rigorous, formal explorations of particular sets of hunches. Dennett says the best light in which to view the formalist failures of philosophy is as if each had been prefaced by the question what would follow if we made these assumptions under these constraints. He cites Fodor: a philosophical theory has, often enough, the form "Let's try looking over here." (p. 4).
- Every formal system in philosophy must be "motivated", and the informal task of providing that motivation typically contributes more philosophical illumination (or at least doctrine) than the system itself. Tactical considerations often masquerade as first principles (p. 4).

**The tactical choice (p. 5).** Dennett starts from the objective, materialistic, third-person world of the physical sciences. He calls this the orthodox choice in English-speaking philosophy, with Nagel (*The View From Nowhere*, 1986) its most notable detractor. Dennett proposes to compare the two starting points briefly to see what he might be missing.

**Nagel's view** (quoted by Dennett):
- Dennett is not certain Nagel thinks he can *prove* Dennett's starting point is a mistake, but says Nagel certainly *asserts* it. Nagel writes that there are things that "cannot be adequately understood from a maximally objective standpoint". A great deal is essentially connected to a particular point of view, and the attempt to give a complete account in objective terms detached from these perspectives inevitably leads to false reductions or to outright denial that certain patently real phenomena exist at all (Nagel p. 7, quoted p. 5).
- Nagel writes that, to the extent such no-nonsense theories have an effect, they merely threaten to impoverish the intellectual landscape for a while, and that in the name of liberation these movements have offered intellectual repression (Nagel p. 11, quoted p. 5).
- Nagel says certain forms of perplexity, such as that about freedom, knowledge and meaning, embody more insight than any proposed solutions (Nagel p. 4, quoted pp. 5–6).

**Dennett's replies:**
- Their intuitions differ. Nagel is oppressed by the prospect of an evolutionary explanation of the intellect; Dennett is exhilarated (p. 5).
- Dennett sees philosophy as continuous with the physical sciences. This grounds his modesty about method and his optimism about progress. Dennett says that to Nagel this is mere scientism (p. 5).
- Dennett reads Nagel as offering no escape from perplexity, and calls that impasse a *reductio ad absurdum* of Nagel's method. Nagel embraces it (pp. 5–6).
- Nagel offers assertions, not arguments. His arguments beg the question against Dennett's position. Dennett assumes the true theory of mind will overturn some prior convictions, so he is not cowed by having counterintuitive implications of his view pointed out; he says any theory that makes progress is bound to be *initially* counterintuitive (p. 6).
- The question-begging is mutual (p. 6). Dennett does not presuppose Nagel's alternative must be wrong, nor that everything worth taking seriously is accessible from his own starting point. He is impressed by its proven yield of (apparent) comprehension and its promise (p. 6).
- Nagel claims to show that the attempt to reconcile objective and subjective is "essentially incompletable". Dennett grants he might be right, but remains utterly unpersuaded. Some join Nagel in suspecting something subtly incoherent in the more or less standard scientists' vision of the world. Dennett asks, as questions, whether the self-styled objectivist covertly depends on a prior commitment to irreducible points of view, and whether the goal of reducing these points of view to biology, chemistry and physics defeats itself. He mentions a rumour that physics is turning materialism into idealism, and David Moser's remark that quantum particles do sometimes seem to be "the dreams stuff is made of" (p. 6).
- He chooses not to confront these suspicions at the outset. The orthodoxy of his starting point *might* be due as much to social and political factors as to philosophical justification. He can see the plausibility of Nagel's diagnosis (a vain effort to grow up too early), though he does not believe it (p. 7).
- His tactical hunch: even if so, the best way forward is to start here and let revolutions "foment from within". He bets we can see more and better if we start here, now, than with some other tack. The only way he knows to convince his reader is to get on with the project and let the results speak for themselves (p. 7).

**Intuition note.** Dennett does not claim to prove his starting point is right. He picks it because he bets it will let us see more and better, he says the question-begging is mutual, and he says he does not presuppose that Nagel's rival starting point must be wrong.

### Folk Science and the Manifest Image (pp. 7–11)
**Why a second perspective is needed.**
- Human doings are among the most complicated phenomena. Methods used for landslides, germination and magnetism give a few inroads, but most behaviour is "hopelessly unpredictable" that way. People are even less predictable than the weather, if one uses meteorologists' and biologists' techniques (p. 7).
- Another perspective, familiar since childhood, makes sense of this complexity: *folk psychology*, built on mentalistic concepts such as belief, desire, knowledge, fear, pain, expectation and intention (p. 7).

**Folk physics as a parallel (pp. 7–8).**
- Folk physics is our system of savvy expectations about middle-sized objects. You leap up when a glass tips, you know not to mop water with a fork, and you expect a pushed swing to swing back (pp. 7–8).
- Some of it might be innate, but at least some is learned. Infants cringe at looming shapes virtually from birth. After about six months (crawling, stereovision) they show reluctance to venture out over the edge of the "visual cliff" (a clear glass surface extending out over a tabletop), though they have never learned from bitter experience about falling (Gibson 1969). Children have to learn from particular experiences that they cannot walk on water and that unstable towers of blocks will topple (p. 8).
- Some of it seems supported by innate perceptual bias. In an animation of "falling" circles, if the acceleration is tampered with, one instantly sees an invisible force pushing them (p. 8).
- Innate or irresistible is no guarantee of truth. Academic physics is often counterintuitive. Naive physics of liquids would not predict siphons or pipettes. A clever uninitiated person could "deduce" that gyroscopes, virtual images in parabolic mirrors and sailing upwind are impossible (p. 8).

**Folk psychology is similar (pp. 8–9).**
- Its interpretations are so natural that they are almost impossible to suppress. Examples: watching someone pick blueberries with no idea what he is doing; seeing two children tugging a teddy bear without thinking they both *want* it; being startled when a blind person fails to react, because we expect people to *believe the truth* about what is before their eyes (p. 8).
- Some categories seem to get an innate perceptual boost. Inconclusive infant evidence suggests faces are served by innate, somewhat specialized visual mechanisms. An adult who could not read a threatening gesture would be suspected of brain damage. Yet much must be learned before we are adept at reading others mentalistically (p. 9).
- Its intuitions are probably no more irresistible, initially, than those of folk physics. But there are few well-known uncontroversial cases of science directly discrediting a folk-psychological intuition. Dennett suggests this may be because academic psychology and the neurosciences are relatively undeveloped and unauthoritative (p. 9).

**The "siphons and gyroscopes of psychology" (pp. 9–10).**
- Churchland (1986) says that inadequacies of the commonsense framework can be hidden while the brain functions normally, and show with a damaged brain. So look first at puzzling abnormal cases: blindsight, split-brain phenomena, blindness denial and hemi-neglect (p. 9).
- Academic psychology has no established theory of these to set against our folk incredulity, so they remain controversial (p. 9).
- Other challenges to everyday hunches: perceptual illusions such as the Ames room, masochists (reputed to like pain), idiot savants, photographic memories, multiple personalities and alleged psychic powers (pp. 9–10).

**Philosophers' trust in folk concepts (pp. 10–11).**
- Such challenges should make us cautious about *a priori* claims drawn from analysing everyday concepts. Yet philosophers have invested surprising authority in them (p. 10).
- Example: debates on self-deception and weakness of will. Nobody doubts the phenomena. The controversy is how, if at all, they can be described coherently in terms of belief, knowledge and intention. Articles such as Davidson's (1969) on how weakness of will is possible try to say exactly what one must believe, think, know, intend and want in order to suffer a genuine case. The paradoxes and contradictions have discouraged few participants. It is apparently obvious to them that the folk-psychological categories learned in infancy are the right ones to use, whatever Nagelian perplexity follows (p. 10).
- Dennett says we have all learned to be more skeptical of the dictates of folk physics. Even the "undeniable introspective fact" that you can feel "centrifugal force" cannot save folk physics, except for the rough-and-ready purposes it has always served. How to express our diminished allegiance to the categories of folk physics has been a central topic in philosophy since the seventeenth century, when Descartes, Boyle and others began to ponder the metaphysical status of colour, felt warmth and the other "secondary qualities". Those discussions have traditionally kept the folk-psychological counterparts unchallenged: conscious perceptions, sensations and beliefs about the external world (p. 10). Kripke (1972) is cited as an example (p. 10).
- A few (Quine, Dennett, Churchland, Stich) wondered whether traditional problems in philosophy of mind are problems with the *whole* framework of folk-psychological concepts. They recommended putting those concepts in the same jeopardy as folk physics. They disagree on the verdict, but agree on the concepts' vulnerability in principle (pp. 10–11).

**Why we trust folk science (p. 11).**
- Sellars calls this world view the *manifest image*, as opposed to the *scientific image*. It is no accident that we have it: our nervous systems were designed to make needed distinctions swiftly and reliably, and to ignore what we usually can ignore (p. 11).
- Usually, especially in the dealings most important to daily life, folk science works. Folk physics keeps us warm, fed and out of collisions. Folk psychology lets us co-operate on multiperson projects, learn from each other and enjoy local peace. These benefits would be unattainable without extraordinarily efficient, reliable expectation-generation (p. 11).
- Open questions: What organizes this capacity? Are there general "laws" of folk physics that we internalize and unconsciously exploit to generate our expectations about inanimate objects? How do we acquire a *general* capacity to interpret our fellow human beings? (p. 11).
- Dennett has no account of our talent as folk physicists. For folk psychology he does have an explanation: "we make sense of each other by adopting the intentional stance" (p. 11).

**Intuition note.** Folk psychology, like folk physics, usually works as an everyday tool. But Dennett says that being innate or irresistible is no guarantee of truth, and that its concepts are vulnerable in principle. Whether they must be revised is a verdict he leaves for later chapters.

## Toy example
*(Ours, not Dennett's.)* Three people each want an item and believe it is in a certain room. Assume we know each person's wants and beliefs.

| Person | Wants | Believes it is in | Folk-psychological prediction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | coffee | kitchen | goes to kitchen |
| Ben | stapler | office | goes to office |
| Cara | tea | kitchen | goes to kitchen |

One rule ("goes where she believes the wanted thing is") needs 2 facts per person, so 3 × 2 = 6 facts, and gives 3 predictions. Neuroscience-level prediction would need the state of each brain, which this table never uses. The point: the mentalistic vocabulary lets us predict at a level where physical-science methods are said to fail (p. 7). It does not show the vocabulary is *correct* at the neural level, which the unit leaves open (p. 11).

## Concepts
- [[folk psychology]] — the everyday perspective using belief, desire, fear, intention etc. to make sense of people (p. 7)
- [[intentional stance]] — Dennett's proposed explanation of why folk psychology works; only named here, defined in later chapters (p. 11)
- [[folk physics]] (new) — our savvy expectations about how middle-sized objects react, perhaps partly innate and at least partly learned; often contrary to academic physics (pp. 7–8)
- [[manifest image]] (new) — Sellars's term for the everyday world view, as opposed to the scientific image (p. 11)
- [[third-person starting point]] (new) — Dennett's tactical choice to start from the objective, materialist view of the physical sciences (p. 5)
- [[mentalistic concepts]] (new) — belief, desire, knowledge, fear, pain, expectation, etc. (p. 7)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Folk psychology as a revisable framework ↔ theory-theory: ToM as a tacit theory open to revision — both treat everyday mind-reading as theory-like, though this unit does not say "theory".
- [structural-analogy] Folk physics and folk psychology as expectation-generating systems ↔ predictive models in cognitive science: both are efficient systems that generate expectations.
- [metaphor] Phototropic moth's "a priori" truth (p. 4) ↔ cognitive illusions of species ↔ curse of knowledge/reality bias: a built-in shortcut feels like necessary truth.

## Questions this raises
- What exactly is the intentional stance, and how does it explain folk psychology's success?
- If folk-psychological categories might be revised, which parts survive, and what would count as scientific discrediting?
- Can Nagel's concern that objective accounts miss perspective-bound phenomena be answered without begging the question?
- How can folk psychology be innate in part (faces) and learned in part, and how could we tell?
