# Mid-Term Examination: Compare and Contrast

## L0 — One line
Dennett suggests that many of the big quarrels among Quine, Sellars, Chisholm, Putnam, Davidson, Bennett, Fodor, Stich and himself over the status of intentional attributions are amplified minor differences, because just about everyone accepts Brentano's irreducibility thesis (intentional talk cannot be reduced to physical talk) and agrees, with different emphases, that such attribution is not purely descriptive.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The question: how do nine philosophers differ on the status of attributing beliefs and desires? Dennett suggests that many of the most striking differences are amplified minor ones. Chisholm noted the apparent irreducibility of intentional idioms (belief-talk), and Quine reached the same verdict by another route. Sellars proposed a middle path through function, and Quine's "double standard" (strictly there are no beliefs, but talking as if there were is practically indispensable) previewed later work. Dennett says the live issues are which principle of interpretation to use (Normative or Projective) and how seriously to take the double standard. Realists such as Fodor (who treat beliefs as real inner states), he says, face a gap Davidson and Dennett avoid.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of "beliefs and desires" as everyday explanations of behaviour; no formal math. Terms are defined as they appear.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Dennett says many of the most salient oppositions among these philosophers appear to be amplified products of minor differences of judgment or taste, or of tactical overstatement (pp. 339–340).
- Chisholm noted the apparent irreducibility of intentional idioms, and Quine, from a different starting point, reached the same verdict that they cannot be strictly reduced to physical science. Quine linked this to indeterminacy of translation (p. 340).
- Sellars staked out a middle position, the "Equator". The language of meaning is a distinct mode of discourse that appeals ineliminably to function. Dennett credits this as the birth of contemporary functionalism (p. 341).
- Quine's "double standard" says strictly there are no propositional attitudes, yet the idiom is practically indispensable and "dramatic". Dennett sees a preview of Putnam, Davidson, Bennett, Stich and himself here (p. 342).
- Rival principles of interpretation: Normative (attribute what the creature ought to have) and Projective (attribute what I would have). Dennett traces both back to Quine, and mild versions tend to merge (pp. 342–344).
- Accepting Brentano's thesis mainly for Quine's reasons (indeterminacy) makes one less inclined to strict Realism about content. Davidson and Dennett, the most wholehearted Quinians on this score, see no issue here, while Dennett says a major gap looms for Realists such as Fodor (and Burge, Dretske, Kripke) (p. 345).
- Dennett thinks Fodor's Mentalese theory looks not so very different from Sellars's, but that Fodor resisted procedural semantics. In his own survey, Fodor admits the "idealization problem" is unsolved and that, as things now stand, we have no adequate account of the semanticity of mental representations (pp. 346–347).
- Stich and Churchland reach Quine's destination by different routes. Dennett suggests the remaining Dennett–Davidson gap is a minor difference over how seriously to take the double standard (pp. 348–349).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The question and the strategy of the answer (pp. 339–340)
**The exam question:** compare and contrast Quine, Sellars, Chisholm, Putnam, Davidson, Bennett, Fodor, Stich and Dennett on the fundamental status of attributions of intentionality (p. 339). *Intentionality* here is the meaning or content of psychological states (p. 341).

**Author's strategy:** it would be strange and distressing if one side in each controversy were flat wrong about something important. These theorists share books, tradition, evidence and methodology, so we should expect them to be making common cause (p. 339). From one perspective agreement predominates and progress is visible. Many of the most salient oppositions look like amplified products of minor differences of judgment or taste, or of "tactical overstatement". People tend to exaggerate the importance of what an interlocutor misses, and radical-sounding claims attract attention (pp. 339–340). Philosophers are typically not eager to accept such "bland and ecumenical" resolutions, but it can be enlightening to recall how much agreement there is (p. 340).

### The agenda-setting texts: Chisholm and Quine (pp. 340–341)
- **Chisholm** (1956) noted the "logical obstreperousness" and apparent irreducibility of intentional idioms. He posed the quandary for those who wanted to unify mind and science (p. 340).
- Chisholm asked what the significance would be if belief sentences cannot be rewritten non-intentionally. He felt the question was itself philosophically significant but was not prepared to answer it (p. 340).
- **Quine**, starting from radical translation, reached the same verdict: intentional idioms cannot be strictly reduced to the language of physical science. He wrote that Brentano's irreducibility thesis "is of a piece with the thesis of indeterminacy of translation" (1960, p. 221; quoted p. 340).
- Quine agreed about the importance of the question and, since unification of science has always loomed large as a goal for him, was prepared to answer it. One may take the Brentano thesis as showing either the indispensability of intentional idioms and the need for an autonomous science of intention, or their baselessness and the emptiness of such a science. Quine's attitude, "unlike Brentano's, is the second" (1960, p. 221; p. 340). Dennett labels this radical behaviorism, an instance of what is now called eliminative materialism (p. 340).

**Dennett's geographical map:** these two passages are "slightly misleading" (p. 341).
- Chisholm has defended Brentano's mentalistic pole (North) heroically.
- Quine has been exploring more temperate territory despite his "South Pole" vow.
- Anscombe, Geach and Taylor made Northern explorations.
- It became commonly accepted that intentionality is neither as unapproachable by physical science as Brentano and Chisholm suggested, nor as easily dismissed as Quine suggested. Dennett concludes that some sort of unifying compromise must be possible, between a rigorous "reduction" of the mental to the physical and a brusque denial of the phenomena of mind (p. 341).

### Sellars and the "Equator" (pp. 341–342)
- **Sellars** staked out the Equator (p. 341). His claim: "'…' means – – –" is the core of a unique mode of discourse, as distinct from describing and explaining empirical fact as prescription and justification are (Chisholm and Sellars 1958, p. 527; p. 341).
- What was unique, on Sellars's analyses, was its ineliminable appeal to functional considerations. Dennett says contemporary *functionalism* (mental states defined by their roles) was born this way. He adds that later varieties were enabled or inspired by what Sellars left open, though this is not widely acknowledged (p. 341).
- Footnote 2 says Sellars's influence has been ubiquitous but almost subliminal, if one judges by the few citations to Sellars among functionalists. Putnam, Harman and Lycan were quite directly influenced; Dennett, Fodor, Block and Lewis show it largely at second hand, mainly via Putnam (p. 341, n. 2).

**Intuition note.** Chisholm and Quine start from different places (Chisholm's quandary; Quine's radical translation) yet reach the same verdict that intentional talk resists reduction to physical science. Sellars's move, as Dennett presents it, is to treat it as a unique mode of discourse, distinct from describing and explaining empirical fact, with an ineliminable appeal to function.

### Quine's "double standard" (p. 342)
- Dennett: Quine was not really at the South Pole. He was setting out limits on any functionalist compromise. The section "The Double Standard" (1960, pp. 216–21) previews Putnam, Davidson, Bennett, Stich, Dennett and others (p. 342).
- **Quine's view:** if we are limning the true and ultimate structure of reality, the canonical scheme is austere. It has no propositional attitudes, only the physical constitution and behavior of organisms (p. 221; p. 342). A *propositional attitude* is a state like believing that p.
- So strictly there are no beliefs or desires. Yet the idioms are "practically indispensable" and "essentially dramatic" (p. 219). Intentional vocabulary needs an element of interpretation, not just brute facts (p. 342).
- Dennett: Quine and Sellars agree intentional attribution is not purely descriptive, and nearly everyone since has concurred, with different emphases. Most have thought Quine's claim that the idioms are merely *practically* indispensable understated their centrality, but if the "merely" were taken out there might be little left to debate (p. 342).

### Two rival principles of interpretation (pp. 342–344)
Dennett: the continuing dispute is how to play the interpretation game. Two chief rivals have seemed to emerge (pp. 342–343):
- **Normative Principle:** attribute the attitudes a creature "ought to have" given its circumstances. Its sub-varieties are the Principle of Charity (Davidson, Lewis) and the Assumption of Rationality (Dennett, Cherniak). Bennett's *Rationality* (1964) prefigured much of this (p. 343).
- **Projective Principle:** attribute the attitudes one supposes one would have oneself in those circumstances (p. 343). Grandy contrasted a "principle of humanity" with charity. Stich gave the most detailed defense (p. 343).

**Quine as father of both** (p. 343):
- On charity, Quine says wanton translation imposes our logic on them. A speaker's silliness, beyond a certain point, is less likely than bad translation (1960, p. 59). A footnote credits Wilson 1959 with the idea under that name. Dennett adds that Sellars also appreciated normative considerations in individuating functional roles.
- On projection, Quine says that in indirect quotation we project ourselves into the speaker's imagined state of mind (1960, p. 219). Quine credits Davidson for this discussion. Dennett says Sellars's analysis of functional classification relative to *our* language is the *locus classicus* of projectivism (pp. 343–344).

**Merging and problems** (p. 344):
- Levin divides contestants into Rationalizers and Projectors, noting mild versions tend to merge. Dennett (chapter 4) argues the opposition is at most a matter of emphasis (p. 344).
- Quine's problem with projection: "Casting our real selves thus in unreal roles, we do not generally know how much reality to hold constant" (1960, p. 219; p. 344).
- Stich examines these quandaries in detail. Dennett says they are resolvable, to the extent they are, only by normative considerations: project only what is *best* of ourselves, but what counts as best is itself a matter of interpretation (p. 344).

**Why Quine is untroubled** (p. 344): taken "dead seriously", attribution raises the problem of which principle gives the *actual* attitudes. For Quine the problem does not arise, since the dramatic idiom is a practical necessity governed by pragmatic considerations. He wrote that it will often happen that there is just no saying whether an affirmation of propositional attitude counts as true or false, even given full knowledge of its circumstances and purposes (1960, p. 218; p. 344). In 1970 he added that the problem is not hidden facts a study of brain physiology might uncover. He doubts the very significance of whether the foreigner *really* believes A or B (pp. 180–81; p. 344).

**Intuition note.** Normative says "what should a rational creature think here?" and Projective says "what would I think here?" Dennett says the quandaries of projection are resolvable, to the extent they are, only by normative considerations about what is best, and that the opposition between the two is at most a matter of emphasis. Quine doubts that any further fact, such as hidden brain facts, settles which attribution is the real one.

### Realists versus Quinians (p. 345)
- Dennett's review: almost everyone accepts Brentano's irreducibility thesis. If one accepts it mainly for Quine's reasons (indeterminacy of radical translation), one will not be inclined to be a strict Realist about attributions of propositional attitude, or about psychological content (p. 345).
- Quine: to accept intentional usage at face value is to postulate translation relations as somehow "objectively valid though indeterminate in principle" relative to the totality of speech dispositions (1960, p. 221; p. 345).
- Dennett's claim: much of the debate has been "displaced and distorted" because participants have not faced this implication. Davidson and Dennett, the most wholehearted Quinians on this score, see no issue where a major lacuna looms for Realists such as Fodor (and Burge, Dretske, Kripke; see chapter 8) (p. 345).
- **Putnam:** his oscillations over the years trace his exploration of the costs and benefits of agreeing with Quine on indeterminacy. Most recently he ends up more or less with Quine. He writes that belief–desire explanation belongs to interpretation theory and is holistic and interest-relative. There may be sentence-analogues and predicate-analogues in the brain "but not concepts" (1983, p. 154; p. 345).

### Fodor and Sellars (pp. 345–347)
- **Fodor** pursued an independent Northern course, defending both irreducibility and Reality of intentional states. Unlike Chisholm, Anscombe, Geach and Taylor, he tries to make them acceptable to physical science by grounding them in the "syntax" of physically realized mental representations (p. 345).
- In *Psychological Explanation* (1968a) he turned from Quine, Ryle and Wittgenstein. He sketched mental events as inner processes identified by functional properties. This looked like a radical alternative to the Behaviorist Dogma that "what goes on inside doesn't settle anything" (pp. 345–346).
- Dennett: Fodor's *Language of Thought* (1975) looks not so very different from Sellars's view, given its recognition of teleological presuppositions of functional taxonomies (p. 346). In Sellars's Myth of our Rylean Ancestors, people with a behavioristic language postulate inner episodes of Mentalese, identified by functional role or, equivalently, by meaning (p. 346).
- **Perhaps the crucial difference** (Dennett's claim): in discussing "language entry rules" and "language exit rules" for Mentalese, Sellars recognized the need for a *sort of* "behavioristic" analysis of the semantic properties of inner representations (what would later be called *procedural semantics*). Fodor resisted this step, taken explicitly by Dennett (1969, chapter 4), because it threatened the role he hoped Mentalese would fill (p. 346). Footnote 4: Fodor (1975) does not cite Sellars, and in 1981a he mentions Sellars (1956) once, as remarkably prescient (p. 346, n. 4).
- Chisholm and Sellars both saw the impossibility of *grounding* meaning in primitive semantic properties of an inner language. For Fodor an inner language is a more powerful way of *settling* interpretation questions. Quine, Sellars, Davidson and Dennett (1973) have always insisted (and Putnam now agrees) that it is a mere re-posing of the problem of radical translation (pp. 346–347).
- Dennett says that to maintain his position Fodor has had to swing perilously close to the North pole of real, intrinsic intentionality, even as he makes fun of it, as in a passage about *Dasein* (1980, p. 71; p. 347).

### Fodor's own "exam answer" (p. 347)
Dennett reads Fodor's 1985 *Guide to Mental Representation* as follows:
- Fodor frankly defends Realism and lists Dennett's instrumentalism as the "First Anti-Realist option". He does not take himself to refute it, but after some glancing blows he sets it aside, saying it does seem possible to doubt that a coherent instrumentalism about the attitudes is going to be forthcoming (p. 347).
- Dennett's reading: every position Fodor then surveys downstream of the fork away from Dennett's is, by Fodor's own account, so beset with quandaries that one might almost suppose Fodor to be cataloguing an unintended *reductio ad absurdum*. Fodor admits the "idealization problem" (Dennett: charity in disguise) is unsolved, and sees "no reason to assume that the problem can be solved" within Realistic boundaries (p. 97). He ends with the observation "But of the semanticity of mental representations, we have, as things now stand, no adequate account" (p. 99; p. 347).

### Bennett, Stich, Churchland (pp. 347–348)
- **Bennett** (1976) explicitly rejected Quine's indeterminacy thesis. Drawing on Taylor's analysis of teleological explanation, he attempted a detailed Realistic theory of *registrations*, the inner representational states of animals, of which beliefs are the special human subvariety. Like Dennett, and unlike Fodor and Davidson, he starts with the needs of nonlanguage-using animals (pp. 347–348). Dennett: the two parallel tracks are separated by little more than their disagreement over indeterminacy and its implications for a "firm underlying theory" of "conceptual structures" (p. 348).
- **Stich** (1983) is described as a curving route back to the Equator: an exhaustive *reductio* of the hypothesis that a strict projectivist principle lets one be a Realist about content. Stich ends up joining Quine's double standard: strictly speaking there are no beliefs, though speaking as if there were is a practical necessity (p. 348).
- **Churchland** (1981) reaches the same destination for different reasons and seems to disagree with Dennett and Putnam. He does not find the double standard attractive. He grants that for practical purposes we will go on speaking of beliefs, but for him this is a dismissive acknowledgment, not a prelude to interpretation theory as it is for Davidson, Dennett and Putnam (p. 348).

### Dennett versus Davidson (pp. 348–349)
- **Haugeland's observation** (quoted): Dennett differs from Davidson in ontological outlook. Davidson restricts discussion to events, while Dennett includes states, processes and structures. Haugeland calls the surface difference superficial: the deeper divergence is that Davidson's purpose is to show each mental event is the *same event* as some physical event, with an argument that depends on a doctrine about causal relations he applies only to events. Dennett, on Haugeland's account, not only does not share that aim but apparently would not accept the conclusion: beliefs are specifiable, at best, by a sort of rational "equilibrium" in the intentional framework, so their status as entities, if distinctive, should reflect that framework. Haugeland adds that perhaps Dennett should agree with Ryle that "existence" is not a generic word (Ryle 1949, p. 23). In Haugeland's view Davidson would find this utterly unacceptable and Heidegger would find it congenial (pp. 348–349).
- **Dennett's response:** Haugeland is right. Dennett should and did agree with Ryle (1969, pp. 6–18) while attempting to reconcile that with his agreement with Quine on the implications of radical translation (p. 349).
- Dennett infers that Davidson's steadfast *ontological* realism about beliefs reveals that he has always wanted to take Quine's double standard with a grain of salt: he still hankers to take propositional attitudes more seriously than Quine would recommend, despite agreeing about indeterminacy. The "deep divergence" Haugeland discerns can thus also be seen as the amplified effect of a minor difference of opinion about just how seriously to take the double standard (p. 349).

### Closing observation (p. 349; the text breaks off mid-sentence)
Convergence among philosophers with different attitudes, aspirations and methods strengthens conviction, as do the curious patterns of non-citation. Dennett and Davidson, and Dennett and Bennett, virtually never cite each other despite parallel paths. Almost no one cites Sellars while reinventing his ideas. Dennett begins to explain this ("The comprehension time lag, I…") but the extract ends there (p. 349).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A linguist hears natives say "gavagai" in 4 scenes. A rabbit is present in all 4. Food of some kind is present in 2 of them. Three translation schemes:
- A: "rabbit". Assertion true in 4 of 4 scenes.
- B: "rabbit-stage". Assertion true in 4 of 4 scenes.
- C: "food". Assertion true in 2 of 4 scenes.

A charity-style rule ("prefer the scheme making more of their assertions true") gives scores of 4/4 = 1, 4/4 = 1 and 2/4 = 0.5. It rules out C, but A and B tie. This mirrors the chapter's point: charity narrows interpretations without picking one "real" attribution (Quine's indeterminacy, p. 345). A Projective rule would ask what *I* would say in these scenes, and need not separate A from B either.

## Concepts

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Projective Principle (attribute what I would have) ↔ theory of mind: simulation theory — both use oneself as a model for others.
- [structural-analogy] Normative Principle / Assumption of Rationality ↔ theory of mind: rational-agent assumption — both predict from what a rational agent would do.
- [metaphor] Quine's "dramatic idiom" ↔ theory of mind: folk-psychological explanation as useful interpretation, not a description of inner facts.

## Questions this raises
- If Normative and Projective principles both need a standard of "best", what independent standard fixes it (p. 344)?
- Is Dennett's claim that Davidson differs from him only in how seriously he takes the double standard fair, given Davidson's view that mental events are physical events (p. 349)?
- Does Fodor's "idealization problem" really amount to charity in disguise, as Dennett says (p. 347)?
- Can a Realist about content avoid indeterminacy, as Bennett attempted, without Fodor-style Mentalese (pp. 347–348)?
