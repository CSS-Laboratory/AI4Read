# True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

## L0 — One line
To be a believer is to be a system whose behaviour is reliably predictable by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires; the patterns this stance finds are objectively real, but they are imperfect and can leave belief attribution indeterminate in some cases.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks what it means to say someone believes something. Two views compete: *realism* (belief is an inner fact, like a virus) and *interpretationism* (attribution depends on the interpreter's interests). He rejects the choice. Belief is objective, but we see it only by adopting the **intentional strategy**: assume the system is rational, work out what beliefs and desires it *ought* to have, and predict its action. This works for people, animals, some machines and, a little, even thermostats. The patterns it finds are real, and an imagined Martian physicist would miss them. They are also imperfect, so rival interpretations can tie. A closing "Reflections" section uses Conway's Game of Life to explain "real patterns".

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Two stances beside the intentional one.** The physical stance predicts from physical constitution and laws. The design stance predicts from what the thing is designed to do. The intentional stance is the third option, used when the other two are impractical (pdf 14).
- **The intentional strategy is a recipe.** Treat the object as a rational agent. Work out the beliefs it ought to have, then the desires it ought to have. Predict that it acts to further its goals in the light of its beliefs (pdf 14).
- **Attribution rests on "ought".** Belief attribution is governed by what the system *ought* to believe given its exposure and interests. Desire attribution is governed by what it *ought* to want. The consequence is that true believers mostly believe truths, and false beliefs and bizarre desires need "special stories" (pdf 15–16).
- **Definition of believer.** "What it is to be a true believer is to be an intentional system", one whose behaviour is reliably and voluminously predictable by the strategy (pdf 13). There is no sharp line between thermostats and people, only degrees (pdf 22).
- **The patterns are objective.** The Martian thought experiment shows that Laplacean physics alone would miss real patterns. Examples are the many physically different ways of ordering 500 shares of General Motors that have the same market effect (pdf 18–19).
- **Mild realism.** Because no one is perfectly rational, the patterns have gaps. In those cases there may be no fact of the matter about what someone believes, and that this is so is itself an objective fact (pdf 20).
- **Richer connection to the world narrows meaning.** Richer sensing and behaviour narrow the set of acceptable interpretations of a system's internal states. This is how "meaning" gets fixed for complex systems (pdf 21–22).
- **Why it works: two questions.** The easy answer is that evolution designed us to be rational. The hard answer concerns the machinery inside. Dennett thinks some version of the language-of-thought hypothesis may be right, but he refuses to call it obvious (pdf 22–23).
- **Reflections: Game of Life.** Conway's Life world shows how a pattern can be real and visible only from a higher-level stance. Dennett ties his view to Quine's and Davidson's indeterminacy and to Parfit's "empty questions" (pdf 24–26).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: Maugham's "Death Speaks" and the problem of belief (pdf 12–13)
- The chapter opens with the story of the merchant's servant in Baghdad. The servant flees Death to Samarra. Death explains that his gesture was only surprise at seeing the servant in Baghdad, because they had an appointment in Samarra that night (pdf 12). Dennett uses the fatalism of this story later (pdf 19).
- Social scientists talk about belief constantly, and they also talk about *talk about belief* (pdf 12). Attribution looks dark and risky for exotic beliefs. It is also disputed for animals, infants, computers and robots, and for people whose beliefs seem contradictory or wildly false (pdf 12–13).
- **Anecdote.** A man in a bar phoned a biologist to ask "Are rabbits birds?" He said "Damn!" on hearing "No". Could he really have believed that rabbits were birds? It "would take a bit of a story" to accept that (pdf 13).
- Sometimes attribution looks like hermeneutics and *Verstehen*. At other times it looks as easy as "counting beans in a dish". In the second case one is tempted to think the belief could be confirmed by *finding something inside the believer's head*, so physiological psychology could in principle override any "black box" method (pdf 13).

### Two opposing views and Dennett's thesis (pdf 13)
- **Realism.** Having a belief is like being infected with a virus: an objective internal matter of fact that an observer may guess at reliably (pdf 13).
- **Interpretationism.** Having a belief is like being immoral, having style, or "making a good wife". The answer depends on what you are interested in (pdf 13).
- These are usually seen as exclusive and exhaustive. Dennett thinks this is a mistake. His **thesis**: belief is a perfectly objective phenomenon (so he is "a realist"). But it can be discerned only from the point of view of someone adopting a certain *predictive strategy*, and its existence can be confirmed only by assessing that strategy's success (so he is "an interpretationist") (pdf 13).
- To a first approximation, the **intentional strategy** treats the object as "a rational agent with beliefs and desires and other mental stages exhibiting what Brentano and others call *intentionality*" (pdf 13).
- A **system** whose behaviour is well predicted this way "is in the fullest sense of the word a believer". Dennett defines an **intentional system** as one "whose behavior is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy" (pdf 13). He admits his earlier arguments (1971, 1976b, 1978a) won few converts (pdf 13).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett wants "belief" to be neither a hidden inner object nor a matter of taste. It is a real feature of behaviour, namely the predictability you get when you treat the system as rational.

### The Intentional Strategy and How It Works

**Astrology as a contrast case (pdf 13–14).**
- An astrological strategy takes a birth date and feeds it into an algorithm. It is popular, but we have good reasons to think it does not work. Its successes are luck or vagueness (pdf 14).
- Suppose some people were "astrological systems" whose behaviour *was* predictable that way. We would then want to know **how** the strategy works (its rules) and **why** it works. Astrologers might have no good opinion about the second question. "Having a good strategy is one thing; knowing why it works is another" (pdf 14).
- So far as we know, the class of astrological systems is empty (pdf 14).

**Physical stance (pdf 14).**
- Determine the physical constitution and the physical impingements, then use the laws of physics. This is Laplace's grand and impractical strategy, but it has local versions (the cook predicting an overheated pot). That it always works *in principle* is a dogma of the physical sciences (pdf 14).

**Design stance (pdf 14).**
- Ignore the physical details and assume the object has a design. Then predict that it will behave *as it is designed to behave* (pdf 14).
- Examples: most computer users have no idea of the physical principles behind the machine's reliability, yet they predict it well. Almost anyone can predict when an alarm clock will sound without knowing whether it is spring-wound or has silicon chips. More accurate prediction needs a less abstract level of design description (pdf 14).
- Only the *designed* behaviour is predictable this way. For a clock filled with liquid helium, "revert to the physical stance" (pdf 14). Many biological objects (plants, animals, kidneys, hearts, stamens, pistils) can be predicted from the design stance (pdf 14).

**Intentional stance (pdf 14).**
1. Decide to treat the object as a rational agent.
2. Work out what beliefs it ought to have, given its place in the world and its purpose.
3. Work out what desires it ought to have, on the same considerations.
4. Predict that the agent will act to further its goals in the light of its beliefs.

A little practical reasoning from those beliefs and desires will in many, but not all, instances yield a decision about what the agent ought to do. That is what you predict it *will* do (pdf 14).

> **Intuition note.** The three stances differ in how much detail they ignore. Physics ignores nothing. Design ignores material. The intentional stance ignores mechanism, but assumes rationality.

**Attributing beliefs (pdf 14–15).**
- Truisms: sheltered people tend to be ignorant, and exposed people come to know all about things. Exposure to $x$ (sensory confrontation over a suitable time) is the *normally sufficient* condition for knowing about $x$ (pdf 15).
- The threshold for accepting abnormal ignorance is high. "I didn't know the gun was loaded", said by someone present, sighted and awake, meets "utter skepticism" (pdf 15).
- We learn only the *relevant* truths. For instance, I do not typically learn the ratio of spectacle-wearers to trousered people in a room. So the first **rule**: attribute as beliefs all the truths relevant to the system's interests (or desires) that its experience to date has made available (pdf 15).
- The rule over-attributes, because we forget. It also misses false beliefs. But any false belief "requires a special genealogy, which will be seen to consist in the main in true beliefs" (pdf 15).
- Two paradigm cases of genealogy: (1) $S$ falsely believes $p$ because $S$ truly believes that Jones told him $p$, that Jones is clever, and that Jones did not intend to deceive him. (2) $S$ falsely believes there is a snake on the barstool because $S$ truly believes he seems to see one, is in a bar, and so on. "The falsehood has to start somewhere" (hallucination, illusion, misperception, memory deterioration, fraud), but the false beliefs grow "in a culture medium of true beliefs" (pdf 15).
- Arcane and sophisticated beliefs arise by tracing a lineage of mainly good reasoning from beliefs already attributed. So "true believers mainly believe truths". If beliefs could be counted, all but the smallest portion (say, under ten percent) would be attributable under the first rule (pdf 15). Dennett doubts that counting beliefs is possible (pdf 15).
- **Footnote 1 (pdf 15).** Dennett distinguishes *belief* from *opinion*. Opinions are linguistically infected, relatively sophisticated states, roughly states of betting on the truth of a formulated sentence. Given this distinction, "most beliefs are true" is nearly trivial. Democritus's systematically false physics would be a vanishing fraction of his beliefs. The other beliefs include humdrum standing beliefs and the millions of occasional perceptual ones. The theory-laden-observation objection is answered by pointing out that his own theory need not burden his observations, and that even his least theoretical compatriots had theory-laden observation beliefs.

**The fundamental rule and desires (pdf 15–16).**
- The first rule is a *derived* rule. The fundamental rule is: attribute those beliefs the system *ought to have* (pdf 16).
- Desires follow the same pattern: we attribute the desires the system *ought to have*. On a first pass these are the familiar highest desires: survival, absence of pain, food, comfort, procreation, entertainment. Citing one of these typically ends the "Why?" game (pdf 16).
- Derived rules: attribute desires for things the system believes are good for it, and for things it believes are best means to other ends it desires. Bizarre or detrimental desires, like false beliefs, require special stories (pdf 16).

**Language, desire and belief (pdf 16).**
- Language lets us formulate very specific desires (the example is a two-egg mushroom omelette, French bread and butter, and a half bottle of lightly chilled white Burgundy). It also *forces* us to commit to desires more stringent than we would otherwise have (pdf 16).
- To get what you want you must say what you want. Saying it often means being more specific than you antecedently meant. Then, being a person of your word, you acquire an interest in satisfying exactly what you declared (pdf 16).
- Illustration: "I'd like some baked beans, please." "Yes sir. How many?" We are socialized to accept such demands without noticing them (pdf 16).
- The same happens with belief: our linguistic environment forces precise verbal expression on convictions that lack hard edges. Focusing on the results while ignoring the distortion may mislead us into thinking beliefs and desires are obviously "sentences stored in the head". These are only special cases and may not model the whole domain (pdf 16).

**Rationality (pdf 16).**
- Start from perfect rationality and revise downward as circumstances dictate. Assume people believe all the implications of their beliefs and no contradictory pairs. This causes no clutter, because one only needs the system to reach the implications relevant to the prediction at hand (pdf 16).
- Irrationality and finitely powerful inference raise "knotty problems", set aside here (see chapter 4 and Cherniak 1986) (pdf 16).

**Do people use it? Does it work? (pdf 16–17).**
- People use it "all the time". It is the only such strategy we know. It works with people almost all the time (pdf 16).
- Dennett's illustration: why would it be a bad idea to let each Oxford college grant degrees as it sees fit? The answer is easy to generate, and it consists of reasons people have for giving others reasons for acting. The strategy is so habitual that we overlook its role in shaping our expectations (pdf 16–17).
- It also works on most mammals (for example, designing better traps), birds, fish, reptiles, insects, spiders and even clams. Once a clam believes there is danger, it will not relax its grip until it is convinced the danger has passed (pdf 17).
- It works on some artifacts. The chess computer will not take your knight because it knows a line of play would lose its rook, and it does not want that. The thermostat turns off the boiler once it "comes to believe" the room is warm enough (pdf 17).
- It works on plants. In a locale with late spring storms, plant apple varieties that are *cautious* about concluding that it is spring. It even works on lightning: an electrician explained that lightning "wants" the best path to ground but gets tricked into second-best, so make a better path more obvious (pdf 17).

> **Intuition note.** The strategy is cheap and good. Using it asks nothing about brain mechanisms, which is why it covers everything from people to clams.

### True Believers as Intentional Systems (pdf 17–22)

**Which systems "really" have beliefs? (pdf 17–18)**
- The list above mixes serious attributions, dubious ones, pedagogical metaphors, *façons de parler* and frauds. Sorting the "real" believers from the "as if" ones would be a "Sisyphean labor". Even in the worst cases the strategy does work "at least a little bit" (pdf 17). This separates intentional systems from objects for which it never works.
- **The lectern.** A lectern can be construed as a fully rational system that believes it is at the centre of the civilized world and wants to stay there. Prediction: it stays put. Is it an intentional system? Dennett says the strategy "does not recommend itself" here, since we get no predictive power we did not already have. We tailored the beliefs and desires to fit in an unprincipled way (pdf 17).
- **Objection:** the difference is only about our limited capacities. A Laplacean physicist needs neither the design nor the intentional strategy. Engineers can understand a thermostat without anthropomorphizing it. Even the cleverest engineers may lapse into the intentional stance with complex systems such as time-sharing computers or space probes ("asking and telling, trying and avoiding"), but this is "human epistemic frailty" (pdf 17).
- Would it be intolerable to say something is a believer for one observer and not for a cleverer one? That would be a radical interpretationism. Dennett says his presentation sometimes invited this reading, but he wants to discourage it. "The decision to adopt the intentional stance is free, but the facts about the success or failure of the stance, were one to adopt it, are perfectly objective" (pdf 18).

**The power of the strategy (pdf 18).**
- Our attention goes to cases where it is unreliable, which hides its power (pdf 18).
- *Chess.* Many moves are legal, but only about half a dozen have anything to be said for them. The strategy "can dramatically reduce the number of live options" even when it cannot pick one move (pdf 18).
- *Real world.* It cannot predict a stock trader's exact orders or a politician's exact words. But it can predict, with high confidence, that the trader will not buy utilities today or that the politician will side with the unions against his party (pdf 18).
- This coarseness is also a strength. Neutrality about implementation lets us **chain predictions**. If the US Secretary of State announced he was a paid KGB agent, we could predict a chain of "not terribly interesting but perfectly salient" consequences: the President confers with the Cabinet, the Secretary is relieved, a news conference follows, then stories, editorials and letters to editors. This is "an arc of causation in space-time that could not be predicted under *any* description by any imaginable practical extension of physics or biology" (pdf 18).

**Nozick's Martians (pdf 18–19).**
- Suppose beings of vastly superior intelligence are to us as thermostats are to clever engineers. They are Laplacean super-physicists. They see Wall Street as congeries of subatomic particles, yet predict the ink marks on the "Closing Dow Jones Industrial Average" tape days in advance, treating nothing as an intentional system (pdf 18).
- Would we then not be believers from their point of view? If so, belief is "in the eye of the beholder", provided the beholder shares our intellectual limitations (pdf 18).
- Dennett's reply: the Martians would be missing something perfectly objective, namely the **patterns** describable from the intentional stance and only from it, which support generalizations and predictions (pdf 18).
- **Stockbroker.** The Martians predict the exact finger motions and vocal-cord vibrations as a broker orders 500 shares of General Motors. But indefinitely many different patterns of motions, even by different individuals, could have been substituted without changing how the market runs (pdf 19).
- Analogy: there are indefinitely many ways of *being a spark plug*. One has not understood an engine unless one sees that varied devices fit the socket. Likewise there are indefinitely many ways of ordering 500 shares of GM, and "societal sockets" where any of them has about the same effect. There are also "societal pivot points" where what people do depends on whether they *believe that p* or *desire A*, and on nothing else in which they differ (pdf 19).

**The prediction contest (pdf 19).**
- A Martian and an Earthling compete. The Earthling hears Mrs. Gardner on the phone: "You're coming home early? Within the hour? And bringing the boss to dinner? Pick up a bottle of wine on the way home, then, and drive carefully." He predicts that a metallic vehicle will stop in the drive within an hour and release two humans, one holding a paper bag with a bottle in it (pdf 19).
- The Martian reaches the same prediction, but only by using vastly more physical information. An example is the deceleration of the car at intersection $A$, five miles away, to avoid a collision. The Earthling's performance would "look like magic" to him (pdf 19).
- **Fatalism.** The result looks as inexplicable as the appointment in Samarra. Fatalists and astrologers are wrong, but "almost right". There *are* patterns in human affairs that impose themselves "not quite inexorably but with great vigor", absorbing physical variations that might as well be random. They are characterized by the beliefs, desires and intentions of rational agents (pdf 19).
- **A flaw Dennett notes in his own thought experiment.** The Martian treats the Earthling as an intelligent being he can wager against, that is, as having beliefs and desires. The moral is "the unavoidability of the intentional stance with regard to oneself and one's fellow intelligent beings". That unavoidability is itself interest-relative, since one can take a physical stance towards oneself. But one must keep an intentional stance towards oneself at a minimum, and towards others *if* one wants to learn what they know (cf. Hampshire) (pdf 19).
- **Footnote 2 (pdf 20).** An Oxford audience member said a Martian who included the Earthling in his physical-stance purview would not be surprised, since he would have predicted the X-ray modulations. Dennett replies that the Martian's output would read like a Ouija board, and would be baffling as a *true sentence of Martian*.
- **Footnote 3 (pdf 20).** Intelligent beings who did not communicate, predict or observe are hard to call "intelligent".

**Objective but imperfect patterns (pdf 20).**
- The objective fact is that the intentional strategy "works as well as it does, which is not perfectly". No one is perfectly rational, unforgetful, all-observant, or immune to fatigue, malfunction or design imperfection. This leads to circumstances beyond the strategy's power to describe. The analogy is a melted amplifier, whose wiring diagram you cannot draw in normal design terms (pdf 20).
- In even mild cognitive pathology (contradictory beliefs, self-deception), the canons of interpretation fail to yield "clear, stable verdicts" (pdf 20).
- **Strong realism** would claim the person really has some particular beliefs that the strategy cannot divine. **Milder realism** (Dennett's) says there is *no fact of the matter* in these degenerate cases. But "*when* and *why* there is no fact of the matter is itself a matter of objective fact", so this is not a surrender to relativism (pdf 20).
- One can grant **interest relativity**. Different cultures may attribute different beliefs to the same person. There are still objective facts about how well each rival scheme predicts. Two rival schemes may be about equally good, and that is itself something about which there can be a fact. The presence of one pattern does not rule out another (pdf 20).
- The "bogey" of radically different, equally warranted interpretations is metaphysically important but practically negligible for humans, the largest and most complex intentional systems (pdf 20). **Footnote 4:** McCarthy's cryptography analogy. The larger the cipher text, the less chance of dual, systematically unrelated decipherings. McCarthy 1979 is cited for the principles of the stance applied to machines, thermostats included (pdf 20).

> **Intuition note.** Dennett's "mild realism" is like saying a map can be accurate or inaccurate (an objective matter) while also granting that at some coastlines no map can be drawn uniquely. Where it cannot be drawn uniquely is itself a fact about the coast.

**The thermostat and the growth of meaning (pdf 20–22).**
- Dennett has stressed our kinship with clams and thermostats. The "perverse claim" remains: *all there is* to being a true believer is being a system whose behaviour is reliably predictable via the intentional strategy. So *all there is* to believing that $p$ is being an intentional system for which $p$ occurs as a belief in the best (most predictive) interpretation (pdf 20).
- This apparently shallow, "instrumentalistic" criterion puts a *severe constraint* on the internal constitution of a genuine believer. It thus "yields a robust version of belief after all" (pdf 20).
- **Thermostat.** Grant it about half a dozen beliefs (the room is too cold or too hot, the boiler is on or off) and fewer desires. It has no concept of heat or boiler, so **de-interpret**: it believes the $A$ is too $F$ or $G$, and if it wants $A$ more $F$ it should do $K$. Attached to other devices, the same mechanism could control a tank's water level or a train's speed (pdf 21).
- **Enrich the attachments (pdf 21).** Give it more than one way to learn the temperature (an "eye" that detects huddled, shivering occupants, an ear). Give it geographic facts, so it can conclude it is probably cold if it is in Winnipeg in December. Give it behavioural versatility (choosing fuel, buying from the cheapest dealer, checking weather stripping). This adds internal complexity and gives each belief-like state "more to do". It provides more occasions for derivation from other states and for serving as premises.
- Formally, "the class of indistinguishably satisfactory models of the formal system embodied in its internal states gets smaller and smaller" as we add such complexities. Eventually a unique semantic interpretation is practically (but never in principle) dictated. We then say the device has beliefs *about heat* and *this very room*, because we cannot imagine another niche where it would work (pdf 21).
- The original thermostat's state is about *that* boiler because it is *fastened* to it. This is the ancestor of *de re* belief (footnote 5: see chapter 5). But it was too easy to substitute another minimal link and change the meaning (pdf 21).
- With richer systems, substitution without changing the system's organization becomes hard. There is a **two-way constraint**. Fixed in a state, the device demands a specific environment. Unfixed, in a changed environment its sensory attachments drive it into a new state that works there. This tight relation is what we call *mirroring* or **representation** (pdf 21).
- Italic principle (pdf 22): we do not attribute beliefs only to things with internal representations. Rather, when the strategy works for an object, we try to interpret some internal states as representations. What makes an internal feature a representation "could only be its role in regulating the behavior of an intentional system".

**Twin Earth (pdf 22).**
- There is "no magic moment" in the transition from thermostat to a system that "really" has internal representation. Fancier thermostats and house robots have more demanding representations. We are connected to the world so multifariously that almost no substitution is possible, though it is imaginable (pdf 22).
- Putnam's Twin Earth is identical to Earth except for a property below your discrimination thresholds (twin "water" has a different chemical analysis). Swapped there, you would never know, like the simple control system that cannot tell whether it regulates temperature, speed or water volume (pdf 22).
- Your internal organization puts a far more stringent demand on substitution, so Earth and Twin Earth must be virtual replicas (pdf 22).
- Which boiler are *your* beliefs about? The one in your cellar. Fixing referents requires facts about your embedding in the world, as with the thermostat. The principles and problems of interpretation are the *same* for thermostat and person. The differences are of degree, "but nevertheless of such great degree" that understanding a simple system's internals gives little basis for understanding a human's (pdf 22).

> **Intuition note.** A system's "beliefs" get more specific the more tightly it is tied to its surroundings. A thermostat's states are almost interchangeable between jobs. A person's states fit only one world.

### Why Does the Intentional Strategy Work? (pdf 22–23)
- The question is ambiguous (pdf 22).
- **Thermostat.** One answer: it is well designed to be comprehensible from this stance. This is true but uninformative. The other answer, how the machinery works, is easily available (pdf 22).
- **Person.** First answer: evolution designed us to be rational, to believe what we ought and want what we ought. This is true and brief (an answer Herbert Spencer would applaud, as it was a Spencer Lecture) but "strikingly uninformative". The harder version asks how Nature's machinery works, "and we cannot yet give a good answer to that question" (pdf 22).
- **Skinnerian behaviourist answer.** Belief and desire talk is shorthand for unimaginably complex histories of response and reinforcement. "Wants some ice cream" means that ice-cream ingestion was reinforced in the past, creating a propensity. We guess at these histories inductively, and the guesses are embodied in our intentional claims. This would tell us little about the internal machinery (pdf 22–23).
- **Language of thought answer.** The account of how the strategy works and of how the mechanism works will *coincide*. For each predictively attributable belief there is a functionally salient internal state, decomposable into parts like the sentence expressing the belief. The logical form of the propositions is copied in the structural form of the states. Brains would be symbol-manipulating systems, roughly like computers (pdf 23).
- Dennett thinks some attenuated version "will prove correct", but it is **not obvious**. Two different empirical claims are confused: (1) intentional description yields an objective, real pattern in the world (confirmed "beyond skepticism"), and (2) this pattern is *produced by* another real pattern roughly isomorphic to it in the brain. Doubting (2) is not doubting (1) (pdf 23).
- **Reason for (2): combinatorial explosion.** Designing systems of increasing complexity runs into combinatorial explosion. Increasing a parameter by about ten percent tends to increase internal complexity "by orders of magnitude". Yet the brain, "finite, compact, reliable, and swift", has solved the problem. It must use elegant, generative, extendable principles of representation. We have only one model of such a system, a human language, so the argument is "what else could it be?" (pdf 23).
- This is a good reason to pursue the hypothesis as scientific tactics, but its "inevitable rightness is far from assured". "One does not well understand even a true empirical hypothesis so long as one is under the misapprehension that it is necessarily true" (pdf 23). **Footnote 6:** all language-of-thought models so far fall victim to combinatorial explosion in one way or another, which should temper enthusiasm for "the only game in town" (Fodor's phrase) (pdf 23).

### Reflections: Real Patterns, Deeper Facts, and Empty Questions (pdf 24–26)
This is an added commentary section Dennett attached to the reprinted lecture.

**Aim (pdf 24).** The main source of disquiet about Dennett's position has been its "knife-edge" balance on the observer-relativity of attributions. Is belief in the eye of the beholder? Are there objective truths about belief, or only useful fictions? His critics say he tumbles into one abyss or the other. He maintains a *sort* of realism: the patterns the Martians miss are really there to be noticed or overlooked (pdf 24).

**The Game of Life (Conway; Gardner 1970) (pdf 24–25).**
- The world is a huge grid. Its intersections are *cells*, each ON or OFF. Each cell has eight neighbours (four adjacent plus four diagonal). Time advances in discrete ticks (pdf 24–25).
- **The law** (pdf 25): each cell counts how many of its eight neighbours are ON. If exactly two, it stays in its present state. If exactly three, it is ON next instant whatever its current state. Under all other conditions it is OFF.
- Dennett calls this the "entire, deterministic physics" of the Life world. It helps to think in biological terms (ON as births, OFF as deaths, instants as generations) (pdf 25).
- One can predict any configuration perfectly. It is "a fine Laplacean playpen", where finite creatures can adopt the physical stance and predict "with supreme confidence" (pdf 25).
- Some configurations are interesting: things that blink back and forth between two configurations, things that grow and disintegrate, "glider guns" emitting "gliders" (configurations that reproduce themselves translated a few cells over), puffer trains, eaters and so on. Once their behaviour is known, one can adopt the **design stance** and design larger assemblages (pdf 25).
- One triumph is a **universal Turing machine** in Life, a configuration whose behaviour can be read as the state-switching and symbol-reading and -writing of a simple computer that can be programmed to compute any computable function (pdf 25).
- **The "Turing machine stance" (pdf 25).** Ignoring both the Life physics and the design details, calculate the function the machine computes and translate the output back into ONs and OFFs. This works provided no stray gliders or debris destroy the machine.
- Is the pattern "real"? "So long as it lasts it is", and it may survive a long time if "armor" protects it. The pattern may owe its existence to a designer's intentions, but its robustness is independent of historical facts about its origin. Nothing is hidden in Life ("there is no backstage"), yet it takes a "big leap of insight" to see the unfolding as a Turing machine's computation. Ascending to that view is optional and hard for many (pdf 25).

**Application to the intentional stance (pdf 25–26).**
- The intentional stance gives a vantage point for discerning similarly useful patterns. They are objective ("there to be detected"), but not "out there" independently of us. They are partly composed of our own "subjective" reactions, "patterns made to order for our narcissistic concerns" (Akins 1986). It is easy for us to see them, and they are visible only from that stance (pdf 25).
- **Footnote 1 (pdf 25).** Anscombe's "order which is there whenever actions are done with intentions" is as hard to locate as the patterns in Life. They are obvious if you look "in the right way" and otherwise invisible.
- The Martians might find it extremely difficult, but could aspire to know our regularities as we aspire to know the world of the spider or the fish (pdf 26).

**Sort of realist (pdf 26).**
- Dennett declines Rorty's (1979, 1982) radical perspectivalism. But when objective patterns fall short of perfection, as they must, there will be "uninterpretable gaps". Rival interpretations can "tie for first place", so that no further fact could settle what the system "really believed" (pdf 26).
- This is an extension of **Quine's thesis of the indeterminacy of radical translation** (1960). It applies to patterns in internal "behaviour" as well as to external stimulus meanings. "Radical translation begins at home" (Quine 1969) (pdf 26). The Quine 1970 passage says the black-box metaphor is misleading. The problem is not hidden facts, and expecting a distinct physical mechanism for every purported mentalistic distinction is a mistake (pdf 26).
- Dennett's 1975 "Brain Writing and Mind Reading" applied the same point to a "language of thought": the same indeterminacy would affect its translation (pdf 26). **Footnote 2:** Wheeler (1986) shows Derrida can be read as supplementing the Davidson–Quine line. For Quineans it is already obvious that speech and thought are "brain-writing". The "covert belief" that inner speech is directly expressive of thought is found in non-Quinean circles, and it is exposed to attack in chapter 8 (pdf 26).
- **Davidson (1974a), quoted by Dennett (pdf 26):** indeterminacy "marks the fact that certain apparent distinctions are not significant". The quotation continues on printed p. 41 (pdf 26).

**Parfit and "empty questions" (pdf 26).**
- Parfit (1984) argued that principles of personal identity leave open puzzling indeterminate cases. People find this hard to accept: "Am I about to die?" must be Yes or No. Dennett says we are equally attracted to the view that *the contents of our beliefs must be determinate* ("Do I believe there is milk in the fridge?") (pdf 26).
- **Parfit's club example.** A club meets for years, then stops. Years later, some members form a club with the same name and rules. Is it the *very same* club? If there is no rule or legal fact deciding it, and the people would not give an answer, the claim "This is the same club" is "neither true nor false". Though there is no answer, there may be nothing we do not know. Parfit calls such a question **empty** (pdf 26).
- Dennett says that in the club case we accept that there is no "deeper fact". For personal identity the supposition that there *must* be one "dies hard", and it is one of the brute convictions Nagel (1986) cannot abandon (pdf 26). The text of the unit ends here, at the start of the Nagel quotation (pdf 26).

> **Intuition note.** An "empty question" is a question that feels like it hides a fact but doesn't. Dennett suggests "what does she *really* believe?" can sometimes be one.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
We use Dennett's own Life rule to show a pattern that is real, objective, and visible only at a higher level.

**The rule as a formula (added step).** Let $n_t(c)$ be the number of ON neighbours of cell $c$ at tick $t$ and $s_t(c)\in\{0,1\}$ its state (1 = ON). The author's rule (pdf 25) is
$$s_{t+1}(c)=\begin{cases}1 & n_t(c)=3\\ s_t(c) & n_t(c)=2\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

**Start (tick 0):** three ON cells in a row, $(0,0),(1,0),(2,0)$ (coordinates $(x,y)$). All other cells are OFF.

**Physical-stance computation of tick 1.**
- Cell $(1,0)$ (ON): its ON neighbours are $(0,0)$ and $(2,0)$, so $n=2$. It stays ON.
- Cell $(0,0)$ (ON): its only ON neighbour is $(1,0)$, so $n=1$. It goes OFF. By symmetry $(2,0)$ also goes OFF ($n=1$).
- Cell $(1,1)$ (OFF): its neighbours are $(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(0,1),(2,1),(0,2),(1,2),(2,2)$. Three of these are ON, so $n=3$. It turns ON. By symmetry $(1,-1)$ turns ON ($n=3$).
- Cell $(0,1)$ (OFF): its ON neighbours are $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$, so $n=2$. It stays OFF. The same holds for $(2,1)$, $(0,-1)$ and $(2,-1)$.
- Cell $(-1,0)$ (OFF): its only ON neighbour is $(0,0)$, so $n=1$. It stays OFF. The same holds for $(3,0)$.
- Cells $(-1,\pm1)$ and $(3,\pm1)$ have at most 1 ON neighbour. They stay OFF.

Result: ON cells are $(1,-1),(1,0),(1,1)$, a vertical bar.

**Tick 2.** By the same calculation turned by 90°, the vertical bar becomes the horizontal bar again. So the pattern has period 2.

**Higher-level prediction.** Instead of 100 rounds of neighbour counting, say "this is a thing that blinks between two configurations, with period 2". At tick $t$ the bar is horizontal if $t$ is even and vertical if $t$ is odd. At $t=100$, $100 \bmod 2 = 0$, so it is horizontal. The shortcut is a *real pattern* (it holds exactly), but it is visible only if one adopts that description. It also holds only as long as no stray glider hits it, which parallels Dennett's point that such patterns last only "so long as" debris does not destroy them (pdf 25).

## Concepts
- [[intentional-stance]] — predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have (pdf 13, 14)
- [[physical-design-stance]] — the physical stance (laws and constitution) and the design stance (predict from designed function) as alternatives (pdf 14)
- [[rational-agent-assumption]] — start from perfect rationality and revise downward; the agent acts to further its goals given its beliefs (pdf 14, 16)
- [[propositional-attitude]] — beliefs and desires with propositional contents ("believes that $p$"), attributed by the strategy (pdf 20)
- [[intentionality]] — the "aboutness" of mental states (Brentano), the feature the strategy ascribes to its objects; also *de re* aboutness fixed by causal embedding (pdf 13, 21)
- [[folk-psychology]] — the everyday belief–desire practice that Dennett describes as "the only [strategy] we all know now" (pdf 16)
- [[theory-of-mind]] — attributing beliefs and desires to others in order to predict their behaviour; the unit is a philosophical analysis of this practice (pdf 13, 14)
- [[real-patterns]] (new) — patterns that are objectively there to be detected from some stance, even if invisible from others (pdf 18, 25)
- [[interpretationism-vs-realism]] (new) — the opposed views of belief as interest-relative interpretation or as an inner fact; Dennett's "sort of realism" lies between (pdf 13, 26)
- [[indeterminacy-of-interpretation]] (new) — rival interpretations can tie, so that no fact settles what a system "really" believes (pdf 20, 26)
- [[language-of-thought]] (new) — the hypothesis that brains have internal symbol structures mirroring the logical form of beliefs (pdf 23)
- [[combinatorial-explosion]] (new) — design problems whose internal complexity grows by orders of magnitude with small increases in inputs; Dennett's argument for generative representations (pdf 23)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] intentional stance as inversion of a rational-planning model ↔ computational cognitive science: inverse planning and Bayesian theory of mind (infer beliefs and desires from observed actions assuming rational action) — both start from the rational-agent assumption.
- [structural-analogy] the "real pattern" that survives many physical realizations ↔ complexity and emergence: macro-variables that are predictive independently of micro-details — both identify higher-level descriptions by their predictive efficiency.
- [structural-analogy] "class of indistinguishably satisfactory models gets smaller as links are added" ↔ statistics: identifiability, where more data narrow the set of models consistent with the data — the same logical shape, without the formal apparatus.
- [metaphor] McCarthy's cryptography point (a longer cipher text makes dual decipherings less likely) ↔ information theory: unicity distance — the intuition is the same, but this unit gives no formula.
- [metaphor] attributing "ought-to-have" beliefs ↔ economics: revealed-preference and rational-expectations modelling, which also attribute the beliefs a rational agent would have — loose, since Dennett gives no formal model.

## Questions this raises
- If the best interpretation of a system decides what it believes, how should we compare two "rival" interpretations in practice, and what counts as a tie?
- Does the stance work as well for AI systems whose behaviour is not designed to follow human norms? Is its success there evidence of belief or only of a good prediction tool?
- Dennett argues that most beliefs must be true. How does this interact with experiments that find systematic, predictable errors in human belief?
- Is the claim that the intentional pattern is "produced by" an isomorphic brain pattern (the language-of-thought hypothesis) testable, and how would evidence for it differ from evidence for the strategy itself?
