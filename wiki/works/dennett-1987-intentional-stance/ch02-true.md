# True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

## L0 — One line
Belief is an objective pattern in behaviour that shows up only when you treat a system as a rational agent (the intentional stance), and the success of that prediction is what makes a system a believer.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks what it is to believe. He answers that belief is a real, objective pattern in behaviour, visible only to someone who treats the system as a rational agent (the intentional stance) and confirmed only by how well that prediction works. Any system predicted well this way is a true believer, though simple thermostats differ from humans enormously in degree. Why the strategy works is a separate, open question; a language of thought is a plausible hypothesis, not a certainty. The position sits between simple realism and relativism, and allows some cases with no fact of the matter.

**Prerequisites:** the everyday idea of beliefs and desires explaining action; basic logic (implication, contradiction); an idea of what a thermostat and a deterministic rule system are. No mathematics beyond one counting rule (Game of Life).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- There are three prediction strategies: physical (laws of physics), design (what it was built to do), intentional (treat it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires). The intentional one is used when the others are impractical (pp. 16–17).
- Belief and desire attribution follows one fundamental rule: attribute what the system *ought* to have, given its situation and purposes. Ideal rationality is the starting point and is revised downward (pp. 17–21). A consequence: true believers mainly believe truths (p. 19).
- Intentional-stance success is objective once the stance is adopted, even though adopting it is a free choice (p. 24). It gives a very powerful tool for predicting even at coarse grain (pp. 24–25).
- Dennett's reply to Nozick's Martian objection (super-intelligent beings who predict us by physics alone) is that the intentional stance reveals a real pattern (many physical realisations, same social effect) that such a pure physicist would miss (pp. 25–27).
- Imperfect rationality leaves gaps where there is no fact of the matter about what is believed. When and why there is none is itself an objective fact. This is a "milder" realism, not relativism (pp. 28–29).
- A thermostat is a degenerate intentional system (one with only a minimal link to the world). As connections to the world are enriched, the range of acceptable interpretations shrinks, and a unique one becomes practically dictated (pp. 30–33).
- Why the strategy works has easy answers (design, evolution) and a hard one (how the machinery works). Dennett thinks some version of a language of thought (internal states structured like sentences) will prove correct, and argues for it from combinatorial explosion (internal complexity multiplying very fast as a system's inputs and behaviours grow), but says it is not necessarily true (pp. 33–35).
- The Game of Life (a grid world governed by one fixed rule) illustrates real patterns that are visible from a particular point of view; the discussion links the view to Quine's indeterminacy (rival interpretations of the same evidence can tie) and Parfit's empty questions (questions with no answer, where nothing may be left unknown) (pp. 37–41).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 13–15)
- Epigraph: Maugham's "Death Speaks." A servant flees to Samarra to avoid Death. Death says her gesture was only surprise at seeing him in Baghdad, since they had an appointment in Samarra that night (p. 13). Dennett uses it later as an image of "fatalistic inevitability" (p. 27).
- The chapter was first given as a Herbert Spencer Lecture at Oxford, November 1979 (note on p. 13).
- Social scientists talk constantly about belief. Belief attribution looks risky for exotic or religious beliefs, for animals, infants, computers, or for contradictory or wildly false beliefs. The example is a man in a bar who asked a biologist "Are rabbits birds?" to settle a bet (pp. 13–14).
- In ordinary cases attribution looks easy. It then seems plausible that it could be confirmed by finding the beliefs themselves inside the believer's head (p. 14).
- Two opposing views (Dennett says they are baldly stated and typically seen as mutually exclusive and exhaustive, p. 15):
  - **Realism:** having a belief is like having a particular virus, an objective internal fact (pp. 14–15).
  - **Interpretationism:** having a belief is like being immoral, having style, or making a good wife. We typically answer such questions by saying that it depends on what one is interested in (p. 15).
- **Dennett's thesis:** belief is a perfectly objective phenomenon, but it can be discerned only from the point of view of someone who adopts a certain predictive strategy. Its existence can be confirmed only by assessing that strategy's success. This apparently makes him both a realist and an interpretationist (p. 15); he calls the view that a theorist must choose only one of the two a mistake (p. 15).
- Plan: describe the strategy (the intentional stance), then argue that any system well predicted by it is a believer in the fullest sense. To be a true believer is to be an *intentional system*: a system whose behaviour is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy (p. 15). He admits earlier arguments won few converts (p. 15).

*Intuition note:* Dennett offers a third option between the view that belief is a hidden fact in the brain and the view that attributing belief is only a matter of interpretation. Belief is real, but you detect it by seeing whether a certain kind of prediction works.

### The Intentional Strategy and How It Works (pp. 15–22)
**Three strategies.**
- *Astrological strategy* is a deplorably popular but unreliable strategy. Dennett imagines "astrological systems" for which it works. We would then want to know *how* it works (its rules) and *why* it works. "Having a good strategy is one thing; knowing why it works is another" (p. 16). As far as we know that class is empty (p. 16).
- *Physical stance:* determine the physical constitution and the impinging inputs, then apply the laws of physics. It is Laplace's impractical dream in grand form. Local versions are usable (chemist, or cook with a pot). That it will always work in principle is a dogma of the physical sciences, Dennett says, setting aside quantum indeterminacies (p. 16).
- *Design stance:* ignore physical details and predict that the object will behave as it is designed to. Examples are the computer user and the alarm clock. Only designed behaviour is predictable this way, so a clock full of liquid helium needs the physical stance. Many biological things are also designed systems (p. 17).
- *Intentional stance:* (1) decide to treat the object as a rational agent; (2) work out what beliefs it ought to have, given its place in the world and purpose; (3) work out what desires it ought to have on the same grounds; (4) predict it will act to further its goals in light of its beliefs. Practical reasoning from these beliefs and desires yields a decision in many, but not all, instances (p. 17).

**Belief attribution.**
- Truisms: exposure to x (sensory confrontation over a suitable period) is the *normally sufficient* condition for knowing about x. The threshold for accepting abnormal ignorance is high. Example: someone who says he did not know the gun was loaded, though observed to be present, sighted and awake during the loading, meets utter skepticism (p. 18).
- Normally, people come to know only the *relevant* truths. First rule: attribute as beliefs all truths relevant to the system's interests (desires) that its experience to date has made available. This attributes somewhat too much and misses false beliefs (p. 18).
- False beliefs need a special genealogy, which consists in the main of true beliefs. Two paradigm cases are a false belief based on trusting Jones, and a false belief that there is a snake on the barstool. The falsehood has to start somewhere (hallucination, illusion, misperception, memory deterioration, fraud), but the false beliefs that result grow against a background of true beliefs (p. 18).
- Sophisticated beliefs, true or false, require tracing a lineage of mainly good reasoning from beliefs already attributed. So true believers *mainly* believe truths. *If* anyone could devise an agreed method of counting beliefs (Dennett doubts this), all but the smallest portion (say less than ten percent) would be attributable under the first rule (p. 19).
- Footnote 1 (p. 19): the idea that most beliefs must be true seems obvious to some (support for it is found in works by Quine, Putnam, Shoemaker, Davidson and Dennett) and incredible to others, probably because each side is calling a different phenomenon belief. Dennett's technical *opinion* is a linguistically infected state, roughly a bet on the truth of a formulated sentence. Even if every claim attributable to Democritus's physics were false, those claims are a small fraction of his beliefs. These include countless humdrum beliefs (which house he lived in, and so on). Objection ("it may be urged"): all his beliefs are theory-laden, so they are false if his theory is. Dennett's reply: why should his explicit, sophisticated theory be the one that burdens his everyday observations? If he forgot the theory, his observation beliefs would be largely untouched, so the catalogue would not be mainly false (p. 19).
- The first rule is derived from the *fundamental rule: attribute those beliefs the system ought to have* (p. 20).

**Desire attribution.**
- Desires are attributed by the same "ought": the familiar basic list (survival, absence of pain, food, comfort, procreation, entertainment). Citing one of them ends the "Why?" game (p. 20).
- Derived rules: attribute desires for things the system believes good for it, and for things it believes best means to other ends it desires. Bizarre or detrimental desires need "special stories," like false beliefs (p. 20).
- Language lets us express very specific desires (the mushroom omelette) and also forces us to commit to desires more stringent than we would otherwise have. The baked-beans dialogue ("How many?") illustrates this (pp. 20–21).
- The parallel in belief: our linguistic environment forces us to give precise verbal expression to convictions that lack hard edges (p. 21). Concentrating on the *results* of this social force can mislead one into thinking beliefs and desires are like sentences stored in the head. Sentence-like belief and desire are special cases that may not be reliable models for the whole domain (p. 21).

**Rationality.**
- One starts with the ideal of perfect rationality (people believe all implications of their beliefs and no contradictory pairs) and revises downward as circumstances dictate. Clutter is not a problem, since one is interested only in the implications relevant to the system's present predicament (p. 21).
- Irrationality and finite inference powers raise knotty problems, set aside here (see ch. 4 and Cherniak 1986) (p. 21).

**Use.**
- People use the strategy "all the time," and it works with people almost all the time. As evidence, we find it easy to say why Oxford colleges should not each grant degrees whenever they like. The answer is generated by reasoning about what people would then do (pp. 21–22).
- It also works on most other mammals most of the time, and on birds, fish, reptiles, insects, spiders and clams (a clam that believes there is danger will not relax its grip until convinced it has passed). It works on some artifacts: a chess-playing computer "knows" a line of play would lose its rook and "does not want" that. A thermostat turns off the boiler when it "believes" the room is warm enough (p. 22).
- It works on plants (apple varieties "cautious" about concluding it is spring) and even on lightning, which an electrician described as always wanting the best path to ground (p. 22).

*Intuition note:* The strategy asks what a sensible agent would do here. Dennett's examples show how far the question can be stretched, from people down to lightning, with varying reliability.

### True Believers as Intentional Systems (pp. 22–33)
**Is there a line between real and "as if"?**
- This is a "motley" mix of serious attributions, metaphors and frauds. Sorting real from "as if" would be a "Sisyphean labor" or settled by fiat. Better: even in the worst cases the strategy works at least a little bit, which distinguishes intentional systems from objects for which it never works (pp. 22–23).
- *Lectern objection:* the lectern "believes" it is at the centre of the civilized world and "wants" to stay there. Its behaviour is predicted accurately, so is it an intentional system? Dennett: for one thing, the strategy does not recommend itself here, because it gives no predictive power we did not already have. We tailored the beliefs to fit. With people, animals and computers the strategy is often the only practical way to predict (p. 23).
- *Objection ("it will be urged"):* this is only a difference in our limited capacities. A Laplacean physicist needs neither design nor intentional strategy. Engineers understand thermostats; the cleverest engineers may find it practically impossible to think about complex systems without lapsing into the intentional stance, but that is just "human epistemic frailty" (p. 23).
- Dennett asks whether it would be intolerable to hold that a creature is a believer for one observer but not for a cleverer one. That would be a radical interpretationism. His presentation has sometimes invited this reading and he now discourages it: Adopting the stance is a free choice, but once one adopts it, the facts about its success or failure are perfectly objective (p. 24).

**The power of the strategy.**
- In chess there are many legal moves but only a few (perhaps half a dozen) with anything to be said for them. Even when no single move is picked out, the strategy "dramatically" reduces live options (p. 24).
- It cannot predict the exact trades of a stock trader or a politician's exact words. It can say with high confidence that the trader will not buy utilities today, or that the politician will side with the unions against his party. This coarseness is a strength, because it allows *chaining predictions* (p. 24).
- Example: the US Secretary of State announces he is a paid agent of the KGB. We can predict dozens of salient consequences and consequences of consequences (Cabinet meeting, news conference, editorials, letters). This is an "arc of causation" not predictable under *any* description by any imaginable practical extension of physics or biology (pp. 24–25).

**Nozick's Martians (Objection, Nozick, p. 25).**
- Martians, who are Laplacean super-physicists, could predict even the ink marks on the Dow Jones tape from the microphysical level without treating us as intentional systems. Are we then not believers from their point of view? If so, belief is in the eye of the beholder, provided the beholder shares our limits (p. 25).
- **Dennett replies:** if the Martians did not also see us as intentional systems, they would miss something perfectly objective, namely the *patterns* describable only from the intentional stance that support generalizations and predictions (p. 25).
- *Stockbroker:* indefinitely many different finger motions and vocal-cord vibrations could replace the actual ones without disturbing the market. As there are indefinitely many ways of being a spark plug, there are indefinitely many ways of ordering 500 shares of General Motors, and societal "sockets" and "pivot points" where only *believing that p* or *desiring A* matters (p. 26).
- *Prediction contest:* Mrs Gardner takes a phone call from someone she calls "dear", who is coming home early with the boss, and she asks him to bring wine. The Earthling predicts a vehicle will stop in the drive within an hour and two people will get out, one with a bag containing a bottle. The Martian can make the same prediction only with vast knowledge of physical interactions. His performance would look like magic to anyone without the intentional strategy (pp. 26–27).
- Fatalists such as astrologers are wrong but "almost right": some patterns in human affairs impose themselves, not quite inexorably but with great vigour, absorbing perturbations that might as well be random. These patterns are characterized by the beliefs, desires and intentions of rational agents (p. 27).
- *Flaw in the story:* the Martian treats his Earthling opponent as an intelligent being with beliefs and desires. The moral Dennett draws: the intentional stance is unavoidable with regard to oneself and one's fellow intelligent beings (p. 27).
  - This unavoidability is interest relative. One may adopt the physical stance toward an intelligent being, oneself included, but not to the exclusion of an intentional stance toward oneself at minimum, and toward one's fellows *if* one intends to learn what they know (Hampshire) (p. 27).
- "Where there are intelligent beings, the patterns must be there to be described, whether or not we care to see them" (p. 28). If Martians observe, theorize, predict and communicate, they view themselves as intentional systems (p. 28).
- Footnote 2: an Oxford audience member said a Martian who included the Earthling in his physical purview would not be surprised by the prediction. Dennett: the Martian's prediction would still appear as a written Martian sentence. It would be baffling how a mechanism disguised as a Martian yields a *true* sentence that was so isolated from the events the Martian needed to know about (p. 28). Footnote 3: Dennett cannot see what would lead us to call intelligent beings *intelligent* if they had no use for communicating, predicting or observing (p. 28).

*Intuition note:* Knowing all the physics is not the same as seeing every pattern. The pattern of ordering shares because one believes something stays the same across countless physical variants. That sameness is real, but it only appears at the level of beliefs and desires.

**Imperfection and "mild" realism (pp. 28–29).**
- It is an objective fact that the strategy works as well as it does, and that this is not perfectly. No one is perfectly rational, unforgetful or all-observant. A partly melted amplifier cannot be given a clear wiring diagram; likewise, in even the mildest pathology (people who seem to hold contradictory beliefs or to deceive themselves) the strategy fails to yield clear, stable verdicts about which beliefs and desires to attribute (p. 28).
- **Strong realist:** the person really does have particular beliefs that the strategy cannot divine (p. 28). **Dennett's milder realism:** in such degenerate cases there is *no fact of the matter* about exactly which beliefs and desires a person has. This is not relativism, because "when and why there is no fact of the matter is itself a matter of objective fact" (pp. 28–29).
- He grants interest relativity: different cultures may attribute different beliefs to the same person. Then there are further facts about how well each rival scheme predicts. Two schemes may be about equally good and better than any others. That this is so can itself be a fact. "The objective presence of one pattern (with whatever imperfections) does not rule out the objective presence of another" (p. 29).
- The bogey of radically different, equally warranted interpretations is theoretically (even metaphysically) important but practically negligible for humans. Footnote 4: McCarthy's analogy to cryptography. The larger the corpus of cipher text, the less chance of dual, systematically unrelated decipherings (p. 29).

**Thermostat to human: degree, not kind (pp. 29–33).**
- The "perverse claim" is that *all there is* to being a true believer is being a system reliably predictable via the intentional strategy. *All there is* to believing that p is being an intentional system for which p occurs as a belief in the best (most predictive) interpretation. Yet for the truly interesting systems this apparently shallow, instrumentalist criterion puts a severe constraint on internal constitution, and so "yields a robust version of belief after all" (p. 29).
- Take a thermostat. We might grant it about half a dozen beliefs (that the room is too cold or too hot, that the boiler is on or off, and that it should turn on the boiler if it wants the room warmer). That imputes too much, since it has no concept of heat. *De-interpret:* it can believe the A is too F or G. Attached to other devices, it could regulate water in a tank or a train's speed (p. 30).
- Now enrich it: a vision system and an ear, geographical facts (Winnipeg in December), behavioural versatility (choose fuel, buy it from the cheapest dealer, check weather stripping). Each belief-like state then has more to do (more occasions for derivation and for use as a premise), and the semantics of the dummy predicates F and G is enriched (p. 30).
- Formally, the class of indistinguishably satisfactory models of the formal system embodied in its internal states shrinks as we add this, until "a unique semantic interpretation is practically (but never in principle) dictated" (cf. Hayes 1979). Then the device has beliefs *about heat* and *about this very room*, not only because of its actual location in and operations on the world, but because we cannot imagine another niche where it would work (pp. 30–31).
- The simple thermostat's belief is about *that* boiler because it is *fastened* to it. Footnote 5: this is the ancestor of the family of ideas under *de re* belief (p. 31). As systems get perceptually richer and more versatile, substituting links without changing the system's organization gets harder. Device and environment constrain each other ever more specifically. The usual way of saying this is that the organism *mirrors* the environment, or that a *representation* is implicit in its organization (p. 31).
- Dennett: it is not that beliefs and desires are attributed only to things with internal representations. Rather, when we find an object for which the intentional strategy works, we try to interpret some internal states as representations. "What makes some internal feature of a thing a representation could only be its role in regulating the behavior of an intentional system" (p. 32).
- No magic moment separates thermostat from a system that "really" has an internal representation of the world. We are so intricately connected to the world that almost no substitution is possible. Putnam's Twin Earth (a world identical except for a property below our powers of discrimination): if you were swapped with your twin you would never be the wiser, just as a simple control system cannot tell what it regulates. But our internal organization is so demanding that Twin Earth would have to be a virtual replica of Earth, or you would change state dramatically on arrival. Your beliefs about "the boiler" are about the boiler in your cellar (p. 32).
- The principles and problems of interpretation are the *same* for people and thermostats. The differences are differences of degree, but the degree is so great that understanding a simple system's internals tells little about a human's (pp. 32–33).

*Intuition note:* The more kinds of input and output a system has, the fewer ways its inner states can be read. A bare thermostat can be read as being about temperature, tank water or train speed; a richly connected system can be read in practically only one way.

### Why Does the Intentional Strategy Work? (pp. 33–35)
- The question is ambiguous. For a thermostat, one answer is "it is well designed," true but uninformative. The other answer, how the machinery works, is easy to find (p. 33).
- For a person, the first answer is that evolution has designed us to be rational. This guarantees that the strategy is a safe bet on us but is strikingly uninformative. Dennett says we cannot yet give a good answer to the harder question (how Nature's machinery works) (p. 33).
- **Skinnerian view:** imputed beliefs and desires are shorthand for complex descriptions of prior histories of response and reinforcement (pp. 33–34). Even if true, this says little about the internal machinery (p. 34).
- **Language-of-thought hypothesis (currently more popular):** for each predictively attributable belief, a functionally salient internal state exists, decomposable like the believed sentence into words or terms. Inferences are mirrored by physical causal processes, and the logical form of the believed proposition is copied in the structural form of the state (p. 34). Dennett thinks, provided one allows the bold claim to be greatly attenuated, some version will prove correct, but he does not think it obvious (p. 34).
- Two empirical claims should not be confused. (1) Intentional description yields an objective pattern in the world, "confirmed beyond skepticism." (2) That pattern is *produced by* another, roughly isomorphic pattern in the brain. Doubting (2) is not doubting (1) (p. 34).
- Dennett's argument for (2): as we move from thermostat to robot to human, designs run into *combinatorial explosion* (a 10% increase in a parameter can multiply internal complexity by orders of magnitude). The brain has solved this, so some elegant, generative, extendable principles of representation must be responsible. "We have only one model of such a representation system: a human language." So the argument comes down to asking what else it could be (p. 35).
- Dennett thinks this is good enough reason to pursue the hypothesis as scientific tactics, but it is far from assured that the hypothesis must be right. Footnote 6: all language-of-thought models so far fall victim to combinatorial explosion in some way, which tempers enthusiasm for what Fodor calls "the only game in town" (p. 35). Closing line: one does not well understand even a true empirical hypothesis while thinking it necessarily true (p. 35).

*Intuition note:* The question of why belief-talk works has a cheap answer (good design) and a costly one (what the brain does). Dennett's guess for the costly one is sentence-like internal structure, but he says its being inevitably right is far from assured.

### Reflections: Real Patterns, Deeper Facts, and Empty Questions (pp. 37–41)
**The balancing act (p. 37).** Many have been uneasy about whether, on Dennett's view, belief attribution depends only on the beholder, or whether all attributions are just useful fictions; Dennett says he tries to place his view on a knife-edge between simple realism and simple relativism. He insists it is a *sort* of realism: the patterns the Martians miss are objectively there to be noticed or overlooked (p. 37).

**Game of Life (Conway; pp. 37–39).**
- A grid; cells are the intersections; each cell is ON or OFF; each has eight neighbours (four adjacent, four diagonal). Time advances in discrete ticks (pp. 37–38).
- The one law (p. 38): count how many of the eight neighbours are ON. If exactly two, the cell keeps its state. If exactly three, it is ON next instant whatever its state. Otherwise it is OFF.
- This is a deterministic "Laplacean playpen": one can adopt the physical stance and predict with perfect accuracy (p. 38).
- Interesting configurations (things that blink back and forth, gliders, glider guns, eaters, and so on) support the *design stance* and a universal Turing machine (p. 38).
- With the "Turing machine stance" one ignores both the Life physics and the design details, computes the function the machine computes, and translates the output back. The prediction holds provided no stray gliders or debris destroy it (p. 39).
- Is the pattern real? So long as it lasts it is, and "armor" can make it survive. It *may* owe its existence to the designer's intentions, but its reality, in the sense of longevity or robustness, is strictly independent of historical facts about its origin. Seeing it is another matter: nothing is hidden in the Life world, but it takes a leap of insight to see the unfolding *as* the computation, and ascending to that view is optional (p. 39).
- Dennett claims the intentional stance gives a vantage point on similarly useful patterns. They are objective ("there to be detected") but not "out there" entirely independent of us, since they are partly composed of our own "subjective" reactions. They are patterns "made to order for our narcissistic concerns" (Akins 1986), perceived easily by us from the intentional stance and, he says, only from that stance (p. 39). Footnote 1: Anscombe's remark about an order that is there whenever actions are done with intentions left its location unstated. Dennett now sees what she may have intended: the order is as hard to place as patterns in the Life world, and obvious if you "look at" the world in the right way (p. 39).
- Martians might find the patterns extremely difficult but can aspire to know them, as we can aspire to know the world of the spider or fish (p. 40).

**Indeterminacy (pp. 40–41).**
- Dennett declines Rorty's radical perspectivism. When objective patterns fall short of perfection, there are uninterpretable gaps, and rival interpretations can tie for first place, so that no further fact could settle what the system really believed (p. 40).
- This extends Quine's indeterminacy of radical translation to internal "behaviour" too. "Radical translation begins at home" (Quine, quoted p. 40). Quine (1970) is quoted: the problem is not one of hidden facts to be uncovered by brain physiology, and expecting a distinct mechanism for every purported mental distinction is a further step from expecting one for every genuinely distinct state. Whether the foreigner "really" believes A or B is a question whose significance Quine would doubt (p. 40).
- Dennett's 1975 paper *Brain Writing and Mind Reading* applied the same considerations to translating any "language of thought" (p. 40). Footnote 2: Wheeler reads Derrida as supplying arguments for Quinean positions (p. 40).
- Davidson (quoted, p. 41): indeterminacy marks that certain apparent distinctions are not significant. If there is indeterminacy, it is because when all the evidence is in, alternative ways of stating the facts remain open.
- **Parfit** (personal identity, quoted p. 41): principles for personal identity leave open puzzling indeterminate cases. Parfit (quoted) says most of us are inclined to believe that a question such as whether one is about to die must have a simple Yes or No answer, that is, that identity must be determinate. The club example: a club lapses and some members later form a club with the same name and rules. If no rule or fact settles whether it is the *same* club, the claim that it is the same club is neither true nor false, and the question is *empty* (p. 41).
- Dennett says we are just as strongly attracted to the view that the contents of our beliefs must be determinate, and resist the suggestion that whether I believe there is milk in the fridge could lack a yes/no answer. In the club case no "deeper fact" would settle things, but for personal identity the supposition that there is one "dies hard." He then begins to cite Nagel's brute convictions, and the unit's pages end there (p. 41).

*Intuition note:* Saying there is no further fact does not mean that anything goes. The objective facts (prediction success) can leave some questions open, and it is an objective fact that they do.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Part A: a level of description that is real.** In the Game of Life, put three ON cells in a horizontal row at positions (−1,0), (0,0), (1,0). All other cells are OFF.
- Cell (−1,0): ON neighbours = only (0,0), so 1 → OFF (rule: otherwise OFF).
- Cell (0,0): ON neighbours = (−1,0) and (1,0), so 2 → keeps its state, ON.
- Cell (1,0): ON neighbours = only (0,0), so 1 → OFF.
- Cell (0,1): its neighbours include all three ON cells, so 3 → ON. Likewise (0,−1) → ON.
- Cell (−1,1): ON neighbours = (−1,0) and (0,0), so 2 → stays OFF. The same holds for the other corner cells.
- Next state: ON cells at (0,−1), (0,0), (0,1), a vertical row. Applying the same law again gives back the horizontal row (by symmetry, added step).

Physical-stance prediction takes one count per cell per tick. A higher-level pattern (this configuration flips between horizontal and vertical every tick) predicts all future ticks in one sentence. It is true only as long as nothing collides with it, as Dennett says of the Turing machine pattern (p. 39).

**Part B: rival interpretations scored by success.** Over 10 observed choices, Scheme A (Sam believes it will rain and wants to stay dry) predicts 8 correctly. Scheme B (Sam believes the forecast is unreliable but wants to look prepared) also predicts 8. Random guessing between two options gives expected 5 of 10. Success rates: A = 8/10 = 0.8, B = 8/10 = 0.8, chance = 0.5. Both beat chance, and they tie. If no further data separate them, then (on Dennett's view, p. 29, p. 40) there is no further fact about which belief Sam "really" has. That the schemes tie is still an objective fact about the data.

## Concepts
- [[intentional-stance]] — predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires it ought to have; Dennett calls it the "intentional strategy" (p. 15, p. 17).
- [[physical-design-stance]] — physical stance: predict from physical constitution and laws; design stance: predict from what it was designed to do (pp. 16–17).
- [[rational-agent-assumption]] — start from ideal rationality (believe implications, no contradictions) and revise downward; attribute what the system *ought* to have (pp. 17, 20–21).
- [[intentionality]] — what Brentano and others call the feature of mental states such as beliefs and desires; here the states the strategy attributes (p. 15).
- [[true-believer-as-intentional-system]] (new) — a system whose behaviour is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy (p. 15).
- [[interpretationism-vs-realism]] (new) — two views of belief attribution (like a virus vs. like having style); Dennett argues that the two need not be treated as mutually exclusive (pp. 14–15).
- [[real-patterns]] (new) — objective regularities that support generalizations and predictions; the intentional ones are visible only from the intentional stance and are composed partly of our own reactions (pp. 25, 39).
- [[interest-relativity]] (new) — attributions can depend on the attributor's interests; rival schemes may tie, and that is itself a fact (p. 29).
- [[language-of-thought]] (new) — hypothesis that the structural form of internal states copies the logical form of the propositions believed; Dennett thinks some version will prove correct, but not that it is obvious (p. 34).
- [[combinatorial-explosion]] (new) — internal complexity multiplies when inputs or behaviours increase slightly; the problem the brain must somehow have solved, which Dennett uses to argue for the language-of-thought hypothesis (p. 35).
- [[indeterminacy-of-translation]] (new) — Quine's thesis, extended by Dennett to internal states: rival interpretations can tie, with no further fact (pp. 40–41).
- [[empty-question]] (new) — Parfit's term: a question with no answer where there may be nothing we do not know (p. 41).

## Canonical instances
- [[thermostat-case]] — degenerate intentional system; enriching its connections narrows its interpretation (pp. 22, 30–32).
- [[lectern-case]] — a trivially predictable object whose "beliefs" are tailored, so the strategy adds nothing (p. 23).
- [[martians-nozick]] — super-physicists who might miss objective intentional patterns (pp. 25–28).
- [[game-of-life]] — a deterministic microworld showing patterns at different levels and stances (pp. 37–39).
- [[twin-earth]] — Putnam's near-identical planet, used to show how little substitution a richly connected system tolerates (p. 32).
- [[death-speaks]] — Maugham's Samarra story, epigraph and image of "fatalistic inevitability" (pp. 13, 27).
- [[democritus-case]] — even if his explicit physics were all false, most of his beliefs would be true (p. 19, note 1).
- [[chess-computer]] — "knows" a line loses its rook and "wants" to avoid it (p. 22); chess predictions cut options to about half a dozen (p. 24).
- [[parfit-club]] — a club reconvened with no rule or fact: an empty question (p. 41).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] intentional stance, attribute what the system *ought* to want and believe, revised downward ↔ theory-of-mind: rational-agent assumption / inverse planning with noisy (Boltzmann) rationality — both infer mental states from behaviour via a model of an approximately rational agent.
- [structural-analogy] shrinking class of "indistinguishably satisfactory models" as connections are enriched (p. 30) ↔ statistics/machine learning: identifiability in inverse reinforcement learning, where more varied behaviour narrows the set of consistent reward functions.
- [structural-analogy] success-defined belief (the best, most predictive interpretation, p. 29) ↔ AI evaluation: judging machine ToM by behavioural prediction, and the construct-validity worry about what that measures.
- [structural-analogy] Life patterns visible at several levels (physical, design, Turing machine; pp. 38–39) ↔ social science: emergent, multi-level description in agent-based models.
- [metaphor] the claim that fatalists are almost right (p. 27) ↔ social science: robust macro-patterns that absorb micro-level noise.

## Questions this raises
- If belief is "whatever the most predictive interpretation says," how should a researcher decide between two interpretations that tie? Do contested test items in false-belief research show such ties?
- Dennett puts weight on an argument from combinatorial explosion for a language of thought. Does a system trained on language, whose inner states are not obviously sentence-like, test or strain this argument?
- Dennett says there is no magic moment between a thermostat and a system that really has an internal representation of the world; the differences are of degree (p. 32). Which degrees (perception, versatility, learning) would be the right ones to measure in an AI system?
- Is the combination of a free choice to adopt the stance with objective facts about its success (p. 24) stable, or does it quietly depend on the observer's interests (pp. 29, 39)?
