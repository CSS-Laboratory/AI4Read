# Evolution, Error, and Intentionality

## L0 — One line
Dennett argues that if our minds are products of natural selection, our intentionality (the "aboutness" of our mental states) is *derived*, like a vending machine's, so there is no deeper fact fixing what our thoughts "really" mean; this rejects the doctrine of "original intentionality".

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett finds a hidden split among philosophers (Fodor, Searle, Dretske, Burge, Kripke). They hold that human mental states have "original" meaning, unlike artifacts such as coin-slot machines. Dennett uses a vending-machine "two-bitser" and a hibernation robot to argue the opposite. Evolution is a designer without foresight, so we are artifacts too. Which of a state's occasions count as errors then depends on what it is "for". This is read from the design and its prowess, and can stay indeterminate. He argues that Dretske's, Fodor's and Burge's accounts of error run into the same problem. He concludes that realism about meanings needs realism about functions, and that original functionality has no bedrock.

**Prerequisites:** the intentional stance (predicting a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires); the idea of natural selection; the Twin Earth thought experiment (named here but only briefly described).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **The hidden disagreement.** Fodor and others accept that artifacts have only *derived* intentionality (meaning borrowed from users) but insist that human minds have *original* intentionality. Dennett calls this the most fundamental point of disagreement between them and him (pp. 288, 294).
- **The two-bitser.** A coin detector's states count as accurate or mistaken only relative to the intentions of its designers and users. After a move to Panama, the same physical state would have a different set of errors (pp. 291–293).
- **Evolution as designer.** The thought experiment of a robot built to protect a sleeper is meant to suggest that its "mental" life would probably look very much like ours. If we insist that no artifact has more than derived intentionality, then the same holds of us, relative to our genes and natural selection (pp. 295–298).
- **Selection without representation.** Dennett takes natural selection to be blind and to involve no representation at all (a footnote allows one sense in which it does). Yet it has proved sensitive to reasons and can "choose" designs for one reason rather than another (pp. 299, 317 fn. 7).
- **No deeper facts.** When more than one reading of a function is well supported, Dennett says no further fact settles which is right. He says there is nothing like a text to consult in Mother Nature's mind (p. 300).
- **The disjunction problem.** The problem: what makes a state mean "quarter" (so a slug causes an error) rather than "quarter or slug" (so there is never an error)? Dennett argues that Dretske's and Fodor's answers fail or rely on the appeal to design purposes (teleology) that Fodor resists, and that Burge must give up his Realism about content (pp. 301–313).
- **Intensionality reappears.** Biologists' talk about macromolecules shows no failure of substitution (the usual mark of intentional talk). Dennett argues that it reappears one level down: natural selection "appreciates" reasons under specific descriptions (pp. 315–316).
- **Conclusion.** Dennett argues that we get better grounds for function by reading it off present prowess than off creators' statements. There is no ultimate "User's Manual", so there is no more bedrock for original functionality than for original intentionality (pp. 319–321).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the end of Chapter 7 (p. 286)
- Page 286 is the last page of the preceding chapter on adaptationism. Dennett says adaptationist thinking may often be unable to answer questions about the actual history (etiology) of a design. It can still formulate, and perhaps confirm, a *functional analysis* of the design (p. 286).
- Whether a design has a free-floating (unrepresented) rationale or a represented one may be indiscernible in the design. This uncertainty is independent of confirming the rationale (p. 286).
- Historical facts are equally neutral when we move in the other direction: even when found, they are unable to resolve questions about the rationale on which our interpretation of the design depends (p. 286).

### Introduction: the Great Divide (pp. 287–289)
- Dennett found in fall 1985 what he took to be a submerged disagreement. An earlier draft with deliberately oversimplified battle lines (the good guys against the bad guys) drew many responses. They mostly confirmed his division of the field (p. 287).
- The trigger was a discussion of a chapter of Fodor's *Psychosemantics* ("Meaning and the World Order"). That chapter concerns Dretske's attempts to solve the problem of *misrepresentation* (error: a state that "says" something false). Dennett proposed starting with a coin-slot tester on a vending machine accepting a slug (p. 288).
- **Fodor's reply:** the case is irrelevant, because Searle is right that such artifacts have no intrinsic or original intentionality, only *derived* intentionality (p. 288).
- **Definition (Dennett's description of the doctrine):** *original* (intrinsic) intentionality is the claim that, whereas some of our artifacts may have intentionality derived from us, we have it utterly underived. In the text's phrase, we are "Unmeant Meaners". Dennett has never believed this (p. 288).
- Dennett cites Dretske (1985), who draws a sharp contrast between people and computers. Dretske says machines lack something essential to membership in the society of rational agents, and people do not (pp. 288–289).
- Dennett says Burge and Kripke also seemed to him to fall on Searle's side. His claim, to be shown, is that the problem of error impales "all and only" those who believe in original or intrinsic intentionality (p. 289). Whether "original" and "intrinsic" mean the same is left to be approached indirectly (p. 289).
- **Haugeland's statement of the distinction** (quoted): artifacts have meaning only because we give it to them, so it is "derivative". Genuine understanding, he says, is intentional in its own right and not derivatively (p. 289).
- Dennett's illustrations of the opponents' view: an encyclopedia has derived intentionality, and automated into a question-answering system it is still a tool. A chess computer, on this view, has artificial, derived goals, and its internal states about board positions are likewise only derived intentionality, not original (pp. 289–290).
- Dennett calls this a persuasive theme and says it is not really an argument. It has convinced more than a few thinkers that no artifact could have our kind of intentionality. He proposes to suppose this is the doctrine and see where it leads (p. 290).

*Intuition note.* "Derived" means the meaning is borrowed from the people who use the thing. "Original" means a mind's meaning needs no such borrowing. The whole chapter asks whether humans really belong in the second group.

### The Case of the Wandering Two-Bitser (pp. 290–294)
- **Setup.** A US soft-drink machine has a transducer (a device turning physical input into a signal) for accepting and rejecting US quarters. Call such a device a *two-bitser*. When a quarter goes in, it enters state $Q$. This state "means" (Dennett uses scare-quotes) that it perceives or accepts a genuine US quarter now (p. 290).
- It "makes mistakes": it sometimes enters $Q$ for a slug or foreign object, and sometimes rejects legal quarters. Mistakes follow patterns. Dennett says at least some of them could be predicted by someone with enough knowledge of physics and design parameters, for example that objects of kind $K$ would reliably fool it (pp. 290–291).
- If $K$ became common, designers would build better transducers, and so on, until diminishing returns. No mechanism is foolproof, and it is not cost-effective to guard against negligible abuses (p. 291).
- **Dennett's first conclusion.** The only thing making the device a quarter-detector, rather than a slug-detector or quarter-or-slug-detector, is the *shared intention* of its designers, builders, owners and users. Only relative to that context are some occasions of $Q$ "veridical" (accurate) and others "mistaken" (p. 291).
- Dennett expects Fodor, Searle, Dretske, Burge and Kripke to agree that this is a textbook case of derived intentionality (p. 291).
- **Panama.** A two-bitser could be installed in a Panamanian machine. Quarter-balboas minted 1966–1984 are indistinguishable from US quarters by standard vending machines, since they are struck from US quarter stock. Dennett cites a coin dealer for this (p. 291).
- In Panama the machine enters the same physical state when a US quarter, a $K$-object or a balboa goes in, but a different set of occasions counts as mistakes. US quarters are now slugs, inducers of error just as much as objects of kind $K$ are (p. 292).
- Should we say state $Q$ still occurs, or a new state $QB$? Dennett says there is "considerable freedom" in what to say, since it is an artifact and talk of its intentionality is "just metaphor" (p. 292).
- **Proper function** (Millikan 1984). The original purpose is its *raison d'être*, so relative to that function we can identify its veridical states and errors. It could also be pressed into new service (balboa-detector, doorstop, weapon), with a period of indeterminacy at the transition (p. 292).
- Dennett's thought experiment: give the machine a counter. It stands at 1,435,792 after ten years and is not reset in transit. On its debut in Panama it reads 1,435,793. Does that tip the balance toward saying it has not yet switched to balboa-detection? (p. 293)
- **Dennett's answer (with Millikan).** Whether the debut counts as $Q$ or $QB$ depends on whether, in its new niche, it was *selected for* its capacity to detect balboas, for instance by the Panamanian Pepsi-Cola franchise holder. If so, its first "perceptual" act is a correct identification, because that is what it is now *for*. If it arrived by mistake or coincidence, the debut means nothing, though once its utility is recognized and the relevant authorities press it into service in the new role, its subsequent states would count as tokens of $QB$ (p. 293).
- Nothing intrinsic to the two-bitser, considered narrowly, all by itself and independently of its prior history, would distinguish it from a genuine balboa-detector made to order (p. 293).
- **Where the disagreement lies.** Dennett expects Fodor et al. to let him say all this about an artifact, since there is no "deeper" fact to find (pp. 293–294). They part company when he applies the same morals and the same pragmatic rules of interpretation to humans. Fodor and company are sure that deeper facts exist about what a person's state *really* means, independent of any interpreter. Dennett calls this belief in "original intentionality" (p. 294).
- Dennett says this explains several puzzles for him. These are why Fodor dislikes evolutionary hypotheses as much as artificial intelligence, why Dretske goes to such lengths over error, and why Burge's anti-individualism and Kripke's rule-following discussion always struck him as labors wasted on breaking down an unlocked door (p. 294).
- **The opponents' view (as Dennett reports it).** They might agree with him about the transported two-bitser, but they say humans are not just fancier two-bitsers. Believing one perceives a US quarter, or real water as opposed to XYZ, is "no metaphor" (p. 294).
- **Jones and the schmorse** (Twin Earth example). Jones thinks he sees a horse. On Twin Earth, schmorses look just like horses. Taken there and shown a schmorse, either he is still in the (mistaken) state of believing he sees a horse, or he is for the first time in the state of believing he sees a schmorse (or perhaps, Dennett adds, he is in neither, so violently has his cognitive system been assaulted). Those who find it irresistible that he is really in one state or the other, however hard to determine which, believe in original intentionality (p. 295). Dennett lists on that side Fodor, Searle, Dretske, Burge, Kripke, Chisholm, Nagel, Popper and Eccles. On his side he lists the Churchlands, Davidson, Haugeland, Millikan, Rorty, Stalnaker, Quine, Sellars, Hofstadter, Minsky and almost everyone in AI (p. 295).
- Dennett says he cannot hope to refute the opposing tradition in the short compass of a chapter. He promises a thought experiment and a diagnosis of the perplexities his opponents entangle themselves in (p. 295).

*Intuition note.* In the two-bitser case, the machine's physics is fixed, but which inputs count as errors changes with its job. If human meanings work the same way, their error conditions also depend on a wider setting, not only on what is inside the head. This is Dennett's claim, and his opponents deny it for humans.

### Designing a Robot (pp. 295–298)
- **Thought experiment.** You want to live in the 25th century and your body can be kept alive only in a hibernation capsule. The capsule needs protection and energy for over 400 years. You cannot count on descendants (p. 296).
- Two strategies, copied from nature (roughly plants versus animals): a fixed "plant" installation, which cannot move out of harm's way, or a mobile robot with sensors that protects the capsule. You choose the robot. It must "choose" actions that further your interests, and "bad" moves are those that would incapacitate it for its sole *raison d'être* (p. 296).
- You had best rely on economies, giving it no more discriminatory prowess than it will probably need, and it would be wise to give it enough sophistication to calculate the benefits and risks of cooperating with other robots. It will be capable of self-control and of deriving its own subsidiary goals, some of which may be ill advised, and it may even embark on suicidal actions (p. 297).
- **Fodor et al.'s claim, as Dennett reports it.** The robot has no original intentionality, only that derived from its role as your protector. Its states would be "as if" deciding, seeing and planning (p. 297).
- Dennett notes the robot has been given "planning", "learning" and "communicating" abilities. It would probably perform very creditably in a Turing Test, and would almost certainly need control structures rich in self-monitoring. Its "opinions" about its own states would be very good evidence, probably the best easily available, of what they "mean" metaphorically speaking. The two-bitser was given no such capacity to sway our judgments with confident avowals (pp. 297–298).
- **Implication of standing firm.** If no artifact has more than derived intentionality, then, Dennett says, our own intentionality is exactly like that of the robot. This is a variation on Dawkins's (1976) picture of us as survival machines for our selfish genes. Our intentionality would be derived from the intentionality of our genes: they, and not we, are the Unmeant Meaners (p. 298).

*Intuition note.* The robot story is built so that the "designer" is us, and the robot's job is only a derived one. Dennett then points out that the evolutionary story has a similar shape, with our genes in the role of the sleeper, although he also notes (p. 299) that evolution involves no foresighted engineer. If one accepts that the robot's meaning is derived, consistency seems to force the same verdict on us.

### Reading Mother Nature's Mind (pp. 298–300)
- **The embarrassment.** The account derives our literal intentionality from genes, whose intentionality is a paradigm of "as if" (pp. 298–299). The disanalogy: the robot had a foresighted engineer, but natural selection has no conscious, deliberate designer (p. 299).
- Natural selection removes the intelligent Artificer from the account of origins. Genes are stupid and do not do the designing. Mother Nature, or more literally the long, slow process of evolution, does (p. 299).
- Its "uncanny capacity" is to mirror some properties of mind. It has no foresight and no purpose. Yet it has proved very sensitive to rationales and can "choose" a design for one reason rather than another, without representing the choice or the reasons. Hearts were chosen for circulating blood, not for the rhythm of their beating (p. 299).
- Dennett takes there to be no representation at all in the process, and yet it certainly seems that we can give principled explanations of evolved design features that invoke, in effect, what Mother Nature had in mind. In footnote 3 he quotes Millikan (1986): to suppose otherwise is to suppose our cognitive life an accidental epiphenomenal cloud over mechanisms that evolution devised with other things in mind (pp. 299–300, fn. 3).
- **Analogy.** Just as the Pepsi franchise holder selects the two-bitser *for* its talent and adopts it *as* a balboa-detector, evolution selects an organ *for* oxygenating blood and establishes it *as* a lung. Behaviours, perceptions and beliefs can be identified only relative to such design "choices" or evolution-"endorsed" purposes (p. 300).
- **Why the idea is resisted** (Dennett's hunch): it has two unobvious implications that some find unpalatable.
  1. If we are artifacts, we have no special authority about what our thoughts mean, or whether they mean anything. Dretske and Burge have already given up privileged access and can accept this (p. 300).
  2. There are no deeper facts fixing the meanings of our thoughts. Where more than one interpretation of proper function is well supported, Dennett says there is no fact of the matter. When we go to read Mother Nature's mind, he says there is no text to be interpreted (p. 300).

*Intuition note.* Natural selection never writes down a reason, but the designs it leaves behind behave as if someone had one. Dennett's claim is that functions are read off such reasons from the intentional stance, with no deeper fact behind them, and he says the same then holds for meaning. (He also calls the intentionality of natural selection "just as real", p. 318.)

### Error, Disjunction, and Inflated Interpretation (pp. 301–313)

**Dretske's two kinds of meaning (pp. 301–302)**
- Dretske distinguishes *natural meaning* ($\text{meaning}_n$), defined so that misrepresentation is ruled out, and *functional meaning* ($\text{meaning}_f$), which allows a state to "say" something false. A short-circuited doorbell does not mean$_n$ that the button is pressed, whatever it was designed to indicate (p. 301).
- But if functions are *assigned* (by designers), $\text{meaning}_f$ is "tainted" by their purposes. The two-bitser's $Q$ is such a case (p. 301). Dretske needs *natural* functions, independent of our interpretive purposes, defined in terms of the organism's "own cognitive economy" (p. 301).
- Dennett: the obvious move is to substitute Mother Nature's purposes. Dretske follows this path warily, but sees a problem: the biological version of the question whether $Q$ means "quarter" or "balboa" or "$F$ or $G$ or $K$". He wants an interpretation principle that does not "artificially inflate" natural functions (p. 302).
- **Frog's eye** (Lettvin et al. 1959). A frog catches a lead pellet. If the eye's signal "tells" the frog that a fly is coming, the eye passed on mistaken information. If it merely signals a dark moving patch, the eye told the truth and the error belongs later in processing (p. 302).
- Dennett's point: if we interpret strictly minimally, the frog never errs. Any signal can be "de-interpreted" by adding disjuncts (fly *or* pellet *or* dark spot *or* …) back to brute $\text{meaning}_n$, unless we relativize to an assumption of the Normal (Millikan's sense) function (p. 302).

**Inflation as a virtue: Braitenberg's symmetry detector (pp. 303–304)**
- Dretske worries about overendowing states with content. Dennett replies that, given Mother Nature's stinginess, this abstemiousness risks missing the "point" of her inventions (p. 303).
- Many creatures have hardware sensitive to symmetry about a vertical axis. The deflationary description is that it signals an instance of symmetry about a vertical axis on the retina. Braitenberg (1984) asks what in nature presents vertical symmetry: only other animals facing the viewer (rear views are often symmetrical too, but less strikingly). So the detector tells (roughly) that someone is looking at you (p. 303).
- Dennett asks whether it is inflationary to call this transducer a predator-detector (or a predator-or-mate-or-rival-detector), and whether a fast, powerful but not foolproof detector might be the best design, with false alarms a small price. Ecologically insignificant symmetries count as false alarms only if the special-purpose wiring is *supposed* to tell the organism, roughly, that someone is looking at it (p. 303).
- The demand for precision of content independent of interpretation is the hallmark of Dretske's programme and of much philosophy of language and mind. For the symmetry-detector, he says, there is no "principled" answer beyond what can be supported by the functions we can discover in its normal operation in nature (pp. 303–304).
- Artifact hermeneutics (Dennett's name for interpreting artifacts through costs and benefits of design choices) upgraded the two-bitser from disk-detector to quarter-detector. Dretske resists the same principle for nature because it would not give what the event *really* means in the sense of original intentionality (p. 304).
- In footnote 4 Dennett argues against Dretske's claim that animals need states rich in information to survive. Some bacteria survive with only north-detectors, and other creatures get by with mere symmetry-detectors (p. 304, fn. 4).
- Dennett says Dretske seems to conflate (a) something's meaning something to or for an organism with (b) the organism's being in a position to know that fact from the inside (p. 304). He quotes Dretske: unless symbols have intrinsic meaning, independent of our communicative intentions, the meaning "must be irrelevant" to what the machine does (p. 305).
- **Dennett's reply.** Meaning must make a real difference to the organism's life, which Dretske rightly insists on. But in an organism that meaning, while independent of *our* purposes, is not independent of Mother Nature's. So it is just as derived and just as subject to indeterminacy as the two-bitser's (p. 305).

**Dretske's learning-history move (pp. 305–307)**
- Dretske tries to secure "functional determination" through associative learning. Repeated exposure may establish an internal state type with a definite, unique function. He says an artifact could (logically) *acquire* original intentionality, though not *have* it at creation (p. 305).
- Dennett says the move will not work and cites Fodor (1987): (1) it needs a sharp line between a learning period and the later period when meaning is fixed. Any such line is arbitrary (Fodor asks whether a whistle blows). (2) It cannot cover innate representations. Dretske accepts this: he does not think there can be innate concepts or beliefs (pp. 305–306).
- **Cuckoo.** A newly hatched baby cuckoo looks for other eggs and rolls them out of the nest. It has no inkling of the functional meaning, but the meaning is there for and to the organism, unless "to" requires reflecting on or avowing it. The question is how far the rationale is the fledgling's and how far free-floating (the author refers back to chapter 7). Dretske treats this as all-or-nothing (p. 306).
- Dennett's diagnosis: Dretske seems to be trying, at one stroke, to draw an all-or-nothing line between free-floating and "fully appreciated" rationales, and to remove all interpretive slack from the "actual" meaning (p. 306). He also separates syntax from semantics (echoing Searle). Even if such a threshold could be motivated, a learning history could not break through it and show an organism what its states "really meant" (p. 307).
- **Symmetry argument.** If a learning history worked for learned representations, the very same move could work for innate ones "learned" by ancestors through natural selection. Soft-wiring in a lifetime corresponds to hard-wiring over the species' lifetime. The whistle problem returns. What began as a two-bitser can become a q-balber, and a wrist bone can become a panda's thumb (Gould 1980) (p. 307).

**Fodor's account (pp. 307–309)**
- Dennett suggests that a good Fodorian motto would be: no representation without misrepresentation. He too draws the derivative/original distinction. In the quote Dennett gives, Fodor says he is prepared for it to turn out that smoke and thermostats represent only relative to our interests, and that only mental states, hence only mental representations, have semantic properties in the first instance (pp. 307–308).
- Fodor faces the *disjunction problem*: what grounds say that the state means "quarter here now" (so a slug causes error) rather than "quarter or quarter-balboa or slug of kind $K$ or …" (so there is never an error)? Fodor resists the "teleology/optimality" answer: he says he is not sure it is false but finds it thoroughly unsatisfying, and holds that the less Pop-Darwinism the better (p. 308).
- Dennett's guess is that Fodor sees that any evolutionary story, however well supported, leaves the indeterminacy of the transported two-bitser. Fodor wants real original meaning for our mental representations (p. 308).
- **Fodor's own account (quoted).** Misidentifying a cow as a horse would not have caused "horse" tokening *except that* "horse" independently means horse. The causal dependence is asymmetric: horses cause "horse" tokenings without any cow–"horse" connection being needed (pp. 308–309).
- Dennett: this is spelled out in counterfactuals, but the nagging problem returns. How does Fodor establish that his "horse" means *horse* and not *horse-or-other-quadruped-resembling-a-horse*? Either he takes Searle's introspective route, or appeals to design considerations, the teleology story he wants to resist (p. 309).

**Burge's anti-individualism (pp. 309–311)**
- **Individualism, in Burge's words.** An individual's intentional states could not differ given the individual's physical, chemical, neural or functional histories, specified non-intentionally and independently of physical or social conditions outside the individual's body (p. 309).
- Dennett: individualism is false of two-bitsers. Moving one to Panama changed the meaning of its state from $Q$ to $QB$ with nothing changed inside (p. 309). Attributing meaning to functional states depends on assumptions about what it is supposed to do. Burge's thesis is a special case of the familiar point that functional characterizations are relative to the environment and to optimality assumptions (pp. 309–310).
- Burge's "person $P$" argument becomes the two-bitser argument by substitution of "two-bitser", "$Q$", "quarter", "quarter-balboa" (p. 310).
- **Difference.** Burge leaves no room for indeterminacy. He chooses to ignore generalized arguments that mentalistic ascriptions are deeply indeterminate, and announces his Realism about intentional content (p. 310). So he seems to argue that intrinsic, original intentionality is as context-sensitive as derived intentionality (p. 310).
- Dennett says that Burge's reliance on Marr's vision theory depends on optimality assumptions about what Mother Nature "had in mind" (pp. 310–311). Burge's own statement is that individuation is governed by the assumption that the subject has adapted to its environment enough to get veridical information under normal conditions (p. 311).
- **Dennett's argument.** Burge's method must assume that the dictated attributions are those that come out veridical *and useful*. Without "useful", disjunctive dissipation of content follows, since veridicality can always be bought by adding disjuncts. Utility is not an objective, determinate property, as the two-bitser showed. So Burge must give up Realism about intentional content (p. 311).

**Putnam and "glug" (pp. 311–312)**
- Putnam's Twin Earth examples presuppose the same Realism. With the two-bitser, nature's laws do not suffice to fix what its state really means, short of making misrepresentation impossible (pp. 311–312).
- **Glug.** A tribe uses "glug" for the explosive marsh gas. Everything they have met is methane, but they are unsophisticated about chemistry. Shown acetylene, they call it glug. Is that a mistake? Presumably gaseous hydrocarbon and its subspecies are natural kinds, so the laws of nature do not favour one reading (p. 312).
- **Dennett's view:** if, as seems likely, no answer can be wrung from the intentional stance in their case, the meaning of their belief is simply indeterminate: it is not just that he and they cannot tell; he says there is nothing to tell. **Putnam's view (Realist):** there is a further fact, however inaccessible to interpreters (p. 312). Realists divide on whether the natives have better access to it (p. 312).

**Kripke and meaning rationalism (pp. 312–313)**
- Searle and Fodor are reluctant to rely on privileged access, but Kripke is willing. Kripke (1982) says a machine's meaning is relative to the intentions of its designer and that malfunction depends on the designer's program (p. 313).
- Kripke is unwilling to say the same of humans. The passage calls it bizarre that we lack "direct" access to whether we mean plus or quus (Dennett's gloss: $Q$ or $QB$ in the two-bitser's case). Dennett notes that this may not be Kripke speaking *in propria persona*, since it occurs in a response he imagines Wittgenstein giving to a skeptic (p. 313).
- Dennett calls this a powerful expression of the intuition behind belief in original intentionality. Millikan calls the doctrine *meaning rationalism*, and her book (1984) aims to topple it. **Dennett's conclusion:** something has to give. Abandon meaning rationalism (that you are unlike the cuckoo in having privileged access to your meanings), or abandon naturalism, the view that you are a product of natural selection and so have derivative, potentially indeterminate intentionality (p. 313).

### Is Function in the Eye of the Beholder? (pp. 314–321)

**The objection of incoherence (p. 314)**
- Some see an irresolvable tension in using intentional idioms for a process Dennett insists is mechanical, goalless and without foresight. Brentano called intentionality the mark of the mental, while Darwin eliminated Mind from origins. The challenge could be put to Dawkins: why call natural selection a watchmaker that is blind and not even trying to make watches? (p. 314)
- **Macromolecules** (Rosenberg 1986b, quoting Stryer). Biochemists say synthetases "discriminate", make "errors", "correct" and "avoid" things. It seems obvious this is mere "as if" intentionality, a theorist's fiction (p. 314).
- **Cash value.** Rosenberg's view is that every cognitively describable macromolecule state has a unique physical characterization and a unique disjunction of consequences. Dennett says this may be more an ideal than an accomplished fact. Likewise, for vending machines we assume there are no mysteriously powerful coin detectors, yet we can identify them as coin detectors long before we know how the competence is achieved (p. 315).
- Intentional characterizations are needed to keep track of what is to be explained, and even after mechanical explanations are complete, as the level against which to measure Mother Nature's "bargains" (p. 315).

**Intensionality reappears at a deeper level (pp. 315–316)**
- **Further challenge.** Rosenberg accepts that failure of substitution ("intensionality") is a mark of intentionality. He notes that biologists' attributions to macromolecules do not show it: one can substitute freely. The enzyme does not recognize the error it corrects *qua* error, and the synthetase has no conception of isoleucine *qua* intermediate (p. 315).
- Dennett's reply is that the missing intensionality reappears one level down. Isoleucine is "desired" only *qua* intermediate, as an unsubstitutable part of a design whose rationale is "appreciated" by natural selection. While the enzyme does not know it corrects errors qua errors, Mother Nature "does". It is only qua error that the eliminated items provoked the proofreading competence (p. 316).
- Biologists tempted to renounce all function talk are right that there is no stable intermediate position. Saying that eyes are for seeing commits one to Sober's (1984) *selection for* (not merely selection *of*) features. Such claims resist substitution: just as George IV wondered whether Scott was the author of *Waverley* without wondering whether Scott was Scott, natural selection "desired" isoleucine as intermediate without desiring that isoleucine be isoleucine (p. 316).
- Evolutionary processes can be described without intentional language, but at the cost of cumbersomeness, lack of generality and unwanted detail. That loses the pattern that supports prediction and counterfactuals (pp. 316–317).
- The "why" questions about the robot's engineering have parallels in organisms. If we work out these rationales, Dennett says, we will be left having to attribute, though not in any mysterious way, an emergent appreciation of them to natural selection itself (p. 317).

**How selection does this without intelligence (pp. 317–318)**
- It does not seek rationales, but when it stumbles on them the brute requirements of replication ensure it "recognizes" their value. The illusion of intelligence arises from our limited perspective: evolution may well have tried all the "stupid moves" as well as the smart ones, but the stupid moves, being failures, disappeared from view and we see only the string of triumphs (p. 317).
- Footnote 6 reports Schull's view that, thanks to the Baldwin effect, species can pretest some phenotypic possibilities. Footnote 7 says there is, in one sense, representation in selection after all: subpopulations "evaluate" design options and return their verdicts to the gene pool (p. 317, fns. 6–7).
- The original reasons were Nature's, not ours or our mammalian ancestors'; Nature appreciated them without representing them (but see fn. 7). We reason-representers are a late, specialized product. Representation gives us *foresight*, which Mother Nature wholly lacks (p. 317).
- **Dennett's claim:** our intentionality is highly derived, in just the same way as that of robots, books and maps. A shopping list in the head has no more intrinsic intentionality than one on paper (p. 318). We may call our intentionality real, but it is derived from that of natural selection, which is "just as real" (p. 318).
- If there is any original intentionality, original in the sense of being derived from no other source, that of natural selection deserves the honour. This ends the regress with a source "blind and unrepresenting". He quotes Millikan: "The root purposing here must be unexpressed purposing." (p. 318)
- Since in the beginning was not the Word, there is no text to consult about function and meaning. The idea that a word could wear its meaning on its sleeve and so settle such questions is itself a dead end, he says (p. 318).

**One more illusion: determinate function in designed things (pp. 318–319)**
- We think conscious design gives a good model of determinate function, since designers can tell us their intentions. But such historical facts have no guaranteed significance for the future. Someone set on making a pocket watch might make a useless one or a superb paperweight (p. 318).
- Insisting a thing is essentially what its creator intended is, in literary criticism, the Intentional Fallacy. Users' endorsements, if taken as predictors of *future* significance, are as fallible as creators'. Even the Pepsi franchise holder may be a fool (p. 319).
- So we get better grounds for reliable functional attributions by reading function off the discernible prowesses of the objects, rather than off the history of design development (p. 319).
- Dennett's thesis: we must abandon the idea that there is one determinate right answer to "What is it for?" If no deeper fact settles that question, none can settle its twin, "What does it mean?" (p. 319). Footnote 8 ties this to Quine's indeterminacy of translation and attack on essentialism (p. 319, fn. 8).

**Adaptationism and the panda's thumb (pp. 319–321)**
- The adaptationist debate in biology is another front in the same war (referring back to chapter 7). Jacob and Gould stress that Mother Nature is a satisficer and opportunistic tinkerer, not an "ideal engineer" (p. 319). The panda's thumb is a wrist (sesamoid) bone pressed into service as a thumb. Gould says it wins no prize in an engineer's derby but does its job; Dennett adds that it does its job excellently, and that is how we can be so sure what its job is: it is obvious what the appendage is *for* (p. 320).
- **Darwin (quoted by Gould):** an organ not originally formed for a purpose may be said to be specially contrived for it if it serves that end now (p. 320). Gould is a critic of adaptationism and finds a "paradox" in tinkering plus teleology. Dennett: no paradox, only the "functional indeterminacy" Dretske and Fodor shun (p. 320).
- **Dennett's claim:** Mother Nature does not commit herself explicitly to *any* functional attributions. All depend on the mind-set of the intentional stance, in which we assume optimality in order to interpret. The thumb was no more really a wrist bone than a thumb (p. 320).
- Footnote 9 reports Sober's question whether the very first dorsal fins on a *Stegosaurus* were an adaptation for cooling, with three options (Sober prefers option 3, that they are adaptations only when they occur in later organisms). Sober's discussion (p. 306 of his book, as cited in the footnote) casts Mother Nature as Dawkins's crew coach and says one need not psychoanalyze the coach to find out what is being selected for. Dennett adds that adopting the intentional stance will at least help with the reverse engineering needed to answer such questions (pp. 320–321, fn. 9).
- **Final conclusion.** After Darwin destroyed the Argument from Design, there is "no ultimate User's Manual" in which real functions and meanings are officially represented. There is no more bedrock for original functionality than for original intentionality. "You can't have realism about meanings without realism about functions." (p. 321)

*Intuition note.* The key move is that whether a state is "right" or "wrong" depends on what it is *for*. Nothing inside the thing, and no creator's say-so, has to settle what it is for. When good readings conflict, Dennett says there is no further fact, and so none about "true" meaning either.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A coin gate accepts any disk whose weight is between 5.6 g and 5.8 g. Over a test run in Panama we insert 10 US quarters (5.67 g), 10 balboas (5.67 g) and 10 slugs (5.0 g).
- Physical behaviour: the 5.67 g disks are accepted ($10 + 10 = 20$), and the slugs rejected (10). This is the same whatever we say it "means".
- Reading A, "US-quarter-detector": acceptances that are errors = the 10 balboas; rejections that are errors = 0. Total errors $= 10$.
- Reading B, "balboa-detector": the 10 US quarters are errors. Total errors $= 10$.
- Reading C, "quarter-or-balboa-detector", or "disk in 5.6–5.8 g": errors $= 0$.

The count of errors changes with the reading, though the device and its inputs do not. This is the disjunction problem: the more disjuncts we add, the fewer errors. Dennett's tactic is to pick the reading by what the device was *selected for* in its niche. If neither reading is selected for, there is no further fact to settle the choice between A and B.

## Concepts
- [[intentionality]] — "aboutness" of states; here a contested property: real in humans (opponents) or derived and interpretation-relative (Dennett) (p. 288)
- [[original-intentionality]] (new) — meaning claimed to be underived and intrinsic; "Unmeant Meaners" (p. 288)
- [[derived-intentionality]] (new) — meaning an artifact has only because of its users' purposes (pp. 289–291)
- [[intentional-stance]] — attributing content to a system by assuming optimality, applied here also to evolution (pp. 300, 311, 320)
- [[misrepresentation-and-disjunction-problem]] (new) — what grounds treating a state as *mistaken* rather than as correctly meaning a wider disjunction (pp. 301–302, 308)
- [[proper-function]] (new) — the function a thing has relative to what it was selected for; fixes error conditions (Millikan) (p. 292)
- [[artifact-hermeneutics]] (new) — Dennett's term for interpreting artifacts by costs and benefits of design (pp. 304, 311)
- [[free-floating-rationale]] (new) — a rationale for a design not represented by any agent (pp. 286, 306)
- [[selection-for]] (new) — natural selection picks features for specific reasons, not merely picks them (p. 316)
- [[referential-opacity]] — failure of substitution ("intensionality"); here said to reappear in what natural selection "desires" (pp. 315–316)
- [[semantic-indeterminacy]] (new) — when several readings are well supported, no further fact settles which is right (pp. 300, 312)
- [[meaning-rationalism]] (new) — Millikan's name for the doctrine that we have privileged access to our own meanings (p. 313)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] The disjunction problem (a state may "mean" any broader disjunction) ↔ inverse planning / inverse reinforcement learning: many goal or reward descriptions fit the same behaviour, so extra constraints are needed to choose one.
- [structural-analogy] Error is judged relative to what a device was selected for ↔ construct validity in LLM evaluation: whether an output counts as a "mistake" depends on which capacity the test is taken to measure.
- [metaphor] The absence of any text to read in Mother Nature's mind ↔ Clever Hans-type worries: attributing a mental state from prowess alone may leave more than one well-supported reading.

## Questions this raises
- If function is read from present prowess and not from design history, how should we decide among readings when two are equally supported? Dennett says there is then no fact of the matter.
- Dennett's reply to Rosenberg puts "intensionality" at the level of natural selection. Is that a literal use of the term, or only an analogy with referentially opaque belief contexts?
- Does the claim that intentionality is derived from selection make our own self-reports about meaning (the "avowals" of p. 298) evidence only, and not authoritative?
- Fodor and Dretske are said to fail on the error problem, and Burge's argument to need teleology. Would they accept the way Dennett restates their positions with the two-bitser?
