# Fast Thinking (Dennett, ch. 9)

## L0 — One line
Dennett sets beside Searle's claim that no program can suffice for a mind a different, coherent empirical claim, that perhaps only organic brains are fast enough to run the right program in real time, and argues that Searle's real concern is consciousness.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Searle's Chinese Room argument claims that having a program is neither sufficient for nor equivalent to having a mind. Dennett separates Searle's thesis (S) from a different, coherent empirical claim (D): no electronic digital computer could be programmed to control the swift, intelligent activity of normal humans, perhaps because none could run the right program fast enough. He argues that strong AI is committed only to a suitably fast, embodied program, that Searle's real worry is consciousness rather than semantics, and that Searle's three propositions all have faults. It shows where Dennett locates the disagreement between Searle and strong AI.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a computer program as a formal rule-following procedure; syntax vs semantics (form vs meaning); serial vs parallel computing; the Chinese Room thought experiment (assumed known; Dennett does not retell it).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Dennett calls the Chinese Room an intuition pump, not the argument. He tests Searle's three-premise argument by first attacking its ambiguous conclusion (pp. 323–324).
- He distinguishes (S), Searle's claim that no program by itself suffices, which he thinks incoherent as Searle means it, from (D), a coherent empirical claim about speed (pp. 324–325).
- Only a concretely running program is a real challenge to AI. Dennett thinks programming a thing could give it a mind, but that it is empirically unlikely the right programs run on anything but organic brains (pp. 325–326).
- Speed is "of the essence" for intelligence. Arguments from Flatland, serial computers, Sejnowski's estimate, Monod and weather simulation are inconclusive, but Dennett says they show it would be foolish to bet against (D) (pp. 326–330).
- AI as science survives even if (D) is true. Its method of "opportunistic oversimplification" is not refuted, though connectionists suggest low-level detail may be needed (pp. 330–331).
- Dennett claims strong AI is committed only to this: a program fast enough to interface with a body's sensors and effectors literally has a mind, whatever the material (p. 332).
- Searle holds that a perfect real-time mimic of a brain might still lack intentionality, which Dennett calls a strange empirical question because it lacks any intersubjective evidence, and whose causal powers he calls mysterious. Dennett accepts only a "bland" behaviourism (pp. 333–334).
- Dennett's diagnosis: Searle apparently runs together the underivability of semantics and the underivability of the consciousness of semantics. His case rests on intuitions about consciousness, not on the simple argument (pp. 335–336).
- Dennett rejects Searle's propositions 2 and 3 and finds proposition 1 faulty. Embodied running syntax suffices for derived intentionality, which is the only kind there is (pp. 336–337).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The chapter has no numbered sections. The headings below are ours and follow the author's order.

### Opening: Searle's argument and Dennett's strategy (pp. 323–325)
- **Searle's view:** the Chinese Room argument is meant to show the futility of "strong AI". Searle defines strong AI as the view that an appropriately programmed digital computer with the right inputs and outputs would thereby have a mind in exactly the sense that human beings have minds. Searle keeps calling the argument "very simple" (p. 323).
- **Dennett:** he will not retell the Chinese Room story. It is an intuition pump, not the argument itself, as Searle admits. Searle says its point is that the man in the room has all the syntax we can give him but does not thereby acquire the semantics (p. 323).
- Searle's argument, verbatim (p. 324):
  - Proposition 1. Programs are purely formal (syntactical).
  - Proposition 2. Syntax is neither equivalent to nor sufficient by itself for semantics.
  - Proposition 3. Minds have mental contents (semantic contents).
  - Conclusion 1. Having a program, any program by itself, is neither sufficient for nor equivalent to having a mind.
- Dennett accepts Searle's challenge to say what is wrong. He starts with the conclusion, which he calls subtly ambiguous. Many supporters are surer of the conclusion than of the path to it (p. 324).
- Why are some people so sure? Perhaps partly because they intensely want it true, and perhaps because they mistake it for a more defensible neighbour (p. 324).
- Two propositions that might seem to say the same thing (p. 324):
  - **(S)** No computer program by itself could ever be sufficient to produce what an organic human brain, with its particular causal powers, demonstrably can produce: mental phenomena with intentional content.
  - **(D)** There is no way an electronic digital computer could be programmed so that it could produce what an organic human brain, with its particular causal powers, demonstrably can produce: control of the swift, intelligent, intentional activity of normal human beings.
- Searle endorses (S). Dennett argues for (D), which he is not convinced is true. He takes (D) to be a coherent empirical claim, says (S), as Searle means it, is incoherent, and says Searle confirmed in personal correspondence that (D) is not his claim. He also says his defence of (D) is consistent with his defence of strong AI (pp. 324–325).
- The crucial difference: both men are impressed by the brain's causal powers but disagree about which powers matter and why. Dennett's task is to isolate Searle's supposed powers and show how strange they are (p. 325).

*Intuition note.* (S) says no program by itself could ever supply what the brain supplies. (D) says no electronic computer could be programmed to supply it, and Dennett's suggested reason is that none could run the program fast enough. The two are easily confused, but Dennett says his defence of (D) is consistent with his defence of strong AI, and that (D) is not what Searle claims in (S).

### "A program by itself" (pp. 325–326)
- In one trivial sense, no program by itself can do anything. A program lying unimplemented on the shelf, an abstract sequence of symbols, cannot even add 2 and 2. That sense is irrelevant to AI (p. 325).
- The real challenge is the claim that no running, material embodiment of a formal program could "produce intentionality" purely by embodying that program. Dennett assumes Searle means this (p. 325).
- **Searle's view, as Dennett states it:** take a material object that lacks the power of causing mental phenomena. Programming it, that is reorganizing the conditional dependencies of the transitions between its states, cannot give it that power. The crucial causal powers of brains have nothing to do with the programs they run (pp. 325–326).
- **Dennett's view:** such programming is precisely what could give something a mind, (in the only sense he thinks makes sense). But it is empirically unlikely that the right programs can be run on anything but organic human brains (p. 326).
- He then offers a series of arguments that he calls inconclusive, each of which concedes something while costing something (p. 326).

### Argument 1: Flatland and the price of fewer dimensions (pp. 326–327)
- A skeptic objects that intelligent beings could not live in two-dimensional Flatland. A richly interconnected brain needs wires to cross, and crossings need a third dimension (p. 326).
- **Dennett:** plausible but false. Von Neumann proved that a universal Turing machine can be realized in two dimensions, and Conway has built one in his two-dimensional Life world. Crossovers can be avoided, for instance by "stoplight" intersections where parcels of information take turns (p. 326).
- The price is speed of transaction. In principle (if time were no object) a two-dimensional being with any interconnection could exist (p. 326).
- But speed is "of the essence" for intelligence. Something too slow to track the environment is not practically intelligent. All this shows is that relative speed is important. If the relevant events ran 100 times slower, an intelligence could slow down by the same factor without loss; in our universe it would be worse than moronic (pp. 326–327).
- Dennett's Parkinson's aside, put as a question: are victims with "orthostatic hypotension" demented, or, as some have suggested, just terribly slowed down but otherwise normal? The condition is crippling even if it is "merely" a change of pace (p. 327).
- **Conclusion (modest, well-supported, empirical):** nothing that was not three-dimensional could produce control of the swift, intelligent, intentional activity of normal humans (p. 327).

### Argument 2: serial machines and massive parallelism (pp. 327–328)
- Almost all digital computers are von Neumann machines: serial, doing one thing at a time. In principle they can compute anything any computer can. But many cognitive computations, such as pattern recognition and memory searching, cannot be done in reasonably short real time by them, even at the speed of light (p. 327).
- Realistic real-time speed needs massively parallel hardware. The brain gives every evidence of a massively parallel architecture, with millions or billions of simultaneous channels, presumably not by accident (p. 327).
- So the causal powers needed for swift, intelligent control can be achieved only in a massively parallel processor such as a brain. Dennett says he has not attempted an a priori proof and settles for "scientific likelihood" (p. 327).
- Why not electronic parallel hardware? Electronic transmission is orders of magnitude faster than in nerve fibres, so such a system could be thousands of times faster. Dennett answers that this may or may not be so (p. 327).
- **Sejnowski** (forthcoming): the brain averages $10^{15}$ operations per second, five orders of magnitude faster than the current electronic massively parallel processors. The ratio of computation speed to cost has changed by an order of magnitude five times in the last 35 years (the source says the ratio 'decreased'; see Reading notes), so one might extrapolate to brain-matching hardware in a few decades. Sejnowski surmises that existing electronic technology cannot reach the goal, and perhaps optical computing will (p. 328).

### Argument 3: neurons as compute-intensive molecular machines (pp. 328–330)
- Even $10^{15}$ operations per second may not be nearly enough, if the brain makes maximal use of its materials. We might need organic computing. Dennett says this is where the argument for (D) becomes highly speculative and controversial (p. 328).
- **Supposition** (not very likely, "hardly disproved"): a single neuron's input-output function depends on subcellular organic molecules, for instance enzyme-level information processing. Then a model of the neuron might not duplicate its information-processing in real time. The model would be tiny and swift, but not as tiny and swift as the molecules it models (p. 328).
- **Monod** (1971) describes a similar idea: the "cybernetic" power of a cell equipped with hundreds or thousands of microscopic entities, cleverer than the Maxwell-Szilard-Brillouin demon. Dennett adds that the complexity of molecular activity in neuron cell bodies may very well have only local significance, unconnected to the neurons' information-processing tasks, in which case the point lapses (pp. 328–329).
- **Objection (Llinas, in conversation):** individual molecules process quickly, but they cannot propagate and amplify their effects swiftly. Axon spikes are orders of magnitude larger, and slow diffusion would squander the time gained. Other neuroscientists are less sure diffusion is the only mechanism, but offer no positive alternative models. Dennett concludes that the Monod-inspired argument for the "inescapable biologicality" of mental powers is inconclusive at best and very likely forlorn (p. 329).
- Still mildly instructive. It might prove that inorganic reproduction of the cognitively essential functions would run slower than the real thing. Weather is a complex process that cannot be simulated accurately in real time even by the fastest supercomputers, even though the equations are understood. Brainstorms may be as hard to simulate (p. 329).
- **If** brainstorms prove as hard to simulate as the weather, then, since speed of operation really is critical to intelligence (Dennett's premise), merely having the right program is not enough, unless "right program" means one that can run at the right speed for its inputs and outputs. Dennett's analogy: someone defends Reagan's SDI by saying the right control software could be written but would run a thousand times too slow to intercept missiles (p. 329).
- **If** the brain fully uses molecular-level speed, then the speed price of any substitution of material or architecture will be too costly (pp. 329–330).
- So (D) might be true for an entirely nonmysterious reason: no electronic digital computer could run the right program fast enough. It is not a knock-down argument, but Dennett says it would be foolish to bet against (D) (p. 330).

*Intuition note.* Dennett's point is not that brains hold a magic ingredient. A program that is correct but too slow, like a weather model that finishes after the storm, may be useless as a controller. If the brain's speed depends on its biology, that biology matters only as a source of speed.

### What (D) means for AI (pp. 330–331)
- Is (D) just the wager AI has made? "Not quite." If AI is science, aimed at theories of mind, slow computers would limit realistic experiments but would not undercut a claim to have found the essence of mentality. If AI is engineering, the failure would be crushing for builders of humanoid intelligence. But it would be as theoretically irrelevant as the stunt of building a gall bladder out of atoms (p. 330).
- A more interesting hope for AI: AI modelling works by "opportunistic oversimplification" (partitions, averages, idealizations), hoping some molar (large-scale) behaviour is independent of micro-details (pp. 330–331).
- **Dennett's example:** an action-planning model needs layout information from vision. Designers replace the whole visual system with a vision "oracle" giving one of only 256 reports, that is, eight bits. The target competence might be that of a five-year-old or a dog. The bet may or may not be good. Dennett says there is plenty of evidence that humans use only a tiny fraction of the information their senses could supply. If this bet fails, one searches for another oversimplification (p. 331).
- It is not obvious that any combination of AI's simplified models can approximate human behaviour, even orders of magnitude slower. Failure would not impeach AI's methodology, any more than failures to predict real weather impeach meteorological oversimplifications (p. 331).
- If AI models must go all the way down to the neuronal or subneuronal level to achieve good results, that is a serious blow to some of the traditional AI aspirations. But New Connectionists or Parallel Distributed Processing groups themselves suggest such low-level detail will be required to produce significant practical intelligence in artificial minds. This is a radical split within AI, so deep that Haugeland (1985) coined the acronym GOFAI (Good Old Fashioned AI) for the traditional view (p. 331).

### Weak AI, strong AI, and what strong AI is committed to (pp. 331–333)
- Has Dennett switched to defending "weak AI" (modelling mental phenomena by computer)? Searle has no objection to weak AI. He opposes what he calls the strong AI belief that the appropriately programmed computer literally has a mind, together with its "antibiological claim" that the brain's specific neurophysiology is irrelevant to the study of mind (p. 332, citing Searle 1982, p. 57).
- **Dennett's version of strong AI**, which he thinks most partisans would accept (p. 332):
  - Neurophysiology is relevant only for providing the right hardware engineering for real-time intelligence. If enough speed can be got from parallel silicon architectures, neurophysiology is inessential, though valuable for hints about architecture.
  - Take two implementations of the same program, one running six orders of magnitude (about a million times) slower. Searle's favourite example is a machine of beer cans tied with string. Both "compute the same function".
  - By virtue of nothing but greater speed, the fast one has causal powers the slow one lacks: the causal control powers to guide a locomoting body through the real world. One may claim the fast one is "literally a mind" and withhold that from its slow twin.
  - This is not a mysterious emergent effect of sheer speed. What matters is relative speed in the interaction between environment and organism. Dennett says slowing down the outside world sufficiently could produce the same effect, if that made sense.
  - An appropriately programmed computer that is fast enough to interface with the sensory transducers and motor effectors of a body (robot or organic) literally has a mind, whatever its material.
- **Dennett's claim:** this is all strong AI is committed to, and Searle has offered no reason to doubt it. It might still turn out to be true, as (D) proclaims, that only organic neural tissue can do it. That might make the dispute look trifling, but Dennett says it is not (pp. 332–333).

### Searle's two concessions and the empirical question (pp. 333–334)
- Searle concedes (1) that just about any system can be described as instantiating a formal program, so, as he puts it, "I suppose", in that sense all our brains are digital computers (Searle 1984, p. 153). He concedes (2) that, for all he knows, one could build a silicon device that perfectly mimics the real-time input-output behaviour of a human brain. Dennett has just given reasons to doubt (2) (p. 333).
- **Searle's view, as Dennett reports it:** even then, we would have no reason to suppose the device, like the organic brain, "produces intentionality". Searle calls this an empirical question, but Dennett says he does not say how it could be investigated (p. 333).
- Searle insists he never claimed to show that an organic brain is essential for intentionality. On Dennett's reading, you know (by some sort of immediate acquaintance, apparently) that your own brain produces intentionality, whatever it is made of. If your head were opened and silicon chips found, that would settle it for you but not for anyone else. For a third party, perfect mimicry of control would be no reason to suppose a mind, since, as Searle put it in personal communication, control powers by themselves are irrelevant (pp. 333–334).
- **Dennett's reply:** how was it established that organic brains produce intentionality? Perhaps only some do, for example, left-handers' brains might only mimic. Asking them is no help, since their brains may be Chinese rooms (p. 334).
- It is a strange empirical question that has no intersubjective evidence. On Searle's view the crucial contribution of neurophysiology might be entirely undetectable from outside. A body without a real mind could fend for itself as well as one with a mind (p. 334).
- **Dennett's own position (D):** neurophysiology is probably so important that if he saw an entity with the real-time cleverness of C3PO in *Star Wars*, he would bet a considerable sum that it is controlled, locally or remotely, by an organic brain (p. 334).
- Searle calls this "behaviorism". Dennett accepts only the bland behaviourism of the physical sciences, not a narrow Skinnerian, Watsonian or Rylean dogma. In this sense behaviour includes all intersubjectively observable internal processes (gut, RNA). Searle's powers are mysterious because they have, by his own admission, no telltale effect on behaviour. Dennett's are the powers needed to guide a body through life: seeing, hearing, acting, talking, deciding (p. 334).

*Intuition note.* Dennett prefers causal powers that show up in publicly observable behaviour, inside or outside the body. By Searle's own admission, the powers Searle imagines have no telltale effect on behaviour, which is why Dennett calls them mysterious.

### The crux: consciousness, not semantics (pp. 335–336)
- Dennett reviews. Searle criticizes AI for ignoring neurophysiology. Dennett suggested biochemistry may matter by providing operating speed, but this is not the power Searle has in mind (p. 335).
- **Searle's view:** there is a clear distinction between the brain's causal powers to produce mental states ("bottom-up causal powers") and its powers to produce input-output relations. Input-output causation enough to run a robot "implies nothing whatever" about the former, and a successful robot could be a complete zombie (p. 335, citing ms. pp. 4–5).
- **Dennett's diagnosis:** the crux for Searle is consciousness, not "semantics". Searle thinks it can be confirmed by anyone who reflects that strong AI would fail to tell a "total zombie" from a being with real, intrinsic intentionality. His real topic is "what it is like" to be you and understand Chinese. His case depends on the first-person view of the man in the room, though he calls the Chinese Room a mere reminder of premise two (p. 335).
- Dennett says Searle has apparently confused a claim about the underivability of semantics from syntax with a claim about the underivability of the consciousness of semantics from syntax. For him genuine understanding is inextricable from consciousness. He does not consider unconscious semantics (p. 335).
- Dennett does not discuss consciousness in full here and refers to earlier work (*Brainstorms*, chs. 8–11; Hofstadter and Dennett 1981). He says Searle's case does not hinge on the simple argument but on deep-seated intuitions about consciousness and its apparent unrealizability in machines (pp. 335–336).
- Searle insists he is a materialist, yet some of his supporters are modern Cartesians (Eccles, Puccetti). He proclaims that somehow, without details, the brain's biochemistry ensures no human is a zombie. Dennett says this imputes to biochemistry the same wondrous power Descartes imputed to immaterial souls, making it no less wondrous, mysterious or, in the end, incoherent (p. 336).

### Replies to Searle's three propositions (pp. 336–337)
- **Proposition 2** (syntax is neither equivalent to nor sufficient for semantics): Dennett says it may still be held true if one makes the simple mistake of talking about syntax on the shelf, an unimplemented program. But embodied, running syntax, the "right program" on a suitably fast machine, is sufficient for *derived* intentionality. Dennett says that is the only kind of semantics there is, as argued in chapter 8 (see also ch. 3). He rejects proposition 2 "with arguments" (p. 336).
- **Proposition 1** (programs are purely formal): Dennett says there is something amiss with it. Whether a program is identified by its formal features is contested in law, for example whether one can patent a program or only copyright it. Lawsuits ask whether programs that do the same things in the world count as the same program even if syntactically different. If details of embodiment are included in the specification and are considered essential, the program is not purely formal (and is arguably patentable). Without some embodiment details fixed, by the internal semantics of the machine language, a program is not even a syntactic object but a pattern of marks "as inert as wallpaper" (pp. 336–337).
- **Proposition 3** (minds have mental contents): an implication of chapter 8 is that this is false given what Searle means. There is no such thing as intrinsic intentionality, especially if it is viewed, as Searle can now be seen to require, as a property to which the subject has conscious, privileged access (p. 337).

## Toy example
*Ours, not from the text.* It illustrates the main idea: two implementations of one program differ only in speed, and only the fast one can control a body.

- A program takes 100 steps to compute where to put a hand to catch a ball.
- The fast machine does 1 step per millisecond: $100 \times 1\ \text{ms} = 100\ \text{ms} = 0.1\ \text{s}$.
- The ball arrives after 0.5 s. $0.1 < 0.5$, so the fast machine acts in time.
- The slow twin is $10^6$ times slower (six orders of magnitude, as in Dennett's beer-can case). Each step takes $10^6 \times 1\ \text{ms} = 1000\ \text{s}$.
- 100 steps take $100 \times 1000\ \text{s} = 100{,}000\ \text{s}$, and $100{,}000 / 3600 \approx 27.8$ hours.
- Both compute the same function. Only the fast one has the causal control to catch the ball. Dennett's "slow the outside world" alternative would mean the ball took more than 27.8 hours to arrive.

## Concepts
- [[intentionality]] — the directedness (aboutness) of mental states; here Dennett argues all of it is derived, with no intrinsic kind (pp. 336–337).
- [[derived-intentionality]] (new) — the only kind of semantics there is, which embodied, running syntax is sufficient for; developed in ch. 8, only invoked here (p. 336).
- [[intrinsic-intentionality]] (new) — Searle's idea of intentionality a subject accesses consciously and privately; Dennett denies it exists (p. 337).
- [[chinese-room-argument]] (new) — Searle's argument that program-running is not sufficient for mind; Dennett calls the room an intuition pump (p. 323).
- [[strong-ai]] (new) — the view that an appropriately programmed computer literally has a mind; Dennett's version requires a fast, embodied program (pp. 323, 332).
- [[weak-ai]] (new) — using the computer as a tool to model mental phenomena; Searle is enthusiastic about it (p. 332).
- [[real-time-speed-constraint]] (new) — speed relative to the environment is essential for intelligence; a correct but too-slow program is not enough (pp. 326, 329).
- [[massively-parallel-processing]] (new) — hardware doing many operations at once; claimed necessary for real-time cognition (p. 327).
- [[opportunistic-oversimplification]] (new) — AI modelling by deliberately simplified stand-ins, hoping molar behaviour is independent of micro-details (p. 330).
- [[gofai]] (new) — Haugeland's acronym for traditional AI, set against New Connectionists (p. 331).
- [[bland-behaviorism]] (new) — Dennett's term for the physical sciences' practice of explaining intersubjectively observable behaviour, internal or external, not a Skinnerian dogma (p. 334).
- [[zombie]] (new) — in Searle's argument, a robot with right input-output but no mental states or consciousness (p. 335).

## Canonical instances
- [[chinese-room]] — Searle's thought experiment, treated as an intuition pump (p. 323).
- [[flatland-two-dimensions]] — von Neumann's and Conway's two-dimensional universal machines show that fewer dimensions cost speed, not possibility (p. 326).
- [[weather-simulation]] — a well-understood process that cannot be simulated in real time; brainstorms may be similar (p. 329).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's "same function, different speed, different causal control" ↔ theory-of-mind modelling: exact inverse-planning inference may be too slow for real-time use, so only a fast approximation can act — the same trade of speed against fidelity.
- [structural-analogy] Searle's "perfect mimic may still lack intentionality" ↔ construct validity in LLM evaluation: identical behaviour on tests need not show the same underlying capacity. Dennett's answer is to rely on public evidence.
- [structural-analogy] "Opportunistic oversimplification" (vision oracle of 8 bits) ↔ simplified agent models in Bayesian theory of mind: simplify and hope the molar behaviour survives.
- [metaphor] Searle's "total zombie" ↔ the explicit/implicit ToM contrast: behaving as if tracking mental states vs having them.

## Questions this raises
- If (D) is an empirical claim about speed, what observations could confirm or refute it, given that the arguments Dennett reviews (Sejnowski, Monod, Llinas) leave it inconclusive?
- Dennett says derived intentionality is "the only kind there is" (ch. 8). How does that fit with predicting others by attributing beliefs and desires?
- Is Dennett's "bland behaviourism", which includes internal processes that are intersubjectively observable, enough to rule out a robot being a zombie, or does it just define the question away?
- Do modern parallel or learned systems change the relevance of the speed argument, or the GOFAI vs connectionist split Dennett mentions?
