# True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

## L0 — One line
To be a true believer is just to be a system whose behavior is reliably and extensively predictable by treating it as a rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have — Dennett's "intentional stance."

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Dennett asks what it is to really believe something, and answers via a predictive method he calls the intentional strategy: treat a system as a rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have, then predict its actions from practical reasoning. Whatever is reliably and extensively predictable this way counts as a "true believer" — an intentional system. The strategy works on people, animals, and some machines, and sometimes only it works. Its success reveals real, objective patterns in behavior that a purely physical description can miss, though the patterns are imperfect and partly shaped by our own interests, and why the strategy works remains, for humans, an open empirical question.

**Prerequisites:** the everyday idea of explaining action by belief and desire; no formal logic or math is needed; familiarity with simple thought experiments helps.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Dennett contrasts three predictive strategies — the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance — which differ in what they assume about a system (p. 16-17).
- Belief-attribution rule: attribute as beliefs the truths relevant to a system's interests that its experience has made available; exposure is only *normally* sufficient for true belief, so true believers mostly believe truths, and any false belief needs a special genealogy traced back to true ones (p. 18-19).
- Desire-attribution rule: attribute the desires a system *ought* to have given its situation, starting from basic desires like survival and comfort; rationality is idealized at first and revised downward only as needed (p. 20-21).
- The strategy applies, with varying literalness, to people, animals, some artifacts, plants, and even lightning — but trivial "counterexamples" like a lectern fail to qualify because the stance adds no real predictive power there (p. 21-24).
- Against Nozick's imagined Martians: the intentional stance reveals *real patterns* in behavior — patterns stable under indefinitely many ways the underlying physical details could differ — that even a complete physical description would miss (p. 25-27).
- Even a thermostat is a very degenerate intentional system; richer causal links to the world make belief-like states more semantically determinate, and the Twin Earth case shows the same principle applies to human belief (p. 29-33).
- The question "why does the strategy work?" splits into an easy, uninformative evolutionary answer and a hard, open question about brain machinery; Dennett thinks some attenuated "language of thought" hypothesis will prove correct, but insists this is not obvious (p. 33-35).
- In the 1987 postscript, Conway's Game of Life illustrates patterns that are real yet visible only from an optional vantage point, and Quine's indeterminacy of translation is extended to argue that some belief-attribution questions may simply have no determinate answer — without this being mere relativism (p. 37-41).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening puzzle: realism vs. interpretationism (p. 13-15)
The chapter opens with Maugham's "Death Speaks" fable, in which a servant flees to Samarra to escape Death, not knowing Death already has an appointment there — a story about an inescapable pattern that the characters cannot see from inside the action. Dennett uses it later as an image for the patterns his strategy reveals.

He then notes that belief-talk looks sometimes "dark, risky, and imponderable" (exotic or false beliefs) and sometimes as objective as "counting beans" (mundane cases like believing there is milk in the fridge) (p. 13-14). These reflections congeal into two opposing views, neither of which, he says, fairly represents any serious theorist, but which are treated as mutually exclusive: **realism** (belief is an objective internal fact, like infection by a virus, discoverable in principle by finding something inside the believer's head) versus **interpretationism** (belief-attribution is a matter of interpretation, like judging whether someone "has style") (p. 14-15).

**Dennett's own thesis:** belief is a perfectly objective phenomenon, but it "can be discerned only from the point of view of one who adopts a certain predictive strategy, and its existence can be confirmed only by an assessment of the success of that strategy" (near-quote, p. 15) — which makes him "apparently" a realist and "apparently" an interpretationist at once.

### The Intentional Strategy and How It Works (p. 15-22)
Dennett first notes one strategy that does not work (astrology), then distinguishes three predictive strategies that do, in increasing order of abstraction from physical detail:

- **Astrological strategy:** uses birth date/hour; Dennett judges it does not in fact work (p. 16).
- **[[physical-design-stance|Physical stance]]:** predict from the system's physical constitution and the laws of physics; this is the Laplacean ideal, always available *in principle*, sometimes impractical (p. 16).
- **[[physical-design-stance|Design stance]]:** ignore physical details and assume the system is designed for some function; predict that it will behave as designed (e.g., an alarm clock). This only predicts the system's *designed* behavior — a malfunctioning or physically abused system requires falling back to the physical stance (p. 16-17).
- **[[intentional-stance|Intentional stance]]:** treat the object as a rational agent; figure out the beliefs it ought to have given its place in the world and purpose, the desires it ought to have on the same grounds, and predict that practical reasoning from these beliefs and desires will, in many but not all cases, yield a decision about what to do (p. 17).

**Belief-attribution rule.** Dennett starts from truisms: sheltered people are ignorant; exposure to something over time is the *normally sufficient* condition for coming to have true beliefs about it (not a guarantee — our threshold for excusing "abnormal ignorance" after exposure is high) (p. 18). We do not believe *all* truths our experience makes available, only the *relevant* ones. So the rule is: **attribute as beliefs all the truths relevant to the system's interests that its experience to date has made available** (p. 18). This rule over-attributes somewhat (we are all forgetful) and does not by itself capture false belief. Any false belief requires a special genealogy traced mainly through true beliefs — e.g., S falsely believes p because S truly believes Jones told him p and that Jones is reliable; or S falsely believes there is a snake on the barstool because S truly believes he seems to see one and is sitting near it (p. 18). A footnote adds that this is a *derived* rule: if beliefs could be individuated and counted, Dennett estimates that less than ten percent of a person's beliefs would be false — illustrated with Democritus, whose explicit (false) physical theory affects only a small fraction of his overall stock of mundane beliefs (p. 19, n.1).

**Desire-attribution rule:** attribute the desires the system *ought* to have — a baseline list of survival, absence of pain, food, comfort, procreation, entertainment, which normally terminates the "why do you want that?" game (p. 20). Derived rules connect desire to belief (we attribute desires for things a system believes good for it, or believes to be means to other desired ends). Language complicates this: verbal declarations of desire ("I want a two-egg mushroom omelette…") let us (and force us) to commit to far more specific desires than we would otherwise have had reason to satisfy — illustrated by the "I'd like some baked beans" / "How many?" exchange (p. 20-21).

**[[rational-agent-assumption|Rationality]]:** one starts with the ideal of perfect rationality (believing all implications of one's beliefs, no contradictions) and revises downward only as circumstances dictate; this creates no practical problem of "clutter," since one only needs the implications relevant to the system's present behavioral predicament. Cases of outright irrationality raise further interpretive problems Dennett sets aside here (p. 21).

**Who uses it, and on what.** Everyone uses the strategy on people, almost always successfully; it works on most other mammals most of the time (e.g., designing traps by reasoning about what a creature believes or wants to avoid), and — with decreasing confidence — on birds, fish, reptiles, insects, spiders, and clams (a clam won't release its shut shell until "convinced" danger has passed). It also works on some artifacts (a chess computer "knows" a move loses material and "doesn't want" that; a thermostat "believes" the room has reached temperature), on plants (orchards should use apple varieties "cautious about concluding" it is spring, given late frosts), and even, loosely, on lightning, which an electrician described as "wanting" to find the best path to ground (p. 21-22).

> **Intuition note:** The intentional stance is a shortcut: instead of tracking physics or internal wiring, you ask "what would a rational agent with this creature's situation believe and want, and what would it then do?" It works remarkably often — on anything from chess programs to clams — precisely because so many systems in the world behave *as if* pursuing goals in light of information.

### True Believers as Intentional Systems (p. 22-33)
Dennett calls this a "motley assortment" of serious attributions, dubious ones, pedagogical metaphors, and outright frauds (his own assessment, p. 22). Rather than trying to sort which of these systems "really" believe, he observes: even in the worst cases, where we are sure the strategy works "for the wrong reasons," it still works, at least a little. This fact distinguishes the class of **[[intentional-system|intentional systems]]** from objects for which the strategy never works at all (p. 23).

**The lectern challenge.** A lecture-hall lectern can be "construed" as a rational agent believing it is at the center of the world and desiring to stay there; this correctly predicts that it stays put. Does this make it an intentional system? Dennett says no: the strategy gives no predictive power we did not already have, and the belief/desire assignment was reverse-engineered, unprincipled, to fit behavior we already knew. By contrast, for people, animals, or computers the intentional strategy is often the *only* practical method available, and it genuinely extends our predictive power (p. 23).

**Objection (reported, "it will be urged"):** this may be no difference in nature, only a reflection of our limited capacities as scientists — a Laplacean omniscient physicist could predict a computer, or a live human body, by physics alone, without the design or intentional strategies (p. 23). **Dennett's reply:** engineers can fully understand a simple thermostat without anthropomorphizing it, but even the cleverest engineers find it practically impossible to keep track of more complex systems (a time-sharing computer, a remote space probe) without lapsing into intentional talk; this is just a more advanced case of human epistemic frailty. He resists classifying such artifacts with "true believers" — ourselves — on such variable, parochial grounds, and rejects as intolerable the idea that something could be a believer from one observer's viewpoint and not from a cleverer observer's. His resolution: **the decision to adopt the intentional stance is free, but the facts about the stance's success or failure, once adopted, are perfectly objective** (p. 23-24).

**Power of the strategy.** In chess, the strategy rarely picks a single best move, but drastically narrows "many" legal moves down to "a few" with anything to be said for them (p. 24). In real-world cases it cannot predict fine-grained actions (a trader's exact buy/sell order, a politician's exact words) but can predict less specific outcomes with high confidence (the trader *won't* buy utilities today; the politician *will* side with the unions) — a strength, since this neutrality about implementation details lets the strategy be *chained*: Dennett's example has a hypothetical KGB-defection announcement by the US Secretary of State triggering a long, confidently predictable chain of consequences (Cabinet meeting, dismissal, news coverage, editorials, letters to editors) that "no imaginable practical extension of physics or biology" could predict (p. 24-25).

**Nozick's Martians.** Dennett reports an objection originally raised by Robert Nozick: suppose vastly superior Martians, for whom we are as simple as thermostats are to clever engineers, could predict all our behavior — even the Dow Jones closing numbers — by Laplacean microphysics alone, never needing the design or intentional stance. Would they then be *right* that, from their point of view, we are not really believers at all, making our status as believers nothing objective but merely "in the eye of the beholder" — provided the beholder shares our intellectual limitations (p. 25)? **Dennett's answer:** the Martians would be missing something perfectly objective — the **[[real-pattern|real patterns]]** in human behavior describable only from the intentional stance, which support generalizations and predictions (p. 25).

He illustrates with a stockbroker ordering 500 shares of General Motors: the Martians could predict the exact finger and vocal-cord motions of the call, but if they fail to see that *indefinitely many different* motions — even by different individuals — could have been substituted without disturbing the market's subsequent operation, they have missed a real pattern. Just as indefinitely many physical objects can serve as "a spark plug" in an engine, there are indefinitely many ways of *ordering 500 shares of GM*; there are societal "pivot points" where the outcome depends only on what people believe and desire, not on the countless other ways they might differ (p. 26).

He then develops an Earthling-vs-Martian prediction contest: both observe a phone call: Mrs. Gardner answers, and — repeating back what her husband has just told her (that he is bringing the boss home for dinner) — she instructs him to "pick up a bottle of wine on the way home." The Earthling, using ordinary folk reasoning, predicts a car with two people and a wine bottle arriving within the hour — "a bit risky... but a good bet." The Martian reaches the same prediction only by tracking an immense physical causal chain (e.g., a near-collision avoided five miles from the house) (p. 26-27). To anyone lacking the intentional strategy, the Earthling's success would look like "magic," as inexplicable as the fatalistic inevitability of the Samarra story from the epigraph (p. 27).

Dennett explicitly connects this to fatalism: fatalists (he names astrologers) believe a pattern in human affairs is inexorable, imposing itself "come what may." **"These fatalists are wrong, but they are almost right"** (quote, p. 27): there *are* patterns in human affairs that impose themselves with great vigor — though not quite inexorably — absorbing physical "noise," and these are exactly the patterns captured by talk of beliefs, desires, and intentions (p. 27).

Dennett then flags a flaw in his own thought experiment: for the Martian to wager and compete with the Earthling, he must already treat him as an intelligent being with beliefs and desires — so if the Martian sees the pattern in one Earthling, he cannot consistently fail to see it in others. The moral drawn is **the unavoidability of the intentional stance with regard to oneself and one's fellow intelligent beings** — itself interest-relative: one can adopt the physical stance toward an intelligent being (including oneself) but not *to the exclusion of* also keeping an intentional stance toward oneself, and toward others if one intends to learn what they know (credited to Stuart Hampshire) (p. 27). In the main text (not a footnote), Dennett adds that the Martians, since they observe, theorize, predict, and communicate, cannot lack the relevant concepts: "where there are intelligent beings, the patterns must be there to be described, whether or not we care to see them" (p. 28).

> **Intuition note:** Dennett's reply to the Martians is that total physical omniscience is not the same as seeing *everything worth seeing*. A pattern like "the trader won't buy utilities" is robust under countless physical substitutions, the way an engine still runs whichever brand of spark plug you screw in — and only a stance that tracks beliefs and desires can see that kind of robustness.

**Imperfection of the patterns.** The intentional strategy "works as well as it does, which is not perfectly" (p. 28): no one is perfectly rational, unforgetful, or immune to fatigue or malfunction. This produces cases — contradictory beliefs, self-deception — where the canons of interpretation yield no clear, stable verdict, much as physical damage can make an artifact's normal design-level description break down (p. 28).

**Strong realism vs. Dennett's milder realism.** A **strong realist** (view Dennett rejects) holds that in these degenerate cases the person really does have some particular belief or desire that the strategy simply fails to find. **Dennett's own ("milder") view:** there is *no fact of the matter* about exactly which beliefs/desires to attribute in such degenerate cases — but this is not relativism, because *when and why* there is no fact of the matter is itself an objective matter (p. 28-29). One can even grant that different cultures, with different interests, would attribute somewhat different beliefs to the same person, while maintaining that there are further objective facts about how well each rival scheme actually predicts — and it may be an objective fact that two rival schemes are about equally (and maximally) good (p. 29). A footnote (citing John McCarthy's cryptography analogy) notes that the larger the corpus of observed behavior, the less chance there is of two systematically unrelated but equally warranted interpretations — so this "bogey" is theoretically important but **practically negligible** once we restrict attention to the richest intentional systems we know: human beings (p. 29, n.4).

**The thermostat.** Dennett returns to "the lowly thermostat" as the most degenerate interesting case. Grant it roughly six possible beliefs and fewer desires (room too hot/cold; boiler on/off; "if it wants the room warmer it should turn on the boiler"). But this already imputes too much — the thermostat has no concept of *heat* or *boiler*, since the identical mechanism, rewired, could regulate a water tank or a train's speed instead. So we **de-interpret**: it believes "A is too F or G," and "if it wants A to be more F it should do K" (p. 29-30).

Now enrich the thought experiment: give the device more ways to learn about temperature (an "eye" for shivering occupants, an "ear," geographical facts such as being in "Winnipeg in December"), plus behavioral versatility (choosing fuel, comparing dealers, checking weather-stripping). Each addition gives the belief-like states "more to do" — more occasions to serve as premises or conclusions in internal reasoning — which enriches the semantics of the dummy predicates F and G. Formally, as more such connections are added, the class of models that would equally satisfy the internal formal system shrinks; eventually a (practically, never in-principle) unique semantic interpretation is dictated, and we say the device has beliefs *about heat* and *about this very room* — not only because of its actual location and operations, but because we cannot imagine another niche in which it would work (p. 30-31).

A thermostat's belief is about *this* boiler specifically because it is **fastened** to it; in a minimal device it would be "too easy to substitute a different link and completely change the meaning" of the state (a footnote calls this the ancestor of ideas later grouped under "de re belief," p. 31, n.5). As systems grow richer, substituting their actual links to the world gets harder without changing the system's organization: a two-way constraint of growing specificity emerges — fix the device in one state and it demands a specific environment to work properly; change the environment without fixing the state, and sensitive sensors will drive it into an appropriate new state. This tight coupling is why one says the organism "mirrors" the environment, or that there is a **representation** of the environment implicit in its organization (p. 31). Dennett states: **"What makes some internal feature of a thing a representation could only be its role in regulating the behavior of an intentional system"** (quote, p. 32).

**Twin Earth.** There is no "magic moment" where a simple thermostat becomes a system with a genuine internal representation — richer thermostats, then robots, then us, differ only in degree (p. 32). Humans are so multifariously connected to the world that almost no substitution is possible — though it is *conceivable*, via Putnam's Twin Earth: a planet identical to Earth down to imperceptible detail, except that what they call "water" has a different chemical composition, beneath our threshold of discrimination. Were you swapped with your Twin Earth replica, you would never notice — just as the simple thermostat cannot tell whether it is regulating temperature, speed, or water volume. But your rich internal organization makes the substitution demand far stricter: Earth and Twin Earth would need to be virtual replicas for you not to change state dramatically (p. 32).

Which boiler are *your* beliefs about when you believe "the boiler is on"? The one in your actual cellar — because fixing the referents of belief requires facts about your actual embedding in the world, exactly as with the thermostat. "The principles, and problems, of interpretation... are the same" for thermostats and people; "the differences are of degree, but nevertheless of such great degree that understanding the internal organization of a simple intentional system gives one very little basis for understanding the internal organization of a complex intentional system, such as a human being" (near-quote, p. 32-33).

### Why Does the Intentional Strategy Work? (p. 33-35)
The question "why does it work?" is ambiguous between two kinds of answer. For a simple thermostat: "because it was well-designed to be easily comprehended from this stance" is true but uninformative about the *actual design features* responsible — fortunately, for a thermostat, those features are easy to find (p. 33).

For a person, the easy answer is: evolution designed humans to be rational, so using the intentional strategy on us is a safe bet — true, brief, and "an answer Herbert Spencer would applaud" (a nod to the lecture's original occasion) but "strikingly uninformative." The harder question — how does the machinery Nature gave us actually work? — Dennett says **"we just do not know"** (p. 33).

Two reported doctrines:
- **Skinnerian behaviorist (reported view):** belief/desire talk is shorthand for unimaginably complex histories of reinforcement — e.g., "wants ice cream" means past ice-cream eating was reinforced, creating a propensity under complex background conditions. In the absence of the detailed history, intentional-stance claims are our shrewd inductive guesses. Even if true, this account tells us little about how the internal machinery regulates such propensities (p. 33-34).
- **Currently more popular view (cognitive science / "language of thought"):** the strategy-level account and the mechanism-level account will roughly *coincide* — each predictively attributable belief corresponds to a functionally salient internal state, decomposable into parts that parallel the words of the sentence expressing the belief; the inferences we attribute are mirrored by physical, causal processes in the brain, and the logical form of a believed proposition is copied in the structural form of the corresponding internal state. This is the **[[language-of-thought|language-of-thought]]** hypothesis explored in various versions in cognitive science. Dennett: **"provided one allows great latitude for attenuation of the basic, bold claim, I think some version of it will prove correct"** (near-quote, p. 34) — but he stresses this is **not obvious or inevitable** (p. 34).

He distinguishes two different empirical claims that are often conflated: (1) intentional-stance description yields an objective real pattern — the pattern the Martians missed — which is "confirmed beyond skepticism"; (2) that real pattern is *produced by* another real pattern, roughly isomorphic to it, inside the brain. Doubting claim (2) is not doubting claim (1); reasons exist for believing (2), "but they are not overwhelming" (p. 34).

**The combinatorial-explosion argument** for (2): as the complexity of a system we try to design increases (more inputs, more degrees of freedom, more vocabulary), the internal complexity needed tends to increase by orders of magnitude — enough to swamp the largest machines. Yet the brain has "somehow solved" this: it is finite, compact, reliable, swift, and capable of learning new behaviors and vocabularies almost without limit. Some elegant, *generative*, indefinitely extendable representational principle must be responsible. The only known model of such a system is a human language — so the argument for a language of thought reduces to "what else could it be?" This is, Dennett says, good enough reason, as scientific tactics, to pursue the hypothesis as far as it will go, but its "inevitable rightness is far from assured" (p. 34-35). A footnote adds that every language-of-thought model proposed so far has itself fallen victim to combinatorial explosion in some way, which should temper enthusiasm for what Fodor calls **"the only game in town"** (p. 35, n.6).

**Skip path:** carry forward — the strategy/mechanism distinction (claim 1 vs. claim 2) and the combinatorial-explosion motivation for a language of thought, without the derivation details.

> **Intuition note:** Even if the intentional stance works perfectly as a predictive shortcut, that alone does not tell us the brain is internally built like a sentence-processor. Dennett keeps these two questions apart: *that* the pattern is real, and *what inside the head* (if anything isomorphic) produces it.

### Reflections: Real Patterns, Deeper Facts, and Empty Questions (1987 postscript, p. 37-41)
Dennett frames the main source of ongoing criticism of his view: is belief attribution objective, or are all attributions "useful fictions" (observer-relative)? His Nozick discussion tried to place his view on a "knife-edge" between simple realism and simple relativism — a position critics keep trying to collapse into one extreme or the other (p. 37). He reaffirms: "My view is, I insist, a *sort* of realism," since the patterns the Martians miss are really, objectively there (p. 37).

He illustrates with **Conway's Game of Life** (citing Gardner 1970): a grid of cells, each ON or OFF, each with eight neighbors, evolving in discrete time steps by one rule: *"If [a cell's ON-neighbor count] is exactly two, the cell stays in its present state... If the answer is exactly three, the cell is ON in the next instant whatever its current state. Under all other conditions, the cell is OFF"* (quote, p. 38). This single rule is the entire deterministic "physics" of the Life world — a "Laplacean playpen" where the physical stance always yields perfect prediction (p. 38).

Higher-level patterns emerge from this physics: blinkers, gliders, "glider guns," puffer trains, eaters, and other named configurations. Once their behavior is understood, one can adopt a **design stance** toward them, and even build a configuration that realizes a **universal Turing machine** — programmable to compute any computable function (p. 38). Adopting the **"Turing machine stance,"** one ignores both the Life-world physics and the machine's design details and simply computes the function being realized, translating the result back into an ON/OFF configuration — a correct prediction, provided no stray noise (e.g., a passing glider) disrupts the machine (p. 39).

**Is this higher-level pattern "real"?** Yes, Dennett says, so long as it lasts — and if it has "armor" against noise, it may last a long time; its reality (its longevity and robustness) is strictly independent of historical facts about the designer's intentions, though the pattern *may* owe its existence to them (p. 39). *Seeing* the pattern is a separate matter: nothing is hidden in the Life world ("there is no backstage"), yet it takes "a big leap of insight" to see an unfolding configuration *as* a Turing-machine computation; reaching that vantage point is optional and can be difficult (p. 39).

**Main claim:** the intentional stance provides an analogous vantage point onto similarly useful, objective patterns in behavior — but these patterns, while objective, are "not *out there* entirely independent of us," since they are composed partly of our own "subjective" reactions to what is out there: patterns "made to order for our narcissistic concerns" (citing Akins 1986). It is easy for creatures constituted as we are to perceive the patterns visible from the intentional stance — and only from that stance (p. 39). A footnote connects this to Anscombe's remark about **"an order which is there whenever actions are done with intentions"** (quote, Anscombe 1957, p. 80, discussed p. 39): Dennett says he could not for years make sense of *where* this order was located, but now sees it is exactly as hard to locate as the patterns in the Life world — visible if you look at the world the right way, generally invisible otherwise.

Dennett declines Rorty's "radical perspectivalism" (citing Dennett 1982a), but maintains that because objective patterns always fall short of perfection, "uninterpretable gaps" remain: it is always possible in principle for rival intentional-stance interpretations to tie for first place, so that no further fact settles what a system "really" believed (p. 40).

He connects this to **Quine's (1960) thesis of the [[indeterminacy-of-translation|indeterminacy of radical translation]]**, here extended from external behavioral dispositions ("stimulus meanings") to internal dispositions too, quoting Quine: **"radical translation begins at home"** (1969, p. 46, quoted p. 40). A longer Quine quote (1970, pp. 180-81) argues the "black box" metaphor is misleading: the problem is not one of hidden facts awaiting discovery by brain physiology; expecting a distinct mechanism for every genuinely distinct mental state is one thing, expecting one for every distinction traditional mentalistic language happens to draw is another. **"The question whether... the foreigner really believes A or believes rather B is a question whose very significance I would put in doubt"** (quote, Quine 1970, p. 40). Dennett notes his own earlier paper, "Brain Writing and Mind Reading" (1975), extended this theme, exposing the error of hoping to "find something in the head" to settle what Quine's view leaves indeterminate — the same considerations apply to translating any "language of thought" discovered once one trades behaviorism for cognitive science (p. 40). Davidson (1974a, quoted p. 41) is cited making a parallel point: indeterminacy does not mean failing to capture real distinctions; it means that certain apparent distinctions are not significant, because when all the evidence is in, "alternative ways of stating the facts remain open" (quote, p. 41).

Dennett then reports **Parfit's (1984)** extension of indeterminacy to **personal identity**: we are strongly inclined to believe identity questions ("Am I about to die?") must have a determinate yes/no answer — Parfit's "our identity must be determinate" (quoted, p. 41). Dennett draws the parallel: we are just as strongly attracted to the view that the contents of our beliefs must be determinate (reprising the milk-in-the-fridge example), yet Parfit shows cases where no determinate answer is available. Parfit's example (quoted at length, p. 41): a club ceases to meet for years, then some former members start a club with the same name and rules — is it the *same* club, or a new one merely like it? If there is no rule and no further fact settling this, "the claim 'this is the same club' would be neither true nor false." Yet **"though there is no answer to our question, there may be nothing that we do not know... When this is true of some question, I call this question empty"** (quote, Parfit, p. 41).

The excerpt closes mid-argument: Dennett notes that in the club case we accept there is no "deeper fact," but for personal identity "the supposition that there is — must be — just such a deeper fact dies hard," calling this one of the "brute convictions" that Nagel (1986) cannot abandon (p. 41) — the discussion is cut off here at the end of the supplied pages.

> **Intuition note:** The Game of Life shows that a pattern can be completely real (robust, predictively useful) while being visible only from one particular, optional point of view — and Dennett argues belief is like that: real, but the kind of real that sometimes runs out of determinate answers, the way "is this the same club?" can run out of an answer without anyone being ignorant of anything.

## Toy example
(Ours, illustrating the intentional strategy's core move: deriving a prediction from assumed rational belief + desire.)

Consider a simple garden sprinkler controller with one soil-moisture sensor, reading a percentage from 0 to 100.

- **Design-stance facts given:** it is wired to water the garden whenever the sensor reads below a threshold of 30%.
- **Intentional-stance reconstruction (our own, not the author's example):** we treat the controller as a rational agent. We ask what desire it "ought" to have, given its purpose: *keep the soil moisture at or above 30%.* We ask what belief it "ought" to form from its sensor reading. Today the sensor reads 22%. By the belief-attribution rule, it should come to believe: "the soil moisture (22) is below the desired level (30)." By practical reasoning (desire: moisture ≥ 30; belief: moisture = 22 < 30; only means available: watering raises moisture) it follows that the rational thing to do is water.
- **Prediction:** the sprinkler turns on. Check: 22 < 30, so the predicted action matches the design-stance fact. If instead the sensor read 35, then 35 ≥ 30, and the intentional stance predicts no watering — again matching the device's simple arithmetic rule.

This shows, on a toy scale, what Dennett's thermostat discussion argues at length: a minimal, "de-interpreted" belief ("A is too F") and desire ("A should be more G") can be read off a device's design, and the prediction is just the practical-reasoning conclusion from comparing the two numbers (p. 29-30).

## Concepts
- [[intentional-stance]] — treating a system as a rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have, given its situation, to predict its behavior (p. 17).
- [[physical-design-stance]] — the physical stance predicts from physical constitution and laws of physics (always available in principle); the design stance predicts from an assumed function, ignoring physical detail, and fails under malfunction (p. 16-17).
- [[intentionality]] — the "aboutness" of mental states, credited by Dennett to "Brentano and others"; beliefs and desires are intentional states (p. 15).
- [[rational-agent-assumption]] — start from the ideal of perfect rationality (closed under implication, no contradictions) and revise downward only as needed, for just the inferences relevant to the system's present predicament (p. 21).
- [[intentional-system]] (new) — any system whose behavior is reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy; "what it is to be a true believer" (p. 15); includes very degenerate cases like thermostats (p. 29).
- [[real-pattern]] (new) — an objective pattern in a system's behavior, robust under indefinitely many variations in physical implementation, visible only from a particular stance (here, the intentional stance); central to the reply to Nozick's Martians and later illustrated with Conway's Game of Life (p. 25-26, 37-39).
- [[language-of-thought]] (new) — the hypothesis that the inferences attributed to a rational believer are mirrored by physical, causal processes in the brain whose structure parallels the logical form of the beliefs; reported as the currently popular but unproven candidate explanation for why the intentional strategy works (p. 34).
- [[indeterminacy-of-translation]] (new) — Quine's thesis that rival systematizations of the evidence for a translation (or belief attribution) can tie for first place, so no further fact decides between them; extended by Dennett from external to internal "behavior" (p. 40).

## Canonical instances
- [[lectern-counterexample]] (new) — a lectern can be "construed" as a rational agent, correctly predicting that it stays put, yet is disqualified as an intentional system because the stance adds no genuine predictive power (p. 23).
- [[thermostat-intentional-system]] (new) — the minimal, "degenerate" intentional system; used to show belief-content becomes determinate only as causal connections to the world are enriched (p. 29-33).
- [[nozick-martians]] (new) — superintelligent beings needing no design or intentional stance; used to argue that purely physical omniscience can still miss real, objective behavioral patterns (p. 25-28).
- [[gm-shares-multiple-realizability]] (new) — a stockbroker's order for 500 GM shares, realizable by indefinitely many different physical motions, illustrating a real pattern analogous to the many physical ways of "being a spark plug" (p. 26).
- [[earthling-martian-phone-call]] (new) — the Mrs. Gardner telephone-call example, where ordinary intentional-stance reasoning reaches the same correct prediction as brute physical tracking, but with vastly less information (p. 26-27).
- [[conways-game-of-life]] — Conway's cellular automaton, used in the 1987 postscript to show a higher-level, objectively real pattern (a Turing machine built from gliders) that is visible only from an optional "stance" (p. 37-39).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Dennett's rational-agent assumption (start from an ideal of perfect rationality, revise downward) ↔ cognitive science: Bayesian theory of mind / inverse planning, which formalizes "invert a rationality assumption to infer unobserved beliefs/desires from behavior" with Bayes' rule — same basic inferential move, only qualitative here.
- [structural-analogy] The thermostat-to-person continuum, where Dennett insists "the principles... are the same," differing only in degree of richness of world-connections ↔ discussions of degrees or "grades" of representation/cognition in philosophy of mind.
- [structural-analogy] Real patterns robust under indefinitely many physical substitutions (GM-shares example) ↔ multiple-realizability arguments in philosophy of mind, where a mental-state type is realized by many distinct physical configurations.
- [metaphor] "Lightning wants to find the best path to ground" ↔ the intentional stance applied to undesigned physical systems — a case Dennett himself flags as a looser, more metaphorical extension of the strategy, not on a par with belief attribution to people (p. 22).

## Questions this raises
- If belief content is only as determinate as the predictive strategy's success allows, how should we treat borderline cases — simple animals, or AI systems — where rival interpretations tie?
- Chapter 4 ("Making Sense of Ourselves," mentioned p. 21) is promised to deal with irrationality; how would relaxing the rationality assumption change which beliefs/desires get attributed?
- How could Dennett's claim (2) — that an internal, roughly isomorphic "language of thought" pattern produces the real pattern — ever be confirmed or disconfirmed, given that he says doubting it does not touch claim (1)?
- Is there really no difference in principle between Parfit's "empty" club-identity question and a supposedly empty question about exactly what someone believes, or does belief content have constraints (e.g., the thermostat's "fastening") that club identity lacks?
