# De re and de dicto Dismantled; Reflections: About Aboutness

## L0 — One line
Dennett argues that the philosophers' de re / de dicto split, as usually drawn, does not mark a special kind of belief, and that a realistic psychology should work with notional worlds and treat propositions only as rough, indirect measures of information.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Quine and others split belief attributions into relational (de re) and notional (de dicto). Dennett asks whether this marks a real psychological difference. His answer is no. He says the usual alignment of issues runs together three things: the style of attribution, specific versus general belief, and the idea of strong aboutness. Attempts to define strong aboutness by causal ancestry give a distinction he finds psychologically inert. Russell's Principle (you cannot judge about an object without knowing which object) is, he says, the source of a lion's share of the theorists' troubles. A better psychology describes an agent's notional world, and propositions are convenient but imprecise units, more like dollars than like numbers.

**Prerequisites:** predicate logic (∃, ∀, "quantifying into" a clause); reference versus meaning; the idea of possible worlds; Twin Earth and Fregean conditions from earlier parts of the chapter (not in this unit).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Quine uses opacity as a quarantine, but he accepts that relational statements such as "There is someone whom Ralph believes to be a spy" are indispensable. This produces the relational/notional distinction (pp. 174–175).
- Dennett sees three confusions fostered by the way Quine sets the problem: attention only to relations to individuals; two attribution styles read as two kinds of belief; general beliefs taken to be nonrelational (p. 176).
- General beliefs are relational too (snakes believed slimy). To say who is jeopardized by a general belief, one needs a relation through an attribute, not through a set (pp. 176–179).
- Relational vs notional is a different contrast from specific vs general. Both kinds of belief can be attributed in both styles. The idea of non-relational "pure de dicto" belief as internal "mental sayings" is, he says, a myth (pp. 181–186).
- Strong aboutness (direct reference or causal history) is, Dennett argues, at most a difference of degree, and he sees no psychological use for it. Weak aboutness is enough to allow quantifying in, though this can mislead (pp. 190–199).
- Russell's Principle must be abandoned. Whatever is right in it belongs to notional-attitude psychology (pp. 200–202, 204).
- Propositions are a heuristic overlay, more like dollars than like numbers. Dennett claims there are no natural universal units of semantic information (pp. 206–208).
- Backward-looking origin facts, which he calls inert, cannot carry the theoretical weight their defenders give them (pp. 210–211).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### De re and de dicto Dismantled

**Terms.** In the orthodox summary Dennett quotes from Sosa, belief *de dicto* is belief that a certain *dictum* (proposition) is true, and belief *de re* is belief about a particular *res* (thing) that it has a property (p. 182). Quine's own terms are *relational* and *notional* (p. 175). *Referential opacity* means a context where the symbols are not interpreted in their normal role, for instance as terms denoting what they normally denote, and so cannot be bound by quantifiers (p. 174).

**Why relations to things matter (pp. 174–175).**
- Chisholm lists facts about Oliver B. Garrett: he once lived in Massachusetts, the police seek him, the speaker first learned of him in youth, and the speaker believes him to be in hiding. Dennett says such facts are not trivial. We need some theory able to assert that relations between things and believers hold, and no methodologically solipsistic theory can (p. 174).
- The same conclusion is forced on Quine, who must give up, reluctantly, the program of treating all belief attribution as referentially opaque. Nonrelationality is the essence of Quine's concept of referential opacity. Frege said such terms refer to their senses. Quine distrusts senses, propositions and their kin, so he handles each belief-predicate by analogy with direct quotation, as a *lumpy predicate* that relates the believer only to a closed sentence (p. 174).
- Dennett says Quine needs opacity only as a quarantine barrier protecting the healthy, extensional part of a sentence from the infected part. Quine's maxim of shallow analysis is to expose no more logical structure than seems useful (p. 175).
- Quine can stave off most demands for relational construals, but faced with the sort of case Chisholm describes he recognises an itch that must be scratched. He also stresses the urgent information in "There is someone whom I believe to be a spy" (p. 175).
- He is thus led to acknowledge relational and notional attributions. Others say de re and de dicto, and Quine acknowledges that it comes to the same thing. Dennett says it does come to the same thing in the literature, but would not have if Quine had meant what he ought to have meant (p. 175).

**Three confusions fostered by Quine's setting of the problem (p. 176).** Dennett says Quine himself is not clearly prey to any of them.
1. Attention is fixed on relations to *particular concrete individuals* (almost always persons), which leads to a sort of institutional blindness to other relations.
2. Quine follows Adolf Meier's advice and avoids explicitly relational construals except when forced. Dennett says this helps create the illusion that there are two *types of belief*, not just two styles of attribution. Quine's acknowledgment that sometimes an explicitly relational assertion is needed then becomes, for others, an imagined demonstration of two sorts of belief.
3. Combining the first two, relational beliefs are identified with beliefs about single individuals. That tempts one to conclude that *general* beliefs are wholly nonrelational. This subliminal conclusion lets an unrecognised vacillation about general beliefs undermine otherwise well-motivated projects.

Footnote 24: Quine (1969) is led to a view that virtually annuls the contrast between portentous and trivial beliefs. Quine says this at first seems intolerable but grows on one; Dennett agrees it does, with suitable cultivation, which he promises to provide (p. 176, n. 24).

**General beliefs are relational too (pp. 176–177).**
- Examples: (1) Many people wrongly believe that snakes are slimy. (2) Snakes are believed by many to be slimy. (3)–(6) include snow believed by virtually everyone to be cold, and charity thought by some superior to faith and hope (p. 177).
- (2) is a property snakes have, about as important as their scaliness. It is an ecological fact: people try to get rid of things they think slimy. Many a snake has died through someone's *general* belief without ever meeting its killer. So the effect does not reduce to a conjunction of cases of particular snakes believed slimy by particular people (p. 177).
- Dennett says the Quinian quarantine tends to hide that you cannot make such claims, in a form usable in arguments in the obvious ways, unless you can make them in a way that permits explicit relations to be expressed (p. 177).

**The Herb–Sam case (pp. 177–179).**
- Sam is an Iranian living in California. Herb believes all Iranians in California should be deported immediately, but has no inkling of Sam's existence. Supposing Herb is an authority or an influential citizen, Sam would regret this belief, and a world where people hold it is worse for Sam. Dennett says Sam is *jeopardized* by it, and so are all Iranians living in California (p. 177).
- Suppose Herb also believes all marijuana smokers should be publicly whipped. Whether that jeopardizes Sam depends on whether Sam smokes marijuana (p. 178).
- Something follows from (7) Sam is an Iranian living in California and (8) Herb believes all Iranians living in California should be deported immediately. That same something does not follow from (7) and (9) (the whipping belief). What follows licenses the conclusion that Sam is jeopardized by the belief in (8). It is *not* (10) Herb believes *of Sam* that he should be deported. No belief of the sort that impresses Quine and Chisholm follows from (7) and (8) (p. 178).
- Candidate (11), a relation to the set of Iranians in California, fails. If Sam leaves California, the relevant set changes but Herb's belief does not. As Dennett puts it, "it is not set membership that jeopardizes Sam, but attribute-having" (p. 178).
- Hence (12): there is an attribute (Iranicalifornihood) such that Sam has it and Herb believes *of it* that anything having it should be deported immediately. Dennett remarks: not just quantifying in, but quantifying over attributes (p. 178).
- A circumlocution (13) tries to soften the ontological blow, but he says it will not work without *ad hoc* provisos (p. 179):
$$(\exists\alpha)(\exists c)\big(\alpha \text{ denotes } c \;\&\; \text{Sam is a member of } c \;\&\; \text{Herb believes-true } \ulcorner (x)(x \text{ is a member of } \alpha \supset x \text{ should be deported immediately})\urcorner\big)\tag{13}$$
- Dennett's recommendation: if one is going to take belief talk seriously at all, one might as well go all the way and permit quantification over attributes and relations, as well as individuals and classes, in all positions within belief contexts (p. 179). Quine explicitly renounces this course (n. 25).

> *Intuition note.* If we want to say who a belief endangers, we need to link the believer to things in the world, even when the belief names no one. The link runs through a property the person has, not through a list of people.

**General beliefs are "about" something (pp. 179–180).**
- Dennett says the Putnamian arguments imply that even general propositional attitudes, if they are genuinely propositional in meeting Frege's first two conditions, must imply a relation between believer and things. My belief that all whales are mammals is about whales. My Doppelgänger's counterpart belief might not be (p. 179).
- An objection: the logical form $(x)(Fx \supset Gx)$ is as much about cabbages and kings as about whales, and by being about everything it is about nothing. Dennett says this does little to still the intuition that someone who believes whales are fishes is wrong *about whales* (p. 179).
- Unicorns: the belief that there are no unicorns can be said to be about unicornhood, which is nowhere instantiated. If there were unicorns it would be a false belief about unicorns, as the belief that there are no blue whales is (today) a false belief about blue whales (pp. 179–180).
- Dennett declines a tug-of-war over "about". He separates a strong sense (which much of the de re literature seeks) from a weaker, much clearer sense whose essence is denotation (p. 180).
- Weak aboutness: if I believe the shortest spy is a woman, there may be a sense in which my belief is not about anyone. Yet it is true or false depending on the actual person who is the shortest spy, the satisfier of the description I used. That is a relation I bear to one person in virtue of my belief, which he calls *weak aboutness*. If Rosa Klebb is the shortest spy, it might be misleading to say I was thinking about her, but in the weak sense it would be true (p. 180).

**The second confusion examined (pp. 181–182).**
- Quine's pair: (14) $(\exists x)$(Ralph believes that $x$ is a spy); (15) Ralph believes that $(\exists x)(x \text{ is a spy})$. Quine says that if Ralph is like most of us, (15) is true and (14) is false (p. 181).
- Dennett: the pair illustrates two attribution styles ((14) ontologically committing, (15) reticent). It also illustrates two sorts of psychological state: a *specific* belief in (14) and a *general* belief in (15). Many conclude, without warrant, that (14) and (15) show a relational kind and a notional kind of belief. They ignore that both beliefs can be attributed in both styles (p. 181).
- Some relational claim other than (14) may be true in virtue of (15), e.g.
$$(\exists x)(x \text{ is spyhood} \;\&\; \text{Ralph believes } x \text{ to be instantiated}).\tag{16}$$
  Perhaps there are also merely notional readings, other than (15), that follow from (14). Contrasting (14) with (15) is comparing apples and oranges (p. 181).
- The true notional counterpart of relational (17) $(\exists x)$($x$ is believed by Tommy to have filled his stocking with toys) is not (18) Tommy believes that $(\exists x)(x$ has filled his stocking with toys). It is something we have no formal expression for, rendered with scare-quotes as (19): *Tommy believes "of Santa Claus" that he has filled his stocking with toys*. Notional-world talk lets us say that Santa inhabits Tommy's notional world without committing us to Santa's existence (p. 182).
- In the other direction, to distinguish my general belief that all water is H₂O from its notional twin in my Doppelgänger, we will have to say, relationally, that *water* is what I believe to be H₂O (p. 182).
- Dennett says this is not meant in itself to show that there are two importantly different sorts of belief, or more than two. It shows that a familiar alignment of the issues is a misalignment (p. 182).

**The myth of non-relational "pure de dicto" beliefs (pp. 182–184).**
- Sosa's summary (20) is cited as orthodoxy, "not defended". Dennett also considers a swapped definition (21), with *of* and *that* exchanged, and sets the question aside to look at the word *dictum* (pp. 182–183).
- *Dictum* is ambiguous between what is uttered (the words) and what is expressed. Propositions are viewed either as sentence-like things or as abstract meanings, and the de re / de dicto literature keeps these conveniently undistinguished (p. 183).
- This permits the covert influence of an incoherent picture: propositions as *mental sayings*, wholly internal, owing their identities only to their intrinsic properties and hence wholly nonrelational. Yet they are supposed to be not mere sentences but propositions. A de dicto attribution then looks like a methodologically solipsistic fixing of content. Dennett compares this with qualia as apparently intrinsic features of minds (n. 29, p. 183).
- The subconscious metaphysics is that mentally embracing a sentence somehow causes the proposition the sentence expresses to be believed. Dennett finds mildly astonishing the apparent willingness of many psychologists and AI theorists to accept this setting without misgivings (p. 183).
- In *Word and Object* Quine renounces propositions and similar entities (creatures of darkness) in favour of lumpy predicates of direct quotation. In 1956 he said that a believer in propositions cannot object to "believes-true S" on grounds of obscurity, since it is definable in his terms. Dennett says this tells us how the believer in propositions is to understand Quine's predicates, not how Quine does. Given Quine's flight from intension, one can suppose he does not think they make much sense. They are a device that lets an imaginary translator move safely on, confident he has made at least enough new predicates never to use one predicate for two differently intended attitude claims. Dennett calls them notional to a fault, but also inert (p. 184).

**Impure referentiality (pp. 184–186).**
- Three Quine passages: (A) in the opaque sense, *wants* is not a relative term relating people to anything at all; (B) if belief is taken opaquely, Tom's believing that Cicero denounced Catiline expressly relates Tom to no man; (C) the commissioner's looking for the chairman of the hospital board is taken as not purely referential, although it seems to have far more bearing on the chairman than "Tully was a Roman" has on Tully (pp. 184–185).
- Dennett says that in all these cases there is clear evidence of what we might call *impure referentiality*. Wanting a sloop in the notional sense does not relate one to any particular sloop, but it still bears on sloops, since sloops would be fewer if nobody wanted relief from slooplessness. In his view, one cannot believe *that* Cicero denounced Catiline without believing *of* Cicero that he did (p. 185).
- Quine's (22) *Giorgione was so-called because of his size* has the name doing double duty. Castañeda, Kiteley, Loar and Hornsby develop the normally dual role of singular terms in attitude clauses, e.g. (24) *Michael thinks that that masked man is a diplomat, but he obviously is not*. Dennett says this gets part of the story right but misses the extension to *general* terms. When we quantify over attributes, as in (12) and (16), a general term in an attitude clause normally plays a dual role, as suggested by (25) *Herb believes that all Iranians in California should be deported, but none of them have anything to fear from him* (pp. 185–186).

**Notional attitudes and propositional attitudes (pp. 186–187).**
- Dennett's usage: relating a subject's narrow-psychological states (his notional attitudes) to his broad, propositional states is a matter of looking for the best fit. His notional world is taken as already determined, and his notional attitudes are lined up with a set of propositional attitudes (p. 186).
- Once the notional/relational and specific/general distinctions are kept apart, many claims made to characterise "de re belief" apply across the board and fail to distinguish a special sort of belief (p. 186).
- A seriously confused subject may have no good fit. An irrational one will have no flawless fit, since no possible world could suit him. A badly misinformed one will mismatch the actual world (p. 186).
- A child who believes in Santa Claus has Santa in his notional world, and no one in the real world is a suitable counterpart. So some of his notional attitudes cannot be traded in for any propositional attitudes. Donnellan holds that what such a child says expresses no proposition. Dennett adds that the child's state is a notional attitude that determines no propositional attitude. McDowell and Field endorse versions of this (p. 186).
- This can be made to appear outrageous to anyone who insists on viewing beliefs as nothing but propositional attitudes. For that person must say that no one has beliefs about Santa Claus, and no one could; some people just think they do. Dennett's alternative is that Santa-believers have notional attitudes in their place (p. 186).
- Notional attitudes still give understanding and leverage for the child's behaviour. Tommy tells the man with the phony beard what he wants because he believes the man is Santa. Genuine propositional attitudes can be derived from merely notional ones when the world occasionally obliges (p. 187).
- The reverse problem is an embarrassment of riches. At Shakey's Pizza Parlor there are too many real objects as candidate *relata*. This is not restricted to specific beliefs. Putnam's *All elms are deciduous*, said by someone who cannot tell an elm from a beech, raises the same problem for general beliefs, where different attributes are the candidates (p. 187).
- Going relational, specific notional claims are traded for specific relational ones, and general for general. Ralph's banal belief cited notionally in (15) can be cited relationally as (16). His portentous belief cited relationally in (14) could also be cited notionally if there were a reason, for instance if pranksters had duped him about a man who never was. The necessary conditions for sustaining a relational attribution are entirely independent of the specific/general distinction (p. 187).

> *Intuition note.* "Notional vs relational" is about how we describe someone's belief. "Specific vs general" is about which belief it is. Dennett says that many claims made for de re belief apply across the board, so the relational/notional contrast alone does not pick out a special kind of belief.

**Vividness, substitution and direct reference (pp. 187–190).**
- (26) Tom believes that whales are mammals is a general belief if any is. Tom cannot truly be said to believe that unless we can say of whales that he believes them to be mammals. Formally, such a claim would license quantifying in, as in (27) $(\exists x)(x = \text{whalehood} \;\&\; \text{Tom believes of } x \text{ that whatever instantiates it is a mammal})$. Dennett then asks whether Tom must have a *vivid name* of whalehood (Kaplan) (pp. 187–188).
- Compare (28): if all Bill knows about whales is that they are the largest mammals, he has a very unvivid name of whalehood. Most of us are in closer rapport with whales. With (29) dugongs, we are cognitively remote, so there is an intuitive pull against saying Tom believes dugongs to be mammals. A monolingual German who has acquired "Dugongs are mammals" as a true sentence does not count as believing that dugongs are mammals. Someone who knows English and believes the sentence true believes it in a very minimal sense, roughly the state most of us are in. A book, a film or a zoo visit makes one more secure as a believer (p. 188).
- Kaplan first proposed vivid names as a condition of genuine de re belief, but held there was no threshold of vividness, and he has since abandoned the condition. Dennett says vividness, or what amounts to vividness as Kaplan characterised it, is a condition on *all* belief: one must be richly informed about the world to be said to have beliefs. The idea that de dicto beliefs have no vividness requirement is a symptom of the mental-sayings picture (p. 189).
- Footnote 33: Thomason suggests some of these problems belong to the logic of plurals. Dennett thinks little but naturalness is lost if the examples are recast as beliefs that *all* snakes are slimy, all whales mammals, and so on (n. 33).
- *Substitution.* It is said that co-designative expressions may be substituted in de re but not de dicto attributions. If all general beliefs have relational renderings, Dennett says substitution is allowed there too, under the same pragmatic constraints for securing reference without misleading (p. 189). Examples: (30) *My wife wants me to buy her a sweater the color of your shirt*; (31) *Your grandfather thought that children who behave the way you're behaving should be sent to bed without supper*; and Russell's (32) *I thought your yacht was longer than it is* (pp. 189–190).
- *Direct reference.* Donnellan, Kaplan and others claim a fundamental difference between direct reference and reference by definite description. Postponing the merits, Dennett says the supposed distinction fails to mark a special feature of specific belief about particular objects. Demonstration works for things, (33) *This is the Eiffel Tower and that is the Seine*, and for properties, (34) and (35). A Frenchman's *je ne sais quoi* is a quale-property that he can predicate of something only through an identifying definite description. In an intensional context such a description often must be read transparently, as in (30), (31) or (36) *Rubens believed that women who look like you are very beautiful* (p. 190).

**Two views on specific belief (pp. 190–192).**
- With these set aside, Dennett asks what the general/specific distinction is. It should be drawable at the notional level, since we want to separate specific Santa-Claus-about states from general ones (p. 190).
- **Definite Description view:** the only distinction to mark is between believing *all Fs are Gs* (general) and believing *just one F is G* (specific). Specific beliefs are viewed as adequately captured by Russell's theory of definite descriptions (p. 191).
- **Special Reference view:** it does not deny that distinction in logical form but insists that even the beliefs the other view calls specific are properly general. There is a further category of truly specific beliefs, more strongly *about* their objects, because they pick them out by direct reference unmediated by descriptions (p. 191).
- Dennett's complaint: neither view as typically expressed makes much plausible contact with the empirical issue of what is literally in believers' heads that makes a state a belief about a particular object. Taken at literal face value the Definite Description view would be preposterous sententialism, and its adherents gesture at recognitional mechanisms. The critics gesture at unspecified causal intimacies and genealogical routes. Since no progress is possible at those levels of metaphor, he suggests perhaps it is best to revert to traditional philosophical terms for a while (p. 191).
- In favour of the Description view, a Russellian description does cull one object from the domain when all goes well. Against it, the Special Reference side holds that Russell's expansion reveals disguised generality: *the man who killed Smith is insane* says some $x$ is identical with any person who killed Smith and is insane. This generality is also revealed to intuition, it is claimed, by the "whoever" test: *whoever killed Smith* casts a net into the domain and does not point at anyone (pp. 191–192).
- Dennett separates two motivations entangled in the Special Reference view: a metaphysical one (essentialism) and a psychological one (p. 192).

**The metaphysical issue (pp. 192–195).**
- (37) Tom believes that the shortest spy is a woman. If we treat it relationally, as we must if we want to know whether it is true, the proposition is weakly about some actual person; suppose Rosa Klebb, so the belief is true. If Tiny Tim Traitor were the shortest spy, Tom would have a false belief. Dennett separates narrow-psychological state from propositional attitude, and what *could have been* from what *may become* the case (p. 192).
- Tom's narrow state could stay constant for a year while the title "shortest spy" changed hands unnoticed. He then believed something true and later something false, so different propositions. The changes in Tom are Cambridge changes (Geach), like ceasing to be nearer the North Pole than the oldest living plumber born in Utah (pp. 192–193).
- (38) Tom believes that the youngest member of the Harvard class of 1950 graduated with honors. Here the proposition does not change from day to day, and whoever the belief is about (in the weak sense), it is about that person for all eternity (p. 193).
- Fix Tom's notional attitude. There are worlds where he believes no proposition at all (no Harvard, no Twin Harvard). There are worlds where he believes a proposition about Twin Harvard. And there are worlds where he believes the very same proposition but someone else is the youngest graduate. The identity of the proposition is tied to the attribute, not to its bearer, and likewise in (37). That is the doctrine the Special Reference view must sustain if (37) and (38) are to be distinguished from genuinely specific beliefs, and it requires making sense of the claim that someone else could have been the shortest spy (p. 193).
- (39) Tom believes the person who dropped the gum wrapper (whoever it might be) is a careless slob. Suppose, since some say it makes a difference, that Tom saw only the discarded wrapper. The belief is weakly about the one individual who dropped it. Had someone else dropped it, the very same notional and propositional attitude could have been about someone else. Dennett asks what difference it could make if Tom sees the person, as in (40) (pp. 193–194).
- The Special Reference believer insists that had Tom seen someone else drop it, he would have had a different belief, since it would be strongly about a different person. Dennett says this is not a claim about Tom's narrow state. Even if the short, fat person's twin brother had dropped it in indistinguishable circumstances, Tom's notional attitude could be the same, but the propositional attitude would differ, because the notional state was caused by a different individual in some special way (p. 194).
- Footnote 34: not all causal theorists carve cases alike. Vendler would insist that even where Tom does not see the wrapper dropped, the belief is rigidly and strongly about that person, since only one person could have dropped that wrapper. Dennett suspects this extreme position is the only stable one for a causal theorist, but allows he may have misread Vendler: perhaps someone else could have dropped that wrapper, though no one else could have left that footprint (n. 34, pp. 194–195).
- **Amy and Beth.** Tom's lucky penny Amy is replaced overnight by an imposter, Beth. (41) *Tom believes he once took the penny in his pocket to Spain* asserts a false belief about Beth, and would have been a true belief about Amy had history gone differently. On the Special Reference theory there are further beliefs: (42), (43) strongly about Amy, and (44), (45) strongly about Beth, each essentially about its object (p. 195).
- Dennett: we need not grant such beliefs in order to grant that Tom has beliefs about both pennies, since (41) is already weakly about Beth and (46) *the penny he took to Spain is in his pocket* is weakly about Amy. Where a belief is weakly about an object as the lone satisfier of a description, one can insert *whoever it might be*. This will often sound odd, as in (47) *Tom believes that his wife (whoever she might be) is an excellent swimmer*, but only because of a pragmatic implication of an outlandish possibility, unless it is merely taken to suggest that the speaker cannot pick Tom's wife out of a lineup (pp. 195–196).

**The psychological issue (pp. 196–199).**
- Another reason to separate a special class of de re beliefs might be to mark where we may quantify in. Dennett says that cannot be the reason, since we may quantify in whenever a belief is only weakly about an individual, although this would often be very misleading. Pointing at Rosa Klebb and saying Tom believes her to be a woman would mislead, because a relational claim normally implies the believer can identify or reidentify the object. Nothing about how a belief is acquired could guarantee this against all future misadventures, so *any* theory of de re belief would license potentially misleading relational claims (p. 196).
- *Thoughts A and B.* In a committee meeting I think: Thought A, the youngest person in the room was born after Roosevelt's death. Then I look at Bill and wonder whether Thought A is about him: Thought B. Surely Thought B is about Bill more strongly than A is, one feels. Dennett says this is an illusion; there is only a difference of degree. B is weakly about whoever is the only person I am looking at and whose name I believe to be Bill. Had Bill's twin taken his place unnoticed, B would have been about the twin, or, more likely, I would be in a state like Tom's at Shakey's, with no psychologically perspicuous propositional attitudes (pp. 196–197).
- *George, Gracie and Hoover.* Gracie says Hoover believes of George that he did it. George replies that Hoover knows only that *whoever* fits his description is the murderer, so George is safe. Dennett says George has misdiagnosed his situation. Poirot likewise says he does not know who the murderer is but has deduced that the murderer is the only person in the drawing room with the pantry key, and then identifies and arrests him. If Hoover's description were the only person in Clancy's bar with yellow mud on his shoes, the jig could soon be up (p. 197; also n. 35, the dime-in-the-phone-booth contrast).
- One is a (minimal) suspect if one satisfies *any* definite description Hoover takes to pick out Smith's murderer. Trivially, the murderer is a minimal suspect even if Hoover is baffled and merely believes a single culprit committed the crime. This would be objectionable only if there were a principled way to distinguish minimal from genuine de re suspects, and Dennett says there is not (pp. 197–198).
- So, as Quine suggests, the apparently sharp psychological distinction between (48) Hoover believes that someone (some *one*) murdered Smith and (49) Someone is believed by Hoover to have murdered Smith collapses. The logical difference in the speaker's ontological commitment remains (p. 198).
- A baffled Hoover would naturally deny to the press that anyone is believed by him to be the murderer. What he denies is knowing more than anyone who knows only that the crime was committed. If he had wrestled with the murderer in daylight without knowing who it was, any causal theory would say he has a de re belief about that person, yet it would be most disingenuous for him to claim a suspect (p. 198).
- Causal intimacy in the past usually separates weaker from stronger relations, and the stronger ones have implications for future conduct. In unusual situations the normal implications do not hold. Dennett infers from dislocations that the causal requirement is not in itself necessary or sufficient, since effects are as important as causes. What *is* necessary is the creation of a notional object in the subject's notional world. This seldom happens without causal commerce of some sort, but special cases (the Westwood Village peg and the door, not described on these pages, and Poirot's culprit) force the result. They show an independence in principle, if seldom in practice, between psychologically salient states and their metaphysical credentials (pp. 198–199).
- *Two options for de re belief (pp. 199–200).*
  - Option 1: treat it as having no behavioural consequence. One view would not suppose that anything about Tom's likely behaviour follows from the truth of (50) *Tom believes of the man shaking hands with him that he is a heavily armed fugitive mass murderer*. Dennett says this view acquiesces in what might be called the psychological opacity of semantic transparency. He can see no obstacle to defining such a variety of attitude but no use for it, since nothing of interest seems to follow from a true attribution. If $a$ is believed by me to be $F$, it does not follow that I do not also believe $a$ to be not-$F$, and so on (p. 199).
  - The quest assumed interesting, important relations between believers and objects. It ends with relations of intermittent and unprojectible interest. Hence Dennett sees no motive for denying that one believes *of* the shortest spy that he is a spy (p. 199).
  - Option 2: make de re belief a psychologically distinct phenomenon. Then it cannot be a well-named theory, for it must be a theory of distinctions within *notional* attitude psychology. If $a$ is in my notional world and I believe it $F$, I do not also believe it not-$F$, but only because notional objects are creatures of one's beliefs (Schiffer). Having created them, we can then see what real things, if any, they line up with, but not from any position of privileged access (p. 200).

> *Intuition note.* Dennett asks whether the concept of special de re belief is supposed to play a marked role in explaining behaviour. Strong, causal aboutness can be defined, but the causal facts can vary without any change in psychologically salient states. He sees no use for it as a psychological concept.

**Russell's Principle (p. 200).**
- Evans's *Russell's Principle*: it is not possible to make a judgment about an object without knowing what object you are making a judgment about. Russell preserved it through knowledge by acquaintance, and so concluded that we can only judge *about* certain abstract objects or certain of our own internal states (p. 200).
- Dennett says Russell's wording, with its "terms", prepares the way for the sort of theory Chisholm abhors, one that supposes "the mind cannot get beyond the circle of its own ideas". The way out is to give up Russell's Principle (cf. Burge 1979), and with it the idea of a special sort of de re belief intimately and strongly about its objects (p. 200).
- A grain of truth remains in Russell's idea of a special relation between a believer and some of the things he thinks with. To discuss it one must turn to notional-attitude psychology and to the engineering question of how to design a cognitive creature with our kind of notional world (p. 200).

**Promising distinctions Dennett lists (pp. 201–202).** He says the paper has grown several times its intended size and saves detailed examination for other occasions.
- *Different ways of thinking of something*: a purely notional set of distinctions (p. 201).
- *Episodic thinking vs believing*: we have beliefs about things we cannot now think about, because we are temporarily unable to gain access, not because the beliefs are unconscious or tacit. A theory of action needs an account of how we recognise and keep track of things and exploit beliefs in thought (p. 201).
- *Explicit vs virtual representation* (chapter 6): in a rental car I expect the right front tire to have a safe tread, but have almost certainly not unconsciously represented it as a separate item of belief. Having attended to something is a real difference of some importance in psychology, not the de re / de dicto difference (p. 201).
- *Linguistically infected beliefs (opinions) vs the rest*: no dog could have the opinion that it was Friday or that the shortest dog was a dog. Some suppose this means animals without language cannot have de dicto belief. Dennett says this distinction is independent of the de re / de dicto debates (p. 201).
- *Artifactual or transparently notional objects vs other notional objects*: we may conjure imaginary things (a dream house) or reconstruct a crime in imagination, and our constructed notional worlds are not always meant as fiction (pp. 201–202).
- Ignoring these distinctions has contributed to the confusion, Dennett says. The case of believing the shortest spy is a spy is commonly presumed clear, but the literature leaves many possibilities unseparated: logical acumen versus a penchant for tautologies; the shortest dugong; believing versus thinking; what sincere denial shows. With good psychological accounts of notional attitudes, some puzzles will be solved and others discredited. He doubts there will be any residue (p. 202).

### Reflections: About Aboutness (pp. 203–211)

**Background and four claims (pp. 203–204).**
- Fodor opened *Psychological Explanation* with self-mockery about philosophers straightening out psychology in a free afternoon. Dennett says that in 1978 he and Stich, at Woodfield's Fulbright workshop at Bristol, had a similar hunch about de re / de dicto theory. After more than a year their differences remained, and each contributed a separate paper to Woodfield's 1981 anthology: Dennett's "Beyond Belief" and Stich's "On the Ascription of Content", which later became the heart of Stich's *From Folk Psychology to Cognitive Science* (p. 203).
- Dennett calls "Beyond Belief" an unfinished project and lists what he takes to be its four chief claims (pp. 203–204):
  1. *Propositions*: there is no stable received view. The two schools, propositions as sentence-like things and as sets of possible worlds, are strongly incompatible.
  2. *Notional worlds*: the path from propositional-attitude psychology to language-of-thought psychology is a route to fantasyland. A more realistic psychology uses propositions only indirectly, by characterising the "world" the mechanisms are designed to cope with.
  3. *Russell's Principle* must be abandoned. For all its introspective appeal, it is the source of a lion's share of the theorists' misfortunes.
  4. *De re / de dicto*: there is no stable, coherent view. Accounts are vitiated by an elusive ambiguity between a psychologically salient and projectible distinction and a metaphysically crisp but psychologically inert one.
- These mostly negative theses were meant to upset those who thought the puzzles (Ortcutt, the shortest spy, Twin Earth) could be handled in the orthodox, Russellian tradition. Dennett calls the orthodoxy an illusion and a by-product of the sociology of the discipline: experts writing for experts tend to presuppose more agreement than exists. Philosophers of language and logic, in the main, had only tangential psychological concerns and invented just enough plausible psychology to cover the puzzles. The result is theorists talking past each other (p. 204).

**Propositions (pp. 205–208).**
- Dennett grants that belief in an orthodoxy persists, and that his is not the only iconoclastic vision. If there is one thing the rest of academia seems to need from philosophers, it is a theory of propositions or of something like them. *Information* is used as a mass noun in neuroscience, ethology, economics and literary criticism, yet we have no sound, mutually recognised understanding of what it is (p. 205).
- Information in the sense of formal information theory (bits, bytes) is well understood but content-neutral. One videodisc can store the whole Grolier Encyclopedia or three hours of Bugs Bunny, with roughly the same amount of information. To ask *what information* passed when Philby talked to the Russians or the frog's eye to the frog's brain, we need *semantic information*. Despite ingenious attempts to extend information theory, we have no better way of individuating it than by talking of propositions (pp. 205–206).
- Propositions are treated as the ultimate medium of information transfer, as logically distinct, observer-independent, language-neutral morsels, and they play a foundational role as atoms in many projects. The predicate calculus presupposes a canonical imaginary language and tends to confer spurious respectability on sententialism. Cognitive and social psychologists take themselves to catalogue beliefs by listing questionnaire responses, and AI uses predicate calculus for beliefs. Dennett asks whether, if his criticism is right, these enterprises are in jeopardy, or whether the puzzles can be safely put under practical-purposes clauses (p. 206).
- His own defense of the intentional stance relies on propositions and, he says, shows the limits of the weight he thinks can be placed on them. The main source of the problems is the fund of intuition about the graspability of propositions. So long as one relaxes that grip, treating propositions as only indirectly and approximately measuring psychological states (as Churchland recommends), they play their limited role rather well. So long as one recognises that proposition talk is only a *heuristic overlay*, a useful but sometimes treacherous approximation, systematically incapable of being made precise, one can get away with the pretense that all informing is modelled on telling, i.e. sending a dictum from A to B (p. 206).
- What the frog's eye tells the frog's brain cannot be captured whole in a sentence, nor perhaps can the information in sold spy blueprints be captured in a predicate-calculus formula. One can typically find a sentence that usefully distils the gist, which can be held to express the proposition at issue to a first approximation (p. 207).
- *Proposition as canonical sentence* is blocked because any informing is relative to a particular intentional system (frog, brain, defense establishment, spymaster), whose capacities for uptake differ in incommensurable ways that defy any lingua franca. *Proposition as set of possible worlds* recognises and builds on this agent relativity (p. 207; n. 2 credits Stalnaker 1984). Footnote 2 notes the standard, presumably crushing objection: there is only one necessary proposition, yet mathematicians believe and disbelieve many logical truths. Stalnaker's reply treats such beliefs as beliefs about particular formulae, and Dennett finds this has independent motivation (n. 2).
- A proposition treated as a particular exhaustive sorting of agent-detectable possibilities permits in principle a more precise determination of what an agent believes, but limits the generality of inter-agent claims. What Tom, Pierre, Sherlock and Boris (chapter 3, not in this unit) have in common cannot be precisely stated in possible-worlds terms, since each partitions the worlds via idiosyncratic concepts of *Frenchman*, *murder* and *Trafalgar Square* (pp. 207–208). Dennett claims nothing is *precisely* in common (p. 208).
- Consequence: any parcelling of semantic information sets a somewhat arbitrary, potentially distorting standard. Dennett complicates Churchland's proposal that propositions are to psychology as numbers are to physics: propositions "turn out to be more like dollars than like numbers" (p. 208). Asking what proposition (in Standard Scheme P) something stores, transmits or expresses is like asking what it is worth in U.S. dollars. It is a useful, unifying, somewhat systematic if often procrustean question, not a question about real money. His summary: "There are no real, natural, universal units of either economic value or semantic information" (p. 208). A live goat's worth varies with place, time and person, and he takes it that no one supposes such estimates home in on a unique real value. He asks why we should think there are unique real contents of psychological states (p. 208).

> *Intuition note.* Asking "which proposition does she believe?" is like asking what a goat is worth in dollars. You can usually work out an answer in a chosen standard, but the standard is ours, not nature's.

**Notional Worlds (pp. 209–210).**
- Notional world psychology tries to capture the organism's own contribution to belief, in isolation. It is one approach to what has come to be called narrow conceptual role semantics (p. 209).
- To honour the intuition that semantic information is *in* the organism in a way that makes a difference, imagine sending the organism alone from A to B and counting on the recipients to do the best possible radical interpretation, reconstructing its notional world. To the extent that rival notional worlds are equally well supported by all the facts about the organism available at B, the information at issue is simply not there (p. 209).
- Stalnaker (unpublished) doubts the method is as fruitful as Dennett claims. In Dennett's example, Martians receive a comatose human body. Their task is a special case of Quinian radical translation (or Davidson's and Lewis's radical interpretation). The body is cut off from the environment, but by hypothesis the Martians can determine how it would respond to any stimulus, so they can run as many hypothetical *gavagai?*-experiments as they like. Presumably they would use their calculations to induce the body, hypothetically, to recount its biography in its native tongue. Supposing they control for lying and forgetfulness, they should get a description of a notional world that fails to distinguish Boston from Twin Boston but is otherwise remarkably specific. Hofstadter describes the exercise (p. 209).
- Dennett does not propose notional world psychology as a methodology for empirical psychologists, only as a way of getting explicit about the commitments of theory in rare dislocation cases. The indirectness of descriptions of possible worlds is useful for characterising what Santa-believers share without committing the theorist to a Santa Claus in the world or a "Santa Claus" in each brain. The notional world format reminds us, as Jackendoff (1985) puts it, that "information is in the mind of the beholder" (pp. 209–210).

**Russell's Principle (p. 210).** Millikan's sustained attack on meaning rationalism (1984, 1986) helped Dennett see the pernicious role of Russell's Principle. In "Beyond Belief" he imagined a counterfactual Fodor claiming to find a Mentalese text given in the hardware. Chapter 8, he says, shows he was closer to the truth than he supposed, and Fodor is far from alone in wanting first-person privileged access for a Fregean grasping of propositions (p. 210).

**De re / de dicto (pp. 210–211).**
- Dennett says attempts to define the distinction in terms of causal ancestry fail in instructive ways. He rebaptises strong (de re) aboutness *original direct reference*, one of a family with *original intentionality* and *original functionality* (chapter 8), all of which founder on the possibility of what he calls inert historical facts (p. 210).
- Origins are important, but so are current propensities. If one is interested in usefully projectible properties, one must treat backward-looking conditions as only normally highly reliable signs, symptoms, not criteria (p. 210).
- He grants that one can define a causal version of de re belief. He says it is just as easy to define a version of original intentionality so that some things really have states with certain contents and behaviourally identical things do not, because of their ancestry. In neither case does one capture anything of value unless one puts a somewhat mystical value on brute genealogical fact (pp. 210–211).
- Defenders usually reply by charging the opposition with positivism about truth. Dennett says this deflects his criticism. He acknowledges idle, uncheckable truths, which he calls inert facts, but insists they cannot have the theoretical importance their defenders give them. What cannot be checked cannot make a difference, and we mislead ourselves when we make much of theoretical distinctions that cannot be relied on to make one (p. 211).
- "Beyond Belief" closes with a somewhat wistful look at unfinished tasks. Dennett names Millikan's and Stalnaker's work as signposts, and highly recommends books by Jackendoff (1983) and Fauconnier (1985) (p. 211).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Idea: jeopardy runs through *attributes*, and a belief's content is not changed by who currently has the attribute (Herb–Sam, p. 178).

Four people:
- Sam: Iranian, lives in California (CA), no marijuana.
- Ali: Iranian, CA, smokes.
- Bob: not Iranian, CA, smokes.
- Cy: Iranian, lives in Texas, no marijuana.

Herb (knows none of them, and is influential) believes R1: everything with Iranicalifornihood (Iranian and living in CA) should be deported. He also believes R2: all marijuana smokers should be whipped.

Step 1. R1 jeopardizes those with Iranicalifornihood: Sam, Ali. That is 2 people.
Step 2. R2 jeopardizes smokers: Ali, Bob. That is 2 people.
Step 3. Sam is jeopardized by R1 only. He is not jeopardized by R2, since he does not smoke.
Step 4. Everyone jeopardized by at least one belief: {Sam, Ali, Bob}, 3 people. Cy is safe.
Step 5. Sam moves to Texas. He no longer has Iranicalifornihood. R1 is unchanged. Now R1 jeopardizes only Ali (1 person), and the total set is {Ali, Bob}, 2 people.

The set "Iranians in CA" changed from {Sam, Ali} to {Ali}, but Herb's belief did not change. Sam's safety depended on his having the attribute, not on set membership. This is a relation between Herb's general belief and Sam, with no belief *of Sam* involved.

## Concepts
- [[de-re-de-dicto]] (new) — orthodox gloss (Sosa): de re is belief about a particular thing, de dicto is belief that a proposition is true. Dennett says the supposed distinction is unstable, with an incoherent picture behind it, and often psychologically inert (pp. 182–183, 204).
- [[referential-opacity]] — contexts where terms do not play their normal denoting role and cannot be bound by quantifiers. Quine uses it as a quarantine; Dennett finds clear evidence of "impure referentiality" in his own examples (pp. 174–175, 185).
- [[propositional-attitude]] — here the broad state attributed by fitting a subject into the real world, in contrast to notional attitudes (p. 186).
- [[notional-world]] (new) — the world of the subject's notional attitudes, which may contain non-existent items (Santa). It lets psychological states be characterised indirectly through descriptions of a "world" (pp. 182, 204, 210).
- [[notional-attitude]] (new) — the narrow-psychological state. It is matched to propositional attitudes by a search for best fit, and may fail to determine any propositional attitude (p. 186).
- [[weak-aboutness]] (new) — relation of a belief to the unique satisfier of the description it uses; Dennett's minimal sense of "about" (p. 180).
- [[strong-aboutness-de-re]] (new) — the supposed intimate "about" via direct reference or causal history. Dennett rebaptises it original direct reference and finds it inert (pp. 191, 210).
- [[specific-vs-general-belief]] (new) — belief about one thing vs about all Fs; Dennett says it is independent of the relational/notional contrast (pp. 181, 187).
- [[russells-principle]] (new) — you cannot judge about an object without knowing which object it is. Dennett says it must be abandoned (pp. 200, 204).
- [[impure-referentiality]] (new) — opaque constructions still bear on things (sloop-wanting; believing *that* Cicero... entails believing *of* Cicero, in Dennett's view) (p. 185).
- [[semantic-information]] (new) — what information transfers, as opposed to its bit-measure. Propositions are our imprecise units for it (pp. 205–206).
- [[propositions-as-dollars]] (new) — propositions are a useful standard for measuring semantic information, with no natural, universal units (p. 208).
- [[inert-fact]] (new) — an idle truth about origins that cannot make a difference to behaviour or projectible properties (pp. 210–211).
- [[intentional-stance]] — Dennett's own defense of it relies on propositions but only as a heuristic overlay (p. 206).
- [[radical-interpretation]] (new) — reconstructing an agent's notional world from the facts about it. Illustrated by Martians (p. 209).

## Canonical instances
- [[ralph-spy-14-15]] (new) — Quine's pair showing relational vs notional styles, which Dennett says also differ in specific vs general content (p. 181).
- [[herb-sam-deportation]] (new) — a general belief jeopardizes a person via an attribute, not a set (pp. 177–179).
- [[tommy-santa-claus]] (new) — notional world containing a non-existent; some Santa attitudes determine no propositional attitude, while others yield one when the world obliges (pp. 182, 186–187).
- [[shortest-spy-rosa-klebb]] (new) — weak aboutness, identity conditions and Cambridge changes of beliefs (pp. 180, 192–193).
- [[amy-beth-penny]] (new) — Special Reference theory implies extra beliefs strongly about each coin (p. 195).
- [[hoover-george-poirot]] (new) — "minimal suspect": no principled line between minimal and de re suspects (pp. 197–198).
- [[dugongs-are-mammals]] (new) — vividness is a condition on all belief (pp. 188–189).
- [[martian-comatose-body]] (new) — radical interpretation of a notional world (p. 209).
- [[snakes-believed-slimy]] (new) — general belief as an ecological fact about snakes (p. 177).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] notional world as the world an agent takes itself to be in ↔ theory-of-mind/POMDP: an agent's belief state about the world — both describe an agent by what it takes the world to be like.
- [structural-analogy] Martians' hypothetical *gavagai?*-experiments to reconstruct a notional world ↔ inverse planning: inferring beliefs and desires by testing how behaviour would respond to counterfactual inputs.
- [structural-analogy] child with beliefs about Santa Claus (notional attitude, no proposition) ↔ false-belief tasks: attributing a belief whose content does not match reality, and explaining behaviour by it (p. 187).
- [metaphor] propositions as dollars ↔ construct validity in evaluation: a convenient standard for measuring something, not a natural unit of it.

## Questions this raises
- If strong aboutness only adds inert historical facts, what exactly would a notional-attitude theory have to say about how a creature keeps track of things over time (pp. 199–201)?
- How good is the Martian method for deciding when two notional worlds are equally well supported, and how much of a belief's content vanishes when they are (p. 209)?
- If propositions are only a measuring standard, does this change what belief attribution in the intentional stance predicts (p. 206)?
- Where exactly is the line between a "minimal suspect" and someone Hoover has a belief about, if Dennett's claim that there is no principled line holds up (p. 198)?
