# Evolution (Mitchell, ch. 5)

## L0 — One line
Darwinian natural selection, acting on small random variation in inherited traits, is the proposed way life gains organization and apparent design without a designer; its Mendelian and statistical foundations were built up in the Modern Synthesis, and that synthesis was later challenged by Gould and others (pdf 98–117).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The second law says the entropy of an isolated system increases, yet living systems seem to grow more organized. Who does the work? Mitchell traces the answer: early evolutionists (Buffon, Erasmus Darwin, Lamarck) preceded Darwin. Darwin and Wallace proposed natural selection. Mendel's discrete heredity first seemed to clash with Darwin. Fisher, Haldane and Wright then unified the two in population genetics, the "Modern Synthesis". From the 1960s, Gould, Eldredge and others challenged its three pillars: gradualism, the primacy of selection, and explaining macro-scale evolution by gene-level processes. Mayr and Dawkins defended it. Gould, Eldredge and virtually all biologists still accept basic Darwinism. Later chapters reuse these concepts.

**Prerequisites:** the second law of thermodynamics (entropy as disorder); basic probability (a 1-in-4 chance); the idea of inherited traits.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Life looks like a counterexample to rising entropy. On the Darwinian view Mitchell describes, natural selection does the "work", with energy from organisms metabolizing sunlight and food (pdf 98, 107).
- Lamarck's "inheritance of acquired characteristics" was widely rejected. Mendel's experiments disconfirmed it, and genetics later made it seem impossible (pdf 101–102, 108).
- Darwin's theory combines overproduction of offspring, random heritable variation, selection of adaptive variants and gradual change. Wallace and Matthew had similar ideas. Darwin got the credit mainly because of his more coherent argument and evidence (pdf 105–107).
- Mendel showed heredity works through discrete factors (roughly what we call genes), whose values are alleles, with dominant and recessive forms. This contradicted blending inheritance and gave quantitative predictions (pdf 109).
- The apparent clash between Darwinism (continuous variation) and Mendelism (discrete) was resolved by the 1920s: many genes per trait make continuous-looking physical traits (phenotypes). Fisher, Haldane and Wright showed the two theories compatible and gave a mathematical framework, population genetics; this unification, together with that framework, was later called the Modern Synthesis (pdf 110–111).
- Random genetic drift is chance change in allele frequency. It is stronger in small populations. Fisher and Wright disagreed bitterly over its importance (pdf 111–112).
- The Synthesis rested on three pillars: selection as the major mechanism; gradual change on small random variation; macro-phenomena explained by micro-level processes (pdf 112).
- Gould, Eldredge and others challenged the three pillars: punctuated equilibria (long stasis, then short bursts of change), contingency and constraints (Gould), selection at higher levels. Mayr and Dawkins defended the Synthesis. Gould, Eldredge and virtually all biologists keep core Darwinism (pdf 113–117).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: entropy and life (pdf 98–99)
- The second law: the total entropy of an isolated system always increases until it reaches its maximum (pdf 98). Mitchell says this gloomy picture runs through art, literature and religion (pdf 98).
- Life is a "counterexample". Living systems are complex, in the middle ground between order and disorder, and (by our intuitions) have become more intricate over time (pdf 98).
- Decreasing entropy requires work. Who does it? Some religions say a deity. Darwin proposed the "invisible hand" of natural selection (pdf 99).
- Mitchell quotes Dennett calling it the best idea anyone has had. Chapter 18 will describe how some of these concepts are being modified (pdf 99).

### Pre-Darwinian notions of evolution (pdf 99–102)
- Evolution means "gradual change". Until the eighteenth century, the prevailing view in the West was that species were created and stayed largely unchanged (pdf 99–100).
- Buffon (mid-1700s) compared species, said the earth is much older than the Biblical 6,000 years, and suggested all organisms descend from one ancestor, with no mechanism. The Catholic Church in France burned his books (pdf 100).
- Erasmus Darwin also believed in descent from a single ancestor and proposed mechanisms that were precursors of natural selection (pdf 100).
- Lamarck (1809): new organisms arise spontaneously from inanimate matter and evolve by inheriting acquired characteristics. Example: wading birds stretch their legs, the longer legs are passed on, and legs lengthen over generations (pdf 100–101).
- Lamarck also asserted a "tendency to progression" toward more "advanced" forms, with humans at the top (pdf 101).
- Almost all contemporaries rejected his ideas, including evolutionists, who were unconvinced by his examples; his empirical data were weak (pdf 101).
- It seems Darwin was, at least at first, favorably impressed, and he also believed that inheritance of acquired characteristics was one mechanism in addition to natural selection. That belief did not survive in "Darwinism" (pdf 102).
- Genetics made such inheritance seem almost certainly impossible. By about 1900 Lamarck's theories were no longer taken seriously in evolutionary biology (pdf 102). Freud held a Lamarckian view of instinct (pdf 102).

### Origins of Darwin's theory (pdf 102–107)
- Darwin sailed on the H.M.S. *Beagle* (1831–1836) as naturalist and the captain's dining companion, collecting specimens and keeping notebooks (pdf 103).
- Four influences fed his thinking (pdf 103–104):
  - Lyell: gradual processes can produce large effects over long periods.
  - Malthus: population growth leads to competition for resources, which Darwin generalized to a "struggle for existence".
  - Adam Smith: self-interested individuals can produce benefit for the whole community (the "invisible hand").
  - His own observations: wide variation and apparent adaptation.
- Galápagos finches: species otherwise similar differ in beak size and shape. Darwin showed they had common ancestors who had evidently migrated to different islands, and that beak type fits each species' food source. Darwin hypothesized that geographical isolation plus local conditions led to the evolution of these finch species from a small number of ancestors (pdf 104).
- The theory as it came together: more offspring are born than limited food can support. Offspring differ randomly in small ways from their parents. Traits that help survival and reproduction spread. Over very long times, new species form with traits adapted to their environments. Darwin called this "natural selection" (pdf 105).
- He delayed publishing, wanting more data and fearing to distress religious people, including his wife. In 1858 Wallace sent him a manuscript with essentially the same ideas. The two papers were read together to the Linnean Society, and the *Origin of Species* appeared by the end of 1859 (pdf 105–106).
- Patrick Matthew had published something similar twenty-eight years before the *Origin* (so about 1831, by our arithmetic) in an appendix to an obscure book. Darwin acknowledged him in 1860 (pdf 106).
- Mitchell concludes the idea was "in the air". Darwin gets the credit mainly because his book was more coherent and gave a great deal of evidence. He turned natural selection from plausible speculation into a well-supported theory (pdf 106).
- **Darwin's theory, summarized by Mitchell (pdf 106–107):**
  1. All species descend from a common ancestor; life is a branching tree.
  2. Natural selection occurs when births exceed what resources support, so individuals compete.
  3. Traits are inherited with variation. The variation is "in some sense random", with no force or bias toward higher fitness. Darwin himself accepted Lamarck's view that such forces exist.
  4. Variations adaptive in the current environment are likely to be selected, so those traits increase over generations.
  5. Change is constant and gradual, through accumulation of small, favorable variations.
- On this view, the result is the appearance of "design" without a designer, from chance, selection and long times. Entropy decreases (more organization) through the work done by natural selection. The energy comes from organisms metabolizing energy from sunlight and food (pdf 107).

*Intuition note:* In the Darwinian picture, order can grow because organisms keep taking in energy. Each generation, selection keeps the variants that do well. Over long times this builds structures that look designed.

### Mendel and the mechanism of heredity (pdf 107–109)
- Darwin's theory did not explain how traits pass from parent to offspring or where variation comes from. DNA as the carrier was not discovered until the 1940s. Mendel's work was "rediscovered" in 1900 (pdf 107).
- Mendel studied pea plants over eight years, partly to test Lamarck's claims. Each trait he examined (seed smoothness, pod color, stem height and others) had two distinct forms (pdf 108).
- Finding 1: offspring did not take on traits acquired by parents. Lamarckian inheritance did not occur (pdf 108).
- Finding 2: heredity works through discrete "factors", one contributed by each parent per trait. These roughly correspond to genes, so the medium of inheritance seemed discrete, not continuous (pdf 109).
- Terms (modern terminology, pdf 109):
  - Each plant has a pair of genes per trait.
  - An **allele** is the "value" a gene encodes (e.g., tall or dwarf).
  - For each trait, one allele is **dominant** and the other **recessive** (for stem height: tall is dominant, dwarf is recessive). Only dwarf/dwarf plants are dwarf; tall/tall and tall/dwarf plants are tall.
- Example: two tall/dwarf plants cross-pollinate. Both parents are tall, but there is a 25% chance that both pass on the dwarf gene, giving a dwarf/dwarf child (pdf 109).
- Mendel used probability to predict successfully how many plants show the dominant or recessive form. His results contradicted "blending inheritance". He did not know what the factors were made of (pdf 109).
- His 1865 paper was published in an obscure journal and was not seen as important until 1900 (pdf 109–110).

*Intuition note:* Inheritance behaves like passing on separate cards, not mixing paints. Together with dominance, this is why a recessive trait can vanish in one generation and return in the next (two tall parents can have a dwarf child).

### The Modern Synthesis (pdf 110–113)
- Mendel's discreteness seemed to oppose Darwin's continuous variation. Many early Mendelians held to mutation theory: variation comes from mutations, possibly very large, which themselves drive evolution, with selection only a secondary mechanism. Darwin's side held that variations must be very small and gradual. Darwin's phrase: "Natura non facit saltum" (pdf 110).
- By the 1920s the opposition was shown to be false. Most traits are determined by many genes with several alleles each. Discrete genetic variation can give continuous-seeming variation in the phenotype (the physical traits). Darwinism and Mendelism were complementary (pdf 110).
- Neither side had the conceptual framework (many genes per trait) or the mathematics. The tools came in the 1920s–30s, largely from Fisher. Fisher, Haldane and Wright showed the two theories compatible and gave **population genetics**, the mathematics of allele dynamics under Mendelian inheritance and selection. This unification, together with the population-genetics framework, was later called the "Modern Synthesis" (pdf 111).
- **Random genetic drift** (pdf 111). Certain alleles can become "dominant" (that is, common) in a population merely by chance.
  - Example: neither tall nor dwarf alleles affect fitness, but by chance the dwarf allele is in more plants. If each plant has about the same number of offspring, dwarf will likely be more frequent next generation.
  - With no selective advantage, one trait will eventually reach 100% of the population.
  - Drift is stronger in small populations, since in large ones small fluctuations tend to cancel.
- Wright thought drift played a significant role in evolutionary change and the origin of species. In Fisher's view, drift played at best an insignificant role. The dispute became bitter, and communication ended by 1934 (pdf 111–112).
- **Principles of the Synthesis**, almost universally accepted by biologists for about fifty years (pdf 112):
  1. Natural selection is the major mechanism of change and adaptation.
  2. Evolution is gradual, through selection on very small random variations, which are not biased toward "improvement". The sources are random mutations and recombinations.
  3. Macro phenomena, such as new species, can be explained by gene variation and selection.
- Its architects believed they had solved the main problems, though the molecular basis of genes and the source of variation were unknown. Tattersall: it ended decades of infighting (pdf 112–113).

### Challenges to the Modern Synthesis (pdf 113–117)
- **Gradualism.** From the 1960s–70s, paleontologists Gould and Eldredge said the fossil record shows long periods of no morphological change, punctuated by relatively short periods of large change that produce new species. This is **punctuated equilibria**. Defenders of the Synthesis said the fossil record was too incomplete to support such an inference. Mitchell says the pattern has also been widely observed in laboratory evolution experiments and simplified simulations (pdf 113).
- **Primacy of selection.** Gould held that historical contingency and biological constraints are at least as important as selection (pdf 115).
  - *Contingency*: random accidents large and small (e.g., meteor impacts causing extinctions).
  - Gould's "tape of life": restart it with slightly different initial conditions. The Synthesis would presumably answer that similar organisms would appear. Gould says contingency would make the replay much different (pdf 115).
  - *Constraints*: limits on what selection can create. Physical constraints rule out, for example, a creature that defies gravity. Gould and many others argue biological constraints exist too (pdf 115).
  - So not all traits need be adaptations; some may be accidents or side effects. Gould criticized "strict adaptationists" (pdf 115).
- **Micro explains macro.** Gould's group proposed that some large-scale phenomena require selection at levels above genes and individuals, perhaps whole species (pdf 116).
- **Molecular evidence** (pdf 116):
  - Kimura (1960s): a theory of "neutral evolution", based on protein evolution, challenging the central role of selection.
  - Eigen and Schuster (1970s): behavior analogous to punctuated equilibria in RNA viruses. In their theory the unit of evolution is a collective of mutated copies, a "quasi-species", not one virus.
- **The debate** (pdf 116): Gould (1980) wrote that the synthetic theory is "effectively dead, despite its persistence as textbook orthodoxy". Eldredge and Tattersall contended that the view of evolution due to the Modern Synthesis "is one of the greatest myths of twentieth-century biology". Mayr wrote that in his opinion nothing is seriously wrong with the Synthesis and it does not need to be replaced. Dawkins said cumulative selection is the only known theory capable in principle of explaining organized complexity. Mitchell says skepticism toward gradual selection as the major, if not the only, force is increasing (chapter 18).
- **Common ground** (pdf 116–117): Gould, Eldredge and virtually all biologists accept that evolution has occurred over four billion years and continues, that all species come from one ancestor, that selection has been important, and that no "intelligent" force directs evolution.

*Intuition note:* The dispute is about how much selection explains, and about the pace and the level at which it acts. It is not about whether evolution happened.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Selection (main idea).** A population has 100 individuals; 10% carry variant A. Suppose, for illustration, that each A-carrier leaves 2 offspring and each other individual leaves 1, and A is inherited.
- Generation 1 offspring: A: 10 × 2 = 20; others: 90 × 1 = 90; total 110. Fraction A = 20/110 ≈ 0.182.
- Scale to the same proportions: A = 0.182, others = 0.818. Generation 2 weights: 0.182 × 2 = 0.364 and 0.818 × 1 = 0.818, total 1.182. Fraction A = 0.364/1.182 ≈ 0.308.
- So A rises 0.10 → 0.18 → 0.31 with no designer, only differential reproduction.

**Mendel cross (text's number).** Cross tall/dwarf × tall/dwarf. Each parent passes tall (T) or dwarf (d) with chance 1/2. The four equally likely combinations are T/T, T/d, d/T, d/d. Only d/d is dwarf: 1/4 = 25%, matching the text (pdf 109).

## Concepts
- [[natural-selection]] (new) — survival and reproduction advantages spread heritable variants when births exceed resources (pdf 105–107)
- [[lamarckian-inheritance]] (new) — acquired characteristics passed to offspring; rejected by almost all contemporaries, later disconfirmed by Mendel and judged almost certainly impossible after genetics (pdf 100–102, 108)
- [[mendelian-inheritance]] (new) — discrete factors (roughly genes), one per parent per trait, whose values are alleles, dominant or recessive (pdf 109)
- [[modern-synthesis]] (new) — Darwinism plus Mendelism plus population genetics, with three pillars (pdf 111–112)
- [[random-genetic-drift]] (new) — chance change in allele frequency, stronger in small populations (pdf 111)
- [[punctuated-equilibria]] (new) — long stasis broken by short bursts of change and new species (pdf 113)
- [[historical-contingency]] (new) — random accidents shaping life, so a replay could differ. Related to [[path dependence]], but Mitchell does not speak of "lock-in" (pdf 115)
- [[biological-constraints]] (new) — limits, beyond physical law, on what selection can create (pdf 115)
- [[emergence]] — only loosely: the Synthesis's third pillar says macro-level phenomena follow from micro-level processes, and the challengers deny this in part (pdf 112, 116)
