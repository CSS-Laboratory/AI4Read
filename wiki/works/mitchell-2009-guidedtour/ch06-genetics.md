# Genetics, Simplified

## L0 — One line
A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a protein, and the cell machinery that copies and decodes DNA is itself encoded in that DNA.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Mitchell gives background genetics for later chapters. Question: how do inheritance and variation work physically? Answer: chromosomes carry DNA. DNA is a string of four bases (A, C, G, T) that pair A–T and C–G. A gene is a DNA substring coding for a protein. Cells make proteins in two steps: transcription (DNA copied into messenger RNA, or mRNA) and translation (mRNA used to build a protein). Pairing also lets DNA copy itself. Variation comes from copying errors, external mutations and sexual recombination (mixing of the parents' chromosomes). The machinery doing all this is itself coded in DNA, a self-reference. This basic picture was worked out by the mid-1960s. Mitchell says chapter 18 will show the real picture is more complicated.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school biology (cells, nucleus, proteins).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Chromosomes in the nucleus carry heredity. Sutton proposed this in 1902, and Morgan's fruit-fly experiments verified it. (pdf 119)
- DNA, not protein, was shown to be the material that genes are made of by the mid-1940s. (pdf 119)
- Watson and Crick found the double helix in 1953; the genetic code was broken in the early 1960s. A gene could then be defined molecularly as a DNA substring coding for a protein. (pdf 119)
- Code: each amino acid corresponds to a triple of bases (a codon). The code is the same in almost every organism. (pdf 120)
- Protein synthesis has two steps: transcription in the nucleus, then translation at the ribosome (a cell structure that reads mRNA) using tRNA (transfer RNA, which carries an anticodon and its amino acid). (pdf 121–123)
- Base pairing is also the key to DNA replication. Errors occur about once per 100 billion nucleotides and cause mutations. (pdf 123–124)
- Self-reference: the decoding machinery is encoded in the DNA it decodes. (pdf 124)

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pdf 118).** Some challenges to the Modern Synthesis have found support in molecular-biology results. Chapter 18 covers them; this chapter is background and can be skipped by readers who know genetics.

**Chromosomes and cell division (pdf 118–119).**
- All organisms consist of cells. The nucleus holds chromosomes, long molecules named "colored bodies" because they stain easily. (pdf 118)
- In mitosis a cell divides into two identical cells, and the chromosomes copy themselves. It serves growth, repair and maintenance. (pdf 118)
- Meiosis makes eggs and sperm (germ cells) in diploid organisms, where chromosomes come in pairs (23 pairs in humans). One diploid cell becomes four germ cells, each with half the chromosomes. The original pairs are cut and recombined. Fertilization fuses two germ cells to restore the pairs. (pdf 118)
- Result: a child's genes are a mixed-up version of its parents' chromosomes. This is a major source of variation in sexual reproduction. (pdf 119)

*Intuition note:* Mitosis copies a cell exactly. Meiosis shuffles and halves the chromosomes, so a child's genes are a mixed-up version of its parents' (a major source of variation in sexual reproduction).

**Historical steps (pdf 119–120).**
- Sutton (1902) proposed chromosomes are made of "genes" matching Mendelian factors, and showed that meiosis gives a mechanism for Mendelian inheritance. Morgan verified this a few years later. (pdf 119)
- Chemists knew of RNA and DNA by the late 1920s. Some suspected DNA was the substrate of genes, others suspected nuclear proteins. DNA was shown right by the mid-1940s. (pdf 119)
- Open questions then: how DNA causes traits, how it copies itself, and how variation arises at the DNA level. These were answered, at least in part, within about ten years. (pdf 119)
- Double helix, 1953. Genetic code broken in the early 1960s. Then came translation, DNA copying, and variation via copying errors, external mutations and recombination. Mitchell calls this a "tipping point" in genetics. (pdf 119–120)

**The mechanics of DNA (pdf 120).**
- The phenotype (all physical traits) arises largely from proteins and their interactions. Proteins are chains of amino acids. (pdf 120)
- DNA is a string of nucleotides, each containing one of four bases: A, C, G, T. In humans DNA is double-stranded, with A pairing with T and C with G (base pairs), as in TCCGATT… over AGGCTAA…. The strands form a double helix (fig. 6.1). (pdf 120–121)
- A gene is, roughly, a subsequence of DNA coding for one protein, via the amino acids that make it up. Each amino acid corresponds to a triple of bases, a codon. The author's examples: AAG for phenylalanine, CAC for valine. (pdf 120)

**Transcription (pdf 121–122, fig. 6.2).**
- In the nucleus, the enzyme RNA polymerase unwinds part of the DNA. It builds a messenger RNA (mRNA) from one DNA strand. (pdf 121)
- The mRNA is an "anticopy": C in the gene gives G, A gives U (RNA's version of T). The original can be reconstructed from it. This continues until the gene is fully transcribed. (pdf 121–122)

**Translation (pdf 122–123, fig. 6.3).**
- The mRNA moves to the cytoplasm. A ribosome reads it one codon at a time. Each codon meets a matching anticodon (complementary bases) on a transfer RNA (tRNA). Example: mRNA codon UAG, anticodon AUC. (pdf 122)
- Each tRNA carries its anticodon and the matching amino acid. Hofstadter calls tRNA "the cell's flash cards." (pdf 122)
- The ribosome detaches the amino acids and links them into a protein. A stop-codon signals it to release the protein. (pdf 123)
- Transcription plus translation is the gene's *expression*. (pdf 123)

*Intuition note:* The cell reads DNA in three-letter words and turns each word into one building block of a protein. Pairing rules do the matching.

**Energy (pdf 123).** This happens constantly at thousands of sites in each of trillions of cells, yet, as Mitchell says, while you sit watching TV all this subcellular activity burns fewer than 100 calories per hour. The processes are partly fuelled by random molecular motion, powered by the "ambient heat bath." (pdf 123)

**Replication and mutation (pdf 123–124).**
- Before mitosis, enzymes unwind and separate the DNA strands. For each strand, other enzymes add new nucleotides (A to T, C to G). Each strand becomes a new two-stranded DNA, and each daughter cell gets one. Many cell processes keep this on track. (pdf 123)
- About once per 100 billion nucleotides an error (e.g., a wrong base) occurs, causing a mutation. (pdf 124)

**Self-reference (pdf 124).** The mRNA, tRNA, ribosomes and polymerases that transcribe, translate and replicate DNA are themselves coded in DNA. So is the machinery that synthesizes nucleotides. Hofstadter: "The DNA contains coded versions of its own decoders!" Mitchell says this self-referential circularity would no doubt have pleased Turing, had he lived to see it explained. (pdf 124)

*Intuition note:* The instructions contain the recipe for the readers of the instructions. Mitchell calls this a self-referential circularity.

**Closing (pdf 124).** The basics were understood by the mid-1960s. Nobel prizes: 1962 (Crick, Watson, Wilkins, DNA structure) and 1968 (Korana, Holley, Nirenberg, the genetic code). The mysteries seemed mostly solved, but chapter 18 shows it is more complicated than anyone thought. (pdf 124)

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Use the text's strands: top TCCGATT, bottom AGGCTAA.
- **Transcription** of the top strand (T→A, C→G, G→C, A→U): T→A, C→G, C→G, G→C, A→U, T→A, T→A gives mRNA **AGGCUAA**.
- **Reconstruction** (reverse each letter: A→T, G→C, C→G, U→A): A→T, G→C, G→C, C→G, U→A, A→T, A→T gives TCCGATT, the original. This matches the text's claim that an anticopy lets us rebuild the original.
- **Codons:** the first six letters AGGCUA split into AGG and CUA. Their anticodons are UCC and GAU.
- **Replication:** separate the strands. Top TCCGATT gets new partner AGGCTAA; bottom AGGCTAA gets new partner TCCGATT. Two identical double strands result.
- **Mutation:** if the new partner of the top strand had a wrong base in position 3 (say A instead of G), the copy would differ from its parent.
