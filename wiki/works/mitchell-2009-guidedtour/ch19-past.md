# The Past and Future of the Sciences of Complexity

## L0 — One line
Mitchell thinks no single set of useful principles will cover all complex systems; modest "common principles" might arise through a feedback cycle with studies of specific systems; and the field still lacks the right vocabulary and mathematics.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Horgan argued in 1995 that complexity science is unlikely to find useful general principles. Mitchell thinks no single set of useful principles will cover all complex systems, but modest "common principles" might arise in a feedback cycle with studies of specific systems. She reviews earlier efforts: cybernetics (1940s science of control and communication), general system theory (a 1950s search for principles valid for all systems), and physics-based work by Prigogine and Haken. She reports mixed views among researchers. She says the field lacks the right vocabulary and mathematics; it is "waiting for Carnot", a physicist who originated key thermodynamics concepts. She thinks the task needs more a Newton than a Carnot. The chapter takes stock of the field.

**Prerequisites:** the earlier chapters' topics by name (logistic map, chaos, emergence, self-organization, feedback, scaling, networks); no math needed.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Horgan (1995) made two main criticisms: complexity is unlikely to yield useful general principles, and computer modeling makes it a "fact-free science" (p. 167).
- Mitchell argues that even a correct physics "unified theory" (one theory of the basic forces) would be far from the end of complex systems science. Elementary particles would still be the wrong vocabulary for explaining complexity (p. 169).
- Mitchell suggests it might be better to scale back from "general" to "common" principles. These might be part of a feedback cycle: knowledge of specific systems is synthesized into them, and they give new ideas back (p. 171).
- Cybernetics (1940s control-and-communication science) and general system theory (a 1950s search for principles valid for all systems) also sought common principles. Cybernetics drew both enthusiasm and disparagement. Attempts to build a rigorous mathematical framework were not generally successful for either, yet several modern fields grew from the questions they posed (pp. 172–175).
- In the fourteen survey responses Mitchell saw, most doubt "universal laws" and see defining complexity as a problem. Most are still enthusiastic about the field (pp. 176–177).
- Mitchell says perhaps the most significant contributions so far are questioning long-held assumptions and new ways of conceptualizing complex problems. She also describes extended concepts and an emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration (pp. 177–178).
- Mitchell calls the lack of the right vocabulary the crux of the problem: terms like emergence are ill-defined. She says the task needs more a Newton than a Carnot and asks whether a "calculus of complexity" can be invented; she notes it is unclear whether a unifying theory exists (pp. 179–181).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: Horgan's attack (pp. 167–168)
- In 1995 John Horgan wrote a *Scientific American* article attacking complex systems and the Santa Fe Institute. The cover asked whether complexity is a sham (p. 167).
- His two main criticisms: useful general principles are unlikely, and heavy computer modeling makes complexity a "fact-free science." He also called it "pop science", called researchers "complexologists", and speculated that the word has little meaning but is kept for its "public-relations value" (p. 167).
- He quoted Mitchell: all complex systems are aspects of the same principles "at some level", but she doesn't think that will be very useful. She says he chose her most negative comment from an interview of an hour or more (p. 167).
- Her letter was not published. The episode made her think harder about what "general principles" might mean (pp. 167–168).
- Horgan's article grew into the book *The End of Science*, which claims the really important discoveries have all been made. The expanded chapter predicts that chaos, complexity and artificial life will not reach insights comparable to Darwin's theory or quantum mechanics (p. 168).

### On Unified Theories and General Principles (pp. 169–172)
- A "unified theory" (GUT) usually means physics' goal of one theory of the basic forces. String theory is one attempt, and there is no consensus that it works or that a GUT exists (p. 169).
- **Mitchell's argument:** even if string theory were correct, it would not end complex systems science. Particles and strings are "the wrong vocabulary" for complexity (p. 169).
- Analogy: answering the question why the logistic map is chaotic by just writing down its equation $x_{t+1} = R\,x_t(1-x_t)$. The explanation is "in some sense" embedded in the equation, but not in the sense of human understanding (p. 169).
- Crutchfield, Farmer, Packard and Shaw: interaction of components at one scale can lead to global behavior at a larger scale that in general cannot be deduced from the components (p. 169).
- Most complex systems researchers would probably say a unified theory is not a meaningful goal at this point (pp. 169–170). Physics has mass and energy (unified by $E = mc^2$) and four forces, at least three unified. Complex systems science does not yet know its elemental "stuff" or "force" (p. 170).
- **Gordon's view** (ecologist Deborah Gordon): talk of complexity, self-organization and emergence offers "smoke and mirrors", giving names to what we can't explain. A general theory may be possible, but we have none yet. She advises studying specific systems to see whether general laws exist. Hoping for general principles can lead us to ignore what doesn't fit a model (p. 170).
- **Mitchell agrees** that general principles such as the claim that every complex system exhibits emergent properties are not very useful, because (as Gordon says) they only give names to what we can't explain. She thinks Gordon is correct in her implication that no single set of useful principles will apply to all complex systems (pp. 170–171).
- She suggests it might be better to talk of *common* principles instead. They give new insight into, or new conceptualizations of, a set of systems that would be hard to get by studying them separately and drawing analogies afterward (p. 171).
- Feedback cycle: finding common principles might be part of a cycle in which knowledge about specific systems is synthesized into common principles. These then provide new ideas for understanding the specific systems, and the two inform, constrain and enrich each other (p. 171).
- Proposals already seen in the book (p. 171):
  - universal properties of chaotic systems;
  - von Neumann's principles of self-reproduction;
  - Holland's balance of exploitation and exploration;
  - Axelrod's general conditions for the evolution of cooperation;
  - Wolfram's principle of computational equivalence;
  - Barabási and Albert's preferential attachment;
  - West, Brown and Enquist's fractal circulation networks for scaling.
- She believes her chapter 12 principles of adaptive information processing might be useful to people studying specific systems; they include "randomness and probabilities are essential." After a lecture, a neuroscientist speculated where randomness might come from in the brain and what it is for. Some listeners had not thought of the brain that way (p. 171).
- Feedback also runs from specific to general. Audience members named complex adaptive systems that they believed did not follow all her principles. This made her question the generality of her claims (p. 172).
- Facts about nature are sometimes found wrong too, and common principles might help direct skepticism. Einstein supposedly quipped "change the facts" if they don't fit the theory; Mitchell says that depends on the theory and the facts. The more established a theory, the more skeptical one should be of contradicting facts. The more convincing the contradicting facts, the more skeptical one should be of the principles. Science is "an endless cycle of proud proposing and disdainful doubting" (p. 172).

*Intuition note:* Mitchell doubts that one set of useful principles covers every complex system. She suggests a smaller goal: common principles that might help people studying particular systems. Those people can also test the principles by pointing out exceptions.

### Roots of Complex Systems Research (pp. 172–176)
- The search for common principles is old, especially in physics. It became most prominent after computers were invented. By the 1940s some scientists proposed strong analogies between computers and organisms (p. 172).
- **Cybernetics.** The Macy Foundation sponsored interdisciplinary meetings in the 1940s, with titles about feedback, circular causal systems and teleological (purpose-based) mechanisms in biological and social systems (p. 172).
  - Norbert Wiener was a prime mover. His anti-aircraft control work convinced him that the science of complex systems should focus on feedback, control, information, communication and purpose ("teleology"), not mass, energy and force (pp. 172–173).
  - Participants included von Neumann, McCulloch, Mead, Bateson, Shannon and Ashby (p. 173).
  - Wiener named the field cybernetics, from the Greek for "steersman". He summed up cybernetics as the whole field of control and communication theory, in machines or in animals (p. 173).
  - Questions asked: what are information and computation? What analogies link organisms and machines? What does feedback do? How do meaning and purpose arise from information processing? (p. 173)
  - Lasting work (pp. 173–174):
    - von Neumann's self-reproducing automaton;
    - Ashby's *Design for a Brain*;
    - McCulloch and Pitts' neurons as logic devices, an impetus for later neural networks;
    - Mead and Bateson's cybernetic ideas in psychology and anthropology;
    - Wiener's *Cybernetics* and *The Human Use of Human Beings*.
  - Reception was split (p. 174). Bateson ranked the discovery of cybernetics with the Treaty of Versailles among the two most important events of his life. Delbrück found the meeting he attended empty and inane; Savage likened one later meeting to bull sessions among a very elite group.
  - Historian William Aspray writes that in the end Wiener's hope for a unified science was not fulfilled. Quoting a participant, he says cybernetics had more extent than content; it ranged over too many subjects, and its theory was too meager and cumbersome (p. 174).
- **General System Theory** (1950s, Ludwig von Bertalanffy) aimed at principles valid for "systems" in general. A system here is any collection of interacting elements that produce system-wide behavior, which Mitchell says can describe just about anything (p. 174).
  - Rapoport's main themes: preservation of identity amid change, organized complexity, goal-directedness (p. 175).
  - Maturana and Varela tried to make sense of the first two themes with *autopoiesis*: a self-maintaining process by which a system, such as a cell, continually produces the components that make up the system (p. 175).
- Like cybernetics, general system theory is very appealing, but attempts to construct a rigorous mathematical framework that explains and predicts common properties were not generally successful. The central questions of these efforts seeded AI, artificial life, systems ecology, systems biology, neural networks, systems analysis, control theory and the sciences of complexity. Cybernetics and general system theory are still active in some quarters but largely overshadowed by these offspring disciplines (p. 175).
- **Physics-based efforts** (p. 175): Haken's Synergetics and Prigogine's dissipative structures and nonequilibrium theories attempt to integrate thermodynamics, dynamical systems theory and critical phenomena to explain self-organization in physical and biological systems.
  - Prigogine and Nicolis proposed a vocabulary: nonequilibrium, stability, bifurcation and symmetry breaking, long-range order (pp. 175–176).
  - These efforts have not yet produced the general vocabulary Prigogine envisioned, let alone a unifying general theory (p. 176).

*Intuition note:* Earlier attempts at unification produced appealing questions and useful offspring fields. Attempts at a rigorous mathematical framework that predicts were not generally successful. A few survey respondents worry that today's field may share that fate.

### Five Questions (pp. 176–178)
- Modern complex systems science is still, like its forebears, not a unified whole but a collection of disparate parts with some overlapping concepts. It is unified by common questions, methods and a wish for rigorous mathematical and experimental work beyond the older analogies (p. 176).
- Carlos Gershenson sent five questions to colleagues, including Mitchell: why you began, how you define complexity, favorite concept, most problematic concept, the field's future (p. 176).
- Mitchell has seen fourteen responses (p. 176). Common opinions (pp. 176–177):
  - Most dismiss "universal laws" as too ambitious or too vague.
  - Most think defining complexity is among the most problematic aspects, and likely the wrong goal altogether.
  - Many think the word "complexity" is not meaningful.
  - Most do not believe there yet is a "science of complexity" in the usual sense.
  - A few worry the field will share cybernetics' fate: intriguing analogies without a rigorous predictive theory.
- Most respondents are nonetheless highly enthusiastic. Life, brain and social sciences keep showing more complexity as scientists look closer, and this needs new concepts and theories (p. 177). Several respondents voiced views similar to one who saw some form of complexity science taking over scientific thinking as a whole (p. 177).
- **Contributions Mitchell names** (perhaps the most significant being the questioning of assumptions and new ways of conceptualizing problems) (pp. 177–178):
  - Individual discoveries (Brown, Enquist and West on metabolic scaling; Axelrod on cooperation).
  - Questioning long-held assumptions. Chaos shows that intrinsic randomness is not necessary for random-looking behavior. Genetics findings challenged the role of gene change in evolution. Chance and self-organization challenged the centrality of natural selection.
  - Wider recognition of nonlinearity, decentralized control, networks, hierarchies, distributed feedback, statistical representations of information and essential randomness.
  - Broadened concepts: information and computation applied to living and social systems; adaptation and evolution beyond biology; life and intelligence perhaps extended to self-replicating machines and analogy-making programs.
  - Emphasis on interdisciplinary collaboration, seen as essential for progress on the most important problems.
- At SFI summer schools, students in the early 1990s were excited. By the early 2000s the ideas had permeated many disciplines and students were "blasé", sometimes disappointed it seemed "mainstream". Mitchell supposes this should be counted as a success (p. 178).

### The Future of Complexity, or Waiting for Carnot (pp. 178–182)
- Mitchell sees two branches (pp. 178–179):
  - refine and apply complexity tools in specific areas (physics, biology, epidemiology, sociology, political science, computer science; also neuroscience, economics, ecology, climatology, medicine);
  - the more controversial branch: view the fields from a higher level and seek rigorous explanatory and predictive theories of commonalities and emergent phenomena.
- At a meeting a participant said "complexity" is now a cliché and "we should start over" (p. 179).
- The crux: we lack vocabulary. Terms like complexity, self-organization and emergence name shared phenomena but do not characterize them rigorously. New, better-defined terms are needed, including for how functionality, purpose or meaning arise (p. 179).
- Much of the work integrates dynamics, information, computation and evolution, so a new vocabulary and a new kind of mathematics must be forged from that integration (p. 179).
- Steven Strogatz: we may be missing the conceptual equivalent of calculus. An "ultracalculus", if handed to us, could be forever beyond human comprehension; he says we just don't know (p. 179).
- The right concepts and mathematics are needed to understand, predict and sometimes direct self-organizing systems. Developing them is the field's greatest challenge (pp. 179–180).
- **"Waiting for Carnot":** Sadi Carnot originated key concepts of thermodynamics. The field awaits comparable concepts and mathematics (p. 180).
- Mitchell says the task needs more a Newton than a Carnot. Newton faced a similar problem of vague language (Gleick's description). Calculus gave a lexicon for change and motion: infinitesimal, derivative, integral, limit. These concepts already existed in fragments, and Newton related them and unified them, which let him create the science of dynamics (pp. 180–181).
- Candidate attempts at a "calculus of complexity" (p. 181):
  - Wolfram: cellular-automata building blocks of dynamics and computation, which he thinks form a fundamental theory of nature;
  - Prigogine's followers: a small list of physical concepts;
  - Bak: self-organized criticality, presented as a general theory of self-organization and emergence;
  - Crutchfield: computational mechanics, integrating dynamical systems, computation theory and statistical inference.
- Each is still far from a comprehensive theory and remains active research. Mitchell notes it is still unclear whether such a theory exists; it may be that complexity arises by very different processes in different systems (p. 181).
- She has presented likely pieces of a theory, if one exists, in information, computation, dynamics and evolution. What is needed is to see their deep relationships, "the simplicity on the other side of complexity" (p. 181).
- Pursuing this requires an adventurous spirit and willingness to risk failure and reproach. She closes with André Gide: one doesn't discover new lands "without consenting to lose sight of the shore" (pp. 181–182).

*Intuition note:* Mitchell calls the lack of the right vocabulary the crux of the problem: words like complexity, self-organization and emergence are ill-defined. She compares today to the time before calculus, when the needed ideas existed in pieces. She says it is unclear whether a unifying theory exists at all.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
The text says the logistic map's behavior is "embedded" in its equation but is not understood just by quoting it (p. 169). Take $x_{t+1} = R\,x_t(1-x_t)$ with $R=4$ (our choice). Run two starts, $0.20$ and $0.21$:
- Start 0.20: $x_1 = 4(0.2)(0.8) = 0.64$; $x_2 = 4(0.64)(0.36) = 0.9216$; $x_3 = 4(0.9216)(0.0784) = 0.28901376$.
- Start 0.21: $x_1 = 4(0.21)(0.79) = 0.6636$; $x_2 = 4(0.6636)(0.3364) \approx 0.89294$; $x_3 = 4(0.89294)(0.10706) \approx 0.38239$.
- Gaps: $0.01 \to 0.0236 \to 0.0287 \to 0.0934$.

The equation fully fixes these numbers, yet "nearby starts drift apart" is a statement at another level. You see it only by comparing runs, not by re-reading the formula. This is the sense in which a lower-level law is not the explanation.

## Questions this raises
- What would count as a "common principle" being useful, and how would one test it against specific systems?
- Are the "building blocks" Mitchell lacks (the analogues of mass, energy and force) the integrated concepts of dynamics, information, computation and evolution?
- Why did cybernetics produce lasting offspring fields yet fail as a unified theory? Could modern complexity share that fate?
- What kind of mathematics could be a "calculus of complexity", and could it be beyond human comprehension (Strogatz)?
