# Prince, S. J. D. (2023). *Understanding Deep Learning*. MIT Press.

## In one paragraph
Deep neural networks are flexible fitted equations. The book builds them step by step: a parameterised model, a loss, and training by gradient descent. It then adds depth, convolutions, residual connections and transformers. Losses are presented as likelihoods, and training relies on backpropagation and careful initialization. The book then asks why some models generalize and how regularization helps. Later chapters use the same machinery for graphs, generative models (GANs, flows, VAEs, diffusion) and reinforcement learning. The last chapters admit that no theory predicts when deep learning works, and they treat ethics and value alignment as part of the subject. Two appendices cover the mathematics and probability the text relies on. For LLM readers, it supplies the background that transformer papers assume.

## The path through the work
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch01-introduction]] — Deep neural networks are flexible fitted equations that underlie the cutting-edge methods in supervised, unsupervised (generative) and reinforcement learning, so they deserve both technical study and ethical scrutiny.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch02-supervised]] — A supervised model is a parameterised function $y=f[x,\phi]$, trained by choosing the parameters $\phi$ that minimise a loss measuring the mismatch with training pairs, then checked on separate test data.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch03-shallow]] — A shallow network computes several linear functions of the input, clips each with an activation function, and sums them with weights. With ReLU activations this gives a piecewise linear function that, with enough hidden units, can approximate any continuous function.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch04-deep]] — A deep network (more than one hidden layer) is a ReLU-based piecewise linear function that can produce many more linear regions for a given number of parameters than a shallow one; two ways to picture this are that layers "fold" the input space or clip what the previous layer produced.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch05-loss]] — Treat the network as outputting the parameters of a probability distribution over the label, then train by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the training data. This yields least squares, binary cross-entropy and multiclass cross-entropy as special cases.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch06-fitting]] — Training a model means repeatedly moving its parameters downhill on the loss surface. Random minibatches (SGD) help against local minima and saddle points, and momentum and Adam make the progress more efficient.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch07-gradients]] — Backpropagation computes every parameter gradient efficiently by running the network forward once, storing the activations, and then sweeping backward while reusing earlier results. He initialization sets the weight variance (2 divided by the layer's input width, for ReLU networks) so that the forward-pass variance of the signals stays stable from layer to layer; for equal-width layers the gradient variance is stable too.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch08-measuring]] — Training error says little about generalization: test error is made of noise, bias and variance, it can behave unexpectedly as capacity grows (double descent), and hyperparameters should be chosen on a separate validation set rather than the test set.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch09-regularization]] — Generalization can be improved by adding a penalty to the loss, by the implicit biases of SGD, and by many heuristics (early stopping, ensembling, dropout, noise, transfer learning, augmentation) that smooth the function, add data, combine models or find wide minima.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch10-convolutional]] — A convolutional layer computes each hidden unit as the same weighted sum of nearby inputs at every position, which gives far fewer parameters and a useful translation-based inductive bias for images.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch11-residual]] — Letting each layer add a change to its input (a residual connection), plus batch normalization to tame the growing variance, makes much deeper networks trainable.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch12-transformers]] — A transformer lets the tokens in a sequence exchange information through dot-product self-attention (in an encoder, every token with every other token), which shares parameters across positions and makes the connection strengths depend on the content.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch13-graph]] — Because node order in a graph is arbitrary, each layer of a graph neural network must be permutation-equivariant; a layer that updates every node from its neighbours with shared parameters satisfies this, and the same network body serves graph-, node- and edge-level tasks.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch14-unsupervised]] — Unsupervised models learn from unlabelled data; generative models, especially probabilistic ones, are judged by desirable properties and by imperfect metrics such as test likelihood, inception score, Fréchet inception distance and manifold precision/recall.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch15-generative]] — A GAN trains a generator to turn noise into samples by using a discriminator's ability to tell real from fake as the learning signal. Training is difficult: the discriminator can become too good, and the learning can be unstable.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch16-normalizing]] — A normalizing flow turns a simple base density into a complex one with an invertible deep network, so it can both sample and compute exact likelihoods, at the price that every layer must be invertible and have a cheap Jacobian determinant.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch17-variational]] — A VAE is a neural network that computes a lower bound (the ELBO) on the log-likelihood of a nonlinear latent variable model, so that a model whose likelihood cannot be computed exactly can still be trained.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch18-diffusion]] — A diffusion model fixes a process that gradually turns data into white noise, then trains a network to undo it one small step at a time. The ELBO training objective reduces to predicting the added noise by least squares.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch19-reinforcement]] — Reinforcement learning chooses actions in a Markov decision process so as to maximize expected discounted return, either by learning values (Bellman consistency, Q-learning, deep Q-networks) or by directly following gradients of a stochastic policy (policy gradients, actor-critic); offline RL can instead be treated as supervised sequence prediction (decision transformer).
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch20-does]] — Deep networks are surprisingly easy to fit and surprisingly good at generalizing, and the evidence points to overparameterization, architecture and training details, but there is still no predictive theory.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch21-deep]] — Many AI ethics problems can be read as value-alignment problems (misspecified objectives, or information gaps between humans and systems); the authors also argue that ML research is value-laden, that responsible AI research is a collective action problem (good outcomes need cooperation), and that many social and ethical problems cannot be solved by technical innovation alone.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch22-mathematics]] — This appendix is a reference sheet of the function, linear-algebra and matrix-derivative tools that the deep-learning text relies on.
- [[prince-2023-udl/ch23-probability]] — Joint, marginal and conditional distributions, expectation rules, the normal distribution, sampling and distribution distances are the probability toolkit this appendix presents as a primer for machine learning, where the author says probability underlies losses, generative models and reinforcement learning.

## Big ideas across units
- **Learning as loss minimization.** A model is a parameterised function. A loss scores its fit, and training searches for the best parameters. This is set up in ch02, given a probabilistic form in ch05, run with SGD in ch06 and backpropagation in ch07, and reused as negative log-likelihood or the ELBO in ch14, ch16, ch17 and ch18.
- **Likelihood and distribution distances.** Common losses come from maximum likelihood. KL divergence recurs in ch05, ch17, ch18 and ch23. The Jensen–Shannon and Wasserstein distances appear in ch15 and ch23. Exact likelihood (ch16) and bounds on it (ch17, ch18) shape the generative models.
- **Capacity, depth and why depth helps.** Hidden units give piecewise linear regions (ch03). Depth multiplies them per parameter (ch04). Ch08 shows that more capacity does not simply mean worse test error (double descent). Ch10 and ch11 show depth helping and then needing residual connections. Ch20 says depth seems needed but has no definitive explanation.
- **Generalization and regularization.** The gap between training and test performance is introduced in ch02 and ch08. Ch09 gives explicit and implicit regularizers. Ch10's inductive bias, ch11's regularizing batch normalization and ch20's flat minima and weight norm continue the thread.
- **Making deep networks trainable.** Non-convex losses and saddle points (ch06), initialization and vanishing or exploding signals (ch07), shattered gradients and residual connections (ch11), and vanishing gradients in GANs (ch15) all concern keeping learning stable. Ch20 asks why training works at all.
- **Architecture as built-in assumptions.** Convolution shares weights across positions (ch10). Self-attention shares parameters and makes connections content-dependent (ch12). Graph layers are permutation-equivariant (ch13). Transformers for images need more data because they lack convolution's bias (ch12).
- **Families of generative models and their trade-offs.** Ch01 and ch14 set up the taxonomy and the evaluation metrics. GANs (ch15) give samples but no probabilities. Flows (ch16) give exact likelihood. VAEs (ch17) give a bound. Diffusion models (ch18) are simple to train but slow to sample.
- **Ethics and alignment.** Ch01 lists the concerns, ch19 mentions RLHF, and ch21 treats value alignment, bias, transparency and collective responsibility.

## How to read it
- **Essential for the LLM focus:** ch12 (Transformers), with its foundations in ch02, ch05 (loss as likelihood, softmax, next-word prediction), ch06–ch07 (fitting and backpropagation) and ch11 (residual connections and normalisation, which ch12 lists as prerequisites).
- **Foundations that build in order:** ch02 → ch03 → ch04, and ch05 → ch06 → ch07. Ch08 and ch09 depend on ch05 and the SGD material. Ch10 leads into ch11 (which needs He initialization from ch07).
- **Useful background:** ch01 for vocabulary. Ch08–ch09 for generalization. Ch20 for open questions. Appendices ch22 and ch23 can be consulted when a chapter's prerequisites call for them.
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch13 (graphs; needs ch10 and ch12), ch15, ch16 and ch21. Ch14–ch18 form a sequence, and ch18 expects the VAE and ELBO from ch17. Ch19 (reinforcement learning) is separate except for its section on the decision transformer, which needs transformer decoders (ch12), and its Notes linking RL to RLHF.
