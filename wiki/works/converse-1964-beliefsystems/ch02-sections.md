# Recognition of ideological dimensions and constraint among idea-elements

## L0 — One line
In the mass public, understanding of "liberal–conservative" is thin and attitudes on specific issues are weakly tied together, much more so than among elites. The ties between social position and vote also weaken as sophistication falls, and opinion leading does not appear to make up for this.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Converse asks whether ordinary voters grasp the abstract ideological terms that elites use, and whether their specific opinions hang together. Using 1956–1960 survey data, he finds that only about 17% understand "liberal–conservative" broadly. About 37% cannot give any meaning, and most of those in between hold a narrow "spend–save" reading. Issue attitudes are far less correlated in the mass sample than among 1958 congressional candidates, and elite pairings often fail to hold in the public. Class–vote links are stronger among the more sophisticated. The "opinion leader" defence gives little support to the claim that fragmented beliefs are irrelevant. Elite-based assumptions about "the public" are therefore misleading.

**Prerequisites:** Levels of conceptualization (from the earlier part of the chapter); correlation and ordinal association coefficients (gamma-type); reading cross-tabulations; the idea of a survey sample.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Converse draws on the psychological distinction between recognition, recall and habitual use of a concept. His earlier coding measured active use. In 1960 he tested recognition and meaning of "liberal/conservative" directly (pp. 18–19).
- Only about 17% of the sample gave a broad-philosophy meaning (stratum I). About 37% gave no meaning (strata IV–V), and about 46% fell between (strata III and II) (pp. 22, 24).
- Most narrow readings equate liberal–conservative with "spend–save" on government money. Such a narrow connotation gives little reason to use the continuum as an organizing frame (pp. 22–24).
- Sophistication goes with education and political activity. Rallies over-represent the sophisticated relative to the electorate, so the "visible public" is unrepresentative (pp. 25–29).
- Party loyalty is weak in the most sophisticated cell. Converse takes this as one small case of his hypothesis that group-objects are likely to be less central at the top, and as at least a partial explanation of the old belief that independents are informed (pp. 28–29).
- Constraint among specific issue beliefs is much higher among 1958 congressional candidates than in the cross-section. The mass public's strongest within-domain average (.23) is below the elite's domestic–foreign average (.25) (pp. 30–32).
- Elite assumptions about which beliefs go together often do not carry over to the mass. In the aid-to-education/internationalism example, assuming no connection would have been closer to reality for the public than assuming the elite pattern (pp. 33–34).
- In the total sample the class–vote correlation rises with level of conceptualization. Converse takes this to cast doubt on the claims that belief organization is epiphenomenal or that opinion leading transmits behavior without understanding (pp. 35–37).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### End of Section III: the 1952 Democratic defectors (p. 18)
- Converse's account of Democrats who voted for Eisenhower is that they defected despite their quasi-ideological perceptions, not because of them. Their attitudes toward group interests stayed constant, and they voiced misgivings about an Eisenhower vote on those grounds. Admiration for Eisenhower's war record, honesty, family life and (in 1952) his potential to end the Korean War outweighed these worries (p. 18).
- Ideologues and near-ideologues felt a similar personal attraction but seemed more careful to keep to ideological considerations. Their Democratic defection rates were lower still (p. 18).
- Conclusion: the idea of a changing ideological mood in the mass public seems to have had little relevance to the 1952 exchange of partisan elites. The "optical illusion" arises because educated, involved people may take for granted that a Democratic-to-Republican shift probably means a change from liberal to conservative, but the assumption cannot be extended far into the electorate (p. 18).

### IV. Recognition of ideological dimensions of judgment
**Why recognition matters.** Dimensions like liberal–conservative are efficient frames and are used in the more ambitious media treatments of politics. A person who barely understands them finds such discussions obscure (p. 18).
- Psychology distinguishes recognition, recall and habitual use. More concepts can be recognized when presented than can be recalled from indirect cues, and more can be recalled than are active unprompted (p. 18).
- The earlier coding of levels of conceptualization targeted only the last status, activation or salience. Presented with the terms directly, many more people would recognize them (pp. 18–19).

**The 1960 question sequence** (reinterview of the 1956 sample) (p. 19):
1. Is either party more conservative or more liberal than the other? This was the first time the sample was asked about these terms.
2. If yes: which party? Then an open question on what the respondent has in mind (the meaning question).
3. If "no difference": the respondent was asked what people generally consider. Converse wanted to separate the genuinely cynical from those hiding ignorance of the terms.
4. If the respondent guessed which party others consider more conservative, a parallel meaning question was asked about what people have in mind. Those who guessed did poorly on it.

**Coding of meanings.** Top priority went to broad philosophical differences, of four main types (p. 20):
- posture toward change;
- posture toward the welfare state, socialism, free enterprise or capitalism;
- posture toward the expanding power of the federal government;
- relationship of government to the individual.

Descending priorities followed: interest groupings; spending/saving, fiscal policy and prosperity; specific issues (unemployment compensation, highways, tariffs); and foreign policy. All later data use each respondent's "best answer" (p. 20).

**Distribution.**
- About 37% (three in eight) supplied no meaning. This includes 8% who tried to say which party was more conservative but gave up on meaning. The weakest 29% form stratum V and the 8% form stratum IV (p. 20).
- Using a label, a polar meaning and a party creates four possible patterns of association, and all four occurred. Reversing the labels is one error (Democrats "conservative" because they defend the working man). A wrong party perception is another (Republicans "liberal" for pushing progressive legislation) (pp. 20–21).

| Pattern (label–meaning–party) | % of those giving some answer |
|---|---|
| Conservative = Conservative meaning = Republican (and Liberal–Liberal–Democrat) — "proper" | 83 |
| Labels and meanings swapped, parties as usual | 5 |
| Meanings match labels, parties swapped | 6 |
| Both swapped | 6 |

(Table 2, p. 21; the answerers are slightly more than 60% of the sample.)

- The 83% is inflated by an unknown degree, because respondents whose connotations were too impoverished to judge (for example, defense spending) are counted as "proper" (pp. 21–22).
- Table 2 is not proof of pure guesswork, but it resembles the probable results if 20–25% of respondents were guessing randomly about how labels, meanings and parties go together (p. 22).
- Confused people may or may not feel confused. Either way, they are unlikely to be less confused when they meet such terms in political communication. Those who are wrong without realizing it could hear that Goldwater was an extreme conservative and take that to mean he favored more federal spending (p. 22).
- Setting aside the confused group (stratum III) leaves slightly more than 50%. Converse takes this as a **maximum** estimate of reasonable recognition (p. 22).
- Within this half, 17% of the whole sample gave "broad philosophy" best answers (stratum I). The rest, with narrower definitions, are stratum II (p. 22).

**The "spend–save" reading.**
- Very close to a majority of all best responses, and two-thirds to three-quarters of stratum II responses, said in essence that Democrats are liberal because they spend freely and Republicans conservative because they economize (pp. 22–23).
- "Conservative" often seemed to draw its meaning from "conservation". One respondent called Republicans conservative because they were thrifty with money and natural resources (p. 23).
- This is partly accurate matching, since the share of national wealth spent publicly has long been a key liberal–conservative dispute. Whether it shows understanding depends in no small measure on whether the person sees it as self-contained or links it to other broad questions (p. 23).
- Rare respondents link spend–save to other problems (e.g. wanting more spending to create jobs). When further links are suggested, they more frequently go only as far as policy (the interest-rate answer). Generally, the sample comments seem to show a narrow belief system (pp. 23–24).

**Summary portrait.** About 17% (stratum I) grasp the distinction broadly. About 37% (strata IV and V) are entirely vague. For the 46% between, stratum III shows uncertainty and guesswork, and stratum II appears to have the terms under control but a fairly limited set of connotations, mostly spend–save (p. 24). Converse asks, rhetorically, why one should bother calling a party conservative if saying it is against spending conveys the same information (p. 24).

> **Intuition note.** Recognizing a word is more widespread than using it. When the meaning is narrow, there is little reason to use the word as a broad frame. Among the people who recognize the terms, a large share have only such narrow meanings.

- Strata: I = proper matching and broad understanding; II = proper matching, narrow definition; III = recognition but matching error; IV = attempted matching but no meaning; V = no apparent recognition (note a, p. 25).
- A perfect fit was not expected, even apart from coding error. The earlier coding was not limited to liberal–conservative and did not require defining the terms. It was intentionally lenient, and recognition exceeds active use. The two measures were nonetheless highly correlated (p. 25).

**Education, age, sex (Table 4, p. 26).**
- Mean education differs by almost seven years between the upper-left cell (2% of the sample) and the lower-right cell (11%). Sixty-two percent of the lower cell have less education than the least educated person in the upper cell (p. 25).
- Mean years run from 14.9 (ideologue, stratum I) to 8.2 (no issue content, stratum V), in fairly regular progression. Ideologue cells in strata III–V have too few cases (p. 26).
- Women have higher mean education than men but are disproportionately in the lower-right quadrant. Age correlates negatively with education, yet political sophistication can accumulate with age. Education achieves it faster and more strikingly (p. 26).

**Activism (Table 5, pp. 26–27).** The cell entries are mean counts of participation acts besides voting, across 1956 and 1960 (maximum possible 15, highest observed 14). They run from 3.8 (ideologue/stratum I) to 0.4 (no issue content/stratum V). About 20% of the most sophisticated cell did nothing beyond voting. Two of the thirteen most active people fall in the lower-right half, and their activism is "probably" better explained by social gratification (pp. 26–27).

**Visibility and rallies (Table 6, pp. 27–28).**
- Public reaction is perceived mainly through participation. The local politician fights to keep ideas simple, yet even his grass-roots audience is, Converse suspects, highly self-selected and quite sophisticated (pp. 27–28).
- Using the 1960 count of rallies attended, Converse simulates a "typical" rally. The three upper-left cells hold 6% of the electorate but over 15% of the rally (5+5+6). The three least sophisticated lower-right cells hold 21% of the electorate and 1% of the rally (p. 28).
- On election day the single opposite-corner cell, which does not attend at all (11% of the electorate), outweighs the three upper-left cells numerically, even at 100% turnout (p. 28).

**Party loyalty and the "independent" (pp. 28–29).**
- In the most sophisticated cell, self-described independents outnumber strong partisans, although this cell does the most vigorous, often partisan, activity. Moving diagonally toward the centre, strong partisans far outnumber independents. Otherwise there is a slight tendency for loyalty to decline diagonally, and the most independent cell is the lower right (pp. 28–29).
- Two implications. First, it is a small case of the earlier hypothesis that group-objects (here the party) are likely to have less centrality among the most sophisticated and more "lower down". Second, it partly explains why the assumption persisted that independents are informed and involved. Early cross-section studies (Lazarsfeld and colleagues) and later work found the opposite (p. 29).
- That perception is easier to understand if the informed observer's "public" is only the top 5, 10 or 15% of the sophistication ordering. The sample survey has destroyed the illusion that this is the full public (p. 29).

> **Intuition note.** People who are visible (rally-goers, activists, the articulate) tend to be more sophisticated than the typical citizen. Judging "the public" from them is likely to give a distorted picture.

### V. Constraints among idea-elements
**Claim.** Converse holds that the use of basic dimensions like liberal–conservative betokens (is a sign of) a contextual grasp that permits many specific idea-elements to be organized into tighter, "constrained" wholes. He feels there are many crucial consequences: with such organization new events have more meaning, retention of past information is far better, and behavior increasingly approximates "rational" models that assume relatively full information (pp. 29–30).

**Critic's view (reported):** abstract dimensions are superficial. Poorly educated people are merely inarticulate, and the organization is there in their specific attitudes and behavior whether or not they can put it in words (p. 30).

**Converse's reply.** If organization existed apart from articulateness, the question of articulation would be trivial. As a cold empirical matter, he says, the claim does not seem valid. He treats constraint and organization as very nearly identical. So when he hypothesizes that constraint loses its range very rapidly from the sophisticated few toward the "grass roots", he is contending that the organization of specific attitudes into wide-ranging belief systems is absent as well (p. 30).

**Table 7 (p. 31).** The elite sample is 1958 candidates for Congress, and the mass sample is the 1958 national cross-section. The issues were a purposive selection of salient domestic and foreign controversies, with comparable questions. Entries are tau-gamma coefficients (Goodman–Kruskal), chosen for sensitivity to scalar as well as correlational constraint. The mass sample was asked about keeping soldiers abroad rather than military aid (notes a–b, p. 31). Converse says that the particular elite and beliefs are "beside the point". He expects the same contrast for editors, writers or other interested groups, and for beliefs from other domains (e.g. clergy vs church members) (pp. 30, 32). Examples of cells:

| Pair | Elite | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| Employment–aid to education | .62 | .45 |
| Employment–federal housing | .59 | .08 |
| Aid to education–isolationism | .35 | −.17 |

**Table 8 (p. 32): average coefficients.**

| | Within domestic | Domestic–foreign | Within foreign | Issues–party |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elite | .53 | .25 | .37 | .39 |
| Mass | .23 | .11 | .23 | .11 |

- Constraint is read from absolute values of the coefficients, not their algebraic values (p. 32).
- Both populations show less constraint between domains than within them. Converse reads the lower values as boundaries between relatively independent belief systems (p. 32).
- The strongest within-domain constraint for the mass public (.23) is less than the elite's domestic–foreign constraint (.25). For the public, party preference seems by and large set off in a belief system of its own, relatively unconnected to issue positions (average .11, versus .39 for the elite) (p. 32).
- The mass sample includes college-educated people and ideologues in appropriate numbers. They contribute whatever organization the matrix shows but are grossly outnumbered (p. 32).
- The elite matrix is of the sort suited to factor analysis, which reduces correlated variables to fewer organizing dimensions. The mass matrix is of the type textbooks advise against factoring, since inspection shows virtually nothing to discover. Factor-type dimensions are what people usually presume in talk of "ideological postures" (pp. 32–33).

**Other evidence (p. 33).** McClosky found similar elite–mass differences in constraint on propositions about democracy and freedom (delegates to national conventions vs a cross-section). Prothro and Grigg, among others, show that support for familiar principles of freedom and tolerance is rapidly obscured when questions turn to specific cases. Converse reads this as less "cynical lip service" than a lack of contextual grasp. Both inconsistent opinions are honestly held, and the person keeps psychologically independent beliefs because he does not see that the case and the principle belong to one system (p. 33).

**Algebraic signs (pp. 33–34).**
- Sophisticated observers assume that beliefs that "go together" among elites go together in the mass as well. Table 7 shows this is "a very dangerous" assumption (p. 33).
- Example: a politician favoring federal aid to education could be predicted to be more internationalist, since both were associated with liberalism in the 1950s. The prediction is right more often than chance for elites. For the public, assuming no connection would have come closer to reality (pp. 33–34).
- All elite correlations that differ significantly from zero have signs a newspaper reader could predict. Democrats tended to favor more welfare activity and internationalism. That knowledge anticipates all the signs except one: −.18 linking military aid with the Republican Party. That is not surprising either, since Republicans of the period accepted only military foreign aid (p. 34).
- Treating the elite signs as the culturally "proper" signs, the algebraic differences between comparable entries estimate how wrong it is to extend elite assumptions to the mass. Scanning the two matrices with this in mind enhances Converse's sense of high discrepancy (p. 34).

**Recap (p. 34).** Unfamiliarity with abstract frames among the less sophisticated is more than a problem of articulation. It runs parallel to a general decline in constraint among the specific beliefs those frames help organize. The mass public cannot be said to share elite ideological patterns at the specific level any more than the abstract frames.

> **Intuition note.** If two issue attitudes are strongly linked in a person's head, knowing one helps predict the other. Among candidates it often does, and among voters it often barely does. In some pairs the linking sign differs, so elite "common sense" can mislead.

### Constraints and overt behavior (pp. 34–37)
**Counter-hypothesis (reported, no single source named).** Fragmented belief systems are inconsequential. Psychological states are "epiphenomenal" (a by-product that does no causal work), because social structure channels behavior regardless of actors' cognitions. A variant is the opinion-leader model. The less knowledgeable rely on informal communication from the more informed, so sociostructural patterns of behavior might appear even though followers lack the leaders' coherent understanding (pp. 34–35).

**Test (Figure 1, p. 35).** The graphs show the rank-order correlation (tau-b) of occupation status with 1956 presidential vote preference, within levels of conceptualization: ideologues (I), group benefit (G), nature of times (T), no issue content (N). Panels are (a) total sample, (b) women, (c) men (p. 35).
- If social position produced behavior regardless of intervening belief organization, no particular slope across the bars would be expected (p. 35).
- In (a), differences in belief organization produce very marked and orderly differences in how far partisanship reflects social position. In Converse's judgment, the doctrinaire "epiphenomenal" view is defended more by vehemence than by empirical evidence (pp. 35–36).
- A perfectly functioning opinion-leader model would also yield roughly rectangular (equal-height) bars. So the slope in (a) can be taken as a commentary on the practical imperfections of opinion leading in this domain (p. 36).
- Ideologues and near-ideologues are opinion leaders par excellence. They tend to be disproportionately well educated but include representatives from all broad social milieux, differ sharply from the less sophisticated in attention to new events and in information about past ones, and report more attempts at persuading others. Converse assumes, from much social data, that most of this informal communication goes to others in their own milieu. Since their partisanship is rather clearly geared to class, he supposes opinion leading should spread the class–behavior link whether or not the rationales spread (p. 36).
- The family is one relationship with overwhelming evidence of vigorous opinion leading: the wife is very likely to follow her husband's opinions. Splitting by sex approximates leader/follower subsets. Women show a relatively flat, disordered progression and men a steeper one, as expected if followers' belief systems matter less (pp. 36–37).
- Wives tending to double their husbands' votes is relatively trivial from a system view. If the family is the basic voting unit, Figure 1(c) suggests that spreading proper behavior without understanding through opinion leading is a very feeble mechanism, at least for political decisions of this sort (p. 37).
- Among those with no issue content (evidently in the men's panel, given the comparison with women), partisanship shows none of the residual effects that systematic opinion following would leave, and such effects are visible among comparable women. Opinion leading exists, but it is doubtful that it is the dominant, effective phenomenon sometimes supposed (p. 37).

**Generality (p. 37).** Converse is convinced that the declining constraint between a social-structure term and a political choice, moving from more to less sophisticated, is "powerful and general". It is powerful because variation by sophistication is extremely large, and no other discriminating variables begin to separate populations so cleanly and sharply as these measures. It is general because it replicates across time (in every instance with appropriate data, for class and partisanship). It has some "incipient" claim to generality for other terms, e.g. religion (Protestant vs Catholic) and partisanship (p. 37).

> **Intuition note.** The class–vote link is strong for people who think about politics and fades for those who do not. This looks like the link being produced by understanding, not just by social position. Followers copying leaders does not seem to fill the gap.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: show how an elite–mass gap in constraint is measured. For a 2×2 table of support/oppose on two issues, a gamma-type coefficient is $(ad-bc)/(ad+bc)$. It is related to, but not identical with, the author's tau-gamma, and we use it only for hand computation. Here $a$ = both support, $b$ = support only on Issue 1, $c$ = support only on Issue 2, $d$ = both oppose.

- Elite (100 people): $a=40,\ b=10,\ c=10,\ d=40$. Then $ad=1600$, $bc=100$, and coefficient $=(1600-100)/(1600+100)=1500/1700\approx .88$.
- Mass (100 people): $a=30,\ b=20,\ c=20,\ d=30$. Then $ad=900$, $bc=400$, and coefficient $=500/1300\approx .38$.
- Mass with the "wrong" elite pairing: $a=20,\ b=30,\ c=30,\ d=20$. Then $ad=400$, $bc=900$, and coefficient $=-500/1300\approx -.38$.

Reading: the mass is less constrained than the elite (.38 vs .88 in absolute value). If the elite pairing is taken as "proper", the third table shows the mass can even carry the opposite sign, and then assuming no link (0) is closer to reality than assuming the elite link (+.88). This mirrors pp. 32–34.

## Concepts
- [[belief-system-constraint]] — predictability of one attitude from others. Converse treats constraint and organization as very nearly identical and measures it by the size of correlations among specific issue attitudes (p. 30)
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — here indexed by understanding of liberal–conservative, 1956 level of conceptualization, education and activity. It sorts populations "so cleanly" (pp. 22, 37)
- [[levels-of-conceptualization]] (new) — Converse's coding of how respondents organize politics (ideologue, near-ideologue, group interest, nature of the times, no issue content). It was defined earlier and is used here as a classifier (p. 25)
- [[recognition-vs-active-use]] (new) — distinction between recognizing a term when presented and using it spontaneously. It is the basis of the 1960 test (p. 18)
- [[spend-save-reading]] (new) — narrow equation of liberal/conservative with spending versus economizing (pp. 22–23)
- [[elite-mass-constraint-gap]] (new) — elites show much higher inter-issue correlations than the mass public (pp. 30–32)
- [[opinion-leader-model]] (new) — the less informed take behavioral cues from informal communication of the better informed (p. 35)
- [[epiphenomenal-beliefs-claim]] (new) — the claim that social structure fixes behavior independently of beliefs, so beliefs do no work (pp. 34–35)
- [[group-centrality]] (new) — group-objects such as the party are said to be more central at lower sophistication levels (p. 29)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Constraint matrix suitable for factor analysis ↔ psychometrics/latent-variable modelling: an organizing dimension shows up as shared correlation — the author himself links the two (p. 32)
- [metaphor] "Visible/operative public" ↔ selection bias in survey research: observers see only the self-selected tail (p. 29)
- [structural-analogy] Opinion-leader transmission without understanding ↔ cue-taking and heuristics: followers copy behavior without reasons (pp. 35–36)

## Questions this raises
- How much of the low mass correlation reflects missing organization, and how much reflects measurement problems in the survey items? Converse argues for the first (p. 30), and measurement error is not discussed on these pages.
- If narrow readings like spend–save are common, does the choice of a few coded categories shape the picture of mass thinking (pp. 20, 24)?
- Why does constraint appear in the elite sample only for certain pairs (e.g. near zero for military aid with other items)? What do such domain boundaries mean (p. 32)?
- Would other channels of opinion leading than the family show the flat pattern seen among women (pp. 36–37)?
