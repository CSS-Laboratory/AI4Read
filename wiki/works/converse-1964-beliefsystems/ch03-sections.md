# Converse on groups, stability and issue publics (Sections VI–X)

## L0 — One line
Mass publics hold few constrained, stable policy beliefs, because abstract political ideas need contextual information that most citizens lack; what they do hold centres on visible social groups and narrow issue publics.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Converse asks why mass belief systems show so little constraint (how far beliefs go together) and whether this reflects idiosyncratic but coherent beliefs. Visible social groups are highly central objects for the less well informed, though this fades at the lowest information levels. Panel data show that issue answers are very unstable over time, and one issue fits a model of a perfectly stable core plus purely random responders. Large parts of the public are therefore outside any given issue public (the people with real opinions on it). Elite and mass explanations of historical movements (abolition, Nazism) may diverge. Missing contextual information, he suggests, lies behind many findings that appear mainly among the well educated.

**Prerequisites:** correlation coefficients; panel (repeated-interview) data; the Section I–V ideas of constraint, centrality and "contextual information"; Table 7 (elite versus mass issue intercorrelations).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- For unbiased issue samples the elite show more constraint than the public. Items that explicitly involve one visible group can reverse this, because groups are the objects most central for the poorly informed (pp. 38–40).
- Group centrality is predicted to be low at the very top (elite), highest in the upper-middle, and low again at the bottom. At the bottom, people lack the group cognition or the "linking" information. This is an actuarial proposition (true of typical populations, not of every case) and varies with how gross the cues are (pp. 40–44).
- Over two years only about 13 in 20 mass respondents stay on the same side of an issue, against 10 in 20 by chance. Party identification is far more stable than any issue (pp. 44–46).
- Instability implies low constraint, not idiosyncratic coherence. Low stability "virtually ensures" low constraint (p. 47).
- A "black and white" model (a perfectly stable hard core plus random responders) fits the private-enterprise item virtually perfectly. The other items depart only modestly, which Converse supposes are due to a small "third force" of converts (pp. 48–51).
- Issue publics (the people who hold real opinions on a given issue) vary in size, none is a clear majority, and their overlap is far from Guttman-cumulative (nested, so that membership in a narrow public would imply membership in a broader one). Removing people outside them narrows the elite–mass gap in constraint (pp. 52–53).
- Constraint declines with information. This has consequences for left–right elite strategies and for explanations of mass support for elite ideologies, as in the abolition and Nazi cases (pp. 54–64).
- Converse expects hypotheses about integrated belief systems to show results mainly among relative elites, since many findings appear only, or most sharply, among the well educated (p. 65).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### VI. Social groupings as central objects in belief systems (pp. 38–44)

**The prediction (pp. 38–39).** For an unbiased sample of controversial items, Converse expects more internal constraint among elites than among their publics. He says it is possible to bias the choice of items so that public constraint exceeds elite constraint. The reason is that visible social groupings are highly central objects for the less well informed (p. 38).

He illustrates with six hypothetical items, all involving "Negroes" as a group (p. 38):
- keeping Negroes out of professional athletics;
- government action for fair treatment in jobs and housing;
- cutting peanut and cotton subsidies, with the crops described as raised mainly by Negroes in the South;
- federal school aid only to schools admitting Negroes;
- state control of voting even if it hurts the Negro in the South;
- foreign aid to places like Africa rather than Britain and France.

In every case but one (Africa) the group involvement is explicit. Some adults would not know that Africa's population is largely Negro, so constraint for that item would be lower for them (pp. 38–39).

He predicts a substantial level of constraint in the mass, probably above the general "mass" level in Table 7. For elites, the items cut across abstract dimensions they customarily use (states' rights, economic development strategy, federal role in education), so constraint would be somewhat lower (p. 39).

Converse's explanation is that for most of the public the central object is the familiar group, not abstract relations among parts of government. Such questions take on meaning only with much political information. The items then "boil down" for many respondents to one question: sympathetic, indifferent or hostile to Negroes? (p. 39).

**Evidence (pp. 39–40).** He says there is no direct empirical evidence for the illustration, only fragmentary findings (p. 39).
- Cross-section sample: the school-desegregation item and the fair-treatment (F.E.P.C.) item, both involving Negroes, correlate .57. That is well above the highest of the 28 intercorrelations in the mass half of Table 7 (p. 39).
- Elite sample: the parallel question asked about federal aid to *segregated* schools, so comparability is imperfect. Some elites answered "no" because they opposed federal school aid in general. After setting aside 7% of the elite sample for this, the correlation with the F.E.P.C. item is only .31, on the low side of elite domestic-issue correlations (pp. 39–40).
- Across the 28 Table 7 "trials", only three had a same-sign mass correlation larger in absolute size than the elite one. The differences were .02, .04 (both trivial) and .08. The Negro pair is a large reversal: mass constraint is .26 greater by simple difference of coefficients (p. 40).

*Intuition note.* When every item points at the same visible group, ordinary people would tend to answer all items with one feeling about that group. Elites cut the same items along several abstract dimensions, so their constraint would be somewhat lower. The test rests on one isolated pair of items, which Converse calls "striking initial support".

**Reconciling two findings (pp. 40–41).** Two classes of findings have been reported (p. 40):
1. Groups as attitude objects have higher centrality for the mass than for the elite.
2. The alignment of social-group membership (class, religion) with political behaviour is sharpest among the most involved and sophisticated third of a mass sample and fades as involvement declines.

Figure 2 is a schematic (Converse: its details are "sheer fancy", but the gross contours seem empirically justified) (p. 40). The left shape plots political information. The right shape plots the centrality of groups *qua* groups. Centrality is thin at the top, widest in the upper-middle range, and thin again at the bottom (p. 41, image). The "relative elite" of the mass sample, the top 2, 5 or 10 percent, is what impressionistic observers take to be "the public" (p. 40). At the very top he has seen "glimmers" of the inverse relation between group centrality and sophistication. Examples are the falling-off of party loyalty at the "top" of the mass sample and the lowered constraint on the Negro items in the elite sample (p. 41).

**Why centrality falls at the bottom (pp. 41–43).** The findings that lead to this decline come from situations where the actor himself must see a link between group membership and a party or policy. The link is not made explicit, unlike in the Negro battery. He needs (a) a cognition of the group as an entity and (b) interstitial "linking" information on why a party or policy matters to the group. Key proposition: as general political information declines, the probability rises that some key piece of this information is missing (p. 41).

- *Group cognition.* Groups vary in visibility. Racial groups ("in the skin") are highly visible if specimens are present or the person has been vividly informed. Groups with buildings, meetings and officers (church, clergy) are more visible than social classes. Class and nation are less visible (p. 42). Most people know that some have more means or status, but this may stay at the level of reactions to the simple fact that some people are born handsome and others homely, or it may proceed to cognitions of "real" bounded groups, as Marx knew (p. 42).
- *Nation.* Nationalist ideologies, which hinge on a simple group object, might be deduced to penetrate mass populations better than the single-tax ideology of the physiocrats and Henry George. This holds particularly for Western nations whose primary education carves the nation into young minds (p. 42). But Znaniecki reports that most peasants in nineteenth-century Tsarist Russia were unconscious of belonging to a Russian society. A 1934–35 study in the Pripet marshes found nearly half of the ethnically White Ruthenian inhabitants did not know their nationality existed (p. 42). Decades after the literati take a nation concept for granted, substantial proportions may never have heard of it (p. 43).
- *Linking information.* The typical missing element is the linking information. Many US voters cannot predict that any party preference will emerge among different class groupings. The least involved citizens' partisan behaviour is essentially random with respect to social class (p. 43).

**Caveat: Figure 2 is "actuarial" (pp. 43–44).** It holds for most situations, given the typical distribution of information. When cues linking group and party or policy are very gross, they penetrate even the less informed and the centrality curve tapers off less rapidly (p. 43).
- The 1960 linking information was "The Democratic candidate is Catholic." It spread through almost the entire population at a speed Converse suspects impossible for more complex contextual information (p. 43).
- The linking information that made class unusually relevant after World War II was vague and contextual. With his data, the religious link in 1960 registered to some degree even among the least sophisticated Protestants and Catholics. The incremental impact of class cues in the earlier period had not registered at these lower levels (p. 43).
- One could "tamper" by giving a poorly informed person a tiny increment of linking information. It would be too small to change overall information visibly, yet large enough to raise one group's centrality considerably. Still, Figure 2 is valid in an actuarial sense, because in natural populations the probability of holding such information declines with overall information (p. 44).

*Intuition note.* Knowing that a group exists is not enough. A person must also know which party goes with his group. Short, vivid links (a candidate's religion) spread fast, while vague contextual ones (class politics) had not registered among the least sophisticated, so the shape of the curve depends on the type of cue.

### VII. Stability of belief elements over time (pp. 44–52)

**Why a stability test (p. 44).** The correlations so far are aggregate cumulations, which, he says, cannot be seen as commenting incisively on individuals' belief structures (p. 44).

**Objection (counterhypothesis, p. 44):** low constraint among the less sophisticated may only mean that their belief configurations are *idiosyncratic*. Each person's beliefs might form an interdependent whole, but because they are outside the cultural flow of information about what goes with what, they combine beliefs in many ways. Converse believes the evidence lets him "reject it rather categorically". If beliefs were idiosyncratic but meaningful, answers by the same people to the same items over time should be fundamentally stable. "They do not." (p. 44)

**Data (pp. 44–45).** A four-year longitudinal study asked the same people the same questions several times, usually about two years apart. For the typical Table 7-type item, only about 13 of 20 people locate themselves on the same *side* in successive interviews, when 10 of 20 would by chance (p. 45).

There are no comparable elite panel data. But the fit between issue answers and congressional roll-calls suggests that individual congressmen's roll-call stability would estimate elite stability. Converse says it is "probably no exaggeration" that 18 of 20 congressmen would take the same positions after two years. He is "very confident" that elite–mass differences in constraint are mirrored in differences in temporal stability (p. 45).

**Two predictions from centrality (p. 45).** Centrality and stability were said earlier to be highly related. He therefore predicts that:
1. Pure affect toward visible population groupings is highly stable even in a mass public, much more than policy beliefs bearing on those groups.
2. Policy items that bear more explicitly on a group's fortunes are less stable than group affect but more stable than items requiring contextual information.

**Figure 3 (pp. 45–47).** Stability is measured as a rank-order correlation (tau-beta) between individuals' 1958 and 1960 positions on the same item (p. 46). Reading approximately from the figure, the ordering from most to least stable is:

| Item | Approximate level |
|---|---|
| Party identification | about .70 |
| School desegregation | about .45–.50 |
| F.E.P.C. | about .40 |
| Guaranteed employment | about .40 |
| Isolationism | about .35–.40 |
| Federal aid to education | about .35–.40 |
| Foreign economic aid | about .30–.35 |
| Foreign military aid | about .30–.35 |
| Federal housing | just under .30 |

- Party identification is the only longitudinal item touching on pure affect toward a visible population grouping. Its stability lies in a completely different range from even the most stable issue item (p. 45).
- Converse calls this ironic. In theory the party is a means and policy the end, and ends should be more stable and central. The reversal for the mass public is a dramatic special case of the generalization that party and affect toward it are more central than the policy ends (pp. 45–46).
- Among issues, the most stable are those bearing on the Negro, with the federal job-guarantee item nearly as stable. Stability declines as referents become more remote, from jobs and Negroes to foreign-policy means (p. 47).
- The least stable is the domestic issue on government versus private enterprise in housing and utilities. Sophisticated judges would probably call it the most "ideological" item. Its elite counterpart is central to the main dimension in Table 7 (p. 47).
- The item names "government" and "private business", which Converse describes as visible population groupings. Converse expects affect toward "big business" to be reasonably stable, though probably less than for Negroes or Catholics. But the item does not make clear which group profits from which arrangement. Lacking cues, the citizen "innocent of ideology" is likely to make "capricious constructions" (p. 47).

**Conclusion on stability and constraint (p. 47).** The longitudinal data show that low constraint in the mass is not produced by well-knit but idiosyncratic belief systems. These beliefs are "extremely labile" over time. Great instability is *prima facie* evidence of very low centrality. Instability in one belief sets an upper limit on the orderly constraint that can show up between that belief and another, even a perfectly stable one. An aggregate might show high stability despite low constraint, but low stability "virtually ensures" low constraint. So understanding instability is understanding low constraint (pp. 47–48).

*Intuition note.* If a belief wobbles randomly between interviews, the orderly link it can show with any other belief at one time point is capped. This gives a one-way logic: low stability implies low constraint, while high stability does not by itself imply high constraint.

**Figure 4: turnover pattern across three waves (p. 48).** With waves $t_1, t_2, t_3$:
- The instability indicator between $t_2$ and $t_3$ is essentially the same as between $t_1$ and $t_2$. So issue differences in response stability are highly reliable over a medium range (four years) (p. 48).
- Quite generally, $t_3$ positions are predicted "fully as well" from $t_1$ alone as from $t_2$ alone. The figure labels all three links .3: $t_1\to t_2$, $t_2\to t_3$ and $t_1\to t_3$ (p. 48).

Converse says it "can be shown" that no single meaningful change process shared by all respondents generates this pattern (footnote 37). Allowing a limited number of processes in the population, only two models could generate it (p. 48):
1. Some respondents deliberately take the opposite side at each successive measurement. This is "sufficiently nonsensical" to reject out of hand (p. 48).
2. A mixture of two polar processes, the **black and white model**. There is a "hard core" of opinion on an issue that is crystallized and perfectly stable. The rest of the population gives statistically random response sequences. The model does not fix the proportions, which are empirical. The size of the turnover correlations between any two times is a simple function of these proportions (p. 49).

Converse says the model is credible in assuming that many people, lacking information on a dimension, give meaningless opinions that vary randomly. He is uncomfortable with the rigid division, which leaves no "gray" area of meaningful conversion. But randomness is "discouraging" substantively and "empowering" mathematically, because aggregate randomness has predictable consequences (p. 49).
- If the model fit, some random people would by chance give the same response at all three trials. Their paths look like those of perfectly stable respondents. They could not be tagged, but their number could be estimated well.
- The model allows a rigorous goodness-of-fit test (p. 49).

**First test (pp. 49–50).** He chose the item most likely to fit on a priori grounds: the private enterprise versus government item (housing, electrical power). It is the most "ideological" yet the most unstable. The model needs some people with unswerving beliefs, others with no beliefs, and no middle group in evolution. This item seemed "sheltered" from meaningful change in two senses (p. 50):
- it concerns a basic area of controversy, so ideologically-minded people would not be readily dissuaded;
- events in the period (Little Rock, exposés of waste in foreign aid) could evolve opinion on other items, but little would shake true beliefs on this one.
Its relationships are also remote and abstract for most people, so meaningless responses are likely (p. 50).

**Result.** The fit to the three-wave data was "virtually perfect". Converse concludes that, for this issue, the only change occurring was random change (p. 50). The other items fit somewhat worse. No other item strays a great distance, but none would likely survive significant goodness-of-fit tests (p. 50).

**Interpreting the poorer fit (pp. 50–51).** Beyond the exact model, he says any significant departure leaves no mathematical anchor: unless restrictive assumptions are inserted, very many models could account for the data (p. 50). Because one issue fits perfectly, he supposes that the others depart only because of a "third force" of people undergoing meaningful conversion from one genuine opinion at $t_1$ to an opposing one at $t_3$. This force is small and the dominant phenomenon remains the two segments. Any third force disrupts the fit, and the degree of departure is a function of its size (pp. 50–51). He says this view cannot be subjected to any unequivocal mathematical test. It depends on one excellent fit and approaches to fit elsewhere, and competing attitude models, he says, lose all plausible ring against the private-enterprise result (p. 51).

*Intuition note.* Converse's picture is that an issue splits people (exactly so for the private-enterprise item, approximately for the others) into those who really hold a view and those who answer by something like a coin flip. Random people look stable only by luck. His conclusion rests on the one near-perfect fit plus plausibility for the rest.

**Objection (reliability, p. 51).** The turnover might show that the questions are poorly written and unreliable. **Converse replies:** the items pose real political controversies, and citizens' difficulty with them says something about how they understand the real debate. Moreover, the black and white model says an instrument is perfectly reliable for one class of people and totally unreliable for another, so reliability is not inherent "solely in the instrument" (p. 51).

**Reliability check (p. 51).** He selected small subsets whose open-ended answers showed "self-starting" concern with a controversy (for example the Negro question). Their turnover correlations on the relevant issue items "increased substantially", beginning to approach party-identification levels (Figure 3). So extreme instability goes with lack of information or at least interest, and item reliability is adequate for people with pre-existing concern (p. 51).

**Substantive conclusion (pp. 51–52).** Large portions of an electorate lack meaningful beliefs, even on issues of long elite controversy. Virtually none of the common empirical methods allows for this; they assume everyone can be located on every measured dimension. On any one dimension, very substantial portions do not belong at all and should be set aside as outside that issue public. Political effects of a controversy (letters to editors, vote changes) are felt only among its "members". So the mass public fragments into many narrower issue publics (p. 52).

### VIII. Issue publics (pp. 52–53)

From the longitudinal data on eight issues, Converse sketches crude boundaries of eight issue publics (p. 52).
- **Size.** Publics vary in size, and none embraces a clear majority of the electorate. Size is almost perfectly correlated with the ranking of issue stability in Figure 3. The smallest, for the "ideological" private-enterprise issue, includes less than 20 percent of the electorate (p. 52).
- **Overlap.** One logical possibility is concentric rings, cumulative in Guttman's sense (anyone in a 20% public would nearly surely be in a related 40% public). Reality is not that neat. Memberships and overlaps are dispersed but not entirely random (pp. 52–53).
  - People in all eight publics exceed chance expectation by a factor greater than five. The same holds for people in none.
  - Pairwise and larger intersections also exceed chance, by much smaller increments that are nevertheless relatively large where the issues' content suggests overlap.
  - Departure from a Guttman structure is "extreme". Different controversies excite different people to real opinion formation. One man cares about Negro policy and is indifferent to other areas, and his neighbour may care about foreign aid (p. 53).
- **Constraint inside the intersections.** For people at the intersection of any pair of publics, both beliefs matter to them, and correlations tend to be much stronger. A matrix parallel to Table 7 for them looks more like the elite matrix than the mass matrix. The matrix is "spurious": few people contribute to all cells, many to none, and cell composition varies. Still, removing people outside the publics in question, through indifference or ignorance, closes much of the gap between mass and elite constraint (p. 53).

### IX. Summary (pp. 53–64)

**The mass picture (pp. 53–54).** Fragmented issue publics, feeble constraint and lack of understanding of overarching ideological frames are related. For the involved citizen, absorbing contextual information links the initial policy interest to policy differences elsewhere, and these configurations can be described economically in ideological abstractions. Most of the mass public does not proceed so far. Concrete issues may capture attention and form opinions, but the engagement remains narrow. A realistic picture neither omits issues nor presumes coherence. It captures the fragmentation, narrowness and diversity of these demands (p. 54).

**Parsimony versus explanatory power (pp. 54–55).** Describing a poorly constrained system forces a choice between parsimony and explanatory power. The dilemma arises only if one insists on the issue or ideological base of mass politics. Many threads cancel out. With information on basic party loyalties, sudden disruptions of economic expectations, and "inelegant" short-term factors such as candidate popularity, mass political phenomena are not hard to predict economically. But such accounts do not reach the issue or ideological base (p. 54). Choosing economy means following one or two threads. A good choice likely involves visible, competing population groupings (pp. 54–55).
- Lipset in *Political Man* did this with social class. Class is one of the more reliably prominent threads. In the American 1950s it would undoubtedly be the single most visible and persistent ideological thread (p. 55).
- The cost is explanatory power. Class concerns need only mean (as is probably the case) that they made some greater or lesser contribution to the significant political behaviour, largely voting, of 20 to 40 percent of the American population. This leaves a clear imprint on mass politics but, he says, does not constitute even substantial explanation (p. 55).
- In pluralist societies with other visible cleavages, these may penetrate mass publics more than class. For religion (Netherlands, Austria, US), Converse sees fair reason to believe it is as important as, if not more important than, class. In France, the "clerical question" predicts left–right partisanship better than class issues, despite elite efforts to treat it as a "phony" issue (pp. 55–56).

**Consequences of declining constraint (pp. 56–57).** The most important insight is that ideological constraint declines with decreasing political information. It is present among elites and disappears rather rapidly downward (p. 56).
- *Social strata.* Constrained belief systems are necessarily more common in upper strata. Upper strata have thus supported conservative or rightist parties more predictably than lower strata have supported leftist ones (p. 56).
- *Elite asymmetries.* Converse says elites recognise their "natural" clienteles in the upper and lower strata, and that the lower clientele numerically outweighs the upper. Leftist elites thus enjoy a "natural" numerical superiority but a less dependable, less solidary clientele. The rightist clientele is more limited but more dependable (p. 56). As a result there is increasingly overt stress on group loyalty and cohesion as one moves from right to left:
  - less party fragmentation on the left where institutions allow multiple parties;
  - more stringent legislative party discipline toward the left;
  - rightist candidates more likely to run as individual notables "above the parties" (pp. 56–57).
- *Interest groups.* Trade unions often create or ally with leftist parties or publicize endorsements, especially unions with less sophisticated members. Business and professional associations give non-partisan exhortations and keep a "proper" distance from rightist parties. For leftist parties, transmitting gross, simple group cues is a functional imperative. For rightist parties there is much to lose and nothing to gain from such publicity, since the tactical need is to avoid alienating potentially large-scale "haphazard" support from the lower-status clientele (p. 57).

**Miscalculations (p. 57).** Observers, more often academic commentators than practical politicians, forget the low incidence of constraint in the mass. Where an elite with a distinctive ideology captures a broad surge of support, the supporters' true motivations may have little or nothing to do with the distinctive beliefs of the endorsed elite. Converse believes these discontinuities are common and become more certain to the degree that (1) the elite ideology's distinctive elements are abstractions or referents remote from the clientele's experience, and (2) the clientele is recruited from the less informed strata (p. 57). He applies this to two cases.

**Case 1: Abolition and the Republican Party (pp. 58–62).**
- *The puzzle.* Converse accepts the evidence of elite and near-elite ferment. The American Anti-Slavery Society's 200,000 members in seven years after 1833 amount, if taken at face value, to between 3 and 4 percent of the adult nonsouthern population. That is vigorous compared with the 5 to 15 percent he treats as the visible political public. But the Republican Party, conceived in Wisconsin and born in Michigan under what Converse calls the pure banner of abolition, won 46 percent of the nonsouthern popular presidential vote two years later. What part did abolition beliefs play in winning the mass base? (p. 58)
- *Why doubt.* Among causes assumed to resonate with the mass, few seem less plausible than abolition. Panics, free western land, railroad rates and immigrant job competition had immediate impacts. Abolition had concrete meaning mainly in the South. Converse says he is tempted to the heresy that very substantial parts of the nonsouthern population were only dimly aware that slavery or a controversy about it existed (pp. 58–59).
- *McCarthy analogy.* The elite controversy over internal communism was bitter, yet during the televised hearings Stouffer found that 30 percent of a cross-section could not think of any senator or congressman investigating it. In 1952 the slogan was "Corruption, Korea and Communism." Mass responses were strong to corruption and Korea and almost nil to the third, although the population was far more literate, leisured and media-exposed than in 1850. Lesson: no necessary connection between the noise, acrimony or duration of an elite debate and its mass penetration. A better guide, he says, seems to be the character of the issue itself (p. 59).
- *Ohio letters.* A student found no references to abolition in letters of the 1850s and 1860s kept in a small Ohio community, though writers were of the upper stratum. Letters on the eve of the Civil War increasingly referred to disruption of the Union. Mass penetration is easier to understand once the South was in clear rebellion, and Converse thinks that stage was likely reached at a mass level much later than usually assumed (p. 60).
- *Awareness versus opinion.* Today a very large majority knows of Little Rock and the University of Mississippi, but opinion among nonsouthern whites is "far from intense". The true issue publics are disproportionately Negroes and southerners. A salience measure that registers two-thirds of nonsouthern Negroes as intensely concerned qualifies scarcely one nonsouthern white in ten. Most intensely concerned northern whites live near Negroes or are inordinately interested in politics. In the hinterland, he says, opinion is superficial or indifferent (p. 60).
- *Voting patterns (pp. 60–61).* Assuming deep humanitarian concern in the hinterland would imply "an astonishing decline" in empathy. Fringe votes for earlier abolitionist parties (Liberty, Free Soil) were never strong in Boston and New York, centres of intellectual ferment, but were concentrated in smaller towns outside them. In 1856, once the Whigs were no longer an alternative and mass support had to go to the Republicans or the anti-Catholic Know-Nothings, patterns changed:
  - the largest northeastern centres, where intellectuals pursued abolition and Catholic immigrants gathered, gave the Know-Nothings their clearest mass support and the Republicans their weakest harvest of former Whig or Free-Soil votes;
  - surrounding areas with the strongest earlier abolition-party support moved more votes to the Republicans, though Know-Nothings cut in there too;
  - the "least blemished" Republican successes were in the deeper hinterland, which had given feeblest support to abolition earlier (p. 61).
- *Conclusion.* He suggests there was probably an important discontinuity between elite abolitionism and the early mass success. Its size is unknown. Converse has "some confidence" it was large enough to shock students of elite documents. He adds that, from the point of view of historical outcomes, all that is important is that this conjunction of circumstances occurred and was interpreted as it was by elites in North and South. But recognising such discontinuities may improve understanding of history (pp. 61–62).

**Case 2: The mass base of the Nazi Party (pp. 62–64).**
- *Framing.* Converse follows Bendix in separating top leaders, party members and the masses whose sudden surge converted the Nazis from an extremist fringe to prominence. He treats economic collapse as the critical catalyst for both organizational strength before 1930 and the 1930 electoral success. He then agrees that explanatory paths for mass and elite diverge. His interest is only the relation between the new Nazi voters and the movement's ideology (p. 62).
- *Who.* There was a connection between higher turnout and the Nazi surge in 1928–32. Bendix noted a gain of about 5½ million Nazi votes in 1930 over 1928 coinciding with an influx of nearly 4¼ million adults who had not voted in 1928 (see Reading notes on these figures). That excludes about 1,760,000 young first-time voters, who probably flocked to the Nazis disproportionately (pp. 62–63). Among older voters the most dramatic shifts from other parties came in rural areas and among peasants (Heberle) (p. 63). Converse concludes that the mass base was disproportionately recruited from customary nonvoters, the young and the peasantry (p. 63).
- *Sophistication.* Chronic nonvoters lie at the bottom of any political-sophistication scale. In a Table 3-type matrix, voting fidelity declines sharply from upper left to lower right. The young are the least sophisticated age grade despite higher education. In American data, information and involvement fall from urban to rural areas, and farmers know less of the normal political process than the lower urban occupational echelons. Heberle's data suggest the most isolated and worst-educated farmers shifted most. Converse concludes that the Nazi mass base was "one of the more unrelievedly ill-informed clienteles" a major party has assembled in a modern state (p. 63).
- *Heberle's view.* Heberle, anxious to show that Nazi popularity in Schleswig-Holstein was not due to an ingrained antidemocratic bias, found it incredible that the Nazis were so widely acceptable to these supposedly sober and freedom-loving North Germans, and looked for continuity with earlier ideational movements there. He found common threads too meagre to be more than accidental, with antithetical elements predominating. He concluded that rural people respond to politics less in terms of ideologies and general ideals than in terms of the concrete gains and losses offered by one party relative to another, and hoped they would later "revert" to their innocuous past attitudes (pp. 63–64).
- *Converse's reading.* Even with an average-education clientele, one could doubt prior awareness of Nazi ideology. Given the actual base, he says the question verges on the absurd (p. 64). The Nazis promised change in a near-collapsed system. Converse believes large numbers of citizens in any society (particularly those without long-term affective ties to traditional parties) would gladly support ad hoc promises of change without concern for ideology, and would lack the contextual information to assess it. He thinks this would hold for any mass public, not just one with a brief democratic tradition (p. 64).
- *Mechanism.* The Nazis promised farmers a moratorium on or abolition of debts. They had a disciplined organization to spread the propaganda. In the late 1920s they apparently made a conscious discovery that rural votes were ripe, having been discouraged in urban areas. Communists had preceded them among peasants in an earlier, less propitious period and relaxed their efforts. Converse says there is no reason to believe that, if the Communists had arrived "freshly" at the same point with similar vigour and sketchy propaganda, European history would not have turned dramatically differently. All evidence suggests that, here, the link between specific ideology and mass response was "probably of the weakest" (p. 64).

*Intuition note for both cases.* Elite documents record an ideology. Converse infers from voting patterns, turnout and information levels that the mass followers may have been moved by other things. He presents this as circumstantial inference and not as proof.

### X. Conclusion (p. 65; the supplied pages end mid-sentence)

Converse describes a recurring analytic sequence (p. 65):
1. An hypothesis seems reasonable and is tested on the general population, with at best "uninteresting trace findings".
2. The sample is subdivided by education, isolating the 10 percent with college degrees or 20 percent with some college.
3. The hypothesis is then often clearly confirmed among the most educated, with results shading off to zero in the less educated majority.

He does not claim this always happens. Hypotheses are sometimes rejected for all groups, and rarely a relationship is sharper among the less educated. But there is a "strikingly large class" of cases where confirmation occurs only, or most sharply, among the well educated. After the fact it is usually easy to see that the process assumed depends on broad contextual information about currents of ideas, people or society that educated people take for granted but is not widely shared (p. 65). So he expects hypotheses about wide-ranging, highly integrated belief systems to show results among relative elites and be largely disconfirmed below them. He gives the authoritarian-personality "ideological" patterns as an impression, and says recent results on Weber's Protestant-ethic hypothesis "seem to hint" at the same pattern (p. 65). The text then begins to state the aim of the paper and breaks off (p. 65).

## Toy example (ours; not from the text)
Illustrate the black and white model on one binary issue ("left" = +1, "right" = −1) with 1,000 people over three waves.
- Hard core: 400 people (200 always +1, 200 always −1).
- Random: 600 people, each wave an independent 50/50 coin.

*Turnover correlation.* Each wave has mean 0 and variance 1. The covariance between two waves is the sum over groups of group share × (product of answers):
- hard core: $0.4\times(+1)=0.4$ in total, since each of the 400 gives $(+1)(+1)$ or $(-1)(-1)=+1$;
- random: expected product is 0, so contribution 0.

Hence $r=0.4/1=0.4$ for $t_1t_2$, $t_2t_3$ and $t_1t_3$. This is the flat pattern of Figure 4, in which the $t_1\to t_3$ correlation equals the one-step correlations.

*Same side across two waves.* $0.4 + 0.6\times0.5 = 0.7$, so 14 of 20 people. Converse reports about 13 of 20, versus 10 of 20 by chance.

*Random people who look stable.* The chance of the same answer in all three waves is $2\times(1/2)^3=1/4$, so $0.6\times 1000\times 0.25 = 150$ random people. Stable-looking people total $400+150=550$, of whom $150/550\approx 27\%$ are not truly stable. This is the "pollution" Converse says could be estimated (p. 49). With the model's proportions known, the correlation depends only on the hard-core share in this coding. This is our own computation, since the source gives no formula.

## Concepts
- [[belief-system-constraint]] — how far beliefs go together (defined in earlier sections); here shown to fall with information, and not to hide idiosyncratic coherence (pp. 38, 44)
- [[centrality]] (new) — how central a belief or object is within a person's belief system. Centrality and temporal stability are said to be "highly related" (p. 45).
- [[group-centrality]] (new) — visible social groups as high-centrality objects for poorly informed people. Centrality of groups first rises, then falls, as information declines (pp. 39–41).
- [[linking-information]] (new) — "interstitial" or contextual information telling a person why a party or policy is relevant to a group (p. 41).
- [[actuarial-proposition]] (new) — Figure 2 is a generalization about typical populations and not a law for every case (p. 43).
- [[political-awareness-sophistication]] — amount of political information. It governs group-politics links, constraint and stability (pp. 40–44).
- [[heuristics-cues]] — gross, simple group cues (a Catholic candidate, union endorsements) reach even the poorly informed (pp. 43, 57). Converse does not use the word "heuristics" here.
- [[black-and-white-model]] (new) — a perfectly stable hard core plus randomly responding remainder, with no gray area. A "third force" of converts explains departures (pp. 49–51).
- [[turnover-correlation]] (new) — correlation between the same individuals' positions at two times, measured by tau-beta in Figure 3 (p. 46).
- [[issue-public]] (new) — the people who have genuine opinions on a given dimension of controversy. Others "do not belong" on it (p. 52).
- [[guttman-cumulative-structure]] (new) — nested issue concerns, where membership in a narrow public implies membership in a broader one. Rejected as a description of issue publics (p. 52).
- [[non-attitudes]] — meaningless, randomly varying opinions of people lacking information (p. 49). Converse does not use this term in these pages.
- [[party-identification]] (new) — "pure affect" toward a visible population grouping, highly stable compared with issues (pp. 45–46).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Black and white model (stable core plus random responders) ↔ mixture or latent-class models: a population split into types with different response processes.
- [structural-analogy] "Instability sets an upper limit on constraint" (p. 47) ↔ measurement error: unreliability caps the correlation any measure can reach with others.
- [structural-analogy] Linking information and gross group cues (pp. 41–43) ↔ heuristics/cues: simple cues let uninformed people act as if informed.
- [structural-analogy] Issue publics (p. 52) ↔ rational ignorance: indifference to most issues as a pattern across the electorate.
- [metaphor] Elite–mass "discontinuity" (pp. 57, 62) ↔ principal–agent problem: the agent's (elite's) beliefs need not match the clientele's.

## Questions this raises
- If one issue fits the black and white model and the others fit nearly, how large would the "third force" of true converts have to be before a different model is needed, and how would one detect it without strong restrictive assumptions (pp. 50–51)?
- Converse infers mass non-attachment to ideology from turnout, voting patterns and information levels in the abolition and Nazi cases, and admits the evidence is circumstantial (p. 61). What kinds of direct evidence could test it?
- Does the finding that party identification is far more stable than any issue (p. 45) mean that parties shape issue positions, or only that people care more about groups than policy? The text asserts the latter's centrality, not a causal direction.
- When gross cues reach even the uninformed (p. 43), are the resulting group–party links as stable as those among the sophisticated?
