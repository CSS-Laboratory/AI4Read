# Converse (1964), Introduction and Sections I–III

## L0 — One line
Ideological "constraint" (what goes with what) has sources that are less logical than psychological and less psychological than social, partly through diffusion of information from elites; Converse argues it fades quickly as information declines down the social ladder, so mass publics are expected to organize politics much less as elites do.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Converse asks how belief systems differ between political elites and mass publics, and why analysts miss the difference. He defines a belief system by "constraint": how well one attitude predicts others. Constraint comes from logic, psychology and, above all, society. Elites create and spread "packages" of ideas. Information about what goes with what is expected to spread further than information about why. Hence constraint, abstraction and range shrink as information falls. Open-ended interviews from the 1950s sort Americans into five levels of conceptualization. Only a small minority use a liberal–conservative yardstick. Elite-style explanations of mass voting, such as a "revolt of the moderates," are therefore doubtful.

**Prerequisites:** correlation and spurious correlation; survey sampling and open-ended versus structured questions; the idea of liberal–conservative ideology in 1950s US politics.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A belief system is a set of ideas and attitudes bound by constraint (in the static case, how well one attitude predicts others). Constraint comes in degrees and can be measured as an average (p. 3).
- Elements differ in centrality. When ideas conflict, the element more likely to change is, by definition, the less central one (pp. 3–4).
- Constraint is less logical than psychological, and less psychological than social. Two social sources are shared social niches and diffusion of idea "packages" by a few creative synthesizers (pp. 5–9).
- What-goes-with-what information is simple and, on Converse's premise, diffuses much more readily; the why is complex and diffuses less readily. The two are scalar: one can hardly grasp why ideas go together without knowing that they do (pp. 9–10).
- Moving down the information scale, constraint declines, range narrows and central objects shift from abstract principles to groups and leaders, then to family and job. This is not just a bottom-layer pathology (pp. 10–11).
- Elite commentators suffer "optical illusions": they talk mostly to other elites and generalize from them (p. 11).
- A yardstick such as liberal–conservative conveys much information only if the user grasps its constraints and the system really is constrained (p. 12).
- Five levels of conceptualization (ideologue, near-ideologue, group interest, nature of the times, no issue content) are ordered a priori and corroborated by information, education and involvement (pp. 14–16). Few fall in the top two (Table 1, p. 17).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 1–3)
- Belief systems have resisted measurement. Converse says attitude measurement has advanced, but its tools are still limited (p. 1).
- **Thesis:** belief systems differ in important and predictable ways between elites and the masses "numbered" within their spheres of influence. These differences are easily overlooked or miscalculated, which poses problems of representation and measurement (p. 2).
- The ordering of belief strata loosely resembles a vertical line through an organization. At the top is a narrow leadership. At the bottom is a large base that Michels noted was rarely "there." The fit between the two orderings is not perfect or necessary (p. 2).
- Attending to numbers suggests a democratic bias and a possible confusion of numbers with power. The logic of numbers often collides with the logic of power. Numbers are one resource of power, and their weight varies by political context: none in a stable oligarchy, more in democracy (p. 2).
- Even in democracies, elites' *perception* of numbers matters more than actual numbers in normal times. Claims to numbers confer legitimacy on demands. In nondemocratic systems they matter sporadically, as threats of coercion (p. 3).

### I. Some clarification of terms (pp. 3–5)
- Converse avoids "ideology" as muddled and uses **belief system**: a configuration of ideas and attitudes bound together by constraint or functional interdependence (p. 3).
- **Static constraint:** how well we can predict that a person holds further attitudes, given one known attitude. Example: someone opposed to expanding Social Security is probably conservative and probably opposes nationalization, federal aid to education and so on (p. 3).
- **Dynamic constraint:** the probability that a change in the perceived status of one idea-element would psychologically require compensating changes elsewhere. Logical propositions are the most obvious form, though in some ways the most trivial. Psychological constraint can be as strong without being logical (p. 3).
- **Centrality:** idea-elements differ in their role in the system. Suppose a person favours a policy, likes a party, and knows they agree. The party then reverses its stand, unmistakably. He can change his view of the party or of the policy. By definition, the element more likely to change is the less central one (p. 4).
- Centrality can shift over time. The miser makes money an end, not a means (p. 4).
- **Range:** belief systems differ in the number of objects they refer to. Baptism rituals are narrow. A system linking control of production, religion and aesthetics is extremely wide (p. 4).
- The focus is wide-range systems giving some centrality to political objects. These are close to "ideologies." Social diffusion is treated by hypothesis, not by definition (pp. 4–5).

*Intuition note:* Constraint is predictability. If knowing one opinion tells you little about another, the two are not bound together. Centrality is defined by which opinion is more likely to give way when two collide.

### II. Sources of constraint on idea-elements (pp. 5–11)
Converse's claim: constraint is less logical than psychological, and less psychological than social (p. 5).

**Logical sources (pp. 5–6).**
- Some constraints are purely logical. One cannot hold that expenditures should rise, revenues fall, and the budget balance improve, all at once (p. 5).
- Objective logical constraint is felt only if the elements are brought together in one belief system. Among American adults, those favouring more welfare services tend to be more insistent on cutting taxes (p. 5).
- McGuire's experiment: syllogism propositions were scattered across a long questionnaire given to students. One week later, responses had changed so that measured inconsistency fell significantly. McGuire concluded that merely "activating" related ideas close together was enough to sensitize people to inconsistency (pp. 5–6).
- Elites "think about" political ideas far more than mass publics do. So Converse expects strict logical inconsistencies to be far more prevalent in a broad public (p. 6).
- A legislator who insists on tax-cutting probably opposes welfare expansion. A voter who voted for him out of enthusiasm for tax cuts cannot be predicted to oppose welfare. The assumption that elite constraint is mirrored among followers is, he argues, "very likely" fallacious (p. 6).

**Psychological sources (pp. 6–7).**
- Few wide belief systems rest on logic in the strict sense. Marxism's "iron logic" swept people away without recasting into propositions (p. 7).
- Examples of unexpected combinations: the Shakers prize technological progress, unlike the Amish, and produced a prototype washing machine. Peronism and Italian Fascism also surprised observers (p. 7).
- Converse takes this diversity as testimony to an absence of strict logical constraints among such elements, if any be needed. Yet elites experience their systems as logically constrained clusters. Often a "crowning posture," such as social Darwinism, acts as glue and is highly central (p. 7).

**Social sources (pp. 8–10).** There are two.
1. Combinations of ideas co-occur because of interests and information in social niches. Example: reform within the Catholic Church to bring priests closer to workers would be supported mainly by the lower clergy. Predictions are fallible: the temperance movement once allied with the urban left. These correlations resemble spurious correlations with a common prior cause, and arguments then supply a quasi-logical rationale. This type is closest to the classic meaning of "ideology" (p. 8).
2. Creation and diffusion. Shaping credible wholes is an act of creative synthesis by a minuscule proportion of any population. Elements are diffused in "packages" that consumers see as natural wholes. Any set of relatively intelligent consumers initially sympathetic to the crowning posture shows more consensus on its implications, as a result of socially diffused what-goes-with-what cues, than if each member worked them out alone (pp. 8–9).

- Constraint through diffusion depends on information transmission; if transmission fails, there is little constraint save that from the first social source. There are two classes: **what goes with what** and **why** (p. 9).
- They are scalar: one can hardly know why two ideas go together without knowing that they do. Example: most Americans know "Communists are atheists" as a bare fact. Converse would be surprised if more than a quarter even attempted to say why, and he is sure most of those answers would be incoherent or irrelevant (p. 9).
- The "why" is close to what Downs called "contextual knowledge." Someone with it can guess other constraints, for example how a true believer will respond to new situations (p. 9).
- **Premise:** the first type diffuses much more readily because it is less complex (p. 10).
- Information differences in a cross-section are "staggering." Very little "trickles down" far. Ranking on information is largely due to education, modified by specialized interests (p. 10).

**Consequences of declining information (pp. 10–11).**
- Contextual grasp of standard political belief systems fades very rapidly, almost before one passes beyond the roughly 10 percent of Americans who in the 1950s had completed standard college training. Simple what-goes-with-what information, and even the identity of objects, increasingly goes missing (p. 10).
- Constraint declines across the universe of idea-elements and the range of relevant systems narrows. One expects many small clusters with little constraint among them, quite often even for sheer logical constraint (p. 10).
- Central objects shift from remote, abstract "ideological" principles, to social groupings or charismatic leaders, to immediate experience such as family, job and associates (pp. 10–11).
- Most of politics, especially on the national or international stage, is remote and abstract. So these changes begin rapidly below a very thin stratum. They are not a pathology of a thin bottom layer; they matter for the bulk of mass behaviour (p. 11).
- **Elite "optical illusions":** the public commentator (probably a fraction of 1 percent) talks mostly to peers, meets little challenge to his assumption of shared contextual grasp, may dismiss low information as true of only 10 to 20 percent of voters, and generalizes "facilely" from his informal contacts (p. 11).

*Intuition note:* Many people can learn that two positions "go together" through social diffusion, without learning why. Few learn the reasoning that would let them work out new cases. So elite-style coherence thins out fast as information falls.

### III. Active use of ideological dimensions of judgment (pp. 11–17)
- **Economy:** constraint and economy are companions. A yardstick such as the liberal–conservative continuum lets an actor locate parties, leaders, legislation and court decisions with little effort. It also serves analysts' parsimonious description (pp. 11–12).
- A single word like "conservative" conveys much information only if (1) the actor understands the constraints behind it (none if he does not know the term) and (2) the system really is relatively constrained (p. 12).
- **Test case:** the early-1950s story of a swing toward conservatism or "revolt of the moderates." As description of elites it is apt: Eisenhower replaced Truman, and Congress briefly became more Republican (pp. 12–13).
- As an account of mass motives it assumes that elite constraints are mirrored in the public, so that a voter switching to the Republicans saw himself as choosing the more conservative option. Converse says this assumption "seems thoroughly implausible" given his knowledge of cross-section attitude data. The liberal–conservative continuum is an elegant high-order abstraction, not a typical tool of the "man in the street" (p. 13).
- **Method:** the interview protocols included structured items and lengthy open-ended material in the respondent's own words. Highly educated or involved respondents were expected to use "too conservative" and similar shorthand. They proved a very small minority (pp. 13–14).
- Converse then classified the policy-relevant evaluative dimensions used instead. The levels were ordered a priori by breadth of contextual grasp (p. 14).

**Levels of conceptualization (pp. 14–16):**
- **I. Ideologues:** actively use an abstract, far-reaching dimension as a yardstick. The dimension did not have to be liberal–conservative, but that was almost the only such dimension that occurred empirically (p. 14).
- **II. Near-ideologues:** mention such a dimension peripherally, or use it in ways that cast doubt on their grasp (p. 14).
- **III. Group interest:** no overarching dimension. Parties are judged by treatment of social groupings, for example disliking a party for helping one group too much, or endorsing one for farm prices. The more sophisticated see a conflict between business or rich and labor (p. 14).
- Why level III people are not ideologues: they differ in social origin, in judgmental processes and in overt reactions. They see politics as group interests and are likely to follow advice if properly advised about their group's interest. Without it, and since their interest is weak, they lack the grasp to respond. Converse calls this **ideology by proxy** (pp. 14–15).
- **Illustration:** a self-described strong Socialist backed Socialists as pro-working man and he was a working man. Yet he wanted private business to handle electric power and housing, and his other issue answers were uncorrelated with socialist doctrine. Converse thinks that if told "good Socialists" demand government intervention, he would have answered differently. His system "began and ended" with Socialists, working man, non-Socialists and the rich. Calling him a full ideologue assumes shared background information that he lacks (pp. 15–16).
- **IV.** Residual policy evaluation, in two modes. (a) A nature-of-the-times response: praise or blame for war, peace, prosperity or depression, with no sense that groups fare differently. (b) A single narrow policy, such as Social Security, evoking gratitude or anger, not seen as representing broader party posture (p. 16).
- **V.** No policy significance. These people are party loyal but unable to say what the party stands for, or focus on candidate personality, or pay too little attention to say anything (p. 16).
- **Validation of the ranking:** independent measures of political information, education and involvement all declined sharply and monotonically down the levels. Each retained some residual relation with the others controlled (p. 16).

**Table 1 (p. 17).** Distribution by level (percent):

| Level | Total sample | 1956 voters |
|---|---|---|
| I. Ideologues | 2½ | 3½ |
| II. Near-ideologues | 9 | 12 |
| III. Group interest | 42 | 45 |
| IV. Nature of the times | 24 | 22 |
| V. No issue content | 22½ | 17½ |

- The distribution runs against the elite assumption that most of the public conceptualizes politics like the highly educated. On its face it lends little support to the "revolt of the moderates" assumption (p. 17).
- The Republican resurgence depended mainly on normally Democratic identifiers crossing party lines. Key defections occurred in the two bottom levels at a rate "very significantly greater" than in the group-interest or more ideological levels. So the change was mainly the work of those for whom a liberal–conservative dimension was most far-fetched (p. 17).
- Among group-interest Democrats, the more sophisticated knew Eisenhower was more pro-business than Stevenson. Those who defected did not do so out of a desire for a business-oriented administration (p. 17). The source unit breaks off mid-sentence here.

*Intuition note:* The same election result can be described at elite level as a swing to the right. But the voters who actually moved were largely those not thinking in left–right terms at all.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Measure constraint between A = "opposes expanding Social Security" and B = "opposes nationalization." Use 1 for opposes. Take 5 elite and 5 mass persons.
- Elite A: 1,1,1,0,0. B: 1,1,1,0,0. All 5 agree. Of 3 A-holders, 3 hold B (100%). Of 2 non-A, 0 hold B (0%). Knowing A predicts B perfectly.
- Mass A: 1,1,1,0,0. B: 1,0,0,1,0. Agreement: person 1 yes, 2 no, 3 no, 4 no, 5 yes = 2/5. Of 3 A-holders, 1 holds B (33%). Of 2 non-A, 1 holds B (50%). A does not help predict B; the sign even reverses.
This is a crude version of the static "predictive success" idea (p. 3). The elite pattern would be misread as the mass pattern if one assumed constraint is mirrored downward (p. 6).

## Concepts

- [[belief-system]] (new) — configuration of ideas and attitudes bound by constraint or functional interdependence (p. 3)
- [[centrality]] (new) — role of an element; the element more likely to change when elements clash is, by definition, the less central one (p. 4)
- [[range-of-belief-system]] (new) — breadth of objects a belief system refers to (p. 4)

- [[what-goes-with-what-vs-why]] (new) — two scalar information levels; the premise is that the first diffuses more readily (pp. 9–10)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Constraint as predictability ↔ statistics: correlation between item responses — both measure how much one variable predicts another.
- [structural-analogy] Diffusion of "packages" from creative elites ↔ political science: party cues and heuristics — followers use supplied cues instead of reasoning from principles.
- [metaphor] Elite "optical illusions" ↔ sampling bias: generalizing from a non-representative sample of acquaintances.

## Questions this raises
- How can constraint be measured across many items and groups, and does it rise or fall with education?
- If the mass level is group-interest or cue-driven, what makes elite cues credible to followers?
- Do levels of conceptualization capture stable traits, or do the same people shift level with the question asked?
- What does the mass public do when elite packages change (for example, a party reverses its stance)?
