# Iyengar, S., Sood, G., & Lelkes, Y. (2012). Affect, not ideology: A social identity perspective on polarization. *Public Opinion Quarterly*, 76(3), 405–431. doi:10.1093/poq/nfs038

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[iyengar-2012-affect/main]].

## In one line
The authors argue that mass polarization in the US is better measured as partisans' growing dislike of each other (affective polarization) than as divergence in policy positions, and that campaign exposure is a more plausible driver than policy disagreement, though not the only one.

## The gist
Debate about mass polarization has centred on whether partisans' policy views moved apart. The authors instead use social identity theory: if party is a real group identity, partisans should dislike the out-group. Using surveys from 1960 to 2010, they find stable in-party warmth but falling out-party ratings, rising social distance (marriage question) and more negative trait stereotypes. By 2008 US social distance and trait stereotyping exceed British levels. Policy attitudes are only weakly to moderately related to partisan affect. Campaign exposure, especially negative advertising, is associated with higher affect, though the effects are modest. The paper matters because it moves the target of polarization research from issue positions to group hostility.

**Prerequisites:** survey measures (feeling thermometer), OLS regression and interpreting coefficients, basic social identity / in-group–out-group ideas, the maximalist–minimalist polarization debate.
