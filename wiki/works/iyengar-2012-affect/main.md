# Affect, Not Ideology: A Social Identity Perspective on Polarization

## L0 — One line
The authors argue that mass polarization in the US is better measured as partisans' growing dislike of each other (affective polarization) than as divergence in policy positions, and that campaign exposure is a more plausible driver than policy disagreement, though not the only one.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Debate about mass polarization has centred on whether partisans' policy views moved apart. The authors instead use social identity theory: if party is a real group identity, partisans should dislike the out-group. Using surveys from 1960 to 2010, they find stable in-party warmth but falling out-party ratings, rising social distance (marriage question) and more negative trait stereotypes. By 2008 US social distance and trait stereotyping exceed British levels. Policy attitudes are only weakly to moderately related to partisan affect. Campaign exposure, especially negative advertising, is associated with higher affect, though the effects are modest. The paper matters because it moves the target of polarization research from issue positions to group hostility.

**Prerequisites:** survey measures (feeling thermometer), OLS regression and interpreting coefficients, basic social identity / in-group–out-group ideas, the maximalist–minimalist polarization debate.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The authors propose defining polarization by social distance (affect toward the out-party), treating policy distance as only one way to define it. In their view, because social identity requires negative feeling toward out-groups, then to the extent that party is a meaningful group affiliation, affect is the more appropriate test (pdf 2).
- In-party thermometer ratings stayed near 70, while out-party ratings fell about 15 points since 1988 and averaged just above 30 in 2008 (pdf 8–9).
- Partisan affect beats ideology as an identity: liberal/conservative ratings were flat, and party polarization exceeds racial and religious polarization (pdf 10–12).
- Social distance (inter-party marriage) and trait stereotypes were weak in the US in 1960 and are now strong. The UK was the higher-affect country in 1960, but the US was higher in 2008 and 2010 (pdf 12–17).
- Policy preferences predict net partisan affect (in-party minus out-party rating) only moderately to weakly. Sorted partisans (liberal Democrats, conservative Republicans) are only modestly more polarized in affect. The coefficient on strength of identification is large and rose between 1988 and 2004 (pdf 17–20).
- Campaign exposure (battleground residence, volume of negative ads, the 2008 panel) is associated with higher affect, but the effect sizes are modest (pdf 20–23).
- The authors call campaign messaging a "more plausible" cause. They say media fragmentation may also contribute, and they stress that the causes are not settled (pdf 23–24).
- Implications they draw: biased perceptions, difficulty persuading the out-party, and doubts about the legitimacy of the other side's rule (pdf 24).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Abstract and Introduction (pdf 1–3)
- **The debate.** "Maximalists" (Abramowitz 2010) say partisans' policy views became more extreme. "Minimalists" (Fiorina and Abrams 2009) say most Americans remain centrist and that any centrifugal movement reflects *sorting*, i.e. a closer link between party and ideology (pdf 1). Elites have polarized on issues on every indicator cited (pdf 1–2).
- **Disputes on the mass public.** Some argue elite polarization is an unintended result of institutional changes and not a response to the masses (pdf 2). Others cite fewer moderates and a near doubling, 1972–2004, of the ideological distance between non-activist Democrats and Republicans (pdf 2).
- **The authors' alternative.** Policy division is only one definition of polarization. In their view the more diagnostic indicator is whether partisans see each other as a disliked out-group (pdf 2). The definitional test of social identity needs positive feeling for one's own group and negative feeling toward opposing groups (pdf 2).
- **Hypotheses.** Affect may mix ideological disagreement and group-centric impulses. They claim partisan affect is inconsistently related to policy preferences. They call it more plausible that merely identifying with a party suffices to trigger negative views of the opposition, and that prolonged campaigns reinforce this (pdf 2–3).
- **Claims of the paper.** Partisans increasingly dislike the out-party and impute negative traits to its rank and file. Affect reaches interpersonal judgments, exceeds polarization along other cleavages, and is significantly higher in the US than the UK. The authors describe the evidence as unequivocal that Americans are affectively polarized along party lines (pdf 3).

### Theoretical Framework (pdf 3–4)
- Minimal-group experiments (e.g. overestimating dots) show that trivial or random group membership triggers in-group favouring and out-group negativity. The authors say these findings suggest group-based affect is an ingrained human response (pdf 3).
- Social identity theorists take **identity salience** as the basis for predicting prejudice: the more salient the affiliation, the more biased the beliefs about in- and out-group members (pdf 3). Salience can depend on dispositions (loyalty) or on the information environment (reminders) (pdf 4).
- Expectations: strong partisans carry more biased perceptions, and campaigns heighten the salience of partisan identity among all identifiers (pdf 4).

*Intuition note:* Social identity theory says that belonging to a group, even a trivial one, makes people like their own side and dislike the other. On the authors' argument, to the extent that party is such a group, hostility toward the other party is the more appropriate test of that identity. They also argue that merely identifying with a party suffices to trigger it, so it need not come from policy disagreement.

### Revisiting Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail (pdf 4)
- By the authors' account, presidential campaigns have become longer, better funded, more televised and more heavily covered than the "soft-spoken" 1972 campaign (pdf 4).
- Candidates spend more time attacking than promoting themselves (Geer 2010). Journalists recycle negative messages. The 2004 Swift Boat ad generated more news stories than the war in Iraq (pdf 4).
- Cable (Fox News, MSNBC) and the Internet give partisans commentary that confirms their out-group stereotypes. Exposure to harsh campaign rhetoric is therefore "one potential explanation" (pdf 4).

### Research Design, Data Sets, Measures (pdf 4–8)
- **Plan.** Compare in- and out-party thermometer trends; social distance (marriage) and stereotypes, 1960–2010; US vs UK; party vs gender, race and religion; then two explanations: spillover from ideology and campaign exposure (pdf 4–5).
- **Why the UK.** British parties are rooted in class. Since party identity there reflects both ideological and social cleavages, higher inter-partisan affect is expected, which makes the UK a stringent baseline (pdf 5).
- **Data.**
  - ANES (time series, 1988 and 2004 cross-sections): AAPOR 3 response rates 60–71 percent (pdf 5).
  - YouGov, March 2008 US/UK: opt-in respondents matched to an RDD target sample on race, gender, age, education and imputed party ID; pooled AAPOR 1 rate 42.1 percent (pdf 5).
  - YouGov 2010 eleven-nation study, US part: 42 percent (pdf 5–6).
  - Almond–Verba 1959–60: US 83.3 percent, UK 59 percent (pdf 6).
  - 2004 Blair Center Election Study: N = 2,837, completion 68.1 percent (pdf 6).
  - 2008 AP-Yahoo! News panel: cumulative response rate 9.8 percent over nine waves (pdf 6).
  - Wisconsin Advertising Project: 2004 ads in the top 100 markets, coded for negativity. 2004 is used because 2008 presidential ad data were unavailable (pdf 6).
- **Thermometer.** A 0–100 scale. 0 is "cold" and 100 is "warm". 50 means not knowing the group (before 1968) or no particular warmth or coldness (after 1968) (pdf 6).
  - Targets were "Democrats/Republicans" (1964–1982) and "Democratic/Republican Party" (1978–2008). Both were asked in 1980 and 1982 (pdf 7).
  - **Net partisan affect** is the difference between in- and out-party ratings of self-identified partisans, rescaled to 0–1. For mean comparisons both raw and differenced scores are used. For correlational analysis only the net difference is used (pdf 7).
  - AP-Yahoo 2008 used a four-point favorability scale instead (pdf 7).
- **Marriage item.** Almond–Verba: pleased / displeased / no difference. YouGov 2008: not at all / somewhat / very upset. The 2010 study offered options from very unhappy to very happy (pdf 7).
- **Traits.** YouGov 2008 used nine traits (patriotic, closed-minded, intelligent, hypocritical, selfish, honest, open-minded, generous, mean). Almond–Verba used seven items, including "selfish people", "intelligent people" and "fascists and militarists" (pdf 7–8).
- **Partisanship.** The ANES party ID question, used to create party dummies and a strong-partisan dummy (pdf 8).

### Results 1: In- and out-party affect (pdf 8–11)
- **Expectation.** Out-party ratings should fall. The elite–follower policy divide might also lower in-party ratings (pdf 8).
- **Finding.** In-party ratings average around 70, stable for three decades. Out-party ratings fell about fifteen points since 1988 (pdf 8, fig. 1). The share rating the out-party below 50 rose from about .40 (1980s) to .53 (1990s), .56 (2004) and .63 (2008) (pdf 8).
- **Footnote 7 check.** On a 0–1 likes-dislikes scale (1996, 2004, 2008), in-party liking rose from 0.71 to 0.76 and out-party liking fell from 0.39 to 0.37 (pdf 9).
- **Benchmarks.** In 2008 the out-party mean was just above 30. For comparison: Catholics rated by Protestants 66, Big Business by Democrats 51, gay men and lesbians by Republicans 42, people on welfare by Republicans 50 (pdf 9).
- **Activists** (three or more of five campaign activities). Activists' in–out gap exceeds non-activists' by about 13.3 points in each survey. The linear trend among non-activists is also significant. Activists rose from about 5 percent to nearly 8.5 percent of respondents (pdf 10). The authors say this is "likely partly" due to affect motivating participation (pdf 10).
- **Ideological groups.** Both in- and out-group ratings of liberals and conservatives stay roughly stable (fig. 2). Democrats rate conservatives at 45–50. Republicans rate liberals 5–8 points lower. The authors conclude ideology is a weaker basis of subjective political identity than party (pdf 10).
- **Parties vs partisans (Table 1, 1980 and 1982).**

| Year | In-group | Out-group | Diff. | In-party | Out-party | Diff. | Diff. of diffs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1980 | 71.74 | 52.78 | 18.97 | 71.97 | 46.54 | 25.43 | –6.46* |
| 1982 | 72.71 | 49.03 | 23.68 | 73.80 | 44.26 | 29.54 | –5.86* |

  - In-party and in-partisan ratings barely differ. Out-partisans are rated about 5 points higher than the out-party (p < .01).
  - The correlation between the two measures is r = .69. The authors read this as perhaps showing that dislike of parties extends to people in them. They point to a "person positivity bias" to explain the lower hostility toward people (pdf 11).
- **Electoral outcomes.** Using 1984 and 1992 pre/post waves, there is no evidence that "the agony of defeat" affects out-party sentiment. In 1984 Republicans' ratings of Democrats were more negative after the election (p < .01) (pdf 11).
- **Other cleavages.** Per fig. 3, race and religion are weaker identities than partisanship. Over three decades the partisan cleavage is greater than racial or religious ones (pdf 11).

*Intuition note:* In-party warmth changed little over time. The authors read the data as showing that it is the standing of the out-group that has changed.

### Results 2: Social distance (pdf 11–14)
- **Marriage, 1960.** In the US 5 percent of Republicans and 4 percent of Democrats would be displeased by an out-party marriage. Only 2 and 3 percent would be pleased by an in-party marriage, so party barely mattered for family matters (pdf 12). In the UK 12 percent of Conservatives and 3 percent of Labor supporters would be displeased. The average was significantly higher in the UK (p < .05) (pdf 12).
- **Marriage, 2008 (somewhat or very upset).** Republicans .27, Democrats .20 (difference p < .05). The authors note the party drawing from the upper tail of wealth shows more distance, as in 1960 Britain (pdf 13). UK: Conservatives 10 percent, Labor 19 percent. The authors suggest the Labor increase *may* reflect Thatcherism and a weakened union movement. UK mean .15 vs US mean .24 (p < .001) (pdf 13).
- **Marriage, 2010 (somewhat or very unhappy).** Republicans .49, Democrats .33 (p < .01). The US level exceeds the UK's (p < .01) (pdf 14).
- **Caveat (footnote 9).** The mode (face-to-face vs online) and the wording changed. The authors think the size of the differences makes an artifact improbable, but "the real effect may be somewhat smaller" (pdf 14).

### Results 3: Trait ratings (pdf 14–17)
- **Table 2 (mean number of traits, positive or negative). The 1960 traits are not the same as the 2008 ones.**
  - 1960 US: positive traits in-party .47, out-party .35; negative in-party .01, out-party .08. UK: positive in-party .34, out-party .12 (pdf 15).
  - 2008 US: positive traits in-party 2.86, out-party .62; negative in-party .22, out-party 1.93. UK: positive 1.93 vs .42; negative .13 vs 1.29 (pdf 15).
- **1960.** In-party bias in positive ratings was modest: 0.10 for US Republicans, 0.12 for Democrats, 0.25 for Conservatives, 0.18 for Labor. The cross-national averages were 0.12 (US) and 0.22 (UK), p < .01. Out-party negative stereotyping differed by less than 0.1 in the US, was slightly higher in the UK, and the cross-national difference was .05 (p < .01) (pdf 16).
- **Change.** The authors state that US in-party bias "climbed from 0.47 to 2.86" (pdf 16). These are the in-party averages in Table 2 (see Reading notes).
- **Common traits.** Intelligence in-group favouritism rose from 0.06 to 0.48 (US) and from 0.17 to 0.29 (UK). The out-party "selfish" rating rose from 0.21 to 0.47 (US) and moved from 0.29 to 0.28 (UK) (pdf 16).
- **Stereotypes in the UK, 2008.** In-party positive bias rose to 0.41 among Conservatives and stayed at 0.22 among Labor. Negative beliefs about the other side rose to 0.3 (Conservatives) and 0.28 (Labor). The authors conclude that by 2008 party stereotypes were significantly more polarized among Americans (pdf 16).
- **IRT check (footnote 13).** Treating traits as an indicator of a latent partisan affect, an IRT model reproduces the US > UK pattern. Results are available from the authors (pdf 16).
- **Summary of the three indicators.** The authors say partisan identity is increasingly tied to a sharply divided, us-against-them view of politics (pdf 17); they ascribe positive traits to the in-party and negative traits to opponents, and there is enough animosity to matter in interpersonal life (pdf 17).

*Intuition note:* In 1960 party was hardly a marker of who one would accept in the family or how one saw other people, especially in the US. By 2008 and 2010 party carries clear social meaning in the US, more so than in Britain, where party also reflects class.

### Possible Explanations (pdf 17–23)

#### Spillover from ideological to affective polarization (pdf 17–20)
- **Sorting test.** If ideological disagreement has contributed to affect, sorted partisans (liberal Democrats, conservative Republicans) should show considerably sharper thermometer gaps. The authors report their gaps are only modestly higher (table A4, online) (pdf 17).
- **Regression design.** The 1988 and 2004 ANES. Two issue dimensions, social welfare and cultural. Missing data imputed with Amelia II (25 data sets). Confirmatory factor analysis, a two-factor solution, gives factor scores signed so that higher means more conservative for Republicans and more liberal for Democrats. Net in–out thermometer is regressed on both scores, strength of identification, political interest and demographics. All variables are scaled 0–1. Confidence intervals use Rubin's multiple-imputation degrees of freedom (pdf 18). The models are split by party (footnote 17) (pdf 18).
- **Results (Table 3; n = 830, 954, 592, 485; adjusted R² .16, .17, .25, .27).** Rows are cultural / economic / strong identifier coefficients (pdf 19):

| | Cultural | Economic | Strong identifier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Democrats 1988 | –.06 | .20* | .18* |
| Republicans 1988 | .04 | .12* | .17* |
| Democrats 2004 | .05 | .19* | .26* |
| Republicans 2004 | .04 | .19* | .22* |

  - The authors find that social welfare (economic) preferences more strongly predict affect than cultural preferences, which have no effect. This is "surprising" given the heat of cultural issues (pdf 18).
  - The 12–20-point thermometer difference between the least and most ideological partisans is equivalent to the difference between weak and strong identifiers (pdf 18–19).
  - The strong-identifier coefficient is stronger in 2004. The authors say this suggests a rising premium for partisan identification beyond issue preferences (pdf 19).
  - In-party-only regressions give uniformly weaker policy coefficients than out-party-only regressions; outside Social Welfare preferences among Democrats in 1988, the in-party policy coefficients are always insignificant (pdf 19–20).
  - Dropping the strength-of-identification predictor raises the policy coefficients, but this partly reflects omitting a social identity indicator (footnote 20) (pdf 18).
- **Conclusion of the section.** The non-effect of sorting and the moderate-to-weak policy effects are "at odds with" the view that affect is driven *primarily* by ideology. The two indicators appear to tap distinct forms of polarization (pdf 20). The authors add that the weak link fits the literature showing weak ideological underpinnings of partisan identity: most Americans have difficulty locating parties on issue scales. They say partisan identities are primarily affective attachments (pdf 20).

*Intuition note:* Policy views do relate to how much one dislikes the other party, but weakly. Simply being a strong party identifier matters about as much as being at the ideological extreme.

#### Exposure to negative campaigns (pdf 20–23)
- **Identification idea.** Strategic campaigning (electoral college incentives, polls) exposes otherwise similar voters to very different amounts of campaigning depending on where they live. Battleground-state residents should show stronger affect (pdf 20).
- **Test 1: Blair 2004.** Net thermometer is regressed on battleground status, interest, party, strength of ID, gender, age, race and education. Battleground states are those with a vote difference below 5 percent in 2000 (list on pdf 20). This is a reduced-form estimate of all campaign differences, not an IV estimate, because there are no ad-recall measures (footnote 22) (pdf 20).
  - Table 4 Model 1: battleground b = .02* (N = 2,276, adjusted R² .27) (pdf 21).
  - Model 2: attack-ad volume in the state b = .19*, other ads b = –.11* (N = 1,882, adjusted R² .28). A unit change is about 130,000 ads. Attack and contrast ads are coded negative; presidential, gubernatorial, Senate and House ads are pooled (pdf 21).
  - The authors acknowledge the effect is modest. They explain that ads are only a small part of campaign information and are recycled in national news (pdf 21–22).
- **Test 2: AP-Yahoo 2008 panel.** A hierarchical linear model with random effects for respondent and media market. Predictions: affect rises as the campaign progresses, more steeply in battleground states (pdf 22). Table 5 (N = 1,401): battleground –.01 (no significant effect), wave .02*, wave × battleground .02*, strong identifier .36* (pdf 22). The authors read the interaction as showing that the campaign's effects doubled among battleground-state residents (pdf 23).
- **Conclusion.** The authors say their analyses suggest that negativity and general exposure both contribute to affective polarization, and that campaigns reinforce partisan identity and confirm stereotypes; they call campaigns a possible cause (pdf 23).

*Intuition note:* Because candidates concentrate effort in a few states, living in one is a rough proxy for hearing more campaign messages. The effects are small, but the direction fits the claim that campaigns sharpen partisan feeling.

### Discussion (pdf 23–24)
- The answer to "is the public polarized?" depends on the indicator. The authors argue on construct validity grounds for affect. Most people do not think about parties in ideological terms, and ties to politics are affective and acquired early (pdf 23).
- "Principled" dislike is only a small component. Campaign rhetoric is a more plausible explanation (pdf 23).
- Campaign exposure is "very likely not the strongest factor, much less the only factor". Technology lets people choose congenial sources, news providers slant more, and sources that denigrate the out-party are likely to increase animus (pdf 23–24).
- **Consequences (authors' expectations).** Because of partisan bias in economic perceptions, voters will fail to credit out-party incumbents when the economy grows and fail to penalize in-party incumbents whose performance is suspect. Elites will struggle to persuade out-partisans. Those who impugn opponents' motives are less likely to treat their policies as legitimate and may also be less satisfied with institutions that respond to popular will. ANES "satisfaction with democracy" shows a growing partisan divide since 2000. The authors say dissatisfaction can escalate into protest, and in some cases violence (pdf 24).
- **Closing.** Partisans are affectively polarized; "less clear" what underlies it. The authors say the evidence suggests campaigns are implicated, they see reasons to believe media fragmentation is a further contributory factor, and future research will need to address these explanations more systematically (pdf 24).

## Toy example (ours, not from the paper)
Three respondents of one party rate their own party and the other party (0–100) in two years.

- Year A: in-party 70, 75, 65. Mean = (70 + 75 + 65)/3 = 210/3 = 70. Out-party 45, 50, 40. Mean = 135/3 = 45. Net affect (in − out) = 70 − 45 = 25.
- Year B: in-party unchanged: mean 70. Out-party 30, 35, 25. Mean = 90/3 = 30. Net = 70 − 30 = 40.
- Net affect rose by 40 − 25 = 15, and the whole rise comes from the out-party rating falling 15. This is the shape of the pattern the paper reports (stable in-party near 70, falling out-party).
- Share of out-party ratings below 50: Year A = 2/3 (45 and 40 are below 50; 50 is not). Year B = 3/3. The share rises from .67 to 1.00, like the paper's below-50 indicator.
- Policy views are held fixed here, so any such change in net affect is, by construction, not due to policy movement, and this is the kind of change the authors argue ideology-based measures miss.

## Concepts
- [[affective-polarization]] — partisans' growing dislike of the other party; here the preferred definition of mass polarization, measured by thermometers, social distance and traits (pdf 2)
- [[partisan-sorting]] — closer association of party and ideology; the minimalists' account of what centrifugal movement exists (pdf 1)
- [[social-identity-theory]] (new) — group membership requires positive in-group and negative out-group sentiment; here the test for whether party is a group identity (pdf 2)
- [[identity-salience]] (new) — the more salient an affiliation, the more biased beliefs about in- and out-group; depends on loyalty and on reminders (pdf 3)
- [[social-distance]] (new) — the classic concept (Bogardus) on which the authors base their definition of polarization; they treat the inter-party marriage item and trait stereotypes as indicators of it (pdf 1, 4, 7)
- [[feeling-thermometer]] (new) — 0–100 group rating scale. [[net-partisan-affect]] (new) — in-party minus out-party rating, rescaled 0–1 (pdf 6–7)
- [[person-positivity-bias]] (new) — people respond more favourably to individuals than to abstract targets; the authors cite it as making it unsurprising that out-partisans are rated above the out-party (pdf 11)
- [[selective-exposure]] — choosing agreeable news sources; raised as a further possible contributor (pdf 23–24)
- [[partisan-perceptual-bias]] — partisans see the economy differently; given as a consequence of affect, not tested here (pdf 24)
- [[negative-campaigning]] (new) — attack and contrast ads (both coded "negative"); proposed driver of out-party animus (pdf 4, 21)

## Canonical instances
- [[almond-verba-marriage-item]] — 1960 US partisans mostly indifferent to inter-party marriage, versus 2008/2010 levels (pdf 12–14)
- [[ideological-group-vs-party-thermometers]] — ratings of liberals and conservatives flat while party out-group ratings fall (pdf 10)
- [[battleground-state-design]] (new) — battleground residence and ad volume used as proxies for campaign exposure (pdf 20–22)
- [[swift-boat-ad-2004]] — attack ad that drew more coverage than the Iraq war, as an example of recycled negativity (pdf 4)
- [[agony-of-defeat-test]] (new) — 1984 and 1992 pre/post-election out-party ratings showing no effect of losing (pdf 11)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Minimal-group in-group favouritism ↔ political-science: party identity producing out-party hostility without policy disagreement — same group-membership logic applied to a natural group.
- [structural-analogy] Battleground residence as exposure proxy ↔ causal inference: reduced-form (intent-to-treat style) estimate, with ad volume the "treatment" (the authors themselves mention an instrument, pdf 20).
- [metaphor] Net partisan affect as "social distance" ↔ sociology: Bogardus-style social distance scale, applied to party instead of race or ethnicity.

## Questions this raises
- Does the thermometer measure dislike of party elites, voters or a stereotype? The authors say they cannot tell (pdf 7).
- If campaigns drive affect, why are US out-party ratings falling steadily when campaign effects are small? Do cable and online media explain the rest?
- Could affect also shape which policy positions partisans adopt (reverse causality), given the authors' own concern that party placements may be endogenous to affect (pdf 18, footnote 16)?
- How much of the 1960–2008 contrast is real, given changes in survey mode, wording and trait lists?
