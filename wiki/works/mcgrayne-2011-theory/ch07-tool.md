# From Tool to Theology

## L0 — One line
In the postwar boom, Bayes' rule moved from a practical tool toward an all-encompassing philosophy as Good, Savage and Lindley worked to make it respectable and logically coherent, and zealous advocacy helped fuel decades of conflict.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
How did Bayes' rule go from a lowly practical method to an all-encompassing philosophy? After the war, US statistics boomed and split between pure and applied camps. Three men drove the Bayesian revival: Jack Good (hampered by government secrecy and by trouble explaining his ideas), Jimmie Savage (subjective probability, utility, decisions) and Dennis Lindley (Bayesian departments in Britain). Savage answered the objectivity objection by arguing that opinions converge as data accumulate. The likelihood principle and one-off events gave Bayes practical appeal. Zealotry, with Savage as the extreme case, provoked dogmatic opposition. By the 1960s the philosophical case was largely settled; how to apply it was not.

**Prerequisites:** Bayes' rule in words (prior, likelihood, posterior); the idea of frequentist vs Bayesian statistics.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Wartime success of statistics brought money, growth and splits between pure and applied mathematicians (pp. 97–98).
- Frequentist leaders Neyman and Fisher fought each other bitterly. Bayesian sympathizers saw frequentism as loosely connected ad hoc tests, and Bayes as having an overall rationale (pp. 98–99).
- Good's book, developing Turing's idea, was the first publication heralding the Bayesian revival. His bird-watcher example was a way to reveal a classified codebreaking idea: give unseen cases a small nonzero probability (pp. 99–100).
- Lindley and Savage tried to put statistics on axioms. They later realized they could not move logically from their axioms to frequentist ad hoc methods, and that they had been sliding toward Bayes (p. 101).
- Savage's *Foundations of Statistics* (1954) tied probability to utility, i.e. to decisions. It answered the objectivity objection: opinions converge as data pile up (p. 103).
- The likelihood principle and one-time events gave Bayes practical appeal over frequentism, but Bayesians lacked computing tools (pp. 104–105).
- Bayes acquired a quasi-religious aura. Kass warned that its power depends on the validity of its probabilistic inputs (p. 105).
- Zealotry, especially Savage's, hurt relationships. Lindley built Bayesian departments in Britain (pp. 106–107).

## L3 — Walkthrough
*(The chapter has no numbered sections; headings below follow its order.)*

**Postwar boom (pp. 97–98).** Bailey was turning Credibility into Bayes' rule for insurers. Meanwhile the war had raised the status of applied mathematicians. The Office of Naval Research was the first federal agency formed expressly to fund science. The Navy supported much US mathematical and statistical research until the National Science Foundation arrived in 1950. The Army, Air Force and National Institutes of Health also funded it (p. 97). The statistics "capital" moved from Britain to the US. The number of theoretical statisticians grew a hundred-fold (p. 97). Even Bayesians could get jobs. Some were evangelists seeking mathematical respectability; others were practitioners who wanted to do science (p. 98). Pure and applied mathematicians split. Statisticians formed their own departments, but tension over theory versus application persisted (p. 98).

**Neyman, Fisher and the frequentist camp (pp. 98–99).** Neyman's Berkeley laboratory was then the largest statistics center. It became ingrown, and most hires over 40 years were frequentists (p. 98). Neyman and Fisher were both anti-Bayesian yet insulted each other to the end. Neyman said at 85, "[Bayes] does not interest me. I am interested in frequencies." (p. 99) To Bayesian sympathizers, frequentism looked like loosely connected ad hoc tests. The joke was that you could redo an unwelcome result with a different test. Bayes' rule seemed to have an overall rationale (p. 99).

*Intuition note:* The Bayesian pitch was unity. Bayes' rule seemed to have one overall rationale, whereas frequentism looked like a collection of separate tests.

**Jack Good (pp. 99–101).** Three men set out to make Bayes respectable and coherent: Good, Savage and Lindley (p. 99). Good's *Probability and the Weighing of Evidence* (draft 1946, published 1950) developed Turing's idea. Cold War secrecy caused much of the delay, and few understood it at first. Lindley said Good "did not get his ideas across" (p. 99). After the war Good often used equal priors to decide which hypotheses to follow up (p. 99).

*Bird-watcher example (p. 100).* Good wrote about bird watching to hint at a classified Turing technique for finding letter groups in the German submarine code. Suppose a watcher sees 180 species, many represented by only one bird. Then many species must have been missed entirely. Counting missing species as zero, as a frequentist would, asserts they can never be found. Turing instead gave them a tiny nonzero probability. He wanted to learn about rare letter groupings absent from his intercepted messages. By estimating the frequency of the missing ones, he could use Bayes to estimate the probability of such groupings in a larger sample and in the next Enigma message (p. 100). The author says DNA decoders and AI analysts later adopted the same technique (p. 100).

Good moved from post to post and settled at Virginia Tech in 1967, working far from the Bayesian mainstream. In the 1960s US Bayesian work was concentrated at Chicago, Wisconsin, Harvard and Carnegie Mellon (p. 100). He circulated his "Private List" of over 800 articles and four books, many marked classified. He could reveal the Enigma success only as the British declassified it (pp. 100–101). He remained an independent voice (p. 101).

**Lindley and Savage become Bayesians (pp. 101–102).** Lindley wanted to make statistics a respectable branch of mathematics based on axioms and theorems, as Kolmogorov had done for probability (p. 101). In 1954 he found that Savage had done it better. Each thought he had only put traditional techniques on a rigorous footing. Later they realized they could not move logically from their axioms to frequentism's ad hoc methods. Lindley said they were "fools" for not seeing the consequences (p. 101). Savage was nearly blind and was repeatedly judged unfit for study, but he earned a doctorate. He worked with Milton Friedman at Columbia, then went to Chicago (p. 102). Fermi used Bayes' theorem and told a student to use it. Feynman suggested Bayes for comparing physics theories (p. 102).

**Savage's *Foundations of Statistics* (pp. 103–104).** Savage found Borel, Ramsey and de Finetti, who had legitimized subjectivity (p. 103). His 1954 book was the third Bayesian landmark of the 1950s, after Bailey and Good (1950) and Lindley's paper (1953). He turned Bayes from inference into decision-making (p. 103). He called himself a subjectivist and a personalist. Probability was a measure of belief, usable for a bet such as a horse race, where bettors share information but judge differently and the race cannot be replicated. Expert opinion was to be quantified and included. He argued that rational people make subjective choices to minimize expected losses, combining utility (quantified reward) with probability (quantified uncertainty) (p. 103).

*Objection:* if priors differ between researchers, what happens to objectivity? *Savage's answer* (elaborating Jeffreys): as data increase, subjectivists move into agreement, as with the greenhouse effect or smoking and lung cancer. With little data they disagree; with piles of data they agree. Lindley: "That's the way science is done." (p. 103)

*Intuition note:* Different starting beliefs matter most when data are scarce. Enough shared evidence pulls people toward the same conclusion.

The book did not use the word "Bayesian" and mentioned Bayes' rule once. It spread slowly. Readers hoped for a how-to manual, and Bayesians lacked computing machinery, so they were limited to simple integrals (p. 104). Savage himself said he was not much inclined to rely on high-speed machines (p. 104). His examples were whimsical (aspirin and rabbits' ears). Lindley said statistics might have gained from more operational methods (p. 104). Savage first argued for subjective priors using frequentist aspects. He thought he came to Bayes "seriously only through recognition of the likelihood principle" (p. 104).

*Likelihood principle (as McGrayne states it, p. 104):* all the information in experimental data is captured in the likelihood part of Bayes' theorem, and the prior played no role. Practically, scientists could stop an experiment when satisfied or out of resources, whereas non-Bayesians had to continue until a frequency criterion was met. Bayesians could focus on what happened, not on what could have happened under Neyman–Pearson's sampling plan (p. 104). By the early 1960s Savage fully accepted Bayes (p. 104).

**Why Bayes appealed (pp. 105–106).** Frequentism came from genetics and biology, so it concerns groups and large aggregations. Schrödinger said "The individual case [is] entirely devoid of interest." Bayesians could handle one-time events, such as a plane being late or the US being at war in five years (p. 105). They could combine information from different sources and treat observables as random variables. To Savage's question about whiskey and snake bite, frequentists were stumped, while Bayesians said it "probably does more harm than good" (p. 105).

Bayes now looked like a philosophy or "state of mind." **Spiegelhalter:** many scientists reject using probability for lack of knowledge or one-off events. **Kass:** the theorem feels elegant and powerful, but, he said, captivated advocates tend to overlook that its power depends on the validity of its probabilistic inputs (p. 105). Zealots provoked "dogmatic opposition," and the fight "alienated many bystanders" (p. 106).

**Savage's end and Lindley's campaign (pp. 106–107).** Savage became a "messianic Bayesian." He and Lindley saw Bayes as the one-and-only winner-take-all method; they felt it was right and other views wrong, and that compromise was neither necessary nor desirable (p. 106). **Kruskal:** those who disagreed were treated as inimical or stupid, which strained old friendships (p. 106). Savage left Chicago in 1960 amid turmoil and told his colleagues, "I proved the Bayesian argument in 1954. None of you have found a flaw in the proof and yet you still deny it. Why?" (p. 106). He moved to Yale in 1964 and died in 1971 at 53 (p. 106). Lindley built Bayesian groups at Aberystwyth and then at University College London, whose statistics department was a "temple of frequentism" when he arrived (p. 107). He held that Bayes is "a way of looking at the whole of statistics." The UK got ten Bayesian departments; Berkeley stayed anti-Bayesian (p. 107). By the 1960s the philosophical rationale was largely settled, but how to apply Bayes remained controversial (p. 107). Lindley's influence as a teacher and organizer bore fruit in the next generation, while Savage's book spread Bayesian methods to the military, business, history, game theory, psychology and beyond; despite his whimsical examples, he personally encouraged researchers who applied Bayes to life-and-death problems (p. 107).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two scientists test whether a coin is biased (B: P(heads) = 0.8) or fair (F: 0.5). Each head multiplies the odds B:F by 0.8/0.5 = 1.6. Scientist A starts at P(B) = 0.5 (odds 1). Scientist C starts at P(B) = 0.1 (odds 1/9). After 10 heads, the factor is 1.6¹⁰ ≈ 109.95.
- A: odds 109.95, so P(B) = 109.95/110.95 ≈ 0.991.
- C: odds 109.95/9 ≈ 12.22, so P(B) = 12.22/13.22 ≈ 0.924.

After 20 heads the factor is 109.95² ≈ 12,089:
- A: 12,089/12,090 ≈ 0.99992.
- C: odds ≈ 1343.2, so P(B) ≈ 1343.2/1344.2 ≈ 0.99926.

The gap between the two shrinks from 0.4 at the start to under 0.001, which is Savage's agreement-with-more-data point.

## Concepts

- [[prior-distribution]] — prior opinion, which may differ between researchers (p. 103)
- [[likelihood]] — the part of Bayes' theorem giving the probability of new data (p. 104)
- [[frequentist-vs-bayesian]] — frequentism as ad hoc tests about frequencies and groups; Bayes as a unified rule that also handles one-off events (pp. 99, 105)

- [[convergence-of-opinion]] (new) — on Savage's argument, subjectivists with different priors move into agreement as data accumulate (p. 103)
- [[utility-and-decision]] (new) — combining quantified reward with quantified uncertainty; Savage argued that rational people choose so as to minimize expected loss (p. 103)
