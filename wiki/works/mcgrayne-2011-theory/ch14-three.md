# Three Mile Island

## L0 — One line
In the 1970s Bayes' rule was stuck in a divided, small community, but the Rasmussen nuclear-safety report used Bayesian methods without naming them, and the 1979 Three Mile Island accident revived that report; from 1981 industry studies used Bayes openly.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how Bayes' rule fared in the 1970s and how it got back into public use. By then the Bayesians had lost leaders, were few, and quarrelled among themselves. Frequentists held an "uneasy upper hand" and led centres such as Berkeley and Stanford. Rasmussen's team was asked to study nuclear-plant safety. It had no data on core melts, so it turned to equipment failure rates and expert opinion, with Bayesian analysis. To avoid an argument over methods it called its approach "subjectivistic" and used Raiffa's decision trees instead of Bayes' equation. The 1979 Three Mile Island accident made the report look prescient. From 1981 industry studies used Bayes openly. Since then, risk analysis has used frequentist and Bayesian methods side by side.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond the idea of a prior (starting) belief updated by data; "core melt" means nuclear fuel melting in a reactor; a "decision tree" is a diagram of choices and their probabilities; "frequentist" means a statistician who treats probability only as long-run frequency.

## L2 — Key ideas
- By the 1970s Bayesianism was in the doldrums: leaders had died or moved on, and the community was small (perhaps 100 at the 1979 Valencia conference) (pp. 176–177).
- The messianic hope that Bayes would replace frequentism faded; pragmatists spoke of synthesizing Bayesian and non-Bayesian methods, and Mosteller and Tukey agreed the least controversial ideal was a frequency-based prior or a "gentle" prior that new data can overwhelm (p. 177).
- Bayesians also fought each other (subjective vs objective priors) and sometimes used frequentist methods themselves (p. 178).
- Lindley expected change to come through applied statisticians, not theoreticians (p. 178).
- The Rasmussen team had no core-melt data, so it used equipment failure rates plus expert opinion in a Bayesian way (p. 179).
- It avoided the word "Bayes" and used Raiffa's decision trees, but the report was full of probability distributions for unknowns (p. 180).
- The 1979 Three Mile Island accident made the report look prescient and revived it (p. 180).
- From 1981 industry studies openly used Bayes. Risk analysis now uses frequentist and Bayesian methods in many fields (p. 181).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: the long battle (p. 176).** Mosteller and Tukey, in 1967, described the "battle of Bayes" as raging for over two centuries. The narrator summarises the history: Bayes turned his back on his rule, Laplace glorified it, it was both used and undermined in the 1800s, derided in the early 1900s, and used in secrecy in the Second World War. By the 1970s it was "sliding into the doldrums" (p. 176).

**Loss of leadership (pp. 176–177).** Savage died in 1971. Jeffreys and Jaynes campaigned in vain for Bayes in physical science, and Jaynes's fervor put colleagues off. Lindley quit administration in 1977. Good moved to Virginia Tech. Madansky moved from RAND to business. Box turned to quality control and Japanese industry. Raiffa turned to public-policy negotiation. Schlaifer tried to program computers (pp. 176–177).

**A small community (p. 177).** When Berger became a Bayesian in the 1970s, he could track almost all activity. Almost every well-known Bayesian came to the first international Bayes conference (Valencia, 1979) — perhaps 100 in all. The aim of replacing frequentism was gone. Pragmatists spoke of synthesis. Mosteller and Tukey agreed that the least controversial ideal was a frequency-based prior, or a "gentle" prior based on beliefs but ready to be overwhelmed by new information (p. 177).
- Box, Hunter and Hunter (1978) left Bayes out of *Statistics for Experimenters* because it was too controversial to sell, and the book was a bestseller (p. 177).
- Swinburne (1979) put personal opinions into both prior and data and concluded that God was more than 50% likely to exist. Later he put Jesus' resurrection at "something like 97 percent". The narrator says many nonstatisticians counted his lack of careful measurement against Bayes (p. 177).
- Berkeley (Neyman) and Stanford stayed strongly frequentist (p. 177).
- Without powerful computers, many Bayesians and anti-Bayesians retreated into theory. Chernoff moved toward applied work. He was not a Bayesian, but he advised Holmes: "Start out as a Bayesian thinking about it, and you'll get the right answer" (pp. 177–178).

**Infighting (p. 178).** At a 1976 conference Berger saw half the room yelling at the other half. Priors split between the subjective (Savage) and the objective (Jeffreys), and no definitive experiment could decide between them. Diaconis was angry when Pratt used frequentist methods because of computer limits. Lindley blamed Diaconis for using frequency methods in an article. By 1978 Neyman–Pearson frequentists had "an uneasy upper hand" (p. 178).
- De Finetti predicted a Bayesian paradigm shift after about 50 years, i.e. after 2020. Efron put the probability of a Bayesian twenty-first century at .15 (p. 178).
- Lindley: change is slower than he expected. Talking to a statistician for an hour did not convert him. He thought the shift would come through applied statisticians. On how to encourage it: "Attend funerals" (p. 178).

*Intuition note:* Bayesians had a broad system of theory, yet in the 1970s they were held back by a lack of powerful computers, few realistic applications and open quarrels among themselves.

**The Rasmussen study (pp. 179–180).**
- Setting: Eisenhower launched nuclear power in 1953. Twenty years later, private firms ran 50 plants with no comprehensive safety study. When Congress debated absolving owners of liability, the Atomic Energy Commission ordered a study (p. 179).
- Leader: Norman Rasmussen, a physicist and engineer, not a statistician (b. 1927; MIT nuclear engineering from 1958) (p. 179).
- Problem: no plant accident had ever occurred. Like Madansky at RAND, the team used failure rates of pumps, valves and other equipment. These were not enough, so it turned to expert opinion and Bayesian analysis (p. 179).
- Politics: frequentists considered expert judgment subjective and not reproducible, and banned it. After Vietnam, trust in experts fell. Congress cancelled the SST in 1971. "No Nukes" protests were spreading (p. 179).
- Disguise: normally Bayes' theorem would combine opinion and failure rates, but the panel wanted no fight over method. They used Raiffa's decision trees, which had Bayesian roots, and called their approach "subjectivistic". They thought this kept them from being Bayesians (pp. 179–180).
- The 1974 report was full of probability distributions for failure rates and human error, which frequentists did not assign to unknowns. Its only Bayes reference was in appendix III: the Bayesian interpretation "can also be used" (p. 180).
- Fate: some statistics were damned. In January 1979 the Nuclear Regulatory Commission withdrew support (p. 180).

**Three Mile Island and after (pp. 180–181).** Two months later the Three Mile Island-2 core was damaged, and *The China Syndrome* appeared. The nuclear industry collapsed. About 20% of U.S. power came from 104 plants in 2003, but no new facility had been ordered since 1978 (p. 180).
- The report now looked prescient. Earlier experts thought severe core damage was very unlikely but catastrophic. The report concluded the reverse: damage was more probable than expected, but consequences would not always be catastrophic. It identified human error and release of radioactivity outside the building. It also identified the sequence of events that caused the accident (p. 180).
- In 1981 two industry-supported studies used Bayes' theorem and admitted it. They combined equipment-failure probabilities with specific data from the Zion and Indian Point plants (p. 181).
- Since then risk analysis has used both frequentist and Bayesian methods in chemical safety, nuclear plants, waste repositories, Mars contamination, bridges and mineral exploration. Risk analysis is also identifying so-called "unuseful" safety regulations that can presumably be abandoned (p. 181).
- Subjective judgment still bothers many physical scientists. Avoiding the word "Bayes" is "no longer necessary—or an option" (p. 181).

*Intuition note:* When direct data are lacking, the Rasmussen team felt it had no choice but to ask experts; Bayes' theorem would normally be the way to combine that opinion with failure-rate data, but the team avoided naming it.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
An expert says a pump's chance of failing per use is either 0.01 (belief 0.7) or 0.10 (belief 0.3). Test data: 1 failure in 10 uses.
- Likelihood for $p=0.01$: $10\times0.01\times0.99^9 = 0.1\times0.9135 = 0.09135$.
- Likelihood for $p=0.10$: $10\times0.10\times0.9^9 = 1\times0.3874 = 0.3874$.
- Prior × likelihood: $0.7\times0.09135=0.06395$; $0.3\times0.3874=0.11623$. Sum $=0.18018$.
- Posterior: $0.06395/0.18018=0.355$ for $p=0.01$; $0.11623/0.18018=0.645$ for $p=0.10$.

The sparse data shifted belief from 70% on "reliable" to about 35.5%. Opinion and data are combined in one calculation.
