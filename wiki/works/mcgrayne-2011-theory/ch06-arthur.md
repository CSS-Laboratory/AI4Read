# Dead and Buried Again; Arthur Bailey

## L0 — One line
After WWII Bayes' rule was widely treated as disreputable, but insurance actuary Arthur Bailey argued in 1950 that the actuaries' working Credibility methods were Bayesian, and declared that they worked.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Where did the first public challenge to the anti-Bayesian status quo come from after the war? Chapter 5 shows Bayes' rule scorned in universities and government. Chapter 6 shows the challenge coming from a business actuary, Arthur Bailey. Taught to distrust priors, he found that insurers who revised old rates with new claims data had methods that worked. In a 1950 paper he announced that their Credibility formula derives from Bayes' theorem and urged restoring prior knowledge to statistical theory. After his early death, actuaries, his son, and theorists Savage, de Finetti and Bühlmann carried his ideas further. Here Bayes' rule lives on in insurance practice while academic statistics scorns it.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a prior and of updating it with data; the contrast between frequentist and Bayesian statistics. An actuary is an insurance statistician who sets premium rates; Credibility is the actuaries' way of weighting extra evidence by how believable it is.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Bayes' rule came out of WWII more suspect, because its wartime successes were classified. One prominent statistician noted a lingering idea that inverse probability was unsound, and a bureau statistician half-jokingly called a Bayesian colleague "un-American" (p. 87).
- "Bayes" still meant equal priors, and unless declared otherwise a statistician was considered a frequentist (p. 87).
- Frequentist work framed nearly all papers in one leading journal and most land-grant agricultural studies. The 1956 ASA president barely mentioned Bayes. Visiting statistician David Blackwell, not yet a Bayesian, told a RAND economist that a one-off war question made no sense (pp. 87–88).
- Actuaries kept using Bayesian techniques because they were practical business people. They asked how much present rates should change (p. 92).
- Bailey tried to prove these methods unsound, failed, and came to prefer them. He concluded that only a "suicidal" actuary would use Fisher's maximum likelihood (p. 92).
- In 1950 he announced that the Credibility formula derives from Bayes' theorem, and he attacked frequentism (pp. 93–94).
- In 1955 chief actuary Longley-Cook, asked about midair collisions with no past experience of them, warned of 0 to 4 in ten years. The text says it proved correct (pp. 94–95).
- Bailey's ideas reached university theorists slowly: Mayerson wrote about them in the early 1960s, and Savage, de Finetti and Bühlmann helped spread them (pp. 95–96).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Chapter 5. Dead and buried again
- With its wartime successes classified, Bayes' rule emerged from the war more suspect. Statistics books and papers stressed that they did not use it. When Jack Good discussed the method at the Royal Statistical Society, the next speaker opened with "After that nonsense . . ." (p. 87).
- "Bayes" still meant equal priors. It did not yet mean making inferences or predictions by updating observational data (p. 87).
- Examples of hostility (p. 87):
  - The National Bureau of Standards suppressed a report to Aberdeen Proving Ground in the 1950s because it used subjective Bayesian methods.
  - A bureau statistician half-jokingly called a colleague "un-American" for being Bayesian.
  - Harvard Business School professors called Bayesian colleagues "socialists and so-called scientists".
  - A prominent statistician wrote that a lingering idea remained "in some quarters" that inverse probability was "not quite nice" and unsound. Unless declared otherwise, a statistician was considered a frequentist.
- The Bayesian community was small and isolated. Prewar work by Ramsey, Jeffreys and de Finetti lay unread. Nearly all papers in the *Annals of Mathematical Statistics* concerned issues framed by Neyman's frequentist work of the 1930s (p. 87).
- Thanks to Fisher's genetics research and Kempthorne's anti-Bayesian stance, agricultural studies at most land-grant institutions relied on frequentism (pp. 87–88).
- Gertrude Cox, ASA president in 1956, barely mentioned Bayes. The first practical article on how scientists could use Bayesian analysis would not appear until 1963 (p. 88).
- **Blackwell's reply (1950, RAND):** An economist preparing an Air Force budget asked visiting statistician David Blackwell how to assess the probability of a major war within five years. Blackwell, who had not yet become a Bayesian, said the question just didn't make sense. His reasoning was that probability applies to long sequences of repeatable events, this was a unique situation, and the probability was 0 or 1. The economist said several other statisticians had told him the same (p. 88).
- **Lindley's verdict:** the "upstart" Bayesian movement was being "contained, largely by being ignored". Another statistician recalled that many thought Bayes was "dead and buried" (p. 88).

*Intuition note:* Blackwell's reply shows the frequency view of probability at work. If probability applies only to long sequences of repeatable events, a unique event such as a war gets no meaningful probability from statistics (p. 88).

### Chapter 6. Arthur Bailey
**Background (pp. 91–92).**
- After WWII the first public challenge to the anti-Bayesian status quo came from a Bible-quoting business executive and insurance actuary, Arthur L. Bailey, not from the military or university statisticians (p. 91).
- His father was fired and blackballed by Boston banks. Bailey studied actuarial statistics at Michigan (BS 1928) (p. 91).
- After nine years "in bananas" at United Fruit, in 1937 he moved to New York to set premium rates for the American Mutual Alliance (p. 91).

**His conversion (p. 92).**
- Underwriters there used semi-empirical "sledge hammer" Bayesian techniques developed in 1918 for workers' compensation. University statisticians had long since virtually outlawed them (p. 92).
- Actuaries would not discard prior knowledge. They asked "How much should the present rates be changed?", not what the new rates should be (p. 92).
- Example (author's): a Bayesian estimating someone's ice cream consumption would combine recent personal data with national dessert trends (p. 92).
- Bailey's professors, influenced by Fisher and Neyman, had taught that priors were "more horrid than 'spit'" (a polite actuary's words). The teaching was that statisticians should have no prior opinions about their next experiments and should use only directly relevant observations, rejecting peripheral, nonstatistical information. No standard methods existed for judging the credibility of prior knowledge or combining it with other statistical information (p. 92).
- In his first New York year Bailey tried to prove the actuarial procedures mathematically unsound. After intense struggle he realised that actuarial sledgehammering worked, and he even preferred it to the elegance of frequentism. He wanted a large volume of data to weigh more than a small sample, and this felt surprisingly "logical and reasonable" (p. 92).
- **Bailey's conclusion:** only a "suicidal" actuary would use Fisher's maximum likelihood, which assigned zero probability to nonevents. Many businesses file no claims, so that method would give premiums too low to cover future losses (p. 92).

**The paper (pp. 92–94).**
- He spent WWII studying the problem, working alone, isolated from academics and from actuarial colleagues (pp. 92–93). In 1947 he became chief actuary of the New York State Insurance Department (p. 93).
- He was building a mathematical foundation to justify using current rates as priors in Bayes' theorem. Reviewing Whitney's workers' compensation mathematics, he affirmed the Bayesian roots of Credibility theory. His notation was old-fashioned and hard to follow (p. 93).
- He tried to assign partial weights to supplementary evidence according to its credibility, meaning its subjective believability. Like Molina, he used Laplace's more complex system rather than Bayes' own (p. 93).
- He opened the paper with a biblical justification for believing in prior beliefs (p. 93).
- He read his best-known paper on May 22, 1950, as a Kemper vice president. Its title: "Credibility Procedures: Laplace's Generalization of Bayes' Rule and the Combination of Collateral [that is, prior] Knowledge with Observed Data" (p. 93).
- Message to actuaries (pp. 93–94):
  - He praised them for standing almost alone against the statistical establishment and for staging the only organized revolt against the frequentists' sampling philosophy.
  - Their practice went "beyond anything that has been proven mathematically", but "it works".
  - He announced that the Credibility formula was derived from Bayes' theorem. He noted that Bayes' friend Richard Price would today be considered an actuary.
  - He ended with a call to reinstate prior knowledge in statistical theory.
- Richard von Mises of Harvard praised the speech. He wrote that he hoped it would make the attacks on Bayes theory, initiated by Fisher, "fade out" (p. 94).
- Bailey died of a heart attack on 12 August 1954, aged 49 (p. 94).

*Intuition note:* The text presents Bailey's approach as modifying old data with new rather than discarding the old. It gives partial weights to supplementary evidence according to its credibility, and lets a large volume of data weigh more than a small sample (pp. 92–93).

**Longley-Cook's forecast (pp. 94–95).**
- At the 1954 Christmas party, INA's CEO (dressed as Santa) asked chief actuary L. H. Longley-Cook for the probability of two planes colliding in midair. There had never been a serious commercial midair collision. The author says an orthodox statistician had to answer with a resounding no (p. 94).
- On 6 January 1955 he warned of "anything from 0 to 4" air-carrier collisions over ten years. He based this on available data on airline accidents in general and advised raising premium rates and buying reinsurance (p. 95).
- **Author's report:** the prediction proved correct two years later. A Grand Canyon collision killed 128. A collision four years after that over New York killed 133 (p. 95).

**Later developments (pp. 95–96).**
- Bailey's son, Robert A. Bailey, used Bayesian techniques to justify merit rates for good drivers. Merit rating was regarded as unsound because one car has inadequate credibility. With Leroy Simon he showed that Canadian safe-driving discount data could be used to update existing U.S. statistics (p. 95).
- Robert Bailey also rated insurance companies using nonstatistical, subjective information, such as the quality and drinking habits of managers (p. 95).
- In time, enormous amounts of data made Bayes' rule obsolete in insurance, as the slide rule became obsolete (p. 95).
- To the few actuaries who understood him, Bailey was a da Vinci or Michelangelo. News percolated slowly to theorists. In the early 1960s Mayerson wrote about Bailey's role in Credibility theory (p. 95).
- Savage, then in Ann Arbor, later visited de Finetti. At a conference in Trieste de Finetti spread the word, and it was the first time most statisticians had heard of Bailey (pp. 95–96).
- Bühlmann had studied in Neyman's Berkeley department in the 1950s, "when it was kind of dangerous to pronounce the Bayesian point of view". He built a general Bayesian theory of credibility, which statisticians carried far beyond insurance. He renamed the prior a "structural function", believing this helped Continental Europe escape some of the "religious" quarrels over Bayes' rule (p. 96).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Aim: update a prior claim rate with a small new sample. Take a prior worth 100 cars with 2 claims, so prior mean $= 2/100 = 0.02$. A new fleet of 50 cars has 0 claims.
- Maximum likelihood on the new fleet alone: $0/50 = 0$. A zero premium is the problem Bailey described (p. 92).
- Combined (pseudo-count Beta–Binomial update): $(2+0)/(100+50) = 2/150 \approx 0.0133$.
- Weights: prior $100/150 \approx 0.667$, data $50/150 \approx 0.333$. Check: $0.667\times0.02 + 0.333\times0 \approx 0.0133$.
- With 500 cars and 0 claims: $2/600 \approx 0.0033$, so more data pulls the estimate further from the prior.

This illustrates modifying old rates with new data in proportion to data volume. It is not Bailey's own formula, which these pages do not give.

## Concepts
- [[frequentist-vs-bayesian-inference]] — as described here: Bailey's professors taught using only directly relevant observations, and Blackwell said probability applies to long sequences of repeatable events. The dispute is over priors and one-off events (pp. 87–88, 92).
- [[prior-distribution]] — prior knowledge such as current insurance rates. Bailey was building a foundation to justify using it as the prior in Bayes' theorem (pp. 92–93).
- [[bayes-theorem]] — the rule for combining prior knowledge with observed data. Bailey announced that the Credibility formula was derived from it (pp. 93–94).
- [[credibility-theory]] (new) — actuarial theory developed for workers' compensation. Bailey tried to assign partial weights to supplementary evidence by its credibility (subjective believability) and affirmed its Bayesian roots (p. 93).
- [[likelihood]] — Bailey rejects Fisher's maximum likelihood for insurance because it assigns zero probability to nonevents (p. 92).
- [[subjective-probability]] — subjective, nonstatistical information, such as the quality of a company's managers, used in ratings (p. 95).
