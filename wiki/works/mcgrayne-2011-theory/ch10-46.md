# 46,656 Varieties

## L0 — One line
In the 1950s–60s Bayesian ideas multiplied, opened cracks in frequentist positions (Stein's averages, fiducial probability) and gave answers that could differ sharply from p-values, but few people applied them publicly to real problems, so Bayesians stayed stymied until they could prove their method superior.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks how the Bayesian–frequentist fight developed once Bayes' rule was revived. The answer is a mix of theory and personalities. Bayesian "varieties" multiplied. Stein's shrinkage turned out to match a Bayes-based insurance formula. Lindley challenged Fisher's fiducial probability and showed that Bayesian and p-value answers can differ strongly. Fisher and Neyman refused priors they could not believe. Medicine, the NSA, the CIA and law tried Bayes, with mixed results. Computing power was limited and institutional support was thin. The author concludes that few people publicly applied Bayes to real problems, so Bayesians were "stymied" until they could prove their method superior (p. 136).

**Prerequisites:** what a prior is, and a sample mean versus a prior guess; the idea of a p-value (a "how surprising is this under the null hypothesis" number).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Prior-free frequentist statistics was opposed to Bayes' subjective prior, and Bayesian theory split into many "varieties" (p. 129).
- Stein's shrinkage beat simple averages for related quantities. Bayesians read it as using a hidden prior, which Stein rejected (pp. 130–131).
- Stein–James and the actuarial credibility formula are the same formula, and Bayesian in origin (p. 131).
- Cracks formed in Fisher's favourite theory, fiducial probability, and the likelihood principle added another fissure (p. 132).
- Lindley's Paradox: with a precise hypothesis and huge data, Bayes and p-values can give opposite answers (p. 133).
- Fisher's objection: not knowing is not the same as assigning equal probabilities. He used Bayes when he had trustworthy priors (p. 134).
- Empirical Bayes was a compromise, approximations eased the computing burden, and Bayesian institutions were built slowly (pp. 134–135).
- Medicine, NSA, CIA and law tried Bayes with mixed results (pp. 135–136).
- Few public real-world applications left the revival unproven (p. 136).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Noisy schism and many varieties (p. 129).**
- The dispute centred on the subjective prior. Fisher and Neyman found importing outside knowledge "anathema". Bayesian theorists on the defensive tried to avoid priors altogether (p. 129).
- **Good's claim:** "at least 46656" interpretations of Bayes, more than there were statisticians. Many varieties attracted only their creators, and some modern statisticians say the hairsplitting produced little pathbreaking theory (p. 129).
- The term "inverse probability" was giving way to "Bayesian inference". **Shafer's view:** much that has been written about the history of probability is distorted by an English-centric point of view (p. 129).
- **Lindley (2008):** he is "almost convinced" Laplace mattered more than Bayes. He admits his ignorance of Laplace may be cultural, and that he is biased (p. 130).

**Box and Stein's Paradox (pp. 130–132).**
- Box, Fisher's son-in-law, came to see statistics as closer to science than to mathematics. At Wisconsin in 1960 his course "became more and more Bayesian" (p. 130).
- **Box:** "Comparing averages looked right to me until Stein" (p. 130).
- Stein studied estimating a mean. His method, called shrinkage, produced more accurate predictions than simple averages. He avoided discussing Bayes (p. 130).
- It applies to comparing *related* statistics: chicken-breed egg yields, batting averages, roofing-company compensation exposure (pp. 130–131).
- **Bayesian reading, not Stein's:** Stein, like farmers who would laugh at a "million eggs" hen, effectively used a hyperdistribution about "chicken-ness". A hot .400 start by a mediocre batter should be read against prior knowledge (p. 131).
- Stein and James simplified the method in 1961. Their formula is identical, apart from symbols, to Whitney's actuarial credibility formula. The text layer gives Whitney as $x = P + z(p-P)$ and Stein–James as $z = \bar y + c(y-\bar y)$. This reading is partly reconstructed, because the bar marks are garbled. Both pull related data toward the mean (p. 131).
- Only Arthur Bailey had recognised its Bayesian roots (p. 131).
- **Stein:** Bayes' framework was a "negative" and subjective priors "completely inappropriate" (p. 131).
- Box found that Bayesian methods improved predictions for time series, and that without them Stein's Paradox did not work there (p. 131). **Box:** "You can't take numbers out of context" (p. 132).
- Frequentists and Bayesians battled for years over Stein's Paradox, in part because neither side seemed to be all right or all wrong (p. 132).

*Intuition note:* Shrinkage means pulling each group estimate toward the common mean, so related estimates cluster more tightly. Bayesians explained why this helps by a prior about similar cases (a hyperdistribution), but Stein did not accept that explanation.

**Cracks in Fisher's position (p. 132).**
- Fiducial probability: Lindley showed in 1958 that with uniform priors it gives the same answers as Bayes.
- **Savage:** it tries "to make the Bayesian omelet without breaking the Bayesian eggs." **Box:** it was beginning to look like "a sneaky way of doing Bayes."
- Birnbaum derived Barnard's likelihood principle from generally accepted frequentist principles. It says only observed data matter, not data that might have arisen but did not. **Another frequentist** complained that Birnbaum was proposing "to push the clock back 45 years," but said this at least put him ahead of the Bayesians, who wanted to turn the clock back 150 years. **Savage** called it a "historic occasion."

**Lindley's Paradox and p-values (pp. 132–133).**
- Lindley (1957), elaborating on Jeffreys, showed that a precise hypothesis tested with vast data can make the two approaches give "diametrically opposite results."
- Jahn (1987), who concluded this supported psychokinesis, reported 104,490,000 random-generator trials, 18,471 more "influenced" results than chance (0.018%), p-value as small as 0.00015. Even with p as small as 0.00015, the frequentist would reject the hypothesis that the couch-sitter has no influence, while the same evidence convinces a Bayesian that it is almost certainly true.
- "Six years later" (the text gives no year), Savage, Lindman and Edwards showed the two can differ by significant amounts even with everyday-sized data. A Bayesian with any sensible prior and a sample of 20 gets an answer ten or more times larger than the p-value.

*Intuition note:* A tiny p-value says the data would be surprising if chance alone acted. It does not say how plausible chance is compared with the alternative, which a prior helps decide.

**Personal clashes (pp. 133–134).**
- Lindley says he found "a very basic, serious error" in Fisher's third book: that fiducial probability does not obey the rules of probability, and he produced an example. By his account Fisher was furious (p. 133).
- Lindley recalls that his paper on prior distributions at Neyman's 1960 Berkeley symposium caused "the only serious, public row" of his statistics career, with Neyman furious in public; he says Savage leapt to his defence (p. 133).
- Box proposed equal priors (one-fifth each for five hypotheses). **Fisher replied:** thinking you don't know is not the same as thinking all possibilities are equally probable. Box later agreed. The author says this distinction kept Fisher from accepting Bayes' rule. Like Neyman, Fisher agreed he would use Bayes–Laplace if he ever saw a prior he could believe, and he did so for experiments where he knew his laboratory animals' pedigrees (p. 134).

**Compromises and institutions (pp. 134–135).**
- Empirical Bayes estimates the initial probabilities from relative frequency, then applies the rest of Bayes' rule. Egon Pearson made an early stab in 1925, Turing used a variant in the Second World War, Robbins proposed it in 1955, and Neyman recommended it. Empirical Bayesians had little impact on mainstream theory and almost none on applications until the late 1970s (p. 134).
- Laplace's continuous form needs integration, which became hopelessly difficult as unknowns grew, given the computing capability of the time. Jeffreys, Lindley and Wallace worked on asymptotic approximations (p. 134).
- DeGroot wrote a Bayesian decision-theory text. Zellner raised funds and started conferences, so subjective probability had a big impact in economics. The International Society for Bayesian Analysis and the ASA Bayesian section date from the early 1990s (pp. 134–135).

**Applications (pp. 135–136).**
- Medicine: Ledley and Lusted proposed Bayesian diagnosis in 1959, publishing in *Science*. Warner built a 1961 program from 1,000 children with heart disease, using 54 tests. Gorry and Barnett showed that only seven or eight tests were needed, as long as they were relevant to the patient's symptoms and given one at a time in proper sequence. Few physicians used these systems (p. 135).
- NSA: at least six articles in 1960–72. **Leahy (1960):** Bayes is "one of the more important" techniques and was used in almost all successful NSA cryptanalysis. A quoted remark says only a handful of NSA mathematicians knew all the ways Bayes can be used (pp. 135–136).
- CIA: dozens of experiments. Bayesian analyses were judged more insightful but too slow, and were dropped for lack of computer power (p. 136).
- Law: **Tribe (1971)** attacked "mathematical or pseudo-mathematical devices". Many courtroom doors then closed to Bayes (p. 136).
- **Author's conclusion:** few people publicly applied Bayes, so much speculation was moot; until Bayesians could prove in public that their method was superior, they were "stymied" (p. 136).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three breeds have sample mean yields of 200, 250 and 300 eggs per hen per year. The prior mean for all breeds is $P = 250$. Use the credibility form $x = P + z(p-P)$ with $z = 0.5$. We chose $z$; the text does not say how it is set, nor does it define the symbols, so we assume $P$ is the shared mean and $p$ a breed's own mean.
- Breed 1: $250 + 0.5(200-250) = 250 - 25 = 225$.
- Breed 2: $250 + 0.5(0) = 250$.
- Breed 3: $250 + 0.5(300-250) = 275$.

Each estimate moves halfway toward the shared mean. A smaller $z$ would shrink more.
