# McGrayne, S. B. (2011). *The Theory That Would Not Die: How Bayes' Rule Cracked the Enigma Code, Hunted Down Russian Submarines, and Emerged Triumphant from Two Centuries of Controversy*. New Haven: Yale University Press.

## In one paragraph
This is a narrative history of Bayes' rule, the method of revising a starting belief with new evidence. Bayes first worked it out, and Laplace turned it into a general tool. Frequentist statisticians then pushed it out of respectable theory, even though practical users kept it alive. These users included artillery officers, insurance actuaries, wartime codebreakers and searchers for lost submarines and bombs. It slowly regained status through people such as Good, Savage, Lindley, Cornfield and Raiffa. Around 1990 the Markov chain Monte Carlo method (MCMC) and cheap computers made its calculations feasible. Today it runs through science, business, the web and theories of the brain. The book shows why the Bayesian–frequentist quarrel became so heated. It also gives human context for later technical texts. McGrayne argues that the rule finally won because it worked, not because philosophies changed.

## The path through the work
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch01-causes]] — Thomas Bayes devised, and left unpublished, a way to move from observed effects back to their probable cause by revising a starting guess with new data. Richard Price published it.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch02-man]] — Pierre Simon Laplace, independently of Bayes, built the "probability of causes" into the general rule we now call Bayes' rule, and the author says the rule was really his achievement, even though usage keeps Bayes' name.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch03-many]] — After Laplace, theorists attacked Bayes' rule as subjective and frequentists almost wiped it out, yet practitioners (artillery, telephone and insurance men, a geophysicist) kept using it.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch04-bayes]] — In the Second World War, leading mathematicians and other non-statisticians used Bayes' rule in secret to break codes, aim guns and hunt U-boats, and secrecy then kept the story hidden for decades.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch06-arthur]] — After WWII Bayes' rule was widely treated as disreputable, but insurance actuary Arthur Bailey argued in 1950 that the actuaries' working Credibility methods were Bayesian, and declared that they worked.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch07-tool]] — In the postwar boom, Bayes' rule moved from a practical tool toward an all-encompassing philosophy as Good, Savage and Lindley worked to make it respectable and logically coherent, and zealous advocacy helped fuel decades of conflict.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch08-jerome]] — Jerome Cornfield, a history graduate, used Bayes' rule to link case-control data to the risk among smokers, which helped advance the hypothesis that smoking causes lung cancer, and later became a Bayesian by conviction.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch09-there]] — In 1957–58 Albert Madansky used a Bayesian prior that was not all at zero to argue that an accidental H-bomb explosion could not be ruled out, even though none had ever happened.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch10-46]] — In the 1950s–60s Bayesian ideas multiplied, opened cracks in frequentist positions (Stein's averages, fiducial probability) and gave answers that could differ sharply from p-values, but few people applied them publicly to real problems, so Bayesians stayed stymied until they could prove their method superior.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch11-business]] — In the 1960s Robert Schlaifer and Howard Raiffa turned Bayes' rule into practical tools for business decisions under uncertainty, but McGrayne says their attempt to make Bayes standard in business teaching and statistics ultimately failed.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch12-who]] — Mosteller and Wallace used Bayes' rule, in odds form and with marker words, to attribute 12 disputed Federalist papers to Madison, and found that the prior mattered little.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch13-cold]] — According to colleagues and McGrayne, John Tukey used Bayesian methods for NBC's election forecasts for almost two decades while never admitting it, and his secrecy was one reason few statisticians realized how much Bayes' rule was being used.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch14-three]] — In the 1970s Bayes' rule was stuck in a divided, small community, but the Rasmussen nuclear-safety report used Bayesian methods without naming them, and the 1979 Three Mile Island accident revived that report; from 1981 industry studies used Bayes openly.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch15-navy]] — In Cold War searches for lost bombs, submarines and boats, Bayes' rule slowly proved its worth: expert-built priors were updated with search results, and growing computer power made this practical.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch16-eureka]] — Bayes' rule became widely usable around 1990 when Gelfand and Smith showed that Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling can replace the impossible integrals, with cheap computers arriving at the same time.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/ch17-rosetta]] — By the 2000s Bayes' rule had become a working tool across science, business, the web, translation and theories of the brain, with the chapter pointing to users who cared about results, cheap computing and vast data archives.
- [[mcgrayne-2011-theory/app-appendixes]] — Appendix A jokes that statisticians' camps resemble religions; Appendix B shows that a positive routine mammogram in a woman in her forties still means only about a 3% chance of cancer, because the disease is rare.

## Big ideas across units
- **Prior + data → updated belief, applied again and again.** Bayes' table experiment, Laplace's work, Turing's bans, the Federalist word counts, Tukey's election-night updating, Navy search grids and the mammogram example all follow this scheme. Units: ch01, ch02, ch04, ch12, ch13, ch15, app.
- **The long quarrel over subjectivity and the frequentist–Bayesian split.** The quarrel runs from the attacks on Bayes' guess, through Fisher, Neyman and Pearson, to Savage's zealotry and Lindley's Paradox. It ends with the "cooling" of the fight and the religion joke in Appendix A. Units: ch01, ch03, ch06, ch07, ch10, ch14, ch17, app.
- **Practice kept the rule alive while theory rejected it, often unnamed or secret.** Examples are artillery, Credibility formulas, wartime codebreaking, Tukey's NBC system and the Rasmussen report's "subjectivistic" label. Units: ch03, ch04, ch06, ch13, ch14, ch15.
- **Choosing a prior: equal odds, "gentle" priors, and how much it matters.** The shortcut of equal priors was a target of criticism and a working compromise. In the Federalist study the prior mattered little next to the data model. Units: ch01, ch02, ch03, ch04, ch09, ch10, ch12, ch14.
- **Borrowing strength across related data.** This idea appears as actuarial Credibility, Stein's shrinkage, empirical Bayes and Tukey's "borrowing strength". Units: ch03, ch06, ch10, ch13.
- **Rare or one-off events with no frequency record.** Frequency counts give no help for accidental H-bombs, nuclear core melts, lost submarines or unprecedented midair collisions. Bayes can use priors built from expert judgment. Units: ch06, ch09, ch14, ch15.
- **Decisions and utility.** Savage tied probability to decisions, Raiffa and Schlaifer built decision trees and elicitation, and Madansky's result led to safety measures. Units: ch07, ch09, ch11, ch14.
- **Computing as the long bottleneck.** Missing computing tools held Bayes back until MCMC, the Gibbs sampler and BUGS, and later cheap computers and big data, made it widely usable. Units: ch07, ch10, ch12, ch15, ch16, ch17.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch01 and ch02 give the idea and its origin. ch03 explains why the rule was banished. ch16 and ch17 show the payoff. Appendix B is a short worked example of the rule.
- **Dependencies from the material:**
  - ch03 introduces Credibility, which ch06 develops, and which ch10 links to Stein's shrinkage.
  - ch04's wartime ideas lead into Good's work in ch07, and Savage's utility ideas reach Madansky (ch09) and Raiffa and Schlaifer (ch11).
  - ch11's decision trees reappear in ch14.
  - ch16 answers the computing problem raised in ch07, ch10 and ch12.
  - ch12 and ch13 both show Bayesian methods applied to public problems by statisticians. David Wallace appears in both.
- **Skippable on a first pass:** The case studies in ch08, ch09, ch12, ch13, ch14 and ch15 are good illustrations but not needed for the main thread. The main thread is ch01–ch03, ch06, ch07, ch10, ch16, ch17. ch04 can be read alone as a standalone wartime story. Appendix A is a humorous aside.
- The chapter prerequisites are light: basic probability, the idea of a prior updated by data, and later the frequentist/Bayesian contrast. No chapter needs more than high-school mathematics.
