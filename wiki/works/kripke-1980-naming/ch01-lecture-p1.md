# Lecture I (part 1): Names, descriptions, a priori vs. necessary, and possible worlds

## L0 — One line
Kripke argues that proper names are probably not disguised descriptions, that "a priori" (epistemic) and "necessary" (metaphysical) are distinct notions, and that possible worlds are stipulated, so no qualitative criterion of cross-world identity is needed to ask what might have happened to Nixon.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke asks how names refer and what "necessary" means. He argues against the Frege–Russell view that a name abbreviates a definite description, and says its cluster-of-descriptions variants keep its spirit, a topic he postpones. He separates using a description to give a name's meaning from using it to fix its reference. He then holds that "a priori" (a matter of knowledge) and "necessary" (a matter of how things might have been) are different notions. He defends the intuitive sense of saying an object might have lacked a property, whatever it is called. He rejects the picture of possible worlds as foreign countries that need qualitative identity criteria. The distinctions prepare his later arguments about names and necessity.

**Prerequisites:** definite descriptions (phrases like *the x such that φx*); Frege's sense/reference; basic idea of possible worlds; quantifiers; prime numbers.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A **name** (proper name) differs from a **definite description**. "Designator" covers both. "Referent" of a description means the unique object that satisfies it, not the speaker's intended object (pp. 24–26, fn. 3).
- The Frege–Russell view (names are abbreviated descriptions) has strong-looking motivations: fixing reference, informative identities, existence questions. Kripke nevertheless thinks it pretty certain that the view is false, though he cannot answer every argument for it here (pp. 27–29).
- In Kripke's opinion, cluster-concept theories (the referent is whatever fits most of a family of descriptions; Wittgenstein, Searle) give up the letter of Frege–Russell but keep its spirit (pp. 30–31).
- A description theory may be offered as a theory of meaning or only of reference-fixing. Some attractive consequences, such as the analysis of existence statements, are lost, or at least not clearly kept, without the meaning version (pp. 32–33).
- On the traditional (Kantian) characterization, *a priori* is epistemic: knowable independently of experience. *Necessary* is metaphysical. They are not obvious synonyms; analytic is stipulated to imply both; necessary truths are not obviously certain (pp. 34–39).
- The Goldbach conjecture (every even number above 2 is a sum of two primes) illustrates that something may be necessary if true while nobody knows it a priori (pp. 36–37).
- The objection that only statements, not objects, are necessary or contingent (Quine's example of the number 9) is met by appeal to intuition about "Nixon" (pp. 39–42).
- Possible worlds are stipulated, not discovered. Demanding qualitative identity criteria, as in Lewis's counterpart theory, is unnecessary (pp. 42–45).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Preface and opening (pp. 22–24)
- Footnote 1: the talks were given without text or notes and are lightly edited. Compression meant that some objections go untreated. The sections on scientific identities and the mind-body problem are thin. Existence statements and empty names are omitted altogether (p. 22).
- Kripke says the topics of reference and necessity matter widely, since tools involving them are used throughout analytic philosophy. Materialism and the "identity thesis" get involved in questions about what is necessary or contingent in the identity of properties (pp. 22–23). He also hopes to speak about substances and natural kinds (p. 23).
- Kripke offers a deliberately surprising view of his own, which he says he will probably not defend. It is common to say that some predicates with null extension are empty only as a matter of contingent fact, and he does not dispute that in general. But the usual example, *unicorn* (under certain circumstances there would have been unicorns), is something he thinks is not the case (p. 24).
  - Perhaps, on his view, the truth is not that it is necessary that there be no unicorns, but just that we cannot say under what circumstances there would have been unicorns (p. 24).
  - He also thinks that even fossils matching everything the unicorn myth says would not show that there were unicorns (p. 24).

### Names and descriptions (pp. 24–26)
- **Name** = proper name (person, city, country). **Definite description** = a phrase of the form *the x such that φx*. **Designator** = common term for both (p. 24).
- If exactly one thing satisfies φ, it is the **referent** of the description in the logician's sense (pp. 24, 26).
- **Donnellan's point:** a speaker may use a description to single out something that does not satisfy it. Example: "the man over there with the champagne in his glass" said of a man who has only water (p. 25).
  - Kripke still uses the phrase *referent of the description* for the object that uniquely satisfies it (pp. 25–26).
  - Footnote 3 gives a parallel for names: two men think a distant leaf-raker is Jones, but he is Smith. Kripke wants his use of "refer" to satisfy the schema that the referent of X is X, for any name or description X (p. 25, fn. 3).
  - He tentatively believes, against Donnellan, that these remarks matter little for semantics or truth-conditions, though perhaps for speech-acts. A speaker with false beliefs may refer to something other than the **semantic referent**. He thinks this requires no theory that names are ambiguous and no change to Russell's theory of descriptions (p. 25, fn. 3).
- **Mill's doctrine:** names have denotation but not connotation (p. 26).
  - Example: "Dartmouth" may be so called because it lies at the mouth of the Dart. Had the river moved, the place could still properly be called "Dartmouth" (p. 26).
  - Kripke suggests changing the terms: perhaps the name does "connote" the Dart to some people, but in some way it still has no sense, since at least the town's location is not part of its meaning. Someone denying that Dartmouth lies at the Dart's mouth would not contradict himself (p. 26).
- Not every *the F* phrase is always used as a description rather than a name. *The Holy Roman Empire* and *The United Nations* seem, Kripke says, to be names rather than descriptions, since the things can be so called though they do not fit the words. "God" may be unclear (pp. 26–27).

**Intuition note:** A name can keep being used for the same place after the facts that inspired it change. Kripke takes this to show, for "Dartmouth", that the descriptive suggestion is not part of the name's meaning.

### The Frege–Russell view and its apparent strength (pp. 27–29)
- **Frege–Russell view** (as Kripke uses it): a proper name properly used is a definite description abbreviated or disguised. Frege said the description gives the name's *sense* (p. 27).
- Footnote 4: Strictly, Russell says that names do not abbreviate descriptions and have no sense, but also that what we call names do abbreviate descriptions and so are not really names. Perhaps only demonstratives used for objects of acquaintance are real names. Kripke reports Russell as saying ordinary "names" have sense, and deviates from Russell in two stated ways (p. 27, fn. 4).
- Kripke grants that the reasons for this view "are really very powerful" and that it is hard to see how it or a variant can fail (p. 27). Three arguments follow, on the Frege–Russell side:
  1. **Reference-fixing.** Names such as "Walter Scott" cannot be fixed by pointing (Russell's acquaintance). Reference seems fixed by what we know. Asked who "Napoleon" is, I give a uniquely identifying description. On this argument Mill's view appears to give no account (p. 28).
  2. **Identity statements.** "Hesperus is Phosphorus" was discovered, so it is not just a statement that an object is identical with itself. A natural thing to say is that its real content is that the evening thing is the morning thing (pp. 28–29).
  3. **Existence.** It seems natural to think that asking whether Aristotle existed questions whether anything answers to the properties associated with the name (p. 29).
- **Kripke's verdict:** he is not entirely able to see his way through every such problem and will not have time to discuss them all, but "I think it's pretty certain that the view of Frege and Russell is false" (p. 29).
- Footnote 6: "Frege–Russell" covers only versions with a substantive theory of reference. Quine's "Socratizer" proposal is a language reform, not such a theory. The same problems recur: how is the extension of "Socratizes" fixed? (p. 29, fn. 6).

### Cluster concepts (pp. 30–31)
- In Kripke's opinion, many who say Frege–Russell is false abandon its letter but keep its spirit through **cluster concepts** (p. 30).
- **Frege's own concession:** opinions on the sense of "Aristotle" may diverge (e.g. *Plato's disciple and the teacher of Alexander the Great* versus *the Stagirite teacher of Alexander the Great*). This is tolerable if the nominatum is the same, but should be avoided in a perfect language (p. 30).
- **Kripke's argument (p. 30):**
  - Even a single speaker asked for a substitute description may be quite at a loss. Any particular thing he knows about Aristotle he may feel clearly expresses a contingent property.
  - If *Aristotle* meant *the man who taught Alexander*, then *Aristotle taught Alexander* would be a mere tautology.
  - But it is not, since we could discover it false. So that description is not part of the name's sense.
- **The common way out:** a family of descriptions. **Wittgenstein**, *Investigations* §79, on "Moses" (p. 31):
  - Wittgenstein says that *Moses did not exist* may mean several things.
  - He asks whether one is always ready to substitute any one of those descriptions for the name. One might say *the man who did what the Bible relates, or at any rate a good deal of it*, but how much? Has one decided how much must be proved false before giving up the proposition?
- **Searle** is a *locus classicus*: the referent is whatever satisfies enough or most of the family (p. 31). It may look more plausible than Frege–Russell. Kripke will return to it (p. 31).

### Meaning versus reference-fixing (pp. 31–33)
- Two ways to read either the single-description or the cluster theory (pp. 31–32):
  1. The description or cluster **gives the meaning** of the name.
  2. The description is not synonymous with the name but **determines the reference**. On this theory, if one's beliefs about Walter Scott fit Salvador Dali much better, the reference is Dali, not Scott (p. 32).
- Ziff denies that names have meaning but still uses picture 2 for reference (p. 32, fn. 9).
- **Consequence (Kripke):** if the theory is not a theory of meaning, some solutions are not clearly right (p. 33).
  - If *Moses* means *the man who did such and such*, then *Moses did not exist* says that no one person did such and such (p. 33).
  - If *Moses* is not synonymous with any description, then even if a description determines its reference, statements with the name cannot in general be analysed by swapping in a description, though they may be materially equivalent to such statements. The analysis of singular existence and identity statements would then need a special argument (p. 33).
  - In any case, Kripke thinks it false that *Moses exists* means that at all, so he says the special argument need not be sought (p. 33).
- Footnote 10: determinists who say someone else would have done what Moses did cannot be refuted by a theory of the meaning of "Moses exists" (p. 34, fn. 10).

**Intuition note:** A description might only help us find the referent without being what the name means. Kripke says some of the theory's attractions are lost, or at least not clearly kept, if the description does not give the meaning.

### Terminology: a priori, necessary, analytic, certain (pp. 34–39)
- Terms such as "a priori", "analytic", "necessary", and sometimes "certain", are often treated as meaning the same. Kripke will not use "a priori" and "necessary" interchangeably (p. 34).
- **A priori** is an epistemological notion. Kripke gives what he takes to be the traditional characterization from Kant: a priori truths are those that can be known independently of any experience (p. 34).
  - The "can" brings in another modality: possible for whom (God, Martians, minds like ours)? It might be best to ask whether a particular person knows something a priori or believes it on a priori evidence (pp. 34–35).
  - *Can be known a priori* does not mean *must be known a priori*; Kripke calls the opposite view a mistake. Example: people believe a number is prime because a computer says so, so on a posteriori evidence. Yet perhaps someone who made the requisite calculations could know it a priori (p. 35).
- **Necessary** here is a notion of metaphysics, not epistemology (the epistemic and physical senses are set aside). If something is true, might it have been otherwise? If not, it is necessary; if so, contingent. This has nothing to do with anyone's knowledge (pp. 35–36).
  - It is a philosophical thesis, not an obvious definitional equivalence, that everything a priori is necessary or that everything necessary is a priori (p. 36).
- **Goldbach conjecture** (classical view of mathematics assumed): every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes (p. 36).
  - If false, there is an even $n>2$ such that for no primes $p_1,p_2<n$ does $n=p_1+p_2$. This is verifiable by direct computation, and so necessary, if arithmetical computations are necessary (p. 36).
  - If true, could there have been an even number that is not a sum of two primes? Such a number would be among 4, 6, 8, 10, …, and each can be shown by direct computation to be a sum of two primes. So the conjecture cannot be contingently true or false (pp. 36–37).
  - But now nobody knows a priori whether it is true (p. 37).
  - Whether a finite mind could settle it a priori is open. Hilbert thought every mathematical question is decidable by intuitive proof. Others disagreed. Gödel shows that no one formal system decides all mathematical questions (p. 37).
  - Conclusion: that it is necessary, if true, does not imply that anyone knows it a priori. Kripke says it does not even seem to follow, without further philosophical argument, that anyone could know it a priori. The "could" involves another modality, and the assertion that there is such a way is not trivial (pp. 37–38).
- "Necessary" and "a priori" are not obvious synonyms. A connecting argument would be needed. Kripke says he will argue below that they are not even coextensive. He thinks there are necessary a posteriori truths and probably contingent a priori truths (p. 38).
- **Why people identify them**, as Kripke guesses (p. 38):
  1. If something is true in all possible worlds, we ought to be able, with enough effort, to run through the worlds in our heads and see this, hence know it a priori. Kripke: this is not obviously feasible.
  2. If something is known a priori, it must be necessary, since it was known without looking. This needs the thesis that no way of knowing the actual world without looking fails to be a way of knowing every world. That thesis is vague but not trivial.
- Kripke's point is that the notions differ, and that it is not trivial to infer from a priori knowability to necessity, or from only a posteriori knowability to non-necessity (pp. 38–39).
- **Analytic:** stipulated as true by virtue of meaning and true in all possible worlds by virtue of meaning. Then analytic truths are both necessary and a priori (p. 39).
  - Kant's example *gold is a yellow metal* strikes Kripke as an odd example, since it can turn out false (p. 39).
- **Certainty** is another epistemological notion. Necessary truths are not obviously certain. A priori belief may fall short of certainty, as with a possibly mistaken proof (p. 39).

**Intuition note:** "Necessary" asks how the world could have been. "A priori" asks how someone could know. Goldbach illustrates that, if the conjecture is true, it is necessary even though nobody knows a priori that it is true.

### De re modality, descriptions, and intuition (pp. 39–42)
- **Distinction:** essentialism (modality *de re*, necessity of a particular having a property) versus modality *de dicto* (necessity of statements). Some will grant the second but not the first (p. 39).
- **Objection (as reported by Kripke):**
  - Only a statement or state of affairs is necessary or contingent. Whether a particular has a property necessarily depends on how it is described (p. 40).
  - Quine's example: 9 is necessarily odd, but picked out as the number of planets, it is not necessary that it is odd (with eight planets the number would not be odd) (p. 40).
  - Likewise Nixon's winning is a contingent property of Nixon only relative to our referring to him as *Nixon* (assuming the name does not mean *the man who won the election at such and such a time*). If he is designated as the man who won in 1968, it is a necessary truth that the man who won in 1968 won in 1968 (p. 40).
  - This is perhaps closely related to the view that we refer to particulars by descriptions (p. 40).
- **Kripke's reply (p. 41):** the notion of a necessary or contingent property has intuitive content and means something to the ordinary man.
  - One man, pointing at Nixon, says that he is the guy who might have lost. Another objects that this holds only if he is described as *Nixon*. Kripke says the second man is the one with a philosophical theory (p. 41).
  - The first would say that "the winner" and "the loser" do not designate the same objects in all possible worlds, while "Nixon" is just a name of this man (p. 41).
  - Asking whether Nixon's win was contingent asks whether, in some counterfactual situation, this man would have lost (p. 41).
  - Kripke takes intuition to be heavy evidence: people who find the notion unintuitive "have intuition reversed" (p. 42).
- Footnote 12: the example takes electoral victory to be accidental to Nixon. If accidental properties are meaningful, essential ones are too. This does not say that there are any essential properties, though Kripke thinks there are. The usual argument (that whether a property is accidental or essential depends on how it is described) is thus not the view that all properties are accidental, and is also not the idealist view that all properties are essential (p. 42, fn. 12).
- Footnote 11: Kripke works with an intuitive notion of necessity. Whether I might not have given the lectures is a different question from how anyone knows that I did (pp. 39–40, fn. 11).

### Identity across possible worlds (pp. 42–45)
- **Source of the objection:** the question of essential properties is equivalent (Kripke says it is) to the question of identity across possible worlds. For Nixon, which person in another world, if any, is Nixon? The objector says: surely a criterion of identity must be given (p. 42).
  - Numbers may seem easier. If position in the number series makes 9 what it is, a world with eight planets has a number of planets that is not our 9 (p. 43).
- **Kripke's response:**
  - Adequate necessary and sufficient identity conditions that do not beg the question are rare. Mathematics is the only case he knows even within a world. He knows none for material objects or people over time (p. 43).
  - More objectionable is the wrong picture: a possible world as a foreign country we observe. There we see only qualities, so we would need property-based means to tell who is Nixon (p. 43).
  - He says that some logicians' formal treatments may encourage this picture: "A prominent example, perhaps, is myself" (p. 43).
- **The right picture:** a possible world is not a distant country seen by telescope. It is given by the descriptive conditions we associate with it (p. 44).
  - To say "in another world I would not have given this lecture", we imagine that situation. We specify only what is relevant, though in theory a total description would settle everything (p. 44).
  - The description may include that the world contains Nixon and that he lost. If such a world is possible, then the man who lost in it is Nixon, since that is part of the description of the world (p. 44).
  - "'Possible worlds' are stipulated, not discovered by powerful telescopes." (p. 44)
- If one insists on purely qualitative descriptions (a man with a dog Checkers, who resembles a David Frye impersonation), one must ask whether he resembles Nixon enough to be Nixon. Kripke says we do not ordinarily think of counterfactual situations this way (pp. 44–45).
- **Lewis's counterpart theory** is "a very explicit and blatant example" of the qualitative approach. Kripke's footnote 13 criticises it (p. 45, fn. 13):
  - On Lewis's interpretation of quantified modality, universal instantiation fails for formulas with modal operators: $(y)\big((x)A(x)\rightarrow A(y)\big)$ fails. (The implication sign is garbled in the text layer; it is read here as the conditional, since the law in question is universal instantiation.)
  - For example, $(\exists y)\big((x)\,\Box\,(x\neq y)\big)$ is satisfiable but $(\exists y)\,\Box\,(y\neq y)$ is not (p. 45, fn. 13).
  - Kripke says it seems to him an additional argument against the plausibility of Lewis's philosophical views (p. 45, fn. 13).
  - On Lewis's view, similarities across worlds determine a counterpart relation, and a counterpart is never identical with the thing itself. So when we say *Humphrey might have won*, we are talking, on that view, about someone else. Kripke says Humphrey probably could not care less about this (p. 45, fn. 13).
  - Kripke says the issues common to Lewis's view and the usual transworld identification include the supposition that worlds are like dimensions of a larger universe, given only qualitatively, so identity or counterpart relations must rest on resemblance (p. 45, fn. 13).
- Footnote 14 names a David Kaplan paper on transworld identification as another source of the views criticised. It is unpublished and no longer represents Kaplan's position (p. 45, fn. 14).
- The pages end mid-sentence: "We just say 'suppose this man had…" (p. 45).

**Intuition note:** In Kripke's picture we begin by saying of a given man what might have happened to him. We do not first rebuild him from a bundle of qualities in another world.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two possible worlds. In $w_1$ (actual), Nixon wins the 1968 election. In $w_2$, Humphrey wins and Nixon loses. We stipulate, as Kripke allows, that $w_2$ contains Nixon (p. 44).

| Sentence | $w_1$ | $w_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| "The winner won" (description) | the winner is Nixon; true | the winner is Humphrey; true |
| "Nixon won" (name) | true | false (Nixon lost) |

- "The winner won" is true in every world we considered. So with the description fixed, there is no way for the winner to lose.
- "Nixon won" is false in $w_2$. So the man Nixon might have lost, as the first speaker says (p. 41).
- The designator "the winner" picks out Nixon in $w_1$ and Humphrey in $w_2$. "Nixon" picks out the same man in both (p. 41).
- Parallel with 9 (p. 40): in a world with 8 planets, *the number of planets is odd* is false, while *9 is odd* stays true.
- Goldbach check (p. 37): $4=2+2$, $6=3+3$, $8=3+5$, $10=3+7$. Each is a computation whose result is the same however the world goes. That is why the conjecture, if true, is not contingently true.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Cluster theory of names ↔ analytic-philosophy: Wittgenstein's family resemblance — both give an overlapping set of features in place of one definition.
- [metaphor] Possible worlds as "foreign countries" (the picture rejected) ↔ geography: exploration — Kripke uses the image to reject the idea that worlds are discovered.
- [structural-analogy] Quine's "Socratizer" paraphrase ↔ logic: Russell's elimination of descriptions — a name is replaced by a predicate plus description (fn. 6).
- [structural-analogy] Gödel incompleteness at p. 37 ↔ logic: limits of formal systems — used here only to show that "decidable a priori" is not trivial.

## Questions this raises
- If a description fixes reference without giving meaning, how can a speaker whose descriptions are false of the referent still refer to it?
- What would an example of a necessary a posteriori truth, or a contingent a priori truth, look like (p. 38 says Kripke will argue below that both kinds exist)?
- How does the stipulation view of worlds handle cases where a stipulation might not describe a genuinely possible world (p. 44 says it might be a question whether such a world is possible)?
- Do the objections to Lewis in fn. 13 depend on his formal model, or on the philosophical view behind it?
