# Kripke, Lecture I (part 2): rigid designators, reference-fixing, and the cluster theory of names

## L0 — One line
Kripke holds that names are rigid designators (terms that pick out the same object in every possible situation where it exists), that a description may fix a name's reference without giving its meaning, so that the a priori and the necessary can come apart, and that the cluster-of-descriptions theory of names is wrong.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke asks how we can speak about what might have happened to a particular thing, and what this implies for names. He answers that we simply stipulate whom we mean, so no purely qualitative criteria of transworld identity are needed. He says names are rigid designators, unlike descriptions such as *the U.S. President in 1970*. A description may fix a name's reference without giving its meaning, and this separates the a priori from the necessary. He then states the cluster theory of names as six theses, says he thinks all but the first are false, and adds a noncircularity condition that Kneale's theory violates. He takes these points to count against description theories of names.

**Prerequisites:** possible-world talk ("might have been"); definite descriptions ("the F"); a priori (knowable without experience) vs. necessary (true in every possible situation); the Frege–Russell view that a name stands for a description; *rigid designator* (a term that designates the same object wherever that object exists); *cluster theory* (a name's reference is fixed by, or its meaning is, a weighted set of descriptions the speaker believes true of the bearer); *criteria of transworld identity* (purely qualitative conditions for being the same individual in another possible situation).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Asking whether Nixon might have lost is about that man; no purely qualitative description of the counterfactual situation is needed. Necessary properties can be discussed without sufficient conditions (pp. 46–47).
- A rigid designator designates the same object in every possible world. Kripke says he will maintain that names are rigid; he shows that *the President of the U.S. in 1970* is not (pp. 48–49).
- "Transworld identification" is unproblematic for objects we can refer to and stipulate about. Some sense remains in questions about a complex thing's constituents (table and molecules). We start from objects, not from worlds (pp. 49–53).
- A definition may fix a reference (a rigid *one meter*) rather than give a synonym (*the length of S at $t_0$*). Hence *S is one meter long at $t_0$* is contingent, yet it seems knowable a priori by the one who fixed it (pp. 55–56).
- Kripke criticises Frege for using "sense" for both meaning and way of fixing reference. If a description-theory is kept only as a theory of reference, existence statements and identity statements between names need another analysis (pp. 58–59).
- The cluster theory is stated as six theses. Kripke thinks theses (2)–(6) are false; the necessity thesis (6) fails because Aristotle might have lacked all the commonly attributed properties (pp. 61–66).
- The Moses and Jonah cases suggest that a story about a person can be substantially false while the person existed (pp. 66–67).
- Condition (C): a theory of reference must not be circular. Kneale's *the man called 'Socrates'* violates it (pp. 68–70).

### 1. Intuitions of possibility concern the man himself (pp. 46–47)
- If we have an intuition that this man's electoral loss was possible, it is about that possibility. It need not be identified with the possibility of a man qualitatively described (looks, political views) having lost (p. 46). We can point to the man and ask what might have happened to him (p. 46).
- **Objection (a possible interlocutor):** whether Nixon could have had other properties is equivalent to whether the criteria of identity across possible worlds include that he lacks them. **Kripke replies:** the usual notion of a criterion demands purely qualitative necessary *and sufficient* conditions for being Nixon. Questions about necessary conditions can be asked without any sufficient conditions (p. 46).
- Example: it seems we cannot imagine Nixon as an inanimate object; perhaps he could not have failed to be human. That would make "in all worlds where he exists he is human (or at least not inanimate)" a necessary fact about Nixon, with no demand for qualitative sufficient conditions (p. 46).
- Even if such conditions existed, his view would not require finding them before asking whether Nixon might have won (p. 47).
- The human-being question is not epistemological. Nixon might turn out to be an automaton, but whether he *might have* not been human, given that he is, is not a question about knowledge (p. 47). Likewise for the table's molecular composition: discovering it was a scientific achievement, but there is some feeling that nothing could be this very object and not be so composed (p. 47).

*Intuition note:* Asking what could have happened to a thing is different from asking what a thing must be like in order to count as that thing. We can do the first without answering the second.

### 2. Rigid designators (pp. 47–49)
- Contrast: why does it seem more "essential" that 9 is greater than 7 than that the number of planets is? The suggested answer: the number of planets might have been different, while nine could not (p. 48).
- **Definition (quasi-technical):** a *rigid designator* designates the same object in every possible world; otherwise it is *nonrigid* or *accidental*. Objects need not exist in all worlds. When we call a property essential we usually mean the object has it in any case where it would have existed. A rigid designator of a necessary existent is *strongly rigid* (p. 48; restated p. 49).
- Kripke says one intuitive thesis he will maintain is that names are rigid designators. Test: someone else (Humphrey) might have been U.S. President in 1970, but no one other than Nixon might have been Nixon. So *the President of the U.S. in 1970* is not rigid (pp. 48–49). The man might not have been *called* Nixon (p. 49).
- Those who say rigidity requires prior criteria of transworld identity have, he says, "reversed the cart and the horse" (p. 49): we refer rigidly to Nixon and stipulate that we speak of what might have happened to him, so identification is unproblematic in such cases (p. 49).
- Footnote 15 (p. 48): the phrase "possible world" can mislead (a "foreign country" picture); better to say simply that Humphrey might have won. He hopes the apparatus has been very useful for the model theory of quantified modal logic, but it has encouraged philosophical pseudo-problems.
- Perhaps one source of the demand for purely qualitative descriptions of counterfactual situations is confusing the epistemological with the metaphysical, a prioricity with necessity. If someone identifies necessity with a prioricity and thinks objects are named by uniquely identifying properties, he may think the properties used to identify the object, being known a priori, must be used to identify it in all worlds (p. 49). Against this, Kripke repeats: (1) things are generally not "found out" about a counterfactual situation, they are stipulated; (2) possible worlds need not be given purely qualitatively. He adds that we will see shortly that the properties an object has in every counterfactual world have nothing to do with those used to identify it in the actual world (pp. 49–50). Footnote (p. 49): not every object has a name; demonstratives and free variables can serve as rigid designators.

*Intuition note:* *The President in 1970* asks "who fits this role in the situation?", so the answer can vary. *Nixon* points at one man, so the answer does not vary.

### 3. What sense is left in "transworld identification" (pp. 50–53)
- Kripke says something can be said for the "problem". Although the statement that England fought Germany in 1943 perhaps cannot be reduced to statements about individuals, in some sense facts about nations are not facts "over and above" facts about persons: a description of persons alone can be a complete description of the world, from which the facts about nations follow. Similarly, perhaps, for a table and its molecules (p. 50). We can ask whether England still exists in a situation described in terms of people, or whether a bunch of molecules would constitute the very same table T. The criteria are for complex particulars in terms of more basic particulars. If there is some open texture in the relation, we can hardly expect hard and fast criteria; in concrete cases we may be able to answer, though sometimes the answer may be indeterminate (pp. 50–51). He thinks similar remarks apply to identity over time (p. 51).
- Footnote (p. 51): identity here can be vague and may seem intransitive. A counterpart notion, without Lewis's underpinnings, may have some utility (strict identity for molecules, a vague counterpart relation for tables). It seems to Kripke utopian to suppose we will ever reach ultimate basic particulars whose identity is never vague.
- **Three differences from the usual conception.** (i) No level of description is privileged; *this table might have been placed in another room* is proper as it stands (p. 51). (ii) It is not assumed that necessary and sufficient conditions for which collections of molecules make up this table are available (pp. 51–52). (iii) The criteria relate particulars to other particulars, not to qualities. If counterfactual situations must be described purely qualitatively, whether the table in question would be this table is moot, because reference to objects has disappeared (p. 52).
- **Bare particulars.** Kripke denies that his view assumes propertyless substrata: Nixon *is* a Republican and might have been a Democrat; some properties may be essential (p. 52). He denies that a particular is a bundle of qualities: if a quality is abstract, a bundle is more abstract, not a particular. The question "object behind the bundle, or nothing but the bundle?" is a false dilemma (p. 52).
- Holding the table in his hands, he can point to it, and asking whether it might have been in another room is, by stipulation, asking about it. Essential properties are not used to identify the object in another world, nor need they be those used to identify it in the actual world (p. 53). Theorists who say we identify objects by resemblance in the most important respects are wrong: Nixon might have avoided politics (p. 53).
- Conclusion: we begin with objects we can identify in the actual world, then ask what might have been true of them, not with worlds and then criteria (p. 53).

### 4. Reference-fixing vs. synonymy: the meter (pp. 53–57)
- The Frege–Russell view (names introduced by description) could be a theory of the meaning of names or only of their reference (pp. 53–54). Illustration: let 100°C be the temperature at which water boils at sea level; or let one meter be the length of stick S in Paris (p. 54).
- Wittgenstein (*Investigations* §50, as Kripke cites it) says one cannot say of the standard meter that it is, or is not, one meter long; this only marks its role in the language game. Kripke finds this very puzzling and thinks Wittgenstein must be wrong: he asks why a stick, say 39.37 inches long, would not be one meter long. He then proceeds on the supposition that the stick is one meter long (p. 54).
- Fix the definition at a fixed time $t_0$. **Objector (a hypothetical holder of "everything a priori is necessary"):** by definition S is one meter long at $t_0$, so this is necessary. **Kripke:** the speaker uses the definition to *fix the reference*, not to give the meaning. He marks out a length by an accidental property (that a stick has that length), and may still say that had heat been applied to S at $t_0$, S would not have been one meter long (pp. 54–55).
- Why: *one meter* is meant to designate rigidly one length in all worlds, which in the actual world is the length of S at $t_0$. *The length of S at $t_0$* is not rigid, since S might have been longer or shorter (p. 55). The definition, properly read, says that *one meter* is to be a rigid designator of the length that is in fact S's length at $t_0$, not that the phrases are synonymous (p. 56).
- **Result:** for someone who has fixed the metric system by S, it would seem that he knows a priori that S is one meter long at $t_0$, yet the statement is contingent. Kripke concludes that in this sense there are contingent a priori truths (p. 56). Footnote 21 (p. 56): he does not call it analytic, because he stipulates analytic truths to be both necessary and a priori. Footnote 20 (p. 55): philosophers of science may treat *one meter* as a cluster concept; Kripke asks us to suppose the stated definition is the only standard.
- Same distinction for names. If *Aristotle* meant *the greatest man who studied with Plato*, it would not be rigid, since another man might have been Aristotle (unless essential properties were used in the description). If the description only fixes the referent, *suppose Aristotle had never gone into philosophy* need only mean *suppose that man had never…* (p. 57).
- Hesperus: a reference may have been fixed by apparent position. It is not then part of the meaning that Hesperus had that position: had a comet hit it, we would say Hesperus would not have occupied that position, not that it would not have been Hesperus. *Hesperus* is rigid; *the body in yonder position* is not (pp. 57–58). Kripke says he will hold that names are always rigid designators (p. 58).

*Intuition note:* A description can be a pointer used once to pick out the target, not the target's definition. After the pointing, the name goes with the target, wherever the target goes.

### 5. Consequences for Moses, existence, and Frege (pp. 58–60)
- Frege and Russell certainly seem to have the full-blown theory on which a proper name is not rigid and is synonymous with the description that replaces it; another theory would use the description only to determine a rigid reference (p. 58).
- If *Moses* means *the man who did such and such*, then if no one did these things Moses didn't exist, and perhaps that is an analysis of *Moses didn't exist* (p. 58). If the description only fixes a rigid reference, this is not what the sentence means: Moses might have stayed in the Egyptian courts and maybe no one would have done what the Bible relates, and he would still have existed (p. 58).
- If so, *Moses exists* does not mean *the existence and uniqueness conditions of a description are fulfilled*, and this is not an analysis of singular existential statements. Making the theory one of reference gives up some of its advantages: existence statements and identity statements between names need some other analysis (p. 59).
- Kripke criticises Frege for using *sense* in two senses: the meaning, and the way reference is determined. Frege identifies them and supposes both given by definite descriptions. Kripke will ultimately reject the second supposition too, but even if it were right he rejects the first (p. 59). The two senses correspond to two senses of *definition*.
- Footnote 22 (p. 59): Kripke says we shall see below that for most speakers, apart from those who first give an object its name, the referent is determined by a causal chain of communication rather than by a description. In formal modal semantics a term's "sense" is usually the function from worlds to referents; for a rigid designator it is constant. So *one meter* has a constant function though its reference is fixed by *the length of S*. Footnote (pp. 59–60): Kripke finds alleged ambiguities between rigid and nonrigid readings of descriptions dubious. David Kaplan's operator *Dthat* turns a description into a term rigidly designating its actual satisfier.
- $\pi$ (p. 60): Kripke has only a vague intuitive feeling that $\pi$ is not short for *the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter*, nor for a cluster of definitions, but is a name of a real number that is necessarily that ratio. Here both $\pi$ and the description are rigid, so the meter argument does not apply (p. 60).

### 6. The cluster theory and the necessity of the disjunction (pp. 60–64)
- A popular substitute for Frege–Russell, adopted even by Strawson: a name is not a disguised description, but abbreviates, or has its reference determined by, a cluster of descriptions (pp. 60–61). The *stronger* version defines the name synonymously as the cluster, making it necessary that Moses had the *disjunction* of the properties. Kripke thinks this very implausible (p. 61).
- **Searle (quoted by Kripke):** if we replaced *Aristotle* by *the teacher of Alexander*, it would be necessary that the man referred to taught Alexander, but contingent that Aristotle went into pedagogy; Searle suggests it is necessary that Aristotle has the inclusive disjunction of the properties commonly attributed to him (p. 61). **Kripke:** with *necessary* as used here, this must be false (unless some very interesting essential property is commonly attributed): most such things Aristotle might not have done, and in a situation where he did not do them we would still describe it as one where *Aristotle* did not do them (p. 61).
- This is not Russell's scope distinction. *The man who taught Alexander might not have taught Alexander* is true on one scope reading; but it is also true *of the man Aristotle* that he might not have gone into pedagogy (p. 62). Footnote 25: Aristotle might not have been *called* Aristotle, just as $2\times2$ might not have been called *four*; loose speech that confuses use and mention does not threaten the thesis.
- Exceptions: date and period of life might more easily be imagined as necessary. But for someone with only a vague cluster of famous achievements, possessing the whole disjunction is just a contingent fact about Aristotle (pp. 62–63).
- Such a speaker might know the disjunction a priori if he fixes the reference that way, and it still would not be necessary. So a priori would not imply necessary if the cluster theory were right; the meter case is a clearer example (p. 63). If the thesis that all a priori truth is necessary is to be immune from this sort of counterexample, it needs to be modified; unmodified, it leads to confusion about reference. Kripke has no idea how to restate it, or whether that is possible (p. 63). Footnote 26: it seems plausible that in some sense fixing a system of measurement teaches no new information; any reformulation must not be trivial; the converse thesis would still be false; he will use *a priori* so that truths following from a reference-fixing definition count as a priori.

### 7. The six theses of the cluster theory (pp. 64–66)
Kripke calls it a nice theory whose only defect (which he thinks it shares with philosophical theories generally) is that it is wrong; he hopes not to propose another in its place, since he is sure that would be wrong too if it is a theory (p. 64). The speaker is A.
1. For every name 'X' there corresponds a cluster of properties: the family of $\varphi$ such that A believes "$\varphi X$". True, because it can just be a definition (p. 64).
2. One of the properties, or some jointly, are believed by A to pick out an individual uniquely (p. 64). (Whether A is right is a further thesis.)
3. If most, or a weighted most, of the $\varphi$'s are satisfied by one unique object $y$, then $y$ is the referent of 'X' (p. 64).
4. If the vote yields no unique object, 'X' does not refer (p. 65).
5. "If X exists, then X has most of the $\varphi$'s" is known a priori by the speaker (p. 65).
6. That statement expresses a necessary truth in the speaker's idiolect (p. 65).

- Weighting: some properties plausibly count more; weight 0 disenfranchises. Kripke believes that Strawson, to his surprise, explicitly favours equal weights; footnote 27: Strawson pools several speakers' properties and requires only a sufficient plurality (p. 65).
- (6) need not be a thesis if the cluster only fixes reference (p. 65). Subsidiary theses analyse *'Moses exists' means 'enough of the $\varphi$ are satisfied'*. Even the reference-only theorist holds a priori that if not enough are satisfied, X does not exist; only the meaning theorist also holds it necessary (p. 66). Kripke says theses 2–6 are, he thinks, all false (p. 64).

### 8. The properties are often wrongly specified; Moses and Jonah (pp. 66–68)
- **Wittgenstein (quoted by Kripke):** *Moses does not exist* may mean the Israelites had no single leader, or their leader was not called Moses, or no one accomplished all the Bible relates (p. 66). Kripke reads the gist as: we know a priori that if the Biblical story is substantially false, Moses did not exist (p. 66).
- Kripke asks whether we really know a priori that if Moses existed he did some or most of these things. The story might be a complete legend, or a substantially false account of a real person. In the latter case a scholar could say that Moses existed though what is said of him is substantially false. Jonah: Biblical scholars generally hold he existed while taking the story's details as substantially false (pp. 66–67). This suggests the Biblical story is not the right cluster.
- Footnote 28 (pp. 67–68): Ginsberg (quoted by Kripke) calls Jonah a historical personage and the book fiction. The evidence comes from II Kings, but such evidence could have been available without it. One may say that the Jonah of the book never existed as one says that the Hitler of Nazi propaganda never existed (note 28, p. 68).

### 9. The noncircularity condition and Kneale (pp. 68–70)
- If all we believe is that the Bible relates such and such of him, reference is thrown back to the Bible's reference (p. 68).
- **Condition (C):** for any successful theory, the account must not be circular; the properties used in the vote must not involve reference in a way that is ultimately impossible to eliminate (p. 68).
- **Kneale's view:** proper names are not, as Mill supposed, signs without sense. It may be informative to tell a man that the most famous Greek philosopher was called Socrates, but it is trifling to say that Socrates was called Socrates, because the hearer could not understand the speaker's use of the name unless he already knew that it means *the individual called 'Socrates'* (pp. 68–69).
- **Kripke:** that isn't trifling on any view (maybe the Greeks didn't call him *Socrates*; he will check the quotation next lecture). Kneale's argument would equally show that *horse* means *the things called 'horses'*. Anyone who knows the use of *is called* knows that *quarks are called 'quarks'* expresses a truth without knowing what a quark is, so this is not a good argument that the trifling statement shows the meaning (p. 69).
- As a theory of reference it violates (C): asked to whom a speaker refers by *Socrates*, it answers that he refers to the man to whom he refers, so no reference would get off the ground (p. 70). Russell sometimes uses *the man called 'Walter Scott'*. If these are the only descriptive senses available, the *calling* relation, not the description, determines reference (p. 70).

## Toy example (ours)
A rod R has length 50 cm at time $t_0$ in the actual world $w_1$. In a counterfactual world $w_2$ it was heated, so R is 52 cm at $t_0$.
Fix *one ell* as a *rigid* designator of "whatever length R actually has at $t_0$".

| | length of R at $t_0$ | referent of *one ell* |
|---|---|---|
| $w_1$ | 50 cm | 50 cm (fixed from $w_1$) |
| $w_2$ | 52 cm | 50 cm (same length) |

*R is one ell long at $t_0$*: in $w_1$, $50=50$, true; in $w_2$, $52\ne 50$, false. So it is contingent. The person who fixed the unit via R knows it is true in $w_1$ without measuring, so it is a priori. Contingent and a priori hold together, because the description fixed the reference but is not the meaning.

## Concepts
- [[rigid designator]] — designates the same object in every world where it exists (pp. 48–49); names are claimed to be rigid, *the President in 1970* is not (p. 49).
- strongly rigid (new) — a rigid designator of a necessary existent (p. 48).
- transworld identity criteria (new) — purely qualitative necessary and sufficient conditions for being a given individual; Kripke says rigid reference does not wait on them (pp. 46, 49).
- essential property (new) — usually: a property true of the object in any case where it would have existed (p. 48).
- reference-fixing vs. synonymy (new) — a description may pick out the referent without giving the term's meaning (pp. 55–57).
- contingent a priori (new) — *S is one meter long at $t_0$*: it seems knowable a priori from the reference-fixing, yet is contingent (p. 56).
- [[sense (Sinn) vs. reference (Bedeutung)]] — Kripke says Frege's "sense" runs two things together: meaning and way of determining reference (p. 59).
- cluster theory of names (new) — a name's reference is determined by, or its meaning equals, a weighted cluster of descriptions; six theses (pp. 61, 64–65).
- noncircularity condition (C) (new) — a theory of reference must not use reference in a way that cannot be eliminated (p. 68).
- [[use vs. mention]] — appears in footnote 25: *might have been called 'Aristotle'* vs. *might not have been Aristotle* (p. 62).
- [[theory of descriptions]] — only Russell's scope distinction is invoked, not developed (p. 62).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] rigid designator vs. descriptions ↔ programming: a constant vs. a function re-evaluated in each environment — one value across worlds vs. a value that varies with the situation.
- [structural-analogy] Kaplan's *Dthat* (evaluate the description in the actual world, then freeze the result) ↔ computing: evaluating an expression once and storing the value.
- [structural-analogy] cluster theory's weighted vote ↔ computational social science: classifying an entity by weighted feature-matching; Kripke's objections (errors, contingency, circularity) resemble failure modes of such matching.

## Questions this raises
- If descriptions only fix reference for names, what does the fixing for most speakers (the causal chain of footnote 22)?
- Can the reference-fixing pattern explain operational definitions of scientific units, or other defined constructs?
- If "a priori implies necessary" must be modified, what modification avoids triviality (footnote 26)?
- Does the molecule/table analysis leave vague cases genuinely indeterminate?
