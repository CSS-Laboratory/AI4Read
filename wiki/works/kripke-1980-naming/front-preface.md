# Preface to *Naming and Necessity* (1980 printing)

## L0 — One line
Kripke leaves the lectures almost unchanged and uses the preface to trace where rigid designation came from and to correct common misreadings of it: it is not a scope convention, it is unaffected by shared names, and possible worlds are abstract states like dice outcomes.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke prefaces the 1980 printing. He keeps the text almost unchanged and asks two questions: where did the ideas come from, and what do readers commonly misunderstand? He traces rigid designation to modal logic and the thesis that identity is necessary. He then answers misreadings. Shared names do not touch rigidity. Rigidity is not a wide-scope convention. Possible worlds are abstract states, as in school dice probability. His view does not imply that codesignative names are fully interchangeable. He admits he leaves hard problems open, such as propositions in belief contexts and nonexistent referents. The preface matters because it fixes what the rigidity thesis does and does not claim.

**Prerequisites:** first-order quantifiers; the modal operator □ ("necessarily"); Russell's analysis of definite descriptions; scope of an operator; counting outcomes in basic probability.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Conservative reprinting.** Kripke only fixes printing errors and unclear phrasing. He stands by the main theses and does not use the preface to fill gaps or answer serious criticism (pp. 1–2).
- **Identity is internal.** From $(x)\Box(x=x)$ and Leibniz's law, objects cannot be "contingently identical" (setting aside the caveat that $x$ need not exist). This is independent of how natural-language names work (pp. 3–5).
- **Three theses, kept apart.** (i) identical objects are necessarily identical; (ii) true identities between rigid designators are necessary; (iii) identities between ordinary names are necessary. Only (iii) concerns natural language (p. 4).
- **Rigidity versus Russell.** For *Aristotle was fond of dogs*, the truth condition in counterfactual situations concerns the same man. On Russell's analysis it concerns whoever fits the description (pp. 6–7).
- **Shared names are irrelevant.** For each reading of a sentence, the rigidity question can be asked separately (pp. 7–10).
- **Rigidity is not a scope convention.** Simple sentences have no modal operator, yet rigidity applies. The contrast between names and descriptions survives explicit small scope (pp. 10–14).
- **Why Russell erred, and Kripke's reply.** Kripke's stipulated-language thought experiment separates reference fixing from synonymy and epistemic from modal questions (pp. 14–15).
- **Possible worlds as abstract states.** The 36 states of two dice are miniature possible worlds. No "transworld identification" problem arises, and worlds need not be given qualitatively (pp. 15–20).
- **No universal substitutivity.** Rigidity does give interchange of codesignative names in modal contexts, but Kripke never intended full interchangeability. He has no official doctrine about propositions or names in epistemic contexts (pp. 20–21).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The preface has no numbered sections. I group it by topic and give page ranges.

### Editorial policy (pp. 1–3)
- Kripke dropped plans to revise extensively. Revision would delay a cheaper separate edition, and there is something to be said for preserving a work in its original form, "warts and all" (p. 1).
- Changes are limited to correcting printing errors and clarifying some sentences (p. 1).
- Footnote 56: the letter-nomenclature, garbled in the first printing, is corrected. Kripke says the argument now seems to him to have problems he did not know then, which at least require further discussion (p. 1).
- On Salmon's criticism of that footnote (n. 2): Kripke has not studied it carefully. He says it seems likely that Salmon's criticism is related to his own but not the same, and that it reconstructs the argument in a way that does not match his exact intent and makes it unnecessarily weak. He also says he thinks he had no ambition rigorously to prove "essentialism from the theory of reference" alone in that footnote, which was so compressed that readers might reconstruct its details in different ways (p. 1).
- He stands by the principal theses. The preface will describe the background and genesis and discuss a few common misapprehensions. It adds little on the more substantive problems (p. 2).
- Short replies may do more harm than good, because a confused reader may think the objection must be cogent if this is all the reply Kripke can give (p. 2).
- Some published criticisms go undiscussed. Kripke leaves it to the reader to decide whether each is too frivolous, too substantive, or just out of space (n. 3, p. 2).
- Kripke recommends that new readers not read the preface first, but return to it for clarification, if necessary, after the main text. It is not written to be completely self-contained (pp. 2–3).

### Genesis of the ideas (pp. 3–5)
- Most views were formulated around 1963–64. The work grew out of earlier model-theoretic work on modal logic ("possible worlds" semantics) (p. 3).
- It seemed to Kripke, as Wiggins has said, that the **indiscernibility of identicals** (identicals share all properties) was as self-evident as the law of contradiction. It is $(x)(y)(x=y \wedge Fx \supset Fy)$, and it is not the identity of indiscernibles (n. 4, p. 3).
- Alleged counterexamples involving modal properties "always turned out" to rest on confusion. The contexts did not express genuine properties, or scopes were confused, or coincidence of individual concepts was confused with identity of individuals (p. 3).
- Derivation, ignoring the fact that $x$ need not necessarily exist (p. 3):
  1. $(x)\Box(x=x)$.
  2. Leibniz's law.
  3. Therefore identity is "internal": $$(x)(y)(x=y \supset \Box\, x=y)$$
  4. Counterexample check: a pair of distinct objects makes the antecedent false. A pair consisting of one object and itself makes the consequent true (p. 3).
- Consequence: if 'a' and 'b' are rigid designators, 'a = b', if true, is a necessary truth. If they are not rigid, no such conclusion follows about the *statement*, though the *objects* are necessarily identical (p. 3).
- **Three theses** (p. 4):
  - (i) Identical objects are necessarily identical.
  - (ii) True identity statements between rigid designators are necessary.
  - (iii) Identity statements between what we call "names" in actual language are necessary.
  - (i) and (ii) are theses of philosophical logic, independent of natural language. (i) concerns objects and (ii) is metalinguistic. (ii) roughly follows from (i), and the analogous deduction for nonrigid designators is fallacious. Rigid designators are possible in a formal modal language, so no commitment about natural-language names has yet been made (p. 4).
  - From (ii), all that strictly follows about natural-language "names" is: either they are not rigid, or true identities between them are necessary (p. 4).
- Kripke says he supposes that at one time he vaguely supposed, influenced by prevailing presuppositions, that ordinary names must be nonrigid because there are obviously contingent identities between them. But (i) already showed that objects cannot be "contingently identical" (p. 4).
- Talk of "contingently identical" objects served as a "philosophical crutch". It let philosophers treat designators as nonrigid and as rigid at once. Uniquely identifying properties can coincide contingently, but objects cannot be contingently identical (pp. 4–5).
- The 1963–64 realization was that the presuppositions against necessity of identities between ordinary names were incorrect, and that the natural intuition that ordinary names are rigid can in fact be upheld (p. 5). A symbol that is not rigid is so unlike ordinary names that it should not be called a "name" (n. 5, p. 5).
- Sequence of steps (p. 5):
  1. Distinguish using a description to *give a meaning* from using it to *fix a reference*.
  2. Reject the description theory as an account of meaning, while leaving its validity for reference fixing untouched.
  3. Question whether it even gives a correct account of how names' references are fixed. The result is in the second lecture.
  4. Similar remarks apply to natural-kind terms. The other leading ideas then "fit into place".
- Kripke pays tribute to the Frege–Russell complex of ideas: its coherence explains its long appeal, and he never placed the description theory of proper names among ideas that have little or no appeal for him (p. 5).

*Intuition note.* The argument is short. The only candidate counterexamples to $x=y \supset \Box\, x=y$ are pairs of distinct objects or an object paired with itself, and neither works. Kripke treats talk of "contingently identical" objects as a confusion that let philosophers treat designators as nonrigid and as rigid at once. Whether ordinary names are rigid is a separate question about natural language (pp. 3–5).

### Rigid designation restated (pp. 6–7)
- Take (1) *Aristotle was fond of dogs.* Presumably everyone, even Russell, agrees that there is a certain man such that (1) is true iff he was fond of dogs (p. 6, n. 6).
- **The rigidity thesis** (subtle points aside) is that the same paradigm governs counterfactual situations. (1) truly describes a counterfactual situation iff that same man would have been fond of dogs had it obtained. Situations where he would not have existed are set aside (p. 6; n. 7).
- **Russell's view:** (1) should be analysed as something like (2) *The last great philosopher of antiquity was fond of dogs*, and then as (3) *Exactly one person was last among the great philosophers of antiquity, and any such person was fond of dogs* (pp. 6–7).
- Assuming Aristotle was the last great philosopher of antiquity, the actual truth conditions of (3) agree extensionally with those of (1). Counterfactually they can differ widely: if someone else had been the last great philosopher, that person's fondness for dogs would settle (1) (p. 7).
- Kripke assumes Russell is right that descriptions can at least sometimes be read nonrigidly. If some philosophers are right that descriptions also have a rigid sense, his main thesis is unaffected, since it contrasts names with nonrigid descriptions (n. 8, p. 6).

*Intuition note.* The test is what a sentence says about other possible histories. Names keep tracking the same man there, while *the last great philosopher* tracks whoever fits the description in that history.

### Misconception 1: two people can share a name (pp. 7–10)
- **Objection (some critics):** the fact that two people can have the same name refutes rigidity (p. 7).
- Kripke admits he wrote as if each name had a unique bearer. He does not think it a major oversimplification as far as rigidity is concerned, and believes many (probably not all) important issues about names would be largely unaffected (p. 7).
- A possible terminology: count phonetically identical names for distinct objects as distinct names, like homonyms. He is sympathetic but not demanding it. Distinctness of referents is sufficient for distinctness of names (p. 8; nn. 9–10).
- Rigidity concerns a sentence once its reading is fixed, by context. Ambiguous words, indexicals, syntactic ambiguities and which Aristotle is meant must be settled for the sentence to express a single proposition. Russell too needs this before offering (3) (p. 9).
- Kripke's objection to Russell applies to each reading separately: if Russell were right, no proposition expressed by a reading of (1) would obey the rule of rigidity (assuming 'Aristotle' is a proper name in every reading). The rule is that there is a single individual and a single property such that, at every counterfactual situation, the truth condition is that individual's having the property there. Kripke relies on Russell's practice of reading ordinary names nonrigidly (pp. 9–10).
- Treating names as demonstratives would not change this. 'this', 'I' and 'you' are rigid even though their reference varies with context (n. 12, p. 10).

### Misconception 2: rigidity reduces to scope (pp. 10–14)
- **Objection (many readers):** the intuitions for rigidity could be handled by reading names as nonrigid designators with wide scope, like wide-scope descriptions (p. 10).
- **Kripke's reply:** he recognizes in the monograph that some intuitions could be interpreted as scope effects, so the objection is partly justified, but it seems to him wrong to suppose that all of them could be. See p. 62 with n. 25, and the intuitive test on pp. 48–49 (pp. 10–11).
- **Stronger objection (Dummett):** rigidity simply is the convention that a name be read with scope over all modal operators. Kripke calls this "a technical error" (p. 11 and n. 13).
- Argument: (1) and (2) are simple sentences with no modal operators, so no scope distinctions arise. Yet rigidity applies. 'Aristotle' in (1) is rigid and the description in (2) is not (pp. 11–12). The doctrine concerns truth conditions at counterfactual situations of all sentences, including simple ones (p. 12).
- Footnote 15 states a relation. Rigidity in simple sentences is equivalent, ignoring nonexistence, to the claim that when a modal operator governs a simple sentence with a name, large and small scope readings are equivalent. That is not a convention that only the wide reading is allowed; the equivalence makes sense only where both readings are admissible (n. 15, p. 12).
- **Picture test:** on the doctrine of rigidity, a picture purporting to represent a situation correctly described by (1) must purport to depict Aristotle himself as fond of dogs. A picture purporting to represent someone else, even one with all of Aristotle's identifying properties, does not represent such a situation. Kripke asks whether this does not, in itself, obviously represent our intuitions regarding (1) (p. 12). The question is rhetorical; he evidently expects the answer yes.
- **Modal sentences.** The monograph held that *It might have been the case that Aristotle was not a philosopher* is true, while *It might have been the case that the greatest philosopher of antiquity was not a philosopher* is not, contrary to Russell's theory. The latter would express a truth if the description were read, contrary to Kripke's intent, with wide scope (p. 13).
- **Kripke's point:** the contrast holds even if every sentence is explicitly given small scope, for example by a colon after 'that' (p. 13).
- The contrary view must say language and thought cannot keep the distinction straight. Kripke asks how we could then have made it. Consider (4) *What (1) expresses might have been the case*. Kripke asks whether it expresses the desired assertion with no scope ambiguity, and what else would. Implied answer: yes. His claim is that our understanding of (4) conforms to rigidity, and no situation where anyone but Aristotle was fond of dogs is relevant (pp. 13–14).

*Intuition note.* Scope only matters when there is an operator to take scope over. Because rigidity already shows up in operator-free sentences, it cannot be just a scope habit. Kripke argues that the "might have been" tests show it cannot be removed by fixing scopes either.

### Why Russell went wrong, and Kripke's reply (pp. 14–15)
- Kripke claims a direct intuition of the rigidity of names, shown in truth conditions of particular sentences. Secondary phenomena about what we would say give indirect evidence (p. 14).
- **Why Russell** (Kripke's explanation): (a) Russell did not consider modal questions, and rigidity was rarely considered after him. (b) Russell thought powerful philosophical arguments required a description theory of names and an eliminative theory of descriptions. Russell acknowledged his views conflicted with naive reactions (p. 14).
- **Kripke's thought experiment** (a sketch like the 'identity and schmidentity' one on p. 108 of the main text): imagine a formal language in which a rigid designator 'a' is introduced by stipulating that it denotes the unique object that actually has property F, when talking about any situation, actual or counterfactual. Kripke says it seemed clear that the speaker would then, by that very act, be in a position to say *I know that Fa*, yet 'Fa' would express a contingent truth, provided F is not an essential property of the object (p. 14).
- Lessons (pp. 14–15):
  1. Epistemic questions should be separated from necessity and contingency.
  2. Fixing a reference is not giving a synonym.
  3. This situation indicated that the evidence usually cited for synonymy of names and descriptions could instead be rationalized by this model, which also satisfies the rigidity intuition.
  4. Kripke says the burden of argument then seemed to fall heavily on the philosopher who wished to deny the rigidity intuition.
- The observation that speakers ordinarily do not fix references by identifying descriptions of the usual type came later (p. 15).

### Possible worlds (pp. 15–20)
- In the monograph Kripke argued against misuses: possible worlds as distant planets, and spurious problems of "transworld identification". He suggests *possible state (or history) of the world* or *counterfactual situation* as terms that might be better, and notes that "worlds" talk can often be replaced by modal talk. He does not repudiate possible worlds, nor regard them as a mere formal device (pp. 15–16).
- **Dice example** (p. 16):
  1. Two dice, A and B, each have 6 possible results.
  2. So there are $6\times 6 = 36$ possible states of the pair, one of which is actual.
  3. Total eleven arises from just two states, (A5, B6) and (A6, B5).
  4. So $P(\text{eleven}) = 2/36 = 1/18$, assuming equiprobable states.
- These 36 states are literally miniature "possible worlds", as long as we fictively ignore everything but the dice and what they show. The "actual world" here is the state actually realized. It is not the concrete physical sum of the two dice (pp. 16–17).
- No phantom entities are posited, and no question about "counterparts" or "another dimension" arises. Asking for a "criterion of transstate identity" is confused, since the state (A 6; B 5) is *given as such* and distinguished from (B 6; A 5) (p. 17).
- The possibilities are not given purely qualitatively. If they were, there would be 21 distinct possibilities, not 36. The 21 are 6 doubles plus $\binom{6}{2}=15$ unordered pairs of different faces (added step). Nor need A and B differ in some other respect such as colour. No commitment to "bare particulars" follows (p. 17).
- Kripke says possible worlds are little more than the miniworlds of school probability on a larger scale. But the general notion raises extra problems: total ways the world might have been involve more idealization. The philosopher must not let the apparatus pose questions not supported by the original intuitions of possibility (p. 18).
- In practice a "counterfactual situation" can be a miniworld restricted to relevant features (p. 18).
- Worlds can be taken, for philosophical or technical purposes, as abstract entities. The probabilist's "sample space" is just such a space of possible worlds. There are no special grounds to suppose that worlds must be given qualitatively, or that there need be any genuine problem of "transworld identification" (p. 19).
- The "actual world" in this sense should not be confused with the enormous scattered object around us. Non-actual worlds are not phantom duplicates of that object (pp. 19–20).
- Footnote 17: Kripke does not categorically assert that there are qualitatively identical but distinct world-states. He holds that if there is a philosophical argument excluding them, it cannot rest merely on the supposition that worlds must be stipulated purely qualitatively (p. 18).
- Footnote 18: he does not take possible worlds as a reductive analysis of modality. Modal locutions come earlier in our thought. The main motivation was that the analysis let modal logic be treated by set-theoretic model-theoretic techniques. Restricting to a narrower class of miniworlds would leave the issues about rigid designators and modal semantics essentially the same (p. 19).
- Footnote 16: Kripke judges some of the worst misinterpretations of rigidity would have had less currency under a rigorous possible-worlds presentation. He chose not to give one, and may do so elsewhere (p. 15).

*Intuition note.* A "world" can be specified in terms of particular individuals, not only qualitatively, and the dice show how this works with no mystery. Which die has the six is part of how the state is specified, so no further test for identifying the die across states is needed.

### Misconception 3: universal substitutivity of names (pp. 20–21)
- **Misreading (critics and sympathizers):** his doctrines imply that 'Cicero' and 'Tully' sentences express the same proposition, are believed together, or are equivalent for all semantic purposes (p. 20).
- Kripke says Russell seems to have held this for "logically proper names". It seems congenial to a purely "Millian" picture, but Kripke says he never intended to go so far (p. 20).
- Kripke's view that the sentence 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' could sometimes be used to raise an empirical issue while 'Hesperus is Hesperus' could not shows, he says, that he does not treat the two as completely interchangeable. It also indicates that the mode of fixing the reference is relevant to our epistemic attitude toward the sentences (pp. 20–21).
- Footnote 20 (reporting Lockwood): Mill does not take 'Cicero is Tully' to mean the same as 'Cicero is Cicero'. Lockwood reports that Mill holds instead that it means 'Cicero' and 'Tully' are codesignative, and that Mill sees such a metalinguistic component in all assertions involving names. Kripke has not investigated Mill's interpretation further and has no view on his exact doctrine (p. 20).
- Kripke has "no 'official doctrine'" on what propositions these sentences express, on whether they are objects of belief, or on treating names in epistemic contexts. He is unsure the apparatus of propositions does not break down here. See 'A Puzzle About Belief' (p. 21 and n. 21).
- Rigidity does imply interchangeability of codesignative names in modal contexts, subject to the caveat about possible nonexistence (n. 21, p. 21).
- **Footnote 21 on rigidity** (p. 21):
  - Kripke ignores delicate questions about possible nonexistence of an object.
  - He distinguishes *de jure* rigidity (reference stipulated to be a single object, in actual and counterfactual speech) from *de facto* rigidity (a description whose predicate happens to be true of one unique object in each world; his example is 'the smallest prime' for the number two).
  - His thesis is that names are rigid *de jure*, but the monograph is content with the weaker assertion of rigidity.
  - Because names are rigid de jure, he says a name designates its referent even when speaking of situations where the referent would not have existed. He says people persuaded him these questions deserve more careful discussion.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take Russell's (3) and Kripke's rigid reading of (1). Let A be the man Aristotle and Y be another philosopher. Consider three situations:
- S1 (actual): A is the last great philosopher of antiquity, and A is fond of dogs.
- S2: Y is the last great philosopher and dislikes dogs. A is still fond of dogs.
- S3: Y is the last great philosopher and likes dogs. A dislikes dogs.

| Situation | (1) rigid: "A is fond of dogs" | (3) Russell: "last great philosopher was fond of dogs" |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | A likes dogs → true | last great = A, likes → true |
| S2 | A likes dogs → true | last great = Y, dislikes → false |
| S3 | A dislikes dogs → false | last great = Y, likes → true |

The two analyses agree in S1 (the actual case) and differ in S2 and S3. That is Kripke's point: they agree extensionally and diverge counterfactually (p. 7).

## Concepts
- [[rigid designator]] — in this preface, rigidity is stated as a rule about truth conditions: there is a single individual and a single property such that, at every counterfactual situation, the proposition's truth condition is that individual's having the property there; if 'a' and 'b' are rigid designators, a true 'a = b' is necessary (pp. 3, 6, 9–10)
- [[identity-as-internal-relation]] (new) — $(x)(y)(x=y \supset \Box\, x=y)$; objects cannot be "contingently identical" (p. 3)
- [[indiscernibility-of-identicals]] (new) — Leibniz's law: identicals have all properties in common (n. 4, p. 3)
- [[reference-fixing-vs-meaning-giving]] (new) — a description can fix a reference without giving the name's meaning or being a synonym (pp. 5, 14–15)
- [[theory of descriptions]] — Russell's analysis of "the F is G" as exactly-one-F-and-it-is-G, here applied to names, giving nonrigid truth conditions (pp. 6–7)
- [[rigidity-vs-scope]] (new) — rigidity is a doctrine about truth conditions of all sentences, not a convention of wide scope for names (pp. 10–13)
- [[possible-worlds-as-abstract-states]] (new) — worlds as abstract states or histories, like the 36 dice states, not remote concrete planets (pp. 15–20)
- [[de-jure-vs-de-facto-rigidity]] (new) — stipulated rigidity versus accidental rigidity of a description (n. 21, p. 21)
- [[universal-substitutivity]] (new) — the doctrine that codesignative names are interchangeable in all contexts; Kripke says he never intended it (pp. 20–21)
- [[Frege's puzzle]] — "Hesperus is Phosphorus" may be informative while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not; Kripke says this indicates that the mode of fixing the reference is relevant to our epistemic attitude (pp. 20–21)

## Canonical instances
- [[aristotle-fond-of-dogs]] — the central example for rigid versus Russellian truth conditions (pp. 6–7)
- [[two-dice-miniworlds]] — 36 equiprobable states; probability of eleven is 1/18; no demand for a criterion of transstate identity (pp. 16–17)
- [[stipulated-rigid-designator-a]] (new) — 'a' introduced as the unique actual F: the speaker can say 'I know that Fa', yet 'Fa' is contingent (p. 14)
- [[hesperus-phosphorus]] — informative identity despite codesignation (pp. 20–21)
- [[smallest-prime]] — a de facto rigid description, designating two (n. 21, p. 21)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Rigid designator vs. nonrigid description ↔ formal semantics: a constant function from worlds to objects vs. a function that varies by world — a gloss we propose; the text does not state it.
- [structural-analogy] Possible worlds as abstract states ↔ probability theory: the sample space of equiprobable outcomes. Kripke calls the sample space "just" such a space (p. 19).
- [metaphor] "Philosophical crutch" of contingent identity ↔ philosophy of mind: debates about materialism, which Kripke mentions only as philosophical discussions "at that time" (p. 4).

## Questions this raises
- If a name's reference is fixed by a description but its meaning is not, what exactly does the speaker know when they say "I know that Fa" about 'a'?
- If rigidity cannot be reduced to scope, do the "might have been" tests give independent evidence for it?
- What goes wrong with "propositions" in belief contexts, where Kripke says he is unsure that the apparatus of propositions does not break down (p. 21)?
- How does *de jure* rigidity for names apply when the referent would not have existed (p. 21)?
