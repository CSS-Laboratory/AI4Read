# Kripke, Lecture III (part 2): Theoretical identities, apparent contingency, and the mind–body identity thesis

## L0 — One line
Kripke argues that scientific identities such as heat's being molecular motion are necessary but known only a posteriori, and that the analogy meant to make pain's being C-fiber stimulation a similar contingent discovery cannot be carried over.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke finishes Lecture III by extending his theory of names to natural-kind and natural-phenomenon terms such as 'gold', 'heat' and 'light'. He holds that such terms are rigid designators whose reference is fixed by contingent properties (e.g. a sensation), so identities such as heat = molecular motion are necessary but a posteriori. The apparent contingency is explained, he says, by a qualitatively identical evidential situation. He then applies this to the mind–body identity thesis: pain, unlike heat, has no contingent 'appearance' separate from itself, so he argues that the usual analogy fails and that identity theories face serious modal problems. He suspects this tells against usual forms of materialism, without claiming proof.

**Prerequisites:** rigid designator (a term that names the same thing in every possible world); necessary (true in all possible worlds) vs. a priori (knowable without experience) vs. a posteriori (known only through experience); fixing a reference vs. giving a meaning; de re modality (what is possible for a particular thing); Descartes's argument for mind–body distinctness; Frege–Russell descriptivism (a term has a descriptive sense).

## L2 — Key ideas
- The terms 'light' and 'heat' do not mean whatever produces certain sensations in us. The sensation fixes the reference of the term, and the referent could have existed without producing the sensation (pp. 131–133).
- Natural-kind terms and terms for natural phenomena resemble proper names more than has been realised. Kripke "more or less" backs Mill on singular terms but rejects him on general terms (pp. 134–135).
- Five recapitulated points: reference-fixing is not meaning; characteristic properties of a kind are fallible; science finds essences; the reference passes along causal chains; and the way sensed phenomena are identified seems crucial to us, which may create an illusion of necessity (pp. 134–140).
- Kripke holds that theoretical identities are generally identities between two rigid designators, so they are necessary a posteriori. He also holds that discoveries of species essence are not a change of meaning (pp. 138, 140).
- Saying that an identity might have turned out otherwise is, for Kripke, loose talk: for some necessary a posteriori truths, in a qualitatively identical evidential situation a corresponding qualitative statement might have been false. It is not a possibility for the identity itself (pp. 140–144).
- The identity theorist cannot just grant the Cartesian intuitions. Identity between rigid designators is necessary, so the theorist must explain them away (pp. 144–148).
- The heat analogy fails for pain. Heat has an intermediary, the sensation S, whereas pain is picked out by its own essential phenomenological quality, so no "felt as" gap exists (pp. 150–154).
- Kripke thinks the considerations tell heavily against the usual forms of materialism, but he does not claim a proof. In fn. 77 he calls the mind–body problem wide open (p. 155).

### A. Light, heat, lightning: reference fixed by a contingent property (pp. 131–134)

**Light.** When we speak of other possible worlds we talk about this phenomenon, not about whatever gives us the visual impression. There might have been light that did not help us see, and something else might have helped us see. The way we identified light fixed a reference (p. 131).

**Heat.** We identify and sense heat by the sensation it produces in us (p. 131).
- The way heat is fixed might be so important to the concept that, for someone who detects heat only with an instrument and cannot feel it, we might want to say, if we like, that the concept differs even though the referent is the same (p. 131).
- Still, 'heat' does not mean whatever gives people these sensations. People might not have been sensitive to heat while heat still existed. And if light rays gave people the sensation because of different nerve endings, light would have given the sensation, not heat (p. 131).

**Can we imagine heat not being molecular motion?** We can imagine having discovered that it was not. Kripke says it seems to him that any case that seems to show heat as something else is really a case in which creatures with different nerve endings are sensitive to something else (say light) and feel what we feel as heat. In that situation light, or a stream of photons, would not have been heat. It would only have produced the sensations we call "sensations of heat" (pp. 131–132).

**Lightning.** The same holds for "lightning is electricity". An apparatus might fool people with a flash that looks like lightning but is not an electrical discharge. That would not be lightning (p. 132).

**General pattern.**
- A referent is fixed, for the real world and for all possible worlds, by a contingent property: producing such sensations in us. It is contingent that people exist at all (pp. 132–133).
- We do not know a priori which physical phenomenon this is. We discovered it is molecular motion. That gives an essential property of the phenomenon, which in all possible worlds is molecular motion (p. 133).
- The identifying property, producing the sensation, is contingent. The phenomenon could have existed without being felt as heat (p. 133).
- Kripke is simplifying here. It might be part of the nature of human beings to have a heat-sensitive neural structure, and then this too could turn out necessary (p. 133).
- fn. 68: some think a different "form of heat" other than molecular motion is possible. Kripke is disinclined to accept this. Holders of that view can replace 'heat', 'light', 'pain' by 'our heat', 'our light', 'our pain'. It would make little difference to the lectures (p. 133).

*Intuition note.* The sensation is only a pointer used to pick out the phenomenon. Once the phenomenon is picked out, it stays the same thing in every possible world, whether or not anyone feels it.

### B. Recapitulation (pp. 134–140)

Kripke announces a recapitulation before turning to the mind–body identity thesis (p. 134).

**First: natural-kind terms resemble proper names.**
- This holds for species names, both count nouns ('cat', 'tiger') and mass terms ('gold', 'water', 'iron pyrites'). It also holds for terms for natural phenomena ('heat', 'light', 'sound', 'lightning') and, "presumably, suitably elaborated", for adjectives ('hot', 'loud', 'red') (p. 134).
- **Mill:** definite descriptions have denotation and connotation, genuine proper names have denotation only, and general names have connotation. A general term is defined by a conjunction of properties, as in definition by genus and differentia (p. 134).
- Kripke says Kant's "gold = yellow metal", if Kant did hold it, may stem from this tradition (p. 134).
- **Frege and Russell:** they disputed Mill on singular names but endorsed him on general names, so all terms have a sense. Later theorists replaced the conjunction by a cluster of properties, only enough of which need apply (pp. 134–135).
- **Kripke's view:** it reverses Frege and Russell. It (more or less) endorses Mill on singular terms and disputes him on general terms (p. 135).

**Second: reference-fixing is not meaning.**
- Properties carried by the way a term's reference was fixed are a priori but perhaps contingent. Analytic properties, given by meaning, are necessary. For species as for names, the reference-fixing description is not a synonym of the term (p. 135).
- A name's reference is typically fixed in an initial baptism, by ostension or description. Otherwise it is usually determined by a chain from link to link (p. 135).
- **Imagined baptism of gold:** "Gold is the substance instantiated by the items over there, or at any rate, by almost all of them." This identity is not completely necessary, since gold might have existed even if the items had not. It is a priori in the same sense as (and with the same qualifications as) the definition of a meter as the length of S: it fixes a reference (p. 135 and its footnote).
- Kripke believes that, in general, terms for natural kinds (animal, vegetable, chemical kinds) get their reference fixed this way, as the kind instantiated by (almost all of) a sample. "Almost all" allows some fool's gold, which is rejected as not really gold (p. 136).
- If the sample proves radically non-uniform, reactions vary: we may declare two kinds, or drop the term. These options are not exhaustive (p. 136).
- A purported new kind K, defined by a uniform sample J, may turn out to belong to a known kind L. Observational error had led us to think J had a characteristic C excluding it from L. Then we would say K does not exist. Had L not been identified, we might instead have said K existed but we were wrong about C (p. 136).
- To the extent that the notion 'same kind' is vague, so is the original notion of gold; ordinarily the vagueness does not matter in practice (p. 136).
- For a natural phenomenon perceptible to the senses the fixing is simple: heat is that which is sensed by sensation S. The identity fixes a reference, so it is a priori but not necessary, since heat might have existed though we did not. 'Heat', like 'gold', is a rigid designator whose reference is fixed by its 'definition'. Electricity is identified as the cause of certain experimental effects (pp. 136–137).

**Third: characteristic properties are fallible.**
- Properties believed at least roughly characteristic of the kind and true of the original sample are used to place new items. "Properties" may include larger kinds, such as animality and felinity for tigers (p. 137).
- These properties need not hold a priori of the kind. The yellowness of gold may be an illusion, or some gold may be white (p. 137).
- An item may have all the original characteristics and still not belong. An animal may look like a tiger and not be one. Elements in one column of the periodic table can resemble each other. Such failures are exceptions but occur (p. 137).
- A priori, all we can say is that it is an empirical matter whether the characteristics apply universally, or even ever, and whether they are jointly sufficient for membership (p. 137).
- Joint sufficiency is extremely unlikely to be necessary, but it may be true. Kripke says that, as far as he knows, any animal looking just like a tiger is a tiger. It is still metaphysically possible that there should have been look-alikes that were not tigers (pp. 137–138).
- Universal applicability may be necessary, if true. 'Cats are animals' has turned out necessary. For many statements subsuming one species under another, we know a priori that, if true at all, they are necessarily true (p. 138).

**Fourth: science finds essences.**
- A material object is pure gold if and only if its only element is that with atomic number 79. The "if and only if" can be taken as strict (necessary) (p. 138).
- In general, science attempts, by investigating basic structural traits, to find the nature, and so the essence, of a kind. Theoretical identifications such as heat's being molecular motion are necessary but not a priori (p. 138).
- The type of property identity used in science seems to be associated with necessity, not with a prioricity or analyticity. Kripke's example is the coextensiveness of 'hotter than' with higher mean molecular kinetic energy:
$$\text{For all bodies } x, y:\ x \text{ is hotter than } y \iff x \text{ has higher mean molecular kinetic energy than } y.$$
 This coextensiveness is necessary but not a priori (p. 138).
- The philosophical notion of attribute seems to demand a priori and analytic coextensiveness as well (p. 138).
- Discoveries of species essence are not a change of meaning. We need not even assume that the biologist who denies that whales are fish has a different concept of fishhood from the layman. He corrects the layman, discovering that it is a necessary truth that whales are mammals and not fish. Neither statement was supposed to be a priori or analytic (p. 138).

**Fifth: samples grow and the term travels.**
- The original sample is augmented by new items (p. 139).
- The species name may be passed from link to link, as with proper names. Many who have seen little or no gold can use the term. Their reference is fixed by a causal (historical) chain, not by items (p. 139). Kripke does not spell out an exact theory (p. 139).
- For proper names, different speakers may fix the reference differently with little importance if the referent is the same. Species names are probably similar, though the temptation to say the metallurgist has a different concept of gold may be somewhat greater (p. 139).
- For sensed phenomena, the way reference is fixed seems overwhelmingly important to us. A blind man who uses 'light' as a rigid designator for the same phenomenon seems to have lost a great deal, perhaps a different concept ("concept" is used non-technically) (p. 139).
- Identifying light in a certain way seems crucial though it is not necessary. The intimate connection may create an illusion of necessity. Kripke thinks this, with the remarks on property identity, may be essential to understanding the dispute over primary and secondary qualities (p. 139).
- fn. 71: yellowness is not a dispositional property, though it is related to a disposition. Its reference is fixed by a description of the manifest property of objects that causes them, under normal circumstances, to be seen as yellow. 'Yellow' does not mean that something tends to produce such a sensation. Redefining it in terms of some circumstances C either circularly involves yellowness or turns the definition into a scientific discovery. The physical scientist may then identify the property in more fundamental terms (p. 140).

*Intuition note.* A description can start a name's life without being its meaning. Later science may overturn the description (yellow gold, whales as fish) and still be talking about the same thing. For sensed phenomena, by contrast, the way we identify them seems crucial to us, and this close connection may create an illusion of necessity.

### C. Theoretical identities and the objection from apparent contingency (pp. 140–144)

**Claim (Kripke's).** According to the conception he advocates, theoretical identities are generally identities between two rigid designators, so they are examples of the necessary a posteriori (p. 140).

**Objection (an imagined objector):** (p. 140–141)
- You admit heat might have turned out not to be molecular motion, and gold not to be element 79. Elizabeth II might have turned out not to be George VI's daughter. The table might have turned out to be made of ice from the Thames. Hesperus might have turned out not to be Phosphorus (pp. 140–141).
- The objector argues: if Hesperus might have turned out not to be Phosphorus, then Hesperus might not have been Phosphorus; to deny this is to deny the self-evident modal principle that what is entailed by a possibility must itself be possible (p. 141).
- The objector adds that the 'might' cannot be merely epistemic, as with Fermat's Last Theorem possibly turning out true or false. In mathematics it was impossible for the answer to turn out otherwise. In your cases, he says, it really is logically possible that gold should have turned out to be a compound (p. 141).

**Kripke's reply.**
- He grants the objector's point: if the table could not have been made of ice, it could not have turned out to be made of ice, since its being able to turn out so entails that it could have been so (pp. 141–142).
- Kripke thinks the intuition means simply that there might have been a table looking and feeling just like this one, in this position, which was made of ice. The speaker could have been in a qualitatively identical epistemic situation, with the same sensory evidence, about a table made of ice (p. 142).
- This is akin to the counterpart theorists' situation. Kripke says he is speaking loosely. This table could not have had a different origin (p. 142).
- Something like counterpart theory applies only because we are interested in what might be true of a table given certain evidence, not of this table. Applying counterparts to genuine de re modalities is, from Kripke's standpoint, "perverse" (p. 142).
- General answer: "Any necessary truth, whether a priori or a posteriori, could not have turned out otherwise." For some necessary a posteriori truths, in appropriate qualitatively identical evidential situations an appropriate qualitative statement might have been false (p. 142).
- The loose statement that gold might have turned out to be a compound should be replaced (roughly) by this: it is logically possible that there should have been a compound with all the properties originally known to hold of gold (pp. 142–143).
- The inaccurate statement that Hesperus might have turned out not to be Phosphorus should be replaced by the true contingency that two distinct bodies might have occupied, in the morning and evening, the positions actually occupied by Hesperus-Phosphorus-Venus (p. 143).
- The Fermat case gives a different impression because no such analogue suggests itself, except that an unproved conjecture can be true or false (p. 143).
- fn. 72: saying that gold might turn out not to be an element is correct, since the 'might' is epistemic. Saying that gold might have turned out not to be an element, Kripke seems to mean metaphysically, so it is subject to the correction above (p. 143).

**A simpler paradigm, often usable to at least approximately the same effect, for identities of rigid designators.** Let $R_1$ and $R_2$ be rigid designators. Then $R_1 = R_2$ is necessary if true. Their references may be fixed by nonrigid designators $D_1$ and $D_2$, for example the heavenly body in such-and-such position in the evening (morning) sky. Then $D_1 = D_2$ may be contingent. This is often what leads to the erroneous view that $R_1 = R_2$ might have turned out otherwise (pp. 143–144).

*Intuition note.* "Might have turned out otherwise" describes how things could have looked to us, not how the thing itself could have been. The same evidence could have come from a different object or set-up, and that is what is contingent.

### D. The identity thesis (pp. 144–155)

**Types of identification (p. 144).** Three kinds are distinguished: a person with his body; a particular sensation or state with a particular brain state (Jones's pain at 06:00 with his C-fiber stimulation); and a type of mental state with a type of physical state (pain with C-fiber stimulation). Each raises analytical problems, rightly raised by Cartesian critics, that cannot be avoided by a simple appeal to an alleged confusion of synonymy with identity (p. 144). One may accept some identities and deny others. For example, Nagel and Davidson are notable examples of philosophers who accept identities of particular sensations with brain states but deny identities of mental and physical types (fn. 73, p. 144). Kripke concentrates on type–type identity (p. 144).

**Person and body (pp. 144–146).**
- **Descartes's argument (as reported by Kripke):** the mind could exist without the body, so they are distinct. He could equally have argued from the body existing without the mind (pp. 144–145).
- Kripke calls inadmissible the response that grants the premise but denies the conclusion. If "Descartes" and "B" are rigid designators and Descartes were identical to B, the identity would be necessary. Neither could exist without the other (p. 145).
- The supposed analogue is the first Postmaster General being the inventor of bifocals. It fails because the description of the inventor of bifocals is not a rigid designator. A world without bifocals is not thereby a world without Franklin. A philosopher who wishes to refute the Cartesian conclusion must therefore refute the Cartesian premise, and that task is not trivial (pp. 145–146).
- fn. 74: the body exists without the mind (and presumably the person) when it is a corpse, which, if accepted, would already show person and body distinct. A view that the person is nothing over and above the body would face similar modal difficulties. Kripke does not discuss functional-state views (p. 145).

**Particular pain A and brain state B (pp. 146–148).**
- Prima facie it is logically possible that B existed (Jones's brain in that state) without any pain, so without A. The theorist cannot grant this cheerfully, since if $A = B$ the identity must be necessary (p. 146).
- A theorist could say being a pain is only a contingent property of A. But "can any case of essence be more obvious" than that being a pain is necessary for each pain? That theorist must also say being a sensation is contingent for A (p. 146).
- Kripke says many theorists use the Franklin paradigm: some contingent property of the brain state is supposed to make it a pain, typically its causal role (fn. 75: Armstrong; Lewis). On their view it is then contingent that the state is mental at all. This seems to Kripke self-evidently absurd (pp. 146–147).
- **Converse direction (pain without the brain state).** Being a brain state, even of a specific type, is essential to B. The identity theorist must therefore hold that A could not have existed without a specific configuration of molecules. If $A = B$, any essential property of one is essential to the other (pp. 147–148).
- The theorist must explain the Cartesian intuitions away, showing how they are illusory. This task may not be impossible, since we have seen how some apparent contingencies turn out necessary. But it is obviously not child's play (p. 148).

**Type–type identity: pain and C-fiber stimulation (pp. 148–154).**
- The supposed analogues are heat/molecular motion and water/hydrogen hydroxide. The usual view holds that pain/C-fiber identification is contingent (p. 148).
- 'Pain' is a rigid designator. 'C-fiber stimulation' is too, provided 'C-fibers' is (Kripke supposes so, while admitting the supposition is risky). So the identity, if true, must be necessary (pp. 148–149).
- This means no C-fiber stimulation without pain, and no pain without C-fiber stimulation. Kripke finds this surprising but does not dismiss it yet (p. 149).
- fn. 76: Kripke uses "correlated" neutrally. The dualist and the identity theorist both accept a correspondence. The dualist says it is irreflexive, and the identity theorist says it is a special case of identity (p. 149).
- If the identity theorist could show the apparent possibility is an illusion like the one for heat, he would rebut the Cartesian by exposing the premise as false (pp. 149–150).
- Kripke thinks this unlikely to succeed. The heat strategy (qualitatively identical epistemic situations, or contingently coinciding reference-fixers) works as follows: someone could sense a different phenomenon in the way we sense heat. Or creatures might not get $S$ from molecular motion but get it from something else (pp. 150–151).

*Why the analogy fails (pp. 151–153).*
- **"Felt as" argument.** With heat, what seemed possible is molecular motion existing without being felt as heat, that is, without producing $S$. For C-fibers, if stimulation could exist without being felt as pain, it could exist without pain. That would flatly contradict the supposed necessary identity (p. 151).
- The identity theorist holds that the physical state is identical to the mental state, not that it produces it. Heat has an intermediary (the sensation); the mental case has none (pp. 151–152).
- **Epistemic situation.** Someone can be in the same epistemic situation as if there were heat without heat, by feeling $S$. Or he can lack $S$ in the presence of heat. But "To be in the same epistemic situation that would obtain if one had a pain is to have a pain" (p. 152).
- **Reference-fixing.** 'Heat' is rigid, with reference fixed by an accidental property (producing $S$). Pain is picked out by its essential property, its immediate phenomenological quality. If any phenomenon is picked out exactly as we pick out pain, it is pain (pp. 152–153).
- **God thought-experiment.** To make heat identical to molecular motion, it would seem that God needs only to create the molecular motion. The substantive task is making it felt as heat, by creating sentient beings. For C-fibers, God would seem to need an additional act to make the stimulation felt as pain rather than as a tickle or warmth. If such things are within His powers, the relation cannot be identity. Making a man the inventor of bifocals takes extra work, but making an existing pain a pain takes none (pp. 153–154).

**Summary of the case (p. 154).**
- Identity cannot hold contingently between objects. If the identity thesis is correct, the apparent contingency cannot lie between the mental and physical states. It also cannot lie, as for heat, between the phenomenon and the way it appears, because for mental phenomena there is no appearance beyond the mental phenomenon (p. 154).
- The reverse possibility (pain without C-fiber stimulation) raises problems that the heat analogy also cannot resolve (p. 154).
- Kripke says he has discussed similar problems more briefly for equating the self with the body, and for particular mental and physical events. He suspects the theorist who identifies particular mental and physical events faces problems fairly similar to those of the type–type theorist and cannot appeal to the standard analogues (pp. 154–155).

**Conclusion (p. 155).** That the usual moves are unavailable is no proof that none exist. Kripke suspects that the considerations "tell heavily against the usual forms of materialism". Materialism, he thinks, must hold that a physical description is complete and that mental facts follow from physical facts by necessity. No identity theorist seems to him to have made a convincing argument against the intuitive view that this is not so (p. 155).

**fn. 77 (p. 155).** Identity theorists have positive arguments that Kripke has not answered. Some seem to him weak or ideological, but others strike him as compelling, and he cannot answer them at present. Rejecting the identity thesis does not mean accepting Cartesian dualism. His view on origin from a particular sperm and egg suggests rejecting the Cartesian picture. He regards the mind–body problem as "wide open and extremely confusing".

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
We model "Hesperus = Phosphorus" with two possible worlds.

- **w1:** one body $p$ occupies the evening position and the morning position.
- **w2:** $p$ occupies the morning position, and a different body $q$ occupies the evening position.
- Actual world: w1.
- $R_1$ = 'Hesperus'. It rigidly names the actual evening body, $p$.
- $R_2$ = 'Phosphorus'. It rigidly names the actual morning body, $p$.
- $D_1$ = the body in the evening position and $D_2$ = the body in the morning position, read as descriptions. These are nonrigid.

| World | $D_1$ | $D_2$ | Is $D_1 = D_2$? | $R_1$ | $R_2$ | Is $R_1 = R_2$? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| w1 | $p$ | $p$ | yes | $p$ | $p$ | yes |
| w2 | $q$ | $p$ | no | $p$ | $p$ | yes |

- "$R_1 = R_2$" is true in 2 of 2 worlds, so it is necessary. We learn it only by observation, so it is a posteriori.
- "$D_1 = D_2$" is true in 1 of 2 worlds, so it is contingent.
- A speaker in w2 who fixed one name by the description $D_1$ and another by $D_2$ would be in a situation qualitatively like ours, but his two names would pick out different bodies. The contingency belongs to $D_1 = D_2$, not to $R_1 = R_2$ as we use those names.
- This is the pattern of pp. 143–144.

## Concepts
- [[rigid designator]] — a term denoting the same object in every possible world; Kripke applies it to 'heat', 'gold', 'pain', 'C-fibers' (pp. 135–136, 148–149).
- [[necessary a posteriori]] — true in all possible worlds, but known only by empirical investigation; theoretical identities such as heat = molecular motion (p. 140).
- [[causal-historical theory of reference]] — a species name's reference passes along a chain; users need not have seen the sample (p. 139).
- [[reference-fixing-vs-synonymy]] (new) — the description that fixes a referent carries a priori but perhaps contingent properties, and is not a synonym of the term (pp. 131, 135).
- [[natural-kind-term]] (new) — species names and terms for natural phenomena treated as akin to proper names, not as definitions by genus and differentia (pp. 134–135).
- [[essential-property]] (new) — a property something could not have lacked (it is necessary of it), such as heat's being molecular motion or a pain's being a pain (pp. 133, 146).
- [[epistemic-vs-metaphysical-possibility]] (new) — talk of how things might have turned out is reduced to a qualitatively identical evidential situation, whereas an epistemic 'might' expresses lack of a priori certainty (pp. 141–143; fn. 72, p. 143).
- [[identity-theory-of-mind]] (new) — the thesis that mental states or events are identical to physical ones, in token or type versions (pp. 144–149).
- [[sense (Sinn) vs. reference (Bedeutung)]] — Kripke rejects the Frege–Russell view that general terms carry a sense, whether a conjunction or a cluster (pp. 134–135).
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — analytic properties come from meaning and are necessary; fixing a reference does not make a property analytic (p. 135).

## Canonical instances
- [[heat-molecular-motion]] — heat identified by sensation S; the identity is necessary a posteriori (pp. 131–133, 136, 150–151).
- [[gold-atomic-number-79]] — gold is pure iff its only element has atomic number 79 (p. 138).
- [[lightning-electricity]] — a flash that looks like lightning but is not an electrical discharge is not lightning (p. 132).
- [[whales-and-fish]] — a biologist's correction is a discovery of a necessary truth, not a change of meaning (p. 138).
- [[table-made-of-ice]] — the table's apparent possibility of turning out to be ice is reduced to a look-alike table made of ice (pp. 141–142).
- [[hesperus-phosphorus]] — $R_1 = R_2$ necessary; $D_1 = D_2$ contingent (pp. 141, 143–144).
- [[franklin-bifocals]] — Franklin's being the inventor is contingent, unlike a pain's being a pain (pp. 145–146).
- [[pain-c-fiber-stimulation]] — Kripke's test case for the type–type identity thesis (pp. 148–154).
- [[descartes-body-argument]] — mind without body gives distinctness; Kripke says that whoever wishes to refute the conclusion must refute the premise (pp. 144–146).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Reference fixed once at baptism, then carried rigidly ↔ programming: a value evaluated once and stored as a constant, versus a function re-evaluated in each context. This resembles the $D$/$R$ contrast (pp. 143–144).
- [structural-analogy] "Qualitatively identical evidential situation" ↔ statistics/inference: observationally equivalent worlds cannot be told apart by the evidence. Kripke uses this to explain apparent contingency (p. 142).
- [structural-analogy] Counterpart-style talk used only for evidence-relative claims ↔ counterpart-theoretic modal semantics (the text mentions counterpart theorists, p. 142).

## Questions this raises
- Is the move from talk of what might have turned out otherwise to qualitative contingencies fully general? Kripke says he has given no general paradigm (p. 143).
- Does the argument about pain depend on the claim that pain is picked out by its essential immediate phenomenological quality (p. 152)? Could a functionalist or causal-role view deny this?
- What would the identity theorist have to say to explain the Cartesian intuitions away, given Kripke's remark that this task is obviously not child's play (p. 148)?
- What follows for materialism if mental facts must follow from physical facts "by necessity" (p. 155)? How does this relate to Kripke's admission that identity theorists have strong positive arguments he cannot answer (fn. 77)?
