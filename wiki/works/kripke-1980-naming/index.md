# Kripke, S. A. (1980). *Naming and Necessity*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. (Lectures given at Princeton, January 1970; first printed in D. Davidson & G. Harman (eds.), *Semantics of Natural Language*, Reidel, 1972.)

## In one paragraph
Kripke argues against the Frege–Russell view that a proper name is a disguised definite description. A name is a rigid designator: it picks out the same object in every possible situation where that object exists. Its reference comes from a chain of communication back to an initial baptism, not from descriptions in the speaker's head. A description may fix a name's reference without giving its meaning. So "necessary" (a matter of how things might have been) and "a priori" (a matter of how we can know) are different notions. True identities between rigid terms, such as "Hesperus is Phosphorus", are therefore necessary yet known only empirically. Natural-kind terms such as "gold" and "heat" work much like names. Kripke then applies this to the mind–body identity theory and finds that the usual heat analogy fails for pain.

## The path through the work
- [[kripke-1980-naming/front-preface]] — Kripke leaves the lectures almost unchanged and uses the preface to trace where rigid designation came from and to correct common misreadings of it: it is not a scope convention, it is unaffected by shared names, and possible worlds are abstract states like dice outcomes.
- [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p1]] — Kripke argues that proper names are probably not disguised descriptions, that "a priori" (epistemic) and "necessary" (metaphysical) are distinct notions, and that possible worlds are stipulated, so no qualitative criterion of cross-world identity is needed to ask what might have happened to Nixon.
- [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]] — Kripke holds that names are rigid designators (terms that pick out the same object in every possible situation where it exists), that a description may fix a name's reference without giving its meaning, so that the a priori and the necessary can come apart, and that the cluster-of-descriptions theory of names is wrong.
- [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]] — In general, names do not get their reference from descriptions the speaker associates with them but from a chain of communication back to a baptism, and because names are rigid, a true identity between names is necessary even though it need not be known a priori.
- [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]] — A true identity between names is necessary even though one may not know it a priori, and words for natural kinds such as "gold" and "tiger" fix a kind by samples, so, if Kripke's argument is right, scientific discoveries about what the kind is are necessary truths rather than definitional ones, provided the properties found form the basis of what the substance is.
- [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]] — Kripke argues that scientific identities such as heat's being molecular motion are necessary but known only a posteriori, and that the analogy meant to make pain's being C-fiber stimulation a similar contingent discovery cannot be carried over.
- [[kripke-1980-naming/addenda]] — Kripke clarifies several claims of the lectures: unicorns and fictional names, the Kant point on necessity and the a priori, the noncircularity condition, and reference shifts.

## Big ideas across units
- **Names are not disguised descriptions.** Kripke attacks the Frege–Russell view and its cluster-of-descriptions variants. He first states the six theses, then refutes them by counterexample (Gödel/Schmidt, Feynman, Jonah) and by circularity. Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]].
- **Fixing reference is not giving meaning.** A description can pick out the referent (the metre stick, the contingent effect of light on us) without being a synonym. This is why the necessary and the a priori can separate. Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/front-preface]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]].
- **Rigid designation.** Names designate the same object in every possible world, unlike descriptions. The preface fixes what the thesis does and does not claim: it is not a scope convention, and it does not mean full substitutivity. Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/front-preface]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]].
- **Possible worlds are stipulated, not discovered.** We start from the object and ask what might have happened to it, so no qualitative criteria of transworld identity are needed. The preface adds the dice-state picture. Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/front-preface]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]].
- **The causal-historical picture of reference, and noncircularity.** Reference passes along a chain back to a baptism. Condition (C) rules out circular accounts. The addenda refine "passing the buck" and note that present intentions can override a chain (Madagascar). Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/addenda]].
- **Necessity is not a priority.** "A priori" is epistemic and "necessary" is metaphysical. Kripke illustrates this with Goldbach, a rigid metre, and Hesperus/Phosphorus, and in the addenda he criticises Kant's assumption that what is known to be necessary must be known a priori. Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/addenda]].
- **Necessary a posteriori truths and essence.** True identities between rigid designators are necessary. Origin and kind-membership are plausible essential properties found empirically. Apparent contingency is explained by a qualitatively identical evidential situation. Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]], [[kripke-1980-naming/addenda]].
- **Natural-kind terms behave like names, with consequences for mind and body.** "Gold", "tiger", "heat" and "light" are rigid kind terms, so scientific identifications are necessary. This is why the identity theory of mind faces modal problems, since pain has no separate contingent "appearance". Units: [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]], and, for the mind–body analogy, [[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]].

## How to read it
- **Essential (the catalog's focus):** Lecture I ([[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch01-lecture-p2]]), Lecture II ([[kripke-1980-naming/ch02-lecture]]) and Lecture III ([[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p1]], [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]]).
- **Dependencies:**
  - Part 2 of Lecture I builds on part 1 (the name/description distinction, a priori vs. necessary, stipulated worlds).
  - Lecture II tests the six-thesis cluster theory stated at the end of Lecture I and relies on rigidity and reference-fixing from there.
  - Lecture III recaps the attack on descriptivism and relies on the reference-fixing/synonymy distinction from Lecture II.
  - Part 2 of Lecture III extends part 1 to heat and light, then to the mind–body identity thesis.
  - The addenda assume all the lectures.
- **The preface:** the catalog calls it a useful summary. It clarifies what rigidity claims and corrects misreadings, so it helps both before and after the lectures. It also covers the necessity of identity and the dice picture of worlds.
- **Can be skipped or postponed on a first pass:** the addenda, which are short clarifications. If you care only about language, you can postpone the mind–body section of [[kripke-1980-naming/ch03-lecture-p2]]. The preface's later parts on possible worlds and substitutivity are also less central.
