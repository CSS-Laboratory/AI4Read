# Addenda to *Naming and Necessity*

## L0 — One line
Kripke clarifies several claims of the lectures: unicorns and fictional names, the Kant point on necessity and the a priori, the noncircularity condition, and reference shifts.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
These short notes answer questions about the lectures. Kripke explains why no possible species or person can be called "the unicorns" or "Holmes", and why finding a matching animal would not by itself prove myth-unicorns existed. He argues that Kant appears to assume, wrongly, that a proposition known to be necessary must be known a priori. He restates when "passing the buck" in fixing reference is noncircular and when it risks error. He concedes that baptisms are not always identifiable, and that a present intention can override an older reference chain (Madagascar). He ends with remarks on Donnellan and Kaplan and an open question about physical necessity.

**Prerequisites:** the lectures' main ideas: description theory of names, historical-chain picture of reference, natural kind terms, a priori vs. necessary, possible worlds.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- If the myth was about one particular species, there is no possible species of which we can say it would have been the unicorns, because the myth underdetermines internal structure (p. 157).
- Discovering animals that match the myth would not by itself prove the myth was about them; a historical connection is needed (p. 157).
- Fictional names do not name particular possible-but-unactual people; Kripke withdraws his 1963 wording (p. 158).
- Kant appears to hold that a proposition known to be necessary must be known a priori (known independently of experience); Kripke replies that mathematical truths can be learned from a machine or a mathematician (p. 159).
- Kripke's necessary a posteriori cases share the mathematical character: they cannot be contingently true, so empirical knowledge of them is knowledge of necessity (p. 159).
- Reference-fixing by passing the buck (letting another speaker's use fix my reference) is noncircular provided the other speaker's description does not ultimately involve my references. Even then it can misfire, e.g. in the Gödel/Schmidt case (pp. 160–161).
- Initial baptisms (first namings) may be unidentifiable. Present intentions can override historical chains, as with "Madagascar" (pp. 162–163).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### (a) Unicorns (original pp. 23–4)
Kripke separates two theses (p. 156).
1. **Metaphysical:** no counterfactual situation is properly describable as one in which there would have been unicorns.
2. **Epistemological:** discovering animals with all the features the myth gives unicorns would not by itself prove there were unicorns.

**Argument for 1** (pp. 156–157):
- Tigers are an actual species. As argued in lecture 3, tigers cannot be defined just by appearance. A different species with a different internal structure could look like tigers ("fool's tigers"). We may be misled into thinking otherwise because no such species actually exists, so in practice appearance is enough to identify the species.
- Unicorns are a mythical species with no actual instances. Several distinct hypothetical species could have the myth's external appearance, with different internal structures (reptilic, mammalian, amphibious).
- Kripke supposes the myth meant one particular species but gives too little information about internal structure to fix a unique one. Then no actual or possible species is such that we can say it would have been the unicorns.

**Argument for 2** (p. 157):
- A story describing a substance that looks like gold may be about "fool's gold". As with proper names, what is discussed is fixed by the historical connection of the story to a substance.
- Perhaps the myth was pure invention. Matching animals would then be coincidence, and a historical connection would still have to be shown.

**Fictional names** (pp. 157–158):
- Discovering a detective like Holmes would not show that Conan Doyle wrote about him. Doyle may have written pure fiction with a coincidental resemblance; this is theoretically possible, though fantastically unlikely in practice.
- Granted there is no Holmes, one cannot say of any possible person that he would have been Holmes had he existed. Darwin or Jack the Ripper might have performed Holmes's exploits. Kripke asks "For if so, which one?"; the implied answer is that none can be singled out.
- Kripke therefore withdraws the 1963 sentence "Holmes does not exist, but in other states of affairs, he would have existed". It wrongly suggests that "Holmes" names a particular possible-but-nonactual individual (p. 158).
- The substantive point of that passage stands. In other worlds some actual individuals may be absent and new ones may appear. If a free variable in an open formula $A(x)$ is assigned an individual, a problem arises, in a model-theoretic treatment of modal logic, as to whether the formula is to get a truth-value in worlds where that individual does not exist (p. 158).
- He admits the remarks are cryptically brief and expects to elaborate in a forthcoming work on existential statements, empty names and fictional entities (p. 158).

*Intuition note:* A description that fits several possible kinds equally well does not pick out one of them. Appearance alone does not settle a kind; it seems to suffice in practice only because the actual world has no "fool's" cases.

### (b) "Can" to "must", first paragraph of original p. 35
- Stroud drew Kripke's attention to a related mistake in Kant. Kant says experience does not teach us that a thing cannot be otherwise. He then calls necessity and strict universality sure criteria of a priori knowledge (*Critique* B 3–4, quoted p. 159).
- Kant thus appears to hold that a proposition known to be necessary is known a priori, not merely knowable that way (p. 159).
- Kripke objects: a mathematical truth can be learned a posteriori from a computing machine or a mathematician. Kant cannot reply that experience shows truth but not necessity. One knows a priori that a mathematical statement cannot be contingently true, so if true it is necessary (p. 159).
- All the necessary a posteriori cases in the text share this character. Philosophical analysis shows they cannot be contingently true, so empirical knowledge of their truth is automatically empirical knowledge of their necessity. This covers identity statements and essence. It may give a clue to a general characterization of a posteriori knowledge of necessary truths (p. 159).
- Even if the computer case were the only objection, Kant could still hold (1) every necessary truth is knowable a priori, or (2) every necessary truth, if known at all, must be knowable a priori. Both use the obscure notion of possible a priori knowledge. Restricted to a priori knowledge of a standard human sort, Kripke argues against both in the text (pp. 159–160).
- Kripke holds that propositions contemporary philosophers count as "empirical" can be necessary and known to be so (p. 160).
- Correction: he could not find in Kant the characterization of a priori truth as truth knowable independently of experience. As far as Kripke can see, Kant refers only to a priori knowledge of particular statements. The text incautiously ascribed this to Kant (p. 160).
- Kant uses "necessary" for a type of proposition and "a priori" for a mode of knowledge, so he does not treat them as synonyms. Kripke says it is clear from the opening pages of the Critique that Kant regards the thesis that knowledge that something is necessary must be a priori knowledge as an important, though obvious, substantive thesis (p. 160).

*Intuition note:* "Necessary" describes how a truth holds. "A priori" describes how someone knows it. Learning a necessary truth by asking an expert keeps the first and loses the second.

### (c) The noncircularity condition
- Remark on original p. 68, clarified: a definition of Jonah as the man referred to by that name in the Bible has been misread as necessarily violating noncircularity. It does not, provided the description theory can account for the Biblical authors' reference independently of ours (p. 160).
- Strawson-style buck-passing is noncircular provided the other speaker's description does not ultimately involve the original speaker's references. "Let 'Glumph' be a name of the thing Jones calls 'Glumph'" is fine unless Jones says the same of Kripke (pp. 160–161).
- Still, such determinations are open to a different objection, error. In general, a speaker cannot be sure from whom he picked up his reference. A layman's "Let Gödel be the man to whom the experts attribute the incompleteness theorem" can fail if the experts know Schmidt proved it. The speaker then cannot know a priori (Thesis 5, from the main text, not stated here) that the determination gives the right result (p. 161).
- If the speaker uses his own reference as the paradigm (for example, fixing it as the man he now calls Glumph, or as the man he believes to have proved the theorem), the determination is circular. The exception is where his reference was already fixed another way, and then that other way is the determining condition (p. 161).
- Often such determinations risk both circularity and error, since those to whom one passes the buck may pass it back. Community versions (the man all of us in Community C call Glumph), if meant as the determination used throughout the community, are blatant cases of both. An individual may err if the community knows of the Gödel–Schmidt fraud and he does not. Even with error waived, the determination is circular if all or most speakers use it (pp. 161–162).
- A different proposal: let the name denote the man called by that name by the people one got it from (whoever they are), provided one's present determination satisfies the conditions sketched in the lectures and any other needed conditions. Footnote 38 says this would be a trivial fulfilment of the description theory in terms of the present view, if only the present view were not somewhat loose and did not already involve the speaker's own reference (his intention to agree in reference with those he got the name from) (p. 162).
- Even then, this description would hardly be the sort a speaker gives when asked "Who is Napoleon?". It would occur only to speakers who have mastered a complex theory of reference, and that theory, not their description, would explain how reference is determined (p. 162).

*Intuition note:* Passing the buck works only if the chain ends somewhere independent of me. If it loops back to me it is circular. If it leans on others' beliefs it can be wrong.

### (d) Initial baptism, original p. 96
- Footnote 70's "initial sample" is oversimplified. Likewise there need not always be an identifiable initial baptism of a proper name. Kripke thinks such complications will not radically alter the picture. Names without identifiable baptism are probably rarer than species cases (p. 162).

### (e) Santa Claus, original pp. 93, 96–7
- According to Evans, "Madagascar" was a native name for part of Africa. Marco Polo, wrongly thinking he followed native usage, applied it to an island. Evans uses this to support the description theory; Kripke does not (p. 163).
- The island usage is now so widespread that it overrides the historical link. Lewis notes the same could have happened if the natives' name designated a mythical place (p. 163).
- So real reference can shift to another real reference, fictional to real, and real to fictional. In each case a present intention to refer to an entity (or fictionally) overrides the original intention to preserve the chain (p. 163).
- Kripke suggests the phenomenon is perhaps roughly explicable by the predominantly social character of names: they are used to communicate. Normally this means intending to use a name as it was transmitted. In the Madagascar case it means the present intention to refer to the island wins (p. 163). Miller's "George Smith"/"Newton" case is probably similar (p. 163).
- A precise account needs more apparatus. In particular, a present *intention* to use a name for an object must be distinguished from a mere present *belief* that the object is the only one with some property. He leaves this for further work (p. 163).

### (f)–(g) Credits and open questions
- The historical acquisition picture of naming is apparently very similar to views of Keith Donnellan. Chastain's suggestions were similar but with more of the old description theory (p. 164).
- Kaplan's work on "Dthat" has been extended to a "logic of demonstratives". He says a good deal of the paper's argument can be formally represented there (p. 164).
- The third lecture suggests that much of what is called mere physical necessity is actually necessary "tout court". How far this goes is left for further work (p. 164).

## Toy example (ours)
Reference-fixing as a chain. Ann fixes the name Zed as whatever man Bob calls Zed. Bob fixes Zed as whatever man Ann calls Zed. Each determination points only to the other, so no man is ever picked out. This is circular (compare Kripke's Jones/Kripke case, pp. 160–161). Now let Bob instead fix Zed as the man who walked into the café on 3 May, so his reference is fixed independently. Ann's determination is noncircular. It can still misfire: if Bob is wrong about the café man, Ann inherits the error and cannot know a priori that she is safe (p. 161).

Unicorn count: the myth says "one-horned, white, horse-sized". Suppose candidate species A (reptilic), B (mammalian) and C (amphibious) each fit. No fact in the myth favours any of them. So the answer to which of them would have been the unicorns is none in particular, and no unique species can be called the unicorns (pp. 156–157).

## Concepts
- [[causal-historical-theory-of-reference]] — a name refers through a chain of transmission from an earlier use. Kripke notes complications: unidentifiable baptisms, shifts (pp. 157, 162–163).
- [[necessary-a-posteriori]] — necessary truths known empirically. In Kripke's cases analysis shows they cannot be contingently true (p. 159).
- [[noncircularity-condition]] (new) — a reference-fixing description must not ultimately rely on the speaker's own references (p. 160).
- [[reference-shift]] (new) — a present intention to refer to something overrides the older chain (p. 163).
- [[fictional-names-no-possible-referent]] (new) — no possible person or species can be singled out as what a fictional name would have named (pp. 157–158).
- [[necessity-vs-a-priority]] (new) — in Kant's usage as Kripke reports it, necessary applies to a type of proposition and a priori to a mode of knowledge (p. 160).

## Canonical instances
- [[unicorns-and-fools-tigers]] — a mythical species cannot be fixed by appearance (pp. 156–157).
- [[sherlock-holmes-fictional-name]] — no possible individual would have been Holmes (p. 158).
- [[goedel-schmidt-case]] — the layman's description may give the wrong referent (p. 161).
- [[madagascar-reference-shift]] — the now-widespread island usage, which began with Marco Polo's mistaken application, overrides the native name's historical link (p. 163).
- [[goldbach-conjecture-example]] — mathematical propositions are known a priori not to be contingently true, yet a mathematical truth can be learned a posteriori (p. 159).
- [[jonah-biblical-description]] — a Bible-based description that is not necessarily circular, provided the authors' reference can be accounted for independently of ours (p. 160).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] buck-passing chains of reference ↔ network science: trust or authority propagation. A loop gives no independent anchor, like circular endorsement.
- [metaphor] Madagascar reference shift ↔ computational linguistics: semantic change in word usage, driven by current speakers' use.

## Questions this raises
- What exactly separates a present intention to refer to an object from a belief that it uniquely has some property?
- If analysis shows a statement cannot be contingently true, does that explain all necessary a posteriori knowledge?
- How could formal semantics handle empty and fictional names without possible-but-nonactual individuals?
- How far can "physical necessity" be pushed into necessity tout court?
