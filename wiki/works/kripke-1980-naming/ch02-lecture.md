# Kripke, Lecture II: against the cluster-of-descriptions theory, a better picture, and identity statements

## L0 — One line
In general, names do not get their reference from descriptions the speaker associates with them but from a chain of communication back to a baptism, and because names are rigid, a true identity between names is necessary even though it is not known a priori.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke tests the six-thesis description theory of names from Lecture I. Thesis (6) fails because names are rigid designators, so a description that fixes reference does not yield a necessary truth. Theses (2)–(5) also fail: speakers refer even when they know little, believe falsehoods, or are wrong about the bearer. Repairs based on what most people believe about someone fail, mainly by circularity. Kripke offers a picture, not a theory: a name passes from speaker to speaker back to an initial baptism. He then turns to identity statements. "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is not known a priori, yet if true it is necessary, because both names are rigid.

**Prerequisites:** Lecture I's notions (possible worlds; rigid designator = a term that names the same thing in every possible world; a priori = knowable by reasoning alone, versus necessary = true in every possible world); definite descriptions; initial baptism = the first act of naming; basic modal talk.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- **Thesis (6) is dropped.** A name designates its bearer rigidly. That the bearer satisfies the description used to fix its reference is not a necessary truth (Hitler, the meter stick, the yard) (pp. 75–78).
- **Fixing reference is not giving meaning.** A description can fix a name's reference without being synonymous with it. Thesis (5) has nothing to do with necessity and so survives this argument, though all of (2)–(5) hold only in a rare class of cases, usually initial baptisms (the first act of naming) (pp. 78, 96 n. 42).
- **Theses (2)–(4) fail by counterexample.** Feynman, Gödel/Schmidt, Peano/Dedekind, Columbus and Jonah show that speakers refer with too little information, with false information, or with descriptions true of no one (pp. 80–87).
- **Noncircularity condition (C) sinks the repairs.** (C) says the properties used to fix reference must not themselves involve reference in a way that cannot be eliminated. Defining a name by what most people attribute to its bearer, and Strawson's borrowed-reference footnote, either run in circles or depend on what the speaker remembers (pp. 88–90).
- **Better picture.** Reference runs along a chain of communication back to the bearer. What matters is the actual chain, not what the speaker thinks it was (pp. 91–93).
- **Not a theory.** Kripke gives no necessary and sufficient conditions. Baptism and the intention to keep the reference are the rough ingredients, and he is sympathetic to the view that analyses of reference that never mention reference are apt to fail (pp. 93–97).
- **Identity statements.** Kripke holds that characteristic theoretical identities, such as heat and molecular motion, are necessary, not contingent. It does not seem to him that mind–brain identities could turn out necessary or contingent in the same way, so he says the analogy has to go (pp. 98–100).
- **Hesperus is Phosphorus.** The statement is not a priori, but if true it is necessary. Kripke explains the apparent contingency by the possibility of qualitatively identical evidence in worlds where the names name other things (pp. 100–105).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 0. Recap of the theses (p. 71)
Lecture I ended with a description theory of names, listed on the board. The extracted text drops the numerals (see Reading notes). The theses, in order:
1. To each name 'X' corresponds a cluster of properties φ, namely those φ such that A believes 'φX'.
2. A believes that one of these properties, or some jointly, picks out an individual uniquely.
3. If most (or a weighted most) of the φ's are satisfied by one unique object y, then y is the referent of 'X'.
4. If the vote yields no unique object, 'X' does not refer.
5. 'If X exists, then X has most of the φ's' is known a priori by the speaker.
6. That statement expresses a necessary truth (in the speaker's idiolect).
(C) For any successful theory, the account must not be circular. The properties used in the vote must not involve reference in a way that cannot be eliminated.

(C) is a condition on satisfying the theses, not a thesis. The theses must not be satisfied in a way that gives no independent determination of reference (p. 71).

### 1. Circularity: Kneale's example (pp. 71–73)
- Kneale (Kripke checked the book) says it is trifling to be told that Socrates was called 'Socrates'. Kneale concludes that 'Socrates' simply means 'the individual called "Socrates"'. Kripke says Russell sometimes gives a similar analysis (p. 72).
- In the past tense this is not circular, since one could decide to use 'Socrates' for whoever the Greeks so called. But then it is not trifling: it may be false. The Greeks may have pronounced the name differently. It is false that Isaiah was called 'Isaiah' (the prophet would not have recognized the name), and the Greeks did not call their country anything like 'Greece' (p. 72).
- Amend it to say that Socrates is called 'Socrates' by us, or at least by me. In some sense this is fairly trifling, but Kripke does not think it necessary or analytic. 'Horses are called "horses"' is trifling, yet 'horse' does not mean 'the animal called a "horse"' (p. 72).
- As a theory of reference this is a vicious circle. 'I shall use "Glunk" for the man I call "Glunk"' gets nowhere without an independent determination of the referent. It is a good case of violating (C) (p. 73).
- Theses (5) and (6) have converses. On the theory, it is a priori for the speaker that if any unique thing has most of the φ's it is X. It is also necessary that if anything has most of them it is X. So on the theory, "something is X iff it uniquely has most of the φ's" is both a priori and necessary (p. 73).
- (5) and (6) just say that a sufficiently reflective speaker grasps the theory. Kripke's objections will not be that some speakers are unaware of it (p. 73).

### 2. Thesis (6) fails (pp. 74–78)
**The Searle move.** Philosophers have noted that if only one description fixes the referent, then 'Aristotle taught Alexander' seems necessary, which it is not. Searle therefore moves from a single description to a cluster. Searle says it is contingent that Aristotle ever went into pedagogy, while suggesting it is a necessary fact that Aristotle has the inclusive disjunction of the properties commonly attributed to him (p. 74).

**Kripke's reply.** This is not so. In no intuitive sense of necessity is it a necessary truth that Aristotle had the properties commonly attributed to him. A deterministic view of history on which great men are destined for their deeds might say so, but that is not a result of a theory of names (pp. 74–75). Kripke admits an illusory 'gut feeling' that it is sort of analytic that Hitler was evil, but says probably not: Hitler might have spent all his days quietly in Linz (p. 75).

**Rigid designation applied.**
- Suppose we fix the reference of 'Hitler' as the man who had more Jews killed than anyone else in history. In a counterfactual situation where someone else did, we would not say that other man was Hitler. We use the name for that man even when describing other worlds (p. 75).
- The meter: even if the length is fixed by the standard stick, it is contingent that the stick is one meter long. If stretched, it would be longer. 'One meter' designates a certain length rigidly, though the length is picked out by an accidental property (p. 75).
- The yard, originally King Henry I's outstretched arm (finger tip to nose): it is not necessary that this distance is a yard. The reason is not that a cluster of other criteria is in play. A user with a single criterion can still say counterfactually that, had the King's arm been foreshortened, the distance would not have been a yard (p. 76).

> **Intuition note.** A description can pick out a thing without being part of what the name means. The name then follows the thing into other possible worlds, even worlds where the thing lacks the picking-out property. (Pp. 75–76)

**Against transworld identification and counterpart theory.** Some theorists hold that a possible world is given only qualitatively. They identify Aristotle's counterparts with whatever most resembles him in his most important properties, weighted by importance (Lewis is quoted, p. 76). Kripke calls these notions incorrect. Aristotle's and Hitler's most important properties might have been lacking altogether. Important properties need not be essential, unless one uses 'importance' to mean essence (p. 77).

**Two clarifications on rigidity** (pp. 77–78):
- Rigidity concerns the term as used in our language when we talk about counterfactual situations. We do not call 'two plus two equals four' contingent because people might have used those words to mean something else. Describing a world where we all speak German, we still speak English with our meanings.
- A rigid designator need not designate something that exists in every world. In 'suppose Hitler had never been born', 'Hitler' still rigidly refers to something that would not exist there.

**Verdict:** Thesis (6) is crossed off as incorrect (p. 78).

### 3. What survives, and the picture behind the theses (pp. 78–80)
- Thesis (5) is not about necessity. Example: I use 'Hesperus' for the body seen in a certain place in the evening. It is not necessary that Hesperus was ever seen in the evening, since that depends on contingent facts such as observers being there. But it may be a priori for me that if there is a Hesperus it is the thing I saw there (p. 78).
- Without (6), theses (2)–(4) have many counterinstances. Even when they are true, (5) is usually false. That the referent coincides with the vote is an empirical "accident" the speaker could not know a priori. All of (2)–(5) hold only in a rare class of cases, usually initial baptisms (p. 78).
- The picture of naming behind the theses is a "mental ceremony". I think of a unique description, for example 'By "Cicero" I shall mean the man who denounced Catiline', and use the name rigidly for the man who in fact did so. There may be cases where this happens, if one stretches 'description' and 'name': 'Hesperus' for the heavenly body over there, or the police name 'Jack the Ripper' for whoever did the murders (p. 79).
- Footnote 33 gives Neptune as an even better case of reference fixed by description rather than ostension. Leverrier named the planet before anyone saw it, as the cause of certain perturbations. At that stage 'if such perturbations are caused by a planet, they are caused by Neptune' was a priori but not necessary. He could have believed that had Neptune been knocked off course earlier, some other object might have caused the perturbations (p. 79 n. 33).
- Footnote 34 (after Donnellan): the fixing description may turn out false of its object. 'Phosphorus' was fixed as 'the morning star', which is not a star. A more cautious substitute is then what fixes reference (p. 80 n. 34).

### 4. Thesis (2): the properties pick out someone uniquely (pp. 80–82)
- Thesis (1) is a definition. Thesis (2) seems, in some a priori way, to have to be true: if the properties fit two people, there seem to be no grounds for saying which one is meant. The usual properties are famous deeds, such as Cicero's denouncing Catiline. Even Ziff, who denies names have meaning, accepts this as a picture of how reference can be fixed (p. 80).
- Kripke rejects it. Most people, when they think of Cicero, just think of a famous Roman orator, with no belief that there was only one. A man in the street who says Feynman is a physicist or something may think this picks out no one uniquely, yet Kripke still thinks he uses 'Feynman' as a name for Feynman (p. 81).
- Even a unique description may break (C). 'The man who first denounced Catiline' contains another name. We must not say that Catiline was the man denounced by Cicero, which fixes only a pair A, B such that A denounced B (p. 81).
- 'Einstein discovered relativity' picks Einstein out uniquely. But many people cannot say what the theory is and, when asked, say it is Einstein's theory, a plain vicious circle (pp. 81–82).
- A student answered that the encyclopedia gives the theory's details. Kripke jokes this is a transcendental deduction of the existence of encyclopedias. Kripke says the reference might work even if there had been no encyclopedias (p. 82).
- Conclusion: Thesis (2) seems false (p. 82).

### 5. Thesis (3): the vote yields the referent (pp. 82–85)
- Since (2) is wrong, Kripke asks why any of the rest should work. The associated picture is that only by giving unique properties can you know who someone is. He sets that question aside, but notes that if you know only that Einstein discovered relativity, you can know who Einstein is and also who discovered relativity. This seems a blatant violation of a noncircularity condition, yet it is how we talk, so a picture suggesting that condition would seem to be the wrong picture (p. 83).
- **The Gödel–Schmidt case.** A well-educated speaker states the incompleteness theorem independently and attributes it to Gödel. Suppose, fictionally, that a man Schmidt proved it. His body was found in Vienna, and his friend Gödel got hold of the manuscript. On the theory, 'Gödel' as used by ordinary speakers refers to Schmidt. Kripke says it seems to him that we are not referring to Schmidt: we simply are not. 'The man who published it' can be undercut by changing the story further (pp. 83–84).
- Such cases are not rare. People "know" that Peano discovered the Peano axioms; Kripke has been told Dedekind did, with credit in Peano's footnotes. On the theory we would all have been referring to Dedekind. We were not (pp. 84–85).
- Laymen say Einstein invented the atomic bomb, and 'Columbus' is tied to the roundness of the earth or to the discovery of America. Kripke thinks the Columbus claims are probably untrue, yet people do not refer to some Greek or Norseman. (3) "seems simply to be false" (p. 85).
- Footnote 36 (the attributive reply): the cluster theory would make 'Peano discovered the axioms' trivially true. A defender might say there is an "attributive" use of names (after Donnellan). 'Gödel proved incompleteness' refers to Gödel, but 'Gödel used a diagonal argument' might refer to whoever proved the theorem. Kripke thinks no ambiguity need be postulated. As with Smith and Jones, referring "in an appropriate sense" to Jones when I mistake him for Smith does not make 'Smith' ambiguous. Even if such uses exist, the theory would still be false in general, but applicable to more cases than he thought (p. 85 n. 36).

> **Intuition note.** If a name just meant whoever fits the story the speaker believes, a mistaken story would shift the name onto someone else. Kripke holds that we do not in fact use names that way. (Pp. 83–85)

### 6. Thesis (4) and Thesis (5) (pp. 86–87)
- **Thesis (4).** The vote may fail to yield a unique object (Cicero, Feynman). It may also yield no object at all: false beliefs may be true of no one. Variation: Gödel was present when a proof simply materialized by a random scattering of atoms, and arithmetic is in fact complete, so the proof is flawed. Still 'Gödel' may refer (pp. 86–87).
- Jonah: biblical scholars think he existed, not because anyone was swallowed by a big fish. The conditions may be true of no one and the name still has a referent. Possibly no one deserves to be called "the inventor" of the bomb (p. 87).
- **Thesis (5).** Even where (3) and (4) hold, a typical speaker does not know a priori that they do. Kripke believes his belief about Gödel is correct and the Schmidt story a fantasy, but this belief is hardly a priori knowledge (p. 87).
- Footnote 37 (a Donnellan-style "referential" use): if a name abbreviated a referential description, theses (2)–(6) could fail yet the name would still be a description. Kripke rejects this. A referential description such as 'the man drinking champagne' is withdrawn once the speaker sees it does not fit. If a Gödelian fraud were exposed, Gödel would no longer be called 'the author of the theorem' but would still be called 'Gödel'. So the name does not abbreviate the description (p. 87 n. 37).

### 7. Attempts to rescue the theory (pp. 87–90)
- One may vary the descriptions, and most attempts meet counterexamples (p. 88).
- **Proposal:** 'Gödel' means 'the man most people think proved incompleteness', not 'whoever proved it'. Two replies:
  1. *Counterexample.* Suppose most people now ascribe the axioms to Dedekind. A speaker with the old belief still refers to Peano and holds a false belief about Peano, not a true belief about Dedekind (pp. 88–89).
  2. *Circularity, more significantly.* That most of us think Gödel proved it presupposes that we refer to Gödel when we say 'Gödel proved incompleteness'. If we all referred to Schmidt we would be attributing the theorem to Schmidt. We cannot fix the reference by 'the man to whom it is commonly attributed' without independent criteria. Otherwise we would only be saying that we attribute the achievement to the man to whom we attribute it, with no independent criterion of reference. This violates (C) (p. 89).
- **Passing the buck.** Strawson's footnote (quoted, p. 90) lets an identifying description refer to another speaker's reference, so that one reference may borrow its credentials from another, and that from another, though Strawson says the regress is not infinite. Kripke: I might mean 'the man Joe thinks proved it', and Joe passes the question to Harry. Such chains do in general exist for a living man, but one will not know what they are, what descriptions others use, whether they loop, or whether they lead back to the right man. One may not remember whom one heard it from. So the move cannot be used with confidence as an identifying description (p. 90).

### 8. The picture: a chain of communication (pp. 90–93)
- Kripke asks what the true picture is and floats, rhetorically, that perhaps reference does not take place at all, since we do not know that our identifying properties are right or unique. He then asks what does make my use of 'Cicero' a name of him (pp. 90–91). The picture behind the cluster theory is a speaker alone in a room, fixing reference privately by 'By "Gödel" I shall mean whoever proved incompleteness'. One can do this, and then Schmidt would be the referent (p. 91).
- Most of us do not. A baby is born and named by his parents. They talk to friends, others meet him, and the name spreads from link to link, as if along a chain. A speaker at the far end can refer to Feynman without remembering from whom he heard the name and without being able to identify him uniquely. He does not know what a Feynman diagram is and might confuse Feynman with Gell-Mann. A chain of communication reaches the man himself; the speaker did not fix the reference by a private ceremony (pp. 91–92).
- **Contrast with Strawson.** Strawson's insight is real, but he puts it in a footnote and keeps the cluster theory in the main text. He apparently requires the speaker to know from whom he got the reference ('the man Jones calls "Gödel"'). Kripke's view sets no such requirement (p. 92).
- **The Cicero–Jones case.** The speaker heard 'Cicero' from Smith and others who use it for the Roman orator. He wrongly believes he got it from Jones, who uses 'Cicero' for a German spy. On Strawson's paradigm the referent is the spy. On Kripke's view it is the orator. What matters is the actual chain of communication, not how the speaker thinks he got the reference (pp. 92–93).

> **Intuition note.** On this picture, the community, not the individual's private definition, secures reference. A speaker who knows almost nothing about Feynman can still talk about him if the name was handed down to him link by link. (Pp. 91–93)

### 9. Not a theory, and a defence of the method (pp. 93–97)
- **Not a full theory.** Not every causal chain gives reference. There may be a causal chain from our 'Santa Claus' to a historical saint, but children probably do not refer to him. More conditions are needed. Kripke says he is sort of too lazy at the moment to supply them, and that he wants only to present a better picture than the received view (p. 93).
- **Is he unfair?** He stated the description theory, perhaps, more precisely than its advocates did. He allows that his own picture, stated as theses, might also turn out false, but says the difference is that his examples show not a technical slip but that the whole picture of giving ourselves properties that qualitatively uniquely pick out an object seems wrong from the fundamentals. If more details were filled in, his picture might be refined to give more exact conditions for reference (pp. 93–94).
- Kripke is sympathetic to Bishop Butler's "Everything is what it is and not another thing" in the nontrivial sense that analyses of reference in terms that never mention reference are apt to fail. Each analysis still has to be examined, not dismissed by maxim. What he says is true is that we refer to a man in virtue of our connection with other speakers in the community, going back to the referent himself (p. 94).
- **Where the description picture may be true:** Kripke gives as possible cases Jack the Ripper; 'Hesperus'; being introduced to someone and told his name (this can be forced into 'the guy I'm meeting now' if one has never heard the name otherwise); the first baptizer who points at a star and says 'That is to be Alpha Centauri'. In general it fails (pp. 94–95).
- **Newton and George Smith.** Kripke supposes that students told that Newton's sole achievement was first thinking a force pulls things to the earth get a false belief about Newton, though they had never heard of him. A teacher says that George Smith first squared the circle, picking a name that happens to be his neighbour's. He wants to teach that someone did so, not a belief about any particular man. Kripke is "not sure" the students have a false belief about the neighbour, even though a causal chain runs back to him (pp. 95–96).
- **Rough statement of a theory** (p. 96):
  - An initial "baptism" occurs. The object may be named by ostension or the reference fixed by a description.
  - When the name is passed on, the receiver must, Kripke thinks, intend, on learning it, to use it with the same reference as the man he heard it from.
  - Counterexample to sufficiency of mere transmission: hearing 'Napoleon' and using it for my pet aardvark does not satisfy this. A divergence like Santa Claus might be such a failure to keep the reference fixed.
- Kripke notes that the outline hardly eliminates the notion of reference: it takes the intention to use the same reference as given, and uses baptism (p. 97). The George Smith case casts doubt on whether the conditions suffice. Why should the pupils' belief not be about some other George Smith? The Newton-and-apple case is easier, since it conveys a common misconception about Newton (p. 97).
- Footnote 42: a description used in a baptism fixes reference but is not synonymous with the name. The description theory's primary application is to initial baptisms. Most baptisms are of objects the baptizer is acquainted with and can name by ostension. The theory's inspiration was long-dead famous figures, and these are exactly the cases it explains wrongly on Kripke's view (p. 96 n. 42).
- Footnote 44: once the fixing description is not synonymous with the name, the description theory presupposes naming or reference. Kripke says the noncircularity requirement is crucial to the description theory having any value. Russell, at least, saw that ostension or primitive reference is required (p. 97 n. 44).

### 10. Identity statements (pp. 97–105)
**Setting (pp. 97–99).**
- Statements of identity may be necessary or contingent. Everyone agrees identities made with descriptions can be contingent, such as "the man who invented bifocals was the first Postmaster General" (p. 98).
- Questions of interest: identities between names ('Hesperus is Phosphorus', 'Cicero is Tully'), and theoretical identifications (light with electromagnetic radiation of certain wavelengths or a stream of photons; heat with molecular motion; sound with a wave disturbance) (p. 98).
- **The commonly held thesis:** these theoretical identities are obviously contingent. Identity theorists in philosophy of mind welcome this. They say pain is a certain state of the brain or body, such as stimulation of C-fibres. Objectors say the pain–brain correlation was an empirical discovery, so the two must be different things. Identity theorists reply that there are contingent identities (bifocals, light/photons, water/a hydrogen–oxygen compound), so psychophysical identities can be contingent too. Motivations include avoiding a "nomological dangler" (p. 99).

**Kripke's position, stated in advance (pp. 99–100).**
1. Characteristic theoretical identifications such as 'Heat is the motion of molecules' are not contingent but necessary, in the highest degree (Kripke adds, whatever that means), not merely physically necessary. Kripke does not prejudge whether physical necessity might turn out to be that, at least for this sort of example.
2. The way these have turned out necessary does not seem a way in which mind–brain identities could turn out either necessary or contingent. He concludes that this analogy has to go, and says it is hard to avoid concluding that the two are different.

He first discusses identities between names (p. 99).

**Quine and Marcus (pp. 100–101).**
- Marcus holds identities between names are necessary, calling names "mere tags". If you really use 'Cicero' and 'Tully' as names, you are committed to holding that 'Cicero is Tully' is necessary (p. 100).
- **Quine's reply (quoted):** we tag Venus as 'Hesperus' one evening and 'Phosphorus' one morning. Discovering that we tagged the same planet twice is empirical, and not because the proper names were descriptions (p. 100). Kripke recalls another example he thinks Quine gives: Everest and Gaurisanker, which Quine got from Schrödinger and says is factually false. Kripke says one can imagine it as true, and it still illustrates the point (pp. 100–101).
- Kripke recalls Marcus holding that a good dictionary should tell you whether two names have the same reference. This does not seem true, and many people see it as a consequence of the view that name-identities are necessary. So that view has usually been rejected (p. 101).
- Russell concluded there should be no empirical question whether two names co-refer. This fails for ordinary names but holds for naming one's own sense datum ('this', 'that'). So, Russell concluded, ordinary 'names' cannot be genuine names (p. 101).

**Kripke's diagnosis (pp. 101–105).**
- One can use 'Cicero' and 'Tully' for the same man without knowing it. So we do not know a priori that a name-identity is true. It does not follow that, if true, it is contingent. There is a strong feeling that what cannot be known by a priori reasoning must be contingent, and Kripke thinks the feeling is wrong (p. 101).
- **The argument.**
  - Suppose 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are rigid names of Venus. In every possible world they refer to Venus, so Venus is Venus there, whatever anyone says there (p. 102).
  - A man in another world who calls two different stars 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus', even under the same conditions, is not (it seems to Kripke) in a world where Hesperus is not Phosphorus. If he pointed to Venus twice he would make the identity true there as well. So at least once he did not point to Venus, and then 'Phosphorus' might not have referred to Phosphorus (p. 102).
  - Phosphorus might not have been at the morning position at all, and something else there might have been called 'Phosphorus'. That is still not a case where Phosphorus was not Hesperus. Someone could even use our identifying descriptions in such a world (pp. 102–103). Kripke says there could not have been such a case, given that Hesperus is Phosphorus (p. 103).
- **Why it seems that it might turn out either way.** The 'might' can be merely epistemic, as with the four color theorem, which might turn out true or false without its eventual status being non-necessary. It expresses ignorance (p. 103).
- **A stronger sense in this case (pp. 103–104).** Our evidence is that we see a body in the evening and call it 'Hesperus', and one in the morning and call it 'Phosphorus'. A possible man could have exactly that evidence, in a qualitatively identical epistemic situation, and find empirically that he named two different bodies. But in that world 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are not used as names of this planet. In that sense it could have turned out either way, but not as to whether Hesperus is Phosphorus.
- **Conclusion (p. 104).** Using the names as we do now, if Hesperus and Phosphorus are the same body, then in no other possible world can they differ. Two things are true: we do not know a priori that Hesperus is Phosphorus and can find out only empirically, because we could have had qualitatively indistinguishable evidence, fixing the two names by positions of two planets, without the planets being the same. Footnote 48 points to a more elaborate discussion in Lecture III, with a form of counterpart theory (p. 104 n. 48).
- **The leftover contingent truth (p. 105).** It is contingent that the star seen over there in the evening is the star seen over there in the morning, since there are worlds where Phosphorus is not visible in the morning. That contingent truth should not be identified with 'Hesperus is Phosphorus'. It could be only if it were necessary that Hesperus is visible in the evening there, or Phosphorus in the morning. Neither is necessary, even though that is how we picked out the planet (p. 105).

> **Intuition note.** The names, not the evidence we used to introduce them, fix which object we talk about in every possible world. The evidence could have been the same with different objects. That explains why the discovery is empirical while the identity, once true, cannot fail. (Pp. 103–105)

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Ann names her kitten 'Zorb' at its baptism. Ann tells Cara, "Zorb is a kitten." Cara tells Ben. Ben has three beliefs: φ1 "Zorb caught the barn mouse", φ2 "Zorb lives on Elm St.", φ3 "Zorb is black".

Facts: the kitten Ann baptised is black, lives on Oak St., and caught no mouse. Another kitten, Mia, caught the barn mouse and lives on Elm St.

**Cluster theory vote.** φ1 and φ2 are true of Mia, so Mia gets 2 of 3. φ3 is true of Zorb, so Zorb gets 1 of 3. Mia wins 2 > 1, so 'Zorb' as Ben uses it would refer to Mia. This is like Gödel/Schmidt (p. 84).

**Kripke's picture.** Ben's use descends from Cara, from Ann, from the baptism. Ben intends to use the name as Cara does (p. 96). So 'Zorb' refers to Ann's kitten, and Ben's beliefs φ1 and φ2 are false of it, as with Peano (p. 85).

**Rigidity check.** Suppose Zorb had caught the mouse. 'Zorb' would still name that kitten, and Mia would not be Zorb. Whether Zorb catches mice is contingent. So even a description that fixed the reference would give no necessary truth (p. 75).

## Concepts
- [[rigid designator]] — a term denoting the same thing in every possible world, as used in our language, even where the thing does not exist (pp. 75, 77–78)
- [[causal-historical theory of reference]] — Kripke's "picture", not a full theory: reference comes via a chain of communication back to a baptism (pp. 91, 96)
- [[necessary a posteriori]] — here, name-identities and theoretical identities held necessary though not known a priori (pp. 99, 101, 104)
- [[cluster-description theory of names]] (new) — Theses (1)–(6): a name refers to what satisfies most of the speaker's associated descriptions (p. 71)
- [[reference-fixing vs. meaning]] (new) — a description may fix a name's reference without being synonymous with it (pp. 75, 96 n. 42)
- [[noncircularity condition]] (new) — condition (C): the reference-determining properties must not essentially involve reference (pp. 71, 73, 89)
- [[initial baptism]] (new) — the first naming, by ostension or by description. The description theory fits it best (pp. 78, 96)
- [[epistemic possibility vs. metaphysical possibility]] (new) — saying something might have turned out either way can express ignorance, not contingency (p. 103)

## Canonical instances
- [[kneale-socrates-called-socrates]] — circular description theory ('the man called "Socrates"') (pp. 72–73)
- [[godel-schmidt]] — fictional case where the described man is not the named man (pp. 83–84)
- [[peano-dedekind]] — real misattribution; speakers still refer to Peano (pp. 84–85)
- [[feynman-layman]] — reference without unique identification (pp. 81, 91)
- [[jonah]] — the name refers though the story's conditions fit no one (p. 87)
- [[neptune-leverrier]] — reference fixed by description before the planet was seen (p. 79 n. 33)
- [[standard-meter-and-yard]] — a measure fixed by an accidental property still rigidly designates a length (pp. 75–76)
- [[hesperus-phosphorus]] — an a posteriori name-identity said to be necessary (pp. 100–105)
- [[george-smith-circle-squarer]] — doubtful test case for transmitting reference (pp. 95–97)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Chain of communication back to a baptism ↔ social epistemology: testimony passing information from person to person — both depend on trusted transmission, not each speaker's own check.
- [metaphor] Reference determined by the community, not private ceremony ↔ computational social science: network diffusion of a label from person to person — the label's referent is set by the spread, not by each adopter.
- [structural-analogy] Epistemic "might have turned out either way" vs. metaphysical contingency ↔ Bayesian confirmation: uncertainty about which state holds vs. what is possible — uncertainty is about us, not about the world.

## Questions this raises
- What conditions (beyond intention to keep reference) would stop a chain from drifting, as with 'Santa Claus', or being hijacked, as with 'Napoleon' the aardvark?
- If the George Smith teacher's statement gives pupils a false belief about no one in particular, how should reference transmit through an utterance not aimed at the bearer?
- Is physical necessity necessity in the highest degree? And if heat/molecular motion is necessary, why exactly can the same not hold for pain and C-fibres (Kripke only says the analogy "has to go")?
- How are identities between names that are "tags" related to Russell's view that ordinary names are not genuine names (p. 101)?
