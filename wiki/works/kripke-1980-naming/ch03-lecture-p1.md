# Lecture III (part 1): identity, essence, and natural kind terms

## L0 — One line
A true identity between names is necessary even though one may not know it a priori, and words for natural kinds such as "gold" and "tiger" fix a kind by samples, so, if Kripke's argument is right, scientific discoveries about what the kind is are necessary truths rather than definitional ones, provided the properties found form the basis of what the substance is.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kripke recaps his attack on description theories of names. He asks what status three kinds of claim have: true identity statements, claims about an object's essential properties, and scientific identifications such as "gold has atomic number 79". His answer: true identities between names are necessary even if not known a priori; it seems to him an object's origin could not have been different; and natural kind terms ('gold', 'tiger') are much closer to proper names than is ordinarily supposed. Because they fix a kind by samples, he argues, discovered facts about what the kind is come out necessary. The argument depends on separating necessity from a priority.

**Prerequisites:** possible worlds and counterfactuals (ways things might have been); necessary (true however things might have been) vs. contingent; a priori (knowable without experience) vs. a posteriori; reference-fixing vs. synonymy (Lecture II); the idea of a rigid designator.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Recap: a name's reference is not generally fixed by identifying descriptions the speaker believes. In many cases where a description does fix it, it only fixes the reference by contingent marks and is not a synonym of the name (pp. 106–107).
- Identity is the relation of a thing to itself, not a relation between names. Kripke's invented "schmidentity" (a relation holding only between a thing and itself) suggests the relational view was probably not needed, and probably not workable, for the puzzles it was designed for (pp. 107–108).
- A true identity between names is necessary, though it may not be known a priori. Hesperus could not have failed to be Phosphorus (pp. 108–109).
- Essence may be discovered empirically. It seems to Kripke that this woman could not have come from different parents, nor this table from a different block of wood, though many later changes in their histories could have gone differently (pp. 110–114).
- "Gold" and "tiger" refer to kinds, not to clusters of properties. Something can have every identifying mark and not be gold or a tiger. Tigers might turn out to lack all the marks (pp. 118–121).
- If his reasoning is right, then given what gold is, having atomic number 79 is necessary for gold; a lookalike (fool's gold) in a counterfactual situation would still not be gold (pp. 123–125).
- The same reasoning is applied to cats, water, heat and light. For light, Kripke says reference is fixed by a contingent effect on us and the name then follows the stuff (pp. 125–130).
- Kripke says Mill was roughly right about singular names but wrong about general names (pp. 127–128).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Recap of Lectures I–II (pp. 106–107)
- Kripke recaps two things he has argued, before turning to identity statements:
  - First, the reference of a name is generally not fixed by uniquely identifying properties the speaker believes true of the referent. Such properties need not be uniquely specifying. Even when they are, they may fit something else or nothing, as when the speaker has erroneous beliefs about a person (p. 106).
  - In these cases the reference seems to be determined by the speaker's membership in a community of users, the name being passed on by tradition from link to link (p. 106).
  - Second, even where a description does fix a referent, as in some special cases such as some initial baptisms, in many cases it is not a synonym but fixes reference by contingent marks, and the name then refers to the object even in counterfactual situations where the object lacks those marks. The metre was the example (pp. 106–107).

### Identity statements (pp. 107–110)
- **Views under attack.** Some philosophers, even Frege at one early stage of his writing, took identity to be a relation between names. Kripke reports their reasoning: 'Cicero is Tully' can express an empirical discovery, so (they think) one cannot really be saying of the object that it is identical with itself (p. 107).
- **Rosser's account** (*Logic for Mathematicians*): $x=y$ iff 'x' and 'y' are names for the same object; Rosser remarks that the corresponding statement about the object itself, that it in no way differs from itself, is trivial, and so presumably cannot be what we mean (p. 107).
- **Kripke's reply:**
  - On Rosser's view, Kripke says, identity would apply very rarely, since, as far as Kripke knows, outside one political movement no one has ever been named 'x' (p. 107).
  - In $x=y$, 'x' and 'y' are variables, not names. A statement such as: for every $x$ and $y$, if $x=y$ then $y=x$ (Kripke says "something like that") contains no names and says nothing about names. It would be true even if humans had never existed or never produced names (pp. 107–108).
- **Schmidentity.** Suppose identity were a relation between English names. Then he stipulates an artificial relation, 'schmidentity' (not an English word), which holds only between an object and itself. The question whether Cicero is schmidentical with Tully can arise, with the same problems. Kripke thinks that anyone who reflects seriously will see that the original account was probably not necessary, and probably not possible, for the problems it was meant to solve, and that identity should be taken as the relation between a thing and itself (p. 108).
- **Footnote on p. 108.** The device will fail to convince a philosopher who argues that such an artificial concept is logically impossible. Some philosophers have thought that a relation, being essentially two-termed, cannot hold between a thing and itself. Kripke calls this position plainly absurd: someone can be his own worst enemy, and "no richer than" is reflexive. Identity or schmidentity, he says, is nothing but the smallest reflexive relation (p. 108, n.).
- **Conclusion.** An identity statement between names, when true at all, is necessarily true, even though one may not know it a priori (p. 108).
- **Hesperus and Phosphorus.** Possible worlds may exist where two different planets were seen in those evening and morning positions. At least one of them, and maybe both, would not have been Hesperus. So that is not a situation in which Hesperus is not Phosphorus. It could also be a situation in which some other planet was called 'Hesperus', but even then Hesperus itself would still be Phosphorus (p. 109).
- **Footnote on p. 109.** We describe the counterfactual situation in our language, so 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' keep their actual reference. What the people there called the planets, or whether they used the same descriptions, is irrelevant.
- **Source of the confusion.** Some of the problems that bother people come from confusing what can be known a priori in advance with what is necessary. Certain statements, of which the identity statement is a paradigm on Kripke's view, are necessary if true at all. One knows a priori, by philosophical analysis, that if it is true it is necessary (p. 109).
- **Qualification on existence.** There might have been no Venus, hence no Hesperus or Phosphorus. Then there is a question whether the identity statement would be true, false, or neither (p. 109; the same three options hold for 'Hesperus is Hesperus', p. 110, n.). Kripke leaves aside whether "necessary" means never false or true in all worlds. To be more careful, Kripke says, we could replace the statement by the conditional *If Hesperus exists then Hesperus is Phosphorus*, cautiously taking only the latter to be necessary. That conditional raises the problem of singular existence statements, which description theorists often say cannot be made. Kripke disagrees but does not pursue it (p. 110).

> **Intuition note.** Names refer to the same object in every situation we describe. So if "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" name one planet, no possible situation separates them. What can fail is our knowing in advance that they are one.

### Essential properties and origin (pp. 110–115)
- Kripke says claims about essential properties of objects (de re modality, about the object itself) are correctly treated only if a priority and necessity are distinguished. Essence may be discovered empirically (p. 110).
- **Sprigge's debate** (Mind, 1962), as quoted by Kripke; Sprigge presents an internalist (a believer that some properties are essential) and an anti-essentialist:
  - **Internalist:** the Queen must have been born of royal blood; the internalist also suggests we cannot imagine her at no stage of her existence being human (pp. 110–111).
  - **Anti-essentialist:** a news bulletin that she was adopted would be no contradiction, so the claim is synthetic (p. 110).
  - **Dilemma in Sprigge's text:** if the anti-essentialist concedes she could not have been always a swan, he admits an internal property. If he says it is contingent that she was ever human, he says what is hard to accept (p. 111); the text asks whether we can really consider it conceivable that she never was human.
- **Kripke's diagnosis.** The announcements (adoption, angel, automaton) involve no contradiction, and the Queen might be discovered to be such things. But that does not answer whether this woman could have lacked royal blood or humanity. Royal blood is complicated, since the family line's attaining royal power seems contingent. So he supposes it contingent that her blood was royal (pp. 111–112).
- **Reformulated question.** Could this woman herself have been born of different parents, say Mr and Mrs Truman? Discovering that she was would involve no contradiction (p. 112).
- **Stipulations.** Suppose she actually came from her royal parents. "Parents" means the people whose body tissues are the sources of the sperm and egg (p. 112).
- **Kripke's verdict.** A Truman child resembling her, even one who became queen, would, it seems to Kripke, be another woman with many of her properties. Supposing Elizabeth herself was never born, she would not have existed at all. One can be convinced only by reflecting on how to describe the situation, and Kripke himself is convinced (pp. 112–113).
- **What could have varied.** She could have become a pauper, or been switched like Twain's character. The history of the world may diverge from some time on. But being born of different parents is harder to imagine: it seems to Kripke that anything coming from a different origin would not be this object (p. 113).
- **The table.**
  - We may not know what block of wood this table came from. We could conceivably discover it was ice from the Thames, but suppose not (pp. 113–114).
  - Making a table out of another block, or of ice, in the same position and looking the same, is, it seems to Kripke, imagining another table, not this table made of wood or ice (p. 114).
- **Footnote on p. 114: the origin principle.** A principle suggested by these examples: if a material object originates from a hunk of matter, it could not have originated from any other matter. Some qualifications might have to be stated, because the notion of a hunk of matter is vague; but in a large class of cases the principle is perhaps susceptible of something like proof, which runs as follows (using the necessity of identity for particulars):
  - Let $B$ name a table, $A$ the wood it came from, $C$ another piece of wood.
  - Suppose $B$ is made from $A$, and simultaneously another table $D$ is made from $C$.
  - Then $B \neq D$. So even if $D$ were made alone and no table made from $A$, $D$ would not be $B$.
  - Strictly this uses the necessity of distinctness, not of identity.
  - The same considerations support both. Suppose $X\neq Y$. If both were identical to some $Z$ in another world, then $X=Z$ and $Y=Z$, hence $X=Y$.
  - Alternatively, the principle follows from the necessity of identity plus the Brouwersche axiom, equivalently symmetry of the accessibility relation between worlds.
  - The argument applies only if making $D$ from $C$ does not affect the possibility of making $B$ from $A$, and vice versa.
- **Footnote on pp. 114–115: material and other points.**
  - Kripke separates the temporal question (what can an object survive?) from the timeless modal one (what could it not have failed to have?). The question of the table turning into ice is irrelevant to the modal one.
  - Ordinarily it is impossible to imagine the table made from any substance other than its actual one without going back through the entire history of the universe. A second suggested principle, that the substance an object is made of is essential, comes with several complications.
  - Other points made there:
    - (1) Asking what might have happened to an object usually means asking whether the universe could have gone on as it did up to some time and then diverged. That time may precede the object's creation.
    - (2) Not only origin and substance are essential. Being a table (roughly) seems to be an essential property of the table.
    - (3) Whether an object essentially has a property can be vague.
    - (4) Apparent ordinary-language counterexamples to the origin principle exist. He is convinced they are not genuine but cannot analyse them here.
- **Footnote on pp. 115–116: Geach's "nominal essence."** On Geach's view a sortal ("that man") used in baptism by pointing is, in some sense, part of the meaning of the name. If Kripke understands Geach correctly, nominal essence concerns a priority, not necessity. His replies:
  - A sortal used in ostension need not be a priori true of the object. he asks whether Dobbin could not turn out not to be a horse, Hesperus a planet rather than a star, or Lot's guests angels rather than men (pp. 115–116, n.).
  - There is a gap between premise and conclusion, since few speakers learn names by ostension (p. 116, n.).
  - The "Nancy" example: a mathematician's wife overhears a muttered name and wonders whether Nancy is a woman or a Lie group. Kripke asks why her use of the name is not a case of naming; if it is not, the reason is not indefiniteness of her reference (p. 116, n.).

> **Intuition note.** Kripke tests essence by asking whether we could still be talking about *this very thing* while changing one feature. On his tests, later history could have varied, but origin, he thinks, could not. Appearance-matching substitutes, such as a similar table or a similar woman, are other things, not this thing.

### Theoretical identifications: gold (pp. 116–119)
- Philosophers have been interested in "light is a stream of photons", "water is H₂O", "lightning is an electrical discharge", and "gold is the element with atomic number 79". To assess them, Kripke first asks about the status of substances such as gold (p. 116).
- **Kant's view** (*Prolegomena*, quoted): analytic judgements are a priori even with empirical concepts, as in "Gold is a yellow metal", since one needs no experience beyond one's concept of gold as a yellow metal. Kripke notes this may mean 'gold' just means 'yellow metal'. If so, it is strange. Supposing not, Kant at least thinks it is part of the concept, known a priori and not possibly discovered false (p. 117).
- **Metals.** Kripke sets aside "gold is a metal". He does not know the chemistry, and it is hard to say what a metal is (p. 117). A tempting view is that there are two concepts of metal, a phenomenological one replaced by a scientific one. He rejects this, but it can be refuted only after he develops his views, so he does not use it (p. 118).
- **Yellowness.**
  - Suppose an optical illusion or a demon made gold look yellow, and it is really blue. Would papers announce "there is no gold"? Kripke thinks not. They would announce that gold has turned out not to be yellow but blue (p. 118).
  - He thinks the reason is that we use 'gold' as a term for a certain kind of thing that others discovered. We connect to it as part of a community. The kind has identifying marks, some of which may not be true of it. Something with all the marks may be a different kind, such as iron pyrites (fool's gold) (pp. 118–119).
  - It seems to him this is not because the meaning of 'gold' changed. We discovered further properties of gold, true of it and not of pyrites (p. 119).
- **Footnote on p. 116: financial pages.** Some financial pages said that he may have spoken too soon (apparently about gold's diminishing interest in financial circles) when the lectures were delivered in January 1970.

### Tigers and cluster concepts (pp. 119–122)
- **Ziff's example** (*Semantic Analysis*), quoted by Kripke: a dictionary definition of "tiger" as a large carnivorous quadrupedal feline, tawny with stripes, is not what "tiger" means. "A three-legged tiger" is no contradiction in terms, which it would be if "quadruped" were part of the meaning. Many philosophers explain this by a "cluster concept" (pp. 119–120).
- **Kripke's cases:**
  - If explorers were deceived by an illusion and the animals were three-legged, he thinks we would say that tigers have three legs, not that there turned out to be no tigers (p. 120).
  - Animals that look like tigers but are discovered to be, say, very peculiar reptiles would not lead us to conclude that some tigers are reptiles. We would rather conclude they are not tigers, because they are not of the species we called the species of tigers (p. 120).
  - This is not because an old concept was replaced by a scientific definition. It holds of the concept of tiger before tigers' internal structure was investigated. We suppose tigers form a species or natural kind, and can imagine a lookalike differing internally without knowing what the structure is (pp. 120–121).
- **Conclusion.** Tigers might lack all the identifying properties. So 'tiger', like 'gold', does not mark a cluster concept. Possession of most of the properties need not be a necessary condition for membership in the kind, nor need it be a sufficient one (p. 121).
- **Fallibility.** We may be wrong that tigers form a single kind. In advance we suppose they probably do, because experience shows that such things, living together, looking alike and mating, usually form a kind. If two kinds of tigers have something to do with each other but not as much as thought, they may form a larger biological family; if nothing at all, there are two kinds. This depends on history and on what we find out (p. 121).

### Putnam and analyticity (pp. 122–123)
- Kripke finds Putnam the philosopher who most recognises these considerations, which developed independently. In "It Ain't Necessarily So", Putnam says statements like "cats are animals" are "less necessary" than "bachelors aren't married" (p. 122).
  - Cats might turn out to be automata (Putnam's example) or strange demons (Kripke's addition). Then, on Putnam's view and, Kripke thinks, also his own, the inclination is to say cats turned out not to be animals, not that there were no cats (p. 122).
  - Kripke adds that the original concept of cat is that kind of thing, where the kind is identified by paradigmatic instances, not picked out by a qualitative dictionary definition (p. 122).
- Kripke agrees that the argument indicates such statements are not known a priori and hence are not analytic. Whether a kind is a species of animals is empirical (pp. 122–123).
- Perhaps Putnam's "necessary" is epistemological. The open question is whether the statements are necessary in Kripke's non-epistemological sense (p. 123).
- **Footnotes.**
  - Putnam does not use the necessary/a priori apparatus. In an earlier paper he seems closer to the cluster theory in some respects, suggesting that it applies to proper names. Kripke credits Rogers Albritton's example (p. 122, n.).
  - Kripke takes an analytic truth to depend on meanings in the strict sense and so to be both necessary and a priori. If truths known a priori via reference-fixing counted as analytic, some analytic truths would be contingent. Kripke says some (though not all) of the cases often adduced to discredit the analytic-synthetic distinction, especially those involving natural phenomena and kinds, should be handled via reference-fixing. Kant's "gold is a yellow metal" is not even a priori and is far from analytic (pp. 122–123, n.).

### Necessity of scientific identifications (pp. 123–127)
- **Gold and atomic number.**
  - We could discover that gold lacks atomic number 79, since atomic theory could be false (p. 123).
  - Suppose scientists have found that having atomic number 79 is part of the very nature of the substance. Now take a counterfactual situation where pyrites or another counterfeit fills the places where gold actually is. Kripke thinks we would not say gold would then have failed to be an element. We would say a substance that is not gold had been found there (p. 124).
  - Whether people would have called it 'gold' is irrelevant. We do not describe it as gold. Other lookalike pairs exist, such as elements in one column of the periodic table (p. 124 and n.).
  - Conclusion: if this consideration is right, it tends to show that statements representing scientific discoveries about what the stuff is are not contingent but necessary in the strictest possible sense, provided these properties form the basis of what the substance is (p. 125).
  - To the extent that properties such as colour and metallic properties follow from the atomic structure of gold, they are necessary properties of it too, though unquestionably not part of the meaning of 'gold' and not known with a priori certainty (p. 125).
- **Cats.**
  - Of Putnam's example Kripke says a very surprising discovery was made: nothing was found to go against our belief that cats are animals. It seems to him that this truth is necessary (p. 125).
  - If demons in cat form took the place of cats, they would not be cats but "demons masquerading as cats". The same holds for reptile-structured lookalikes, "fool's cats" (p. 126).
- **A particular object.**
  - Molecular theory discovered that this object is composed of molecules. It seems to Kripke that an ethereal entelechy in this very position, looking the same, could not be this thing (p. 126).
  - Many things might have happened to it (transport to the Kremlin, being hewn into bits). The one thing we cannot imagine happening to it, at least if his way of seeing it is correct, is that it, given that it is composed of molecules, should still have existed without being composed of molecules (pp. 126–127).

> **Intuition note.** The name 'gold' picks out a stuff by samples and surface marks. After science tells us what the stuff is, a counterfactual world with a lookalike has no gold in it. On Kripke's view the identity is discovered after the fact, yet it holds in every situation where gold exists.

### Kinds as near-names; Mill (pp. 127–128)
- On Kripke's view, terms for natural kinds are much closer to proper names than is ordinarily supposed. The old term "common name" suits predicates for species ('cow', 'tiger'). The considerations also apply to certain mass terms such as 'gold' and 'water' (p. 127).
- **Mill's view, as Kripke reports it:**
  - Singular names are connotative if definite descriptions and non-connotative if proper names (p. 127).
  - All general names are connotative. 'Human being' is the conjunction of properties giving necessary and sufficient conditions (p. 127).
- **Later tradition.** The modern logical tradition, as represented by Frege and Russell, seems to hold Mill wrong about singular names but right about general ones. More recent philosophy has followed suit, except that it often replaces defining properties by clusters, for both proper names and kind terms (p. 127).
- **Kripke's position.** Mill was more or less right about singular names and wrong about general ones. Some general names ('foolish', 'fat', 'yellow') may express properties. In a significant sense, 'cow' and 'tiger' do not, unless being a cow counts trivially as a property. Certainly they are not short for dictionary conjunctions (pp. 127–128).
- Whether science can discover empirically that certain properties are necessary of cows or tigers is a further question, which he answers affirmatively (p. 128).
- **Footnote on p. 128.** Kripke gives no criterion for a "pure property" (a Fregean intension). Even 'yellowness' has a referential element, as it is rigidly designated as the external physical property sensed through the visual impression of yellowness. The phenomenological quality of the sensation can be regarded as a quale in a pure sense. He says perhaps he is rather vague about these questions.

### Water, heat and light (pp. 128–130; text ends mid-argument)
- **Water.**
  - Water was identified by feel, appearance and perhaps taste. Kripke thinks that a substance with these marks but a different atomic structure would be a fool's water, not water, in the actual world and in counterfactual situations (p. 128).
  - If the substance took another form, like the alleged polywater, with different identifying marks, it would still be water because it is the same substance (pp. 128–129).
- **Heat.**
  - Heat is an external phenomenon perceived by feeling, not an internal sensation. It is the motion of molecules. Temperature corresponds to mean molecular kinetic energy, but Kripke avoids temperature because of how the scale is set (p. 129 and n.).
  - Heat was originally identified by its effect on nerve endings or touch (p. 129).
- **Light.**
  - Light is a stream of photons, or a form of electromagnetic radiation. It was originally identified by the visual impressions it produces (p. 129).
  - It seems to Kripke that if people were blind, light would still exist and just not affect their eyes. This seems to him a good example of fixing a reference: we fix what light is by the fact that it is whatever, out in the world, affects our eyes in a certain way (p. 130).
  - If sound waves gave creatures visual impressions like ours, it seems to Kripke that we would not say sound is light, but that the creatures are sensitive to sound in the way we are to light (p. 130).
  - The text breaks off mid-sentence at the foot of p. 130.

> **Intuition note.** In each case a contingent effect on us picks out the stuff, and the name then follows the stuff, not the effect. Changing the effect, as with blindness or sound-sight, changes how things appear to us. It does not change what light is.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Name 'blorp' is introduced for "the clear, sweet liquid in Lake L". Three possible worlds:

| World | Liquid in L | Its look/taste | Is Q-stuff (structure Q) around? |
|---|---|---|---|
| W0 (actual) | Q-stuff | clear, sweet | yes |
| W1 | R-stuff (different structure) | clear, sweet | no |
| W2 | Q-stuff | green, bitter | yes |

Test the sentence "blorp has structure Q".

- **Description-as-synonym test.** Read "blorp" as "the clear, sweet liquid in L".
  - W0: that liquid is Q-stuff, so the sentence is true.
  - W1: that liquid is R-stuff, so it is false.
  - W2: nothing satisfies the description, so it is false or truth-valueless.
  - It is true in 1 of 3 worlds, so contingent.
- **Reference-fixing test.** The description only fixes reference in W0. 'Blorp' then rigidly names the Q-stuff.
  - W0: true. W1: no blorp exists (R-stuff is "fool's blorp"). W2: true.
  - True in every world where blorp exists: 2 of 2. This is the conditional form Kripke uses ("if it exists…").
- Now test "blorp is clear and sweet". It is true in W0 and false in W2, so contingent. A person in W0 before chemistry cannot know a priori that blorp has structure Q. So the first sentence is necessary yet not a priori.

## Concepts
- [[necessary a posteriori]] — a truth, such as an identity between names or a kind's composition, true in all worlds yet found out empirically (pp. 108–110, 125).
- [[rigid designator]] — a name used to refer to the same object even in counterfactual situations (p. 107); the footnote on p. 114 glosses 'B', the name of a table, as a name (rigid designator).
- [[causal-historical theory of reference]] — in such cases reference seems to be determined by the speaker's place in a community of users, the name passed on from link to link, not by the speaker's descriptions (p. 106).
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — here analytic means depending on meaning in the strict sense, hence necessary and a priori; Kant's gold example is not analytic (pp. 117, 122–123 n.).
- [[Frege's puzzle]] — "Cicero is Tully" can express an empirical discovery, which led some to treat identity as a relation between names (p. 107).
- [[reference-fixing vs. synonymy]] (new) — a description may pick out a referent by contingent marks without being what the name means (pp. 106–107, 119).
- [[necessity of identity]] (new) — true identities between names are necessary; Kripke argues from the Hesperus/Phosphorus case (pp. 108–109).
- [[schmidentity]] (new) — an artificial relation holding only between an object and itself, used to suggest that the names-relation account of identity was probably neither necessary nor possible for the problems it was meant to solve (p. 108).
- [[essential property (de re modality)]] (new) — a property an object could not have lacked; its discovery may be empirical (p. 110).
- [[origin essentialism]] (new) — the suggested principle that an object coming from a certain hunk of matter could not have come from other matter (pp. 113–114 and n.).
- [[natural kind term]] (new) — a term such as 'tiger', 'gold' or 'water' fixing a kind by paradigm cases, not by a cluster of properties (pp. 120–121, 127).
- [[cluster concept]] (new) — the view that a term applies if most of a set of properties are satisfied; Kripke rejects it for kind terms and names (pp. 120–121, 127).
- [[fool's-gold test]] (new) — a lookalike with the same identifying marks but a different substance is not the kind, in actual and counterfactual situations (pp. 119, 124).

## Canonical instances
- [[Cicero–Tully]] — an identity statement that can express an empirical discovery (p. 107).
- [[Hesperus–Phosphorus]] — a true identity statement that is necessary (pp. 108–109).
- [[Queen Elizabeth and the Trumans]] — the question whether this woman could have had different parents (pp. 112–113).
- [[the wooden table / ice table]] — origin essentialism in a material object (pp. 113–114).
- [[gold and fool's gold (iron pyrites)]] — a kind versus lookalike; necessity of atomic number 79 (pp. 118–119, 124–125).
- [[tiger / three-legged tiger]] — kind terms versus a dictionary definition (pp. 119–121).
- [[cats and demons]] — cats might turn out not to be animals, but given they are animals, it is necessary (pp. 122, 125–126).
- [[water, fool's water, polywater]] — water as a substance across changes of appearance (pp. 128–129).
- [[heat and light]] — reference fixed by effects on humans; blindness and sound-sight counterfactuals (pp. 129–130).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] the symmetry of the accessibility relation (Brouwersche axiom) used in the origin argument ↔ modal logic: the same axiom, stated by Kripke's own footnote (p. 114 n.).
- [structural-analogy] rigid designation (a name held fixed across worlds) ↔ formal semantics: a constant symbol interpreted by the same element in every model.
- [structural-analogy] reference passed "from link to link" in a community ↔ social-network analysis: a term's meaning anchored by transmission chains and not by each user's private definition.
- [metaphor] "fool's gold" lookalikes ↔ statistics: proxy measures that share surface indicators with a construct but not its underlying nature.

## Questions this raises
- Does the origin argument, which relies on separate lumps of matter not affecting each other, hold for objects whose origins overlap or are vague?
- If "gold" fixes a kind by samples, what happens if the samples turn out to belong to two kinds? Kripke only says this "depends on the history" (p. 121).
- Where exactly is the line between properties that follow from a kind's nature (necessary) and those that do not?
- How does the proviso "provided these properties form the basis of what the substance is" (p. 125) get settled in practice?
