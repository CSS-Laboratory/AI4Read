# Communication Theory as a Field (part 1: diagnosis, two principles, Tables 1–2, first three traditions)

## L0 — One line
Communication theory can become a coherent field not through one unified theory but through a shared "matrix" in which diverse traditions argue with each other, built on a constitutive metamodel and on theory understood as metadiscourse.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Craig asks why communication theory is not yet a field and how it could become one. His diagnosis: its ideas came from many disciplines and were collected or imported, not argued over. His remedy is not a unified theory but a shared, contestable matrix of assumptions that lets theories disagree productively. Two principles build it: the constitutive model of communication used as a metamodel (a model about models), and theory seen as metadiscourse (talk about talk) that draws on everyday talk about communication. Seven traditions then become alternative vocabularies, each with its own definition of communication and its own objections to the others. This part covers the argument, Tables 1–2, and the rhetorical, semiotic and phenomenological traditions.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a scholarly discipline; Kuhn's term "incommensurable" (explained in L3); the transmission model of communication (sender → message → receiver); no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Diagnosis:** communication theory has many theories but no field. Authors rarely engage each other, and textbooks barely overlap: only 7% of 249 "theories" appear in more than three of seven books (p. 120).
- **Roots:** incoherence comes from multidisciplinary origins and from "sterile eclecticism" (cataloguing borrowed ideas) and "productive fragmentation" (borrowed research programs that work only inside their own domains) (pp. 121–123).
- **Goal:** not a unified theory, but "dialogical-dialectical coherence": theories must engage each other in argument. At a minimum, being a discipline means that many scholars agree that they disagree about consequential matters worth discussing (pp. 123–124).
- **Principle one:** the constitutive model is read as a second-order metamodel, not as a rival to the transmission model. Transmission can be one way of constituting communication symbolically. Craig believes that failing to separate the levels is a category mistake, a mix-up of logical levels (pp. 126–128).
- **Principle two:** communication theory is theoretical metadiscourse (discourse about discourse). It takes plausibility from the everyday metadiscursive commonplaces (taken-for-granted beliefs about communication) that it appeals to, and interest from those it challenges. It can inform practice (pp. 128–132).
- **Matrix:** seven traditions are distinguished by their definition of communication, their problems, vocabulary, commonplaces, and cross-tradition objections (Tables 1–2, pp. 132–134).
- **Rhetoric vs semiotics vs phenomenology:** the first three traditions are close in places and sharply opposed in others. The disputes concern judgment, meaning, truth and authenticity (pp. 135–140).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Abstract and introduction (pp. 119–121)
- Craig reconstructs communication theory as a *dialogical-dialectical* field (one held together by argument among traditions) using two principles: the constitutive model as a metamodel, and theory as metadiscursive practice (p. 119).
- His claim: despite many theories, communication theory as an identifiable field "does not yet exist". Craig says we appear to work mainly in separate domains: books and articles seldom mention other work on communication theory except within narrow specialties and schools, and theorists apparently neither agree nor disagree about much. There is no canon of general theory, no common goals, and no contentious issues that divide them (pp. 119–120).
- **Evidence from textbooks.** Anderson (1996) analysed seven textbooks and found 249 distinct "theories". 195 appeared in only one book. So 22% appeared in more than one book, and only 18 theories (7%) in more than three. Craig says it seems likely that, if this were a field, more than half of the introductory textbooks would agree on more than 7% of its essential contents, so the conclusion that it is not yet a coherent field seems inescapable (p. 120).
- He believes it can and should become a field, to the extent that theorists engage with socially important questions that cut across disciplines. The four points of the essay are: (1) why the field has not emerged; (2) its potential lies in a dialogical-dialectical *disciplinary matrix* (a shared but contestable set of assumptions that enables argument across traditions), not a unified theory; (3) the matrix is built from a constitutive metamodel plus theory as metadiscourse engaged with everyday metadiscourse; (4) seven traditions appear as seven alternative vocabularies for theorizing communication as a social practice (pp. 120–121).

### Roots of Incoherence (pp. 121–123)
**Multidisciplinary origins.** Communication theory arose in many disciplines. Littlejohn (1982) traced contributions from literature, mathematics and engineering, sociology and psychology. Budd and Ruben (1972) had chapters from 24 disciplines, from anthropology to zoology (p. 121). The discipline first tried to be an interdisciplinary clearinghouse. Craig values this spirit but says it made the field hard to envision as coherent. The approaches are, in Kuhn's sense, *incommensurable*: they neither agree nor disagree, but bypass each other because they conceive the shared topic so differently (p. 121).
- Dance (1970) reviewed 95 definitions from the 1950s–60s and distinguished 15 conceptual components. He suggested communication might be theorized as a "family" of concepts, to avoid dissension (pp. 121–122). Craig says Dance, working in a positivist tradition that held the concept of *theory* stable, perhaps underestimated the difficulty of integrating definitions from incommensurable agendas. It became conventional wisdom (e.g. Fisher 1978; Murphy 1991) that arguing over definitions is pointless (p. 122). Craig asks what is worth arguing over, if not the field's primary concept (p. 122).

**From sterile eclecticism to productive fragmentation.**
- *Sterile eclecticism* (Peters, 1986, as reported by Craig): the discipline used the word "communication" as an institutional legitimizing device (Schramm and others) with no coherent definition of focus or mission. It claimed all of communication theory, like "an intellectual Taiwan" claiming to be all of China (Peters, p. 545, quoted on p. 122).
- Craig says perhaps the most egregious case was Shannon's mathematical theory: scholars touted it as evidence of scientific status although they had no part in creating it, often understood it poorly, and seldom used it (p. 122).
- Craig's image: the Roach Motel. Theories check in but never check out; scholars seized ideas from their home disciplines, entombed them, and added few original ideas (p. 122).
- *Productive fragmentation* (Craig's reading, which he says may differ somewhat from Peters's): leading scholars saw the problem and built systematic research programs, but most were borrowed from other disciplines. So much political communication research was "political science as practiced in the field of communication" (Peters, p. 548), and much interpersonal research is experimental social psychology done in the field (p. 123).
- Borrowing is useful in itself. The problem is that borrowed goods sustained institutional claims without a distinctive focus. The fragments are productive in their own domains but could not, as used, cohere into more than the sum of their parts. While the discipline stays fragmented, textbooks stay eclectic and theories multiply with no field (p. 123).

> **Intuition note.** Piling up theories from other disciplines is like a museum of imports. Each piece may be good, but nothing makes the pieces talk to each other.

### Reconstructing Communication Theory as a Field (pp. 123–132)

**The goal: dialogical-dialectical coherence (pp. 123–124).**
- The goal is not a "chimerical" unified theory. No active field has one, and a perfectly coherent field would be static and dead (p. 123). The goal is diversity, argument and debate, even at the cost of occasional lapses into academic sniping. It should be a state in which we understand that we all have something important to argue about (pp. 123–124).
- Disciplinarity does not require suppressing diversity. At minimum, being a discipline means many of us agree that we disagree about consequential matters worth discussing. Craig cites Shotter: "a conversational community with a tradition of argumentation" (p. 124).
- *Dialogical-dialectical coherence* means a common awareness of complementarities and tensions among types of theory, so that they cannot develop in total isolation but must engage each other in argument (p. 124). The matrix rests on two principles (p. 124).

**Principle One: the constitutive model as metamodel (pp. 124–128).**
- Since the late 1980s the concept of communication has again become a subject of discussion. Defining it constructs a "communicational" perspective on social reality and defines the scope of a discipline distinct from the others (p. 124).
- **Constitutive (ritual) model, as proposed by others** (Carey, Deetz, Pearce, Shepherd and more). It is defined by contrast with the *transmission* (informational) model, in which communication is sending and receiving messages or transferring information from one mind to another. Critics claim the transmission model still dominates lay and academic thought (p. 125).
  - Peters traces it to 18th-century empiricism, with individualistic, ultimately solipsistic assumptions. Carey, Deetz, Pearce and Shepherd variously argue that it is philosophically flawed, paradoxical and ideologically backward, and should be supplemented or supplanted by a model of communication as a constitutive process that produces and reproduces shared meaning (p. 125).
  - Four themes of this literature (pp. 125–126): (1) ideas about communication have evolved historically; (2) theories are *reflexive* (they draw on everyday thinking and can reinforce or change it, so theory and culture are mutually constitutive); (3) theories have practical, including political, implications and serve some interests more than others (e.g. a transmission model can serve technical experts by highlighting the value of experts as reliable sources); (4) communication can be a legitimate discipline only if it takes a communicational perspective radically distinct from, but at least equal in status to, psychology, sociology, economics and linguistics (pp. 125–126).
  - On theme (4), communication is not a secondary phenomenon explained by prior psychological, sociological, cultural or economic factors. It is the primary, constitutive social process that explains them. Theories of communication from other disciplinary perspectives are, strictly speaking, not within communication theory, according to this fourth theme (p. 126). Deetz: new disciplines arise when existing explanations fail to guide responses to new social issues. Today these concern who participates in constructing identities, social order and codes. Deetz endorses a "communication perspective" in which inner world, outer world, social relations and means of expression are reciprocally constituted (p. 126).
  - These arguments are usually practical, not purely theoretical: the constitutive model answers problems such as eroded cultural foundations, diversity and interdependence, and demands for democratic participation. Craig adds that, just as a transmission model can bolster experts' authority, a constitutive model can, he hopes, serve freedom, toleration and democracy (p. 126).
- **Craig's own position.** He largely agrees, but favors a *pragmatic* interpretation that does not necessarily reject other models, such as transmission, for practical purposes. He takes the constitutive model to be a **metamodel** that opens a conceptual space in which many theoretical models can interact (p. 126).
  - A *first-order model* is a perspective on communication highlighting certain aspects (transmission: messages flow from sources to receivers). A *second-order model (metamodel)* is a perspective on models. The constitutive metamodel pictures models of communication as different ways of constituting the communication process symbolically for particular purposes (p. 127).
  - Failing to distinguish the levels is, Craig believes, a **category mistake** producing two confusions (p. 127).
  - *Confusion 1 (paradox).* The constitutive model typically denies that any concept has a true essence outside its constitution in communication. So calling it the "true" model would seem self-contradictory. Transmission versus constitutive is therefore not a binary choice. The transmission model "as usually presented" is scarcely more than a straw figure. Regarded as one way of constituting communication symbolically, it is perfectly consistent with the constitutive model. The constitutive model does not say what communication really is, but implies it can be constituted in many ways, including as transmission (p. 127). Transmission-like notions retain cultural currency. There may be good reasons to use them: distinguishing sources and receivers, mapping information flow, treating messages as containers of meaning, or communication as an intentional act. They can also be defended as cultivating alertness to the diversity of perspectives and the dangers of distortion (p. 127).
  - *Confusion 2 (restriction).* Unless it is distinguished as a metamodel, the constitutive model may tend to confuse communication itself with communication as theorized in limited traditions, and so unduly restrict the field. It is perhaps most easily confused with the *sociocultural* tradition, where communication produces and reproduces social order. That confusion can create the false impression that the cybernetic and sociopsychological traditions are not genuine communication theories. Craig says, as he will show, that they can be reconstructed under the metamodel as alternative types of communication explanation (pp. 127–128).
  - A worry: the pragmatic "more theories the better" line might bring back sterile eclecticism or productive fragmentation. Craig's reply is the second principle: diversity can be coherent if understood as metadiscourse in a practical discipline (p. 128).

> **Intuition note.** The "constitutive" idea works one level above specific models. It says that any model of communication, including "sending messages", is itself something people build through communication. So it does not have to defeat the sending-messages picture. It can treat that picture as one useful picture among others.

**Principle Two: communication theory as metadiscourse (pp. 128–132).**
- **Source: Taylor (1992).** Taylor's critique of language theory from Locke on sees technical theorizing of language, interpretation and understanding as derived from everyday practices of talking about what we say and do (p. 128). Theories turn commonplaces of practical metadiscourse (e.g. that people ordinarily understand each other) into axioms or hypotheses. Each theory gains plausibility by appealing rhetorically to some commonplaces and challenging others. Language theory as a whole then becomes a closed, self-referential game. Taylor's way out is to drop the pseudoproblem of how communication is possible and study practical metadiscourse empirically (pp. 128–129).
- **Practical metadiscourse** is intrinsic to communicative practice: communication is something we do and something we refer to reflexively as we do it. Example: Ann says to Bill, "you can't possibly know what I'm talking about". She appeals to a commonplace about meaning (that true understanding comes only from personal experience), probably to undermine Bill's assertion (p. 129).
- **Craig's generalisation.** All communication theory, not just language theory, is metadiscourse ("talk about talk") that derives much of its plausibility and interest from appealing rhetorically to everyday metadiscursive commonplaces. Examples: sociopsychological trait theories seem plausible because people think communication styles reflect personality. Communication apprehension theory is a more sophisticated version of everyday talk about shyness (p. 129).
- **Difference from Taylor.** Taylor treats language theory as a closed game cut off from the pragmatic functions of practical metadiscourse. Craig envisions an open field that emerges from, extends and informs practical metadiscourse. The task is not to deconstruct communication theory (Craig's aside: it is already a mess) but to *reconstruct* it as a theoretical metadiscourse in dialogue with everyday metadiscourse (p. 129).
- **Practical discipline.** In a practical discipline, theory supplies conceptual resources for reflecting on communication problems. It does so by conceptually reconstructing practices within abstract, reasoned, normative idealizations. Because many perspectives are possible, the field becomes a forum for discussing the relative merits of alternative practical theories. That discussion is theoretical metadiscourse (p. 130).
- **Two starting points.**
  - *Inductive (practice):* "communication" is already a richly meaningful concept in the lifeworld (McKeon: we tend to think all problems are communication problems; Katriel & Philipsen: we often need to sit down and talk to work out relationship problems; Carey: we ritually avow that communication is the only tie that can hold a diverse society together). So theory can be built by critically studying everyday practice and reconstructing people's own "situated ideals". This is "grounded practical theory" (Craig & Tracy, 1995) (p. 130).
  - *Deductive (theory):* communication is already an important category in many disciplines, which offer vocabularies that can be critically reconstructed. This is the route taken in this essay (pp. 130–131).
- **Working assumption.** Every one of the incommensurable ideas is potentially relevant to practice. A speculative reason: communication may have been theorized in so many disciplines during the 20th century partly because it became a culturally important category of practice, consistent with reflexivity. Legitimizing a discipline can mean showing relevance to salient themes (p. 131).
- **Method (loosely following Taylor).** Theoretical metadiscourse theorizes practical metadiscourse and (a) appeals rhetorically to certain commonplaces, which makes a theory seem plausible and commonsensical to laypeople, and (b) skeptically challenges other commonplaces, which makes it seem interesting, insightful, or even absurd. This combination of plausibility and interestingness is the theory's *presumptive practical relevance* (p. 131). Since theories are relevant in different and often conflicting ways, theoretical metadiscourse turns back on itself to debate the differences and so constitutes itself as a dialogical-dialectical field (pp. 131–132).

> **Intuition note.** Theories sound believable because they match things people already say about communication. They sound interesting because they dispute other things people say. Mapping what each theory affirms and denies helps show where theories may conflict.

### A Sketch of the Field: Seven Traditions (pp. 132–140)

**Method and caveats (pp. 132–135).**
- Table 1 identifies each tradition by its definition of communication, its definition of communication problems, its metadiscursive vocabulary, the commonplaces that make it plausible, and the commonplaces it challenges. Table 2 gives *topoi* (stock arguments) for argument across traditions: the distinctive objections each tradition would typically raise against each tradition's way of analyzing practice (p. 132).
- Craig calls the traditions "instrumental constructions rather than essential categories" that still represent recognizable scholarly communities. They reflect his own biases and limitations, and he invites others to point these out. Several correspond to chapters in Littlejohn's (1996b) textbook (p. 132).
- The scheme differs from classifying theories by disciplinary origin, level of organization, type of explanation or underlying epistemology. It divides the field by underlying conceptions of communicative practice. Theories then have something to agree and disagree about, and that something is communication, not epistemology (pp. 132–135).

- *Cybernetic:* plausible because of the identity of mind and brain, the value of information and logic, and (my reading of a garbled cell) that complex systems can be unpredictable. The cell lists as commonplaces it challenges: that humans and machines differ, that emotion is not logical (garbled cell, my reading), and the linear order of cause and effect.

- *Sociocultural:* plausible because the individual is a product of society, every society has a distinct culture, and social actions have unintended effects. It challenges individual agency and responsibility, the absolute identity of the self, and the naturalness of the social order.

It challenges the naturalness and rationality of the traditional social order and the objectivity of science and technology.

**The Rhetorical Tradition: communication as a practical art of discourse (pp. 135–136).**
- Rhetoric (from the ancient Greek sophists on) theorizes communication as a *practical art of discourse*. It explains why participation in discourse, especially public discourse, matters and how it occurs, and holds out the possibility that practice can be improved through critical study and education. Problems are *social exigencies* (needs that call for a response) resolved by artful discourse that persuades audiences (Bitzer, 1968) (p. 135).
- **Plausible because** people agree that rhetoric is powerful, that hearing several sides before judging is good, that communicators vary in wisdom and skill, and that skill can be improved by instruction and practice. Once public advocacy is seen as one of many practices (conversation, news reporting, CD-ROM design), all communication can be theorized as a practical art, which is why it now accords with common sense to treat communication as a practical discipline (pp. 135–136).
- **Interesting because** it challenges the beliefs that mere words matter less than actions, that true knowledge is more than opinion, and that plain truth is something other than strategic adaptation to an audience (p. 136). Rhetorical theorists have disputed for over two millennia the places of emotion and logic, whether rhetoric is good, bad or neutral, whether it has its own subject matter, and whether theory helps practice (p. 136).

**The Semiotic Tradition: communication as intersubjective mediation by signs (pp. 136–138).**
- Semiotics (the study of signs) has ancient roots, but as a distinct tradition of communication theory it can be said to originate with Locke (Book III). It runs through Peirce and Saussure, who founded two different semiotic disciplines, and on to current theories of language, discourse, interpretation, nonverbal communication, culture and media (p. 136). Communication is theorized as mediation between different perspectives. Problems are primarily problems of (re)presentation and transmission of meaning, of gaps between subjectivities that can be bridged, if only imperfectly, by shared sign systems (pp. 136–137).
- **Locke and Taylor.** Locke argued that we cannot take for granted that people understand each other. Taylor shows how all later language theories can be construed as replies to this skeptical argument (p. 137). Semiotic theory now commonly asserts that signs construct their users ("subject-positions"), that meanings are public and ultimately indeterminate, that understanding is a practical gesture rather than a psychological state, and that codes and media are not neutral channels but have sign-like properties of their own (McLuhan: the medium can itself become the message) (p. 137).
- **Plausible because** it appeals to: communication is easiest with a shared language; words can mean different things to different people; meanings are often conveyed indirectly; some ideas suit certain media (a picture is worth a thousand words; email should not be used for delicate business negotiations) (p. 137).
- **Interesting when** it challenges: ideas exist in people's minds, words have correct meanings, meanings can be made explicit, communication is a voluntary act, and we use signs as tools to represent and share thoughts (p. 137).
- **Rhetoric and semiotics.** They are closely akin, and hybrids exist (Burke 1966; Kaufer & Carley 1993). Rhetoric can be thought of as the branch of semiotics that studies the structures of language and argument mediating between communicators and audiences. Semiotics can be thought of as a theory of rhetoric studying the resources for conveying meaning (p. 137). They also differ sharply. Peters notes Locke understood communication as an *alternative* to speech, rhetoric or discourse. In modernist thought, communication stands for reason, truth, clarity and understanding, and rhetoric for traditionalism, artifice and manipulation. In postmodernist thought this is largely turned on its head: for poststructuralist semioticians all communication is rhetoric, if rhetoric means language use for which reason, truth, clarity and understanding can no longer be upheld as norms (pp. 137–138). In the rhetorical tradition rhetoric means something different: communication designed to appeal to an audience and inform its judgment on matters of opinion and decision (p. 138). The debate is practically important because it concerns the normative basis of everyday concepts like judgment, meaning and truth in practical metadiscourse (p. 138).

**The Phenomenological Tradition: communication as the experience of otherness (pp. 138–140).**
- The mainly 20th-century tradition runs from Husserl through existential and hermeneutic phenomenologists and includes Buber, Gadamer and Rogers. Communication is theorized as *dialogue* or experience of otherness. It explains the interplay of identity and difference in authentic relationships and cultivates practices that enable and sustain them (p. 138).
- Authentic communication rests on direct, unmediated contact with others. Understanding begins in prereflective experience arising from bodily existence in a shared lifeworld. Once the dualisms of mind–body and subject–object are set aside, phenomenologists argue, direct contact is a very real and utterly necessary human experience, though it may be fleeting and easily degraded into some form of inauthenticity (p. 138). Example: when I feel a cold or angry glance, I first experience it as a direct expression of the other's coldness or anger toward me, not as an external sign of an inner state open to interpretation. In this I experience both commonality and difference, "not only the other as other to me but myself as other to the other" (p. 138).
- **Challenges to others.** It challenges the semiotic notion that understanding can be mediated only by signs, and the rhetorical notion that communication involves artful or strategic use of signs. Dialogue cannot be "planned, pronounced, or willed" (Anderson, Cissna & Arnett, 1994, quoted p. 139). Conscious goal seeking, however benevolent, "annihilates dialogue" by interposing one's goals and strategies against direct experience. So the problem of communication is the necessity, yet inherent difficulty (arguably practical impossibility), of sustained authentic communication (p. 139).
- **Plausible** through appeals to the beliefs that we should treat each other as persons (I-Thou) not things (I-It), that differences deserve respect, that we should seek common ground, avoid polarization and strategic dishonesty, and that honesty is the best policy, supportive relationships are essential, and the best relationships involve reciprocity and nondomination (p. 139). **Interesting** because it upholds dialogue as ideal while showing how hard it is to sustain, challenges faith in techniques for good communication, and problematizes distinctions between mind and body, facts and values, and words and things (p. 139).
- **Relations to rhetoric and semiotics (pp. 139–140).**
  - It shares with rhetoric the search for common ground and with semiotics the view that the core problem is intersubjective understanding. It differs sharply from rhetoric on authenticity versus artifice, and from semiotics on the relation between language and meaning (p. 139).
  - From a rhetorical view, phenomenology can seem naive or idealistic. From a phenomenological view, rhetoric can seem cynical or pessimistic. Combined, they typically yield an "antirhetorical rhetoric" of dialogue and openness (Brent; Foss & Griffin; Freeman, Littlejohn & Pearce) or a hermeneutical rhetoric downplaying theory and method (Gadamer; Leff) (p. 140).
  - With semiotics: phenomenology questions the distinction between words and things and the assumption that communication needs sign mediation (Stewart). Mixtures can produce a theoretical compound that is explosive in a deconstructive way, if not impenetrably dense (Chang; Lanigan). Reply: the traditional semiotician says signs need stable meanings for communication to occur (Ellis), and the traditional phenomenologist says language use is direct contact (Stewart) (p. 140).
- **What is at stake: Peters (1994).** It is commonly said that interpersonal interaction is the basic form of communication and mass communication a poor substitute. Peters, who elsewhere criticized Lockean semiotics, here uses the semiotic assumption of a "gap" between transmission and reception to argue mass communication is more basic: "No distance is so great as that between two minds" (p. 140). Peters acknowledges that both matter but are hard to combine, because of a lasting tension between specific and general modes of address. He says only dialogue satisfies needs for companionship, friendship and love, while mass communication expresses an "equally noble impulse" toward normative universality (p. 140). Peters finds in the distinction "hidden utopian energies", and says it potentially illuminates our plight as creatures who belong both to a family and to a polis (p. 140).

> **Intuition note.** The three traditions disagree about what is basic in communication: artful persuasion, signs bridging a gap, or direct contact. These are real disagreements, not mere differences in method. The mass vs interpersonal example shows the same dispute in a concrete practical question.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Scenario.** A manager tells a team that they just need to talk things through.
- *Rhetorical reading:* talk as an art for a situation that needs collective judgment. It appeals to the commonplace that hearing several sides helps judgment (p. 135).
- *Semiotic reading:* the team's words may mean different things to different members, so the risk is a gap between viewpoints. It appeals to the commonplace that miscommunication is a constant danger (p. 137).
- *Phenomenological reading:* real contact needs openness, not strategy. If the manager is "talking it through" to reach a planned outcome, this reading says the goal-seeking undermines dialogue (p. 139).

**Counting the Table 2 cells.** There are 7 traditions as objectors and 7 as targets, so $7 \times 7 = 49$ cells. On my reading of the layout, seven are a tradition's own internal criticism (the diagonal), so $49 - 7 = 42$ cross-tradition objections. For just the three traditions above, $3 \times 3 = 9$ cells, of which $3$ are self-critiques and $9 - 3 = 6$ cross-objections. For example, the phenomenological objection to rhetoric (strategy is inauthentic) is one of those 6. Each of the 6 is a point the two sides can argue about, which is Craig's "dialogical-dialectical coherence" in miniature.

## Concepts
- [[communication-as-practical-discipline]] — theory offers reflective resources for everyday talk about communication, built from practice (inductive) and from theory (deductive) (p. 130)
- [[seven-traditions]] — rhetorical, semiotic, phenomenological, cybernetic, sociopsychological, sociocultural, critical; "instrumental constructions" (pp. 132–133)
- [[transmission-view]] — communication as sending and receiving messages or transferring information between minds; Craig holds it can be one usable first-order model, consistent with the constitutive metamodel (pp. 125, 127)
- [[ritual-view]] — here the "constitutive, or ritual" model, producing and reproducing shared meaning; Craig recasts it as a metamodel (pp. 124–126)
- [[constitutive-metamodel]] (new) — second-order perspective: models of communication are ways of constituting it symbolically (p. 127)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] disciplinary matrix and incommensurability ↔ philosophy of science: Craig uses Kuhn's term "incommensurable" (p. 121) for rival traditions; he also speaks of a shared "disciplinary matrix" (p. 124).

## Questions this raises
- If transmission is just one way of constituting communication, what could ever count as a bad way to constitute it? Does the metamodel have any critical force?
- Does dividing the field by conceptions of practice, not by epistemology, really remove the incommensurability Craig describes, or move it into the vocabularies?
- How well do the commonplaces in Table 1 hold across cultures? Craig himself notes the scheme reflects his own biases (p. 132).
- Could the 7% textbook-overlap measure be re-run to test whether a matrix-based curriculum raises agreement?
