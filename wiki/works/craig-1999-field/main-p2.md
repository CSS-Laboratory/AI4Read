# Communication Theory as a Field (part 2): four traditions and the work ahead

## L0 — One line
Communication theory can be treated as one field of seven traditions, each built on a unique model of communicative practice. This gives theorists a shared set of problems to argue about, and the field claims only to be useful, not to hold absolute truth.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This part completes Craig's map of seven traditions by sketching four: cybernetic, sociopsychological, sociocultural and critical. Each theorizes communication differently, defines "communication problems" differently, and both appeals to and challenges everyday commonplaces about communication. Craig then sets an agenda: explore the field, create new traditions, and apply theory to practical metadiscourse (everyday talk about communication). A candidate tradition counts only if it offers a unique model of communicative practice. He asks theorists to address their work to the field as a whole, to contribute to interdisciplinary research, and to teach the field as a resource for reflecting on practice. The field claims only to be useful.

**Prerequisites:** the earlier part of the essay (the practical-discipline idea, "metadiscourse", the theoretical matrix of Tables 1–2, which are not reproduced in these pages); basic idea of feedback and of social-psychological experiments.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Cybernetic tradition:** communication is information processing, and problems are breakdowns in information flow (noise, overload, structure–function mismatch). It treats mind versus matter as only a functional distinction, and it stresses systemic complexity and unintended consequences (pp. 141–142).
- **Sociopsychological tradition:** communication is expression, interaction and influence, mediated by psychological predispositions. Problems call for manipulating the causes of behavior to produce measured outcomes. It demands experimental evidence and challenges the assumption that humans are rational (pp. 143–144).
- **Sociocultural tradition:** communication (re)produces social order. Interaction depends on shared patterns and also creates them. Problems are gaps across space and time that deplete shared patterns (pp. 144–146).
- **Critical tradition:** authentic communication occurs only through discursive reflection that questions presuppositions and unmasks ideological distortion. On the critical theorist's view, merely reproducing or producing social order is not yet authentic (pp. 146–149).
- **Each tradition appeals to some commonplaces and challenges others**, which is why each can start reflection in everyday metadiscourse (pp. 142–148, 152).
- **Test for membership in the field:** a tradition needs a unique model of communicative practice that is not logically redundant. The set of seven is distinguishable but not exhaustive. A "biological tradition" fails this test as far as Craig knows (pp. 150–152).
- **Three tasks ahead:** exploring (across and within traditions), creating new theory, and applying theory to practical problems. These can merge into one activity (pp. 149–152).
- **Disciplinary practice:** address work to the field, enrich interdisciplinary research, and teach the field as a resource for practical reflection. The field claims only to be useful (pp. 152–154).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The Cybernetic Tradition: Communication as Information Processing (pp. 141–142)
**Epigraphs and scope.** Craig opens with quotations that tie "communication theory" to Wiener's cybernetics and to information theory (p. 141). He says modern communication theory originated in the cybernetic tradition (Shannon, Wiener, von Neumann, Turing). The tradition extends to systems and information science, cognitive science and AI, functionalist social theory, network analysis, and the Batesonian school of interpersonal communication (p. 141).

**Model of communication.** Communication is theorized as information processing. It explains how complex systems, living or nonliving, function and why they often malfunction (p. 141). Cybernetics "epitomizes" the transmission model (p. 141). Communication problems are breakdowns in information flow from noise, information overload, or mismatch between structure and function. The resources offered are information-processing technologies and methods of systems design, analysis and management, and on the "softer" side, therapeutic intervention (p. 141).

**Plausibility via commonplaces.** The tradition appeals rhetorically to everyday materialism, functionalism and rationalism (p. 141). For cybernetics the mind–matter distinction is only functional, like software versus hardware. Thought is only information processing. So individual thought can be called "intrapersonal communication", and groups, societies and eventually robots can also "think" (p. 141; note 18 cites Bateson and Dennett for this functionalist view of mind). Craig's example is Commander Data as perhaps the most "human" member of the Enterprise crew, where denying this is "soft-headed sentimentality", which he calls a two-edged criticism (p. 141).

**Challenges to commonplaces.** It points to surprising analogies between living and nonliving systems. It questions the significance of consciousness and emotion, and the distinctions between mind and matter, form and content, real and artificial (p. 141). It also challenges simple linear cause and effect. It stresses the unpredictable complexity of feedback and the likelihood of unintended consequences (p. 142). Its practical lesson is that the whole is greater than the sum of the parts. Communicators should therefore take a systemic viewpoint and not hold individuals responsible for systemic outcomes that no individual can control (p. 142).

**Relations to other traditions.**
- With rhetoric it shares the valorizing of technique and artifice (note 19: Kaufer & Butler as a rhetoric–cybernetics hybrid). Rhetoric is artful discourse that informs practical judgment, whereas information processing is merely a mechanism that performs functions (p. 142).
- It resembles semiotics in collapsing human agency into symbol-processing systems (note 20: blends in Cherry, Eco, Wilden). But semiotics has problems with "information" because it reduces semantic content to function, such as feedback or uncertainty reduction (p. 142).
- It resembles phenomenology in stressing the emergence of meaning among elements of a system (note 21: Krippendorff's movement toward phenomenology). The phenomenologist requires sincerity for authentic dialogue. The cybernetician, like the semiotician, replies that we can never really know whether anyone, even oneself, is sincere. Craig adds that from a cybernetic view there are probably better ways to evaluate the reliability of information (p. 142).

*Intuition note.* Cybernetics asks how information flows through any system, so humans and machines can be compared on the same terms. Its practical value is seeing problems as system problems, not as individuals' faults. Craig notes that semiotics objects that this reduces meaning to mere function (p. 142).

### The Sociopsychological Tradition: Communication as Expression, Interaction, and Influence (pp. 142–144)
**Model.** This is the tradition of 20th-century experimental social psychology, which Craig says continues to predominate in much of what is called "communication science" (p. 143). Behavior of humans or other organisms expresses psychological mechanisms, states and traits. Through interaction with others' expressions it produces cognitive, emotional and behavioral effects (p. 143). Communication is, in short, how individuals interact and influence each other, face-to-face or through media, one-to-one, one-to-many or many-to-many (p. 143).

**Mediation.** In all formats, interposed elements mediate between individuals. This is contrary to the phenomenological view. Semiotics has signs and sign systems as mediators. Social psychology has psychological predispositions (attitudes, emotional states, personality traits, unconscious conflicts, social cognitions), as modified by emergent effects of interaction, including media technologies and institutions (p. 143). Note 23 cites Meyrowitz's three metaphors for media (conduits, languages, environments); Craig maps conduits to the sociopsychological tradition, languages to the semiotic and environments to the sociocultural (p. 156).

**Problems and practice.** The tradition explains the causes and effects of social behavior and cultivates practices that attempt intentional control over them. Problems are situations calling for effective manipulation of the causes of behavior to produce objectively defined and measured outcomes (p. 143).

**Plausibility.** It appeals to beliefs that communication varies with personality, that judgments are influenced by social context and biased by strong beliefs, attitudes and emotions, and that group processes such as leadership and conflict affect outcomes and so should be managed (p. 143).

**Challenges.** It challenges the commonsense premise that humans are rational and our faith in personal autonomy, through demonstrations of human weakness and irrationality (pp. 143–144). Note 22 (Herman) says psychology's rise as a cultural worldview was fueled partly by wars and other events that called rationality and autonomy into question (p. 156). It requires rigorous experimental evidence for causal claims. It criticizes rhetoric for lacking proof that its techniques work, and cybernetics for ignoring motivation, personality and emotion (p. 144).

**Practice and morals.** Like cybernetics it valorizes technique: expert application of psychological manipulation and therapy. A sociopsychological theory of rhetoric tends to see rhetoric as a technology of manipulation, not an art of discourse that informs the receiver's judgment (p. 144). It has its own moral imperative: communicators should make responsible choices based on scientific evidence about likely consequences of their messages (p. 144).

*Intuition note.* Here communication matters because of what it causes in people. Claims count only if tested, and the typical help offered is a technique backed by evidence. This fits everyday practical concern with the causes and effects of communication, and it leads this tradition to see rhetoric as a technology of manipulation rather than an art of discourse (p. 144).

### The Sociocultural Tradition: Communication as the (Re)Production of Social Order (pp. 144–146)
**Model.** Sociocultural theory is the "discovery" of communication, largely since the 19th century and partly under semiotic influence, within sociology and anthropology (p. 144). Communication is a symbolic process that produces and reproduces shared sociocultural patterns. It explains how macrolevel social order is created, sustained and transformed in microlevel interaction (p. 144). "(Re)production" marks a paradoxical reflexivity. Everyday interaction depends on preexisting shared patterns and so largely "reproduces" the order. But interaction also needs improvisation that, collectively and in the long run, "produces" the order that makes interaction possible (pp. 144–145).

**Central problem and debate.** A central problem of the theory is to find the right balance, that is, to sort out the complex relations between production and reproduction, micro and macro, agency and structure, local culture and universal natural law (p. 145). The main axis of debate is structural theories, which give priority to stable macro patterns, versus interpretive or interactionist theories, which give priority to local co-creation and negotiation by members (p. 145). Note 24 lists attempts at balance: structuration theory, practice theory, ecological models (p. 156).

**Communication problems.** Problems are gaps across space (diversity, relativity) and time (change) that disable interaction by depleting the stock of shared patterns. Conflict, misunderstanding and coordination difficulty increase when shared rituals, rules and expectations are scarce (p. 145). The theory addresses technological change, breakdown of traditional orders, urbanization, mass society, bureaucratic rationalization, postmodern fragmentation and globalization. Such perturbations disrupt interaction but also enable new meanings and new means of communication (p. 145).

**Hybrids.** "Pure" sociocultural theory may be hard to find. Examples are social action media theory (sociocultural, phenomenological, semiotic), CMM theory (interactionist social theory with cybernetic and dialogical concepts), and conversation analysis (interactionist, phenomenological, semiotic roots) (p. 145). Rhetorical theory in the 20th century took a sociocultural turn. Quoting Arnold, Craig notes that acculturation can be seen as rhetorical learning. Social order then supplies the materials of rhetoric, while rhetoric becomes a method, conscious or not, for constituting social order (p. 145).

**The sociocultural "voice".** It criticizes social psychology for individualism, inattention to macrosocial forces and insensitivity to cultural differences. It criticizes classical rhetoric for naive assumptions about agency, and semiotics for abstracting signs from sociocultural context (pp. 145–146).

**Commonplaces.** It appeals to beliefs that individuals are products of their environments, that groups develop norms, rituals and worldviews, that change is hard, and that interventions have unintended consequences. It challenges the taking for granted of personal identity, the view of institutions as inevitable, ethnocentrism, and overattributing moral responsibility to individuals for largely societal problems such as poverty and crime (p. 146). It cultivates practices that acknowledge diversity, value tolerance and understanding, and emphasize collective over individual responsibility. Craig says everyday talk of blame and responsibility has been influenced by this tradition (Bowers & Iwi, 1993) (p. 146).

*Intuition note.* Communication is how a society keeps itself going and also how it changes. Problems arise when people no longer share enough patterns to coordinate. This makes the tradition strong on diversity and social change, and it moves moral responsibility from individuals to society.

### The Critical Tradition: Communication as Discursive Reflection (pp. 146–149)
**Model.** Craig traces critical communication theory to Plato's Socratic dialectic, a method of reaching truth by questions that provoke reflection on contradictions (pp. 146–147). **Habermas (1984), as Craig reports:** every act of communication aimed at mutual understanding has an instability, a built-in telos toward articulating, questioning and transcending presuppositions judged untrue, dishonest or unjust (p. 147). On this view (voice: critical theory as Craig presents it), communication that is only transmission-reception or ritual sharing is "inherently faulty, distorted, incomplete". Authentic communication occurs only through discursive reflection. Its transcendence can never be fully achieved, but the reflective process is progressively emancipatory (p. 147).

**Lineage and problem.** The tradition runs from Marx through the Frankfurt School to Habermas, or through late Marxism and post-Marxism to political economy, critical cultural studies, feminist theory, postcolonial and queer theory (p. 147; note 26 on Habermas's centrality). The basic "problem of communication" is material and ideological forces that preclude or distort discursive reflection. Communication so conceived explains how injustice is perpetuated by ideological distortion. Justice can potentially be restored through practices that enable critical reflection or consciousness-raising, which unmask distortions and enable liberating political action (p. 147).

**Commonplaces.** It appeals to beliefs about the omnipresence of injustice, power overcoming truth and reason, and discourse's liberating potential, and to values of freedom, equality and reason (p. 147). It challenges the naturalness of the social order, the validity of authority and tradition, including traditional beliefs about reason, which it claims distort reason in service of capitalism, racism and patriarchy. It also challenges the neutrality of science and technology, cultural individualism, and instrumental reason (the assumption that rationality consists entirely in means–ends calculation, where the ends can only be voluntaristically chosen based on individual interests) (p. 147). Craig says it is, or at least tries to be, the most deeply practical kind of theory, although its notion of practicality often clashes with commonsense notions (pp. 147–148). In the tradition of Marx, its aim is not to understand the world but to change it through praxis, or theoretically reflective social action (p. 148).

**Hybrids and relation to others.** Any mode of theory can turn critical, as in critical rhetoric and critical semiotics (p. 148). Craig finds most interesting the efforts to work through contradictions between critical theory and other traditions (Condit, Farrell in rhetorical theory) (p. 148). Critical theory is inherently a critique of social-order reproduction, which is sociocultural theory's central theme (p. 148). Craig believes the critical model of practice differs radically from the (re)production model. For the critical theorist, an activity that only reproduces or even produces social order is not yet authentic communication. For order to rest on genuine mutual understanding, as opposed to strategic manipulation, oppressive conformity or empty ritual, communicators must repeatedly articulate and discuss their differing assumptions about the objective world, moral norms and inner experience (p. 148).

**Relation to phenomenology.** Discursive reflection resembles phenomenological dialogue but adds a dialectical aspect. For the critical view, dialogue is an ideal that existing conditions may make unlikely. A dialogue model is defective if it does not move participants to reflect on conditions that disable dialogue. Dialectical questioning unmasks those conditions and points to changes that would make genuine dialogue possible (p. 148). Ideology critique and consciousness-raising follow a similar pattern. Krippendorff's (1995) theory of "undoing" power, a cybernetic–phenomenological hybrid, seems considerably more sanguine than most critical theories about whether insight alone, without concerted political action, can change the world (p. 148).

**Disputes.** Critics charge critical theory with politicizing scholarship and asserting a universal norm from a priori ideology. The critics disagree among themselves. Some say science should say nothing about norms, others that norms should rest on empirical criteria, others that norms are only local (p. 149). Critical theory replies that other traditions are blind to their own ideological presuppositions and falsely claim neutrality. In its view, local practices and empirical outcomes cannot be taken at face value but must always be judged in light of a reflective analysis of the distorting effects of power and ideology (p. 149).

**Craig's evaluation.** He says the most useful contribution of critical theory may be a deeper appreciation of discursive reflection as a practical possibility intrinsic to all communication. Craig says practical metadiscourse always has the potential to develop into a truly reflective discourse that engages theory with practice (Craig, 1996b). So the critical tradition confirms that reflective discourse, and hence communication theory, matter for everyday practice (p. 149).

*Intuition note.* In this tradition communication is a process of questioning the assumptions that people normally take for granted. It is valued when it moves toward a freer, more just order. Craig suggests its most useful contribution may be a deeper appreciation of discursive reflection as a practical possibility within all communication.

### Working the Field: Concluding Reflections (pp. 149–154)
**The Work Ahead (p. 149).** Three tasks: exploring the field (mapping issues and traditions), creating new traditions and ways of schematizing the field, and applying theory by engaging practical metadiscourse.

**Exploration (pp. 149–150).** Two modes: "traversing" the traditions (complementarities and tensions among them) and "spelunking" into each (internal complexity) (p. 149). The matrix invites locating agreement and disagreement. Notions of strategy and technique are salient in several traditions, and crossing traditions problematizes them. Craig names four field-defining problems (p. 150):
- strategy versus authenticity (rhetoric or social psychology versus phenomenology);
- intentionality versus functionality (rhetoric or phenomenology versus cybernetics);
- proving the effectiveness of techniques (social psychology versus rhetoric);
- instrumental reason as ideological distortion (critical theory versus cybernetics or social psychology).

Each tradition is internally complex and open to many interpretations. Craig suggests that theoretical fields may look like fractals: each tradition, when magnified, shows a dialogical-dialectical structure of several traditions much like that of the whole field. Zoomed out, communication theory is one tradition in a megafield of the human sciences (p. 150). An ideal "user-friendly" representation might be an interactive hypertext (p. 150). Note 27 adds that on cybernetic principles, and given Miller's (1956) information-processing limit, a level of the scheme should have about seven "chunks", which is the number of traditions here (p. 156).

**Creation (pp. 150–152).** Each of the seven traditions rests on a unique model of communicative practice. They are a distinguishable set but not a logically exhaustive one. The field is open to new traditions, provided each new one rests on a unique model that, once integrated (possibly with other traditions redefined), is not logically redundant with any other (p. 150).

Craig offers four candidate reconstructions, each posed as questions (p. 151):
- **Feminist:** communication as connectedness. How does it differ from phenomenological dialogue, and how would it resituate feminism relative to critical theory?
- **Aesthetic:** communication as embodied performance. How does it differ from semiotic and sociocultural models, and how would it reposition rhetoric and critical theory?
- **Economic:** communication as exchange, where each message has an exchange value that equates to its meaning. What would it look like if separated from its entanglements with critical theory, phenomenology and social psychology?
- **Spiritual:** communication as communion on a nonmaterial or mystical plane. How does its transcendence relate to that of phenomenology (dialogue), sociocultural theory (culture) and critical theory (reflection)?

He says the standard is rigorous. His test case is a **biological tradition**. He is "not aware of any distinct, biological way of theorizing communicative practice" that would not be better described as semiotic, sociopsychological or cybernetic (p. 151). Biological principles such as development or natural selection can perhaps explain communication theorized as mediation by signs, interaction or information processing, but he is unaware of a unique biological conceptualization of the practice itself (pp. 151–152). A tradition that fails the standard is logically outside the field (p. 152). This does not rule out someone discovering one. New theorizations may also emerge from grounded practical theory (Craig & Tracy, 1995). Craig concludes that we have every reason to expect new traditions to keep appearing, so we should not hope, nor need worry, that the work of creating theory will ever be completed (p. 152).

**Application (p. 152).** Application means engaging theoretical metadiscourse with practical metadiscourse on real problems, where theory can best be tested for relevance and usefulness (note 28: methods of testing are beyond this essay). Each tradition supplies a vocabulary. Mastering several lets one view a problem from several points of view and choose the apt one (p. 152; note 29 cites Jonsen & Toulmin that application is rhetorical and perspectival, not formally deductive; Craig adds, citing Craig 1996b, that alternative theories offer limited, complementary perspectives). Because each tradition appeals to some commonplaces and challenges others, each vocabulary can provoke metacommunicative reflection. Example: asking whether someone is too "strategic" can use rhetoric and phenomenology and lead to the paradoxes of radically authentic communication (p. 152). Such discourse can move along a theory–practice continuum and, in its more theoretical moments, can become indistinguishable from theoretical metadiscourse. In those moments of intersection, exploring, creating and applying theory merge into one activity (p. 152).

**Implications for Disciplinary Practice (pp. 152–154).** Theorists now have something important to argue about, the social practice of communication, so they should stop ignoring each other and address the field. Craig says that as a result of doing so, there will be a field of communication theory (pp. 152–153). Following Anderson (1996), Craig suggests three things (p. 153):
1. **Write to the field.** Show awareness of relevant traditions, engage central themes, highlight practical implications, and respond to criticisms anticipated from other traditions. Cross-tradition arguments may be technically naive in places. They still signal relevance and give specialists entry points to correct and deepen them. That is what dialogical-dialectical coherence might look like in practice (p. 153).
2. **Interdisciplinary centrality.** Every tradition points to interdisciplinary areas that other communication perspectives can enrich. Craig cites Tracy's discourse-studies approach, marked by normative and applied interests, awareness of audience and focus on problems and strategies, a blend of rhetorical, sociopsychological and other influences (p. 153).
3. **Teaching.** Undergraduates want something practical and comprehensible. They get fragments of a subject, "up to 249 theories and still counting" (p. 153). The analysis in Tables 1 and 2 invites teaching the field as a resource for reflecting on practical problems, so that going deeper moves into practical concerns, not away from them (p. 153). Students who want to do original research cannot ignore the need to specialize (Reeves, 1992), but an overview helps them address wider audiences. Specialists can focus on "their own" row and column of Table 2 and leave other cells to other specialists (p. 154).

**Closing image (p. 154).** The tables can scaffold a scheme of rhetorical invention (commonplaces and stock arguments) for preparing students to join the discipline's discourse, as rhetoric prepares citizens for public affairs. The field marks out a common space for theoretical metadiscourse. Craig ends: it "is not a repository of absolute truth. It claims no more than to be useful" (p. 154).

*Intuition note.* The seven traditions work like seven vocabularies for the same social practice, none final. A new vocabulary earns a place only if it describes communicative practice in a way the others do not. The field's justification is that these vocabularies help people think and act about real communication problems.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
**Case:** a manager emails a new deadline. Half the team misses it. Each tradition names a different problem (vocabulary from pp. 141–148):
- *Cybernetic:* a flow breakdown, such as noise or overload from 40 unread emails. Fix: redesign the channel (e.g., one notice board). The blame lies with the system, not the people.
- *Sociopsychological:* the message failed to change behavior, perhaps because recipients' attitudes (resentment) mediate. Fix: test message variants and measure compliance.
- *Sociocultural:* the team lacks shared rituals or rules about deadlines (e.g., a new hire from a different work culture). Fix: build shared expectations.
- *Critical:* ask whose interests the deadline serves and whether staff may question it. Fix: open discussion of assumptions.

**Counting check on Craig's "own row and column" advice (p. 154).** A 7×7 matrix has $7 \times 7 = 49$ cells. One specialist's row plus column is $7 + 7 - 1 = 13$ cells (added step: the shared diagonal cell is counted once). So $49 - 13 = 36$ cells are left to specialists in other traditions. Note 15 (p. 155) says the diagonal cells of Table 2 indicate each tradition's reflexive self-criticism.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Cybernetic "whole greater than the sum of parts" ↔ complex-systems thinking in computational social science: system outcomes are not attributable to any one agent.
- [structural-analogy] Sociocultural (re)production ↔ micro–macro models of social order: interaction depends on and also creates structure.
- [metaphor] Fractal field structure ↔ multilevel or hierarchical clustering: the same dialogical pattern recurs at each level of granularity.
- [structural-analogy] Sociopsychological demand for experimental proof ↔ causal inference in the social sciences: causal claims need rigorous evidence.
- [metaphor] Seven "chunks" at one level (note 27) ↔ cognitive load limits in information design.

## Questions this raises
- Can the "unique model of communicative practice" test be applied consistently? Who judges logical redundancy, and do the seven pass it?
- If traditions are only complementary vocabularies, how can their empirical claims (e.g., sociopsychological effects) be tested or compared?
- Does the critical tradition's claim about authentic communication conflict with the sociopsychological demand for neutral evidence, and can Craig's pragmatic criterion ("useful") settle it?
- Would a biological tradition become possible if a distinctive theory of communicative practice, not only explanations of it, appeared?
