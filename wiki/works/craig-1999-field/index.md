# Craig, R. T. (1999). Communication Theory as a Field. *Communication Theory*, 9(2), 119–161. DOI: 10.1111/j.1468-2885.1999.tb00355.x

## In one paragraph
Craig asks why communication theory is not yet a coherent field. His answer is that its ideas came from many disciplines and were collected or imported rather than argued over. He does not call for one unified theory. Instead he proposes a shared "matrix" in which theories can disagree productively. It rests on two principles: the constitutive model of communication used as a metamodel, and theory understood as metadiscourse, or talk about everyday talk about communication. Within this matrix he sets out seven traditions: rhetorical, semiotic, phenomenological, cybernetic, sociopsychological, sociocultural and critical. Each defines communication differently, so each sees different problems and raises different objections to the others. The paper matters because it gives theorists a common set of problems to argue about, and it shows which tradition a theory speaks from. The field claims only to be useful, not to hold absolute truth.

## The path through the work
- [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]] — Communication theory can become a coherent field not through one unified theory but through a shared "matrix" in which diverse traditions argue with each other, built on a constitutive metamodel and on theory understood as metadiscourse.
- [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]] — Communication theory can be treated as one field of seven traditions, each built on a unique model of communicative practice. This gives theorists a shared set of problems to argue about, and the field claims only to be useful, not to hold absolute truth.

## Big ideas across units
- **Coherence through argument, not unification.** The field needs "dialogical-dialectical coherence", where theories engage each other, rather than one grand theory. Being a discipline means agreeing that you disagree about consequential matters. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]], [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]].
- **Theory as metadiscourse.** Theory is discourse about everyday discourse on communication. It gets its plausibility from the commonplaces it appeals to and its interest from those it challenges. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]] (the principle), [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]] (each tradition appeals to some commonplaces and challenges others).
- **Seven traditions as alternative vocabularies.** Each tradition has its own definition of communication, its own problems and its own objections to the others. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]] (rhetorical, semiotic, phenomenological), [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]] (cybernetic, sociopsychological, sociocultural, critical).
- **The constitutive model as a metamodel.** It sits at a second level and does not compete with the transmission model, which can be one way of constituting communication. Confusing the levels is a category mistake. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]] (developed there), [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]] (the transmission and ritual views are linked again).
- **Disagreement between traditions is productive.** The traditions clash over judgment, meaning, truth, authenticity, rationality and the sufficiency of social order. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]], [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]].
- **A test for what counts as a tradition.** A candidate needs a unique model of communicative practice that is not logically redundant. The seven are distinguishable but not exhaustive. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]], building on the matrix in [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]].
- **A practical, useful discipline.** Theory can inform practice. The agenda is to explore, create and apply, and to teach the field as a resource for reflecting on practice. Units: [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]], [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]].

## How to read it
- Both units are essential, since together they form one argument. The material does not mark either as skippable.
- Read [[craig-1999-field/main-p1]] first. It builds the argument, the two principles and the matrix (Tables 1–2), and it covers three traditions.
- [[craig-1999-field/main-p2]] depends on it. It assumes the practical-discipline idea, metadiscourse and the matrix, and it completes the map with four more traditions and the concluding agenda.
- Tables 1–2 are not reproduced in these pages, so check them in the original when following the traditions.
- Some background helps: the idea of a scholarly discipline, the transmission model (sender → message → receiver), Kuhn's term "incommensurable", and the basic ideas of feedback and of social-psychological experiments.
