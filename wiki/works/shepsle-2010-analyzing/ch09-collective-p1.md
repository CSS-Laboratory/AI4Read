# Collective Action (part 1/2): groups, cooperation and coordination

## L0 — One line
Mass action is puzzling because one person's contribution is tiny and costly, and Shepsle shows how the number of contributors needed ($k$ out of $n$), the cost, the presence of several good outcomes, and conflict of interest affect how likely a group is to cooperate.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do people join collective action when one person's effect is minute and the cost is real? Shepsle first criticises the group-based view of politics, which assumes groups simply exist. He then treats a group as many-person cooperation over a good that all enjoy once it exists. With unanimity required and benefit exceeding cost, contributing is likely. With only $k$ of $n$ needed, there are two equilibria, and, in Shepsle's judgment, success tends to become less likely as $k$ falls relative to $n$ (apart from a possible uptick for very small $k$). When several goals are equally good, coordination is a problem. When members also disagree about which goal, the group typically needs an institutional solution. This matters because group action cannot be taken for granted.

**Prerequisites:** utility (payoffs), Nash-equilibrium idea ("no one wants to change their own choice"), the two-person cooperation game of the previous chapter, basic probability.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Mass protest is puzzling: it appears that one person's instrumental benefit (the effect of his or her own contribution on the outcome) is small, while the cost in time, money or risk is large (p. 263).
- The older "group basis of politics" assumed that common interest naturally yields organization. Case 9.1 finds weak evidence that groups form in proportion to interests (pp. 266–268).
- A group goal is modelled as a good that everyone enjoys once it is produced, whether or not they contributed (p. 269).
- If everyone must contribute, each member values the goal at $B$, contributing costs $C$, and $B>C$, "all contribute" is a likely focal-point outcome (a salient outcome on which expectations converge), although "none contribute" is also an equilibrium (pp. 270–272).
- If only $k<n$ contributors are needed, the equilibria are "none" or "exactly $k$". Pressure to contribute rises with $k$ relative to $n$, so smaller $k$ can lower the chance of success (pp. 272–274).
- Shepsle claims that large $n$ and large $C$ make failure more likely. Large $B-C$ makes success more likely, though footnote 8 notes a countervailing effect (p. 275).
- When several goals are equally good, coordination is hard. The chance that $n$ coin-flippers match is $(1/2)^{n-1}$ (p. 277).
- Conflict of interest over which goal makes coordination harder still. Groups typically institutionalize a solution (pp. 278–279).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the puzzle (pp. 262–264)
- Shepsle recalls the protests of the 1960s (free speech, civil rights, Vietnam, environment) and their later offshoots (pp. 262–263).
- Because we are used to mass action, we overlook that it is puzzling. One attendee's contribution to the outcome is "bound to be minute", while participation costs time, possibly money and risk. So the instrumental benefit looks small and the cost large (p. 263).
- Related puzzles: conventions (driving side), coordination (an orchestra), fair shares (cleaning a common room). Much of life happens in groups, so we often commit the "fallacy of false personification", talking as if groups had a life of their own (p. 264).

### The group basis of politics (pp. 264–266)
- Large societies are discussed in terms of interest groups, such as farmers vs consumers and unions vs employers. Peak associations are groups of groups (pp. 264–265).
- For much of the first half of the 20th century, the study of politics was the study of groups. In Bentley's picture, the policy status quo is a point and each group is a vector. Its direction is the change it wants and its magnitude is its political strength. The new status quo is the resultant of all the vectors (pp. 265–266).
- The shared axiom was that common interest, however defined and arrived at, leads naturally to coherently motivated organizations. Politics is then about how these organizations oppose and support one another (p. 266). The view takes groups as given.

**Intuition note:** The group view treats politics like physics: add up the pushes. Its weak point is that it assumes the groups exist rather than explaining where they come from.

### Case 9.1: Who is represented? (pp. 267–268)
- **Pluralist hypothesis (the pluralists' belief):** groups form naturally from shared interests, so organizations should be roughly proportional to the number of people sharing an interest. Shepsle calls the evidence "quite weak" (p. 267).
- Schlozman and Tierney, mid-1980s: 4% of the population was looking for work, but only a handful of Washington organizations represented them (p. 267).
- In the table (p. 268), the ratio is % of organizations divided by % of adults. For managerial/administrative it is 10.10 (7% of adults, 71.0% of organizations). For the retired it is 0.07, and for those looking for work 0.03.
- At least a dozen groups represent senior citizens but none the middle-aged. Ducks have a lobby and turkeys none (p. 267).
- This inability to explain which interests get organized led scholars to study the dynamics of collective action. Mancur Olson's work is called the best-known challenge to the pluralists (pp. 267–268).

### Cooperation accounts: collective action as multiperson cooperation (pp. 268–275)
**Setup.** Each member chooses "contribute" or "don't contribute". If enough contribute, the group goal is reached, and everyone enjoys it whether or not they contributed. Shepsle calls such a goal a **public good** (as in Hume's marsh-draining game). Goods where non-contributors can be denied access (tolls, club dues, tickets) are set aside as "not quite public goods". Only the polar case is analysed (p. 269, n. 5).

**Two complications (pp. 269–270).**
1. The outcome may be dichotomous (goal attained or not) or continuous. It is almost always possible to turn a dichotomous situation into a continuous one (e.g., degrees of victory in war), but the chapter keeps the dichotomous case for simplicity (pp. 269–270).
2. The **production function** says how many contributors are needed (p. 270).

**Unanimity required (pp. 270–272).** Each member values the goal at $B>0$ utiles. Contributing costs $C>0$ utiles. Everyone has the same $B$ (n. 6).
- If $C>B$, nobody contributes whatever others do, because $B-C<0$ (p. 270).
- If $B>C$, there are two equilibria. In the "equilibrium trap" nobody contributes. A lone contributor $j$ would get $-C$ and no benefit. Everyone would be better off if all contributed (p. 271).
- Shepsle argues that the trap is not very likely. Each member reasons: not contributing gives 0; contributing while all others do gives $B-C>0$; contributing while someone else does not gives $-C$. Everyone knows everyone is essential and that others know it (p. 271).
- So members will often coordinate on contributing. Shepsle calls "everyone contributes" a **focal point** (Schelling) (p. 272).

**Intuition note:** When every single person is essential and the net benefit is positive, each person knows the goal fails without his or her contribution and gains nothing by holding back. The group can then settle on "all contribute", although "none" remains an equilibrium too.

**Only $k$ of $n$ needed, $0<k<n$ (pp. 272–274).** Ms. $j$'s payoffs depend on how many *others* contribute (Display 9.1, p. 272):

| $j$'s choice | fewer than $k-1$ others | exactly $k-1$ others | $k$ or more others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribute | $-C$ | $B-C$ | $B-C$ |
| Do not contribute | $0$ | $0$ | $B$ |

- Contributing pays only if exactly $k-1$ others contribute, since only then is she essential. She cannot know which column holds (p. 272).
- Shepsle's analysis suggests two equilibria: nobody contributes, or exactly $k$ do. Contributors are essential, and non-contributors gain nothing by joining (pp. 272–273).
- This is something of a "knife-edge" condition. Exactly $k$ individuals must believe that they, and only they, are likely to contribute. Shepsle claims this is a harder problem than unanimity. The tipping point $k$ is a crucial determinant of success (p. 273).
- Example with $n=100$ (p. 273), where the claims are Shepsle's judgments rather than computations:
  - $k=100$: unanimous contribution is a likely occurrence.
  - $k=95$: members think nearly everyone must contribute, so, it seems to Shepsle, there is a tendency for $k$ (or possibly even more than $k$) to contribute.
  - $k=85$: considerable pressure, but less.
  - $k=25$: many members calculate their contribution is not needed. Ms. $j$ might take her children shopping instead of joining the 24 others at the river clean-up (pp. 273–274).
- When $k$ is very small, nearly everyone bails out and the "rational" forecast is failure. But some people "do the right thing" anyway. They enjoy participating, or they have internalized a value system favouring contribution. If only a few are needed, the goal may still be achieved, so there may be an "uptick" (p. 274).
- **General conclusion:** strategic and psychological pressures to contribute rise as $k$ gets large relative to $n$. Holding $n$ fixed, the likelihood of sufficient contribution declines as $k$ declines. This is "nonobvious" because a smaller $k$ looks like an easier goal (p. 274).

**Intuition note:** A smaller requirement makes each person feel less needed. The goal looks easy, so most people leave the work to others, and fewer than $k$ may end up contributing.

**Other parameters (p. 275).** Holding $k$ fixed, Shepsle claims the tendencies get more pronounced as:
- $n$ grows. This is nearly like shrinking $k$, but not quite, because identification with the group becomes more tenuous in a large group.
- $C$ grows, holding $n$, $k$ and $B-C$ fixed. The group tends to fail, since the psychological risk of paying $-C$ without reaching $k$ inhibits contribution.
- $B-C$ grows. The prospect of enough contributors grows, because people take psychological risks for important goals.
- **Caveat (n. 8):** the analysis is plausible as a first cut but not satisfactory as a fully strategic analysis. Large $B-C$ also pushes colleagues to contribute, which may let a person rationalize letting others do the work. Weighing these effects needs deeper game theory (p. 275).

### Multiperson cooperation and coordination (pp. 276–279)
Two complications are added to the group's single shared goal (p. 276).

**Nonuniqueness (pp. 276–278).** Members are indifferent between several goals, say opera or ballet, so long as all choose the same one.
- Display 9.2 (p. 276): if all others choose opera, $j$ gets $B$ from opera and $0$ from ballet. If all others choose ballet, she gets $0$ from opera and $B$ from ballet. If there is no consensus among the others, she gets $b<B$ whatever she does.
- Only the first two columns matter. There are two equilibria. Game theory says only that the group should coordinate, not how. Each person should in effect "flip a coin" (p. 277).
- Probability of coordination by coin-flips: for $n=2$ it is $1/2$ (two of four outcomes). For $n=3$ it is $1/4$, and for $n=4$ it is $1/8$. In general $(1/2)^{n-1}$, and for $n=10$ it is $.00195$ (p. 277).
- Repeat play may help, as in the two-person case. Examples are the side of the road and going to the opera in even months and the ballet in odd months (pp. 277–278).
- Communication is deliberately set aside. It can be costly, asymmetric and strategically complex (n. 9, p. 278).

**Conflict of interest (pp. 278–279).** Display 9.3 gives member $j$ the payoff $B_j$ if all coordinate on opera and $\beta_j$ if all coordinate on ballet. The Greek letter is garbled in the scan; see Reading notes. The text considers the case where both exceed $b$ (p. 279).
- The interesting case with conflict of interest is where $B_j>\beta_j$ but $B_k<\beta_k$ for members $j$ and $k$. Members must then overcome nonuniqueness and also differences of opinion (p. 279).
- It may be easy to resolve if five of six prefer opera (p. 279). For a group of 15 (5 intense opera, 5 intense ballet, 3 mild opera, 2 mild ballet), an institutional solution is needed (p. 279).
- Shepsle says most situations with both nonuniqueness and conflict of interest need one. Groups typically institutionalize a solution rather than wait for it to appear. The page breaks off in the middle of a sentence introducing "the important insight" of a named author, whose name is cut off at "Man-" (p. 279).

**Intuition note:** If two outcomes are equally good for everyone, nothing in the payoffs picks one. If members also rank them differently, the choice becomes a dispute. In a complicated case Shepsle says some institutional solution (rules or procedures) is needed, although repeat play may help.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take $n=3$, $k=2$, $B=5$, $C=2$. Payoffs to member $j$, by number of *other* members contributing, with $k-1=1$:
- Others $=0$ ($<1$): contribute $-2$, don't $0$.
- Others $=1$: contribute $5-2=3$, don't $0$.
- Others $=2$ ($\ge 2$): contribute $3$, don't $5$.

Check each profile:
- **None contribute:** a deviator gets $-2<0$, so this is an equilibrium.
- **Exactly two contribute:** a contributor who drops out faces 1 other and gets $0<3$. The non-contributor who joins faces 2 others and gets $3<5$. So this is an equilibrium.
- **All three contribute:** one who drops out gets $5>3$, so this is not an equilibrium.

The group needs exactly two, and which two is not settled by payoffs. Coin-flipping over "I'm in / out" would not guarantee exactly two.

Coordination with 3 members flipping between opera and ballet: the chance all match is $2\cdot(1/2)^3=1/4=(1/2)^{3-1}$.

## Concepts
- [[collective-action-problem]] — acting together is puzzling because one person's effect is small and the cost real (p. 263)
- [[public-good]] — once the group goal is produced, all enjoy it whether or not they contributed. Shepsle's polar case ignores exclusion (p. 269)
- [[selective-incentive]] — footnote 5 notes that denying benefits to non-payers (tolls, dues, tickets) makes contributions easier to elicit if the benefit is highly valued. The term itself is not used here (p. 269, n. 5)
- [[production-function]] (new) — how many contributors the group goal requires (p. 270)
- [[focal-point]] (new) — a salient outcome ("everyone contributes") on which expectations converge (Schelling) (p. 272)
- [[equilibrium-trap]] (new) — the "nobody contributes" equilibrium that all would prefer to leave (p. 271)
- [[tipping-point-k]] (new) — the number of contributors $k$ needed. Pressure to contribute rises with $k/n$ (pp. 273–274)
- [[coordination-nonuniqueness]] (new) — several equally good group goals, so the problem is which one to pick (p. 276)
- [[conflict-of-interest-in-coordination]] (new) — members rank the group's alternative goals differently (p. 279)
- [[pluralist-group-theory]] (new) — the view that common interest naturally yields organized groups, which then support and oppose one another (p. 266)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] $k$-of-$n$ contribution threshold ↔ political science: revolutionary thresholds and cascades — both make action depend on how many others act.
- [structural-analogy] Opera/ballet coordination with two equilibria ↔ institutions: rules that select among equally good outcomes — Shepsle says groups typically institutionalize a solution.
- [metaphor] Bentley's group vectors summing to a resultant ↔ spatial model of voting: policy as a point pushed in a space — the picture is similar, but the mathematics is not the same.
