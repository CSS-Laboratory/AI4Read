# Public Goods, Externalities, and the Commons (part 1/2): public goods and their political fixes

## L0 — One line
Public goods tend to be undersupplied because non-payers still enjoy them, so supplying them depends on political fixes, and every fix is itself political and imperfect.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why are goods that nobody can be excluded from, and whose use does not use them up, hard to supply? Shepsle argues that producers who cannot elicit payment are reluctant to supply the good, so it tends to be undersupplied. The remedies are political: coercive feudal dues, protected port monopolies, a dominant enforcer such as Saudi Arabia inside a cartel (sellers who restrict supply to raise price), or government supply. Public supply is itself a collective-action problem for consumers. Its politics is also about the producers who profit and about the incentives of legislators and bureaucrats. No remedy is ideal. Privatization, cap-and-trade and radio-licence allocation show that these choices have winners and losers decided in politics.

**Prerequisites:** free riding and collective action (earlier chapters); revenue = price × quantity; supply and demand (less supply, higher price).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Goods are classified by two properties, excludability and jointness of supply. Public goods lack both private-good properties. (p. 307, p. 309)
- Because non-payers cannot be excluded, a producer who cannot elicit payment is reluctant to supply the good, so it tends to be undersupplied. This is why political arrangements are substituted for market exchange. (p. 309)
- Coase's lighthouse case: private lighthouses existed in England, paid for by tie-in sales (lighthouse fees bundled with port charges). This worked only because a monopoly position was politically protected. (pp. 310–311)
- A cartel's higher price is a public good for its members. Cheating threatens it, and OPEC lasted as long as it did because Saudi Arabia enforced quotas. (pp. 312–313)
- Public supply is not automatic. Getting government to supply a public good is itself a collective-action problem (a group benefit that each individual is tempted to leave to others). (p. 315)
- Consumption and production differ: public goods are usually produced with substantial private input. Producers are less likely than consumers to be plagued by collective-action problems (the defense industry is Olson's "privileged groups", able to overcome them), so they are likely to lobby hard. (pp. 316–318)
- Public provision distorts incentives: legislators want local dollars, and short time horizons truncate research. (pp. 320–321)
- Fixes include monopoly privilege, antitrust waivers (exemption from laws against price-fixing), subsidy, public provision, privatization, cap-and-trade (tradable pollution permits) and property rights. None is ideal, and all are political. (pp. 321–324)

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 306).** Social dilemmas arising from the properties of goods are closely linked to cooperation and collective action. Markets are human constructions that need political understandings and institutions. Politics can break markets, but "politics makes markets, too" (p. 306).

**Defining terms (pp. 306–309).**
- Private goods have two properties: *excludability* (the owner may keep others from enjoying the good) and *solitary supply* (use depletes the good). Toothpaste is the example (p. 307).
- Public goods lack both. They are *nonexcludable* (anyone can enjoy them whether or not she paid) and *jointly supplied*, also called nonrivalrous (one person's use does not reduce what others get). Examples: national defense, lighthouse services (p. 307).
- Defense example: a feudal lord's castle and knights deter robbers within about twenty miles. Residents benefit whether or not they pay dues. The service is deterrence, which cannot be parcelled out very effectively to some and denied to others (pp. 307–308). Robbery is only made less frequent, not abolished (fn. 3, p. 307).
- Mixed cases (Display 10.1, p. 309):

| | Excludable: yes | Excludable: no |
|---|---|---|
| **Jointly supplied: yes** | Madonna concert | Public goods: defense, lighthouse |
| **Jointly supplied: no** | Private goods: toothpaste, BMW 320i | Crowded Cape Cod beach |

  A concert is excludable by walls and turnstiles. A beach is nonexcludable but crowded, so use by more families lowers others' pleasure (p. 308). Joint supply fails if crowding beyond some limit affects quality (fn. 4, p. 308). The author focuses on the two ideal types (p. 308).

*Intuition note:* "Public" here is about two properties of consumption, not about who owns or pays for the good. Check each good by two questions: can I keep non-payers out, and does their use leave less for others?

**Public goods and politics (pp. 309–314).**
- Because a public good is nonexcludable, it can be enjoyed without paying. A producer who cannot elicit payment is reluctant to supply it. Even an imperfect way to extract payment would likely give much less than direct payment would. Everyone is worse off (p. 309).
- Feudal case: peasants may well be willing to pay something for protection, certainly as much as guarding themselves would cost them. If the lord cannot elicit payment, he is less disposed to provide protection, or very much of it. Feudal institutions coerced labor, a younger son and crops, so politics (coercion) substituted for market exchange (pp. 309–310).
- **Lighthouses.** The textbook claim is that a lighthouse warns every passing ship (nonexcludable) without being used up (joint), so no ship will willingly pay, and a private firm that cannot earn a normal return will not be inclined to invest (p. 310). Coase showed that in England, at least, private entrepreneurs provided lighthouses along the western coast (p. 310). The lighthouses stood near harbors, and the ships that needed them were mainly those coming into port. Ships not landing stayed farther out. So users could be roughly distinguished from non-users (pp. 310–311).
- The payment mechanism was a tie-in sale: a monopolist of the waterfront priced lighthouse services together with docking, wharf and warehouse use (p. 311). The monopoly is crucial. If there were many wharf owners, competitors would undercut the tie-in price. So the entrepreneur must enjoy politically protected monopoly (p. 311).
- Footnote 6: the wharf owners might jointly finance a lighthouse themselves. Today that would be price-fixing collusion and a violation of antitrust law, so an antitrust waiver would also be a political necessity (p. 311).
- **OPEC (1970s).** Let $P_c, Q_c$ be the competitive price and quantity, with revenue $R_c = P_c \times Q_c$. If the cartel restricts quantity to $Q_{opec} < Q_c$, the price rises to $P_{opec} > P_c$, and $R_{opec} = P_{opec} \times Q_{opec}$. Under 1970s conditions some pair $(Q_{opec}, P_{opec})$ gave $R_{opec} > R_c$ (p. 312). Members need quotas that add up to $Q_{opec}$ and must honor them (p. 312).
- The higher price is a public good for OPEC, and a public bad for everyone else. It is nonexcludable (every exporter, member or not, gets the prevailing price). It is jointly supplied (one supplier selling at that price does not deny it to another) (p. 312).
- Cheating: if each member's extra output is too small to move the price, it sells above quota at the high price. If all cheat, supply rises, price and revenue fall, and each member has incentives to begin a further round of cheating. The cartel unravels (pp. 312–313).
- Why OPEC lasted a while: Saudi Arabia was much larger than the other members. It enforced quotas with carrots and sticks. It also had the most to gain and wanted to be dominant in the Arab world (p. 313). Fn. 7 cites Alt, Calvert and Humes (1988) for a strategic analysis (p. 313).
- Summary: public goods are underproduced because private incentives conflict with what production needs. They are produced only if contributions can be assured, and the solution is political (pp. 313–314).

*Intuition note:* In each case (feudal lord, lighthouse, cartel) the author describes a political solution: coercive dues, protected monopoly, or a dominant member who takes on enforcement. Ordinary market exchange handles these problems poorly.

**Public supply (pp. 314–323).**
- Governments commonly provide lighthouses, police, army, courts, utilities, health and roads. The state can use its authority to require payment, via taxes or user charges (pp. 314–315). Footnote 8: publicly provided goods are not always public goods. Mail delivery could be provided by the market. Publicly provided goods reflect the political advantages of interests (p. 315).
- **Paradox.** Public provision needs political pressure: bills, appropriations, agencies. But the benefit is widely spread and does not depend on having contributed to the lobbying. So why lobby rather than free-ride? Public supply therefore becomes a collective-action problem (p. 315).
- **Less conventional answer: consumption vs. production.** Public or private status concerns consumption properties. Public goods are usually produced with substantial private input (p. 316). Example: the interstate highway system, begun in 1956, was financed and specified by government but built by private contractors (p. 316). A construction contract is excludable and solitarily supplied, so highway building is a private good in production (pp. 316–317).
- Who lobbies? Consumer groups (AAA, Truckers) and political entrepreneurs (legislators of transshipment districts such as Chicago and Denver) probably played a part (p. 317). Those who gain most directly, and are less plagued by collective-action problems, are producers: concrete makers, contractors, equipment makers, owners of rights-of-way (p. 317). So the politics of public supply is "as much about the production of public goods as it is about their consumption" (p. 317). Consumers' interests often stay latent (p. 317).
- Defense: the "military-industrial complex" are Olson's "privileged groups", able to overcome collective action and motivated by profit (p. 318). Example (2009): President Obama sought to eliminate production of some F-22 fighters. Senator McCain supported him, but most legislators fought it, because businesses in more than forty states and three hundred districts held subcontracts. Unions and local officials joined the lobby (p. 318).
- **Complaints about public supply** (p. 318): the worry is that public actors lack good incentives ("who will guard the guardians"). The question is how well government does the job, not whether it can.
- Example: scientific knowledge such as $e = mc^2$ is a public good to the extent it cannot be patented or copyrighted (pp. 318–319). Applied research is often secret and patentable, so, to the degree that property rights can be assigned, it is essentially a private good. Basic research is not patentable and tends to be underproduced privately, so agencies such as NSF, NIH, NASA, DOE and DoD fund it (p. 319).
- *Distortion 1: constituency.* Merit-based NSF funding would concentrate money in a few pockets of excellence. Most legislators' districts would do poorly and would not be disposed toward NSF (p. 320). Administrators foresee this and create programs in which less-endowed areas compete, such as science education (p. 320). Fn. 10: arts funding would be even more concentrated (p. 320).
- *Distortion 2: time horizons.* Early research phases are cheap and invisible. Politicians prefer visible big facilities, so pressure truncates the long incubation of research into a shorter horizon (p. 321).
- Conclusion on fixes: monopoly privilege, antitrust waivers, subsidy of private production and outright public provision each give extraordinary privilege or authority to someone whose incentives may not match the social aim (lord: prestige; firm: profits; bureaucrat: turf and budget). The public good is the "incidental by-product" (pp. 321–322); the author calls this "perhaps an overly cynical view" (p. 322).
- Experiments: *privatization* (selling state assets), which may come with technology that reduces "publicness" (p. 322). Fn. 12: electronic lighthouses received only by buyers of a detector; cable boxes; metered household water; metered effluent (p. 322). *Cap-and-trade* (Obama initiative): set a pollution target, issue permits (given or auctioned), allow use or trade, so the right to pollute goes to those who most need polluting technology (pp. 322–323). *Deregulation* relaxes bureaucratic command and control. All are political, with winners and losers decided in political arenas (p. 323).

*Intuition note:* Government supply moves the free-rider problem rather than removing it. The people with the strongest incentive to push the good through are often the ones who make money producing it.

**Case 10.1: Public goods, property rights, and the radio spectrum (pp. 323–324).**
- The radio spectrum is nonexcludable and jointly supplied. But simultaneous broadcasts on the same or neighboring frequencies interfere, so allocation is needed (p. 323).
- The FCC allocates frequencies and gives exclusive rights to particular ones. Broadcasting without rights is illegal. Property rights make the good excludable and prevent crowding (pp. 323–324).
- The FCC has historically given licenses free, by application or lottery. The Clinton administration explored auctions and at one point predicted $4.4 billion over four years (p. 324). The recurring questions are political: who benefits, who is hurt, is it fair, which values guide management of public resources (p. 324).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two oil producers; competitive total output is 10 units at price 10, so revenue is $10 \times 10 = 100$. Assume price depends only on total output: total 8 → price 14; total 9 → 12; total 10 → 10. Quota: each produces 4. Total revenue is $8 \times 14 = 112 > 100$, so each gets $4 \times 14 = 56$.

If A cheats with 5 units while B keeps 4: total 9, price 12. A gets $5 \times 12 = 60 > 56$. B gets $4 \times 12 = 48$.

If both cheat with 5 each: total 10, price 10, each gets $5 \times 10 = 50$. If A honors (4) while B cheats (5): total 9, price 12, so A gets $4 \times 12 = 48$, less than 50.

So whatever B does, A earns more by cheating (60 > 56 and 50 > 48). Both cheat and each gets 50 < 56. The high price (a public good for both) collapses unless some enforcer, like Saudi Arabia, makes honoring the quota worthwhile.

## Concepts
- [[public-good]] — nonexcludable and jointly supplied (nonrivalrous) good (p. 307)
- [[free-rider]] — enjoys a good, or a cartel's price, without paying or complying; here shown by cheating on quotas and by not lobbying (pp. 309, 313, 315)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — public supply of public goods itself needs collective action among individuals and groups (p. 315)
- [[excludability]] (new) — the owner can keep others from enjoying the good (p. 307)
- [[jointness-of-supply]] (new) — also called nonrivalry. One person's use does not diminish what is available to others (p. 307)
- [[tie-in-sale]] (new) — pricing lighthouse services together with port services to collect payment (p. 311)
- [[privileged-group]] (new) — Olson's term for groups able to overcome collective action and with an incentive to do so (p. 318)
- [[public-provision]] (new) — government supplies public goods. Public goods and publicly provided goods are not the same (pp. 314–315, fn. 8)
- [[production-vs-consumption]] (new) — publicness concerns consumption. Production is mostly private and has its own politics (p. 316)
- [[privatization]] (new) — selling state assets to the private sector, sometimes using technology that reduces publicness (p. 322)

## Canonical instances
- [[feudal-protection]] — lord's deterrence as a public good, paid by coerced dues (pp. 307–310)
- [[coase-lighthouse]] — private English lighthouses financed by a tie-in sale and protected monopoly (pp. 310–311)
- [[opec-cartel]] — higher price as a public good for the cartel, with Saudi Arabia as enforcer (pp. 312–313)
- [[interstate-highway-system]] — public in consumption, private in production; producers lobby (pp. 316–317)
- [[f22-production-lobby]] — subcontractors across states resist ending a weapon program (p. 318)
- [[nsf-basic-research]] — merit vs. constituency incentives and short horizons (pp. 319–321)
- [[radio-spectrum-allocation]] — property rights make a public good excludable (pp. 323–324)
- [[cap-and-trade]] — tradable pollution permits (pp. 322–323)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] OPEC quota cheating ↔ collective action: free-riding on a jointly enjoyed benefit unravels cooperation, as in the earlier chapters.
- [structural-analogy] Saudi Arabia enforcing quotas ↔ selective incentive: carrots and sticks given to members to induce compliance.
- [structural-analogy] Consumer vs. producer lobbying ↔ Olson's group theory: producer groups are profit-driven and able to overcome collective action, so they are "privileged".
- [metaphor] NSF spreading grants to districts ↔ principal–agent problems: officials respond to their own survival incentives.

## Questions this raises
- Could the wharf owners have jointly financed a lighthouse without a monopoly or antitrust waiver (fn. 6)? What would hold them together?
- How far do technologies such as meters or cable boxes make a public good excludable, and who gains from that?
- If producers lobby effectively, how can we tell whether public provision serves consumers or mainly producers?
- Which fixes (monopoly, subsidy, public provision, privatization) have the least damaging incentive distortions?
