# Shepsle, K. A. (2010). *Analyzing Politics: Rationality, Behavior, and Institutions* (2nd ed.). New York: W. W. Norton. 560 pp.

## In one paragraph
Shepsle teaches political science as explanation, not just description or judgment, using simple models built on rational choice. Individuals have preferences and beliefs and act to serve them. Groups are not like individuals, though. Majority rule can cycle, and Arrow's Theorem shows that no reasonable voting method avoids this problem entirely. So group outcomes depend on procedures and on who controls the agenda. Large groups also struggle to cooperate: free-riding means public goods are undersupplied and externalities and commons problems go uncorrected. Societies respond by building institutions, based on division of labor, jurisdictions, and delegation with monitoring. Shepsle applies this to legislatures, bureaucracies, leaders, courts and cabinets. The method is meant to travel to any group setting, "small p" as well as "big P" politics.

## The path through the work
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch01-it]] — Shepsle argues that the study of politics has moved from describing and judging toward explaining and analyzing, and that this book teaches simple models for doing so.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch02-rationality]] — In Shepsle's model, a rational person has complete, transitive preferences and beliefs about how actions lead to outcomes, and picks the action that serves them best (under risk, the action with the highest expected utility).
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch03-getting]] — Even when every member has a complete, transitive preference ordering and votes sincerely, majority voting can give a cycling group preference, so the voting procedure (for example who sets the agenda) can decide the outcome.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch04-group]] — Even when every individual has consistent preferences, majority rule can produce a cycling group preference. Arrow's Theorem shows this is a general trade-off between coherence and fairness, which restricting the domain of preferences (for example to single-peaked ones) can ease.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch05-spatial-p1]] — Majority rule gives a stable choice (an empty-winset point) when preferences are single-peaked on a line, or radially symmetric in more dimensions, but in multidimensional spaces almost any other arrangement produces chaos.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch05-spatial-p2]] — Once preferences sit on a line, majority rule pulls outcomes toward the median ideal point, and institutional rules (agenda power, amendment rules, dimension-by-dimension voting) decide how far and how reliably.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch05-spatial-p3]] — With one dimension, pure majority rule and single-peaked preferences, the median participant's ideal point attracts outcomes; in one-dimensional legislatures, procedural rules limit which outcomes are possible; and the author reports that a classroom experiment pretty much matches the Plott and McKelvey theorems.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch06-strategic]] — Rational actors look ahead and reason backward from the end of a process, so they may vote or act against their sincere preference now to get a better final outcome.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch07-voting]] — With the same voters and preferences, different ways of casting and counting votes can produce different outcomes, so a group's choice depends on the method as well as on preferences, and it is hard to say what the group's "will" is.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch08-cooperation]] — Self-interested people can capture the gains from cooperation without internalized morals or a Leviathan (Hobbes's all-powerful enforcer) when their relationship is ongoing with no known end; internalized values and third-party enforcers can also produce cooperation by changing the payoffs, though each has dangers.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch09-collective-p1]] — Mass action is puzzling because one person's contribution is tiny and costly, and Shepsle shows how the number of contributors needed ($k$ out of $n$), the cost, the presence of several good outcomes, and conflict of interest affect how likely a group is to cooperate.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch09-collective-p2]] — Large groups struggle to organize because individuals free-ride (enjoy benefits without contributing), so groups can be sustained through selective incentives, entrepreneurs or experiential motives. Voting shows the same problem: in a mass election, purely instrumental calculation says most sensible people should abstain.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch10-public-p1]] — Public goods tend to be undersupplied because non-payers still enjoy them, so supplying them depends on political fixes, and every fix is itself political and imperfect.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch10-public-p2]] — Externalities and commons problems are public-good problems in which private and social incentives clash, and every fix (taxes, regulation, property rights, group rules) depends on politics.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch11-institutions]] — Institutions turn recurring problems into routines through divided, specialized, jurisdiction-based labor with delegation and monitoring, so results do not depend only on the quality of the people involved.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch12-legislatures]] — Legislators with different goals need cooperation, and committees with delegated agenda powers, kept in check by assignment rules, amendment rules and leaders, are, the author argues, one institutional way a legislature gets it.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch13-bureaucracy]] — Whether bureaucrats or politicians have the upper hand depends on the model: Niskanen gives bureaus an information advantage, Miller–Moe give rough bargaining parity, and McNollgast treat bureaus as agents of politicians, who can anticipate bureaucratic drift and try to reduce it.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch14-leadership]] — Leaders can be seen as agents of followers whose support they need; they also steer the agenda, seize opportunities, and may build a reputation for sanctioning rebels.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch15-courts]] — Shepsle argues that higher-court judges can be modelled as "legislators in robes" with policy preferences, whose influence often works through other actors' anticipation of their reactions rather than through visible rulings.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch16-cabinet]] — In parliamentary democracies, parliament chooses a government (a package of ministries), and a spatial example illustrates how rational parties can reach stable governments, even minority ones.
- [[shepsle-2010-analyzing/ch17-final]] — Shepsle closes by claiming that the rational-choice method of analysing politics is portable: it applies to any group setting, private or public, and across many disciplines.

## Big ideas across units
- **Explanation through simple models.** Politics is studied by stylized models, with rationality as the basic building block. Units: ch01, ch02, ch17.
- **Rational individuals do not make a rational group.** Majority cycles, Arrow's Theorem, and the limits of restricting preferences (single-peakedness, radial symmetry) are central. Units: ch03, ch04, ch05-p1, ch07, ch17.
- **Procedure and institutions shape outcomes.** The same preferences can give different results under different rules, such as agenda procedures, open or closed rules, and voting methods. Units: ch03, ch05-p2, ch05-p3, ch07, ch11, ch12, ch17.
- **Agenda control is power.** When majorities cycle, whoever controls the agenda or the sequence of votes can steer the result. Units: ch03, ch04, ch05-p1, ch05-p2, ch06, ch12, ch14.
- **Foresight and strategy.** Actors reason ahead, so observed votes or events cannot be read at face value. Units: ch06, ch07, ch13, ch14, ch15.
- **Cooperation and collective action are hard.** Free-riding leads to undersupplied public goods and mishandled externalities and commons. Selective incentives, entrepreneurs, repeated play, enforcers and group rules are partial answers. Units: ch08, ch09-p1, ch09-p2, ch10-p1, ch10-p2, ch17.
- **Delegation creates principal–agent problems.** Specialization brings expertise and also a control problem, which shapes committees, bureaus, leaders, courts and cabinets. Units: ch11, ch12, ch13, ch14, ch15, ch16.
- **Median positions and stability in space.** The median ideal point attracts outcomes in one dimension, and empty-winset points mark stable choices. These ideas return in institutional settings. Units: ch05-p1, ch05-p2, ch05-p3, ch15, ch16.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch02 (the model of choice), ch03–ch04 (group choice and Arrow), and ch05 (spatial models and winsets). The catalog marks ch03–04 and the institutions chapters ch05, ch06, ch11–13 and ch15 as the focus. Ch06 (strategic behavior) is needed for the later institutional arguments.
- **Dependencies:** ch03 needs the preference ordering from ch02. Ch04 needs ch02 and ch03. Ch05 needs ch04 (universal domain, cycles). Ch06 needs ch05. Ch09 needs ch08 (the Prisoners' Dilemma and equilibrium). Ch10 needs ch08 and ch09. Ch11 needs Part III (public goods, collective action). Ch12 needs ch05, ch11 and the principal–agent idea. Ch13 needs ch11 and ch12. Ch14 needs ch13, ch09 and the agenda material. Ch15 needs ch05 and ch13. Ch16 needs ch05, ch07 and the principal–agent idea.
- **Can be skipped on a first pass:** ch05-p3 (the conclusion, the classroom experiment and the problems), the electoral-system detail in ch07, and ch14 (leadership). The institutional applications in ch10, ch16 and ch17 can also wait, although the core of ch17 is only a short recap.
