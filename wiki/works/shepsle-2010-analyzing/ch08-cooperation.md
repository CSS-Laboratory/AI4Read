# Cooperation (two-person case)

## L0 — One line
Self-interested people can capture the gains from cooperation without internalized morals or a Leviathan (Hobbes's all-powerful enforcer) when their relationship is ongoing with no known end; internalized values and third-party enforcers can also produce cooperation by changing the payoffs, though each has dangers.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shepsle asks how people can cooperate when each gains by letting the other pay. In a one-shot Prisoners' Dilemma, such as Hume's marsh-draining game, not cooperating is a dominant strategy, so both end worse off. Cooperation can still arise in three ways. Repeated play with no known end can reward conditional cooperation (tit-for-tat: copy the other's last move). Internalized values and external enforcers change the payoffs. Each route has a danger: tit-for-tat can echo a bad start into feuds, values often fail, and enforcers have their own incentives. Lab experiments show cooperation above zero but far below one, which Shepsle links to altruism and trust. The chapter treats two-person cooperation as a building block for larger groups.

**Prerequisites:** reading a 2×2 payoff matrix; dominant strategy; the idea of an equilibrium (no one gains by changing alone).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Hobbes's state of nature is a world with no cooperation. Effort spent on self-protection is necessary but becomes wasteful if people restrain themselves or if social mechanisms restrain them, and the chapter asks whether a third way exists besides morals and a Leviathan (an entity that commands, detects and punishes) (pp. 233–234).
- Hume's marsh game (the Prisoners' Dilemma) gives each farmer a dominant strategy not to cooperate. Rational individual choice then yields an outcome both disprefer: the "paradox of cooperation" (pp. 237–238).
- Shepsle presents the Cold War nuclear standoff and pork-barrel politics as further instances of the paradox of cooperation (pp. 239–241).
- If the number of repetitions is finite and commonly known, the logic is that play unravels back to one-shot play (Shepsle finds this less compelling when the number is very large). Cooperation can sometimes pay when the end is unknown (pp. 242–244).
- Tit-for-tat (cooperate first, then copy the other's last move) can be read as a norm of reciprocity resting on self-interest, but it can also echo a bad start into feuding (pp. 244–245).
- Internalized values (for example the Mafia code of silence, omerta) and third-party enforcement both change the payoff matrix. Enforcers face their own incentives, so the question of who checks the enforcers arises (pp. 247–252).
- Experiments show cooperation in one-shot and finitely repeated PDs is above 0 and well below 1. Dictator and trust games measure altruism and trust separately (pp. 254–259).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction (p. 231).** Chapters 8–10 look at what people do in groups. Cooperation is group activity "in the small"; collective action is its analog "in the large" (Ch. 9). Ch. 10 links both to public goods. Shepsle seeks a middle ground between psychology's "isolated individualism" and sociology's "groupthink" (p. 231).

**A world with no cooperation (pp. 232–234).**
- American "rugged individualism" was qualified by faith in families and in neighborliness (barn-raisings, volunteer fire departments, posses). Modern urban life is often portrayed as lacking this spirit, though Shepsle calls it only a pale approximation of a world of no cooperation (p. 232).
- Hobbes's state of nature: life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." People must guard against nature and against each other (p. 233).
- Protection effort is necessary but is wasteful in either of two cases: it could be redirected to production if people restrained themselves (moral principles) or if social mechanisms restrained them (civil society) (p. 233).
- Shepsle doubts values suffice. Value systems conflict and have probably killed many people. Scarcity breeds conflict (pp. 233–234).
- Most have bet on civil society: a command backed by the capacity to detect and punish. Hobbes named this entity Leviathan, the solution to the "problem of order" (p. 234).
- Shepsle questions the dichotomy between internalized values and Leviathan and promises a third alternative. Cooperation may emerge and persist without either, through "ruthless self-interest" (p. 234).

*Intuition note:* Protection effort is wasteful when everyone must guard against everyone. The chapter asks whether cooperation can arise without internalized morals or a Leviathan.

**The simplest case: two-person cooperation (pp. 235–238).**
- Individual choices weigh private costs and benefits. Group goods often have non-private benefits (p. 235).
- Hume's two farmers share a marsh. Draining it benefits both (e.g., no mosquitos). Each values it at 2 utiles (a made-up unit of value, p. 236, n. 1). Working alone costs 3. Working together costs each 1 (increasing returns) (pp. 235–236).
- Payoffs (Display 8.1; A's payoff first):

| | B drains | B does not |
|---|---|---|
| **A drains** | 1, 1 | −1, 2 |
| **A does not** | 2, −1 | 0, 0 |

- Reasoning for A: if B drains, A gets 1 by draining and 2 by not draining. If B does not, A gets −1 by draining and 0 by not draining. So not draining is dominant (pp. 236–237). B's reasoning is the same (p. 237).
- A second reason: B is likely not to drain, which reinforces A's own incentive (p. 237).
- Result: neither drains, each gets 0, yet each could have got 1. This is the "paradox of cooperation", which Shepsle also calls the rational-individual/irrational-society conundrum in another form (p. 238).
- Footnote 3: the matrix is the Prisoners' Dilemma. Both silent: payoff 1. Both squeal: 0. One squeals: squealer 2, other −1 (p. 236).
- Hume's corn farmers: each says he has no kindness for the other and no reason to expect gratitude, so neither helps, and both lose their harvest for want of mutual confidence and security (p. 238).

**Case 8.1 (pp. 239–241).**
- Cold War payoffs (US, USSR): both maintain 0,0; both disarm 10,10; one disarms: the other gets 100 and the disarmer −100. Each prefers to maintain whatever the other does. Footnote: actual disarmament came in "baby steps," not all-or-nothing (pp. 239–240).
- Pork-barreling: each legislator gains by targeting projects to their own district. All would be better off with no pork, but the "cooperative dividend" is not stable (p. 241).

**Two-person cooperation with repeat play (pp. 241–245).**
- Display 8.1 is a one-shot game. Real societies have repeated encounters (pp. 241–242).
- If the game is played exactly twice and both know it, the second play is one-shot, so both defect. Then the first play is effectively last, so both defect. Generally, a finite, commonly known number of plays unravels into a string of one-shot games (pp. 242–243).
- Footnote 5: in Shepsle's opinion the logic is quite compelling if the number is small. If it is very large, it becomes less compelling, and it seems to him that players have a strong incentive to seek ways of pretending their rationality is disabled. He calls this a complicated question in game theory (p. 243, n. 5).
- Real societies do not know when they end, so players may act as if the society is unending. Each may risk cooperating at first: the worst case is being exploited once and then refusing to cooperate. If both take the chance, mutual cooperation gives 1 utile and may encourage each to try again. This is the "shadow of the future" (pp. 243–244).
- Axelrod's tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then do what the other did last time. It is conditional cooperation (p. 244).
- Shepsle says it is a tiny step from farmers playing tit-for-tat in repeated play to the claim that a norm of reciprocity exists. The farmers have not internalized a principle like the Golden Rule, and no Leviathan makes them cooperate (p. 244).
- The "evil twin": if the relationship starts badly, tit-for-tat echoes the failure. It would look more like a blood feud or civil war, and Shepsle says many ethnic, tribal, racial and interpersonal hostilities look like tit-for-tat run out of control (pp. 244–245).
- Conclusion: cooperation dividends can be captured as a rational response to circumstances. Dogma or enforcers may reinforce this, but Shepsle believes they would have a much harder time if they could not rely on the cooperators' self-interest (p. 245).

*Intuition note:* Cooperation by tit-for-tat is a bargain with the future. It may work when the prospect of ongoing cooperation is worth more than the gain from cheating once (p. 253). The same rule can echo hostility if the relationship starts badly (p. 244).

**Alternative mechanisms (pp. 245–252).**

*I. Internalized values (pp. 245–247).*
- People do internalize cooperative values, which may make them "nice" at first meetings so reciprocity can start. But this does not explain why one value set rather than another is internalized, and values often fail (pp. 245–246).
- Mafia example: soldiers who swore omerta. Shepsle says the payoffs of Display 8.1 are wrong for them, and gives Display 8.2 (A first; B is a very large number):

| | B: don't squeal | B: squeal |
|---|---|---|
| **A: don't squeal** | 1, 1 | −1, −B |
| **A: squeal** | −B, −1 | 0, 0 |

- Only one payoff in each off-diagonal cell changes: the squealer's (p. 246).
- Two equilibria: both squeal (0 each) or neither squeals (1 each). An equilibrium here means neither player gains by changing alone if the other holds firm (pp. 246–247).
- In Shepsle's view, game theory gives too little psychological weight to −B. He suspects neither soldier would risk being the only one to squeal, and says omerta frequently wins out. Internalized values, he concludes, can produce cooperation even in one-shot games, but they do so by changing the game (p. 247).

*II. External enforcement (pp. 247–252).*
- When it is explicitly someone else changing the payoffs, Shepsle says it is probably more appropriate to call this external enforcement. Footnote 9: the two mechanisms may not always be distinct, since people anticipate sanctions and act as if they had internalized the value (pp. 247–248).
- Third-party enforcement is the principal mechanism of neoclassical economics, usually assumed costless and error-free (p. 248).
- Seller example: a guarantee alone may not be credible, since the seller may disappear or dispute the terms. A third party who can make the seller deliver makes the promise credible, so the buyer might well be prepared to buy (pp. 248–249). Footnote 10: without enforcement, a buyer may still buy but demands a lower price. That price gap is like an insurance premium (p. 249).
- In the Mafia case with the payoffs of Display 8.1, the Don's enforcement transforms 8.1 into 8.2. As long as both mafiosi believe they are playing Display 8.2, the Don never has to display his might; to those ignorant of the Don's existence, the prisoners might seem simply honest, or it might be thought that there is "honor among thieves" (p. 249).
- Shepsle says the enforcer must be examined on three matters. The text lists "costly enforcement," and the rest of the list is not in the pages available (p. 250).
- Surviving fragment: enforcers may favor their own (a traffic officer lets a brother-in-law off but nails a "radical lawyer"; a rabbi favors his son) (p. 252).
- Enforcers are vulnerable to "inappropriate incentives." The state is the community's enforcer, but its monopoly of force can be used for officials' own purposes, which prompts the saying "Who will guard the guardians?" The visible text breaks off mid-sentence after this (p. 252).

*Intuition note:* Values and enforcers change the game rather than leaving the original one in place; they make defecting costlier. An enforcer's power may itself be used for the enforcer's own purposes (p. 252).

**Preliminary conclusion (p. 253).** The two-person world is not always happy. Internalized values and enforcers offer promise but have dangers. Repetition may allow cooperation to develop, because long-term relationships may be too valuable to jeopardize by cheating, but it has a darker side of jealousy, feuding and revenge. Humankind has left the state of nature "only imperfectly." The two-person society is a module for the larger society treated in the next chapter (p. 253).

**Experimental Corner: altruism and trust in the PD (pp. 253–259).**
- PD experiments: Minas et al. (1960) found 38% of players cooperated and 62% defected. Mutual cooperation occurred in about 16% of plays. Results elsewhere varied with payoffs, rounds, communication, gender and nationality (p. 254).
- Cooperation in one-shot and finitely repeated PDs is consistently above zero (against equilibrium prediction) but far below one. Why? Altruism and trust are two candidates (pp. 254–255).
- Shepsle says PD experiments are the wrong way to ascertain the relative importance of altruism and trust (a defection could reflect either), so separate games were created (pp. 255–256).
- Dictator game (altruism, no trust needed): A splits a budget with passive B. Forsythe et al.: with $10, average gift was $2.33. 21% gave $0 and 21% gave $5; the rest gave $1–$4. Other tests (higher stakes, varied anonymity): average gifts are 15–30%. Selfish and generous types exist across societies (p. 256).
- Footnote c: Henrich et al. (15 societies) found 0% most common only among US college students. Among the Orma in Kenya the most common offer was 50% (p. 257).
- Andreoni and Miller multiply the gift by a factor p (between 1/3 and 3). About 40% were selfish, 25% fairness-seekers, 11% welfare-maximizers, and 24% idiosyncratic (pp. 257–258).
- Trust game: A can invest money with B (it grows), and B decides how much to return. Croson and Buchan: an average of 67% invested in international experiments; US experiments have generally found lower rates, nearer 50%. No significant gender difference in trust, but women returned more (p. 258, n. f).
- Shepsle's reading: many people are fairly giving but a fair number are not, and trust appears quite widespread even among anonymous strangers; together these help explain PD cooperation above theory's prediction, though still only 16% (p. 259).

**Problems (pp. 259–261).** Applications of the marsh game and repeat play; the Stag Hunt; a coordination game; rewarding cooperation; institutions that foster cooperation (see Exercises).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Use Display 8.1 payoffs. Suppose A plays tit-for-tat and B knows it. Suppose the game lasts n rounds; we only compare two plans for B and do not claim they are B's only options.
- B always cooperates: 1 per round, total n × 1 = n.
- B defects in round 1, then always: round 1 gives B 2 (A cooperated). Then A defects from round 2, so each later round is (0, 0). Total = 2 + 0 = 2.
- Comparison: n > 2 means cooperating is better. With n = 3, cooperation gives 3 and defecting gives 2. With n = 1 (one-shot), 1 < 2, so defection wins.
- The unraveling in a known, finite game is a different argument and is not shown here (p. 243).

## Concepts
- [[collective action problem]] — the "in the large" analog of two-person cooperation (Ch. 9; p. 231); in the two-person case here, gains from cooperation can go uncaptured because each prefers the other pays (pp. 238, 253)
- [[free rider]] — the farmer who lets the other drain the marsh and gets payoff 2 at no cost (p. 236) (loose use; Shepsle does not use the term here)
- [[prisoners-dilemma]] (new) — a payoff structure where not cooperating is dominant yet mutual cooperation beats mutual non-cooperation (pp. 236–238)
- [[dominant-strategy]] (new) — a strategy giving a higher payoff whatever the other does (p. 237)
- [[nash-equilibrium]] (new) — an outcome where neither player gains by changing alone (p. 246)
- [[paradox of cooperation]] (new) — rational individuals each choose not to cooperate and so both lose (p. 238)
- [[tit-for-tat]] (new) — cooperate first, then copy the other's last move (p. 244)
- [[shadow of the future]] (new) — the prospect of future dividends makes cooperating now attractive (p. 244)
- [[norm of reciprocity]] (new) — behavior of repaying cooperation with cooperation, here arising from self-interest (p. 244)
- [[third-party enforcement]] (new) — an outsider imposes sanctions so promises become credible (pp. 248–249)
- [[institutions]] — the state and enforcers as Hobbesian solutions to the problem of order (p. 234)
- [[altruism-and-trust]] (new) — two social values the PD combines; the dictator and trust games measure them (pp. 254–259)
