# It Isn't Rocket Science, but ...

## L0 — One line
Shepsle argues that the study of politics has moved from describing and judging toward explaining and analyzing, and that this book teaches simple models for doing so.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks what kind of political science this book offers. Shepsle says that at the end of World War II the field was mainly thick description and reformist judgment. Careful measurement later improved description, and only from the 1960s did systematic attention turn to "why" questions. The book teaches elementary models: stripped-down stylizations of real situations. Models become theories when abstract features are made concrete for a specific case. Part II treats individual and group choice, Part III collective action, and Part IV institutions. Shepsle also finds Easton's definition of politics useful but too narrow. He treats politics as group life in general, from the White House to the kitchen table. The aim is to help readers explain and predict political events.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond everyday knowledge of politics (Congress, parties, elections).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Two of the most significant shifts of emphasis since World War II, in Shepsle's account: from describing to explaining, and from judging to analyzing (p. 3).
- Thick description is careful and comprehensive but indiscriminate. Accumulated detail does not always add up to much more than a pile of facts (p. 4).
- Scholars and reformers of the period paid scant attention to why a practice existed or why a reform would work (p. 5).
- Careful measurement and systematic description are needed before empirical regularities can be identified. Robust regularities provoke curiosity and "why" questions; answers to "why" are explanations, and getting there requires analysis (pp. 5–6).
- A model is a deliberately simplified stylization. In Shepsle's usage, a theory is a model made concrete for a specific application (pp. 7, 9).
- The book builds up from individual choice to group choice, collective action and institutions (pp. 7–10).
- Politics is defined broadly as group life, so "small p" politics counts alongside "capital P" politics (pp. 11–12).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (p. 3).** Shepsle says the book pretends to be modest, but really isn't. He describes a "sea change" and concentrates on two of the most significant shifts of emphasis: describing → explaining, and judging → analyzing (p. 3).

**A Science of Politics? (pp. 3–6)**
- Popular press and coffee-break talk portray politicians as scoundrels. This talk is rich in description and unforgiving in judgment. Rarely is it more than storytelling and hand-wringing (pp. 3–4).
- At the end of World War II, political science was mainly detailed contemporary description plus political history. A major book on Congress was both a compendium of current practice and a history of how that practice evolved (p. 4).
- Geertz's term for this mode is *thick description*. Shepsle calls it typically careful, comprehensive and "detailed to a fault", but indiscriminate. Detail does not always add up to much more than a pile of facts (p. 4).
- Footnote 3: numbers, even a census, "do not speak for themselves" until analyzed (p. 4).
- The evaluative side was mostly reformist sentiment. The same books complained about the Speaker, the dominance of committees, the filibuster, lobbyists, and the majority's intolerance of the minority (pp. 4–5).
- Scholars and commentators gave attention neither to explaining the evils they described nor to defending how the proposed reforms would play out (p. 5). Example: committee history was codified without asking why legislatures use committees or why the division of labor takes its particular form (p. 5).
- Reformers also paid scant attention to four things: why the practice existed, why the legislature would adopt the reform, why the reform would cure the problem, and why the reformed arrangement would not itself be tinkered with or revert (p. 5).
- Over the next twenty years description improved: data collection, more precise measurement, and statistics for causal inference. But only from the 1960s did systematic attention turn to "why", which Shepsle calls the principal interrogative of science (p. 5).
- Shepsle sees a movement along a scientific trajectory: stories and anecdotes → thick description and history → systematic measurement → explanation and analysis (p. 5).
- Careful, systematic description is essential to scientific inquiry, and from it comes the possibility of identifying empirical regularities (p. 6). Regularities, especially robust ones that seem to recur often and under varied circumstances, pique curiosity. His examples (p. 6):
  - the incumbency effect;
  - Duverger's Law (proportional representation goes with more parties);
  - the "democratic peace" hypothesis;
  - Pacific Rim versus African and Latin American growth without conspicuous resource differences.
- He will not answer these questions here. He will focus on how to think through to answers. This moves political science closer in form to the physical and natural sciences (p. 6).

*Intuition note:* Measuring carefully tells you what happens. A "why" question asks for an explanation, and Shepsle says getting one requires analysis. His point is that careful description is needed first, but it is not itself explanation.

**Models and Theories (pp. 7–10)**
- The book's main purpose is to give theoretical tools, not specific details studied in other courses (p. 7).
- A *model* is a stylization that approximates some real situation "in very crude fashion". It is purposely stripped down because real events are often far too complicated to understand directly (p. 7). Footnote 4: a sphere standing for a planet or an atomic nucleus is an example (p. 7).
- Chapter 2: a model of choice in which a person is described only by wants and beliefs. It gives hunches about a generic individual (p. 7).
- Chapters 3–4 and the rest of Part II: only in Robinson Crusoe's world are choices made entirely in isolation, so the model expands to groups. Features are added step by step and intuitions grow more sophisticated (pp. 7–8).
- Part III: other kinds of groups that operate differently from the voting groups of Part II. Example: farmers with a mosquito-infested marsh. If others eradicate the mosquitoes, a non-cooperator benefits and pays nothing. If others fail, he bears a cost only if he would make a difference, "and maybe not even then" (p. 8). The question is why any farmer would cooperate. This calls for a model of the logic of participation and collective action (p. 8).
- Cases in point illustrate ideas. Chapter 2 uses politicians' risky career choices. Chapter 3 uses congressional leaders' maneuvers over tax policy in the Civil War, the Great Depression and the Reagan years (pp. 8–9). Shepsle admits these cases would not pass a historian's muster. He wants theoretical principles, not thick description (p. 9).
- A *theory*, in his usage, is an embellishment of a model. Abstract features become concrete and specific. A specific group, time and issue force choices such as group size, votes needed, amendability, who may move, and whether a defeated motion can be re-proposed (p. 9). Footnote 5: usage of "model" and "theory" varies among philosophers of science, and he claims no superiority (p. 9).
- Part IV comes closer to theory. It treats institutions, including Congress–bureaucracy relations and cabinet government in multiparty European parliaments (pp. 9–10).
- New in this edition: problem sets and discussion questions, and "Experimental Corner" sections describing experiments in economics and political science (p. 10).

*Intuition note:* A model is like a map: it leaves out most of the territory so that the main structure is visible. A theory is the same map redrawn for one particular place and time.

**Politics (pp. 10–12)**
- The goal is to give tools for analyzing political events. Shepsle believes that understanding and applying the book's concepts will help the reader predict and explain them (p. 10).
- The reader's job is to engage and apply the concepts. Shepsle *thinks* readers will then begin to see the world differently. They will understand why groups struggle to cooperate or decide, why people grumble without acting, and why some problems may be solvable and others not (p. 11).
- Easton defined politics as "the authoritative allocation of values for a society" (p. 11). Shepsle calls it useful, but says it leaves out more than he would like. Office politics is not "authoritative" and does not allocate values for a whole society, yet ordinary usage calls it politics (p. 11).
- Shepsle's own definition: politics is "utterly indistinguishable" from group life generally. It consists of individuals interacting, maneuvering, dissembling, strategizing and cooperating as they pursue whatever they pursue in group life (pp. 11–12).
- Benefit: it demystifies politics. It covers "capital P" politics (White House, Kremlin) and "small p" politics (faculty meeting, kitchen table). He admits some ambiguity and is content with it (p. 12).

*Intuition note:* Shepsle treats politics as individuals interacting in group life as they pursue whatever they pursue. So his subject includes both "capital P" and "small p" politics.

## Toy example
(Ours, not from the text; it illustrates how a stripped-down model turns a puzzle into an explanation, following the mosquito-marsh puzzle on p. 8.)

Three farmers. Eradicating the mosquitoes succeeds if at least 2 of the 3 pay a cost of 10 each. Each farmer gets a benefit of 15 if it succeeds. Look at farmer A.
- Both others pay: A pays → 15 − 10 = 5. A does not pay → 15 − 0 = 15. Free riding is better.
- Exactly one other pays: A pays → success, 15 − 10 = 5. A does not pay → failure, 0. Paying is better, because A makes the difference.
- No other pays: A pays → failure, 0 − 10 = −10. A does not pay → 0. Not paying is better.

The model keeps only wants (benefit, cost) and the success rule. Even so, it explains why cooperation is shaky: A pays only if exactly one other farmer pays.

## Concepts
- [[thick-description]] (new) — detailed, careful, comprehensive description, but indiscriminate; Geertz's term used by Shepsle (p. 4)
- [[empirical-regularity]] (new) — a pattern identified through systematic description; especially robust ones, which seem to recur often and under varied circumstances, provoke "why" questions (p. 6)
- [[explanation-vs-description]] (new) — description says what happens; explanation answers "why" and needs analysis (p. 5)
- [[model]] (new) — purposely stripped-down stylization approximating a real situation (p. 7)
- [[theory]] (new) — a model embellished with concrete, specific features for a particular application (p. 9)
- [[collective action problem]] — here the question why anyone cooperates when non-cooperators share the benefits (p. 8)
- [[institutions]] — a topic of Part IV; no definition is given in this unit (p. 9)
- [[politics-broad-definition]] (new) — politics as group life generally, not only Easton's "authoritative allocation of values" (pp. 11–12)

## Canonical instances

- [[duvergers-law]] — proportional representation goes with more parties than alternative voting arrangements, e.g. district systems where the top vote-getter wins (p. 6)
- [[democratic-peace]] — democracies rarely fight each other (p. 6)
