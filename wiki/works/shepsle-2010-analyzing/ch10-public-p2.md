# Public Goods, Externalities, and the Commons (part 2/2)

## L0 — One line
Externalities and commons problems are public-good problems in which private and social incentives clash, and every fix (taxes, regulation, property rights, group rules) depends on politics.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
When people ignore the effects of their actions on others (externalities) or overuse a shared resource (the commons), outcomes fall short of what is best for the community. Shepsle reviews three fixes for externalities: taxes and subsidies, regulation, and new property rights such as marketable pollution permits. Each has problems, because politics shapes what gets taxed and how permits are set, and control machinery may be abused. For commons, he cites Ostrom: users can sometimes build their own rules. A fishing case, the five-problem summary (Display 10.2) and the Fehr–Gächter experiment on costly punishment of free-riders follow. Solutions are political in both advocacy and implementation.

**Prerequisites:** public goods and free-riding; Prisoners' Dilemma; supply and demand and marginal cost; basic payoff (utility) functions.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- An externality is a public good (or "bad") that typically arrives as an unintended by-product of voluntary activity. By deduction, positive ones are undersupplied and negative ones oversupplied relative to the community's optimum (p. 325).
- In a classroom market where each sale charges every participant 1 point, buyers and sellers count only the damage to themselves and ignore the damage to everyone else (pp. 326–327).
- A tax or subsidy can make parties act as if they counted the external effect. Its rate is hard to set without precise knowledge, and what gets taxed or subsidized is often driven by political influence rather than by externality control (pp. 328–332).
- Regulation (a bureaucracy sets and enforces standards) and redefined property rights (such as cap-and-trade: tradable pollution permits under a fixed total) are the other fixes. All of them turn the problem into one of giving good incentives to the people who run the control machinery (pp. 332–334).
- A commons is a free lunch for its owners, so each user's gain from adding a cow clashes with the social cost of everyone doing so (pp. 335–337).
- Ostrom finds that successful commons "constitutions" share design features, so commons management is a political problem (p. 338).
- A fishing case gives evidence for three points: property rights (individual transferable quotas, ITQs: tradable catch shares), political agreement (straddling stocks: fish that migrate between national and international waters) and the importance of enforcement (Caspian sturgeon) (pp. 339–342).
- Fehr and Gächter find that subjects pay to punish free-riders even with no material gain. Contributions rise sharply, contrary to the theory's prediction of no difference (pp. 344–347).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Externalities (pp. 325–334)
**Definition.** An externality is a special public good, typically the unintended by-product of voluntary activity that is imposed on others. It is jointly supplied and unavoidable (p. 325). Examples of positive externalities are roses in a neighbour's garden, vaccinating children, and condom use. Examples of negative ones are high-sulfur coal smoke, litter and boom boxes (p. 325).

**Result.** Since externalities are public goods ("bads"), positive ones are undersupplied and negative ones oversupplied relative to the community's optimum. As illustration, neither the neighbour planting roses nor the factory burning coal takes others' preferences into account (p. 325).

*Intuition note:* Neither the rose-planter nor the coal burner takes the neighbours' preferences into account. So, relative to the community's optimum, there are too few roses and too much smoke.

**The classroom experiment** (Plott's design, run by the author; p. 325 n. 13).
- Half the students are buyers and half sellers. A buyer's schedule gives the points the experimenter pays per unit bought (e.g. 30, 28, 25, …). A seller's schedule gives her cost per unit (e.g. 12, 15, …). Points are added to the midterm score (pp. 325–326).
- Trading is a double oral auction. If buyer and seller agree on price $p$, the buyer's profit on a first unit is $30-p$. The seller's profit on her second unit is $p-15$. Both profit if $15<p<30$ (p. 326).
- Buyers' ceilings fall and sellers' floors rise, so trade eventually stops (p. 326).
- The twist: each sale charges 1 point to every buyer and seller, parties and non-parties alike (p. 327). Parties then figure buyer profit $30-p-1$ (so $p<29$) and seller profit $p-15-1$ (so $p>16$). The range narrows from 15–30 to 16–29 (p. 327, n. 15).
- Parties never take into account the damage to others, even though the experimenter gives them a table showing it (p. 327). The author notes the same students denounce polluters elsewhere (pp. 327–328).

*Intuition note:* Besides the price and its own schedule, each pair counts only the 1 point it pays itself. A sale can therefore go ahead even when the damage to the rest of the market is larger than the pair's own gain (our inference from the example, not stated in the text).

**Solution 1: taxes and subsidies** (pp. 328–332).
- Taxes discourage negative externalities and subsidies encourage positive ones (p. 328).
- Suppose a sales tax $t$ falls on the seller. Her profit becomes $p-15-1-t$, which raises the minimum price she needs. Fewer sales then occur, so fewer externalities are generated (p. 328). Some of the tax is passed on to the buyer (p. 328 n. 16).
- An "optimal" tax internalizes the full externality. The aim is not to eliminate sales, unless the externality overwhelmed the market's benefits (p. 328). Externalities are not driven to zero, because this would forgo profitable exchanges (p. 329 n. 17).
- Positive externalities work the same way, with a subsidy encouraging more sales (p. 328).
- Real examples. Taxes: sulfur in coal, gasoline, solid-waste effluents. Subsidies: vaccination, higher education, public transport, carpool lanes, solar power, frontier land (p. 329).
- *Worked rate.* With six buyers and six sellers, each sale causes twelve points of damage. Two points fall on the parties and are already counted. The ten points falling on non-parties are ignored. A tax of ten points per transaction forces the parties to act as if they considered these, and the equilibrium number of sales is then socially optimal (p. 329).
- **Shortcoming 1: the rate.** The precise quantities known in the experiment are rarely known in reality. Rates are "often little better than educated guesses" and may worsen things (p. 330). Footnote 18: if the damage were wrongly guessed at two points per person, the tax would be twenty per sale and no sales would occur (p. 330 n. 18).
- **Shortcoming 2: what gets taxed.** Some goods are taxed or subsidized for externality reasons, but the author says it is impossible to claim that control of externalities had much bearing on such policies overall. Many goods are taxed or subsidized because the political machinery exists and is influenced by beneficiaries. Others escape taxes for the same reason, so "optimal" taxes "get steamrolled" in politics (p. 330).
- *Case: air pollution (1970s).* High-sulfur coal from Pennsylvania, Kentucky and West Virginia causes acid rain. Low-sulfur coal from the western US is a clean alternative, so a tax on sulfur content would have pushed switching (p. 331). Senator Robert Byrd of West Virginia, Senate majority leader, used Senate veto power. He thwarted the tax despite a strong environmental lobby and a sympathetic administration, and accepted a milder rule requiring scrubbers on smokestacks (p. 331).
- Protection of key supporters is overcome only in dire fiscal circumstances, such as the late-1980s US deficits. Tax-or-subsidy solutions are "only occasionally effective" because politics constrains them (p. 332).

**Solution 2: regulation** (p. 332). A bureaucracy (agency, bureau, commission) sets standards, prices, fees or practices for activities generating externalities. Statutory authority spells out its purposes and discretion, and it enforces through administrative procedures or the courts. Example: the EPA under the Clean Air Act specifies the kind of scrubber (p. 332).

**Solution 3: respecified property rights** (pp. 332–334).
- Part of the problem is poorly specified ownership. Nobody owns the air, so anyone can dump in it. Nobody owns the dormitory common room, so it is a mess (p. 332).
- *Cap and trade.* Marketable permits each allow a standardized quantity of pollution. The EPA sets the total number of permits and a market arises (p. 333).
- *Example.* A Los Angeles factory holds a 10-"dirt" permit (a fictitious unit). If it pollutes only 5 dirts, it can sell the other 5 for cash or for something else of value, such as a promise of an 8-dirt permit in five years (p. 333).
- Pollution now has a cost for the producer, because permits have alternative uses. The factory retrofits if retrofitting costs less than the permit sale value or purchase cost. Polluters have incentives to cut pollution, and permits flow to those who value them most, for whom buying is cheaper than cutting emissions (p. 333).
- Politics is central in two places: the aggregate amount permitted (a judgment call) and the initial distribution ("political dynamite") (pp. 333–334). The total is partly self-correcting: environmental groups such as Friends of the Earth or the Audubon Society can buy permits and retire them or resell them elsewhere (p. 334).

**Section conclusion.** None of the three solutions is free of problems. Once the machinery exists it "may be used or abused", so the externality problem becomes one of giving incentives to those who run the control apparatus. This is the "who will guard the guardians" problem, to be examined in Part IV (p. 334).

*Intuition note:* Once the machinery to tax, regulate or redefine ownership exists, it may be used or abused (p. 334). So the problem becomes one of giving good incentives to those who run it.

### The Problem of the Commons (pp. 335–339)
- A commons is owned by everyone and "therefore is the responsibility of no one". Examples: grazing land, green space, fisheries, irrigation systems, urban water, the atmosphere (p. 335).
- In a village grazing field, a villager adding an animal counts his costs but not the cost of grazing. Without restraint the commons is overgrazed and destroyed. This is Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (pp. 335–336).
- The problem is a metaphor for others: too many lobstermen harvesting small lobsters off New England; development drawing down and polluting the Cape Cod aquifer, with salt-water intrusion; and atmosphere pollution plus forest destruction, which reduces the atmosphere's capacity to replenish itself (p. 336).
- **Worked numbers.** A hundred villagers each graze two cows (200 in all), a number the commons can sustain. Each considers adding one cow. If one does, the herd goes from 200 to 201 and the damage is hardly noticeable. If all do, grazing rises 50 percent, beyond carrying capacity, and each is worse off than before. Overgrazing is, in many respects, "the large-number analog" of the Prisoners' Dilemma and Hume's marsh-draining game (p. 337).
- Preservation of the commons is a public good (p. 337).
- **Comment 1: property rights.** Commons problems arise from imperfectly specified property rights. If one owner, or well-defined private plots, replaced common ownership, the owner would have the incentives of private ownership. English enclosure and marketable pollution permits are examples of reallocating ownership from community to individuals (pp. 337–338).
- **Comment 2: politics (Ostrom).** Communities have long coped with commons problems through "commons constitutions". These make, monitor, enforce and change rules in an orderly way (p. 338). Successful ones have these features (p. 338, citing Ostrom pp. 88–102):
  - clearly defined boundaries;
  - rules congruent with local needs and conditions;
  - individual rights to formulate and revise the rules;
  - monitors ultimately responsible to the community;
  - graduated punishments;
  - low-cost dispute resolution;
  - relative freedom from external governmental authorities.
- **Conclusion.** If rights cannot be parcelled into private bundles, then, to encourage the cooperation needed to preserve the commons and discourage overuse, users must enter a political agreement, a form of "self-enforcing self-restraint" (pp. 338–339).

*Intuition note:* Each user gets the full gain of an extra animal and bears only a small share of the damage. With many users the small shares add up to ruin. Ostrom's point is that groups can sometimes change these incentives by their own rules.

### Case 10.2: Fishing and the tragedy of the commons (pp. 339–342)
- By 1994, two-thirds of assessed fish stocks were overexploited or fully to heavily exploited (UN FAO, as cited) (p. 339).
- **Property rights.** Iceland and New Zealand use individual transferable quotas (ITQs). Owners keep or sell rights, and the government sets the total. The case says there is "considerable debate" over ITQs (pp. 339–340).
- **New England.** The 1977 ban on foreign trawlers within 200 miles was partly meant to prevent overfishing. Federal loans then spurred a boat-building boom. Fishermen lobbied against quotas, and in 1982 the New England Fishery Management Council dropped quotas and limits. Catches fell by over a third in four years, and some fishermen came to favour rational allocation (p. 340).
- **Straddling stocks.** Stocks that migrate between national and international waters account for about one-fifth of the world catch, so countries cannot declare ownership of them. Countries have been forced to cooperate, using the UN as a forum for monitoring and enforcement. The case says this is as Ostrom's theory predicts (pp. 340–341).
- **Sturgeon.** About 90 percent of the world's sturgeon stocks are in the Caspian Sea. Soviet quotas were enforced by armed inspectors. After the Soviet Union's collapse in 1992 the chain of command broke down and enforcement declined, and sturgeon fishing rose rapidly. Officials quoted warned that stocks could be depleted within three to five years and urged agreed rules (p. 341). The author asks whether a deal on sturgeon among new states is really as easy as a US–Soviet missile agreement (p. 342).

### Experimental Corner: Punishing free-riders (pp. 344–348)
- **Claim (Fehr and Gächter).** Free-riding generally causes very strong negative emotions among cooperators, and there is a widespread willingness to punish free-riders even if punishment is costly and brings no material benefit (p. 344).
- **Theory's prediction (author's report).** Punishment is costly, so no free-rider expects anyone to punish. The mix of cooperation and free-riding should be the same with or without punishment (p. 345).
- **Design.** Four treatments cross punishment/no punishment with "strangers" and "partners". Partners play repeatedly with the same people. In the strangers treatment the probability of meeting a previous co-player is under 5 percent. This removes reputation-building as a material motive for punishing (p. 345).
- **No-punishment game.** Each player gets $y$ tokens and contributes $g_i$, with $0\le g_i\le y$. The payoff is
$$\pi_i = y - g_i + \alpha\sum_j g_j,\qquad 0<\alpha<1. $$
Each token contributed returns only $\alpha<1$ to the contributor, so $g_i=0$ is dominant (p. 346).
- **Punishment game.** Contributions are announced. Player $j$ can assign punishment points to $i$ (written $p_i^j$ here; the text layer's notation is garbled). Each point reduces $i$'s payoff by 10 percent, not below zero. The cost of punishing, $c(\cdot)$, is strictly increasing in points assigned. The revised payoff, as reconstructed from the text layer and the author's description, is
$$\pi_i^R = \pi_i\Big[1-\tfrac{1}{10}\sum_{j\ne i}p_i^{j}\Big] - c\Big(\sum_{j\ne i}p_j^{i}\Big).$$
That is, the original payoff is reduced by 10 percent per point received and by the cost of the points $i$ assigns to others (p. 346).
- **Results** (endowment 20 tokens, strangers treatment). Mean contribution was 3.7 without punishment and 11.5 with it, more than triple. In the last period it was 1.9 versus 12.3. With punishment in the partners treatment, contributions approach full cooperation, averaging 17.0 (p. 347). Punishment also occurred where it could not pay later (p. 347).
- **Authors' conclusion.** Even highly rational people have a consequential emotional life with a strong propensity to punish. Everyday examples are drivers' rage at queue-jumpers, workers' disapproval of "scabs", and shunning of messy dorm residents (p. 347).
- **Two puzzles the author poses.** Why are people (possibly hard-wired) inclined to punish, and might an evolutionary story help? Why don't they free-ride on punishing? The suggested answer is that we may take emotional pleasure in expressing disapproval, and that part of our identity may be tied to it (p. 348). This is offered as a possibility ("may reside").

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Two buyers and two sellers. Each sale charges 1 point to all 4 participants, so total damage per sale is 4 points. Two are borne by the parties and 2 by non-parties, so the "optimal" tax is 2 (same logic as p. 329).

- *Pair A:* buyer value 10, seller cost 4. Gain from trade $=10-4=6$. Social net gain $=6-4=+2$, so trade is good.
- *Pair B:* buyer value 7, seller cost 4. Gain $=3$. Social net gain $=3-4=-1$, so trade is bad.

No tax:
- Pair A trades if $10-p-1>0$ and $p-4-1>0$, i.e. $5<p<9$. It trades.
- Pair B trades if $7-p-1>0$ and $p-4-1>0$, i.e. $5<p<6$ (e.g. $p=5.5$: buyer gets 0.5, seller gets 0.5). It also trades, which is socially bad.

Tax $t=2$ on the seller (seller profit $p-4-1-2$):
- Pair A needs $p>7$ and $p<9$, e.g. $p=8$. It trades.
- Pair B needs $p>7$ and $p<6$. This is impossible, so it does not trade.

The tax makes the pair count the 2 points falling on others.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Overgrazing the commons ↔ game theory: large-number Prisoners' Dilemma (the text draws this analogy, p. 337).
- [structural-analogy] Public goods game payoff $y-g_i+\alpha\sum g_j$ ↔ collective action: each contributor gets only a fraction of the return, so free-riding dominates.
- [structural-analogy] Costly punishment of free-riders ↔ selective incentives: a private penalty on non-contributors, here supplied by peers.
- [structural-analogy] "Guard the guardians" in externality control ↔ principal–agent problem: the controllers need incentives too.
- [metaphor] Tax making parties "act as if" they count others' damage ↔ ethics: imposing the impartial spectator's view by coercion.
