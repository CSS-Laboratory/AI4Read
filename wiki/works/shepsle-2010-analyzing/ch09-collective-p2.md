# Collective Action, part 2: Olson, entrepreneurs, ideology, and the calculus of voting

## L0 — One line
Large groups struggle to organize because individuals free-ride (enjoy benefits without contributing), so groups can be sustained through selective incentives, entrepreneurs or experiential motives. Voting shows the same problem: in a mass election, purely instrumental calculation says most sensible people should abstain.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do groups with common interests form and persist? Olson answers that organization cannot be assumed. Large, anonymous groups tend to stay "latent" (unorganized), while small groups are "privileged" (better able to organize). Large groups can still mobilize through private selective incentives (benefits only for contributors; the by-product theory). Wagner adds political entrepreneurs who bear organizing costs for private gain. Ideology and "experiential" behavior (doing something because it is rewarding in itself) are compatible with rational choice. The Riker–Ordeshook model of voting shows the problem: a person should vote only if the chance of making or breaking a tie exceeds twice the cost. In mass elections that chance is tiny, so Riker and Ordeshook add experiential benefits to explain turnout.

**Prerequisites:** expected utility (Chapter 2); the n/k collective-action game of Display 9.1 and Hume's marsh-draining game (earlier in this chapter and Chapter 8); basic probability and algebra.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Olson: common interest does not automatically produce group organization; formation and maintenance are the central problems of group life (p. 280).
- Large groups tend to be anonymous, make individual contributions seem negligible, and find enforcement hard, so many stay latent (unmobilized). Small "privileged" groups (better able to overcome free-riding) do better, and large or powerful members may be exploited by small ones (pp. 280–282).
- Case evidence (NATO/Warsaw Pact, the CBI) is consistent with the exploitation of the large by the small (p. 283).
- By-product theory: large groups elicit contributions through selective incentives (benefits only for contributors), so the group goal is achieved as a by-product (pp. 284–286).
- Wagner: political entrepreneurs organize latent groups for private payoffs (votes, a share of the dividend, glory) and monitor slackers. They complement selective incentives (pp. 286–289).
- Behavior can be instrumental (investment) or experiential (consumption). Both are compatible with rationality, and an explanation using only one is partial (pp. 289–291).
- Survey evidence: members of economic groups join mainly for selective benefits, members of noneconomic groups mainly for collective benefits. Outside patrons often help to form groups (pp. 291–292).
- Riker–Ordeshook: vote for Jackson rather than abstain iff $p_2+p_3>2C$. In a mass election with small $C$ this is very unlikely to be satisfied, so Riker and Ordeshook add experiential benefits (pp. 293–300).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(Here $n$ = group size and $k$ = number of contributors needed, as used in this part; see p. 280, p. 302.)

### Olson's logic of collective action (pp. 280–283)
- Olson (1965) challenged the pluralist assumption that common interests turn automatically into group organization. Individuals are tempted to free-ride, have trouble coordinating on several possible objectives (nonuniqueness), and may disagree on which common interest to pursue (conflict of interest) (p. 280).
- Olson is most persuasive on large groups and mass action: $n$ is very large and $k$ is relatively small, so no individual is significant. This is Hume's marsh-draining game "writ large", with a dominant strategy of not contributing (p. 280).
- Olson's three reasons why large groups have it worst (p. 280–281):
  1. They are anonymous, so group identity is hard to forge.
  2. One person's contribution seems not to matter. The example is a household head writing to a council member about property taxes.
  3. Enforcement is hard. Slackers cannot be excluded from benefits, and in a large anonymous group it is hard to know who contributed and to punish slackers.
- Result: many large groups with common interests stay **latent** (unmobilized) (p. 281).
- Small groups face the same problem (the marsh game), but Olson argues they overcome it more often and more fully. Members are more open to interpersonal persuasion. Each contribution matters more ($k$ large relative to $n$). Contributors and slackers are known, so punishment (from judgmental pressure to ostracism) is easier. Repeat play permits tit-for-tat (pp. 281–282).
- Olson calls such groups **privileged**. They often prevail over larger groups: producers over consumers, owners of capital over owners of labor, party elites over mass members (p. 282).
- Within groups, inequality among members may help the group achieve its goals but often produces unequal contribution. Large or powerful members are under intense pressure to contribute because they "make a difference", while weaker members may not contribute. The author says there is evidence that the US and the USSR paid more than their fair share in their alliances (p. 282).
- **Case 9.2:** the US and USSR subsidized their allies. Their size and belief in the alliance handicapped them in negotiating a fairer division (p. 283). Marsh's study of British firms in the Confederation of British Industry (CBI) found large firms more dependent on the group, while small firms are more likely to free-ride. A large-firm director said they would need to create the CBI if it did not exist. A small-firm executive said they would benefit "whether we were members of the CBI or not" (p. 283).

*Intuition note:* The bigger you are, the more your absence hurts the group, so you cannot easily shirk. Small members know the group may succeed without them, so they can free-ride.

### Olson's by-product theory (pp. 283–286)
- Olson's two conclusions: groups are hard to create and maintain, and smaller groups are less afflicted than larger ones. This explains the power of small numbers even in democracies that "count noses" (pp. 283–284).
- Mass movements (free speech, civil rights) do arise. Olson does not deny this but says it needs explaining (p. 284).
- **By-product theory:** large groups elicit contributions by giving members something besides the group goal. Contributors, and only contributors, receive private benefits, which are **selective incentives**. The group goal is then achieved "as by-products" (p. 284).
- Example: minimum-wage earners lobby for a higher wage, which benefits everyone. Suppose that only dues-paying contributors get cheap term life insurance, health-club rates, winter airfares, counseling and a bowling league. Solidarity can also be a selective incentive (pp. 284–285).
- A group providing only collective goals may have a hard time, especially if large and anonymous, and so may remain latent. Groups therefore seek things separate from the main mission that can be withheld from non-contributors. Examples: bowling in worker groups, mugs and sweatshirts in public-radio drives, trade statistics for trade associations, and camping discounts in environmental groups (pp. 285–286).
- The author finds the theory plausible but seemingly incomplete in several respects. To some, it takes too little account of leaders; to others, it strikes a cynical chord by leaving out genuinely nonmaterial motives (p. 286).

*Intuition note:* If joining pays off privately, people join even when their own effort would not change the group goal. The group goal then comes along almost free.

### Political entrepreneurs (pp. 286–289)
- **Wagner's** 1966 review of Olson's book argued that groups are formed and maintained not only by selective incentives but also by the efforts of leaders, called **political entrepreneurs**. Olson's theory was too pessimistic and said little about group internals (pp. 286–287).
- Wagner's special by-product theory: selective benefits may accrue to those who organize and maintain latent groups (p. 287).
- Examples: Senator Robert Wagner (1930s) had electoral reasons to help organize workers (the Wagner Act, 1935). Congressman Claude Pepper (1970s) had electoral reasons to organize the elderly (p. 287). The Wagner Act legalized the closed shop, so workers there could not free-ride (n. 12, p. 287).
- A political entrepreneur sees a cooperation dividend not currently enjoyed (a latent group). For a price (votes, a share of the dividend, glory or perks) he bears organizing costs, monitors for slackers and sometimes punishes them, for example by expulsion (pp. 287–288).
- The author's illustration is a "no doubt apocryphal" story of the Yangtze barge-pullers. A guide explains that the whip-holder is hired by the pullers to monitor effort. His mere presence is enough, so he rarely uses the whip (p. 288).
- Entrepreneurs are **complements** to selective incentives. Both help, and sometimes both are needed. Groups that organize at a low level often create leaders and leadership institutions to raise activity and the dividend. Wagner thus makes room for institutional solutions (pp. 288–289).

*Intuition note:* Monitoring is itself a service. Group members, or outsiders with a private motive, can pay for it so that slacking is harder to get away with.

### Political ideology and belief systems (pp. 289–291)
- Some people contribute without selective incentives or overseers because they have internalized a value system (an ideology or belief system) that makes contributing a priority (p. 289).
- Rational-choice accounts (multiperson cooperation, by-product theory, entrepreneurs) tend to give ideology "short shrift" because they ignore where an ideology comes from. In the author's view an ideological explanation is bound to fall short unless it can account for its origin and survival (p. 289).
- Behavior can be **instrumental** (directed to an objective; "investment") or **experiential** (done because one likes it; "consumption"). The author maintains the second view is entirely compatible with rational accounts. Economists likewise do not explain where tastes originate (pp. 289–290).
- In experiential behavior the behavior itself, not its consequences, generates utility. The author's example is the 1963 March on Washington: few thought their own participation changed the legislation. They came to belong to a movement, hear King, identify with others and have an adventure (p. 290).
- Insisting on only instrumental or only experiential rationality gives a partial explanation. This is clearest in voting (pp. 290–291).

### Case 9.3: What does the evidence say? (pp. 291–292)
- Surveys suggest people join for a combination of reasons. Members of economic groups (unions, farm groups, business associations) more often join for selective material benefits, and often disagree with the group's political goals (Moe). Members of noneconomic groups (Common Cause, Sierra Club) join mainly for collective benefits (Hansen) (p. 291).
- The author reads these as instrumental behavior for economic groups and experiential behavior for noneconomic groups (p. 292).
- A study of 35 national organizations (Knoke) found members seek inducements closest to their central interests. The author takes this as showing ideology can be incorporated into rational choice (p. 292).
- Wagner is supported by the finding that outside support is often vital for forming groups (Hansen; Walker: large, wealthy institutions such as foundations). Salisbury notes that such patron institutions are themselves Olsonian privileged groups. If entrepreneurs include patron institutions, "Wagner's argument holds up quite well" (p. 292).

### Theories of voting and collective action (pp. 293–301)
- Election participation is collective action and "cannot be assumed, but rather must be explained". A strictly instrumental analysis is the starting point but not the last word (p. 293).
- **Setup (Riker–Ordeshook).** Two candidates, Jackson (J) and Kendall (K). Ms. j has utility $u$ and $u(J)>u(K)$, so the benefit of Jackson winning is $B=u(J)-u(K)>0$. Voting costs her $C$ utiles. If she were the only voter, she should vote for Jackson if $B>C$, and need not bother if $C>B$ (p. 294). There are $n$ eligible voters in total (pp. 293–294).
- **Five states of the world** (what the other $n-1$ voters do, if she abstains) (p. 294):
  - $S_1$: Jackson loses by more than one vote.
  - $S_2$: Jackson loses by exactly one vote.
  - $S_3$: tie.
  - $S_4$: Jackson wins by exactly one vote.
  - $S_5$: Jackson wins by more than one vote.
- Her vote has no effect in $S_1$ and $S_5$. In $S_2$ a vote for Jackson produces a tie. In $S_3$ it breaks a tie. In $S_4$ a vote for Kendall would make a tie, which she would not want (pp. 294–295).
- **Display 9.4** (payoffs). $L$ is a fifty-fifty lottery for ties, so $u(L)=\tfrac12u(J)+\tfrac12u(K)$ by the expected utility theorem (p. 295).

$$
\begin{array}{l|ccccc}
 & S_1 & S_2 & S_3 & S_4 & S_5\\\hline
\text{Vote Jackson} & u(K)-C & u(L)-C & u(J)-C & u(J)-C & u(J)-C\\
\text{Vote Kendall} & u(K)-C & u(K)-C & u(K)-C & u(L)-C & u(J)-C\\
\text{Abstain} & u(K) & u(K) & u(L) & u(J) & u(J)
\end{array}
$$

- Voting for Kendall never beats voting for Jackson in any state (footnote 14 checks the cases involving $L$). So she either votes for Jackson or abstains (pp. 295–296).
- **Display 9.5.** Normalize $u(J)=1$, $u(K)=0$, so $u(L)=\tfrac12$. The state probabilities are $p_1,\dots,p_5\ge 0$, summing to 1 (pp. 296–297). Payoffs for "vote Jackson": $-C,\ \tfrac12-C,\ 1-C,\ 1-C,\ 1-C$. For "abstain": $0,\ 0,\ \tfrac12,\ 1,\ 1$ (p. 297).
- **Expected utilities** (p. 297):

$$EU(\text{vote J}) = p_1(-C)+p_2(\tfrac12-C)+p_3(1-C)+p_4(1-C)+p_5(1-C)=\tfrac12p_2+p_3+p_4+p_5-C$$

$$EU(\text{abstain}) = p_1(0)+p_2(0)+p_3(\tfrac12)+p_4+p_5=\tfrac12p_3+p_4+p_5$$

- She should vote for Jackson iff $EU(\text{J})>EU(\text{abstain})$. (Added step: the difference is $\tfrac12p_2+\tfrac12p_3-C$.) This reduces to the voting condition (p. 297):

$$p_2+p_3>2C \tag{voting condition}$$

- In words: she should vote if the probability of making a tie ($S_2$) or breaking one ($S_3$) exceeds twice the cost (p. 297).
- **Implications** (p. 298):
  1. The costlier voting is, the less likely she is to vote. If $C\ge\tfrac12$ she should never participate, since probabilities cannot satisfy the inequality.
  2. She is most disposed to vote if she expects a close election, since $p_2+p_3$ serves as a definition of "close".
  3. Troubling: take $C=1/1000$ against $u(J)-u(K)=1$. She should vote iff $p_2+p_3>2/1000$. With about 125,000,000 prospective US voters, that probability is infinitesimal and far below $2/1000$. So most sensible people "should not vote".
- One interpretation (the author's) is that instrumental calculation alone is insufficient. Riker and Ordeshook respond by conceding an **experiential** basis for voting: voting has consumption as well as investment value (pp. 298–299). Three sources (p. 299):
  - A belief system that values participation, so abstention is frowned on by neighbors, spouse and children.
  - Punishments for non-participators (fines, neighborhood watchdogs, shop stewards, party activists checking who has not voted).
  - Fun: chatting in line, next-day conversations.
- Formally, an extra consumption payoff is added in the participation rows (vote J, vote K) but not in "abstain". These benefits may suffice to induce some who would not vote on instrumental grounds. The author does not give the demonstration (pp. 299–300, n. 18).
- **Closing point.** The chapter mostly treated one group in isolation. The author suggests that voting shows many collective action situations pit groups against each other. Mr. k, a Kendall supporter, faces an interdependent problem. This requires game theory, which the author leaves to the reader (pp. 300–301).

*Intuition note:* One vote matters only if the rest are almost exactly tied. In a mass election that chance is minuscule, so an instrumental voter's expected gain is usually below the cost. Turnout therefore needs reasons beyond the outcome.

## Toy example (ours)
Let $C=0.1$ and let Ms. j's beliefs be $p=(0.20,0.15,0.10,0.25,0.30)$, which sums to 1.
- $EU(\text{vote J}) = 0.20(-0.1)+0.15(0.4)+0.10(0.9)+0.25(0.9)+0.30(0.9) = -0.02+0.06+0.09+0.225+0.27=0.625$.
- $EU(\text{abstain}) = 0+0+0.10(0.5)+0.25+0.30=0.60$.
- Difference $=0.025$. Check with the rule: $p_2+p_3=0.25>2C=0.20$, and $(0.25-0.20)/2=0.025$ ✓. She votes.
- Now take a mass election with $p_2+p_3=0.0001$ and $C=0.001$. Then $2C=0.002>0.0001$, so she abstains. The difference is $0.00005-0.001<0$.
- Our extension: add a consumption payoff $d$ in the voting rows only, so the difference becomes $\tfrac12(p_2+p_3)-C+d$. With $d=0.002$ the difference is $0.00005-0.001+0.002=0.00105>0$, and she votes.

## Concepts
- [[collective-action-problem]] — group formation and maintenance cannot be assumed, because individuals are tempted to free-ride (p. 280).
- [[free-rider]] — member who enjoys group benefits without contributing; easier to be one in large, anonymous groups (p. 280).
- [[selective-incentive]] — benefit given only to contributors, which makes the group goal a by-product (p. 284).
- [[latent-group]] (new) — a large group with common interests that fails to mobilize (p. 281).
- [[privileged-group]] (new) — a small group better able to overcome free-riding, coordination and conflict problems (p. 282).
- [[by-product-theory]] (new) — Olson's explanation of large-scale collective action through selective incentives (p. 284).
- [[political-entrepreneur]] (new) — Wagner's organizer who bears organizing and monitoring costs for a private payoff (pp. 286–288).
- [[instrumental-vs-experiential-behavior]] (new) — behavior aimed at outcomes (investment) versus behavior valued in itself (consumption) (pp. 289–290).
- [[calculus-of-voting]] (new) — Riker–Ordeshook model: vote iff $p_2+p_3>2C$, ignoring consumption benefits (p. 297).
- [[paradox-of-voting]] — the chance of making or breaking a tie is tiny, so instrumental voting seems irrational (p. 298).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Voting condition $p_2+p_3>2C$ ↔ decision theory: expected-utility comparison of two actions under state uncertainty.
- [structural-analogy] Political entrepreneur/whip-holder monitoring ↔ principal–agent problem: a hired monitor addresses unobservable effort.
- [structural-analogy] Small groups' tit-for-tat enforcement ↔ repeated-game cooperation: repeat play sustains contribution.
- [structural-analogy] Voter paradox ↔ rational ignorance: tiny decisiveness makes individually costly effort unattractive.

## Questions this raises
- Can the consumption benefit $d$ be large enough to explain turnout without making the theory empty?
- How do two opposing groups (Ms. j and Mr. k) interact strategically in mobilization?
- When do selective incentives or entrepreneurs fail, for example if monitoring is costly or entrepreneurs' interests diverge from the group's?
- Do the survey results on economic versus noneconomic groups hold for other groups and periods?
