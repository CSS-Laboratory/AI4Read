# Spatial Models of Majority Rule: Conclusion, Experimental Corner, Problems

## L0 — One line
With one dimension, pure majority rule and single-peaked preferences, the median participant's ideal point attracts outcomes; in one-dimensional legislatures, procedural rules limit which outcomes are possible; and the author reports that a classroom experiment pretty much matches the Plott and McKelvey theorems.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The conclusion looks back at what the spatial model has shown. Under one-dimensional majority rule with single-peaked preferences, outcomes move toward the median's ideal point. In one-dimensional legislative politics, procedure (open or closed rule) limits the possible outcomes to a few, and different rules allow different few. Countervailing forces, such as fear of losing extremist support or agenda control, may keep convergence from being complete. The Experimental Corner reports a Fiorina–Plott-style experiment run with students. In the author's experience, with radially symmetric ideal points (and distance preferences, odd group size) groups settle on one member's ideal point; without symmetry, the status quo keeps wandering. The problem set has ten exercises on related topics.

**Prerequisites:** single-peaked preferences; median voter idea; status quo; Plott and McKelvey theorems (not stated on these pages; only named here).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- With one dimension, pure majority rule and single-peaked preferences, competition moves toward the median's ideal point, even with few voters at the center (pp. 142–143).
- In one dimension, under the open rule, only two outcomes are possible: the status quo or the median legislator's ideal (p. 143).
- In one dimension, under the closed rule, three outcomes are possible, depending on where the status quo and the two medians lie (p. 143).
- Different procedures allow different small sets of outcomes, so institutions matter profoundly (p. 143).
- Convergence to the center need not be complete: fear of losing extremist support and powerful agenda setters may pull against it (p. 144).
- Many early applications assume honest voting; strategic behavior is the next chapter's topic (p. 144).
- In the author's classroom experiment, Plott conditions pretty much give convergence to one point and McKelvey conditions give wandering without settling. Extra information gave about the same results (pp. 146–148).
- The problems apply these ideas to agendas, committees, open/closed rules and Downs's model (pp. 148–155).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Conclusion (pp. 142–144)
- The spatial model recurs later. Chapter 6 relaxes the naive-voter assumption; Chapter 7 moves beyond majority rule (p. 142).
- Shepsle says the model captures many details of political competition (between candidates or between motions) in a fairly clean and simple manner, and that over the past fifty or so years political scientists have used it as the principal building block for analysing political rivalries (p. 142). Its great advantages are its relative simplicity, its analytical power and the "surprises" it produces (p. 144).
- **Centripetal pull.** With one dimension, pure majority rule and single-peaked preferences, the median participant's ideal point is powerful, in elections and legislatures alike (p. 142). The surprise is that this is not "where the votes are": movement to the median is an equilibrium tendency even with very few voters at the center (p. 143).
- **Legislative procedure (one dimension).**
  - *Open rule:* the committee either "closes the gates" and the status quo stays, or it moves a motion and the amendment sequence drives the outcome to the median legislator's ideal. No other outcomes are possible (p. 143).
  - *Closed rule:* three possibilities. (i) Gates stay closed (possibly because the status quo lies between the two medians' ideals) and the status quo remains. (ii) If the committee median's ideal lies between the status quo and the legislative median's ideal, the committee's ideal is proposed and passes. (iii) If the legislative median's ideal lies between the status quo and the committee median's ideal, the outcome is the point closest to the committee median that leaves the legislative median just indifferent to the status quo (p. 143).
- *Intuition note:* A procedure works like a filter. Each rule leaves only a few outcomes standing, and different rules leave different ones, so institutional rules matter for which outcome emerges (p. 143).
- **Limits of convergence.** The centripetal tendency is always present, but there may be countervailing tendencies (p. 144). In Downs's model a politician may fear losing extremist supporters, to abstention or a third-party entrant, if he converges too much. In legislatures an agenda setter may block convergence, either by passing something she likes better or by keeping the gates closed (p. 144).
- Shepsle's verdict: not a perfect explanatory tool, but "a pretty good start" (p. 144). Many early applications assume "honest" voting, that is, voting for one's favorite without regard to later consequences. Rational people sometimes do not vote for their favorite; this is strategic or sophisticated behavior (p. 144).

### Experimental Corner: group choice under majority rule (pp. 145–148)
- **Setup** (a slight variation on Fiorina and Plott 1978, run by Shepsle with his own students) (pp. 145–146):
  - An odd number of members, here five, each with an ideal point $x_1,\dots,x_5$ in a two-dimensional space. Preferences are Euclidean: closer points are preferred, and equidistant points are indifferent (pp. 145–146).
  - Each dimension runs from 0 to 100. The status quo $x_0=(100,100)$. Payment is higher for outcomes nearer one's ideal. Each subject knows his or her own preferences and payments, not others' (p. 146).
  - A randomly chosen subject proposes a change. A majority vote decides, and a tie means failure. Any subject may make a privileged motion to end the session. A time limit ends it automatically (p. 146).
- **Plott conditions** (distance-based preferences, odd number of subjects, radially symmetric ideal points, as in Figure 5.8; no figure content appears in the text layer of these pages). Theory says the outcome should be $x_2$, which can defeat any other point (p. 146). In Shepsle's experience over nearly two decades, subjects start tentatively, then motions move the status quo toward the "center of things" and closer to $x_2$. Occasional mistakes are quickly corrected, and sessions rarely hit the time limit (p. 147).
- **Without radial symmetry.** No equilibrium like $x_2$ exists and the McKelvey Theorem applies. Majorities keep approving moves without settling, and the time limit ends sessions more often. The outcome lies somewhere in the middle of the distribution of preferences. It is "not exactly chaos," but less regular (pp. 147–148).
- **Information twist.** Telling subjects all ideal points should not matter in theory, and results were about the same. The chatter among subjects did reveal envy and competitiveness when some approved status quo looked to benefit the person sitting across the table (p. 148).
- **Conclusion.** At least in this rather carefully controlled setting, pure majority rule with only elementary institutional features pretty much works the way the Plott and McKelvey theorems suggest. Plott and Fiorina conclude that this theory is superior to many competitors (p. 148).
- *Intuition note:* The theory predicts a single stopping point when ideal points are symmetric, and wandering without settling on a point when they are not. The classroom sessions look like that, though they are the author's reported experience, not tabulated data.

### Problems and Discussion Questions (pp. 148–155)

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
One dimension, legislature median $M=50$, committee median $C$, closed rule.
- *Case (i):* $SQ=60$, $C=70$. The legislative median accepts only points within 10 of 50, i.e. $[40,60]$. The committee, at 70, prefers 60 to everything in $[40,60)$. No proposal helps it, so the gates stay closed and the outcome is 60.
- *Case (ii):* $SQ=20$, $C=40$. The committee's ideal is between $SQ$ and $M$. The legislative median compares distances: $|50-40|=10<|50-20|=30$. So 40 passes.
- *Case (iii):* $SQ=20$, $C=90$. $M$ is between them. $M$'s distance to $SQ$ is $30$, so $M$ accepts $[20,80]$. The point closest to 90 in that set is 80, and $|50-80|=30=|50-20|$, so $M$ is indifferent. The outcome is 80.
- *Open rule, for contrast:* the result is either $SQ$ (gates closed) or $M=50$.

## Concepts
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — actors' ideal points in a policy space; closer outcomes preferred (model named p. 142; ideal points and 'closer is better' appear in the experiment, p. 145)
- [[median-voter-theorem]] — one dimension, single-peaked preferences: the median participant's ideal point has a "magnetic attraction" and majority politics is subject to "centripetal forces" (p. 142); movement toward it is "an equilibrium tendency" in pure majority rule (p. 143)
- [[single-peaked-preferences]] — the term is only named on p. 142 and not defined on these pages; the Verba problem describes the idea: an ideal point, with policies further away increasingly less desirable (p. 150)
- [[agenda-control]] — a powerful agenda setter may prevent convergence by proposing and passing something she likes better or by keeping the gates closed (p. 144)
- [[strategic-voting]] — "sophisticated" voting that may not back one's favorite; contrasted with honest voting (p. 144)
- [[institutions]] — procedural rules that change which outcomes are possible (p. 143)
- [[open-rule-closed-rule]] (new) — open: a sequence of amendments is permitted after a motion (p. 143). Closed: not defined on these pages; the outcomes listed on p. 143 are consistent with the committee's motion being voted as proposed (our inference).
- [[euclidean-preferences]] (new) — prefer points closer in straight-line distance to one's ideal; indifference curves are circles (p. 145)
- [[plott-theorem]] (new) — distance preferences, odd number of voters, radially symmetric ideal points make a voter's ideal point the equilibrium; in the example, $x_2$ (pp. 146–147)
- [[mckelvey-chaos-theorem]] (new) — without radial symmetry, majority rule has no equilibrium like $x_2$; in the experiment, majorities move the status quo around without settling (p. 147)
- [[winset]] (new) — term used in Problem 6 ("empty winsets") with no definition on these pages (p. 153)
