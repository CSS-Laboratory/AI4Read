# Legislatures (Shepsle, ch. 12)

## L0 — One line
Legislators with different goals need cooperation, and committees with delegated agenda powers, kept in check by assignment rules, amendment rules and leaders, are, the author argues, one institutional way a legislature gets it.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do real legislatures have committees, jurisdictions, agenda powers, and leaders, when abstract majority-rule theory sets them aside? Shepsle's answer: legislators with different goals and preferences need cooperation, but face four problems: majority cycles (no stable majority winner), a mismatch between interest and one-vote influence, under-supplied information, and weak compliance by the executive. Legislatures respond by dividing jurisdictions among committees, delegating gatekeeping (deciding what reaches the floor), proposal, conference and oversight powers to them, and then monitoring committees through assignments, amendment rules and leadership. The design lets most members have more say where they care most. It matters because it shows structure and procedure, not just median voters, explain legislative life.

**Prerequisites:** spatial model of policy (ideal points, status quo), median voter / Black's theorem, majority cycling, public goods and collective action, principal–agent delegation, open vs closed amendment rules (Ch. 5).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Three premises: legislators pursue policy goals (instrumental behavior), their preferences are heterogeneous, and no view predominates (diversity). So consensus must be built, which means cooperation. (pp. 371–372)
- Four enduring problems drive institutional design: majority cycles, interest–influence mismatch, information as a public good, and compliance after a law passes. (pp. 373–378)
- Vote trading across 435 members is too costly and unenforceable, and a leader brokering every deal would face an almost superhuman burden. Committee specialization is the alternative. (pp. 375–376)
- A jurisdictional arrangement partitions policy dimensions among committees. Committees get delegated gatekeeping, proposal, conference-bargaining and oversight powers, which the parent body can revoke. (pp. 380–386)
- Parent chambers control committees through assignments (representative = committee median close to the chamber median; outlier = far from it) and amendment control rules. Specialist committees are looser on assignment but face open rules. (pp. 388–393)
- Krehbiel's information argument: protecting committee proposals pays for expertise. Retained amendment rights deter at least the more egregious pork-barrel opportunism. (pp. 394–395)
- Case 12.2 argues that committee power is also a channel for interest-group influence, one less visible than floor votes. (pp. 387–388, 396–397)
- Layers of structure create a need for coordination: committee chairs (seniority or election) and party leaders. (pp. 397–401)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (p. 369)
The chapter puts earlier tools on display in one institution. It combines the spatial analysis of majority rule (Part II) with the analysis of cooperation and collective action (Part III), within the chapter-11 framework for institutions (p. 369).

### Cooperation among legislators (pp. 369–373)
- **Legislators' goals.** Legislators want to please those who supply campaign resources and past supporters, and many have personal agendas (p. 370). The author proposes to treat all those to whom a legislator is accountable, including himself or herself, as his or her *constituents*. *Delegates* act as perfect agents of those who sent them. *Trustees* do what they think is right. Most are mixes (p. 370).
- **Premise 1: instrumental behavior.** Legislators come with purposes that motivate them to pursue specific public policies (p. 371).
- **Premise 2: preference heterogeneity.** Members give priority to different policy realms (Cape Cod: shipping, fishing; Philadelphia: welfare, schools, jobs; Montana: ranching, mining). They also hold different opinions on any one issue, such as environmental regulation versus marketable permits versus doing nothing (pp. 371–372).
- **Premise 3: diversity.** Diversity is abundant enough that no group's view predominates, so consensus must be built through support, compromise, deals, promises and threats (p. 372).
- **Why institutions.** One-shot cooperation runs into suspicion, temptation to exploit, and the cost of securing compliance. Spreading cooperation across issues and time ("I'll scratch your back…") still leaves the risk of reneging (pp. 372–373). Cooperation on recurring matters is facilitated by institutionalization. Division and specialization of effort, regular procedures, jurisdictions, delegated agenda powers and monitoring form a "cooperative governance structure" (p. 373).

*Intuition note:* If a large share of a legislature agreed, you would mainly need fast voting rules. Because they disagree and no group is large enough to win alone, deals are needed, and deals need rules that keep people honest.

### Underlying problems (pp. 373–379)
1. **Majority cycles.** Heterogeneity and diversity mean no view predominates. If issues are multidimensional there is no majority-rule equilibrium: any status quo can be beaten, and so can whatever beats it, without end (p. 374). Procedures must cut through this, but procedures giving asymmetric advantages across all issues would not be tolerated. The best a legislator can hope for is some say in the issue areas he or she cares about most. The Philadelphia member would probably be content to trade away influence on mining and fishing for a disproportionate say on inner-city schools (p. 374).
2. **Matching influence and interest.** Each legislator has one vote per motion. This is like a consumer given $1 bills each earmarked for a different good, so votes cannot be pooled on a priority topic (pp. 374–375). Vote trading could relieve the frustration (promises about how to vote, not literal votes). It has at least two problems: negotiating and bookkeeping among 435 traders on many issues is enormously costly, and such deals are not enforceable contracts, so a reneger can only be refused future business (p. 375). Party leaders as brokers face a "superhuman" information burden. Johnson and Byrd had great reputations, yet probably not every possible deal was struck. Leaders have a role, but not mainly as intermediaries in a vote market (pp. 375–376).
3. **Information.** Legislators vote for instruments (policies), not outcomes, so voting intelligently requires knowing how instruments produce effects (pp. 376–377). Few people know this beyond the superficial. Policy-relevant information is a public good and, from Part III, public goods are undersupplied. Institutional arrangements that give some legislators incentives to produce, evaluate and disseminate it would let public resources be used more effectively (p. 377).
4. **Compliance.** Courts, executives and bureaucrats join in making policy. Without monitoring after a bill becomes law, policy may be implemented otherwise than the law says. It is not practical for all 435 representatives and 100 senators to supervise agencies, so oversight too "must be institutionalized" (pp. 377–378).

**Summary of the section (p. 378):** since majorities cycle, procedural regularities are needed to bring closure, but they risk advantaging some over others unless asymmetric advantages are held in check and widely distributed. Unequal interests with equal votes create gains from exchange. Information and monitoring are public goods that need incentives. Institutions respond to these problems, but also to others (wartime, environmental crises). Solutions change over time and are not always the best. Legislatures are also "battlefields" over whether and how public authority is deployed (pp. 378–379).

### Legislative structure and procedure (pp. 379–397)
Working assumptions: $n$ legislators ($n=435$ House, $100$ Senate, $650$ Commons), the three premises, and policy as a spatial formulation with each dimension salient to at least some legislators (p. 379). The author argues legislators choose division and specialization of labor, regular procedure, jurisdictions, and delegation plus monitoring in order to facilitate cooperation and other gains from exchange (pp. 379–380).

**Jurisdictional division and specialization of labor (pp. 380–383).**
- Policy dimensions: $D=\{d_1,d_2,\dots,d_m\}$ (p. 380).
- A jurisdictional arrangement is $J=\{J_1,J_2,\dots,J_t\}$. Each dimension of $D$ is in exactly one $J_k$, so $J$ partitions $D$ into mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive groups (pp. 380–381).
- Example: with $D=\{d_1,\dots,d_5\}$, one arrangement is $J=\{J_1,J_2,J_3\}$, $J_1=\{d_1,d_2\}$, $J_2=\{d_3\}$, $J_3=\{d_4,d_5\}$ (p. 381).
- Members $N=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$ are grouped into $t$ committees, a committee system $C=\{C_1,\dots,C_t\}$. Each member is on exactly one committee (footnote 3 relaxes this: serving on more than one is allowed in Congress and most state legislatures; what is normally forbidden is having none). Each committee is assigned one jurisdiction, e.g. $J_1$ (agriculture) to $C_1$ (p. 381).
- Two political processes lie beneath the notation:
  - *Assignment of issues to committees.* New issues often fit no single jurisdiction. Energy in the 1970s was spread over Commerce, Armed Services, Ways and Means, Science and Technology, Agriculture and others. Tobacco was contested by Commerce and Agriculture. Jurisdictional conflict is "raw stuff of politics" because jurisdiction gives leverage over resolution (pp. 381–382).
  - *Assignment of members to committees.* It decides who has extraordinary influence in each jurisdiction. It is usually run within parties, which fill vacancies with new and returning members (pp. 382–383).

**Delegation of jurisdictional authority (pp. 383–387).**
- Committee jurisdiction and authority are created by the parent legislature, which can take them away, so committees are agents with provisionally delegated authority (pp. 383–384). Footnote 7: the Constitution lets each house set its own rules. Footnote 6: many parliamentary committees do little, with ministers and civil servants dominant.
- **Gatekeeping.** There is always a status quo. In many legislatures committees have, in practice, exclusive authority over opening the gates. This is a "slight exaggeration" per the author, and it makes a committee an agenda monopolist in its jurisdiction (p. 384). The monopoly is provisional. The parent's power to revise the standing decision at any time is its "club behind the door." A discharge petition (majority signatures) can bring a bill to the floor directly (p. 384).
- **Proposal power.** Any member may submit a bill, but it goes to the committee and usually languishes. In a typical House year nearly 15,000 bills are submitted and fewer than 1,000 are taken up. Real proposal power lies with committee majorities (pp. 384–385). Footnote 8: committee power is limited, since abuse can prompt the parent to stack the committee, depose a chair, or remove policies from the jurisdiction.
- **Interchamber bargaining (after-the-fact).** In a conference between bicameral chambers, conferees come mostly from the committee of original jurisdiction. In a sample of 1980s Congresses, only 7 of 1,388 House conferees, and 7 of 1,180 Senate occasions, were not from the "right" committee (pp. 385–386). Footnote 10: 49 of 50 states are bicameral (Nebraska is not); Everett, Massachusetts is the one city mentioned.
- **Oversight (after-the-fact).** Laws are rarely self-implementing, and statutes are often vague. The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 told committees to be "continuously watchful." Committees use staff and hearings, and agencies anticipating this surveillance try to keep their congressional "masters" content, which adds leverage (p. 386–387).

*Intuition note:* Instead of 435 people bargaining on every issue, each small committee gets both before-the-fact and after-the-fact power on one topic. Members then have more say where they care most. But the parent keeps the right to take that power back.

**Case 12.1 — Campaign contributions (pp. 387–388).** Charles Keating said of his $1.4 million to five Senate Banking Committee members ("the Keating Five"): "I certainly hope so" (p. 387). If groups are rational and want to influence legislation, they should give to influential legislators. Banks give to banking committee members, food and tobacco producers to agriculture committees, doctors and insurers to health committees. Chairs often get a larger share (p. 388).

**Monitoring and control (pp. 388–395).**
- *The tension.* Take a one-dimensional jurisdiction. By Black's Theorem (Ch. 5) the chamber's preferred policy is the median legislator's ideal point $x_m$. The committee prefers the median committee member's ideal point $x_c$. Only by accident are they equal. This is a principal–agent tension (p. 389).
- Its form depends on the order of $x_m$, $x_c$ and the status quo (written $x_0$ here; the symbol is garbled in the scan):
  - If $x_0$ lies between the two medians, they want to move policy in opposite directions. The committee refuses to open the gates. If it opened them, its proposal would be voted down or amended away from $x_c$ (p. 389).
  - If $x_m$ lies between $x_c$ and $x_0$, or $x_c$ lies between $x_m$ and $x_0$, both can improve, depending on how bills are handled on the floor (Ch. 5). The tension stays even in the best case and grows with the distance between $x_c$ and $x_m$ (p. 389).
- *Two ordinary controls:* committee assignments and amendment control rules. An "extraordinary" one is stripping jurisdiction or disbanding the committee. Footnote 11 gives HUAC (renamed 1969 House Internal Security Committee) as an example of a committee ended after most members "had had enough" (pp. 389–390).
- **Assignments.** *Representative* committees ("little legislatures") have small $|x_m-x_c|$. *Outlier* committees have large $|x_m-x_c|$ (p. 390). Why not always use representative committees? Because of preference heterogeneity, many jurisdictions interest few members. Merchant Marine and Fisheries interests coastal districts. Fewer than a quarter of the House wants Agriculture or Banking. Post Office interests Washington-area suburbs. In contrast, Appropriations, Ways and Means, Armed Services and Budget matter to nearly every district (pp. 390–391). The author's expectation: free flow to specialist committees, even if they look like outliers, but careful monitoring to make generalist committees representative. House evidence is consistent: on average $|x_m-x_c|$ is larger for specialist than generalist committees (footnote 12: some scholarly controversy, mainly about measuring preferences) (pp. 391–392). Footnote 13: generalist committees are stacked so that the committee median is relatively close to the chamber median.
- **Amendment control rules.** The U.S. House customarily crafts a rule for each proposal. It sets debate time, its division between sides, and which amendments are in order. Committees can control the gates and the proposal but "do not dictate final outcomes" (pp. 392–393). Specialist committees' proposals normally get an open rule (nearly unrestricted amendment), so even outliers' products must pass a chamber able to make wholesale changes. Generalist committees often get restrictive rules, at the extreme a closed rule (no amendments) (p. 393). Footnote 14: specialist committees also need watching when proposals spend revenue from the common pool.
- **A puzzle and two answers.** Why do outlier-specialist proposals get some protection, and why does the chamber keep the right to amend representative-generalist proposals? (pp. 393–394)
  - **Krehbiel's argument:** legislating happens under great uncertainty about how proposals relate to problems. Encouraging committee members to become experts and share expertise cuts through it. At least partial protection of their proposals is "the price" the legislature is prepared to pay for reliable information, so the protection system is an incentive system for expertise (p. 394).
  - *Why keep amendment rights:* at least part of the answer is that no committee is representative in every respect. There is one way any committee is unrepresentative: its members represent specific districts and not the districts of others. On pork-barrel matters, unchecked members would steer most goodies to their own districts. Retained amendment rights deter at least the more egregious cases (p. 395). Footnote 17 gives Mendel Rivers and Robert Byrd as examples of such steering.

*Intuition note:* The chamber trades control for expertise. To get expertise it gives committee proposals at least partial protection, and because no committee is representative in every respect it nearly always keeps some way to revise them.

**Case 12.2 — Interest group influence (pp. 396–397).** Author's claim: the popular vote-buying view ("contributions buy votes") has little empirical support, since studies show little evidence that large PACs' contributions affect voting patterns (p. 396). Instead, contributions may buy influence through committee proposal power (introducing favorable legislation), gatekeeping (blocking harmful bills, which is hard to measure because it is unseen) and oversight (in the Keating case, the five senators used oversight to induce regulators to ease supervision) (pp. 396–397). Floor voting is public and risky, while committee work is less visible, so traces of influence are best sought in committee deliberation, mark-up and blocking (p. 397).

### Leadership and coordination (pp. 397–401)
- The committee system divides business and specializes labor to cut through indeterminacy, produce informed decisions and give the legislature impact. A by-product, which some consider the main product, is that members can move to committees matching their priorities, within representation constraints. So members have a larger voice where their constituents care most (pp. 397–398).
- Committees are "legislatures writ small." About twenty House standing committees have about a hundred subcommittees, which have gatekeeping, proposal, bargaining and oversight powers in their narrow jurisdictions. Example: a Montana member on Agriculture's Feedgrains Subcommittee (p. 398).
- Institutionalization is "not pure profit." Layers of structure need coordination and leadership. Someone must coordinate across subunits (pp. 398–399).
- **Committee chairs.** Congress long followed a rigid seniority rule. Benefits: a knowledgeable, experienced chair, and no leadership contests that turn the process into "vote grubbing." Costs: senior members may be unenergetic or out of touch, or out of step with the committee and chamber. Example: old-style southern conservatives remained chairs even as they declined within the Democratic Party after 1965 (p. 399–400).
- The House used strict seniority from about 1910 until the mid-1970s. Chairs are now elected by the majority-party members, with a rebuttable presumption that the most senior member is chair. Footnote 19: the Democratic Caucus approved election in 1972, and after 1974 three chairs were defeated. In 1994 the Republicans abandoned both seniority and election, effectively letting their leader appoint chairs (p. 400).
- Party and chamber leaders (Speaker, Majority and Minority Leaders) coordinate chairs. They are chosen by the full house or party caucuses, which keeps them responsive to relevant institutional constituencies (pp. 400–401). Leadership is treated more generally in Ch. 14 (p. 399).

### Conclusion (pp. 401–402)
An economist remarked that knowing median voters meant understanding the House. Shepsle calls this naïve. Treating a legislature as $n$ politicians voting by majority rule is like treating a firm as a boss maximizing profit. Such abstractions suit studies of the larger system, but inside the institution, structure and procedure, authority and control become central: "there's no getting away from politics" (p. 401). The chapter describes the U.S. House and Senate, U.S. state legislatures and parts of Latin America. The British Commons and most continental legislatures are less internally elaborate and more centrally organized around a prime minister and cabinet, which Ch. 16 treats (p. 402).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Chamber of 5 with ideal points on a line: $\{1,3,5,7,9\}$, so $x_m=5$. Committee of 3 members $\{5,7,9\}$, so $x_c=7$ (an outlier: $|x_m-x_c|=2$).

*Status quo $x_0=6$ (between the medians).* The chamber median prefers points within distance $|6-5|=1$ of 5: the interval $(4,6)$. The committee median prefers points within $|6-7|=1$ of 7: $(6,8)$. The intersection is empty, so the committee gains nothing by opening the gates. This matches p. 389: when $x_0$ lies between the medians, the committee refuses to open the gates.

*Status quo $x_0=2$.* The chamber median prefers $|x-5|<3$, i.e. $(2,8)$. The committee median prefers $|x-7|<5$, i.e. $(2,12)$. Here $x_m=5$ lies between $x_0$ and $x_c$, so both can gain.
- *Closed rule* (added step): the committee proposes its ideal point 7. It beats 2 for members 5, 7, 9 (distances to 7 are 2, 0, 2; to 2 are 3, 5, 7), so it passes 3–2 and the committee gets its ideal.
- *Open rule* (added step): the floor can offer 5. Members 1, 3, 5 prefer 5 to 7 (distances 4 vs 6, 2 vs 4, 0 vs 2), so 5 wins 3–2 and the outcome moves to $x_m$. Which rule applies, and how amendments are handled, determines how much of the committee's advantage survives.

## Concepts
- [[institutions]] — here, the structure and procedure of a legislature (committees, jurisdictions, rules), adopted to facilitate cooperation (p. 373)
- [[agenda-control]] — committee gatekeeping and proposal power, provisional and revocable by the parent chamber (p. 384)
- [[principal-agent-problem]] — parent chamber (principal) delegates provisional authority to committees (agents) whose median differs (pp. 384, 389)
- [[median-voter-theorem]] — Black's Theorem applied: the chamber median $x_m$ is the majority's preferred point in one dimension (p. 389)
- [[cycling]] — in multidimensional issues no majority-rule equilibrium exists, which motivates procedures (p. 374)
- [[public-good]] — policy-relevant information and oversight are public goods needing incentives (p. 377)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — cooperation is hard to manufacture and enforce, so institutionalization helps (pp. 372–373)
- [[vote-trading]] (new) — promises to vote in particular ways. Costly to organize and unenforceable in a large assembly (p. 375)
- [[jurisdictional-arrangement]] (new) — $J$ partitions policy dimensions $D$ and is matched to committees (pp. 380–381)
- [[gatekeeping-power]] (new) — a committee's near-exclusive control over opening the gates to changing the status quo (p. 384)
- [[representative-vs-outlier-committee]] (new) — small vs large gap between committee median $x_c$ and chamber median $x_m$ (p. 390)
- [[amendment-control-rule]] (new) — rule on debate and amendments for a bill (open to closed) (pp. 392–393)
- [[seniority-rule]] (new) — the committee chair goes to the longest-serving member (p. 399)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [same-math] Median committee member $x_c$ vs chamber median $x_m$ ↔ political-science canon: median voter theorem — same one-dimensional median logic (p. 389).
- [structural-analogy] Chamber delegating to committees and monitoring them ↔ principal–agent problem: delegation with imperfect alignment, controlled by selection and ex post checks.
- [structural-analogy] Information and oversight undersupplied ↔ public good/collective action: institutions give incentives to a few to produce them (p. 377).
- [metaphor] Legislature as firm with divisional structure ↔ organizational economics: both gain from specialization and bear coordination costs (pp. 398, 401).

## Questions this raises
- If committee advantage depends on the rule (open vs closed), who chooses the rule, and could that choice itself cycle?
- Does the information rationale predict which committees get open vs restrictive rules better than a distributive or party-control rationale?
- How would the analysis change in parliaments with strong cabinets, which the author postpones to Ch. 16 (p. 402)?
- What evidence could show that contributions buy committee-stage influence, given that blocked provisions are unobservable (p. 396)?
