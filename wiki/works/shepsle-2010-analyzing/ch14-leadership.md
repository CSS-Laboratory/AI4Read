# Leadership

## L0 — One line
Leaders can be seen as agents of followers whose support they need; they also steer the agenda, seize opportunities, and may build a reputation for sanctioning rebels.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
What light can the rational-choice tools of earlier chapters shed on leadership? Shepsle treats leaders in three ways: as agents of followers, as agenda setters, and as entrepreneurs. Leaders depend on followers, yet their skills can also serve their own ends. He then presents Calvert's model of reputation. If followers are unsure what punishing costs her, a leader can bluff and may build a reputation that sometimes makes rebels think twice. One message: understanding leadership depends on understanding followership. Leaders must be empowered to do what followers cannot do alone, but not so empowered that they can pursue a private agenda without regard for followers' preferences.

**Prerequisites:** principal–agent problem (Ch. 13); agenda control and majority cycling (Part II); collective action (Ch. 9); expected value and beliefs as probabilities.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Leaders are not just bosses. They need followers' support to reach group goals and to keep their post, so in a sense the leader is also a follower (pp. 446–447, 449).
- Followers face a principal–agent problem (a principal relies on a more skilled agent, who may exploit her): the skills that make someone attractive as a leader can also be used to exploit followers. They can control her by before-the-fact means (reputation) and after-the-fact means (reappointment, post-leadership life) (pp. 448–449, 453).
- Handing the group's discretionary agenda to a leader is often an efficient division of labour. She can extract some "agency slippage" (agenda advantages for herself) but must not be "piggish", and she cannot afford to be rolled (overruled by followers) too often (pp. 451–454).
- Fine-grained procedural discretion, such as bill referral, rules and voting method, is decisive when majority voting can cycle (the Part II problem in which majority votes go round in a circle) (pp. 455–457).
- Entrepreneurs are leaders who often appoint themselves, and may create the group they lead. In a sense this is the richest view of leadership, but also the most vague (pp. 457–460).
- A strong reputation can give "counterintuitive flexibility" (Nixon to China) (pp. 460–461).
- In Calvert's model, uncertainty about the leader's punishment costs lets her bluff and build a reputation that may make some rebels think twice (pp. 462–468).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Introduction (pp. 445–446).** Shepsle uses rational-choice theory to discuss leadership in general, not presidents, mayors and chairs separately. He draws on earlier chapters (p. 445). He sets a second task: to explore the problem facing leaders in self-governing groups. To lead, the leader must induce followers to follow, which may be costly to her because she needs their support to keep her position (p. 446).

**The Leader as Agent (pp. 446–450).**
- The ordinary view is that the leader is proactive and followers are reactive. Shepsle calls this only a partial truth. Leaders need follower support to reach group goals and to keep the post. Even authoritarian leaders have to worry about followers who might, under the right circumstances, move against them (p. 446).
- *Henry Clay.* Elected Speaker as a freshman in 1811, he was re-elected for fifteen years. His first speech said he obeyed "your commands" rather than "my own inclinations" (pp. 446–447). Shepsle reads this as the leader as follower: the reward (staying Speaker) depends on performance, which ties ambition to performance (p. 447).
- Even a leader whose job is secure, such as a civil-service bureau chief, depends on subordinates' cooperation and morale if her goals depend on them (p. 448).
- Leaders are a special kind of agent. They coordinate, reward, punish, and secure allies. They are chosen for skills such as persuading, bullying and mediating. The problem for followers is that these skills also let the leader exploit them. "Who will guard the guardians?" becomes "how will we control our agent?" (p. 448).
- *Controls.* **Before the fact:** reputation, a summary of the candidate's skills, experience, policy leanings and character (pp. 448–449). **After the fact:** the leader "posts her reputation as a bond", so reputation is also an after-the-fact control, since followers have authority over her future (future posts, her current post, "contract renewal", place in history). Whenever others wait in the wings, she knows she is not indispensable. She must answer "What have you done for me lately?" (p. 449).
- Leaders' proactive acts are aimed at impressing followers and earning support. Shepsle says to look beyond a leader's capabilities to her intentions. To the extent these depend on follower support, "the master is servant and the servant master" (pp. 449–450).

*Intuition note.* A leader may look powerful, but to the extent her goals and her post depend on followers, her displays of authority are aimed at winning their support, not at bullying them.

**Case 14.1: FDR and World War II (pp. 450–451).**
- Burke (as told here) told Bristol voters he would be a **trustee**, using his own judgment, not a **delegate**, an unreflective agent of constituents' opinion. He was defeated. Clay cast his role as Speaker as more that of a delegate (p. 450).
- Public opinion before WWII was isolationist. Roosevelt judged that delaying mobilization would be too late. Acting as a trustee, he took secret early action, using his skills as agenda setter and entrepreneur. His room to get ahead of opinion came from reputational capital built in the Depression years (p. 451).
- Shepsle says the answer to "obey or follow own inclination?" depends largely on how a leader reads her mandate as an agent (p. 450).

**The Leader as Agenda Setter (pp. 451–457).**
- The three views of leaders are not mutually exclusive and are compatible with the agent view (p. 451).
- Groups have finite resources and some discretion. Some tasks are mandated by others, but choices on the rest must be made, and a leader helps make them (pp. 451–452).
- *Department chair example.* Discretionary questions include: revise the graduate programme now or later, which specialty to hire, whether to promote a colleague now, whether to negotiate a joint degree. Her input matters because "the buck must stop somewhere", and deciding everything town-meeting style is exhausting and inefficient. Delegating the discretionary agenda is therefore often an efficient division of labour (pp. 452–453).
- Members keep control through three mechanisms: a say in who the chair is (before the fact), how long she survives, and her post-chair life (after the fact) (p. 453).
- The chair weighs these controls against steering the agenda toward her own preferences. For example, she may set up a graduate review committee and not act on a tenure review. She may get "rolled" if a senior colleague, backed by many, forces the tenure case (pp. 453–454).
- Rolling her too often makes it hard to convince principals she has their interests at heart. Followers must also weigh how much damage rolling does to a leader they find satisfactory. They must also weigh the consequences of trying and failing to roll her (p. 454 and n. 6).
- The chair can extract some agenda advantages ("agency slippage") but must be "prudent, not piggish". Normally the problem is least serious early in her term and more problematic near its end, especially for a chair who wants to retire to her own work (the **endgame problem**). Groups expect a "pound of flesh". If the limits are breached, "revolt soon follows" (p. 454).
- *Fine-grained agenda control.*
  - The Speaker and the Parliamentarian decide which committee has jurisdiction over a bill. Discretion is limited: a farm bill cannot go to the Merchant Marine Committee. Since the mid-1970s, multiple referral lets the Speaker split a bill among committees or refer it to several, in sequence or at once (p. 455). Committees then hold gatekeeping and proposal powers before the fact and negotiating and oversight powers after the fact (Ch. 12) (p. 455).
  - Footnote: until 1910 the Speaker made all House committee assignments, and still has influence today (p. 455 n. 7). In most academic departments the chair composes committees, so a chair can use an "artfully crafted" committee to reflect her doubts about a tenure case (pp. 455–456 n. 7).
  - The Rules Committee sets when debate happens, its length, which amendments are in order, and their sequence. The Speaker also rules on points of order (p. 456).
  - From Part II: with a multidimensional bill, only a modicum (a small amount) of preference diversity is needed to produce majority voting cycles. So amendment order and procedural rulings on seemingly minor matters can decide the final outcome (p. 456).
  - Procedural discretion is a crucial resource of all leaders. If every procedural issue were hardwired, the leader would be impotent, but this is rarely the case, since rule-writers would have to anticipate just about every possible contingency (p. 456).
  - *Example.* The chair interviewing four candidates often has the chance to suggest the voting method. She might suggest each member write one name and rank candidates by total votes, or have each submit a full ranking (Ch. 4). Chapter 7 shows many voting systems might be used, any of which she could defend by pointing to attractive criteria it satisfies. Anyone opposing her suggestion will appear to have a private motive. If she has kept her ear to the ground, she can make the suggestion in an informed, strategic way (pp. 456–457).

*Intuition note.* When a group's preferences can cycle, whoever controls the order and the rules can steer the result. Control looks like routine paperwork, but it is real power.

**The Leader as Entrepreneur (pp. 457–460).**
- Entrepreneurs often appoint themselves and perform needed services so a group can reach a collective purpose. They may not belong to the group, and the group may come into being through their activity. The entrepreneur is then an agent who chooses or creates a principal (p. 457).
- This view stresses, perhaps more than the others, the artistry of seizing opportunities (p. 458). Examples: Mario Savio and the Free Speech Movement, Ralph Nader against General Motors, Martin Luther King Jr. in the Montgomery boycott, Malcolm X, Stokely Carmichael and Huey Newton in the Black Power movement. The leader gains ideological satisfaction and personal glory. Some mobilized an existing group and some forged a new one (p. 458).
- Entrepreneurs need not care much about the issue, though they are rarely indifferent. They may enjoy the act of leading, expect gratitude payoffs such as electoral or campaign-finance returns, or be building a career (reputation, contacts, mentors) (pp. 458–459). In a footnote, Shepsle reports from his early-1980s interviews that it was not uncommon for a staff director to send staffers to monitor hearings and find something useful for the boss (p. 459 n. 9).
- Failed entrepreneurs far outnumber famous ones (hundreds, perhaps thousands, for each). The market rewards creativity and insight, but many who think they have them are often mistaken, and sometimes dumb luck matters (p. 459).
- Agenda setting, agency and entrepreneurship are facets of one phenomenon. They differ in how much structure surrounds the leader. Agenda setting is the most structured, agency is broader, and entrepreneurship also creates or transforms structure. In a sense it is the richest conception, but also the most vague (pp. 459–460).

**Reputation and Leadership (pp. 460–468).**

*Case 14.2: Only Nixon could go to China (pp. 460–461).*
- The saying: only Nixon could have gone to China. According to some, only a conservative with sterling anti-communist credentials could have gotten away with recognizing China and visiting it in the early 1970s. A liberal Democrat would have been seen as soft on communism and would not have convinced the public that his or her motives were the nation's interest rather than a private ideological agenda. Shepsle asks whether there is merit in this (p. 460).
- Shepsle's reading: Nixon was a famous "red-baiter", so he bore a high prospective cost with the right wing of his party. He could therefore credibly claim he was serving the nation, not a private agenda. A more liberal president's identical act would have sent an ambiguous signal (p. 461).
- He offers similar readings of Johnson on civil rights and Reagan on deficits (the latter described as "widely believed"). Shepsle's phrase is "counterintuitive flexibility" (p. 461).

*Calvert's model (pp. 462–468).*
- Setting: kindred but not identical group members create a leader to solve collective action problems. Outcomes make members better off on net, but some are less pleased and may defect, e.g. at a crucial vote. The leader must threaten, and sometimes use, sanctions. For coordinating and forging compromise she receives "compensation" (pp. 462–463).
- **Calvert's assumptions:**
  1. The leader must keep minimal group support. Leaders-in-waiting exist, so sanctions are costly, since they hit people whose support she wants. Calvert says each punishment slightly raises the chance of a full-scale rebellion, so the leader faces a budget constraint on sanctions (p. 463).
  2. The leader's benefits and punishment costs are private information. Cultivating uncertainty makes followers think punishment is possible more often. She may even punish when it is "too costly" to mislead followers about her costs. Uncertainty permits bluffing, deceiving and dissembling, which Shepsle calls the raw material of reputations (pp. 463–464).
- **Payoffs (Display 14.1, p. 465).** Here $a>1$, $b$ is a positive number below 1, and the follower's obey payoff and the leader's acquiesce payoff are normalized to 0 (p. 464).

| Behavior | Leader payoff | Follower $t$ payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Follower obeys | $a$ | $0$ |
| Follower rebels; leader acquiesces | $0$ | $b$ |
| Follower rebels; leader punishes | $-x_t$ | $b-1$ |

- The follower does not know $-x_t$. He believes $-x_t=-1$ (costly) with probability $w$ and $-x_t=0$ (costless) with probability $1-w$. The leader knows $x_t$ in advance (p. 465). Because $b<1$, the follower's payoff from a punished rebellion is below 0 (pp. 464–465).
- Followers learn: $w$ changes as they watch the leader. Punishing early, even when it is really costly, might persuade followers that punishment is mostly costless to her, which would bring more loyalty and less need to punish (p. 465). Shepsle's example: a chair excludes a senior colleague from the recruitment committee, taking heat that she knows about in advance and others do not. Later, fewer colleagues oppose her. She is establishing a reputation for not tolerating rebellion, and life would exist mainly in the first row of Display 14.1 (pp. 465–466).
- **Model structure.** The game is repeated exactly twice, with follower $t_1$ and then $t_2$. Follower $t_2$ updates his beliefs about punishment costs after seeing what happens with $t_1$. The cost on each occasion is known to the leader beforehand (p. 466).
- **Results Shepsle reports (p. 467).**
  - If $-x_1=0$, the leader should always punish rebellion.
  - If $-x_2=-1$, she should never punish $t_2$, since the game ends and punishing serves no purpose.
  - If $-x_1=-1$, the best response depends on followers' beliefs about her punishment costs relative to their gains from rebelling. Calvert identifies when $t_1$ should rebel, when the leader should punish for certain, when she should randomize ("flip a coin"), and whether $t_2$ should rebel for certain or with some probability. Shepsle does not give these details (p. 467).
- **Conclusion.** Leaders need a reputation for willingness to sanction. If sanctioning were costless, leaders would be incredibly powerful, perhaps too powerful from followers' standpoint. If it were too costly, they would be too weak to deter defection. With some uncertainty about her costs, a leader may, depending on the details of the situation, build a reputation that gives some rebels second thoughts (pp. 467–468). Calvert's closing passage, quoted by Shepsle, says a successful leader cannot constantly monitor and punish, so followers need "a habit of obedience". Habits do not arise under perfect, costless information. So uncertainty, and the artful manipulation of it, are crucial, whether the leader is a ruthless dictator or a benevolent provider of collective action (p. 468).

*Intuition note.* Followers cannot be sure how much a punishment really costs the leader. A leader who sometimes punishes, even at real cost, may convince them that punishing is cheap for her, so later rebels may think twice and she may have to punish less.

**Conclusion (pp. 468–469).** Understanding leadership depends on understanding followership. Leaders are often invented by followers and depend on them for cooperation and support. They must be empowered to do what followers cannot do alone, but not so empowered that they are able to pursue a private agenda without regard for follower preferences. They must be proactive but ultimately under follower control. Leadership is a second-best solution to group problems, and it brings its own problems, hence the need for control mechanisms (pp. 468–469).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
This simplifies Calvert's game and is not his equilibrium solution. Take $b=0.6$. Suppose follower $t$ believes the leader punishes a rebel only when punishment is costless (probability $1-w$). Otherwise she acquiesces.

- Expected payoff to rebelling: $(1-w)(b-1)+w\,b = b-(1-w)$.
- If $w=0.7$: $0.3\times(-0.4)+0.7\times0.6=-0.12+0.42=0.30>0$, so he rebels.
- If $w=0.2$: $0.8\times(-0.4)+0.2\times0.6=-0.32+0.12=-0.20<0$, so he obeys.
- Threshold: $b-(1-w)=0$ gives $w=1-b=0.4$. He rebels only if $w>0.4$.
- A leader who punishes a first rebel at real cost may push the second follower's $w$ from 0.7 to 0.2 (the numbers are assumed, to show direction only). Then $t_2$ obeys, and the leader earns $a$ without punishing.

## Concepts
- [[principal–agent problem / delegation]] — followers (principals) delegate to a leader whose skills can be used against them (p. 448)
- [[agenda control / agenda setter]] — the leader heavily influences the group's discretionary agenda and the procedures (pp. 451, 453, 455–456)
- [[cycling / intransitivity]] — multidimensional bills need only some preference diversity to cycle, so procedural control can be decisive for the outcome (p. 456)
- [[collective action problem]] — leaders are created to help solve such problems (pp. 458, 462)
- [[reputation-as-bond]] (new) — the leader stakes her reputation as a bond, a before- and after-the-fact control (p. 449)
- [[agency-slippage]] (new) — the leader's private agenda gains within limits followers tolerate; more problematic near the end of a term (the endgame) (p. 454)
- [[trustee-vs-delegate]] (new) — Burke's trustee deploys his own intelligence and expertise for constituents' best interests as he sees them; a delegate simply acts on constituency opinion (p. 450)
- [[political-entrepreneur]] (new) — a leader who seizes opportunities and may self-appoint or create the group (pp. 457–458)
- [[calvert-leader-reputation-model]] (new) — repeated leader-follower game with private information on punishment cost (pp. 464–468)

## Questions this raises
- When is a leader better modelled as a trustee, and when as a delegate? Who decides what followers "need"?
- What limits agency slippage in practice, and does a longer term make it worse (endgame)?
- How would Calvert's results change with more than two followers or periods?
- How can followers tell a sincere costly signal (Nixon) from a bluff?
