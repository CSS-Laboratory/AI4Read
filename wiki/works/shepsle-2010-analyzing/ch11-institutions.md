# Institutions: General Remarks

## L0 — One line
Institutions turn recurring problems into routines through divided, specialized, jurisdiction-based labor with delegation and monitoring, so results do not depend only on the quality of the people involved.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Shepsle describes how communities institutionalize responses to recurring, important problems. His answer is that organized procedures supply incentives for collective action, so performance is no longer only a function of the "quality" of the actors. He offers a four-part framework rather than a formal definition: (1) division of labor and regular procedure, (2) specialization, (3) jurisdictions, and (4) delegation with monitoring. His focus is official institutions such as legislatures, but the analysis also fits firms, universities and families. Delegation brings gains from expertise but also a control problem (moral hazard). Institutions respond to outside pressure and to their members' wishes, and they change over time. The take-home message (of Part IV, emphasized again here) is that institutions, not just individual preferences, matter for collective results.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of public goods and collective action (Part III); simple majority versus supermajority voting; the basic idea of a principal and an agent.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Responses to recurring, consequential problems are often institutionalized; rare, minor ones would most likely be handled ad hoc (p. 355).
- With institutions, collective results do not depend only on the character or preferences of actors; the methods of doing business may have a salutary effect. Hume makes this point, and it is the take-home message of Part IV, emphasized again here (pp. 356–357).
- A division of labor and regular procedure lowers transaction costs, helps members plan their participation, and checks leaders. Procedures can be changed permanently (amendment) or temporarily (suspension) (pp. 358–361).
- Specialization uses members' differing talents and builds expertise. Many institutions evolve from unspecialized to specialized forms, but some, such as the Supreme Court, stay unspecialized (pp. 361–364).
- Jurisdictions bundle activities and give specialists disproportionate influence. Pressures to adopt them come both from the environment and from members (pp. 364–366).
- Delegation to specialists creates a control (moral hazard) problem, so monitoring accompanies delegation. It is an instance of the principal–agent relationship (pp. 366–367, n. 8).
- An institution is a governance structure blending individual ambition and organizational purpose, and it changes over time (p. 368).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 355–357)
- Part III covered cooperation on public goods, externalities and commons. We would not expect a community to spend much effort on intermittent, minor problems; most likely these are handled ad hoc, sometimes successfully, sometimes not. Example: a Mississippi town and a freak snowstorm every other decade (p. 355).
- For recurring, consequential problems the community develops routines run by organizations with resources and authority. Example: Cambridge's Department of Public Works, with an annual snow-removal budget (p. 355).
- Collective action then happens because standard procedures give actors appropriate incentives (p. 355).
- The focus is on official institutions (executives, legislatures, bureaucracies, courts). The analysis also fits families, firms, houses of worship, universities, unions and the like, each a "political community" (p. 356).
- Consequence: performance is no longer only a function of actors' "quality". Collective action is not "held hostage" to altruism. Even if "men are not angels" (Madison), the methods for doing political business may have a salutary effect (p. 356).
- **Hume's view (quoted):** people should not leave government to chance but provide a system of laws and institutions. In the smallest court or office, stated forms and methods are "a considerable check on the natural depravity of mankind". The same men can run one part of a government wisely and another weakly because of differing forms (pp. 356–357).
- Take-home message of Part IV: institutions, not just individual preferences, matter for collective results (p. 357).

*Intuition note:* A good routine can make ordinary people produce good outcomes, like a well-designed road keeping average drivers safe. The same people may do better or worse depending on the rules of the unit they work in.

### A framework (p. 357)
Shepsle avoids a formal definition ("put their highlighters away") and gives four components (p. 357).

### 1. Division of labor and regular procedure (pp. 357–361)
- Tasks often allow preliminary structuring. Examples: club meetings (officer reports, old business, new business), a school board's "citizens speak" slot, the House calendar (pp. 357–358).
- Purpose 1, efficiency: procedures bring order and economize on transaction costs (the costs of doing business) (p. 358).
- Purpose 2, strategy: a division of labor defines a strategic context in which members decide whether, when and how to participate. Choices may depend on what has already happened and on what is coming (p. 358).
  - A member's preference on new business may depend on how old business is resolved. So old business is dispatched first. Otherwise members judge with insufficient information and issues may have to be revisited (pp. 358–359).
  - Shepsle calls this "strategy enhancing" (p. 359).
- Purpose 3, empowerment: known procedures check arbitrary leaders. Leader discretion is partly a reward, a selective incentive in Olson's terms. Without regular procedure it becomes "a blank check" (p. 359).
- Procedures are not fixed. Constitutions, bylaws and standing rules contain mechanisms for change, in two forms (pp. 359–360):
  - **Amendment:** a permanent change in the status quo, assumed to apply to future business unless amended again (p. 360).
  - **Temporary suspension:** a "short-circuiting" device for one matter, after which the old rules return (p. 360).
- Example of amendment: until the mid-1970s, Senate cloture (a motion to close debate and force a vote) needed two-thirds of those present and voting. That let a minority of 34 senators (sometimes fewer) filibuster. The rule was amended, mainly to break southern senators' hold on civil rights bills, so that 60 senators could vote cloture. The change became a standing rule (p. 360).
- Footnote 2: defenders of the old rule said it aided deliberation. Detractors said it let a disciplined minority extract concessions or defeat the majority (p. 360).
- Example of suspension: the House often suspends its rules to avoid procedural complexity. A combined motion to suspend the rules and pass a bill needs two-thirds of those present and voting; ordinary passage needs a simple majority (p. 361).
  - The device works only for relatively uncontroversial legislation. Procedure then reverts to normal (p. 361).

*Intuition note:* Rules do more than tidy things up. They fix an order of play, so players can think ahead, and they stop leaders acting on whim. Amendment changes the game for good; suspension is a one-off exception.

### 2. Specialization of labor (pp. 361–364)
- Actors differ in talents, interests and preferences. Specialization lets an institution use this "human capital" (pp. 361–362). Examples: professors in classroom versus lab, legislators in policy areas (p. 361).
- Not all institutions specialize. Perhaps the most prominent American national institution lacking specialization is the Supreme Court: all justices hear all arguments, deliberate and write. Footnote 4 says this is a slight overstatement, because justices have reputations in particular areas. To the author's knowledge, no satisfactory explanation has been provided (p. 362, n. 4).
- Footnote 5 (a reader's suggestion, hedged "may reflect"): the lack of specialization may reflect a deeper ban on trading across issues (logrolling) (p. 362).
- Institutions often evolve from unspecialized to specialized form.
  - Market analogy: production often begins as piece-rate artisanal work; the wage system is often associated with mass production, where labor becomes specialized (p. 363).
  - Congress and most state legislatures began unspecialized. By the War of 1812, if not earlier, Congress used standing committees (p. 363).
- Advantage: specialization gives members incentives to "learn their area", which may make the institution more effective (p. 364).

### 3. Jurisdictions (pp. 364–366)
- A jurisdiction is a bundle of activities. Members assigned to it become jurisdictional specialists (p. 364).
- Examples: university disciplines assigned to departments. Firms organized by function (finance, advertising, etc.) or by product line, so there are alternative ways to bundle. Legislatures organized into policy jurisdictions held by committees (p. 364).
- Committee members tend to have disproportionate influence, not only because they become the most knowledgeable but also because they get forms of agenda power (to be developed in the next chapter) (p. 365).
- A side effect: specialists may know little outside their own bailiwick. A Harvard political scientist typically knows more about Stanford's political science department than about Harvard's chemistry department (p. 365).
- Pressures toward these structures come from the environment: inefficient firms struggle to survive, legislatures that do not specialize are outmaneuvered by the executive, and universities lose faculty and students (pp. 365–366).
- They also come from inside. Self-governing institutions are to some degree creatures of their members, who want influence where their interests lie. The institutional profile balances external requirements against internal desires (p. 366).

### 4. Delegation and monitoring (pp. 366–367)
- Costs of jurisdictions: a unit left unchecked might not serve the whole. Examples: an R&D division, a political science department, and the Armed Services Committee showering its members' districts with military facilities (p. 366).
- Delegation exploits specialization but risks the collective objectives (pp. 366–367).
- Monitoring therefore accompanies delegation. Examples: financial controls in firms; the dean's resource allocation and hiring/firing. A legislative majority controls committee assignments, jurisdictions and final disposition of bills (p. 367).
- Decentralization supports delegation and incentives to develop expertise. Delegating also creates a control problem, called moral hazard by organization theorists. It is probably impossible to solve entirely, but institutions normally set up mechanisms both to monitor subunit performance and to control behavior "wildly at odds" with institutional objectives (p. 367).
- Footnote 8: delegation is pervasive, presumably because it improves goal achievement relative to centralization. It is an instance of the principal–agent relationship. Examples: constituency and representative, followers and leaders, assemblies and committees. Some form of monitoring accompanies each (pp. 367–368).

### Conclusion (p. 368)
- Without a formal definition: an institution has a division of activities, a partitioning of individuals, and a matching so that a subgroup has jurisdiction over a subset of activities. It also has monitoring, control and other incentives connecting subgroup activity to the overall mission (p. 368).
- It is a governance structure blending individual ambition and organizational purpose. Institutions are rarely born whole and rarely stay undisturbed; they change with the external environment and with internal pressures (p. 368).

*Intuition note:* Institutions give people room to specialize and then fence that room in. Too little room wastes talent; too little fencing lets specialists serve themselves.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A club has a budget of \$600. Old business: a roof repair costing \$500. New business: a picnic costing \$200. Ana wants the picnic only if the roof repair fails, since \$500 + \$200 = \$700 > \$600.
- **Regular order (old first):** the repair is voted and passes. Ana now knows \$600 − \$500 = \$100 remains, so she votes against the picnic. The matter is settled in one pass.
- **Picnic first:** Ana does not know the repair's fate. If she votes yes and the repair then passes, the club has committed to \$700 against a \$600 budget. The picnic vote must be revisited, wasting time.

This shows the "strategy-enhancing" role of ordering (pp. 358–359).

## Concepts
- [[institutions]] — routinized, authority-and-resource-holding procedures for recurring, important problems; not formally defined here (pp. 355, 368)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — the Part III problem that institutions address by giving actors appropriate incentives (p. 355)
- [[selective-incentive]] — leaders' discretion, partly a reward for shouldering organizational burdens (Olson's term) (p. 359)
- [[principal-agent-problem]] — delegator–delegatee relationship with a control problem; delegation to subunits is one instance (pp. 367–368, n. 8)
- [[division-of-labor-regular-procedure]] (new) — dividing business into units and a fixed sequence; it saves costs, aids planning and checks leaders (pp. 357–359)
- [[specialization-of-labor]] (new) — members do different things according to differing talents, building expertise (pp. 361–364)
- [[jurisdiction]] (new) — a bundle of activities assigned to a subgroup of members (p. 364)
- [[delegation-and-monitoring]] (new) — handing authority and resources to subunits, with controls to keep them on the group's mission (pp. 366–367)
- [[moral-hazard]] (new) — delegatees may pursue private aims at odds with the institution's (p. 367)
- [[transaction-costs]] (new) — the costs of doing business, which regular procedure economizes (p. 358)
- [[amendment-vs-suspension-of-rules]] (new) — permanent versus temporary departure from standard procedure (pp. 359–360)
