# Cabinet Government and Parliamentary Democracy

## L0 — One line
In parliamentary democracies, parliament chooses a government (a package of ministries), and a spatial example illustrates how rational parties can reach stable governments, even minority ones.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The chapter asks whether rational-choice analysis of institutions, which may seem to rest mostly on American examples, also applies to parliamentary systems. Parliament delegates executive power to a cabinet, which delegates policy areas to ministers. Proportional representation (PR) elections usually give no party a majority, so coalitions must be built. Strom's data show four government types. A German example (four parties, sixteen possible governments) uses a spatial model. If parties seek policy, then under the example's assumptions the Christian Democrat–Free Democrat government (CD–FD) has an empty winset (no alternative a majority prefers), so it is stable. Under a veto assumption, an all-CD minority government is stable too. Rational actors reason ahead about institutions in parliamentary settings too, not only in the US.

**Prerequisites:** spatial model with ideal points and indifference curves; majority rule, winsets and cycling (Ch. 5); principal–agent delegation; Duverger's Law (Ch. 7).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Parliamentary systems fuse powers; they do not separate them. Parliament is supreme and the cabinet answers to it (pp. 503–504).
- Most parliamentary democracies use PR, which tends to produce many parties. A majority party is rare, so government formation means coalition building (pp. 504–507).
- Authority runs through chained principal–agent relations: parliament → cabinet → ministers. Parliament's control over governments is called "making and breaking governments" (p. 506).
- Four government types are distinguished, with frequencies from Strom's data (1945–82). Multiparty majority is the most common (.500), and governments are divided about seven-eighths of the time (pp. 510–513).
- Parties may be modelled as seeking office or policy. The author conjectures policy-seeking is more compelling (pp. 517–519).
- In the policy-seeking example, each government (an allocation of portfolios) is associated with a point on a lattice (a grid of 16 policy points). Because choice is limited to these points rather than the whole policy plane, the German example has an equilibrium (an empty-winset government), although multidimensional majority rule typically has none (pp. 522–524).
- A second equilibrium type exists. If no party can be forced into a government against its will, a majority may prefer other governments to the incumbent all-CD government, yet each of those includes CD, which can veto it. This shows how a minority government can arise and survive (pp. 525–527).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pp. 503–504).** Voting, agenda setting and division of labour are not uniquely American. Parliamentary democracies rest on "fusion of powers", not separation of powers. The centrepieces are parliamentary supremacy and cabinet accountability to parliament (pp. 503–504).

**The parliamentary setting — electoral arrangements (pp. 504–505).**
- First-past-the-post (plurality winner per district) is used in the US and Great Britain. It encourages two-party competition per district (Duverger's Law). If the same two parties compete everywhere, one gets a majority (p. 504).
- Most parliamentary regimes, especially in continental Europe, use proportional representation (PR) formulas. These tend to produce many parties, so a single-party majority is rare (p. 504).
- Hybrids exist (Germany, Japan, New Zealand; fn. 1, p. 504).
- Hence multiparty negotiation is usually needed to organize government (p. 505).

**Governmental arrangements (pp. 505–506).**
- Parliament elects the government (cabinet, council of ministers). It is a set of senior politicians, each heading a ministry (p. 505).
- Division of labour occurs at two levels: legislature vs. executive, and ministry vs. ministry. The legislature selects and may replace the executive and considers legislation, above all the annual budget (pp. 505–506).
- Each ministry has jurisdiction over a *ministerial portfolio* (a policy area), "quite parallel" to US congressional committees. The minister and civil servants have considerable discretion (p. 506).
- This is a chain of principal–agent relations. Parliament delegates to the cabinet, and the cabinet delegates to ministers. Both use before-the-fact and after-the-fact controls, and usually the prime minister plays the cabinet's "policeman and maestro" (p. 506).

**Government formation process (pp. 506–509).**
- A cycle begins with a trigger such as an election. Each party has an endowment of seats and policy positions. Parties may be treated as unitary actors because parliamentary parties are far more homogeneous and disciplined than US ones (fn. 5, p. 507).
- If fortunes changed, a new cabinet is highly likely, reflecting a coalition (p. 507).
- Some regimes require a formal investiture vote, and others do not. In all cases the government survives at parliament's pleasure (p. 507).
- A motion of no confidence, if passed, forces resignation. The government may also stake itself on a confidence motion, or attach one to a bill, to raise the stakes (p. 508, fn. 7).
- The head of state appoints a *formateur* (who tries to assemble a government), sometimes after an *informateur* (an elder statesman) advises. The constitution or convention may restrict this choice (p. 508).
- The formateur negotiates a distribution of portfolios that a majority will support. Parties holding portfolios are government parties; the rest are opposition (p. 509).
- The government needs firm control of parliamentary politics, including setting parliament's agenda, but remains subject to parliament's confidence: it "governs subject to keeping the confidence of parliament" (p. 509).

**A menagerie of governments (pp. 509–513).** Table 16.1 crosses the number of government parties (one or coalition) with their seat share (majority or minority). Proportions are from Strom, Western democracies 1945–1982 (p. 511).

- *Unified:* it occurs often in Britain but rarely elsewhere. Its main threat is faction defection (p. 510; the 1994 Major example, fn. 8).
- *Multiparty majority:* ordinary party discipline is typically enough. The bigger threat is a partner leaving, or demanding a portfolio reshuffle (pp. 510–511).
- *Single-party minority:* it survives because the opposition majority cannot agree on an alternative. Example: a 40% social democratic party faces radical-left, extreme-right and religious parties of about 20% each. The author says it is quite likely that whatever the left prefers to the social democrats is opposed by the right and religious parties, and vice versa (p. 512).
- *Minority coalition:* these typically split the opposition (p. 512).
- *Divided government:* in the US this means the president's party lacks a legislative majority. Pundits say it causes gridlock, but fn. 10 notes that Reagan succeeded in his first term without a House majority (pp. 512–513). By Table 16.1, a single-party minority government is structurally the same as US divided government. Multiparty majorities are divided within the executive, and minority coalitions in both ways. European governments are divided about seven-eighths of the time, roughly like postwar America. Parliamentary government is "no cure" for it (p. 513).

*Intuition note:* A government can survive without a majority. Opponents who agree on "no" need not agree on a replacement. Divided government is not special to presidential systems.

**Analyzing government formation (pp. 513–514).** The author develops one detailed example. It typifies many real possibilities but is hardly typical. The aim is to show how rational politicians think through the formation game (p. 514).

**German example: setup (pp. 514–516).**
- Four parties: Christian Democrats (CD, centre-conservative), Social Democrats (SD, left, pacifist, progressive), Free Democrats (FD, free-market, moderate abroad, progressive socially) and Greens (G, more left than SD economically and abroad) (p. 514).
- Strategic structure: rarely a single-party majority. CD can form a majority with any one other party. Excluding CD requires all the rest (pp. 514–515).
- Majority coalitions: CD–SD, CD–FD, CD–G and SD–FD–G, plus any of these with extra parties (p. 515).
- With two key ministries, Finance (F) and Foreign Affairs (FA), there are $4 \times 4 = 16$ possible governments (Display 16.1, p. 516). With $p$ parties and $q$ portfolios there are $p^q$ governments (fn. 12; see Reading notes).

**Party preferences (pp. 516–519).** Two motivations have been seriously entertained (p. 516).
- *Office-seeking:* parties care only about being in office. Assuming all parties care more about Finance than FA, CD ranks: government 1 (both portfolios); then 2, 3, 4 (Finance only); then 5, 9, 13 (FA only); then the remaining nine (excluded), indifferent within each tier (Display 16.2, p. 517). Majority rankings could be computed from these, but that needs 120 pairwise comparisons. The author stops here because there is much indifference, and leaves it as a homework exercise (p. 518).
- *Policy-seeking:* the author conjectures this is more compelling, "arguably" more realistic. Individual politicians love office, but parties are agents of external constituencies that, the author says, don't really care who sits in cabinet but do care about policy. Party leaders therefore check private ambition (pp. 518–519). For the author's purposes, it does not matter whether parties are ideological or merely instrumental (fn. 13).
- Spatial map (Fig. 16.1): the horizontal axis is economic policy (Finance Ministry's jurisdiction), and the vertical axis is foreign policy (FA's jurisdiction). CD is the most activist abroad and fairly conservative economically (pp. 519–520).
- *Strong assumption:* everyone knows that whichever party holds a ministry will implement its ideal point on that dimension. This means others have virtually no influence on a minister, except by toppling the government. The author calls it "very unrealistic", but says all models are unrealistic and the test is insight (p. 520 and fn. 14, pp. 520–521).
- Hence a party with both portfolios implements its ideal point. With one portfolio it implements its ideal on that dimension only. Example: CD (Finance) + FD (FA) implements CD's economic ideal and FD's foreign-policy ideal (p. 521).

**Equilibrium in government formation (pp. 521–527).**
- The four horizontal and four vertical lines through the ideal points intersect in 16 points. These are the *lattice of possible governments* (pp. 521–522).
- Assume parties prefer policies closer to their ideal. For (CD,FD) (first-named holds Finance), draw a circle centred on CD's ideal through the (CD,FD) point. This is CD's indifference curve, and its interior is a preferred-to set. Only two governments are inside it: all-CD and (FD,CD) (p. 522).
- Draw the other three parties' circles through the same point. Each majority's preferred set is the overlap of its members' circles, the shaded "petals" in Fig. 16.1. Some policies are preferred by each of the four majorities, but no lattice government is (p. 523).
- So (CD,FD) has an empty winset against the other fifteen governments. It is an equilibrium: a no-confidence motion fails because a majority will not oust a government without a preferred replacement (p. 523). The author says this was in fact the government that dominated German politics for the last quarter century, with occasional exceptions (fn. 15).
- This is remarkable because multidimensional majority rule usually has no equilibrium (Ch. 5). It exists here because choice is over the 16 lattice points, not the whole plane. The process chooses governments, not policies; otherwise parliament would run the country directly (p. 524).
- Further findings (p. 524): (a) lattice examples with no equilibrium can be built, where majority preferences cycle over all lattice points, but systematic empirical work (Laver and Shepsle) suggests equilibrium is very likely. (b) Two distinct empty-winset points are "virtually impossible". Informal proof (fn. 17): for lattice points $x$ and $y$, either some majority prefers $x$ to $y$, or $y$ to $x$, or they tie. In the first two cases the loser has a non-empty winset, so more than one empty-winset point is possible only under a tie.

*Intuition note:* Parliament can only pick among packages that are available. In this example, choosing among 16 lattice points instead of the whole policy plane is why an equilibrium exists (p. 524).

**A second kind of equilibrium (pp. 524–527).**
- Take the all-CD minority government. The only majority excluding CD is SD–FD–G. Their indifference curves through the CD ideal overlap in the winset $W(CD,CD)$, which contains three lattice points, all including CD (Fig. 16.2, p. 525):
$$W(CD,CD) = \{(CD,FD),(CD,SD),(CD,G)\}$$
(the text layer prints a dash; see Reading notes).
- *Author's added assumption:* no party can be forced into a government against its will, so each party holds a veto over every government it joins (p. 526).
- Then CD can veto every government in the winset, and no government that CD cannot veto is preferred by a majority. So no motion of no confidence succeeds (p. 526).
- How could it arise? Suppose CD once held a majority and governed alone, then lost its majority at an election. As the incumbent it stays until replaced, and the new majority cannot find a veto-proof replacement (p. 526).
- Two equilibrium types: (1) an *empty-winset government* (Fig. 16.1), and (2) a government that other allocations beat, but only through allocations open to veto (Fig. 16.2) (p. 527).

*Intuition note:* A party that already holds its ideal policy loses from any change, so it will not join a coalition to replace itself. The only majority without CD (SD–FD–G) prefers only lattice governments that include CD, and CD can veto those.

**Conclusion (p. 527).** Parliamentary institutions also divide and specialize labour, delegate authority and use before- and after-the-fact controls. If office-seeking drives parties, they include their own at the cabinet table. If policy-seeking drives them, they calculate over implemented policies. The example is one illustration of how to analyze politics in any institutional context (p. 527).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three parties, seats A 40, B 35, C 25. Any two parties form a majority. Policy space: (economy, foreign). Finance sets the first coordinate and FA the second. Ideals: A=(0,0), B=(4,0), C=(0,3).
- *Lattice.* First coordinates are 0 (A or C) or 4 (B). Second coordinates are 0 (A or B) or 3 (C). So there are four policy points: (0,0), (0,3), (4,0), (4,3).
- *Test the all-A government at (0,0).* A is at its ideal, so A prefers nothing else. Only B+C could replace it.
  - B (4,0) is 4 from (0,0). It is 5 from (0,3), since $\sqrt{16+9}=5$, so no. It is 3 from (4,3), so yes.
  - C (0,3) is 3 from (0,0). It is 5 from (4,0), so no. It is 4 from (4,3), so no.
- No lattice point is preferred by both B and C. So (0,0) has an empty winset and is a stable minority government.
- In the continuous plane, B's circle (radius 4) and C's circle (radius 3) have centres 5 apart, so they overlap. For example, (2,1.5) is $\sqrt{4+2.25}\approx 2.5$ from both ideals, so both prefer it. B and C prefer some policy to A's, but no government delivers it.

## Concepts
- [[principal–agent problem / delegation]] — parliament → cabinet → ministers, each delegating with before- and after-the-fact controls (p. 506)
- [[spatial model of voting]] — parties' ideal points on two policy dimensions; closer is better (pp. 519, 522)
- [[cycling / intransitivity]] — in lattice examples with no equilibrium, majority preferences cycle over all lattice points (p. 524)
- [[veto player]] — here, each party has a veto over every government it would join, since it can refuse to join (p. 526)
- [[institutions]] — institutional arrangements of parliamentary democracies, organized on the fusion of powers (p. 503)
- [[agenda control / agenda setter]] — the government sets parliament's agenda (p. 509)
- [[fusion-of-powers]] (new) — parliament supreme, executive accountable to it, in contrast to separation of powers (p. 503)
- [[government-formation]] (new) — formateur-led bargaining over a distribution of portfolios that a majority supports (pp. 507–509)
- [[ministerial-portfolio]] (new) — a ministry's policy jurisdiction, in which the minister has considerable discretion (p. 506)
- [[no-confidence-motion]] (new) — a parliamentary vote that, if passed, forces the government to resign (p. 508)
- [[office-seeking-vs-policy-seeking]] (new) — parties value cabinet seats only, or the policies governments implement (pp. 517–519)
- [[lattice-of-governments]] (new) — the policy points from assigning each portfolio to a party (up to $p^q$ possible governments, fn. 12, p. 515); 16 in the German example (p. 522)
- [[empty-winset-equilibrium]] (new) — a government that no majority prefers any alternative to (p. 523)
- [[divided-government]] (new) — executive's party lacks a legislative majority; in parliamentary systems also splits within coalitions (p. 513)

## Questions this raises

- How often do empirical governments match the empty-winset or veto equilibria (the author cites Laver–Shepsle chs. 6–9)?
- If office-seeking is combined with policy-seeking, what do party rankings of governments look like?
