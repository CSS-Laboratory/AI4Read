# Final Lessons

## L0 — One line
Shepsle closes by claiming that the rational-choice method of analysing politics is portable: it applies to any group setting, private or public, and across many disciplines.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This short closing chapter recaps the four parts of the book. Part I covers individuals, preferences and beliefs. Part II covers group choice by voting, where outcomes depend partly on arbitrary features of the process. Part III covers cooperation and its failures. Part IV covers institutions. Shepsle then draws lessons. To analyse any group situation, ask what people want, what they believe, what actions are feasible, and what rules turn actions into outcomes. History often helps. The method fits "small p" private politics as well as "big p" public politics. It also spans several disciplines and many parts of political science. The reader is sent to the wider literature.

**Prerequisites:** the book's earlier chapters (preferences, majority rule, Arrow's Theorem, collective action, institutions); no new math.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Part I's building blocks are individuals, preferences and beliefs. The book's chosen approach is rational choice (p. 530).
- Groups are not always unitary actors (not always treatable like a single individual with coherent preferences). Majority rule, and by Arrow's Theorem any other "reasonable" aggregation method, cannot guarantee rational group preferences (p. 530).
- Because of this, agenda controllers can manipulate outcomes. Group choice is in many respects "artifactual" (a product of the process), since it depends on process features as well as on preferences (p. 530).
- Many modern difficulties are manifestations of large groups' inability to coordinate. In equilibrium, public goods are underproduced and public "bads" overproduced (p. 531).
- Societies respond by inventing institutions, which variously serve efficiency, coordination, selection, implementation, leadership and validation (p. 531).
- The method is portable. Analysing a group needs wants, beliefs, feasible actions and interaction rules, and often some history (p. 532).
- Politics exists wherever there are groups, not only in official forums (p. 532).
- Rational choice spans several disciplines and many parts of political science, including comparative politics, international relations and political philosophy (pp. 532–533).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Recap of Part I.** It supplied the basic building blocks of political analysis: individuals, their preferences and their beliefs. Shepsle stresses the importance of analysis and says the rational choice paradigm is the analytical approach he chose (p. 530).

**Recap of Part II (group choice by voting).**
- Groups, unlike individuals, cannot always be treated as unitary, because members' aggregated preferences do not always "add up" (p. 530).
- Neither majority rule nor, by Arrow's Theorem, any other "reasonable" method of preference aggregation can guarantee rational group preferences (p. 530).
- Therefore people who control motion making and other agenda mechanisms can strategically manipulate outcomes to their advantage (p. 530).
- Elections and votes can be staged in many ways. So the result of group deliberation is, "in many respects", artifactual (p. 530).
- It depends on members' preferences and beliefs, but also on who makes motions, their order, the voting rule and other features (p. 530).
- Shepsle's evaluation: one might want outcomes to depend on individual preferences and beliefs. It is "considerably less clear" that depending on arbitrary process features is desirable (pp. 530–531).

*Intuition note:* A group verdict is not just the sum of what members want. The path to the vote can change the result, and Shepsle finds it considerably less clear that such arbitrary process features should matter.

**Recap of Part III (groups as ongoing entities).**
- Many group decisions are taken by methods other than voting (p. 531).
- Shepsle took "cooperation" as fundamental. He built up from two-person bargaining (one example: whether or not to drain a marsh) to multiperson coordination and collective action problems (p. 531).
- Many difficulties of modern societies manifest large groups' inability to coordinate behaviour in mutually profitable ways (p. 531).
- Consequently, in equilibrium, public goods are underproduced and public "bads" overproduced. There are too many negative and too few positive externalities. Common resources are mismanaged, overutilized and poorly superintended (p. 531).
- Societies are not utopias, and there is always room for improvement (p. 531).

**Recap of Part IV (institutions).**
- Societies invent institutions to improve matters (p. 531).
- Some institutions allow more efficient performance through division and specialization of labour. Others enhance coordination, standardization and the "rationalization" of expectations (p. 531).
- Some make selections (legislatures, parliaments). Some implement selected options (bureaucracies). Some orchestrate (leadership institutions). Some validate (courts and judges) (p. 531).

*Intuition note:* Institutions are the author's answer to the failures of Part III. They improve efficiency and coordination and handle selection, implementation, leadership and validation.

**Lessons: a portable method.**
- The methodology can be taken along to novel political situations (p. 531).
- Group settings range from two-person partnerships through rooming groups and families to congregations and amateur sports leagues. Politics attaches to all of them (pp. 531–532).
- To analyse such a situation one needs to appreciate:
  - what individuals want;
  - what they believe;
  - which actions are feasible for them;
  - the rules of interaction, the mechanisms that turn rationally chosen individual actions into outcomes (p. 532).
- Some history is often useful. It helps in working out what people want, believe, and think others want and believe (p. 532).

**Lessons: "small p" and "big p" politics.** The method applies to the "small p" politics of private life as well as to the "big p" politics of public life. Politics is not confined to official forums. In his words: "The politics of the kitchen table is as interesting and complex as the politics of the cabinet table" (p. 532).

**Lessons: breadth of rational choice.**
- Rational choice analysis spans political science, sociology, economics, law and philosophy, and "probably" other subjects (p. 532).
- Within political science it is not limited to American politics, though many examples came from there. Chapter 16 illustrated that rational choice approaches to parliamentary politics are well developed (p. 532).
- Other parts of comparative politics are also well developed. International relations (national security, trade, other strategic interaction) has been analysed this way "for many decades" (p. 532).
- Political philosophy also lends itself to the approach. Shepsle finds it hard to imagine a complete examination of the just, good or fair without considering what is rational for those involved (pp. 532–533).

**Close.** If the reader took these lessons away, writing and reading the book was worthwhile. If not, return to Chapter 1. Shepsle encourages reading the growing literature, some of it footnoted in the book. He believes the approach enriches political study, but the reader has "only scratched the surface" (p. 533).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text.)* It illustrates the Part II lesson that the process, not just preferences, can decide the outcome. Three voters rank options A, B, C, best first:
- Voter 1: A > B > C
- Voter 2: B > C > A
- Voter 3: C > A > B

Pairwise majorities:
- A vs B: voters 1 and 3 prefer A, so A wins 2–1.
- B vs C: voters 1 and 2 prefer B, so B wins 2–1.
- C vs A: voters 2 and 3 prefer C, so C wins 2–1.

The majority preference cycles (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A). Now vary the agenda, where the winner of the first vote meets the remaining option:
- A vs B first: A wins, then A vs C, and C wins. **Outcome C.**
- B vs C first: B wins, then B vs A, and A wins. **Outcome A.**
- A vs C first: C wins, then C vs B, and B wins. **Outcome B.**

The preferences are identical in all three runs, yet each option wins under some agenda. Whoever sets the agenda can steer the result.

## Questions this raises
- If group outcomes are partly artifactual, which process features are acceptable and which are arbitrary?
- How far does the same analysis go in small-p settings such as families, and what are its limits?
- Which parts of the analysis (wants, beliefs, feasible actions, rules) are hardest to observe in a real case?
- What role should history play in fixing the preferences and beliefs the analyst assumes?
