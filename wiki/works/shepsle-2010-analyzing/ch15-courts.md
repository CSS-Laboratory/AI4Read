# Courts and Judges

## L0 — One line
Shepsle argues that higher-court judges can be modelled as "legislators in robes" with policy preferences, whose influence often works through other actors' anticipation of their reactions rather than through visible rulings.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Rational-choice models fit elected politicians well, but judges are puzzling. Lifetime tenure and fixed pay weaken the usual incentives. Shepsle first sets out what courts do: they resolve disputes, coordinate expectations beforehand, and interpret rules. He then reviews Posner's motivational "types" of judges. He proposes treating higher-court judges as policy-seeking legislators in robes. In a one-dimensional spatial model with an agency, a court and a legislature, the court's position can shape policy even when the court is inactive, because the agent accommodates it in advance. Shepsle admits that some students of judicial politics doubt this view, and says he shares some of their doubts.

**Prerequisites:** one-dimensional spatial model; single-peaked preferences; median voter theorem; separation of powers; the McNollgast agency-drift model from Chapter 13 (briefly recalled in this unit).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Courts do three jobs: after-the-fact dispute resolution, before-the-fact coordination of expectations, and rule interpretation. Courts matter even when few disputes arise (pp. 475–480).
- Judges are hard to model because tenure and fixed pay weaken the link between performance and reward (pp. 473–474, 486).
- Posner's "types" (nonprofit manager, voter, spectator, game player) each capture a different aspect of judicial behaviour (pp. 481–485). Shepsle reads the review as confirming that judges are enigmatic actors (p. 486).
- Shepsle's own hypothesis is the "legislator in robes": higher-court judges care about policy, and, since big-impact cases are hard to spot in advance, they may treat each case as though it might have impact (pp. 486–488).
- In the separation-of-powers game, a court's median ideal point (the ideal of its middle justice on the policy line) can bound the agency's choice. A court whose median lies inside the legislative equilibrium set (the policies between the House and Senate medians, which neither chamber will move away from) checks bureaucratic drift (the agent's pull toward its own ideal policy) (pp. 490–495).
- Judicial influence is often unobservable because it works through anticipation. Visible court activity should be expected where parameters have changed unnoticed (p. 496).
- Doubts remain: some scholars see jurists as principled, not strategic. Shepsle shares some doubts but replies that principled consistency is not necessarily at odds with a strategic view (p. 497).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 473–474)
- Simple behavioural hypotheses work for elected politicians: reelection, policy goals, influence. Judges resist being classed with politicians and do not sell their services. Their pay is fixed independently of performance (p. 473).
- **Posner's puzzle:** economic analysis can model many actors but "falters" on judges, because judicial employment rules remove carrots and sticks (p. 474).
- Lifetime tenure weakens ties to "constituencies". Some state and local judges are elected, but higher-court judges are typically appointed for life (p. 474, n. 2).
- The chapter has two parts: judges as political players, and courts in separation-of-powers relations (p. 474).

### Courts: a brief overview (pp. 475–480)
- **Dispute resolution.** Courts establish facts, punish violators and compensate victims. Parties can then spend less on self-protection and monitoring (p. 475).
  - Transaction costs are the costs of negotiating, monitoring and enforcing agreements. Courts economise on them (p. 475, n. 4).
  - *Civil* disputes are between private parties. *Criminal* disputes also involve "the public", because a public law is alleged to be violated. The embezzlement example violates both a private promise and a public law (p. 476).
  - The judge manages fact-finding and judgment, sometimes with a jury (p. 476).
- **Coordination.** Courts also work before the fact. Anticipating what happens in court lets parties form expectations and coordinate "in the shadow of the law" (p. 477).
  - The system matters as much for what it does not do (the "Silver Blaze" dog) (p. 477).
  - Incentives work through their power, clarity and consistent administration. Bright-line standards, consistently applied, encourage settlement ahead of time (pp. 477–478).
- **Rule interpretation.** Judges must decide what statutes and principles mean and which fit the case (p. 478).
  - The system is hierarchical. Higher-court rulings constrain lower courts, and federal law and its interpretations often trump state and local law (pp. 478–479).
  - At the top, courts also interpret constitutions. Rulings carry precedential authority (p. 479).
  - **Review.** Congress can amend a statute to reverse a court's statutory interpretation. It cannot reverse a constitutional ruling by ordinary legislation, but it can begin a constitutional amendment (p. 480).
- Conclusion of the section: courts are political institutions and judges political players (p. 480).

*Intuition note:* A court's biggest effect may be invisible. If people expect clear, consistently applied rulings, there may be fewer disputes to resolve. Because rulings can be overridden, courts sit inside politics, not above it.

### Who are the judges, and what do they want? (pp. 481–488)
**Posner's views** (reported by Shepsle):
- *Ordinary people.* Judges are mostly ordinary people, so rationality is a fitting hypothesis (p. 481).
- **The nonprofit analogy.** Official judges draw fixed salaries, cannot keep a residual, and have tenure. Posner expects them on average to work less hard than lawyers of comparable age and talent. The appointment and confirmation filters might select for industrious people (pp. 481–482).
  - Possible utility elements: popularity among court colleagues, prestige, academic reputation, track record (e.g., not being reversed). Posner doubts these are central (p. 482).
- **The voting analogy.** Judging is partly voting. Judges sit alone (district), typically in panels of three (appeals), or among at most nine (Supreme Court), so being pivotal is much more likely than for a mass voter (p. 484).
  - Judges also "vote" with ideas, through opinions that constrain lower courts (pp. 484–485).
- **Spectator and game player.** The judge engages actively with the drama, puzzling over facts and moral principles, and gets pleasure from playing (p. 485).

**Shepsle's own views:**
- He gives somewhat more weight than Posner to ambition. Judges, like other nonprofit professionals, may hope to rise. Odds of advancement are remote, so, as Posner notes, ambition cannot be a tremendously strong motivation. Popularity, prestige and reputation are valued partly for themselves and partly as steps to advancement. He suspects many judges probably exaggerate their chances (pp. 482–484).
- Judges are "enigmatic" because tenure and fixed pay weaken the performance–pay link. Posner's category "judge" is too broad, like lumping presidents with dogcatchers (p. 486).
- He therefore focuses on ambitious, policy-relevant judges of higher courts: "legislators in robes" (p. 486).

**Legislators in robes.**
- Legislators in spatial models have an ideal policy and declining preference with distance. Two possible reasons: they care about policy (philosophy of government), or political ambition leads them to serve constituents' policy interests (pp. 486–487).
- Higher courts shape the legal context for millions and so shape public policy. Big-impact cases are hard to spot in advance. As a first approximation, judges with policy preferences treat each case as possibly consequential (pp. 487–488).
- Shepsle expects most sitting judges to be uncomfortable with this "radical view". He says the view becomes compelling if it leads to interesting insights about how politics works (p. 488).

**Case 15.1 (Bacon; pp. 488–489).** Shepsle says judges share politicians' motives except electoral ambition. The ABA canons (self-interest, independence) probably make judges less venal than elected politicians, because violations give grounds for dismissal. They probably give much weaker incentives to leave ideology outside the courtroom (p. 489).

*Intuition note:* A judge with no reelection worry may still want to shape policy. The model treats the judge's preferences as given, as it does for a legislator.

### Courts and intergovernmental relations (pp. 490–496)
- **Separation of powers.** The legislature formulates policy. The executive implements it. Courts, when asked, rule on its faithfulness to the statute (statutory rationale) or the constitution (constitutional rationale). Legislators unhappy with a ruling can recraft the statute (statutory case) or begin an amendment (constitutional case) (p. 490).
- **Marksist model** (after Brian Marks). Policy space is $[0,100]$, with single-peaked preferences for all actors. Ideals are $x^J_1,\dots,x^J_9$ (justices), $x^S_1,\dots,x^S_{100}$ (senators), $x^H_1,\dots,x^H_{435}$ (representatives), and $x^A$ (agent). Veto issues are ignored. Near 0 is the liberal end, near 100 the conservative end. The affirmative-action illustration puts set-asides near 0, outreach in the middle and opponents near 100 (pp. 491–492).
- **Simplifications.** Each chamber is a simple majority-rule body. Given single-peaked preferences, Black's median-voter theorem lets each majority-rule body (House, Senate, court) be represented by its median ideal, so the analysis focuses on $x^H$, $x^S$ and $x^J$. The model has five parameters $x^Q, x^J, x^H, x^S, x^A$, where $x^Q$ is the status quo (p. 492).
- **Sequence (Display 15.1)** (p. 493):

| Step | Player | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | — | $x^Q$ in place |
| 1 | agent | implements policy $A$ |
| 2 | court | strikes $A$ or lets it stand; if it strikes, names the new policy $J$ |
| 3 | legislature | if unhappy with $A$ or $J$, selects new policy $L$ |

  - Only the statutory rationale is considered. The court can "legislate" once it strikes down. In step 3 any new legislation is implemented and the game ends (p. 493).
- **Figure 15.1** places the points in the order $x^A < x^H < x^Q < x^S$ (p. 494).
  - Any point from $x^H$ to $x^S$ is an equilibrium, because a legislative reaction needs both chambers (p. 494, n. 11).
  - With no court, the agent implements $A = x^H$, the closest policy to its ideal that triggers no legislative response (p. 494).
- **Case analysis by the median justice's ideal $x^J$:**
  - $x^J \in [0, x^A]$ or $x^J \in [x^A, x^H]$: the court cannot improve on $x^H$ and lets $A = x^H$ stand. The court looks irrelevant, but the agent chose as she did because she knew the court would not intervene (pp. 494–495).
  - $x^J \in [x^H, x^S]$: the court can strike any action and name $J = x^J$. This lies in the legislative equilibrium set, so the legislature does not react. An agent who dislikes reversal anticipates this and names $A = x^J$. A court inside the equilibrium set therefore checks bureaucratic drift (p. 495).
  - $x^J \in [x^S, 100)$: the court strikes any action and names $J = x^S$. A policy closer to $x^J$ would trigger a legislative response to a policy in $[x^H, x^S]$ that is worse for the median justice than $x^S$. An anticipating agent names $A = x^S$ and the court lets it stand. Even if the agent chose $x^J$ itself, the court would still strike it and name $x^S$, for the same reason (p. 495).
- **Conclusion of the section.** The court's role may be "subtle and unobservable". Equilibrium is sustained by anticipation, so the court is often inactive because others have already accommodated it. Proactive court involvement is expected where parameters have changed but others have not noticed or reacted properly (p. 496).

*Intuition note:* The court's threat to strike down and rewrite policy is credible only up to the point where the legislature would overrule it. So the agent moves toward the court's preferred point within the legislature's tolerance. An observer then sees agreement and may wrongly conclude that the court does nothing.

### Conclusion (pp. 496–497)
- Shepsle believes the legislator-in-robes view is an extremely plausible way to think about judges in a separation-of-powers regime. It gives provocative and often nonobvious implications that can, in principle, be tested (p. 496).
- **Doubt (Segal and other students of judicial politics).** Higher-court judges are professional jurists with a consistent judicial philosophy. Legislators strategise, jurists do not (p. 497).
- **Shepsle's reply.** He shares some of these doubts, but says consistency in opinions is not necessarily at odds with strategy. When others internalise the court's possible response, the court need not act strategically. The strategic view rests on the potential for intervention and others' belief that judges will act strategically if their preferences are not accommodated (p. 497).

### Case 15.2 (pp. 498–499)
- Shepsle turns to judges' own writings. Jackson says the charge that the Court makes political decisions is answered "with less candor" than any other; Shepsle reads this as a sign of more politics than justices would admit. Oaks says interpretation cannot be free of personal preference. Markey says judges are people with values (p. 498–499).
- **Shepsle adds:** because lawmaking by judges is inevitable, it is nothing to be ashamed of. Congress often delegates lawmaking to courts through "fire extinguisher" oversight, which gives citizens and groups legal standing to work out legislative detail in court (p. 499).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take $x^A = 20$, $x^H = 40$, $x^Q = 50$, $x^S = 60$. This keeps the order of Figure 15.1. The legislative equilibrium set is $[40, 60]$.
- **Court at $x^J = 10$** (left of $x^A$): without a court the agent picks $A = x^H = 40$. The court cannot improve on 40, so it lets 40 stand. Result: 40.
- **Court at $x^J = 50$** (inside $[40,60]$): the court could strike anything and name $J = 50$. This is in the equilibrium set, so the legislature stays quiet. The agent anticipates this and picks $A = 50$. Result: 50, with no visible court action.
- **Court at $x^J = 90$:** the best the court can get is $J = 60 = x^S$, at distance $|90 - 60| = 30$. If the court let a policy near 90 stand, the legislature would move to a point in $[40, 60]$. Any such point other than 60 is farther from 90 than 60 is (e.g., 40 is at distance 50). So the court strikes any other policy and names 60. The agent picks 60 directly. Result: 60.

As the court's median moves from 10 to 50 to 90, policy moves 40 → 50 → 60, although the court never visibly acts in any of the three cases.

## Concepts
- [[legislator-in-robes]] (new) — judges treated as policy-seeking actors like legislators, without electoral ambition (pp. 486, 489)
- [[spatial-model-of-voting]] — actors have ideal points on $[0,100]$ with preferences declining with distance (p. 491)
- [[single-peaked-preferences]] — preferences highest at the ideal point, declining on both sides (p. 491)
- [[median-voter-theorem]] — Black's theorem; the median ideal represents each majority-rule body (p. 492)
- [[principal-agent-problem]] — raised in Problem 2 about lifetime tenure; the model also treats the agency as an agent (pp. 493, 500)
- [[transaction-costs]] (new) — costs of negotiating, monitoring and enforcing agreements; courts reduce them (p. 475, n. 4)
- [[courts-as-coordination-mechanism]] (new) — expected court outcomes let parties form expectations in advance, "in the shadow of the law" (p. 477)
- [[separation-of-powers]] (new) — legislature formulates, executive implements, courts rule on faithfulness to statute or constitution (p. 490)
- [[anticipatory-accommodation]] (new) — actors pre-empt a court's or legislature's reaction, so the reaction is rarely observed (p. 496)
- [[legislative-equilibrium-set]] (new) — policies between $x^H$ and $x^S$; no legislative move is possible because either chamber blocks it (p. 494, n. 11)
- [[institutions]] — courts treated as political institutions in a rules-of-the-game setting (p. 480)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Legislative equilibrium set $[x^H, x^S]$ ↔ veto player: policy stable when both chambers' agreement is needed to move it — similar blocking logic (p. 494, n. 11).
- [structural-analogy] Agent anticipating the court ↔ principal–agent problem: an agent who forecasts a principal's reaction pre-empts it, so no sanction is observed.
- [metaphor] Courts' "shadow of the law" ↔ deterrence in everyday contracting: behaviour shaped by expected enforcement.

## Questions this raises
- Can the model's predictions about unobserved court influence be tested, given that influence works through anticipation?
- If judges have policy preferences, how are they selected, and how do appointments shift the median justice (cf. Problem 3)?
- If judges are strategic only potentially, what observable behaviour would separate a principled jurist from a strategic one?
- How would the results change with a constitutional rationale or with a presidential veto, which the model sets aside?
