# Downs, A. (1957). *An Economic Theory of Democracy*. New York: Harper. Companion article: Downs, A. (1957). An economic theory of political action in a democracy. *Journal of Political Economy*, 65(2), 135–150. doi:10.1086/257897

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[downs-1957-democracy/main]].

## In one line
If parties seek office only for its rewards and information is costly, then ideologies, unequal influence (given unequal wealth), lobbying and widespread voter ignorance all follow as rational (efficient) behaviour, not as signs of irrationality.

## The gist
Downs says most welfare economists and many public finance theorists implicitly assume that government *should* maximise social welfare, and that few ask why governors would want to. Downs instead assumes parties are teams seeking the income, power and prestige of office, so they make policy to win votes, as firms sell products for money. He first assumes perfect information, then costly information. Costly information produces persuasion, ideologies, a spatial logic of party competition, and rational ignorance, because one vote almost never decides an election. Lobbying favours producers over consumers. Downs argues that apathy, ignorance and look-alike parties can be explained as efficient responses, and that a theory of government must be both economic and political.

**Prerequisites:** marginal cost vs marginal return; utility; oligopoly competition (Hotelling's linear market); single-peaked, normal, polarised and multimodal distributions.
