# An Economic Theory of Political Action in a Democracy (Downs, 1957, JPE)

## L0 — One line
If parties seek office only for its rewards and information is costly, then ideologies, unequal influence (given unequal wealth), lobbying and widespread voter ignorance all follow as rational (efficient) behaviour, not as signs of irrationality.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Downs says most welfare economists and many public finance theorists implicitly assume that government *should* maximise social welfare, and that few ask why governors would want to. Downs instead assumes parties are teams seeking the income, power and prestige of office, so they make policy to win votes, as firms sell products for money. He first assumes perfect information, then costly information. Costly information produces persuasion, ideologies, a spatial logic of party competition, and rational ignorance, because one vote almost never decides an election. Lobbying favours producers over consumers. Downs argues that apathy, ignorance and look-alike parties can be explained as efficient responses, and that a theory of government must be both economic and political.

**Prerequisites:** marginal cost vs marginal return; utility; oligopoly competition (Hotelling's linear market); single-peaked, normal, polarised and multimodal distributions.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Downs argues that welfare-economic views of government leave out the governors' motives. He models government as a team of office-seekers whose social function is a by-product of their private motive (pp. 135–137).
- Central hypothesis: parties in a democracy make policy strictly as a means of gaining votes. Whether this serves social welfare depends on how competition shapes their behaviour (p. 137).
- With perfect knowledge, democracy is a system of five simultaneous equations and equal franchise gives equal weight to each citizen. With imperfect knowledge, persuasion, representatives, lobbyists and favour-buyers make influence unequal (pp. 138–141).
- Ideologies are a supply-and-demand response to costly information: voters use them as cheap samples of party differences. Parties cannot change them freely (pp. 141–142).
- In a spatial left–right model, party positions depend on the distribution of voters. A roughly normal distribution gives look-alike parties, a polarised one gives divergence, and a multimodal one makes multiparty systems likely, whose coalition governments tend to be less coherent (pp. 142–145).
- It is irrational for most citizens to buy political information for voting, because the chance of a decisive vote is tiny. This is an indivisible-benefit problem that coercion cannot solve here (pp. 146–148).
- Lobbying needs far more information, so citizens specialise. Producers lobby more than consumers, which produces an anticonsumer bias (pp. 148–149).
- Apathy, ignorance, converging parties and anticonsumer bias are reinterpreted as efficient responses to costly information (p. 149).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### I. The problem (pp. 135–136)
- Economic theory has treated government action as exogenous (determined outside economics). Downs calls this a carry-over from the classical premise that the private sector regulates itself (p. 135).
- Public finance and welfare economics do give rules for what government *should* do. Dalton, Peck and Lerner are cited as examples. Downs says most welfare economists and many public finance theorists implicitly assume that government's proper function is to maximise social welfare (pp. 135–136).
- **Difficulty 1:** "social welfare" is unclear, and there is no agreement on how to maximise it. Arrow concluded that no rational method of maximising social welfare can be found unless strong restrictions are placed on individuals' preference orderings (p. 136).
- **Difficulty 2 (Downs's focus):** even if welfare were defined, why would the men who run government be *motivated* to maximise it? "Should" does not mean "will" (p. 136).
- **Schumpeter's point (cited):** the social meaning of an activity need not supply the motive for it (p. 136).
- Every agent in the division of labour has a private motive and a social function. Example: the coal-miner's social function is removing coal, but his motive is income. Much of economic theory shows that self-interested agents may still perform their functions efficiently, at least under certain conditions (p. 136).
- Hence a theory of government that ignores governors' motives is inconsistent with the main body of economics and stays at a purely normative level. Integration with general equilibrium theory needs a positive approach based on the governors' own selfish motives (p. 136).

*Intuition note:* Economics explains the coal-miner by his wish for income, not by the social need for coal. Downs asks us to treat politicians the same way instead of assuming they are public-spirited.

### II. Definitions, axioms and the central hypothesis (pp. 136–137)
- **Government:** the agency with power to coerce all other agents. Mostly, "government" in the analysis means the governing party (p. 136, n. 8).
- **Democracy** (p. 137):
  - (a) two or more parties compete in periodic elections;
  - (b) the party or coalition winning a majority controls government until the next election;
  - (c) losers do not block winners, and winners do not use office to cripple losers' ability to compete;
  - (d) all sane, law-abiding adults who are governed are citizens, and each has one and only one vote.
- **Axioms** (p. 137):
  1. A party is a *team* (members with identical goals) seeking office solely for income, prestige and power.
  2. The winner has complete control until the next election. There are no confidence votes and no sabotage by the bureaucracy.
  3. Government's economic powers are unlimited.
  4. The only limit is that the incumbent cannot in any way restrict the political freedom of opposition parties or individual citizens, unless they seek to overthrow it by force.
  5. Every agent behaves rationally. Here "rational" means efficient: minimal use of scarce resources, and acting only where marginal return exceeds marginal cost (p. 137, n. 10). It is not the logical or psychological sense.
- **Central hypothesis:** parties formulate policy *strictly* as a means of gaining votes, not to carry out preconceived policies or serve particular groups. Their social function (policy-making) is a by-product of their private motive (office) (p. 137).
- So the government acts to maximise votes. It is an entrepreneur selling policies for votes, and it competes for votes like oligopolists competing for sales. Whether the result maximises social welfare (if definable) depends on how competition shapes its behaviour. Downs says we cannot assume this a priori, just as we cannot assume a firm's output is socially optimal (p. 137).

*Intuition note:* Parties are firms, votes are revenue, policies are products. Whether competition makes the product good for society is a question to be answered, not assumed.

### III. A perfectly informed world (pp. 138–139)
- Voters, being rational, treat elections as a means of picking the government most beneficial to themselves. Each compares the utility income he actually received from the incumbent with what he believes he would have received under each opposition party (assuming opposition parties have taken verbal stands on every issue dealt with). Past performance is thus the main basis of the vote, not promises. This puts the decision on facts, not conjectures, though he adjusts for expected changes in a party's behaviour (p. 138).
- **Five propositions** (p. 138):
  1. Government actions depend on how it expects voters to vote and on the opposition's strategies.
  2. The government expects voters to vote according to (a) changes in their utility incomes from government activity and (b) the opposition's strategies.
  3. Voters actually vote according to (a) changes in utility incomes and (b) the alternatives the opposition offers.
  4. Voters' utility incomes depend on government actions in the election period.
  5. Opposition strategies depend on their views of voters' utility incomes and on government actions.
- These are five equations in five unknowns: expected votes, actual votes, opposition strategies, government actions, and individual utility incomes. So democracy can be viewed like a simultaneous-equation economic system (p. 138). With perfect information, propositions 2 and 3 are identical. With imperfect information they may differ (p. 138, n. 11).
- The incumbent must commit on each issue before the opposition, which can wait. That gives the opposition a strategic advantage and simplifies the analysis of interparty warfare (pp. 138–139).
- Downs does not develop party strategies here, because most conclusions would not carry over to the imperfect world (p. 139).
- One point is stressed. With perfect knowledge, government gives each citizen's preferences the same weight as every other's. This does not mean policies favour all equally, since strategy may lead it to woo some and ignore others. But it never deliberately gives up citizen A's vote to gain B's, since each has one vote and trading cannot gain anything, ceteris paribus. Equal franchise then distributes political power equally (p. 139).

### IV. Imperfect knowledge (p. 139)
- Imperfect knowledge means (1) parties do not always know what citizens want; (2) citizens do not always know what government or opposition has done, is doing, or should do; (3) information to cure both is costly (p. 139).
- Of its many effects, Downs concentrates on three: persuasion, ideologies, rational ignorance (p. 139).

### V. Persuasion and unequal influence (pp. 139–141)
- With perfect knowledge and fixed tastes, no citizen can influence another's vote, and a rational citizen cannot be persuaded (p. 139).
- With ignorance, some voters are uncertain which party they prefer, and persuaders can supply facts. Persuaders want a decision that aids their cause, so they supply only favourable facts. Even with no false data, a biased selection can influence others (pp. 139–140).
- **Consequence 1:** some men are politically more important, since they can swing more votes than they cast. Informing hesitant voters takes scarce resources, so those who command resources have more than proportional influence, ceteris paribus. Equal franchise no longer assures equal influence. Downs says it is irrational for a government to treat citizens with equal deference in this world (p. 140).
- **Consequence 2:** government is ignorant of what citizens want. It sends representatives to sound out and to persuade the electorate. Lack of information thus turns democratic government into representative government, decentralising power from the central planning board to agents. The board keeps decentralising until the marginal vote-gain from conforming to popular desires equals the marginal vote-loss from reduced co-ordination. So a democracy will run on a quasi-representative, quasi-decentralised basis whatever its formal constitution, as long as communication is imperfect. The division of labour pushes the same way, and representatives gain power as communication gets worse (p. 140).
- **Consequence 3:** because voters can be influenced, specialists in influencing them appear. Some pose as "representatives". They tell government that their policies, which benefit themselves, are desired by many, and they try to create that opinion. A rational government discounts their claims but cannot ignore them altogether. It must give them more than proportional weight, because they may have created favourable opinion in the silent mass and their vociferousness signals intensity. Intense-interest citizens base their vote on one issue, so government must pay them more attention than those who see it as one issue among many (pp. 140–141).
- **Bribery:** persuading voters needs resources (TV time, money, precinct captains). A government can sell policy favours for campaign contributions, favourable editorials or influence. Favour buyers need not pose as representatives, and the exchange is rational for both sides (p. 141).
- **Conclusion:** given unequal wealth, inequality of political influence is a *necessary* result of imperfect information. Effective political action requires economic resources to pay information costs. It is not caused by irrationality or dishonesty. Lobbying and government's submission to lobbyists are rational responses. To suppose otherwise is to theorise about a mythical world (p. 141).

*Intuition note:* When voters cannot judge for themselves, those who can pay to inform, persuade or lobby them gain extra weight. One person, one vote then no longer means equal influence.

### VI. Ideologies, spatial competition and party systems (pp. 141–145)
**Ideologies as a response to costly information** (pp. 141–142)
- Ideologies (defined as verbal images of "the good society" and the chief policies to create it, p. 141, n. 13) seem to contradict the hypothesis that parties have no interest in a particular society. Downs argues that they, and many of their characteristics, can be deduced from the office-seeking premise (p. 141).
- Comparing all policy differences costs a staggering amount of time, and voters do not always have enough information to appraise differences or know in advance what problems government will face (p. 141).
- Ideologies help voters in two ways. They focus attention on differences between parties and serve as samples of all the differentiating stands. And if a voter finds a correlation between a party's ideology and its policies, he can rationally vote by comparing ideologies, which cuts his information outlay (pp. 141–142).
- So lack of information creates demand for ideologies, and parties create the supply (p. 142).
- Limits on party freedom (p. 142):
  - A party cannot abandon or radically alter its ideology without seeming unreliable, and rational voters reject unreliable parties.
  - There must be a persistent correlation between ideology and subsequent action, or voters will drop ideological voting.
  - Parties cannot adopt identical ideologies, because they need product differentiation.
  - A successful ideology is soon imitated, so differentiation moves to subtler levels.
- In reality ideologies probably originate in the interests of a party's founders. Once the party is autonomous, the analysis applies fully (p. 142, n. 14).

**The spatial analogy (after Hotelling)** (p. 142)
- A linear scale from 0 to 100, left to right. Assumptions:
  1. Parties can be ordered left to right in a way all voters agree on.
  2. Each voter's preferences are single-peaked at some point and slope monotonically downward on either side (unless the peak is at an extreme).
  3. The frequency distribution of voters along the scale varies between societies but is fixed within one.
  4. A party can move left or right only up to, but not beyond, the nearest party in that direction. "Leaping" would signal unreliability.
  5. In a two-party system, if a party moves away from its near extreme toward the other party, extremist voters at that end may abstain because they see no significant difference.
- Under these conditions Hotelling's conclusion that two parties inevitably converge on the centre does *not* necessarily hold (p. 142).

**Two-party results** (p. 143)
- With the distribution of Figure 1 (the text does not describe its shape), Hotelling is right. If A starts at 25 and B at 75, both move toward 50, since each gains more votes in the centre than it loses at the extremes through abstention (p. 143).
- With the distribution of Figure 2 (a polarised electorate), the parties diverge toward the extremes. Each gains more by moving toward a radical position than it loses in the centre (p. 143).
- Hence stable government in a two-party democracy requires a distribution roughly approximating a normal curve. Then the parties resemble each other, a change of government brings no drastic policy change, and most voters are near the incumbent's position (p. 143).
- With a polarised electorate, a change of party brings radical policy change, and half the electorate always feels the other half imposes repugnant policies. If one party keeps winning, the disgruntled supporters of the other will probably revolt. If the parties alternate, social chaos occurs. Either the distribution must change or democracy will be replaced by tyranny (p. 143).

**Multiparty systems** (pp. 143–145)
- Hotelling's model failed with three firms, because the outer ones converged on the middle one, which then leaped outward, and so on without equilibrium. Here leaping is impossible, because each party must keep ideological continuity. So the model fits multiparty systems without disequilibrium (p. 143).
- Multiparty systems are most likely when the voter distribution is multimodal (Figure 3, modes marked P, R, S). A party at each mode gains nothing by moving. Moving left loses as many votes to the party on its right, or to abstention if it is the extreme-right party. So each party stays put and differentiates itself from its neighbours (pp. 143–144).
- Conditions are thus the opposite of a viable two-party system, where both parties move toward the centre and resemble each other (p. 144).
- Multiparty voters have a wider range of choice, each choice more definitely tied to an ideology. It seems the electorate matters more there (p. 144).
- But the paradoxical result is that the multiparty government is likely to have a less definite, less coherent program. Rarely does one party win a majority, yet government needs at least indirect majority support. So a coalition forms, and some policies of each member must be included to "pay off" its voter cluster. If a majority sits in one narrow band, as is typical in a two-party system, the government can draw all policies from that band. If the voters are so scattered that only a wide band holds a majority, the incoherence necessarily occurs (p. 144).
- Voters in a multiparty system thus get a choice between well-integrated policy-sets, but usually a coalition governs instead. This holds even though two-party voters choose between two similar, relatively unintegrated alternatives (pp. 144–145).

**Number of parties and political equilibrium** (p. 145)
- *Political equilibrium*: no new party can be successfully formed, and no existing party is motivated to move from its position. Success means being able to get elected (p. 145).
- Under proportional representation with the legislature forming the government, many parties can form, since a small share of votes wins some seats and a chance to join a coalition. New parties continue until the distribution is "saturated", with no ideological room for others significantly different (p. 145).
- Where a plurality is enough to win, the limit is tighter. The only sure way to get a plurality is a majority, so small parties tend to combine until two giants are left. Their locations depend on the voter distribution (p. 145).
- The policy position and stability of government are relatively independent of the number of parties. They follow mainly from the voter distribution. If a majority is massed in a narrow range, government is likely stable and effective however many parties exist. If a majority can be won only by a scattering of policies from a broad range, these policies tend to cancel out and net ability to solve social problems is low (p. 145).
- The voter distribution is a long-run variable, and the number of parties is one factor shaping it, because rising generations are influenced by the alternatives offered (p. 145, n. 20).

*Intuition note:* Where the voters sit decides where office-seeking parties sit. Where voters are bunched, two-party competition tends to give look-alike parties, and government is likely to be stable. Where they are polarised, parties diverge; where they are scattered, coalition governments are likely to be incoherent.

### VII. Rational ignorance and the voting decision (pp. 145–148)
- Costly information means no one can know everything relevant, even if data are free, since assimilating data takes time (pp. 145–146).
- **Axiom:** it is always rational to perform any act whose marginal return exceeds its marginal cost. The marginal cost of a "bit" of information is the return foregone by spending scarce resources, especially time. Its marginal return is the gain in utility income from the better decision it allows. With uncertainty, expected costs and returns are used (p. 146).
- A "free" information stream (conversation, small talk, newspapers, radio) reaches everyone as a cost of living in society. Downs excludes it from the question of how much information to obtain specifically to improve decisions (p. 146).
- Two political decisions need information: which party to vote for, and which policies to lobby for. Voting comes first (p. 146).
- The marginal return from voting information is the expected utility gain from voting "correctly". But unless his vote decides the election, whether the "right" party wins does not depend on how he votes, so correct voting yields no utility gain (p. 146).
- Because any one voter is insignificant in a large electorate and voting is cheap, the probability that one vote is decisive is very small. It is not zero, and can be significant if the election looks very close, but usually it is negligible. This holds however large the loss from the "wrong" party winning. If that loss is itself small (similar parties, local elections), the incentive to be informed is practically nonexistent (p. 146).
- **Conclusion:** it is irrational for most citizens to acquire political information for voting. Ignorance is "not a result of unpatriotic apathy" but a highly rational response to the facts of political life in a large democracy (p. 147).
- A rational person may still become informed for four reasons: (1) he enjoys information for its own sake; (2) he thinks the election is close, so a decisive vote is relatively probable; (3) he needs information to influence others' votes, or to persuade government to weight his preferences more; (4) he needs it to lobby. Yet odds are that no election will be close enough, so most citizens rationally stay uninformed. Information beyond the free stream is "a sheer waste of resources" for voting (p. 147).
- **Why the gap with traditional good citizenship?** The benefits of a well-informed electorate are *indivisible*. If most are informed and there is a consensus among them, government must follow their interests to avoid defeat, and each member of the majority benefits whether or not he helped. If no one else is informed, one person's effort cannot produce those benefits, since collective effort is needed. Each individual, taking others' behaviour as given, minimises his cost by staying ignorant. Everyone reasons this way, so no costs are borne and no benefits arise (p. 147).
- The usual escape is for all to agree to be coerced by a central agency, so each pays his share knowing others do too. Everyone is better off if benefits exceed his share of costs (Downs assumes they do). This is a basic rationale for compulsory revenue for defence and other indivisible-benefit operations (pp. 147–148, with Samuelson cited in n. 21).
- This is not feasible for political information, because well-informedness is hard to measure, there is no agreed rule for how much of what kinds of information is "enough", and the interference would cost more utility than a well-informed electorate would probably gain. The most democracies do is compel civics, government and history courses in schools (p. 148).
- So democratic systems are bound to operate at less than maximum efficiency. It is collectively rational but individually irrational for citizens to become informed, and without a mechanism for collective action individual rationality prevails (p. 148).

*Intuition note:* One vote almost never changes the winner, so paying to learn how to vote "correctly" almost never pays. Most citizens would gain if most were informed, but no one gains from being informed alone, so nobody pays.

### VIII. Lobbying and the producer bias (pp. 148–149)
- Rationality applied to lobbying also conflicts with the traditional view of democracy (p. 148).
- An effective lobbyist must persuade government that his policy is already wanted by many, or is beneficial enough that the rest of the electorate will not resent it. He must be extremely well informed in each area: able to design a policy that suits him best, counter opposing lobbyists, and recognise acceptable compromises. Lobbying thus requires much more information than voting, where even informed voters only compare alternatives formulated by others (p. 148).
- The cost is relatively high. Few can become expert in more than one or two policy areas, so citizens specialise, though policies in many areas touch them. Few actively press on government in any one area, so each need not discount his own impact heavily, as he does when voting. For those few, the return from political information may be very high precisely because they are few (p. 148).
- Those best placed to lobby in an area are those whose incomes come from it. Nearly every citizen derives income from one or two sources, so policies affecting them matter greatly. He spends income across many areas, so a change in any one matters little. Men therefore exert direct influence more as producers than as consumers (pp. 148–149).
- Consequently, democratic government is usually biased toward producers and against consumers, even though consumers of a product usually outnumber its producers. Tariff legislation is a notorious example (p. 149).
- This is not consumer apathy. Consumers rationally buy only information whose return exceeds its cost, and savings on any one product are too small to repay the cost of finding out how policy affects it. So they remain rationally ignorant and are exploited, yet it would be irrational to do otherwise. Lobbying is effective because exploiters, exploited and government all behave rationally (p. 149).

### IX. Conclusions (pp. 149–150)
- Rational behaviour in a democracy is not what many normative theorists assume. Political theory has often built models of how citizens ought to behave without the economics of political action (p. 149).
- Much evidence cited to show that democratic politics is dominated by "irrational" (non-logical) forces actually shows citizens responding rationally (efficiently) to an imperfectly informed world. Voter apathy, ignorance of issues, look-alike parties in a two-party system and the anticonsumer bias can all be explained as efficient reactions to imperfect information in a large democracy (p. 149).
- Footnote 22: "rational" and "logical" differ. It is sometimes efficient to act non-logically, when the marginal return from logical thinking is below its marginal cost (p. 149, n. 22).
- Economic theory also suffers, because it neglected political realities. It treated governments as perfect altruists, and so wrongly concluded that government decision-making should follow the same principles in all societies (pp. 149–150).
- If Downs's hypothesis is true, government's goal is the income, power and prestige of office. Methods of reaching this differ greatly between democratic, totalitarian and aristocratic states, so no single theory explains government decision-making in all societies. Government decision-making also cannot be divorced from politics. It depends on the society's political constitution, so a different theory is needed for each type of constitution (p. 150).
- A useful theory of government action must be both economic and political, and economists and political scientists depend on each other for it (p. 150).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Illustrates rational ignorance (Section VII), using the logic that the return to information is the gain from voting "correctly" times the chance that the vote is decisive. Downs gives this in words only. The numbers are invented.

- A citizen believes the "wrong" party would cost him 1,000 utility units.
- Chance his single vote is decisive: 1 in 10,000,000 = 0.0000001.
- Expected return from learning how to vote correctly: 1,000 × 0.0000001 = 0.0001 units.
- Cost of one hour of study: 10 units. Since 0.0001 < 10, learning does not pay, so he stays ignorant.
- Variant: the election is expected to be very close, with chance 1 in 50 = 0.02. Then the expected return is 1,000 × 0.02 = 20 > 10, so study pays. This is Downs's reason (2) for rational informedness (p. 147).

## Concepts
- [[rational ignorance]] — staying uninformed because the information costs more than its expected return to one voter (pp. 146–147, 149)
- [[paradox of voting]] — Downs's version: since a decisive vote is very unlikely, information for voting is not worth buying. He does not discuss turnout itself (p. 146)
- [[public good]] — benefits "indivisible" in nature: received whether or not one contributed. Downs applies this to a well-informed electorate (p. 147)
- [[free rider]] — each individual evades his share of the cost of indivisible benefits if others' behaviour is given. Downs does not use this word (p. 147)
- [[collective action problem]] — collectively rational but individually irrational to become informed. Coercion is the usual escape for defence revenue, but Downs says it is not feasible for information (pp. 147–148)
- [[spatial model of voting]] — a 0–100 left–right scale, single-peaked voters, parties constrained not to leap (p. 142)
- [[single-peaked preferences]] — assumption 2 of the spatial analogy (p. 142)
- [[heuristics / cues]] — Downs's ideologies play this role: cheap samples of party differences (pp. 141–142)
- [[retrospective voting]] — voters judge mainly on the incumbent's performance in the period just ended, adjusted for expected change (p. 138)
- [[institutions]] — Downs links government decision-making to the society's political constitution, so different constitutions need different theories (p. 150)
- [[principal–agent problem / delegation]] — governors' motives differ from social ends. Downs does not use the term, but the problem is the one he poses (p. 136)
- [[folk theory of democracy]] — Downs contrasts his conclusions with the "traditional conception of good citizenship" (p. 147)
- [[economic rationality]] (new) — "rational" = efficient: act only where marginal return exceeds marginal cost. Distinct from logical or psychological senses (pp. 137, 149)
- [[office-seeking party / vote-maximising government]] (new) — the central hypothesis: policy is only a means to votes (p. 137)
- [[political equilibrium]] (new) — no new party can succeed and no party wants to move (p. 145)
- [[producer bias]] (new) — government favours producers over consumers because producers' income depends on a few policy areas (p. 149)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Votes-for-policies exchange ↔ economics: firms seeking sales in oligopoly — same structure of rivals offering differentiated products.
- [same-math] Downs's expected return = probability of being decisive × gain from a correct vote ↔ decision theory: expected value compared with marginal cost. Downs states only the verbal logic, so same-math is conditional on that reading.
- [structural-analogy] Informed electorate as indivisible benefit ↔ public-goods theory: under-provision when contributions are individually costly. Downs cites Samuelson.

## Questions this raises
- If voting information is not worth buying, why do many people vote at all? Downs lists reasons for being informed but does not treat turnout here.
- How well do the claims about two-party convergence survive when voters' distribution, abstention and party reliability are measured empirically? The paper gives no data.
- If the voter distribution is shaped by the number of parties (n. 20), can party systems and voters co-evolve? Does a stable equilibrium exist?
- Does the rational-ignorance argument change if voters care about outcomes for others, or enjoy information for its own sake (reason 1, p. 147)?
