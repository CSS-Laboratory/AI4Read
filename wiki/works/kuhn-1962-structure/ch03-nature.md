# The Nature of Normal Science

## L0 — One line
Normal science is the "mop-up" work of making a new paradigm's early promise real, and Kuhn thinks three kinds of problem (determining significant facts, matching facts to theory, and articulating the theory) exhaust the literature of normal science.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks what researchers do once a paradigm (a shared, accepted model of good work) has been accepted. His answer: at first the paradigm is largely a promise of success. Normal science fulfils it by extending, matching and sharpening it. It does not normally aim at novelty. This narrow focus lets scientists study nature in great depth, and Kuhn says it also builds in a way for the restrictions to loosen when the paradigm stops working. He thinks three classes of problem, experimental and theoretical, exhaust the literature of normal science. Extraordinary problems cannot be had for the asking; they emerge only on special occasions prepared by normal research. Deserting the paradigm is what revolutions turn on.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a scientific theory and law; basic Newtonian physics (gravity, pendulum); Ch. 1–2 sense of "paradigm" and "mature science".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A scientific paradigm is not mainly a pattern for copying, as in grammar. It is more like a legal precedent: something to articulate and specify further (p. 23).
- A paradigm wins because it solves a few acute problems better than rivals. At first it is limited and imprecise, so its success is chiefly a promise (pp. 23–24).
- Normal science is the work of fulfilling that promise. It forces nature into the paradigm's "box" and does not aim at new phenomena or new theories (p. 24).
- Kuhn says the narrowness is essential to scientific progress. It gives depth, and the built-in mechanism relaxes the restrictions when the paradigm stops working (pp. 24–25).
- Kuhn thinks experimental work has only three foci: facts the paradigm shows to be revealing; facts directly comparable with predictions; and facts that articulate the theory. The third is the most important (pp. 25–27).
- Articulation includes finding constants and quantitative laws, and choosing among ways of applying the paradigm. Kuhn denies that such laws come from theory-free measurement (pp. 27–30).
- Theoretical work parallels this: applying the theory to get precise matches with nature, and clarifying or reformulating it. Newton's successors show this (pp. 30–33).
- Three problem classes exhaust normal science, "I think". Extraordinary problems emerge only on special occasions prepared by the advance of normal research (p. 34).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: what is left to do? (p. 23)** Kuhn asks what a paradigm leaves for the united group to resolve. He first warns about the word "paradigm". In grammar, "amo, amas, amat" is a paradigm because it can be replicated for other verbs (e.g. "laudo, laudas, laudat"), and any such example could replace it. In science a paradigm is rarely an object for replication. Like an accepted judicial decision in common law, it is an object for further articulation and specification under new or more stringent conditions (p. 23).

**A paradigm is at first a promise (pp. 23–24).** A paradigm gains status because it beats competitors at a few problems the group sees as acute. Being more successful does not mean being completely successful at one problem, or notably successful at many. Kuhn's examples are Aristotle on motion, Ptolemy's planetary computations, Lavoisier's balance and Maxwell's mathematization of the field. At the start their success is "largely a promise of success" found in selected, still incomplete examples (pp. 23–24). Normal science actualizes the promise in three ways: extending knowledge of the facts the paradigm shows to be revealing; increasing the match between those facts and the paradigm's predictions; and further articulating the paradigm (p. 24).

**Mop-up work (pp. 24–25).** Kuhn says mopping-up engages most scientists throughout their careers. It tries to force nature into the paradigm's "preformed and relatively inflexible box". Phenomena that do not fit "are often not seen at all". Scientists do not normally aim at new theories and are often intolerant of others' new theories (p. 24). Kuhn concedes that perhaps these are defects. The area studied is minuscule and the vision restricted. Yet he holds that these restrictions, born of confidence in the paradigm, are essential. Attention to a small range of esoteric problems forces investigation in unimaginable depth. Normal science also has a built-in mechanism that relaxes the restrictions when the paradigm ceases to function effectively. Meanwhile the profession solves problems it could not have imagined, and at least part of that achievement "always proves to be permanent" (pp. 24–25).

*Intuition note.* Narrow focus is a cost and a tool at once. Kuhn concedes the restrictions may be defects, yet says that, because they come from confidence in the paradigm, they let scientists investigate a small part of nature in great depth. He treats this as essential, not as a flaw alone.

**Experimental problems: three foci (pp. 25–30).** Kuhn thinks there are only three normal foci of factual investigation, and that they are neither always nor permanently distinct (p. 25).

1. *Facts the paradigm shows to be particularly revealing.* Examples: stellar positions, specific gravities, wave lengths, boiling points. Much literature tries to determine them more precisely and in more situations. Complex apparatus such as synchrotrons and radiotelescopes is built because a paradigm assures workers the facts matter. Some scientists, from Tycho Brahe to E. O. Lawrence, gained reputations from the precision and scope of their methods, not from novel discoveries (pp. 25–26).
2. *Facts directly comparable with the paradigm's predictions.* There are seldom many areas where theory can be compared directly with nature. Kuhn says no more than three areas are even yet accessible to general relativity (p. 26). Footnote 2 names the precession of Mercury's perihelion as the only long-standing check point still generally recognized; it also mentions possible further checks. Even where comparison is possible, approximations limit the agreement (p. 26). Examples: telescopes for Copernican annual parallax, Atwood's machine for Newton's second law, Foucault's apparatus for the speed of light in air versus water, and the scintillation counter for the neutrino (pp. 26–27). This type depends even more obviously on a paradigm. The paradigm sets the problem, and "without the Principia" Atwood-machine measurements "would have meant nothing at all" (p. 27).
3. *Work that articulates the paradigm theory.* It resolves residual ambiguities and solves problems the theory had only pointed to. Kuhn calls this class the most important and subdivides it (p. 27).
   - *Physical constants.* Newton's work implied a universal gravitational constant. His own problems could be solved without estimating it, and nobody devised apparatus for a century after the Principia. Cavendish's determination (1790s) was not the last, and repeated efforts followed (pp. 27–28). Other constants include the astronomical unit, Avogadro's number and the electronic charge. Kuhn says few of these efforts would have been conceived, and none carried out, without a paradigm theory "to define the problem and to guarantee the existence of a stable solution" (p. 28).
   - *Quantitative laws.* Examples: Boyle's, Coulomb's and Joule's laws. **The Baconian view (voice: what "we often hear"; Kuhn rejects it):** such laws come from measurements taken for their own sake, with no theoretical commitment. **Kuhn:** history offers no support for so "excessively Baconian" a method (p. 28). Boyle's experiments were not conceivable until air was seen as an elastic fluid to which hydrostatics applied (p. 28). Coulomb needed special apparatus for forces between point charges. Earlier users of ordinary balances found no consistent regularity. His design depended on the prior idea that every particle of electric fluid acts on every other at a distance (pp. 28–29). Since Galileo, such laws have often been correctly guessed with a paradigm years before apparatus could test them (p. 29).
   - *Choosing among ways of applying the paradigm.* This type can resemble exploration and is common in more qualitative sciences. Caloric theory's paradigm applications were heating and cooling by mixture and change of state. Heating by compression could be explained in several ways, for example by mixing gas with a void that has a heat capacity, or by a change in the gases' specific heat with pressure. Many experiments were designed from the theory to distinguish these explanations (p. 29). Once heating by compression was established, all further experiments in that area were paradigm-dependent in this way (p. 30).

*Intuition note.* Kuhn's claim is that the paradigm sets the problem to be solved and may even enter the design of the apparatus. Without the Principia, Atwood-machine measurements would have meant nothing at all.

**Theoretical problems (pp. 30–33).** These fall into nearly the same classes as the experimental ones (p. 30).
- *Predicting facts of intrinsic value* (ephemerides, lens characteristics, radio propagation curves) is a small part of theory. Scientists regard it as hack work for engineers or technicians (p. 30).
- *Confronting theory with experiment.* Many theoretical discussions in the journals aim to show a new application, or to raise the precision of an existing one. This is needed because of the difficulty of linking theory to nature (p. 30).

Kuhn illustrates with dynamics after Newton (pp. 30–32).
- The Principia allowed an unmatched increase in scope and precision. Newton derived Kepler's laws, explained some lunar deviations, and derived results on pendulums and tides. With ad hoc assumptions he derived Boyle's law and a formula for the speed of sound (pp. 30–31).
- Yet the applications were few and imprecise by today's standards. The book was built for celestial mechanics. Adapting it to terrestrial motion under constraint was unclear, and Galileo/Huyghens-style techniques were already succeeding there. Presumably the two could be shown to be special cases of one formulation, but for some time no one saw quite how (p. 31).
- Newton's approximations limited agreement. He treated the pendulum bob as a mass point and mostly ignored air resistance (p. 31). To derive Kepler's laws he neglected attraction between planets, so only approximate agreement could be expected (p. 32).
- Those who questioned Newton's work did not do so because of this limited agreement. The gap left problems for successors: motion of more than two attracting bodies and the stability of perturbed orbits. Euler, Lagrange, Laplace and Gauss did some of their best work here. They also built mathematics for hydrodynamics and vibrating strings (p. 32).
- At least in the more mathematical sciences, most theoretical work is of this sort (p. 33).

*Intuition note.* In Newton's case, limited agreement between prediction and observation left problems for successors to solve with better mathematics. Kuhn says those who questioned Newton's work did not do so because of that limited agreement (p. 32).

**Articulation by reformulation (pp. 33–34).** Not all theory is application. Some aims at clarification by reformulation, and such problems dominate in predominantly qualitative periods. The Principia was hard to apply and much of its meaning was implicit. From Euler and Lagrange to Hamilton, Jacobi and Hertz, mathematical physicists sought an equivalent but logically and aesthetically more satisfying mechanics (p. 33). Reformulations in other sciences have usually changed the paradigm more substantially. The experimental/theoretical split is arbitrary here, since articulation is both. Coulomb used electrical theory to design his equipment, and his measurements refined the theory. The designers of the compression experiments had often made up the theories being compared, so their work yielded a more precise paradigm (pp. 33–34). Kuhn adds that in many sciences most normal work is of this sort (p. 34).

**Conclusion (p. 34).** Determination of significant fact, matching of facts with theory, and articulation of theory "exhaust, I think" the literature of normal science. Extraordinary problems are not "to be had for the asking". Kuhn adds that the three classes do not quite exhaust the whole literature of science, and that resolving extraordinary problems may well be what makes science particularly worthwhile. Extraordinary problems emerge only on special occasions prepared by the advance of normal research. So the great majority of problems, even for the best scientists, usually fall in the three classes, and work under the paradigm can be conducted in no other way. To desert the paradigm is to stop practicing the science it defines. Such desertions are "the pivots about which scientific revolutions turn" (p. 34).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A Newtonian pendulum law is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, so $g = 4\pi^2 L / T^2$, with $4\pi^2 \approx 39.478$. The theory guarantees one stable $g$ and tells us what to measure. This is the "constant" type of articulation (p. 28).
- Run 1: $L=1.00$ m, $T=2.007$ s. $T^2 = 4.028$, so $g = 39.478/4.028 \approx 9.80$ m/s².
- Run 2: $L=0.50$ m, $T=1.419$ s. $T^2 = 2.014$, so $g = 19.739/2.014 \approx 9.80$ m/s² (about 9.803 unrounded).
A tiny difference between runs (e.g. 9.801 vs 9.803) would be treated as a puzzle: the point-mass bob and neglected air resistance are approximations that limit agreement (p. 31). It would lead to better apparatus or corrections, not to abandoning Newton. Without the law, the numbers $L$ and $T$ would not define any problem.

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — Paradigm: an accepted model of work. Kuhn stresses that it is rarely an object of replication but of articulation, and is at first largely a promise. Normal science is the research that fulfils it (pp. 23–24).
- [[paradigm articulation]] (new) — Work that resolves a paradigm's ambiguities and extends it: constants, quantitative laws, choosing applications, reformulation (pp. 27, 33–34).
- [[mop-up work]] (new) — Kuhn's name for normal research: forcing nature into the paradigm's box without aiming at novelty (p. 24).
- [[three classes of normal problems]] (new) — Significant fact, fact–theory matching, articulation of theory (p. 34).
- [[extraordinary problems]] (new) — Problems arising only on special occasions prepared by advanced normal research (p. 34).
- [[Baconian method]] (new) — The view that laws come from theory-free measurement. Kuhn says history does not support it (p. 28).

## Canonical instances
- [[newton-principia-successors]] (new) — A paradigm's early applications were few and imprecise; successors spent decades articulating it (pp. 30–33).
- [[cavendish-gravitational-constant]] (new) — A constant implied by Newton's work, for which no one devised apparatus for a century after the Principia (pp. 27–28).
- [[coulomb-force-law]] (new) — A law found with apparatus designed through theory (pp. 28–29).
- [[caloric-theory-compression]] (new) — Experiments choosing among rival applications of one paradigm (pp. 29–30).
- [[atwood-machine]] (new) — Apparatus whose measurements mean something only given the Principia (pp. 26–27).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] "Preformed box" with a revisable mechanism ↔ Lakatos's research programme: hard core protected while work proceeds in a belt. Both describe disciplined work inside a fixed framework.
- [structural-analogy] Mismatch blamed on approximations (point mass, neglected planets) ↔ confirmation holism: a failed prediction can be absorbed by adjusting auxiliary assumptions.
- [metaphor] Paradigm as legal precedent ↔ common-law reasoning: Kuhn's own comparison for something to be extended, not copied.

## Questions this raises
- What is the "built-in mechanism" that relaxes restrictions when a paradigm fails? How does it work in practice?
- How does a scientist tell an ordinary puzzle (a mismatch to be fixed by approximation) from a sign that the paradigm is failing?
- If observation is shaped by the paradigm, how can facts count as tests of it?
- Is the claim that three classes "exhaust" normal science testable?
