# I. Introduction: A Role for History

## L0 — One line
Kuhn argues that history of science, if it is more than anecdote and chronology and studies past science on its own terms, could replace the textbook picture of science as steady accumulation with a picture of normal science and scientific revolutions (p. 1, p. 6).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks how we should picture science. He argues that textbooks mislead: they suggest science is a stockpile of facts, theories and methods that grows piece by piece. A few historians who study past science in its own terms find this picture hard to sustain. Kuhn sketches an alternative. Normal science works within received commitments and meets anomalies. Extraordinary investigations then lead to scientific revolutions, which change commitments, problems, standards and the scientist's world. He also defends using history to inform theories of knowledge. The distinctions that seem to forbid this, such as discovery versus justification, now look to him like parts of a theory open to scrutiny.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school science history (Copernicus, Newton, Einstein); the idea of a scientific theory versus a fact.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Textbooks are persuasive and pedagogic. An image of science drawn from them is no more likely to fit science than a tourist brochure is to fit a national culture (p. 1).
- Under the accumulation picture, the historian's job is to date discoveries and to explain what that view calls error, myth and superstition, which slowed progress. A few historians find this increasingly hard to do (pp. 2–3).
- Old theories such as Aristotelian dynamics are not unscientific merely because they were discarded. That makes it hard to see scientific development as accretion (pp. 2–3).
- Method and observation restrict belief but do not fix it uniquely. An apparently arbitrary element, made of personal and historical accident, is always a formative ingredient of a community's beliefs (p. 4).
- Normal science rests on commitments taught in rigorous education. It suppresses novelty for a time, but anomalies eventually trigger extraordinary investigations (pp. 5–6).
- Scientific revolutions change theory, problems, standards and the "world" of the scientists. The category is extended to smaller episodes and to discoveries (pp. 6–8).
- Kuhn holds that competition between segments of the scientific community is the only historical process that ever actually results in rejecting or adopting a theory (p. 8).
- Kuhn now sees distinctions such as descriptive/normative and discovery/justification as parts of a theory, so they are open to the same scrutiny as other theories, and history of science is a source of data for it (pp. 8–9).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: the textbook image (pp. 1–2).**
- Kuhn says history, if more than anecdote or chronology, could transform our image of science (p. 1).
- That image comes mainly from finished achievements in classics and textbooks. Their aim is persuasive and pedagogic (p. 1).
- Such texts have often seemed to imply that the content of science is uniquely exemplified by the observations, laws and theories they present. Almost as regularly, they have been read as saying that scientific methods are simply the techniques and logical operations they illustrate (p. 1).
- On this view, scientists are people who add elements to the stockpile of knowledge. Development is piecemeal accumulation (pp. 1–2).
- The historian then appears to have two main tasks (p. 2):
  - date each fact, law and theory to a discoverer and a time;
  - describe and explain the errors, myths and superstitions (this view's labels for past beliefs) that inhibited more rapid accumulation.
- Even from history, Kuhn says, the new concept will not be forthcoming if historical data continue to be sought mainly to answer questions posed by this unhistorical stereotype (p. 1).

**Trouble for the accumulation picture (pp. 2–3).**
- A few historians find that more research makes questions such as when oxygen was discovered harder to answer, not easier. Some of them increasingly suspect these are the wrong sorts of questions (p. 2).
- Studying Aristotelian dynamics, phlogistic chemistry or caloric thermodynamics, they feel more certain that these views, as a whole, were neither less scientific nor more the product of human idiosyncrasy than current ones (p. 2).
- Two options follow. Either call them myths, so myths arise by the same methods and for the same reasons as science. Or call them science, so science has included beliefs incompatible with ours. The historian must choose the latter (p. 2).
- Hence out-of-date theories "are not in principle unscientific because they have been discarded", and development as accretion becomes doubtful (p. 3).

> **Intuition note.** If yesterday's rejected theories were good science, "science" cannot just mean "what we now believe". Then it is hard to see the story of science as simply adding to our current stock.

**The new historiography (p. 3).**
- Some historians have begun to seek the historical integrity of a science in its own time, rather than its permanent contribution to the present (p. 3).
- They ask how Galileo's views relate to those of his own group, not to modern science. They study that group's opinions from the viewpoint that gives them maximum internal coherence and closest fit to nature (p. 3).
- Kuhn says these works are perhaps best exemplified by the writings of Alexandre Koyré (p. 3).
- Kuhn's essay aims to make explicit what this historiography implies for the image of science (p. 3).

**Preview: aspects of science that will emerge (pp. 3–8).**
1. *Method underdetermines belief* (pp. 3–4).
   - Someone who knows what being scientific is, but is ignorant of chemistry or electricity, may legitimately reach any of several incompatible conclusions (pp. 3–4).
   - The conclusion he reaches is probably set by prior experience, accidents of investigation and personal makeup (p. 4).
   - Early stages of most sciences feature competing schools. Each view was partly derived from, and all were roughly compatible with, the dictates of scientific observation and method; Kuhn says none failed in method, since all were "scientific". They differed in what Kuhn will call incommensurable ways of seeing the world and practising science (p. 4).
   - Observation and experience "can and must" drastically restrict the range of admissible belief, or there would be no science, but they cannot alone determine a particular body of belief. An apparently arbitrary element, compounded of personal and historical accident, is always a formative ingredient of a community's beliefs (p. 4).
2. *Arbitrariness is not licence* (pp. 4–5).
   - No group can practise without received beliefs. Effective research scarcely begins before the community thinks it has firm answers about basic entities, their interactions, and legitimate questions and techniques (pp. 4–5).
   - At least in the mature sciences, answers (or full substitutes for answers) to these questions are firmly embedded in the educational initiation for professional practice. Because that education is strict and inflexible, Kuhn says, the answers come to exert a deep hold on the scientific mind (p. 5).
   - Kuhn says that in Sections III–V he will want finally to describe normal research as a strenuous and devoted attempt to force nature into "conceptual boxes" supplied by that education. He will also wonder whether research could proceed without such boxes (p. 5).
3. *Anomaly and revolution* (pp. 5–6).
   - Normal science assumes the community knows what the world is like. It defends this assumption, and often suppresses fundamental novelties because they subvert its commitments (p. 5).
   - So long as the commitments retain an element of the arbitrary, the very nature of normal research ensures that novelty is not suppressed for very long (p. 5).
   - A normal problem may resist the ablest members of the group. Or equipment may fail to perform as expected, revealing an anomaly that, despite repeated effort, cannot be aligned with professional expectation (pp. 5–6).
   - When the profession can no longer evade such anomalies, extraordinary investigations begin. They lead to a new set of commitments (p. 6).
   - These episodes are scientific revolutions, the "tradition-shattering complements" to tradition-bound normal science (p. 6).

> **Intuition note.** The same trained commitments that help account for the efficiency of normal research also lead it often to suppress fundamental novelties. But because the commitments retain some arbitrariness, anomalies the profession can no longer evade subvert the tradition and lead to extraordinary investigations.

**Revolutions defined (pp. 6–7).**
- Sections IX–X will use Copernicus, Newton, Lavoisier and Einstein as the most obvious examples. Kuhn says they display what all scientific revolutions are about more clearly than most other episodes, at least in the physical sciences (p. 6).
- Each case involved three things (p. 6):
  - rejection of a time-honoured theory in favour of an incompatible one;
  - a shift in the available problems and in standards of admissible problems and solutions;
  - a transformation of the scientific imagination, ultimately described as a transformation of the world in which work was done.
- Together with the controversies that almost always accompany them, these are the "defining characteristics" of revolutions (p. 6).
- Kuhn calls it a fundamental thesis that these characteristics can also be retrieved from the study of many other episodes that were not so obviously revolutionary (p. 6).
- Maxwell's equations were as revolutionary as Einstein's for the far smaller group affected, and were resisted accordingly (p. 7).
- For the specialists on whose area of competence a new theory impinges, it implies a change in the rules of prior normal practice, so it reflects on work they have already completed (p. 7).
- Therefore, however special its range of application, a new theory is seldom or never just an increment to what is already known. Its assimilation requires reconstructing prior theory and re-evaluating prior fact, an intrinsically revolutionary process that is seldom completed by one person and never overnight. Kuhn says it is no wonder historians have had difficulty dating it precisely (p. 7).
- Discoveries can be revolutionary too. The commitments that govern normal science specify what sorts of entities the universe does contain and, by implication, those it does not (p. 7).
- So discovering oxygen or X-rays does not simply add an item. Ultimately it does, but only after the community re-evaluates experimental procedures, alters its conception of familiar entities, and shifts its network of theory (p. 7).
- Fact and theory are not categorically separable, "except perhaps within a single tradition of normal-scientific practice" (p. 7).
- That is why, Kuhn says, the scientist's world is qualitatively transformed as well as quantitatively enriched by fundamental novelties of either fact or theory (p. 7).
- Kuhn admits that this extension strains customary usage (pp. 7–8).

**Roadmap (p. 8).**
- Sections II–X develop normal science and revolutions (p. 8).
- Section XI, on the textbook tradition, asks why revolutions have been hard to see (p. 8).
- Section XII describes the revolutionary competition between old and new traditions. Kuhn says this is the process that should somehow, in a theory of scientific inquiry, replace the confirmation or falsification procedures of our usual image of science. He says that competition between segments of the scientific community is the only historical process that ever actually results in rejecting a previously accepted theory or adopting another (p. 8).
- Section XIII asks how development through revolutions fits the apparently unique character of scientific progress. Kuhn says the essay will give no more than the main outlines of an answer, one that depends on characteristics of the scientific community requiring much additional exploration and study (p. 8).

**Defence of the method (pp. 8–9).**
- **Anticipated objection (Kuhn, on some readers' likely doubts):** a set of dichotomies suggests history cannot bring about such a transformation. History is too often called a purely descriptive discipline, yet Kuhn grants that his theses are often interpretive and sometimes normative. Many of his generalizations concern the sociology or social psychology of scientists, while a few conclusions traditionally belong to logic or epistemology. He may even seem to have violated the distinction between the context of discovery and the context of justification. He asks whether this admixture can indicate anything more than profound confusion (pp. 8–9).
- **Kuhn replies:** he was brought up on these distinctions and still supposes that, "appropriately recast", they say something important (p. 9).
- When he applied them to real cases of knowledge being gained, accepted and assimilated, they seemed "extraordinarily problematic" (p. 9).
- They now look like parts of a traditional set of substantive answers to the very questions they were used on, not prior logical distinctions (p. 9).
- This circularity does not invalidate them. It makes them parts of a theory, subject to the same scrutiny as theories elsewhere. If they are to have more than pure abstraction as their content, that content must be discovered by observing them in application to the data they are meant to elucidate (p. 9).
- Kuhn closes with a rhetorical question: how could history of science fail to be a source of phenomena to which theories about knowledge may legitimately be asked to apply? (p. 9)

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text. It only illustrates accumulation versus revolution; it is not a Kuhn claim.)*
A community holds 10 accepted facts under theory A. A new theory B is adopted.
- Accumulation model: B only adds 4 new facts, so the stock is $10 + 4 = 14$, all old facts unchanged.
- Revolution-style outcome: under B, 6 old facts stay as they were, 3 are reinterpreted, and 1 is dropped. Check: $6 + 3 + 1 = 10$. Then 4 new facts are added.
- Items retained as they were plus new items: $6 + 4 = 10$, not 14. Counting the 3 reinterpreted facts gives $13$ items, but those 3 now mean something different, so the count hides the change.

The comparison shows why "how many items were added?" can be the wrong question when theory change re-evaluates prior facts (p. 7).

## Concepts
- [[normal science]] (new) — research firmly based on received commitments, taught in education; it tries to fit nature into "conceptual boxes" (pp. 5, 7).
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — here only anomalies that cannot be evaded, then extraordinary investigations, then revolution. The word "crisis" is not used in this unit (pp. 5–6).
- [[scientific revolution]] (new) — a shift of professional commitments, with changed problems, standards and "world". Kuhn extends it to small episodes and discoveries (pp. 6–7).
- [[incommensurability]] — Kuhn says competing early schools differed in what he will come to call their incommensurable ways of seeing the world and practising science; the term is named but not defined here (p. 4).
- [[underdetermination]] — here: method and observation restrict belief but cannot alone determine one body of belief (p. 4).
- [[context of discovery vs. context of justification]] (new) — a distinction Kuhn says now looks like part of a theory (pp. 8–9).
- [[accumulation (development-by-accumulation)]] (new) — the textbook-derived picture of science growing by added items (pp. 1–2).

## Canonical instances
- [[aristotelian-dynamics-phlogiston-caloric]] — discarded views that a few historians judge, as a whole, neither less scientific nor more idiosyncratic than current ones (p. 2).
- [[copernicus-newton-lavoisier-einstein]] — the most obvious examples of revolutions, displaying what they are about more clearly than most other episodes (p. 6).
- [[maxwell-equations]] — a revolution for a smaller group, and resisted (p. 7).
- [[oxygen-x-rays-discovery]] — discoveries that transform the theory network (p. 7).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Method underdetermines belief (p. 4) ↔ analytic-philosophy: underdetermination of theory by evidence — both say evidence does not fix a unique theory.
- [structural-analogy] Discovery shifts a "network of theory"; fact and theory are not categorically separable (p. 7) ↔ analytic-philosophy: Quine's "web of belief" — both treat observation and theory as interlinked.
- [metaphor] "Conceptual boxes" from education (p. 5) ↔ common usage: a fixed frame that guides how problems are seen.

## Questions this raises
- If out-of-date theories were scientific, what then separates science from non-science? (p. 2)
- Is historical study able to support normative claims about science? (pp. 8–9)
- If only community competition decides between theories, in what sense can science be progressive? (p. 8)
- Can fact and theory be separated outside a single tradition of normal science? (p. 7)
