# IX. The Nature and Necessity of Scientific Revolutions

## L0 — One line
Kuhn argues that paradigm change deserves the name "revolution" because it is settled only by community assent, not by neutral logic, and because it is the *only* route science actually has to genuine novelty.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This chapter asks why paradigm change counts as a "revolution" and why it seems unavoidable. Kuhn first draws a political-revolution analogy: both kinds of revolution begin in a sense of crisis restricted to an affected group, and both are ultimately settled by the assent of a community rather than by a neutral higher standard. He then argues that cumulative, non-destructive science is both historically rare and improbable in principle, since normal research aims at expected problems, so genuine novelty appears only when expectations fail. Even an apparently compatible successor theory, like relativity replacing Newtonian mechanics, silently changes the meaning of its key terms, so paradigm shifts change standards and problems, not only facts.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's earlier vocabulary — paradigm, normal science, anomaly, crisis (Sections II–VIII); the idea of logical derivation/"special case"; elementary Newtonian vs. relativistic mechanics ($v/c$ ratio, $E=mc^2$).

## L2 — Key ideas
- Scientific revolutions are non-cumulative episodes where an incompatible new paradigm replaces an older one, wholly or in part (p. 92).
- Like political revolutions, scientific ones begin with a growing, community-restricted sense that existing institutions/paradigms no longer meet problems they themselves raised; outsiders may not see them as revolutions at all (p. 92–93).
- Paradigm choice cannot be settled merely by normal science's own evaluative procedures, since those depend on the paradigm being judged; arguments for a paradigm are circular — persuasive, but not logically or probabilistically compelling to outsiders (p. 94).
- In principle, science could be cumulative; but after the pre-paradigm period, almost every new theory and new kind of phenomenon has in fact required destroying a prior paradigm (p. 95–96).
- This is not accident: normal research looks for solvable problems using existing concepts and tools, so real novelty appears only when those expectations fail — discovery requires a clash of paradigms (p. 96–97).
- New theories also arise only from stubborn anomalies; a successful new theory must predict differently from its predecessor, so the old theory must be displaced, even for a seemingly "higher-level" theory like energy conservation (p. 97–98).
- Kuhn rejects the view that an old theory can always be rescued as a strict "special case" of its successor: deriving Newton's laws from relativity requires relabeling the same terms ("mass," etc.) to mean physically different things, so no real derivation of Newton's original laws occurs (p. 99–102).
- Because meaning change is unavoidable, successive paradigms differ in what counts as a legitimate problem, a real solution, and an admissible explanation, not only in claims about nature (p. 103–108).
- These standard-shifts are not simple progress toward better criteria; they can later be reversed, so there is no paradigm-independent yardstick for judging them (p. 108–109).
- Because rival paradigms partly define different problems and solutions, their debates have incomplete logical contact, and deciding "which problems matter more" needs criteria from outside normal science — which is what makes paradigm debates revolutionary (p. 109–110).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**A. Why "revolution"? The political analogy (p. 92–94).** Kuhn asks why paradigm change should be called a revolution at all. First aspect: political revolutions begin with a growing sense, often confined to part of the community, that existing institutions no longer meet problems they themselves created; scientific revolutions begin the same way, with a sense that a paradigm has stopped working in some area it had itself opened up (p. 92). This holds for small revolutions too (e.g. assimilating X-rays), and revolutions "need seem revolutionary only to those whose paradigms are affected by them" — astronomers found X-rays a mere addition, but for researchers like Kelvin, Crookes and Roentgen the new radiation both violated one paradigm and created another (p. 92–93). Second, deeper aspect: political revolutions aim to change institutions in ways the institutions themselves forbid, so once a society polarizes into rival camps there is no supra-institutional framework to judge between them, and resolution falls to mass persuasion (p. 93–94). Kuhn claims paradigm choice is parallel: it is a choice between incompatible *modes of community life*, so it cannot be settled merely by normal science's own evaluative procedures, since those procedures depend on the very paradigm at stake — each side's defense of its paradigm is necessarily circular. A circular argument can still be a clear, persuasive exhibit of what science would look like under the new view, but its force is only that of persuasion, never logically or probabilistically compelling for someone outside the circle: "there is no standard higher than the assent of the relevant community" (p. 94).

*Intuition note:* Kuhn is not saying scientists argue irrationally; he is saying that once two paradigms disagree about the very standards used to judge arguments, no neutral umpire is available — only persuasion within or across communities.

**B. Could science be cumulative? (p. 95–96).** Kuhn asks whether paradigm rejection is only accidental human confusion, or intrinsic to science. He first shows it need not be intrinsic: a new phenomenon could in principle leave old paradigms untouched (discovering life on the moon would conflict with existing paradigms, but life elsewhere in the galaxy would not); a new theory could deal with phenomena simply not known before (as quantum theory deals mainly, though not exclusively, with new subatomic phenomena); or a new theory could be a higher-level theory linking old ones without changing them, as energy conservation today links dynamics, chemistry, electricity, optics and thermal theory (p. 95). If science had developed this way throughout, it would be genuinely cumulative. But historically, after the pre-paradigm period, virtually every new theory and almost every new kind of phenomenon has in fact required the destruction of a prior paradigm — cumulative acquisition of unanticipated novelty is almost without exception (p. 96).

**C. Why cumulative novelty is improbable in principle (p. 96–97).** Normal research is cumulative precisely because scientists pick problems solvable with techniques close to those already available; a scientist pursuing such a problem is not merely looking around but knows what he wants and designs instruments accordingly. Unanticipated novelty can therefore emerge only when his anticipations and instruments turn out wrong, and often the resulting discovery is important in proportion to how stubborn the anomaly was. So there must be a conflict between the paradigm that revealed the anomaly and the one that later explains it; Kuhn concludes this was no historical accident — "there is no other effective way in which discoveries might be generated" (p. 97).

**D. Three types of phenomena and new theories (p. 97).** Kuhn divides possible objects of a new theory into three types: (1) phenomena already well explained by existing paradigms — these rarely motivate new theories, and when they do (the "three famous anticipations" of §VII) they are seldom accepted, since nature offers no ground to choose between competitors; (2) phenomena whose nature is indicated by the paradigm but whose details need further articulation — most normal research targets these, aiming to articulate, not replace, the paradigm; (3) recognized anomalies that stubbornly resist assimilation. Only type (3) gives rise to new theories; a paradigm gives every other phenomenon a theory-determined place already (p. 97).

**E. New theories must displace the old (p. 97–98).** A successful new theory resolving an anomaly must make predictions different from its predecessor's — impossible if the two theories were logically compatible — so the new one must displace the old. Even energy conservation, which looks today like a logical superstructure compatible with its component theories, historically emerged from a crisis: an incompatibility between Newtonian dynamics and consequences of the caloric theory of heat, and only after caloric theory was rejected could energy conservation become part of science (p. 97–98). "Logical inclusiveness remains a permissible view of the relation between successive scientific theories" but Kuhn calls it "a historical implausibility" (p. 98).

**F. The positivist objection (voice: an unnamed "most prevalent" objector, closely linked to early logical positivism) (p. 98–100).** Kuhn next addresses a view that would restrict a theory's meaning so it could never conflict with a later theory about the same phenomena — applied paradigmatically to Newton vs. Einstein. **Objection:** Einstein's dynamics cannot have shown Newton's to be wrong, since engineers and many physicists still use Newtonian mechanics successfully; moreover, Einstein's own theory can prove that Newton's equations are as good as our instruments require whenever relative velocities are small compared with light, i.e. subject to a condition like $(v/c)^2 \ll 1$ — so Newtonian theory is a special case of Einsteinian theory. The objection continues: no theory can conflict with its own special case; wherever Newtonians claimed exact validity at all velocities, that claim was itself unscientific overreach; purged of such extravagance, "Newtonian theory has never been challenged and cannot be" (p. 99). Before detailing the objection, Kuhn had already stated his own position (p. 98): Newtonian and Einsteinian dynamics are "fundamentally incompatible," comparable to Copernican vs. Ptolemaic astronomy, and this remains a minority view (footnote citing P. P. Wiener, 1958).

**G. Kuhn's two replies to the objection (p. 99–101).** (i) *It proves too much:* the same style of argument would make any theory ever used successfully immune to attack. Example: phlogiston theory explained why bodies burn, why metals share properties, why combustion forms acids, and why air loses volume in confined combustion. If these had been the theory's *only* claimed phenomena, phlogiston theory could never have been refuted either (p. 99–100). (ii) *It would end research:* to save a theory this way, its legitimate range must be restricted to phenomena and precision already observed (citing Braithwaite) — but pushed further, this forbids a scientist from speaking "scientifically" about any unobserved phenomenon, or relying on a theory beyond past precedent. These restrictions are "logically unexceptionable," but following them would end the research through which science develops: without commitment to a paradigm beyond existing precedent there could be no new puzzles, no surprises, anomalies or crises — the very signposts of extraordinary science. Literal positivist restriction would push the community back toward a pre-paradigm state (p. 100–101).

**H. The logical lacuna: why the "derivation" is spurious (p. 101–102).** Kuhn now asks directly: can Newtonian dynamics really be derived from relativistic dynamics? Sketch of the supposed derivation: let $E_1, E_2,\dots,E_n$ be statements embodying the laws of relativity theory, containing variables for position, time, rest mass, etc. Add a restricting statement, e.g. $(v/c)^2 \ll 1$. Manipulating this enlarged set yields a new set $N_1, N_2,\dots,N_m$, identical in *form* to Newton's laws of motion and gravity (p. 101). **Kuhn's reply:** this derivation is spurious. The $N_i$'s are a special case of relativistic mechanics, but they are not Newton's laws unless reinterpreted in a way impossible before Einstein. The variables still denote Einsteinian space, time and mass — and the physical referents of these differ from the Newtonian concepts bearing the same name (Newtonian mass is conserved; Einsteinian mass is convertible with energy; even where they are measured the same way at low velocity, "they must not be conceived to be the same") (p. 101–102). If we don't change the definitions, the $N_i$'s are not Newtonian; if we do change them, we have not derived Newton's laws "in any sense of 'derive' now generally recognized." The argument explains why Newton's laws *seemed* to work (justifying, say, a driver acting as if in a Newtonian universe), but it has not shown Newton's laws to be a limiting case of Einstein's, because in passing to the limit the fundamental structural elements of the universe — not just the form of the laws — had to change (p. 102).

*Intuition note:* same numbers can come out of both formulas at low speed, but "mass" in each theory is a different concept wearing the same name — so matching numbers do not mean the two theories agree about what exists.

**I. Meaning change as the core of revolution; two kinds of paradigm difference (p. 102–106).** This need to change the meaning of established concepts is, for Kuhn, central to Einstein's revolutionary impact — subtler than geocentrism→heliocentrism or phlogiston→oxygen, but no less destructive, and arguably a prototype because it needed no new objects at all, just a displaced "conceptual network" (p. 102). Taking paradigm differences as necessary and irreconcilable, Kuhn distinguishes: (1) *substantive* differences — what the universe contains and how it behaves (subatomic particles, the materiality of light, conservation of heat vs. energy) — needing no more illustration (p. 103); (2) differences in *methods, problem-field and standards of solution*, since paradigms also govern the science that produced them — a new paradigm can relegate old problems elsewhere, promote once-trivial problems to central status, and redraw the line between real science and mere metaphysical speculation, wordplay, or mathematical exercise; the resulting tradition is often "actually incommensurable" with the old one (p. 103). Case study: before Newton, the "new science" replaced Aristotelian essence-talk (a stone's "nature" pulling it to the center) with mechanico-corpuscular explanation of all sensory qualities via size, shape, position and motion of corpuscles; other qualities counted as illegitimate "occult" appeals (Molière's joke about a "dormitive potency") (p. 104). Newton's three laws of motion grew largely from reinterpreting known facts about colliding corpuscles (Descartes, then Huyghens/Wren/Wallis, then Newton) — the corpuscular paradigm bred both the problem and much of its solution (p. 104–105). Yet Newton's own *Principia* then undercut corpuscularism: gravity as innate attraction between particles was itself an occult quality by corpuscular standards. Scientists could neither do without the Principia nor force it to fit corpuscular standards, so by the mid-18th century they accepted gravity as innate — a genuine (though not retrogressive) reversion to a scholastic-type standard, with innate forces now joining shape and motion as primary properties of matter (p. 105–106).

**J. Further cases of standard-shift (p. 106–108).** By the 1740s electricians spoke of an electric fluid's attractive "virtue" without ridicule; treating action-at-a-distance as a real subject let scientists recognize induction (previously blamed on leaky apparatus), feeding Franklin's analysis of the Leyden jar and a new Newtonian paradigm for electricity. The same Newtonian legitimation of innate forces underlies 18th-century work on chemical affinities, without which Lavoisier's and especially Dalton's later work "would be incomprehensible" (p. 106). Two more brief cases: before the chemical revolution, chemistry aimed to explain substances' qualities via "principles" like phlogiston; Lavoisier's reform removed such principles, costing real and potential explanatory power, so the compensating new standard was that failing to explain a compound's qualities was no longer a mark against a 19th-century chemical theory (p. 107). Maxwell shared the conviction that light waves need a material ether; his own electromagnetic theory gave no such mechanism and was initially rejected for that reason, but once it achieved paradigm status his ether talk came to look like mere lip service, and ether-design attempts were abandoned — a shift linked to the later emergence of relativity theory (p. 107–108).

**K. Standard shifts are not simple progress (p. 108–109).** These shifts would matter less to Kuhn's thesis if they always moved from lower to higher methodological standards — some historians argue history of science shows continuous maturation (footnote citing Gillispie, 1960, called ironically "a brilliant and entirely up-to-date attempt to fit scientific development into this Procrustean bed"). Kuhn argues the case for cumulative standards is even weaker than for cumulative theories: abandoning the search for a mechanical cause of gravity was not a judgment that the problem was inherently illegitimate — "there are no external standards" for such a verdict — and it was later *reversed*: Einstein explained gravitational attraction, returning science (in this respect) closer to Newton's predecessors than his successors; quantum mechanics likewise reversed the chemical revolution's prohibition, letting chemists again explain qualities like color; a similar reversal in electromagnetism may be underway, with space in contemporary physics no longer an inert substratum (p. 108–109).

**L. Paradigms as constitutive of science; why debates are revolutionary (p. 109–110).** Beyond giving a "map" of nature (its theory), a paradigm also supplies the guidance needed to make such maps in the first place — a scientist usually learns theory, methods and standards together, in an inextricable mixture, so paradigm change shifts the legitimacy criteria for both problems and solutions at once (p. 109). This explains why paradigm debates cannot be settled by normal science's own criteria: since schools disagree, incompletely but significantly, about what counts as a problem or solution, they partly talk past each other, and each paradigm will satisfy its own criteria while falling short of its rival's (p. 109–110). Further, since no paradigm solves every problem it poses and no two leave the same problems unsolved, debates always raise the question "which problems is it more significant to have solved?" — answerable only by criteria from outside normal science; this recourse to external criteria is "what most obviously makes paradigm debates revolutionary." Kuhn closes by flagging something still more fundamental: he has argued paradigms are constitutive of science — the next section will argue they are constitutive of nature too (p. 110).

## Toy example (ours)
Two 1 kg carts collide inelastically at a closing speed of $v = 10\text{ m/s}$, so $v/c \approx 3.3\times10^{-8}$ and $(v/c)^2 \ll 1$ — exactly the condition under which Newtonian theory is said to be "derivable" from relativity (p. 99, 101).

**Newtonian bookkeeping:** mass is conserved: $m_{\text{before}} = 1+1 = 2\text{ kg} = m_{\text{after}}$. No conversion between mass and energy is even conceivable.

**Relativistic bookkeeping:** suppose the collision releases $\Delta E = 5\text{ J}$ as heat. That energy corresponds to a mass loss
$$\Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} = \frac{5}{(3\times10^8)^2} \approx 5.6\times10^{-17}\text{ kg}.$$
Measured mass "before" and "after" will agree to any precision our instruments can reach — the numbers match Newton's prediction. But *conceptually* the two theories disagree: Newtonian mass cannot convert into energy at all, while relativistic mass can and (in principle) does. This is exactly Kuhn's point: the formulas agree numerically at low $v/c$, but "mass" names physically different things in the two theories, so no real derivation of Newton's law of mass conservation has occurred (p. 101–102).

## Concepts
- [[paradigm-normal-science]] — the shared framework that supplies a scientific community's puzzles, methods and standards; here shown to also govern what counts as a legitimate problem or solution, not just substantive facts (p. 103).
- [[anomaly-crisis-revolution]] — Kuhn's sequence: anomalies accumulate, provoke crisis, and are resolved only by discarding the old paradigm; here extended to argue this is the *only* route to genuine novelty, not just the common one (p. 96–97).
- [[incommensurability]] — successive paradigms can be not merely incompatible but lack full shared standards of comparison, so debates between them have "incomplete logical contact" (p. 103, 109–110).
- [[paradigm-choice-circularity]] (new) — each side necessarily argues for its paradigm using that paradigm's own standards, so the argument is persuasive but not logically compelling to an outsider (p. 94).
- [[meaning-variance]] (new) — the same term (e.g. "mass") can name physically different quantities in two successive theories, blocking any strict logical derivation of the old theory from the new (p. 101–102).
- [[theory-as-special-case-objection]] (new) — the (here rejected, positivist-linked) view that an old theory survives unchallenged because it can be recovered as a restricted special case of its successor (p. 98–100).

## Canonical instances
- [[xray-discovery]] — X-rays were a mere addition for astronomers but a paradigm-breaking anomaly for radiation researchers like Kelvin, Crookes and Roentgen (p. 92–93).
- [[phlogiston-theory]] — explained metal properties, combustion, acid formation; shows how restricting a theory's claimed scope to already-observed phenomena would make it immune to any challenge (p. 99–100).
- [[newton-einstein-mass]] — the attempted derivation of Newton's laws from relativity theory, defeated by the change in what "mass" refers to (p. 101–102).
- [[newtonian-gravity-occult]] — Newton's innate gravitational attraction was, by corpuscular standards, an illegitimate occult quality, yet became accepted, reverting physics to a scholastic-type standard (p. 105–106).
- [[franklin-leyden-jar]] — Newtonian-licensed action-at-a-distance let scientists recognize induction and build a new electrical paradigm (p. 106).
- [[maxwell-ether]] — Maxwell's own electromagnetic theory undercut the mechanical-ether standard his contemporaries required of light theories (p. 107–108).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Kuhn's political-revolution analogy (crisis restricted to an affected group; resolution by community assent, not a neutral standard) ↔ political theory: revolutions change institutions in ways the old institutions forbid — Kuhn draws this analogy explicitly himself (p. 92–94).
- [structural-analogy] Kuhn's "meaning-variance" of terms like "mass" across paradigms ↔ philosophy of language's **sense (Sinn) vs. reference (Bedeutung)**: the same word-form can be paired with a different concept/referent across theories, blocking naive identity claims (p. 101–102).
- [structural-analogy] Paradigm-choice circularity, where logic and evidence alone cannot settle the choice ↔ philosophy of science's **underdetermination**: both claim that evidence plus logic leave the choice between rival theories open, requiring something beyond them (p. 94, 99–101).

## Questions this raises
- If paradigm defense is necessarily circular, what (if anything) distinguishes a *good* circular argument from a bad one, short of full logical proof?
- Does the meaning-variance argument apply to every historical paradigm change, or only to especially deep ones like Newton→Einstein?
- The chapter ends by promising that paradigms are "constitutive of nature" too — what would that claim mean, and does it commit Kuhn to denying an observer-independent world?
- If standards themselves can later reverse (as with gravity's explanation or chemical qualities), is there any sense in which later science is "better" than earlier science on Kuhn's view?
