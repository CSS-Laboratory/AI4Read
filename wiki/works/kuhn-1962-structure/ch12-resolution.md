# The Resolution of Revolutions

## L0 — One line
Rival paradigms cannot be tested against nature one at a time or settled by proof, so a community moves to a new paradigm through varied persuasion, partly on faith in future promise, as allegiances gradually shift.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks how a scientific community moves from an old paradigm to a new one, and what "testing" means there. He argues that paradigm-testing happens only as a competition between two rival paradigms, after a crisis. Rival paradigms differ in standards, in the meanings of terms, and in the "worlds" their users see. So the choice cannot be forced by proof or by neutral experience, and long resistance is legitimate. Persuasion uses several kinds of ground: solving crisis problems, quantitative precision, surprising predictions, aesthetics, and faith in future promise. No single argument convinces everyone. Allegiance shifts gradually until only a few holdouts remain. He holds that neither proof nor error is at issue; the question is persuasion within a community.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's earlier terms (paradigm, normal science, puzzle, anomaly, crisis, incommensurability, gestalt switch); the basic idea of verification and falsification; Section X's "neutral observation-language".

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Normal-science researchers solve puzzles and do not test the paradigm. Paradigm-testing arises only after crisis and only when a rival candidate exists, as a competition between two paradigms (pp. 144–145).
- Probabilistic verification theories stress comparing theories, which parallels the historical situation. But in their usual forms they rely on a neutral observation-language, which Kuhn has argued cannot exist, so they disguise the situation as much as they illuminate it (pp. 145–146).
- Kuhn says anomalies may not be identified with falsifying instances, and doubts the latter exist. Falsification is a later, separate process that could equally be called verification: the new paradigm's triumph over the old (pp. 146–147).
- Rival paradigms are incommensurable in three ways: standards, meanings of shared terms, and the "world" seen. So the switch happens whole, like a gestalt switch, not step by step (pp. 147–150).
- Lifelong resistance is not a violation of scientific standards. The same assurance that resists also makes normal science possible (pp. 150–152).
- Conversion has no uniform explanation. Effective arguments include solving crisis problems, quantitative precision, and novel predictions (pp. 152–155).
- Early on, a new paradigm has seldom solved more than a few problems, and opponents can usually claim it is little superior. Choosing it then takes faith in future promise, for which aesthetic grounds can sometimes supply a basis (pp. 155–158).
- Victory is a growing shift in the distribution of allegiances, not one group conversion. Late holdouts are not shown to be illogical (pp. 158–159).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the missing step (pp. 144–145)
- Textbooks appear only after a revolution. The chapter asks how a new candidate for paradigm replaces its predecessor (p. 144).
- New interpretations first arise in one or a few minds. Kuhn says their attention has invariably been concentrated on the crisis-provoking problems. Usually they are also so young or so new to the field that they are less committed to the old paradigm (p. 144).
- The questions: how do they convert the profession, and what makes the group abandon one tradition of normal research for another? (p. 144)
- Kuhn says these are the only reconstructions the historian can offer for the philosopher's question about testing, verification or falsification of established theories (p. 144).
- In normal science the researcher is "a solver of puzzles, not a tester of paradigms". The analogy is the chess player trying moves on a stated problem: the trials test only themselves, not the rules of the game (pp. 144–145).
- Such trials are possible only while the paradigm is taken for granted. So paradigm-testing occurs only after persistent failure on a noteworthy puzzle has produced crisis, and then only once the crisis has evoked an alternate candidate (p. 145).
- Testing never consists simply of comparing one paradigm with nature. It is part of the competition between two rival paradigms for the community's allegiance (p. 145).

*Intuition note:* A chess player who tries several moves on a set problem is not testing the rules of chess. A scientist who tries several approaches to a puzzle is likewise not testing the paradigm. Real paradigm-testing needs a rival on the board.

### Parallels and limits: probabilistic verification (pp. 145–146)
- Few philosophers of science still seek absolute verification criteria. No theory can face all possible tests, so they ask for a theory's probability given the actual evidence. One important school compares the power of different theories to explain that evidence (p. 145).
- Kuhn finds this stress on comparison parallel to the historical situation, and thinks it probably points one direction for future discussion of verification (p. 145).
- In their usual forms, however, these theories rely on pure or neutral observation-languages (Section X). One asks us to compare a theory with all imaginable theories fitting the same data. Another asks us to imagine all tests the theory might face. Apparently some such construction is necessary for computing specific probabilities, and Kuhn finds it hard to see how it could be achieved (pp. 145–146).
- Kuhn's objection, conditional on his earlier claim that there is no scientifically neutral language or concept system: any such construction must proceed from within some paradigm-based tradition. It would then have no access to all possible experiences or theories (p. 146).
- So probabilistic theories "disguise the verification situation as much as they illuminate it". The theories and observations at issue are always closely related to ones already in existence (p. 146).
- Kuhn's image: verification is like natural selection. It picks the most viable among actual alternatives in a historical situation. Whether the choice is the best possible, had other alternatives or data existed, cannot usefully be asked, since there are no tools for answering (p. 146).

### Popper and the two-stage account (pp. 146–147)
- Popper (as Kuhn reports) denies that verification procedures exist and stresses falsification, a negative test that necessitates rejecting an established theory. Kuhn says this resembles his own role for anomalies, which prepare the way for a new theory by evoking crisis (p. 146).
- But anomalous experiences may not be identified with falsifying ones. Kuhn writes "I doubt that the latter exist" (p. 146).
- Reason: no theory solves all its puzzles, and its solutions are often imperfect. That imperfect fit defines many normal-science puzzles. If any failure to fit justified rejection, all theories should be rejected at all times (p. 146).
- If only severe failure justifies rejection, Popperians need a criterion of "improbability" or "degree of falsification". Kuhn says they will almost certainly meet the same difficulties as the probabilistic theories (pp. 146–147).
- Kuhn's proposal: both views compress two largely separate processes into one. The anomaly evokes competitors. Falsification is a subsequent, separate process, which "might equally well be called verification" because it consists in the new paradigm's triumph over the old (Kuhn also says this two-stage view has, he thinks, great verisimilitude). The probabilist's comparison of theories belongs in this joint process (p. 147).
- Agreement of fact and theory: historically significant theories have all agreed with the facts "only more or less", so asking how well one theory fits has no precise answer. Asking which of two competing theories fits better does make sense. Example: neither Priestley's nor Lavoisier's theory agreed precisely with observations, yet few contemporaries hesitated more than a decade in judging Lavoisier's the better fit (p. 147).

*Intuition note:* No theory fits the data perfectly, so a failure to fit does not by itself amount to falsification. What can happen is that a rival fits better. Kuhn says the later process of falsification could equally be called verification: the rival's victory.

### Why the choice is not routine: incommensurability (pp. 147–150)
- The two-stage account makes choice look easier than it is. If there were one set of problems, one world, and one set of standards, competition might be settled more or less routinely by counting the problems each paradigm solves. These conditions are never met completely. Proponents of rival paradigms are always at least slightly at cross-purposes and partly talk through each other, as Proust and Berthollet did. Neither can hope to prove his case; Kuhn says the competition is not the kind of battle that proofs can resolve (pp. 147–148).
- **First aspect: standards and problem lists differ** (p. 148).
  - Must a theory of motion explain the cause of attractive forces between particles, or may it simply note them? Newton's dynamics was widely rejected because, unlike Aristotle's and Descartes's theories, it took the second answer. After Newton the question was banished, though general relativity may claim to have solved it (p. 148).
  - Lavoisier's theory, as disseminated in the nineteenth century, inhibited the question why metals are so alike, which phlogistic chemistry had asked and answered. The loss was not permanent: questions about the qualities of chemical substances re-entered science in the twentieth century, with some answers (pp. 148–149).
- **Second aspect: shared vocabulary changes meaning** (p. 149).
  - New paradigms incorporate much of the old vocabulary and apparatus but put old terms, concepts and experiments into new relationships. The result is a "misunderstanding" between schools, though Kuhn says the term is not quite right (p. 149).
  - Laymen who scoffed that space could not be "curved" were "not simply wrong". Previously, "space" meant something necessarily flat, homogeneous, isotropic and unaffected by matter, or Newtonian physics would not have worked. The whole conceptual web (space, time, matter, force) had to be shifted and laid down again on nature whole. Communication across the divide is "inevitably partial" (p. 149).
  - Copernicus's critics meant by "earth" partly a fixed position. His innovation changed the meaning of both "earth" and "motion". Once those changes were made and understood, Descartes and Huyghens could see the earth's motion as a question with no content for science (pp. 149–150).
- **Third and most fundamental aspect: different worlds** (p. 150).
  - Kuhn says, in a sense he cannot explicate further, that proponents practice their trades "in different worlds". One world has constrained bodies falling slowly, the other pendulums repeating their motions. In one, solutions are compounds; in the other, mixtures. One is embedded in flat space, the other in curved space (p. 150).
  - They see different things from the same point in the same direction. This does not mean they can see anything they please. What they look at has not changed, but in some areas they see different things in different relations (p. 150).
  - So a law undemonstrable to one group can seem intuitively obvious to the other. Before full communication, one side must undergo the paradigm shift. Because the transition is between incommensurables, it cannot be made a step at a time, forced by logic and neutral experience. Like the gestalt switch, it must occur all at once (not necessarily in an instant) or not at all (p. 150).

*Intuition note:* Rivals share words like "earth" or "space" but use them inside different webs of meaning. So the move from one side to the other cannot be forced by step-by-step logic and neutral experience. One must first learn to see the other way.

### Conversion and resistance (pp. 150–152)
- Scientists are often not converted. Copernicanism made few converts for almost a century after Copernicus's death. Newton's work was not generally accepted, particularly on the Continent, for over half a century after the *Principia*. Priestley never accepted the oxygen theory, nor Lord Kelvin the electromagnetic theory (pp. 150–151).
- **Darwin** (quoted by Kuhn) did not expect to convince experienced naturalists. He looked instead to young and rising naturalists who could view both sides impartially (p. 151).
- **Planck** (quoted by Kuhn) said a new truth triumphs not by convincing opponents but because they eventually die and a new generation grows up familiar with it (p. 151).
- Past readings took such facts to show that scientists, being human, cannot admit errors even given strict proof. **Kuhn's view:** neither proof nor error is at issue. The transfer of allegiance "is a conversion experience that cannot be forced". Lifelong resistance, especially from those committed by productive careers, is "not a violation of scientific standards" but an index of the nature of research (p. 151).
- The source of resistance is the assurance that the old paradigm will ultimately solve all its problems and that nature can be fitted into its box. It can look stubborn and pigheaded, and sometimes becomes so. But it also makes normal science possible. Through normal science the community exploits the older paradigm and then isolates the difficulty from which a new one may emerge (pp. 151–152).
- Resistance being inevitable and legitimate does not mean no arguments are relevant or that scientists cannot be persuaded. Conversions occur not despite the scientists' being human but because they are human. Some, especially older ones, may resist indefinitely. Conversions come a few at a time until the whole profession again practices under a single, different paradigm (p. 152).

### What kind of answer to expect (pp. 152–153)
- The question concerns persuasion where no proof exists. Kuhn says it is a new question needing a sort of study not yet undertaken, and he offers only a very partial, impressionistic survey. Kuhn says this, combined with what he has already said, suggests that the question of the nature of scientific argument has no single or uniform answer (p. 152).
- Individuals embrace a paradigm for many reasons at once. Some lie outside science (sun worship helped make Kepler a Copernican). Others depend on autobiography and personality. Nationality or the prior reputation of the innovator and his teachers can matter (pp. 152–153). Footnote example: Rayleigh's paper was rejected at first as the work of a "paradoxer" because his name had been omitted, then accepted once the name was in place (p. 153, n. 10).
- So the question should be changed: not what converts a given individual, but what sort of community always re-forms as a single group. Kuhn postpones this to the final section and first examines arguments effective in paradigm battles (p. 153).

### Effective arguments (pp. 153–156)
- **Claim to solve the crisis-provoking problems.** Probably the most prevalent claim, and often the most effective when legitimately made. The trouble is known and attempts to remove it have failed, and "crucial experiments" were recognised before the new paradigm existed. Examples: Copernicus on the calendar year, Newton on reconciling terrestrial and celestial mechanics, Lavoisier on gas-identity and weight relations, Einstein on making electrodynamics compatible with a revised science of motion (p. 153).
- **Quantitative precision.** Such claims are particularly likely to succeed if the new paradigm is strikingly more precise. Kepler's Rudolphine tables helped convert astronomers. Newton's quantitative predictions were probably the single most important reason for his triumph over more reasonable but uniformly qualitative competitors. Planck's radiation law and the Bohr atom persuaded many physicists although both created many more problems than they solved (p. 154).
- **Limits of that claim.** It is rarely sufficient alone and cannot always be made. Copernicus's theory was not more accurate than Ptolemy's and did not directly improve the calendar. The wave theory of light was at first not even as successful as its corpuscular rival on polarization (p. 154).
- **Novel predictions.** When the candidate does not help with the crisis problems, evidence is drawn from other parts of the field, and arguments are especially persuasive if the theory predicts phenomena entirely unsuspected under the old paradigm (p. 154).
  - Copernicus's theory suggested that Venus should show phases and the universe should be vastly larger. Sixty years after his death the telescope showed lunar mountains, Venus's phases and many new stars, which brought many converts, particularly among non-astronomers (pp. 154–155).
  - French resistance to the wave theory collapsed when Fresnel demonstrated a white spot at the centre of a circular disk's shadow. Not even he had anticipated it. Poisson, initially an opponent, had shown it to be a necessary if absurd consequence of the theory (p. 155).
  - Such arguments persuade because of shock value and because the result was obviously not "built into" the theory. Sometimes the same extra strength can be exploited even for phenomena observed long before the theory: Einstein seems not to have anticipated that general relativity would account precisely for the anomaly in Mercury's perihelion, and he felt a corresponding triumph (p. 155).
- Problem-solving arguments are ordinarily the most significant to scientists, but are, he says, neither individually nor collectively compelling (p. 155).
- **Aesthetic arguments.** These appeal to the individual's sense of the appropriate: the theory is "neater", "more suitable", "simpler". They are rarely made fully explicit. They are probably less effective in the sciences than in mathematics, because early versions of paradigms are crude. Yet they can sometimes be decisive: they may attract only a few, but the theory's triumph may depend on those few developing it (pp. 155–156).

*Intuition note:* Kuhn lists several kinds of reason that work in practice. None is a proof. Predictions that were obviously not built into the new theory, such as the white spot, are especially persuasive.

### Why faith and aesthetics matter: paradigm debate is about the future (pp. 156–158)
- A new candidate has seldom solved more than a few problems, imperfectly. Until Kepler, Copernicus's theory scarcely improved on Ptolemy's predictions. Lavoisier's view of oxygen as the whole of the air could not handle the proliferating gases, as Priestley argued (p. 156).
- Apparently decisive arguments (Foucault pendulum, Fizeau experiment) arise much later. Producing them is normal science, and they belong to postrevolutionary texts, not to the debate (p. 156).
- During the debate, opponents can usually legitimately claim the new paradigm is little superior even in the crisis area, and that the old one can presumably be articulated to meet the challenges, as Tycho Brahe's system and later phlogiston theory responded to challenges. They can also cite problems the rival has not solved but which are no problem for the new view: the combustion of hydrogen before water's composition was known, and the preparation of a combustible gas from carbon after oxygen had triumphed (pp. 156–157).
- Outside the crisis area the balance often favours tradition, as when new paradigms destroyed explanations without replacing them (Copernicus on terrestrial motion, Newton on gravity, Lavoisier on common properties of metals) (p. 157).
- Kuhn's conclusion: judged from the start only on relative problem-solving by hard-headed people, the sciences would experience very few major revolutions. Adding incommensurability, they might experience none (p. 157).
- Kuhn says paradigm debates are not really about relative problem-solving ability, though usually framed that way. The issue is which paradigm should guide future research on problems neither can yet fully solve. The decision rests less on past achievement than on future promise. An early adopter must often defy the problem-solving evidence and have faith that the new paradigm will succeed, knowing only that the old one has failed on a few problems. "A decision of that kind can only be made on faith." (pp. 157–158)
- This is one reason why prior crisis matters: scientists without it seldom renounce hard evidence for what may prove a will-o'-the-wisp. But crisis alone is not enough. There must be a basis for faith in the particular candidate, which, Kuhn says, need be neither rational nor ultimately correct. Sometimes only personal, inarticulate aesthetic considerations supply it. Neither Copernicus's theory nor De Broglie's had many other significant grounds of appeal at first, and Kuhn says general relativity attracts men principally on aesthetic grounds even today (p. 158).
- Kuhn denies that paradigms triumph through "some mystical aesthetic". Very few desert a tradition for these reasons alone, and those who do often turn out to have been misled. But a paradigm needs first supporters who develop it to where hard-headed arguments can be produced. Even then, no single argument can or should persuade all. What occurs is an increasing shift in the distribution of professional allegiances (p. 158).

### Closing picture (p. 159)
- Early supporters, if competent, improve the paradigm and show what belonging to its community would be like. If the paradigm is destined to win, persuasive arguments increase, more scientists convert, and experiments, instruments, articles and books multiply, until only a few elderly holdouts remain (p. 159).
- Kuhn says we cannot call the holdouts wrong. The historian may find men such as Priestley who were unreasonable to resist as long as they did, but he will not find a point at which resistance becomes illogical or unscientific. At most he may say that someone who resists after his whole profession has converted has *ipso facto* ceased to be a scientist (p. 159).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take a community of 10 scientists who differ in what persuades them. We invent four arguments, in arbitrary order. We also assume each argument wins over the listed people and that nobody abandons the new paradigm.
- A: the new paradigm solves a crisis problem. This persuades scientists 1–2 (2 converts).
- B: striking quantitative precision. It persuades 3–5 (3 more).
- C: a surprising novel prediction. It persuades 6–8 (3 more).
- D: the growing body of experiments, articles and books done under the new paradigm. It persuades 9 (1 more).
- Scientist 10 is never persuaded.

Share of converts after each argument: A: 2/10 = 20%; B: (2+3)/10 = 50%; C: (5+3)/10 = 80%; D: (8+1)/10 = 90%. No argument gets 100%, and none is decisive alone. The distribution of allegiances shifts step by step, as Kuhn describes (p. 158). Scientist 10 is a "holdout", whom Kuhn would not call illogical (p. 159).

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — the shared framework that makes research puzzle-solving. Here its testing is possible only by competition with a rival (pp. 144–145)
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — anomalies evoke a rival candidate; the rival's triumph is the "falsification/verification" stage (pp. 146–147)
- [[incommensurability]] — Kuhn's three aspects: differing standards and problem lists; shifted meanings of shared terms; practicing "in different worlds" (pp. 148–150)
- [[falsifiability]] — Popper's falsification, as Kuhn reads it. Kuhn doubts falsifying experiences exist, since all theories fit the data imperfectly (pp. 146–147)
- [[Bayesian confirmation]] (related, not identical) — "probabilistic verification theories" that ask a theory's probability given the evidence and compare theories; Kuhn objects that in their usual forms they rely on a neutral language (pp. 145–146)
- [[puzzle-solving-vs-paradigm-testing]] (new) — normal-science trials test only the researcher's attempts, not the paradigm (pp. 144–145)
- [[paradigm-choice-as-conversion]] (new) — a gestalt-like, not forced, transfer of allegiance; no proof, but many kinds of argument (pp. 150–152)
- [[future-promise-and-faith]] (new) — early choice of a paradigm rests on expected future success, which needs a basis for faith that need not be rational or correct (pp. 157–158)

## Canonical instances

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Verification as natural selection among actual alternatives ↔ evolutionary epistemology/biology: selection picks the fittest available variant, not the best possible one (p. 146)
- [metaphor] Gestalt switch of paradigm change ↔ psychology of perception: a whole-pattern switch, not a step-by-step change (p. 150)
- [structural-analogy] Gradual shift in distribution of allegiances ↔ diffusion/threshold models in social science: adoption spreads as individuals hold different thresholds for persuasion (p. 158)
- [structural-analogy] Probabilistic verification needing "all possible theories" ↔ Bayesian confirmation: the prior space of hypotheses must be specified in advance (pp. 145–146)

## Questions this raises
- If paradigm choice rests partly on faith and aesthetics, what separates it from fashion or mere group pressure?
- Kuhn says the better question concerns the sort of community that always re-forms as a single group, and he postpones it (p. 153). What features of the community explain the eventual single-group outcome?
- Is "different worlds" meant literally, or as a claim about perception and meaning? Kuhn says he cannot explicate it further (p. 150).
- Can Kuhn's two-stage account (anomaly, then competition) be squared with Popper's emphasis on falsification (p. 146)?
