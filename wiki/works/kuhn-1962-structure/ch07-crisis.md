# Crisis and the Emergence of Scientific Theories

## L0 — One line
New theories are generally preceded by a period in which normal science visibly fails at its own puzzles, and Kuhn says that such a crisis indicates that an occasion for "retooling" has arrived.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks how big new theories can arise from normal science, which is not aimed at producing them. His answer is that theory change is generally preceded by growing professional insecurity. Established puzzles keep failing, patches multiply, and rival versions of the theory proliferate. He shows this in three cases: Ptolemaic astronomy before Copernicus, phlogiston chemistry before Lavoisier, and ether physics before Einstein. Earlier proposals of the same ideas had been ignored in the absence of crisis. Scientists seldom invent alternatives while a paradigm works. A crisis marks the occasion for doing so.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's earlier notions of paradigm, normal science (puzzle-solving inside a paradigm) and anomaly (a result that violates paradigm-based expectations); basic history of astronomy, chemistry and physics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Discovery and invention overlap, but the discoveries of Section VI were not, at least singly, responsible for the major shifts (Copernican, Newtonian, chemical, Einsteinian); Kuhn now turns to the usually larger shifts that come from new theories (p. 66).
- If awareness of anomaly matters for new phenomena, a similar but more profound awareness is, Kuhn says, a prerequisite for all acceptable changes of theory; he thinks the historical evidence is "entirely unequivocal" (p. 67).
- In the Ptolemaic case, patches raised complexity much faster than accuracy. Some astronomers, such as Domenico da Novara, came to call the tradition cumbersome and inaccurate (pp. 68–69).
- Kuhn treats technical breakdown as the core of crisis. External factors mainly affect the timing, the ease of recognition and the place where breakdown first occurs (p. 69).
- Symptoms of crisis: many competing versions of the theory, a vaguer and less useful theory, and research that resembles pre-paradigm schools (pp. 70–72, 74).
- Kuhn calls the three examples "almost entirely typical": each new theory followed a pronounced breakdown and, Copernicus excepted, came no more than a decade or two after it. He adds, more tentatively, that it may be less typical that the failing problems had long been recognised (p. 75).
- Earlier anticipations of the new theories were ignored in the absence of crisis; Kuhn thinks one reason for the neglect of the combustion theories was that they touched no recognised trouble spot (pp. 75–76).
- Kuhn says alternatives to a theory are not very hard to invent, especially early in a new paradigm, but scientists seldom do so while the paradigm's tools work, since "retooling is an extravagance" (p. 76).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 66–67)
- Section VI's discoveries all helped to cause paradigm changes. Those changes were both destructive and constructive: a wider or more precise account of nature was bought by discarding standard beliefs or procedures (p. 66).
- Kuhn now turns to the "usually far larger" shifts that come from new theories. Fact and theory, discovery and invention, are not categorically distinct, so the two sections overlap. Overlap is not identity: the discoveries of the last section were not, at least singly, responsible for the Copernican, Newtonian, chemical or Einsteinian revolutions (p. 66).
- Question posed: how can such theories arise from normal science, which is even less directed to them than to discoveries? (pp. 66–67)
- Thesis: if awareness of anomaly matters for new phenomena, a similar but more profound awareness is prerequisite to all acceptable changes of theory; Kuhn thinks the historical evidence here is entirely unequivocal (p. 67).
- Kuhn's examples (p. 67):
  - Ptolemaic astronomy was a scandal before Copernicus.
  - Galileo built on difficulties that scholastic critics found in Aristotle.
  - Newton's theory of light began with the failure of earlier theories to explain the length of the spectrum.
  - The wave theory came amid concern about diffraction and polarization anomalies.
  - Thermodynamics came from a collision of two theories, and quantum mechanics from black-body radiation, specific heats and the photoelectric effect.
- In all these cases except Newton's, the awareness of anomaly lasted so long and went so deep that the field can be called in "growing crisis". New theories demand large-scale paradigm destruction, so they are *generally* preceded by pronounced professional insecurity. That insecurity comes from the persistent failure of normal-science puzzles (pp. 67–68). "Failure of existing rules is the prelude to a search for new ones." (p. 68)

*Intuition note:* Kuhn holds that new theories are generally preceded by a period in which the paradigm keeps failing at its own puzzles, so insecurity comes first.

### Example 1: Copernican astronomy (pp. 68–69)
- The Ptolemaic system, developed over about four centuries around the start of the Christian era, was admirably successful. For stars it is still used as an engineering approximation. For planets its predictions were as good as Copernicus' (p. 68).
- Still, predictions never quite matched the best observations, for planetary positions and for precession of the equinoxes. Reducing these discrepancies made up many of the principal problems of normal research for many of Ptolemy's successors (p. 68).
- Each discrepancy could be removed by adjusting the system of compounded circles. Over time, however, astronomy's complexity grew far more rapidly than its accuracy, and a discrepancy fixed in one place was likely to show up elsewhere (p. 68).
- Recognition was slow because the tradition was interrupted and communication was limited. Eventually Alfonso X quipped that he could have advised God on creation. Domenico da Novara held that so cumbersome and inaccurate a system could not be true of nature. Copernicus' preface called the tradition a "monster" (p. 69).
- Recognition that the paradigm was failing at its own traditional problems was prerequisite to Copernicus' rejection of it (p. 69).
- Kuhn mentions other ingredients: social pressure for calendar reform, medieval criticism of Aristotle, and Renaissance Neoplatonism. Technical breakdown would still remain the core of the crisis. In a mature science, external factors mainly determine the timing of breakdown, how easily it is recognised, and where it first occurs. Kuhn puts this outside the essay's scope (p. 69).

### Example 2: Lavoisier's oxygen theory (pp. 69–72)
- Historians disagree about the 1770s crisis in chemistry. Two factors are generally accepted as first-rate: pneumatic chemistry and weight relations (p. 70).
- **Pneumatic chemistry** (the study of gases): using the air pump and other devices, chemists came to realise that air must be an active ingredient in reactions, yet (with a few exceptions, possibly not real exceptions) they still held that air was the only sort of gas. Black's 1756 work showed fixed air (CO₂) was consistently distinguishable from normal air (p. 70).
- Cavendish, Priestley and Scheele then developed techniques that distinguished gas samples. All of them, from Black through Scheele, believed in phlogiston theory and often used it in designing and reading experiments. Scheele first produced oxygen in experiments aimed at dephlogisticating heat (p. 70).
- The results (many gases and properties) left the theory increasingly little able to cope with laboratory experience. None of them proposed replacing it, but they could not apply it consistently. By the early 1770s there were almost as many versions of phlogiston theory as pneumatic chemists. Kuhn calls such proliferation "a very usual symptom of crisis"; Copernicus complained of it too (pp. 70–71).
- **Weight gain on burning/roasting:**
  - Some Islamic chemists knew it, and several 17th-century investigators concluded that metal takes up something from the air.
  - Most chemists then saw no need for that conclusion: reactions alter volume, colour and texture, so why not weight? Weight was not always taken to be the measure of quantity of matter; weight gain seemed an isolated phenomenon, and most natural bodies (e.g. wood) lose weight on roasting (p. 71).
  - In the 18th century the balance became a standard tool and gases could be retained, so more weight-gain cases turned up. Newtonian gravitation led chemists to insist that weight gain means gain of matter (p. 71).
  - This did not lead to rejection of phlogiston theory, since it could be adjusted (negative weight, fire particles entering the body, and others). It did produce many special studies and many versions of the theory (pp. 71–72).
- Lavoisier's sealed note came late in 1772. Before it, weight gain had become an outstanding unsolved puzzle. The phlogiston paradigm was "gradually losing its unique status", and research under it came to resemble pre-paradigm competing schools, "another typical effect of crisis" (p. 72).

### Example 3: Relativity (pp. 72–74)
- Late-17th-century critics such as Leibniz attacked Newton's absolute space. Their critique was purely logical. They did not relate it to problems in applying Newtonian theory, so it died until the late 19th century (pp. 72–73).
- Once the wave theory of light was accepted (after about 1815), light was taken to be wave motion in a mechanical ether governed by Newton's laws. Drift through the ether therefore became detectable in principle. Of the celestial observations, only aberration measurements promised enough accuracy, so detecting drift that way became a recognised problem, and much equipment was built. It detected no drift, so the problem passed to theoreticians (p. 73).
- Fresnel, Stokes and others devised ether theories in which a moving body drags part of the ether. Each explained the null results, including Michelson–Morley. The only conflict was between these articulations, and it never became acute for lack of experimental techniques (p. 73).
- Maxwell's electromagnetic theory (accepted in the 1880s–90s) changed things. Maxwell, a Newtonian, believed it compatible with some articulation of the Newtonian mechanical view, but that articulation proved immensely difficult to produce (pp. 73–74).
- His treatment of moving bodies made no reference to ether drag, which was hard to add. Earlier drift observations therefore became anomalous (p. 74).
- After 1890 experimental attempts to detect ether motion failed, though some analysts thought their results equivocal. Theoretical attempts (Lorentz, Fitzgerald) gave promising starts, revealed new puzzles, and ended in a proliferation of competing theories. Einstein's special relativity (1905) emerged in this setting (p. 74).

*Intuition note:* In each case, breakdown in the normal puzzle-solving of the old paradigm came first, and Kuhn says the new theory seems a direct response to that crisis.

### What the examples show (pp. 74–76)
- Kuhn calls the three cases "almost entirely typical" (p. 74):
  - Each new theory came after a pronounced failure of normal problem-solving.
  - Except for Copernicus (where external factors weighed heavily), breakdown and proliferation came no more than a decade or two before the theory.
  - Kuhn says this "may not be quite so typical": the troublesome problems had long been recognised and were thought solved or nearly so. That helps to explain why the sense of failure could be so acute. Failure on a new sort of problem is never surprising (p. 75).
  - Each solution had been at least partly anticipated when there was no crisis, and the anticipations were ignored (p. 75).
- **Aristarchus.** He is the only complete anticipation of Copernicus (3rd century B.C.). **A view Kuhn rejects:** if Greek science had been less deductive and dogmatic, heliocentrism could have started 18 centuries earlier (the footnote cites Koestler as an extreme statement). **Kuhn replies:** this ignores historical context (pp. 75–76).
  - The geocentric system was far more reasonable then and had no needs that heliocentrism could fulfil.
  - All of Ptolemaic astronomy's development and breakdown came after Aristarchus' proposal, and there were no obvious reasons for taking him seriously.
  - Copernicus' proposal was neither simpler nor more accurate than Ptolemy's, and available observational tests gave no basis for choice.
  - So one factor leading astronomers to Copernicus, and not to Aristarchus, was the recognised crisis: "the time had come to give a competitor a chance" (p. 76).
- Kuhn's other cases give no full anticipations. He thinks "surely" one reason the 17th-century absorption theories of combustion (Rey, Hooke, Mayow) got no hearing is that they touched no recognised trouble spot. The long neglect of Newton's relativistic critics "must largely have been due" to a similar failure in confrontation (p. 76).
- Closing argument:
  - Philosophers of science have shown that more than one theoretical construction can be placed on a given body of data, and in a paradigm's early stages it is not very hard to invent alternates (p. 76).
  - Scientists seldom do so, except in the pre-paradigm stage and on special occasions, because science moves fastest by confident use of a working paradigm's tools (p. 76).
  - Retooling is "an extravagance" reserved for when it is demanded. Crises indicate that such an occasion has arrived (p. 76).

*Intuition note:* Here Kuhn explains why partial anticipations got no hearing: the older system had no needs the new proposal could meet, even Copernicus' fuller proposal was no simpler or more accurate than Ptolemy's, and (he thinks) other theories made no contact with a recognised trouble spot. More generally, while a paradigm's tools work, scientists seldom retool.

## Toy example (ours, not Kuhn's)
A model of 10 measurements has 5 adjustable features. Initially 4 measurements mismatch, so 6 match.
- Patch 1: add a feature (6 total), fix 1 mismatch, break none. Mismatches 3, matches 7.
- Patch 2: add a feature (7 total), fix 1, but a new mismatch appears elsewhere. Mismatches 3, matches 7.
- Patch 3: add a feature (8 total), same result. Mismatches 3, matches 7.

Complexity rose from 5 to 8 features: (8 − 5)/5 = 60%. Accuracy rose from 6 to 7 matches: 1/6 ≈ 16.7%. Complexity grows much faster than accuracy, and a fix in one place shows up as a problem elsewhere. That is the pattern Kuhn describes for late Ptolemaic astronomy (p. 68). Kuhn's own point is that crises indicate that an occasion for retooling has arrived (p. 76).

## Concepts
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — Kuhn's stage of crisis: persistent failure of normal-science puzzles, generating insecurity before a new theory (pp. 67–68)
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — the working framework whose puzzle-solving failures drive crisis; in the chemistry case it was still believed and trusted as a working tool during crisis (pp. 68, 72, 76)
- [[underdetermination]] — Kuhn mentions it as a result philosophers have repeatedly demonstrated: more than one theoretical construction can always be placed on given data; he notes that scientists seldom invent alternates outside the pre-paradigm stage and special occasions (p. 76)
- [[proliferation-of-versions]] (new) — many competing articulations of one theory, a typical symptom of crisis (pp. 71, 74)
- [[retooling]] (new) — constructing new tools or theories, an extravagance reserved for the occasion that demands it; Kuhn says crises signal that such an occasion has arrived (p. 76)

## Canonical instances
- [[ptolemaic-crisis]] — patches raised complexity faster than accuracy before Copernicus (pp. 68–69)
- [[phlogiston-crisis]] — pneumatic chemistry and weight gain made phlogiston theory harder to apply before Lavoisier (pp. 70–72)
- [[ether-drift-crisis]] — null drift results and difficulties with Maxwell's theory formed the setting in which relativity emerged (pp. 72–74)
- [[aristarchus-anticipation]] — heliocentrism proposed early but ignored without crisis (pp. 75–76)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Adding patches that raise complexity faster than accuracy ↔ machine learning: overfitting by adding parameters — both show added structure without matching gain in fit.
- [structural-analogy] Proliferating ad hoc versions of a theory ↔ philosophy of science: Lakatos's degenerating research programme (canon link; not stated in this unit).
- [metaphor] Retooling in manufacture ↔ switching costs in economics: change only when the old tool no longer pays.

## Questions this raises
- How exactly do scientists decide that a failure counts as a crisis rather than another puzzle to solve with a patch?
- In Kuhn's three examples (Copernicus aside) the theory came within a decade or two of breakdown. What counts as "breakdown" for dating, and can the date be fixed independently of the later theory?
- If invention of alternatives is easy early on but rare later, what stops anomalies from being ignored forever?
- How does a crisis relate to the actual choice between old and new theories, given that observation did not decide between Ptolemy and Copernicus (p. 76)?
