# The Invisibility of Revolutions

## L0 — One line
Science textbooks must be rewritten after every revolution and so hide the revolutions; together with the popular and philosophical writing modelled on them, they make science look largely cumulative.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks why scientific revolutions are so rarely seen as revolutions, even when famous examples are given. His answer: much of people's image of science comes from textbooks and works derived from them. These mostly address the paradigms current when they were written. They are rewritten after each revolution, they cut history down to heroes and their "contributions", and they set out knowledge piece by piece. So science looks cumulative, as if built brick by brick. Kuhn argues, using Dalton, Galileo and Newton, and Boyle's "element", that old problems, facts and concepts were often reshaped together by revolutions, not merely added to. This matters because, Kuhn says, more than any other single aspect of science, this teaching form has shaped our image of discovery and invention.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's earlier notions of paradigm, normal science and scientific revolution (as used in this unit); no mathematics.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Textbooks, popularizations and philosophy of science modelled on them are the main authority behind our image of science. They address the completed body of knowledge, most often the paradigms current when they were written. (pp. 136–137)
- Textbooks must be rewritten after every revolution, and in being rewritten they hide the existence of the revolutions that produced them. Unless a reader has lived through a revolution, his historical sense reaches only to the outcome of the most recent ones. (p. 137)
- Textbooks truncate history, then supply a substitute: past scientists appear to have worked on today's problems with today's canons. This makes science seem cumulative. (p. 138)
- Scientists are more tempted to rewrite history partly because research results show no obvious dependence on historical context, and partly because, except during crisis and revolution, their position seems secure. The heroes' names are kept while their works are forgotten or revised. (pp. 138–139)
- Dalton's own accounts of his work and Newton's account of Galileo both read later questions back into earlier work. They hide a reformulation of questions. (p. 139)
- The change in questions and answers accounts far more than new empirical discoveries for the shifts from Aristotelian to Galilean to Newtonian dynamics. (p. 140)
- Kuhn asks whether even textbook "facts" are piecemeal discoveries, and says the whole network of fact and theory has shifted. He states that theories "too" do not evolve piecemeal: they emerge together with the facts they fit, from a revolutionary reformulation. (p. 141)
- The textbook story of Boyle's "definition" of element is bad history. A verbal definition has little content apart from its context of concepts, procedures and applications. (pp. 141–143)

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Purpose and thesis (p. 136).** Kuhn says that more historical examples would probably fail to persuade. The examples are familiar, and people already see them as additions to knowledge, not as revolutions. He suggests there are excellent reasons why revolutions are nearly invisible. Scientists and laymen take much of their image of creative scientific activity from an authority that systematically disguises revolutions, partly for important functional reasons (p. 136). He also says the analysis begins to indicate one of the aspects of scientific work that most clearly distinguishes it from every other creative pursuit except perhaps theology. He says this can be fully developed only in his concluding section (p. 136).

**The authority: textbooks and their relatives (pp. 136–137).**
- Kuhn names textbooks, popularizations, and philosophical works modelled on them. Until recently there was no other significant source of information about science except research practice itself (p. 136).
- All three address an already articulated body of problems, data and theory, usually the paradigms the community holds when they are written (p. 136).
- Textbooks teach the vocabulary and syntax of a contemporary scientific language. Popularizations describe the same applications in everyday language. Philosophy of science, especially English-speaking, analyzes the logical structure of the completed knowledge (pp. 136–137).
- All three record the stable outcome of past revolutions. They need not give authentic information on how the bases were first recognized and accepted. Textbooks in particular have good reasons to be systematically misleading here (p. 137).

**Why textbooks hide revolutions (p. 137).**
- Section II noted that an increasing reliance on textbooks or their equivalent was an invariable concomitant of the emergence of a first paradigm in any field of science (p. 137).
- Kuhn says the concluding section will argue that textbook domination makes a mature science's developmental pattern differ from other fields. For now he takes it for granted that, more than in other fields, both layman and practitioner know science through textbooks and literature derived from them (p. 137).
- Textbooks are vehicles for perpetuating normal science. They must be rewritten, in whole or in part, whenever the language, problem-structure or standards change, so after each revolution. Rewritten, they inevitably disguise the revolution's role and its very existence (p. 137).
- Unless someone has lived through a revolution, his historical sense extends only to the outcome of the most recent ones (p. 137).

*Intuition note.* A textbook aims to teach today's science and need not give an authentic account of how its bases were accepted. Past work that cannot easily be read as contributing to today's problems tends to be left out of its history.

**A tradition that never existed (pp. 138–139).**
- Textbooks contain a little history, mostly references to great heroes. This makes readers feel part of a long tradition. Kuhn says this tradition "never existed" (p. 138).
- They mention only the parts of past work that can be seen as contributions to the paradigm problems. By selection and distortion, earlier scientists seem to have worked on the same fixed problems under the same fixed canons that the latest revolution made seem scientific. So science again seems largely cumulative (p. 138).
- Writing history backward is a universal temptation. Kuhn says scientists are more affected, partly because results show no obvious dependence on historical context, and partly because, except during crisis and revolution, their position seems secure (p. 138). In the scientific community's outlook as Kuhn describes it, more historical detail "could only give artificial status" to idiosyncrasy, error and confusion, and he adds a rhetorical question about why to dignify what has been discarded. Kuhn says the depreciation of historical fact is deeply, and probably functionally, ingrained in the profession's ideology (p. 138).
- He cites Whitehead: "A science that hesitates to forget its founders is lost." Kuhn says Whitehead was not quite right. Sciences need heroes and keep their names, and scientists forget or revise their works (pp. 138–139).

**Examples within single revolutions (pp. 139–140).**
- *Dalton.* All three of his incompatible accounts make him seem interested early in combining proportions. In fact those problems seem to have occurred to him with their solutions, and only when his creative work was nearly complete. The accounts omit the revolutionary step: applying questions and concepts previously restricted to physics and meteorology to chemistry. This reoriented chemists to ask new questions of old data (p. 139).
- *Galileo and Newton.* Newton wrote that Galileo had discovered that constant gravitational force produces motion proportional to the square of the time. Galileo's theorem does take that form when embedded in Newton's dynamical concepts. But Galileo's discussion of falling bodies rarely alludes to forces, much less to a uniform gravitational force that causes bodies to fall. Newton credits Galileo with the answer to a question Galileo's paradigms did not allow (p. 139).
- Kuhn says this change in formulating questions and answers accounts "far more than novel empirical discoveries" for the transitions from Aristotelian to Galilean and Galilean to Newtonian dynamics. The linear picture hides the heart of the most significant episodes (p. 140).

**Arrangement implies a false process (p. 140).**
- Textbooks present experiments, concepts, laws and theories separately and nearly seriatim (one after another). Kuhn calls this unexceptionable as pedagogy (p. 140).
- Combined with the unhistorical tone and the misconstructions, one strong impression is "overwhelmingly likely to follow": science has reached its present state by individual discoveries and inventions added one by one, in a process often compared to "the addition of bricks to a building". The arrangement implies a process that, if it existed, would deny revolutions a function (p. 140).
- Kuhn: "that is not the way a science develops." Many puzzles of current normal science did not exist before the latest revolution. Very few trace back to the science's historic beginning. Earlier generations had their own problems, instruments and canons of solution (pp. 140–141).

**Facts and theories (p. 141).**
- Kuhn asks two questions. Is the constancy of chemical composition a mere fact that chemists could have found in any of their worlds, or one element of a new fabric of fact and theory that Dalton fitted to earlier experience, changing it? Is constant acceleration under constant force a fact always sought, or the answer to a question that first arose within Newtonian theory? (p. 141)
- Kuhn leaves these as questions. The sentences that follow point to the second option: the whole network of fact and theory "has shifted", and theories "too" do not evolve piecemeal (p. 141).
- Theories "fit the facts", but only by transforming previously accessible information into facts that did not exist for the preceding paradigm. So theories do not evolve piecemeal to fit facts that were always there. They emerge with their facts from a revolutionary reformulation of the earlier tradition. In that tradition the scientist's knowledge-mediated relation to nature was "not quite the same" (p. 141).

*Intuition note.* Kuhn's questions suggest that a "fact" like constant composition may belong to a particular framework of questions and concepts. A theory fits facts by turning information that was available before into facts that did not exist for the earlier paradigm.

**Last example: Boyle's "element" (pp. 141–143).**
- Chemistry texts almost always credit Boyle's *Sceptical Chymist* with the concept of a chemical element. This helps pedagogically: it shows chemistry has a past, and that inventing such concepts is a scientist's traditional task (pp. 141–142).
- As history it is mistaken. By Boyle's own account, which Kuhn says was right, his "definition" paraphrased a traditional concept. He offered it to argue that no chemical element exists (p. 142).
- Kuhn says the single mistake is trivial. What is not trivial is the impression created when such mistakes are compounded and built into the text's technical structure (p. 142).
- Concepts such as 'time', 'energy', 'force', 'particle' and 'element' are often not invented or discovered at all. Boyle's definition goes back at least to Aristotle and forward through Lavoisier. That does not mean science had the modern concept since antiquity (p. 142).
- Verbal definitions have little scientific content alone. They are "more nearly pedagogic aids" than full logical specifications of meaning (if there are such). A concept gains full significance only through relations to other concepts, manipulative procedures and paradigm applications (p. 142).
- Hence such concepts can scarcely be invented independent of context, and given the context they rarely need inventing. Boyle and Lavoisier changed the chemical significance of 'element' but not its verbal formula. Einstein likewise did not have to redefine 'space' and 'time' to give them new meaning (pp. 142–143).
- Boyle's actual function: he led a revolution that changed how 'element' relates to chemical manipulation and theory. This made it a different tool and transformed chemistry and the chemist's world. Other revolutions, including Lavoisier's, gave it its modern form and function (p. 143).
- Conclusion: more than any other single aspect of science, the pedagogic form has determined our image of science and of the role of discovery and invention in its advance (p. 143).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Imagine a field with two eras. All numbers are invented to show the mechanism.
- Era 1 scientists published 10 studies. Only 2 addressed questions that Era 2's paradigm still asks.
- A post-revolution textbook cites only those 2 as "contributions". Visible share of Era 1 work: 2/10 = 20%. The other 8/10 = 80% vanish, so Era 1 looks like a short, tidy prelude.
- Era 2's normal science has 5 puzzles. Suppose 3 of them were created by the revolution. The share that did not exist before is 3/5 = 60%. Only 2/5 = 40% trace back to Era 1.
- A student then sees 2 old results leading to 5 current puzzles, like bricks added. This hides that most of the old work asked other questions and that 60% of the puzzles are new.

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Textbook selection of past work ↔ selection effects in data: a record kept only for what survived hides the rest, as survivorship bias does.
- [structural-analogy] Definitions as pedagogic aids that gain content from context ↔ meaning as use: a concept's significance comes from its role in a network of practices.
- [metaphor] "Bricks added to a building" ↔ cumulative growth models: the image Kuhn rejects.

## Questions this raises
- If textbooks must disguise revolutions, how can historians or scientists recover a reliable picture of earlier paradigms?
- What exactly is "functional" about the depreciation of history in science? Is the function purely pedagogic, or does it also help research?
- How does a revolution "close", the question Kuhn posed at the start of the chapter (p. 136)?
- What separates science from other creative pursuits in the way Kuhn promises to develop later (p. 136)?
