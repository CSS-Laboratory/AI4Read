# The Response to Crisis (Kuhn, ch. VIII)

## L0 — One line
Scientists in crisis do not drop a paradigm because of anomalies alone; they reject it only when a rival paradigm is available, and crisis changes their research into a looser, more speculative "extraordinary" kind.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks how scientists respond to crisis. He argues that scientists do not renounce a paradigm because of anomalies, and that no paradigm ever resolves all its problems. Rejecting a paradigm is always also accepting another. Many anomalies are tolerated or later resolved. An anomaly usually evokes crisis only when it is more than an anomaly. Then rival versions of the paradigm multiply and the rules of normal science blur. Research becomes exploratory, is often (not always) joined by philosophical analysis and thought experiments, and often yields new discoveries. A crisis ends in one of three ways. Almost always, the inventors of a new paradigm have been very young or new to the field. This challenges the simple falsification picture of science.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's earlier chapters: paradigm, normal science as puzzle-solving, anomaly, crisis (chs. V–VII); the idea of falsification (refuting a theory by a conflicting observation). Terms used below: paradigm (a shared basis for research practice); counterinstance (an observation that conflicts with a theory); ad hoc articulation (a patch fitted to one problem); gestalt switch (seeing the same marks as a different picture).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Scientists do not renounce a paradigm because of anomalies. A theory that has become a paradigm is declared invalid only if an alternative is available. Rejection is always also acceptance of another paradigm (p. 77).
- Kuhn thinks there is no research without counterinstances. Apart from purely instrumental problems, every puzzle can be seen as a counterinstance. Kuhn thinks there are only two alternatives: either no theory ever faces one, or all theories always do (pp. 79–80).
- Science seems to follow falsification partly because normal science looks like testing, and partly because textbook applications look like evidence (p. 80).
- Some anomalies are tolerated for decades. An anomaly leads to crisis only when it is more than an anomaly, and there is probably no general rule for when (pp. 81–82).
- In crisis, divergent ad hoc articulations proliferate and the paradigm's rules blur. Crises end in one of three ways (pp. 83–84).
- The transition to a new paradigm is a reconstruction, not a cumulative extension. The gestalt switch is a useful but misleading model (p. 85).
- Extraordinary research includes random-looking experiments, speculative theories, philosophical analysis and thought experiments. It often produces new discoveries (pp. 86–89).
- The new paradigm more often comes all at once, and almost always from people very young or new to the field. Symptoms of breakdown are defined independently of revolutions (pp. 90–91).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: what scientists never do (pp. 77–78)
- Kuhn assumes that crises are a necessary precondition for novel theories. He asks how scientists respond to them (p. 77).
- Scientists may lose faith and consider alternatives. They do not renounce the paradigm that led them into crisis. They do not treat anomalies as counterinstances, although in philosophy-of-science vocabulary that is what they are (p. 77).
- Kuhn says this generalization is partly a statement of historical fact. Such examples hint at what later study of paradigm rejection will show more fully: once a theory has the status of a paradigm, it is declared invalid only if an alternative candidate is available to take its place (p. 77).
- No historically observed process resembles the stereotype of falsification by direct comparison with nature. Experience and experiment are still essential to rejection (p. 77).
- The judgment to reject a theory is always based on more than comparing it with the world. Rejecting one paradigm is always also accepting another. The judgment compares both paradigms with nature and with each other (p. 77).
- **Second reason (Kuhn's own argument).** The factual reasons above are themselves counterinstances to a prevalent epistemological theory (p. 78).
  - Kuhn says that, if his point is correct, counterinstances can at best help to create a crisis or reinforce one already in existence. The theory's defenders will devise many articulations and ad hoc changes to remove the apparent conflict, as scientists do with anomaly (p. 78).
  - If these counterinstances are to be more than a minor irritant, that will be because they help permit a new analysis of science in which they are no longer a source of trouble. And if a typical pattern of scientific revolutions applies here, they may then seem like tautologies, i.e. statements of situations that could not have been otherwise (p. 78).
  - Example: for those committed to Newton, the second law behaves like a logical statement no observation could refute. The law of fixed proportion became part of the definition of a chemical compound after Dalton (Section X is promised) (p. 78).
  - Kuhn says something much like this will happen to the generalization that scientists fail to reject paradigms when faced with anomalies: in his words, they could not reject paradigms and still remain scientists (p. 78).

*Intuition note.* Kuhn says scientists do not give up a paradigm because of anomalies alone. He expects defenders of a philosophical theory to answer counterinstances in the same way, by adjusting the theory. A theory that has become a paradigm is declared invalid only if an alternative is available to take its place.

### No research without a paradigm, and none without counterinstances (pp. 79–80)
- Some people have undoubtedly left science because they could not tolerate crisis. Kuhn thinks that is the only paradigm rejection to which counterinstances alone can lead (p. 79).
- Once a first paradigm is found, there is no research without one. Rejecting a paradigm without substituting another is rejecting science itself. Colleagues will see the person as "the carpenter who blames his tools" (p. 79).
- The point works in reverse: there is no research without counterinstances (p. 79).
  - Normal-science puzzles exist because no paradigm ever completely resolves its problems. The few that seemed to (e.g. geometric optics) stopped yielding research problems and became engineering tools (p. 79).
  - Apart from purely instrumental problems, every puzzle can be seen as a counterinstance (p. 79).
  - Copernicus saw counterinstances where other successors of Ptolemy saw puzzles. Lavoisier did so where Priestley saw a solved puzzle. Einstein did so where Lorentz, Fitzgerald and others saw puzzles (p. 79).
- Crisis does not by itself turn a puzzle into a counterinstance. There is no sharp dividing line. By proliferating versions of the paradigm, crisis loosens the rules of puzzle-solving so that a new paradigm can emerge (p. 80).
- Kuhn thinks there are only two alternatives: either no theory ever confronts a counterinstance, or all do at all times (p. 80).

*Intuition note.* If any unsolved problem refuted a theory, every theory would be refuted at every moment. So an unsolved problem alone cannot be what makes scientists give up a theory.

### Why science seemed to fit falsification (pp. 80–81)
- Kuhn names two reasons and says explaining it fully would need philosophical history, which is barred here (p. 80).
  1. Normal science strives to bring theory and fact closer, which looks like testing or a search for confirmation. Its aim is to solve a puzzle whose existence presupposes the paradigm's validity. Failure discredits the scientist, not the theory (p. 80).
  2. Science teaching mixes a theory with its exemplary applications, so readers take the applications as evidence. Kuhn says students accept theories on the authority of teacher and text, not because of evidence, and asks what alternatives or competence they have. The applications are there to teach the paradigm. Treated as evidence, their omission of alternatives would convict the authors of bias, and Kuhn says that charge is unwarranted (pp. 80–81).

### How scientists respond to anomaly (pp. 81–82)
- A discrepancy larger than in other applications need not draw a profound response. Even stubborn ones usually yield to normal practice, and scientists often wait (p. 81).
- Example: for sixty years after Newton's computation, the predicted motion of the moon's perigee was only half the observed value. Occasional proposals to modify the inverse square law were not taken seriously. In 1750 Clairaut showed that only the mathematics of the application had been wrong (p. 81).
- Where no mere mistake seems possible, anomalies still need not cause crisis. Examples: the speed of sound (later resolved unexpectedly through heat experiments) and Mercury's motion (it vanished with general relativity, after a crisis it had played no role in creating) (p. 81).
- So an anomaly that evokes crisis must usually be more than an anomaly. A scientist who examines every anomaly will seldom get significant work done (p. 82).
- Kuhn says there is "probably no fully general answer" to what makes an anomaly worth scrutiny. He calls his cases characteristic but scarcely prescriptive (p. 82).
  - It may challenge explicit, fundamental generalizations: ether drag, for Maxwell's followers (p. 82).
  - It may block applications of practical importance: calendar design and astrology, in the Copernican case (p. 82).
  - Normal science may turn a vexation into a crisis source: weight relations after pneumatic-chemical techniques developed (p. 82).
  - Several such circumstances usually combine. Copernicus's crisis was also fed by the long failure to reduce the residual discrepancies in Ptolemy's system (p. 82).
- Once an anomaly seems more than a puzzle, the profession recognizes it, more eminent people attend to it, and, if it still resists (which Kuhn says it usually does not), many of them may come to see its resolution as the subject matter of their discipline (pp. 82–83).

### The blurring of the paradigm (pp. 83–84)
- Early attacks on the problem follow the paradigm's rules. Later ones involve minor or not-so-minor articulations of the paradigm. No two are alike, each is partly successful, and none is accepted as the paradigm. These are increasingly called ad hoc adjustments (p. 83).
- The rules of normal science become blurred. A paradigm still exists, but few practitioners agree on what it is. Even formerly standard solutions are questioned (p. 83).
- **Copernicus (quoted by Kuhn):** astronomers are like an artist who draws hands, feet and head from different models, giving "monster rather than man" (p. 83).
- **Einstein:** "as if the ground had been pulled out from under one" (p. 83).
- **Pauli** wrote that physics was "terribly confused" and that he wished he had been a movie comedian. Less than five months later he wrote that Heisenberg's mechanics had given him hope and joy in life, though not the solution to the riddle (p. 84).
- Such explicit recognition is extremely rare. Only two effects of crisis seem universal (p. 84):
  1. All crises begin with the blurring of a paradigm and a loosening of the rules. This resembles the pre-paradigm period, except that the area of difference is smaller and better defined (p. 84).
  2. All crises close in one of three ways. (a) Normal science handles the problem after all. (b) The problem resists, is labelled and set aside for a future generation with better tools. (c) A new paradigm candidate emerges, followed by a battle over its acceptance. (c) is the case that most concerns Kuhn (p. 84).

### The transition to a new paradigm (pp. 84–86)
- The transition is far from cumulative, not an articulation or extension of the old paradigm. It is a reconstruction of the field from new fundamentals. It changes some elementary generalizations and many methods and applications (pp. 84–85).
- During the transition period, the old and new paradigms overlap largely but never completely in the problems they can solve. There is also a decisive difference in the modes of solution. Afterwards the profession has changed its view of the field, methods and goals (p. 85).
- Butterfield's phrase, quoted by Kuhn: "picking up the other end of the stick" (p. 85).
- Others compare it to a gestalt switch, as when a bird becomes an antelope. Kuhn says this can mislead: scientists do not see something as something else, they simply see it. Also, they lack the gestalt subject's freedom to switch back. The switch is still a useful elementary prototype (p. 85).
- Because a new theory breaks with one tradition and begins another with different rules, it is likely to occur only when the first tradition is felt to have gone badly astray (pp. 85–86).
- Kuhn says the questions this raises (what is extraordinary research like? how is anomaly made lawlike?) need the competence of a psychologist even more than that of a historian. His account will be tentative (p. 86).

### Timing of the new paradigm (p. 86)
- Often a paradigm emerges, at least in embryo, before a crisis has developed far or been explicitly recognized. Examples: Lavoisier's sealed note, deposited less than a year after the first thorough study of weight relations in the phlogiston theory and before Priestley revealed the full crisis. Also Young's first wave-theory accounts, at an early stage of the crisis in optics (p. 86).
- In such cases a minor breakdown sufficed to induce a new view in someone. Kuhn says what intervened must have been largely unconscious (p. 86).
- In other cases (Copernicus, Einstein, contemporary nuclear theory) much time passes between awareness of breakdown and a new paradigm. Historians can then glimpse extraordinary science (p. 86).

### What extraordinary research looks like (pp. 86–88)
- The scientist first tries to isolate the anomaly and give it structure. He pushes the rules of normal science harder than ever to see where they work, and tries to magnify the breakdown (pp. 86–87).
- He will often seem a man searching at random, trying experiments just to see what will happen, looking for an effect whose nature he cannot quite guess. Since no experiment is conceivable without some theory, he also generates speculative theories. These can be surrendered easily if unsuccessful (p. 87).
- Examples: Kepler on Mars, Priestley on the new gases (p. 87).
- Kuhn's rhetorical questions about contemporary physics: without a crisis, would the effort to detect the neutrino have seemed justified? Without a visible breakdown, would parity non-conservation have been proposed and tested? The implied answer is no (p. 87).
- Extraordinary research is often, though by no means generally, accompanied by philosophical analysis; Kuhn thinks it is particularly in acknowledged crisis that scientists turn to it. Normal science usually keeps creative philosophy at a distance, and probably for good reasons: rules need not be made explicit while the paradigm serves as a model (p. 88).
- Searching for assumptions, even non-existent ones, can weaken a tradition's grip and suggest a new one. Newtonian physics, relativity and quantum mechanics were each preceded and accompanied by such analysis (p. 88).
- Thought experiments (Galileo, Einstein, Bohr and others) expose the old paradigm to existing knowledge and isolate the root of crisis with a clarity unattainable in the laboratory (p. 88).

### Crisis and discovery (pp. 88–89)
- Crisis concentrates attention on a narrow area and prepares scientists to recognize experimental anomalies, so it often proliferates discoveries (p. 88).
- Examples from optics: polarization by reflection (Malus, working on double refraction, an area known to be unsatisfactory). The light spot at the center of a disk's shadow was predicted by the new hypothesis, and its success helped make the hypothesis a paradigm. The colors of scratches and thick plates had been seen before but assimilated to known effects (p. 89).
- Similar multiple discoveries accompanied quantum mechanics from about 1895 (p. 89).
- Kuhn concludes that crisis at once loosens the stereotypes and supplies the incremental data a fundamental paradigm shift needs (p. 89).
- Sometimes the new paradigm is foreshadowed in the structure given to the anomaly. Einstein saw the interrelation of black-body radiation, the photoelectric effect and specific heats before having a substitute for classical mechanics (p. 89).
- More often it emerges "all at once, sometimes in the middle of the night" (pp. 89–90).

### Who invents, and the circularity worry (pp. 90–91)
- How an individual invents a new way of ordering the data "must here remain inscrutable and may be permanently so" (p. 90).
- Almost always the inventors have been very young or very new to the field. Kuhn's explanation: they are little committed to the old rules and so likely to see that the rules no longer define a "playable game" (p. 90).
- In a footnote, Kuhn calls the youth generalization a cliché that needs systematic study. He notes that Lehman's data do not single out fundamental reconceptualizations (p. 90, n. 15).
- 'Revolution' and 'extraordinary science' may have seemed to mean just 'non-normal science', a circularity. Kuhn says the circularity is "no longer unqualified". Sections VI–VIII give criteria of breakdown that do not depend on whether a revolution follows (p. 90).
- These criteria are: proliferating competing articulations, willingness to try anything, explicit discontent, and recourse to philosophy and debate over fundamentals. Kuhn says the notion of normal science depends on their existence more than on that of revolutions (p. 91).

*Intuition note.* Crisis is recognized by how scientists behave: they argue, improvise and philosophize. It is not defined by whether a revolution follows.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Suppose two rival theories, A and B, each face 20 test problems. A solves 19. B solves 18.
- Naive falsification ("reject a theory if any problem is unsolved"): A has 20 − 19 = 1 counterinstance, so it is rejected. B has 20 − 18 = 2, so it is rejected. Nothing is left to work with, so no research is possible. This is Kuhn's point that all theories always face counterinstances (p. 80).
- Kuhn's pattern: both A and B stay in use as long as no rival exists. The one or two leftover problems are treated as puzzles. Only when a rival that handles the trouble spot appears are the two compared, with nature and with each other (p. 77).
- This toy only illustrates the logic that rejection must be comparative. Kuhn does not say that counting solved problems decides the comparison.

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — the shared basis of current research practice; normal science solves puzzles whose very existence presupposes the paradigm's validity (pp. 79–80)
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — an anomaly usually evokes crisis only when it comes to seem more than just another puzzle of normal science; crisis ends in one of three ways (pp. 82, 84)
- [[falsifiability]] — treated as a "methodological stereotype" that history does not match: no direct falsification by comparison with nature (p. 77)
- [[counterinstance]] (new) — an observation conflicting with theory; Kuhn thinks either no theory ever faces them or all always do, and that by themselves they lead only to abandoning science, not to paradigm rejection (pp. 77, 79–80)
- [[extraordinary science]] (new) — research during crisis: loosened rules, random-seeming experiments, speculative theory, philosophical analysis (pp. 86–88, 91)
- [[ad hoc articulation]] (new) — divergent partial adjustments of the paradigm that blur its rules in crisis (p. 83)
- [[gestalt switch]] (new) — a prototype of paradigm change, which Kuhn says can mislead: scientists simply see the thing and lack the freedom to switch back (p. 85)
- [[analysis]] — philosophical analysis of a tradition's assumptions can weaken its grip (p. 88)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Ad hoc modifications that protect a paradigm ↔ confirmation holism (Quine–Duhem): a failed prediction does not say which belief to give up.
- [structural-analogy] Rejection requires an available alternative ↔ research programmes (Lakatos): theories are appraised comparatively and are not discarded on a single anomaly.
- [metaphor] Gestalt switch ↔ perception psychology: Kuhn himself warns the parallel can mislead (p. 85).

## Questions this raises
- If rejection is always comparative, what makes scientists judge one paradigm better than another when they overlap only partly?
- If there is probably no fully general answer to which anomalies cause crisis (p. 82), can crisis be identified except afterwards?
- Does saying scientists "could not" reject paradigms and remain scientists make Kuhn's generalization a definition instead of an empirical claim? (He compares it to Newton's second law, p. 78.)
- Is the claim about young or new inventors true? Kuhn says a glance at lists of contributions gives only impressionistic confirmation (p. 90, n. 15).
