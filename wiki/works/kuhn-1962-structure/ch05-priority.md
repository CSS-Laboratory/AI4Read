# The Priority of Paradigms

## L0 — One line
Kuhn argues that shared paradigms (concrete exemplary solutions) can guide normal science without any full set of rules being abstracted from them, and that paradigms may therefore come first.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The question: what holds a normal-science tradition together if historians cannot find a complete set of shared rules? Kuhn's answer is that paradigms are easier to identify than rules, and that paradigms may guide research without them. Scientists may work by modelling new problems on accepted achievements, much as Wittgenstein says we apply "game" through family resemblance. Kuhn gives four reasons: rules are hard to find, education teaches theory through applications, rules become a topic of debate mainly when paradigms feel insecure, and substituting paradigms for rules helps explain why revolutions can be local. This matters because it makes scientific communities plural and overlapping, not monolithic.

**Prerequisites:** the notions of "paradigm" and "normal science" from earlier chapters; Wittgenstein's "family resemblance" is explained here.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Historians can identify a mature community's paradigms (recurrent standard illustrations in textbooks, lectures, lab exercises) fairly easily. Identifying shared rules is a harder second step (p. 43).
- Scientists can agree that a solution is exemplary while disagreeing about what makes it so. Agreement on a paradigm does not require an agreed rule-based interpretation (p. 44).
- Wittgenstein's family-resemblance account of "game" is the model. Research problems may relate by resemblance and modelling to accepted achievements, not through common rules (pp. 45–46).
- Reasons 1–2: finding rules is very hard. Scientists learn theories through applications and problem-solving, not abstractly (pp. 46–47).
- Reason 3: debates about rules and standards occur in pre-paradigm periods and around revolutions. Rules matter when paradigms feel insecure (pp. 47–49).
- Reason 4: paradigms need not be common to a whole broad group, so one theory can serve as different paradigms for different subspecialties. This helps explain how revolutions can be local (pp. 49–50).
- The helium-atom case shows two specialists answering the same question differently because of their training (pp. 50–51).

## L3 — Walkthrough
Kuhn's chapter has no numbered subsections; I follow the order of the paragraphs.

**Two historical steps (pp. 43–44).**
- Close study of a specialty reveals recurrent, quasi-standard illustrations of theories, in their conceptual, observational and instrumental applications. These are the community's paradigms, shown in textbooks, lectures and lab exercises. Members learn their trade by studying and practising with them (p. 43).
- A "penumbral" area of doubtful achievements exists. The core of solved problems and techniques is usually clear (p. 43).
- Determining shared paradigms is not determining shared rules. Step two compares paradigms with each other and with current research reports. The aim is to find isolable elements, explicit or implicit, that members may have abstracted from the paradigms and used as rules (p. 43).
- Kuhn's experience is that this search is harder and less satisfying. Some generalizations are unproblematic. Others seem "a shade too strong", and some group members would almost certainly reject them. Yet explaining coherence through rules needs a statement of common ground, so the search is a source of "continual and deep frustration" (pp. 43–44).

**Diagnosis (p. 44).**
- Scientists can agree that Newton, Lavoisier, Maxwell or Einstein produced an apparently permanent solution. They may still disagree, sometimes unawares, about the abstract features that make it permanent (p. 44).
- Lack of a standard interpretation or agreed reduction to rules does not prevent a paradigm from guiding research.
- Normal science can be determined in part by "direct inspection of paradigms". Rules may help this but it does not depend on them. A paradigm's existence "need not even imply" that any full set of rules exists (p. 44).
- Footnote 1 cites Polanyi on "tacit knowledge", gained through practice and not articulable explicitly (p. 44).
- Open questions: what then restricts the scientist to a tradition, and what does "direct inspection" mean? Kuhn turns to Wittgenstein, in a more elementary context (p. 44).

**Wittgenstein's argument (p. 45).**
- The question: what must we know to apply "chair", "leaf" or "game" without dispute? The old answer is that we grasp attributes common to all and only the members of the class (p. 45).
- **Wittgenstein's view:** given how we use language and the kind of world we apply it to, no such set need exist. We call a new activity a "game" because it bears a close "family resemblance" to activities already called games. These are natural families, each a network of overlapping, crisscross resemblances. Such a network sufficiently accounts for successful identification (p. 45).
- Only if the families merged gradually into one another would our success be evidence for common characteristics (p. 45).
- Footnote 2: Wittgenstein says almost nothing about the kind of world that supports naming. Part of Kuhn's point therefore cannot be attributed to him (p. 45).

*Intuition note.* We can use a word well even if its instances need not share any single set of features (Wittgenstein, p. 45). Kuhn suggests that scientists may likewise recognise good research without stating its rules.

**Application to science (pp. 45–46).**
- Kuhn says the same "may very well hold" for research problems within a tradition. They may be related by resemblance and modelling to parts of the corpus the community already accepts (pp. 45–46).
- Scientists work from models without necessarily knowing what gave the models paradigm status, and so need no full set of rules. Their coherence may not imply that underlying rules exist (p. 46).
- That scientists do not ask what makes a problem legitimate tempts us to think they know intuitively. It may only show that the question seems irrelevant to their work (p. 46).
- Kuhn's thesis: "Paradigms may be prior to, more binding, and more complete than any set of rules" abstracted from them. So far this is theoretical, and four reasons follow (p. 46).

**Reasons 1–2: difficulty and education (pp. 46–47).**
- Reason 1: the great difficulty of finding the rules, which resembles the difficulty of saying what all games have in common (p. 46).
- Reason 2, a corollary: scientists never learn concepts, laws and theories in the abstract. They meet them together with applications. A new theory is announced with applications, and without them it would not be a candidate for acceptance. Textbooks keep the applications, which are not there merely as "embroidery" or even as documentation (pp. 46–47).
- A student of Newtonian dynamics learns what "force", "mass", "space" and "time" mean less from the incomplete definitions than by observing and taking part in applying them to problem-solving (p. 47).
- Learning by doing continues from freshman course to doctoral dissertation. Problems grow more complex and less precedented but remain modelled on earlier achievements (p. 47).
- One may suppose scientists abstract rules intuitively, but there is little reason to believe it. Scientists discuss particular hypotheses well, but are little better than laymen at characterizing the established bases, legitimate problems and methods of their field. Their ability to do research can be understood without hypothetical rules (p. 47).

**Reason 3: the converse (pp. 47–49).**
- Normal science can proceed without rules only while the community accepts existing problem-solutions without question. So rules should matter, and unconcern about them vanish, when paradigms feel insecure (p. 47).
- Kuhn says this happens. The pre-paradigm period is marked by deep debates over legitimate methods, problems and standards. These serve to define schools and not to produce agreement. Examples: optics and electricity (noted earlier), seventeenth-century chemistry, early nineteenth-century geology (p. 48).
- Such debates are almost absent in normal science but recur before and during revolutions. Examples: Newtonian to quantum mechanics, Maxwell's theory and statistical mechanics, and the reception of Galileo's and Newton's mechanics by Aristotelians, Cartesians and Leibnizians (p. 48).
- When scientists disagree over whether the fundamental problems are solved, the search for rules gains a function. While paradigms are secure they work without agreed rationalization (pp. 48–49).

**Reason 4: local revolutions (pp. 49–50).**
- Puzzle: if normal science is rigid and communities close-knit, how can a paradigm change affect only a small subgroup? Science is not monolithic. Kuhn says that, viewing all fields together, science often seems "a rather ramshackle structure" (p. 49).
- Explicit rules, when they exist, are usually common to a very broad group. Paradigms need not be. Astronomers and taxonomic botanists study different achievements. Even people in related fields may acquire different paradigms as they specialise (p. 49).
- Example: all physical scientists learn quantum-mechanical laws, but not the same applications (basic principles only, chemistry, solid-state physics, etc.). What the theory means to each depends on courses, texts and journals (pp. 49–50).
- So a change in quantum-mechanical law is revolutionary for all of these groups. A change touching only one paradigm application need be revolutionary only for that subspecialty (p. 50).
- Quantum mechanics (or Newtonian dynamics, or electromagnetic theory) is a paradigm for many groups but not the same one for all. It can determine several overlapping, non-coextensive normal-science traditions, and a revolution in one need not spread to the others (p. 50).

*Intuition note.* Kuhn says explicit rules, when they exist, are usually common to a very broad group, while paradigms need not be (p. 49). Where groups learn different applications of one theory, a change in one application need not be revolutionary for the others (p. 50).

**Illustration (pp. 50–51).**
- An investigator (James K. Senior, fn. 7) asked a distinguished physicist and an eminent chemist whether a single helium atom is a molecule (p. 50).
- The chemist said yes, because it behaves like a molecule in the kinetic theory of gases. The physicist said no, because it shows no molecular spectrum (p. 50).
- Presumably both meant the same particle. Their problem-solving experience told them what a molecule must be, and the two experiences did not agree on this point. Kuhn says we shall see how consequential paradigm differences of this sort can occasionally be (p. 51).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Five exemplar problems, each described by features from {1,…,5}: A={1,2,3}, B={2,3,4}, C={3,4,5}, D={4,5,1}, E={5,1,2}.
- **No common feature:** feature 1 is missing from B and C. Feature 2 is missing from C and D. Feature 3 is missing from D and E. Feature 4 is missing from A and E. Feature 5 is missing from A and B. So no feature is shared by all five exemplars.
- **Modelling rule (ours):** call a new problem acceptable if it shares at least 2 features with some exemplar.
- **New problem F={1,2,4}:** overlap with A is 2 ({1,2}), with B is 2 ({2,4}), with C is 1 ({4}), with D is 2 ({4,1}), and with E is 2 ({1,2}). The maximum is 2, so F is accepted.
- **New problem H={6,7,1}:** overlap with A is 1, B is 0, C is 0, D is 1, E is 1. The maximum is 1, so H is rejected.
- The community's judgement works through resemblance to exemplars. It does not use any attribute common to all of them.

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — here, recurrent quasi-standard illustrations of theory in applications, found in textbooks and lab exercises, by which members learn their trade (p. 43)
- [[family resemblance]] — Wittgenstein's idea that class members are linked by overlapping resemblances and not by a common essence; Kuhn extends it to research problems as a possibility (p. 45)
- [[shared-rules]] (new) — isolable elements, explicit or implicit, that members may have abstracted from paradigms and used in research. Kuhn doubts their completeness or existence (pp. 43, 46)
- [[priority-of-paradigms]] (new) — the thesis that paradigms may be prior to, more binding and more complete than any rules abstracted from them (p. 46)
- [[tacit-knowledge]] (new) — Polanyi's term for knowledge acquired through practice that cannot be articulated explicitly (p. 44, fn. 1)

## Questions this raises
- If scientists cannot state the rules, what explains the agreement they do have on which new work counts as good?
- How can a historian tell "no rules exist" from "rules exist but are unarticulated"?
- Kuhn says part of his point goes beyond Wittgenstein. What kind of world is needed to support naming by resemblance?
- How do overlapping paradigm-defined communities decide when a local change becomes a crisis?
