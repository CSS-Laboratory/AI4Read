# The Route to Normal Science

## L0 — One line
Kuhn treats a field's acquisition of one achievement (a "paradigm") that draws practitioners away from rival schools and leaves open problems for the whole group as a sign of its maturity as a science (pp. 10–11, 17–18, 22).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks how a field of study becomes a mature science. His answer: a single scientific achievement, a paradigm, must attract an enduring group of followers away from rival schools, and it must be open-ended enough to leave problems to solve. Before that, fact-gathering is nearly random and schools disagree about fundamentals. Optics before Newton and electricity before Franklin are his main cases. Once a paradigm is accepted, the group stops re-arguing foundations, works on narrow and difficult problems, writes short articles for colleagues, and journals and societies are usually associated with it. Except with hindsight, Kuhn finds it hard to name a criterion that more clearly shows a field is a science.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond high-school science history (corpuscle vs. wave theories of light; basic static electricity). Later chapters build on the terms "paradigm" and "normal science" introduced here.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Normal science** is research firmly based on past achievements that a scientific community accepts for a time as the foundation of its practice (p. 10). Kuhn says the term "paradigm" relates closely to it.
- A **paradigm** is an achievement that (a) is unprecedented enough to draw an enduring group of adherents, and (b) is open-ended enough to leave problems for them (p. 10). Law, theory, application and instrumentation together make up the model (p. 10).
- Learning from shared concrete models means later practice will seldom evoke overt disagreement over fundamentals. Men whose research rests on shared paradigms are committed to the same rules and standards; that commitment and the apparent consensus it produces are prerequisites for normal science (p. 11).
- Before a first paradigm, there were typically competing schools (optics before Newton; electricity before Franklin); Kuhn names exceptions such as mathematics and astronomy (p. 15). Each school stresses the phenomena its own theory explains best, and its books speak to other schools as much as to nature (pp. 12–14).
- Without a paradigm or candidate, all possibly relevant facts are likely to seem equally relevant. Early fact-gathering is far more nearly random than later fact-gathering, and Kuhn says it produces a morass (pp. 15–16). No natural history can be interpreted without some implicit theoretical and methodological belief (pp. 16–17).
- A theory must seem better than its competitors to be accepted as a paradigm; Kuhn says it need not, and in fact never does, explain all the facts (pp. 17–18).
- Acceptance of a paradigm changes the group. Older schools gradually disappear, partly by conversion, and holdouts are ignored. In the sciences, journals, societies and curriculum places have usually been associated with a group's first paradigm. Research reports usually become short articles for specialist colleagues (pp. 18–20).
- Transitions to maturity have seldom been as sudden as Kuhn's schematic account may imply, but neither have they been gradual over a field's whole history. In electricity it came sometime between 1740 and 1780. Except with hindsight, Kuhn finds it hard to name a criterion that more clearly shows a field is a science than having a paradigm that guides the whole group (pp. 21–22).

## L3 — Walkthrough
*(The chapter is one continuous essay, so this follows its order by page.)*

**Defining normal science and paradigm (pp. 10–11).**
- "Normal science" means research based on one or more past achievements that a scientific community acknowledges for a time as the foundation for its further practice (p. 10).
- Today textbooks recount such achievements. They state accepted theory, show its successful applications, and compare these with exemplary observations and experiments (p. 10). Earlier, classics did this job: Aristotle's *Physica*, Ptolemy's *Almagest*, Newton's *Principia* and *Opticks*, Franklin's *Electricity*, Lavoisier's *Chemistry*, Lyell's *Geology* (p. 10).
- They could do so because they shared two characteristics (p. 10):
  1. They were unprecedented enough to draw an enduring group away from competing modes of activity.
  2. They were open-ended enough to leave all sorts of problems for the regrouped practitioners.
- Kuhn calls such achievements "paradigms". They are accepted examples of actual practice (law, theory, application, instrumentation together) from which coherent research traditions spring. The historian describes these traditions under names such as "Ptolemaic astronomy" (or "Copernican"), "Aristotelian dynamics" (or "Newtonian") and "corpuscular optics" (or "wave optics") (p. 10).
- Studying paradigms mainly prepares a student for membership in a scientific community. Because members learned from the same concrete models, practice will seldom evoke overt disagreement over fundamentals (p. 11). Shared paradigms mean shared rules and standards. That commitment and its apparent consensus are prerequisites for normal science (p. 11).
- Kuhn raises, and postpones, two questions: why the concrete achievement is prior, as a locus of professional commitment, to the concepts and laws that may be abstracted from it; and in what sense the paradigm is a fundamental unit that cannot be fully reduced to logically atomic components. He says answers will prove basic when these questions are met in Section V (p. 11). Acquiring a paradigm is a sign of maturity in a field (p. 11).

*Intuition note:* Kuhn defines "paradigm" by what an achievement does for a group: it attracts followers and leaves work to do. Kuhn describes it as a concrete model (law, theory, application and instrumentation together). Men trained on shared models are committed to the same rules and standards (pp. 10–11).

**Optics: paradigm changes and the pre-Newtonian period (pp. 11–13).**
- Today's textbooks say light is photons, with some wave and some particle traits. This view is scarcely half a century old (p. 12). Before Planck, Einstein and others, texts taught transverse wave motion, a paradigm from Young and Fresnel. Before that, Newton's *Opticks* taught light as material corpuscles (p. 12).
- Kuhn calls these transformations scientific revolutions. Successive transition from one paradigm to another via revolution is the usual developmental pattern of mature science (p. 12).
- Before Newton the pattern was different. From remote antiquity to the end of the seventeenth century, no single generally accepted view of light existed. There were competing schools, mostly Epicurean, Aristotelian or Platonic. They held that light was particles from bodies, a modification of the medium, or an interaction of the medium with an emanation from the eye (p. 12).
- Each school drew strength from a particular metaphysic. Each stressed as "paradigmatic" the observations its own theory best explained. Other observations got ad hoc elaborations or stayed open problems (pp. 12–13).
- These schools contributed concepts, phenomena and techniques that Newton drew on. Their creative members were scientists, but a survey of the period may lead one to conclude that the net result was "something less than science" (p. 13).
- Each writer, unable to assume a common body of belief, rebuilt the field from its foundations. Choice of evidence was relatively free. Books were often addressed as much to other schools as to nature (p. 13). Kuhn says this pattern is not unfamiliar in some creative fields today and is compatible with significant discovery (p. 13).

**Electricity before Franklin (pp. 13–15).**
- In the first half of the eighteenth century there were almost as many views of electricity as there were important experimenters (p. 13).
- All the views partly derived from the mechanico-corpuscular philosophy and were real scientific theories. Yet the theories had "no more than a family resemblance" (p. 14).
- Three groups are described (p. 14):
  1. One group took attraction and frictional generation as fundamental ("factional" in the text layer) and tended to treat repulsion as secondary. It tended to postpone study of Gray's conduction.
  2. A small second group treated attraction and repulsion as equally elementary. Even Franklin's theory never quite accounted for the mutual repulsion of two negatively charged bodies. This group could not handle conduction beyond the simplest effects.
  3. A third group started from conduction and tended to speak of electricity as a "fluid" rather than an "effluvium". It had trouble with many attractive and repulsive effects.
- Only through Franklin and his immediate successors did a theory arise that could account with about equal ease for nearly all these effects. It gave the next generation a common paradigm (p. 15).

**How typical this is (p. 15).**
- Mathematics and astronomy (first paradigms in prehistory) and biochemistry (formed by recombining mature specialties) are excluded. For other fields Kuhn calls the pattern "historically typical" (p. 15).
- He suggests similar disagreements before Aristotle (motion), Archimedes (statics), Black (heat), Boyle and Boerhaave (chemistry) and Hutton (historical geology) (p. 15). In parts of biology, such as heredity, the first universally received paradigms are still more recent. Kuhn says it remains an open question which parts of social science have yet acquired any (p. 15).
- History suggests the road to firm consensus is "extraordinarily arduous" (p. 15).

**Why the road is hard: fact-gathering without a paradigm (pp. 15–17).**
- Without a paradigm or candidate, all possibly relevant facts are likely to seem equally relevant. Early fact-gathering is far more nearly random, and is usually restricted to data ready to hand. It includes data from crafts such as medicine, calendar making and metallurgy. Hence technology has often played a vital role in new sciences (pp. 15–16).
- Pliny's writings and the Baconian natural histories show that this collecting produces "a morass". The histories mix facts that will prove revealing (heating by mixture) with ones that for some time remain too complex for theory (dung-heap warmth) and ones we cannot confirm (antiperistasis) (p. 16).
- Any description is partial. Almost none of the early electrical histories mention that chaff attracted to rubbed glass bounces off again; that effect seemed mechanical, not electrical (p. 16).
- Only rarely (ancient statics, dynamics, geometrical optics) do facts gathered with so little theory speak clearly enough for a first paradigm (p. 16).
- No natural history can be interpreted without an implicit body of intertwined theoretical and methodological belief permitting selection, evaluation and criticism. If it is not already in the facts, it must be externally supplied, perhaps by a current metaphysic, by another science, or by personal and historical accident (p. 17). So early on, different people describe the same phenomena differently. What is surprising, and perhaps unique in degree to science, is that such divergences largely disappear (p. 17).

*Intuition note:* Facts do not sort themselves. Some prior belief is needed to decide which facts to collect, which to ignore, and which to count as mistakes. Before a paradigm, different men supply different beliefs, so they describe and interpret the same range of phenomena in different ways (p. 17).

**How a school wins: the Leyden jar and Franklin (pp. 17–18).**
- The divergences disappear to a considerable extent, "apparently once and for all". The cause is usually the triumph of one pre-paradigm school whose beliefs led it to stress only a special part of the pool of information (p. 17).
- Example: electricians who held the fluid view and stressed conduction conceived the idea of bottling the fluid. This produced the Leyden jar, independently developed by at least two investigators in the early 1740s, and it might never have been discovered by casual or random exploration (p. 17).
- Franklin tried to explain the jar from early on. Success there was the most effective argument making his theory a paradigm, though it could not account for all known repulsion (p. 17).
- Kuhn states that to be accepted as a paradigm a theory must seem better than its competitors, but it need not, and in fact never does, explain all the facts it can be confronted with (pp. 17–18).
- The paradigm then did for all electricians what the fluid theory did for its subgroup. It suggested which experiments were worth doing and which were not. It did this far more effectively, partly because the end of inter-school debate ended the constant reiteration of fundamentals, and partly because confidence of being on the right track encouraged more precise, esoteric and consuming work (p. 18). Fact collection and theory articulation became highly directed (p. 18).
- Kuhn says the increased effectiveness of electrical research is evidence for a societal version of Bacon's dictum: "Truth emerges more readily from error than from confusion" (p. 18).
- Footnote 9: Symmer's 1759 two-fluid version divided electricians. They rapidly concluded that no experimental test could separate the two versions, so they were equivalent. Both schools still used the Franklinian theory (p. 18).

**Effects on the group (pp. 18–20).**
- When a synthesis first attracts most of the next generation, older schools gradually disappear, partly by conversion. Those who cling to older views are "simply read out of the profession" and their work is ignored (pp. 18–19).
- The new paradigm implies a new, more rigid definition of the field. Those unwilling or unable to adapt must proceed in isolation or attach themselves to another group; historically they have often stayed in the philosophy departments from which many special sciences were spawned (p. 19). Footnote 11 gives examples: Nollet's last disciples, astrology and "romantic" chemistry (p. 19).
- Kuhn says it is sometimes just reception of a paradigm that turns a group studying nature into a profession or discipline (p. 19). In the sciences (not in medicine, technology or law, which serve external social need), specialised journals, societies and a place in the curriculum have usually been associated with a group's first reception of a single paradigm, at least between about a century and a half ago and the very recent time when the trappings of specialization acquired a prestige of their own (p. 19).
- With a paradigm taken for granted, the scientist need not rebuild the field from first principles. Textbooks cover that. He can start where the textbook ends and focus on the subtlest aspects (pp. 19–20).
- Research reports then change. Usually they are no longer embodied in books like Franklin's *Experiments … on Electricity* or Darwin's *Origin of Species*, but appear as brief articles addressed only to colleagues who share the paradigm. These colleagues prove to be the only ones able to read them (p. 20).
- Today books in the sciences are usually texts or retrospective reflections, and the author is more likely to harm than help his reputation (p. 20). Only in fields that still keep the book (with or without the article) as a vehicle for research communication are the lines of professionalization so loosely drawn that a layman may hope to follow progress in the original reports (p. 20).
- Research reports stopped being intelligible to a generally educated audience in antiquity (mathematics, astronomy), in the later Middle Ages (dynamics, which regained general intelligibility only briefly in the early seventeenth century), and before the end of the eighteenth century (electricity, which began to need translation for the layman). Most other physical sciences followed in the nineteenth century. Similar transitions can be isolated in the biological sciences, and they may well be occurring now in parts of the social sciences (pp. 20–21).
- Kuhn says it is customary, and surely proper, to deplore the widening gulf between the professional scientist and colleagues in other fields, but too little attention is paid to the essential relationship between that gulf and the mechanisms intrinsic to scientific advance (p. 21).

**The transition to maturity (pp. 21–22).**
- One field after another has crossed the divide between what a historian might call its prehistory as a science and its history proper. These transitions have seldom been as sudden or unequivocal as Kuhn's schematic discussion may have implied, but neither have they been gradual over the whole development of the field (p. 21).
- Electricity example: writers in 1700–1740 knew far more than in the sixteenth century, and few new types of phenomena appeared between 1740 and 1790. Yet in important respects the writings of Cavendish, Coulomb and Volta (late eighteenth century) seem further from those of Gray, Du Fay and even Franklin than the writings of these early eighteenth-century men are from the sixteenth century (p. 21).
- Sometime between 1740 and 1780 electricians could first take their foundations for granted. They moved to recondite problems and wrote articles for each other (p. 21).
- Astronomers in antiquity, students of motion in the Middle Ages, of physical optics in the late seventeenth century and of historical geology in the early nineteenth had achieved the same thing: a paradigm able to guide the whole group's research (pp. 21–22). Kuhn adds that, except with hindsight, it is hard to find another criterion that so clearly proclaims a field a science (p. 22).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A made-up field, "tide study", has 12 phenomena (1–12) and three schools of 10 researchers each.
- School A's theory explains {1,2,3,4,5}, B's explains {4,5,6,7,8}, and C's explains {8,9,10,11,12}. Each explains 5 of 12, and each stresses its own set.
- A new theory explains {1,…,9}, i.e. 9 of 12. It beats each rival (9 > 5) but leaves {10,11,12} unexplained. This matches Kuhn's point that a paradigm must seem better than competitors, not explain everything (pp. 17–18).
- Suppose all three schools convert. Assume that before, each researcher spent 40% of time defending fundamentals, and after, 10%. Time on specific problems: before, 30 × 0.60 = 18 researcher-units. After, 30 × 0.90 = 27. The numbers are invented, but they show the direction of Kuhn's claim (p. 18).

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — Here: an achievement that attracts an enduring group and leaves open problems; normal science is research based on it (p. 10). Kuhn says the paradigm is a concrete model (law, theory, application, instrumentation together), not only a theory (p. 10).
- [[pre-paradigm science]] (new) — A stage of competing schools, near-random fact-gathering and repeated debate over fundamentals (pp. 12–16).
- [[scientific community]] (new) — Group whose members were trained on the same models and share rules and standards (p. 11).
- [[scientific revolution]] (new) — Transition from one paradigm to another; successive transitions of this kind are the usual developmental pattern of mature science (p. 12).
- [[family resemblance]] — Used loosely: Kuhn says the electrical theories had no more than a family resemblance (p. 14). He does not define the phrase here.
- [[textbook science]] (new) — Textbooks present accepted theory and applications, and free researchers from rebuilding foundations (pp. 10, 19–20).

## Canonical instances
- [[optics-before-newton]] — Many competing schools, no single view of light, until Newton's paradigm (pp. 12–13).
- [[optics-paradigm-sequence]] — Corpuscular (Newton) → wave (Young, Fresnel) → photon (Planck, Einstein) (p. 12).
- [[electricity-before-franklin]] — Three groups of theories; Franklin's theory gave a common paradigm (pp. 13–15).
- [[leyden-jar]] — Product of fluid-theory focus; Franklin's explanation of it helped make his theory a paradigm (p. 17).
- [[baconian-natural-histories]] — Fact collection without theory as a "morass" (p. 16).
- [[symmer-two-fluid-theory]] — Debate that did not undermine the paradigm, because the versions were judged equivalent (p. 18, fn. 9).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Pre-paradigm schools ↔ analytic-philosophy: rival "schools" in a field that disagree on fundamentals (Kuhn says this pattern persists in some creative fields, p. 13) — only an analogy.
- [metaphor] "Truth emerges more readily from error than from confusion" (p. 18) ↔ Popper/falsifiability canon: bold, directed conjectures beat unguided collecting — only if one reads it that way; Kuhn does not make this link.
- [structural-analogy] No natural history without implicit theory (p. 17) ↔ underdetermination/theory-ladenness: observation needs guiding belief.

## Questions this raises
- If a paradigm "never" explains all facts (pp. 17–18), what makes a mismatch count as a problem and not just a normal gap? (Not answered in these pages.)
- What does it mean that a paradigm cannot be "fully reduced" to logical components (p. 11)? Why is the concrete example prior to rules?
- Is a paradigm accepted because it is better, or because the group needs consensus? The text says "must seem better" (p. 17).
- Which social sciences now count as having paradigms (p. 15)? What would the criterion on p. 22 say about them?
