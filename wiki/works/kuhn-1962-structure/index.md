# Kuhn, T. S. (1962/2012). *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (4th ed., 50th anniversary edition, with an introductory essay by Ian Hacking). Chicago: University of Chicago Press. 264 pp.

## In one paragraph
Kuhn argues that science does not simply pile up facts. Most of the time it is "normal science": a community solves puzzles within a shared paradigm, an accepted model of good work. Puzzles that keep failing become anomalies. When anomalies pile up, the field enters crisis, and a rival paradigm may replace the old one in a scientific revolution. Rival paradigms differ in standards, in the meanings of terms and in the "world" scientists see, so choosing between them is persuasion within a community, not proof. Textbooks hide this pattern and make science look cumulative. Kuhn explains progress by the nature of the scientific community, not by approach to a fixed truth. The Postscript splits "paradigm" into disciplinary matrix and exemplars and rejects the charge of relativism. The book made the social organisation of science a philosophical topic.

## The path through the work
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/front-preface]] — Kuhn explains how studying the history of science, and then comparing natural and social scientists, led him to the idea of "paradigms" and to an essay that space limits made short, schematic and limited in scope.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch01-introduction]] — Kuhn argues that history of science, if it studies past science on its own terms, could replace the textbook picture of steady accumulation with a picture of normal science and scientific revolutions.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch02-route]] — A field's acquisition of one achievement (a "paradigm") that draws practitioners away from rival schools and leaves open problems for the whole group is a sign of its maturity as a science.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch03-nature]] — Normal science is the "mop-up" work of making a new paradigm's early promise real, and three kinds of problem (determining significant facts, matching facts to theory, articulating the theory) exhaust its literature.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch04-normal]] — Normal research resembles puzzle-solving: a solution is assured, the result is largely foreseeable, and a network of rules limits what counts as a solution. These rules may derive from paradigms, but paradigms can guide research without them.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch05-priority]] — Shared paradigms (concrete exemplary solutions) can guide normal science without any full set of rules being abstracted from them, so paradigms may come first.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch06-anomaly]] — A fundamental discovery is an extended process that starts when a paradigm-based expectation fails and ends when the paradigm is adjusted. A precise paradigm makes anomalies stand out, so puzzle-solving normal science, though it aims at no novelties, is very effective at producing them.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch07-crisis]] — New theories are generally preceded by a period in which normal science visibly fails at its own puzzles, and such a crisis indicates that an occasion for "retooling" has arrived.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch08-response]] — Scientists in crisis do not drop a paradigm because of anomalies alone; they reject it only when a rival is available, and crisis turns research into a looser, more speculative "extraordinary" kind.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch09-nature]] — After the pre-paradigm period, revolutions, in which an older paradigm gives way to an incompatible newer one, are historically the rule for new theories. Paradigm choice cannot be settled by the criteria of normal science alone.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch10-revolutions]] — Scientists reach nature only through what they see and do, and a paradigm shapes both. It may therefore be reasonable to say they work in a different world after a revolution, and a revolution is not fully reducible to reinterpreting stable data.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch11-invisibility]] — Science textbooks must be rewritten after every revolution and so hide the revolutions; together with popular and philosophical writing modelled on them, they make science look largely cumulative.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch12-resolution]] — Rival paradigms cannot be tested against nature one at a time or settled by proof, so a community moves to a new paradigm through varied persuasion, partly on faith in future promise, as allegiances gradually shift.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/ch13-progress]] — Kuhn explains scientific progress by the nature of the scientific community (its insulation, its training, its role as sole judge of paradigms) rather than by movement toward a fixed truth, and compares this to Darwinian evolution without a goal.
- [[kuhn-1962-structure/postscript-1969]] — Kuhn splits "paradigm" into a community's disciplinary matrix and its shared exemplars, and uses this split, especially the exemplar sense, to answer charges that his picture of science is subjective, irrational or relativist.

## Big ideas across units
- **Normal science as puzzle-solving inside a paradigm.** A paradigm is an achievement that draws a group and leaves problems open. Normal science articulates it and treats problems as puzzles with assured solutions. Units: ch02-route, ch03-nature, ch04-normal, ch05-priority, postscript-1969.
- **Paradigms guide without complete rules; knowledge is tacit and learned from exemplars.** Family resemblance, learning through applications, and the later exemplar and disciplinary-matrix split. Units: ch04-normal, ch05-priority, postscript-1969.
- **From anomaly to crisis to extraordinary research.** Anomalies appear only against a precise paradigm. Crisis brings proliferating versions and blurred rules, then speculative research and often a new paradigm. Units: front-preface, ch06-anomaly, ch07-crisis, ch08-response.
- **Revolutions are non-cumulative and paradigms are incommensurable.** Old theories are not special cases of new ones, and standards, meanings and "worlds" change. Units: ch01-introduction, ch09-nature, ch10-revolutions, ch12-resolution, ch13-progress, postscript-1969.
- **Observation is shaped by paradigm.** Expectations built into instruments, gestalt-like switches, and the absence of a neutral observation language. Units: ch06-anomaly, ch08-response, ch10-revolutions, ch12-resolution, postscript-1969.
- **Paradigm choice by persuasion, not proof; a challenge to falsification.** Every puzzle can be seen as a counterinstance, testing happens only between rivals, and conversion rests on varied grounds and faith. Units: ch08-response, ch09-nature, ch12-resolution, postscript-1969.
- **Textbooks and the image of cumulative science.** Textbooks hide revolutions and rewrite history, and they also shape education and the community's efficiency. Units: ch01-introduction, ch02-route, ch11-invisibility, ch13-progress.
- **Progress is explained by the scientific community.** A community with insulation, shared standards and sole authority, and with "evolution-from" instead of "evolution-toward". The Postscript argues that this is not relativism and calls for comparative study of communities. Units: ch02-route, ch13-progress, postscript-1969.

## How to read it
- **Essential.** The catalog's focus is chs. II–V (ch02–ch05), chs. VI–X (ch06–ch10) and the Postscript. The Hacking introductory essay is also in the focus list but has no unit here.
- **Dependencies.** The units say each chapter builds on the previous ones. Ch03–ch05 depend on the terms "paradigm" and "normal science" from ch02. Ch06–ch08 depend on the paradigm and puzzle-solving material. Ch09 and ch10 depend on anomaly and crisis. Ch12 depends on incommensurability (ch09), the gestalt switch and the neutral observation language (ch10). The Postscript assumes the whole book.
- **Lighter on a first pass.** Outside the catalog's focus are the Preface, ch01, ch11 and ch13. The Preface states the essay's aims and limits, and ch01 sets out the book's overall argument. Ch11 and ch13 can be read after the core, since they develop the cumulative image and progress, which rest on the earlier chapters.
