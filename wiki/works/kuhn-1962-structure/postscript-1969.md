# Postscript—1969: Kuhn on paradigms, communities, exemplars and theory choice

## L0 — One line
Kuhn splits "paradigm" into a community's whole disciplinary matrix and its shared exemplars (concrete problem-solutions), and uses this split, especially the exemplar sense, in replying to the charges that his picture of science is subjective, irrational or relativist.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Almost seven years after publication, Kuhn revisits his book. One reader counted many uses of "paradigm", so Kuhn separates two senses. One is the disciplinary matrix: everything a scientific community shares (generalizations, models, values, exemplars). The other is exemplars: concrete problem-solutions learned in training. Exemplars carry tacit knowledge, which is systematic but cannot be restated as rules without essential change. Building on this, Kuhn says debates between rival theories whose supporters communicate only partially cannot fully resemble formal proof. Kuhn denies that this makes it irrational: shared values give good reasons, and persuasion and translation help, though conversion is a separate step. He rejects relativism, defends progress as puzzle-solving, and calls for comparative study of scientific communities.

**Prerequisites:** the main ideas of Kuhn's book (normal science, puzzle-solving, anomaly, crisis, revolution, incommensurability, gestalt switch); elementary mechanics (f = ma).

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Community first, paradigm second.** The original definition was circular (a paradigm is what a community shares; a community is those who share a paradigm). Communities should be identified independently, for example by networks and citations, and only then examined for what they share (p. 176).
- **Paradigm has two senses.** The disciplinary matrix is the whole constellation of group commitments. Exemplars are one component of it, the concrete problem-solutions used as models (pp. 175, 186–187).
- **The matrix has four components discussed here:** symbolic generalizations, models, values, exemplars. Kuhn says he gives no exhaustive list (pp. 182, 186). Symbolic generalizations work partly as laws and partly as definitions (p. 183).
- **Exemplars teach a way of seeing.** Students learn by problems to see new situations as like old ones, so that law-sketches (schematic forms of a law) such as f = ma attach to nature. Kuhn now takes this to be the most novel and least understood aspect of the book (pp. 187–191).
- **Tacit knowledge is not subjective intuition.** It is shared, tested, in principle analyzable, and cannot be restated as rules without distortion. Perception differs from interpretation (pp. 191–198).
- **Theory choice cannot be cast as formal proof; it relies on persuasion.** Reasons such as accuracy and simplicity act as values. They can be applied differently by scientists who all honor them, and there is no neutral algorithm for choice (pp. 199–200).
- **Translation and conversion.** Parties to a communication breakdown can act as translators of each other's languages. Translating a theory is not adopting it; conversion is not chosen by deliberation (pp. 202–204).
- **Not relativism.** Kuhn holds that if the criteria for telling earlier from later theories can be completed, as he expects, scientific development is unidirectional and irreversible and later theories are better puzzle-solvers. He finds the idea of a match between a theory's ontology (the entities it says exist) and what is really there illusive in principle (pp. 205–207).
- **Science resembles other fields, yet differs.** To the "is/ought" charge he replies that his theory has consequences for how scientists should behave, and that the circularity between theory and evidence is, he thinks, not vicious. Comparative study of communities is the next task (pp. 207–210).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening (pp. 174–176)
Kuhn says that on fundamentals his viewpoint is very nearly unchanged. He now sees aspects of the original formulation that create needless difficulties and misunderstandings, some of them his own (p. 174). He says that removing these should ultimately provide a basis for a new version of the book. For this edition he made no systematic rewriting: only typographical corrections and two isolable errors, namely the role of Newton's *Principia* in eighteenth-century mechanics (pp. 30–33 of the book) and the response to crises (p. 84) (p. 174, n. 2).

The plan (pp. 175–176):
1. Separate "paradigm" from "scientific community".
2. Distinguish two senses of "paradigm": the sociological sense (a constellation of beliefs, values and techniques shared by a community), and the sense of exemplary past achievements, i.e. concrete puzzle-solutions used as models that can replace explicit rules (p. 175).
3. The second sense is philosophically the deeper one. It is the main source of controversy, including the charge that Kuhn makes science a subjective and irrational enterprise (p. 175).
4. Subsections 4–5 treat tacit knowledge and theory choice; 6–7 treat relativism, the descriptive/normative charge, and application to other fields (pp. 175–176).

### 1. Paradigms and Community Structure (pp. 176–181)

**The circularity.** A paradigm is what a community's members share, and a community consists of men who share a paradigm. Not all circularities are vicious, but this one causes real difficulties (p. 176). Scientific communities can and should be isolated without prior recourse to paradigms. Paradigms can then be discovered by scrutinizing the members' behavior. A rewritten book would open with community structure (p. 176).

**The intuitive notion of community** (pp. 177–178):
- A community is made up of the practitioners of a scientific specialty. They have had similar educations and initiations, absorbed the same technical literature, and drawn many of the same lessons (p. 177).
- The boundaries of that literature usually mark the limits of a subject matter. Competing schools exist in science but are rarer than in other fields, are always in competition, and the competition usually ends quickly (p. 177).
- Within a community, communication is relatively full and professional judgment relatively unanimous. Across communities, communication is sometimes arduous and often produces misunderstanding (p. 177).
- Communities exist at many levels. At the top levels, subject of highest degree, society membership and journals read are ordinarily more than enough to establish membership for the major groupings and their main subgroups, except at the fringes. Empirical problems emerge only at the next lower level, such as the phage group. There one needs conferences, circulation of draft manuscripts, and communication networks including correspondence and citation linkages (pp. 177–178).
- Such groups may typically have about one hundred members, occasionally significantly fewer. Individual scientists, particularly the ablest, usually belong to several groups (p. 178).

Communities of this sort are the units that the book presented as the producers and validators of scientific knowledge. A series of issues need only community structure, not a theory of what is shared (p. 178):

1. **Pre- to post-paradigm transition.** Before it, several schools compete. Afterward, usually one remains, and a more efficient, esoteric, puzzle-oriented practice begins (p. 178). Kuhn now thinks the transition need not (he says "should not") be associated with the *first acquisition* of a paradigm. All communities, including pre-paradigm schools, share the elements he called a paradigm. What changes is its *nature*. Only after the change is normal puzzle-solving possible (p. 179).
2. **Communities are not the same as subject matters.** The book implicitly identified them one-to-one. Historians showed this fails. For example, there was no physics community before the mid-nineteenth century, when parts of mathematics and natural philosophy merged (p. 179). Normal science and revolutions are community-based. "A paradigm governs, in the first instance, not a subject matter but rather a group of practitioners" (pp. 179–180).
3. **Disagreement about theories of matter is no counterexample.** Critics said that disputes over theories of matter show scientists are less unanimous than Kuhn claims. Kuhn agrees with the description but not the inference. Until at least about 1920 theories of matter were tools for many specialist groups, not the province of one community. What a community must agree on depends on what it does. Early nineteenth-century chemists shared Dalton-derived tools (constant proportion, multiple proportion, combining weights) yet could disagree about the existence of atoms (p. 180).
4. **Revolutions need not be big.** A revolution is a reconstruction of group commitments. It need not be large, nor seem revolutionary outside a community of perhaps fewer than twenty-five people (p. 181).

**Crisis.** Kuhn says nothing important depends on crisis being an absolute prerequisite of revolution. It needs only to be the usual prelude, a self-correcting mechanism against permanent rigidity. Revolutions may be induced in other ways, though he thinks seldom. Crises need not arise from the work of the community that experiences them. New instruments such as the electron microscope or new laws such as Maxwell's may arise in one specialty and cause crisis in another (p. 181).

*Intuition note:* Kuhn moves the focus from ideas to people. First find the group; then ask what binds it. A revolution can be a small affair inside a community of perhaps fewer than twenty-five people.

### 2. Paradigms as the Constellation of Group Commitments (pp. 181–187)

A sympathetic reader (Masterman) found at least twenty-two uses of "paradigm" (p. 181). Kuhn attributes most to stylistic inconsistency (Newton's Laws are sometimes a paradigm, sometimes part of one, sometimes paradigmatic). Two usages remain (pp. 181–182).

For the global use he proposes **disciplinary matrix**: "disciplinary" because it is the common possession of a discipline's practitioners, "matrix" because it is composed of ordered elements of various sorts. He would be glad to recover "theory" for this use, but in philosophy of science "theory" connotes a structure far more limited (p. 182). Most things earlier called paradigms, parts of paradigms or paradigmatic are constituents of the matrix, and they function together. He lists the main components, not an exhaustive list (p. 182).

**(a) Symbolic generalizations.** These are expressions used by group members without question or dissent that can readily be cast in logical form, $(x)(y)(z)\,\phi(x,y,z)$ (the text layer has the function symbol missing here and I could not check an image; $\phi$ is reconstructed). They are the formal or readily formalizable components (p. 182).
- Examples: $f = ma$; $I = V/R$; in words, that elements combine in constant proportion by weight, and that action equals reaction; the Joule-Lenz Law, $H = RI^2$ (the text layer shows "H — RI2"; the equals sign is inferred) (pp. 182–183).
- Without such expressions members would have no point at which to attach logical and mathematical manipulation. Taxonomy suggests normal science can proceed with few of them, but a science's power seems quite generally to grow with their number (p. 183).
- They look like laws, but usually serve two functions. They work partly as laws and partly as definitions of their symbols. The balance between these shifts over time (p. 183).
- This matters because laws are often corrigible piecemeal, whereas definitions, being tautologies, are not (p. 183). Part of what accepting Ohm's Law demanded was redefining "current" and "resistance". With the old meanings Ohm's Law could not have been right, which is why it was strenuously opposed while the Joule-Lenz Law was not (p. 183).
- Kuhn says he currently suspects that all revolutions involve abandoning generalizations whose force had partly been that of tautologies. He asks whether Einstein showed that simultaneity was relative or altered the notion of simultaneity, and whether those who heard paradox in "relativity of simultaneity" were simply wrong. He poses these as questions (pp. 183–184).

**(b) Models.** The "metaphysical paradigms" of the original are now described as beliefs in particular models, and the category is widened to include heuristic models (p. 184).
- Examples: heat is the kinetic energy of the constituent parts of bodies; all perceptible phenomena are due to interacting atoms in the void; the electric circuit as a steady-state hydrodynamic system; gas molecules as tiny elastic billiard balls (p. 184).
- Group commitment varies along the spectrum from heuristic to ontological models, but all models supply preferred or permissible analogies and metaphors. This helps determine what counts as an explanation and a puzzle-solution, and which puzzles remain and how important they are (p. 184).
- Members may not have to share even heuristic models, though they usually do. Early-nineteenth-century chemists did not need to believe in atoms (p. 184).

**(c) Values.** These are usually more widely shared across communities than generalizations or models, and give natural scientists a sense of community. Though they function at all times, their particular importance shows when members must identify a crisis or choose between incompatible ways of practicing their discipline (pp. 184–185).
- Probably the most deeply held values concern predictions: accuracy, quantitative over qualitative, consistent satisfaction of permissible error (p. 185).
- Values for whole theories: first, they must permit puzzle-formulation and solution; where possible they should be simple, self-consistent and plausible (compatible with other theories in use). Others exist, such as social usefulness (p. 185).
- Kuhn now thinks it a weakness that the original gave little attention to internal and external consistency as sources of crisis and factors in theory choice (p. 185).
- Values may be shared by men who differ in applying them. Accuracy judgments are relatively, though not entirely, stable. Simplicity, consistency and plausibility often vary greatly between individuals. An inconsistency in the old quantum theory that was insupportable to Einstein was, for Bohr and others, a difficulty expected to resolve itself by normal means (p. 185).
- Different values taken alone often dictate different choices. One theory may be more accurate but less consistent or plausible than another, as with the old quantum theory (p. 185).

*Kuhn's reply to the charge of subjectivity and irrationality* (p. 186): critics took this variability as a weakness. Kuhn gives two points about value judgments in any field.
1. Shared values can determine group behavior even when members apply them differently. Painters did not all paint alike when representation was a primary value, yet the arts changed drastically when it was abandoned. Imagine science if consistency ceased to be a primary value (p. 186).
2. Individual variability may serve functions essential to science. The points where values must be applied are invariably also points where risks must be taken. Most anomalies are resolved by normal means, and most new theories prove wrong. If everyone treated each anomaly as a crisis, science would cease; if no one took high-risk responses, there would be few or no revolutions. Relying on shared values rather than shared rules for individual choice may be the community's way of spreading risk (p. 186).

**(d) Exemplars.** This is the component for which "paradigm" would be entirely appropriate and which first led Kuhn to the word (pp. 186–187). By exemplars he means, initially, the concrete problem-solutions students meet from the start of their education (laboratories, examinations, chapter-end problems). He adds at least some technical problem-solutions in the periodical literature (p. 187). Differences between sets of exemplars provide the "community fine-structure" of science. All physicists begin with inclined plane, conical pendulum, Keplerian orbits, and instruments such as the vernier, calorimeter and Wheatstone bridge. Later their shared symbolic generalizations are illustrated by different exemplars. Solid-state and field-theoretic physicists share the Schrödinger equation, but only its more elementary applications are common to both (p. 187).

*Intuition note:* "Paradigm" in the book covered several different things, among them formulas, pictures of nature, values and worked examples. Kuhn now keeps them apart. His point about values is that sharing them still leaves room for disagreement, and that room may be useful.

### 3. Paradigms as Shared Examples (pp. 187–191)

Kuhn says he now takes the paradigm as shared example to be the most novel and least understood aspect of the book (p. 187).

**The view he opposes.** Philosophers of science have not discussed student problems, treating them as practice in applying what the student already knows. Scientific knowledge is thought to be embedded in theory and rules, and problems merely supply facility in applying them (p. 187). Kuhn argues this localization of cognitive content is wrong. At the start and for some time after, doing problems is a way of learning consequential things about nature. Without exemplars, the laws and theories learned would have little empirical content (p. 188).

**f = ma as a law-sketch.** A sociologist or linguist who finds that "f = ma" is uttered and received unproblematically will not, without much additional investigation, have learned a great deal about what it means or how scientists attach it to nature. Acceptance does not imply agreement about meaning and application (p. 188). The question is how students learn to pick out the relevant forces, masses and accelerations in a given experimental situation (p. 188). Moreover, it is not quite the case that logic and mathematics are applied directly to $f = ma$, which proves on examination to be a "law-sketch" or "law-schema". Its form changes with the problem: free fall, the simple pendulum, a pair of interacting harmonic oscillators, the gyroscope (pp. 188–189). The only equation legible to me in the pages given is for the oscillators:
$$m_1\frac{d^2 s_1}{dt^2} + k_1 s_1 = k_2 (s_2 - s_1 + d)$$
(p. 189). The free-fall and pendulum versions fall on p. 188, where the text layer shows blanks and I had no page image: [unreadable p. 188].

Students learn to design the appropriate version, often one with no literal precedent. Kuhn's clue is that students read a chapter, understand it, and still fail the end-of-chapter problems. The difficulty dissolves when the student sees the problem as *like* one already met. Having grasped the analogy, they interrelate symbols and attach them to nature as before (p. 189). The law-sketch is a tool: it tells the student what similarities to look for and signals the gestalt in which the situation is to be seen (p. 189). After a number of problems, which varies widely between individuals, the student views situations in the same gestalt as other members of his specialists' group. They are no longer the same situations met at the start, and the student has assimilated a way of seeing that is time-tested and licensed by the group (p. 189).

**History: the chain of exemplars** (pp. 189–190):
- Galileo found that a ball rolling down an incline acquires just enough velocity to return to the same vertical height on a second incline of any slope. He saw this as like a pendulum with a point-mass bob (p. 190).
- Huyghens solved the center of oscillation of a physical pendulum by imagining the extended body as composed of Galilean point-pendula whose bonds could be instantly released at any point in the swing. After release, the collective center of gravity at the highest points would rise only to the height from which the original center had begun to fall (p. 190).
- Daniel Bernoulli made the flow of water from an orifice resemble Huyghens's pendulum. He determined the descent of the center of gravity of the water in tank and jet over an infinitesimal interval. He imagined each particle then rising separately to the maximum height reachable with its acquired velocity. The ascent of the individual particles' center of gravity must equal the descent of the water's. From that view, the speed of efflux followed at once (p. 190).

The three problems employ only one law, the Principle of vis viva, "Actual descent equals potential ascent" (p. 191). Its verbal statement, taken by itself, is "virtually impotent" (p. 191). For someone who did not know the problems, it could function only after learning to recognize "actual descents" and "potential ascents" as ingredients of nature. That is learning about the situations nature does and does not present, and it is not acquired by purely verbal means. "Nature and words are learned together." The result is Polanyi's "tacit knowledge", learned by doing science rather than by acquiring rules (p. 191).

*Intuition note:* A formula alone tells you little. You learn what it says by solving problems, because that teaches you which real situations count as cases of it. That knowledge is built into how you look at problems.

### 4. Tacit Knowledge and Intuition (pp. 191–198)

**Against the charge of intuition.** Some readers thought Kuhn made science rest on "unanalyzable individual intuitions". He replies that if he speaks of intuitions at all, they are not individual but are tested, shared possessions of a successful group, which the novice acquires in training. Nor are they unanalyzable in principle. He is experimenting with a computer program to investigate their properties at an elementary level (pp. 191–192).

**What he claims** (p. 192): knowledge embedded in exemplars is not less systematic or analyzable than knowledge in rules. It is a manner of knowing that is "miscontrued" if reconstructed as rules first abstracted from exemplars and then used in their stead. The ability to see a situation as like some and unlike others is not a process that could not be fully explicable in neuro-cerebral terms. But he claims that such an explication will not, by its nature, answer the question "Similar with respect to what?", which is a request for criteria. He argues the temptation to seek criteria (or at least a full set) should be resisted in this case. He says he opposes not system as such but a particular sort of system (p. 192).

**Stimuli and sensations (a digression)** (pp. 192–193):
- Two people at the same place looking the same way receive closely similar stimuli (identical if both eyes could be at the same place). But people do not see stimuli; knowledge of stimuli is highly theoretical and abstract. They have sensations, which need not be the same (p. 192). Color blindness went unnoticed until Dalton's description in 1794 (p. 192).
- Much neural processing lies between stimulus and sensation. Kuhn lists three things known with assurance: very different stimuli can produce the same sensations; the same stimulus can produce very different sensations; the route from stimulus to sensation is in part conditioned by education (p. 193).
- Groups whose members have systematically different sensations on receipt of the same stimuli do in some sense live in different worlds, in his words (p. 193). Kuhn posits stimuli, and their immutability to avoid solipsism, and says he has not the slightest reservation about either posit (p. 193). Our world is populated first by the objects of our sensations.
- Members of the same group (sharing education, language, experience, culture) have good reason to be supposed to share sensations, as full communication suggests. Where group differentiation begins we lack evidence for the immutability of sensation. Supposing it is the same for all groups is, he suspects, "mere parochialism" (p. 193).

**How exemplars work** (pp. 193–195): members of a group learn to see the same things by being shown examples of situations that predecessors learned to see as alike, and as different from other situations. Kuhn's illustrations are:
- successive presentations of one person (mother, as distinct from father or sister);
- natural families (swans versus geese);
- for specialists, "Newtonian situations" subject to a version of $f = ma$, as against situations to which optical law-sketches apply (p. 194).

**Rules or involuntary recognition?** It is tempting to say that exemplars give rules plus the ability to apply them, since recognition is the result of neural processing fully governed by physical and chemical laws (p. 194). But the parallel with the beating of the heart suggests recognition may be involuntary. Speaking of rules and criteria implies access to alternatives, such as disobeying a rule or misapplying a criterion, which, he takes it, we do not have in perception (p. 194, with n. 13 on laws that can or cannot be "broken").
- After having a sensation we do seek criteria and engage in *interpretation*, a deliberative process choosing among alternatives. His examples: exclaiming on a second look that it wasn't mother because she has red hair; a waterfowl's tail feathers (swan or goose?); and wanting to know a general characteristic of a natural family (pp. 194–195).
- All these processes, he says, must ultimately be neural and so obey the same physico-chemical laws. But this provides no reason to suppose that the neural apparatus operates the same way in interpretation as in perception, or in either as in the heartbeat (p. 195).
- Kuhn therefore opposes the attempt, which he describes as traditional since Descartes but not before (the text layer reads "bat not before"), to analyze perception as an interpretive process, an unconscious version of what we do after perceiving (p. 195).

**Why "knowledge"** (pp. 195–196). A properly programmed perceptual mechanism has survival value. Different groups may have different perceptions, but not just any. A group that could not tell wolves from dogs could not endure in many environments. Nuclear physicists must recognize alpha-particle and electron tracks (pp. 195–196). What is built into the stimulus-to-sensation route (i) is transmitted through education, (ii) has been found by trial more effective than its historical competitors in a group's current environment, and (iii) is subject to change through education and through discovery of misfits. Those are characteristics of knowledge. One is missing: no direct access, no rules or generalizations to express it. Rules would refer to stimuli, known only through elaborate theory, so the knowledge stays tacit (p. 196).

**Literal and metaphorical seeing.** The account of sensation is meant literally, as a hypothesis about vision subject to experimental investigation though probably not direct check (p. 196). But talk of "seeing" in the book also had metaphorical functions. We do not see electrons but their tracks, nor currents but a meter needle. Yet the book acted as if we perceive theoretical entities, learn to do so from exemplars, and as if talk of criteria and interpretation would be wrong there too. Kuhn says the metaphor is hardly enough to support such claims and in the long run must be eliminated in favour of literal discourse. He cannot do so here and tries instead to "bulwark" it (pp. 196–197).
- A layman sees water droplets in a cloud chamber and must interpret them. The experienced man literally sees tracks of particles. The tracks are criteria interpreted as indices of particles, by a route that is shorter than, and different from, the layman's (p. 197).
- Likewise the scientist reading an ammeter sees the meter in the context of the entire circuit, and knows its internal structure. The needle's position is a criterion only of the current's value, and he need only decide which scale applies. For the layman the needle is a criterion of nothing except itself (pp. 197–198).
- "Interpretation begins where perception ends." What is left for interpretation depends on prior experience and training (p. 198).

**Footnote 14 (cryptic remarks)** (p. 197). Immediate recognition of members of natural families depends on there being, after neural processing, "empty perceptual space" between the families. If there were a perceived continuum from geese to swans, a specific criterion would be needed. For unobservable entities: if a theory admits nothing else like an electric current, a small number of criteria, varying from case to case, will suffice even though no rules give necessary and sufficient conditions. A corollary: given necessary and sufficient conditions for identifying a theoretical entity, it can be eliminated from the theory's ontology by substitution. Without such rules, entities are not eliminable, and the theory demands their existence (p. 197, n. 14).

*Intuition note:* Kuhn says recognizing a case is like perceiving, not like applying a rule. It can be fully physical and still lack a written criterion. Rules come in later, when we stop and interpret what we saw.

### 5. Exemplars, Incommensurability, and Revolutions (pp. 198–204)

**What he meant.** In the book, Kuhn argued (Sections X and XIII) that parties to theory-choice debates inevitably see differently certain of the experimental or observational situations to which both have recourse. Their vocabularies consist predominantly of the same terms, so they must attach some terms to nature differently, and communication is inevitably only partial. So superiority of one theory cannot be proved in the debate; each side must try to convert the other by persuasion (p. 198). Kuhn says that only philosophers have seriously misconstrued the intent of these arguments. Some reported that Kuhn believes proponents of incommensurable theories cannot communicate at all, that there is therefore no recourse to good reasons, that theory is chosen for ultimately personal and subjective reasons, and that "mystical apperception" decides. Kuhn adds that, more than any other parts of the book, the passages these misconstructions rest on have been responsible for the charges of irrationality (pp. 198–199). Kuhn does not accept this reading.

**Proof and persuasion** (pp. 199–200):
- Kuhn calls his point simple and long familiar in philosophy of science: debates over theory-choice cannot be cast in a form that fully resembles logical or mathematical proof (p. 199).
- In logical or mathematical proof, premises and rules of inference are stipulated from the start. If the parties disagree about conclusions, they can retrace the steps one by one against the prior stipulations, and at the end one or the other must concede a mistake (p. 199).
- Only if the parties find that they differ about the meaning or application of the stipulated rules, so that prior agreement gives no sufficient basis for proof, does the debate continue in the form it takes during scientific revolutions. Such debate is about premises, and its recourse is persuasion as a prelude to the possibility of proof (p. 199).
- Nothing in this implies there are no good reasons, or that they are not ultimately decisive for the group. Nor does it imply the reasons differ from the usual list (accuracy, simplicity, fruitfulness). It does suggest that they function as values and can be differently applied by men who honor them (p. 199).
- If two men disagree about the relative fruitfulness of their theories, or about the relative importance of fruitfulness and scope, neither can be convicted of a mistake or called unscientific (pp. 199–200).
- "There is no neutral algorithm for theory-choice." In this sense the community, not its individual members, makes the effective decision. One must understand how shared values interact with shared experiences to ensure that most members will find one set of arguments decisive (p. 200).

**Changed similarity relations** (pp. 200–201):
- Two men who perceive the same situation differently yet use the same vocabulary must be using words differently (p. 200).
- Normal science depends on grouping objects and situations into similarity sets learned from exemplars, "primitive" in that grouping is done without first answering the question of what they are similar with respect to. A central aspect of any revolution is that some similarity relations change (p. 200).
- Examples: sun, moon, Mars and earth before and after Copernicus; free fall, pendular and planetary motion before and after Galileo; salts, alloys and a sulphur-iron filing mix before and after Dalton (p. 200).
- Since most objects in the altered sets stay together, set names are usually preserved, but transferring a subset is part of a critical change in the network of relations. Moving the metals from the set of compounds to the set of elements was essential to new theories of combustion, acidity, and physical and chemical combination (pp. 200–201).
- Two men who had apparently understood each other may suddenly respond to the same stimulus with incompatible descriptions. The difficulties cluster around phenomena on which theory choice most centrally depends (p. 201).
- These problems, though they first become evident in communication, are not merely linguistic. They cannot be resolved simply by stipulating definitions, because the words were learned partly by direct application to exemplars. The participants cannot resort to a neutral language adequate to both theories or even to both theories' empirical consequences. Part of the difference, he says, comes before the application of the languages in which it is nevertheless reflected (p. 201).

**Recourse: translation** (pp. 201–202). The stimuli are the same. Their general neural apparatus is the same, however differently programmed, and except in a small if all-important area of experience even their neural programming must be very nearly the same. They share a history except the immediate past. They should be able to find out a great deal about how they differ. The techniques needed are not straightforward, comfortable or in the normal scientist's arsenal (p. 201). What participants can do is recognize each other as members of different language communities and become translators (p. 202).
- Take the differences between intra- and inter-group discourse as a subject of study. Find the terms that are used unproblematically within each community but cause trouble between them. (Locutions with no trouble may be translated homophonically.) (p. 202)
- Then use shared everyday vocabularies to elucidate the troubles. Each tries to discover what the other would see and say when given a stimulus to which his own response would differ (p. 202).
- If they refrain from explaining anomalous behavior as mere error or madness, they may become very good predictors of each other's behavior. Kuhn says this is what the historian of science regularly does, or should do, with out-of-date theories (p. 202).
- Translation lets participants experience vicariously the merits and defects of each other's views. It is a potent tool for persuasion and conversion (p. 202). Kuhn's footnote on Quine says Quine seems to assume that a common stimulus yields a common sensation, and so says little about a translator's need to describe the world to which the translated language applies (p. 202, n. 17).

**Persuasion is not conversion** (pp. 202–204):
- To persuade is to convince someone that one's view is superior and ought to supplant his own. This is sometimes achieved without translation (p. 203).
- Each community can usually produce some concrete results describable in sentences both groups understand the same way but that the other community cannot yet account for. If the new view endures and is fruitful, such results grow. Some, especially newcomers to the profession, find these results decisive, reasoning that the proponents of the new view are clearly doing something right and that they must learn how (p. 203).
- Arguments in the shared vocabulary are not usually decisive until a very late stage. Among established members, few will be persuaded without the extended comparisons of translation, even at the price of sentences of great length and complexity (the Proust-Berthollet controversy conducted without "element") (p. 203).
- Translation does not guarantee persuasion. Kuhn says most people find translation threatening and that it is entirely foreign to normal science. Counter-arguments are always available and no rules prescribe how the balance must be struck. But as challenge after challenge is met, he says only blind stubbornness can finally explain continued resistance (pp. 203–204).
- To translate a theory is not to make it one's own. For that one must "go native", thinking and working in the language, not translating out of it. This transition is not made by deliberation and choice. At some point the person finds it has occurred. Or, like many who met relativity or quantum mechanics in middle years, he is fully persuaded yet cannot internalize the view. He may use the theory, but as a foreigner in a foreign environment, parasitic on the natives (p. 204).
- The conversion experience likened to a gestalt switch remains, he concludes, at the heart of the revolutionary process. Good reasons give motives and a climate for conversion. Translation may offer points of entry for the neural reprogramming that must underlie it. Neither constitutes conversion, and conversion is what we must explicate (p. 204).

*Intuition note:* Rivals share many words but have regrouped some objects, so they talk past each other. They cannot settle it by redefining terms, but they can learn each other's language, which helps them understand each other and persuade. Actually coming to think in the new language is something that happens to a person; it is not a decision.

### 6. Revolutions and Relativism (pp. 205–207)

Critics find the position relativistic. The parallel between proponents of different theories and members of different language-culture communities suggests that in some sense both groups may be right. Applied to culture, Kuhn grants this is relativistic. Applied to science it "may not be" (p. 205).

**The argument for progress.** Developed-science practitioners are fundamentally puzzle-solvers. Though theory-choice values derive from other aspects too, demonstrated ability to set up and solve puzzles is, in case of value conflict, the dominant criterion for most members. It is equivocal in application like any value, but a community that makes it preeminent behaves differently from one that does not (p. 205).

Take an evolutionary tree of specialties, with a line drawn up from trunk to branch tip without doubling back, tracing theories related by descent. For two such theories not too near their origin, it should be easy to list criteria letting an uncommitted observer tell the earlier from the later "time after time" (p. 205). Useful criteria: accuracy of prediction, particularly quantitative; the balance between esoteric and everyday subject matter; the number of problems solved. Less useful but important: simplicity, scope, compatibility with other specialties (p. 206). Kuhn says the lists are not yet the ones required but that he has no doubt they can be completed. If they can, development is, like biological development, unidirectional and irreversible. Later theories are better than earlier ones as puzzle-solvers in the often quite different environments where they are applied. That is not relativism, he says, and it shows the sense in which he is a convinced believer in scientific progress (p. 206).

**What this lacks.** The common notion of progress also holds that successive theories approximate the truth, that is, better represent how nature really is, which concerns ontology (the match between a theory's entities and what is "really there") rather than puzzle-solutions or predictions (p. 206). Kuhn says "perhaps" some other way of saving truth for whole theories exists, but this one will not do. He finds no theory-independent way to reconstruct "really there", and the idea of ontological match "illusive in principle". As a historian he sees no coherent direction of ontological development. He grants that Newton improves on Aristotle and Einstein on Newton as puzzle-solving instruments. Yet in some important respects, though not in all, Einstein's general relativity is closer to Aristotle's than either is to Newton's (pp. 206–207). He finds "relativist" a wrong description, and adds that if the position is relativism, he cannot see that the relativist loses anything needed to account for science (p. 207).

### 7. The Nature of Science (pp. 207–210)

**Description versus prescription** (pp. 207–208).
- **Objection (critics):** Kuhn moves between descriptive and normative modes (e.g. passages that open by saying that this is not what scientists do and close by claiming that they ought not to) and so violates the philosophical theorem that "is" cannot imply "ought" (p. 207).
- **Kuhn's reply:** the theorem has become a tag and is no longer everywhere honored. Some philosophers have found contexts where the normative and descriptive are inextricably mixed (p. 207). No subtleties are needed anyway. The book presents a theory of science and, like other philosophies of science, it has consequences for how scientists should behave if their enterprise is to succeed. It may be wrong, but it is a legitimate basis for "oughts" and "shoulds" (pp. 207–208).
- Conversely, one reason for taking the theory seriously is that scientists, whose methods were developed and selected for success, do behave as the theory says they should. His descriptive generalizations are evidence for the theory because they can also be derived from it, whereas other views treat them as anomalous (p. 208).
- He thinks the circularity is not vicious. The theory's consequences are not exhausted by the observations it started from, and he had found parts of it a useful tool for exploring scientific behavior before the book appeared. He adds that no merely circular point of view could provide such guidance (p. 208).

**Application to other fields** (pp. 208–209).
- Some admirers value the book because its theses apply to many fields. Kuhn understands, but is puzzled: the picture of tradition-bound periods punctuated by non-cumulative breaks is borrowed from historians of literature, music, art, political development and others (p. 208).
- If he is original, it is mainly in applying such concepts to the sciences, which were widely thought to develop differently. A second contribution may be the concept of a paradigm as a concrete achievement. He suspects difficulties about "style" in the arts may vanish if paintings are seen as modeled on one another rather than produced in conformity to abstracted canons (pp. 208–209).

**How science is different** (p. 209). The book also meant to show that science is strikingly different. Kuhn says it cannot have been all wrong to say the sciences, after a certain point, progress in a way other fields do not, whatever progress itself may be. Features of this kind: the relative scarcity of competing schools in developed sciences; the community as the only audience and judges of its own work; the special nature of scientific education; puzzle-solving as a goal; the value system used in crisis and decision. None is necessarily unique to science, but together they set the activity apart (p. 209).

**Closing program** (pp. 209–210). Kuhn closes by urging comparative study of communities in other fields. His questions: how one elects and is elected to membership; the stages of socialization; what the group sees as its goals; what deviations it tolerates; how it controls "impermissible aberration" (p. 209). "Scientific knowledge, like language, is intrinsically the common property of a group or else nothing at all" (p. 210). To understand it we need the special characteristics of the groups that create and use it (p. 210).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
*Idea illustrated:* shared values can be honored by everyone and still dictate different choices. No rule forces agreement (pp. 185, 199–200).

Two theories are scored 0–10 on two shared values:

| | Accuracy | Simplicity |
|---|---|---|
| Theory A | 8 | 4 |
| Theory B | 5 | 9 |

Scientist 1 weights accuracy 0.7 and simplicity 0.3:
- A: 0.7·8 + 0.3·4 = 5.6 + 1.2 = 6.8
- B: 0.7·5 + 0.3·9 = 3.5 + 2.7 = 6.2
- She prefers A.

Scientist 2 weights accuracy 0.4 and simplicity 0.6:
- A: 0.4·8 + 0.6·4 = 3.2 + 2.4 = 5.6
- B: 0.4·5 + 0.6·9 = 2.0 + 5.4 = 7.4
- He prefers B.

Both value accuracy and simplicity. Neither has made a mistake in the arithmetic. They differ only in how they weigh the shared values, as in the fruitfulness-versus-scope case Kuhn describes (p. 199). The weighted sum is our device. Kuhn says there is no neutral algorithm for theory choice (p. 200), so the numbers only show how a disagreement can arise.

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — in the postscript, "paradigm" has two senses: the whole disciplinary matrix, and exemplars. Normal science is puzzle-solving by a community on this shared basis (pp. 175, 182)
- [[disciplinary-matrix]] (new) — the ordered whole of a community's shared commitments: symbolic generalizations, models, values, exemplars, and others (p. 182)
- [[scientific-community]] (new) — practitioners of a specialty who share education and literature; typically perhaps about 100 members, occasionally significantly fewer (a revolution can occur in a community of perhaps fewer than 25); identified by networks and citations (pp. 177–178, 181)
- [[symbolic-generalization]] (new) — a formalizable expression accepted without dispute (f = ma); functions partly as law and partly as definition (pp. 182–183)
- [[exemplar]] (new) — a concrete problem-solution learned in training, serving as a model for further puzzles and giving a way of seeing (pp. 187, 189)
- [[tacit-knowledge]] (new) — knowledge learned by doing science with examples, not restatable as rules or criteria without essential change; Kuhn takes the term from Polanyi (pp. 175, 191–192)
- [[similarity-set]] (new) — a grouping of situations done without an answer to the question of what they are similar with respect to; revolutions change some of these groupings (pp. 192, 200)
- [[stimulus vs. sensation]] (new) — stimuli are theoretical posits assumed the same across observers; sensations are what is perceived and may differ between groups (pp. 192–193)
- [[values in theory choice]] (new) — accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness. They are shared, and are applied differently by individuals (pp. 185, 199)
- [[incommensurability]] — in this unit: parties use the same terms but attach some differently to nature, so communication is partial; it does not mean no communication (pp. 198–201)
- [[translation and conversion]] (new) — translation lets each side understand the other; conversion is a non-deliberate shift into a new language and view (pp. 202–204)
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — crisis is the usual prelude to revolution, not an absolute prerequisite; it may be caused outside the community (p. 181)

## Canonical instances
- [[ohm-vs-joule-lenz]] — accepting Ohm's Law demanded, in part, a redefinition of "current" and "resistance", and the law was strenuously opposed, while the Joule-Lenz Law was not (p. 183)
- [[galileo-huyghens-bernoulli-chain]] — three problems solved by seeing each as like the previous; all use only the vis viva principle (pp. 190–191)
- [[einstein-vs-bohr-on-inconsistency]] — the same value (consistency), applied differently to the old quantum theory (p. 185)
- [[chemistry-before-dalton-and-after]] — chemists share Dalton's tools yet disagree on atoms (p. 180); metals moved from compounds to elements (pp. 200–201)
- [[cloud-chamber-and-ammeter]] — seeing particle tracks and current values: experience shortens the route from perception to interpretation (pp. 197–198)
- [[swan-goose-and-mother]] — everyday cases of learned recognition without criteria (pp. 194–195)
- [[phage-group]] — an example of a small community to be found by communication networks (pp. 177–178)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Exemplars teach "seeing as" without explicit criteria ↔ analytic-philosophy: Wittgenstein-style rule-following, where examples fix use and no finite set of criteria does. Both stress learning a practice by cases (the unit itself mentions "family resemblance", p. 189).
- [structural-analogy] Incommensurable theories and the translator's task ↔ analytic-philosophy: Quine's radical translation. Kuhn cites Quine and criticizes his assumption that the same stimulus gives the same sensation (p. 202, n. 17).
- [metaphor] Different stimulus-to-sensation routes ↔ semantic externalism: meaning shaped by the group's environment. The link is loose and not stated in this unit.
- [structural-analogy] Symbolic generalizations as part law, part definition ↔ analytic/synthetic distinction: Kuhn calls definitions "tautologies" and says their force shifts over time (p. 183).
- [structural-analogy] Values applied differently by individuals, serving to distribute risk ↔ computational social science: diversity of strategies in a population. This is our suggestion, not in the text.

## Questions this raises
- How exactly would one identify scientific communities by citation links and communication networks, and does the result match the roughly 100-member groups Kuhn expects (pp. 177–178)?
- If recognition of similarity cannot be reduced to criteria, what is the program Kuhn mentions meant to show about it, and how would we test the claim (pp. 191–192)?
- Can the lists of criteria for telling earlier from later theories (accuracy, problem count, esoteric balance) actually be completed, as Kuhn is confident they can (p. 206)?
- Is Kuhn's "puzzle-solving progress without a truer ontology" stable, given he also says reasons for choice include scope, simplicity and plausibility (pp. 185, 206)?
