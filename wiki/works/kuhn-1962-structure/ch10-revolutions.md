# Revolutions as Changes of World View

## L0 — One line
Because scientists reach nature only through what they see and do, and a paradigm shapes both, it may be reasonable to say that after a revolution they work in a different world, and Kuhn holds that a revolution is not fully reducible to reinterpreting stable data.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks in what sense scientists work in a "different world" after a paradigm change. He argues that scientists reach nature only through what they see and do, so, he suggests, we may want to say they respond to a different world. The usual reply, that only the interpretation of fixed data changes, is for him not the whole story. Data, operations and measurements are themselves shaped by paradigms, and no neutral observation language has yet come close to being devised. His examples are Uranus, the pendulum, oxygen, the Leyden jar and Dalton's atomic theory. The chapter helps explain why rival schools talk at cross-purposes and why, even after accepting a new paradigm, scientists may need a long time to make nature fit it.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's earlier terms (paradigm, normal science, anomaly, crisis); the gestalt "duck-rabbit" picture; basic history of the chemistry and physics examples.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Kuhn suggests that after a revolution scientists respond to a different world, because their only recourse to that world is through what they see and do (p. 111).
- Gestalt switches and inverting-lens experiments are suggestive prototypes, but, Kuhn says, they cannot demonstrate that controlled scientific observation shares these characteristics (pp. 111–113).
- Scientists have no external standard against which to show their vision shifted, so if perceptual switches accompany paradigm changes we may not expect direct testimony and must look for indirect, behavioural evidence. A Copernican convert says he was mistaken to take the moon for a planet, rather than reporting a change of sight (pp. 114–115).
- Historical cases (Uranus, Copernican-era comets, Hauksbee, Franklin, Lavoisier, Galileo's pendulum) are offered as cases of scientists seeing new things when looking at familiar objects, often with familiar instruments (pp. 115–120).
- The standard view says only interpretation changes. Kuhn replies that interpretation can articulate a paradigm but cannot correct it, and crises end through a relatively sudden, gestalt-like event, and new paradigms are born in flashes of intuition that are not interpretation (pp. 120–123).
- Operations, measurements and a "neutral observation language" are not a stable given. Measurements are paradigm-determined, and the closest attempts at such a language start from a paradigm; none has yet come close to a language of pure percepts (pp. 125–127).
- Paradigms fix large areas of experience at once, as when a child learns "mama" or Copernicans changed the meaning of "planet" (pp. 128–129).
- In Dalton's case the same chemical operations came to bear on different regularities, and even the numerical data changed (pp. 130–135).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: paradigms change, and the scientist's world with them (pp. 111–112).** A historian may be tempted to say that when paradigms change, the world changes. Scientists adopt new instruments, look in new places, and see new things with familiar instruments in familiar places. Kuhn grants that nothing like a literal move to another planet happens and that everyday life outside the lab usually continues (p. 111). Still, since scientists reach the world of their research only through what they see and do, "we may want to say" they respond to a different world after a revolution (p. 111).

The gestalt switch (a sudden change in how one organises what is seen) is an elementary prototype. Ducks become rabbits (p. 111). Scientific training involves similar transformations, usually more gradual and almost always irreversible. A student sees lines on a contour map, while a cartographer sees terrain. A student sees broken lines in a bubble-chamber photograph, while a physicist sees subnuclear events (p. 111). The student's world is fixed jointly by the environment and by the normal-scientific tradition he was trained in. When the tradition changes, perception must be re-educated, and the new world will seem, here and there, incommensurable with the old one. This is another reason why schools with different paradigms are always slightly at cross-purposes (p. 112).

**Psychological evidence (pp. 112–113).** In their most usual form, gestalt experiments illustrate only the nature of perceptual transformations and say nothing about the role of paradigms or earlier experience (p. 112). Psychological literature offers more:
- A subject wearing inverting lenses first sees the world upside down and is disoriented. Usually after a confused period the visual field flips and objects look as before. Kuhn says assimilating the anomalous field reacted upon and changed the field itself, and calls this a revolutionary transformation of vision, literally as well as metaphorically (p. 112).
- Subjects in the anomalous playing-card experiment (Section VI) saw only the card types their experience had prepared them for. After enough exposure they recognised anomalous cards on first inspection (pp. 112–113).
- Perceived size and colour also vary with training (p. 113).

Kuhn suspects that "something like a paradigm is prerequisite to perception itself". What one sees depends on what one looks at and on what previous visual-conceptual experience has taught one to see. Without such training there is only William James's "bloomin' buzzin' confusion" (p. 113). He cites Hanson as using gestalt demonstrations similarly. But such experiments are suggestive only. They do not show that controlled scientific observation shares these characteristics, and they cannot demonstrate it directly (p. 113).

*Intuition note.* Experiments on perception show that what we see depends on training. Kuhn uses them only as hints that scientists' seeing may change with their training, not as proof.

**What evidence history can give (pp. 114–115).** A gestalt subject knows his perception shifted because he can switch it back and forth while holding the same paper. He may even learn to see just the lines, without either figure. A card subject is told by the experimenter that the card was a black five of hearts all along. Both demonstrations need an external standard (p. 114). The scientist has no recourse above or beyond what he sees with his eyes and instruments. If a higher authority existed, it would become the source of his data and his vision would become a problem, as it is for the psychologist. A scientist who could switch back and forth would also raise problems. The period when light was treated as sometimes a wave and sometimes a particle was a period of crisis, which ended only with wave mechanics and the realisation that light is a self-consistent entity different from both (p. 114).

So if perceptual switches accompany paradigm changes, we may not expect scientists to attest to them directly. A Copernican convert does not say that he used to see a planet and now sees a satellite, since that would imply a sense in which the Ptolemaic system had once been correct. Instead he says that he once took the moon (or saw it) as a planet but was mistaken; Kuhn says such statements recur after revolutions. If such statements disguise a shift of vision, we must look for indirect and behavioural evidence (p. 115).

**Historical examples (pp. 115–120).**
- *Uranus.* On at least seventeen occasions from 1690 to 1781, astronomers saw a star where Uranus was. One excellent observer saw it on four successive nights in 1769 without noting the motion. Herschel, with a much improved telescope, noticed an unusual apparent disk size, postponed identification, found the motion, and announced a comet. After fruitless attempts to fit a cometary orbit, Lexell suggested that the orbit was probably planetary. When that was accepted, the world of the professional astronomer contained several fewer stars and one more planet. Like an anomalous card, the body no longer fitted the categories (star or comet) of the prevailing paradigm (pp. 115–116).
- *Wider effects.* Kuhn says it was probably so, though the evidence is equivocal, that Herschel's minor paradigm change helped to prepare astronomers for the rapid discovery of asteroids after 1801: twenty in the first fifty years of the nineteenth century, with standard instruments (p. 116). A footnote says Wolf's account makes it hard to explain these discoveries by Bode's Law (p. 116, n. 5).
- *Copernicus and the heavens.* Western astronomers first saw celestial change in the half-century after Copernicus's paradigm. Chinese cosmology did not exclude celestial change, and the Chinese recorded new stars and sunspots much earlier. Late sixteenth-century astronomers also found comets wandering through the space once reserved for immutable bodies, using traditional instruments. Kuhn asks, as a rhetorical question, whether it can conceivably be an accident that this happened then. He adds that the ease and rapidity with which astronomers saw new things in old objects with old instruments may make us wish to say that after Copernicus they lived in a different world, and that in any case their research responded as though they did (pp. 116–117).
- *Astronomy as a special case.* These examples come from astronomy because its reports use relatively pure observation terms. Kuhn says full parallelism with psychological subjects is not needed and we gain by relaxing the standard: if we can be content with the everyday use of 'to see', we may recognise many other shifts in perception that accompany paradigm change. The extended use of 'perception' and 'seeing' will shortly need explicit defence (p. 117).
- *Electricity.* Seventeenth-century electricians guided by effluvium theories said they saw chaff rebound from or fall off electrified bodies. Electrostatic repulsion was not seen as such, with one universally ignored exception, until Hauksbee's large apparatus. Repulsion then became fundamental and attraction needed explaining (pp. 117–118). After Franklin's paradigm, the Leyden jar was seen as a condenser, with two conducting plates and a non-conductor as the new prototype. Other inductive effects were redescribed or newly noted (p. 118).
- *Chemistry.* Lavoisier saw oxygen where Priestley saw dephlogisticated air. He also had to see a compound ore where others saw an elementary earth. Without recourse to a hypothetical fixed nature that he "saw differently", the principle of economy urges that after discovering oxygen he worked in a different world (p. 118).
- *Galileo's pendulum.* Aristotelians, who thought a heavy body moves by nature to a low resting place, saw a body falling with difficulty under constraint. Galileo saw a pendulum, a body that almost repeats its motion forever, and built parts of his dynamics on it, including arguments about weight and rate of fall (pp. 118–119). The shift was not more accurate observation, since the Aristotelian description was descriptively as accurate. Galileo even saw more regularity than we can now find, as in a period independent of amplitude up to 90° (p. 119). Instead, what seems to have been involved, Kuhn says, was genius exploiting perceptual possibilities made available by a medieval paradigm shift: the impetus theory, which holds that continuing motion is due to an internal power implanted by the projector. Galileo was trained in it (p. 119). Buridan described a vibrating string symmetrically, with impetus implanted and consumed. Oresme gave what now appears as the first discussion of a pendulum. Kuhn says this view was, at least in Oresme's case and almost certainly in Galileo's, made possible by the move from the Aristotelian to the impetus paradigm. Until that paradigm was invented, "there were no pendulums, but only swinging stones" for the scientist to see, and pendulums were brought into existence by something very like a paradigm-induced gestalt switch (p. 120).

*Intuition note.* In Kuhn's cases, the same object is seen differently through a different paradigm. For Galileo, Kuhn says the shift did not come from more accurate observation. Uranus was seen as a star, then as a comet, then as a planet, once it no longer fitted the old categories; Herschel's much improved telescope also played a part.

**The standard view and Kuhn's reply (pp. 120–123).**
- **Standard view (the "usual" description):** what changes is only the scientist's interpretation of observations fixed by environment and perceptual apparatus. Priestley and Lavoisier both saw oxygen, and Aristotle and Galileo both saw pendulums (pp. 120–121).
- **Kuhn's reply.** This view can be "neither all wrong nor a mere mistake". It is part of a philosophical paradigm from Descartes that has served well. Kuhn says research in philosophy, psychology, linguistics and art history now suggests this paradigm is "somehow askew", but no viable alternative exists yet (p. 121). He admits the strangeness of saying that the world does not change with a paradigm yet the scientist works in a different world. But a revolution is not fully reducible to reinterpreting stable data:
  - The data are not unequivocally stable. A pendulum is not a falling stone, and oxygen is not dephlogisticated air, so the data collected from them are themselves different (p. 121).
  - Kuhn says the transition from constrained fall to pendulum does not resemble interpretation, and asks how it could in the absence of fixed data to interpret. The scientist who embraces a new paradigm is like the man with inverting lenses, who knows he faces the same objects yet finds them transformed in many of their details (p. 122).

Kuhn does not deny that scientists interpret. Galileo, Aristotle, Musschenbroek and Franklin did. But interpretation presupposes a paradigm and belongs to normal science. Given a paradigm, the scientist knows what counts as a datum, what instruments retrieve it and which concepts apply (p. 122). Interpretation can only articulate a paradigm, not correct it. Normal science leads to anomalies and crises, which end through a relatively sudden, unstructured event like a gestalt switch. Scientists then often speak of scales falling from their eyes or of a lightning flash that floods a puzzle with light, and on other occasions the illumination comes in sleep (pp. 122–123). These intuitions depend on experience gained under the old paradigm. But they are not logically or piecemeal linked to particular items of that experience, as an interpretation would be. They gather up large portions of it and transform them into a new bundle that is linked piecemeal to the new paradigm (p. 123).

*Intuition note.* Interpreting data works inside a framework. Kuhn argues that moving to a new framework is a different kind of act, closer to a sudden re-seeing than to a step-by-step argument from the old data.

**Different data for Aristotle and Galileo (pp. 123–125).**
- *Pendulum.* Seeing constrained fall, the Aristotelian would measure (or discuss, since he seldom measured) weight, vertical height raised, time to rest, and resistance of the medium. Normal research on these could not have produced Galileo's laws (p. 123). Crises and other changes led Galileo to see differently: Archimedes made the medium non-essential, impetus made the motion symmetrical and enduring, and Neoplatonism drew attention to circular form. So he measured weight, radius, angular displacement and time per swing. Given Galileo's paradigms, Kuhn says, pendulum-like regularities were very nearly accessible to inspection, so interpretation proved almost unnecessary. Kuhn counts the period's independence of amplitude among regularities that nature nowhere precisely exemplifies and that we cannot document today (pp. 123–124).
- *Falling stone.* Aristotle saw a change of state rather than a process. His measures were total distance and total time (what we call average speed), and the relevant distance was that to the end point. The scholastics changed this through impetus and the latitude of forms. Distance from the start became relevant, and Aristotle's notion of speed was split into concepts that soon after Galileo became our average and instantaneous speed (pp. 124–125). Galileo was not the first to suggest uniform acceleration. He developed his theorem before experimenting with the inclined plane. He could explain why Aristotle saw what he did, but his immediate experience was not Aristotle's (p. 125).

**Is there something neutral beneath? (pp. 125–127).** One might set aside "immediate experience" and retreat to laboratory operations and measurements, or to a neutral observation-language, perhaps tied to retinal imprints, to recover a stable realm (p. 125). Kuhn asks whether sensory experience is fixed and neutral, and says the epistemology that has most often guided Western philosophy for three centuries answers with an immediate and unequivocal Yes. Without a developed alternative he finds it impossible to relinquish that view entirely; yet it no longer functions effectively, and he now finds the attempts to rescue it with a neutral observation language hopeless (p. 126).
- Operations and measurements are not "the given" of experience but things collected with difficulty. They are selected because they promise fruitful elaboration of a paradigm, so scientists with different paradigms perform different manipulations. Measurements on a pendulum differ from those for constrained fall, and the operations relevant to oxygen are not uniformly the same as those for dephlogisticated air (p. 126).
- A pure observation language would depend on a theory of perception and mind that experimental psychology increasingly strains. The duck-rabbit shows that the same retinal impressions can yield different sights, and inverting lenses show that different impressions can yield the same sight (pp. 126–127).
- The closest attempts to build such a language start from a paradigm (a scientific theory or everyday discourse) and strip out non-logical, non-perceptual terms. The result embodies expectations about nature and fails when they are violated. Goodman, describing his *Structure of Appearance*, says it is fortunate that only existing cases are in question, because "possible" cases are "far from clear" (p. 127 and n. 19). Kuhn concludes that no language limited to a world fully known in advance can give neutral reports on "the given" (p. 127).

**Pendulums as basic experience (pp. 128–129).** So "we may at least suspect" that scientists are right in principle as well as in practice to treat oxygen and pendulums (and perhaps atoms and electrons) as fundamental ingredients of immediate experience. Meter readings and retinal imprints are elaborate constructs to which experience has direct access only when the scientist arranges it. Kuhn says this does not mean a pendulum is the only thing a scientist could see in a swinging stone (another community could see constrained fall), but it suggests that the scientist who looks at a swinging stone can have no experience in principle more elementary than seeing a pendulum. The alternative is not a hypothetical fixed vision but vision through another paradigm (p. 128).

Kuhn says neither scientists nor laymen learn to see the world piecemeal; except when all the conceptual and manipulative categories are prepared in advance, they sort out whole areas together from the flux of experience. The child extending "mama" from all humans to all females and then to his mother also learns some of the differences between males and females and something about how all but one female will behave toward him. The Copernicans who denied the sun the title "planet" changed the meaning of "planet" so that it still made useful distinctions in a world where all celestial bodies were seen differently (pp. 128–129). Paradigms "determine large areas of experience at the same time" (p. 129).

Searching for an operational definition or observation language can begin only after experience has been so determined. A scientist asking what retinal imprints make a pendulum must already recognise one. If he saw constrained fall, the question could not be asked. If he saw a pendulum in the same way as a tuning fork or an oscillating balance, his question could not be answered, at least not in the same way, because it would not be the same question. Such questions belong in a sense to normal science and get different answers after a paradigm change (p. 129).

**Dalton: operations, laws and data (pp. 129–135).** Kuhn now neglects retinal impressions and keeps laboratory operations. After a revolution many old measurements become irrelevant and others replace them, but changes are never total. The scientist is still looking at the same world and mostly using the same language and instruments. If the enduring manipulations have been changed at all, the change must lie in their relation to the paradigm or in their concrete results. Kuhn suggests that Dalton's case shows that both sorts of change occur (pp. 129–130).

- *Affinity theory.* European chemists almost universally believed, through much of the eighteenth century and into the nineteenth, that atoms were held together by mutual affinity, which explained cohesion, dissolving and displacement reactions. It was an admirable paradigm (p. 130). It placed the boundary between physical mixtures and chemical compounds differently from the post-Dalton way. Heat, light or effervescence signalled union, while visible or mechanical separability signalled mixture. Intermediate cases (salt in water, alloys, glass, atmospheric oxygen) were mostly counted as chemical, because the same forces governed them. The arguments for solutions as compounds were strong, including their homogeneity. Oxygen and nitrogen in the atmosphere, if only mixed, should settle by weight, and Dalton, who took the atmosphere to be a mixture, was never satisfactorily able to explain why they do not. Accepting his theory later created an anomaly where there had been none (p. 131).
- *Definition?* Kuhn says one is tempted to call the difference merely definitional; in one sense that may have been so, but not in the sense that makes definitions mere conventional conveniences. The mixture–compound distinction was part of the paradigm and prior to any particular laboratory test, though not to chemistry's accumulated experience. Chemists seeking tests would have sought criteria making solutions compounds (pp. 131–132).
- *Fixed proportions.* While solutions counted as compounds, no experiment alone could have produced the law of fixed proportions. Some compounds were known to have fixed weight proportions, and Richter had noted equivalent-weight regularities, but these were used only in recipes. Counterinstances like glass or salt in water blocked generalisation. The Proust–Berthollet debate ended inconclusively. Proust claimed all reactions occur in fixed proportion and Berthollet claimed they do not. Each had impressive evidence, but the two men necessarily talked through each other. Where Berthollet saw a variable compound, Proust saw a physical mixture, and neither experiment nor changing a definition was relevant. They were as much at cross-purposes as Aristotle and Galileo (p. 132).
- *Dalton himself.* Dalton was a meteorologist studying gas absorption, with a physical paradigm in which affinity played no part. Homogeneity of solutions was a problem for him. To find the sizes and weights of atomic particles he turned to chemistry, assuming atoms combine one-to-one or in simple whole-number ratios. This allowed him to determine sizes and weights, but it also made the law of constant proportion a tautology (p. 133). For Dalton, a reaction without fixed proportions was by that very fact not a purely chemical process. A law that experiment could not have established became a constitutive principle that no single set of chemical measurements could upset (p. 133). Kuhn calls this perhaps our fullest example of a scientific revolution: the same manipulations came to relate very differently to chemical generalisation (p. 133). Berthollet was never convinced, and Kuhn says that given the issue he need not have been (p. 133).
- *Why chemists accepted it.* Dalton's paradigm had wider implications than a new test for mixtures. If atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios, old data should show multiple proportions. Chemists stopped writing the two carbon oxides as 56 and 72 per cent oxygen by weight and wrote that one weight of carbon combines with 1.3 or 2.6 weights of oxygen; when old results were recorded this way, a 2:1 ratio stood out at once. The paradigm also let Richter's work be seen as general and suggested new experiments, such as Gay-Lussac's on combining volumes. Chemists took from Dalton not new experimental laws but a new way of practising chemistry, and only a few older chemists in France and Britain resisted (p. 134).
- *The data changed.* Searching the literature for support, Dalton found some records that fitted, and can scarcely have avoided finding others that did not. Proust's own copper-oxide measurements gave an oxygen weight ratio of 1.47:1 instead of the required 2:1, though Proust might have been expected to reach the Daltonian ratio (p. 134). "It is hard to make nature fit a paradigm", which is why normal-science puzzles are challenging and why measurements made without a paradigm seldom lead to conclusions. Chemists could not accept the theory on evidence alone, because much of the evidence was negative. Even after accepting it they still had to force nature to fit it, which in the event took almost another generation. Then even the percentage composition of well-known compounds was different: "The data themselves had changed." Kuhn calls this the last of the senses in which we may want to say that after a revolution scientists work in a different world (p. 135).

*Intuition note.* Dalton shows three things. The same operation, such as weighing the products of a reaction, can bear on different laws depending on the paradigm. A law once empirical can become a defining rule. And measured numbers themselves shift as chemists work to fit nature to the new framework.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take Kuhn's carbon-oxide figures (p. 134) and see how re-recording the same numbers makes a pattern visible.
- Old way: oxide 1 is 56% oxygen by weight, oxide 2 is 72% oxygen. These two numbers show no obvious relation.
- Carbon share (added step): oxide 1 has 100 − 56 = 44% carbon, oxide 2 has 100 − 72 = 28% carbon.
- Oxygen per one weight of carbon (added step): oxide 1: 56 ÷ 44 ≈ 1.27, which is about 1.3. Oxide 2: 72 ÷ 28 ≈ 2.57, which is about 2.6. These match Kuhn's 1.3 and 2.6.
- Ratio: 2.57 ÷ 1.27 ≈ 2.0, a 2:1 ratio.

The measurements did not change. A paradigm saying atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios told chemists which quantity to compute. The 2:1 ratio then stood out at once, as Kuhn describes (p. 134).

## Concepts
- [[paradigm]] — shared framework that fixes what counts as a datum, instrument and relevant concept; here also what is seen (pp. 112, 122). *(canon: "paradigm / normal science")*
- [[normal science]] — interpretive work that refines, extends and articulates an existing paradigm but cannot correct it (p. 122).
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — normal science leads to anomalies and crises, ended by a relatively sudden, gestalt-like event, not by interpretation (p. 122).
- [[incommensurability]] — after a revolution the new world will seem, here and there, incommensurable with the old; schools with different paradigms are always slightly at cross-purposes (p. 112).
- [[gestalt-switch]] (new) — sudden reorganisation of what is seen, as with the duck-rabbit; Kuhn's elementary prototype for transformations of the scientist's world (p. 111).
- [[theory-laden-observation]] (new) — what one sees depends on what one looks at and on prior visual-conceptual experience; Kuhn suspects something like a paradigm is prerequisite to perception (p. 113).
- [[neutral-observation-language]] (new) — a hoped-for language of pure percepts giving fixed data. Kuhn says the attempts that come closest presuppose a paradigm (p. 127).
- [[constitutive-principle]] (new) — a law that once was empirical becomes a rule no single measurement could upset, as with constant proportion after Dalton (p. 133).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [metaphor] Gestalt switch in paradigm change ↔ philosophy of perception: seeing-as (the same lines seen as duck or rabbit) — both involve the same input organised differently.
- [structural-analogy] Interpretation presupposing a paradigm ↔ confirmation holism: hypotheses are tested together with background assumptions — both deny that data speak independently of a framework.
- [structural-analogy] Fitting measurements to Dalton's paradigm until the numerical data themselves had changed ↔ underdetermination: evidence alone does not fix the theory.
- [metaphor] Different worlds for different paradigms ↔ semantic externalism: the world, partly, fixes what terms like "planet" mean.

## Questions this raises
- If the scientist has no external standard for showing that his vision shifted, what makes the "different world" claim more than a metaphor?
- If interpretation cannot correct a paradigm, how are choices between paradigms made? Kuhn says only that crises end by gestalt-like events here (p. 122).
- How can Galileo explain why Aristotle saw what he did (p. 125) if the two worlds are partly incommensurable?
- Does the Dalton case show that data and theory are inseparable, or only that data are sometimes revised when a theory is trusted?
