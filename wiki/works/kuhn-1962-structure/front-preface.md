# Preface (Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*)

## L0 — One line
Kuhn explains how studying the history of science, and then comparing natural and social scientists, led him to the idea of "paradigms" and to an essay that space limits made short, schematic and limited in scope (pdf 5–10).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
The Preface tells how the essay arose. Kuhn was a physics graduate student. Exposure to historical scientific theory undermined some of his earlier ideas about science (pdf 5). Reading across history, psychology, linguistics and philosophy shaped his view (pdf 6–7). A year among social scientists struck him with how openly they disagree about fundamentals, which natural-science practice normally does not evoke. This led him to "paradigms" (pdf 8). He then says what the short essay leaves out: biology, technology and social context, and detailed engagement with philosophers (pdf 9–10). He closes with acknowledgments (pdf 11–12). It matters because it states the essay's aims and limits.

**Prerequisites:** none beyond a general idea of what "science" and "history of science" are.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The project began when history of science clashed with Kuhn's earlier picture of science, drawn from scientific training and philosophy. This pushed him from physics toward history and then philosophy (pdf 5).
- He credits many sources: historians of science (Koyré, Meyerson, Metzger, Maier) and fields apparently unrelated to history: Piaget's child studies, Gestalt psychology of perception, Whorf on language and world view, and Quine's philosophical puzzles about the analytic–synthetic distinction (which the Preface names but does not explain). He also credits Fleck's monograph (pdf 6–7).
- Comparing social and natural scientists led him to "paradigms": achievements that for a time give a community model problems and solutions (pdf 8).
- He calls the work an "essay" because the Encyclopedia of Unified Science imposed space limits; he thinks the subject will ultimately demand a full book (pdf 8).
- His main aim is to change how familiar data are perceived and evaluated, so schematic form need not be a drawback (pdf 9).
- He proposes research projects, such as studying anomalies (violations of expectation) and crises, and using shifts in footnote citations as a possible index of revolutions (pdf 9).
- He admits that his distinction between an early period of competing schools and a later paradigm-guided period is much too schematic. He thinks that adding external social conditions would not modify his main theses, but would add an important dimension (pdf 9–10).
- He mostly avoids debating particular philosophical positions, and grants that some philosophers may feel he missed their point, though he thinks they would be wrong (pdf 10).

## L3 — Walkthrough
(The Preface has no numbered sections; headings below follow its paragraph order.)

**Origin of the project (pdf 5).**
- The essay reports a project conceived almost fifteen years earlier, when Kuhn was a graduate student in theoretical physics near the end of his dissertation.
- An experimental college course on physical science for non-scientists gave him his first exposure to history of science.
- Out-of-date theory and practice "radically undermined" some of his basic conceptions of science and of the reasons for its special success.
- Those conceptions came partly from scientific training and partly from avocational interest in philosophy of science. They did not fit what historical study showed, yet they are fundamental to many discussions of science.
- Result: a shift from physics to history of science, then back toward the philosophical concerns that had led him to history. The essay is in some part an attempt to explain how he came to be drawn from science to its history in the first place.

**Influences during the Society of Fellows (pdf 5–7).**
- Three years as a Junior Fellow at Harvard gave freedom for the transition (pdf 5).
- He studied Koyré and met the work of Meyerson, Metzger and Maier. More clearly than most other recent scholars, this group has shown what it was like to think scientifically when scientific canons were very different from today's. He questions a few of their interpretations. With Lovejoy's *Great Chain of Being*, their work was second only to primary sources in shaping his conception of what the history of scientific ideas can be (pdf 6).
- He also explored fields apparently unrelated to history, where research disclosed similar problems. He names Piaget's experiments on children's worlds and the transition between them, Gestalt psychology of perception, Whorf on language and world view, and Quine on the analytic–synthetic distinction (pdf 6).
- Fleck's little-known monograph anticipates many of his ideas. With a remark from Francis Sutton, it made him see that his ideas might need to be set in the sociology of the scientific community (pdf 6–7).
- Few references to these works appear later, but he is indebted to them (pdf 7).

**Lectures, teaching and related papers (pdf 7).**
- He gave eight Lowell Institute lectures in March 1951, "The Quest for Physical Theory", a first trial of his developing notion of science.
- For almost a decade, teaching a field he had never systematically studied left little time for explicit articulation of the ideas that had first brought him to it. The ideas still gave implicit orientation to his advanced teaching.
- His later historical papers share this orientation. Some deal with the part played by a metaphysic in creative research. Others examine how the experimental basis of a new theory is accumulated by men committed to an incompatible older theory. This is the development he later calls the "emergence" of a theory or discovery.

**The paradigm insight (pdf 7–8).**
- In 1958–59 at the Center for Advanced Studies in the Behavioral Sciences, among mainly social scientists, he was struck by the number and extent of their overt disagreements about legitimate problems and methods (pdf 8).
- History and acquaintance made him doubt that natural scientists have firmer or more permanent answers to such questions. Yet astronomy, physics, chemistry and biology normally do not evoke controversies over fundamentals.
- Seeking the source of that difference led him to "paradigms". He takes these to be universally recognized scientific achievements that for a time provide model problems and solutions to a community of practitioners (pdf 8).
- Once this fell into place, a draft emerged rapidly.

*Intuition note:* Kuhn doubts that natural scientists have firmer or more permanent answers than social scientists. He saw that natural-science practice normally does not evoke controversy over fundamentals. In seeking the source of that difference he came to the idea of paradigms (shared model problems and solutions). The Preface does not spell out the mechanism here.

**Why it is an essay (pdf 8–9).**
- The work was first meant only for the Encyclopedia of Unified Science, whose space limits forced an extremely condensed, schematic form. Later events somewhat relaxed those limits and made simultaneous independent publication possible, but it remains an essay, not the full-scale book the subject will ultimately demand (pdf 8).
- Since his main aim is to urge a change in the perception and evaluation of familiar data, schematic form need not be a drawback. Readers prepared for such reorientation may find it more suggestive and easier to assimilate (pdf 8–9).

**What is left out or too schematic (pdf 9–10).**
- Much more historical evidence exists, including from biology. He deals only with physical science, partly for coherence and partly because of competence (pdf 9).
- Proposed research: how anomalies (violations of expectation) attract a community's attention, and how crises that may be induced by repeated failure to make an anomaly conform emerge. If each revolution alters the community's historical perspective, postrevolutionary textbooks and research publications should change. A shift in the distribution of literature cited in footnotes "ought to be studied as a possible index" of revolutions (pdf 9).
- Caveats on his own scheme (pdf 9):
  - The pre-/post-paradigm distinction is "much too schematic".
  - Each competing school in the earlier period is guided by something much like a paradigm.
  - There are circumstances, which he thinks rare, where two paradigms coexist peacefully in the later period.
  - Mere possession of a paradigm is not quite a sufficient criterion for the developmental transition discussed in Section II.
- External factors (technology; social, economic and intellectual conditions) are mentioned only in brief asides. Copernicus and the calendar show that external conditions may help turn a mere anomaly into acute crisis. They may also influence the range of alternatives open to someone proposing a revolutionary reform (pdf 10).
- He thinks including such factors would not modify his main theses, but would add an analytic dimension of first-rate importance (pdf 10). Footnote 4 says he takes external factors to be minor only with respect to the problems discussed in this essay (pdf 10).
- Philosophical implications: he tried to point out and document the main ones but usually refrained from detailed discussion of contemporary philosophers' positions. His skepticism is more often aimed at a philosophical attitude than at a fully articulated position. Some may feel he missed their point. He thinks they would be wrong, but the essay is not designed to convince them (pdf 10).

**Acknowledgments (pdf 10–12).**
- The autobiographical fragments acknowledge his main debts. Other debts are discharged by citation, and a full list of personal debts would be almost coextensive with his friends and acquaintances (pdf 11).
- Named: Conant, who introduced him to history of science. Leonard Nash, a teaching collaborator. Stanley Cavell, a Berkeley colleague whose conclusions are congruent with his own (pdf 11).
- Four friends gave the most far-reaching help: Feyerabend, Nagel, Noyes and John Heilbron (pdf 12).
- He has no reason to believe, and some reason to doubt, that the named people approve the whole manuscript (pdf 12).
- He thanks his family, who let the work go on, and says he does not know how to thank them (pdf 12). Signed T. S. K., Berkeley, February 1962 (pdf 12).

## Toy example
(Ours, not from the text.) This illustrates Kuhn's proposed footnote "index" (pdf 9), not any result of his.
- Suppose a field's research reports are sampled before and after a suspected revolution, 40 footnotes each.
- Before: 30 footnotes cite literature older than 20 years, so the share is 30/40 = 0.75.
- After: 10 cite such older literature, so the share is 10/40 = 0.25.
- The shift is 0.75 − 0.25 = 0.50.
- On Kuhn's suggestion, a sharp change like this would be a candidate sign that the community's historical perspective changed. By itself it would prove nothing; he offers it only as a possible index.

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — here only "paradigm" appears: universally recognized achievements giving a community model problems and solutions for a time (pdf 8). "Normal science" is not named in the Preface.
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — anomaly is a violation of expectation. Repeated failure to fit it may induce a crisis (pdf 9). Revolution is not defined here.
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — named only as a puzzle Quine opened up for Kuhn (pdf 6).
- [[external factors in science]] (new) — technology and social, economic or intellectual conditions. Kuhn sets them aside but says they can affect crises and available alternatives (pdf 10).
- [[emergence of a theory or discovery]] (new) — Kuhn's term for the way a new theory's experimental basis is accumulated and assimilated by men committed to an incompatible older theory (pdf 7).

## Canonical instances
- [[Copernicus and the calendar]] — an example of external conditions turning an anomaly into acute crisis (pdf 10).
- [[Piaget's child-development experiments]] — cited as illuminating transitions between a child's successive "worlds" (pdf 6).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Paradigm as shared model ↔ analytic-philosophy: family resemblance / language-game — a shared practice, not explicit rules, unites a community. This is only a candidate link; the Preface does not make it.
- [structural-analogy] Citation-shift index of revolutions ↔ computational social science: citation-network measures of change in a field — a quantitative proxy for a qualitative shift.
- [metaphor] Reorienting how familiar data are perceived and evaluated (pdf 9) ↔ Gestalt psychology of perception (pdf 6): seeing the same material differently. The Preface lists Gestalt work among his reading but does not itself link the two.

## Questions this raises
- Why do natural-science communities normally avoid controversy over fundamentals, while social-science ones do not? What exactly in a paradigm produces this?
- If mere possession of a paradigm is not quite sufficient for the developmental transition, what else is needed?
- How might Kuhn's proposed footnote-distribution index be measured, and what could confound it?
- If external factors are "minor" only for this essay's problems, where do they matter more?
