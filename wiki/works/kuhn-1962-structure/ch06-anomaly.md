# Anomaly and the Emergence of Scientific Discoveries

## L0 — One line
A fundamental scientific discovery is not a single act but an extended process that starts when a paradigm-based expectation fails and ends when the paradigm is adjusted, and, because a precise paradigm makes anomalies stand out, puzzle-solving normal science, though it aims at no novelties, is so effective at producing them.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Kuhn asks how normal science, which aims at no novelties, still produces them. Through three cases (oxygen, X-rays, the Leyden jar) he argues that discovery is an extended process, not one act. It begins when a paradigm-induced expectation fails, continues through exploration, and ends when the paradigm is adjusted so that the anomaly becomes expected. A psychological card experiment shows the same pattern in perception. Anomaly appears only against the paradigm's background, and the more precise the paradigm, the more sensitive an indicator of anomaly (and so of an occasion for paradigm change) it is. This helps explain why normal science, though it tends at first to suppress novelties, is so effective in causing them to arise.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's notions of paradigm and normal science (puzzle-solving under a shared framework); the idea of an "expectation" built into theory and instruments.

## L2 — Key ideas
- **Puzzle:** normal science seeks no novelty, yet novelties keep appearing. Kuhn says that, if this is to be reconciled with his earlier account, research under a paradigm must be a particularly effective way of inducing paradigm change (p. 52).
- **Discovery has a recurrent structure:** awareness of anomaly, then exploration of the anomalous area, then adjustment of the paradigm theory until the anomalous is expected. Until then the new fact is "not quite a scientific fact at all" (pp. 52–53).
- **Oxygen:** asking who discovered it, and when, has no answer of the kind sought. Discovering involves recognizing both *that* something is and *what* it is, so it is a process that takes time (pp. 54–56).
- **Anomaly as prelude:** in oxygen and X-rays, noticing that something went wrong only began the discovery. Further experiment and assimilation were needed (p. 57).
- **Instruments carry expectations:** X-rays, the nitric-oxide "goodness of air" test and uranium fission show that apparatus embodies expectations that can hide or deny new phenomena. Paradigm procedures are still necessary (pp. 59–61).
- **Theory-induced discovery:** the Leyden jar shows that speculative theories, not yet paradigms, can point toward a discovery. The result is often not quite the one expected, and it helps turn the theory into a paradigm (p. 61–62).
- **Perception parallels science:** in the Bruner–Postman card experiment, anomalous cards were first fitted into prepared categories, then caused hesitation, then were seen correctly. Some subjects never adjusted (pp. 62–64).
- **Why normal science produces novelty:** professionalization makes science rigid but gives precision. The more precise the paradigm, the more sensitive an indicator of anomaly it is, and resistance ensures anomalies "penetrate existing knowledge to the core" (pp. 64–65).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening (pp. 52–53).**
- Normal science is cumulative and successful at extending the scope and precision of knowledge (p. 52).
- It does not aim at novelties of fact or theory and, when successful, finds none. Yet novel phenomena and radical theories repeatedly appear (p. 52).
- Reconciling the two requires that research under a paradigm be "a particularly effective way of inducing paradigm change" (p. 52).
- Novelties arise inadvertently in a game played under one set of rules, and absorbing them needs another set of rules (p. 52).
- Kuhn treats discoveries (novelties of fact) first and inventions (novelties of theory) later. He says the fact/theory distinction will prove "exceedingly artificial" (p. 52).
- Discoveries are extended episodes with a recurrent structure (p. 52–53):
  1. Awareness of anomaly: nature has violated paradigm-induced expectations.
  2. A more or less extended exploration of the anomalous area.
  3. It closes only when the paradigm theory has been adjusted so that the anomalous has become the expected.
- Assimilating a new fact demands a "more than additive" adjustment of theory. Until the scientist has learned to see nature differently, the new fact is not quite a scientific fact (p. 53).

*Intuition note:* A discovery is not like finding a coin on the road. You must also change the categories in which the find makes sense, and that takes time.

**Oxygen (pp. 53–56).**
- Facts (p. 53–54):
  - Several other chemists must have had enriched air in a laboratory vessel without knowing it (p. 53).
  - Scheele was first to prepare a fairly pure sample, but his work was published late and Kuhn sets it aside (p. 53).
  - Priestley collected the gas from heated red oxide of mercury during a long normal study of "airs". In 1774 he took it for nitrous oxide. In 1775 he took it for common air with less phlogiston (p. 53).
  - Lavoisier began after Priestley's 1774 work, possibly after a hint. Early in 1775 he called the gas "air itself entire", more pure and more respirable. By 1777, probably with the help of a second hint, he concluded it was a distinct species and a main constituent of the atmosphere. Priestley never accepted this (pp. 53–54).
- The question "who first discovered oxygen, and when?" has no answer of the kind sought. The fact that it is asked is a symptom of something askew in the image of science that gives discovery so fundamental a role (p. 54).
- Priestley's sample was impure. If holding impure oxygen were discovering it, everyone who bottled air would have done so. In 1774 he thought he had a known species. In 1775 he saw "dephlogisticated air", still not oxygen (p. 54).
- Lavoisier's 1775 work has the same problem. Even in 1777 and to the end of his life he held oxygen to be an atomic "principle of acidity". On this view oxygen gas formed only when that principle united with caloric, the matter of heat (p. 55).
- Kuhn asks whether we should therefore say oxygen had not yet been discovered in 1777, and grants that some may be tempted to. But the principle of acidity stayed in chemistry until after 1810, and caloric until the 1860s. Oxygen was a standard chemical substance before either went (p. 55).
- "Oxygen was discovered", though undoubtedly correct in Kuhn's view, misleads, because it suggests a single act like seeing, attributable to one person and one moment. Attributing it to a moment is always impossible, and attributing it to one person often is too. Ignoring Scheele, we can safely say it was not discovered before 1774, and would probably say it had been by 1777 or shortly after. But within those limits or others like them, any dating is inevitably arbitrary (p. 55).
- Discovering involves recognizing both that something is and what it is. It is therefore a process. Only when all the relevant categories are prepared in advance, so that the phenomenon is not of a new sort, can both happen in an instant (pp. 55–56).
- Does discovery involve paradigm change? No general answer yet, but in this case it must (p. 56):
  - From 1777 Lavoisier announced the oxygen theory of combustion, the keystone of the "chemical revolution".
  - The value placed on a discoverer varies with how far the phenomenon violated paradigm-induced anticipations.
  - Oxygen's discovery was not by itself the cause of the change in theory. Lavoisier's 1772 sealed note already recorded his conviction that phlogiston theory was wrong and that burning bodies absorbed part of the atmosphere (p. 56).
  - The oxygen work gave form to that earlier sense that something was amiss. Advance awareness of difficulty must be a significant part of what let Lavoisier see what Priestley could not. The need for a major paradigm revision must be the principal reason Priestley never saw it (p. 56).

*Intuition note:* On Kuhn's account, Priestley held the gas but lacked the categories to see it as new. Lavoisier was already uneasy with the old theory, so he could read the same experiment differently.

**X-rays (pp. 57–61).**
- Kuhn calls this a classic discovery by accident (p. 57).
- Roentgen interrupted a normal study of cathode rays when a distant barium platino-cyanide screen glowed. Seven hectic weeks showed that the cause travelled in straight lines, cast shadows and was not deflected by a magnet. He then convinced himself the agent was not cathode rays but an agent with at least some similarity to light (p. 57).
- Parallels with oxygen: the discovery began with an anomaly (the screen glowed when it should not), but this was only the prelude. Further experimentation and assimilation followed (p. 57).
- Again no single moment of discovery can be named: not the first glow, and not the last week. Another investigator saw the glow and discovered nothing. Kuhn says only that X-rays emerged in Würzburg between 8 November and 28 December 1895 (pp. 57–58).
- **Objection to a paradigm change here:** no obvious theoretical upheaval followed for a decade. The paradigms could not have predicted X-rays but did not obviously prohibit them, unlike phlogiston theory and Lavoisier's reading of Priestley's gas. Radiation of several kinds was already accepted. New chemical elements were routine normal science, an occasion for congratulations rather than surprise (p. 58).
- **Kuhn's reply (p. 59):**
  - X-rays met shock, not mere surprise. Kelvin first pronounced them a hoax.
  - They violated deeply entrenched expectations that Kuhn suggests were implicit in the design and interpretation of laboratory procedures.
  - If Roentgen's apparatus made X-rays, other experimenters had been producing them unawares. Familiar apparatus would now need lead shielding. Completed work would have to be redone because a relevant variable had gone uncontrolled.
  - So X-rays changed fields that already existed, and denied previously paradigmatic instruments their title.
- Using an apparatus in a given way assumes that only certain circumstances will arise. There are instrumental as well as theoretical expectations (p. 59).
- **Oxygen example of an instrumental expectation (pp. 59–60):**
  - The standard "goodness of air" test mixed two volumes of the gas with one volume of nitric oxide over water. With atmospheric air the gaseous residue is one volume, and with any other gas it is greater.
  - Priestley and Lavoisier both found about one volume and classed the gas accordingly.
  - Later, partly by accident, Priestley tried other proportions. With four times the nitric oxide, almost no residue remained.
  - His commitment to the test was a commitment to the non-existence of gases that behave as oxygen did.
- Uranium fission was identified late partly because chemists who knew what to expect tested mainly for elements at the upper end of the periodic table (p. 60).
- Footnote 10 quotes Hahn and Strassman, who say that as "nuclear chemists" they could not bring themselves to a leap that "would contradict all previous experience of nuclear physics" (p. 60, n. 10).
- Should science abandon standard tests? No, that would be "inconceivable". Paradigm procedures are as necessary as paradigm laws and have the same effect: they restrict the phenomenological field accessible at a given time (p. 60–61).
- So X-rays necessitated paradigm change, in procedures and expectations, for a special segment of the community. This helps explain why they could seem to open a strange new world and take part in the crisis leading to twentieth-century physics (p. 61).

*Intuition note:* Instruments are frozen expectations. Using a trusted test means betting that nothing outside its design range will occur.

**The Leyden jar (pp. 61–62).**
- This is a "theory-induced" discovery. The term may seem paradoxical, since much of the earlier discussion suggests that discoveries predicted by theory in advance belong to normal science and yield no new sort of fact (p. 61).
- But not all theories are paradigm theories. In pre-paradigm periods and in crises, scientists usually develop many speculative, unarticulated theories that can point toward discovery. The discovery is often not quite the one anticipated. Only as experiment and tentative theory are articulated to a match does the discovery emerge and the theory become a paradigm (p. 61).
- Electrical research had no single paradigm. Competing theories each failed to order the whole variety of phenomena, and that failure is the source of several anomalies (p. 61).
- One school treated electricity as a fluid and tried to bottle it. Investigators held a water-filled vial and touched the water to a conductor from a running generator. Touching the water with the free hand gave a severe shock (pp. 61–62).
- These first experiments did not yield the Leyden jar. They worked only because the vial was held in the hand while the experimenter stood on the ground (p. 62).
- Investigators had to learn that the jar needs an outer and an inner conducting coating, and that the fluid is not really stored in it. Again one cannot say when the discovery was completed (p. 62).
- The experiments, many by Franklin, forced a drastic revision of fluid theory and supplied the first full paradigm for electricity (p. 62).
- **Common features** of the three cases. Kuhn says that, to a greater or lesser extent (along a continuum from shocking to anticipated results), they characterize all discoveries from which new sorts of phenomena emerge (p. 62):
  - Prior awareness of anomaly.
  - Gradual, simultaneous emergence of observational and conceptual recognition.
  - Consequent change of paradigm categories and procedures, often with resistance.

**The playing-card experiment (pp. 62–64).**
- Kuhn says evidence suggests these features are built into perception itself (p. 62).
- **Design (Bruner and Postman):** subjects identify playing cards shown at gradually longer exposures. Some cards are anomalous, such as a red six of spades or a black four of hearts. Each run ends after two successive correct identifications (pp. 62–63).
- **Results (pp. 63–64):**
  - Normal cards were usually identified correctly, even at short exposures.
  - Anomalous cards were almost always identified as normal, "without apparent hesitation or puzzlement". A black four of hearts might be called the four of spades or of hearts.
  - Kuhn says one would not even like to say the subjects saw something different from what they named.
  - With longer exposure, subjects hesitated and noticed something wrong ("the black has a red border"). Then, sometimes quite suddenly, most identified the card correctly. After two or three such cards, the rest were easy.
  - A few subjects never adjusted. At forty times the average exposure needed for normal cards, more than 10 per cent of anomalous cards were still misidentified. Some of these subjects showed acute distress.
- Kuhn calls this a "wonderfully simple and cogent schema" for discovery, whether as a metaphor or because it reflects the nature of the mind (p. 64).
- In both cases novelty emerges with difficulty, with resistance, against expectation. At first only the anticipated and usual are experienced. Later comes awareness of something wrong, then adjustment of categories until the anomalous is the anticipated. At that point the discovery is complete (p. 64).

*Intuition note:* The subjects' "mistakes" were not noticed as mistakes. Without awareness of trouble, each anomalous card was fitted into a category prepared by prior experience.

**Why normal science produces novelties (pp. 64–65).**
- The first received paradigm usually accounts well for the easily accessible observations. Further work therefore needs elaborate equipment, an esoteric vocabulary and refined concepts that drift from common-sense prototypes (p. 64).
- This professionalization narrows the scientist's vision and builds resistance to paradigm change, so the science becomes rigid. Within the areas the paradigm directs attention to, it gives detail and precision of observation–theory match that could be achieved no other way (pp. 64–65).
- That detail has a value beyond its own interest. Without apparatus built for anticipated functions, the results that lead to novelty could not occur. Even then, novelty ordinarily emerges only for someone who knows exactly what to expect and so can recognize that something has gone wrong (p. 65).
- Anomaly appears only against the paradigm's background. The more precise and far-reaching the paradigm, the more sensitive an indicator of anomaly it is, and hence of an occasion for paradigm change (p. 65).
- Resistance has a use. It ensures scientists are not lightly distracted and that anomalies leading to paradigm change penetrate to the core of existing knowledge (p. 65).
- That significant novelties often emerge at once from several laboratories indicates both the traditional nature of normal science and how completely it prepares the way for its own change (p. 65).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A factory machine fills bags with 500 g of flour. Two quality labs apply different expectations.
- Lab A expects every bag within ±5 g of 500 g. It weighs a bag at 512 g. The deviation is 512 − 500 = 12 g, which is 12 / 5 = 2.4 times its tolerance, so the bag is flagged as anomalous. Lab A investigates and might find a faulty valve.
- Lab B expects only ±50 g. The same bag gives 12 / 50 = 0.24 of its tolerance, so it passes without notice.
- Both labs saw the same bag. Only the one with the more precise expectation had an anomaly to explore. This mirrors Kuhn's point that a more precise paradigm is a more sensitive indicator of anomaly (p. 65). Lab A's later change in its model of the machine, not the first flag, would complete the "discovery" (p. 53).

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — the shared framework in which puzzle-solving occurs. Here it also supplies expectations that make anomalies visible, and it restricts the accessible field (pp. 52, 60–61, 65).
- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — the sequence from awareness of anomaly, through crisis, to large-scale paradigm change. This unit mainly covers the first stage, anomaly. It mentions crises that lead to large-scale paradigm change and says X-rays took part in the crisis that led to twentieth-century physics (pp. 52–53, 61).
- [[discovery-as-process]] (new) — discovery is an extended event of recognizing both that something is and what it is. It cannot be dated to a moment or always to a person (pp. 54–56).
- [[instrumental-expectations]] (new) — assumptions built into standard apparatus and tests about what circumstances will arise. They can hide or deny phenomena (pp. 59–60).
- [[theory-induced-discovery]] (new) — a discovery pointed to by speculative, pre-paradigm or crisis-period theory. It is often not quite the one anticipated, and its articulation helps the theory become a paradigm (p. 61).
- [[resistance-to-novelty]] (new) — the refusal to surrender a paradigm easily. Kuhn says it has a use: anomalies that do break through then penetrate existing knowledge to the core (pp. 64–65).

## Canonical instances
- [[discovery-of-oxygen]] — Priestley, Lavoisier and the impossibility of dating a discovery. Related to the shift from phlogiston to the oxygen theory of combustion (pp. 53–56).
- [[discovery-of-x-rays]] — discovery by accident. It shows that instruments carry expectations (pp. 57–61).
- [[leyden-jar]] — theory-induced discovery within the fluid theory. The experiments helped give electricity its first full paradigm (pp. 61–62).
- [[bruner-postman-card-experiment]] — anomalous playing cards. Kuhn offers it as a schema for scientific discovery, whether as a metaphor or because it reflects the nature of the mind (pp. 62–64).
- [[uranium-fission-identification]] — late recognition, partly because chemical tests were chosen by expectation (p. 60).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Anomaly detected only against a precise expectation ↔ statistics: a deviation is flagged only relative to an expected tolerance. The same reading is "anomalous" under a tight tolerance and not under a loose one.
- [structural-analogy] Instrumental commitment to the "goodness of air" test ↔ measurement validity: a measuring procedure built on assumptions cannot detect cases outside those assumptions.
- [metaphor] Card categories fitted to anomalous cards ↔ analytic-philosophy concept of confirmation holism: observation is read through a prior framework.

## Questions this raises
- If discovery cannot be dated or assigned to one person, how should priority disputes be judged?
- What separates a mere anomaly from one that leads to crisis?
- If instruments embody expectations, how can scientists tell when a trusted procedure is hiding something?
- Does the card experiment show anything about science, or is it only a metaphor, as Kuhn himself allows (p. 64)?
