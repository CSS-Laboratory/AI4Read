# Progress through Revolutions

## L0 — One line
Kuhn explains scientific progress by the nature of the scientific community (its insulation, its training, its role as sole judge of paradigms), rather than by a movement toward a fixed truth (a notion he suggests may have to be given up), and he compares this to Darwinian evolution without a goal.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
If science develops as the essay describes, why does it progress while art, political theory or philosophy seems not to? Kuhn answers in parts. Within normal science, progress is partly a matter of how it looks to the observer, because at most times no competing schools question the community's aims. Mature communities are also very efficient: they are insulated from lay demands, trained from textbooks, and judge their own work. After a revolution the victors see the result as progress, and the community's character makes this self-fulfilling. But the essay's account does not make science a process moving toward truth. Kuhn proposes "evolution-from" what is known, like Darwinian selection with no goal, and admits that why nature allows this success is unanswered.

**Prerequisites:** the essay's earlier notions of paradigm, normal science, crisis and revolution; the basic idea of Darwinian natural selection.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Part of the question is semantic: "science" is largely reserved for fields that progress, so a debate over whether a field is a science may really be asking why it does not advance (pp. 160–161).
- Kuhn says we must learn to treat as causes what are usually taken as effects, and asks whether a field is a science because it progresses rather than the reverse (p. 162).
- Within any school, successful creative work counts as progress. Doubts about progress in non-science come from competing schools that attack each other's foundations (pp. 162–163).
- Normal science is highly efficient because of insulation from laity and society, a single set of standards, and textbook-based education (pp. 163–166).
- After a revolution, the victors and the community's treatment of past literature make the outcome look like progress. Kuhn says the explanation cannot stop at "might makes right", because the choosing authority is a special professional community (pp. 166–168).
- Scientists will be reluctant to adopt a new paradigm unless it seems to solve a recognized outstanding problem and promises to keep much past problem-solving ability. Science gains in depth, not necessarily in breadth, and may have to give up the idea of approaching truth (pp. 169–170).
- Kuhn recommends "evolution-from" over "evolution-toward", using the Darwinian analogy. Why the world permits science remains open (pp. 170–173).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Opening: the problem (pp. 160–161)
- The schematic description of development poses a question: why does science move steadily ahead in ways art, political theory or philosophy does not? The most usual answers were denied earlier in the essay, so Kuhn asks whether substitutes can be found (p. 160).
- Part of the question is semantic. "Science" is mostly reserved for fields that progress obviously. Debates over whether a social science is "really a science" seem to be about definitions (p. 160).
- Kuhn suspects the real questions are: why does my field not advance like physics, and what changes would make it do so? These are not questions that could respond to an agreement on a definition (p. 160).
- If precedent from the natural sciences serves, such questions will stop being a source of concern not when a definition is found, but when the groups that now doubt their status reach consensus about their past and present accomplishments (pp. 160–161).
- It may be significant that economists argue less than practitioners of some other social sciences about whether their field is a science. The text asks whether this is because they know what science is, or because they agree about economics; it poses this as a question and does not state an answer (p. 161).
- Converse case: for centuries painting was seen as cumulative, because its goal was assumed to be representation. Pliny and Vasari recorded the inventions (foreshortening to chiaroscuro) that gave more perfect representation. Art and science were then little separated (Leonardo), and "art" also covered technology and crafts, which were seen as progressive. The cleavage took anything like its present depth only when painting and sculpture unequivocally renounced representation and began to learn again from primitive models (p. 161).
- Our difficulty in seeing the difference between science and technology partly comes from the fact that progress is obvious in both (p. 161).

*Intuition note:* We call a field "science" largely because it visibly progresses. So, as Kuhn says, recognizing this only clarifies the difficulty; it does not by itself explain the progress.

### Progress within normal science (pp. 162–164)
- Recognizing that we call a field science when progress is marked only clarifies the problem (p. 162).
- Kuhn says that the resolution of all but the last sub-question will depend in part on inverting our view of the relation between scientific activity and its community: "recognize as causes what have ordinarily been taken to be effects". Then "scientific progress" and "scientific objectivity" may seem partly redundant. He asks: does a field progress because it is a science, or is it a science because it progresses? (p. 162).
- A mature community works from one paradigm or a closely related set. Seen from inside any community, scientific or not, successful creative work is progress. Theologians and philosophers who refine shared premises contribute to progress within their group. No creative school recognizes work that is a creative success but not an addition to the group's achievement (p. 162).
- If we doubt that non-scientific fields progress, Kuhn argues, this must be because there are always competing schools, each questioning the others' foundations, not because individual schools make no progress. Someone who says philosophy has not progressed stresses that Aristotelians still exist, not that Aristotelianism failed to progress (pp. 162–163).
- Science shows the same doubts in the pre-paradigm period (progress hard to find except within schools) and in revolutions. Opponents of Newton feared a return to the Dark Ages. Opponents of Lavoisier called his rejection of chemical "principles" a retreat to a mere name. A similar, more moderately expressed feeling seems to underlie the opposition of Einstein, Bohm and others to the probabilistic interpretation of quantum mechanics (p. 163).
- So progress seems obvious and assured only in normal science. Part of the answer lies "in the eye of the beholder". Scientific progress is not different in kind from progress in other fields, but the absence of competing schools makes it easier to see. This is not the most important part (p. 163).
- Freed from re-examining first principles, members concentrate on the subtlest phenomena, which raises the efficiency of solving new problems (pp. 163–164).

*Intuition note:* Without rival schools attacking its aims, a community can count its gains clearly. So part of the visibility of progress comes from having no rival schools; Kuhn says this is only part of the answer.

### Insulation and education (pp. 164–166)
- Insulation: mature scientific communities are, in degree, insulated from the laity and everyday demands. No other profession addresses creative work so exclusively to its own members for evaluation. Even the most esoteric poet or most abstract theologian is far more concerned than the scientist with lay approval (p. 164).
- Working for colleagues who share his values, the scientist takes one set of standards for granted and moves on quickly. He can pick problems he has good reason to think he can solve. Unlike the engineer, many doctors and most theologians, the scientist need not choose problems because they urgently need solution, without regard for the tools available (p. 164).
- Social scientists often defend research problems by their social importance (examples: racial discrimination, the business cycle). Kuhn asks which group would solve problems faster (p. 164).
- Education: in the arts, students learn from the works of earlier artists, and textbooks play a secondary role. In history, philosophy and the social sciences, textbooks matter more, but courses also use original sources. Students see many problems and "competing and incommensurable solutions" that they must evaluate themselves (p. 165).
- In contemporary natural sciences, students rely mainly on textbooks until the third or fourth year of graduate work. Textbooks are systematically substituted for the creative literature that made them possible, and few scientists would change this (p. 165).
- Kuhn does not defend the excesses of this training but judges it immensely effective. It is narrow and rigid, but it equips the scientist almost perfectly for puzzle-solving within the textbook tradition. It also equips him to generate significant crises (pp. 165–166).
- Training is not well designed to produce someone who will easily find a fresh approach. But so long as somebody, usually a young person or one new to the field, appears with a new candidate paradigm, the cost of rigidity "accrues only to the individual". Given a generation, individual rigidity is compatible with a community that can switch paradigms. The rigidity may even act as a sensitive indicator that something has gone wrong (p. 166).

*Intuition note:* Narrow training makes the group very good at solving puzzles, and its rigid expectations make failures stand out. As long as someone supplies a new candidate paradigm, the price of rigidity falls on individuals, not on the community.

### Progress through revolutions (pp. 166–168)
- Normal science yields progress inevitably. The second part of the problem is why revolutions also seem to bring progress (p. 166).
- Revolutions end in total victory for one camp. That camp will not call its victory less than progress, since that would admit it was wrong. It can make sure later members see history the same way (p. 166).
- On repudiating a paradigm, the community renounces, as a fit subject for professional scrutiny, most of the books and articles that embodied it. Science education has no equivalent of the art museum or library of classics. The scientist's view of his discipline's past is sometimes drastically distorted into a straight line to the present, that is, progress (p. 167).
- Kuhn grants that the scientist is partly like Orwell's *1984* victim of rewritten history: there are losses as well as gains, and scientists tend to be peculiarly blind to the losses. Footnote 3: science students "know the right answers" and find it hard to analyze older science in its own terms (p. 167).
- But he says no explanation may stop there: that would imply that in the sciences might makes right, a formulation not entirely wrong if it did not suppress the nature of the process and of the authority choosing between paradigms. If authority alone, and particularly nonprofessional authority, decided, the result might be revolution but not scientific revolution. Science depends on vesting the choice in a special kind of community (p. 167).
- Such communities are rare. Every civilization has had technology, art, religion, politics and law, but only civilizations descended from Hellenic Greece had more than rudimentary science. Most scientific knowledge comes from Europe in the last four centuries (pp. 167–168).
- Kuhn says only the most tentative generalizations are possible, yet a number of requisites must already be clear: the scientist must be concerned with problems about nature's behavior. These must be problems of detail, and solutions must be accepted by many (the professional peers). Appeals to heads of state or the populace in scientific matters are forbidden by an unwritten rule. Members must be seen as sole possessors of the rules of the game or of some equivalent basis for unequivocal judgments. Doubting that would admit incompatible standards and raise the question whether truth in the sciences can be one (p. 168).

### The self-fulfilling view of paradigm change (pp. 169–170)
- This list comes from normal science, yet already separates such communities from other professions and explains behavior in revolutions. The community must see paradigm change as progress, and that perception is "in important respects, self-fulfilling" (p. 169).
- The unit of achievement is the solved problem, so few scientists accept a viewpoint that reopens many solved problems. Nature must first undermine security by making prior achievements problematic (p. 169).
- Even then, scientists will be reluctant to embrace a candidate unless convinced that two conditions are met. First, the candidate must seem to resolve an outstanding, generally recognized problem that cannot be met otherwise. Second, it must promise to preserve a relatively large part of accrued concrete problem-solving ability. Novelty for its own sake is not valued. New paradigms "seldom or never" have all the capabilities of predecessors, usually preserve much, and "always" permit additional solutions (p. 169).
- This does not suggest that problem-solving is the unique or an unequivocal basis for paradigm choice; Kuhn notes many reasons why there can be no such criterion (p. 169). The scientific community is a supremely efficient instrument for maximizing the number and precision of problems solved through paradigm change, and a community of specialists will do all it can to ensure the continuing growth of the data it can treat with precision and detail, though it sustains losses in the process (pp. 169–170).
- Losses: some old problems are banished. Revolution often narrows the community's concerns, increases specialization and weakens communication with other groups. Science grows in depth but may not grow in breadth. If science does grow in breadth, that shows mainly as more specialties, not in the scope of any single specialty (p. 170).
- The nature of the community provides a "virtual guarantee" that problems solved and the precision of solutions will grow, at least if any guarantee can be given. Kuhn asks what better criterion than the group's decision there could be (p. 170).
- Kuhn says these paragraphs perhaps indicate that scientific progress is not quite what we took it to be, but that a sort of progress will inevitably characterize science so long as it survives. We may have to give up the notion that paradigm change brings scientists "closer and closer to the truth" (p. 170).

*Intuition note:* Kuhn says progress in the sense of more and more precisely solved problems is virtually guaranteed by how the community works, if any guarantee can be given. Progress in the sense of nearing truth need not occur and may have to be dropped.

### Evolution from, not toward (pp. 170–173)
- "Truth" had entered the essay only in a quotation from Bacon and, later, as the source of the conviction that incompatible rules cannot coexist except in revolutions. The process described is evolution from primitive beginnings, with increasingly detailed understanding, but nothing said makes it evolution toward anything (pp. 170–171).
- Kuhn asks whether a goal set by nature is needed. Can we explain science's existence and success as evolution from the community's current knowledge, rather than toward a full, true account of nature? Substituting evolution-from for evolution-toward may make "a number of vexing problems" vanish, including perhaps the problem of induction (p. 171).
- Darwin analogy: what most bothered many professionals in 1859 was not species change or descent of man from apes, since evolution had been suggested before. It was the abolition of goal-directed evolution. Earlier theories (Lamarck, Chambers, Spencer, the Naturphilosophen) had each stage realize a plan present from the start (pp. 171–172).
- The *Origin* recognized no goal. Natural selection, working on present organisms and environment, produced more elaborate and specialized organisms, even the eye and hand, with no goal. To many, "evolution", "development" and "progress" then seemed self-contradictory (p. 172).
- Kuhn says the analogy can be pushed too far but is "very nearly perfect" for this issue. Resolution of revolutions is selection by conflict within the community of the fittest way to practice future science. Stages are marked by increased articulation and specialization. The whole may have occurred without a set goal or permanent fixed truth (pp. 172–173).
- Remaining questions: why should the process work? What must nature, including man, be like for science to be possible? Why does consensus endure across paradigm changes? Why does paradigm change invariably produce an instrument more perfect in any sense than those known before? Kuhn says that from one point of view all but the first have already been answered, but from another they are as open as when the essay began. The world must also be special, and we know no more about how than at the start (p. 173).
- This problem is as old as science and need not be answered here. Any conception of nature compatible with the growth of science by proof is compatible with the evolutionary view. That view also fits close observation of scientific life, so there are strong arguments for using it (p. 173).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Community A's paradigm solves 10 recognized problems, P1–P10, and fails on one anomaly, P11.
- Candidate B solves P11 and P12 and P13, keeps P1–P8, and loses P9, P10. Condition 1 (resolves an outstanding recognized problem): yes, P11. Condition 2 (keeps a relatively large part of past problem-solving): 8 of 10 kept, which we call "large". Tally: 8 + 1 + 2 = 11 solved problems against the earlier 10.
- Candidate C solves P12–P14 but none of P1–P11. It fails both conditions, so it is rejected despite its novelty.
- Candidate B is accepted. The community's list grows from 10 to 11 and it perceives progress, although P9 and P10 are lost (Kuhn notes on p. 170 that some old problems often have to be banished). Kuhn gives no numeric threshold, and says problem-solving is not a unique basis for choice (p. 169). The numbers only show how a tally can rise while some losses stay hidden.

## Concepts

- [[anomaly → crisis → revolution]] — the community's rigid expectations help generate crises; revolutions end in victory for one camp (pp. 166, 169)
- [[incommensurability]] — used briefly: students of history, philosophy and the social sciences face competing and incommensurable solutions (p. 165)
- [[progress-as-community-relative]] (new) — progress is seen from within a community sharing premises; competing schools obscure it (pp. 162–163)
- [[insulation-of-scientific-community]] (new) — mature scientists write and are judged only for peers, so can pick solvable problems and one standard (p. 164)
- [[textbook-education]] (new) — textbooks replace original literature until late graduate work, which gives a narrow, rigid but effective training (pp. 165–166)
- [[evolution-from-vs-toward]] (new) — science evolves from current knowledge, not toward a pre-set truth; compared with Darwinian selection (pp. 171–173)
- [[problem-solving-as-unit-of-achievement]] (new) — the solved problem is the unit; paradigms are judged partly on keeping and adding solutions (p. 169)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Revolutions as selection by conflict within a community ↔ biology: natural selection of fittest organisms — both produce adapted, specialized results with no pre-set goal.
- [structural-analogy] "Evolution toward truth" being dropped ↔ scientific realism vs. constructive empiricism: whether science aims at truth about unobservables.
- [metaphor] Community as sole arbiter of standards ↔ social epistemology: judging practices by community-level results.

## Questions this raises
- If problem-solving is neither unique nor unequivocal as a criterion, what else guides paradigm choice?
- Is "progress" without approach to truth still progress in an ordinary sense? Does the Darwin analogy support this or only illustrate it?
- Which of the community features (insulation, textbook training) could a social science copy, and would copying them produce progress?
- What must the world be like for science to be possible (p. 173)?
