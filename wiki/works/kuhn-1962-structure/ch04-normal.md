# Normal Science as Puzzle-solving

## L0 — One line
Normal research resembles puzzle-solving: a solution is assured, the result is largely foreseeable, and a network of rules limits what counts as a solution; Kuhn suggests these rules derive from paradigms, but paradigms can guide research even without rules.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Why do scientists work with such devotion on problems whose results are mostly expected? Kuhn's answer is that these problems are puzzles. A solution is assured, the challenge lies in the skill needed to reach the expected result, and rules limit acceptable solutions and methods. These "rules" include laws, instrument preferences, quasi-metaphysical commitments and the general commitments of being a scientist. A paradigm supplies a criterion for choosing problems that can be assumed solvable; this is one reason normal science seems to progress so rapidly. Kuhn then warns that the puzzle metaphor may mislead: normal science is highly determined but need not be entirely determined by rules. Kuhn suggests that rules derive from paradigms, but paradigms can guide research without them.

**Prerequisites:** Kuhn's notions of "paradigm" and "normal science" from earlier chapters (a shared basis that guides research; research carried out within it); "paradigm articulation" (extending a paradigm's scope and precision).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Normal research rarely aims at major novelty. The range of expected, assimilable results is small compared with what imagination can conceive, and an outcome outside it is usually counted as the scientist's failure, not nature's (p. 35).
- The significance of results (adding to the paradigm's scope and precision) cannot by itself account for scientists' passion. The fascination is that the outcome is foreseeable but the way to reach it is in doubt, so success proves one an expert puzzle-solver (p. 36).
- A puzzle is a problem that tests ingenuity or skill. Its outcome need not be important, but a solution must be assured. Many pressing problems are therefore not puzzles (pp. 36–37).
- A paradigm supplies a criterion for choosing problems assumed to have solutions. It can also insulate a community from socially important problems that do not fit the puzzle form (p. 37).
- Puzzles need rules limiting both acceptable solutions and the steps to reach them. If "rule" is broadened (occasionally equated with "established viewpoint" or "preconception"), research problems in a tradition show something much like these puzzle features (pp. 38–39).
- Examples: the wave-length instrument must be tied to theory; the moon's motion problem could not be solved by changing the inverse square law without changing the paradigm (pp. 39–40).
- Kuhn describes four main categories of rule (and says there are others): explicit laws and theories; instrument commitments; quasi-metaphysical commitments; and general commitments of being a scientist (pp. 40–42).
- The puzzle/rule metaphor may mislead. Kuhn suggests that rules derive from paradigms, but paradigms can guide research in the absence of rules (p. 42).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Novelty is not the aim (pp. 35–36).**
- Normal research problems aim little at conceptual or phenomenal novelty. In a wave-length measurement nearly everything but esoteric detail is known in advance (p. 35).
- Coulomb's measurements "need not, perhaps" have fitted an inverse square law, and those working on heating by compression were often prepared for any of several results. Even so, the anticipated range is always small compared with the imaginable range. A result outside it is usually a research failure that reflects on the scientist, not on nature (p. 35).
- Eighteenth-century pan-balance measurements of electrical attraction gave neither consistent nor simple results. They could not articulate the paradigm, so they stayed "mere facts". Only in retrospect, with a later paradigm, can we see what they display (p. 35).
- Coulomb and his contemporaries held that later paradigm, or one giving the same expectations. So Coulomb could design apparatus giving an assimilable result, but this surprised no one, and several contemporaries could predict it. Even paradigm articulation does not aim at unexpected novelty (p. 35).

**Why do the work, then? (p. 36)**
- One part of the answer is that results add to the scope and precision with which the paradigm applies. This cannot explain the devotion (p. 36).
- Computing ephemerides or taking more measurements with an existing instrument gives data that are often just as significant. Scientists regularly spurn such work because it repeats earlier procedures (p. 36).
- Clue: the outcome can be anticipated, but the way to it is very much in doubt. Finishing a normal problem is "achieving the anticipated in a new way". This needs the solution of complex instrumental, conceptual and mathematical puzzles (p. 36).

*Intuition note:* Scientists are not mainly after surprising facts. Like a crossword solver, they enjoy the skill needed to reach an answer that the setup already makes expected.

**Puzzles defined (pp. 36–38).**
- "Puzzles" are the special category of problems that can test ingenuity or skill; Kuhn's examples are jigsaw and crossword puzzles (p. 36).
- Intrinsic interest or importance is no criterion. Pressing problems such as a cure for cancer or a lasting peace are often not puzzles, largely because they may have no solution (pp. 36–37).
- A jigsaw whose pieces are drawn at random from two different boxes is likely to defy even the most ingenious person (though it might not). It cannot test skill, so it is not a puzzle. Intrinsic value is no criterion, but "the assured existence of a solution is" (p. 37).
- A paradigm gives the community a criterion for choosing problems that can be assumed to have solutions while the paradigm is taken for granted. To a great extent these are the only problems the community admits as scientific (p. 37).
- Other problems are rejected as metaphysical, as another discipline's concern, or as too problematic. A paradigm can also insulate scientists from socially important problems that cannot be stated with its conceptual and instrumental tools. Kuhn cites seventeenth-century Baconianism and some contemporary social sciences (p. 37).
- Normal science seems to progress rapidly partly because practitioners concentrate on problems that only their own lack of ingenuity should keep them from solving (p. 37).
- If the problems of normal science are puzzles in this sense, Kuhn says we need no longer ask why scientists attack them with such passion and devotion (p. 37).
- Motives for entering science include usefulness, exploring new territory, finding order and testing established knowledge. The scientific enterprise as a whole sometimes serves these ends, but the individual on a normal problem is almost never doing any of them (pp. 37–38).
- Once engaged, he is driven by the conviction that, if skilful enough, he will solve a puzzle that no one has solved, or solved so well. On most occasions a specialty offers nothing else (p. 38).
- Footnote: the conflict between the individual's role and the overall pattern of scientific development can occasionally cause serious frustration (p. 38, n. 1).

*Intuition note:* A puzzle is a problem where success is guaranteed in principle and only skill is in question. The paradigm filters problems so that scientists mostly face puzzles of this kind.

**Rules (pp. 38–40).**
- A puzzle also needs rules limiting acceptable solutions and the steps to them. Solving a jigsaw is not merely "making a picture". All pieces must be used, plain sides down, interlocked without forcing until no holes remain. A child's or artist's scattered-pieces picture, even if more original, is not a solution (p. 38).
- If "rule" is broadened, sometimes to "established viewpoint" or "preconception", the problems accessible within a research tradition display "something much like" the puzzle characteristics (p. 39).
- Wave-length example: the builder of a wave-length instrument must show, by analysing the apparatus in terms of optical theory, that its numbers are the ones that enter theory as wave lengths. If theory is vague or a component is unanalysed, colleagues may well conclude he "has measured nothing at all" (p. 39).
- Electron-scattering maxima had no apparent significance when first recorded. They became measures of electron wave length only after being related to a theory predicting wave-like behaviour of matter in motion. Then the apparatus had to be redesigned so results correlated unequivocally with theory. "Until those conditions had been satisfied, no problem had been solved" (p. 39).
- Theoretical case: throughout the eighteenth century, attempts to derive the moon's observed motion from Newton's laws consistently failed. Some suggested a law deviating from the inverse square law at small distances. That would have changed the paradigm and defined a new puzzle, not solved the old one. Scientists preserved the rules until, in 1750, one of them discovered how they could be applied successfully (p. 39). Only a change in the rules could have given an alternative (p. 40).

**Four categories of rule (pp. 40–42).**
1. *Explicit laws, concepts and theories* are the most obvious and probably the most binding. While honoured, they set puzzles and limit solutions (p. 40).
   - Newton's laws made quantity-of-matter a fundamental ontological category and inter-particle forces a dominant research topic (p. 40).
   - The laws of fixed and definite proportions set the problem of atomic weights, bounded admissible analyses and told chemists what atoms, molecules, compounds and mixtures were (p. 40).
   - Maxwell's equations and statistical thermodynamics have the same hold today (p. 40).
2. *Instrumental commitments* are more concrete: preferred instruments and their legitimate uses (p. 40).
   - Examples: attitudes to fire in seventeenth-century chemical analysis; physiologists' resistance to Helmholtz's view that physical experiment could illuminate their field; the history of chromatography (pp. 40–41).
3. *Higher-level quasi-metaphysical commitments* are less local and temporary, though still not unchanging (p. 41).
   - Example: after about 1630, particularly after Descartes, most physical scientists assumed the universe was composed of microscopic corpuscles and that all natural phenomena could be explained by their shape, size, motion and interaction (p. 41).
   - This was metaphysical (only shaped matter in motion exists) and methodological (laws specify corpuscular motion; explanation reduces phenomena to it). It also told scientists what problems to pursue. Boyle, for instance, gave special attention to reactions viewable as transmutations, which displayed corpuscular rearrangement (p. 41). Similar effects appear in mechanics, optics and heat (p. 42).
4. *Commitments without which "no man is a scientist"* (p. 42).
   - The scientist must want to understand the world and extend the precision and scope of its ordering. This leads to close empirical scrutiny. Pockets of apparent disorder challenge him to refine observation or articulate theory further. Kuhn says "undoubtedly" there are other such rules held at all times (p. 42).

*Intuition note:* "Rules" here are not only written formulas. They include habits of instrument use, background pictures of what the world is made of, and the general aims of science. Together they help fix what counts as a solved problem.

**Conclusion and caveat (p. 42).**
- This network of conceptual, theoretical, instrumental and methodological commitments is a principal source of the puzzle metaphor. It tells the practitioner of a mature specialty what the world and his science are like, so he can concentrate on the esoteric problems these define (p. 42).
- The metaphor "may be significantly misleading". Rules may not by themselves specify all that the practitioners have in common. Normal science is highly determined but "need not be entirely determined by rules" (p. 42).
- Kuhn therefore introduced shared paradigms, not shared rules, as the source of coherence. He suggests rules derive from paradigms, but paradigms can guide research even without rules (p. 42).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A 3×3 grid is filled with the symbols 1, 2, 3. Rule: each symbol appears once in each row and column. Given: row 1 = 1 2 3, and row 2 starts with 2.
- Row 2, position 3: column 3 already has 3, so it is 1 or 2. Row 2 already has 2, so it is 1. Position 2 is then the remaining symbol, 3. Row 2 = 2 3 1.
- Row 3: column 1 has 1, 2, so the entry is 3. Column 2 has 2, 3, so 1. Column 3 has 3, 1, so 2. Row 3 = 3 1 2.
- Check: every row and column contains 1, 2, 3 once.
A grid of nine 1s is more "original" but breaks the rules, so it is no solution. Here a solution exists by construction of the clues. The challenge is the route, not the answer. This mirrors Kuhn's point that rules fix what counts as solving.

## Concepts
- [[paradigm / normal science]] — normal research carried out within a paradigm; here, work that aims at puzzle solutions, not novelty (pp. 35, 37)
- [[puzzle]] (new) — problem testing skill, with assured solution and rules limiting solutions; importance is irrelevant (pp. 36–38)
- [[rules-of-the-game]] (new) — broadened "rule": laws, instrument commitments, metaphysical commitments, general commitments, sometimes "established viewpoint" (pp. 38–42)
- [[paradigm articulation]] (new) — extending a paradigm's scope and precision; also aims at the expected (pp. 35–36)
- [[paradigm guides without rules]] (new) — rules derive from paradigms; paradigms can guide research without rules (p. 42)

## Questions this raises
- If rules do not fully determine normal science, what does a paradigm supply beyond rules (p. 42)?
- When a puzzle repeatedly fails (as the moon problem did), when is it right to keep the rules, and when to change them (pp. 39–40)?
- If a paradigm insulates scientists from problems that are not puzzles, what is lost (p. 37)?
