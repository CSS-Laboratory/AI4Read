# The Meaning of "Meaning" (Putnam, 1975)

## L0 — One line
The traditional picture of meaning needs two assumptions that cannot both be true (knowing a meaning is a state of the speaker's head, and meaning fixes extension, i.e. the set of things a word applies to), because extension depends on society and on the real nature of the stuff around us.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Putnam asks why semantics is in worse shape than syntax. He argues that the traditional picture of meaning rests on two assumptions: knowing a meaning is a psychological state (in the narrow sense), and meaning determines extension (the set of things a word applies to). They cannot both be true. Thought experiments such as Twin Earth (an imagined planet whose "water" is XYZ) show that extension is fixed partly by society (division of linguistic labour) and partly by the actual nature of the samples (indexicality, rigidity). He proposes to describe meaning as a vector: syntactic markers, semantic markers, stereotype, extension. He also criticises Davidson's truth-definition programme and "California semantics".

**Prerequisites:** extension/intension (Frege–Carnap); possible worlds; Quine's critique of analyticity (as summarised here); basic set theory; the idea of a natural-kind term (a word for a kind such as water or tiger).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Traditional meaning theory rests on (I) knowing meaning is a narrow psychological state (one that presupposes nothing outside the speaker) and (II) intension (the concept tied to a word) determines extension. Putnam argues that no notion satisfies both (p. 136).
- Twin Earth (an imagined planet where "water" is XYZ, not H₂O) gives two speakers in the same psychological state but with different extensions. So "meanings just ain't in the head" (pp. 139–144).
- Division of linguistic labour: for many words, experts and the community fix the extension. The average speaker's state does not (pp. 144–146).
- Natural-kind words have an unnoticed indexical component (their reference depends on the stuff around here, as with "I") and are rigid (they pick out the same thing in every possible world where they designate; Kripke). Hidden structure, if there is one, generally determines membership in the kind in all possible worlds. This yields statements that are metaphysically necessary (true in all possible worlds) but epistemically contingent (not rationally unrevisable) (pp. 146–152, 159–160).
- A realist view of reference is defended against operationalist and antirealist rivals (pp. 153–157).
- A stereotype is the conventional idea that speakers must acquire to count as having acquired a word. It is not analytic and not necessarily true. Linguistic obligation, not unrevisability, makes it "part of the meaning" (pp. 169–177).
- Putnam argues that Davidson's truth-definition programme cannot succeed in principle for most words (pp. 179–181), and that "California semantics" (his label for a Carnap-style picture in which each term is tied to an intension, a function from possible worlds to sets) is committed to the two assumptions he rejects and so fails for natural language (pp. 184–187).
- Proposal: a normal-form "meaning vector" (syntactic markers, semantic markers, stereotype, extension). It keeps (II) by construction and gives up (I) (pp. 190–192).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### Introduction (pp. 131–132)
- Transformational linguistics gives a subtle description of syntax. Some features that look species-specific lie at the level of deep structure (p. 131).
- The drawback for a philosopher: this work does not concern the meaning of words. The dimension of language tied to "meaning" is "as much in the dark as it ever was" (p. 131).
- Putnam's explanation, offered as his opinion: semantics is worse off because the prescientific concept of meaning is in worse shape than that of syntax. General skepticism or nominalism does not help (p. 132).
- The upshot will be that meanings "don't exist in quite the way we tend to think". Electrons likewise don't exist as Bohr thought, but that is not the claim that they don't exist (p. 132).
- Focus: word-meaning, because that concept is more defective than sentence-meaning. Davidson's view that sentence-meaning is primary is commented on later. The reader is asked to assume nothing is clear in advance (p. 132).

### Meaning and extension (pp. 132–133)
- **Extension** of a term = the set of things the term is true of. "Rabbit" has the set of rabbits as extension (p. 132). **Intension** = the concept or sense, the second member of the traditional pair (p. 134).
- Two idealisations (Putnam keeps them). First, a "set" is yes–no, but words like "tree" have borderline cases, and the border is itself fuzzy. Formalising extension properly would need fuzzy sets (p. 133). Second, a word with several senses is treated as several words ("rabbit₁", "rabbit₂"). This assumes discretely many senses, fixed once and for all. Ziff has studied how this distorts things (p. 133).
- "Creature with a heart" and "creature with a kidney" have the same extension (assuming every creature has both) but differ in meaning. The traditional exposition (which Putnam goes on to criticise) argues: if there is a sense of "meaning" in which meaning = extension, there must be another sense, intension (pp. 133–134).

### Intension and extension (pp. 134–136)
- Putnam's complaint about the canonical explanation: it says "in one sense 'meaning' means extension, in the other it means meaning". Extension is precise (given truth and the idealisations), but intension is only the vague, misleading notion "concept". He compares "probability means frequency in one sense, propensity in the other". "Probability" never means frequency (p. 134).
- Consequences of the doctrine (pp. 134–135):
  - Concepts were taken as mental. Frege, Carnap and followers made them abstract entities to keep meanings public. But "grasping" them remained an individual psychological act.
  - Same extension with different intension is possible. The reverse was taken as impossible, and no argument was offered. Putnam says this probably reflects the tradition that a concept is a conjunction of predicates giving necessary and sufficient conditions (footnote 4 links this to theology on "God", p. 135).
  - Carnap, as a verificationist, took the concept to give a criterion (a way of recognising members).
- Two unchallenged assumptions (pp. 134, 136):
  - **(I)** Knowing the meaning of a term is just being in a certain psychological state. Here states of memory and dispositions count as psychological states.
  - **(II)** The meaning of a term (in the sense of intension) determines its extension. Sameness of intension entails sameness of extension.
- Putnam's claim: no notion at all, let alone a notion of meaning, satisfies both. The traditional concept rests on a false theory (p. 136).
- **Intuition note.** Think of meaning as a "file" in the head that must also pick out the right things in the world. Putnam argues that no notion can both be a state of one person's head (I) and fix the world-side set of things the word applies to (II).

### "Psychological state" and methodological solipsism (pp. 136–139)
- In one sense a "state" is simply a two-place predicate (individual, time). Sciences restrict "state" to properties defined by fundamental parameters of the individual. In the sense of "psychological state" that merely means "studied by psychology", knowing the meaning of "water" may be trivially a psychological state, but that is not the sense at issue in (I) (p. 136).
- **Methodological solipsism:** no psychological state, properly so called, presupposes the existence of any individual other than the subject. Traditionally even the subject's body is not presupposed, so a "disembodied mind" could be in the state (p. 136).
- Narrow vs wide states. "Jealous of y" entails that y exists, so it is a psychological state in the wide sense only. Narrow states are those methodological solipsism permits. Keeping the assumption would require reconstruing jealousy so one can be jealous of hallucinations (p. 137).
- Putnam's view: the reconstruction would be worthwhile only if narrow states have significant causal closure. In his opinion, the three centuries of failure of mentalistic psychology are very strong evidence against it (p. 137).
- Argument that narrow states must determine extension (pp. 137–138):
  1. Let A and B differ in extension. By (II) they differ in meaning. By (I) knowing each meaning is a narrow state. (p. 137)
  2. Knowing the meaning of A is not only "grasping an intension"; it is also knowing that this intension is the intension of A. For example, someone who grasps the intension of the German word "Rad" but does not know it is the intension of "Rad" does not know the meaning of "Rad". (p. 138)
  3. So knowing that $I_1$ is the meaning of A and knowing that $I_2$ is the meaning of A are different narrow states, and, assuming A has just one meaning for Oscar in each world, incompatible ones. (p. 138)
  4. Hence there are no two possible worlds $L_1$, $L_2$ in which Oscar is in the same narrow state in all respects but understands A with meaning $I_1$ in one and $I_2$ in the other. (p. 138)
  5. So the same necessary and sufficient condition for falling in A's extension "works" in every possible world where the speaker is in state S. S fixes the intension, and by (II) the intension is a necessary and sufficient condition. (p. 138)
- Platonism vs psychologism: if "grasping" is a narrow psychological state that uniquely determines the Platonic entity, then choosing the entity or the state as "the meaning" is somewhat a matter of convention. Taking the state would not make meanings private, because different people (and epochs) can be in the same state. Frege's argument only refutes identifying concepts with mental particulars (pp. 138–139).
- Consequence of (I) and (II) together: if Oscar and Elmer understand A differently they must be in different psychological states. Two speakers in the same narrow state cannot understand A differently. The state determines intension and hence extension (p. 139).
- **Claim to be shown false:** two speakers can be in exactly the same narrow state while A has different extensions in their idiolects (idiolect = one speaker's own dialect). "Extension is not determined by psychological state." Then anyone who wants to rescue at least one traditional assumption must give up either "state determines intension" or "intension determines extension" (p. 139).
- **Intuition note.** The argument only says: if what you know when you know a meaning is a purely inner state (I), and meaning fixes extension (II), then your inner state must already settle what the word applies to. Putnam will argue that it does not.

### Are meanings in the head? (pp. 139–144)
**Twin Earth water (pp. 139–141).**
- Twin Earth is exactly like Earth except as specified. Its "water" is a liquid with a long chemical formula, abbreviated XYZ, indistinguishable from water at normal temperatures and pressures. Its lakes, rain and so on contain XYZ (p. 140).
- Visitors would first assume "water" means the same, then report: "On Twin Earth the word 'water' means XYZ." The verb "means" can mean "has as extension". But the nominalization "meaning" never means extension (p. 140).
- Two senses: water_E (H₂O) and water_TE (XYZ). The extension of water_E is the set of wholes consisting of H₂O molecules, "or something like that" (p. 141).
- In 1750 neither community knew the chemistry. Oscar₁ (Earth) and Oscar₂ (Twin Earth) may be duplicates in appearance, thoughts and monologue. The extension of "water" was H₂O on Earth and XYZ on Twin Earth in 1750 as in 1950. They were in the same state but understood "water" differently. So extension, and the intuitive "meaning", is not a function of the speaker's state alone (p. 141).
- **Objection:** why think "water" kept the same extension from 1750 to 1950? **Putnam's sketch of an answer:** an ostensive definition ("this liquid is called water", i.e. defining by pointing) presupposes that the sample bears a sameness relation (sameL, "same liquid as") to most of what the community has called "water". If the presupposition fails (the sample is gin), the definition is not meant to be accepted. So the definition conveys a defeasible necessary and sufficient condition, with "fallback" conditions (pp. 141–142).
- sameL is a theoretical relation. Settling it may take an indeterminate amount of investigation, and any answer is revisable. That people in 1750 would have called XYZ "water" and later ones would not does not show a meaning change. What changed was the belief about whether XYZ bears sameL to Lake Michigan's liquid (p. 142).

**Aluminum/molybdenum (pp. 142–143).**
- Suppose pots of molybdenum and aluminum can be told apart only by an expert, and "aluminum" and "molybdenum" are switched on Twin Earth. A lay speaker on each planet may be in the same psychological state. Yet "aluminum" has extension aluminum for Oscar₁ and molybdenum for Oscar₂ (p. 143).
- Difference from water: here only a part of each community (metallurgists) could tell the difference (p. 142).

**Elm/beech (pp. 143–144).**
- Putnam cannot tell elm from beech. Yet the extension of "elm" is the same in his idiolect as in everyone's (all elm trees). His concept of elm equals his concept of beech. So intension = concept cannot be right (pp. 143–144).
- Switch "elm" and "beech" on Twin Earth and give him a molecule-for-molecule Doppelgänger with identical thoughts, sense data and dispositions. The Doppelgänger "means" beech by "elm". "Cut the pie any way you like, 'meanings' just ain't in the head!" (p. 144).
- **Intuition note.** Same inner state, different environment, different referent. So the inner state cannot be what fixes reference. The thought experiment removes everything inside the head from the explanation of the difference.

### A socio-linguistic hypothesis (pp. 144–146)
- There is a **division of linguistic labor.** Not everyone who uses "elm", "aluminum" or "gold" needs a way of recognising them; one can rely on a subclass of speakers (p. 144).
- Gold example: gold matters for many reasons (precious, monetary, symbolic). The community works like a "factory": some wear gold rings, some sell them, some tell real from fake. It is not necessary or efficient that everyone who wears gold also buys, sells or tests it (p. 144).
- Features usually attached to a general name (necessary and sufficient conditions, criteria) are all present in the community as a collective body, though the labour of knowing them is divided (p. 145).
- This presupposes division of non-linguistic labour. Some words show no division ("chair"). More words show it as society and science develop. "Water" showed none before chemistry. Today every speaker can recognise water and probably knows "water is H₂O", but few can tell water from lookalikes. Experts' ways of recognising belong to the collective body (p. 145).
- **Hypothesis of the Universality of the Division of Linguistic Labor:** every linguistic community has at least some terms whose "criteria" are known only to a subset of speakers, with others' use depending on structured cooperation with them (pp. 145–146). It is a hypothesis; primitive peoples might be exceptions, or division of labour might be a fundamental trait of the species (p. 146).
- Consequence: for such terms the average speaker acquires nothing that fixes extension. Only the sociolinguistic state of the collective body does (p. 146).
- Two kinds of tools: a hammer (one user) and a steamship (needs cooperation). Words have been thought of too much on the first model (p. 146).

### Indexicality and rigidity (pp. 146–157)
**Setting up (pp. 146–147).**
- The water-in-1750 example does not involve division of labour in the same way. It does bear on reference and necessary truth (p. 146).
- Two ways to tell someone what a natural-kind term means: an ostensive definition, or a description. The description typically gives markers (e.g. "liquid") plus a **stereotype**, a standardized description of typical features. Central features are generally criteria, or at least necessary conditions (possibly probabilistic). Not all community criteria are in the stereotype, and it may be weak. For elm it is just "common deciduous tree". The tiger stereotype lets one recognise tigers. For molybdenum it may be only "a metal" (p. 147).
- Possible worlds are taken as primitive. Putnam leans heavily on Kripke, though his conclusions were reached independently (pp. 147–148).

**Two theories of "water" (p. 148).** Let $W_1$ be the actual world, with H₂O in the glass, and $W_2$ a world with XYZ in the glass. Putnam gives two theories:
1. "Water" is world-relative but constant in meaning. Water is H₂O in $W_1$ and XYZ in $W_2$.
2. Water is H₂O in all worlds, but "water" does not have the same meaning in $W_1$ and $W_2$.

The Twin Earth case supports (2). The "this" in an ostensive definition is a de re "this". The scope difference:

$$(1')\ \text{(For every world } W)\ \text{(For every } x \text{ in } W)\ (x \text{ is water} \equiv x \text{ bears same}_L \text{ to the entity referred to as "this" in } W)$$

$$(2')\ \text{(For every world } W)\ \text{(For every } x \text{ in } W)\ (x \text{ is water} \equiv x \text{ bears same}_L \text{ to the entity referred to as "this" in the actual world } W_1)$$

- In (1′) "this" is within the scope of "For every world W". In (2′) it refers in the actual world, independent of $W$ (p. 149).
- **Kripke's rigidity:** a designator is rigid (in a sentence) if it refers to the same individual in every possible world in which it designates. Putnam extends this to substance names: "water" is rigid. This follows from intending (2′), not (1′). The demonstrative "this" is also rigid (p. 149).

**Cross-world relations and necessity (pp. 149–151).**
- A relation is cross-world if its extension is a set of pairs of individuals not in the same world (e.g. "same height as"). sameL is understood this way. An entity x in any world is water iff it bears sameL (cross-world) to the stuff we call "water" in the actual world (p. 150).
- Suppose I agree that a liquid with water's superficial properties but a different microstructure isn't really water. Then my recognition methods ("operational definition"), used before the microstructure is known, cannot be an analytic specification of what it is to be water; like the ostensive definition they are only a way of pointing out a standard. Local lookalike stuff with a different microstructure is not water (p. 150).
- Once we discover that water is H₂O, "nothing counts as a possible world in which water isn't H₂O". So, if a "logically possible" statement is one that holds in some logically possible world, it isn't logically possible that water isn't H₂O. Yet we can imagine experiences that would make it rational to believe it isn't. It is conceivable but not logically possible: "Conceivability is no proof of logical possibility" (p. 151).
- Kripke's terms: **epistemically necessary** = rationally unrevisable (assuming there are such statements). **Metaphysically necessary** = true in all possible worlds. A statement can be metaphysically necessary and epistemically contingent (p. 151).
- Kant's and the empiricists' traditions both equated metaphysical and epistemic necessity. Kripke's challenge goes beyond that oscillation (p. 151).
- **Intuition note.** Water's being H₂O had to be discovered by investigation, yet on Putnam's account water is H₂O in every possible world. That is the gap between "true in all possible worlds" and "rationally unrevisable".

**Indexicality (pp. 151–152).**
- Indexical words ("now", "this", "I") have extension varying with context. No one claims intension determines their extension. My Doppelgänger and I both think "I have a headache". The extension of his "I" is him and of mine is me, yet our concepts of ourselves need not differ (pp. 151–152).
- Putnam's theory: words like "water" have an **unnoticed indexical component**. "Water" is stuff bearing sameL to the water around here. So (1) intensions as concepts and (2) intension determining extension cannot both hold for "water", just as for "I" (p. 152).
- Two options for Twin Earth "water": same meaning but different extension (the usual line for "I"), giving up "meaning determines extension". Or, Putnam's choice, a difference in extension is ipso facto a difference in meaning for natural-kind words, giving up meanings as concepts or mental entities (p. 152).
- Kripke's rigidity and Putnam's indexicality "are but two ways of making the same point". Putnam endorses Kripke's remark that reference-fixing by description does not make a name synonymous with the description. Kripke adds that reference of names is rarely or almost never fixed by description (p. 152).

**Gold and Archimedes; realism (pp. 153–157).**
- Putnam's claim: "gold" has not (significantly) changed extension in 2000 years. χρυσός in Archimedes' dialect has the same extension as "gold" in Putnam's dialect. Identification methods have become far more sophisticated (p. 153).
- Suppose X is a metal piece that Archimedes could not tell from gold but modern techniques can. X is not in the extension of "gold", and Putnam holds it was not in the extension of χρυσός either. Archimedes would have mistaken X for gold (p. 153).
- **Alternative view (operationalist):** "gold" means whatever satisfies the operational definition in use at the time (p. 153).
- Motive 1: skepticism about truth. On Putnam's own view, Archimedes was saying X had the same hidden structure as normal local gold. "Who's to say he was wrong?" The obvious answer is "we are, using the best theory available". Putnam says people tend to be strongly antirealist or strongly realist in intuition (pp. 153–154).
  - For the strong antirealist, truth makes sense only intra-theoretically. Extension is tied to truth ("what the term is true of"), so he should reject extension too. He could fall back on "warranted assertibility" (as Dewey did). Then "X is gold" was warrantedly assertible then and is not now, a minimal claim both sides accept (p. 154).
  - Putnam's reply: scientists use terms as if criteria were approximately correct characterisations of theory-independent entities, and as if later theories describe the same entities better. "In my opinion" this is the only hypothesis that explains communicability of scientific results and closure of theories under first-order logic. If truth and extension are used extra-theoretically, accept the realist perspective. Squaring truth with antirealism through an "untenable theory of meaning" is no progress (p. 155).
- Motive 2: dislike of unverifiable hypotheses. Putnam says the situation is not quite that X's status was unknowable. There are situations (melting, conductivity, vaporisation) where X would behave differently. Archimedes could have checked that regularity and concluded "X may not be gold". Told the molecular facts, he would presumably have agreed X isn't gold. Truths that can't be verified exist anyway (e.g. infinitely many binary stars) (pp. 155–156).
- Motive 3: empirical disagreement about speakers' intentions. Putnam says that for present-day English there is no serious doubt that no operational definition gives a necessary and sufficient condition. The intention is never "to make the name synonymous with the description" (pp. 156–157).

### Other senses (pp. 157–160)
- The above is the predominant sense of natural-kind words. They have other senses (Ziff: a continuum) (p. 157).
- sameL: x bears it to y iff (1) x and y are both liquids and (2) they agree in important physical properties. "Importance" is interest-relative. Normally it is structural (what the stuff is made of and arranged as). So one context may take "water" as chemically pure water, another as Lake Michigan stuff; sometimes XYZ used as water counts as water; sometimes a single H₂O molecule or vapour does (p. 157).
- Deviant senses relate to the core sense ("the lemon" = the plastic lemon). Martian "tigers" with silicon chemistry are tigers or not "depending on the context" (pp. 157–158).
- **Misunderstanding 1:** a critic holds that the predominant sense of "lemon" is "anything with enough superficial characteristics", and that hidden structure matters only when "lemon" is a scientific term. Putnam replies (pp. 158–159):
  - The superficial sense is extremely deviant. It would include silicon lemons or machine lemons.
  - The hidden-structure sense is not the technical sense (synonymy with a description of the genetic code). Saying that to be water something must be H₂O does not mean speakers must know it. Concluding that "water" is therefore synonymous with "H₂O" confuses metaphysical with epistemological necessity.
  - Adding "cross-fertile with lemons" to the superficial characteristics presupposes "lemon", so taking this as inconsistent with Putnam's account is question-begging. Moreover, such characteristics give no truth-condition for other worlds. "Grow" is itself a natural-kind verb.
- **Misunderstanding 2:** that members of a natural kind's extension must share a hidden structure. It could have turned out that "water" had none, so that superficial characteristics were the criterion. This does not mean water could have been anything but H₂O. It means a liquid with no hidden structure could have looked like water and filled the lakes (p. 159).
- Cases: diseases (some have a common etiology, such as tuberculosis; some have only symptoms in common; multiple sclerosis was undecided). Jade covers jadeite (sodium and aluminum) and nephrite (calcium, magnesium, iron). If H₂O and XYZ had both been plentiful on Earth, we would say "the xyz kind of water" (p. 160).
- **Summary:** if there is a hidden structure, then generally it determines what it is to be a member of the kind, in the actual world and in all possible worlds. It fixes what can be counterfactually supposed ("water could have been vapor?" yes; "water could have been XYZ?" no). Local water may have several hidden structures, or so many that superficial characteristics become decisive (p. 160).

### Other words (pp. 160–164)
- The account applies to most nouns and some other parts of speech (p. 160).
- **Artifact names ("pencil").** The traditional view is that they are defined by conjunctions or clusters of properties, some necessary, e.g. "pencils are artifacts", and that this is epistemic necessity, indeed analyticity (p. 160).
- **Albritton's example:** we discover that pencils are organisms (nerves, spawning, lettering as camouflage). If this is conceivable (Putnam agrees with Albritton that it is), then it is epistemically possible that pencils turn out to be organisms. It follows that "pencils are artifacts" is not epistemically necessary in the strongest sense and, a fortiori, not analytic (p. 161).
- But Putnam says this has not shown a possible world in which pencils are organisms. It shows a world where organisms are the *epistemic counterparts* of pencils (phrase from Kripke). On Twin Earth, with artifact pencils on Earth and organism "pencils" there, we would be far more likely to say "the things that pass for pencils on Twin Earth aren't really pencils". If both Earths had organisms, we would say "pencils are organisms" (pp. 161–162).
- So whether pencil-organisms are pencils depends on whether local pencils are. If local pencils are artifacts, "pencils are artifacts" is metaphysically necessary but not epistemically necessary. "Pencil" is not synonymous with any description, even loosely. It is "just as indexical as 'water' or 'gold'" (p. 162).
- **Katz on cats-as-robots (voice: Katz, criticised by Putnam).** Putnam earlier discussed cats turning out to be remotely controlled robots from Mars. Katz argues that case should be described as "there are no cats", with "Cats are robots" semantically deviant. Putnam replies (pp. 162–163):
  - Katz's explanation of "Cats are robots" is an all-purpose explanation of how one can say anything.
  - It wrongly predicts "Cats are robots" is deviant and "There are no cats in the world" standard in the case described.
  - "There are no cats" would be standard only in a case like collective hallucination.
  - It is also bad rational reconstruction. We don't treat "cat" as synonymous with a description because we know cats have a hidden structure, and the right methodology is to refer rigidly to things with that structure.
- Katz's view is more plausible for "pencil", where we think we know a (vague) necessary and sufficient condition. We might say either "pencils have turned out to be organisms" or "there are no pencils". So "pencil" can be a natural-kind word or a "one-criterion" word (p. 163).
- Putnam doubts there are true one-criterion words outside stipulation (e.g. "pediatrician", footnote 16). There seems to be a strong tendency for words introduced as one-criterion to develop a natural-kind sense, which seems to be the predominant one for artifact names. The teakettle joke needs it to be conceivable that a person could be a teakettle (pp. 163–164).
- Nearly pure one-criterion words are those whose meaning derives from a transformation ("hunter" = one who hunts). Verbs like "grow" and adjectives like "red" have indexical features. Some syncategorematic words (words with no extension of their own) are more one-criterion. "Whole": "The whole army surrounded the town" means every part took part (analysis due to Kroch) (p. 164).

### Meaning (pp. 164–166)
- Extension is not fixed by a concept in the speaker's head. It is fixed socially (division of linguistic labour) and indexically (the actual nature of paradigm cases, not fully known to the speaker). Traditional theory leaves out exactly the contribution of society and of the real world (p. 164).
- Two routes: (a) keep meaning = concept and give up "meaning determines extension", then "water" would have the same meaning on both Earths with different extensions; or (b) identify meaning with an ordered pair or n-tuple, one component being the extension. Putnam prefers (b). For "elm"/"beech" switched on Twin Earth we would not say "elm" has the same meaning there (p. 165).
- Route (b) makes "meaning determines extension" trivially true and drops the idea that a difference in meaning must reflect a difference in concept or psychological state (p. 165).
- The traditional problem splits into two. (1) How extension is determined: largely a problem for sociolinguistics. Kripke's causal theory of reference, extended by Putnam to natural-kind and physical-magnitude terms, falls here as a special case of social cooperation. (2) How to describe individual competence (pp. 165–166).
- Proper names differ from nouns like "tiger". One can use "Sanders" correctly knowing nothing but the name. One cannot use "tiger" correctly, except per accidens, without knowing a good deal about tigers (p. 166).

### Stereotypes and communication (pp. 166–169)
- A speaker who knows only that "tiger" has a set of physical objects as extension could use it in some sentences, and its socially determined extension would be the set of tigers. Yet we would not say he "knows the meaning" (p. 166).
- Putnam reformulates with "acquired the word". Someone acquires "tiger" if (1) his use passes muster and (2) his situation in the world and community makes the socially determined extension of "tiger" in his idiolect the set of tigers. Clause (2) means Twin Earth speakers with the same habits have not acquired our word if their "tigers" are not tigers (p. 167).
- Reason for the new wording: "knowing the meaning" suggests that acquiring a word is coming to possess a thing called its meaning. Putnam has argued against that (p. 167).
- Why does the snowball speaker ("is that a tiger?") not pass muster? Significant communication requires knowing something about what one talks about. That differs from the mind-boggling ignorance here, which our linguistic conventions prevent "near enough" (p. 168).
- Speakers must know something about stereotypical tigers (stripes), something about elms, and so on. The minimum depends on culture and topic. The hypothetical Cheroquoi, for whom telling elm from beech is obligatory, would count someone who cannot as not knowing "uhaba'". The translation "elm" would then be only approximately correct (pp. 168–169).

### What stereotypes are (pp. 169–171)
- A stereotype is a conventional (perhaps inaccurate) idea of what an X is like. It is, Putnam says, the "sole element of truth in the 'concept' theory" (p. 169).
- One stereotype is required by the community (the speaker may have others), and he must know implicitly that it is obligatory. It must include stripes if his acquisition of "tiger" is to count (pp. 169–170).
- Being in the stereotype does not mean that it is analytic, or true of most, or all normal, or some Xs. Albino and three-legged tigers are not contradictory. In the most important sense of "butterfly", wingless butterflies would still be butterflies (p. 170).
- Why have stereotypes if not necessarily right? Most capture features of paradigm members. The gold stereotype contains "yellow" though pure gold is nearly white. Jewelry gold looks yellow because of copper. The "witch" stereotype is seriously wrong and helps communication only within witch-theory. Putnam says we could hardly communicate if most stereotypes weren't pretty accurate as far as they go (pp. 170–171).

### The "operational meaning" of stereotypes (pp. 171–173)
- Operational definitions of terms have notoriously failed in physics. Putnam says Quine's arguments against a theory of meaning sometimes reduce to demanding operational definitions, and then "should be ignored". But idealised operational definitions have heuristic value (p. 171).
- The theoretical account of a stereotype is in terms of **linguistic obligation**, not explicated here. "Striped is part of the stereotype of 'tiger'" means it is obligatory to acquire that information when acquiring the word, as it is obligatory to mark singular/plural (p. 171).
- An idealised test: the **linguist's confederate** is an adult who has failed to acquire "tiger" and asks what it means. The hypothesis implies that informants will say, inter alia, that tigers are striped. Children's questions work similarly but are messier (pp. 171–172).
- Putnam says he knows from his own competence what he would tell someone: feline, size, yellow with black stripes, sometimes in jungle, fierce. Idiolects vary (p. 172).

### Quine's "Two Dogmas" revisited (pp. 173–177)
- Putnam's stance: Quine's attack on analytic–synthetic was salutary but went too far. Some limited class of analytic sentences can be saved. The attack was later construed as dragging the whole notion of meaning down. Putnam's project is constructive: "to revise the notion of meaning, not to bury it" (p. 173).
- Putnam's reconstruction of Quine: there are two behavioural indexes of analyticity, both unsatisfactory (pp. 173–174).
  1. **Centrality** (immunity from revision). Fundamental physics laws may have it without being called analytic. In real science immunity is a matter of degree. So analyticity would become a matter of degree. Quine says that would be better called "centrality".
  2. **Being called "analytic"** by trained informants (variants: deducible from "meaning postulates"; from logic by substituting synonyms). These need a criterion of synonymy, which leads in circles. The index leaves "analytic" an unexplicated noise.
  - The intersection fares no better. Putnam's gloss on Quine's conclusion: analyticity is either centrality misconceived or nothing (p. 174).
- Many philosophers identify analyticity with highest centrality. Putnam couples Quine with Reichenbach's argument: principles that Kant would count synthetic a priori (normal induction, continuity of space, euclidean character of space) are jointly incompatible with special relativity and general covariance. So either give up identifying analyticity with centrality, or deny it is closed under conjunction, or accept that an analytic sentence may have testable empirical consequences (p. 175).
- The empiricists' "bloated" analyticity aimed to dissolve Kant's problem by identifying apriority with analyticity and then with truth by convention (p. 175–176).
- Sentence/statement distinction (proposed by other philosophers): all sentences are revisable, but some statements are not, because revising a sentence does not change our mind about the statement it expressed just in case the revision is meaning change, not theory change. This reduces to synonymy, and meaning change and theory change cannot be sharply separated. Putnam does not agree that meaning change cannot be defined at all. Discovering a non-euclidean world might change the meaning of "straight line" (if the parallels postulate were in its stereotype) but would not be a mere change of meaning, nor of extension. From the fact that giving up S involves meaning change it does not follow that S is true (p. 176).
- **Applying this to Putnam's view:** being striped is part of the meaning of "tiger" without "tigers are striped" being analytic. A mutation could make all tigers albino. Communication presupposes a stripe-including stereotype held and known by both parties (mutual knowledge "a la Grice, forever"). It presupposes no particular stereotype is correct. Linguistic obligation is not an index of unrevisability or truth (pp. 176–177).
- **The "noise" argument.** Informants' remarks are evidence, but the stereotype is not defined by such remarks. "Big-cat-like is part of the meaning of tiger" means speakers acquire that information with the word and feel obliged to see that learners do too. No circularity of Quine's kind arises (p. 177).

### Radical translation (pp. 177–179)
- Putnam's theory does not solve Quine's radical translation problem by itself. We cannot match stereotypes to translate Cheroquoi, since finding a stereotype involves translating. But requiring that each word approximately match its image in stereotype constrains translation. Once basic vocabulary is translated, stereotypes can constrain and check further work (pp. 177–178).
- Stereotypes do not determine a unique translation. German *Ulme* and *Buche* share elm's stereotype, but *Ulme* = elm and *Buche* = beech. Cognates help but are not foolproof. For Greek *oxya*/*ptelea* one needs an expert who can tell elm from beech. So the linguist must sometimes assess who the experts are before guessing the socially determined extension (p. 178).
- A stereotype wildly different from elm's would disqualify "elm" for *oxya* in all but the most extensional contexts. An extension not even approximately the elms would wipe the translation out altogether (p. 178).
- This enlarges the evidence beyond Quine's base. It lets the linguist convey puzzlement at a word ("what does this mean?"), on the argument that segmentation into words is universal. Putnam does not claim it solves radical translation. It adds constraints. More constraints, including ones on what beliefs can reasonably be imputed to people, may yield a unique translation "or as unique a translation as we are able to get in practice". That is deferred to another paper (p. 179).

### A critique of Davidsonian semantic theory (pp. 179–183)
- **Davidson's proposal (Putnam's interpretation):** a semantic theory modelled on a Tarskian truth definition. Rules give for each word the conditions under which it is true of something (other words treated as syncategorematic). For longer sentences, rules give truth conditions by sentence type, from shorter sentences. Example: if S is $(S_1 \,\&\, S_2)$ then S is true iff $S_1$ and $S_2$ are both true. That clause specifies the meaning of "&" (pp. 179–180).
- **Difficulty:** an extensionally correct clause may say nothing about meaning. "'Water' is true of x iff x is H₂O" is extensionally correct (ignoring impurities) but not a theory of meaning if most speakers don't know water is H₂O (p. 180).
- Davidson's answer, given "in conversation" and relativized to a not-yet-existing theory of translation: we want truth definitions that are simultaneously translations. Putnam: this "comes perilously close to saying that a theory of meaning is a truth definition plus a theory of meaning". Davidson's second contention is that sentences, not words, are the basic units, because evidence is assent and dissent to sentences (p. 181).
- Putnam's verdict: "the theory cannot succeed in principle". It works for special words like truth-functional "and", where the stereotype is a necessary and sufficient condition. For "the great majority of words" the requirements of truth theory and meaning theory are "mutually incompatible, at least in the English–English case", which he calls the basic case (p. 181).
- **Three conditions** for a clause "W is true of x iff ––":
  1. it is extensionally correct;
  2. –– is a translation of W (stereotype of W approximately the same as that of ––);
  3. –– does not contain W or its syntactic variants.
  For "elm" no clause meets all three: extensionally correct clauses without "elm" give a terrible translation. Even where the language has two exact synonyms, the clauses (the text layer's example pairs "heather" and "gorse") can be true without being a theory of meaning. Logicians do not impose (3). "Snow is white" is true iff snow is white is a logician's paradigm, but Tarski would not claim it gives the meaning of "snow is white" (pp. 181–183).
- **Possible interpretation (speculative, voice: Putnam):** Davidson may really think a serious theory of meaning is impossible and only translation functions exist. If so, it is "Quinine skepticism" (as the text layer reads) under cover of a positive contribution. Whole-sentence evidence only is vacuous if sentence-dispositions include answers about words. Otherwise it is "plainly false", because linguists rely on informants' testimony and native intuitions. The Quine–Davidson direction (sentences to words) is the opposite of the direction behind actual successes (p. 183).

### Critique of California semantics (pp. 183–188)
- Putnam's label for a Carnap-inspired approach; no particular philosopher is assigned this exact view (p. 184).
- **Definition.** Given possible worlds, an *intension* is a function $f$ on the space of worlds with $f(x)\subseteq$ entities in $x$. A term $T$ has meaning for speaker $X$ if $X$ associates $T$ with an intension $f_T$. $T$ is true of entity $e$ in world $x$ iff $e\in f(x)$. Carnap spoke of "grasping" intensions, meaning one grasps that $f$ is the intension of $T$. The reply "it describes an ideal language, actual terms have a (fuzzy) set of intensions" is noted (p. 184).
- **Difficulty 1:** "grasping" or "associating" is unexplained. Thinking of an intension as using a word-substitute (brain code, picture) is circular, since reference is defined via intension. Putnam compares Benacerraf's point that philosophies of mathematics explain either what objects are or how we learn about them, not both. Carnap's verificationism may have hidden this. For a non-verificationist the theory hardly counts as one (p. 185).
- **Difficulty 2:** if grasping is a narrow psychological state, the school is committed to (I) and (II). Two humans in the same total state would assign the same extension to every term, which is "totally wrong" for natural language. It ignores division of labour and the rigidity and indexicality of terms (p. 186).
- **Defense (vagueness):** natural language may be treated as having vague terms. Reply: an indexical word is not a vague family of non-indexical words. "I" is indexical but not vague. "Water" is synonymous with neither a description nor a fuzzy set of descriptions. A socially fixed extension is not a vaguely fixed individual one. If simple vagueness were the problem, my not distinguishing elm from beech would make elms borderline beeches (p. 186). "Cooperation isn't vagueness" (p. 187).
- **Defense (eliminate indexicality and division of labour):** replace "water" by "H₂O" and make everyone an expert. Putnam: this cannot be carried out, because "hydrogen" and "H₂O" are indexical like "water", and so is "yellow" and "normal". Even if division of labour could be removed in principle, why model human language on a language lacking a constitutive (conjectured universal) feature? (p. 187).
- Recent modification (Lewis): intensions take a world, a speaker and a context. "Water" has the same intension on both Earths but different extensions. Lewis retains (I) and gives up (II). Putnam does the reverse. He does not object to the formal models, only to the philosophy of language underlying earlier versions (p. 188).

### Semantic markers (pp. 188–190)
- Work for linguistics and psycholinguistics: what can appear in stereotypes, and how to represent them. Putnam adopts, with changes, the Katz–Fodor idea of semantic markers (p. 188).
- "Animal" in the tiger stereotype is qualitatively more central than "striped". It is hard (not impossible) to imagine tigers not being animals; one imagines robots controlled from Mars. Putnam says it is qualitatively harder to revise "all tigers are animals" than "all tigers have stripes" (which is not even true). He denies attributing absolute unrevisability (p. 189).
- Categories like "animal", "artifact", "day of the week" are central and also form widely used classification systems. They resemble syntactic markers ("noun"). Putnam proposes semantic markers be such high-centrality category indicators (p. 189).
- Katz and Fodor originally listed the rest as a "distinguisher" and wanted markers plus distinguisher to give a necessary and sufficient condition. Later Katz made all features analytically so. Putnam says the original distinction was sound if one drops the idea that the pair gives a necessary and sufficient condition and the idea of analyticity (pp. 189–190).

### The meaning of "meaning" (pp. 190–193)
- Putnam's proposal is not the only possible one. (I) and (II) are deeply embedded in meaning-talk and jointly inconsistent with the facts, so any reconstruction has some counter-intuitive consequences (p. 190).
- **Proposal:** define meaning not by an object but by a normal form for describing meaning: a finite sequence, a "vector", whose components include at least (p. 190):
  1. syntactic markers (e.g. "noun");
  2. semantic markers (e.g. "animal", "period of time");
  3. description of the further features of the stereotype;
  4. a description of the extension.
- All components except the extension are hypotheses about the individual speaker's competence. Putnam's table for "water": syntactic markers (mass noun, concrete); semantic markers (natural kind, liquid); stereotype (colorless, transparent, tasteless, thirst-quenching, etc.); extension: H₂O (give or take impurities) (p. 191).
- This does not impute to speakers the knowledge that water is H₂O. It means that (we say) the extension of their term is H₂O. The objection "who are we to say?" is an objection to truth, of which extension is a relative (p. 191).
- Two descriptions that differ only in their descriptions of the (same) extension are equivalent and both correct or both incorrect. The component is the set, not the description of it (p. 191).
- The Twin Earth entry differs only in the last column (XYZ). The same competence is ascribed to Earth and Twin Earth speakers, with different extensions (p. 191).
- (II) is kept "by construction". (I) is given up. The psychological state of the speaker does not determine what he means (pp. 191–192).
- **Paradox:** a German–English bilingual Oscar has "beech" and "Buche" as exact synonyms with identical normal-form descriptions, but he might not know they are synonyms. This fits the failure of (I). The inference "if Oscar understands synonyms $S_1$ and $S_2$, he knows they are synonyms" fails. Omitting the extension from the vector (Lewis's proposal as Putnam understands it) would give "elm" and "beech" the same meaning with different extensions (p. 192).
- Belief: Oscar may believe "this is a 'beech'" (there is a sign) without believing or disbelieving "this is a 'Buche'". The belief is held under one representation and not under another (p. 192).
- **Conclusion.** Putnam finds it amazing how long philosophers identified meaning with a necessary and sufficient condition or a method of verification. His own reflection began when he asked how "gold" could be a "battery of semantical rules" (footnote 25). He sees two root tendencies: treating cognition as purely individual, and ignoring the world beyond the individual's observations. Ignoring division of linguistic labour ignores the social dimension of cognition. Ignoring indexicality ignores the contribution of the environment. A better philosophy of language "must encompass both" other people and the world (p. 193).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Three liquid samples a, b, c are shown to two speakers. Samples a and c are H₂O and b is XYZ.
- Two speakers, Ann on Earth and Bo on Twin Earth, have the same stereotype: "colorless, drinkable liquid". All three samples satisfy it.
- If the stereotype alone fixed extension, both extensions would be {a, b, c}, with size 3.
- Putnam's rule (simplified): extension = samples bearing sameL to the local paradigm, here crudely read as "same chemical formula".
  - Ann's paradigm is H₂O, so her extension is {a, c} (size 2).
  - Bo's paradigm is XYZ, so his extension is {b} (size 1).
- The two meaning vectors then differ only in the last slot: (noun, natural kind, colorless/drinkable, {a, c}) vs (noun, natural kind, colorless/drinkable, {b}). Competence components are identical.
- Ann and Bo have the same stereotype but different extensions ({a, c} vs {b}). So no function from the shared stereotype (inner state) to extension can give both results: one input, two outputs. This is why (I) and (II) cannot both hold.

## Concepts
- [[methodological-solipsism]] (new) — the assumption that psychological states, properly so called, presuppose no individual other than the subject (p. 136).
- [[psychological-state-narrow-wide]] (new) — narrow = allowed by methodological solipsism; wide = e.g. being jealous of someone, which presupposes others exist (p. 137).
- [[twin-earth]] (new) — Putnam's thought experiment for same inner state with different extension (p. 139).
- [[sense (Sinn) vs. reference (Bedeutung)]] — here extension/intension, with intension = "concept", criticised as vague (pp. 132–134).
- [[semantic externalism]] — extension depends on society and environment, not only on the head (pp. 144–146, 164).
- [[division of linguistic labour]] — extension fixed by experts and structured cooperation across the community (p. 144).
- [[rigid designator]] — Putnam extends Kripke's notion to substance names such as "water" (p. 149).
- [[indexicality-of-natural-kind-terms]] (new) — "water" = stuff bearing sameL to the water around here (p. 152).
- [[same-L]] (new) — same liquid as: both liquids and agree in important physical properties; a cross-world theoretical relation (pp. 142, 149–150, 157).
- [[necessary a posteriori]] — metaphysically necessary and epistemically contingent (p. 151).
- [[stereotype]] (new) — the conventional (possibly inaccurate) idea of a kind that speakers must acquire; not analytic (pp. 147, 169–170).
- [[linguistic-obligation]] (new) — what makes a stereotype feature "part of the meaning": the duty to acquire it with the word (p. 171).
- [[semantic-marker]] (new) — high-centrality category indicator, such as "animal" (p. 189).
- [[meaning-vector]] (new) — normal form: syntactic markers, semantic markers, stereotype, extension (p. 190).
- [[one-criterion-word]] (new) — a word defined by a single criterion; Putnam doubts there are true cases outside stipulative contexts, though a few words retain an almost pure one-criterion character (pp. 163–164).
- [[california-semantics]] (new) — Putnam's label for the possible-worlds intension picture with "grasping" (p. 184).
- [[analytic / synthetic distinction]] — Putnam saves a limited class; denies it is identical with centrality (pp. 173–176).
- [[indeterminacy of translation]] — radical translation: stereotype matching adds constraints but does not alone solve it (pp. 177–179).
- [[causal-historical theory of reference]] — Kripke's causal theory, said to belong to sociolinguistics (pp. 165–166).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] division of linguistic labour ↔ social science: division of epistemic labour and trust in experts — a community-level system fixes what individuals only partly know.
- [structural-analogy] reference passed along a chain of speakers (causal theory) ↔ network science: diffusion along ties with no fixed description attached.
- [same-math] extension as a fuzzy set (p. 133) ↔ mathematics: fuzzy-set membership degrees between 0 and 1.
- [metaphor] meaning vector with extension as one slot ↔ computational linguistics: feature vectors for words — only the idea of a structured tuple is shared.
- [structural-analogy] failure of "inner state fixes reference" ↔ philosophy of mind: wide vs narrow content.

## Questions this raises
- If speakers acquire only a stereotype, what exactly counts as "passing muster", and who decides it in a community (pp. 167–169)?
- How are "important physical properties" chosen, given that importance is interest-relative (p. 157)?
- Does the meaning vector keep "meaning" a useful notion if the extension is included "by construction" (pp. 190–192)?
- What further constraints (beyond language) on imputing beliefs would make radical translation determinate (p. 179)?
