# Putnam, H. (1975). The Meaning of "Meaning". In K. Gunderson (ed.), *Language, Mind, and Knowledge*, Minnesota Studies in the Philosophy of Science, Vol. 7 (pp. 131–193). Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press.

## One digest
This work has a single digest, which is also its work-level summary: [[putnam-1975-meaning/main]].

## In one line
The traditional picture of meaning needs two assumptions that cannot both be true (knowing a meaning is a state of the speaker's head, and meaning fixes extension, i.e. the set of things a word applies to), because extension depends on society and on the real nature of the stuff around us.

## The gist
Putnam asks why semantics is in worse shape than syntax. He argues that the traditional picture of meaning rests on two assumptions: knowing a meaning is a psychological state (in the narrow sense), and meaning determines extension (the set of things a word applies to). They cannot both be true. Thought experiments such as Twin Earth (an imagined planet whose "water" is XYZ) show that extension is fixed partly by society (division of linguistic labour) and partly by the actual nature of the samples (indexicality, rigidity). He proposes to describe meaning as a vector: syntactic markers, semantic markers, stereotype, extension. He also criticises Davidson's truth-definition programme and "California semantics".

**Prerequisites:** extension/intension (Frege–Carnap); possible worlds; Quine's critique of analyticity (as summarised here); basic set theory; the idea of a natural-kind term (a word for a kind such as water or tiger).
