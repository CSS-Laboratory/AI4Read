# The "By-Product" and "Special Interest" Theories

## L0 — One line
Large economic pressure groups survive because their lobbying is a by-product of coercion or private benefits offered for other reasons, while small business groups organize voluntarily, and large groups lacking such a source stay unorganized.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
If members of large groups have no incentive to fund a lobby, why do unions, farmers and doctors have lobbies? Olson's answer: such a lobby is a by-product of an organization that can coerce members or sell them private (selective) benefits. Individual industries gain political power differently: they contain few firms, so they can largely organize voluntarily ("special interests"). Business as a whole is a large (latent) group like the others and is not specially powerful. The chapter tests this against unions, professions, business, the Farm Bureau and other farm groups. It then treats noneconomic lobbies and parties, and ends with large groups that never organized (consumers, taxpayers, migrant workers).

**Prerequisites:** Olson's earlier chapters: collective (public) goods, free riding, selective incentives, and the split into privileged, intermediate and latent groups; basic market ideas (monopoly, oligopoly, cooperative, patronage dividend).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- A latent (large) group is mobilized only if some organization can coerce members or offer them a private benefit; the lobby is then a by-product (pp. 133–134).
- Unions got stable membership through collective bargaining and the union shop (compulsory membership); their political power came later and is a by-product of that base (pp. 136–137).
- Professional associations rely partly on subtle coercion (guild-like control) and partly on private services such as malpractice defense, journals and conventions (pp. 137–140).
- Business lobbies are numerous largely because each industry has few firms, often no more than a privileged group and seldom more than an intermediate one, so it can organize voluntarily. This is the "special interest" theory (pp. 143–145).
- Business as a whole is a large latent group; the Chamber of Commerce and the National Association of Manufacturers (NAM) lean on a few big firms and are not disproportionately powerful (pp. 145–148).
- The Farm Bureau's size came from county-agent services and from business affiliates that restrict benefits to members; Olson finds almost no evidence that lobbying accounts for much of its membership (pp. 149–157).
- The theory is not at all sufficient for philanthropic and religious lobbies, and groups working for lost causes run counter to it (pp. 160–161). One explanation Olson gives for weak parties is that they seek collective goods, while machines are strong because they offer individual rewards (pp. 163–165).
- Large unorganized groups fit the theory best and are the ones that lose if it is true (pp. 165–167).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A. The "by-product" theory of large pressure groups (pp. 132–135)
- **The puzzle.** Some large groups (consumers, white-collar workers, migrant farm workers) are unorganized. This seems to contradict the pluralist "group theory". Other large groups (union laborers, farmers, doctors) are organized to some degree. This seems to contradict Olson's theory of latent groups (p. 132).
- **Common feature.** All large economic groups with significant lobbies are also organized for some other purpose. Their lobbies are by-products of organizations that can "mobilize" a latent group with selective incentives (pp. 132–133).
- **Only two sources of selective incentives:** the authority and capacity to coerce, or a source of positive inducements (p. 133).
  - A purely political organization cannot legally coerce members. Compulsory membership there would be unusual in a democracy (p. 133).
  - If an organization has a non-political reason for compulsory membership, or gained that power through another function, it may be able to get the resources to support a lobby from that base (p. 133).
  - An organization that only lobbies has no source of positive incentives. Only one that also sells private goods or offers social or recreational benefits has one (pp. 133–134).
  - Only such an organization can make a "tied sale" of a collective and a noncollective good (p. 134).
- Notes on the mechanism:
  - Spare returns exist because markets with some monopoly power are common, and because economic and political branches can complement each other (n. 2, p. 133).
  - The private benefit's worth must exceed its cost by more than the dues to the lobbying branch. Otherwise the joint offer would not be sufficient to attract members (n. 3, p. 134).
  - An organization may also lobby for noncollective "political" goods, such as individual exceptions to rules or patronage, and use those as selective incentives (n. 4, p. 134).
- **Scope.** The theory is needed only for large (latent) groups. Privileged and intermediate groups can often provide a lobby without selective incentives (p. 134).
- **Why a latent-group member joins.** He cannot be decisive, and he will enjoy the good if others obtain it. So he supports the lobby only if (1) he is coerced into paying dues or (2) he must join to get another, noncollective benefit. Only if one or both hold will latent political power be mobilized (p. 134).
- **Plan.** The chapter argues that unions, farm organizations and professional groups are explained this way, and that business associations are explained by the "special interest" theory (p. 135).

*Intuition note.* A lobby sells something nobody can be excluded from. If it is bundled with something members cannot get otherwise, such as services, joining can become worthwhile, provided the private benefit's surplus exceeds the dues (n. 3, p. 134). The lobby then rides along on the bundle.

### B. Labor lobbies (pp. 135–137)
- Evidence of union political weight: AFL-CIO membership several times that of any other lobby; 60,000–70,000 locals; leaders claimed influence over about 25 million voters; political spending in the millions; about 200 unionists among 1952 Democratic convention delegates or alternates (pp. 135–136). Olson says Slichter's claim that the economy is "laboristic" may have been mistaken, but if so only because business, professional and farm organizations unite in opposing what they see as labor's excessive claims (p. 136).
- **Argument that union political power is a by-product** (p. 136):
  - Unions gained stability and power only after they concentrated on collective bargaining and abandoned mainly political aims (Chapter III).
  - They began to prosper only when they dealt with employers, who alone had the power to force workers to join.
  - It is hard to see how purely political unions could have won the union shop in a democracy.
  - Unions became important in politics long after they forsook political action as a major goal. The Wagner Act, which eased organizing a union with compulsory membership and led to the largest membership rise, came before that.
- **Britain.** A democratic nation is often willing to overlook compulsory membership in bodies that bargain collectively but hesitates over automatic membership in a political body. This shows in the "contract in" versus "contract out" quarrel over contributions to the Labour party. Most Labour party members belong through trade unions (pp. 136–137).
- **Conclusion:** if a democracy would not normally want to make membership in a purely political union compulsory, and compulsion is essential to a stable labor movement of any size, then union political power is a by-product of non-political activities (p. 137).

### C. Professional lobbies (pp. 137–141)
- Professional associations show a pervasive tendency toward compulsion, toward a guild form (Delancey, Key and Grant are cited). Compulsory membership is, per Grant, "the first rule" of the guild system. Olson takes this as evidence for the by-product theory (pp. 137–138).
- **Bar associations.** Many state legislatures require every practicing lawyer to belong to the state bar association. Olson says these are government-enforced closed shops (p. 138).
- **Miniature governments.** Associations are given powers to govern members and discipline them for breaching "ethical" standards. Even without legal compulsion, a practitioner has an interest in good standing (p. 138).
- **AMA, coercion.** Garceau (cited) says a doctor at odds with organized medicine may face a real economic threat. A doctor's critical letter to a congressional committee was withheld by name, and a Denver councilman said the AMA's closed shop beat the AFL and CIO ("pikers") (pp. 138–139).
- **AMA, private benefits.** Olson judges these probably matter more than coercion (p. 139).
  - *Malpractice defense:* Garceau calls it "a prime requisite to private practice". Kessel (n. 24) describes society members testifying for defendants, and higher malpractice insurance costs, or difficulty getting cover, for non-members. He also notes hospital-staff access tied to society membership via the Mundt Resolution (pp. 139–140).
  - *Journals and conventions:* a Michigan survey found 89% of doctors received the AMA Journal, 70% read a state society journal, and under 30% read any other medical literature. Conventions give a "direct return in education" (p. 140).
- Such benefits can be withheld from non-members, unlike political achievements, so they give an incentive to join. AMA power is therefore a by-product of the non-political activities of organized medicine (p. 140).
- **Why not professors?** Olson says the probably most important factor is that the learned societies are independent of the AAUP. If the AAUP could usurp their functions, it could rival the AMA; if journal subscriptions and convention attendance were restricted to AAUP members, professors would probably be as well organized and powerful as doctors. If the AAUP published as many technical journals as the AMA, almost every faculty member would have an incentive to join (p. 141). It had 66,645 members on 1 Jan 1965 (n. 31, p. 141).

*Intuition note.* Doctors join partly because staying out is costly and partly because the association provides services they need. Professors have a political body but not the scientific services. That difference is what Olson (citing Lurie, n. 30) gives as probably the most important factor behind the gap in power.

### D. The "special interest" theory and business lobbies (pp. 141–148)
- **Evidence of many business lobbies.** Excluding Indian tribes, 825 of 1,247 organizations in the Lobby Index represented business. In the *Encyclopedia of Associations*, trade and chamber-of-commerce listings take over ten times as many pages as social welfare ones (p. 141). Schattschneider is quoted on how thin other groups' membership is: 6% of drivers in the AAA, about 15% of veterans in the American Legion (p. 142). Key is quoted on being puzzled that a small minority wields "enormous power" (p. 142).
- **Olson's critique.** Vague generalizations, such as Beard's paraphrase of Webster that political power naturally and necessarily goes to those who hold the property, assert rather than explain. Olson asks why this should be so in a majority-rule democracy (p. 143).
- **Special-interest theory.** Olson says the high degree of organization and power of business must be due in large part to the fact that business is divided into (generally oligopolistic) industries, each with only a fairly small number of firms. The number is often no more than a privileged group, and seldom more than an intermediate one. So industries can usually organize voluntarily for an active lobby (p. 143).
  - Labor, professional and agricultural interests are large latent groups. They can organize and act effectively only when some organization supplies political power as a by-product. Business interests generally organize without such help (p. 143).
  - *Single large firm* (n. 42): it is a "group of one", like a monopolist. For unique legislative interests it will act, and Washington is said to host lawyers and former officials who help individual businesses for a fee, so a market has grown up. Collective goods are not involved in this sphere (pp. 143–144).
- **Concern about "special interests".** Sometimes the "special interests versus the people" contrast is only rhetoric. It may also reflect that small organized groups tend to win over large unorganized ones, for example a tariff or tax loophole at the expense of millions. This is what the privileged/intermediate versus latent distinction leads one to expect (p. 144).
- **Trade associations are small** (p. 144, quoting Schattschneider, with the metal products figures):
  - Of 421 metal products associations, 153 had under 20 members.
  - The median membership was between 24 and 50.
  - In lumber, furniture and paper, 37.3% had under 20 members and the median was 25–50.
  - Schattschneider concludes: "Pressure politics … is essentially the politics of small groups" (p. 145).
  - Key: in almost half of them, nearly 50% of the cost is borne by a handful of members (p. 145).
- **Extra strength.** Many associations also provide statistics, credit references, bill collection and technical advice. Fainsod and Gordon list seventeen such functions (p. 145).
- **Business as a whole is not especially powerful.** Particular industries have disproportionate power on questions of special importance to themselves. The business community as a whole is a large latent group with the same organizing problems as others (pp. 145–146).
  - *Chamber of Commerce of the U.S.:* a "federation of federations" of local chambers. Local chambers are small and attract members through contacts and information. Strength is lost in federation, and local units are individual units in a latent group. The Chamber sells special individual memberships to large businesses, so it has some power but not disproportionate power (p. 146).
  - *NAM:* nominally a few thousand members, but about 5% contribute about half the money, and about 0.8% of members held 63% of directorships (McKean and Cleveland, cited). It spent as much as $5.5 million a year politically, yet its support is sometimes seen as the "kiss of death" (p. 147).
- **Conclusion.** A small group is powerful on matters of one industry, where it is normally the only organized force. It is weaker on national questions, where it faces organized labor and others (p. 147). Olson says this is apparently consistent with the trend: particular interests seem to win loopholes and tariffs, but business as a whole failed to stop social-welfare legislation and progressive taxation (pp. 147–148).

*Intuition note.* Business power is local to each industry. Where few firms share a benefit, each firm's share is large enough to pay for lobbying. When everyone's interests are at stake, business is as latent as any other large group.

### E. Government promotion of political pressure (pp. 148–153)
- **Farm organization has been scarce and unstable** (pp. 148–149).
  - No lasting farm lobby existed until after the Civil War. The Grange (1867) grew fast, was insignificant by the 1880s, and survives as largely a social body (p. 148).
  - Many others (Farmers' Alliances, Greenbackers, Populism and others) died within a few years (pp. 148–149).
  - The Farmers Union (Texas, 1902) lost its southern base after WWI. It built a base in the Missouri Valley states in the late 1930s and 1940s (p. 149).
- **The Farm Bureau was created by government.** The Smith-Lever Act (1914) shared with the states the cost of county agents. Many states made funding conditional on a local farmers' association, which became the "Farm Bureaus". County bureaus formed a state federation, and the American Farm Bureau Federation (AFBF) was founded in 1919 (pp. 149–150).
- **Selective benefits.** Members got technical mailings and first call on the agent. Non-members got last call or none. Dues were an investment (p. 150).
- **Membership path.** It was 466,000 in 1921 and fell steadily to 163,000 in 1933 (p. 150).
  - Olson's explanation is "almost certainly" that new functions (lobbying, cooperatives with agents' help) provoked criticism of a publicly subsidized pressure group. That led to the "True-Howard" agreement limiting the agent's work for the Bureau (p. 151).
  - "Presumably" the reduced agent help weakened the incentive to join (p. 151).
- **1933 turn.** Under the Agricultural Adjustment Act the agents administered the programs. It became expedient to join their organization, and membership rose moderately but did not regain 1921 levels (p. 152).
- **Later loss.** A separate federal administrative system with farmer-elected committeemen weakened the agent and the Bureau. It also became another farm organization that often opposed the Bureau (pp. 152–153).

### F. Farm cooperatives and farm lobbies (pp. 153–159)
- **Illinois.** The Illinois Agricultural Association (IAA) had a tenth fewer members than Iowa's Bureau in 1925, twice as many in 1933, and a bigger lead in 1938 (p. 153).
- **Kirkpatrick-type cooperatives** differ from Rochdale-type ones in two ways (pp. 153–155):
  - Their voting stock is held by the political (lobbying) organization, not by patrons. Business purposes are subordinate to the political arm (p. 154).
  - Benefits are restricted to members. Some of the enterprises, mainly the mutual casualty insurance companies, may sell only to members or prospective members. Marketing and supply co-ops normally do business with anyone but generally pay patronage dividends only to IAA members (pp. 154–155).
  - Staying out can cost far more than the dues, and dues often come indirectly from business earnings. Olson says this arrangement does not exist primarily for business reasons, and quotes IAA publications that say as much (p. 155).
- **Results.** The IAA came to include almost every farmer in the state. The claim that one can hardly farm in Illinois without a Farm Bureau business is described by Olson as no doubt an exaggeration. By 1951 the Illinois Farm Supply Company had paid over $41.5 million in patronage dividends, and Country Mutual had 337,000 policies (pp. 155–156).
- **Spread.** Other state Bureaus copied the model. State Farm and Nationwide both began selling insurance to farmers in affiliation with the Bureau (p. 156).
- **Membership path** (p. 156):

| Year | AFBF members |
|---|---|
| 1933 | 163,000 |
| 1940 | 444,000 |
| 1944 | 828,000 |
| 1947 | 1,275,000 |
| since 1953 | over 1,500,000 |

- **Footnote 86** (p. 156): most unions have been unable to provide, through business activity, enough noncollective benefits to keep their membership, unlike farm groups. Olson offers, "presumably", farmers' special needs for marketing and supplies and their experience running businesses.
- **Conclusion on the Farm Bureau.** Size and stability came from (1) its role as channel for government technical aid and (2) its business institutions. There is "almost no evidence" that lobbying accounts for much membership. Membership fluctuations clearly cannot be explained by changes in its legislative policies or their popularity; the Bureau seems to have grown very rapidly when, if polls and elections can be believed, its policies were least popular. Its lobbying strength seems to have been a by-product of the county agents and the business organizations (p. 157).
- **Farmers Union.** Its insurance companies deal mainly with members. Cooperatives "check off" dues from patronage dividends and pay 5% of earnings to an "Educational Fund" used for lobbying and organizing (pp. 157–158).
- **Grange.** Business affiliates also give incentives to join (p. 158).
- **National Farmers Organization (NFO).** It tried to avoid business or government channels and had trouble getting members (pp. 158–159). Its "holding actions" failed, as the theory would predict. If the NFO later maintained prices by getting farmers to withhold output, without violence or other selective incentives, that "would tend to refute" the theory (p. 159).

*Intuition note.* Farm Bureau dues often come in effect out of the money farmers save or earn by trading with Bureau businesses. The lobby comes free with the bundle. If the lobby were the reason to join, membership should follow policy popularity, and Olson argues it did not (p. 157).

### G. "Noneconomic" lobbies (pp. 159–165)
- **Summary so far.** The by-product theory seems to explain the agricultural, labor and professional lobbies, and with the special-interest theory the business lobbies; the theories appear to account for the main economic pressure groups (p. 159).
- **Scope.** The theory is logically general. It applies to rational individuals with a common goal, and for large groups it need not assume self-interest or purely material interests (Chapter II). It is more useful in some cases than others (p. 159).
- **Veterans' groups** (n. 90): clubs and social life are in general open only to members and their guests, and Legion group insurance goes to members. The bonus won by lobbying goes to every veteran. So their political power is a by-product of social and economic services (pp. 159–160).
- **Where it is not sufficient:** philanthropic and religious lobbies. There the link between member purposes and organization purposes may be so rich and obscure that this theory cannot give much insight (p. 160).
  - If all action is defined as rational (including charity, since the giver gets a noncollective feeling of moral worth), the theory becomes correct by consistency and can no longer be refuted empirically (n. 91, p. 160).
  - A religious lobby can logically be explained as a by-product of a church's selective incentives (for example the Anti-Saloon League). Olson calls this "logically correct" but not very helpful (n. 92, pp. 160–161).
- **Lost causes** run counter to the theory, though their insignificance is consistent with it (p. 161). Olson suggests, with hedges, that economic groups are probably more rational than noneconomic ones, so the theory fits them better (n. 93, p. 161).
- **Mass movements.** For nonrational or irrational behavior, psychology may serve better, for example "alienation" accounts of mass movements (pp. 161–162). Footnote 94 adds that an infinite or incalculably large benefit (a utopia) could make even a large group's members rational contributors, but he says it is not clear that this is the best way to theorize about them (pp. 161–162).
- **Political parties** (pp. 162–164).
  - American parties are weak as formal organizations. Few members attend precinct meetings or pay, and staffs are small. The Democrats had no national headquarters 1924–28 (pp. 162–163).
  - A "paradox": that any one of a vast number of pressure groups (well over 500 organizations had permanent Washington lobbies in the late 1920s) should amount to more as a formal organization than either great party (p. 163).
  - *One explanation Olson gives:* parties usually seek collective benefits. A person's party will win with or without him, so he will not sacrifice for it. Dr. Johnson's remark on the exiled Stuarts is cited: Johnson said people would vote the Stuarts back if a vote could do it, but would not give twenty shillings to bring it about (pp. 163–164).
  - People with personal ambitions get office as a noncollective benefit (700,000 elected officials), and some businessmen donate for individual access (p. 164).
  - *Machines* have big organizations because they offer patronage or graft to individuals, not collective goods (pp. 164–165).
- **Footnote 102 (voting and the threshold)** (pp. 164–165):
  - Voting costs, like a competitive firm's effect on price, fall below a "threshold" and go unnoticed. The analogy is a vise on a hand: light pressure causes no reaction.
  - If the group theory (Bentley–Truman) left out lobbies and considered only voting, it "could be correct".

### H. The "forgotten groups" (pp. 165–167)
- **Groups with no lobby:** migrant farm laborers, white-collar workers, taxpayers, consumers, those with an interest in peace, and those wanting to prevent inflation and depression (pp. 165–166).
- **Argument.**
  - The gains from group action exceeding the costs does not make organization likely (p. 166).
  - Inflation example: everyone could stop inflation by spending less, but virtually no one would expect individuals to do so voluntarily.
  - The rational individual does not curtail spending because his own effort would have no noticeable effect and he gets the benefit of others' price stability anyway.
  - For the same two reasons, a rational member of a large group in a socio-political setting will not make sacrifices for shared objectives.
  - So there is no presumption that large groups will organize; only small groups, or those with an independent source of selective incentives, will (pp. 166–167).
- **Last point.** Large unorganized groups are consistent with the book's argument, and Olson adds that they also suffer if it is true (p. 167).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A latent group of 1,000 farmers. A lobby costs $30,000 to run. Each farmer would pay dues of $30 if all paid, so $30 × 1,000 = $30,000. The lobby's gains go to every farmer whether or not he joined.

- **Lobby alone.** If a farmer joins he pays $30 and gains nothing extra, because one member cannot change the result. If he stays out he pays $0 and still gets the gains. So he does not join: −30 versus 0.
- **Tied sale.** The association also sells farm insurance, available to members only. The insurance is worth $100 to the farmer and costs a $60 price, a surplus of 100 − 60 = $40. This exceeds the $30 dues, so the benefit's surplus is greater than the dues (the condition in n. 3, p. 134).
  - Join: 100 − 60 − 30 = +$10.
  - Stay out: $0 (no insurance, no dues).
  - He joins. At 1,000 members, dues give 1,000 × 30 = $30,000, so the lobby is funded as a by-product.
- **Check on the condition.** If the insurance were worth only $80, the surplus would be 80 − 60 = $20 < $30. Joining gives 80 − 60 − 30 = −$10, so he stays out and the lobby is not funded.

## Concepts
- [[selective incentive]] — a reward or penalty available only to those who join, such as coercion, services or dividends (pp. 133–134)
- [[free rider]] — a member of a latent group who enjoys the collective good without bearing a share of its cost (p. 134)
- [[collective action problem]] — large groups have no tendency to act voluntarily for common interests, even when gains exceed costs (pp. 165–167)
- [[public good]] — in this chapter "collective good", such as lobbying gains that go to all group members; contrasted with noncollective goods (pp. 133–134)
- [[by-product-theory]] (new) — a large group's lobby is funded as a by-product of an organization that coerces members or sells them private benefits (pp. 133–134)
- [[latent-group]] (new) — a large group in which no member has an incentive to contribute voluntarily; here contrasted with privileged and intermediate groups (p. 134)
- [[special-interest-theory]] (new) — industries with few firms form privileged or intermediate groups that organize voluntarily, which explains business lobbies (p. 143)
- [[tied-sale]] (new) — joint offer of a collective good and a private good; a way for an organization to induce a latent-group member to pay (p. 134)
- [[kirkpatrick-cooperative]] (new) — a cooperative whose voting stock is held by a lobbying organization and whose benefits go only to its members (pp. 153–155)
- [[cost-threshold]] (new) — costs or benefits below a level go unnoticed and do not affect action; used for voting and for a firm's effect on price (n. 102, p. 164)

## Canonical instances
- [[american-medical-association]] — selective incentives (malpractice defense, journals) plus subtle coercion (pp. 138–140)
- [[labor-unions-union-shop]] — political strength follows industrial bargaining and compulsory membership (p. 136)
- [[trade-associations]] — small groups, with services as an added incentive (pp. 144–145)
- [[american-farm-bureau]] — membership driven by county-agent services and business affiliates, not lobbying (pp. 149–157)
- [[illinois-agricultural-association]] — the Kirkpatrick model of cooperatives controlled by the lobby (pp. 153–156)
- [[national-farmers-organization-strikes]] — failed holding actions by a group that tried to avoid business and government channels (pp. 158–159)
- [[political-machines]] — strong organization from individual benefits (patronage) (pp. 164–165)
- [[inflation-spending-example]] — rational individuals would not voluntarily curtail spending to halt inflation, however much they would gain as a group (p. 166)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] by-product theory ↔ social capital / civic association: groups built for other reasons later serve collective action — the by-product idea resembles the idea that civic networks lower the cost of cooperation.
- [structural-analogy] Kirkpatrick co-op restriction of benefits to members ↔ design principles (Ostrom): graduated sanctions and exclusion rules — both tie access to a shared resource or good to membership and compliance.
- [structural-analogy] "Imperceptible" individual effect (firm on price, one vote, one inflation-fighting consumer) ↔ paradox of voting: tiny decisiveness gives little incentive to bear costs. Olson uses the cost threshold (n. 102, p. 164) to explain why people do vote, since voting costs fall below it, so the resemblance is partial.
- [structural-analogy] Special-interest theory (concentrated benefits to few, diffuse costs to many) ↔ agenda control and veto players: organized minorities win despite majority rule — a rough resemblance only.

## Questions this raises
- Olson says coercion is subtle in the AMA but probably less important than services. How could one measure the relative weight of each?
- If lobbying is a by-product, do members' views matter? Note 2 suggests leaders may deviate from members' wishes and still keep their membership. What follows for representation?
- The theory is hard to refute if all action counts as rational (n. 91). What observations would count against it? The NFO test is one case.
- Does the by-product logic transfer to non-U.S. settings, and to organizations that exist today?
