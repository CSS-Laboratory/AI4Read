# Introduction

## L0 — One line
Olson claims that rational, self-interested individuals in a large group will not voluntarily act for their shared group interest unless there is coercion or a separate individual incentive, so the common view that groups tend to pursue their interests is unjustified, at least when it rests on the assumption that groups act in their self-interest because individuals do.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Many social scientists assume that groups with a common interest will act to advance it, presumably because individuals act in their self-interest. Olson argues that this does not follow logically. Unless a group is quite small, or there is coercion (force) or some other special device, rational self-interested individuals will not voluntarily work for the group interest, even if all agree on the goal. Large groups need coercion or separate incentives given to individuals. Small groups behave differently: they may act voluntarily, but usually below the group's optimal level. The Introduction previews chapters applying the argument to unions, Marx's class theory, "group theory" and pressure groups. This matters because it challenges a widely shared assumption in the social sciences.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of rational self-interest (individuals compare their own costs and benefits); the idea of a goal that benefits a whole group.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Common view: groups with common interests tend to act on them. It appears in labor-union theory, Marxian class theory, "countervailing power", the pressure-group "group theory" in political science, and sociology (p. 1).
- This view is presumably based on the premise that individuals are rational and self-interested; people suppose it follows logically from that premise (p. 1).
- Olson denies that it follows. Unless the group is quite small, or there is coercion or a special device, rational self-interested individuals will not act for the group interest (p. 2).
- The result holds even with unanimous agreement about the common good and the means to reach it (p. 2).
- Large groups act or organize only with coercion or a separate incentive offered to individuals on condition that they bear costs (p. 2).
- Groups of altruistic or irrational individuals may logically act for the group interest. Olson says later empirical parts will attempt to show this is usually of no practical importance (p. 2).
- Small groups are different: some voluntary action occurs, but usually stops short of the group's optimum, and the small tend to "exploit" the great (p. 3).
- Chapters I–VI move from proof to size, unions, Marx and the state, group theory, and a new theory of pressure groups (p. 3).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### The common view (p. 1)
- Olson says it is often taken for granted, at least for economic objectives, that groups with common interests usually try to further them, much as individuals pursue personal interests (p. 1).
- He says the view appears in popular and scholarly writing: many economists, theories of labor unions, Marxian class action, "countervailing power", US political-science "group theory" of pressure groups, and sociological studies (p. 1).
- The view presumably rests on the assumption of self-interested individuals. Altruism is thought exceptional, and no one is surprised when businessmen seek profits, workers seek wages, or consumers seek lower prices (p. 1).
- The supposed logic: if members share an objective and would all be better off if it were achieved, then rational, self-interested members would act to achieve it (p. 1).

### Olson's thesis (p. 2)
- **Claim:** this does not follow. All members gaining from a group objective does not mean they will act for it, even if all are rational and self-interested (p. 2).
- **Condition:** unless the group is quite small, or unless coercion or another special device makes individuals act in their common interest, rational, self-interested individuals will not act to achieve it (p. 2).
- Olson says the notion that groups act for their common interests is inconsistent with the assumption of individual rationality, not implied by it. The next chapter explains why (p. 2).
- **Large groups:** if members maximize personal welfare, they will not advance group objectives unless (a) coercion forces them, or (b) a separate incentive, distinct from the group goal, is offered to each member on condition that he helps bear the costs. Without (a) or (b), such groups will not form organizations either (p. 2). The result holds even with unanimous agreement on the good and the methods (p. 2).
- Hence the widespread social-science view is unjustified when it rests on the assumption that groups act in self-interest because individuals do (p. 2).
- **Qualification:** there is a logical possibility that groups of altruistic or irrational individuals may sometimes act for group interests. Olson says later empirical parts will attempt to show it is usually of no practical importance. So the customary view appears to have little if any merit (p. 2).

*Intuition note:* All members gaining from a shared goal does not by itself mean that rational, self-interested members will act for it, even when everyone agrees on the goal (p. 2). The Introduction does not give the reason; it says the explanation comes in the following chapter (p. 2) and that the proofs are in Chapter I (p. 3).

### Small groups (p. 3)
- None of the above fully applies to small groups, where the situation is more complicated (p. 3).
- In small groups there may be some voluntary action for common purposes, but in most cases it ceases before reaching the optimal level for the group as a whole (p. 3).
- When costs are shared, there is a "surprising tendency for the 'exploitation' of the great by the small" (p. 3).

### Plan of the book (p. 3)
- **Ch. I:** proofs of all the logical statements above, a theoretical explanation of aspects of group and organizational behavior.
- **Ch. II:** implications for groups of different size. Illustrates that in many cases small groups are more efficient and viable than large ones.
- **Ch. III:** labor unions. Concludes that some form of compulsory membership is, in most circumstances, indispensable to union survival.
- **Ch. IV:** Marx's theory of social classes and some other economists' theories of the state.
- **Ch. V:** political scientists' "group theory". Olson argues it, as usually understood, is logically inconsistent.
- **Ch. VI:** a new theory of pressure groups. It is consistent with Chapter I and suggests that the membership and power of large pressure-group organizations come not from lobbying achievements but as a by-product of their other activities (p. 3).

### Method and audience (p. 3)
- Olson is an economist using economic tools, but says the conclusions matter to sociologists and political scientists. He avoids diagrammatic-mathematical language where feasible. He says many noneconomists will find one or two brief parts of the first chapter obscure and uncongenial, but that all the rest of the book should be clear whatever the reader's discipline (p. 3).

## Toy example
*(Ours, not from the text. Simplifying assumptions: linear benefits, equal shares.)*
A group of 100 people wants lobbying. Each unit of lobbying costs the payer 1 and produces total benefit 3, shared equally by all 100 members.
- One member paying 1 unit gets $3/100 = 0.03$ back. Net: $0.03 - 1 = -0.97$. A self-interested member does not pay.
- This holds for every member, whatever the others do. So nobody pays.
- If all 100 paid: each pays 1 and receives $100 \times 3/100 = 3$. Net: $3 - 1 = +2$ each.
Everyone prefers "all pay", yet no one pays voluntarily. A separate incentive could change this. For example, a private payment of 1.5 to each payer gives net $-0.97 + 1.5 = +0.53$, so paying becomes worthwhile.

## Concepts
- [[collective action problem]] — Rational self-interested members of a large group will not voluntarily act for the shared interest, even if all would gain (p. 2).
- [[selective incentive]] — Olson's "separate incentive", given to individuals on condition that they bear costs; for large groups, either it or coercion is needed before they act (p. 2).
- [[group-theory-of-politics]] (new) — Political-science view that groups act when necessary to further group goals; Olson says that, as usually understood, it is logically inconsistent (pp. 1, 3).
- [[group-size-effect]] (new) — Small groups may act voluntarily but usually below the group optimum, while large groups need coercion or separate incentives (pp. 2–3).
- [[rational-self-interest]] (new) — Individuals pursue their own welfare; the premise used both for the common view and for Olson's rejection of it (p. 1).

## Canonical instances
- [[labor-union]] — Olson concludes that some form of compulsory membership is, in most circumstances, indispensable to union survival (p. 3).
- [[pressure-group]] — Olson's new theory suggests that the membership and power of large ones are a by-product of other activities rather than of lobbying achievements (p. 3).
- [[marxian-class-action]] — Marxian theories of class action are named among those in which the common view has been important (p. 1); Marx's theory of social classes is examined in Ch. IV (p. 3).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Olson's large-group result ↔ game theory: not contributing may be each member's best choice even though all would gain if all contributed — a similar incentive structure, but the unit does not state a game.
- [metaphor] "By-product" theory of pressure-group membership ↔ organization theory: membership may follow from other activities rather than from the group's lobbying (the unit states only the by-product claim).

## Questions this raises
