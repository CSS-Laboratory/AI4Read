# A Theory of Groups and Organizations (part 1: sections A–C)

## L0 — One line
Organizations exist mainly to provide goods that benefit a whole group, and because such goods cannot feasibly be withheld from non-payers, rational self-interested members of a large group have no individual reason to pay for them voluntarily.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Olson asks what organizations are for and whether large groups will provide for their common interests. He argues that organizations mainly serve common interests, which makes their goods "public" or collective: once provided, no member can feasibly be excluded. Using the analogy of firms in a perfectly competitive market, he argues that each member of a large group gains little from his own contribution and gets the benefit anyway, so he has no reason to pay. The state needs taxes for the same reason. The traditional theory of groups, which assumes small and large groups differ only in degree, is questioned by empirical evidence, and Olson turns to group size.

**Prerequisites:** supply and demand, marginal cost, price in perfect competition; the idea of a "good" that people consume.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Organizations typically exist to advance the interests of their members, and above all their *common* interests. Purely personal interests are usually served best by individual action (pp. 5–7).
- Olson uses "group" for a number of individuals with a common interest. Members also keep separate individual interests (p. 8).
- In a competitive industry, firms share an interest in a higher price but have opposed interests over output. Profit-maximizing firms can therefore act against their joint interest (pp. 9–10).
- A lobby for the whole industry faces the same problem. It would be irrational for an individual producer to bear its costs, even if he is convinced the programme helps him (p. 11).
- Emotional or ideological motives do not seem to remove the problem: even states, with patriotism behind them, rely on compulsory taxes, so large private organizations might also have difficulty getting voluntary contributions (pp. 12–13).
- A public good is one that cannot feasibly be withheld from others in the group once anyone consumes it. Any common goal is therefore a public good for its group (pp. 14–15).
- Large organizations cannot support themselves without some sanction or attraction separate from the public good itself, to lead members to bear the costs (pp. 15–16).
- Traditional group theory assumes that participation in voluntary associations is virtually universal and that small and large groups differ in degree, not in kind. Olson questions this and asks how group size affects incentives (pp. 17–21).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A. The purpose of organization (pp. 5–8)
- Most, though by no means all, group action is taken through organizations, so Olson starts from their purpose (p. 5).
- One purpose is characteristic of most organizations, and certainly of nearly all those with an important economic aspect: furthering the interests of their members (p. 5).
- Some organizations may fail to do so out of ignorance, or be captured by their leaders. But organizations often perish if they do nothing for members, and this "must severely limit" the number that fail (pp. 5–6).
- He cites Aristotle, Festinger and Laski for the same view (p. 6).
- He focuses on organizations with a significant economic aspect, such as unions, farm organizations, cartels, corporations (whose "members" are the stockholders) and the state. Philanthropic and religious bodies are not necessarily expected to serve only their members' interests (p. 6, fn. 6–7).
- The interests these bodies serve are mostly **common interests** (p. 7).
  - Personal interests can usually be advanced most efficiently by individual, unorganized action. There is no point in an organization to play solitaire (p. 7).
  - When people share a single purpose, individual action will, Olson says, either not advance it at all or not advance it adequately; he promises to show this (p. 7).
  - So the characteristic and primary function of organizations is to advance common interests, although they may also serve personal ones (p. 7).
- Writers on "group theory" (Bentley, Cattell) also use "group" for people with a common interest. Olson does the same (p. 8).
- Members also have individual interests. Example: all union members want higher wages, but each worker's income also depends on his hours (p. 8).
- Factions do not weaken the approach. Subgroups are treated as units with their own common interests (fn. 11, p. 8).

*Intuition note:* A purely personal interest can usually be advanced best by acting alone. Organization has a function when many people share an interest.

### B. Public goods and large groups (pp. 9–16)
**The market analogy (pp. 9–10).**
- Firms in a perfectly competitive industry have a common interest in a higher price. A uniform price prevails, so no firm gets a higher price unless all do (p. 9).
- Each firm also wants to sell as much as it can, until marginal cost exceeds price. Here interests are opposed, because more output from others lowers every firm's price (p. 9).
- Model (p. 9):
  1. Assume the industry is momentarily in disequilibrium: price exceeds marginal cost for all firms.
  2. Assume adjustment is by existing firms, not entrants.
  3. Assume the industry is on an inelastic portion of its demand curve.
  4. Then output rises, price falls, and (inelastic demand) industry total revenue declines.
- Each firm is, by definition, so small that it can ignore its effect on price (pp. 9–10).
- A firm that restricted output would lose more: price would fall anyway and its output would be smaller. It gets only a small share of the benefit of its own restriction (p. 10).
- So all firms are worse off, yet each has maximized its own profit. The reason is that each firm prefers that the *others* pay the cost, here the output reduction needed for a higher price (p. 10).
- About the only thing that keeps prices from falling is outside intervention (price supports, tariffs, cartel agreements). How does an industry obtain it? (p. 10)

**The lobbying parallel (pp. 10–12).**
- To get a tariff or price support, producers will presumably have to organize a lobby (pp. 10–11). It may have to run a considerable campaign, and if resistance is significant a great amount of money will be required (p. 11).
  - Example: in 1947 the National Association of Manufacturers spent over \$4.6 million, and the American Medical Association spent a similar amount over a somewhat longer period on a campaign against compulsory health insurance (fn. 16, p. 11).
- Just as it was irrational to restrict output, it would be irrational for a producer to sacrifice time and money for the lobby. Neither is in his interest as an individual (p. 11).
- A lobby, union or other organization working for a large group would get no help from rational, self-interested members. This holds even if everyone is convinced the programme is in their interest (p. 11).
- Existing powerful lobbies with mass support do not get it because of their legislative achievements. Later parts of the book give other reasons (p. 12).
- **Objection (critics):** a rational person will pay, because if he does not, others will not either and the organization will fail (p. 12).
- **Olson's reply:** it would be just as reasonable to argue that prices never fall below the monopoly level, since each firm would foresee the chain reaction. In fact this does not happen in a competitive market, nor in a large organization. With many firms, nobody notices one firm's extra output. Likewise, one lost dues payer does not noticeably raise the burden on any other, so nobody believes his withdrawal drives others out (p. 12).
- This argument must at the least have some relevance to economic organizations that are mainly means through which people seek what they seek in the market, such as unions (p. 12).

**Do emotion and ideology change this? The state as test (pp. 12–14).**
- Objection (as Olson poses it): attitudes in organizations are not at all like those in markets, since an emotional or ideological element is often also involved. Does this make the argument practically irrelevant? (p. 12)
- Test case: the nation-state. It has patriotism, ideology, a common culture, and it provides indispensable law and order. Yet no major state in modern history has supported itself by voluntary dues or contributions. Taxes, compulsory by definition, are needed (p. 13).
- Fn. 19 (Weber): ideologically motivated economic action exists, but Weber holds it is even more certain that most people do not act this way (p. 13).
- The reason: the state's most basic services (defense, police, law and order) must be available to everyone if they are available to anyone. Exclusion of non-payers would not be feasible, if indeed possible (p. 14).
- Hence a large private organization may also have trouble getting voluntary contributions (p. 13).

**Definition (p. 14).** A common, collective or public good is any good such that, if any person $X_i$ in a group $X_1,\dots,X_i,\dots,X_n$ consumes it, it cannot feasibly be withheld from the others in that group (p. 14).
- Points in fn. 21 (p. 14):
  - Collective goods are defined relative to a group. A good can be collective for one group and private for another (the parade seen from tall buildings versus the stands).
  - Exclusion need only be infeasible or uneconomic, not technically impossible (Head).
  - Public goods traditionally have two elements: nonexcludability and "jointness of supply". Jointness is *not* a necessary attribute under Olson's definition.
  - At least one type of collective good he considers has no jointness. Few if any of his goods would have enough jointness to qualify as pure public goods, though most show a large measure of jointness.
- Fn. 22 (p. 15): a public good to one group need not serve society as a whole. A tariff is a good to the industry, and its removal is a good to consumers.

**The extension (pp. 15–16).**
- Public-finance writers neglected that achieving any common goal or interest means a public good is provided for that group. By definition no one in the group can be excluded from a common goal's benefit (p. 15).
- It follows that providing public goods is the fundamental function of organizations generally (p. 15).
- Just as the state cannot support itself by voluntary contributions or by selling its basic services, other large organizations cannot support themselves without some sanction, or some attraction distinct from the public good itself, to lead individuals to help bear the burdens of maintaining the organization (pp. 15–16).
- The typical member of a large organization resembles the competitive firm or the taxpayer. His own effort has no noticeable effect, and he enjoys improvements whether or not he helped (p. 16).
- Organizations do not provide *only* collective goods. Governments often provide noncollective goods such as electric power and usually sell them on the market. Large organizations that cannot make membership compulsory must also provide some noncollective goods as an incentive to join (p. 16).
- Collective goods are still the characteristic organizational goods. Ordinary noncollective goods can always be provided by individual action, and only for common purposes is group action ever indispensable (p. 16).
- Fn. 24: it does not follow that *organized* action is always needed to get a collective good; section D (not in this unit) addresses this (p. 16).

*Intuition note:* If you will get the benefit whether or not you pay, and your payment barely changes the outcome, paying is not in your own interest, even though the members share an interest in the benefit.

### C. The traditional theory of groups (pp. 16–21)
- **Traditional view (as Olson describes it):** private groups follow principles entirely different from those governing firms in markets or taxpayers and the state. It is a concern of many political scientists, sociologists and social psychologists. Olson speaks loosely of one theory with two variants (pp. 16–17).
- **Casual variant (p. 17):**
  - Groups are ubiquitous because of a human propensity or "instinct" to form and join associations.
  - Mosca speaks of an "instinct" for herding together and fighting other herds. Olson says Aristotle "may have had" a similar idea. Simmel and Bentley stress the inevitability of group affiliation.
  - The tendency is often thought strongest in the United States.
- **Formal variant (pp. 17–20):**
  - It does not begin with an instinct. It sees modern associations as part of the evolution from primitive to modern societies.
  - Primary groups (so small that members have face-to-face relationships, such as family and kin) predominate in primitive societies. Parsons and MacIver are cited here (p. 18).
  - Structural differentiation follows: as the family loses functions, secondary groups such as unions take them over (p. 18).
  - If MacIver is right that a civilized society has many specific associations where a primitive one has few, the large modern association seems an equivalent of the small primitive group. Both then need the same explanation (pp. 18–19).
- **Olson's comment:** the formal theorists leave this source implicit (p. 19).
  - An "instinct" is no explanation, because it merely adds a word. Any action can be ascribed to an instinct for it (p. 19).
  - Probably some theorists think in functional terms: small groups prevailed because they suited the functions needed, and large associations now do because they alone, or best, perform some functions. The function or interest is the incentive for formation and maintenance (pp. 19–20).
- **Assumptions of the traditional theory (p. 20):**
  - Participation in voluntary associations is virtually universal.
  - Small and large groups attract members for the same reasons.
  - (More sophisticated variant) Where a need for a large association exists, one will tend to emerge and attract members, as a small group does when a small group is needed.
  - Small and large groups differ "in degree, but not in kind".
- **Challenge:** empirical research shows the average person does not typically belong to large voluntary associations. The claim that Americans are "joiners" is "largely a myth" (p. 20).
- **Open questions (p. 21):** is there a relation between group size and coherence, effectiveness or appeal to potential members, and between size and individual incentives to contribute? He cites Simmel: what needs to be known is how the number of individuals affects the form of social life (p. 21).
- **Tension:**
  - Large and small groups both work for a collective benefit that reaches all members. Members share an interest in the benefit but "no common interest in paying the cost". Each prefers that the others pay (p. 21).
  - This makes it seem unlikely that large and small groups differ.
  - Still, Olson says one cannot help feeling, intuitively, that sufficiently small groups would sometimes provide themselves with public goods (p. 21).
- The answer needs a study of costs and benefits at different group sizes. The next section uses rudimentary mathematics (p. 21).

*Intuition note:* The traditional theory explains groups by a joining urge or by the functions groups can perform. Olson does not yet answer it here; he asks whether group size changes individual incentives to contribute (p. 21).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Take 100 identical producers who would gain from a tariff lobby. Assume (our simplification) that each \$10 contributed raises the total benefit by \$20, shared equally. Each producer then gains \$20 ÷ 100 = \$0.20 per \$10 he contributes.
- **If one producer gives \$10 and no one else does:** he pays \$10 and receives \$0.20, a net of $0.20 - 10 = -9.80$.
- **If he gives nothing, whatever the others do:** he receives their benefits at no cost. Whatever the others do, his net is higher by \$9.80 than if he gave.
- **If all 100 give \$10:** total \$1000, total benefit \$2000, each gets \$20. Each nets $20 - 10 = +10$.
- **Result:** the outcome where everyone gives (+\$10 each) beats the outcome where no one gives (\$0 each). Yet a rational producer withholds his contribution, so each ends at \$0 while everyone could have had +\$10. The tiny effect of one contribution (\$0.20) is the "not noticeable" effect Olson stresses.

## Concepts
- [[collective action problem]] — a group with a common interest, whose members individually prefer others to pay, fails to provide its good voluntarily (pp. 10–11, 21)
- [[public good]] — Olson's "collective good": cannot feasibly be withheld from others in the group once one consumes it; jointness not required (p. 14; fn. 21)
- [[free rider]] — the idea (not the term here) of a member who enjoys the benefit "whether he had borne part of the cost or not" (p. 21)
- [[selective incentive]] — idea only: a sanction or attraction "distinct from the public good itself" (pp. 15–16); the term is not used in this unit
- [[common-interest-group]] (new) — Olson's "group": a number of individuals with a common interest (p. 8)
- [[nonexcludability]] (new) — the infeasibility, not necessarily impossibility, of keeping non-payers from the good (p. 14, fn. 21)
- [[jointness-of-supply]] (new) — making a good available to one person means it can be easily or freely supplied to others (the polar case: one person's consumption does not diminish what others get); not a necessary attribute of public goods for Olson (fn. 21, p. 14)
- [[traditional-theory-of-groups]] (new) — casual (instinct) and formal (functional evolution) views that treat small and large groups as differing only in degree (pp. 16–20)

## Canonical instances
- [[competitive-industry-output-expansion]] — firms each raise output, price falls, industry revenue declines (pp. 9–10)
- [[industry-lobby]] — producers seeking tariff or price support, with no rational individual contribution (pp. 10–11)
- [[state-taxation]] — compulsory taxes because defense and law cannot be withheld from non-payers (pp. 13–14)
- [[joiner-myth]] — empirical evidence against the claim that the typical American is a joiner (p. 20)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Output expansion by price-taking firms ↔ game theory: a dominant strategy that leaves all worse off — each firm's best response is the same whatever the others do (pp. 9–10)
- [structural-analogy] Rational non-contribution to a public good ↔ political-science: the paradox of voting — one's own act has a negligible effect on a collective outcome (p. 12)
- [metaphor] Olson's idea of a sanction or attraction separate from the public good ↔ institutions: rules that make cooperation individually worthwhile (pp. 15–16)

## Questions this raises
- If large groups need selective incentives, which types exist, and how do real lobbies and unions use them (p. 12 promises "other reasons")?
- Why might a small group provide itself with a public good (p. 21)?
- Does the "no noticeable effect" argument (p. 12) hold when group members can see each other's behavior?
- How far does the argument apply to groups with strong emotional or ideological bonds, other than the state (p. 13)?
