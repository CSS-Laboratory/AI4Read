# Orthodox Theories of State and Class

## L0 — One line
Olson argues that the public-goods logic economists use for the state also applies to class action, and that, applied to Marx's own assumption of rational self-interest, it predicts that classes will not act.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Chapter IV asks how earlier theories treated the state and social classes as groups serving a common interest. Economists slowly reached a correct public-goods account of the state, which Wicksell stated: a self-interested taxpayer would pay nothing, so taxes are compulsory. Marx described classes as selfish interest groups. Olson replies that class action is a collective-good problem. If class members are rational and selfish, they will not bear the costs of class action, because they get the benefits anyway. Apathy therefore does not show that Marx overrated rationality. It shows his theory is inconsistent insofar as it assumes rational selfishness. An irrational-action reading would be consistent, but Olson thinks it perhaps unlikely and still weak.

**Prerequisites:** public (collective) vs private goods; marginal utility and equilibrium (high-school level); Marx's idea of classes defined by property.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Economists needed more than a generation to reach a clear theory of public goods (goods whose benefits reach everyone) for the state (p. 98). Wicksell's version is the correct one: a utility-maximizing taxpayer would pay nothing, so taxation must be compulsory (p. 100).
- Wicksell stayed with government. He did not treat the general problem faced by all economic organizations, and did not consider how small a "public" must be before the theory fails (p. 100).
- Ritschl rejects the individualistic view. He gives the state communal spirit and the market self-interest. Olson calls this a "curious dichotomy" that makes the state and race, and for Marxian writers the class, metaphysical entities with "objective" needs beyond those of their members (pp. 101–102).
- Marx defined classes by property relations, so class members have common economic interests. He also assumed that individuals are selfish (pp. 103–104).
- Critics such as Mills say Marx overrated rationality because class action is rare. Olson disagrees (pp. 104–105).
- Olson's central argument: a rational member of a large class gets class benefits whether or not he helps, so he has no individual incentive to act. Marx's class is a "large, latent group", one whose members each gain if others bear the costs (pp. 105–107).
- Marx's theory is inconsistent if it assumes rational selfishness. An irrational-action reading would be consistent but is perhaps unlikely and would still be weak (pp. 108–110).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A. The economists' theory of the state (pp. 98–102)
- Most economists accept the "public goods" theory. It implies that the basic services of government can be provided only through compulsion. The idea that the state provides a common benefit is over a century old (p. 98).
- Olson's short history of mistakes (p. 98–100):
  - **Storch** seemed to have a vague idea of collective goods. He argued that individual enterprise could not secure life and property from attack, though it could best supply all other needs. Say endorsed this (p. 98).
  - **Wieser** asked why state services are consumed equally while private income is very unequal. He saw that the state resembles private associations. He erred by saying that the public economy "does not in itself create productive income" (p. 99).
  - **Sax** separated publicly owned enterprises from state activities that benefit all. He wrongly explained support for the state by "altruism". If that were true, taxes would not need to be compulsory (p. 99).
  - **Mazzola** rightly stressed the "indivisibility" of public goods and that state services benefit everyone. He wrongly claimed a "complementarity" between public and private goods. From it he concluded that each citizen gets equal marginal utility from both and is in equilibrium (pp. 99–100).
  - **Wicksell** later pointed out that a taxpayer maximizing satisfaction "will obviously pay nothing whatsoever for public purposes". His payment changes the scope of public services so slightly that he will not notice (p. 100).
- Conclusion: taxes are compulsory exactions that hold the taxpayer in a *disequilibrium* position (p. 100). Wicksell had a correct conception of the problem, whatever one thinks of his practical tax proposal (p. 100).
- Limits: Wicksell discussed only government. He did not treat the problem faced by all economic organizations, nor how small a "public" must be before the theory no longer applies (p. 100).
- Later economists mostly accepted Wicksell's analysis (p. 100).

**Intuition note:** Everyone gains from public services, but one person's payment hardly changes what he receives. So a purely self-interested person would pay nothing, and only compulsion secures payment.

- **Ritschl's view (dissent):** The market runs on self-interest. The state economy runs on communal spirit, which unites members "of the same ilk". Coercion is a means of assuring the full effectiveness of that spirit, which is not equally developed in all members. Even associations and cooperatives are self-interested in their market relations with each other (pp. 100–101).
- **Olson's reply:** This is "exactly the opposite" of his approach. It assumes that self-interest rules among individuals while self-sacrifice rules their relation to the state and associations. The state, race, and for Marxians the class, become metaphysical entities with "objective" needs (pp. 101–102).
- British laissez-faire economists listed a few state functions (defense, police, law and order) but did not say what these have in common. Except for a few imprecise comments by Mill and Sidgwick, it appears that the leading British economists largely ignored the problem of collective goods. Pigou gave collective goods mostly implicit treatment (p. 102).

### B. The Marxian theory of state and class (pp. 102–105)
- In Marx the state is the instrument by which the ruling class dominates the oppressed. Under capitalism it is the "executive committee of the bourgeoisie" (p. 102).
- Classes are "organized human interest groups" and uniformly selfish. They are defined by property relations: owners of productive capital who take surplus value, versus propertyless wage earners (p. 103).
- Why this definition matters: a definition by prestige would not support common interests. Defined by property, members of a class gain or lose together as prices and wages change. The exploited class will eventually see that revolt is in its interest and within its capability (p. 103).
- Individuals are selfish too. Marx attributed much self-interest to capitalism but saw it in all civilization. He treated ideologies as cloaks for vested interests. Only under communism would selfish propensities not control behavior (pp. 103–104).
- Critics therefore call Marx a utilitarian and rationalist. **Mills' view:** class action needs rational awareness of class interests, rejection of other classes' interests, and readiness to use collective political means. He holds that Marxism and liberalism share the rationalist assumption. He takes widespread political apathy as proof the assumption is wrong (pp. 104–105).
- Olson summarizes: critics think most people do not know or care about their class interests, since class conflict is weaker than Marx expected (p. 105).

### C. The logic of the Marxian theory (pp. 105–110)
- **Main argument:** The absence of expected class conflict does not show that Marx overestimated rationality. It is due in part to the predominance of rational utilitarian behavior (p. 105).
- A bourgeois may want a government for his class. But if there is such a government he benefits from its policies whether or not he supported it, since by Marx's own hypothesis it will work for his class interests. And one individual bourgeois presumably cannot exercise a decisive influence on the choice of government. So it is rational to ignore class interests (p. 106).
- A worker would likewise not rationally risk his life for revolution. Olson says it would be just as reasonable to suppose that all workers would voluntarily restrict their hours to raise wages. In both cases the individual gets the benefits whether or not he participates (p. 106).
- Olson adds that actual "Marxian" revolutions were made by small conspiratorial elites exploiting weak governments. Lenin, not Marx, supplied that theory: rely on a committed, self-sacrificing, disciplined minority (p. 106).
- Class action is thus like the pursuit of goals by any large, latent group. "Class legislation" favors the class as a whole, so it gives individuals no incentive for class-conscious action. The worker stands to the proletariat as the taxpayer to the state and the competitive firm to the industry (pp. 106–107).
- Marx sometimes limited "class" to organized groupings. He stressed unions and strikes, and the Manifesto says the real fruit of workers' battles is the "ever expanding union" (p. 107). But the individual worker has no interest in joining a union to raise wages (p. 107). Olson says Marx's own answer to this problem, in a quoted passage, "is hardly very clear" (fn. 27, pp. 107–108).

**Intuition note:** If a class gets its gains whether or not you join, joining costs you and adds almost nothing. A class of selfish people therefore does not act, and apathy could be due, at least in part, to rational selfishness rather than to its absence.

- **Verdict:** Marx's theory of classes is inconsistent insofar as it assumes rational, selfish pursuit of individual interest. Missing class action does not show that economic motivation is weak. It shows there are no individual economic incentives for class action. Critics who call Marx consistent but psychologically unrealistic credit him with a consistency he may not have. Apathy "could logically be due to its strength" (p. 108).
- **Alternative reading:** A theory of irrational, emotional class action would be consistent. Marx was imprecise, so it is conceivable he meant this. It is perhaps unlikely, because he would then have had to stress selfless sublimation of individual interest. Instead he stressed selfishness, defined classes by property, and gave little attention to emotional class consciousness. So he probably did not have *only* such a theory in mind (pp. 108–109).
- Much evidence suggests he offered a rational utilitarian theory, and if so his theory is inconsistent (pp. 109–110). Even a purely irrational theory suffers, since irrational behavior is hard to accept as the motive power of all social change in history (p. 110). Hence, in Schumpeter's words, the class theory is only a "crippled sister" of the Economic Interpretation of History (p. 110).
- Footnote: there is "some possibility" that Marx had a metaphysical, Hegelian-dialectic assertion in mind; to the extent that he did, the criticism above would be frustrated. But Olson says such a metaphysical concept has no part in an empirical discipline like economics (fn. 30, p. 109).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
100 workers. Each can pay 10 into a strike fund. Each payment of 10 raises *every* worker's wage gain by 0.5, whoever paid.
- You pay, others do not: you get 0.5 and pay 10, net $0.5 - 10 = -9.5$.
- You do not pay: you pay nothing and still get 0.5 from every other worker who pays. If nobody else pays, your net is 0.
- All 100 pay: each gets $100 \times 0.5 = 50$ and paid 10, net $+40$.

Everyone is better off if all pay (+40 against 0). Yet whatever the others do, your paying changes your own net by $0.5 - 10 = -9.5$ (for example, if 99 others pay, you net 49.5 by not paying and 40 by paying). This is the logic of the Olson verdict above: rational selfish workers do not act as a class.

## Concepts
- [[public-good]] — a good whose benefits go to everyone ("indivisible"), so state services need compulsory payment (pp. 98–100)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — here applied to class action: rational members do not bear the costs, since benefits come anyway (p. 106)
- [[latent-group]] (new) — a large group with a common interest in which each member finds it to his advantage if others bear the costs of the common goal; Olson's "large, latent group" (p. 106)
- [[marxian-class-as-interest-group]] (new) — a class defined by property relations, whose members share economic interests (p. 103)
- [[wicksell-disequilibrium]] (new) — the taxpayer would pay nothing voluntarily, so compulsory taxes keep him in what "might better be called a disequilibrium position" (p. 100)
- [[institutions]] — the state, unions and organized class groupings as the vehicles of common interest (pp. 98, 107). Here the focus is the incentives to support them.

## Canonical instances

- [[workers-and-union-dues]] — a worker has no individual interest in joining a union to raise wages (p. 107)

## Questions this raises
- If class action does not arise from selfish rationality, what does explain the unions and strikes Marx described?
- Where does a group become small enough that Wicksell's logic stops applying (p. 100)?
- Can an irrational, emotional theory of class action be tested against rational-choice accounts?
- How far do state coercion and group coercion solve the same incentive problem?
