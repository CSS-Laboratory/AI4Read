# Orthodox Theories of Pressure Groups

## L0 — One line
The "group theory" of politics assumes that large economic groups organize whenever they have a reason to. Olson argues this contradicts the self-interest the theory itself assumes, so the theory is inadequate for large economic groups, at least.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Olson asks whether the dominant theories of pressure groups are logically sound. These are philosophical pluralism, corporatism (representation through occupational groups), Commons's institutional economics, and the "analytical pluralism" of Bentley, Truman and Latham. They treat groups as the basic political unit and assume that a group with a reason to organize will organize. Olson argues that for large economic groups this conflicts with self-interest: such members will not voluntarily make sacrifices for a group goal. Small groups can often organize voluntarily, large ones normally cannot. So the contest among groups is not symmetric, and the pluralists' claim that groups balance one another is damaged. The chapter prepares the ground for a new theory.

**Prerequisites:** public (collective) goods; the idea that people act from self-interest; Olson's earlier split of groups into small "privileged" and "intermediate" groups and large "latent" groups (from earlier chapters, referred to on pp. 126–127).

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Many scholars praise pressure groups as beneficial and as balancing one another. Pluralism (a view that voluntary private associations should act as a cushion between individual and state) has probably helped create this mood (pp. 111–113).
- Commons held that economic pressure groups are more representative than a territorially elected legislature. Olson says he went so far as to suggest that the freedom to form them matters more than other democratic freedoms (pp. 114–116).
- Bentley's model makes group pressure the one and only determinant of policy, treats individual and national interests as fictions, and holds the result is for the most part reasonably just (pp. 117–121).
- Truman tried to fill Bentley's gap about why groups form (associations arise as society grows complex). He argues that overlapping memberships and "potential groups" (unorganized groups that could organize if threatened) keep demands moderate (pp. 122–125). Olson says the facts do not necessarily support the first claim; the timing of association growth does not fit it (p. 123).
- Core objection: group theorists assume self-interest in economic groups but also assume groups organize. For large, latent (large and unorganized) groups, at least, these are inconsistent (pp. 126–127, 129).
- Because small groups organize more easily than large ones, group conflict is asymmetric. Small "special interests" can often beat larger groups (pp. 127–128).
- The "potential group" defence is hard to test, and the theory is inadequate unless it shows why a member of a large group would pay (pp. 128–129).
- The assumption that large groups will voluntarily organize whenever needed is, Olson says, an "anarchistic fallacy", which the pluralists and corporatists share with Bakunin and Kropotkin (pp. 130–131).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A. The philosophical view of pressure groups (pp. 111–114)
- **Olson's framing.** Just as Marxians glorify class action, many non-Marxian scholars glorify the pressure group. They tend to write approvingly of its functions, and many say groups generally counterbalance one another, so that no result is unduly favourable to one group and unjustly harmful to the rest of society (p. 111). Olson says it would be difficult to trace exactly how this view arose, but pluralism has probably helped create a favourable climate (p. 111).
- **Pluralism.** This is a political philosophy that wants private associations (especially unions, churches, cooperatives) to have a larger constitutional role, and the state not to control them without limit. It opposes veneration of the nation state but fears anarchistic and laissez-faire extremes, and seeks safety in a society where private associations cushion the individual from the state. It deals with pressure groups only tangentially (pp. 111–112). It tends to create a mood favourable to pressure groups, though that is not its main purpose, because it stresses the spontaneity and voluntary character of private associations against the compulsory character of the state (p. 112).
- **Lindsay (pluralist):** the sphere of initiative, spontaneity and liberty in social life cannot be occupied by the state "with its instruments of compulsion" (p. 112).
- **Sources of the idea.** Gierke and Maitland, concerned with "legal personality" for associations, found in medieval thought an emphasis on spontaneous origin and organic unity. Dewey shared the enthusiasm for voluntary association. Paul-Boncour argued that occupational associations had arisen spontaneously in all ages and countries. Durkheim, like him, was to some degree a "corporatist": he believed in the natural causes and desirable effects of occupationally organized associations (pp. 112–113).
- **Corporate state.** This is government through industrial-occupational groups rather than territorial divisions. It has perhaps some similarity to the view that pressure groups should play a larger role. One version was supported by many Catholic groups, officially encouraged by the Vatican, and backed by de Gaulle. Some syndicalist and fascist thought stressed it, and fascist Italy and Vichy France put it to a degree into practice (pp. 113–114).
- **Sociological pluralism.** A newer type holds that many political groups besides the state are necessary if a society is to avoid susceptibility to "mass movements" like Nazism and Communism (n. 2, p. 112).

*Intuition note:* These writers share a mood: private groups are natural, voluntary and benign, while the state is coercive. Later in the chapter Olson attacks the "natural and voluntary" picture for large groups (p. 130).

### B. Institutional economics and the pressure group: Commons (pp. 114–116)
- Probably the most thoughtful American advocacy of occupational over geographic representation came from Commons. At one point he advocated direct election of representatives for each interest group as the effective legislature (pp. 114–115). Olson notes a shortcoming in a footnote: no one legislator can feasibly trade one interest off against another, so the compromise a continuing democracy needs may be unattainable (n. 13, p. 114).
- Most of the time Commons argued only that pressure groups were the most representative and beneficial forces affecting American economic policy (p. 115).
- **His reasoning.** Markets do not of themselves give fair results, because of unequal bargaining power. These disparities would not be removed by government-promoted collective action unless pressure groups forced through the reforms, since machine politicians and wealthy men controlled the legislatures. So pressure groups were virtually an indispensable means to a just economic order, and group conflict was the vehicle of reform and progress (p. 115). The economist should attach himself to a pressure group or class and advise it on its long-run interest, rather than seek legislation for the whole of society (p. 115).
- **Representativeness.** Commons wrote that economic pressure groups, concentrated in Washington, become an occupational parliament of the American people, more truly representative than the territorially elected Congress (p. 116). Olson says he went so far as to suggest that the freedom to form pressure groups mattered more than any other democratic freedom, and that the traditional legislature was worth preserving mainly because it protects free association (p. 116). Olson notes in a footnote that Commons sometimes argued, "inconsistently it would seem", that some groups, such as farmers, were poorly organized (n. 17, p. 116).
- Kenneth Parsons developed this view. Some aspects of Galbraith's countervailing power have a slight similarity to Commons's theories (p. 116).

### C. Modern theories: Bentley, Truman, Latham (pp. 117–125)
- **Group as basic unit.** Olson says political scientists, more than economists, hold a view strikingly similar to Commons's. Latham reports that American writers on politics increasingly accept the group as the basic political form (p. 117). In Latham's account, early economists saw only isolated individuals and treated combination as pathological. Modern economics speaks of groups, for example "oligopoly" (pp. 117–118).
- **Olson's comment.** Olson notes that the quotation neglects the fact that monopolistic and imperfect competition rest on assumptions as individualistic as perfect competition. He says what is significant is the belief that group interests are the primary forces in economy and politics: for Commons and Latham alike, group interests are dominant and individual interests secondary (p. 118).
- **Analytical pluralists.** Latham links group theory to pluralism. Early "philosophical" pluralists saw the group basis of society but are chided for not studying groups scientifically. The modern group theorists are called "analytical pluralists", and Olson adopts that name (p. 118; n. 26).
- **Bentley.**
  - His *The Process of Government* (first published 1908, n. 27) has inspired most of the political scientists who followed the group approach. He came to politics from an interest in economic life, and thought wealth the main source of group division (pp. 118–119).
  - For Bentley, stating groups adequately amounts to stating everything (p. 119). An interest is equivalent to a group, and Bentley thought no interest could be considered to exist unless it showed up in group action (p. 119).
  - The individual taken in isolation, with a unity of his own apart from society, is a fiction. Likewise the national interest is a fiction, since all group interests pertain to groups comprising only part of a nation (pp. 119–120).
  - He defined groups by their conflict with one another, and he held that no interest group has meaning except with reference to other interest groups (p. 120). This excluded an interest of society as a whole and let him say that the resultant of group pressures is the one and only determinant of policy. Pressure is always a group phenomenon, and "the balance of group pressures is the existing state of society" (p. 120).
  - Olson summarizes the model's assumptions (p. 120):
    1. No effective individual interests.
    2. Every group has its interests.
    3. Those interests always result in group action.
    4. No group interest includes everyone.

    From these assumptions Bentley was able to claim that all things involving government are determined by conflicting group pressures. Footnote 40 lists what Bentley folded into the model: leadership quality, regime type, separation of powers, judicial decisions and the franchise (n. 40, pp. 120–121).
  - **Justice of the outcome.** Groups had power more or less in proportion to their numbers, and the larger interest usually tended to defeat the narrower one. In his wagon-teamsters example, the mass of taxpayers is eventually "bound to win" and require wider tires, even if many are unaware of the dispute (p. 121). Bentley thought logrolling an efficient way to adjust group interests (p. 121; n. 44).
  - **Gap.** Olson says Bentley said very little about why the needs of groups would be reflected in effective pressure, or why groups organize (p. 122). Footnote 45 adds "See however" Bentley's pp. 460–464 (n. 45).
- **Truman on how groups form (pp. 122–123).**
  - As society grows more complex and group needs multiply, new associations arise to stabilize group relations. Truman calls the proliferation "inescapable" (p. 122).
  - Disturbances from a self-regulating market inevitably produced associations of owners, workers and farmers that act on government (p. 123).
  - **Olson's reply.** Truman seems to say that suffering and dislocation will almost inevitably produce organized pressure, but the facts do not necessarily bear this out. By Truman's standard, more associations should have formed during the industrial revolution, yet Truman himself reports the highest rate in recent years, which Olson describes as mainly prosperous and stable (p. 123). Footnote 50 gives examples (British and American unions), concedes that the legal environment may also have mattered, and concedes that Truman is persuasive that discontent is greatest in times of dislocation. Its conclusion: the theory assumes groups arise because there is a dislocation or "need" for them, and this is backed neither by fact nor by theory (n. 50, p. 123).
- **Truman's benign view (pp. 124–125).** Olson says Truman followed Bentley closely, neglected individual interests, and was if anything even less qualified in claiming the equilibrium is just. He gives two reasons:
  1. Overlapping memberships weaken groups that ask too much. Tariff-seeking manufacturers are also consumers and churchmen, so an excessive demand would alienate some of the group's own members (p. 124).
  2. "Potential groups" would organize against special interests that got far out of line, and the fear of them restrains organized interests (pp. 124–125).
  - Truman held that it is accurate to call government the resultant of group activity only when the effects of overlapping memberships and potential groups are counted (p. 125). He belittled almost all attempts to improve legislation and lobbying (p. 125; n. 56). In n. 56 Olson says Truman rightly lists disadvantages of functional representation, but the question remains whether the needs of all groups get reflected in effective pressure.
- **Olson's footnote on efficiency (n. 52, p. 124).** Even a balance that treated every group fairly need not be harmless. If every industry is favoured to a fair or equal degree, the economy as a whole will tend to function less efficiently, and every group will be worse off than if none, or only some, of the special-interest demands had been granted.

*Intuition note:* For the group theorists, politics is the sum of group pushes. Their optimism rests on balance: bigger interests usually beat narrower ones, and sleeping groups wake up when threatened. Olson next tests the "wake up" step.

### D. The logic of group theory (pp. 125–131)
- **The inconsistency (pp. 125–127).**
  - Olson sees an inconsistency in Commons, Bentley, Truman, Latham and some pluralist and corporatist writers. Many of them, especially Bentley, Truman and Latham, take for granted that large economic groups working for their economic interests are fundamental in politics. Self-interested economic groups always play the largest role in their writings (pp. 125–126). Latham says even philanthropic groups serve the members' self-expression and security (p. 126).
  - If groups, at least economic groups, are often interested primarily in their own welfare, that could only be so because the individuals in them are, Olson says (p. 126). The group theorists see benign results not because individuals are altruistic, but because they think groups check one another (p. 126).
  - **Olson's argument.** If individuals in any large group are interested in their own welfare, they will not voluntarily make any sacrifices to help the group reach its political (public or collective) goals (p. 126). Individuals in small ("privileged" or "intermediate") groups may organize voluntarily, but this is not true in large or latent groups (pp. 126–127). The assumption that a group with a reason to organize gives its rational members a reason to support the organization is "logically fallacious, at least for large, latent groups with economic interests" (p. 127).
  - Truman's sociological variant, like the earlier sociological theory (Ch. I), rests on the mistaken belief that large groups could attract support as easily as small primary groups. Olson says earlier chapters showed this assumption to be logically untenable and inconsistent with the evidence (p. 127).
- **Asymmetry and the "special interest" (pp. 127–128).** Small groups will frequently be able to organize voluntarily and large ones normally cannot, so the outcome of group conflict will not be symmetrical. This damages the pluralist view that outrageous demands get counterbalanced. Practical politicians and journalists have long seen that small "special interest" groups have disproportionate power. A small oligopolistic industry seeking a tariff will sometimes get it even if the vast majority loses (pp. 127–128). Small groups are generally organized and active, and large groups normally are not. Key's example: utility lobbyists are "eternally on the job", while consumers' lobbyists are ordinarily absent (p. 128).
- **The "potential group" defence (pp. 128–129).**
  - The pluralists stress the group that, though unorganized, could and allegedly would organize if its interests were seriously threatened. Olson says this is rather difficult to test: if a group does not organize, the pluralist can say the damage was not serious or there was no group interest (p. 128).
  - Truman treats organization as merely a stage or degree of interaction. Bentley thought formal organization amounted to less even than that, and compared it to the singing of armies going into battle: a mere technique with little effect on results (p. 128). **Olson's reply:** a better analogy is a disciplined army versus a leaderless mob. Politicians stress "organization" and the "machine", and textbooks list few if any examples of influence by unorganized groups (p. 129).
  - **Key point.** Even if the pluralists are right about potential groups, their theory is still inadequate unless they can show how damage to a large group's interests would necessarily provide an incentive for each member to sacrifice for the group goal. The member's support will not be decisive, and he would be as likely to get the benefits whether he worked for them or not (p. 129). Their theories, as they relate to large economic groups at least, are therefore logically inconsistent (p. 129).
- **Implications for others (pp. 129–131).**
  - The argument also applies to Commons's claim that the lobbies assembled in Washington are more representative than the territorially elected Congress (p. 129).
  - Olson adds that his theory does not weaken, and might even strengthen, the idea that parliament be elected by each economic group: to the extent that it shows groups would be unequally represented in a territorial legislature, it could be used to support an occupational parliament. It does destroy the corporatist idea that occupational groups naturally form corporate organizations from some spontaneous unity (n. 64, p. 129).
  - It damages, though does not destroy, a few arguments of some of the older "philosophical" pluralists and corporatists. These writers differ a great deal, and Olson's theory can apply only to parts of their views (pp. 129–130). To the extent that they say private occupational groups would have a firm foundation in "natural" unities of interest and function, and would create spontaneous voluntary organizations without the coercive character of the state, they are weakened. The pluralist idea that a rather large private group providing a collective service can be natural, harmonious and voluntary, in contrast with the coercive state, is "mistaken", however valuable other parts of pluralist thought may be (p. 130).
- **The anarchistic fallacy (pp. 130–131).** The pluralist view that private organizations spring up voluntarily has much in common with one aspect of anarchism. Many anarchists believed a voluntary, natural unity would emerge once the state was overthrown. Bakunin said the state's unity is a fiction that artificially produces discord, and that a living unity would arise by free federation from below (p. 130). Kropotkin said a mutual-aid feeling would ensure a spontaneous and natural order (pp. 130–131). Olson says the anarchistic assumption is now regarded as evidence of hopeless eccentricity, but the consistent critic of anarchism must attack with equal force those who suppose that large groups will voluntarily organize whenever the need arises. He names Bentley, Truman, Commons, Latham and many pluralist and corporatist thinkers as fully as guilty of the "anarchistic fallacy" as the anarchists themselves (p. 131). He then asks whether the view that workers will voluntarily support a trade union, or that any large group will organize a lobby, is any more plausible (p. 131).
- **Conclusion.** Because of the inconsistency, the prevailing pluralist theory is not sufficient. The group theory is inadequate for large economic groups, at least, and a new theory is needed, which the next chapter develops (p. 131).

*Intuition note:* Having a reason to organize is not the same as having a reason for each member to pay. Olson says the theory assumes the second follows from the first, and that this assumption is where it fails.

## Toy example (ours, not Olson's)
Each $10 spent on a lobby buys a policy gain of $200 for the whole group, shared equally. The cost is paid only by contributors.
- **Group of 100.** A member who pays $10 gets back $200 / 100 = $2, so his net is $2 − $10 = −$8. He does not pay, whatever others do. If all 100 paid, each would get 100 × $200 / 100 = $200 for a $10 cost, net +$190. So everyone would gain, yet no one pays voluntarily.
- **Group of 4.** A member who pays gets back $200 / 4 = $50, net $50 − $10 = +$40. He pays.

This mirrors Olson's contrast: small groups can often organize voluntarily, large latent groups normally cannot. The small group is then the organized and active one, as in the asymmetry argument (pp. 127–128).

## Concepts
- [[collective-action-problem]] — a group with a shared interest may not organize, because self-interested members of a large group will not voluntarily make sacrifices for the group's goal (pp. 126, 129)
- [[public-good]] — the group's "public or collective" political objective, whose benefits a member is as likely to get whether or not he worked for them (pp. 126, 129)
- [[free-rider]] — the member who is as likely to get the group's benefits whether or not he worked for them (p. 129) (canon link; not stated in this unit as a term)
- [[latent-group]] (new) — large group whose members normally will not voluntarily organize for common interests (pp. 126–127)
- [[privileged-intermediate-group]] (new) — relatively small group where individuals may voluntarily organize for common aims (pp. 126–127)
- [[analytical-pluralism]] (new) — name for modern group theorists (Bentley, Truman, Latham), who hold that group interests drive politics (p. 118; n. 26)
- [[philosophical-pluralism]] (new) — view that private associations should have a larger role and that the state's control should be limited (pp. 111–112)
- [[corporate-state]] (new) — government organized by industrial-occupational groups rather than territory (p. 113)
- [[potential-group]] (new) — unorganized group that could and, pluralists say, would organize if threatened, which restrains others (pp. 124, 128)
- [[overlapping-memberships]] (new) — Truman's claim that members of several groups restrain a group's excessive demands (p. 124)
- [[anarchistic-fallacy]] (new) — Olson's label for the assumption that needed voluntary organization will spontaneously appear (p. 131)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Olson's "anarchistic fallacy" ↔ political theory: the anarchist claim of spontaneous order after the state — both assume needed cooperation appears once a need exists (pp. 130–131).
- [structural-analogy] Potential-group defence ↔ philosophy of science: unfalsifiable explanation — any non-organization is explained away as "no real interest" (p. 128).
- [metaphor] The pluralist "balance of group pressures" ↔ economics: market equilibrium — Olson (n. 52) notes balance may still be inefficient.

## Questions this raises
- If large groups usually fail to organize, how do some (for example unions) ever form? (Olson ends by saying the next chapter develops a new theory.)
- How would one test the potential-group claim without circular reasoning?
- If a perfectly balanced group equilibrium is still inefficient (n. 52), what would improve policy?
- Does Olson's point that small groups organize more easily support or undermine functional representation (n. 64)?
