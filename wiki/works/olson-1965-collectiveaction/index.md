# Olson, M. (1965). *The Logic of Collective Action: Public Goods and the Theory of Groups*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. (2nd printing 1971, with a new preface and appendix.) doi:10.4159/9780674041660

## In one paragraph
Many social scientists assume that groups with a common interest will act to advance it. Olson argues that this does not follow from individual self-interest. A common goal is a public good: members get it whether or not they pay. So a rational member of a large group has no reason to bear costs, even if everyone agrees on the goal. Small groups can often organize voluntarily, but they usually under-supply the good, and their largest members carry a disproportionate share of the burden. Large "latent" groups organize only through coercion or selective incentives, meaning private rewards or punishments. Olson applies this logic to unions, the state, Marx's theory of class, pluralist "group theory" and pressure groups. It explains why small, concentrated interests are often better organized than large, diffuse ones.

## The path through the work
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch00-introduction]] — Olson claims that rational, self-interested individuals in a large group will not voluntarily act for their shared group interest unless there is coercion or a separate individual incentive, so the common view that groups tend to pursue their interests is unjustified, at least when it rests on the assumption that groups act in their self-interest because individuals do.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch01-theory-p1]] — Organizations exist mainly to provide goods that benefit a whole group, and because such goods cannot feasibly be withheld from non-payers, rational self-interested members of a large group have no individual reason to pay for them voluntarily.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch01-theory-p2]] — Whether a group can supply itself with a collective good without coercion or outside inducements depends on the share of benefit each member gets and on whether members' actions are noticeable to one another, so small and large groups seem to differ in kind as well as in degree.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch02-group]] — Small groups often achieve their collective goals voluntarily and large "latent" groups do not, because in a large group no individual's contribution is noticeable, and social pressure works mainly in small groups.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch03-labor]] — Because a union's main product (collective bargaining) is a collective good for a large group, large unions survive mainly through compulsion, and the same logic shows that coercion for collective goods restricts economic freedom whether a union or a government applies it, so what largely decides whether freedom must be curtailed is the provision of collective goods, not the public or private nature of the provider.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch04-orthodox]] — Olson argues that the public-goods logic economists use for the state also applies to class action, and that, applied to Marx's own assumption of rational self-interest, it predicts that classes will not act.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch05-orthodox]] — The "group theory" of politics assumes that large economic groups organize whenever they have a reason to. Olson argues this contradicts the self-interest the theory itself assumes, so the theory is inadequate for large economic groups, at least.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/ch06-by]] — Large economic pressure groups survive because their lobbying is a by-product of coercion or private benefits offered for other reasons, while small business groups organize voluntarily, and large groups lacking such a source stay unorganized.
- [[olson-1965-collectiveaction/app-untitled]] — Olson surveys his later work on collective goods and argues that the "political entrepreneur" idea enriches, and does not overturn, his logic: in large groups the successful entrepreneur is above all an innovator with selective incentives.

## Big ideas across units
- **Shared interest does not produce action.** Rational members of a large group get the benefit whether or not they contribute, even with full agreement on the goal. Units: ch00-introduction, ch01-theory-p1, ch02-group, ch04-orthodox, ch05-orthodox.
- **Collective goods are non-excludable.** Any common goal is a public good for its group, so voluntary payment is irrational. The state's compulsory taxes are the parallel case. Units: ch01-theory-p1, ch01-theory-p2, ch03-labor, ch04-orthodox.
- **Group size changes the logic.** Small and large groups differ in kind, not just degree. In large groups each member's contribution is imperceptible and organization costs are higher. Units: ch00-introduction, ch01-theory-p2, ch02-group, ch05-orthodox, ch06-by, app-untitled.
- **Coercion and selective incentives are what mobilize large groups.** Examples are the union shop, professional services and farm-organization services. Social pressure also counts, but it works mainly in small or federated groups. Units: ch00-introduction, ch01-theory-p1, ch01-theory-p2, ch02-group, ch03-labor, ch06-by, app-untitled.
- **Small groups under-provide, and the largest members carry the burden.** Provision is suboptimal, the great are "exploited" by the small, and bargaining is costly. Units: ch00-introduction, ch01-theory-p2, app-untitled.
- **The logic is applied to existing theories and institutions.** Olson tests it on unions, Marx's classes, pluralist group theory and pressure groups. Units: ch03-labor, ch04-orthodox, ch05-orthodox, ch06-by.
- **Pluralist "group theory" is inconsistent for large groups.** It assumes both self-interest and automatic organization, and it ignores the asymmetry between small special interests and large unorganized groups. Units: ch00-introduction, ch01-theory-p1, ch05-orthodox, ch06-by.
- **Lobbies are by-products, and the largest groups stay unorganized.** Political power comes from an organization that exists for other reasons. Large groups without such a base (consumers, taxpayers, migrant workers) lose out. The political-entrepreneur extension adds to this and does not contradict it. Units: ch05-orthodox, ch06-by, app-untitled.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** the catalog's focus is the Introduction, Chapter I (both parts), Chapter II and Chapter VI. Together they give the argument, the group-size logic and the by-product theory.
- **Dependencies:**
  - ch01-theory-p1 builds on the Introduction.
  - ch01-theory-p2 builds on ch01-theory-p1. It uses derivatives and shares, and it defines the privileged, intermediate and latent groups.
  - ch02-group assumes those group types, which are not defined in its own pages.
  - ch03-labor needs the public-good and free-rider ideas.
  - ch06-by needs the earlier chapters: collective goods, selective incentives and the group types.
- **Applications, skippable first time:** ch03-labor and ch04-orthodox apply the argument to unions, the state and Marx. They are supporting cases and can be read after the core.
- **Bridge to ch06:** ch05-orthodox is a critique of pluralist theories and "prepares the ground for a new theory". Read it before ch06-by if you want the full contrast, or skip it and return later.
- **Optional extra:** the appendix (added in 1971) assumes the book's basic argument. It covers later articles and the political-entrepreneur idea, and it can be left until last.
