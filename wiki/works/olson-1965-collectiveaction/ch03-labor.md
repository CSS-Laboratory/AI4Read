# The Labor Union and Economic Freedom

## L0 — One line
Because a union's main product (collective bargaining) is a collective good for a large group, large unions survive mainly through compulsion, and the same logic shows that coercion for collective goods restricts economic freedom whether a union or a government applies it, so what largely decides whether freedom must be curtailed is the provision of collective goods, not the public or private nature of the provider.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Olson asks why large labor unions exist and last, given that a rational worker gains from union achievements whether or not he supports the union. His answer: smallness and noncollective benefits (insurance, grievance help) can probably now explain only the exceptional union. Compulsory membership and picket lines explain most. He tests this against Perlman's "job consciousness" theory using union history and finds Perlman's account unconvincing. He then argues that a case against the union shop resting solely on "right-to-work" would, if consistent, also condemn taxation and conscription, which are likewise coercion for collective goods. Government restricts economic freedom when it provides collective goods, and need not do so when it produces noncollective goods, even if it is socialist.

**Prerequisites:** the idea of a collective (public) good and the free-rider problem; basic supply and demand (wages, demand elasticity); simple expected-value reasoning.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Early unions were small, local and often in small workplaces. Small groups can better supply themselves with collective goods and can offer social benefits, which may help explain this pattern (pp. 66–67).
- Large national unions survived mainly because membership and strike support were largely compulsory. Compulsion could not create the first unions, but a small union can adopt it later (pp. 68–70).
- Olson holds that violence around strikes is best explained by the incentives a strike creates for non-strikers; employer ideology may explain some violence but must not have been the ultimate cause (p. 71).
- Selective benefits (insurance, grievance handling, federation services) work only in special cases and have weakened. Roughly 95 per cent of unionized workers were covered by some union-security scheme (pp. 72–76).
- Perlman's "job consciousness" explanation of the closed shop is challenged with historical timing and the demand curve for labor. Olson reads union job control as an organizational need (pp. 76–87).
- Low meeting attendance together with overwhelming votes for the union shop is not inconsistent. It is what rational individuals in a latent group would do (pp. 85–87).
- Those who base their case against the union shop solely on "right-to-work" would, to be consistent, also have to accept Wicksell's and Keynes' positions on taxation and conscription (pp. 88–91).
- Government restricts economic freedom when it provides collective goods, not when it produces noncollective goods. The same logic applies to unions and cartels (pp. 94–97).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A. Coercion in labor unions (pp. 66–76)

**History of size.** The first American (and British) unions were small, local and independent. Viable national unions came over half a century later, and the earlier ones, such as the Knights of Labor, failed (p. 66). Early unions arose in small workplaces (building trades, printing, shoemaking), not in big factories. The usual explanation, that skilled workers are easier to organize, cannot be the whole story. Coal mining had mostly unskilled workers, yet its small firms were organized long before the industrial giants (pp. 66–67).

**Olson's partial explanation.** Olson suggests that the historical pattern may be explained at least partly by the fact that small groups can better provide themselves with collective goods than large ones. Higher wages, shorter hours and better conditions are collective goods, and sacrifices are large (a continuing organization; a strike means losing all income). Small unions may also have an advantage in offering noncollective social benefits (p. 67).

**Pressure to grow.** Once a local exists, several forces push it to federate or organize its whole craft or industry. These are market competition (employers cannot pay more than rivals), strikebreakers drawn from unorganized workers, workers who migrate between towns, and political strength. These pressures grow as transport and communication widen markets (pp. 67–68).

**Compulsion is the key to large unions.** Olson calls compulsion by far the most important single factor enabling national unions to survive: membership and strike support were to a great degree compulsory (p. 68). This is old, not modern.
- The Webbs wrote about 60 years earlier that compulsory unionism was as old as trade unionism itself, and described the compulsion as "silent and unseen, but absolutely complete" (p. 68).
- US examples: New York carters (1667, apparently a closed shop), the Cordwainers' 1805 constitution, and a fully developed closed shop in printing by 1840 (p. 69).
- Stockton judged that practically every pre-Civil-War union favored excluding non-members (p. 69).

**Sequence.** Compulsion cannot explain the first small unions, because enforcing it needs an organization. Unorganized workers cannot create a large union even if they see the need for coercion, because they must first organize to have something to enforce it. But a small union can emerge without compulsion and then adopt it, which lets it expand. So early coercion is consistent with the view that unionism began in small groups (pp. 69–70).

**Violence.** Compulsion plus legally free strikebreakers explains why violence is prominent, especially when large national unions are being formed. Employers also used violence (Gould: "I can hire one half of the working class to kill the other half", p. 70).
- **Bell's view:** Social Darwinism in American thought sanctioned industry's resistance to unions (p. 70).
- **Olson's reply:** Employer ideologues may explain some violence. But radical political movements, more offensive to such ideologues, caused less violence than conservative business unionism. So ideology must not have been the ultimate cause (p. 71).
- **Olson's explanation:** During a strike the supply of labor shifts left, so those who keep working (or strikebreakers) get wages that are if anything higher. Strikers get nothing. All the economic incentives therefore favor crossing the picket line, and coercion to stop this is unsurprising (p. 71).
- Violence is apparently greatest when unions first try to organize a firm. If the employer wins early tests the union is apt to disappear. If the union wins, not crossing picket lines becomes habit and peaceful bargaining follows (p. 71).
- **Henry George's view:** labor associations can raise wages only by force, whether applied actively, applied passively or held in reserve. Those who speak of unions raising wages by moral suasion alone are like those who tell of "tigers who live on oranges" (p. 71).
- The coercion argument need not attack unions. Some students argue that when a majority of a bargaining unit votes to strike, law should bar all of the unit from working, leaving compulsion to the police (pp. 71–72).

*Intuition note.* In a strike, each worker who keeps working gets wages that are if anything higher, while strikers get nothing, so each has a reason to cross the picket line. Olson sees this need for coercion as the central explanation of strike violence, and notes that employers also used violence (pp. 70–71).

**Selective incentives (positive).** Some unions give noncollective benefits only to members (p. 72).
- The Amalgamated Society of Engineers (1851), the first viable large British national union, was by Cole's account "a Trade Union and a Friendly Society almost in equal measure" (insurance, unemployment and sickness benefit) (p. 72).
- US railroad brotherhoods attracted members with insurance, since accident rates were high and insurers often refused railroad workers. The conductors' union at first stressed insurance almost exclusively (p. 72).
- In periods when the insurance programs of some railroad unions lost money, those unions had to rely mainly on seniority rights, which contracts guaranteed to members only. The railroad unions were for certain periods the only major national unions without some form of compulsory membership. The Locomotive Engineers' newspaper said the closed shop (for shop craft unions) and the senior rule (for train service brotherhoods) are the "backbone" of both (p. 73).
- Few examples can be found of large unions that support themselves primarily by noncollective benefits. Most unions do offer insurance, welfare benefits, seniority and, above all, grievance handling. A nonmember knows his grievance may be the last handled if he stays out (p. 73).
- Federation provides further strength. Members belong to small locals (small-group advantages). The national supplies experts, a central strike fund ("strike insurance") and access to employment for migrants (p. 74).

**Why these now matter less.**
- Many locals have over a thousand members, too large to be effective social units. Sayles and Strauss found social activity limited to picnics, dances and parties, and it was "not unusual" to see a union put 10 per cent of its treasury into a social affair (p. 74).
- Government social security and unemployment insurance and the spread of private insurers have probably made union insurance much less useful for attracting members (p. 75).
- A legal duty to represent all workers in the unit, given in return for "exclusive jurisdiction", reduces the grievance incentive to join, though representation of nonmembers is presumably less vigorous (p. 75).
- Conclusion: smallness and noncollective benefits "can probably now explain only the exceptional union". Compulsory membership and coercive picket lines are the usual source of membership. Roughly 95 per cent of unionized workers in recent years were covered by "union security" or dues check-off schemes. "Right-to-work" laws exist in some states but are seldom enforced (pp. 75–76).

**Assumption for the rest of the chapter.** Union gains (wages, conditions, legislation) generally cannot be withheld from any worker in the group. So most union achievements offer the rational worker no incentive to join, because his individual effort would not noticeably change the outcome and he would get the benefits anyway. The chapter assumes, in an "oversimplified, but surely basically correct" way, that unions since becoming national work mainly for the common interests of large groups (p. 76).

### B. Labor-union growth in theory and practice (pp. 76–87)

**Perlman's theory (his view).** Perlman said American unions emphasize collective bargaining through "job consciousness", a belief that jobs are scarce, rooted in the pessimism of manual workers. He inferred this from union rules: "job control" (members first hired, last fired), the closed shop as a way to "conserve" scarce jobs, and limits on dismissal to share jobs by a "communism of opportunity" (pp. 76–77). **Olson's contrast:** his presumption, based on the concept of latent groups, is that such policies are vital to any large union's strength and existence and reflect organizational imperatives, not endemic pessimism (p. 77).

**Historical evidence against Perlman** (suggestive only, p. 82).
- *1897–1904:* membership rose from 447,000 to 2,072,000 in a prosperous, high-employment period, so there was less reason for pessimism. Growth followed union-shop strike victories in 1897–98. Recognition strikes rose from 140 (1897) to 748 (1904). Unions demanded written closed-shop terms, which provoked an employer open-shop campaign (NAM attack on the closed shop in 1903; the Theodore Roosevelt administration also helped fan public opposition). Unions won half to two thirds of recognition strikes in 1901–03 but 37 per cent in 1904, and membership dipped only slightly. Olson warns that the strike and lockout figures may be inaccurate (p. 78, note 32).
- *1935–45:* growth came with rising employment, then wartime labor shortage. The Wagner Act (1935, upheld by the Supreme Court in 1937) made bargaining public policy. Recognition then needed only a majority vote in a representation election. It banned company unions and discrimination against unionists, and permitted the closed shop (p. 79). Membership rose strongly in 1937 (the text reads "SS per cent"; see Reading notes). Strikes for union recognition rose from 19 per cent of all strikes in 1933 to 47 per cent in 1935 and 57.8 per cent in 1937, and the workers involved from 73,000 (1932) to 288,000 (1935) to 1,160,000 (1937) (pp. 79–80, note 40). Olson admits the correlation does not prove strikes caused the gains, but judges it "quite likely" that union-security strikes boosted membership (note 40, p. 80).
- *World War II:* membership grew a great deal in 1942–44 with few strikes. The government's "maintenance of membership" rule required anyone who joined to remain a member until the next contract. It added compulsion and freed unions to concentrate on recruiting new members (pp. 80–81).
- *World War I:* it appears there was again overfull employment, and government favor (War Labor Board, railway recognition during nationalization). Toner reports +2,503,100 members from 1915 to 1920, with closed shop conditions expanding (pp. 81–82).
- **Olson's conclusion:** job control and union-shop provisions grew in periods of growing employment or labor shortage. Unions seem to have sought job control "to strengthen, expand, and stabilize unions as organizations", not to protect scarce jobs (p. 82).

**A stronger argument: the demand for labor.** Raising wages reduces the quantity of labor demanded. A wage-raising union therefore cannot be dominated by pessimism about job scarcity, and wage demands conflict with Perlman's "communism of opportunity". If demand were very inelastic, wage gains would cost few jobs, and then workers would have no reason to be pessimistic either (pp. 82–83). Ulman: closed-shop policies were used mainly to bring in new members, although keeping those workers out would presumably make old members' jobs more secure. Olson therefore sees this as inconsistent with the idea that the instruments of compulsion are used mainly to preserve job opportunities (p. 83).

**Why business unionism succeeded.**
- **Perlman:** the AFL (1886, Gompers) succeeded by dropping politics for job control once the frontier closed and optimism turned to pessimism. **Commons:** earlier general organizations failed through emphasis on politics. He tied the change to the passing of the frontier "safety valve" and the widening of the market (pp. 83–84).
- **Olson:** a union bargaining with an employer can often make membership a condition of employment, because members refuse to work with nonunionists. A purely political union has no such lever and does not deal with the employer. It also would have no excuse for compulsion, which would seem "altogether anomalous" in a democracy (p. 84).

**Participation paradox.**
- Participation in most unions is low. Locals studied often had under 5 per cent attendance, and leaders called apathy a major problem (p. 85). Opponents could argue that this shows workers do not want compulsory unionism. But secret-ballot union-shop elections under Taft-Hartley (majority of those eligible needed) showed the opposite. In the first four months unions won all but four of 664 elections with over 90 per cent of voters in favor. In four years 44,795 union shops were authorized and 97 per cent of elections were won. In 1951 the election requirement was dropped (p. 85).
- Over 90 per cent will not attend meetings, yet over 90 per cent vote to compel themselves to belong (p. 86). In a Machinists survey (District 9), attendance fell after fines for absence ended. Only 29 per cent were satisfied with attendance. **The Rosens' inference:** the members were probably inconsistent, since the condition is in their power to change (p. 86).
- **Olson's reply:** they are rational. A strong union is in their interest and high attendance helps, but an individual has no economic incentive to attend, since he gets the benefits anyway and alone cannot add noticeably (p. 86). The Rosens' "inconsistency" is therefore not inconsistency.
- Analogies: voters back higher taxes while paying as little as the law allows. Farmers raise output despite inelastic demand, then vote for controls that cut output (p. 87).

*Intuition note.* Wanting everyone to take part, while not taking part yourself, is consistent when your own effort changes little and the benefit comes either way. This is also why members can vote overwhelmingly for compulsory membership while rarely attending (p. 86).

**Summary of B.** The union shop is not explained by pessimism about jobs. It is highly important to union strength and stability. The union as organization needs "job control". Exceptions: small locals in very small workplaces, large unions with very attractive insurance, and perhaps brief periods of irrational emotional contribution. Olson doubts that large, durable national unions exist without some compulsion, and doubts that ideological outbursts would outlast the Locofocos or the Knights of Labor (p. 87).

### C. The closed shop and economic freedom in the latent group (pp. 88–91)

- **Right-to-work argument (opponents' view):** a firm must please customers, so a union should face the test of an open shop. **Olson:** if the profit motive that moves consumers and businessmen also moves workers, right-to-work enforcement "would bring about the death of trade unions". A rational worker will not voluntarily contribute to a large union (p. 88).
- Arguments in terms of "rights" are therefore misleading. Olson says there are intelligent arguments against unions, but none of them can rest solely on the premise that compulsory unionism restricts freedom unless the argument is extended to all coercion supporting collective services. Taxation for police and courts infringes "rights" no less than the union shop (pp. 88–89). The police and courts are presumably more vital than unions, but that only shifts the debate to whether society is better off with stronger or weaker unions (p. 89).
- **Wicksell's view:** coercion is "always an evil in itself", so taxes need unanimous consent. He saw that collective services cannot be financed through a market, and so required (virtually) unanimous votes on appropriations. Olson notes that this ignores redistribution and hidden preferences (p. 89, note 65). Keynes opposed conscription because it denied the citizen's right to decide whether to fight (p. 90).
- **Olson's argument:** Wicksell and Keynes only apply consistently the premise behind "right to work". The parallel rights would be the "right not to fight" and the "right to spend". In collective bargaining, war and basic government services the benefits go to everyone, and compulsion is involved and "has to be" (p. 90). A consistent critic must follow Wicksell and Keynes or argue that unions are so harmful, ineffective or unimportant that their viability and privileges do not matter (pp. 90–91).
- Caveat: if a closed shop is used to keep certain workers out (e.g. by race), compulsion is not needed for survival and the argument does not apply (note 69, p. 91).
- Union and state are analogous: both mostly provide collective benefits to large groups, and neither the member nor the taxpayer will sacrifice more than he is forced to (p. 91). Olson notes the Hegelian view that the state differs in kind from other organizations, and Aristotle's view that it shares features with other associations (note 70, p. 91).

### D. Government intervention and economic freedom in the latent group (pp. 91–97)

Three aspects of the freedom controversy (p. 92):
1. Whether big government leads to dictatorship (conservatives) or prevents it through planning and welfare (others). Not relevant here (pp. 92–93).
2. Who imposes the restraint, a democratic majority in the group's interest or a dictator. Most people find majority-imposed compulsion less objectionable. In the special case of unanimous self-imposed rules there is no more infringement than in a signed contract. Important, but not central here (p. 93).
3. Economic liberty itself, freedom from coercive control of one's economic life, whatever the political arrangements. This is the aspect studied. Its importance is a matter of values, but its existence is not (p. 94).

**Result.** Goods that everyone in a group must get if anyone does are unsuited to the market and are produced only if everyone is forced to pay a share. Many government services are of this kind. They replace free individual decisions with collective decisions backed by force. Improvements in defense, police and courts at least cannot be financed without in some sense reducing economic freedom, by raising taxes and so reducing freedom to spend (pp. 94–95).

A public corporation producing a product need not reduce anyone's economic freedom. Consumers and workers are not necessarily less free than with a private firm (p. 95). **Conclusion:** government restricts economic freedom when it provides collective goods, but need not when it produces noncollective goods (p. 95).
- The result is paradoxical. Traditional services (army, police) most restrict freedom, while socialist enterprise need not. Conservatives favoring military spending restrict economic freedom as well as socialists (p. 95).
- Governments hold a monopoly on violence, so they can restrict freedom with any activity. Giving noncollective goods away free reduces economic freedom (p. 95). Public corporations that bar private competition could restrict freedom to become an entrepreneur, but this affects few people (note 83, p. 96). The common belief that bigger government means less freedom may owe something to the association of government with traditional services, taxes and conscription (p. 96).

**Final claim.** Whether economic freedom must be curtailed depends largely on whether collective goods are provided, not on public or private institutions. Private cartels that discipline underpricing firms restrict freedom. A large union that tries to fulfill its basic function and survive "must be coercive". This is because collective bargaining is a collective good for a large group, like law, order and defense. Government, a union or any organization can provide noncollective goods without restricting freedom. Many other factors also matter and the subject is more complex than discussed. But the "conventional creed" that unions should not coerce because they are private, and that a larger public sector inevitably costs freedom, rests on an inadequate understanding that ignores the collective/noncollective distinction (pp. 96–97).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A union of 100 workers. Assume (for illustration) that the wage gain each worker receives is proportional to total dues collected. Full dues of 100 × $100 = $10,000 yield a gain of $400 per worker.
- If everyone pays: each nets $400 − $100 = $300.
- Suppose one worker alone stops paying. Dues fall by $100 (1 per cent of $10,000), so everyone's gain falls by 1% × $400 = $4. He saves $100 and loses $4, a net gain of $96.
- So each worker is better off not paying, whatever others do. If all follow this, dues are $0 and the gain is $0, leaving everyone worse off than the $300 net.
- A rule that makes dues compulsory (a union shop) restores the $300. A worker could therefore rationally vote for the union shop (to get $300) while, absent compulsion, not paying voluntarily or attending meetings. This is the pattern of the 90 per cent vote versus under 5 per cent attendance (pp. 85–86).

## Concepts
- [[collective action problem]] — individually rational non-support of a union that benefits all workers; the chapter's main engine (p. 76)
- [[public good]] — here "collective good": a benefit that cannot be withheld from any one in the group (union gains, taxes-funded services) (pp. 76, 94)
- [[free rider]] — a worker who gets union gains without supporting the union; unions are known to worry about him (p. 76)
- [[selective incentive]] — here the "positive" kind: noncollective benefits (insurance, seniority, grievance handling) given to those who join and denied to those who do not (pp. 72–73)
- latent group (new) — a large group seeking a collective good, for which an individual's effort would have no noticeable effect (as used in the chapter's argument; the term is not defined in these pages) (pp. 68, 76, 84)
- compulsory membership / coercion (new) — closed shop, union shop, picket lines and maintenance of membership; Olson's main explanation of large unions' survival (pp. 68, 75)
- job consciousness (Perlman) (new) — Perlman's theory that workers' belief in job scarcity explains union job-control policies; Olson rejects it (pp. 76–77)
- economic freedom (new) — freedom from coercive control of one's economic life, e.g. freedom to spend one's income; the third, "most proper" sense (pp. 93–94)
- small-group advantage (new) — small groups can better provide themselves with collective goods and offer social benefits (p. 67)

## Canonical instances
- [[amalgamated-society-of-engineers]] — the first large British national union to prove viable; it offered a wide range of noncollective benefits (p. 72)
- [[railroad-brotherhoods]] — insurance and seniority as selective incentives; lacked compulsory membership for some periods (pp. 72–73)
- [[wagner-act-and-maintenance-of-membership]] — law and wartime rules that Olson says apparently helped bring about large membership gains in a period of full employment (pp. 79–81)
- [[taft-hartley-union-shop-elections]] — workers overwhelmingly voting to compel themselves (p. 85)
- [[rosen-machinists-attendance]] — dissatisfaction with low attendance without personal attendance (p. 86)
- [[wicksell-unanimity-taxation]] — consistent "no coercion" position applied to taxes (p. 89)
- [[keynes-conscription]] — opposition to conscription as consistent liberalism (p. 90)

## Questions this raises
- If compulsion is "usually essential", what limits abuse of that power by union leaders, and how do members hold them accountable?
- The strike-recognition and membership figures show correlation only (note 40). What evidence would separate Olson's explanation from Perlman's more sharply?
- Does the free-rider logic also explain why workers support unions as an institution (e.g. by voting) but not as individuals?
- Does the collective/noncollective test of "economic freedom" really settle which government activities restrict freedom, given values-based disagreements (p. 94)?
