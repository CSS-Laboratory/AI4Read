# Appendix (added in 1971): Olson's later articles and the "political entrepreneur"

## L0 — One line
Olson surveys his later work on collective goods and argues that the "political entrepreneur" idea enriches, and does not overturn, his logic: in large groups the successful entrepreneur is above all an innovator with selective incentives.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This appendix summarises articles Olson wrote after the book. They cover how small groups interact over collective goods, who produces them efficiently, how far a good's benefits reach ("fiscal equivalence"), and why externalities seem to be growing. It then takes up an idea proposed by other writers: the political entrepreneur who helps a group obtain a collective good. Olson welcomes its useful insight, though he notes errors in that literature. He says it does not contradict the book's conclusions. Small groups may be helped by an entrepreneur who saves bargaining costs. Very large "latent" groups need coercion or selective incentives (rewards only for contributors), so the successful entrepreneur there is above all an innovator with selective incentives.

**Prerequisites:** the book's basic argument (large groups do not provide collective goods voluntarily; selective incentives); the idea of an externality (a cost or benefit that falls on third parties); optimal supply.

## L2 — Key ideas
- Small groups usually under-supply a collective good, and burdens are shared disproportionately ("exploitation of the great by the small"); the article with Zeckhauser models this and tests it on alliances (pp. 169–170).
- Differences in how efficiently different parties produce a collective good matter for policy, and ignoring them confused leading writers (p. 170).
- A good's scope matters. Spillovers outside a jurisdiction, or benefits to only a minority inside it, both tend to lead to under-provision (for a minority, absent "some lucky bargaining"). This supports a case for "fiscal equivalence" and many levels of government (pp. 170–172).
- Externalities and collective goods are probably growing in importance. Olson draws three implications: more government tasks, a need for "social indicators" (measures of welfare or "quality of life"), and a possible increase in social conflict (pp. 172–173).
- Olson holds that economics differs from other social sciences mainly in method and assumptions rather than primarily in the objects of inquiry (pp. 173–174).
- Even small groups often fail to reach optimal supply, because bargaining is costly. An entrepreneur can sometimes help, but success is not guaranteed (pp. 176–177).
- In a large (latent) group an entrepreneur cannot get an optimal supply through voluntary bargains, and normally cannot get any; coercion or selective incentives are needed, so the successful entrepreneur is "above all an innovator with selective incentives" (p. 177).
- Entrepreneurship enriches the model and does not contradict it (p. 178).

## L3 — Walkthrough
The appendix has no numbered sections. The headings below are ours, ordered as in the source.

### Purpose and plan (p. 169)
It surveys Olson's related articles and discusses a research idea proposed by others. Some articles are in economists' language. Others were written for mixed audiences (p. 169).

### 1. "An Economic Theory of Alliances" (with Zeckhauser) (pp. 169–170)
- It treats how members of a small group concerned with a collective good should be expected to interact (p. 169).
- Claim: in most circumstances a small group supplies a less than optimal amount. The sharing of burdens also tends to be disproportionate (p. 169).
- This disproportion is called the "exploitation of the great by the small" in the book, which gave it only a few sentences. The article builds a detailed model, applies it and tests its predictions (pp. 169–170).
- The book abstracts from income effects. The article takes them fully into account (p. 170).
- The model conflicts with Lindahl's "voluntary theory of public exchange", and to a lesser extent with Johansen's updated version, and can be used to show defects in that approach. The article does not explicitly criticise them; a fuller monograph touches on them. Applications cover only international organizations and military alliances. The model can apply to other formal or informal groups with limited numbers (p. 170).

*Intuition note:* The book gave only a few sentences to the uneven sharing of burdens in a small group. The article builds a detailed model of it and tests the model against data.

### 2. Efficient production (p. 170)
The literature neglects how efficiently different parties produce collective goods and externalities. Such differences are often decisive for policy. Ignoring them led Buchanan, Kafoglis and Baumol into logical confusion, as "The Efficient Production of External Economies" argues. A fuller treatment is in "Collective Goods, Comparative Advantage, and Alliance Efficiency" (p. 170).

### 3. Scope of collective goods and fiscal equivalence (pp. 170–172)
- Many writers implicitly assume a collective good reaches everyone in the providing nation-state and no one outside. Some goods are local (local airshed pollution control, neighborhood parks). Others can nearly cover the earth (unpatentable research benefits, an international organization) (pp. 170–171).
- Economists generally understand that a government cannot usually be expected to provide a good near the optimal level when a significant proportion of its benefits fall outside its borders, because it finds it in its interest to ignore those spillovers (p. 171).
- Olson argues a similar, less recognised problem arises when only a minority of citizens would benefit. Take a project whose gain exceeds its cost. If costs are paid by jurisdiction-wide taxes and gains go to a minority, there are more losers than gainers. Without "some lucky bargaining" it gets no majority support, so it is provided, if at all, below the optimal degree (p. 171).
- Hence a case for a separate jurisdiction for each good with a unique domain: "The Principle of 'Fiscal Equivalence'" (p. 171).
- Olson says this is too complicated to justify policy conclusions alone. But the arguments suffice to show that both thoroughgoing centralization and maximum decentralization are unsatisfactory. Efficient government demands many jurisdictions and levels (pp. 171–172).
- The argument also gives a framework for proposals to decentralize urban services in large cities with segregated ghetto populations (p. 172).

*Intuition note:* If a government's area is too small, it ignores outsiders' gains. If the area is too big and only a minority inside gain, the project lacks majority support (barring some lucky bargaining). Either way a worthwhile project may be under-provided or not provided.

### 4. Growing importance of externalities (pp. 172–173)
- Olson presumes collective goods and externalities are becoming more numerous and important. Crowding almost certainly raises external diseconomies: carelessness or noise harms no one in a sparse farm area but imposes costs in a crowded city. Education and research probably gain importance, so external economies "may (though this is not certain)" also grow. Government spending on perceived externalities and public goods has greatly increased (p. 172).
- If the argument is correct, there are three implications:
  1. More problems need government action. This does not imply that the public sector must soon grow beyond present levels, since governments may now do things that could better be left to the private sector. But there has been a secular increase in what governments need to do, and if the apparent growth continues the burden will become greater (p. 172).
  2. National Income is still very useful but less satisfactory as a measure of welfare. Supplementary "social indicators" are needed (congestion, pollution, crime, health). Most measure the quantity, not the money value, of an externality or collective good. See *Toward A Social Report* (p. 173).
  3. Olson argues that more collective goods and externalities can add to divisiveness and conflict. Everyone in a good's domain must accept about the same level and type, while with private goods each person picks his own mix. If correct, Parsons-style sociological explanations of social cohesion are unsatisfactory (p. 173).

### 5. Economics versus other social sciences (pp. 173–174)
- Olson argues that it is not primarily the objects of inquiry, but mainly method and assumptions, that distinguish economics from the other social sciences (pp. 173–174).
- Microeconomic theory is relevant whenever behavior is purposive and resources cannot achieve all purposes. Parsonian sociology is also general, so the two can be contrasted on the same problems (p. 174).
- Barry's *Sociologists, Economists, and Democracy* compares Parsonian writers with Downs and with Olson. They differ at points, but Barry finds a contrast roughly consistent with Olson's (p. 174).

### 6. The political entrepreneur (pp. 174–178)
- **Source of the idea.** Schumpeter's entrepreneur was a businessman in private goods. Wagner, Salisbury and Frohlich–Oppenheimer (also with Young) apply the idea to leaders who help organize collective goods. Olson sees logical errors in this literature, but it holds a useful insight (p. 175, and n. 18, which names errors especially in the *World Politics* article).
- **Wagner's point (n. 14).** With democratic institutions and vote-seeking leaders, an unorganized group may still get some consideration. Olson says this is correct and compatible with the book. But organization matters a good deal, and differences in it often lead to inefficiency and inequity (p. 175).
- **Small groups (p. 176).** Without selective incentives most groups cannot supply an optimal amount, if any. Those that can are small enough to bargain. Yet even then optimality is not assured:
  - If bargaining costs are ignored, members have an incentive to keep bargaining until they reach optimality (p. 176).
  - But individuals will often have an incentive to "hold out" for a better bargain, even to threaten never to participate unless their terms are met, and then need to carry out the threat to stay credible (p. 176).
  - Bargaining costs time. Each day without optimal supply loses benefits, and future benefits are discounted at positive interest rates (p. 176).
  - A changing world means bargaining may have to restart (p. 176).
  - So even small groups often lack an optimal supply, if any (p. 176).
- **Entrepreneur in a small group.** A leader who is trusted or feared, can spot bluffs, or saves bargaining time can sometimes arrange something better for all. If he senses that the outcome is more efficient when each member pays a share of the marginal cost of additional units equal to his share of their benefits, and others do not sense this, he can suggest arrangements leaving everyone better off (as is evident from book pp. 30–31) (p. 176). He may take a gain for himself. Together with some people's liking for leading, this means entrepreneurs are often in ample supply. Success is not certain; sometimes the difficulty and expense are too great for him to succeed or even try (p. 177).
- **Large (latent) groups (p. 177).** An entrepreneur cannot supply an optimal amount through bargains or voluntary cost-sharing, and normally cannot supply any. Each member would get only an infinitesimal share of the gain from his own contribution. Footnote 19 points to one logically possible exception when behavior is rational (book pp. 48–49). Olson writes "I may be wrong", but thinks it occurs so rarely in practice, if at all, that it is hardly worth mentioning (n. 19, p. 177). So coercion or a selective incentive is needed. The gains from organizing such groups are often enormous, so entrepreneurs strive hard, and many fail. Some find or create selective incentives (Chapter 6). The successful entrepreneur is "above all an innovator with selective incentives" (p. 177). Because large groups often sit inside coalitions and contain subgroups, he is often also a maker of bargains (pp. 177–178).
- **Conclusion.** Adding entrepreneurship does not contradict the model's logic or invalidate its conclusions. It enriches the model. Contributions of different authors are cumulative (p. 178).

*Intuition note:* In a small group the entrepreneur can sometimes help by saving bargaining time, being trusted or feared, or guessing who is bluffing. In a large group voluntary bargains normally cannot supply any of the good, so the successful entrepreneur is above all one who finds or creates selective incentives.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A group has 1,000 members. Each $1 contributed buys collective benefits worth $3 to the group, shared equally. Group gain is positive ($3 > $1).
- One member's share of his own $1: $3 / 1,000 = $0.003.
- Net to him: $0.003 − $1 = −$0.997. He does not contribute.
- An entrepreneur offers contributors a private benefit worth $2 to the member, costing the entrepreneur $0.50 (an assumed figure).
- Net now: $0.003 − $1 + $2 = +$1.003. He contributes. (We do not model how the entrepreneur is paid; the text says only that he may get something for himself, p. 177.)

The entrepreneur's role here is the selective incentive, not bargaining.

## Concepts
- [[selective incentive]] — reward that can be given only to those who contribute to the group effort; large groups need it or coercion (p. 177)
- [[collective action problem]] — most groups cannot supply themselves with optimal amounts, if any, of a collective good without selective incentives; those that can must be small enough to bargain (p. 176)
- [[public good]] — the text mostly says "collective good" (and "public goods" once, p. 172); the benefits of such a good may reach only a locality or nearly the whole earth (pp. 170–171)
- [[latent-group]] (new) — a "sufficiently large" group, where an entrepreneur cannot possibly get an optimal supply through bargains or voluntary cost-sharing, and normally cannot get any (p. 177)
- [[political-entrepreneur]] (new) — leader who helps organize collective-good provision (p. 175)
- [[fiscal-equivalence]] (new) — the principle behind the case for a separate jurisdiction for each collective good with a unique domain (p. 171)
- [[exploitation-of-great-by-small]] (new) — disproportionate burden-sharing in small groups (p. 169)
- [[externality]] (new) — benefit or cost of an activity that falls on others; the text gives examples (garbage, noise, education, research) but no formal definition (p. 172)
- [[social-indicators]] (new) — measures of welfare or "quality of life"; most measure the quantity, not the money value, of an externality or collective good (p. 173)
- [[bargaining-costs-holdout]] (new) — delay, hold-out, threats and discounting that often keep small groups from an optimal supply (p. 176)
