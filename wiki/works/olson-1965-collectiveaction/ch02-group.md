# Group Size and Group Behavior

## L0 — One line
Small groups often achieve their collective goals voluntarily and large "latent" groups do not, because in a large group no individual's contribution is noticeable, and social pressure works mainly in small groups.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Olson asks why group size matters for collective action. He argues that small groups are in a better position because some or all members have an incentive to see that the group does not fail, and members may also use social pressure on one another. In a large (latent) group, a member's contribution is imperceptible, so rational members do not contribute, even with perfect consensus and even if they are unselfish. Social rewards and punishments are selective incentives, but they work mainly in face-to-face groups. Large groups can use them only as federations of small groups. Olson concludes that the assumption that small-group results can simply be scaled up is unwarranted for groups with a common interest, and that the traditional theory of voluntary associations seems incomplete.

**Prerequisites:** the distinctions among privileged, intermediate and latent groups (not defined on these pages); collective (public) goods; selective incentives; "rational" as efficient pursuit of one's aims.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- In large groups, members expect their own effort to change little, so contributions to collective goods fall as group size rises (pp. 53, 55).
- Olson cites evidence consistent with this: James's committee sizes, Hare's experiments, and the partnership and corporation examples (pp. 53–56).
- Homans' historical point about durable small groups is accepted, but Olson says it does not follow that large groups can prevent failure by copying small-group methods (p. 57).
- If Olson's distinctions have meaning, the assumption that small-group findings scale up to large groups with a common collective interest is unwarranted, and the traditional theory of voluntary associations seems incomplete (pp. 57–59).
- Perfect consensus does not by itself make a large latent group act. Olson assumes it, which makes his results "all the stronger" (pp. 59–60).
- Social incentives are selective incentives, so they do not weaken the theory and if anything strengthen it. They work mainly in small, face-to-face groups, or in federations of them (pp. 60–63).
- The latent-group result holds for selfless as well as selfish members, provided they are rational. Imperceptible sacrifices are futile (pp. 64–65).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### A. The coherence and effectiveness of small groups (pp. 53–57)
- **Meetings.** In a large meeting, each participant knows his effort will probably not change the outcome. He is affected by the decision however hard he studies, so he may not study the issues as he would if deciding alone. The decisions are public goods to the participants, and each one's contribution shrinks as the meeting grows. Hence organizations create committees and small leadership groups (p. 53).
- **Evidence.** John James found that "action taking" subgroups were smaller than "non-action taking" ones. In a bank the averages were 6.5 and 14 members. Average subcommittee sizes were: U.S. Senate 5.4, House 7.8, Oregon state government 4.7, Eugene city government 5.3 (p. 54). Hare's controlled experiments with groups of five and twelve boys found the groups of five generally superior (p. 54). Simmel is cited as saying small, centripetally organized groups usually use all their energies, while in large groups forces remain "much oftener potential" (p. 54).
- **Partnerships.** With prearranged profit shares, earnings are a collective good to the partners. As partners multiply, each one's incentive to work for the firm weakens. This is only one reason partnerships persist when small, but it could be decisive in a really large one (p. 55). It need not apply to "sleeping" partners who only provide capital, and it does not take account of the fact that in many cases each partner is liable for the firm's losses (p. 55, n. 6).
- **Corporations.** Management tends to control large corporations although stockholders have legal power to dismiss it. The typical stockholder's effort will probably fail. If it succeeds, most of the gain goes to other stockholders, since he owns a trifling share. So he has no incentive to challenge management (p. 55). The exception is the stockholder who wants the manager's position himself, because he is not working for a collective good. Most attempts to overthrow management start with such people (pp. 55–56). Corporations with few stockholders are controlled by them de facto as well as de jure (p. 56).
- **Homans.** Homans says the small group has cohered through history while civilizations collapsed, and infers that, if civilization is to stand, it must keep some features of the small group (pp. 56–57). Olson says this supports his theory but that Homans' deduction is not wholly consistent with it. The inference assumes small-group methods are more effective, which is not necessarily true. The small group is in a better position from the start because some members have an incentive to see that it does not fail. So the large group cannot be assumed to prevent failure by copying small-group methods (p. 57).

*Intuition note:* Whether a group acts well depends on whether its members face the group's incentives personally. In a small group some or all members do. Copying a small group's methods does not give a big group that situation (p. 57).

### B. Problems of the traditional theories (pp. 57–60)
- **Scaling small-group research.** Much small-group research assumes results can be transferred to larger groups by a scale factor (p. 57). Olson says that, if his distinctions have any meaning, the assumption is unwarranted, at least so long as groups have a common collective interest. A privileged group can expect its needs will probably be met one way or another. An intermediate group has a fair chance that voluntary action will work. A large latent group cannot act in its common interest while its members are free to pursue individual interests (p. 58).
- **Voluntary associations.** The traditional theory (Chapter I) explains small and large groups by the same causes. In its sophisticated form it says large associations take over functions of declining small primary groups ("structural differentiation"). Olson asks how members are recruited if a "joiner instinct" is rejected. His answer is that however beneficial the function, an individual in a latent group has no incentive to join, and there is no presumption that a latent group can organize (p. 58). The theory is therefore mistaken to the extent that it implicitly assumes latent groups act as small groups do. This suggests it is incomplete. The cited evidence also indicates much less participation in large voluntary organizations than the theory would suggest (p. 59).
- **Consensus.** Cohesion is often treated as depending mainly on consensus. Olson grants that lack of consensus hurts group action. But perfect consensus on the goal and on the means does not always bring achievement of the group goal (p. 59). In a large latent group there is no tendency to organize voluntarily even with perfect consensus. Olson assumes perfect consensus, which makes his results "all the stronger": if they hold with it, *a fortiori* they hold where consensus is incomplete. He therefore separates obstacles due to lack of consensus from those due to lack of individual incentives (p. 60).

*Intuition note:* Agreement on what the group wants answers the question "what do we want?". It does not answer "why should I be the one to pay?" (pp. 59–60).

### C. Social incentives and rational behavior (pp. 60–65)
- **Social incentives are selective.** People also seek prestige, respect and friendship. Among friends or club members, someone who leaves the burden to others may lose socially more than he gains economically (p. 60). Social status and acceptance are individual, noncollective goods, so social rewards and sanctions are selective incentives that can mobilize a latent group. They can pick out the recalcitrant for ostracism and the cooperative for inclusion. If anything, this strengthens the analysis (p. 61).
- **Other incentives (n. 17).** Moral, erotic and psychological incentives could mobilize a latent group only if they work as selective incentives. A guilt that struck contributors and non-contributors alike could not help (p. 61, n. 17). Olson does not use moral motives to explain his examples, for three reasons (p. 61, n. 17):
  1. Motives cannot be proven empirically, so reliance on them could make the theory untestable.
  2. Other grounds explain the cases.
  3. Most pressure groups work for their own gains.
- **Social pressure needs small groups.** In an oligopoly a price-cutting "chiseler" may be resented. In a perfectly competitive industry the high-output seller is usually admired. Olson suggests there are perhaps two reasons (p. 62):
  1. In a large group each member is so small that snubbing a defector would be pointless, since his act is not decisive.
  2. In a large group not everyone can know everyone, so it is not a friendship group, and a person is not ordinarily affected socially for failing to sacrifice.

  So there is no presumption that social incentives will help latent groups (p. 62).
- **Federal groups.** A latent group divided into small groups that join to form a federation may use social incentives through its parts, if the central body gives them a service. Organizations using selective social incentives to mobilize a latent group must be federations of smaller groups. Otherwise social incentives matter mainly in small groups (p. 63). Privileged and intermediate groups are "twice blessed" with economic and perhaps social incentives. Latent groups always contain more people than could know one another (p. 63). Olson adds that, if the strength of social pressure does vary greatly between small and large groups, this further weakens the traditional theory of voluntary organizations (p. 63).
- **Mass-media pressure (n. 18).** Propaganda might create something like face-to-face pressure. But a group cannot finance such propaganda unless it is already organized, and it may not be able to organize until it has been subjected to it, so this is probably not ordinarily sufficient by itself (p. 63, n. 18).
- **Selflessness.** A critic may say that without social pressure people might act selflessly. Olson replies that the latent-group concept does not assume selfishness. It holds whether behavior is selfish or unselfish, as long as it is rational. Even an unselfish member of a large group would not rationally contribute to a public good, since his contribution would not be perceptible (p. 64). The farmer who restricts output out of concern for other farmers makes a futile sacrifice, like a man holding back a flood with a pail. A rational unselfish farmer would direct his philanthropy where it has a perceptible effect (p. 64). The only requirement is that members generally be rational: pursue their aims, selfish or unselfish, by efficient and effective means (pp. 64–65).
- **Conclusion.** The arguments should at least justify treating large and small groups separately. Olson says they are not meant as attacks on earlier interpretations, though some usual explanations of large voluntary associations may need elaboration. All Olson's argument needs is that large latent groups will not organize merely because, as a group, they have reason to, though smaller groups might. Olson says the rest of the book will attempt to prove that most large U.S. economic organizations had to develop special institutions to solve the membership problem (p. 65).

*Intuition note:* Rational action here means choosing efficient means to one's goals. A kind person with a goal that his contribution cannot noticeably advance has no reason to contribute either (pp. 64–65).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A partnership earns an extra \$100 of profit if one partner works hard. Hard work costs that partner \$30 in effort. Profits are split equally, so each partner gets $1/n$ of any gain.
- $n=2$: the worker gets $100/2 = 50 > 30$, so he works.
- $n=3$: $100/3 \approx 33.3 > 30$, so he still works.
- $n=4$: $100/4 = 25 < 30$, so he shirks.
- $n=10$: $100/10 = 10 < 30$, so he shirks.

Working pays only when $n < 100/30 \approx 3.3$. Total gain is \$100 against a cost of \$30, so working is efficient for the group at any $n$, but only small $n$ makes it individually rational. This is a simplified version of Olson's partnership case (p. 55). The numbers are invented.

## Concepts
- [[privileged-group]] (new) — a small group whose collective needs will probably be met one way or another, because some or all members have an incentive to see that it does not fail (pp. 57–58)
- [[intermediate-group]] (new) — a group with a fair chance that voluntary action solves its collective problem (p. 58)
- [[latent-group]] (new) — a large group that cannot act in its common interest so long as its members are free to further their individual interests (p. 58)
- [[public-good]] — here: a meeting's decisions or a partnership's earnings, shared whatever each member contributes (pp. 53, 55)
- [[selective-incentive]] — an incentive that distinguishes contributors from non-contributors; social and moral incentives count only if they do so (p. 61, n. 17)
- [[social-incentive]] (new) — prestige, friendship, ostracism and similar rewards and sanctions; selective and effective mainly in small groups (pp. 60–62)
- [[federal-group]] (new) — a large group built from small groups that use social incentives on its behalf (p. 63)
- [[perfect-consensus-assumption]] (new) — Olson's assumption that members agree on goal and means. It strengthens his results (p. 60)
- [[collective-action-problem]] — a large latent group's lack of any tendency to organize voluntarily, even with perfect consensus (p. 60)

## Canonical instances

## Questions this raises
- How large can a group be before the "intermediate" group becomes "latent"? The chapter gives no threshold.
- If social incentives work only in small groups, what other selective incentives can large organizations use?
- If moral motives cannot be observed, how can a theory like this be tested?
- Does a federation keep its members contributing, or do the small units themselves free-ride?
