# Manufactured publicity, nonpublic opinion, and the welfare state's two competing publics

## L0 — One line
In the welfare-state mass democracy, elections rest on periodically staged publicity that yields only "nonpublic opinion", while the social-welfare state's own constitutional logic points to a different, still open, route: critical publicity inside organizations.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks what voting and public opinion are in a mass democracy where the liberal public of debating private people has decayed. Empirical voting studies show fixed blocs, discussion confined to like-minded groups, and a floating group that tends to be among the least informed. Parties therefore stage publicity with advertising methods. The result is an "opinion climate" and acclamation, not public opinion. The constitutional side of the welfare state points the other way: basic rights become participatory rights, and publicity should extend to all organizations acting in state-related fashion. Which tendency prevails is open, and Habermas says the conditions for critical publicity are no longer "simply utopian."

**Prerequisites:** the liberal bourgeois public sphere (earlier chapters); basic constitutional rights; the distinction between public opinion and mere opinion.

## L2 — Key ideas
- The voter role still carries liberal expectations of rational, informed, discussing citizens (Berelson's traits). Habermas says the low degree to which voters conform can only be understood sociologically through the public sphere's structural and functional transformation (pp. 211–212).
- Voting traces of the liberal public survive: more active voters are male, married, higher-status, organized (pp. 212–213). Opinion leaders are higher-status, but the informed, active core strata are the least inclined to submit their views seriously to discussion (p. 213).
- Floating voters come predominantly from the less interested, less informed, apathetic citizens. Parties target them through adaptation to a consumer attitude, not through enlightenment (pp. 214–215).
- Mass-media "integration culture" works as an ideology of behavior, and political marketing imitates product advertising. The result is an "opinion climate" and acclamation (pp. 215–218).
- The 1957 Bundestag example shows manipulation tied to real concessions, yet votes summed from it are not public opinion, because they were neither formed rationally nor in discussion (pp. 219–221).
- The social-welfare state continues the liberal state's aim of ordering society. Its basic rights (press, assembly, association, property, family) shift from exclusion to participation and active state guarantees (pp. 222–229).
- Organized private interests produce "refeudalization." Two tendencies compete: staged publicity over a mediatized public versus critical publicity within organizations (pp. 231–233). The outcome is open (p. 235).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### §22 Manufactured Publicity and Nonpublic Opinion: The Voting Behavior of the Population
(The unit begins mid-§21; its first sentence says that something about the organizations stands in inverse relation to staged, manipulative publicity, p. 211. Its subject is not on this page.)

**The citizen as client and voter (pp. 211–212).**
- Citizens entitled to services relate to the state mainly through a demanding, unpolitical attitude of expecting provision, not through participation (p. 211).
- Their "political" interests are reduced primarily to claims specific to occupational branches, and representing these claims effectively requires delegating them to large organizations. Whatever remains to personal decision is taken over by the parties for an election organized as a vote (p. 211).
- The public sphere's decay shows in that parties must now periodically generate a public sphere of sorts. Elections no longer result from an existing conflict of opinions within an institutionally protected public sphere (p. 211).
- Yet parliamentary democracy still counts on the "liberal fictions." The expectations placed on the voter mirror, socio-psychologically, the conditions of the old debating public. The voter should take part in discussion and help find the standard of right action with the general interest in mind (pp. 211–212).
- Berelson lists the voter's "personality structure": interest in public affairs, information, stable principles, accurate observation, communication and discussion, rational behavior, regard for community interest. Sociological constituents of the public sphere have become psychological traits (p. 212).
- Many empirical investigations find that the mass of voters shows these democratic behavior patterns to a low degree, even by superficial criteria such as political activity and participation in discussions. Habermas says that if so, this deviation can only be understood sociologically in connection with the structural and functional transformation of the public sphere itself (p. 212).

**Echoes of the liberal public (pp. 212–214).**
- Ideally the vote ended a public controversy, and voters were predominantly propertied, well-educated heads of households from the urban bourgeois strata (p. 212).
- Today's more active voters echo this: males usually vote more than females, married more than unmarried, higher income and education more than lower, businessmen relatively many, ages 35–55 highest. Members of private associations seem still to vote more than the unorganized (p. 213).
- Katz and Lazarsfeld: political opinion flows vertically down from higher-status groups, unlike fashions and consumption habits, which spread horizontally within strata. Opinion leaders are usually wealthier, better educated, better positioned (p. 213).
- But these interested, informed core strata are the least inclined to submit their views to discussion. An assumed opinion often hardens into rigid habit (p. 213). Opinions that need not bear public exposure do not become public opinion without the communication flow of a rationally debating public (p. 213).
- The most discussion-active (relatively best-informed) people tend to do no more than confirm each other's ideas and at best influence only the hesitant. Political discussions are for the most part confined to in-groups (family, friends, neighbors) with a homogeneous climate of opinion (p. 213).
- Floating voters come mainly from the less interested, less informed, apathetic reservoir. So those most disposed to avoid discussion-formed opinion are the ones most open to influence by the staged election publicity (p. 214).

*Intuition note:* The people who discuss most mostly talk with those who already agree. The people who might be moved tend to avoid discussion, but they are the ones most open to influence by campaigns. So, on this evidence, neither group mainly forms opinion through public argument.

**Immobilization and the target group (pp. 214–215).**
- Most voters are fixed in blocs. Each party's core constituency has two groups: a small "active" minority (party or organization members, or unorganized but well-informed, strongly involved voters who are usually also influential as opinion leaders) and a majority that is equally rigid (p. 214).
- Fixation comes partly from stereotyped perception of group interests and partly from common-sense assumptions and prejudices from past, inherited experiences, specific to generations, denominations, ethnic groups (p. 214).
- Heterogeneous motives enter formally identical votes. They are all the more open to being averaged into an illusory consensus while their presuppositions stay undiscussed (p. 214).
- Between the blocs are independents: compromisers, neutrals, the ambivalent, the apathetic (Janowitz). Depending on the criteria, they are between a fourth and almost half of the electorate and include nonvoters and marginal voters (pp. 214–215).
- Nonvoters are the worst informed and least firmly democratic group. The floating vote is similar, with qualifications: "Independent voters tend to be those who know and care the least" (p. 215).
- Even so, they are the election managers' target. Parties court them through adaptation to their unpolitical consumer attitude, not enlightenment (p. 215).
- Habermas endorses Janowitz's question whether these efforts, which rely heavily on mass media and promotional devices, misuse limited resources. Advertising also affects other voter groups (p. 215).
- Voter participation is far less connected to programmatic goals than to an appealing image of the leading candidates (p. 215).

**Integration culture, ideology, advertising (pp. 215–217).**
- Periodic staging of publicity fits the decayed bourgeois public sphere. The mass-media "integration culture" is unpolitical in intent but is itself a political ideology. Political programs must seek concordance with it, not compete (p. 215).
- Mannheim's diagnosed collapse of political ideology seems to be only one side of the process that Aron calls the end of the ideological age. The other is that ideology adapts to consumer culture and continues to press for conformity (p. 215).
- This false consciousness is no longer a coherent system of ideas but a nexus of other-directed consumption behavior, a "pseudo-realistic replication of the status quo" (pp. 215–216).
- A block quotation (source in note 97, not given here) calls mass-culture ideology a parody of "Become what you are": glorifying duplication of existing conditions without transcendence or critique (p. 216).
- Advertising is the other function taken over by the media-dominated public sphere. Parties influence votes like advertising influences purchases, and an industry of political marketing emerges (p. 216).
- Party agitators give way to advertising experts, neutral in party terms, who market politics in an unpolitical manner. This became dominant after the Second World War with market and opinion research. Resistance in some parties was broken only after electoral setbacks (p. 216).
- The resistance shows that election managers must not only note the disappearance of a genuine public sphere but must in full consciousness promote it themselves. Even politics is socio-psychologically integrated into consumption (p. 216).
- **Riesman (quoted, p. 217):** the "new indifferents" are not necessarily nonvoters. Their political opinions resemble peer-group exchange of consumption preferences, and they believe nothing they do can buy a political package that improves their lives. They tend to view politics as spectators.
- Press and radio, used conventionally, have practically no effect, so media serve as advertising vehicles. Parties address a minority whose vocabulary survey researchers put at about five hundred words (p. 217).
- Party meetings are likewise useful only as advertising events, with those present as unpaid extras for television (p. 217).

**Opinion climate, popularity, limits of manipulation (pp. 217–219).**
- An acclamation-prone mood prevails: "an opinion climate instead of a public opinion" (p. 217).
- Offers calibrated to unconscious inclinations call forth predictable reactions without obligating anyone. The more they work as identification symbols, the less tied they are to programs or issue arguments (pp. 217–218).
- A quoted passage (note 103) says popularity takes the place of individuals' direct relationship to politics, so leaders must be packaged. The popularity index tells a government how much it controls nonpublic opinion (p. 218).
- Popularity is not identical with publicity, but cannot be kept long without it. It is a dependent variable of manufactured publicity, but not of it alone (p. 218).
- Ruling parties therefore create "objective causes," genuine concessions such as lower taxes on alcohol or cigarettes, as publicity vehicles. Adapting to analyzed motives sometimes requires measures that satisfy real needs, so manipulation has natural limits (p. 218).
- Habermas says one should not simply draw the converse conclusion (a quoted formulation, note 104) that the better voters' motives are known, the more the government is manipulated by the "people." The narrower the limits, the stronger the pressure to satisfy motives (p. 218).
- No unambiguous information is available as yet. Even if, hypothetically, narrow limits on manipulation made the acclamation procedure guarantee a far-reaching readiness of government to submit to nonpublic opinion, the conditions of democratic opinion and consensus formation would not be met. The offers reach the subconscious, not will and consciousness (pp. 218–219).
- Such consensus would suit "enlightened absolutism" of an authoritarian welfare regime: "everything for the people, nothing by the people" (p. 219).
- Welfare itself would not be guaranteed. Satisfying a well-established motive does not guarantee that it matches objective interests (p. 219).
- Publicity is a democratic principle not just because anyone may announce opinions. It is realized only as personal opinions evolve through rational-critical debate into public opinion (p. 219).

*Intuition note:* Habermas grants that campaigns at times must give voters something real. But responsiveness to unspoken motives is not democracy, because the voters did not form or test their views by arguing.

**Example: Bundestag election 1957 (pp. 219–221).** Habermas uses it because reliable documentation exists for this party (p. 219). Four measures were, for the most part, decisive for the winning party's publicity work:
1. Restyling the leader's image to offset worries about his age: shown surrounded by his team (p. 219).
2. Appeals to anxiety and security: opponent tied to the Bolshevik danger, the governing party presented as the state itself, slogans in the sense of "no experiments" and holding on to what one has (p. 220).
3. Against price-rise fears: a "holdback agreement" with industry to postpone increases until after the election, plus brand-name and retailer advertising on price stability (p. 220).
4. Social security reform: from May 1957 about 6 million retirees got higher benefits and retroactive payments (p. 220).

All four were tested beforehand and launched by advertising techniques ("the soft sell"; "prosperity for all"). Their individual effectiveness was not assessed. The only binding commitment of the governing parties was assent to the reform, and the opposition also helped pass it, though the government benefited more (p. 220).

- The result is twofold: nonpublic opinion presses the government toward real needs, but the method blocks public opinion in the strict sense (pp. 220–221).
- Decisions made for manipulative purposes escape rational public argument and a plebiscitary no-confidence vote on clearly defined alternatives (p. 221).
- The reform was thoroughly treated in the great daily press but, during its preparatory phase, was never systematically made a topic of public opinion formation. Surveys found most people attached no apposite idea to "dynamic retirement benefits," and it was not an explicit campaign issue. Only its indirect effects served propaganda built on simple improvement stereotypes (p. 221).
- The summed votes were no public opinion because two conditions failed. Opinions were not formed rationally (the symbols matched unconscious processes). And they were not formed in discussion, since reactions stayed private and uncorrected by a debating public (p. 221).
- A disintegrated public was thus reduced to a position where it could be claimed as legitimating compromises without taking part in effective decisions (p. 221).
- Social security belongs to welfare-state protections once left to private autonomy. These conditions ought to be clarified by a politically autonomous public. Yet authority operates less as rational public opinion the more it is generated for an abstract vote amounting to acclamation (p. 222).

### §23 The Political Public Sphere and the Transformation of the Liberal Constitutional State into a Social-Welfare State
**The liberal model (pp. 222–224).**
- The imbalance between what the public sphere does and what a democratic society needs of it is clearest where the transition to the welfare state is legislated (p. 222).
- The first modern constitutions' basic rights guaranteed society as private autonomy, with a limited public authority. The citizenry linked state and society so that political authority would become rational authority (p. 222).
- This assumed the market's inherent justice and equal opportunity to acquire property and independence. Hence the general interest would be guaranteed if market and public sphere were free of domination (pp. 222–223). Power relations would be neutralized in a society of small commodity traders (p. 223).
- The liberal basic rights worked as injunctions, but their intended social function was not merely exclusionary. They were taken to secure equal opportunity in producing wealth and public opinion, though only indirectly, via prohibitions (p. 223).
- So sociologically the constitution was meant to order society as a whole, including private law, against the usual jurist view (p. 223).
- Status negativus (freedom from interference) and status activus (participation) were distinct, as were bourgeois and citizen. Sociologically, their connection is indissoluble (pp. 223–224). The right to vote followed from protecting private dealings in the public sphere (p. 224).
- The two types of right diverged only when the fictitious character of the assumed social order became conscious to the bourgeoisie (p. 224).

**Continuity into the welfare state (pp. 224–226).**
- The change is characterized by continuity, not a break. The welfare state was compelled to shape social conditions to continue the liberal legal tradition of an overall legal order. When the state became the bearer of the societal order, it needed a positive notion of "justice" (p. 224).
- The liberal concept of law was "hollow" in universality and rightness, so formal criteria no longer sufficed. Substantive guarantees, per *iustitia distributiva*, had to replace formal ones (p. 225).
- Distributing growth in the gross national product became a political concern. Public-law interest associations fight over the key, and the state must keep the negotiated balance inside the general interest (p. 225).
- Ipsen reads the welfare clause as a definition of the state's goal. A quoted passage (note 113) adds that its normative effect is an obligation on all state organs to adapt welfare institutions to ongoing demands (p. 225).
- Similar statements hold in other Western democracies, as constitutional text or convention. Examples named: the Weimar Constitution, the French Constitution of 1946, the UN Declaration of 10 December 1948 (p. 225).
- A quoted contrast: liberal freedom is "demarcation" by a self-limiting state. Participation implies an active, distributing state (pp. 225–226). Habermas notes that this contrast abstracts from the functional continuity (p. 226).
- Social rights supplement the liberal ones only because the positive effects of prohibitions no longer come "automatically." Only so can the political order stay faithful to the idea of the public sphere under its transformed conditions (p. 226).
- Liberal rights shift meaning to remain true to their intent. A quoted passage (note 117) raises the question to what extent they should now be reconceived as participatory rights (pp. 226–227).

**Rights reinterpreted (pp. 227–229).**
- In their application to the structurally transformed public sphere, the rights of speech and opinion, association and assembly, and press must no longer be interpreted merely as injunctions but positively, as participation guarantees, if they are to fulfill their original function. Publicist institutions are a societal force that can favor or boycott private interests and "mediatize" individual opinions. Free speech and a free press do not by themselves secure public opinion, and the public is no longer one of equals (p. 227).
- Ridder develops the idea of a freedom of public opinion, giving equal opportunity to take part in public communication. The press's institutional duty to the democratic order complements the old freedom of the press (p. 227).
- Equal access needs an active guarantee by the state (*Gestaltungsgarantie*); mere non-intrusion no longer suffices (pp. 227–228).
- Assembly and association: insofar as they are big, bureaucratized organizations, parties and public-law associations enjoy an oligopoly of effective assembly. Freedom of assembly and association here too needs a guarantee of active promotion (*Gestaltungsgarantie*), which can secure citizen participation only by obligating the organization to fulfill a certain task and to structure its internal order accordingly. To this obligation corresponds the guarantee of certain claims, the so-called party privilege (p. 228).
- Property, free choice of occupation, and training rights partly become participatory, for example claims to a fitting position or education. Partly they are limited by social guarantees (work, tenancy, housing law) (p. 228).
- Rights protecting family, life, liberty, and personal development lose their merely injunctive character. The private sphere has shrunk to family and leisure and needs publicly guaranteed status through democratic participation (pp. 228–229).
- Private autonomy becomes derivative. Social rights now rest on integrating the interests of all state-related organizations, to be done democratically (p. 229).
- **Abendroth (as quoted):** the real alternative is not free individual economic decisions versus state planning. It is whether the masses are subject to the formally private power of those controlling key economic positions, or whether necessary planning is placed under collective control (p. 229). Predictability of legal decisions is limited in both cases, but democratic planning keeps it in general lines, whereas oligopolies bring accidental changes for the weak. The influence of law is thus strengthened by expanding the publicly controlled sphere (pp. 229–230). (The speaker of the closing sentences, note 124, is not clear.)
- **Forsthoff** (whom Habermas calls admittedly justified on this point)**:** the welfare state remains a state financed by taxation (*Steuerstaat*), not per se a society under state tutelage (*Staatsgesellschaft*) (p. 230). Habermas adds that the difference between interfering with income and with control of property is reduced to one of degree. The state would become a society under tutelage only if all politically relevant social power were under democratic control. Abendroth's model therefore marks a direction (pp. 230–231).

*Intuition note:* Under the welfare state, rights that once protected people by keeping the state out now require the state to act. Habermas presents this as continuity: the liberal order's own aims need new means once the market no longer delivers equal opportunity.

**Refeudalization and the two tendencies (pp. 231–233).**
- State and society interpenetrate, producing a "middle sphere" of semipublic, semiprivate relations. What exemption could no longer secure indirectly must now be positively granted, with participation expanded accordingly (p. 231).
- Societal organizations act in a state-related way: in part economic associations that collectively organize formerly individual owners' interests, in part mass organizations that must obtain and defend a private status granted by social legislation. In other words, Habermas says, they must "obtain and defend private autonomy by means of political autonomy" (p. 231).
- Competition of organized interests facing an interventionist administration ("neomercantilism") leads to "refeudalization": political authorities take over functions of commerce and social labor, and societal powers take over political functions (p. 231).
- In the political public sphere, organizations seek compromises with the state and each other, excluding the public as far as possible. They must still obtain plebiscitary agreement from a mediatized public through staged publicity (p. 232).
- Counter-tendency: the welfare state extends the mandate of publicity from state organs to all organizations acting in state-related fashion. In the measure that this is realized, the no longer intact public of individual private people would be replaced by a public of organized private people. Only such a public could, under today's conditions, participate effectively, via the internal public spheres of parties and associations and publicity about their negotiations. Compromises would have to be legitimated by reference to it (p. 232).
- The welfare-state public sphere has two competing tendencies. Collapse of the civil-society public sphere makes room for manipulative publicity over the head of a mediatized public. Continuity with the liberal state preserves the mandate of critical publicity through the very organizations that mediatize the public (p. 232).
- The extent to which critical publicity prevails measures the democratization of the welfare state, defined as rationalization of social and political authority (p. 232).
- The welfare state abandons the liberal fiction that the public sphere became real once it was an organ of the state. Parliament was split from the start, opposing authority yet being an "authority" itself. Publicity must now see itself as a self-generating process competing with the contrary tendency (pp. 232–233).

**Two provisional remarks on conditions (pp. 233–235).** Whether the forces involved can be subjected to the publicity mandate leads back to a problem implicit in the bourgeois public sphere. Liberalism supposed that structural conflicts of interest and bureaucratic decisions could be reduced to a minimum. One aspect is technical, the other economic (p. 233).
1. *Bureaucracy.* Specialized expertise seems to escape supervision by debating bodies (Weber on parliament and executive). Against this, a counterpart has grown within administration: state bureaucracy can be controlled only by society's political bureaucracy, in parties and pressure groups (quoted, note 129). These would need internal control, and, for the technical aspect within one organization, it should not be impossible on structural grounds to relate bureaucratic decisions to quasi-parliamentary deliberation (pp. 233–234).
2. *Competing interests.* The problem is not primarily technical. The disappearance of publicity inside organizations and their flight from publicity result from unresolved plurality of interests, which makes it doubtful that a general interest can emerge as a criterion for public opinion. A structurally ineradicable antagonism would set narrow boundaries for a reorganized public sphere (p. 234).
   - Neutralizing power and rationalizing domination through discussion presuppose a possible consensus, an objective agreement among interests on universal, binding criteria. Otherwise pressure and counterpressure give at best an unstable equilibrium without rationality by the standard of the universal interest (p. 234).
   - Two tendencies could change this. First, high productive forces make it "not unrealistic to assume" that plural interests may lose their antagonistic edge, and the general interest is to bring about an "affluent society" (p. 234). Second, military destruction potential creates risks so total that divergent interests can be relativized, and the universal interest is articulated by negating the international "state of nature." Kant's "perpetual peace" is cited (pp. 234–235).

**Conclusion (p. 235).** Habermas says that minimizing bureaucratic decisions and relativizing conflicts of interest in light of a universal interest can "no longer be disqualified as simply utopian." The outcome of the struggle between critical and staged publicity stays open, and the ascendancy of critical publicity is "by no means certain." Unlike the liberal-era idea, it cannot be denounced as ideology. If anything, it brings to its conclusion the dialectic of that idea, which had been degraded into an ideology (p. 235).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
A town has 100 voters. Blocs: 40 fixed for party A, 40 fixed for party B, 20 floating (the group the text says is least informed, pp. 214–215). A campaign shows candidate A surrounded by his team, with no policy content (cf. p. 219). Suppose 12 of the 20 floaters respond to the image.
- A: 40 + 12 = 52. B: 40 + 8 = 48. Total: 52 + 48 = 100.
- A wins by 4 votes, yet (by construction) no voter changed mind through argument. Suppose the 80 fixed voters never discuss with opponents; the 12 reacted to a symbol.
- In the author's terms, the 52 summed votes make a result, not a public opinion. Neither condition (rational formation; discussion) was met (p. 221).

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — space of debating private people mediating state and society. Here its liberal form is described as decayed in the mass democracy (p. 211)
- [[refeudalization]] — public and private spheres interlock: political authorities take over social functions, societal powers take over political ones (p. 231); the process reaches into the political public sphere, where organizations seek compromises while staging publicity for a mediatized public (p. 232)
- [[nonpublic-opinion]] (new) — opinions held privately and uncorrected by public debate. They exert pressure on government but are not public opinion (pp. 218–221)
- [[manufactured-publicity]] (new) — publicity periodically staged by parties with advertising methods for acclamation (pp. 211, 216)
- [[opinion-climate]] (new) — an acclamation-prone mood replacing public opinion (p. 217)
- [[integration-culture]] (new) — mass-media consumer culture, unpolitical in intent but acting as ideology of conforming behavior (pp. 215–216)
- [[opinion-leader]] — in Katz and Lazarsfeld's finding as reported, usually a higher-status person (wealthier, better educated) through whom political opinion flows vertically (p. 213)
- [[two-step-flow]] — Habermas speaks of a two-tiered communication process mediated by opinion leaders; he does not use the term itself (p. 213)
- [[gestaltungsgarantie]] (new) — state guarantee of active shaping, needed so that rights of press, assembly, and association secure equal participation (pp. 227–228)
- [[social-welfare-state]] (new) — state committed to social rights, which must realize liberal order's aims by positive intervention (pp. 224–226)
- [[public-opinion-strict-sense]] (new) — opinion formed rationally and in discussion; both conditions are required in the author's test (p. 221)

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] vertical flow of opinion via opinion leaders (p. 213) ↔ communication-theory: two-step flow. Habermas cites Katz and Lazarsfeld as a trace of the liberal public sphere, then notes that the opinion leaders are the least inclined to submit their views seriously to discussion.
- [structural-analogy] "opinion climate instead of public opinion" (p. 217) ↔ spiral of silence: both separate perceived opinion distribution from reasoned opinion. Habermas does not mention the spiral concept here.
- [metaphor] political marketing selling "politics in an unpolitical way" (p. 216) ↔ framing and priming: both involve shaping criteria of evaluation, but Habermas works through advertising psychology, not frame analysis.

## Questions this raises
- Habermas says only a public of organized private people could, under today's conditions, participate effectively through publicity inside organizations. What would "quasi-parliamentary deliberation" look like inside a party or union, and how could outsiders check that it exists?
- He treats the affluence and survival-risk tendencies as reasons why interests might converge. Is a universal interest articulated "by negation" enough to serve as a standard for ordinary policy?
- If the relationship between popularity and genuine concessions limits manipulation (p. 218), can this be tested empirically, and what evidence would show whether manipulation or responsiveness dominates?
- How far do the voter-behavior findings Habermas uses (studies from his period) still describe fragmented media environments?
