# On the Concept of Public Opinion

## L0 — One line
Although "the" public opinion is a fiction, Habermas keeps the concept in a comparative sense: an opinion is more public the more critical publicity, working through intraorganizational public spheres, links informal opinions with formal, institutional ones.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks what "public opinion" can still mean when it is both a constitutional-law fiction and the target of manipulative publicity. He argues that the constitutional-law versions (an elite, or the parties and parliament) and the social-psychological version (group attitudes) each lose part of what the concept once meant. His proposal is to keep the concept only comparatively. An opinion is more public the more it emerges from the internal public spheres of parties and associations, and the more these communicate with the external publicist sphere. In the strict sense, public opinion needs critical publicity to mediate between informal, nonpublic opinions and formal, institutional ones. This gives an empirical yardstick for democratic integration in the welfare state.

**Prerequisites:** the earlier chapters' idea of the bourgeois public sphere and its collapse; the contrast between critical and manipulative ("staged") publicity; basic notions of mass media, parties and opinion research. Terms used above: *critical publicity* = openness that subjects power to public scrutiny; *manipulative publicity* = staged publicity that molds its addressee; *intraorganizational public sphere* = debate among the members of a party or association; *publicist sphere* = press and media exchange between societal organizations and state institutions; *quasi-public opinion* = institutionally authorized, formal opinion circulating among decision bodies and the political press; *publicly manifested opinion* = formal opinion staged through the media; *nonpublic opinion* = informal, personal opinion.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- Critical publicity and manipulative publicity are different in kind, not norm versus fact. Habermas says it might be put so: one is premised on public opinion, the other on nonpublic opinion (p. 236).
- Constitutional-law analysis has to maintain public opinion as an institutionalized fiction without being able to identify it directly in citizens' behaviour. Landshut notes that it is still the only accepted basis for legitimating political domination (pp. 237–238).
- Two constitutional-law escapes fail. The elite version saves rationality but loses universal access. The institutional version (parliament, parties) becomes "nondescript" (pp. 238–239).
- Opinion research dissolved the concept: public became mass, then group, and opinion became attitude, then any behaviour (pp. 240–241).
- Even Schmidtchen's survey definition, which ties opinion to domination, leaves it an object of domination and public administration. It ignores the political public sphere (pp. 243–244).
- Habermas distinguishes nonpublic (informal), quasi-public (institutional) and publicly manifested (media-staged) opinions (pp. 245–247).
- Public opinion in the strict sense arises only if critical publicity mediates the informal and formal domains. Today this is possible on a sociologically relevant scale only through intraorganizational public spheres (p. 248).
- Publicness is a matter of degree, so the concept is defined comparatively. Mills's public/mass contrast fits this model (pp. 248–250).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 24. Public opinion as a fiction of constitutional law, and its social-psychological liquidation
**Two publicities, one addressee (pp. 236–237).** "Public opinion" means different things depending on whether it is a *critical authority* (power must be subject to publicity) or an *object to be molded* by staged, manipulative publicity. Both compete in the public sphere and share "the" public opinion as addressee (p. 236). The two are not norm and fact. They belong to social configurations with opposing functional consequences, and each expects different public behaviour. Habermas says it might be put so: one version is premised on public opinion, the other on nonpublic opinion (p. 236). Critical publicity is also an institutionalized constitutional norm. Despite the structural transformation of its social basis, it still determines an important portion of the procedures to which the exercise and balance of power are factually bound. This publicity, with an addressee meeting its behavioural expectations, "exists": not the public as a whole, certainly, but surely a workable substitute. Where and to what extent is an empirical question (p. 237). The competing publicity has its own self-understanding, which borrows from its antagonist (p. 237).

**The constitutional fiction (pp. 237–238).** Habermas says the analysis of constitutional norms has to maintain the institutionalized fiction of a public opinion without being able to identify it directly as a real entity in citizens' behaviour. **Landshut's description:** public opinion is replaced by an in itself indeterminate, mood-dependent inclination, which "has the same effect as shifting cargo on a rolling ship" (p. 237). On the other hand, Landshut recalls that the constitutional institutions of large democratic welfare states count on an intact public opinion, because it is still the only accepted basis for legitimating political domination. In his words, the modern state presupposes popular sovereignty "as the principle of its own truth," and this "is supposed to be public opinion." Without this attribution, modern democracy "lacks the substance of its own truth" (pp. 237–238). Two paths follow (p. 238).

*Path 1, liberal (p. 238).* **Hennis's view:** opinion is harder than ever to form out of mass-media chaos. Special arrangements should give authority to the view of the "relatively best informed, most intelligent, and most moral citizens." **Habermas's criticism:** this saves publicity's rationality at the expense of its universality (general accessibility). Qualifications once gained in commerce and social labour become autonomous hierarchical qualities of representation, because the old basis can no longer be counted on; sociologically, such representativeness can no longer be determined in a satisfactory fashion under the existing conditions (p. 238).

*Path 2, institutional criteria (p. 239).* **Fraenkel's view:** public opinion is the view prevailing in parliament and authoritative for the government. It reigns but does not govern. **Leibholz's view:** the real actors are the parties. The majority party's will is identified with the *volonté générale*. Habermas adds that on this view nonpublic opinion attains existence as "public" only when processed through the parties. **Habermas's criticism:** both accept that unorganized opinion hardly matters in a mass democracy. But replacing the public with its agencies makes the concept "nondescript." We cannot tell whether such opinion arose from public communication or opinion management, so the fiction remains (p. 239). Empirical research then returns "with positivist pathos" to the behavioural level and liquidates the concept socio-psychologically (pp. 239–240).

*Intuition note.* Constitutional law needs public opinion as the basis for legitimating power, but it cannot be identified directly in citizens' behaviour. The elite fix gives up universal access. The parliament-and-party fix leaves the concept nondescript and still fictive.

**Social-psychological liquidation (pp. 240–242).**
- A treatise of 1879 already lamented public opinion as a slogan that spares the "intellectually lazy mass" from thinking (p. 240). **Schäffle** called it a "formless reaction on the part of the masses," meaning the views or preferences of any public. Tarde analysed "mass opinion," detached from political institutions and thus stripped of its "public" character (p. 240). Habermas says the normative spell of constitutional theory was thereby broken: public opinion became an object of social-psychological research, a product of a mass communication process neither bound by rules of public discussion nor concerned with political domination (p. 240).
- Dicey and Bryce kept the functional political context and were charged with empirical unreliability. **Bentley's objection:** no quantitative analysis of what each group really wants. His thesis: "There is no public opinion ... nor activity reflecting or representing the activity of a group or set of groups" (pp. 240–241).
- A later definition reads: attitudes on an issue held by members of the same social group (p. 241). Habermas traces the abstraction. The subject goes from "public" to "mass" to "group," dropping social, historical and institutional conditions. "Opinion" goes from expression on a controversial topic to expression of an attitude to attitude itself, and at the end any mode of behaviour. It includes custom and prejudice, the very things public opinion was once set against (p. 241).
- On this social-psychological view, a group opinion is considered "public" when subjectively it has come to prevail as the dominant one. The member holds a possibly erroneous notion of how many others share his view (pp. 241–242).

**Attempts at synthesis (p. 242).** **Lazarsfeld** insisted that the price of the social-psychological concept is too high if it is held at the expense of eliminating all essential sociological and politological elements, but then did no more than state the desirability of a "classical-empirical synthesis." Studying mass media and opinion processes is a first step. The theorem on the two-step flow of communication is a typical example of how even these studies of communication structures deal better with psychological relationships than with institutional conditions. A more significant step recalls the tie to domination, and Habermas quotes a source (not named on these pages): public opinion is the "corollary of domination" (p. 242). But neither the domination-oriented nor the group concept reaches the informal communication where the category once worked, and where it survives as constitutional fiction (p. 242).

**Schmidtchen (pp. 243–244).** His definition: all behaviours of population groups apt to modify or preserve the structures, practices and goals of the domination system (p. 243). **Habermas's criticism:** this ignores the political public sphere so completely that applying it could not even show that the sphere does not exist. Opinion becomes friction that can be diagnosed and manipulated. Opinion research feeds reliable soundings back to align the population with political goals. Public opinion stays an object of domination even when it forces concessions. On this definition (as Habermas describes it), it need not be verbalized or political, and its relation to domination arises "behind its back." Desires for cars and refrigerators count, if relevant to administration (pp. 243–244).

### 25. A sociological attempt at clarification
**Programme (pp. 244–245).** Survey material does not become public opinion merely by being politically considered. Feedback of group opinions cannot close the gap between the fiction and its liquidation (p. 244). A concept that is historically meaningful, normatively adequate, theoretically clear and empirically identifiable can be grounded only in the structural transformation of the public sphere (p. 244). The conflict between the two publicities is the gauge of democratization. Nonpublic opinions are numerous and "the" public opinion is a fiction. The concept is nevertheless kept *comparatively*, since the welfare state's constitutional reality is a process of realizing an effective political public sphere. Criteria for the degree of publicness follow from that. Such a specification is "the most reliable means" for comparable statements on democratic integration (pp. 244–245).

**Two domains of communication (pp. 245–248).**
1. *Informal, personal, nonpublic opinions*, in three levels of obligatoriness (p. 245):
   - culturally taken-for-granted things, not discussed (e.g. death penalty, sexual morality);
   - rarely discussed biographical experiences, results of "socialization shocks" (e.g. war and peace, security);
   - things made self-evident by the culture industry: often discussed, ephemeral, leisure-time products of media barrage.
   - The third level is shaped in a group-specific "exchange of tastes and preferences" in family, peer group and workplace, with opinion leadership. Like culturally given opinions, these form systems of norms demanding adaptation, but more through social control by "fashions" whose shifting rules need only temporary loyalty. Habermas says the first level may be called subliterary and the third, as it were, post-literary (pp. 245–246). In the eighteenth century, subjectivity needed a public and used literature. Now the culture industry supplies "degenerate" psychological literature as a service for private consumption. Such a group is as little a "public" as pre-bourgeois groups. Group research and opinion research developed together (p. 246). The media act directly or via opinion leaders. Some opinion leaders hold reflected opinions formed through literary and rational controversy. As long as such opinions stay outside the communication network of an intact public, they too count as nonpublic, though they clearly differ from the three other categories (p. 246).
2. *Quasi-public (formal) opinions*: institutionally authorized announcements, proclamations and speeches. They circulate in a narrow circle between the political press and advising or deciding bodies (cabinet, committees, party leadership, interest groups). They can be addressed to a wide public but do not meet the requirements of rational-critical debate in the liberal model; as authorized opinions they are always privileged and achieve no mutual correspondence with the unorganized "public" (p. 247).
3. *Publicly manifested opinions*: formal opinions that link the two domains through staged or manipulative media publicity. Their aim is a plebiscitary follower-mentality. They are distinguished from quasi-public ones (p. 247).
4. A rare link joins rational-critical publicist organs and few individuals forming opinions through literature. Such opinion is capable of becoming public but is actually nonpublic (p. 247). The network of a debating public has collapsed. Its opinion partly decomposed into informal opinions of private citizens and partly concentrated into institutions' formal opinions (p. 247). Unorganized people are addressed by publicly manifested opinions, not by public communication (pp. 247–248).

**Core result (p. 248).** An opinion is public "in the strict sense" only insofar as the two domains are mediated by a third, critical publicity. At sociological scale this is possible today only through private people's participation in formal communication in *intraorganizational public spheres*. A minority already belong to parties and associations under public law. Where such organizations allow an internal public sphere at all levels, not just among functionaries, private opinions and quasi-public opinion can correspond. This tendency may be insignificant for now, and its extent is empirical. It is nevertheless of decisive importance for a sociological theory of public opinion, since it provides the criteria for a dimension in which alone public opinion can be constituted under the conditions of a large democratic state committed to social rights (p. 248).

**Comparative standard (p. 248).** As informal opinions are channelled into the quasi-public circuit, that circuit gains publicity. Since public opinion is by no means simply "there" as such, and since at best one can isolate tendencies working toward generating it, it can be defined only comparatively. The degree of publicness is measured by two things: (a) how far the opinion emerges from the intraorganizational public sphere of members, and (b) how far that sphere communicates with the external publicist sphere. This external sphere is formed by media exchange between societal organizations and state institutions (p. 248).

*Intuition note.* Public opinion is not simply "there" to be found. It can only be defined as a matter of degree: how far people inside organizations can debate openly, and how far those debates connect with open exchange outside.

**Mills (p. 249).** *In a public:* (1) about as many express opinions as receive them; (2) communications allow immediate, effective answering back; (3) opinion readily finds outlet in action, even against authority; (4) authoritative institutions do not penetrate the public, which is autonomous. *In a mass:* (1) far fewer express than receive; (2) answering back is hard or impossible; (3) authorities control the channels of action; (4) institutions penetrate the mass. **Habermas's fit:** the mass criteria hold where the informal domain is linked to the formal only through manipulative or show publicity, so nonpublic opinions lack autonomy. Under welfare-state conditions a public's interconnection can arise only through critical publicity in intraorganizational spheres. This must link the "short-circuited" quasi-public circuit to the informal domain (pp. 249–250).

**Conclusion (p. 250).** If such public controversy prevailed, it could ease forcible consensus generated by pressure and temper forcible conflicts kept out of the public sphere. Conflict, consensus and domination are not untouched by historical development. In the structural transformation of the bourgeois public sphere, Habermas says, one can study how far and in what manner its ability to assume its proper function determines whether domination and power persist as a "negative constant," as it were, of history, or whether, as a historical category itself, domination is open to substantive change (p. 250).

## Toy example (ours, not from the text)
Habermas gives no formula, so this scorecard only illustrates his *comparative* logic with Mills's criterion (1). Take two associations, each with 100 members who all receive messages.
- **Association A**: 40 members speak in internal debates, so the expresser/receiver ratio is 40/100 = 0.40. Its executive reports to an open press, and the press replies.
- **Association B**: 2 members (the board) speak, so the ratio is 2/100 = 0.02. Its board only issues announcements.
- A's ratio is 20 times B's (0.40 ÷ 0.02 = 20). By Mills's criterion, A is closer to a "public," B closer to a "mass."
- Habermas's yardstick adds the external link. A's opinions can enter the media exchange between organizations and state, while B's reach the public only as staged proclamations. A's opinions are therefore *more* public than B's, a difference of degree, not a verdict that either is "the" public opinion.

## Questions this raises
- How would one measure "degree of intraorganizational publicity" empirically, given Habermas says the tendency's extent must be established empirically (p. 248)?
- Can the three layers of nonpublic opinion be told apart in survey data?
- Is Habermas's reliance on party and association members realistic if only a minority belong (p. 248)?
- Does his criticism of Schmidtchen apply to modern opinion-monitoring that feeds back into policy?
