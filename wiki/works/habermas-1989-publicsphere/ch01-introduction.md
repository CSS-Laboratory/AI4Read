# Introduction: Preliminary Demarcation of a Type of Bourgeois Public Sphere

## L0 — One line
The bourgeois public sphere arose in the 17th–18th centuries, after medieval "representative publicness" gave way to a state-and-society split, as private people began to use the press to judge public authority and readied themselves to make it justify itself.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks what "public" and "public sphere" mean, since modern usage mixes meanings from different periods. He sorts the layers historically. The Greek polis/oikos split gave a model. Feudal lords instead staged "representative publicness", a status display with no separate public realm. Early capitalism's traffic in goods and news and the mercantilist tax state developed together, and civil society arose as the counterpart of a depersonalized state. The press, first used by authorities and merchants, became a medium through which educated private people formed a reasoning public. They were then readying themselves to compel authority to justify itself before public opinion. The chapter is a preliminary historical demarcation of the type of public sphere under study.

**Prerequisites:** basic European history (feudalism, absolutism, mercantilism); the Greek polis; the idea of civil society as the sphere of private economic life.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- "Public" has several layered meanings (open to all; state-related; prestige; opinion-bearing public). Habermas hopes that a historical understanding of this "complex" will clarify the concept and help us understand our society (pp. 1–5).
- The Greek polis/oikos division, with household mastery as the condition of citizenship, is a stylized model whose normative force outlasted its social base (pp. 3–4).
- Feudal "representative publicness" was not a social realm but something like a status attribute. The lord displayed his rank "before" the people (pp. 7–8).
- Under absolutism, "public" came to mean state-related and "private" meant excluded from office. This is the first modern separation of public and private (p. 11).
- Early capitalist trade in commodities and news created horizontal dependencies that no longer fitted the estate order, and regular communication. A press in the strict sense existed only once regular news was accessible to the general public (pp. 15–17).
- The mercantilist tax state, with its permanent administration, and civil society arose together. Households' economic life gained public relevance (pp. 17–19).
- The press began as a tool of merchants and administrations. It then carried criticism and reviews. The bourgeois stratum, a reading public, turned from addressee into adversary of authority (pp. 20–26).

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 1 The Initial Question (pp. 1–5)
**The problem.** "Public" and "public sphere" carry meanings from different historical phases. Applied to today's industrial welfare-state society, they fuse into a "clouded amalgam" (p. 1). Ordinary language, jurisprudence, political science and sociology do not seem capable of replacing the traditional categories of public and private, public sphere and public opinion with more precise terms (p. 1). In public-opinion research, empirical techniques made the object dissolve into something elusive. Sociology nevertheless keeps studying public opinion (p. 1).

**Usages listed by Habermas (pp. 1–2):**
- Open to all, as opposed to closed or exclusive (public places) (p. 1).
- State-related: "public buildings" house state institutions. The state is the "public authority" because it promotes the common welfare (p. 2).
- Staged display: a "public reception" involves representation, and "publicity" contains an element of recognition. A "public reputation" is a further shift (p. 2).
- The most common meaning: the public as carrier of public opinion, whose function as critical judge makes court proceedings meaningfully public (p. 2).

**Shifts in the mass media (p. 2).** Publicity was originally a function of public opinion. It has become an attribute of whatever attracts public opinion, and public relations aim to produce it. "Public organs" may mean state organs or the press (p. 2).

**Etymology.** German *Öffentlichkeit* was formed in the 18th century from *öffentlich*, in analogy to "publicity." Heynatz could still find it objectionable at century's end (pp. 2–3). Habermas infers that the sphere presumably first emerged and took on its function then, at least in Germany. It was part of "civil society," which established itself as the realm of commodity exchange and social labour (p. 3).

**The Greek model.** The categories are of Greek origin with a Roman stamp (p. 3).
- The polis was common (*koine*) to free citizens. The oikos was where each person is in his own realm (*idia*) (p. 3).
- Public life (*bios politikos*) was constituted in discussion (*lexis*) and common action (*praxis*), not only in the agora (p. 3).
- The order rested on a slave economy. Citizens were freed from productive labour, but their private autonomy as household masters was the basis of participation. Status in the polis rested on being master of an oikos (p. 3).
- Life's necessities stayed hidden in the oikos. The polis was a realm of freedom and permanence where equals competed to excel (*aristoiein*) (pp. 3–4).

**Afterlife.** Since the Renaissance this model has had a normative power. Habermas says that what continued was the ideological template, not the social formation (p. 4). In the Middle Ages the categories survived in Roman-law definitions. They found technical-legal application again with the modern state and civil society (p. 4). For about a century the social foundations of the bourgeois public sphere have been decomposing. Its scope expands while its function becomes insignificant. Publicity is still an organizing principle of the political order, and apparently more than a leftover ideology (p. 4). A historical understanding promises a sociological clarification of the concept and a "systematic comprehension" of our society (pp. 4–5).

*Intuition note.* The words we use for public life come from several historical layers at once. Habermas hopes that sorting them historically will clarify the concept and help us understand our society.

### 2 Remarks on the Type of Representative Publicness (pp. 5–12)
**No public/private split in feudalism.**
- The Roman contrast was familiar in the Middle Ages but had no standard usage (p. 5).
- The precarious attempt to apply it to fief-based and manorial domination unintentionally provides evidence that no public/private opposition on the ancient or modern model existed (p. 5).
- The lord's position was not comparable to the private authority of the *oikodespotes* or *pater familias*. Manorial authority, though conceivable as a *jurisdictio*, could not be fitted readily into the contrast of *dominium* (private dominion) and *imperium* (public autonomy) (p. 5).
- No status defined the capacity in which private people could step into a public sphere. In Germany manorial authority became private land ownership only in the 18th century (p. 5).
- A quoted passage (note 6) says private and public powers were fused as emanations of one authority (p. 6).

**Germanic *gemeinlich/sunderlich*.**
- The "common" (public access to fountain and market square) contrasted with the "particular" (p. 6).
- Under feudalism the particular included holders of immunities and privileges. It became the core of the feudal regime and of what was "public" (p. 6).
- The parallelism of the Germanic and Roman categories was reversed: the common man became the private man, as in the "private" soldier (p. 6).
- In medieval documents "lordly" and *publicus* were synonyms, and *publicare* meant to claim for the lord (p. 6).

**Representative publicness defined.**
- By institutional criteria, no public sphere as a separate realm can be shown in High Medieval feudal society (p. 7).
- Lordship was nevertheless "publicly represented." This publicness was not a social realm but "something like a status attribute" (p. 7).
- The lord displayed himself as an embodiment of a "higher" power (p. 7).
- A quoted passage says representation makes the invisible visible through the lord's public presence. Only a being with an exalted quality (excellence, majesty, dignity, honour) is representable (p. 7).
- This differs from the delegate-type representation of a national assembly or a lawyer, because it is inseparable from the lord's concrete existence (p. 7).
- As long as the prince and the estates "were" the country and not just its representatives, they could represent it in a specific sense: they represented their lordship "before," not "for," the people (pp. 7–8).

**Its staging.**
- Staging was tied to insignia, dress, demeanour and rhetoric, a strict "noble" code that became the system of courtly virtues (p. 8).
- Virtue had to be embodied and capable of public representation. The joust is an example (p. 8).
- Unlike the Greek public sphere's competitive display of *arete*, knightly publicity was "completely unlike a sphere of political communication" (p. 8).
- It signalled status and had no particular location. It was displayed on feast days rather than court days (p. 8).
- Only ecclesiastical lords had a locale, the church. Liturgy and processions preserve the pattern. In a cited saying the Church is the last pillar of representation left, and an epigram says that it now represents nothing except representation itself (p. 8).
- The laity were "surroundings," excluded but needed. At the core lay an *arcanum* (Latin mass and Bible) (p. 9).

**Court humanism and baroque.**
- The pure form was reached at the French and Burgundian courts in the 15th century. Spanish ceremonial was its petrified version (p. 9).
- A new form came from early-capitalist northern Italy. Humanism entered courtly life. Under the influence of the *Cortegiano*, the humanistically cultivated courtier replaced the Christian knight, and similar types were the gentleman and the *honnête homme* (p. 9).
- The provincial nobility lost its power to represent. Representation concentrated at the prince's court, ending in baroque festivity (p. 9).
- The baroque festival had already lost its public character in the literal sense. It moved into the palace and park (the park appeared mid-17th century) (pp. 9–10).
- Representation still depended on people before whom it was displayed (p. 10).
- Only bourgeois notables' banquets became exclusive, behind closed doors (p. 10).
- A quoted passage contrasts the "homey" bourgeois ballroom with the "festive" palace living quarters. The king's *lever* and *coucher* show the intimate raised to public importance (p. 10).
- Under Louis XIV the concentration of representation at court peaked (p. 10).

**From representation to the state/society split.**
- The aristocratic "society" that emerged no longer had to represent its own lordliness, or at least no longer primarily; it served as a vehicle for representing the monarch (p. 10).
- Only after national territorial power states shattered the feudal foundations could the court nobility become the sphere of "good society" in the 18th century (pp. 10–11).
- The final form of representative publicness was reduced to the court. It was already an enclave within a society that was separating itself from the state. Now, for the first time, private and public spheres became separate in a specifically modern sense (p. 11).
- German *privat* appears only after the mid-16th century. It means not holding public office (p. 11).
- "Public" referred to the state, which under absolutism had objective existence against the ruler's person. State servants were "public persons" (p. 11).
- Authorities were said to serve the public welfare, while subjects pursued private interests (p. 11).

**Disintegration by the late 18th century (pp. 11–12).**
- The Church: religion became a private matter. "Freedom of religion" secured the first sphere of private autonomy. The Church remained a corporate body under public law (pp. 11–12).
- The prince: the public budget was separated from his private holdings. Bureaucracy, army and partly the administration of justice became independent public institutions (p. 12).
- The estates: they developed into parliament and judicial organs (p. 12).
- Occupational groups: they developed into "civil society," the genuine domain of private autonomy opposed to the state (p. 12).

*Intuition note.* Feudal publicness was something a lord *had* and displayed, like an aura, not a place where people talked. "Public" and "private" in the modern sense appear when authority becomes an impersonal state and everyone outside office becomes "private."

### Excursus: The Demise of the Representative Publicness — *Wilhelm Meister* (pp. 12–14)
- Representative forms stayed in force into the early 19th century, especially in backward Germany (p. 12).
- **Wilhelm (letter to his brother-in-law Werner):** the nobleman, in society, court or camp, treats his person and bearing as part of his possessions. A burgher "may acquire merit," but his personal qualities are lost (p. 12). The nobleman, Wilhelm says, is a public person, and his capacities, talents and wealth all seem extras (p. 13).
- **Habermas's reading:** the nobleman was authority insofar as he made it present (p. 13).
- Goethe used "public person" in the traditional representative sense, though contemporary language already used it for a state servant (p. 13).
- The "person" was at once turned into the "cultured personality." Strictly, the nobleman here serves as a pretext for a thoroughly bourgeois idea of free self-actualization, marked by neohumanism (p. 13).
- Goethe's observation is significant for Habermas: the bourgeoisie could no longer create a representative publicness for itself. Habermas glosses this: the nobleman was what he represented, the bourgeois what he produced (p. 13).
- **Wilhelm (as quoted):** the nobleman "has a right to seem," whereas the burgher is "compelled to be" (p. 13). Habermas adds that Goethe advised asking the bourgeois not "What art thou?" but what he has, and says Nietzsche's later aristocratic pretensions adopted a statement that a man proves himself not by what he can do but by who he is (p. 13).
- Wilhelm wants to be "a public person." As a bourgeois, he does not want to feign nobility and seeks the stage as a substitute for publicity (p. 14).
- Habermas suggests the equivocation of "cultured personality" may have allowed theatre to equal representation. He adds that, since the perception of representation's disintegration was so accurate and the inclination to belong to it so strong, there must be more to the matter than a simple equivocation (p. 14).
- Wilhelm's public already carried a different public sphere, so his theatrical mission "had to fail." The theatre had become a platform of the bourgeois public sphere. Figaro had entered the stage, and with him, as Napoleon is reported to have said, the revolution (p. 14).

### 3 On the Genesis of the Bourgeois Public Sphere (pp. 14–26)
**Early capitalism: conservative yet ambivalent.**
- Finance and trade capitalism spread from the 13th century from northern Italy. At first the old power structure absorbed it, as in the assimilation of humanism to courtly culture in Florence (pp. 14–15).
- It was ambivalent. It stabilized the estate power structure, but it also unleashed the elements within which that structure would one day dissolve. These were the traffic in commodities and news (p. 15).

**Commodities.**
- Local town markets were guild-regulated and served domination of the surrounding area more than free exchange (p. 15).
- Long-distance trade created markets that became periodic fairs and, with credit techniques, stock exchanges. Antwerp became a "permanent trade fair" in 1531 (p. 15).
- This produced "horizontal economic dependencies" that could no longer fit the vertical dependencies of the estate system (p. 15).
- The political order stayed unthreatened as long as the old ruling stratum took part only as consumers (pp. 15–16).

**News.**
- From the 14th century merchants organized a guild-like correspondence system and the first regular mail routes (p. 16).
- Postal services and the press institutionalized regular contact almost simultaneously with stock markets (p. 16).
- Merchants were satisfied with a system limiting information to insiders. The urban and court chanceries preferred one serving only the needs of administration. Neither had a stake in public information. The "news letters" of newsdealers were private (p. 16).
- A press in the strict sense exists only once regular news is accessible to the general public. This occurred only at the end of the 17th century. Until then publicity of representation was not fundamentally threatened (p. 16).

**Mercantilist phase.**
- Commodity and news traffic showed revolutionary power only in the mercantilist phase, when national and territorial economies took shape (p. 17).
- Merchant companies on expanded capital, later stock companies, needed political guarantees. Markets became "institutional products" of political and military effort (p. 17).
- The home town was replaced as base by the state territory (the process Heckscher calls the nationalization of the town-based economy) (p. 17).
- The modern state was a state based on taxation, with the treasury bureaucracy at its core. Separating the prince's personal holdings from the state's was, in Habermas's phrase, paradigmatic of the objectification of personal relations of domination (p. 17).
- Local administrations came under state control (Justices of the Peace in Britain, superintendents on the continent) (pp. 17–18).

**Public authority and the public.**
- With the decline of representation, the sphere of public authority took shape in a permanent administration and a standing army (p. 18).
- "Public" in the narrower sense meant "state-related": an apparatus with regulated jurisdictions and a monopoly on legitimate coercion (p. 18).
- The private people, without office and excluded from authority, were its addressees and formed "the public" (p. 18).

**Mercantilism and civil society.**
- Colonial trade monopolies came to serve the home commercial economy. Manufacturers' interests came to prevail over traders'. Dobb remarks that in 17th-century mercantilist literature foreign trade no longer counted per se as a source of wealth, but only insofar as it aided employment of the country's population (pp. 18–19).
- Estate privileges were replaced by royal personal privileges. Production was regulated in detail (p. 19).
- **Habermas:** "Civil society came into existence as the corollary of a depersonalized state authority" (p. 19).
- Household-bound activities entered the public sphere (p. 19).
- **Schumpeter's observation** (that a private sphere was born in contrast to the public) captures only one side, the privatization of economic reproduction. It overlooks the new public relevance. Private economic activity now faced a commodity market expanded under public direction and was of general interest (p. 19).
- **Arendt** calls this the rise of the "social": mutual dependence for sheer survival takes on public significance (p. 19).
- "Economic" once meant the head-of-household's tasks. It acquired its modern sense of profit-oriented business, and "commercial economics" replaced the oikos (p. 20).
- Cameralism placed this economics within "police-science." This shows how closely civil society's private sphere was tied to public authority (p. 20).

**The press.**
- Political journals appeared weekly, then daily by the mid-17th century (p. 20).
- Only a trickle of private-correspondence news passed through the "news letters" filter into print, because recipients did not want it public (p. 20).
- Merchants were called *custodes novellarum*, since public reporting depended on their private exchange of news (pp. 20–21).
- News was filtered by merchants' informal control and by official censorship. Traditional broadsheet items (miracles, murders, fires) continued (p. 21).
- News itself became a commodity. Each item had a price, so selling to more people raised profit. This gave reason to print part of it and sell it anonymously (p. 21).
- **The authorities' interest weighed far more than the sellers' profit motive:** they used the press to promulgate ordinances, so addressees became "the public." Court news transposed publicity of representation into the new form (pp. 21–22).
- Vienna's 1769 ordinance had the authorities compile weekly the decrees judged suitable for the public and send them to the editor (p. 22).
- Official gazettes: Richelieu's patronage of Renaudot's *Gazette* (1631); the London *Gazette* from 1665. Advertisers often passed into government hands (p. 22).
- A Prussian cabinet order of 1727 described the institution as meant to be useful to the public, and the Palatine-Bavarian government addressed a "commercial public" (p. 22).
- Authorities addressed "the" public in principle, i.e. all subjects. Usually they did not reach the common man, but at best the "educated classes" (p. 22).

**The bourgeois stratum.**
- A new "bourgeois" stratum arose with the modern state. Its core was the officials, mostly jurists, plus doctors, pastors, officers, professors and scholars. Below them in a hierarchy came schoolteachers and scribes (p. 23).
- The old "burghers" (craftsmen, shopkeepers) declined with the towns. Great merchants linked up with the state (p. 23).
- Capitalists and scholars were "bourgeois" but not burghers in the traditional sense (p. 23).
- This stratum was the real carrier of the public, "from the outset a reading public." By the close of the Baroque period it could no longer be integrated in toto into noble culture. This created a tension between "town" and "court" (p. 23).
- State authorities evoked in this stratum a resonance. The *publicum* became aware of itself as the authority's opponent (p. 23).
- The public sphere of civil society developed to the extent that public concern about the private sphere was no longer confined to the authorities but was seen by subjects as properly theirs (p. 23).

**The critical zone.**
- Mercantilism did not favour state enterprise. It promoted private businesses run in a capitalist manner, in a bureaucratic way. This created an ambivalence of public regulation and private initiative (p. 24).
- Broad strata were affected as consumers. Taxes, duties and official interventions in household finance, not dress codes, became the target of a "critical sphere" (p. 24).
- Society separated a private domain from public authority and made life's reproduction a subject of public interest. The zone of continuous administrative contact therefore became "critical" also in the sense that it provoked the judgment of a public using its reason (p. 24).
- The public could take up this challenge all the better because it required merely a change in the function of the instrument the administration had already used, the press (p. 24).

**From information to criticism.**
- From the last third of the 17th century, periodicals offered instruction, criticism and reviews. Examples are the *Journal des Savants* (1665), *Acta Eruditorum* (1682) and Thomasius's *Monatsgespräche* (1688) (pp. 24–25).
- In the first half of the 18th century learned articles entered the daily press (p. 25).
- When a Hanover paper (from 1729) printed articles and reviews, the Prussian King took control. Professors in law, medicine and philosophy had to submit pieces in turn; in general the scholars were to inform the public of useful truths (p. 25).
- Bourgeois writers still used reason at the ruler's behest. Soon they were to think their own thoughts, directed against the authorities (p. 25).
- **Frederick II (rescript of 1784):** a private person has no right to pass public judgment on sovereigns' actions or to publish such reports, on the ground that he lacks complete knowledge of circumstances and motives (p. 25).
- Habermas takes Prussia as a static model of what in France and especially Britain had become fluid early in the century (p. 25).
- The public sphere was casting itself loose as a forum where private people, forming a public, readied themselves "to compel public authority to legitimate itself before public opinion" (pp. 25–26).
- The *publicum* became the public, the *subjectum* the reasoning subject, and the receiver of regulations the authorities' adversary (p. 26).

**Word history (p. 26).**
- British "public" spread from the mid-17th century, replacing "world" or "mankind." French *le public* and German *Publikum* (spreading from Berlin) replaced "the world of readers" (*Lesewelt*) and "world" (p. 26).
- Adelung distinguishes the public gathered around a speaker from the *Lesewelt*. Both are a "critical (*richtend*) public" (p. 26).
- Whatever is submitted to the public's judgment gains *Publizität*. English "publicity" was borrowed from French at the end of the 17th century. In Germany the word appears in the 18th (p. 26).
- *Öffentliche Meinung* formed in the second half of the 18th century in analogy to *opinion publique*. British "public opinion" arose at about the same time, though "general opinion" had been used long before (p. 26).

*Intuition note.* Authorities and news-sellers made news public for their own reasons (merchants themselves wanted news kept among insiders). Once a reading bourgeois stratum existed, the same press let private people judge the authorities. A channel for orders became a channel for criticism.

## Toy example
*(Our own illustration; not from the text.)* It illustrates the news-as-commodity step (p. 21), in which publication follows from selling to more buyers.

A newsdealer sells one item as a handwritten newsletter for 6 coins to each of 5 merchants. Assume copying costs nothing. Revenue = 5 × 6 = 30 coins.

He then prints the same item once, at a fixed cost of 20 coins, and sells it anonymously for 1 coin to 100 buyers. Revenue = 100 × 1 = 100. Profit = 100 − 20 = 80 coins, versus 30.

Printing is now more profitable (80 > 30). Publication here needs no wish to inform the public. The profit motive alone suffices.

Habermas adds a second push. If the authorities then add their own ordinances to the sheet, the buyers become "the public" in the proper sense (p. 21).

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — here a historical category. In its bourgeois form it is a forum where private people, as a public, were readying themselves to compel public authority to legitimate itself before public opinion (pp. 25–26). Habermas also describes a Greek model and feudal representative publicness, which differ from it (pp. 3, 7).
- [[representative-publicness]] (new) — feudal publicity as a status attribute. The lord displays his rank before the people, and there is no separate social realm (p. 7).
- [[public-authority]] (new) — "public" in the narrow sense: state-related, with permanent administration, army and monopoly of legitimate coercion. Private people are its addressees (p. 18).
- [[civil-society]] (new) — the domain of private autonomy and commodity exchange, which arises as the corollary of depersonalized state authority (pp. 3, 19).
- [[private-person-reasoning-public]] (new) — the *subjectum* turned reasoning subject and the *publicum* turned public (p. 26).
- [[polis-oikos]] (new) — the Greek division between the common sphere of free citizens and the household (pp. 3–4).
- [[mercantilism-and-state-formation]] (new) — the phase in which national territorial states, tax bureaucracy and trade policy took shape and within which state and civil society became differentiated (pp. 17–20).
- [[news-as-commodity]] (new) — the traffic in news became subject to market laws, and its sale motivated printing (p. 21).
- [[publicity-publicness]] (new) — in Habermas's account, originally a function of public opinion and the principle that matters submitted to the public's judgment gain *Publizität*. It later became an attribute of whatever attracts opinion (pp. 2, 26).

## Canonical instances
- [[greek-polis-model]] — the polis/oikos split as a stylized ideological template (pp. 3–4).
- [[wilhelm-meister-letter]] — Habermas reads in Wilhelm's letter the bourgeois's inability to represent and the theatre's turn into a platform for the bourgeois public sphere (pp. 12–14).
- [[prussian-and-viennese-press-control]] — the 1727 Prussian order, the 1729 Hanover paper episode, the Vienna 1769 ordinance and Frederick II's 1784 rescript (pp. 22, 25).
- [[antwerp-permanent-fair]] — an example of the rise of stock-exchange-type markets (p. 15).
- [[custodes-novellarum]] — merchants as gatekeepers of news into print (pp. 20–21).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] Merchants' and censors' filtering of which private news becomes public (pp. 20–21) ↔ communication-theory: selection and emphasis in framing — both concern what gets into the public channel.
- [metaphor] "Representation before the people" (p. 8) ↔ ritual view of communication: display that maintains a status order rather than transmitting information.
- [structural-analogy] News's commercial logic (selling to more people) ↔ mass-media economics: wider audience as profit motive.

## Questions this raises
- If the public sphere's scope is expanding while its function declines (p. 4), what specifically in later chapters explains the loss of function?
- Habermas says the bourgeois stratum that carried the public was a reading public from the start (p. 23). How far did it extend beyond educated, propertied men?
- The Greek public sphere and the bourgeois public sphere both rest on a private base (household, civil society). What differs in how the private base conditions participation?
- Habermas says the press began as an instrument of authority and commerce (pp. 21–22). What would show that its change of function was real rather than just rhetoric?
