# The Transformation of the Public Sphere's Political Function (part 1/2)

## L0 — One line
Once the press and the other publicist institutions were commercialized and organized interests and the state apparatus bargained political compromises outside parliament, publicity changed from a critical principle wielded by a reasoning public into a principle of managed integration, with staged publicity aimed at consent (a refeudalized public sphere).

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
Habermas asks how the public sphere's political function changed once its institutions were commercialized and became complexes of societal power. His answer has several parts. Commercialization, concentration and advertising turned the press from an amplifier of the public's debate into a business. Public relations then staged consent for private interests. Parties, associations and bureaucracy bargain compromises outside parliament and use publicity to gain prestige, not to invite critique. He rejects attempts to restore the liberal public sphere by shrinking publicity. He instead proposes extending publicity requirements to parties, associations and politically influential media, so that organizations control each other publicly.

**Prerequisites:** the liberal (bourgeois) model of the public sphere from earlier chapters; the public/private distinction; basic ideas of parties, parliament, market competition and oligopoly.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- The press passed through phases: news trade, literary journalism, then advertising-based business. Each phase changed what the press did for the public. (pp. 181–185)
- Commercialization made the press a gate through which privileged private interests enter the public sphere. Editors lost autonomy to publishers, and concentration produced chains and trusts. (pp. 185–187)
- Radio and television were put under public or semipublic control in several countries. Otherwise, Habermas says, their publicist function could not have been sufficiently protected from the encroachment of their capitalistic one. Private ownership, once the liberal guarantee of independence, in many ways threatened the critical functions of publicist institutions. (p. 188)
- The flood of advertising is not explained by market liberalization. Scientifically directed marketing became necessary only as oligopolistic restriction of the market increased. Entertainment and advertising train consumption. (pp. 189–192)
- Public relations ("opinion management") differs from advertising. It addresses citizens as a public, hides private intent behind a pretended general interest, and "engineers consent". (pp. 193–195)
- Interest compromises are bargained outside parliament. Special-interest associations gain power on account of their private character, and associations and parties use publicity work to build prestige and acclamatory consent rather than critical debate. Habermas calls this refeudalization. (pp. 197–201)
- The dominant party type is now the mass integration party. De facto the delegate receives an imperative mandate from his party, and parliament tends to become a place where predetermined decisions are put on record. This contrasts with the liberal-era party of dignitaries and the free mandate. Courts show a similar distortion. (pp. 201–207)
- Restoration by cutting back publicity cannot work. Habermas proposes publicity inside and between organizations, with mutual control by rival organizations. (pp. 207–210)

## L3 — Walkthrough

### 20. From the Journalism of Private Men of Letters to the Public Consumer Services of the Mass Media: The Public Sphere as a Platform for Advertising

**Opening thesis.** The shift in the function of publicity rests on a shift in the function of the public sphere, which is best documented in the press. Two things happen. As the press is commercialized, the line between circulating a commodity and exchanging communications among a public is levelled, so the public/private line blurs. But to the extent that only certain political guarantees could safeguard the continued independence of its institutions, the public sphere ceased to be exclusively part of the private domain (p. 181).

**Phase 1: news business.** The newspaper trade grew out of private correspondence as a small handicraft business with modest profit-seeking. The publisher cared about it purely as a business, and his work was organizing and collating the flow of news (pp. 181–182). When papers began to carry ideologies and viewpoints and met competition from literary journalism, a political element joined the economic one. Habermas cites Bücher. Papers became carriers and leaders of public opinion and instruments of party politics, an editorial function was inserted between gathering and publishing news, and the publisher became a "dealer in public opinion" (p. 182).

**Phase 2: literary journalism.** The crucial turn came earlier, with the continental "scholarly journals" and the British moral weeklies and political journals. Individual authors used the periodical to print critical-rational reflections with pedagogical intent. Commercial purpose receded and such ventures often lost money. In Britain they were often pet projects of the money-aristocracy. On the continent they came from scholars and men of letters, who first bore the economic risk themselves (p. 182).
- Entrepreneurial functions passed gradually to publishers, but the editor remained "editor" and "author" in one. Around 1800 editors often still shared the profits (pp. 182–183).
- Old-style dailies without debate kept the traditional entrepreneur-type. Dumont in 1805 was author, editor, publisher and printer of the *Kölnische Zeitung* (p. 183).
- Cotta appointed Posselt as editor, which divided publicist and economic functions. The lead article spread in the daily press in the first half of the century. Cotta's *Allgemeine Zeitung* stayed subsidized for decades, so conviction still outweighed business (p. 183).
- Publishers gave the press a commercial basis without commercializing it. A press that grew out of the public's reasoning remained an institution of that public, acting as a "transmitter and amplifier". It was no longer a mere vehicle of information, but not yet a medium for culture as an object of consumption (p. 183).
- Revolution is the prototype: in Paris in 1789 between February and May, 450 clubs and over 200 journals sprang up (pp. 183–184).
- Before the political public sphere was legalized, a journal's appearance and survival meant joining the fight over how much freedom public opinion would get. Old news journals were censored but could not resist in their own columns. An authoritarian state treated the press as a trade. The editorializing press defended the public's critical function, and profit was secondary at most (p. 184).

**Phase 3: advertising-based business.** Only after the bourgeois constitutional state legalized a political public sphere was the press freed from taking ideological sides. It could then pursue profit. Around the 1830s in Britain, France and the United States, the way was paved for this transition. With much lower prices per copy and many more buyers, publishers could sell more advertising space. Bücher's statement that the paper becomes an enterprise producing advertising space as a commodity, made marketable by an editorial section, refers to this phase (p. 184). The press became a private enterprise again, now at the level of advanced capitalism. By mid-century some papers were stock companies (p. 185).

- Commercial reorganization soon became a necessity. The technical apparatus demanded more capital and more commercial risk, and subordinated editorial policy to business efficiency. Examples are the *Times* on a high-speed press in 1814 and, later, the telegraph (p. 185).
- Habermas says the history of the big dailies in the second half of the century proves that the press became manipulable to the extent that it was commercialized (p. 185).
- Once editorial and advertising sections were marketed together, the press ceased to be an institution of private people as a public. It became an institution of certain participants, as private individuals, and "the gate through which privileged private interests invaded the public sphere" (p. 185).
- Editing became specialized. Selecting and screening news mattered more than the lead article. Since the 1870s a paper's rank depends more on its publishers than on its publicists, and publishers expect editors to follow orders in the profit interest (pp. 185–186).
- The political party press also restricts editors. Individualistic publicist-politicians (e.g. Wirth's *Deutsche Tribune*) lasted to mid-century. Party-controlled presses began in the first half of the century in Britain and France and in the 1860s in Germany. The editor then answered to a supervisory committee and became an employee under directives (p. 186).

**Concentration.** In the last quarter of the century the first newspaper trusts formed: Hearst, Northcliffe, Ullstein and Mosse (p. 186). Transmission technology and monopolistic news agencies homogenized news. Shared plates, insert factories and matrices (from about 1870–80 in Anglo-Saxon countries) homogenized the smaller papers, which often became dependent on city papers (pp. 186–187). Concentration seems smaller for the press than for the twentieth-century media (film, radio, television). Their capital needs and publicist power were so large that in some countries they were put under government direction or control from the start. This turned private institutions of a public of private people into public corporations (*öffentliche Anstalten*) (p. 187). Habermas gives examples from news agencies: Reuters, the Agence France Presse and the Deutsche Presse-Agentur (pp. 187–188).
- Newspapers and film stayed largely private. Radio and television were organized as public or semipublic corporations in Britain, France and Germany. Otherwise their publicist function could not have been protected from their capitalistic one. In the United States they went to private business (p. 188).

**The reversal.** In the liberal model, publicist institutions were protected from public authority because they were in private hands. Now, because they are commercialized and concentrated, they have become complexes of societal power, and staying private threatens their critical functions (p. 188).
- The mass media reach much further than the liberal press. They have also moved out of the public sphere into the sphere of commodity exchange. The more their publicity effect grows, the more open they are to pressure from private interests (p. 188).
- Formerly the press transmitted and amplified the public's debate. Now "this debate gets shaped by the mass media to begin with" (p. 188).
- Privileged private interests are not *eo ipso* representative of private people as the public. The liberal separation left competition among private interests to the market, outside the conflict of opinions. When the public sphere became a field for advertising, property owners as private persons acted directly on private persons as the public (pp. 188–189).
- The change was met halfway by press commercialization. It was also pushed by advertising needs that arose independently out of economic configurations (p. 189).

> *Intuition note.* The liberal model has the press pass on the public's debate. Once the press is commercialized and concentrated, it is a business that private interests can use. Habermas's conclusion is that the debate is now shaped by the mass media to begin with. The liberal safeguard, private ownership, in many ways becomes the threat.

**Why advertising grew.** It is not explained by market liberalization. Scientifically directed marketing became necessary only as oligopolistic restriction of the market increased (p. 189). Mass-production plants lose flexibility: output is dictated by the capacity of the unified machine process (quoted, p. 189). A long-term sales strategy was therefore needed, and competition through price gave way to indirect competition. Firms generate markets of clienteles tied to their products (p. 189). The falling transparency of the market, usually taken as the motive for advertising, is "in good part" its consequence. Brand products are hard to compare because their exchange value is partly set by psychological manipulation. Habermas links big business and oligopolistic restriction to the "soap operas": a flood of advertisement pervading the media's integration-oriented culture (pp. 189–190).

**Advertising's growth and reach.** The word *réclame* appeared in France in 1820. Advertising is a phenomenon of advanced capitalism, and it reached notable scope only in the later nineteenth century. Better firms long disliked it. In the eighteenth century ads filled about a twentieth of intelligence-journal space and almost exclusively concerned curiosities (p. 190). Agencies arose mid-century, with Hansenstein's in Germany in 1855. Agencies bought press space on subscription and thereby brought an important part of the press under their control (p. 190). Figures given for West Germany:
- Over 2,000 advertising firms. Agencies handle only about a third of ad spending, and enterprises spend the other two-thirds directly (p. 190).
- In 1956 about 3 billion DM, about 3% of private expenditure. In 1955 advertising was 1.3% of GNP, against 1.9% in Britain and 2.3% in the US (pp. 190–191).
- In 1957 at least half of regular daily-paper readers also read ads. 65% of the radio audience tuned into programs designed for advertising, and almost a third of them claimed to do so daily (p. 191).
- Media exposure in general rises with social position, but here the relation was reversed. Lower-status groups were reached more. Habermas explains this by the trickling down of formerly elite commodities and by consumption as symbolic self-elevation (p. 191).

Advertising also created its own publications. In 1955 every fifth household in the Federal Republic held at least one company catalogue. In-house and customer magazines were almost half of all periodicals published for the West German market, and they made up over a quarter of all copies (p. 191). Entertainment and programs, even their non-commercial parts, stimulate consumption and channel it. Habermas cites Riesman, who says that the future occupation of all children is to be skilled consumers. The "culture of harmony" invites exchanging opinions about consumer goods and exerts "soft compulsion" of consumption training (pp. 191–192).

**Why the transformation followed.** An invasion of advertising need not have transformed the public sphere. It could have been separated off, like the classified section (p. 192). But a public sphere split off in this way never crystallized. Presentation of privileged private interests fused with political interests from the start. When horizontal competition between commodity owners entered via advertising, capitalism's competitive basis was already drawn into party conflict. Vertical class competition had also entered the arena, and the public sphere was torn between the "two nations". So the public presentation of private interests took on political significance. Large-scale advertising almost always also became more than business advertising, if only because it was the most important factor in the financial calculations of papers and journals (pp. 192–193). Economic advertising became aware of its political character only in public relations (p. 193).

**Public relations.** PR comes from the United States. It began with Ivy Lee, who developed publicity techniques at a policy-making level to defend big business (Standard Oil, the Pennsylvania Railroad) against reformers. Between the wars large firms adjusted strategy to PR. In the US it fitted the climate of national consensus after 1940. It spread widely, including in Europe, only after the war, and in the advanced West it has come to dominate the public sphere over the last decade (p. 193).
- "Opinion management" differs from advertising because it expressly claims the public sphere as a political arena. Ads address private people as consumers. PR addresses "public opinion", the citizens as a public and not directly as consumers (p. 193).
- The sender hides business intentions behind the role of someone concerned with the public welfare. This borrows from the classic idea of a public of reasoning private people and uses its legitimations. Accepted functions of the public sphere get integrated into competition among organized private interests (p. 193).
- PR goes beyond the sales pitch. It invades the process of public opinion by creating news events or exploiting attention-getting events. It uses feature and picture publicity, human-interest topics, dramatic presentation and calculated stereotypes. It aims at reorienting opinion through new authorities or symbols that will be accepted. A textbook recommends twenty methods for making or creating news (pp. 193–194).
- PR fuses news and advertising. The ad must not be recognizable as private self-presentation. The object gets the authority of public interest, creating the illusion that a public of critical private people freely forms its opinion about it. "Engineering of consent" is the central task. The consumers' readiness involves the false consciousness that they contribute responsibly to public opinion (p. 194).
- Because publicity arrives by a detour through feigned general interest, it creates for the firm, branch or system a "quasi-political credit", a respect like that shown toward public authority (p. 194).

**Consensus without rationality.** The resulting consensus has little in common with unanimity reached by mutual enlightenment. The "general interest" that alone allows rational agreement disappears as privileged private interests adopt it for themselves. The old basis for convergence of opinion has collapsed, and a new one is not brought about merely because private interests hold on to a faked version of the general interest (p. 195).
- Where consent is made by opinion-molding services under a sham public interest, criteria of rationality are lacking. Critique gives way to conformity with presented persons, and consent coincides with good will evoked by publicity (p. 195).
- Publicity once meant exposing political domination to the public use of reason. It now adds up reactions of an uncommitted friendly disposition. To the extent that public relations shapes it, the public sphere of civil society again takes on feudal features: suppliers display showy pomp before customers ready to follow (p. 195).
- A second sense of refeudalization is that mass entertainment integrated with advertising subjects even the state to its code. Private firms evoke in customers the idea that they act as citizens in consumption. The state therefore has to "address" its citizens like consumers, and public authority competes for publicity (p. 195).

> *Intuition note.* Advertising says "buy this" to consumers. PR says "this is in the public interest" to citizens, while serving a private firm. Because the sender hides its aim, the audience feels free and reasoned although consent has been prepared for it.

### 21. The Transmuted Function of the Principle of Publicity

**Second apparatus.** At the close of the 1920s a congress of the German Sociological Society (topic: public opinion) acknowledged the "journalistic activation" of offices, parties and organizations (p. 196). **Brinkmann's view:** the free press and the "official releases" of bureaucracies are opposed, and the information bureaus are an adversary and perhaps even master of the press. Habermas calls this antithesis ill-considered, because bureaucratic PR uses the existing mass media and strengthens its position through them. He calls the observation itself sound. A second apparatus arose beside the great publicist institutions to meet the publicity needs of state and interest associations. **Brinkmann:** it offers diverse "opinions" but seeks to shape and hold sway over public opinion in a way that is essentially anything but public (p. 196). Its forms deviate from the liberal ideal. State bureaucracy borrowed them from big private enterprises and interest associations, and only in conjunction with these did administrations acquire a "publicist character" (p. 196).

**Bureaucracy and the transfer of power.** In the welfare state the bureaucracy's power grew. A less obtrusive countervailing process moved power from government to societal groups (pp. 196–197).
- In its "discretionary structuring" the bureaucracy itself became a producer, dealer and distributor. The executive had to act in a way that complemented, and partly replaced, authoritarian rule from above with an arrangement with the "public". This meant unofficial participation of special-interest associations and routine transfer of some tasks to them (p. 197).
- **Werner Weber (as cited):** large areas became components of an "estate system" of administration that functions alongside the state (p. 197).
- Agreements were made outside parliament, bypassing the state's institutionalized public sphere, but both sides prepared and accompanied them noisily with publicity work. The public sphere and parliament lost some bridging functions (p. 197).
- Bureaucracy, associations and parties (the "transformers" between state and society) were strengthened. The public sphere, largely stripped of its original functions, was now made under their patronage to contribute differently to integrating state and society (p. 197).

**Compromise by bargaining.** The earlier arrangement: the market settled the many substantive decisions of a commercial society "apolitically". The public stayed free of competition among individual private interests, so political compromise could be handled by rational debate and cast in general laws (p. 198).
- When collectively organized private interests had to take political form, conflicts that could not be settled in classical parliamentary form came into the public sphere. Their settlements bore the marks of the market. Compromise was "haggled out" through pressure and counterpressure, resting on an unstable balance of power between state apparatus and interest groups (p. 198).
- "Bargaining" arose alongside the older forms, hierarchy and democracy. The forum expanded, but the haggling moved to extraparliamentary sites, because balancing interests was still tied to the liberal claim of legitimation by the common welfare. The move came formally, by delegating state competences to organizations, or informally, by de facto shifts (p. 198).

**Collective bargaining.** Where lasting equilibrium or "peace" between employers and employees cannot be expected, removing coercive state arbitration can create an autonomous domain for quasi-political power. The two sides no longer act in private autonomy. They act within the public sphere as part of the political realm and so are subject to the democratic demand for publicity. But the resulting compromises are kept away from parliamentary legislation and stay outside the state's institutionalized public sphere (p. 199).

**Factual divestiture.** Official removal of competence is paralleled, to a greater extent, by a factual one. State–society integration required temporary compromises and direct exchange of particularist favors without the political public sphere (p. 199). Special-interest associations and parties remain, in principle, private associations. Many lack legal standing, yet they fill public positions and perform functions of the political public sphere. The associations' stated aim is to transform many private interests into a common public interest, and to show the association's special interest credibly as the general interest (pp. 199–200).
- Special-interest associations have power on account of, not in spite of, their private character. They can manipulate public opinion without being controlled by it (p. 200).
- From a "mediatized" public they need acclamatory consent, or at least benevolent passivity without obligations. They need it for compromise-making that is mostly internal manoeuvring but requires public credit (p. 200).

**Publicity work and representation.** Publicity work seeks prestige for one's own position without making the matter to be compromised a topic of public discussion. Organizations display "representation". Habermas cites a passage saying that associations under public law want to show their members publicly, more than to represent them formally to the outside (p. 200). Old representative publicity is not revived. Yet it leaves traits in the refeudalized public sphere, whose mark is, on **Schelsky's observation**, that large organizers in state and society manage the propagation of their own positions (p. 200). The aura of personally represented authority returns. PR concerns opinion as reputation. The public sphere becomes a court where prestige is displayed, not where critical debate takes place (pp. 200–201).

**Publicity reversed.** Publicity once had to be won against monarchs' secret politics, to subject persons and issues to critical debate. Today publicity is achieved with the help of the secret politics of interest groups. It earns prestige for a person or issue and makes it ready for acclamatory assent in a climate of "nonpublic opinion" (p. 201).
- The phrase "publicity work" shows that a public sphere must now be brought about deliberately, case by case. Occasions for identification have to be created: the public sphere must be made, because it is no longer simply there (p. 201). **Altmann** calls this "communification" (p. 201).
- It mobilizes inarticulate readiness to assent, which can if needed become plebiscitary acclamation. The new public sphere still relates to the civil-society one insofar as its institutional forms of legitimation remain in force. Staged publicity has political efficacy only to the extent that it can credibly suggest, or cash in on, a capital of potential votes. The parties do the cashing in (p. 201).

> *Intuition note.* Old publicity put power on display so that people could argue about it. New publicity displays organizations so that people will approve them without arguing. Prestige replaces debate.

**From the dignitary party to the integration party.** Habermas draws on Max Weber's account of the liberal-era party of dignitaries (*Honoratiorenpartei*) (p. 201).
- Educated and propertied men founded local clubs and voter associations. Few members were professional politicians and politics was an honorific avocation. The press was the one permanent institution. Communication flowed freely between local discussion centers and parliament. The loose union fit a power-free flow of communication within a single public (pp. 201–202).
- Parties understood themselves as "formations of opinion" (Haym, Rochau). **Treitschke** abandoned the idea of a party of opinion: class interests are bound to party lines more closely than parties admit. **Naumann** at century's end demanded a class party for liberals (p. 202).
- Meanwhile the institutions of social-convivial exchange lost power or collapsed. As the press moved to commercial mass circulation, parties were reorganized on a mass basis. Equal citizenship rights changed party structure. The new parties were supralocal, bureaucratic, and aimed at ideological integration and mobilization of the voting masses (pp. 202–203). In Britain Gladstone introduced the caucus system. Local committees lost importance, and systematic propaganda replaced gatherings to hold delegates to account. This propaganda had "the Janus face" of enlightenment and control, information and advertising, pedagogy and manipulation (p. 203).
- Interdependence of political events increased. The public sphere lost its communal basis, its clear boundary toward the private sphere and the "world public", and its transparency (p. 203).
- An "integration party", often not clearly distinguished from class parties, took hold of voters temporarily to get acclamation. It did not try to remedy their political immaturity. It is today the dominant type. The decisive issue for these parties is who controls the coercive and educational means to influence voting. The parties are not in the hands of the public but of those who control the apparatus (p. 203).

**The delegate's status.** The idea of parliamentarianism implied rejection of the imperative mandate. In 1745 a member of the House of Commons declared that the elected gentleman is the representative, or attorney, of the people of England. Burke and Blackstone developed the doctrine of the free mandate, which entered all bourgeois constitutions (p. 204).
- In the liberal state the free mandate was complemented by opinion formation within a reasoning public. Sociologically it meant less the representative's independence as such than a guarantee of parity among private people in the public. The delegate was in closer contact with constituents than now. Protection of his independence was meant to keep parliament within the public. It was not meant to give him a privileged status, only to stop delegation from making him underprivileged (p. 204).
- That direct contact was lost as parties became integral parts of a system of special-interest associations under public law. Parties are today neither class parties nor interest groups themselves. Their interlocking with organized interests gives them a paramount position, and parliament is degraded to a committee for airing party lines (pp. 204–205). **Quoted (source not named in the text):** the member becomes an "organizational-technical intermediary" within the party, obeying its directives in conflict (p. 205).
- **Kirchheimer:** advocate-type MPs give way to functionaries. Parliament now includes party functionaries and many representatives of associations (corporate lawyers, lobbyists, specialists). The individual delegate takes part in forming his party's majority but finally decides by the party line (p. 205).
- Enforcing the principle that, in certain contexts, minorities of delegates must adopt majority opinions turns pressure for compromise into a constraint that enables external unity. De facto, the delegate gets an imperative mandate from his party. Parliament tends to become a place where instruction-bound appointees register predetermined decisions. Carl Schmitt noted a similar trend in Weimar (p. 205).

**Parliament as stage.** Rubber-stamping of resolutions haggled out behind closed doors shows party consensus outward (p. 205). **Quoted (source not named):** parliament is no longer an assembly of wise men chosen by privileged strata, who sought to convince each other by argument in public discussion. **Friesenhahn's description:** it is a "public rostrum" where government and parties justify their program to the nation, which takes part through radio and television, and the opposition attacks it openly (p. 206). Habermas says this captures only one side, the expansion of publicity, not the transmutation of its function (p. 206).
- Parliamentary deliberation once ensured continuity between pre-parliamentary and parliamentary discussion. It was part and center of the public as a whole. It no longer is, and cannot be, because the public sphere's structure has changed (p. 206).
- **Quoted passage (source not named):** if broadcasting Bundestag sessions is meant to give listeners participation in the representatives' work, radio and television are inadequate and disrupt it by biasing the debates. Real deliberation has moved into committees and party caucuses, and parliamentary deliberation has become secondary to "documentation" (p. 206).
- Before the expanded public the proceedings are stylized into a show. Publicity loses its critical function for staged display, and arguments become symbols one can answer only by identifying with them (p. 206).

**Managed integration, and the courts.** From a critical principle wielded by the public, publicity becomes a principle of managed integration, wielded by staging agencies: the administration, special-interest groups and above all the parties (pp. 206–207). Consumer culture distorts publicity in the judicial realm as plebiscitary distortion does in parliament. Trials that the mass media document and hawk serve the packaging of proceedings for a mass culture of consumers, not control of justice by citizens (p. 207).

**Revisionist reactions.** After Napoleon, Welcker and Feuerbach championed publicity in parliament and courts, together with a free political daily press. Today people want to shield these bodies from a plebiscitary public. The Bundestag's Senior Council recommended ending direct transmission. Criminal lawyers and judges demand that radio and television be kept out of courtrooms. The aim is to reduce publicity to guaranteeing public access for those physically present (p. 207).
- Habermas notes the distinction between public sphere and publicity. **Eberhard Schmitt** (quoted) would keep it even in trials of persons of contemporary significance. The public may learn what is alleged, the findings and the sentence, but facial expressions of defendants and witnesses are irrelevant to any legitimate interest in information (pp. 207–208).
- **Habermas's verdict:** such reactive measures cannot contribute toward reinstating the public sphere in its original function. Trying to restore the liberal public sphere by cutting back its plebiscitarily expanded form will only serve to weaken even more the residual functions that remain (p. 208).

**Habermas's constructive proposal.** The welfare-state constitution still binds state organs to publicity, so a permanent process of opinion and consensus formation can be influential at least as a freedom-guaranteeing corrective to the exercise of power. A quoted passage (source not named) adds that its manifestations may legitimately consist in power not legally sanctioned, presuming they are fully public and confront state power publicly (p. 208).
- A public sphere taken over by societal organizations can perform critique and control, beyond joining compromises, only to the extent that it is "radically subjected" to publicity requirements and again becomes a public sphere in the strict sense (p. 208).
- Under changed conditions the intention of the classical demands can be protected from "reactionary misdirection" if they are supplemented by unorthodox demands. Publicity should be extended to institutions that have lived off others' publicity: primarily parties, but also politically influential mass media and public-law special-interest associations (p. 209).
- To satisfy these functions, the organizations' inner structure must first follow the principle of publicity, with intra-party and intra-association democracy that allows unhampered communication and rational-critical debate. The intraorganizational public sphere must also be linked to the public sphere of the whole public (p. 209).
- Their activities also need far-reaching publicity: pressure on the state, use of power against each other, and relations of dependence and economic intertwining. For example, organizations should disclose the source and use of their funds (p. 209).
- In Germany the constitution provides the means to extend publicity requirements from parties to associations under public law. Political journalism, like all institutions with privileged influence through display and manipulation, should also be subject to the demand. Sociologically, this makes the democratization of societal organizations engaged in state-related activity a topic of discussion (pp. 209–210).
- All publicistically influential institutions are bound to publicity because turning societal power into political power needs criticism and control as much as legitimate political domination does (p. 210).

**Conclusion of this part.** Habermas says the idea of publicity, once the rationalization of domination through critical debate of private people, is today realizable only as a rationalization of the exercise of societal and political power. This rationalization is limited because of the plurality of organized private interests. It would work through mutual control by rival organizations committed to publicity in their internal structure and in their dealings with each other and the state (p. 210). Only in proportion to such advances can a political public sphere again evolve, a society with "real presence in a coherent and permanent process of integration" (quoted from a cited source, p. 210). The welfare-state public sphere lags behind. The public sphere made for elections and mobilized only at intervals lets PR publicity dominate, and organizations install it the more they evade the demand for publicity. A quoted election study (source not named) notes the advantage for a party of having no members, acting only at election time like an advertising firm. The pages end mid-sentence, in a sentence about a process of public communication evolving in the medium of the parties (p. 210).

> *Intuition note.* Habermas holds that cutting back publicity cannot restore the liberal public sphere. He proposes instead that organizations that wield power be open to scrutiny and scrutinize each other. This is a limited, "rationalized" version of the old ideal.

## Toy example (ours, not from the text; all numbers invented)
Main idea: when the price per copy falls and circulation grows, advertising revenue becomes decisive. This is the economic root of the "gate" claim on p. 185.

- **Liberal-era paper:** 1,000 copies × \$0.10 = \$100 sales. Costs \$120. Result: \$100 − \$120 = −\$20, a loss covered by the editor's or a patron's money (as with the subsidized journals, p. 182).
- **Ad-based paper:** 10,000 copies × \$0.02 = \$200 sales. Ad space sold: \$250. Revenue = \$200 + \$250 = \$450. Costs (bigger plant) \$400. Result: +\$50. Advertising share = 250 ÷ 450 ≈ 55.6%.
- **Leverage:** suppose one advertiser who buys \$150 of space leaves. Revenue = \$450 − \$150 = \$300. Result = \$300 − \$400 = −\$100. The paper's survival now depends on keeping such buyers content. This illustrates how private interests can gain a channel into the public sphere (p. 185).

## Concepts
- [[public-sphere]] — here, the realm of private persons as a public, debating critically. Its institutions are changed by commercialization and interest politics (p. 181).
- [[refeudalization]] — the public sphere takes on feudal traits: pomp, aura of personal prestige and acclamation replace critical debate (p. 195, p. 200).
- [[publicity-principle]] (new) — publicity as exposure of domination to the public use of reason. It has become a principle of managed integration (pp. 195, 206–207).
- [[commercialization-of-the-press]] (new) — the press becomes a business selling ad space. This makes it manipulable and a gate for private interests (pp. 184–185).
- [[public-relations-opinion-management]] (new) — PR addresses citizens as a public, hides private intent behind the public interest, and creates news; its aim is "engineering of consent" (pp. 193–194).
- [[representative-publicity]] (new) — the old publicity of personally represented authority; modern publicity has affinity with it, but it is not revived (p. 200).
- [[integration-party]] (new) — a mass party that temporarily mobilizes voters for acclamation without remedying political immaturity (p. 203).
- [[free-vs-imperative-mandate]] (new) — the free mandate guaranteed parity within the public. De facto the delegate now has a party imperative mandate (pp. 204–205).
- [[plebiscitary-acclamation]] (new) — inarticulate readiness to assent, mobilized by staged publicity and cashed in by parties (p. 201).

## Canonical instances
- [[buecher-three-phases-of-the-press]] — news trade, literary journalism, advertising business (pp. 182–184).
- [[paris-1789-journals]] — 450 clubs and over 200 journals; prototype of the press as the public's own institution (pp. 183–184).
- [[ivy-lee-public-relations]] — origin of PR in defending Standard Oil and the Pennsylvania Railroad (p. 193).
- [[riesman-consumption-training]] — entertainment and advertising train the consumer from childhood (p. 192).
- [[brinkmann-second-apparatus]] — the late-1920s observation of the "journalistic activation" of bureaucracies and associations (p. 196).
- [[collective-bargaining-publicity]] — a case of quasi-political power outside parliament (p. 199).
- [[bundestag-and-courtroom-broadcasting]] — reactive efforts to shield bodies from a plebiscitary public (pp. 206–208).

## Bridges (candidates)
- [structural-analogy] PR "making news" through staged events ↔ communication-theory: agenda setting and framing. Both concern selecting and emphasizing what the public attends to, though Habermas's is a critical-theory account.
- [structural-analogy] Entertainment and advertising as "consumption training" ↔ communication-theory: cultivation. Both claim steady, cumulative exposure to a medium's world shapes outlooks.
- [metaphor] Staged acclamation via "good will" ↔ communication-theory: the media as a stage for display. Publicity as display and prestige-building, not a model of causal effects.

## Questions this raises
- How far do the German figures (1955–57) and the 1920s–50s sources generalize to other countries and later media?
- If publicity is extended to parties, associations and media, who enforces it, and how would intra-organizational democracy be tested?
- Habermas says the public's debate is now shaped by the mass media to begin with, and that the media are open to pressure from private interests. How could this claim be tested empirically against audiences' ability to resist?
- What does the remainder of the chapter (part 2) say about elections and public opinion, which this part leaves mid-sentence?
