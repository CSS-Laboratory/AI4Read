# Introduction by Thomas McCarthy (to Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*)

## L0 — One line
McCarthy presents Habermas's book as a historical-sociological account of the emergence, transformation and disintegration of the bourgeois public sphere. He poses the question whether such a sphere can be reconstituted under radically different conditions, and he thinks it fair to say that no single work has fused the book's disparate lines of inquiry into a whole of comparable insight and power.

## L1 — The gist (≤ 120 words)
This is McCarthy's introduction, not Habermas's own argument. McCarthy summarises the book. A liberal public sphere arose between civil society and the state. The bourgeoisie used it to hold absolutist authority to account through critical discussion. Later, as capitalism developed, state and society became intertwined, and in welfare-state democracies the sphere became a field of competition among organised interests. The press and broadcasting served less to inform and debate than to manage consensus and promote consumer culture. The ideal of rational public discussion stays central to democratic theory. McCarthy then lists seven scholarly audiences for whom the book is timely. He thinks no single work has fused these lines of inquiry into a whole of comparable power.

**Prerequisites:** only a general idea of absolutism, liberalism, the welfare state and mass media; no maths.

## L2 — Key ideas (5–10 bullets)
- McCarthy: the book is a historical-sociological study that combines sociology, economics, law, political science and social and cultural history (pdf 10).
- The liberal public sphere lay between civil society and the state, and critical discussion of general interest was institutionally guaranteed in it. It took shape under a developing market economy (pdf 10).
- The bourgeoisie replaced a sphere in which the ruler's power was *represented* before the people with one in which the people publicly *monitored* state authority (pdf 10).
- Habermas, in McCarthy's report, traces the interdependent development of literary and political self-consciousness, weaving together the novel, journalism, reading societies, salons and coffee houses (pdf 10–11).
- The sphere carried an internal contradiction: the "basic rights of man" in its constitutive catalogue were in fact restricted to a certain class of men (pdf 11).
- Expansion of the public and the intertwining of state and society ended the liberal sphere. Opinion is taken into account, but its character is indicated by the terms in which it is addressed ("public opinion research", "publicity", "public relations work"). Media serve less as organs of information and debate than as technologies for managing consensus (pdf 11).
- The ideal of rationalising authority through informed discussion survives. McCarthy says one could do worse than view Habermas's later work through the question "is democracy possible?" (pdf 11).
- McCarthy argues the book speaks to feminist, political, literary, sociological, media and legal scholarship, and that, in having fused these lines of inquiry, it "remains paradigmatic" (pdf 12–13).

## L3 — Walkthrough

**Opening: why the translation matters (pdf 10).** McCarthy says there is no good reason the book was not translated sooner. He thinks earlier translation would likely have helped Anglo-American readers see how Habermas's later, more abstract work grew out of the concrete issues raised here (pdf 10).

**Summary of the book's account (pdf 10–11).** All of the following is McCarthy's report of Habermas.
- *Genre.* A historical-sociological account of the emergence, transformation and disintegration of the bourgeois public sphere. It tries to grasp the preconditions, structures, functions and inner tensions of this central domain of modern society (pdf 10).
- *The liberal public sphere.* It stood between civil society and the state, and critical public discussion of matters of general interest was institutionally guaranteed there. It formed in the circumstances of a developing market economy (pdf 10).
- *Clash with absolutism.* The emerging bourgeoisie met the "arcane and bureaucratic" practices of the absolutist state. It gradually replaced a sphere where the ruler's power was merely represented before the people with one where state authority was monitored by the people through informed, critical discourse (pdf 10).
- *Literary and political self-consciousness.* Habermas traces their interdependent growth. He weaves together the rise of the novel, literary and political journalism, and reading societies, salons and coffee houses. McCarthy calls the result a *Bildungsroman* (a story of formative development) of this "child of the eighteenth century" (pdf 10–11).
- *Contradiction.* The sphere's catalogue of "basic rights of man" was in fact restricted to a certain class of men (pdf 11).
- *Expansion and tension.* With further capitalist development the public body grew beyond the bourgeoisie. It now included groups systematically disadvantaged by the free market, who sought state regulation and compensation (pdf 11).
- *End of the liberal sphere.* The resulting intertwining of state and society in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries meant its end (pdf 11).
- *Welfare-state public sphere.* It is a field of competition among conflicting interests. Organisations representing diverse constituencies negotiate and compromise among themselves and with officials, while excluding the public from their proceedings (pdf 11).
- *Public opinion today.* It is taken into account, but not as unrestricted public discussion. Its character and function are indicated by the terms in which it is addressed: "public opinion research," "publicity," "public relations work" (pdf 11).
- *Media.* Press and broadcasting serve less as organs of public information and debate than as technologies for managing consensus and promoting consumer culture (pdf 11).

*Intuition note.* One way to read the story is as a change in who takes part. First, informed critical discussion by the public (in practice restricted to a certain class) monitors the state. Later, organised interests negotiate while excluding the public, and public opinion is addressed through research and publicity.

**The surviving idea and the guiding question (pdf 11).** McCarthy distinguishes the historical structures from the idea. The structures reflected the particular interests that produced them. The idea it claimed to embody was rationalising public authority through the institutionalised influence of informed discussion and reasoned agreement, and it remains central to democratic theory (pdf 11). In a post-liberal era the classical model is "no longer sociopolitically feasible", so the question is whether the sphere can be reconstituted under radically different conditions. McCarthy adds: "In short, is democracy possible?" He says one could do worse than view Habermas's work of the twenty-five years since *Strukturwandel* through this question, but it is not the only or best reason to publish now (pdf 11).

**Why the book suits the present (pdf 12).** McCarthy lists seven groups and what he expects each to gain:
1. Feminist theorists: a case study in the sociostructural transformation of a classic form of the public/private division.
2. Political theorists: an empirical-theoretical account of the interdependencies that shaped and limited the democratic practice of justice, which they will value after the Rawls-centred discussion.
3. Literary critics dissatisfied with purely textual approaches: a cultural-sociological account of the literary public sphere.
4. Comparative-historical sociologists: macro-structural analysis combined with interpretive access to actors' shifting meanings.
5. Political sociologists: participation, economy-polity relations and public opinion in a new light.
6. Communications and media researchers: the account of literary journalism and of the press becoming one of several mass media of a consumer society, and a framework for future research.
7. Legal theorists: a way to analyse gaps between claim and reality that avoids the dead end of pure deconstruction (pdf 12).

**Closing judgement (pdf 12–13).** Significant work has been done in all these areas since publication. McCarthy still thinks it fair to say that no single work or body of work has fused these lines of inquiry into a unified whole of comparable insight and power. In this respect the book "remains paradigmatic" (pdf 12–13).

## Toy example
*Our own illustration, not from the text.* A town has 100 residents and 4 interest organisations.
- *Liberal-style discussion:* all 100 may take part in debating a road plan. Share of residents who can take part = 100/100 = 100%.
- *Organisation bargaining:* only 4 delegates, one per organisation, negotiate with an official. Share in the room = 4/100 = 4%. The other 96 are told the result through "publicity".

The numbers are invented. They only show the contrast McCarthy draws between open discussion and negotiation among organisations that excludes the public (pdf 11).
