# Habermas, J. (1962/1989). *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere: An Inquiry into a Category of Bourgeois Society* (T. Burger, Trans.). MIT Press.

## In one paragraph
Habermas traces how a public of private people arose between state and civil society in the 17th–18th centuries. In coffee houses, salons and the press, these people used reasoned debate to make public authority justify itself. He shows that this bourgeois public sphere was both an ideal and an ideology. Its claim to universal access rested on the fiction that property owners and human beings were the same. When state and society became intertwined, the sphere lost its basis. A debating public became a culture-consuming public. The commercial press, public relations and bargaining among organized interests turned critical publicity into staged, manipulative publicity ("refeudalization"). Habermas keeps "public opinion" only as a comparative yardstick. He suggests that critical publicity inside parties and associations is the open route to renewal.

## The path through the work
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch00-introduction]] — McCarthy presents Habermas's book as a historical-sociological account of the emergence, transformation and disintegration of the bourgeois public sphere. He poses the question whether such a sphere can be reconstituted under radically different conditions, and he thinks it fair to say that no single work has fused the book's disparate lines of inquiry into a whole of comparable insight and power.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch01-introduction]] — The bourgeois public sphere arose in the 17th–18th centuries, after medieval "representative publicness" gave way to a state-and-society split, as private people began to use the press to judge public authority and readied themselves to make it justify itself.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch02-social]] — In Habermas's account, the eighteenth-century bourgeois public sphere developed out of a literary public, itself shaped by the audience-oriented intimacy of the conjugal family, into a political public that opposed rule by secrecy and demanded general, rational law, resting on the "fictitious identity" of property owner and human being.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch03-political]] — The bourgeois public sphere took on political functions first in Britain, then on the Continent, and it was credible as the organizing principle of the constitutional state only while liberal-capitalist conditions made "property owner" and "human being" seem the same, so its idea of dissolving domination into reason was both ideology and more than ideology.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch04-bourgeois-p1]] — The idea of "public opinion" took shape when "opinion" (uncertain judgment, reputation) was merged with "critique," and Kant gave it its classic form as the principle of publicity linking politics to morality, while tying it to a property-owning public.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch04-bourgeois-p2]] — Habermas argues that Hegel and Marx unmasked the bourgeois public sphere as ideology, while Mill and Tocqueville, once the public widened, came to treat public opinion as a power to be limited, so neither the liberal nor the socialist model fitted what the public sphere became.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch05-social]] — When state and society interpenetrated, the private sphere and the reasoning public that grew out of it lost their basis, and critical publicity gave way to manipulative publicity and culture consumption.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch06-transformation-p1]] — Once the press and the other publicist institutions were commercialized and organized interests and the state apparatus bargained political compromises outside parliament, publicity changed from a critical principle wielded by a reasoning public into a principle of managed integration, with staged publicity aimed at consent (a refeudalized public sphere).
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch06-transformation-p2]] — In the welfare-state mass democracy, elections rest on periodically staged publicity that yields only "nonpublic opinion", while the social-welfare state's own constitutional logic points to a different, still open, route: critical publicity inside organizations.
- [[habermas-1989-publicsphere/ch07-concept]] — Although "the" public opinion is a fiction, Habermas keeps the concept in a comparative sense: an opinion is more public the more critical publicity, working through intraorganizational public spheres, links informal opinions with formal, institutional ones.

## Big ideas across units
- **A public arises from the split between state and civil society.** Representative publicness gives way to a reasoning public of private people, helped by the press, the market and the mercantilist state. Units: ch00, ch01, ch02, ch03.
- **The literary public prepares the political public.** The intimate sphere of the family, coffee houses and salons, letters, novels and criticism shape a public that later demands general, rational law. Units: ch02, ch03.
- **Idea and ideology.** The sphere claimed universal access but rested on the "fictitious identity" of property owner and human being. Hegel and Marx criticize it, and Mill and Tocqueville react to that critique. Units: ch00, ch02, ch03, ch04-p1, ch04-p2.
- **The publicity principle and the concept of public opinion.** The term has a history, from "opinion" through the physiocrats, Rousseau and Kant to Hegel, Marx, Mill and Tocqueville. It is later dissolved by opinion research and redefined comparatively. Units: ch03, ch04-p1, ch04-p2, ch06-p2, ch07.
- **State and society interpenetrate.** The liberal separation dissolves. Private law and public law merge, the family becomes a consumer unit, and organized interests take on public functions. Units: ch04-p2 (Tocqueville's fear of bureaucracy), ch05, ch06-p1.
- **From a debating public to a consuming public.** Commercialized culture and media discourage "talking back" and split the public into specialists and consumers. Units: ch05, ch06-p1, ch06-p2.
- **Manipulative publicity and refeudalization.** Advertising, public relations, integration parties and staged elections aim at acclamation, not critique. Units: ch00, ch05, ch06-p1, ch06-p2, ch07.
- **Critical publicity inside organizations as the open route.** Mutual public control among organizations, and participatory rights in the welfare state, hold out a possible renewal. Units: ch06-p1, ch06-p2, ch07.

## How to read it
- **Essential:** ch02 (the model of the bourgeois public sphere), ch05 (the structural transformation), ch06-p1 and ch06-p2 (the changed political function), and ch07 (the closing concept of public opinion). These carry the central historical-normative argument.
- **Useful background:** ch01 gives the historical layers of "public" and the origins of civil society. ch03 gives the model case of Britain and the idea-and-ideology contradiction.
- **More specialist:** ch04-p1 and ch04-p2 are intellectual history, covering Kant's publicity principle, the history of the term "public opinion", and Hegel, Marx, Mill and Tocqueville. They are the likeliest to skip on a first pass, but they explain why the bourgeois model was later seen as ideology.
- **Optional frame:** ch00 is McCarthy's introduction, not Habermas's argument. It can be read first as a map or last as a summary.
- **Dependencies:**
  - ch03 builds on ch02's literary public and family.
  - ch04-p2 continues ch04-p1.
  - ch05 assumes the bourgeois model from earlier chapters, and its argument is developed in ch06.
  - ch06-p2 continues ch06-p1.
  - ch07 assumes the earlier chapters' bourgeois public sphere and its collapse.
